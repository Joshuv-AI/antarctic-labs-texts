Investigation and characterization of the GhPPO gene family and its expression in response to fibre cell growth and biotic stress in Gossypium hirsutum L.
Authors: Li-Min Lv, Hui-Hui Xie, Dong-Yun Zuo, You-Ping Zhang, Hai-Liang Cheng, Qiao-Lian Wang, Zhi-Fang Li, Guo-Li Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCotton is an important crop that provides a natural fiber source for the textile industry. Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) is a type of ubiquitous metalloproteinase in plants, which play crucial roles in regulating plant growth and development, as well as plant resistance against biotic and abiotic stresses. Although the whole genome sequence of Gossypium hirsutum L. (G. hirsutum) has long been published, this gene family has not yet been well studied in cotton.ResultsIn the present paper, we have identified the 51 PPO genes in cotton, and deeply analyzed 15 PPO genes in G. hirsutum, which are named GhPPOs. We found that three conserved domains - tyrosinase, DWL, and KFDV, were present in all 15 GhPPOs. We also analyzed the molecular characterization, phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, chromosome locations, 3D structure, and expression profiles of all the PPO genes identified. Based on the phylogenetic analysis, these PPO genes were classified into five groups (I, II, III, IV and V). The 15 GhPPO genes are distributed across eight chromosomes, and gene structure analysis showed that nine GhPPOs are devoid of introns. Analysis of cis-acting elements in GhPPOs promoters indicated their potential fundamental roles in response to plant growth, development, and stresses. Transcript profiling and RT-qPCR analysis revealed that GhPPO3/11 showed high expression levels during cotton fibre elongation, and GhPPO3/5 can be induced in response to cotton bollworm infestation. Moreover, the 3-dimensional structures of GhPPO3/ 11 and GhPPO3/ 5 were predicted by homolog modeling, and we found that all of them possess similarity spatial structures, with two independent domains, which could be the structural basis for performing biological functions.ConclusionsThese findings not only provide valuable insights into the phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, cis-acting elements, and functional characteristics of upland cotton PPOs, but also shed light on their potential roles in cotton fibre elongation and response to biotic stresses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11906-1.Keywords: PPO gene family, Cotton fibre, Expression profiles, Biotic stress, 3D-structure, Gossypium hirsutum

Introduction

Polyphenol oxidases (PPOs) are binuclear, copper-included enzymes, which are found in most of eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms [1]. At present, research on PPOs has made significant advancements in understanding their genetic basis, biological functions, and structural characteristics. According to the amino acids coordinating copper ions (CuA and CuB), which bind oxygen molecules in the active site, PPOs are classified as type III copper enzymes [2]. Based on the specificity of the substrates catalyzed by PPOs, they are divided into three categories: tyrosinases (E.C. 1.14.18.1), catechol oxidases (E.C. 1.10.3.1), and laccases (E.C. 1.10.3.2) [3]. At present, the three-dimensional crystal structures of PPO have been obtained in many plant species, such as catechol oxidase in sweet potato [4], the structure of jrTYR [5], the structure of aurone synthase from Coreopsis grandiflora (AUS1) [6], and the crystal structure of Grenache Berry PPO [7], among others. The elucidation of the three-dimensional structure has provided compelling evidence for an in-depth understanding of the functional mechanisms of PPOs.

The subcellular localization characteristics of proteins determine their functions in cellular compartments. Therefore, research on the subcellular localization of plant PPOs has widely attracted the attention of researchers. In general, three conserved regions—an N-terminal cTP, a CuA and CuB domain, and a C-terminus extension—can be found in almost all plant PPOs [8]. In higher plants, most of PPOs are localized in the plastids, such as the thylakoid membrane of chloroplasts and in vesicles of other non-green plastids [3, 9, 10]. Upon transforming Nicotiana tabacum with the PPO-GFP fusion gene derived from Yali, we were able to observe green fluorescent signals within the chloroplasts of the transformed plant cells, providing experimental evidence that the PPO gene expressed in chloroplasts [11]. Additionally, it is also reported that PPOs exist in the the mitochondria, as well as in soluble/non-membrane-bound forms, cell walls, microsomes, or cytosol [2]. For example, the existence of membrane-bound, non-membrane-bound, and soluble fractions of PPOs has been reported in apple fruit [2]. All the above results demonstrate the complexity of the subcellular localization of PPOs.

Many reports have shown that the functions of PPOs respond to biotic and abiotic stress in plants, such as the herbivores, pathogens, drought, cold, heat, and salt stress and so on [12, 13]. Recently, numerous PPO genes have been identified and characterized in a wide range of plant species, fungi, and bacteria [6, 12], such as the PPO gene from apple (Malus domestica), named MdPPO1 [14, 15], and two PPO genes, Sb06PPO1 and Sb06PPO2, identified in sorghum. The expression of Sb06PPO1 in sorghum is regulated by hormones and abiotic stresses [16]. SlPPO1, the tomato PPO gene, is involved in the browning reaction and the synthesis of antimicrobial compounds [12, 17]. In addition, PPO genes have been shown to play a role in pathogenicity and defense mechanisms in fungi [12]. Meanwhile, tomato PPO plays a role in resistance to both cotton bollworm [Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner)] and beet armyworm [Spodoptera exigua (Hübner)] through genetic modification technology [18]. All of these evidences indicate that PPOs are capable of responding to a variety of stress-inducible reactions. In addition, in the process of plant growth and development, there are also some reports on the functional roles of PPO. Some of the PPOs have had their functions revealed in growth and development, pollination, and browning [19–24].Yu et al.. reported a polyphenol oxidase gene Phr1, which was responsible for the PHR (phenol reaction) phenotype during rice grains [25]. Comparisons of metabolites and transcripts of the pollen between WT and NtPPO-RNAi, or overexpression NtPPO10 showed the NtPPOs regulate pollen germination in tobacco [26]. So far, there have been very few reports on the PPO of cotton. Yuan et al.. revealed that thiourea-mediated inhibition of polyphenol oxidase activity effected the cotton fiber elongation [27]. PPOs from Coker 312 (an embryogenic cultivar) and R405-2000 (a non-embryogenic cultivar), showed different highest affinity levels for PPO’s substrates, catechol and 4-methylcatechol [28]. However, research on PPOs in cotton is still limited.

With the development of genome sequencing technology, the genomes of many species have been sequenced [29–31]. The upland cotton standard cultivar, TM-1, has had its genome sequenced and re-sequenced in recently years [32–34]. These genome data provide a realistic basis for large-scale studies of gene families. At present, the PPO gene family has been reported in many species, including wheat PPOs [19], eggplant PPOs [20], poplar PPOs [21, 22], banana PPOs [23], and tobacco PPOs [24]. It is worth mentioning that NtPPOs potentially play a role in the pollen tube growth after pollination through purines and flavonoid compounds [24]. Cotton fiber are also single-cell trichomes and develop from the ovule epidermis, it is possible that PPOs have similar functions in cotton fiber growth and development.

Upland cotton is an essential cash crop that provides not only cotton fibres, but also the protein from cotton seeds and edible oils. The cotton fibres are an important natural fibre resource that has provided abundant raw materials for the textile industry [35]. Cotton fiber is a single cell differentiated from the epidermal ovule, and it is the product of polar elongation of ovule epidermal cells [36], and its development consists of four overlapping stages including fiber initiation (0–5 DPA, Days Post-Anthesis), elongation (5–25 DPA), second cell wall biosynthesis (20–45 DPA), and dehydration and maturation (45–50 DPA) [37], these four stages together generally last for about 50 days. Based on our recent comparative transcriptome analysis of two cotton varieties, n2 (only containing lint fiber) and XZ142FLM (a fuzzless-lintless mutant), we also found significant up-regulation of specific PPO genes in elongating cotton fibers (unpublished transcriptome data). This observation leads us to hypothesize that polyphenol oxidase genes may play functional roles in cotton fiber development.

Hence, the PPO gene family of cotton can be explored in upland cotton (G. hirsutum L.). In this paper we mainly analyzed the PPO gene family of G. hirsutum, identified their structural and evolutionary characteristics, and detected the PPO expression patterns in cotton fibres at different development stages and in response to cotton bollworms. This work can contribute to a deeper understanding of the genome-wide information on cotton PPO genes, and provide new insights into the function of PPOs in cotton fibre development and against the resistance to cotton pests.

Results

Identification of PPO gene family in cotton

In this study, a total of 51 PPO genes were identified in five cotton species, including Gossypium hirsutum (G. hirsutum), Gossypium barbadense (G. barbadense), Gossypium arboreum (G. arboreum), Gossypium raimondii (G. raimondii), and Gossypium herbaceum (G. herbaceum) (Additional files 2: Table S3). Among the various cotton species, upland cotton (G. hirsutum) is an essential cash crop that provides not only cotton fibres, but also the protein from cotton seeds and edible oils. Therefore, we will mainly focus on the comprehensive analysis of the PPO gene family in G. hirsutum. Fifteen PPO genes were obtained in G. hirsutum, and they were successively designated GhPPO1 to GhPPO15 (Table 1, Additional file 2: Table S3). According to the analysis of PPO conserved domains, all 15 GhPPOs contain three conserved domains, which are named tyrosinase (domain I), DWL (domain II) and KFDV (domain III), respectively (Table 1).

Prediction analysis of biochemical characteristics and subcelullar localizations of GhPPOs

To clearly understand the biochemical characteristics of the PPO family in upland cotton, we analyzed the length of deduced protein sequences, isoelectric point (pI), and protein molecular weight (MW) (Table 1). Among the 15 GhPPOs, the amino acids of deduced protein sequences range from 548 (GhPPO12) to 627 (GhPPO11), the MWs of GhPPO protein range from 62.08 (GhPPO9) to 71.53 (GhPPO11) kDa, and the pI values of PPO family members range from 5.67 (GhPPO9) to 7.99 (GhPPO13), with an average of 6.64 (Table 1). The subcellular localization of proteins confers their function characteristics in cellular compartments. To predict the functional characteristics of upland cotton PPOs, we have executed the subcellular location analysis of 15 GhPPOs using the online tool DeepLoc-2.1. The subcellular location results of predicted 15 GhPPOs were showed in the Table S1. The prediction results show that eleven GhPPOs locate in the plastid, and the subcelluar organelle locations of the other four GhPPOs involve in the cytoplasm, lysosome, vacuole, and Golgi apparatus (Table S1). These different location information suggest that GhPPOs play roles in multiple functions within cells. Meanwhile, we have proceeded the prediction analysis of location and potential cleavage site of the N-terminal presequence of 15 GhPPOs with online tool TargetP 2.0, the analysis results were showed in Table S2. Of these 15 GhPPOs, except for GhPPO10 and GhPPO13, all of the rest 13 GhPPOs were predicted the cleavage site of subcellular location or signal peptide (Table S2). These findings will facilitate further elucidation of the underlying subcellular roles and molecular mechanisms of GhPPO genes in cellular metabolic regulation.

Analysis of phylogenetic relationships, gene structure and motifs of PPO genes

To explore the evolution and differentiation of cotton PPO family proteins, 69 PPO amino acid sequences from 7 species were used to construct an unrooted phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1, Table S3). These PPOs were divided into 5 groups (I-V). Group I was the largest group, with 22 members (I), and the other groups contained six (III), 13 (IV and V), or 15 (II) members, respectively (Fig. 1). Among these seven species, there is one monocotyledon species and six dicotyledon species. Interestingly, four PPO genes were found in the monocotyledonous O.sativa, which is fewer less than in dicotyledons (Fig. 1); however, PPO genes were absent in Arabidopsis.

Chromosomal location and gene duplication analysis of the cotton PPO gene family

G. hirsutum is an allotetraploid crop, with a genome containing 52 chromosomes (AtAtDtDt; 2n = 4x = 52). To better understand the distribution of the 15 GhPPOs on the chromosomes, their chromosomal location was identified in G. hirsutum using the online tool GSDS. The results showed that all 15 GhPPOs were located on eight chromosomes. Four and five of the 15 GhPPOs were distributed on chromosomes Ghir_A06 and Ghir_D06, respectively (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the other six chromosomes (Ghir_A03, Ghir_A09, Ghir_A012, Ghir_D02, Ghir_D09, and Ghir_D012) each contained one GhPPO (Fig. 3). These results show that the GhPPOs are unevenly distributed across the chromosomes.

Investigation of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of GhPPO genes

Gene expression levels were generally regulated by cis-elements in their up-stream promoter region [38]. To investigate the cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of GhPPO genes, non-coding sequences about 2000 bp upstream of the coding region of each GhPPO gene were identified and extracted. All of the non-coding sequences were scanned to confirm the presence of abundant regulatory motifs involved in hormones, stresses, and development-related responses using the online tool plantCARE. The analysis results of all the cis-elements were summarized and shown in the plus and minus strands, respectively. (Fig. 5A, B; Additional file 2: Table S4, Table S5). Based on their function, the cis-elements were classified into hormone-related elements, stress- or environment-responsive elements, development and metabolism elements, and others.

The cis-acting elements within the 2000 bp upstream sequence of the upland cotton GhPPO genes. A The cis-acting elements distributed in the plus strands. B The cis-acting elements distributed in the minus strands

Many hormone-related elements, consisting of auxin (AuxRR-core), gibberellin (P-box), methyl jasmonate (TGACG-motif, CGTCA-motif), salicylic acid (TCA-element), and abscisic acid (ABRE-box), were significantly enriched in the promoter regions of GhPPO genes. These hormone-related elements were distributed not only in the plus but also in the minus strands (Figs. 5A and 6B; Additional file 2: Tables S4, S5). A number of cis-elements responsive to stress or environmental factors were also found in the plus or minus strands of the promoter regions of GhPPO genes; they were involved in light responsiveness (G-Box), anaerobic induction (ARE), and drought-induced elements (MBS) (Additional file 2: Tables S4, S5). In addition, there were also abundant cis-elements related to development and metabolism in both the plus and minus strands of the promoter regions of GhPPO genes. Their functions were related to circadian control, meristem or endosperm development, flavonoid biosynthesis, and seed development (Additional file 2: Tables S4, S5). Moreover, many cis-elements were related to MYB and MYC transcription factors were predicted by the online tool plantCARE; however, their functions were not been sufficiently researched. These are precise the areas that should be studied in the future.

Expression profile and RT-qPCR analysis of GhPPOs during the development of cotton fibre

To analyze the expression features of GhPPOs during cotton fibre development, we constructed the expression heatmap of the 15 GhPPOs using transcriptome data (Fig. 6A). The 15 GhPPOs displayed different expression patterns. Nine GhPPO genes were almost undetectable in the transcriptome data from 11 different stage samples. However, the expression of GhPPOs in fibre samples showed obvious differences. GhPPOs clustering in the same branch generally exhibited similar expression patterns (Fig. 6A). GhPPO11 and GhPPO12 were preferentially expressed during the fibre initiation and elongation stages (0–10 DPA), while GhPPO7 showed obvious up-regulated expression from 1 DPA to 7 DPA (Fig. 6A). These results suggested that they might have similar or complementary functions during cotton fibre development.

To further identify the key GhPPOs involved in fibre cell growth, six GhPPOs that were predominantly expressed at different stages of cotton fibre development were selected to examine their expression levels using RT-qPCR (Fig. 6B). Based on the experimental results, the expression patterns of these GhPPOs were almost consistent with the transcriptome data at each stage of fibre development (Fig. 6B). Notably, the expression peak of GhPPO7 appeared at 3 DPA and 5 DPA. In addition, the expression levels of GhPPO11 and GhPPO12 showed an increase and then a decline from 0 DPA to 10 DPA (Fig. 6B), which coincides with the rapid growth stage of cotton fibre during this period.

Analysis of expression pattern of GhPPOs in different tissue and induced by cotton boll worm

To explore the potential functions of GhPPOs in different tissues, we analyzed the transcriptome data of different tissues in G. hirsutum to detect the expression patterns of GhPPOs in root, stem, leaf, bud, and corolla (Fig. 7A). Six GhPPOs, including GhPPO1, GhPPO2, GhPPO4, GhPPO5, GhPPO6, and GhPPO10, were almost undetectable in all examined tissues due to their low transcriptional levels (Fig. 7A). In addition, nine GhPPOs showed different expression patterns in these tissues (Fig. 7A). GhPPO3 was preferentially expressed only in the stem. GhPPO7, GhPPO8, and GhPPO11 showed similar expression patterns, with high expression in stem, leaf and bud. GhPPO9 and GhPPO15 were preferentially expressed in the other four tissues, except for the corolla. GhPPO12 showed higher expression levels in stem, leaf, bud, and corolla than in the root. The expression levels of GhPPO13 were similar to those of GhPPO12; therefore, there were significant differences in transcriptional abundance between GhPPO12 and GhPPO13 (Fig. 7A). Meanwhile, we selected eight GhPPOs from the 15 GhPPOs to verify their relative expression levels using RT-qPCR (Fig. 7B). The results showed that the expression patterns of these eight GhPPOs were consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 7A, B).

PPOs are usually induced in response to both biotic and abiotic stresses in plants [12]. To investigate the functions of GhPPOs in response to biotic stresses in cotton, we examined the expression levels of the 15 GhPPOs induced by cotton bollworm using RT-qPCR (Fig. 7C). The results showed significant differences were observed in cotton leaf tissue when fed by cotton bollworms. Five GhPPOs, including GhPPO1, GhPPO4, GhPPO8, GhPPO12 and GhPPO15, showed up-regulated expression after cotton bollworm feeding at 24 h, with a more significant difference than at 0 h (Fig. 7C). In addition, five GhPPOs, including GhPPO2, GhPPO7, GhPPO9, GhPPO13, and GhPPO14, showed up-regulated expression starting from 12 h (Fig. 7C). Meanwhile, we found that the up-regulated expression of GhPPO3, GhPPO5, and GhPPO6 started earlier, at 6 h, after cotton bollworm feeding (Fig. 7C). GhPPO3 showed the highest relative expression level among the 15 GhPPOs. These results suggest that GhPPO3 could play an important role in responding to cotton bollworm damage. Interestingly, there was only one gene, GhPPO11, which showed up-regulation starting from 3 h after cotton bollworm feeding. In contrast, the expression of GhPPO10 was not induced by cotton bollworm (Fig. 7C). In conclusion, the expression profiles of GhPPOs showed complex responses to cotton bollworm feeding.

Analysis of the three-dimensional structure of special GhPPOs in cotton fibres and introduced by cotton bollworm

The three-dimensional structures (3D) of proteins are fundamental for correctly performing their functions. To elucidate the action mechanism of a protein, it is necessary to predict its three-dimensional structure. Therefore, we selected some special GhPPOs for sequence alignment, analysis of secondary structure, and construction of their 3D structure models (Fig. 8, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). GhPPO7 and GhPPO11, which showed a sharp up-regulation in expression during the fibre elongation stage, were selected for sequence alignment and 3D structure construction (Fig. 8). Based on the tool ENDscript, the multiple sequence alignment results of the two GhPPOs and MdPPO1 showed that they had similar sequence characteristics: relatively independent N-terminal, C-terminal domains, and the putative proteolytic region of activation (Fig. 8A). Both GhPPO7 and GhPPO11 contained six conserved histidines, which were absolutely conserved amino acids in the N-terminal domain (Fig. 8A).

To further elucidate the possible biological function of GhPPO7 and GhPPO11, which are preferentially expressed in cotton fibers, both were subjected to protein structure homology modeling using the online tool SWISS-MODEL. The structures of both GhPPO7 and GhPPO11 consisted of two domains, as showed in Fig. 8. The N-terminal domains of GhPPO7 and GhPPO11 consisted of ten α-helices and five β-strands based on secondary structures analysis (Fig. 8A), and the N-terminal domains were the active centres of GhPPOs in the tertiary structure (Fig. 8B, C). In each active centres, two copper ions were coordinated by three histidine residues contributed by the four helices of the α-bundle (Fig. 8B, C). The CuA site of GhPPO7 was coordinated by His 90, His 111, and His 120 (Fig. 8B). These three histidine residues were located in α2-helix, the loop region connecting α2 and α3 helices, and in α3-helix, respectively (Fig. 8A). The CuB site was coordinated by His 260, His 264, and His 294 (Fig. 8B). These residues were found in the middle of helices α8 and α9 (Fig. 8A). Similarly, the CuA site of GhPPO11 was coordinated by His 90, His 111, and His 120 (Fig. 8C). The three histidine residues were located in the same positions as in GhPPO7 (Fig. 8A). The CuB site was coordinated by His 264, His 268 and His 298 (Fig. 8C), and these residues were also found at the middle of helices α8 and α9 (Fig. 8A).

Meanwhile, the GhPPO3 and GhPPO5 underwent sequence alignment, secondary structure analysis, and homology modeling using the online tools ENDscript and SWISS-MODEL. The results are shown in Figure S2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The CuA site of GhPPO3 was coordinated by His 90, His 111, and His 120 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). These three histidine residues were located in α2-helix, the loop region connecting α2 and α3 helices, and in the middle of α3-helix, respectively (Fig. S2A). The CuB site was coordinated by His 241, His 245 and His 275 (Fig. S2B), and these residues were found in helices α8 and α9 (Fig. 8A). Similarly, the CuA site of GhPPO5 was coordinated by His 87, His 108, and His 117 (Fig. S2C). The three histidine residues were located in the same positions as in GhPPO3 (Fig. S2). The CuB site was coordinated by His 241 His 245 and His 275 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2C), and these residues were also found in the helices α8 and α9 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

Discussion

PPOs are ubiquitous copper-containing metalloenzymes in plants, fungi, and some bacteria. They play important roles in stress response, food fields, plant growth, and development [25, 39–41]. So far, with the completion of whole genome sequencing in many species, analysis of PPO genes has been conducted in different plants, such as potato, tomato, wheat, eggplant, olive, apple, poplar, and tobacco, and so on [19–24, 42–44]. However, PPO genes have not found in some lower plants and higher plants, such as green algae and Brassica napus, especially not in Arabidopsis [45].

In this paper, we have completed the identification of the PPO gene family in cotton, with 51 PPO genes identified across five species (Additional file 2: Table S3). Of these, the results showed that 15 PPO genes in upland cotton were identified, and they were named GhPPOs (Table 1; Additional file 2: Table S3). All the GhPPO proteins had three conserved domains (tyrosinase, DWL, and KFDV) (Table 1). The conserved structural domains were consistent with those in others species, such as poplar [22], olive [44], and banana (Musa acuminate) [23], and so on. These conserved features might endow GhPPOs with similar biological functions. In addition, it was reported that Introns can usually impact several vital aspects of eukaryotic organisms, such as proteomic plasticity, genomic stability, stress response and gene expression [46]. However, we found that most of the GhPPO genes do not have introns in this study. Based on the gene structure analysis of GhPPO genes, except for GhPPO 2, GhPPO 9, GhPPO 12, GhPPO 13, GhPPO 14, and GhPPO 15, the remaining nine GhPPO genes do not contain introns (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, we found that the six GhPPO genes with introns showed similar expression patterns in some tissue organs (Fig. 7). These results suggested that GhPPOs could play distinct functions during cotton growth, development, and stress response.

Molecular weight (MW) and isoelectric point (pI) serve as critical biophysical determinants for characterizing protein structure-function relationships. In this systematic investigation of the GhPPO gene family in G. hirsutum, we characterized 15 members through gene family analysis. Computational profiling revealed substantial MW heterogeneity among paralogs (62.08–71.53 kDa), which is consistent with predictions in other species [22–24]. This divergence has been hypothesized to stem from alternative splicing events during mRNA maturation, generating transcript variants ultimately translate into protein isoforms with differential amino acid composition and consequent MW variations. Experimental validation via SDS-PAGE coupled with mass spectrometry will be required to resolve these theoretical predictions [47]. The functional diversification of paralogous proteins within gene families is fundamentally mediated by divergence in their amino acid composition post-translation, which confers distinct isoelectric points (pI) to individual isoforms. The subcellular localization of proteins confers their function characteristics in cellular compartments. Subcellular localization profiling predicted that GhPPO13 contains a canonical nuclear localization signal (NLS) within its N-terminal domain (Table S2). Structurally, NLS motifs are characterized by clusters of basic residues such as lysine (K) and arginine (R) that maintain positive charges under physiological pH conditions [48]. This cationic property facilitates nuclear import through selective recognition via nuclear pore complexes. The functional activity of GhPPO13 is likely dependent on electrostatic interactions with negatively charged nuclear components, including histones and nucleic acids. Nevertheless, the precise biological roles and regulatory mechanisms governing GhPPO13 nuclear dynamics require systematic interrogation using molecular genetics approaches coupled with proteomic analyses in the future.

We constructed a phylogenetic tree from seven species and divided into five groups to further determine the evolution and origin of PPO genes (Fig. 1), consistent with the results of the phylogenetic tree constructed using Populus trichocarpa [22]. Meanwhile, we analyzed the synteny relationships between GhPPOs of G. hirsutum, and PPOs in G. barbadense, G. herbaceum, G. arboreum, and G. raimondii, respectively. The results showed that four pairs of homologous genes were found between G. hirsutum and G. barbadence, there are two pairs of homologous gene between G. hirsutum and G. raimondii, and G. herbaceum, respectively. And there is only one pair of homologous genes between G. hirsutum and G. aboreum (Fig. 4). G. herbaceum (A1) and G. raimondii (D5) are both diploid cotton species, whereas G. barbadense and G. hirsutum are allopolyploid cotton species formed through chromosome doubling [32]. The most recent genome sequencing analysis of cotton suggests that G. herbaceum (A1) and G. raimondii (D5) are the likely genome donors of G. hirsutum [49]. The differences observed in the number of homologous gene pairs during the synteny analysis between G. hirsutum GhPPOs and other cotton species in this study further support this hypothesis.

At present, the expression profiles of PPO genes in various tissues have been studied in some species [50–53]. These results showed that there were distinct gene expression patterns of PPO genes in different plants. According to the transcriptome data of cotton fibre, eight GhPPO genes were preferentially expressed in different development stages of cotton fibre, yet their expression patterns weren’t exactly the same and showed temporal differences during cotton fibre development (Fig 0.6 A). Meanwhile, we found that the remaining seven GhPPO genes had very low or hardly any expression (Fig. 6A). These results suggested that some PPO genes could play a vital role during cotton fibre development. Wei et al. reported that knockdown of NtPPOs would affect pollen growth after pollination [24]. Cotton fibres were the products of polar elongation of a single cell. Cotton fibre elongation shares a similar process to that of pollen tube growth. Hence, we speculate that some of the GhPPOs might share similar functions with PPOs that promote pollen tube growth in tobacco.

With the development of the bioinformatics in recent years, a large number of bioinformatics online tools have been developed and widely put into use in the field of biological research [13, 22, 24, 54], such as MEME Suite, a Motif-based sequence analysis tools [55]; GSDS, a Gene Structure Display Server [56]; SWISS-MODEL, a fully automated protein structure homology-modelling server [57], and Plant CARE, a database of plant cis-acting regulatory elements and a portal to tools for in silico analysis of promoter sequences [58], and so on. Certainly, the results predicted by bioinformatics tools are the first step in understanding the biological functions of genes. Next, we will further validate the functions of the genes through experiments such as gene knockout and overexpression technology. In this paper, based on the possible potential functions of GhPPO genes during cotton fibre development, we have utilized gene editing and transgenic techniques to create gene knockout mutants and overexpression transgenic lines (data not shown in this paper). Meanwhile, according to prediction results of cis-regulatory elements using the Plant CARE, we have also obtained a MYB transcription factor which could regulate the GhPPO7 gene. Subsequently, we will further elucidate the functions and regulatory mechanism of GhPPOs in the development of cotton fibres.

By the 1990 s, biologists began to recognize the importance of plant PPOs in response to the pathogens and the infestation of herbivores [12]. Up to now, extensive molecular research through transgenic and non-transgenic systems has partly revealed the sophisticated underlying mechanisms of PPOs in defense against herbivores and pathogens [12, 18]. In this research, we have also analyzed the expression pattern of GhPPO genes induced by the cotton bollworm. Most of the GhPPO genes were able to respond to the feeding induction by cotton bollworm (Fig. 7C). This findings are consistent with previously reported roles of PPO in insect resistance in tomato [18]. These results suggested GhPPOs and their metabolism products could have important functions in defense against herbivores, especially the cotton bollworm.

So far, the crystal structures of PPOs from many of plants have been analyzed, such as catechol oxidase in sweet potato [4], the tyrosinase from walnut [5], the crystal structure of Grenache (Vitis vinifera) polyphenol oxidase [7], apple [59], and so on. These studies have helped us understand the roles of the enzyme. In order to speculate about the functions of GhPPOs in their high expression levels in cotton fibres and response to cotton bollworm (Figs. 7 and 8), we have constructed the homology 3D-structure models of several GhPPOs, such as GhPPO3, GhPPO5, GhPPO7 and GhPPO11(Figs. 7 and 8, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). MdPPO1_dCP (PDB No. 6ELS) [14] served as the 3D-structure modelling template for GhPPO3, GhPPO5, GhPPO7 and GhPPO11. Based on the analysis results of sequence alignments and 3D-structure models, all four GhPPOs - GhPPO3, GhPPO5, GhPPO7 and GhPPO11 - contained six histidine residues, which were consistent with MdPPO1 (Fig. 8, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). These six histidine residues form the catalytic center with two copper ions, CuA and CuB. These structure features are also typical of PPOs in plants [54, 60]. In addition, there is a linker, known as the proteolytic region of activation, in the MdPPO1 sequence, which was shown to undergo peptide-induced self-cleavage afterwards [14, 59]. We found similar structures in the GhPPO3, GhPPO5, GhPPO7, and GhPPO11 sequences, which we have named the putative proteolytic region of activation (Fig. 8, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Moreover, the N-terminal and C-terminal domains were connected by the putative proteolytic region. Hence, we inferred that the structures within GhPPO3, GhPPO5, GhPPO7, and GhPPO11 might function in a manner analogous to that of MdPPO1.

Based on the current tertiary structure prediction results, all four GhPPOs (GhPPO3, GhPPO5, GhPPO7, and GhPPO11) exhibit conserved structural architectures, forming two distinct structural domains connected by a flexible linker region. However, the catalytic activity of polyphenol oxidases (PPOs) is closely associated with the microenvironment of their enzymatic active sites, and these enzymes demonstrate strict substrate specificity [61]. Therefore, the catalytic mechanisms and molecular functions of the four GhPPOs identified in this study require further investigation. Critical features such as key amino acid residues governing catalytic activity, substrate-binding pocket characteristics, and dynamic conformational changes during substrate recognition should be thoroughly explored. Future studies focusing on these structural determinants will elucidate the molecular basis underlying their functional divergence in fiber elongation and stress responses.

Building on these findings, we have successfully generated CRISPR-Cas9-mediated mutant cotton plants targeting GhPPO7 and GhPPO11, and developed transgenic lines overexpressing these genes, with detailed findings to be published in a forthcoming study. Additionally, we have initiated preparations for generating transgenic cotton lines targeting GhPPO3 and GhPPO5 to confer resistance against cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera). We anticipate that future development of novel cotton cultivars combining enhanced fiber quality traits (e.g., tensile strength, micronaire value) with durable insect resistance will be achieved through targeted genetic engineering or CRISPR-based genome editing strategies.

Conclusions

In a word, 51 PPO genes were identified in five cotton species, and then the 15 GhPPOs of G. hirsutum were deeply analyzed. The results showed that all of them had three conserved domains: tyrosinase, DWL, and KFDV. Therefore, these GhPPOs were typical PPOs. Based on the phylogenetic and chromosome location analysis, the 15 GhPPOs were classified into five groups and located eight chromosomes. Gene structure analysis showed that nine GhPPOs were absent of introns. Analysis of cis-acting elements in the GhPPOs promoters indicated their potential fundamental roles in response to plant growth and development, and stresses. Through comprehensive analysis of the expression profiles and protein structures of GhPPOs, we identified two crucial genes, GhPPO7 and GhPPO11, which were responsive to cotton fiber development. Additionally, we have also identified two genes, GhPPO3 and GhPPO5, which were significantly induced by the feeding of cotton bollworm. These findings not only provided valuable insights into the phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, cis-acting elements, and functional characteristics of upland cotton PPOs but also shed light on their potential roles in cotton fibre elongation and response to biotic stresses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions and collected the experimental samples

To study the expression profiles of GhPPO genes during cotton fiber development, the experimental material, upland cotton standard line TM-1 (Gossypium hirsutum L.), was planted at the experimental field (36°5′34.8″N, 114°31′47.19″E) of the Institute of Cotton Research of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Anyang, Henan, China, which has a semi-humid and subtropical monsoon climate. The soil was clay loam. The average temperatures in July and August 2023 were 28.47 °C and 26.47 °C, respectively. The total rainfall for July and August amounted to 269 mm and 141.20 mm, respectively. Field irrigation and weed control were conducted according to the conventional management practices of the experimental field. Cotton was planted on 22 April and harvested on 23 October in 2023 at a planting density of 60,000 plants ha−1. We had commenced the collection of experimental samples in July and completed it by the middle of August in 2023. The specific sampling procedures were as follows. When the cotton flowers opened, each opening flower was marked on the day of flowering, which was designated as 0 day post anthesis (DPA). Subsequently, cotton boll samples were gathered at − 3, − 1, 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, and 30 DPA. The collected bolls were dissected to obtain the ovules and fibres. For samples from 0 to 7 DPA, all ovules were collected, and for samples from 10 to 30 DPA, fibres were collected. Individual cotton seedlings with six leaves were infested with 10 1 st -instar cotton bollworm larvae. The leaves were collected induced at 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-infestation [62]. All the leaves, ovules, and fibre samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately and stored at −80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer.

Identification and sequence analysis of the cotton GhPPO genes

The cotton genomic data were obtained from the CottonFGD website (https://cottonfgd.org/) [63]. To identify the PPO gene family members in G.hirsutum, G.barbadence, G.herbaceum, G.aboreum and G.raimondii genome, the conserved domain Tyrosinase (PF00264), PPO1_DWL (PF12142) and PPO1_KFDV (PF12143) were used as a query. Searching the target protein sequences were conducted by the BLASTP and HMMER3.0 software [64]. Then, all candidate PPO protein sequences were submitted to the NCBI CDD (Conserved Domain Database) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) [65], where conserved domains were identified. To get a rapid search, we performed this work by the Batch Web CD-Search Tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) [65]. We executed the search program using default parameters. Next, we analyzed the molecular weight (MW) and isoelectric point (IP) of the identified GhPPO proteins by the ExPaSy online tools (https://www.expasy.org/resources/) [66].

Phylogenetic tree construction

In order to analyze phylogenetic relationships of PPO genes among the different plant species. The full length predicted amino acid sequences of the PPO protein from the 7 plant species, included in G.hirsutum (Gh), G.barbadence (Gb), G.herbaceum (Gher), G.aboreum (Ga),and G.raimondii (Gr), Oryza sativa (Os) and Glycine max (Gm) (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/), were aligned using ClustalW. A Neighbor-Joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed by MEGA 6.0 software [67] with pairwise deletion option, poisson correction model and uniform rates. Bootstrap test was executed with 1000 replicates for evaluating the statistical reliability of the phylogenetic tree, and the rest of parameters were default.

Analysis of GhPPO genes structure and motif

The Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS, http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) was used to draw the exon–intron structures of GhPPO genes based on the full-length genomic sequences and the corresponding coding sequences obtained as described above [56], the results of analysis were saved as the SVG format image. Motifs of GhPPO protein sequence were analyzed by the online tool MEME (http://meme-suite.org/index.html) [68]. According to the required file format, we submitted the GhPPO sequences into these online tools. At the end, the output images of gene structure and motif were further modified with the Adobe Illustrator CS3 software (version 13.0.0).

Chromosomal location and duplication of PPO genes

Chromosomal position information of each GhPPO gene was obtained from the upland cotton genome annotation, a file of chromosome length of TM-1 was maken. And then, Chromosomal locations of all the GhPPO genes were draw by the the online tool MG2C (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.0/), and the relative locations of PPO genes were shown on the respective chromosomes. Collinearity analysis of cotton PPO genes was carried out by the TBtools [69]. The Visual graphics was further modified by the Adobe Illustrator CS3 software (version 13.0.0).

Analysis of cis-elements in the promoter regions of GhPPO genes

The promoter regions (2000 bp sequence upstream of the initiation codon in the genomic DNA sequence) of the GhPPO genes were identified by searching the G. hirsutum genome database (https://cottonfgd.org/) [63]. Then these promoter sequences were then analyzed by online Tool PlantCARE to get the cis-elements of every GhPPO genes (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [58]. And then the mapping cis-elements of promoter were draw by the Adobe Illustrator CS3 software (version 13.0.0).

Expression profiles of the GhPPO genes in diverse tissues and different cotton fiber development stages

Total RNAs were extracted from the samples of tissues, ovules and fibres. All the diverse samples were carried out transcriptome analysis by Illumina platform. For the transcriptome sequencing, three biological duplicates were conducted for the experimental samples. Subsequently, libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded RNA Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and sequencing was performed on the HiSeq2000 system. The raw data in FASTQ format were first processed to obtain clean reads, and then the Q20, Q30 and GC content of the clean reads were calculated. The clean reads were obtained by removing the low quality reads and adaptors, which were mapped to the reference genome of Gossypium hirsutum using HISAT2 (version 2.2.0) [70]. The sequencing results of gene expression were shown by FPKM values. The gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie (version 2.1.4) to calculate FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) [71]. The cotton fiber transcriptome datasets utilized for graphical analyses in this study were generated in our lab and originally published in Zuo etal’ paper [72]. Raw sequencing reads have been deposited in the NCBI SRA repository (BioProject accession number: PRJNA1067724, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1067724). FPKM values of GhPPO genes were selected from the transcriptome results, and convert data format to logFPKM values, a heatmap was drawn with the logarithm values by the HemI software (version 1.0) [73].

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative-PCR analysis

The total RNA of all the different samples was extracted by the RNAprep Pure Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat. no. DP441; TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The RNA samples were examined by agarose gel electrophoresis, and then the concentration and quality were analyzed by the NanoDrop ONEc (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The total RNA was treated with DNase I to remove gDNA contamination. RNA samples with three biological replicates were used as templates for reverse transcription. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). All reverse-transcript cDNA samples were diluted 10 times and stored at − 20 °C for the real-time quantification PCR (RT-qPCR) experiment. The specific RT-qPCR primers were showed in supplementary files (Additional file 2: Table S6). The expression of ubiqutin-7 gene (GenBank No.AY189972) was used as the internal control. The RT-qPCR assays were performed using QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR instrument and conducted three technical replicates (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The reagents of RT-qPCR were used with the TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II Kit (TaKaRa, Code No.RR820A). The reagents and procedure of RT-qPCR assays performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (TaKaRa, Code No.RR820A). Relative level of gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [74]. The statistical analysis of RT-qPCR assays results were conducted for one-way ANOVA multiple comparison test in Microsoft Excel for Student’s t test. The Visual graphics was further modified by the Adobe Illustrator CS3 software (version 13.0.0).

Analysis of subcellular location, secondary structure and homology model

All of the GhPPO protein sequences were predicted their subcellular location with DeepLoc-2.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.1/), and potential cleavage site of the N-terminal presequence with Target P-2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TargetP-2.0/). Moreover, the secondary structure of GhPPOs and multiple sequence alignment were conducted by the online tool ENDscript 3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/cgi-bin/ESPript.cgi) [75]. For multiple sequence alignment and homology model, the N-terminal transit peptides of GhPPO protein sequences were removed. The three-dimensional (3D) structure was predicted using the online tool SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/ interactive, accessed on 8 January 2024) [57], and the visual graphics was further modified by the online tool PyMOL software (https://www.pymol.org/).
