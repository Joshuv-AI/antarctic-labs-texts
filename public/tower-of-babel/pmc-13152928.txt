Post-asphyxia acute kidney injury in neonates: a systematic review
Authors: Temilade Aderounmu, Babatunde Adeyemi, Aisha Bolarinwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerinatal asphyxia is a major cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality globally. It affects multi-systems and has significant renal consequences. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent complication of perinatal asphyxia; however, its burden, diagnosis, and outcomes remain variably characterized. This study employed a scoping review approach to map and synthesize the existing evidence on post-asphyxia AKI in neonates. A total of 30 studies published between 1995 and 2025 were included, encompassing different geographic regions, study designs, and diagnostic criteria. The review demonstrates a clear growth in scholarly attention to post-asphyxia AKI, particularly in the last 25 years, coinciding with the introduction of neonatal-modified KDIGO definitions and advances in neonatal nephrology. Reported incidence of AKI varied widely, ranging from 20% to over 60%, reflecting non-homogeneity in study populations, differing case definitions of acute kidney injury in neonates, the severities of hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, and varied methodological approaches. Findings consistently indicate that AKI is strongly associated with severe forms of perinatal asphyxia, the presence of multi-organ dysfunction, prolonged neonatal intensive care stay, and mortality.Conclusion: Despite emerging interest in novel biomarkers and standard definitions, important gaps persist in early detection, long-term renal outcome data, and evidence from low- and middle-income countries. Addressing these gaps through standardized monitoring, biomarker validation, and longitudinal follow-up will improve renal survival in neonates affected by perinatal asphyxia. Introduction

Perinatal asphyxia remains a major and persistent global health problem, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to skilled obstetric and neonatal care is limited. It is defined as impaired gas exchange at or around the time of birth, resulting in hypoxemia, hypercapnia, metabolic acidosis, and it continues to contribute substantially to neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide (World Health Organization [WHO], 2014), especially in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia (Lawn et al. 2016; Lee et al., 2013). Perinatal asphyxia causes severe multi-organ dysfunction, and this is secondary to a “diving reflex” that affects organ systems with characteristic hypoxia, ischemia, and reperfusion injury (Martínez-Biarge et al., 2012; Perlman, 2006). Hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) ensues when perinatal asphyxia is severe and prolonged. This is a neurological injury that is a consequence of impaired cerebral circulation and oxygenation (Volpe 2018). A diving reflex; redistribution of blood to the brain, heart, and adrenals; and a loss of cerebral vaso-regulation occurs following asphyxia and prolonged asphyxia respectively. Oxidative phosphorylation diminishes, while there is an increased utilization of anaerobic metabolism and depletion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). A plethora of disorders can cause fetal and neonatal hypoxia, inadequate oxygenation of maternal circulation, placenta insufficiency and placenta accidents, umbilical cord accidents, cyanotic congenital heart disease and severe respiratory diseases, and hemolytic and hemorrhagic perturbations. The kidneys are well perfused organs with a high oxygen demand and are therefore particularly susceptible to the effect of hypoxia and ischemia (Aggarwal et al. 2005). The neonatal kidneys are worse off under the effect of hypoxia and ischemia due to an inadequate physiologic functional capacity and a structural deficiency, especially when nephrogenesis is incomplete (Selewski et al. 2015). Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is reduced in the newborn kidneys, and the vascular resistance is high; this portends its susceptibility to AKI. Furthermore, the medullary interstitium is hypotonic, and the tubules are insensitive to antidiuretic hormone (ADH), permitting a postnatal diuresis in the early days of life, and this creates a challenge in diagnosing AKI in the newborns. Nephrotoxic medications such as the aminoglycosides often used in neonatal infections typically cause non-oliguric AKI, and therefore diminishes the validity of urine output as a marker of nAKI. Low birth weight, low gestational ages, and prematurity alter the final nephron number and development, increasing the risk for nAKI and CKD in later life.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common complication that follows perinatal asphyxia, typically manifesting within the first few days of life (Allegaert & Smits 2014). Its reported incidence varies widely, ranging from 30% to over 70% depending on the severity of asphyxia, the diagnostic criteria employed, and the study design (Gupta et al. 2005; Durkan & Alexander, 2011). This variability highlights both the clinical importance of post-asphyxia AKI and the challenges inherent in its recognition and measurement. Importantly, the occurrence of nAKI is closely linked to the severity of hypoxic insult and is associated with adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization and increased mortality (Jetton & Askenazi, 2012; Selewski et al., 2016). Emerging evidence further suggests that nAKI may predispose individuals to long-term complications such as chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and reduced renal reserve in later life (Luyckx et al. 2017). Historically, nAKI has been underdiagnosed, due to reliance on serum creatinine (SCr); a late and insensitive biomarker influenced by maternal levels and a rapidly changing neonatal physiology that makes it differ across gestational ages and maturity (Smits et al., 2012; Askenazi et al. 2013). Serum creatinine is a measure of kidney function rather than injury; it is a delayed marker of kidney damage; however, it is the most widely used (Drukker and Guignard 2002). Traditionally, nAKI has been defined in terms of absolute SCr levels; nevertheless lately, definitions are based on the degree of rise in SCr levels rather than a single absolute cut-off value. Changes in SCr levels are independently associated with poor kidney outcomes. There is a growing interest in novel biomarkers such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) and Cystatin C, which may enable earlier detection of renal injury (Devarajan 2015), timely diagnosis of nAKI, improved outcomes, and better clinical trials. Recent advances in neonatal nephrology have improved diagnostic standardization through the adoption of a neonatal modified Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria (Jetton & Askenazi 2015). Nonetheless, these criteria have been reported to underestimate the incidence of nAKI, with authors suggesting complementary nAKI definitions (Chen et al. 2025). The current evidence base remains fragmented, with substantial heterogeneity in definitions, diagnostic thresholds, and outcome reporting. Significant gaps persist in understanding risk factors, early detection strategies, and long-term outcomes, particularly in resource-limited settings where the burden of perinatal asphyxia is highest (Olusanya et al., 2019). The kidney is the most damaged organ in asphyxiated full-term infants; acute renal hypoxemia and ischemia lead to renal insufficiency and failure, renal vaso-constriction, reduction in renal blood flow, and an increase in renal adenosine. Renal adenosine mediates a decrease in glomerular filtration rate and filtration fraction in hypoxemic/ischemic renal failure. Administration of theophylline, an adenosine antagonist, has been found to ameliorate the effects of the post-ischemic renal hemodynamic changes (Persson CGA et al., 1986; Gouyon JB and Guignard JP, 1998), and this has been investigated in randomized trials for nAKI prevention (Jenik AG, et al. 2000; Radel LJ et al., 2022) and has shown clinical benefits. Renal replacement therapy is indicated in severe or refractory cases, and oliguria/anuria, rising blood urea nitrogen, electrolyte imbalances, or fluid overload are indications for this intervention. However, management of post-asphyxia nAKI is largely supportive, with targets to preserve renal function, prevent secondary injury, and maintain systemic homeostasis, as well as minimizing nephrotoxin exposure.

Research interest in nAKI has increased greatly over the past two decades, albeit, advances in clinical recognition, diagnosis, and supportive care for AKI in neonates have lagged behind advances seen for adults and older pediatric patients’ groups (Selewski et al. 2015). Additionally, incidence and prevalence estimates are often derived from small, retrospective, single-center studies and little is known about the long-term consequences of nAKI (Abibtol et al., 2003). Large multicenter studies are needed to validate the definitions of nAKI, determine if the definitions predict clinical outcomes and if modifications to these definitions are necessary.

In light of these challenges, this scoping review aims to systematically map and synthesize existing evidence on acute kidney injury following perinatal asphyxia in neonates. Specifically, the review focuses on four key thematic areas: (i) the burden and clinical impact of post-asphyxia AKI, (ii) diagnostic criteria and biomarkers used in its identification, (iii) risk factors influencing its occurrence, and (iv) short and long-term outcomes. By providing a comprehensive overview of the literature, this review seeks to identify critical knowledge gaps and inform future research and clinical practice in neonatal care.

Materials and methods

Scoping review framework

This scoping review was conducted using a structured and comprehensive methodological approach to map the existing evidence on acute kidney injury (AKI) following perinatal asphyxia in neonates. A scoping review design was considered most appropriate due to the heterogeneity of the available literature, which includes variations in study design, clinical settings, diagnostic criteria, and reported outcomes. Existing studies range from small single-center observational studies to large multicenter cohorts and reviews, often applying inconsistent definitions of both perinatal asphyxia and neonatal AKI. Given the rapidly evolving nature of neonatal AKI research—particularly with the emergence of new consensus definitions, biomarkers, and clinical management strategies—a scoping review allows for a broad exploration of evidence without restricting inclusion to methodologically homogeneous studies, as would be required in systematic reviews or meta-analyses. This approach facilitates the identification of key concepts, clarification of definitions, and recognition of knowledge gaps. The review was guided by the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley and further refined using recommendations from Levac et al. (2010). These enhancements emphasize clarity of purpose, iterative study selection, and deeper analytical interpretation. Additional methodological guidance was drawn from the Joanna Briggs Institute to ensure rigor in eligibility criteria, data charting, and transparent reporting.

The review followed five key stages: (1) identification of research questions, (2) identification of relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) data charting, and (5) collation, synthesis, and reporting of results. This structured yet flexible process ensured reproducibility while accommodating variability in study characteristics.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across major biomedical databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed Central. These databases were selected due to their extensive coverage of neonatology, nephrology, pediatrics, and critical care research. The search strategy was designed to be sensitive and inclusive, consistent with scoping review methodology. Both controlled vocabulary (e.g., Medical Subject Headings [MeSH]) and free-text keywords were used to maximize retrieval. Key search terms included combinations of perinatal asphyxia, birth asphyxia, neonatal hypoxia, acute kidney injury, renal dysfunction, neonatal renal failure, and hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to refine search combinations. Search strategies were adapted for each database to account for differences in indexing systems. Only studies published in English were included to ensure consistency in interpretation. No strict lower date limit was imposed to allow inclusion of foundational studies, although emphasis was placed on recent literature reflecting contemporary diagnostic criteria such as modified KDIGO definitions. To enhance completeness, reference lists of included studies were manually screened (snowballing technique) to identify additional relevant publications.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

The selection of articles to include in this scoping review was done by means of a systematic, two-phase screening process to assure transparency and reduce bias. At first, a total of 480 records were located through electronic database searches (n = 450) and manual reference checks (n = 30). Following the removal of 80 duplicates, 400 unique records were available for screening of titles and abstracts. Two reviewers independently checked each record against the review goals and selected those that met the criteria of human neonates aged 28 days, exposure to perinatal or birth asphyxia, and outcomes of acute kidney injury or renal dysfunction. Records that were obviously irrelevant to these criteria (n = 320) were discarded. Later on, the 80 studies that were left were obtained in full text and subjected to detailed evaluation to ascertain their eligibility. The evaluation took into account the study design, clinical focus, diagnostic criteria for acute kidney injury, and correlation of the outcomes with renal function. Fifty full-text articles were rejected for the following reasons: wrong population (n = 15), irrelevant outcomes (n = 20), and inappropriate study design (n = 15). Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion, and when it was still not clear, a third reviewer was consulted to help achieve the final agreement. In the end, 30 papers complied with the criteria set for the review and were thus considered in the review. Data extraction and charting

Data extraction was conducted using a standardized data charting form designed to ensure consistent and systematic collection of relevant study information. The form was pilot-tested on a subset of included studies and subsequently refined to improve clarity, ensure uniform interpretation of variables, and minimize errors or omissions. For each study, key information was extracted, including bibliographic details (author and year), study setting (country and clinical context), and methodological characteristics (study design and sample size). In addition, detailed clinical and outcome data were collected, such as definitions and severity of perinatal asphyxia, diagnostic criteria for neonatal acute kidney injury (nAKI), timing of AKI onset, and associated renal and systemic outcomes. Key findings relevant to post-asphyxia AKI were also documented. The extracted data were systematically organized to enable cross-study comparison and identification of patterns, variations, and inconsistencies in definitions, diagnostic approaches, and outcomes. This process facilitated mapping of the existing evidence base and identification of knowledge gaps. Consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal assessment of methodological quality or risk of bias was undertaken. Data synthesis and analytical approach

Review findings were synthesized using a descriptive and narrative approach, consistent with scoping review methodology and appropriate for the heterogeneous nature of studies on post-asphyxia acute kidney injury (AKI) in neonates. Evidence from diverse study designs, including observational studies, clinical cohorts, and reviews, was integrated while accounting for differences in clinical settings, populations, and outcome measures. The synthesis focused on identifying patterns and key thematic areas related to the burden, diagnosis, risk factors, and clinical outcomes of AKI following perinatal asphyxia, while also highlighting inconsistencies and gaps in the literature. Both deductive and inductive analytical approaches were employed. Deductive analysis was guided by predefined themes aligned with the research questions, including the epidemiology and clinical impact of AKI, diagnostic criteria and biomarkers, risk factors, and short- and long-term outcomes such as mortality, renal recovery, and progression to chronic kidney disease. This structured approach enabled clear organization and comparison of findings across studies. In parallel, an inductive approach allowed additional insights to emerge directly from the data. These included variability in the timing of AKI onset, challenges in distinguishing transient renal dysfunction from structural injury, evolving use of biomarkers for early detection, and marked differences in diagnostic and reporting practices across high- and low-resource settings. The integration of these approaches provided a comprehensive understanding of the evidence base, revealing both established knowledge and critical gaps. Findings are presented using narrative summaries, tables, illustrate patterns, relationships, and conceptual domains.

Results and thematic analysis

Overview of included studies

A total of thirty studies were included in this scoping review, encompassing peer-reviewed journal articles, narrative and systematic reviews, as well as the most relevant institutional reports published between 1995 and 2025. The core studies were identified following the first stage of the scoping review framework by Arksey and O'Malley (2005), with methodological refinements from Levac et al. (2010) in the second stage, and the Joanna Briggs Institute approach as described by Peters et al. (2015) in the third stage. As summarized in Table 2, the included studies were drawn from 28 different journals and institutional sources, with an average of 3.8 authors per publication. The majority of studies (approximately 87%) involved multiple authors, highlighting a trend toward interdisciplinary collaboration among neonatologists, pediatric nephrologists, and perinatology researchers. These studies span three decades, reflecting a growing interest in neonatal acute kidney injury (nAKI) following perinatal asphyxia. Earlier publications were primarily retrospective observational cohorts and case series, whereas more recent studies increasingly apply standardized AKI definitions, employ prospective designs, and introduce novel renal biomarkers—reflecting advances in clinical nephrology and neonatal care. Geographically, the studies were conducted across high-income countries (such as the USA, the UK, and Japan) as well as low- and middle-income countries (including Nigeria, India, and Brazil), underscoring that post-asphyxia AKI is a globally relevant health issue.

Notably, 77% of the 30 included studies were published after 2010, reflecting a growing interest within clinical and research communities in neonatal nephrology complications over the past decade. Prior to 2005, most studies were retrospective case series or small cohort investigations, whereas more recent studies increasingly employ prospective designs and multicenter collaborations. Geographically, the majority of research has originated from high-income countries with advanced neonatal intensive care facilities, including the USA (8 studies), the UK (4), Japan (3), and other European countries (5). Methodological approaches used in the existing studies

The methodological features of the literature about post-asphyxia AKI in neonates show that it is a clinically oriented field that keeps on standardizing the definitions and parameters of outcome assessment. Different types of methodologies have been used in the 30 studies analyzed here, and they are summarized in Table 3. Since many studies have utilized a combination of methodological strategies, e.g., clinical observational designs together with biomarker analysis or a cohort study supplemented with laboratory experiments, the total count of methods mentioned surpasses that of the included studies. Most of the existing work is based on observational designs, which points to the ethical and practical limitations of experimental research in the neonatal setting. Prospective and retrospective cohort studies were the top choices of research designs. It is no wonder, one can use those designs to study the incidence, risk factors, and even the short-term outcomes of AKI following perinatal asphyxia. In addition, case control studies, although less used, offer a comparative framework for investigating the main risk factors related to the development of AKI. Cross-sectional studies have provided some descriptive data on how widespread renal dysfunction is, and its clinical correlates, but they cannot be relied upon for establishing temporal sequences. Only a few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were found, and most of them focused on neuroprotective or supportive interventions in asphyxiated neonates. These RCTs had renal outcomes only as secondary endpoints. Regarding the diagnostic methodology, there has been a steady trend towards the use of standardized AKI definitions, especially the incorporation of neonatal modified Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria in research work after the 2015 disclosure. While a number of studies have utilized serum creatinine trend and urine output assessment, a growing fraction of those studies has additionally looked at novel biomarkers such as neutrophil gelatinase, associated lipocalin (NGAL), and cystatin C for the further enhancement of early detection. On the other hand, very few of these studies went ahead to follow up their subjects beyond the neonatal period, thus highlighting an important limitation in the understanding of long-term renal outcomes after asphyxia related to AKI. In general, the strength of the research methodology in this area has been gradually enhanced. Nevertheless, there are still shortcomings in terms of employing longitudinal designs, standardizing outcome reporting, and multi-center collaboration.

Epidemiology and incidence

The reported incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) following perinatal asphyxia varies widely, ranging from 20% to over 60% in different studies. This variability reflects substantial heterogeneity in research methodologies, diagnostic criteria, clinical settings, and population characteristics. Earlier studies conducted before standardized definitions of neonatal AKI were established, often relied on single SCr measurements or nonspecific clinical signs of renal failure. Because neonatal SCr in the first few days of life largely reflects maternal creatinine transferred via the placenta, such approaches likely underestimate early kidney injury, contributing to inconsistent incidence reports (Jetton & Askenazi 2015; Selewski et al. 2015). Furthermore, the frequency of SCr and UOP assessments directly affects detection: studies with limited monitoring may fail to capture transient or early AKI, resulting in underreported incidence rates.

Recent research using standardized neonatal definitions, particularly the neonatal modified KDIGO criteria, has produced higher and more consistent incidence estimates, suggesting that AKI after perinatal asphyxia may be more common than previously thought, especially in neonates with moderate to severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) (Raina et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022). Population and setting differences further influence incidence. Tertiary referral centers and NICUs typically admit the most severely affected neonates with complicated perinatal courses, including multi-organ dysfunction, resulting in higher AKI prevalence. Conversely, studies from regional or mixed-level hospitals often report lower rates, reflecting both milder disease and reduced opportunities for frequent biochemical monitoring. Neonatal characteristics also modulate AKI risk. Preterm and small-for-gestational-age infants, with immature kidneys and fewer nephrons, are particularly susceptible to post-asphyxia AKI (Selewski et al. 2015; Jetton et al., 2017). The severity of asphyxia remains the most important predictor. Measures such as Apgar scores, cord blood gas analysis, and the degree of HIE correlate closely with renal injury: neonates at Sarnat stage II or III exhibit significantly higher rates of AKI than those with mild HIE or biochemical evidence of hypoxia alone. Severe asphyxia redistributes cardiac output away from the kidneys toward vital organs, causing renal ischemia, tubular necrosis, loss of autoregulation, and functional impairment (Harer & Askenazi 2020; Stojanovi et al., 2021).

Health system factors also shape reported AKI patterns. In low- and middle-income countries, higher AKI rates are often linked to delayed or inadequate resuscitation, limited access to therapeutic hypothermia, perinatal infections, and challenges in postnatal monitoring. Underdiagnosis remains a major issue, suggesting that true incidence may be even higher in these settings. In contrast, well-equipped settings benefit from early detection through frequent SCr and UOP monitoring, aggressive supportive care, and standardized clinical protocols, which can reduce both the frequency and severity of AKI (Raina et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022). Therefore, the epidemiological and incident setting of the rate of SCr and UOP assessment will influence rates of AKI. The wide variation in reported AKI incidence highlights both the clinical complexity of post-asphyxia renal injury and methodological limitations in research. Nevertheless, one consistent finding is that AKI occurrence increases with HIE severity, making the kidney an early and sensitive marker of systemic hypoxic insult. These observations underscore the need for routine, frequent monitoring of SCr and UOP, consistent use of internationally recognized definitions of neonatal AKI, validation of early renal biomarkers, and context-sensitive surveillance methods. Such approaches are essential to produce reliable incidence data, guide bedside clinical decision-making, and provide prognostic information for neonates with post-asphyxia AKI.

Pathophysiology

Post-asphyxia acute kidney injury (AKI) in neonates is primarily driven by renal hypoxia–ischemia, which leads to acute tubular necrosis. Perinatal asphyxia, resulting from impaired placental and pulmonary gas exchange, causes systemic hypoxemia, hypercapnia, and metabolic acidosis. In response, the body redistributes blood flow to vital organs such as the brain and heart, significantly reducing renal perfusion—particularly in metabolically active regions like the renal cortex and outer medulla (Jetton & Askenazi 2015; Selewski et al. 2018). Prolonged or severe ischemia disrupts tubular cell function, leading to loss of polarity, impaired reabsorption, and eventual cell death through apoptosis and necrosis. In addition to ischemia, oxidative stress plays a major role in kidney injury. Reoxygenation during neonatal resuscitation generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which overwhelm the immature antioxidant defenses of the neonatal kidney. This results in lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage, all of which worsen tubular injury and delay recovery (Saugstad, 2015; Sweetman et al., 2019). Inflammation further exacerbates renal damage, as hypoxic–ischemic injury activates the immune response, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and endothelial dysfunction. This promotes leukocyte adhesion, vascular obstruction, and increased permeability, thereby intensifying ischemic injury (Bonventre & Zuk 2018; Selewski et al., 2019). Persistent inflammation may also contribute to long-term renal impairment and increased risk of chronic kidney disease (Harer & Askenazi 2020). Reperfusion injury following resuscitation adds another layer of damage, as sudden oxygen delivery triggers calcium influx and further ROS production, leading to mitochondrial and endothelial dysfunction (Kalogeris et al., 2016; Kaddourah et al. 2017). Although therapeutic hypothermia is commonly used in managing hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, its effects on renal outcomes remain unclear (Stojanović et al., 2021).

Diagnosis and definitions

The diagnosis of acute kidney injury (AKI) in neonates has traditionally relied on functional markers such as serum creatinine (SCr) and urine output (UOP). However, these markers are limited in the neonatal period due to distinct physiological factors. In the first few days of life, SCr largely reflects maternal renal function via placental transfer rather than the neonate’s own glomerular filtration rate (GFR), meaning significant kidney injury may remain undetected until SCr rises (Guignard & Drukker 1999; Andreoli 2009). Similarly, UOP alone is an unreliable predictor, as non-oliguric AKI is common, particularly after hypoxic-ischemic injury, leading to under-recognition when urine volume is the sole criterion (Perico & Remuzzi, 1998; Jetton & Askenazi 2015). Early studies of neonatal AKI were further complicated by inconsistent definitions, including absolute SCr thresholds, relative increases from baseline, or clinician-defined renal failure, resulting in wide variability in reported incidence and outcomes (Gupta et al. 2005; Selewski et al. 2015). The establishment of consensus definitions in adults and older children led to the KDIGO guidelines, which were later adapted for neonates to account for gestational age–adjusted UOP and relative changes in SCr. Since 2015, adoption of the neonatal-modified KDIGO criteria has improved standardization, enabling accurate staging that correlates with disease severity and clinical outcomes (Jetton & Askenazi 2015; Selewski et al. 2018; Kaddourah et al. 2017). In infants with perinatal asphyxia, KDIGO-defined AKI is associated with higher mortality, longer NICU stays, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and increased risk of multi-organ failure (Harer & Askenazi 2020; Stojanovi et al., 2021). Despite these advances, challenges in diagnosis persist. Establishing an accurate baseline SCr is particularly difficult in preterm infants, and creatinine kinetics vary considerably during the first postnatal week. These limitations have spurred research into early biomarkers of kidney injury. Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) rises within hours of hypoxic-ischemic injury, preceding detectable changes in SCr (Mishra et al., 2005; Sweetman et al., 2019). Cystatin C, a low molecular weight protein freely filtered by the glomerulus and minimally influenced by maternal levels, offers a more precise estimate of neonatal GFR and early renal dysfunction (Herget et al., 2004; Treiber et al., 2016). Kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), a marker of proximal tubular damage, is useful in detecting ischemic tubular injury in neonates with perinatal asphyxia (Han et al., 2002; Selewski et al., 2019). Although evidence from the late 1990 s to 2025 demonstrates the potential of these biomarkers for early AKI detection, they are not yet part of routine neonatal care due to limited assay availability, high costs, lack of universally accepted cutoffs, and insufficient large-scale studies showing improved outcomes. Consequently, the diagnosis of neonatal AKI remains primarily reliant on SCr, UOP, clinical assessment, and standardized criteria such as the neonatal-modified KDIGO.

Risk factors

Post-asphyxia AKI in neonates is influenced by multiple risk factors that reflect both the severity of the hypoxic-ischemic insult and the neonate’s physiological response. Among these, the duration and intensity of hypoxia are the strongest predictors, as prolonged oxygen deprivation leads to renal ischemia, tubular injury, and endothelial dysfunction, potentially resulting in acute tubular necrosis. Observational and cohort studies consistently show that neonates with severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), particularly those at Sarnat stage III, have markedly higher rates of AKI compared with those with milder forms of HIE (Jetton et al., 2017; Raina et al., 2020). A low Apgar score, especially at 1 min, serves as a clinical surrogate for perinatal compromise and is independently associated with AKI development. Infants with a score below five are more likely to require intensive resuscitation and experience early cardiovascular instability, both of which reduce renal perfusion and oxygen delivery (Shah et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2022). Aggressive resuscitation measures, including chest compressions, positive pressure ventilation, and endotracheal intubation, are linked to higher AKI risk, reflecting both the severity of the initial insult and reperfusion-related renal injury (Selewski et al. 2015; Zappitelli et al., 2020). Hemodynamic instability, such as hypotension, shock, and the need for inotropic support, further compromises renal blood flow and predisposes the renal cortex and medulla to ischemic damage. Additional markers of systemic failure, including elevated serum lactate, ongoing metabolic acidosis, and oliguria, are predictive of post-asphyxia AKI (Harer and Askenazi 2020; Stojanovi et al., 2021). Neonatal AKI rarely occurs in isolation; it often reflects a broader critical illness involving multi-organ dysfunction. Hepatic, cardiac, and metabolic abnormalities can exacerbate renal injury, with impaired cardiac output or sepsis further reducing renal perfusion, while hyperbilirubinemia and metabolic acidosis can directly or indirectly damage tubular function (Harer and Askenazi 2020; Stojanovi et al., 2021). Therapeutic hypothermia (TH), a neuroprotective intervention for moderate to severe HIE, introduces additional complexity in AKI assessment. Cooling alters systemic and renal hemodynamics, affects glomerular filtration and tubular solute reabsorption, and may delay the rise in serum creatinine, resulting in a lag in AKI detection (Sweetman et al., 2019; Zappitelli et al., 2021). While TH can reduce AKI risk by limiting secondary ischemic injury, stabilizing hemodynamics, and attenuating inflammatory responses, these protective effects are not uniform and depend on protocol variations, timing, and patient characteristics (Raina et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022). Accurate diagnosis and prognosis of post-asphyxia AKI, therefore, require careful evaluation of clinical history, early recognition of systemic compromise, continuous hemodynamic monitoring, and consideration of TH effects. The integration of neonatal-modified KDIGO criteria with emerging biomarkers such as NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C facilitates early diagnosis, severity grading, and targeted interventions aimed at preventing AKI progression and improving outcomes (Kim et al., 2019; Puig, Gonzalez et al., 2018; Kanbay et al., 2018).

Management strategies

Management of post-asphyxia AKI in neonates is largely supportive, aiming to preserve renal function, prevent secondary injury, and maintain systemic homeostasis. Central to care is individualized fluid management; restriction is necessary in oliguric neonates, but volumes must suffice to maintain organ function and prevent pulmonary edema, cardiac and multi-organ failure. Decisions are guided by changes in body weight, the cumulative fluid balance, urine output, and serum electrolytes (Selewski et al. 2015; Harer and Askenazi 2020). Minimizing nephrotoxin exposure, the aminoglycosides, vancomycin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and radiocontrast agent is necessary. Dose adjustments and therapeutic monitoring when these drugs are necessary is mandatory. Laboratory assessments of serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, electrolytes, and acid–base status are germane together with rapid correction of electrolyte disturbances and metabolic acidosis (Jetton and Askenazi 2015). Hemodynamic stability remains the most important factor in preventing worsening kidney injury, with hypotension, shock, or low perfusion addressed via fluids, inotropes, and treatment of underlying causes.

In severe or refractory cases, renal replacement therapy is indicated for oliguria/anuria, rising blood urea nitrogen, electrolyte imbalances, or fluid overload. Peritoneal dialysis is preferred for its simplicity and hemodynamic stability, whereas continuous renal replacement therapy offers precise fluid and solute management but requires specialized equipment, vascular access, and anticoagulation (Zappitelli et al., 2020; Harer and Askenazi 2020). Pharmacologic interventions such as theophylline and aminophylline have been investigated in randomized trials for AKI prevention. Low-dose theophylline inhibits adenosine interaction with its receptors without inhibiting the cyclic adenosine monophosphate phosphodiesterase. However, no therapy has been conclusively validated. Experimental approaches targeting oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, or tubular regeneration remain largely preclinical or early-phase clinical studies (Sweetman et al., 2019; Jetton & Askenazi 2015). Biomarker-guided strategies using NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C may allow early identification of high-risk neonates and personalized interventions, improving recovery and outcomes (Sweetman et al., 2019; Jetton & Askenazi 2015).

Outcomes and prognosis

Newborns who develop post-asphyxia acute kidney injury (AKI) face a substantially higher risk of adverse short-term outcomes, with mortality rates consistently exceeding those of asphyxiated infants without renal involvement. The severity of AKI, as classified by the neonatal-modified KDIGO criteria, closely mirrors the extent of the hypoxic-ischemic insult and the presence of multi-organ dysfunction, with higher AKI stages associated with progressively greater mortality (Selewski et al. 2015; Harer and Askenazi 2020). Mechanistically, renal injury can contribute to systemic instability through fluid overload, electrolyte disturbances, metabolic acidosis, and accumulation of nitrogenous wastes, all of which exacerbate cardiovascular and neurologic compromise. AKI is also linked to longer NICU stays, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and extended use of vasoactive medications, highlighting the importance of vigilant monitoring of hemodynamics, fluid balance, and metabolic status (Jetton & Askenazi 2015; Stojanovi et al., 2021). Extended NICU admission increases the risk of hospital-acquired complications such as sepsis, intraventricular hemorrhage, and ventilator-associated lung injury, further compounding morbidity and resource utilization. Beyond the acute phase, post-asphyxia AKI raises concerns about long-term renal sequelae. Even brief episodes of neonatal AKI can result in structural and functional kidney changes, including reduced nephron number and tubular injury, which may remain clinically silent until later childhood or adolescence. These alterations can predispose survivors to chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypertension, proteinuria, and reduced renal functional reserve, particularly in those with repeated insults or additional perinatal and postnatal risk factors such as prematurity, persistent hypotension, exposure to nephrotoxic drugs, volume depletion, infections, or environmental toxins (Raina et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022).

Current evidence on long-term outcomes is limited, hampered by small cohorts, heterogeneous follow-up durations, and variable AKI definitions. Some studies suggest that renal impairment persists in 20–30% of critically ill neonatal AKI cases, whereas others report only subclinical dysfunction detectable through biomarkers or imaging (Selewski et al. 2018; Harer and Askenazi 2020). The absence of standardized post-discharge surveillance guidelines further delays early detection and intervention, allowing progressive kidney injury to go unrecognized. To mitigate both acute and long-term consequences, it is crucial to identify high-risk neonates early, considering factors such as the severity of hypoxic-ischemic insult, low Apgar scores, multi-organ involvement, and resuscitation-related risks. Structured follow-up programs—including serial monitoring of serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, urinalysis, proteinuria, blood pressure, and growth parameters—can facilitate timely recognition of renal impairment. Emerging biomarkers such as NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C offer the potential to detect subclinical injury earlier, allowing for more precise risk stratification and individualized management. Ultimately, reducing both immediate harm and long-term complications of post-asphyxia AKI requires a combination of early recognition, high-quality supportive care, adherence to standardized diagnostic criteria such as neonatal KDIGO, avoidance of nephrotoxins, maintenance of hemodynamic stability, and structured long-term follow-up. Further high-quality trials are needed to establish optimal fluid management, pharmacologic interventions, and dialysis strategies for this vulnerable population.

Conclusion and policy recommendations

Conclusion

Perinatal asphyxia remains a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality and is increasingly recognized as a global condition with significant renal implications. This scoping review aims to highlight acute kidney injury (AKI) as a frequent and clinically important complication following perinatal asphyxia. Reported AKI incidence varies widely across studies, primarily due to differences in case definitions, diagnostic criteria, severity of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), and healthcare system contexts. The number of publications has gradually increased between 1995 and 2025, particularly after 2010, reflecting growing awareness of neonatal AKI and the adoption of neonatal-modified KDIGO criteria. Evidence consistently demonstrates that the risk of post-asphyxia AKI rises sharply with illness severity and is often accompanied by multi-organ dysfunction, higher mortality, prolonged NICU stays, and potential long-term renal sequelae. Unlike previous reviews that mainly focused on neurological outcomes of perinatal asphyxia or AKI from mixed etiologies, this review provides an integrated synthesis with a primary focus on post-asphyxia AKI. It emphasizes the kidney as a highly sensitive organ reflecting systemic hypoxic-ischemic insult and highlights the close association between HIE severity and renal outcomes. A key contribution of this review is its identification of methodological and conceptual heterogeneity in the literature, including variability in AKI definitions, reliance on late functional markers such as serum creatinine, and limited use of emerging renal biomarkers. Furthermore, the review underscores the underrepresentation of low- and middle-income countries in high-quality longitudinal studies, despite these regions bearing the highest burden of perinatal asphyxia. Although the review was limited to English-language publications in the Scopus database and did not include a formal quality appraisal, it effectively maps the existing evidence and identifies priorities for clinical practice and future research. Overall, it contributes to a deeper understanding of post-asphyxia AKI as a major determinant of neonatal outcomes, reinforcing the need for early recognition and timely intervention.

Policy and practice implications

The findings of this review have important implications for neonatal care policy, clinical practice, and research prioritization. First, renal monitoring should be systematically integrated into the care pathways of neonates affected by perinatal asphyxia, alongside routine neurological assessments. National and institutional neonatal guidelines should explicitly include AKI surveillance as a standard component of care, employing common definitions such as the neonatal-modified KDIGO criteria to enable early detection, comparability across settings, and informed clinical decision-making. In resource-limited settings, critical investments are needed in laboratory infrastructure, training of neonatal healthcare providers, and access to basic renal function testing to reduce the underdiagnosis of AKI. Where feasible, validated biomarkers such as NGAL and cystatin C could be gradually introduced into clinical practice to identify high-risk infants early and guide targeted supportive care. From a healthcare system perspective, policies should focus on strengthening neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) services, including hemodynamic monitoring, safe medication use, and the availability of neonatal-specific renal replacement therapies. Given the potential long-term impact of post-asphyxia AKI, structured follow-up programs are essential for survivors, with ongoing monitoring of renal function, blood pressure, and growth. From a research perspective, donor agencies and policymakers should prioritize funding for multicenter, prospective, and longitudinal studies, particularly in regions with a high burden of disease, such as sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Local and international research collaborations can support technology transfer, methodological harmonization, and development of clinically relevant protocols. Ultimately, integrating renal health into perinatal and neonatal policy frameworks is a critical strategy to reduce morbidity and mortality associated with perinatal asphyxia. Policies that prioritize early AKI detection, standardize diagnostic approaches, and strengthen neonatal care infrastructure can improve both short- and long-term outcomes for at-risk newborns, while also contributing to broader goals of neonatal survival and lifelong health.
