Integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying post-preservation pollen viability alterations in Paeonia lactiflora
Authors: Ren Ruifen, Zhu Qinlu, Guo Jiayi, Ji Zhe, Yang Xiuyun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A significant viability decline is frequent in preserved pollen, so exploring the mechanism of viability variations is of great significance. In this study, fresh pollen of Paeonia lactiflora was used as the experimental material to comparatively analyze changes in viability, physiological traits, transcriptome profiles, and metabolome features after storage at different temperatures, aiming to elucidate the mechanisms responsible for differences in pollen viability variation. Pollen storage at 4℃ showed a significant decrease in viability, accompanied by considerably severe oxidative damage. In contrast, pollen storage at -196℃ exhibited the opposite trends in viability and oxidative damage. By comparison, storage at -20℃ had no significant effect on pollen viability or oxidative stress indicators. Differentially expressed genes involved in fatty acid metabolism displayed distinct transcriptional responses to different temperatures. Differentially accumulated metabolites were predominantly categorized into lipids, amino acids and derivatives, and phenolic acids. Transcriptomic and metabolomic data were co-enriched in linoleic acid metabolism and α-linolenic acid metabolism pathways. Key regulatory genes for jasmonic acid biosynthesis (LOX2 and JMT2.) played crucial roles in the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways. The expression trends of transcription factors TIFY, MYC2, COI1, DBB, and MYB were consistent with the changes in pollen viability, whereas JAR6 exhibited an opposite expression trend relative to pollen viability variation. Additionally, exogenous application of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid at appropriate concentrations exerted significant regulatory effect on the viability and oxidative stress indicators of storage pollen. Collectively, these results suggest that linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolism regulate the differences in oxidative stress responses of pollen under different storage temperatures by modulating the jasmonic acid biosynthesis pathway, thereby inducing variations in pollen viability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07968-8.

Introduction

Pollen, produced in the stamens of seed plants, contains the haploid genome of a species [1]. This characteristic establishes it as a key material not only for germplasm conservation, exchange, and hybridization but also for breeding-related research and applications [2]. Pollen storage is not only an effective approach to overcoming temporal and geographical barriers in parents plant hybridization but also an crucial means of conserving plant genetic diversity. Up to now, pollen preservation methods include refrigeration at 3–5℃, low-temperature freezing (−20℃ to −40℃), freeze-drying storage, vacuum drying, organic solvent preservation, cryopreservation (−80℃, −196℃), among others [3]. A large number studies have compared changes in pollen viability following different preservation methods, revealing significant variations in the effects of these methods on pollen viability [4–6]. Additionally, these studies have confirmed that the most widely used approaches are refrigeration at 3–5℃, freezing at −20℃, and cryopreservation at −196℃ [7, 8].

Therefore, in response to the common preservation methods, researchers conducted extensive comparisons of preservation effects of viability differences after storage at different temperatures. Such as in Kiwi, Date and Paspalum pollen storage, the storage effects of −18℃ or −20℃ remained relatively stable for about 3 months, but the viability of pollen retained a high level after storage at −196℃ for one year, and the duration for pollen viability at 4℃ storage was shorter than that at −20℃ and − 196℃ [4–6]. Also in Litchi chinensis, Paeonia suffruticosa, and Carya cathayensis pollen preservation, viability after − 196℃ cryopreservation was higher than that storage at −20℃ or 4℃ [9–11]. Although among the three common preservation temperatures, −196℃ storage seems to have a better preservation effect over a short period of time compared to the other two temperatures. However, after long-term storage at −196℃, the pollen viability also shows various changing trends, and in most cases, it is mainly a decline [12]. Therefore, in order to further optimize the pollen preservation technology, it is necessary to conduct in-depth research on the underlying mechanisms based on the differences in pollen viability after preservation.

However, existing studies on the mechanism by which different storage temperatures affect pollen viability have mainly focused on the physiological level, and such research remains relatively scarce. Ren et al. [13] studied the pollen of two Paeonia lactiflora cultivars from different provenance after storage at −20℃ and − 196℃, and found that differences in antioxidant systems were one of the primary reasons for variations in viability after storag. Furthermore, Huang et al. [14] reported that the extent of ROS-induced oxidative damage to membrane lipids and proteins accounted for difference in pollen viability following storage at 4℃, −20℃, −40℃, −80℃ and − 196℃. Nevertheless, most existing studies mostly have centered on the physiological aspects, with a primary focus on investigating differences attributed to species/varieties or preservation duration. Therefore, it is crucial to further explore the metabolic and genetic mechanisms underlying changes in pollen viability after preservation.

Metabolites, as intermediates and end products of biological processes, are more easily readily linked to phenotypic traits [15]. Pollen preservation primarily aims to mitigate viability decline by reducing the respiratory rate of plant material [16], it is well known that the core objective is to halt or slow overall metabolism. Metabolomics effectively reveals alterations in biological metabolic processes and elucidates the underpinnings of physiological and biochemical phenomena [17, 18]. To date, however, no metabolomic studies have been reported on the preservation of plant germplasm resources. Transcriptomics, a critical link between genomic genetic information and proteomic functional data, seeks to uncover the molecular mechanisms of pathways and trait regulatory networks in biological processes [19]. While transcriptomics has been applied to investigate the preservation mechanism of plant germplasm resources, such studies remain relatively scarce, particularly in preservation. The only existing report focuses on the mechanism of pollen storage at −196℃ in Pinus sylvestris, which effectively revealed the physiological responses and associated molecular mechanism underlying cryopreservation [20]. In addition,, transcriptome has contributed significantly to elucidating preservation mechanisms for other plant material germplasm materials, including cryopreservation of Picea abies embryogenic callus [21], Borjahaji meristems [22] and Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings [23]. With advances in sequencing technology, the integration of metabolomics and transcriptomics enables comprehensive analysis of differentially expressed genes and metabolites based on physiological, growth, and developmental traits. This multi-omics approach facilitates the identification of key metabolic pathways and genes, as well as the revelation of their regulatory mechanisms [24]. Building on the aforementioned research, a combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis is necessary to investigate the biosynthesis and molecular regulatory mechanisms of plant metabolites in a more comprehensive and in-depth manner [25].

There is a paucity of mechanistic studies on pollen viability changes following storage at different temperatures. Moreover, most of the existing research is limited to the physiological level, failing to probe into the metabolic and molecular levels. Accordingly, in this study, fresh pollen was used as the control. Building on the observed differences in the pollen viability and physiological indicators after storage at different temperatures, we comprehensively compared and analyzed metabolomic and transcriptomic changes in pollen after storage at 4℃, −20℃, and − 196℃. To explore the molecular mechanism underlying the pollen viability changes after storage, thereby providing theoretical support for the long-term and effective preservation of plant germplasm resources.

Materials and methods

Pollen collection

Pollen of Paeonia lactiflora ‘Da Ban Hong’ used in this study was collected from Caijiaya Village, Linxia Hui Autonomous Prefecture, Gansu Province (E 103°41′57″, N 35°10′26″). The plants material was identified by plant taxonomists Zhang Kongying from Luoyang International Peony Garden, and its taxonomic identity was verified. To date, no voucher specimen of this material has been deposited in a publicly available herbarium, but a duplicate specimen is preserved in the Specimen Museum of Shanxi Agricultural University. During the full flowering period, flower buds approaching anthesis were selected for anther collection, which was performed under optimal temperature and humidity conditions (air relative humidity ≤ 60%, temperature optimally 25–30℃). The collected anthers were spread evenly on the cellophane and air- dried naturally at room temperature (23 ± 2℃) for 24 h. Subsequently, pollen was isolated by sieving through an 100-mesh sieve.

Pollen storage

Each 0.1 g aliquot of pollen was wrapped in 3.5 × 3.5 cm tin foil, then transferred to a 2 mL freezing tube, labeled clearly, and arranged neatly in a cryobox. The cryobox was then directly placed for storage at 4℃, −20℃ and in liquid nitrogen (−196℃). After 3 months of preservation, pollen storage at 4 °C and − 20 °C was thawed at room temperature for 25 min. For pollen storage at −196℃, a two-step thawing protocol was adopted: cryotubes were first rinsed under running water (15 ± 2 °C) for 3 min, followed by equilibration in 20℃ water for 25 min to complete thawing.

Pollen germination determination

Pollen germination rates were determined using the hanging drop culture method [26]. The germination medium was composed of 10% (w/v) sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich S7903) and 0.1% (w/v) boric acid (Sigma-Aldrich B6768). Prior to the assay, absorbent cotton was moistened with distilled water and evenly placed in petri dishes to maintain a humid environment. For each biological replicate, 15 µL of the pollen germination medium was pipetted onto a coverslip, and 20 µL of the same medium was added to a glass cavity slide. Approximately 100 µg (0.0001 g) of pollen grains were transferred onto the coverslip using a dissecting needle and gently mixed with the medium. The coverslip was then inverted and placed over the glass cavity slide to form a hanging drop. Subsequently, the slide was transferred to the pre- humidified petri dish and incubated at 25℃ for 4 h in the dark. Each treatment was performed with four biological replicates, and the average germination rate was calculated. Pollen germination was observed under a Leica DM-500 microscope (20 × objective). For each replicate, three non-overlapping random fields of view were examined, with at least 150 pollen grains counted per field. A pollen grain was considered germinated if its pollen tube length was at least twice the the diameter of pollen grain.

Pollen physiological index determination

Superoxide anion (O2·-) content was determined by the hydroxylamine oxidation method [27]. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radical (·OH) contents were measured with Nanjing Jiancheng kits (A064-1-1, A018). Malondialdehyde (MDA) content and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were assayed as described by Li et al. [28], employing the thiobarbituric acid reduction and nitroblue tetrazolium reduction, respectively. Catalase (CAT) activity (hydrogen peroxide reduction method) and AsA content (ferric chloride reduction method) were measured with slight modifications to the protocols reported by Prochazkova et al. [29] and Kampfenkel et al. [30], respectively. Glutathione (GSH) content and glutathione reductase (GR) activity were determined using Nanjing Jiancheng kits. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was assayed as described by Nakano and Asada [31] with minor modifications. Detailed experimental procedures for all assays are provided in Ren et al. [32] and the corresponding kit manuals.

RNA sequencing

Combined ethanol precipitation and CTAB-PBIOZOL methods were used for RNA extraction. Specifically, total RNA was isolated with CTAB-PBIOZOL reagent (BioFlux BPC1521), followed by phase separation using chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (24:1) and dissolution in DEPC-treated water (Diethylpyrocarbonate, Sigma D5758). RNA quality verified by a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (RNA HS Assay Kit Q32852) and Qsep400 Bioanalyzer, with qualified samples meeting the criteria of RIN > 8.0 and 260/280 ratio ranging from 1.8 to 2.0. Poly(A) + mRNA was then enriched from high-quality total RNA using Oligo(dT)25 magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher 61005). RNA-seq libraries were constructed with NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB E7645S) and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Majorbio BioTech) with 150 bp paired-end reads, ensuring each sample generated 20 million reads. Fresh pollen was used as a control, and transcriptome sequencing was performed on pollen samples that storaged at 4℃, −20℃, and − 196℃ for 3 months. Each storage condition (including the control) was set with three biological replicates, resulting in the construction of 12 RNA-seq libraries in total.

Preparation and extraction of samples for broadly targeted metabolic analysis

Vacuum freeze-drying was performed as follows:, pollen (0.6 g) was placed in a freeze-dryer (Scientz-100 F) for vacuum freeze-drying, then ground (30 Hz, 1.5 min) into a powder using a grinder (MM 400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. To 50 mg of the lyophilized pollen powder, 1200 µL of pre-cooled 70% methanol-water extract was added. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged alternately every 30 min, for a total of six times. Subsequently, 1200 µL of methanol- water internal standard extract was added, and the sample was vortexed for 30 s every 30 min (six times total). After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was collected by aspiration. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous filter membrane, transferred to injection vials, and subjected to UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

UPLC-MS/MS conditions

The filtered supernatant (samples extracts) was analyzed via ultra- performance liquid chromatography - tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC- MS/MS), using an ExionLCTM AD UPLC system coupled with a MS/MS detector. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm particle size, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The mobile phases consisted of two components: phase A (ultrapure water containing 0.1% formic acid) and phase B (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid). The elution gradient was 5% phase B for 0.00 min, and then the percentage of phase B was increased linearly to 95% within 9.00 min and maintained at 95% for 1 min. Then the proportion of phase B was reduced to 5% immediately after the hold period, followed by equilibration at 5% until 14 min (0.35 mL/min), column temperature 40℃, injection volume 2 µL.

Additionally, the electrospray ionization (ESI) temperature was set to 550℃, and the ion spray voltage (IS) was adjusted to 5500 V in positive ion mode and − 4500 V in negative ion mode. Ion source gas I (GSI), ion source gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) were set to 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) was set to ‘high’. The quadrupole (qQQ) scans were performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, with nitrogen used as the collision gas and its flow rate set to a ‘medium’. The declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for each MRM ion pair were further optimized. During each elution period, a specific set of MRM ion pairs was monitored, which corresponded to the metabolites eluting in that time window.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Reverse transcription of RNA was performed using the FastKing One-Step gDNA Removal and First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Premix (KR118-02, Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing), a reagent specifically designed for simultaneous genomic DNA (gDNA) elimination and first-strand cDNA synthesis. Primers targeting the gene of interest were designed via IDT’s online PrimerQuest tool (https://sg.idtdna.com/Primerquest/Home/Index) and subsequently synthesized by Beijing RiboXingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing), detailed primer sequences are provided in Table S1. PCR amplification was carried out with cDNA as the template, using a total reaction volume of 20 µL. The reaction mixture consisted of 1 µL cDNA template, 2 µL upstream primer, 2 µL downstream primer, 5 µL double-distilled water (ddH2O), and 10 µL SYBR Premix EX Taq (TAKARA, Otsu, Japan). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was employed as the internal reference gene, and the relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−∆∆Cq method. Each treatment group was subjected to three biological replicates.

Pollen Linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid and jasmonic acid determination

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was employed for the quantification of linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, and jasmonic acid. Commercial ELISA kits of α-linolenic acid and jasmonic acid were purchased from Shanghai Meiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with the corresponding catalog numbers listed as follows: MO-P34071R and MO-Z15593. Linoleic acid determination kit was provided by Shanghai Keaibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., the catalog number is CB10204-Go. Pollen (0.1 g) was ground with 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.0) to form a homogenate at 4℃. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 xg for 20 min at 4℃, and the resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. The supernatant (50 µL) was mixed with 100 µL of detection antibody, followed by incubation at 37℃ for 60 min. Subsequently, substrate A (50 µL) and substrate B (50 µL) were added sequentially, and the mixture was incubated at 37℃ for 15 min in the dark. After the reaction, 50 µL of stop solution was added, and the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was measured within 15 min. Each treatment was performed in six biological replicates, and the results were expressed as the mean value.

Addition of exogenous Linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid

Pollen storage at −196℃ for 3 months was used as the experimental material, and we evaluated the effects of linoleic acid (L812256-1 g, Shanghai MacLean Biochemical Technology Co., LTD, China) at 0.5 mM and α-linolenic acid (L812361-5 mL, Shanghai MacLean Biochemical Technology Co., LTD, China) at 25 µM. For pollen storage at 4℃ for 3 months, a specific inhibitor of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid (CP-24879 hydrochloride, Sigma, C9115) was applied at a concentration of 25 µM. The respective regulator solution (linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, or inhibitor) were added to stored pollen at a ratio of 1:100 (w/v), followed by incubation in the dark at 37℃ for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 2000×g for 30 s, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting sediment was washed three times with 0.01 mol/L PBS (pH = 7.4). The residual pollen was used for the determination of pollen viability and physiological indices.

Statistical analysis

Sequencing data were filtered using fastp software to yield high-quality clean reads for downstream analyses [33]. Subsequently, de novo assembly of the clean reads was performed with the Trinity program to generate longer transcript contigs [34]. Hierarchical clustering of the assembled transcripts was conducted using Corset software [35], and unigene sequences were aligned against databases including KEGG, NR, and DIAMOND BLASTX software [36] to acquire annotation information of unigene. Coding sequence (CDS) prediction was performed on the assembled transcripts using TransDecoder (https://github.com/TransDecoder/) to obtain the amino acid sequences corresponding to the transcripts. Transcripts expression levels were quantified using RSEM software [37]. Gene expression levels were estimated by calculating the Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values, which accounts for transcripts length. Differential expression analysis between sample groups carried out using DESeq2 [38], with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) screened under the criteria of | log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Plant transcription factors were identified using iTAK software [39, 40].

Mass spectrometry data were processed using the Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB SCIEX Company, USA). Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with a triple quadrupole mass spectrometry was employed for metabolite quantification [41]. Based on the constructed OPLS-DA model (Fig. S1), differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified using the following criteria: variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, and fold change (FC) ≥ 2 -fold upregulation or ≤ 0.5-fold downregulation. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) with grouping, KEGG pathway analysis, and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) were performed using R-based modeling packages [42]. These analyses aimed to elucidate the metabolic pathways associated with post-storage changes in pollen viability. Each sample was analyzed with three biological replicates.

An integrated analysis of the two omics datasets (transcriptome and metabolome) was performed using the orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) model. Based on the analysis results of DEGs and DAMs, mapped the same comparison group of DEGs and DAMs onto kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway diagrams to identify co-enriched pathways. Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated between the quantitative expression levels of DEGs and the relative contents of DAMs. Finally, pairs of DEGs and DAMs with an absolute Pearson’s correlation coefficient > 0.8 and P < 0.05 were selected for expression trend analysis.

One-way analysis of variance ANOVA with a significance level of P < 0.05 was performed using SPSS 17.0 software (Version 17.0 SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to analyze pollen viability and physiological indices. Meanwhile, Microsoft Excel 2013 software (Microsoft Corp., Richmond, CA, USA) was utilized for data collation, statistical summary, and chart generation.

Results

Changes in the viability of pollen after storage at three temperatures

After 3 months of storage, pollen storage at 4℃ exhibited significantly lower viability than fresh pollen; while pollen storage at −20℃ showed no significant difference compared with fresh pollen. In contrast, pollen storage at −196℃ displayed significantly higher viability than fresh pollen (Fig. 1-A, Fig. S2).

Changes in the oxidative stress indicators of pollen after storage at three temperatures

After storage at 4℃, the levels of H2O2 and •OH in pollen significantly increased, while those in pollen stored at −20℃ shoued no significant difference compared with fresh pollen. In contrast, H2O2 content in pollen stored at −196℃ was significantly lower than that in fresh pollen (Fig. 1-C, D). For the oxidative damage marker MDA: its content significantly increased in pollen stored at 4℃, showed no significant difference between − 20℃ stored pollen and fresh pollen, and decreased significantly in pollen stored at −196℃ (Fig. 1-E). The CAT activity, AsA and GSH content in 4℃ stored pollen all decreased significantly, while there was no significant difference between the pollen stored at −20℃ and fresh pollen, but increased significantly in −196℃ stored pollen (Fig. 1-G, K, L). The activities of APX and GR significantly showed distinct patterns, both significantly increased in pollen after storage at 4℃. At −20℃, APX activity remained unchanged compared with fresh pollen, while GR activity decreased significantly. At −196℃, APX activity decreased significantly, whereas GR activity showed no significant difference (Fig. 1-H, I). Notably, the O2•- content and GPX activity in the pollen showed no significant changes after storage at different temperatures (Fig. 1-B, J).

Transcriptomic analysis of pollen after storage at three temperatures

This study investigated the physiological changes and underlying molecular mechanisms iof pollen after storage at different temperatures. A total of 12 libraries were constructed, generating 58.51 Gb of clean data in total, with an average of 5.96 Gb per sample. Each library yielded 1989-24,570,000 valid data, with Q30 bases percentage exceeding 92.89% and GC contents greater than 48.11% across all libraries (Table S2). In total, 41,485 genes were identified through annotation analyses.Among these, 28,646 genes were annotated in the NR database, and 22,355 genes were annotated in the KEGG database (Table S3). Collectively, 41,485 genes, accounting for approximately 50% of the total detected genes, were successfully annotated across all databases used in this study.

Between fresh pollen and pollen storage at 4℃, 214 DEGs were identified, including 96 up-regulated and 118 down-regulated genes. A total of 231 DEGs were detected between fresh pollen and pollen storage at −20℃, with 129 up-regulated and 102 down-regulated. Between the pollen storage at −196℃ and fresh pollen, 373 DEGs were identified, with 251 up-regulated and 122 down-regulated (Fig. 2-a, b, c). To verify the transcriptome data, 15 genes were randomly selected for qRT-PCR validation based on the differential expression patterns of genes in pollen stored at different temperatures. The expression trends of most selected genes were consistent with the sequencing results, which confirms the reliability of the transcriptome data (Fig. S3).

DEGs were functionally annotated based on the most relevant biochemical, metabolic, and signaling pathways, and pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to identify significantly enriched pathways in pollen stored at different temperatures. Based on the KEGG enrichment results, the top 20 most significantly enriched pathways (FDR < 0.05) are presented as scatter plots (Fig. 2-d, e, f). Pollen after storage at 4℃ showed significant enrichment in the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway, while pollen after storage at −20℃ was significantly enriched in the ABC transporters pathway. In contrast, pollen stored at −196℃ was primarily enriched in pathways including photosynthesis-antenna proteins, metabolic pathways, carbon metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism.

Transcription factors (TFs) associated with changes in pollen viability

We further analyzed the transcription factors associated with changes in pollen viability after storage at different temperatures. The results showed that FAR1 and COI1 were significantly downregulated in pollen stored at 4℃, and these two transcription factors act as positive regulators linked to the decline in pollen viability post-storage. In contrast, NAC1, C3H, TIFY, TUB, MADS-M-type, DBB, MYB-related, MYC2, and COI1 function as positive regulators associated with the increase in pollen viability after storage. Notably, FAR1, NAC1, TUB, MADS-M-type, TIFY, DBB, MYB-related, and COI1 exhibited extremely low expression or even no expression in pollen stored at −20℃. Among these transcription factors, two genes (one from the FAR1 family and one from the COI1 family) were significantly downregulated in pollen stored at 4℃. Additionally, nine genes showed significantly upregulated expression in pollen stored at −196℃, with one gene each derived from the NAC2, C3H, TIFY, TUB, MADS-M-type, DBB, MYB-related, MYC, and COI1 families (Fig. 3). Collectively, the high expression of these transcription factor genes appears to contribute to the enhanced viability of pollen after storage at different temperatures.

Metabolomic analysis of the pollen after storage at three temperatures

A total of 1320 metabolites were detected in this study (Fig. S4). Among them, 124 DAMs were identified between the pollen storage at 4℃ and fresh pollen, with 76 up-regulated and 48 down-regulated metabolites(Fig. S4-a). These DAMs were primarily categorized into lipids, amino acids and their derivatives, and phenolic acids (Fig. S4-d). Meanwhile, 82 DAMs were identified between pollen storage at −20℃ and fresh pollen, including 43 up-regulated and 39 were down-regulated metabolites (Fig. S4-b). The main classes of these DAMs were flavonoids, amino acids and derivatives, lipids, and phenolic acids (Fig. S4-e). In contrast, 66 DAMs were detected between fresh pollen and pollen storage at −196℃, with 30 were up-regulated and 36 were down-regulated (Fig. S4-c), these were mainly lipids, phenolic acids, amino acids and their derivatives (Fig. S4-f). Notably, the common DAMs across all storage temperature groups compared with fresh pollen were predominantly lipids, amino acids and their derivatives, and phenolic acids(Fig. S4).

Qualitative and quantitative analyses were also performed on the detected metabolites. After calculating the fold change (FC) values, we sorted these values in ascending order and generated a dynamic distribution map of metabolite content differences. The top 10 up-accumulated and top 10 down-accumulated DAMs were labeled in the map (Fig. 4). Notably, the results suggest that the down-accumulation of 15(R)-hydroxy linoleic acid*, randosrandos acid A*, α-hydroxy linoleic acid*, methyl linoleate, and 12,13-epoxy-9-octadecenoic acid may contribute to the changes in pollen viability after storage at these temperatures. Additionally, the up-accumulated of 17-hydroxy linolenic acid, 13 S-hydroxy-9Z,11E,15Z-octadecatrienoic acid, 13(S)-HODE, as well as the down-accumulated of 13(S)-hydroxyoctadeca-9Z,11E-dienoic acid*, α-hydroxylinoleic acid*, and 12-Oxo-phytodienoic acid, are likely key metabolites mediating changes of pollen viability after storage at these temperatures.

Metabolites interact with each other to form metabolic pathways in organisms. All identified DAMs were annotated using the KEGG database, and the annotation results were categorized and visualized through KEGG pathway and MetMap analyses. The results showed that most DAMs in pollen storage at 4℃ were enriched in metabolic pathways, linoleic acid metabolism, biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, biosynthesis of amino acids, biosynthesis of cofactors, α-linolenic acid metabolism, and 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolic pathway (Fig. 5-a). Discussion

Pollen storage constitutes a crucial and effective strategy for conserving plant genetic diversity [2]. Preservation methods significantly differ in their impacts on pollen viability. In this study, we found that the pollen viability of P. lactiflora ‘Da Ban Hong’ exhibited varied responses to different storage temperatures: it decreased after storage at 4℃, remained relatively stable (with no significant changes) at −20℃, and even increased following storage at −196℃. These findings are consistent with the findings of prior studies. For example, the pollen viability of Areca catechu and Actinidia chinensis gradually declines during storage at 4℃ [4, 43], the viability of Cocos nucifera, Phoenix dactylifera and Phalaenopsis orchids pollen shows no significant fluctuations when storage at −20℃ [44–46], and the pollen viability of Glycine max, P. suffruticosa ‘Shimadaijin’ and P. lactiflora pollen increases significantly after storage at −196℃ [32, 47, 48]. Evidently, pollen viability displays distinct patterns of variation under different storage temperatures, and to a certain extent, lower temperature yield superior preservation outcomes. Additionally, the results of this study reveal that changes in oxidative stress indicators are closely correlated with the variation trends of pollen viability after storage at different temperatures. This is consistent with existing study, which confirms that oxidative stress induced during the pollen preservation process is one of the primary factors leading to the changes in pollen viability [13, 14, 49].

Recent research has elucidated that the temperature-dependent viability variations observed in preserved peony pollen may originate from corresponding fluctuations in pollen metabolic activity [50]. In the present study, transcriptional profiling identified 214, 231, and 373 DEGs through comparisons between between fresh pollen and the pollen following storage at 4℃, −20℃, and − 196℃, respectively. KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs demonstrated that linoleic acid metabolism, α-linolenic acid metabolism, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites are the core metabolic pathways governing post-preservation changes in pollen viability (Fig. 2). Concurrently, metabolic profiling results indicated that the DAMs in pollen after storage at 4℃, −20℃ and − 196℃ were primarily categorized as lipids, amino acids and their derivatives, and phenolic acids (Fig. 5). Distinct temperature-specific patterns of lipid DAMs were observed: after 4℃ storage for 3 months, all top 10 up-regulated DAMs belonged to the lipids class; following storage at −196℃, seven out of the top 10 DAMs were lipids; in contrast, storage at −20℃ led to a marked reduction in the number of lipid DAMs (Fig. 4). Specifically,, metabolites including 17-hydroxyllinolenic acid, 13 S-hydroxy-9Z,11E,15Z-octadecatrienoic acid, etc.were significantly upregulated, while rabdosia acid A* and 15(R)-hydroxylinoleic acid* were significantly downregulated (Fig. 4), all of which are classified as unsaturated fatty acids [51]. As essential endogenous metabolites, lipids play fundamental roles in numerous biological processes across plant and animal systems [51, 52]. Notably, as pivotal components of lipids, unsaturated fatty acids are critical for plant cold stress responses. They modulate fatty acid unsaturation through metabolic regulation, which in turn influences the distribution of signaling molecules in the cell membrane and the activity of membrane-localized receptors [53, 54]. This regulatory cascade modulates cold stress-responsive signaling pathways, ultimately enhancing cellular cold tolerance [53, 54]. Taken together,.these results confirm that lipid metabolism acts as the central regulatory pathway underlying the divergent changes in pollen viability observed under different storage temperature conditions, particularly the metabolism of unsaturated fatty acids.

Linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid are the most abundant unsaturated fatty acids in plant systems. They not only play key roles in plant metabolic processes but also modulate functions through their respective metabolic pathways. Accumulating studies have demonstrated that linolenic acid metabolism exerts a crucial regulatory role in plant cold stress responses [55]. Specifically, linolenic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolism via the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway, generating signaling molecules such as jasmonic acid [56]. This compound can induce plant defense responses, enhance cold tolerance, maintain membrane stability, and mitigate oxidative stress in plants [56]. While the specific function of linoleic acid as an essential unsaturated fatty acid, which in regulating plant cellular activities has not been reported, its involvement in modulating enzyme activities and inducing changes in cell viability has been well-documented in animal systems [57, 58]. This discrepancy may originate from the unique adaptive capacity of plant cells: they can upregulate the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, thereby sustaining membrane fluidity and enhancing cold stress tolerance [59, 60]. In the this study, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses demonstrated that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) associated with pollen viability changes after storage at different temperatures were co-enriched in the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways (Fig. 6). These results collectively indicate that during pollen storage under varying temperature conditions, linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid as the key components of unsaturated fatty acids, which serve as the core determinants of pollen viability variations following storage.

In addition, KEGG analysis revealed that the expression patterns of key regulatory genes in the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway were consistent with the changes in pollen viability after storage at different temperatures, such as LOX,AOS, AOC and JMT2 (Figs. 7 and 8). Previous stusies have demonstrated that LOX, AOS, AOC, and JMT serve as key genes mediating the conversion of α-linolenic acid to other unsaturated acid derivatives [61, 62]. Among these genes, LOX and JMT play pivotal roles in plant cold stress responses, and their expression is crucial under low-temperature conditions [63, 64]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the expression of LOX, AOS, AOC and JMT2 genes exerts a significant effect on the changes in pollen viability after storage at different temperatures, as these genes are closely involved in the α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid metabolic pathways.

Existing studies have established that α-linolenic acid, released via lipase-mediated hydrolysis of membrane lipids, serves as the primary substrate for unsaturated fatty acid metabolism and subsequently triggers jasmonic acid biosynthesis during plant stress responses [55, 65]. Notably, the earlier description of jasmonic acid release from membrane lipids via lipase activation is inconsistent with current metabolic models, instead lipase activation liberates fatty acids (e.g., α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid) from membrane lipids, which then act as precursors for jasmonic acid synthesis. Previous research has identified two jasmonic acid family biosynthesis pathways in Arabidopsis thaliana: the octadecane pathway, initiated from α-linolenic acid pathway, and the dihydrojasmonic acid pathway [66]. Specifically, α-linoleic acids are converted to 12- oxo linolenic acid and 9-oxo linoleic acid, respectively, by lipoxygenase (LOX) and subsequent enzymes, these intermediates then undergo cyclization to ultimately produce jasmonic acid [67]. However, research on the jasmonic acid synthesis, signaling transmission and transduction mechanism in the α-linoleic acid-dependent pathway remains insufficiently systematic. In this study, α-linolenic acid is first oxidized by LOX2, then converted to 12-OPDA through the combined catalytic action of AOS and AOC (Fig. 8). 12-OPDA is subsequently reduced and undergoes three rounds of beta-oxidation to generate jasmonic acid, thereby facilitating jasmonic acid accumulation during its synthesis peak [68]. Importantly, the transcriptional expression of AOS, AOC, and LOX genes is induced by jasmonic acid, low-temperature stress, and other stress conditions that trigger jasmonic acid biosynthesis, then forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies the expression of these genes [21, 69]. Collectively, these findings indicate that temperature variations differentially regulate LOX (lipoxygenase) expression levels in pollen during storage. Elevated LOX activity then catalyzes the oxidation of both α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid substrates. These oxidized intermediates are further metabolized by AOS and AOC to promote jasmonic acid accumulation under different storage temperatures, which in turn modulates the differential expression of the jasmonic acid synthesis regulator (JMT2) across varying low-temperature storage regimes.

Secondly, in the linoleic acid metabolism pathway, the LOX2 and LOX5_1 genes exhibited highly similar expression patterns, both negatively regulating the metabolites 9-OxoODE and 12-OxoODE metabolites (Fig. 7). In the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway, the AOS, JMT2, and AOC genes also showed coordinated expression patterns, all positively regulating the metabolites 12-OPDA and 9(S)-HpOrE (Fig. 8). Notably, expression pattern analysis revealed that genes associated with jasmonic acid biosynthesis were strongly upregulated, such as LOX2 and JMT2 (Fig. 8). These results align with the findings of Bhardwaj et al. in Caragana jubata [64], where cold stress markedly upregulated jasmonate pathway genes (e.g., CjLOX). This upregulation, in turn, modulated multiple signaling molecules (including oxylipins and jasmonates), which further regulated metabolic adjustments such as linoleic acid accumulation.Accordingly, under stress exposure, linoleic acid is released via the action of LOX. This process acts as the initiating step of the entire unsaturated fatty acid metabolic cascade and promotes jasmonic acid synthesis, which was a critical mediator of the plant stress defense response [51]. These results suggest that linoleic acid metabolism is involved in regulating jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis, thereby affecting changes in pollen viability after storage at different temperatures. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation require further investigation. Meanwhile, numerous studies have indicated that linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid can interact indirectly with each other by regulating plant physiological processes, such as antioxidant response and tolerance against adverse environmental conditions [55]. In the present study, LOX2, a key regulatory gene involved in both linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways, showed consistent variation trend with pollen viability after storage at different temperatures. Notably, the LOX2 gene was also highly enriched in both metabolic pathways (Figs. 7 and 8). Collectively, these results indicate that the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways may synergically contribute to modulating pollen viability changes after storage at different temperatures by regulating jasmonic acid biosynthesis. Furthermore, LOX2 functions as a key interactive regulatory hub in the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways during the storage of pollen at different temperatures.

The regulation of transcription factors constitutes another key component of plant response to low-temperature stress [70]. A variety of transcription factor families, including WRKY, NAC, C2H2, MYB, and bHLH, are involved in plant cold stress responses: they enhance cold tolerance by activating the expression of cold-responsive genes. Their simultaneous participation in the jasmonic acid signaling pathway provides an additional regulatory layer, further facilitating plant adaptation to low-temperature stress [71, 72]. In this study, we observed that the expression trends of transcription factors TIFY, MYC2, COI1, DBB and MYB were consistent with the changes in pollen viability after storage. Specifically, their expression was downregulated after storage at 4℃, showed no significant differences after storage at −20℃, and was significantly upregulated after storage at −196℃. In contrast, JAR6 exhibited an expression trend opposite to that of pollen viability across different storage temperatures (Fig. 3). Among these transcription factors, MYC2, COI1, TIFY, JAR6, and DBB are key components of both the jasmonate signaling pathway and plant cold stress response. This is consistent with the findings of Qiu et al. in Nymphaea candida, members of the MYC, COI1B, and TIFY gene families, core components of the jasmonate signaling pathway, were consistently upregulated throughout the cold acclimatization phase [73]. Similarly, in studies on cold stress of Vitis vinifera, TIFY transcription factors were found to be induced by jasmonic acid, specific genes such as VvJAZ4, 5, 9, 11, and VvZML were further confirmed to be cold-responsive [74]. In Oryza sativa, the OsDBB gene has been shown to respond to low-temperature stress, potentially by participating in the methyl jasmonate regulatory pathway [75]. Furthermore, MYB transcription factors play pivotal roles in plant stress responses. Extreme temperatures can induce MYB proteins to specifically bind to cis-elements in the promoter regions of their downstream target genes, thereby activating or repressing the transcriptional expression of these genes to regulate plant stress tolerance [76]. For example, MYB15 negatively regulates cold tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana by suppressing the expression levels of CBF genes [77]. In contrast, Arabidopsis thaliana overexpressing OsMYB4 positively regulates the transcriptional expression of cold-responsive genes: it enhances the expression levels of AtCor15a, AtCor78, and AtPAL2, thereby improving low-temperature tolerance [64]. In this study, our analysis revealed that α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid metabolic pathways were significantly activated in pollen storage at different temperatures, their metabolites are subsequently converted to jasmonic acid through a series of enzymatic reactions. JAR6 is likely to activate the expression of downstream transcription factors (e.g., MYC2) by binding to the COI1 receptor [78], which in turn regulates the expression of a range of defense genes to enhance plant immune responses and adaptive capacity. These transcription factors not only regulate the expression of cold stress resistance-related genes (e.g., LOX2, AOC, AOS) but also have affect the expression of jasmonate synthesis-related enzymes (e.g., JMT2). This further increases jasmonic acid levels, induces the expression of key enzymes genes in the secondary metabolite synthesis pathway, and ultimately triggers downstream cold stress response cascades [56, 79].

In addition, existing studies have demonstrated that linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid exert pivotal regulatory effects on oxidative stress in plants[70]. However, their regulatory roles remain controversial: under stress conditions, excessive accumulation of α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid in cells triggers the overproduction of ROS, thereby inducing oxidative stress [80, 81]. Conversely, other have shown that high levels of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid in plant cells can mitigate oxidative stress [82–84]. In this study, exogenous application of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid at appropriate concentrations markedly reduced the pollen viability after − 196℃ storage. These adverse effects were accompanied by enhanced the intracellular ROS accumulation, aggravated membrane lipid oxidative damage, and decreased the content of certain antioxidant substances. In contrast, treatment of pollen with specific inhibitors of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid notably enhanced the pollen viability after 4℃ storage, reduced the levels of ROS and MDA, and elevated the levels of several antioxidant-related substances. Collectively, these findings suggest that linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid exert a negative regulatory role in oxidative stress of pollen after storage at different temperatures. Meanwhile, extensive research has validated an interaction between jasmonic acid and ROS in plant cells: jasmonic acid can exacerbate the intracellular ROS accumulation [85, 86]. Of particular relevance to this study is the observation that genes associated with JA biosynthesis were highly enriched in the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways. Therefore, during the viability fluctuation of pollen under different temperatures, linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid may modulate the onset of oxidative stress by regulating the biosynthesis of jasmonic acid, thereby influencing the changes of pollen viability after storage. However, the specific mechanism underlying this regulatory process awaits further in-depth study.

Conclusion

For the first time, this study integrated transcriptomics, metabolomics and physiological approaches to elucidate the mechanism underlying post-storage pollen viability alterations after storage at different temperatures. These findings offer a theoretical basis for long-term, effective preservation of pollen.

Transcriptome analysis revealed that the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways are dynamically activated during the process of pollen viability alteration. Consistently, metabolomics results indicated that lipids and phenolic acids are the most abundant metabolite classes prominently associated with pollen viability changes after storage. Notably, metabolite-gene correlation network analysis further confirmed that both linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolism play pivotal roles in mediating pollen viability changes after storage.Moreover, a key characteristic of these two pathways is the marked upregulation of genes involved in jasmonic acid biosynthesis, such as AOS, AOC, LOX2 and JMT2, in both pathways. Furthermore, regulators of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid regulators exerted pronounced regulatory effects not only on pollen viability after storage but also on oxidative stress-related indicators. Taken together, these results indicate that the linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid metabolic pathways are central to modulating pollen viability changes after storage at different temperatures, with this regulation mediated by the control of jasmonic acid biosynthesis, which in turn dictates pollen viability outcomes after storage.

These findings indicate that the pathways signiffcantly enriched with DEGs and DAMs are involved in the viability alterations of Paeonia lactiflora pollen storage at different temperatures. Furthermore, further exploration of the physiological and molecular response mechanisms underlying pollen viability changes after storage at different temperatures will not only shed novel insight into the regulatory role of these unsaturated fatty acids in suppressing pollen viability loss during storage, but also lay a foundation for the innovations in pollen preservation technology, including the development of unsaturated fatty acid-based cryoprotective agents.
