Correlation between functional impairment and quantitative MRI assessment of ankle cartilage in chronic ankle instability
Authors: Zichun Liu, Shanshan Zheng, Haoxiong Li, Xiao'ao Xue, Tian Xia, Yuwen Zhang, Ying-Hua Chu, Qing Li, Weijun Tang, Shangxuan Shi, Yinghui Hua, Rong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic ankle instability (CAI) is characterized by persistent ankle pain, instability, and recurrent sprains. Previous studies have investigated functional impairments and cartilage degeneration separately, but the relationship between them in CAI remains unclear and unexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare the outcomes of functional impairments and cartilage composition between individuals with CAI and healthy controls to develop targeted interventions to improve joint health and prevent cartilage degeneration in patients with CAI.MethodsThis case–control study enrolled 20 individuals with CAI and 25 healthy controls. Dynamic balance measured using the Y-Balance Test (YBT) and static postural control was quantified by analyzing the trajectory of the center of pressure (CoP). T2 mapping of the talocrural joint was conducted using a 3-T magnetic resonance imaging scanner to assess ankle cartilage composition. Clinical questionnaire such as the Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) was used to evaluate the self-reported functional instability.ResultsThe T2 values were significantly higher in medial center (MC), medial posterior (MP), and lateral posterior (LP) (P < .05) subregions of the talar cartilage in the CAI group compared with the control group, indicating greater cartilage degeneration. Higher T2 values in the MC subregion were correlated with poorer CAIT scores (r = −0.484, P=.031) and YBT reach distances on the injury side (r ranged from −0.721 to −0.557, P < .05). The T2 elevations were also associated with reduced YBT reach distances on the healthy side and increased CoP excursions during eyes-closed postural control testing (eyes open: r = −0.585, P=.001; eyes closed: r = −0.469, P=.037).ConclusionsThis study provides evidence of functional impairments and cartilage degeneration in individuals with CAI. The observed biomechanical compensations and increased cartilage T2 values may contribute to the development of CAI and potentially increase the risk of long-term joint degeneration. These valuable insights into the pathophysiology of CAI may aid in developing targeted interventions to mitigate long-term joint degeneration.

Introduction

Chronic ankle instability (CAI) is a common condition characterized by persistent ankle pain, instability, recurrent sprains, and impaired biomechanics and sensorimotor functions following lateral ankle sprains. It is a considerable risk factor for developing ankle post-traumatic osteoarthritis, accounting for 14.6% of all ankle osteoarthritis cases.1 Approximately 80% of individuals with long-standing CAI (≥10 years) exhibit talar cartilage degeneration.2

The underlying pathophysiology of CAI is multifaceted in nature. Extant research has consistently demonstrated that alterations in functional biomechanics and concomitant cartilage degeneration play pivotal roles in the development and progression of CAI.1,3,4 The deficits in functional impairments such as postural control and balance have been observed in ankle instability, contributing to the risk of recurrent ankle sprains and further injury. These deficits are often caused by the changes in proprioception and muscle function surrounding the ankle joint.5 Postural stability, which encompasses both static and dynamic conditions, is critical for maintaining proper alignment and movement patterns during activities.6 Therefore, the decline in balance and postural control in patients with CAI is a central aspect contributing to the overall functional impairments seen in this condition.

Several studies have suggested that cartilage damage in CAI results from ongoing biomechanical stress and improper loading, leading to degeneration.7,8 Patients with CAI often have unstable gait and muscle control problems, which can stress joint cartilage.8 T2 mapping has emerged as a valuable tool for assessing cartilage health in patients with CAI.9 This technique can detect early cartilage degeneration before the occurrence of visible morphologic changes.10 Previous studies have reported elevated T2 values in the talocrural cartilage of individuals with CAI, suggesting altered cartilage composition and early cartilage degeneration11, 12, 13.

Although previous studies have investigated postural control and balance with cartilage degeneration separately in CAI, the relationship between these factors remains unclear and unexplored.1 We believe that cartilage damage and balance are linked because CAI-related balance deficits may alter force distribution, causing joint instability. This instability increases stress on the cartilage, which accelerates damage. Understanding the interplay between altered postural control and balance accompanied by cartilage composition may provide valuable insights into the pathophysiology of CAI and guide the development of targeted interventions to mitigate long-term joint degeneration.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between postural control, balance, and cartilage degeneration in individuals with CAI. We hypothesized that individuals with CAI would exhibit abnormally reduced postural control and balance, along with increased cartilage degeneration compared with healthy controls, and that these factors would be interrelated.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This case–control study involved 20 individuals with CAI (14 men and 6 women) and 25 healthy controls (17 men and 8 women). Participants in the CAI group were recruited from the local society and met the following inclusion criteria: (1) a history of at least 1 substantial lateral ankle sprain that required non-weight-bearing or immobilization and happened at least 12 months ago, (2) the most recent sprain occurring at least 3 months prior to testing, (3) recurrent episodes of ankle instability and “giving way,” and (4) a score of ≤24 on the Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT). Healthy controls had no history of substantial ankle sprains or instability and scored ≥27 on the CAIT. The exclusion criteria for both groups included a history of lower-extremity surgery, neurologic conditions, or other musculoskeletal injuries within the last 3 months. In addition, participants with surgical indication for pre-existing ankle cartilage degeneration, as confirmed by an orthopedics & sports medicine surgeon based on clinical assessment and available imaging, were excluded.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Both groups of participants attended the T2 mapping magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning session. The CAI group attended the additional behavioral session on balance and postural control–related outcomes on the same day. The behavioral measurements were not conducted for the control group because this study focused on the correlation analysis of the affected group, using healthy participants primarily as a control for ankle MRI.

The demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded during the initial interview of the participants, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Tegner scale for activity level, injured side, self-reported duration of CAI, CAIT questionnaire assessing the feeling of instability, numeric rating scale (NRS) for assessing ankle pain during static state or walking, and total number of recurrent sprains.

Functional test for patients with CAI

Participants were advised to refrain from engaging in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for 24 h before undergoing the tests to minimize the risk of fatigue influencing the results.

Dynamic balance test——Y-Balanced test

Dynamic balance was assessed using the Y-Balance Test (YBT). Prior to the formal assessment, participants underwent 6 practice trials to familiarize themselves with the test protocol. Each trial required participants to reach as far as possible with their reaching foot in 3 directions: anterior (A), medial posterior (MP), and lateral posterior (LP).14,15 All trials were conducted barefoot to simulate natural balance conditions. Throughout each trial, participants were instructed to maintain balance on the contralateral leg, always keeping their hands on their hips.

Each participant undertook 3 formal trials in each direction following the practice trials. A trial was considered invalid and subsequently repeated if the participant failed to return to the starting position, was unable to maintain a single-leg stance, touched down with the reaching leg, or removed their hands from their hips.16 Only data from the 3 valid trials, both in raw form and normalized to each participant's lower limb length, were included in the analysis.

Static balance test——Center of Pressure (CoP)

Static balance test was assessed during three 15-s eyes-open and then three 15-s eyes-closed trials of single-leg stance.17 The participants were allowed to practice each task as much as needed to become comfortable with the assessment procedures. Participants were asked to perform a single-leg stance barefoot on a force plate and maintain their balance for 15 s.

The trial was consider invalid and subsequently repeated if (1) the nontested limb made contact with the force platform; (2) the nontested limb made contact with the stance limb; (3) the participant took a step with the stance limb; (4) the participant removed the hands from the chest; or (5) the participant abducted the hip or laterally flexed the trunk into >30°.18

The key variables included in the analysis were traditional linear measures of the center of pressure (CoP), average displacement length of the CoP trajectory, and a 95% confidence interval (CI) of the ellipse area of the CoP, which are widely used in CAI research.

Quantitative T2 mapping

Participants were scanned supine with the foot/ankle complex in neutral position (90° to the shank). A custom-made orthoplast splint and an elastic bandage were used to ensure that the lower leg muscles remained relaxed during the scan. All participants arrived 30 min before the scan and remained seated to unload the ankle cartilage.

Cartilage degeneration is a common feature of CAI and can be assessed using advanced imaging techniques such as T2 mapping.19 T2 mapping provides information regarding the biochemical composition and structural integrity of cartilage. Increased T2 values indicate cartilage degeneration, collagen disruption, and increased water content.19,20 In this study, cartilage composition was assessed using T2 mapping of the talocrural joint. MRI scans were acquired using a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a 15-channel coil. T2 mapping was performed using a multi-echo times (TE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time = 5420 ms; 10 echo times = 11.5, 23, 34.5, 46, 57.5, 69, 80.5, 92, 103.5, and 115 ms; field of view = 160 × 160 mm2; matrix size = 384 × 230; voxel size = 0.4 × 0.4 × 2 mm3; slice thickness = 2 mm; number of slices = 40; flip angle = 180°; and phase encode direction as head/foot. The total acquisition time was 7:04 min. The process resulted in excellent intrarater and interrater reliability among trained individuals.

T2 maps were reconstructed using a pixel-wise, mono-exponential, non-negative least-squares fit algorithm implemented in MATLAB (version R2023b; MathWorks, MA, USA). T2 values were quantified for the talocrural cartilage, including the talus and distal tibia, using a semi-automated segmentation approach (Horos v4.0.1, https://horosproject.org/). On sagittal T2 maps, the talocrural cartilage was further segmented into six subregion: medial anterior (MA), medial center (MC), medial posterior (MP), lateral anterior (LA), lateral center (LC), and lateral posterior (LP).21,22 Specifically, the medial and lateral subregions were defined as the medial and lateral halves of the talocrural cartilage across three consecutive sections, referred to coronal images22; anterior, center, and posterior subregions were defined as anterior third, middle third, and posterior third of the talocrural cartilage.23,24 Mean T2 values were then calculated for each subregion (Fig. S1). Two experienced radiologists (R. L.,12 years, and WJ. T, 22 years), blinded to the group assignment, performed the segmentation and T2 value quantification.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and anthropometric data were compared between groups using chi-square tests. The mean T2 values of the talus cartilage were analyzed between groups using unpaired t-tests. Subregion T2 values of talus cartilage and functional parameters were compared using unpaired t-tests, spearman correlation analysis with limb (involved/uninvolved for CAI, dominant/nondominant for controls). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and R/RStudio (version 4.3.2, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses.

A significant negative correlation was observed between the MC subregion and both the CAIT scores and YBT scores in different directions, indicating that more cartilage degeneration in the MC subregion was associated with poor balance and postural control.

Further, the LP subregion demonstrated significant negative correlations with pain during static and walking conditions, as measured using the NRS, highlighting a link between cartilage degeneration and increased pain. Additionally, the MP subregion was negatively correlated with certain YBT scores, implying associations with physical and functional measures. A positive correlation was identified between the CoP amplitude in medial-lateral direction when eye closed in health controls and LP subregion T2 values, suggesting that a greater dorsiflexion range might be correlated with less cartilage degeneration. These findings illustrated the complex relationships between cartilage health and functional outcomes in CAI, emphasizing the need for targeted clinical interventions.

Poor dynamic balance performance on the injury side was associated with greater T2 values in the talar cartilage subregion (MC and MP subregions). On the injury side, greater T2 values at MC were associated with lower anterior (r = −0.557), left posterior (r = −0.630), and right posterior (r = −0.630) reach distances in YBT tests, and the significant associations were interpreted as strong. Similarly on the injury side, greater T2 values at MP were associated with lower left posterior (r = −0.467) and right posterior (r = −0.449) reach distances in YBTs, and the significant associations were interpreted as moderate.

However, no significant associations were noted among all eyes-open and eyes-closed postural control variables on the injury side and T2 values in either area of talar cartilage. This lack of association might be attributed to the noncompliance of participants with CAI during the eyes-open and eyes-closed postural control tests.

In addition, poor dynamic balance and postural control on the healthy side were associated with talar T2 values. Greater T2 values at MC on the injury side were associated with lower left posterior (r = −0.658) and right posterior (r = −0.569) reach distances in YBT and higher maximal CoP excursions on the x-axis during eyes-closed postural control (r = 0.469) on the healthy side. Similarly, greater T2 values at MP on the injury side were associated with lower left posterior reach distances (r = −0.459) in YBT on the healthy side, and greater T2 values at LP on the injury side were associated with higher maximal CoP excursions on the x-axis during eyes-closed postural control (r = 0.553) on the healthy side. All significant associations on the healthy side were interpreted as moderate to strong, which might be attributed to maladaptive changes within the central nervous system among participants with CAI.

Discussion

Our study investigated talar cartilage degeneration using T2 mapping in individuals with CAI and its association with dynamic and static balance test. Our study explored this relationship to develop targeted interventions aiming to improve joint health and prevent cartilage degeneration in these patients. A landmark association was highlighted between cartilage degeneration and self-reported symptoms, including disability, ankle instability, pain, and reduced dorsiflexion range of motion. These bilateral deficits suggested possible maladaptive changes in the central nervous system beyond local ankle pathology, affecting both sides of the body. These findings emphasize the need for comprehensive management strategies for CAI that can address local tissue changes and central neural mechanisms. Future research should explore interventions focusing on improving cartilage health, proprioception, and neuromuscular control to enhance functional outcomes and reduce injury risk.

Increased T2 values in the CAI group

In our study, the CAI group exhibited significantly higher T2 values compared with healthy controls in the MC, MP, and LP subregions of the talar cartilage. Elevated T2 values are generally interpreted as indicators for early cartilage degeneration due to its sensitivity to cartilage water content and collagen network. Previously published ankle studies (such as Golditz et al.25 and Kim et al.26) have reported an increase of 5 to 10 ms in T2 values, even when the cartilage appeared morphologically intact at arthroscopy, compared with healthy controls or copers. In our study, the absolute elevation in the significantly affected subregions (MC, MP, LP) was slightly higher, ranging from 11 to 16 ms (e.g., MC subregion: 60.55 ms vs. 44.25 ms).

We believe this magnitude of elevation is still clinically meaningful and the slight variance from previous literature is highly plausible for two primary reasons. First, absolute T2 values are inherently sensitive to specific scanner hardware and acquisition parameters. Our study utilized a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a specific 10-echo multi-echo spin-echo sequence (TE: 11.5–115 ms) and a mono-exponential non-negative least-squares fit algorithm. Differences in TE spacing, field strength, and fitting algorithms can easily introduce systematic shifts of several milliseconds in quantitative mapping compared to other institutional protocols.20,27 Second, unlike some previous studies that averaged T2 values across the entire global talar cartilage layer, our methodology employed a semi-automated segmentation into six distinct subregions. This localized approach is more sensitive to spatial heterogeneity, allowing us to capture the hotspots of abnormal zones.

Therefore, we believe that although our absolute elevation is slightly higher than the 5-10 ms typically reported, the significant elevation in T2 values, which still strongly aligns with the established literatures, might be a clinically meaningful biomarker for identifying at-risk cartilage regions.

Furthermore, longitudinal studies using T2 mapping have demonstrated progressive increases in T2 values over time in patients with CAI, indicative of ongoing cartilage degradation28, 29, 30. These findings highlighted the potential of T2 mapping as a tool for monitoring disease progression and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions.

Associations between T2 values and clinical measures

Prior studies have demonstrated a correlation between T2 values and clinical symptoms, suggesting the potential of T2 value as a biomarker for cartilage health.11,26,31,32 However, T2 alterations are not uniformly distributed across the talar dome. Therefore, we performed the region-specific correlations between T2 mapping of talar cartilage and assessments of functional impairments and balance control in individuals with CAI. Region-specific correlations between talar cartilage T2 values and functional outcomes have been observed, including measures of dynamic balance, postural control, and self-reported disability. Specifically, higher T2 values in the MC subregion demonstrated a correlation with increased subjective ankle instability and poorer dynamic balance; higher T2 values in the MP subregion also exhibited a correlation with poorer dynamic balance; higher T2 values in the LP subregion exhibited a correlation with higher pain levels during static and walking conditions.

A potential explanation for the observed region-specific correlations is that CAI produces nonuniform alterations in cartilage stress and loading pattern, thereby promoting various cartilage degeneration in regions. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and longitudinal studies are needed to further elucidate the plausible explanation underlying these relationships. Collectively, we hope these established correlations between T2 values and clinical measures could offer valuable insights for assessment and treatment planning, potentially leading to more targeted interventions addressing both cartilage health and sensorimotor function.

Nevertheless, although we observed significant associations between subregional cartilage T2 values and functional outcomes, we also noted some certain outliers (e.g., individuals with relatively elevated T2 values who maintained less severe functional deficits, or vice versa). We believe the lack of strict linear correlation in certain outliers is plausible given the multifactorial nature of functional impairment in CAI. Specifically, in our cohort, participants demonstrated varying degrees of pain and kinesiophobia (Table 1), which may adversely influence dynamic balance performance and self-reported function as suggested in prior literature.5 Conversely, some individuals may develop effective compensatory neuromuscular strategies that preserve task performance and partially mask early functional decline. Thus, these correlations should be interpreted as group-level trends rather than at the individual level, and larger longitudinal studies are needed to better explain individual-level variability.

Bilateral deficits in dynamic balance and postural control

Previous studies have increasingly recognized the complex interplay between cartilage health and sensorimotor function in individuals with CAI5,33, 34, 35. Early research primarily focused on these aspects separately, with cartilage studies examining structural changes and degradation and sensorimotor research investigating balance, proprioception, and neuromuscular control deficits. However, more recently, growing attention has been directed toward the potential connections between these two domains.

Previous studies have demonstrated that individuals with CAI exhibit both cartilage degeneration and sensorimotor deficits. For instance, T2 mapping and T1ρ relaxation time studies have demonstrated increased T2 values in the talar cartilage of patients with CAI, indicating early degenerative changes.1,36 Concurrently, several studies have consistently reported impaired postural control, altered gait biomechanics, and decreased dynamic balance in this population.1,17,37 Nevertheless, the direct relationship and causal mechanisms remain unclear, particularly in cross-sectional studies as ours. Based on current biomechanical and neurophysiological literatures, we consider the relationship between functional impairment and cartilage degeneration in CAI to be bidirectional vicious cycle. First, functional impairment may initiate cartilage degeneration. Following an initial lateral ankle sprain, residual mechanical laxity and primary sensorimotor deficits may lead to altered joint kinematics, shifting joint loading and creating excessive shear stress on talar cartilage. Over time, such abnormal loading may disrupt the collagen network and increases water content, manifesting as the elevated T2 values we observed.1 Second, cartilage degeneration may further amplify functional decline. As early structural cartilage degeneration develop, they may alter local peripheral mechanoreceptors and further drive maladaptive changes to central nervous system, which eventually manifests as worsening dynamic balance and self-reported functional decline.5 Together, functional impairment and cartilage degeneration could reinforce each other and contribute to the development of CAI.

Consistent with this hypothesized model, our study revealed that greater MC and MP T2 values were associated with poorer YBT performance on the injury side, and significant associations were also observed between T2 values and YBT scores on the healthy side. Additionally, increased T2 values were related to greater CoP excursions during eyes-closed postural control on the healthy side. These findings suggested that cartilage degeneration in the talar region and functional impairments in dynamic balance and postural control may be coupled and could reflect interacting local and central mechanisms in CAI.

Interventions targeting cartilage health, proprioception, and neuromuscular control may be beneficial in improving functional outcomes and reducing the risk of further injury in this population. However, further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm our hypothesis between functional impairment and cartilage degeneration in CAI.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of our study was the comprehensive evaluation of the correlation between traditional balance and postural control–related outcomes and cartilage composition in individuals with CAI. The use of advanced techniques, such as 3-dimensional motion capture and T2 mapping, allowed for precise quantification of biomechanical parameters and cartilage composition, respectively.

However, this study had certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish causal relationships between gait alterations and cartilage degeneration. Longitudinal studies are needed to further elucidate the temporal associations between these factors.

Second, mechanical instability severity was not quantified using stress radiography; thus, we could not examine dose–response relationships between instability and elevated T2 values. Future studies should include standardized stress radiography.

Third, T2 mapping is sensitive to cartilage matrix alteration but not disease-specific between osteoarthritis (OA) and osteochondral lesions (OCLs). Future studies should incorporate standardized morphological assessment of both OA- and OCL-related features (e.g., cartilage defect grading, bone marrow edema, and subchondral changes), ideally with arthroscopic reference when clinically available, to better disentangle these overlapping pathologies.

Fourth, subchondral edema, a major potential source of pain and functional limitation in CAI, was not systematically assessed in this study because our primary focus was cartilage T2 mapping. Given that abnormal joint loading in CAI may affect both cartilage and subchondral bone, unmeasured edema-related pain could have contributed to the observed functional impairments and pain and may have confounded our analyses. Future longitudinal studies should incorporate standardized MRI assessment of subchondral edema alongside T2 mapping.

Finally, the sample size was relatively small, potentially limiting the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted.

Conclusions

This study explored the association between talar cartilage degeneration and balance in individuals with CAI using MRI T2 mapping. Notably, these individuals demonstrated significantly higher T2 values in the talar cartilage regions compared with healthy controls, indicating increased degeneration. This degeneration was linked to poorer performance in dynamic balance tests and greater CoP excursions during postural control tests, even on the non-injury side.
