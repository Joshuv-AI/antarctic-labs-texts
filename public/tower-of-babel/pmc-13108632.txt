Citrate‐Dependent Activation of Acetyl CoA Carboxylase Promotes Renal Lipid Accumulation in a Rat Model of Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Marta Sanz‐Gómez, Beatriz Linillos‐Pradillo, Rubén Fernández de la Rosa, Isabel Aránguez, Reinhold Kreutz, Antonia García, Coral Barbas, Adrián Plaza, María S. Fernández‐Alfonso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAbnormal accumulation of lipids within the kidney contributes to the progression of chronic kidney disease, but the underlying metabolic mechanisms remain unclear. Citrate is a central metabolite in cellular energy regulation and is frequently elevated in advanced kidney disease, yet its impact on renal lipid metabolism has not been investigated. This study tested whether increased circulating citrate promotes lipid accumulation in the kidney by activating acetyl‐CoA carboxylase independently of adenosine monophosphate‐activated protein kinase.MethodsMale Munich Wistar Frömter rats, a genetic model of chronic kidney disease, were studied together with HEK293 cells exposed to citrate. Protein expression, phosphorylation, and polymerization were assessed using western blotting, q‐RT‐PCR, and native gel electrophoresis. Renal lipid composition was characterized by untargeted high‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry, and citrate levels were semi‐quantified using capillary electrophoresis–time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry.ResultsMWF rats exhibited elevated plasma citrate, reduced vitamin D and bone mineral density, and increased renal fibrosis and mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, MWF showed higher expression of ATP‐citrate lyase and fatty acid synthase, together with reduced phosphorylation of acetyl‐CoA carboxylase and AMP‐activated protein kinase, increased renal lipid deposition, and elevated fatty acid content. In HEK293, citrate treatment reproduced these effects, inducing acetyl‐CoA carboxylase polymerization, lipid droplet formation, impaired mitochondrial activity, and increased superoxide production.ConclusionThis study identifies citrate‐dependent activation of acetyl‐CoA carboxylase as a driver of renal lipid accumulation and remodeling independently of AMP‐activated protein kinase, revealing a novel metabolic mechanism linking systemic citrate to kidney injury and potential therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) refers to structural and functional impairment of the kidneys, characterized by a progressive loss of glomerular filtration rate (GFR), persistent inflammation, interstitial fibrosis, and metabolic dysfunction [1]. CKD currently affects more than 10% of the global population, with higher prevalence in low‐ and middle‐income countries compared to high‐income nations [2, 3, 4] and is estimated to become the fifth leading cause of death worldwide by 2040 [3].

Ectopic lipid deposition (ELD) refers to the abnormal accumulation of lipids within non‐adipose tissues, including the kidneys. In CKD, ELD has emerged as a key feature of renal metabolic dysregulation and a potential contributor to tubulointerstitial fibrosis [5, 6, 7, 8] even in the absence of obesity or diabetes. Although ELD has been linked to CKD progression, the causal relationship remains incompletely established [5]. However, compelling evidence shows that renal tubular lipid accumulation induces mitochondrial dysfunction, critical for energy‐demanding tubular cells, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [9], inflammation [10], fibrosis [11], and apoptosis [10], in a process collectively termed lipotoxicity. A central mechanism underlying ELD in CKD is the imbalance between excessive fatty acid (FA) uptake and/or synthesis, and defective fatty acid oxidation (FAO) [9], where key FAO‐related enzymes such as carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT‐1) are consistently downregulated [9], contributing to fibrosis [11].

Acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism by regulating the balance between FA synthesis (anabolic pathway) and FAO (catabolic pathway). ACC catalyzes the conversion of acetyl‐CoA to malonyl‐CoA, the first committed step in lipogenesis [12, 13]. Importantly, malonyl‐CoA also serves as a potent inhibitor of CPT1, thereby reducing mitochondrial fatty acid entry and β‐oxidation [11, 12, 14]. In the kidney, ACC activity is tightly linked to lipid homeostasis. Overactivation of ACC promotes lipid accumulation by FAO suppression, fostering a metabolic environment conducive to ELD which strongly correlates with fibrosis severity in renal tubular epithelial cells [9].

Regulation of ACC occurs at multiple levels, with phosphorylation at Ser79 being one of the key inhibitory mechanisms. This phosphorylation is mediated by adenosine monophosphate‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), a cellular energy sensor that responds to fluctuations in intracellular AMP/ATP ratios. In healthy renal tubular cells, AMPK acts to inhibit ACC, reducing lipogenesis and promoting FAO [14]. However, in CKD, AMPK signaling is often disrupted due to sustained metabolic stress, characterized by impaired AMPK phosphorylation and activation [15]. This loss of AMPK‐mediated ACC inhibition permits unchecked ACC activity, promoting lipid synthesis at the expense of FAO and contributing to fibrosis. Pharmacological activation of AMPK with metformin mitigates renal fibrosis, but this protective effect relies on functional downstream inhibition of ACC [16]. Thus, ACC emerges as a central metabolic node in CKD progression, where its sustained activation drives renal lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and fibrosis.

Another regulator of ACC is citrate, which prevents AMPK‐mediated phosphorylation at the Ser79 residue. In physiological conditions, the balance between these two mechanisms allows cells to rapidly toggle ACC activity in response to energy status and substrate availability [17]. Interestingly, elevated plasma citrate levels have been reported in CKD patients [18, 19], along with reduced renal citrate excretion [20]. Plasma citrate negatively correlates with creatinine clearance and GFR, suggesting a relationship between declining renal function and systemic citrate accumulation [19, 20]. Due to these observations, it is plausible that excess circulating citrate in CKD may drive ACC activation independently of AMPK phosphorylation by promoting its polymerization. This mechanism could impair FAO, promote lipid synthesis, and thus contribute to renal ELD and fibrosis.

Despite clear associations between citrate, ACC activation, and renal lipotoxicity, no studies to date have directly investigated whether high citrate levels can promote ACC polymerization and ELD, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased ROS, inflammation, and fibrosis. Thus, we hypothesize that in CKD, elevated plasma citrate levels lead to ACC allosteric overactivation (via polymerization), triggering ELD and subsequent renal fibrosis, independently of AMPK phosphorylation status. This study aims to explore this previously unrecognized citrate–ACC–lipotoxicity axis in CKD, potentially unraveling a new pathogenic mechanism linking mineral‐bone disorders, lipid metabolism dysregulation, and fibrosis.

Results

In 22 and 45w MWF rats, the presence of CKD was evidenced by significantly elevated proteinuria, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and plasma creatinine levels compared with age‐matched Wistar controls (Figure 1A–C). In addition, renal expression of tubular injury markers (Kim‐1 and Ngal), the proinflammatory cytokine Tnf‐α, the profibrotic mediators Tgf‐β, and Col1a1 were markedly upregulated in MWF kidneys relative to Wistar animals (Figure 1D–H). No differences in kidney weight normalized by total body weight were detected between groups (Figure S1).

ACC phosphorylation at Ser79 was significantly higher in Wistar kidneys at 45w compared to 22w (Figure 2C), while no increase of p‐Ser79‐ACC was observed in MWF animals at 45w. To elucidate whether the age‐associated increase in p‐Thr172‐AMPK was functional, the level of p‐Ser1177‐eNOS, one of its endothelial targets, was measured. A significant increase was observed at 45w in both strains, with no age‐matched strain‐associated differences (p > 0.05 Wistar vs. MWF) (Figure 2D). p‐Thr172‐AMPK and p‐Ser79‐ACC values normalized by their tubulin showed a positive Spearman's correlation (r = 0.6809; p = 0.0268*) in Wistar animals which disappeared in MWF (r = −0.3149; p = 0.4091) (Figure 2E).

The renal expression of ACC1 cytosolic isoform (Acaca) was significantly higher in MWF (Figure 2F), with no changes in ACC2 mitochondrial isoform (Acacb) (Figure 2G). Higher ORO staining was evident in MWF kidneys with lipid droplets specially located in the tubular kidney parenchyma (Figure 2H).

Lipidomic Profile of Total Kidneys Showed Higher Concentration of Fatty Acid Species in MWF Rats

Volcano plot analysis of the renal lipidome (Figure 3A) revealed a clear asymmetric distribution of differentially regulated lipid species between MWF and Wistar kidneys. Most of the significantly downregulated lipids in MWF rats corresponded to complex structural classes, including multiple PC, PE, CL, SM, and TG. In contrast, significantly upregulated species were predominantly FA, notably FA(24:4), FA(22:5), and FA(16:2). Integration of multivariate and univariate analyses identified a total of 18 lipid species that remained statistically significant in MWF compared with Wistar controls (Figure 3B). Untargeted LC–MS lipidomics demonstrated a coordinated increase in renal FA in MWF rats, with FA(20:1) and FA(22:4) remaining significant after multiple testing correction, indicating enhanced availability of long‐chain fatty acids. Conversely, several PC and DG species were significantly reduced. Alterations in sphingolipid metabolism were also evident, with differential regulation of Cer, HexCer, and SM. The broader univariate analysis (Figure S2) confirmed and extended these findings, demonstrating widespread depletion of membrane‐associated glycerophospholipids (PC, PE), sphingolipids, and DG species, together with a consistent increase in multiple FA and LPC species in MWF kidneys. Moreover, no differences in TG were found between groups (Figure S2).

Plasma levels of citrate were measured in both rat strains at 45w (Figure 4A). We observed a significant 1.5‐fold increase in MWF plasma citrate levels compared to Wistar (p < 0.05). Since more than 90% of total body citrate is stored in bone tissue [21], we assessed bone mineral density (BMD) of femur and Vit D (25‐OH‐cholecalciferol) levels, which were significantly lower in MWF (Figure 4B–D). Total kidney citrate content was not significantly different between MWF and Wistar kidneys (Figure 4E).

Expression of Fatty Acid Synthesis Enzymes Is Increased in the Kidneys of MWF Rats, While the Levels of Mitochondrial Respiratory Chain Complexes I, II, and III Are Reduced

Next, we characterized citrate intracellular availability by measuring gene expression of citrate transporters, that are, plasma membrane sodium‐citrate co‐transporters Slc13a2, Slc13a3 and Slc13a5, introducing citrate into the cell, and mitochondrial citrate‐malate antiporter Slc25a1, exporting citrate from mitochondrial intramembrane space to the cytosol. No changes in renal gene expression of Slc13a2 nor Slc13a3 were detected between strains, while Slc13a5 was significantly lower in the MWF kidneys compared to Wistar (p < 0.05) (Figure 5A–C). No differences were observed in the expression of Slc25a1 between strains (Figure 5D).

Since ELD is primarily driven by impaired FAO, which is tightly coupled to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), total OXPHOS complexes were measured to address the mitochondrial respiratory chain integrity. A significant decrease was observed in complexes I, II, and III in MWF kidneys compared to Wistar (p < 0.05) although no differences were detected in complexes IV and V levels (p > 0.05) (Figure 5E,F).

ELD is also driven, in part, by upregulation of ATP‐citrate lyase (ACLY) and fatty acid synthase (FASN) promoting de novo lipogenesis from citrate‐derived acetyl‐CoA [22]. Both Acly and Fasn gene expression were significantly higher in the MWF group compared to Wistar (p < 0.05) (Figure 5G,H). The renal gene expression of the mitochondrial membrane fatty acid transferase Cpt1 was significantly lower in the MWF group compared to Wistar (p < 0.05) (Figure 5I) with no differences in the expression of the FA membrane transporter Cd36 (Figure 5J).

Citrate Impairs p‐Thr172‐AMPK‐p‐Ser79‐ACC Signaling and Increases Lipid Accumulation in HEK293 Cells

To establish causality, we next studied the effect of citrate (0.5–1.5 mM) in vitro on HEK293 cells under basal conditions and followed by AICAR (1 mM) treatment for AMPK stimulation after the expression of citrate transporters (SLC13A2, SLC13A3, SLC13A5 and SLC21A5) in the HEK293 cell line was confirmed (Figure S3). Citrate 1.5 mM, but not (citrate 0.5 mM) caused a significant reduction in both, p‐Thr172‐AMPK and p‐Ser79‐ACC compared with CT cells. AICAR 1 mM elicited a significant increase in both p‐Thr172‐AMPK and its target p‐Ser79‐ACC compared with CT cells even in presence of 0.5 mM citrate (compared with AICAR 1 mM alone) but this effect disappeared with 1.5 mM citrate. Regarding p‐Ser79‐ACC, no effect was observed for citrate 0.5 mM + AICAR 1 mM but it was significantly reduced in cells pretreated with 1.5 mM citrate and AICAR 1 mM compared with AICAR 1 mM alone (Figure 6A–C). Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between p‐Thr172‐AMPK and its canonical target p‐Ser79‐ACC following treatment with 1 mM AICAR alone (r = 0.8356, p = 0.0192). However, this correlation was lost in the presence of citrate (r = 0.3616, p = 0.2743, n.s.).

To relate these findings to ELD, cells were stained with ORO after the citrate treatments (0.5 and 1.5 mM; 24 h). A significant increase in ORO staining was observed at both citrate concentrations (Figure 6D,E). Moreover, citrate 1.5 mM induced a significant reduction of mitochondrial activity, evidenced by MTT assay, compared with CT (Figure 6F) while no differences in neutral red assay were observed (Figure 6G).

Citrate (1.5 mM) also elicited a significant decrease in OXPHOS complex I protein expression, with no differences in complexes II, III, IV, or V (Figure 6H,I). Because impaired OXPHOS leads to excess superoxide anion production, we determined superoxide anion by the fluorescent staining with DHE (10−6 M). Citrate (0.5 mM) elicited a qualitative increase in DHE fluorescence compared with the untreated cells, which was further increased by 1.5 mM citrate (Figure 6J).

Citrate 1.5 mM induced a significant reduction of the monomeric form of ACC (~250 kDa) (Figure 7A,B) compared to untreated cells. Moreover, no changes in high molecular weight ACC (> 800 kDa) were observed as an effect of neither 0.5 nor 1.5 mM citrate compared to CT (Figure 7A,C), whereas a significant increase of the percentage of ACC in a polymeric status was detected in the presence of 1.5 mM citrate (Figure 7D).

In native PAGE of kidney lysates, ACC appeared as a diffuse, higher‐molecular‐weight smear, reflecting polymer formation (Figure 7E). The relative quantification of the ACC monomeric form (~250KDa) was significantly lower in the MWF kidneys (Figure 7F). Moreover, the broader ACC‐positive smear observed in the Wistar group showed no significant differences compared to the MWF group (Figure 7G), whereas the percentage of polymeric ACC over the total was significantly higher in MWF kidneys compared to Wistar (Figure 7H), suggesting an increased ACC polymerization and heterogeneous oligomeric states, consistent with citrate‐driven enzyme activation.

Discussion

This study identifies citrate‐induced polymerization of ACC as a potential novel mechanism driving ectopic lipid deposition in CKD. In HEK293 cells, citrate promotes ACC overactivation by bypassing AMPK‐dependent inhibition, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and lipid accumulation. In MWF rats, elevated plasma citrate levels, associated with bone resorption and vitamin D deficiency, coincide with impaired renal AMPK‐ACC signaling, inflammation, fibrosis, and FA accumulation. These findings support citrate‐dependent ACC polymerization as a mechanistic link between systemic metabolic disturbances and renal lipotoxic injury.

The male MWF rat is a suitable polygenic model of CKD that mimics CKD progression in patients [23, 24, 25] showing a development of kidney damage with age, characterized by increased proteinuria, uremia, kidney inflammation, fibrosis, and ELD. Lipid droplets were detected in the tubules of MWF kidneys by ORO staining (Figure 2H), confirming the presence of ELD, and its FA nature was further confirmed by lipidomics (Figure 3). This was coincident with reduced kidney function, high tubular damage, inflammation and fibrosis (Figure 1), in line with previous findings implicating ELD as a key driver of renal functional and structural decline in advanced CKD [6, 9, 26]. For this reason, we sought to gain deeper insight into the underlying molecular mechanisms, with a particular focus on the role of ACC. From the two ACC isoforms expressed in the kidney, ACC2 (Acacb), the mitochondrial isoform, primarily regulates FAO [27], while ACC1, located in the cytosol, drives FA synthesis by producing malonyl‐CoA [28]. ACC2 (Acacb) expression was unchanged, whereas ACC1 (Acaca) was markedly upregulated in MWF kidneys (Figure 2F,G), consistent with prior ACC1 induction in tubular cells in CKD and a minor, less responsive role for ACC2 in the kidney [9, 16, 29]. This underscores the predominant role of ACC1 in renal lipotoxicity since it (i) catalyzes ATP‐dependent carboxylation of acetyl‐CoA into malonyl‐CoA, fueling de novo FA synthesis and (ii) simultaneously inhibits FAO by suppressing CPT1, the rate‐limiting enzyme for mitochondrial fatty acid import [30, 31]. The reduced expression of Cpt1 (Figure 5I) further reinforces a FAO deficit, as described in folic acid CKD models [11, 13]. The resulting metabolic imbalance, favoring de novo lipogenesis while limiting FAO, promotes ELD implicated in tubular injury and fibrosis [6, 9, 26]. This is supported by the significant increase in FA (20:1) and FA (22:4) and the consistent upward trend across multiple saturated and unsaturated FA. The concomitant reduction in DG and PC species, together with SM alterations, suggests redistribution toward lipid storage, membrane remodeling, and activation of lipotoxic signaling pathways [32]. Lipid droplets buffer excess fatty acids (FAs) by storing them as neutral lipids, mainly triacylglycerols (TAG), thereby protecting cells from lipotoxicity [33]. Unexpectedly, while ORO staining revealed an increased abundance of lipid droplets in kidneys from MWF rats, lipidomics showed that total TAG levels were not increased and even tended to decrease, whereas FAs accumulated markedly. This dissociation suggests that excess FAs are not efficiently esterified into TAG. Since FA esterification and sequestration into lipid droplets represents a key detoxification pathway [34], impaired esterification could limit TAG synthesis and promote accumulation of non‐esterified FAs. Such lipid imbalance is highly cytotoxic and can trigger mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling [9]. Together, these findings support a model in which increased FA synthesis de novo combined with defective esterification promotes FA accumulation and renal lipotoxicity in MWF kidneys.

A central novelty of our findings lies in the dysregulation of ACC. In physiological conditions, ACC is activated by citrate‐induced polymerization [17, 35]. The elevated plasma citrate levels in 45‐week‐old MWF (Figure 4A) resemble the alterations reported in CKD patients [18, 19]. This rise in citratemia might be explained due to reduced urinary citrate excretion with declining GFR [20]. Moreover, since more than 90% of the total body content of citrate is part of the bone [21], the observed enhancement of citratemia in MWF could be attributed to an increased bone resorption related to lower VitD levels, as described in CKD patients [2, 36], also related to VitD deficiency [37]. Thus, the observed reduction in VitD (Figure 4D) may enhance bone resorption, reflected by lower femoral BMD (Figure 4B,C), thereby contributing to increased plasma citrate levels. Regarding citrate intracellular trafficking, no differences in the total kidney citrate content were detected. This reflects the high dynamics of intracellular citrate levels since citrate is continuously produced in the mitochondria and exported to the cytosol where it fuels lipid synthesis and metabolic signaling, making its cellular concentration strongly dependent on metabolic state [38, 39]. The reduced expression of the cell membrane Slc13a5 symport (Figure 5C) might be the result of a compensatory effect because of the increased citratemia. However, this downregulation appears insufficient since Slc13a2 and Slc13a3 expression was not affected (Figure 5A,B), and the intracellular FA synthesis remains upregulated by the overexpression of key enzymes such as Acc1, Fasn, and Acly. A protective role of the reduced Slc13a5 expression reported that pharmacological inhibition of SLC13A5 improved renal outcomes in CKD, including higher GFR and lower BUN, thereby supporting SLC13A5 blockade as a potential therapeutic approach [40].

Another mechanism of physiological ACC regulation is AMPK‐mediated phosphorylation at Ser79 [30], which stabilizes an open, inactive form, preventing dimerization of the BC domain and reducing catalytic activity [17, 41]. In MWF kidneys, AMPK phosphorylation (p‐Thr172) was elevated with age (Figure 2B) [42] but still less active than in healthy age‐matched Wistar (Figure 2B). In the central nervous system, higher intracellular citrate levels lead to the inhibition of AMPK, reflecting a high energy status [43]. Moreover, upregulation of Acly in MWF kidney (Figure 5E) directly interacts with AMPK reducing its activity [13], which may collaborate in lowering renal p‐Thr172‐AMPK levels. However, AMPK phosphorylation did not translate into inhibitory phosphorylation of ACC in MWF (Figure 2C,E). Since eNOS phosphorylation in Ser1177, another AMPK target [44], paralleled that of AMPK (Figure 2D), these data argue against a global defect in AMPK activity. In addition to AMPK, ACC activity is also regulated by other kinases, such as protein kinase A [45]. However, under conditions of citrate‐dependent overactivation, the inhibitory influence of these kinases on ACC would be expected to be functionally reduced. Since CKD alters metabolic and signaling pathways in renal cells [46], this could influence ACC phosphorylation status in vivo, an aspect not directly examined here and warranting further investigation.

To establish causality, we modeled the interaction citrate‐AMPK‐ACC in vitro in HEK293 cells exposed to citrate (0.5–1.5 mM) in basal conditions or under AMPK activation by AICAR. Citrate 1.5 mM not only reduced basal AMPK and ACC phosphorylation but also blunted their phosphorylation by AICAR, abolished the AMPK–ACC correlation, and promoted lipid droplet accumulation (Figure 6), establishing citrate as a dominant inhibitor of AMPK‐ACC signaling. Thus, high citrate overrides AMPK‐mediated control, driving ACC activation by polymerization, as shown by the significant reduction of the monomeric form of ACC and the increment in the percentage of ACC in polymers in cells. This occurs by stabilization of the closed, active dimer, which facilitates filament formation [17, 35, 41, 47]. Although we could not detect the high citrate‐promoted ACC overpolymerization previously described [47], probably because of methodological discrepancies between working with purified recombinant protein or with HEK293 cells (Figure 7A,B), we could identify an increase in the percentage of total ACC that was polymerized (Figure 7D). These in vitro findings closely mimicked those in MWF kidney, where high plasma citrate appeared to blunt the age‐associated increase in AMPK activity observed in control Wistar rats. In accordance, looking at the native WB of renal total ACC, the monomeric form of ACC was less present in MWF kidneys (Figure 7E,F), that was not accompanied by an increase in the polymerized form (Figure 7E,G) but by a higher percentage of ACC included in the polymer (Figure 7H), as previously observed in cells with high dose citrate (1.5 mM), which accounts for increased ACC activity [17, 35]. These findings corroborate the hyperactivation of ACC by polymerization, a typical allosteric effect described for high citrate levels [17, 35, 48]. This also relates to the lower renal p‐Ser79‐ACC levels in MWFs and its independence of AMPK activity. Moreover, they point to a citrate‐sensitive, AMPK‐independent mechanism of ACC activation in MWF kidneys, providing mechanistic insight into how metabolic reprogramming via ACC upregulation contributes to CKD progression, and explaining the loss of correlation between AMPK activation and ACC inhibition observed in both MWF kidneys and HEK293 cells.

ELD triggers mitochondrial damage in renal cells by promoting inflammation through activation of the innate immune system and fibrosis [6, 9]. In accordance, mitochondrial dysfunction was detected in MWF kidneys and citrate‐treated HEK293 cells shown by reduced OXPHOS complexes I and III, with accompanying ROS overproduction. Complex I dysfunction elevates superoxide generation [49], while complex III impairment disrupts electron transfer from FADH2 through ubiquinone, thereby limiting FAO [50]. Importantly, reduced mitochondrial activity in HEK293 cells was not due to cytotoxicity but specifically linked to citrate, as demonstrated by decreased MTT signal without cell death, pointing to citrate as a direct mediator of mitochondrial dysfunction [49, 51].

This study has certain limitations. The in vitro experiments were conducted in human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells, which, although valuable for mechanistic validation, do not fully reproduce the metabolic phenotype of mature renal tubular epithelial cells in vivo. Nevertheless, HEK293 cells retain key renal characteristics and are widely employed in nephrology research to investigate molecular mechanisms involving AMPK, ACC, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial function [52, 53]. Their use in this study therefore provides a relevant and controlled human‐based system to confirm the direct effects of citrate on ACC–AMPK regulation, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and lipid accumulation. Finally, although the correlations between plasma citrate, ACC activity, and renal lipotoxicity in MWF rats are consistent, future studies using kidney‐specific models and direct metabolic flux measurements are warranted to establish causality more precisely.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The Munich Wistar Frömter (MWF) rat represents a genetic model for the development of albuminuria and moderate hypertension that arises spontaneously and progressively with age, exhibiting a phenotype that resembles that of CKD patients [23, 25, 54]. It is characterized by an inherited nephron deficit, which induces compensatory glomerular hypertrophy [25], promoting the onset of albuminuria from 6 weeks of age and CKD over time [55]. MWF rats were supplied by Prof. Reinhold Kreutz (Charité Universität, Berlin, Germany), and Wistar rats were purchased from Janvier Labs (Le Genest‐Saint‐Isle, France) at 22 and 45 weeks of age (22w and 45w). Animals were kept in a 12 h‐dark–light cycle at a constant room temperature (22°C–25°C) and humidity (40%–70%) and fed a standard rodent chow diet (Rettenmayer & Söhne Rosenberg, Germany) and water ad libitum. Only male rats were used because the progression of albuminuria is more pronounced in males than in females [25], related to the action of testosterone through the androgen receptor [56]. In addition, this model shows a gradual decrease in renal podocytes with age, accompanied by glomerulosclerosis and renal fibrosis [25]. Animals were anesthetized with i.p. administration of ketamine hydrochloride (80 mg/kg) and xylazine hydrochloride (12 mg/kg) and euthanized by exsanguination. Plasma, kidneys, and femur were obtained for further determination. Number of animals per group was n = 6 to reach a significance level of 5% (p < 0.05), with a required power of 80% and a difference to be detected of 1.6 standard deviations. No animals died during the experimental procedures.

The study was approved by the Instituto Pluridisciplinar UCM (ES‐280790000086) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Comunidad de Madrid (PROEX 205/18, approved in December 2018), following the European Community guidelines for the ethical care of experimental animals. All experiments were conducted at the Instituto Pluridisciplinar, with every effort made to minimize animal suffering in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments; [57]).

Plasma Measurements

The carotid arteries of the rats were catheterized in the presence of heparin, and blood was collected and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to obtain plasma. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until used. Vitamin D (25‐OH cholecalciferol) and creatinine were determined on a sample of heparin‐anticoagulated plasma by the Eurofins Megalab laboratory (Valencia, Spain) following standard procedures. Determination of plasma citrate levels was performed using a specific colorimetric kit (MAK333‐Merck) according to the manufacturer's specifications.

Urinary Protein Excretion Measurement

Urine was collected by placing the animals in metabolic cages for 24 h after a 1‐day adaptation period, and urinary protein concentration was determined with the Protein assay reagent (Biorad) following the manufacturer's protocol. The urinary protein excretion or proteinuria was calculated by dividing the urinary protein concentration between the urine excretion in 24 h and was corrected by tissue weight, expressed as mg of protein/24 h/g of tissue.

Untargeted Lipidomics by UHPLC‐QTOF‐MS

Kidney tissue was disrupted and homogenized following a previously published protocol [58], with specific modifications detailed in the Figure S4. The resulting tissue extracts were subsequently analyzed in both positive and negative ionization modes using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system coupled to an Agilent 6545 quadrupole time‐of‐flight (QTOF) mass spectrometer, as previously described [59] (Supporting Information S1).

Semi‐Quantification of Citric Acid Using CE–TOF–MS

A 7100 capillary electrophoresis system (Agilent Technologies) coupled to a 6224 TOF Mass Spectrometer (Agilent Technologies), equipped with an electrospray ionization source (ESI), was used to analyze citric acid in kidney tissue. The method followed the protocol previously published by López‐López et al. [60], with modifications in the applied voltage, as detailed in the Supporting Information S1.

Data acquired from LC–MS (positive and negative modes) and CE‐MS analysis were pre‐processed with Agilent MassHunter Profinder Software (B.10.00, Agilent Technologies). Quality control and quality assurance procedures were applied according to published quality guidelines [61]. For more details about UHPLC–MS and CE‐MS data pre‐processing and further evaluations carried out, go to the Supporting Information S1.

Micro Computed Tomography Studies

The left femur of each animal was extracted, cleaned, immediately fixated with 4% PFA and stored at 4°C until the micro‐CT analysis. Micro‐CT assays were carried on with an Albira ARS II (Bruker, Germany) in the BioImac facilities (Madrid, Spain) following standard procedures. The parameters for the acquisition were 400uA, 45 kV, 0.125 mm spatial resolution. After the acquisition, bone density values were analyzed with Pmod 4.2 software (Zurich, Switzerland).

HEK293 Cell Culture

Cell culture studies were conducted using the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293. Briefly, cells were seeded in 75 cm2 flasks and maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Biowest) with high glucose with L‐glutamine and sodium pyruvate, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biowest), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Biowest). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Once cells reached 70%–80% confluence, they were subcultured using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Merck) and used between passages 3 and 7. Treatments included incubation for 24 h with sodium citrate (Merck) 0.5 or 1.5 mM, and untreated cells served as controls (CT). Cells were not synchronized by serum starvation, as serum deprivation markedly activates AMPK (Thr172 phosphorylation) [62] and would confound the metabolic readouts of this study.

For cell culture experiments, n denotes independent biological replicates (separate culture flasks/passages processed independently), whereas technical replicates within the same plate were not considered independent.

Oil Red O (ORO) Staining

The right kidney of each animal was frozen in OCT (15 212 776, Fisher Scientific) and carefully cut in 5 μm slices in a cryostat microtome and fixed in pure cold acetone for 10 min. Slides were quickly washed with distilled water and incubated for 12 min in filtered ORO (Sigma, O0625) 0.5% in isopropanol (Fisher Scientific). Slides were rinsed twice in distilled water and incubated in Harris hematoxylin (Sigma HHS‐16) for 2.5 min, rinsed in water, and mounted with a cover glass with glycerol 100% to avoid desiccation. Slides were observed under the 20× optical microscope (Leica DM 2000 Led) immediately after the staining.

For HEK293 cell staining, HEK293 cells were seeded in an 8‐well plate and left for 24 h at 37°C, humidity and 5% CO2 until they reached confluence. On day 2, cells were treated with either 0.5 or 1.5 mM sodium citrate or vehicle (PBS 1×) for 24 h. After the treatments, filtered 0.5% ORO was added to the wells for 12 min, washed with water, and observed under the 40× optical microscope (Leica DM 2000 Led) with immersion oil. After imaging, cells were dissolved in 99% isopropanol, and optical absorbance was measured at 520 nm with a spectrophotometer (ELx808; Bio‐Tek).

MTT Assay

HEK293 cells were seeded in a 96‐well plate and left for 24 h until reaching confluence. On day 2, cells were treated with either 0.5 mM or 1.5 mM sodium citrate or vehicle (PBS 1×) for 24 h. Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide salt (Merck) 5 mg/mL was added to the culture during the last 4 h of incubation. DMEM was removed and cells were dissolved in DMSO (Panreac), and the absorbance at 570 nm was determined on a plate reader (ELx808; Bio‐Tek). Rotenone (50 μM, 24 h), a well‐known inhibitor of NADH‐CoQ reductase (R8875 Merck, Spain), was used as a positive control of mitochondrial function damage.

Neutral Red Assay

HEK293 cells were seeded in a 96‐well plate and left for 24 h until reaching confluence. On day 2, cells were treated with either 0.5 mM or 1.5 mM sodium citrate or vehicle (PBS 1×) for 24 h. Neutral red (Sigma) 4 mg/mL was added to the culture during the last 2 h of incubation. DMEM was removed, and cells were dissolved in DMSO (Panreac), and the absorbance at 570 nm was determined on a plate reader (ELx808; Bio‐Tek). Chloroquine (C6628 Merck, Spain) (50 μm, 24 h) was used as a positive control of lysosomal damage.

Superoxide Anion Detection

HEK293 cells were incubated with dihydroethidium (Molecular Probes, USA) (DHE 3 × 10−6 M) for 30 min in the dark in DMEM for superoxide detection. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), mounted with DAPI, 1 μg/mL; (Molecular Probes, USA) in the dark. DAPI and observed under the 20× fluorescence microscope fluorescence (excitation wavelength 546 nm and emission wavelength 620 nm) (Leica DM 2000 Led).

Western Blot

The expression of proteins was assessed by Western blot as previously described [63] in lysates of rat whole kidney or HEK293 cells in the presence of sample buffer (bromophenol blue, glycerol, beta‐mercaptoethanol, Tris‐Gly) and heated (5 min; 95°C). Specifically, the following proteins were analyzed: AMPKα phosphorylated at Thr172 (anti pThr172‐AMPKα; 62 kDa; Rabbit polyclonal 1:500; 1672531S Cell Signaling Technology), ACC phosphorylated at Ser79 (anti pSer79‐ACC; 265 kDa; Rabbit polyclonal 1:500; 1673661S Cell Signaling Technology), and eNOS phosphorylated at Ser1177 (anti pSer1177‐eNOS; 07‐428‐I Invitrogen) kDa. α‐tubulin (50 kDa) was used as a loading control (Anti α‐tubulin mouse polyclonal 1:5000; ab56676 Abcam). Secondary detection was performed using HRP‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG (1:2000; SC2004 Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti‐mouse IgG (1:10000; NA931V GE Healthcare), respectively. Proteins were separated using 7% or 12% SDS‐PAGE gels prepared with the following composition: 5.1 mL H2O, 2.5 mL 1.5 M Tris–HCl pH 8.8, 50 μL 20% SDS, 2.3 mL 30% acrylamide/bisacrylamide, 50 μL 10% ammonium persulfate, and 5 μL TEMED. Semi dry transfer was performed to preassembled nitrocellulose membranes (BioRad) with the Trans Blot Turbo device (BioRad). Equal protein loading (15–30 μg per lane) and consistent transfer were confirmed using Ponceau red staining.

The expression of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complexes was assessed in total kidney extract by immunoblotting in the presence of sample buffer (bromophenol blue, glycerol, beta‐mercaptoethanol, Tris‐Gly) at room temperature. A commercial antibody cocktail targeting multiple OXPHOS subunits was used at a 1:250 dilution (438 099 Invitrogen), along with an anti‐α‐tubulin antibody at a 1:5000 dilution (ab56676 Abcam) as a loading control. Membranes were imaged with an image documentator Syngene GBOX‐CHEMI (Thermo Fisher) after a 5 min long incubation in ECL (ECL‐Prime, Amersham). Picture quantification was performed by normalizing the signal intensity of each protein to tubulin (protein/tubulin ratio compared to control group (Wistar)). To enhance resolution and detection accuracy, gels were segmented prior to transfer based on molecular weight markers, aligning with the expected sizes of the target proteins.

Clear Native PAGE and Immunodetection

Kidneys and HEK293 cells were lysed in the Native PAGE sample preparation kit (10 165 392; InvitrogenTM) in presence of 1% digitonin (InvitrogenTM) and protease inhibitor cocktail (A32965, Fisher Scientific) at 4°C. Protein concentration was assessed, and samples were placed in a two‐phase handmade polyacrylamide gel (8%–4%). Electrophoresis was carried out in absence of SDS or any other denaturing agent at 4°C during approximately 2 h at 150 V constant in cathode buffer (50 mM Tricine; 15 mM Bis‐Tris; pH = 7) between the gels and anode buffer (50 mM Bis‐Tris; pH = 7) outside the gels. After PAGE, wet transfer to a methanol‐activated PVDF membrane (RPN303F, Cytiva) was done overnight at 4°C and 40 mAmp constant in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris; 192 mM Glycine; 5% methanol; 0.02% SDS; pH = 8.3). Transfer was confirmed with Ponceau Red staining and then washed and blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBS‐T and incubated overnight 4°C with the anti‐Acetyl Coa Carboxylase antibody (Mouse 05‐1098 Millipore; 1:500) followed by the incubation with HRP‐conjugated goat anti mouse antibody (Goat NA931V, Thermo Fisher), and images were detected with a Syngene GBOX‐CHEMI (Thermo Fisher) after 5 min of incubation in ECL (ECL‐Prime, Amersham).

RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the kidney using Trizol Reagent (Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000/c spectrophotometer (Fisher Scientific). For reverse transcription, 500 ng of RNA were converted into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad) with random hexamer primers. Primer specificity, optimal annealing temperatures, and amplicon sizes were verified for each primer pair. Quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed on a CFX96 Real‐Time PCR Detection System (BioRad) using 10 ng of cDNA per reaction. Samples from 6 to 10 biological replicates per group were run in duplicate. Each reaction contained a 1:1 mix of forward and reverse intron‐skipping primers at a final concentration of 3 μM, along with 2× SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Gene expression was normalized to the housekeeping genes Gapdh and Atpaf‐1. All primer sequences used are provided in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Results were expressed as the arithmetic mean ± the standard error of the arithmetic mean (S.E.M.). Normality Shapiro–Wilk test was performed before data analysis. Comparisons of the results obtained between individual groups were made using 2‐way ANOVA or a t‐test (parametric) or Mann Whitney U (non‐parametric) to analyze differences between experiments. Significantly different groups were considered when p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 9 software (San Diego, USA) was used for statistical analysis and ROUT method (Q = 1%) was used to identify and exclude outliers. The free software ImageJ (Java Community Process, USA) was used for image analysis. For UHPLC–MS analysis, probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) was applied, as previously described by Cuevas‐Delgado et al. [58]. This normalization strategy allows correction for dilution effects in untargeted lipidomics datasets, improving comparability across samples. For citric acid measured by CE‐TOF‐MS, the Mann–Whitney test was performed. Details on UHPLC–MS and CE‐MS data pre‐processing, and uni‐ and multivariate statistics are provided in the Supporting Information S1.

Conclusions

Altogether, our data support a model in which high plasma citrate in CKD, potentially originating from increased bone resorption, drives ELD in tubular cells through citrate‐dependent ACC activation and AMPK uncoupling. The resulting imbalance between lipogenesis and β‐oxidation leads to ELD, mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS production, and fibrosis. Importantly, this mechanism highlights CKD as a metabolic disorder beyond its traditional classification as “non‐metabolic”.

From a translational perspective, these findings open two avenues. First, ACC emerges as a promising therapeutic target: pharmacological inhibition of ACC, or its upstream regulator ACLY (e.g., with bempedoic acid), may reduce renal lipotoxicity. Second, the clinical use of citrate as an anticoagulant in dialysis deserves reconsideration, as exogenous citrate could potentially exacerbate renal injury in patients already vulnerable to metabolic citrate overload.
