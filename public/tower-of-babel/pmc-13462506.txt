Type 2 Diabetes and Hemodialysis: Metabolic Challenges and the Role of Intradialytic Exercise
Authors: Lola T. Daminova, Sitorakhon U. Muminova, Katherine R. Tuttle, Botir T. Daminov, Shavkat K. Muminov, Durdona Sh. Adilova, Guzal B. Kasimova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is the leading global cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESRD). Patients receiving hemodialysis often have a high cardiometabolic risk profile, including insulin resistance, glycemic variability, chronic inflammation, sarcopenia, and altered mineral metabolism. In this setting, standard glycemic markers are often unreliable, supporting the use of additional non-pharmacological strategies, particularly structured physical activity, alongside standard treatment. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on metabolic dysregulation in hemodialysis patients with T2DM and evaluates the potential role of intradialytic exercise as a multi-target intervention.MethodsA focused narrative search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science (2014–2025). Randomized trials, observational studies, clinical guidelines and narrative reviews addressing key metabolic and musculoskeletal complications, CKD-MBD, and intradialytic exercise in advanced kidney disease were considered.ConclusionsIntradialytic exercise represents a feasible adjunct strategy targeting interconnected metabolic pathways in patients with T2DM and ESRD. Further large-scale prospective studies are warranted to define the optimal intensity, modality and timing of intradialytic exercise and to clarify its long-term cardiometabolic and clinical benefits.

Introduction

The global prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) continues to rise, accompanied by an increasing burden of both macrovascular and microvascular complications. These complications substantially worsen disease outcomes, increase the risk of early disability, and markedly reduce quality of life. According to the 11th edition of the IDF Diabetes Atlas, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes worldwide in 2024, corresponding to a prevalence of 11.1% of adults aged 20–79 years worldwide, corresponding to about 537 million individuals, with more than 40% remaining undiagnosed. 1

T2DM is the leading global cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD). In the United States, diabetes accounts for an estimated 44% of incident ESRD cases.2-4 In the Republic of Uzbekistan, the prevalence of T2DM is also steadily increasing, with more than 412,000 registered patients. T2DM is a leading cause of CKD and a major driver of its progression. In 2024, approximately 214,000 patients with CKD were registered nationwide, including 5,765 individuals receiving maintenance hemodialysis, reflecting an annual increase of nearly 17%. 5 A substantial proportion of these patients have diabetes-related kidney disease, which accelerates renal function decline and worsens the overall clinical course of CKD.

Globally, the coexistence of T2DM and CKD is associated with a highly unfavorable cardiovascular risk profile. Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, accounting for more than half of all deaths. According to the United States Renal Data System (USRDS, 2024), approximately 50% of deaths among dialysis patients are attributable to cardiovascular causes, with sudden cardiac death accounting for nearly one quarter of these cases. Notably, sudden cardiac death accounts for nearly one quarter of these deaths.6-8 The coexistence of T2DM and ESRD on dialysis creates a malignant cardiometabolic phenotype that necessitates targeted, multilevel strategies to improve outcomes.

Beyond glycemic and cardiovascular abnormalities, disturbances of mineral metabolism play a central role in patients with T2DM and CKD. Chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) is highly prevalent among diabetic patients receiving dialysis, affecting approximately 58% to more than 80% of this population. These abnormalities substantially contribute to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality and raise the risk of arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. 9

Patients with T2DM receiving dialysis are therefore characterized by a distinct metabolic milieu marked by glycemic volatility, profound insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, sarcopenia, and disordered mineral metabolism. Conventional glycemic markers and management strategies are often inadequate in this setting. Consequently, there is growing interest in adjunctive, non-pharmacological interventions such as structured intradialytic exercise defined as supervised physical activity performed during hemodialysis sessions. Emerging evidence suggests that intradialytic aerobic and resistance training can improve glycemic control, enhance insulin resistance, attenuate systemic inflammation, and improve physical function in dialysis patients.10-12

This narrative review examines the complex interaction between T2DM and CKD in patients receiving hemodialysis, highlights key challenges in glycemic and mineral metabolism management, and critically evaluates the potential role of intradialytic exercise as a therapeutic strategy.

Methods

Sources and Literature Search

This narrative review was developed through a focused analysis of contemporary literature addressing T2DM in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis. A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, covering publications from January 2014 through March 2025. The search strategy combined terms related to diabetes, CKD, and dialysis, with keywords reflecting major metabolic and clinical pathways relevant to this population, including insulin resistance, glycemic variability, continuous glucose monitoring, sarcopenia, chronic inflammation, CKD–MBD, fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23), and intradialytic exercise. Priority was given to randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, systematic and narrative reviews, large observational studies, and international clinical practice guidelines published by major professional organizations, including the American Diabetes Association and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO). Studies conducted in non-dialysis CKD or non-diabetic populations were included selectively, when mechanistic insight considered directly applicable to patients with T2DM undergoing hemodialysis. As a narrative review, no formal quality assessment or quantitative synthesis was performed; the literature was summarized to highlight key evidence and remaining uncertainties.

Pathophysiological Interplay Between T2DM and CKD

Insulin Resistance and Uremic Milieu

Advanced CKD intrinsically promotes insulin resistance (IR) even in individuals without diabetes, through multiple mechanism including accumulation of uremic toxins, metabolic acidosis, oxidative stress, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. In patients with T2DM, these uremic related factors compound pre-existing IR, resulting in a profound impairment of insulin action.13-15 Progressive loss of renal function also reduces insulin clearance, prolonging insulin half-life and further complicating glycemic management.

At the cellular level, the uremic milieu is associated with impaired insulin receptor signaling, reduced activation of downstream pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–Akt, and diminished translocation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) in skeletal muscle. Deficiency of active vitamin D due to loss of renal 1α-hydroxylation, along with activation of the renin–angiotensin system, further exacerbates IR and endothelial dysfunction.14,16 Skeletal muscle, the principal site of insulin-mediated glucose disposal, is therefore disproportionately affected in patients with concurrent T2DM and advanced CKD.

Sarcopenia and Muscle Wasting

CKD represents a catabolic state characterized by progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength, commonly referred to as uremic sarcopenia. T2DM frequently coexists with sarcopenic obesity, while insulin resistance directly impairs muscle protein synthesis. In end-stage renal disease, anabolic resistance of skeletal muscle to both insulin and amino acids is markedly pronounced, while persistent inflammation accelerates proteolysis through cytokine-mediated activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway.17,18

The resulting muscle wasting leads to frailty, reduced exercise capacity and diminished functional independence. Importantly, sarcopenia and insulin resistance reinforce one other in a self-perpetuating cycle: loss of muscle mass limits peripheral glucose uptake, aggravating hyperglycemia and compensatory hyperinsulinemia, which in turn further suppress anabolic signaling in muscle. Concurrent increase in visceral adiposity, particularly in patients with T2DM, amplify metabolic dysfunction and systemic inflammation.19,20 This maladaptive muscle–insulin resistance–muscle feedback loop contributes substantially to adverse clinical outcomes in diabetic dialysis patients.

Chronic Hyperglycemia and Inflammatory Pathways

Hyperglycemia in T2DM promotes the formation and accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). In patients undergoing dialysis, reduced renal clearance leads to marked AGE accumulation, which activates the AGE–RAGE (receptor for AGE) signaling axis. Engagement of RAGE triggers downstream activation of nuclear factor κB, oxidative stress pathways, and pro-inflammatory gene expression, thereby promoting endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis.21-23

Alterations in adipokine profiles further contribute to the inflammatory burden. Patients with T2DM and CKD typically exhibit elevated circulating levels of leptin and resistin, alongside reduced adiponectin concentrations. Leptin and resistin promote systemic inflammation through activation of NF-κB signaling, leading to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, as well as macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Conversely, reduced adiponectin levels—which under physiological conditions suppress NF-κB activity, improve insulin sensitivity, and exert anti-inflammatory effects—remove an important protective mechanism, thereby further amplifying systemic inflammation. In addition, glycated albumin may directly bind to RAGE on vascular and immune cells, enhancing inflammatory signaling independently of ambient glucose levels. 24 Together, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and adipokine imbalance create a profound pro-atherogenic milieu that accelerates vascular calcification, arterial stiffness, and maladaptive cardiac remodeling. 25 These intersecting mechanisms largely account for the exceptionally high burden cardiovascular events observed in patients with T2DM and ESRD receiving maintenance hemodialysis. Challenges in Glycemic Control in Dialysis Patients

Glycemic Control in Dialysis

Managing blood glucose in ESRD patients with T2DM is notoriously difficult. As renal insulin clearance declines, patients can experience unpredictable swings between hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia. During dialysis, the use of glucose-containing dialysate can cause fluctuations in plasma glucose; some patients develop intradialytic hypoglycemia unawareness. 26 Standard markers of long-term glycemic control are less reliable: Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is often deceptively low in dialysis patients despite significant hyperglycemia. This is because the lifespan of erythrocytes is shortened in the uremic milieu and many patients have anemia treated with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, both of which lower HbA1c independent of true glycemia. 27 It is not uncommon for diabetic dialysis patients to have HbA1c in a seemingly “optimal” range (e.g. ∼6–7%) while experiencing high glucose variability and persistent hyperglycemia on continuous glucose monitoring. 28 Glycated albumin is sometimes used as an adjunct marker of glycemic control; however, its interpretation may also be influenced by malnutrition and hypoalbuminemia. The net effect is that clinicians must individualize glycemic targets and therapies, balancing the risk of hypoglycemia (to which dialysis patients are highly susceptible) against the harms of uncontrolled hyperglycemia.29,30 Evidence synthesized from multiple studies demonstrates that continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) is uniquely capable of capturing clinically relevant glycemic patterns during hemodialysis, notably intradialytic hypoglycemia and post-dialysis hyperglycemic rebounds, which are often missed by standard laboratory assessments. CGM consistently shows a higher mean amplitude of glucose excursions on days of dialysis and provides superior detection of time below range compared with HbA1c. Importantly, CGM-guided management has been associated with safer insulin adjustments, reduced hypoglycemic episodes, improved patient satisfaction, and better-informed clinical decision-making. 31

Careful adjustment of insulin and oral agents is required, with frequent monitoring. Many antihyperglycemic drugs have altered pharmacokinetics in ESRD. For instance, metformin is contraindicated or used with caution due to lactic acidosis risk; sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are largely ineffective when GFR is very low; and some sulfonylureas pose prolonged hypoglycemia risk.32,33

Although newer incretin-based therapies—including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors provide glucose dependent insulinotropic effects with a favorable safety profile in chronic kidney disease, robust evidence in dialysis populations remains limited. While current guidelines endorse GLP-1 receptor agonists for patients with advanced CKD, emerging real-world data suggests that these agents may achieve clinically relevant reductions in HbA1c with a lower risk of hypoglycemia than insulin, particularly when used as insulin-sparing therapy. Nevertheless, achieving adequate glycemic control without increasing hypoglycemia or cardiovascular risk remains a major unmet clinical need in patients receiving dialysis, underscoring the necessity for safer, individualized glucose-lowering strategies and further dedicated studies in this high-risk population.34-37 The challenge is compounded by patients’ reduced appetite and erratic oral intake, and by dialysis-related shifts in volume and insulin sensitivity. Ultimately, achieving stable glycemic control in this population remains an unmet need, as both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia are linked to higher mortality in dialysis patients.

Mineral and Bone Disorder in Diabetic Dialysis Patients

Advanced stage CKD is associated with disturbances in bone and mineral metabolism, however, T2DM confers a distinct CKD-mineral and bone disorder phenotype, frequently characterized by low bone turnover, commonly referred to as adynamic bone disease. Diabetic dialysis patients often exhibit inappropriately low or normal parathyroid hormone (PTH) concentrations relative to the degree of renal failure, reflecting suppressed parathyroid gland activity. 38 In the large COSMOS cohort (n=6,797 hemodialysis patients), median intact PTH levels were 195 pg/mL in patients with diabetes compared with 214 pg/mL in nondiabetic counterparts. Similar finding has been reported in other cohorts, with diabetic patients on dialysis demonstrating PTH concentrations that are 30–40% lower than those observed in non-diabetic patients.39,40

Diabetic dialysis patients also tend to have lower serum phosphate concentrations, partly due to dietary factors and impaired skeletal phosphate release. While lower PTH and phosphate levels may appear favorable when viewed in isolation, their coexistence reflects impaired skeletal buffering capacity and characterizes adynamic bone disease, a condition associated with increased risks of vascular calcification and fractures.40,41 Consequently, diabetic patients may meet guideline-recommended biochemical targets while harboring a substantially higher burden of cardiovascular calcification.

Role of FGF-23 as a Cardiometabolic Mediator

FGF-23, produced mainly by osteocytes, often increases early in diabetic kidney disease and reaches very high levels in dialysis-dependent kidney failure. Several studies suggest that FGF-23 levels increase earlier and to a greater extent in patients with diabetic CKD compared with non-diabetic CKD at comparable levels of renal function, potentially due to more severe phosphate retention, vitamin D deficiency, or inflammation.42,43 By the time patients initiate dialysis, FGF-23 concentrations are typically markedly elevated, often exceeding 5,000 RU/mL. Importantly, FGF-23 is increasingly recognized not only as a biomarker of mineral dysregulation but also as a mediator of adverse cardiometabolic outcomes. Elevated FGF-23 levels have been associated with insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, left ventricular hypertrophy, and increased mortality.40,44 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain these associations. FGF-23 may contribute to insulin resistance indirectly through systemic inflammation, α-klotho deficiency, and disruption of insulin-signaling pathways. In addition, FGF-23 can bind fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4) on hepatocytes in a klotho-independent manner, activating the PLCγ/calcineurin/NFAT signaling cascade and increasing the production of inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and CRP. Furthermore, FGF-23 directly activates FGFR4 on cardiomyocytes, promoting hypertrophic gene expression through the PLCγ/calcineurin/NFAT pathway and contributing to left ventricular hypertrophy independently of blood pressure or volume overload. These mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the observed associations between elevated FGF-23 concentrations, metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, cardiovascular remodeling, and adverse clinical outcomes in CKD.45,46 Large population-based studies support these associations. In the CRIC (Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort) study, elevated FGF-23 concentrations were linked to higher insulin resistance indices, elevated inflammatory markers, and increased risk of incident diabetes. 44 Similarly, analyses from the CRIC study demonstrated correlations between FGF-23 levels, inflammatory markers, and insulin resistance, particularly among participants with diabetes. 47 Collectively, these findings suggest that FGF-23 may represent a mechanistic link between disordered mineral metabolism, metabolic dysfunction, and cardiovascular risk in diabetic CKD. In parallel, diabetic patients with ESRD frequently exhibit a distinct low-bone-turnover phenotype characterized by relative suppression of parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion and impaired skeletal buffering capacity. Several mechanisms may contribute to relative parathyroid gland suppression and low-turnover bone disease in diabetic patients with ESRD. Chronic hyperglycemia and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) may impair parathyroid gland function and reduce skeletal responsiveness to PTH. Diabetes is also associated with decreased osteoblastic activity, autonomic dysfunction, and chronic inflammation, all of which favor a low-bone-turnover state. In addition, intensive treatment with calcium-containing phosphate binders, active vitamin D analogues, or calcimimetics may lead to oversuppression of PTH secretion. Role of Intradialytic Exercise: Mechanisms and Evidence

Rationale for Exercise During Dialysis

Given the complex and interrelated metabolic disturbances characteristic of patients with T2DM and ESRD receiving maintenance hemodialysis, there is a strong physiological rationale for integrating structured physical exercise into dialysis care.48,49 Hemodialysis sessions provide a unique, controlled environment in which patients are continuously monitored for several hours, typically three times per week, allowing safe supervision of hemodynamic status, glycemic fluctuations, and vascular access during physical activity. 50

Mechanistically, intradialytic exercise directly counteracts several pathological processes of T2DM and CKD. At the cellular level, skeletal muscle contractions during exercise stimulate glucose uptake through insulin-independent pathways, primarily via enhanced translocation of glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) to the muscle cell membrane. This mechanism acutely lowers circulating glucose concentrations during and immediately after exercise, thereby attenuating intradialytic and post-dialysis hyperglycemic excursions. With repeated training, intradialytic exercise induces sustained improvements in insulin sensitivity through upregulation of insulin receptor signaling, increased muscle glycogen storage capacity, and enhanced mitochondrial function.51,52 Additionally, regular exercise training leads to improved cardiorespiratory fitness and muscle strength, which are particularly relevant in populations characterized by frailty and reduced physical function.

Beyond Glucose Metabolism, Exercise Exerts Anti-Inflammatory and Anabolic Effects

During intradialytic exercise, contracting skeletal muscles release myokines, including IL-6 in its muscle-derived form, which trigger an anti-inflammatory cascade characterized by increased levels of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra), along with attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Over time, regular training has been associated with lower circulating levels of CRP and TNF-α in patients with CKD, although results across studies remain heterogeneous.53,54 Systematic reviews in hemodialysis and CKD populations report that structured exercise interventions may reduce systemic inflammatory markers such as CRP and TNF-α, although findings remain heterogeneous across studies. Some trials have also reported improvements in serum albumin, reflecting changes in inflammatory and nutritional status.55,56 Exercise also mitigates the muscle wasting cycle: it stimulates muscle protein synthesis, improves appetite, and increases muscle fiber cross-sectional area, thereby helping to stabilize or increase lean body mass when combined with adequate nutrition. This may directly combat uremic sarcopenia, improving strength and functional capacity (e.g., gait speed, sit-to-stand performance). 57

Clinical Evidence on Glycemic and Metabolic Outcomes in Dialysis

A growing number of trials have evaluated intradialytic exercise in CKD patients, including those with T2DM. Most studies have been relatively short-term (2–6 months) and involved aerobic training (cycling or treadmill during dialysis) or combined aerobic and resistance exercises.58,59

A prospective study involving 60 hemodialysis patients (35% with diabetes) demonstrated a significant reduction in fasting blood glucose following a 6-month intradialytic cycling program (98.73 ± 23.18 to 89.55 ± 12.49 mg/dL; P = 0.001), accompanied by favorable changes in lipid parameters and renal biochemical markers. 59 In contrast, a randomized controlled trial of 64 hemodialysis patients, approximately half of whom had T2DM, reported no significant change in fasting glucose after a 12-week intradialytic aerobic exercise intervention (group and time interaction β = 5.80 mg/dL; P = 0.73), suggesting that shorter-duration or moderate-intensity programs may be insufficient to modify glycemic status. 60

In a prospective interventional study of 28 hemodialysis patients (≈25% with diabetes), a 4-week supervised rehabilitation program assessed fasting glucose, fasting insulin, HbA1c and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). Baseline HbA1c was approximately 6.2%, consistent with the known attenuation of glycemic markers in ESRD. No significant improvements were observed in fasting glucose, HbA1c or insulin resistance indices over the short intervention period. However, significant baseline inverse correlations were reported between resistin and fasting insulin (r = −0.66; P = 0.01) as well as HOMA-IR (r =−0.68; P = 0.007) in patients without metabolic syndrome, indicating potential exercise-related modulation of metabolic signaling despite the absence of short-term glycemic improvement.61,62 In general, regular intradialytic exercise leads to modest but significant improvements in glycemic indices. More stable daily glucose profiles are observed with exercise, reflecting sustained skeletal muscle glucose uptake during and after dialysis compared with sedentary periods. For instance, a recent 12-month trial (the DiaTT study, 2023) in >900 dialysis patients found those randomized to intradialytic endurance strength training had fewer hospital days and improved physical quality of life scores compared to usual care. While diabetes-specific outcome data remains limited, patients with diabetes are likely to derive equal or greater benefit given their higher baseline cardiometabolic risk. 62

Effect on Glucose Homeostasis, Inflammation, and Bone-Mineral Axis

Glucose Homeostasis

Exercise favorably influences glucose metabolism in patients receiving hemodialysis through several complementary mechanisms. Acutely, intradialytic exercise increases skeletal muscle glucose uptake via insulin-independent pathways, leading to lower blood glucose levels during and immediately after dialysis and potentially attenuating post-dialysis rebound hyperglycemia variability.52,59 With regular training, exercise results in modest but consistent improvements in glycemic control, reflected by small reductions in HbA1c and decreased glycemic variability.59,63 Continuous glucose monitoring studies show fewer hyperglycemic excursions and increased time spent within the target glucose range on dialysis days when exercise is performed. 31 These sustained effects are likely mediated by improved insulin sensitivity, associated with increased GLUT4 expression in skeletal muscle and enhanced mitochondrial function. 64 Although most trials report stable use of antidiabetic medications, some patients have been able to reduce insulin doses or oral glucose-lowering therapy following prolonged intradialytic exercise. Overall, these findings support exercise as an adjunct “metabolic therapy” that complements pharmacological management of diabetes in ESRD.56,65,66

Inflammation and Nutritional Status

Chronic low-grade inflammation is common in hemodialysis patients, particularly in those with diabetes. Although IL-6 acutely increases during a single session of intradialytic exercise as a muscle-derived myokine, this transient rise is mechanistically distinct from chronic systemic inflammation. Repeated intradialytic exercise induces long-term anti-inflammatory adaptations through myokine-mediated modulation of immune cell activity and cytokine production.53,55 Accordingly, several studies have reported reductions in circulating inflammatory markers, including IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP, following regular intradialytic exercise. 55 Longer interventions combining intradialytic exercise with nutritional support have also demonstrated improvements in protein–energy wasting and physical functioning. 67 However, cytokine responses are not uniform across studies, likely reflecting short intervention duration, limited sample sizes, or reduced training responsiveness in older patients. Importantly, even when circulating cytokine levels change minimally, exercise may shift immune cell phenotypes toward a less pro-inflammatory state, including reductions in pro-inflammatory monocytes and preservation of regulatory T cells, potentially conferring cardiovascular benefit. 56 Exercise also improves nutritional status by enhancing appetite and stimulating muscle protein anabolism, which can increase serum albumin and prealbumin levels. As higher serum albumin is a strong predictor of survival in dialysis patients, these effects are clinically meaningful. Overall, intradialytic exercise represents an anti-inflammatory and anabolic strategy targeting the malnutrition–inflammation complex of ESRD.

Bone-Mineral Axis

The effects of intradialytic exercise on CKD-MBD are less well studied, but available evidence and mechanistic considerations suggest potential benefits. Improvements in insulin sensitivity and reductions in systemic inflammation may indirectly influence key regulators of bone turnover, including PTH and FGF-23.41,66,67 Chronic inflammation suppresses osteoblast activity and promotes bone loss; therefore, reducing inflammatory burden through exercise may support more balanced bone remodeling.

In a 6-month randomized controlled trial, intradialytic exercise was associated with improved markers of mineral metabolism, including lower serum phosphorus and PTH levels. 68 Better phosphate balance may in turn attenuate excessive FGF-23 stimulation, particularly as inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α are known drivers of FGF-23 production. Although direct interventional evidence remains limited, mechanical loading during exercise may enhance skeletal responsiveness and potentially benefit patients with low-turnover (adynamic) bone disease.68,69 Resistance-based exercise, where feasible, could be especially relevant for preserving bone mass.

Overall, while definitive clinical data are lacking, current understanding supports exercise as a potential modulator of the CKD–MBD axis. By improving muscle mass and function, enhancing phosphate control, and reducing inflammation, intradialytic exercise may help alleviate selected disturbances in mineral metabolism, particularly serum phosphorus and PTH levels. 68 Further targeted studies are needed to clarify its effects on PTH, FGF-23, and long-term bone outcomes.

Novelty and Contribution of the Review

This narrative review presents an integrated, pathophysiology-driven perspective on type 2 diabetes mellitus in patients with end-stage renal disease receiving maintenance hemodialysis, a population characterized by profound metabolic and cardiovascular vulnerability. Unlike prior reviews that primarily focus on isolated aspects of glycemic control or physical rehabilitation, this article synthesizes evidence across insulin resistance, glycemic variability, inflammation, sarcopenia, and CKD–MBD, highlighting their interconnected nature. A key contribution of this review is the positioning of intradialytic exercise as a multi-target metabolic intervention rather than a purely rehabilitative strategy. By linking molecular mechanisms with clinical and translational implications, the review clarifies how supervised exercise during dialysis may simultaneously modulate multiple pathophysiological pathways. This integrative framework may inform more comprehensive and individualized approaches to diabetes management in the dialysis setting.

Practical Implications and Future Directions

From a clinical perspective, intradialytic exercise represents a feasible and adaptable intervention that can be incorporated into routine dialysis care for patients with T2DM and ESRD. Exercise protocols can be individualized according to functional status, comorbidity burden, and vascular access location, allowing participation even among frail patients. When performed under medical supervision, intradialytic exercise is generally well tolerated and does not appear to increase the risk of intradialytic hypotension or severe hypoglycemia.

Potential synergies may be achieved by combining intradialytic exercise with complementary interventions. Integration with targeted nutritional support may enhance muscle protein anabolism and help counter protein–energy wasting, while the use of continuous glucose monitoring may facilitate safer glycemic management around exercise sessions. In addition, exercise may interact favorably with therapies targeting CKD–MBD by influencing phosphate handling, systemic inflammation, and mechanical skeletal loading. Collectively, these mechanisms may contribute to improved cardiometabolic health, although definitive effects on cardiovascular events remain to be established.

Emerging evidence suggests that sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a key regulator of cellular metabolism, mitochondrial function, inflammation, and aging, may represent an additional mechanistic link between exercise and improved cardiometabolic health in patients with T2DM and ESRD. Physical activity has been shown to enhance SIRT1 activity, potentially contributing to improved insulin sensitivity, reduced oxidative stress, and preservation of skeletal muscle function. Although evidence in hemodialysis populations remains limited, SIRT1 may represent a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for future studies evaluating the long-term metabolic effects of intradialytic exercise.70,71

Future research should prioritize large, multicenter randomized trials designed to evaluate long-term clinical outcomes, including cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, specifically in patients with T2DM receiving maintenance hemodialysis, as evidence in this subgroup remains limited. Further studies are also needed to define the optimal type, intensity, and duration of intradialytic exercise and to develop diabetes-specific protocols for patients with neuropathy, foot ulcers, or limited mobility.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, it does not follow a formal systematic review methodology and therefore may be subject to selection bias in the identification and interpretation of relevant studies. Second, the available evidence regarding intradialytic exercise in patients with T2DM receiving maintenance hemodialysis remains limited, with many studies characterized by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and heterogeneous exercise protocols. Third, relatively few studies have specifically focused on patients with both T2DM and ESRD, and therefore some mechanistic insights were extrapolated from broader CKD or hemodialysis populations. Finally, evidence regarding the long-term effects of intradialytic exercise on cardiovascular outcomes, mortality, CKD–MBD parameters, and patient-reported outcomes remains insufficient, highlighting the need for large, well-designed randomized controlled trials.

Conclusion

Patients with T2DM and ESRD receiving maintenance hemodialysis represent a high-risk group for cardiovascular complications due to the coexistence of diabetic and uremic metabolic disturbances. Intradialytic exercise is a safe and feasible intervention that can improve glycemic control, enhance insulin sensitivity, reduce inflammation, and preserve muscle function. When performed under medical supervision, physical activity during dialysis improves quality of life and may become an important component of comprehensive dialysis care.
