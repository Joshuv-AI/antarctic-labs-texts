Hybrid dynamical systems modeling of power systems
Bukunmi Gabriel Odunlami, Marcos Netto, Yoshihiko Susuki

Abstract
The increasing integration of renewable energy sources has accentuated the already complex dynamics of power systems, challenging the adequacy of established continuous-time modeling approaches. There is a need for modeling frameworks that can capture the intricate interplay between continuous dynamics and discrete events characterizing modern grid operations. Hybrid dynamical systems offer a rigorous foundation for representing such mixed dynamics and have emerged as a valuable tool in power system analysis. Despite their potential, existing studies remain focused on isolated applications or case-specific implementations, offering limited generalizability and guidance for model selection. This paper addresses that gap by providing a comprehensive overview of hybrid modeling approaches relevant to power systems. It critically examines key formalisms, including hybrid automata, switched systems, and piecewise affine models, evaluating their respective strengths, limitations, and suitability across system analysis, stability assessment, and control design tasks. In doing so, the paper identifies open challenges and outlines future research directions to support the systematic application of hybrid systems modeling and methods in renewable-rich, converter-dominated power systems.

Introduction

Electric grids are undergoing a profound transformation, driven in part by the integration of renewable energy sources (RES). While new technologies advance decarbonization and energy sustainability goals, they also introduce faster switching dynamics, higher uncertainty, and novel control-driven instabilities. Unlike legacy synchronous generators, RES are typically connected to the grid via power electronic converters, which lack inertia and respond to control signals on much faster timescales. This transition has led to more volatile power flows, reduced frequency stability margins, and a growing number of system events driven by discrete operations.

For decades, power grid dynamics have been represented using continuous-time models, which assume that system variables evolve smoothly. However, modern power systems are increasingly subject to discrete events resulting from internal operations or external disturbances. While continuous dynamics evolve over a range of values, discrete events occur instantaneously, triggering abrupt transitions between states. Examples include the sequential tripping of power system components

1

, microgrid islanding and reconnection to the grid
[
2

, and fault clearance by protective devices
[

. Other examples, along with their occurrence and implications in renewable-rich power grids, are provided in

To account for such discrete phenomena, continuous models introduce approximations or simplifying assumptions. These may, for instance, obscure transient behaviors or yield flawed assessments of stability
[
4
]
. In RES-dominated networks, these limitations can have practical consequences for protection coordination, system planning, and real-time control. It is therefore no longer sufficient to model power system dynamics solely with continuous-time differential-algebraic equations. Switching and discrete events must be systematically incorporated, as they can influence system dynamics in ways that current models cannot capture.

As power networks evolve into decentralized, converter-dominated architectures with heightened reliance on digital control and communication layers, modeling approaches must reflect both smooth physical processes and abrupt discrete transitions within a unified structure
[
5

HDS methods have gained traction in several engineering domains and are increasingly relevant in power systems with high levels of renewable integration. Additionally, they have been used to model cyber-physical interdependencies between network behavior and discrete cyber processes. Applications in this area include addressing cybersecurity challenges such as false data injection attacks, switching attack events, and the impact of communication delays on the performance of control systems

Early efforts focused on systems exhibiting switching, friction, and impact phenomena prevalent in many engineering applications. 1.

A comprehensive review of HDS applications in power systems with high penetration of RES. This includes diving into broad research categories such as modeling, analysis, control, and design.

An identification and discussion of key research gaps, highlighting methodological limitations and opportunities for future research in HDS modeling of modern power systems.

The paper is not an exhaustive review of HDS methods. While many domains, including physics and biology, have utilized HDS for various modeling tasks, this review focuses explicitly on power systems applications.

2
Overview of HDS modeling frameworks

This section presents a concise overview of foundational HDS formalisms that have found relevance in converter-dominated and renewable-rich power systems. The goal is to enhance understanding of how these frameworks are structured and how they may be applied in modern power system modeling. We begin by characterizing the structural behavior of general hybrid systems, followed by a focused summary of each modeling approach.

In this paper, the term
hybrid system
is used in accordance with Definition 1, and should not be confused with broader uses of ”hybrid” that imply combining different methods, models, or technologies.

Definition 1.

(Hybrid dynamical system): A hybrid dynamical system is one that exhibits both continuous and discrete dynamics—characterized by differential or difference equations governing continuous behavior and discrete transitions triggered by logic rules, thresholds, event conditions, or switching mechanisms.

Hybrid systems can exhibit a variety of structural behaviors. In some cases, transitions between discrete modes may induce changes to the system dynamics, parameters, or state space. In other cases, discrete events occur without altering the underlying continuous dynamics. For instance, consider a generator delivering power through two parallel transmission lines with identical electrical properties (e.g., impedance
R+jX
). A maintenance-driven switch from one line to the other constitutes a discrete mode change. In principle, the system’s continuous dynamics, such as the voltage at the generator’s terminal or the power delivered to the load, remain the same before and after the switching.

:
Illustrative example: Single generator-load bus with switching between two identical transmission lines.

Yet, such transitions often coexist with protection schemes and stochastic phenomena, including uncertainties in switching times and event triggers, which may lead to non-deterministic transitions and ambiguous mode definitions. Accordingly, in the following subsections, we discuss prominent hybrid system modeling approaches, including mixed logical dynamical (MLD) systems, hybrid automata (HA), switched systems, and set-oriented methods. For each, we summarize its mathematical foundations and suitability within power system applications.

Definition 2.

(Differential inclusion): A differential inclusion generalizes classical differential equations by allowing the derivative of the state to belong to a set of possible values rather than being uniquely determined.

This generalization to differential inclusions makes the framework particularly useful in converter-dominated power systems, where switching events, control constraints, and operational uncertainties often prevent the definition of a unique flow function. The flexibility to model system evolution within a set of admissible dynamics enables a more faithful representation of complex behaviors such as ambiguities in switching due to protection logic or hysteresis, nondeterministic transitions induced by software-based controls or communication delays, and uncertainties arising from converter responses, renewable intermittency, and load fluctuations.

In such a context, classical ordinary differential equation-based formulations may be inadequate to represent the full range of behaviors emerging from uncertain transitions and structural ambiguities. In contrast, differential inclusion frameworks enable the modeling of:

•

Uncertain or time-varying control responses, as observed in droop-based or virtual inertia schemes.

•

Non-deterministic state evolution resulting from ambiguous switching conditions or event-triggered logic.

•

Complex interactions among dynamic constraints, such as current and voltage limits, or state-of-charge bounds in energy storage systems.

For example,
[
22
]
formulates a hybrid system model of shipboard power systems with integrated energy storage and renewable generation, leveraging the flexibility of differential inclusions to examine switching transients and coordinated power flows. Similarly, the modeling and design of a cyber-physical power system
[
23
]
applies this framework, where continuous grid dynamics evolve alongside discrete dynamics from controllers, relays, and communication networks.

2.3
Switched systems modeling framework

A switched system is a particular case of a hybrid system characterized by a family of subsystems and a rule that governs switching among them. It can be expressed as:

\dot{x}(t)=f_{\sigma(t)}(x(t),u(t))

(9)

where
\sigma(t)\in\mathcal{Q}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
is the switching signal selecting the active mode at time
t
, and
f_{i}(\cdot)
defines the dynamics of each subsystem. Each mode may correspond to distinct converter controls, operating states (e.g., islanding or grid-following), or resource management logic, such as battery charging or generator dispatch.

A central challenge in such systems is understanding how frequent mode transitions—triggered by protection schemes, control logic, or renewable intermittency impact overall system stability. As shown in
[
36
,
37
]
, the properties of the switching signal, such as dwell time or mode sequences, critically influence the system’s behavior.

To account for all possible trajectories induced by switching events in a large network, (

]

\dot{x}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}f_{q}(z)\\
0\end{pmatrix}&x\in C\\
\begin{pmatrix}z\\
q^{+}\end{pmatrix}&x\in D\end{cases}

(10)

where
x=\begin{pmatrix}z\\
q\end{pmatrix}
, with

as the continuous state and

as the discrete mode. This is useful when dealing with a switched system as a subsystem in a large application modeled by (

Switched system models are particularly fitting for stability analysis in modern power systems, as explored in
[
38
]
. For example, a switched system approach enables structured analysis of how inverter control modes adapt to varying grid conditions or how backup sources take over during renewable dropouts
[
39
]
.

Beyond stability, switched systems support optimization-based coordination of distributed energy resources. In
[
40
]
, the economic dispatch problem in hybrid microgrids is modeled as an optimal control problem over switching dynamics, where the timing and sequencing of power source transitions (e.g., diesel generators, batteries, renewable units) are decision variables. To address the complexity of determining optimal switching instants, the authors employ a time-scaling transformation and introduce penalty functions to enforce minimum dwell-time constraints. This approach not only improves operational efficiency and reduces fuel costs but also mitigates excessive switching, which ultimately prolongs battery life and reduces mechanical stress on generators.

2.4
Mixed logical dynamical systems modeling framework

The MLD framework represents a control-centric subclass of hybrid systems, where continuous dynamics are augmented with logic-based constraints. It models a system behavior using linear equations that combine real-valued and binary variables, subject to linear inequality constraints. Following
[
24
]
, the general form is:

\displaystyle x^{+}

\displaystyle=A_{t}x+B_{1t}u+B_{2t}\delta+B_{3t}z

(11)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=C_{t}x+D_{1t}u+D_{2t}\delta+D_{3t}z

(12)

s.t.

\displaystyle E_{2t}\delta+E_{3t}z\leq E_{1t}u+E_{4t}x+E_{5t}

(13)

where
x
,

, and

respectively represent the state, input, and output vectors. The binary variables

z
includes auxiliary variables introduced during logic-to-inequality transformations.

This formulation enables logic-based decisions to be embedded directly into state-update equations. It thus provides a unified structure for casting control problems as mixed-integer optimization problems, which is especially advantageous when deployed with model predictive control (MPC). For example,
[
41
]
demonstrates its use for predictive energy management in plug-in hybrid electric vehicles, where mode-switching logic and power constraints are co-optimized to reduce fuel consumption. This logic-based optimization approach is also extended to residential and microgrid applications. For instance,
[
42
]
models residential energy systems (rooftop PV, battery storage, micro-CHP) using the MLD approach, replacing embedded on/off logic with linear constraints to enable flexible decision-making under operational and economic limits. The resulting MPC optimizes storage, generation, and power flows, thereby improving self-consumption, reducing costs, and enhancing resource utilization. In a related study,
[
43
]
proposes a DC microgrid formulation that incorporates renewables, storage, loads, and generators, utilizing the DC bus energy as a state variable and integrating continuous dynamics with logic constraints to mitigate voltage fluctuations and minimize unnecessary battery switching.

These examples demonstrate how the MLD framework supports scalable and optimization-ready modeling of complex systems. Its ability to represent both logical rules and continuous physics in a single formalism makes it particularly compelling for energy management strategies involving operational switching and constrained multi-energy coordination.

2.5
Model equivalence and other relevant frameworks

Other hybrid modeling formalisms, such as piecewise affine (PWA) systems and hybrid Petri nets (HPN), have also found application in power system contexts, including renewable energy integration
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
and microgrid coordination
[
47
]
. Some of these can be interpreted as particular cases or extensions of the core frameworks discussed earlier. For example, a PWA system can be viewed as a switched system where the dynamics transition between affine subsystems is based on the state’s position relative to a set of linear hyperplanes partitioning the state space
[
24
]
. This provides a flexible means of modeling mode-dependent behaviors or nonlinearities through linear approximations. The interested reader is referred to
[
48
,
49
]
for details of these models.

Interestingly, these models can be equivalent under certain conditions—such as well-posedness, boundedness of input, state, and output variables, as well as discretization and inequality approximations
[
50
]
. For example, any well-posed PWA system can be transformed into an MLD system and vice versa. Indeed,
[
50
]
demonstrated this using five models, although the author acknowledges that their approach makes mild assumptions.

Since choosing a modeling framework is case-dependent, model equivalence becomes significant, as it allows the transfer of analysis techniques, control design methods, and verification tools between different models. Practically, it means that one can choose the modeling framework that best suits the analysis or task at hand, knowing that the results will be valid across equivalent models.

It is also worth noting that there could be combinations of multiple modeling methods to tackle complex power systems problems. Furthermore, machine learning techniques have been incorporated, including neural-network-based HA
[
51
]
and probabilistic transition rules, as seen in stochastic HA
[
52
]
.

2.6
Model selection of HDS methods in power systems

System requirements, modeling objectives, and computational feasibility influence the choice of a hybrid modeling approach.

MLD models are particularly advantageous for control and optimization problems, making them suitable for systems where decision-making under constraints is crucial. Their compatibility with optimization algorithms, such as mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), enhances their tractability in complex control tasks
[
53
]
. A key drawback of this framework is its assumption of linear system dynamics and constraints, which may not always be applicable in power systems.

Conversely, switched systems and PWA models excel in capturing nonlinear dynamics and state-dependent switching behaviors. PWA models in particular can avoid mathematical artifacts such as Zeno behavior by enforcing piecewise affine transitions that prevent infinite switching in finite time
[
54
]
. This ensures physically meaningful transitions between control modes. However, the PWA model relies on the assumption that the system can be approximated by a set of affine dynamics over predefined regions, which can be challenging to achieve in large-scale power networks characterized by strong nonlinearities such as saturation limiters and deadbands. Power systems frequently violate the assumptions of piecewise linearity, making accurate PWA abstraction challenging. For this reason, the unabstracted system-level behavior is more faithfully represented using switched system models, which naturally subsume PWA as a special case but allow for broader nonlinear mode-dependent dynamics without requiring explicit affine partitioning.

HA are well-suited for modeling event-driven systems where discrete interactions significantly govern system evolution. Their formulation is especially intuitive when applied to systems with linear guard conditions and reset maps that define how state variables are updated upon entering a new mode. Similar to HA, HPN is effective in modeling systems where the timing and sequencing of events are critical—for instance, coordinating energy flow in a hybrid renewable energy system where solar, wind, and battery components must interact based on availability, demand, and priority constraints.

Set-oriented methods aim to generalize and unify multiple hybrid modeling paradigms within a single mathematical framework. They offer a high degree of abstraction and can encapsulate uncertainty and non-determinism.

2.6.1
Non-differentiable reset maps in model selection

Another important modeling consideration often overlooked when selecting a hybrid framework is the ability to express reset maps in a differentiable form, which is essential when requiring access to sensitivities—such as in the computation of saltation matrices for trajectory analysis
[
55
]
, or in hybrid extensions of Kalman filtering
[
56
]
and observer design. These tasks require access to the Jacobian of the reset function, or at least a consistent linearization of how state trajectories respond to discrete transitions.

Typical examples of non-differentiable resets include:

1.

Limiters
in inverter controllers, excitation systems, and governors, which immediately clip current, voltage, or control signals to prescribed minimum or maximum values.

2.

Breaker operations
, which instantaneously reset voltage or current states in response to protection triggers.

3.

Deadband controllers
that exhibit sudden state transitions once thresholds are crossed.

4.

State latching or memory elements
, which introduce discrete state changes based on internal logic.

5.

Anti-windup schemes
, where integral states in control loops are forcibly clamped or reset to prevent instability.

While most hybrid frameworks include some mechanism for updating the state during mode transitions, not all provide a reset map in a form that supports differentiation. In the MLD and PWA frameworks, such resets are typically encoded through logical constraints or affine switching conditions rather than expressed as explicit functions of the pre-transition state. While these models can replicate the observable effects of resets, they often do so in ways that obscure the underlying reset function or render it non-differentiable, posing challenges for tasks that rely on sensitivity analysis or gradient-based estimation.

In contrast, HA explicitly incorporates guard conditions and reset maps as part of their formal semantics, making them well-suited for capturing and analyzing non-differentiable transitions. Similarly to HA, the jump set of a set-oriented method can encode a differentiable or non-differentiable reset map. The switched system model can also accommodate such behavior if their mode transition logic is formulated to include clear state update rules.

2.7.1
Grid-connected inverter under mode switching

Consider a grid-connected inverter whose underlying dynamics switch between two fundamentally different operating modes: grid-following (GFL) and grid-forming (GFM)
[

]

hybrid model
with explicit mode switching and reset logic, or (ii) a

that smooths over switching events. Mode transitions are triggered by the measured grid voltage magnitude

\lvert V_{\text{grid}}\rvert

Grid-following inverters are a vital component of modern power systems, allowing the connection of renewable energy resources to the grid. Understanding their dynamic behavior is crucial for enhancing performance and ensuring stability, particularly as power systems evolve to accommodate increasing levels of renewable generation. The GFL inverters operate in a current-controlled mode, regulating the injected active and reactive power while synchronized to the grid voltage through a phase-locked loop. Here, the inverter is modeled in the synchronous

4.1
Model development

This section focuses on the formulation and development of HDS models for power system applications, including the integration of renewable energy, power plants, and transmission networks.

We argue that positive sequence (phasor domain) models are the most widely adopted for power system dynamics. These models take the form of DAEs and face several challenges. Discrete events cause abrupt changes in the algebraic constraints, which can alter the DAE index and lead to numerical instability during simulation. Similarly, estimation tasks are further complicated by the sensitivity of DAE-based models to the choice of measured variables, where unsuitable configurations may yield ill-conditioned or singular sensitivity matrices. Moreover, accurate initialization of algebraic states is essential as small errors in initial conditions can propagate through the coupled differential-algebraic structure, leading to divergence or inaccurate post-event trajectories. To overcome these limitations,
[
73
]
introduces a hybrid system model that combines discrete variables with continuous dynamics. Logical rules driven by continuous state inputs govern discrete events, and the resulting discrete-augmented DAE (DAD) framework incorporates continuous state variables, algebraic variables, and discrete variables, all of which interact within a parameter-dependent structure.

The DAD framework is developed to model power system phenomena driven by on-load tap changers, generator protections, and relay operations, where these discrete events have a significant impact on system behavior. The notation in this formalism can become complex, requiring compact representations and advanced computational techniques for large-scale power systems. Nevertheless, its hierarchical and object-oriented approach provides modularity and flexibility for managing large and complex networks.

Building on this,
[
74
]
extends the model by addressing some of its limitations, introducing the differential, switched algebraic, and state-reset (DSAR) modeling framework. The motivation is to simulate power system dynamics under large disturbances in a systematic manner. It refines the DAD framework by incorporating:

1.

Switched algebraic equations for state transitions.

2.

State-reset equations for discrete events.

In this approach, system behavior follows a differential-algebraic structure between discrete events. Events, such as fault clearances or load shedding, are triggered by changes in specific variables, leading to state resets. Each power system component is modeled autonomously, with algebraic equations establishing interconnections.

One key improvement in the DSAR formalism is symbolic differentiation, which facilitates the generation of partial derivatives necessary for implicit numerical integration. The modular design also allows for integration with different subsystems, such as renewable energy sources, where discrete switching actions and continuous power flow interactions must be considered. While the DSAR model improves upon the DAD model, challenges remain. Finding exact event times between integration steps and handling state resets still requires more advanced numerical techniques.

Following
[
73
,
74
]
, the work in
[
75
]
proposes the differential algebraic impulsive switched (DAIS) modeling framework, which addresses the limitations inherent in the DAD and DSAR frameworks, particularly the assumption of no discontinuities in the dynamic state variables. This approach considers impulsive actions and provides an improved representation of power system behavior during large disturbances.

The DAIS modeling framework uses differential equations to represent continuous dynamics, impulsive actions to model state resets triggered by discrete events, and switched algebraic equations to manage constraint switching. Using a similar case study to that in
[
73
,
74
]
of an on-load tap changer, it provides a more rigorous description of the sudden changes associated with tap position adjustments and dynamic constraint switching that occur during discrete events. Though the DAIS is a realization of the HA model, here it assumes constant state-space dimensions across events. This assumption simplifies the equations used in the model but does not fully represent the variability in power systems. Handling the switching and reset events within the DAIS model introduces additional complexity to the numerical simulations. Each event requires the system to reinitialize states and recompute interactions, which can be computationally intensive.

Reference
[
77
]
proposes the
hybrid input/output automata (HIOA)
framework for modeling power systems, extending the concept of HA by explicitly incorporating input and output interactions. This approach categorizes variables into input, internal, and output variables. A significant enhancement in the HIOA model is the composition of multiple HA, that is,

H_{1}\times H_{2}=(U_{1}\cup U_{2},X_{1}\cup X_{2},Y_{1}\cup Y_{2},\dots)

(31)

where
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are individual hybrid automata, and the union of their sets (such as inputs
U
, states
X
, and outputs
Y
) represents the combined system. This approach enables the modeling of more complex systems by integrating different subsystems, each represented by its hybrid automaton. Using this approach, the study simulates load variations and relay operations to assess the system’s response to disturbances. The model identifies key operating states—normal, alert, emergency,
in extremis
, and restorative
[
117
,
118
]
—and examines the role of load shedding in preventing cascading failures.

The formulation in
[
76
]
focuses on modeling the grid-side converter of a doubly fed induction generator (DFIG) using HA. Its primary objective is to represent the interaction between continuous electrical dynamics and discrete switching events that regulate power exchange between the turbine and the grid. Using space vector pulse width modulation as an illustrative case, the paper develops a hybrid system framework that integrates switching patterns with dynamic states to describe grid-side power control.

The modeling of a microgrid is approached in
[
82
]
by combining multiple models. The problem formulation centers on the need for a flexible model that can simulate the complex interactions between renewable energy sources, energy storage systems, and varying electrical load demands. By utilizing a discrete hybrid automata (DHA) to manage load switching and grid connection (discrete events), and a PWA system to handle the continuous dynamics of battery charging and discharging, a cohesive framework is presented. This resulting model is then transformed into an MLD for the possible optimization of costs.

An interesting application of HA to power systems, within the context of cyber-physical systems, is found in
[
84

4.1.1
Summary and potential research directions

The reviewed literature highlights progress in hybrid modeling for renewable systems and other power applications, marking a shift from continuous-time DAEs to more advanced approaches. Each development improves the representation of power system dynamics. However, modifications are often case-specific, as the choice of a hybrid model depends on the application. This flexibility can be advantageous, allowing for tailored solutions, but it also introduces challenges in standardization and implementation.

Many research directions in this area remain underexplored. For example, one issue across all methods is the computational burden associated with simulating hybrid systems. Handling state resets, determining exact event times between integration steps, and managing interactions between continuous and discrete states require advanced numerical techniques and computational tools. The possibility of state space explosion in large-scale power systems necessitates critical considerations on the subject of scalability and modularity. Power systems are vast and complex, and models need to be flexible enough to integrate various components and subsystems seamlessly.

4.2
State and parameter estimation

Given the inherent variability and uncertainty in renewable energy sources, meaningful modeling of HDS requires precise identification of state parameters. The study in
[
86
]
is the first to explore this concept in the context of the hybrid nature of power systems. The author presents a comprehensive theoretical framework for understanding and analyzing the identifiability of hybrid systems. Here,
identifiability
refers to the ability to uniquely determine the model parameters based on the observable outputs. The paper examines the conditions under which the model parameter estimation process might fail, depending on whether the model is locally identifiable. The author concludes that parameter identifiability is contingent upon the linear independence of the corresponding trajectory sensitivities of those parameters.

Ref.
[
87
]
presents one of the earliest applications of moving horizon estimation for fault detection in hybrid systems, using a steam generator as a benchmark. The system is modeled using the MLD framework, which captures both continuous dynamics and discrete switching behaviors such as sensor activation logic. The fault detection task is cast as a moving horizon estimation problem, where a cost function is optimized over a finite horizon to minimize deviations between predicted and measured sensor outputs, while enforcing consistency with the system’s evolution. To manage complexity, the following procedure is applied:

1.

Divide sensors into subsets.

2.

Solve each estimation problem as a mixed-integer quadratic program.

3.

Compute residuals between estimated and measured outputs.

4.

Identify faults when deviations become significant.

This method demonstrates the viability of real-time sensor fault detection using hybrid systems formalism as the base model.

An interesting model of a hydraulic wind power transfer system is addressed in
[
45
]
, where the challenge lies in capturing its strong nonlinearities caused by actuator hysteresis and external disturbances. To tackle this, PWA system identification is applied, as a single linear model is insufficient for representing the system’s behavior across different operating conditions. The study focuses on identifying system parameters and operating points to construct an efficient PWA model for improved state estimation and control. The case study demonstrates how hydraulic pumps transfer wind-generated energy to drive electric generators, validating the approach with over 91% accuracy against experimental results.

4.2.1
Summary and potential research directions

The identification problem in hybrid systems may include both model structure identification and parameter estimation, which can be challenging due to the interaction between continuous and discrete dynamics. In other words, the identification of hybrid systems involves determining unknown model parameters and identifying switching patterns to obtain a data-driven model. The complexity of these systems often leads to difficulties in classifying data points, determining the optimal number of submodels, and avoiding issues such as overfitting. The effectiveness of the hybrid model, especially within the context of state estimation, is sensitive to the accuracy of the model structure. If the hybrid model fails to represent the interactions between continuous and discrete states accurately, or if the logical relationships within the hybrid model’s formalism are not clearly defined, the resulting state estimates may be unreliable.

The pioneering work in

86
]
establishes a foundation for future research. However,

, their application in power systems remains limited. Moreover, most existing approaches focus solely on piecewise affine system identification.

We can therefore summarize several promising directions for future research. One avenue is the extension of identification methods to broader classes of hybrid systems. Since PWA systems are equivalent to multiple hybrid models, most studies on hybrid system identification have concentrated on them. However, the availability of diverse identification methods is particularly important in power systems, where it is not always practical to model every aspect purely from first principles. In such cases, data-driven approaches provide a valuable means of capturing system behaviour. Another direction lies in optimizing the MLD formalism and the moving horizon estimation framework to address the increasing scale and complexity of modern power systems. A key challenge noted in
[
87
]
is the trade-off between estimation horizon length and computational effort: longer horizons improve fault detection accuracy but significantly increase computation time. Additionally, decomposing the optimization problem into smaller sub-problems for each sensor output can inadvertently reduce observability, since each sub-problem isolates the perspective of a single sensor and neglects interactions across the system. Finally, the application of hybrid system identification methods to renewable energy sources connected to power grid represents a natural and impactful extension of current research trends.

4.3
Stability and control

A fundamental requirement in power systems is ensuring stability, particularly in response to faults or other disturbances, across different operating points. Most approaches rely on single-mode stability analysis, which is limited in representing the full complexity of hybrid systems where discrete events can significantly alter system dynamics. To address these challenges, several HDS approaches have been proposed.
