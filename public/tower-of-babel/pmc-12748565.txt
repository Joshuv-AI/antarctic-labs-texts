Mental health assessment model for college students based on facial expression recognition
Authors: Shaohong Chen, Chun Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the increasingly prominent mental health issues in universities, how to achieve rapid and objective identification of psychological states has become a research focus. The research proposes a mental health assessment model for college students that integrates facial expression recognition and deep learning technology, combining dynamic and static facial expression information to raise the accuracy and efficiency of recognition. This model is based on a dual branch structure, using a hierarchical sliding window Transformer with optical flow graph input to extract dynamic features, and MobileViT with convolution and attention fusion to extract static peak frame features. Cross branch feature interaction and fusion are achieved through a cross attention module. The findings denote that the accuracy of the proposed model reaches 0.945, the F1 value is 0.934, and the AUC value is as high as 0.963. At the same time, the average response time of the model is only 2.2 s, and the training time is 2.6 s, which has better computational efficiency while ensuring accuracy. The proposed method shows significant advantages in the accuracy of abnormal state recognition, training stability, and generalization ability. The research results indicate that the fusion model can serve as an effective supplement to the psychological health warning system in universities, providing intelligent technical support for emotion recognition driven psychological interventions.

Introduction

In recent years, with the increasing academic pressure, social relationships, and employment competition in universities, mental health problems among college students have been on the rise. Anxiety, depression, emotional disorders, and other phenomena have gradually become common, causing serious interference with learning efficiency and interpersonal relationships, and even triggering extreme events. It is urgent to establish an efficient and accurate psychological state recognition mechanism. However, traditional mental health assessment methods mainly rely on self-assessment scales and face-to-face interviews. Although they have a certain clinical basis, their results are highly dependent on the subjective expression of the subjects, and there are limitations such as evaluation lag, emotional masking, and response bias, which are difficult to meet the daily large-scale screening and real-time intervention needs of universities1,2. Facial expressions, as a direct external reflection of individual emotional changes, have the merits of high frequency, easy collection, and low interference, and have been broadly utilized in emotion recognition and human-computer interaction system design. However, microexpressions usually have characteristics such as short duration, weak intensity, and fast changes. Traditional image processing methods based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) are difficult to balance their spatial details and temporal evolution features, and their recognition accuracy and model generalization ability are greatly limited3. A mental health assessment model that integrates Transformer and Cross Attention Block (CAB) modules is developed, aiming to provide a technical foundation for psychological early warning in universities and promote the practical application of emotion recognition in the field of psychological intervention. The innovation of the research lies in the design of a dual-branch feature extraction (FE) structure that integrates optical flow information with static peak frames. Compared to previous single-stream or unimodal designs, this dual-path configuration explicitly addresses the limitations of traditional models in dealing with weak-intensity, fast-evolving micro-expressions. At the same time, recent studies in affective computing have highlighted the need to align facial expression recognition with interpretable psychological constructs-such as stress, anxiety, or emotional dysregulation-to enhance real-world utility. However, existing models often lack a standardized mapping between facial action units and mental health categories, limiting their application in educational or clinical contexts. This research contributes to filling this gap by embedding emotional state classification within a mental health assessment framework and validating the architecture on temporally annotated micro-expression datasets. The proposed framework not only improves technical performance but also paves the way for data-driven, non-invasive psychological monitoring solutions in university environments.

The contributions of this study can be summarized as follows: (1) A novel dual-branch cross-attention architecture is proposed, which integrates optical-flow–based dynamic features with MobileViT-based static features. Unlike previous unimodal or shallow fusion models, this framework captures both temporal micro-expression dynamics and fine-grained spatial details. (2) The model is explicitly framed within the context of psychological health assessment, mapping emotion categories to mental health states (normal, mild, moderate, severe). This establishes a unique bridge between facial expression recognition and mental health monitoring. (3) Through ablation and statistical testing, the study provides empirical evidence that micro-expressions can serve as reliable computational proxies for mental health assessment, thereby offering a scientific foundation for integrating affective computing into psychological intervention systems.

Existing psychological research has consistently shown that persistent patterns of emotional expressions-especially negative micro-expressions such as fear, sadness, and disgust-are highly correlated with underlying mental health conditions such as anxiety, depression, and emotional dysregulation. Therefore, by capturing these transient facial signals and aggregating them over time, it is feasible to infer psychological health levels (e.g., normal, mild, moderate, severe) with quantifiable confidence. The proposed model explicitly leverages this bridge between emotional expression and psychological condition by mapping predicted emotion distributions to psychological state categories under a supervised learning setting.

Related works

Many scholars have conducted research on facial expression recognition. Xie et al. proposed an algorithm based on cross layer contrastive learning and multi-scale attention fusion to address the issues of inconsistent learning intensity at different layers and insufficient shallow feature learning in CNN for facial expression recognition. The research results showed that the algorithm effectively enhanced shallow feature learning and refined semantic alignment, achieving excellent performance of 92.21% and 89.50% on datasets such as RAF-DB and FERPlus, respectively4. Naveen Kumar et al. developed a method to raise the classification accuracy of facial expression recognition systems by integrating improved local phase quantization and directional gradient histogram discrimination information, combined with multi-stage stacked deep convolutional autoencoders. The research results indicated that through two-stage FE based on overall and component-based approaches, this method achieved accuracies of 96.7%, 94.7%, and 93.5% on CK+, KDEF, and JAFFE datasets, respectively, significantly reducing misclassification rates and verifying the effectiveness of the model5. Xia et al. developed a multi-granularity and multi-scale feature fusion network to address the challenges of occlusion and pose changes in facial expression recognition in real-world scenarios. This network gradually mined local and global information by generating facial puzzles, and combined shallow multi-scale feature fusion strategies to capture subtle differences in facial expressions. The research findings indicated that the raised model performed superior on the field FER benchmark dataset, significantly outperforming existing advanced methods, verifying its robustness and effectiveness6.

Sathya et al. designed a new method of integrating convolutional recurrent neural networks to improve the accuracy of facial expression prediction, and constructed an adaptive neural fuzzy inference system as the ensemble layer. The research results showed that this method achieved an accuracy of 99.52%, F1 value of 99.35%, and AUC value of 0.95 on facial recognition and EMOTIC datasets, which is significantly better than existing methods and verifies its efficiency and superiority7. Gao et al. designed a biocompatible and capacitive dual-mode electromyography sensor to solve the problems of poor robustness and insufficient data in facial expression recognition using single-mode sensors. The performance was improved through a 3D stacking and dual coupling structure. The research results indicated that the proposed model had both good biocompatibility and antibacterial properties. The CNN-assisted recognition system constructed based on it significantly improved the accuracy of expression recognition, demonstrating the practical application potential of flexible sensor technology in expression monitoring8. Wang et al. proposed a student facial expression recognition method grounded on enhanced YOLOv5 to solve the problem of detecting cyberbullying in virtual classrooms in online education. The research results showed that by introducing coordinate attention module, adopting EIoU loss function and focus loss, the proposed model achieved high accuracy on multiple datasets, effectively improving the accuracy of the model and providing a new strategy for preventing cyberbullying9. Zhu et al. proposed a robust multimodal emotion recognition model based on diffusion and transformer in conversation context to address the problem of missing random modal data in multimodal emotion recognition in conversational environments. The research results showed that the model significantly improved the accuracy and robustness of emotion recognition when processing tasks on MELD and IEMOCAP datasets10. Wang proposed a lightweight road segmentation framework that combines knowledge generation and distillation to address the challenge of deploying high-performance segmentation models on resource constrained edge devices in generative AI. The research results show that the proposed method achieves an F1 measurement and average accuracy of 96.33% and 94.02% respectively on KITTI Road, with few parameters and fast inference, achieving an excellent balance between accuracy and efficiency11.

In summary, numerous scholars have carried out in-depth research on intelligent recognition of mental health status, and related models have made positive progress in facial expression classification and emotion recognition. Existing approaches primarily fall into three categories: (1) traditional machine learning models with handcrafted features, which struggle with generalization in complex scenes; (2) CNN-based deep models, which can effectively extract spatial features but often lack the capacity to model long-range temporal dependencies in micro-expressions; and (3) multimodal fusion models that integrate speech, EEG, or physiological signals, which, although more comprehensive, often face challenges in sensor synchronization, data alignment, and computational complexity. Recent developments also show growing interest in few-shot learning, self-supervised strategies, and lightweight architectures aimed at improving the adaptability of facial expression systems in real-world applications. Despite these advancements, limitations remain in terms of dynamic feature extraction, robustness under occlusion and noise, and the interpretability of results in clinical or educational settings. Therefore, developing a unified, efficient, and psychologically grounded framework that bridges fine-grained expression recognition with mental health semantics has become a critical research frontier.

Although these studies have advanced facial expression recognition, several limitations remain. Many CNN-based models excel at capturing local spatial features, but they often lack the ability to model long-range temporal dependencies, which are essential for detecting subtle micro-expressions. Transformer-based approaches improve global context modeling, yet most of them are designed for macro-expressions and do not adequately handle the weak intensity and short duration of micro-expressions. Furthermore, shallow fusion strategies frequently employed in prior work are insufficient for effectively combining heterogeneous feature types, such as static frames and dynamic motion cues. As a result, these methods may achieve competitive results on macro-expression benchmarks but struggle to generalize to fine-grained and transient micro-expression scenarios. The proposed model addresses these limitations through two innovations. First, the dual-branch architecture explicitly separates dynamic optical-flow features from static apex-frame features, ensuring that both temporal variation and peak spatial information are preserved. Second, the introduction of the CAB module enhances cross-branch interaction by allowing features from one branch to directly inform the representation learning of the other, thereby mitigating the risk of information loss that occurs with simple concatenation or late fusion. Together, these design choices strengthen the model’s ability to capture subtle and short-lived facial cues, leading to improved recognition accuracy, robustness, and generalization compared with previous approaches.

Transformer-based mental health assessment model

Construction of a psychological health assessment model based on multi-branch transformer

In the college population, identifying and intervening in psychological problems has always been an important task in mental health management. Traditional methods rely on paper and pen scales and face-to-face interviews. Although they have certain scientific validity, they have significant shortcomings in practical applications, such as long cycles, strong subjectivity, and difficulty in achieving real-time dynamic monitoring. Although traditional CNNs can extract local facial features, their performance in time-dependent modeling and long-range context awareness is limited. Therefore, a psychological health assessment model based on multi-branch Transformer is developed, which is a deep learning model based on self attention mechanism. The core idea is to capture the interrelationships between elements in the input sequence through self attention mechanisms, without relying on traditional recurrent neural networks or CNNs, thus greatly improving the efficiency of parallel computing and the ability to model long-distance dependencies. In the Transformer architecture, there are usually two parts: an encoder and a decoder. The encoder is responsible for converting input information into high-dimensional semantic representations, while the decoder generates output sequences based on these representations. Its structure is shown in Fig. 1.

As shown in Fig. 1, the network framework is divided into three stages: preprocessing, FE, and classification. In the preprocessing stage, the system first extracts the starting and peak frames of micro expressions from consecutive video frames, and calculates the optical flow map based on the changes between these two frames to capture subtle motion information. In the FE stage, the model designed two parallel FE paths: the first path inputs the optical flow graph into the Swin Transformer module, which adopts a hierarchical structure and sliding window mechanism to capture local spatial dependencies and cross regional contextual information. The second path directly inputs the peak frames into the MobileViT module, which combines the local modeling ability of CNNs with the global perception ability of Transformers to extract static facial features. The features extracted from the two branches are concatenated in the fusion layer and mapped to a unified feature space through a fully connected layer. Finally, the Softmax classifier outputs the class probability distribution of microexpressions12.

As shown in Fig. 3, Transformer utilizes a multi-layer stacked self attention mechanism to mine long-range dependencies between different regions in the image, thereby capturing global semantic information across regions. The output of the Transformer is remapped back to the original spatial size and fused with the original local features. This structure effectively preserves local texture information while introducing global context to strengthen the feature expression ability of the model in image recognition tasks.

Mental health assessment model based on CAB-transformer

Although the multi-branch Transformer-based mental health assessment model has significant advantages in FE and global modeling, it may suffer from insufficient capture of fine-grained features when dealing with complex facial expression changes, especially in the selection of facial region features that are susceptible to noise interference. Therefore, the study introduced the CAB module to improve it. CAB is an improved attention mechanism designed to enhance feature interactions between different branches. In traditional self-attention, features are weighted within the same channel, but the cross-branch dependencies are often overlooked. The CAB module addresses this by introducing bidirectional cross-attention between the global feature token and local patch features, thereby reinforcing complementary information. A more detailed schematic of the CAB data flow is presented in Fig. 4. In this diagram, arrows explicitly denote the direction of information exchange: Branch 1 (global token) serves as the query for Cross-Attention 1, while Branch 2 (patch features) serves as the query for Cross-Attention 2. Their outputs are subsequently fused through addition and normalization to form the final enhanced representation. The CAB employs 4 attention heads, with an embedding dimension of 256 and an internal feed-forward expansion ratio of 4. A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied to mitigate overfitting. These hyperparameters were selected based on validation performance and computational trade-offs. Its structure is shown in Fig. 4.

As shown in Fig. 5, the framework is divided into three main stages, namely preprocessing, FE, and classification. In the preprocessing stage, the starting and peak frames of microexpressions are first extracted from consecutive video frames, and the dynamic changes of facial muscles are captured using optical flow methods. After generating the optical flow map, it is input into the FE stage. The FE part includes two parallel paths: the first path inputs the optical flow graph into Swin Transformer, which extracts local and global features through self attention mechanism and generates feature representations containing global context. The second path inputs peak frames into MobileViT, which combines the advantages of CNNs and Transformers to extract static facial expression features and output corresponding feature blocks. The features of the two paths are fused through CAB, which enhances the interaction between different branch features and improves the model’s ability to focus on key information. Finally, the fused features are mapped through a fully connected layer, and the final micro expression category probability is output through a Softmax classifier to evaluate mental health. To achieve psychological state estimation, the model outputs were mapped from emotion class predictions to mental health levels. Based on prior clinical literature and empirical labeling of the SAMM dataset extensions, emotions such as joy and neutrality were treated as indicators of normal or mild states, while sadness, anger, fear, and disgust were associated with moderate or severe psychological distress. A scoring mechanism was applied, where emotion occurrence frequencies over observation windows were accumulated and normalized into a 4-level psychological state label. This transformation enables the facial expression model to serve not only as a classifier but also as a mental health assessment engine grounded in observable emotional patterns.

Unlike traditional facial expression recognition systems that focus solely on emotion classification, the proposed model explicitly frames expression recognition as a component of psychological assessment. The extracted features and classification outputs are interpreted not only as emotion categories (e.g., sadness, anger, happiness) but also as proxies for mental health states (e.g., normal, mild, moderate, severe). This mapping provides the necessary bridge between facial expression analysis and psychological health evaluation.

In the optical flow branch, intermediate feature tensors exhibited strong responses in dynamic facial regions such as the eyebrows, nasolabial folds, and periocular muscles-areas typically associated with stress-related micro-expressions. The static peak-frame branch focused on fine-grained spatial textures, especially at the mouth corners and upper cheeks. Notably, the cross-attention mechanism effectively bridged these two sources: global context vectors from the Swin Transformer stream modulated the attention weights over MobileViT feature patches, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to associate motion cues with static intensity peaks. This internal alignment between attention dynamics and psychological signal regions provides empirical support for the proposed dual-branch structure, even in the absence of direct visualizations, and enhances interpretability in practical applications such as emotion tracking and early warning of mental distress.

Performance study of mental health assessment model based on CAB-transformer

The experimental environment for the research was Windows 10 system, Core i7-3320 M, 8GB memory, NVIDIA Geforce GTX2080Ti GPU, 8GB video memory. The dataset used the publicly available SAMM Dataset, which contains 159 video clips of micro expressions expressed by 32-bit subjects in an unintentional state. The videos were collected at a high frame rate of 200 frames per second, and each video was accompanied by detailed action unit labeling and emotion category labels, such as anger, disgust, happiness, sadness, etc., while providing accurate labeling of the start frame, peak frame, and end frame. Following standard practice, the dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets in a subject-independent manner to avoid identity bias. 60% of the subjects were used for training, 20% for validation, and the remaining 20% for testing. Each split ensured balanced representation of different emotion categories to maintain fairness across subsets. The SAMM dataset was adopted due to its detailed temporal labeling and high frame rate, making it suitable for testing fine-grained micro-expression recognition models. However, the study acknowledge that SAMM was not originally designed for university-specific psychological assessment. Its use here is intended for methodological validation rather than direct scenario matching. To bridge this gap, future research will focus on collecting domain-specific datasets that capture psychological expressions of university students in authentic environments such as classrooms, dormitories, and examinations. To ensure subject-independent evaluation and avoid identity bias, we adopted the widely used leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation strategy. In this protocol, data from one subject is held out for testing, while the remaining subjects are used for training, and the process is repeated until all subjects have been tested. The final results are reported as the average performance across all folds. This evaluation approach is consistent with best practices in micro-expression recognition research and provides a more rigorous assessment of the proposed model’s generalization ability. The study selected standard Transformer and VGG16 as comparison models, and the results are shown in Fig. 6.

Discussion

The experimental findings demonstrate that the proposed CAB-Transformer consistently outperforms baseline models across accuracy, F1, and AUC measures. This improvement is largely attributable to the dual-branch design, where the optical-flow branch captures dynamic temporal information of micro-expressions and the MobileViT branch extracts static spatial features. The cross-attention module further enhances information exchange between the two branches, allowing the system to focus on subtle facial movements that are often difficult to capture using conventional single-stream networks. A key insight from the results is that combining dynamic and static cues leads to more reliable recognition of micro-expressions, which are typically short in duration and weak in intensity. The fusion of complementary feature types enhances both robustness and sensitivity, enabling the model to maintain high performance under subject-independent validation. This suggests that expression recognition systems can achieve greater accuracy when designed to integrate heterogeneous feature representations rather than relying on a single modality.From an application perspective, the study provides a pathway for linking facial expression recognition with psychological health assessment. By mapping expression categories to psychological states such as normal, mild, moderate, and severe, the model moves beyond technical classification and offers a framework for non-invasive mental health monitoring. This highlights the broader significance of the work, showing how deep learning techniques can be applied to sensitive and socially relevant domains. At the same time, several limitations must be acknowledged. The current experiments are limited to one dataset, which does not fully capture the diversity of expressions in real university scenarios. Although the use of LOSO cross-validation strengthens subject-independent testing, cross-dataset evaluation would provide stronger evidence of generalizability. In addition, the approach remains dependent on supervised learning with relatively limited annotated samples, which may restrict scalability. Future research should consider extending the framework with self-supervised learning or multimodal data sources such as speech, physiological signals, and contextual behaviors to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of psychological states.

In summary, the proposed CAB-Transformer not only advances technical performance in micro-expression recognition but also opens a conceptual avenue for applying such models to mental health assessment. This dual contribution underscores the potential of integrating computer vision with psychological evaluation, while also pointing to future directions for improving robustness, interpretability, and real-world applicability.

Conclusion

In response to the shortcomings of current mental health assessment methods in micro expression modeling and real-time performance, a multi-branch CAB-Transformer model that integrates dynamic and static features was developed to achieve high-precision and low latency recognition of college students’ psychological states. The experimental results showed that under the condition of 500 training samples, the CAB-Transformer achieved an accuracy of 0.945, with precision and recall rates of 0.938 and 0.931, respectively, and an F1 value of 0.934, all of which were superior to VGG16 and standard Transformer, with an accuracy improvement of about 5.8% and 7.3%, respectively. In terms of AUC value, CAB-Transformer reached 0.963, significantly higher than VGG16’s 0.915 and Transformer’s 0.901, indicating that the model had stronger discriminative ability for different psychological states. In terms of computational efficiency, the average training time of CAB-Transformer was 2.6 s, and the inference response time was 2.2 s, which was reduced by 1.1 s and 1.2 s respectively compared to Transformer. This indicated that the proposed model effectively controlled computational resource consumption while improving performance. In addition, in the identification of psychological abnormalities, CAB-Transformer showed significantly fewer misclassified samples in the TP and TN quadrants sample sets compared to the control model, demonstrating strong generalization ability and robustness. While the core technical contribution lies in the design of a dual-branch cross-attention expression recognition architecture, its significance extends to the domain of psychological health assessment by offering an interpretable and non-invasive mechanism for identifying abnormal emotional states among students. While the technical core lies in an improved recognition system, the scientific contribution of this work is twofold. First, it demonstrates that integrating cross-attention with dual-branch modeling significantly enhances robustness in micro-expression analysis, contributing to the broader body of knowledge on temporal-spatial feature fusion. Second, by validating the system in the context of psychological health assessment, it advances interdisciplinary understanding of how non-verbal cues can be computationally linked to mental health states. This positions the work not merely as an engineering solution, but as a step toward scientific grounding of AI-driven psychological assessment. However, the current model still has slight recognition bias in scenarios where the sample size of extreme emotional states is insufficient. Future research will combine multimodal data such as speech, physiological signals, and social behavior to further optimize the model structure, and expand it to campus psychological warning systems to achieve a more practical and valuable full process psychological state monitoring and intervention mechanism.

Although the research focuses on rapid psychological state recognition in university contexts, it should be noted that there is currently no widely available micro-expression dataset specifically collected from college students in natural university scenarios. To ensure reproducibility and methodological benchmarking, the study employed the publicly available SAMM dataset, which provides high-quality annotations for micro-expressions. While SAMM does not explicitly represent expressions captured in academic settings, it serves as a proxy for evaluating the effectiveness of the proposed CAB-Transformer framework. Future work will aim to establish or adapt datasets more representative of real-world university contexts to further validate the practical deployment of our model.
