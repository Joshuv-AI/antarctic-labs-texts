Influence of gravity variations on the activity of neuronal spheroids in an acoustic trap
Authors: Pierre-Ewen Lecoq, Guillaume Viraye, Chloé Dupuis, Xavier Benoit-Gonin, Jean-Luc Aider, Jean-Michel Peyrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong biological models, cell culture constitutes an important paradigm that allows rapid examination of cell phenotype and behavior. While cell cultures are classically grown on a 2D substrate, the recent development of organoid technologies represents a paradigmatic shift in biological experimentation as they pave the way for reconstructing of minimalist organs in 3D. Manipulating these 3D cell assemblies presents a considerable challenge. While there is growing interest in studying the behavior of cells and organs in the space environment, manipulating 3D cultures in microgravity remains a challenge. But with cellular research underway aboard the International Space Station (ISS), optimizing techniques for handling 3D cellular assemblies is essential. Here, in order to cultivate 3D models of spheroids in microgravity, we developed and used an acoustic bioreactor to trap levitating cellular organoids in a liquid cell culture medium. Indeed, in a Bulk Acoustic Wave (BAW) resonator, spherical objects, such as cells, can be maintained in an equilibrium position, inside a resonant cavity, away from the walls. In the acoustic trapping plane, gravity is counterbalanced by the acoustic radiation force (ARF) making it possible to maintain an object even in weightlessness. A dedicated setup was designed and built to perform live calcium imaging during parabolic flights. During a parabolic flight campaign, we were able to monitor the calcium activity of 3D neural networks trapped in an acoustic field during changes in gravity during different parabolas. Our results clearly demonstrate that variations in gravitational force correlate with changes in calcium activity.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Neuroscience, Neurology, Aerospace engineering, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Microgravity impairs physiology and health of the astronauts. When living on the International Space Station (ISS), many physiological changes are observed, such as bone density loss, muscle atrophy, cardiovascular and hemodynamic alterations, metabolic changes, endocrine disorders, and sleep irregularities. It also causes vestibular and ocular structural dysfunction induced by increased intracranial and intra-ocular pressure, leading to SANS (Spaceflight-Associated Neuroocular Syndrome)1,2. While these result from prolonged exposure to microgravity and body fluids reshuffle, most of the syndromes are reversible upon return to Earth. Yet, questions are raised on the consequences of longer stay and/or exposure to space radiations that may cause more prolonged deficits.

From a neurological standpoint, studies of astronauts have shown that spaceflight is associated with several short-term effects—such as space motion sickness, ventricular enlargement with increased intracranial pressure, vestibular dysfunction, and spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS). These findings have raised concerns about possible long-term consequences for the brain, including an increased risk of brain tumors, premature aging, and accelerated neurodegeneration3,4. Extensive fMRI analysis of the brain structure of astronauts brain has recently suggested that astronauts experience subtle but significant changes in the brain connectome5. These phenomena are either related to microgravity itself, via mechanical unloading, or to intracranial fluid redistribution and/or to space radiation.

With the growing interest in the space industry and preparation for long-term stays in the space environment, it is of paramount importance to understand the mechanisms underlying space-specific physiological adaptations and to determine whether or not the space environment triggers pathophysiological conditions.

Although physiological studies provide valuable insight into the broader effects of microgravity on organ systems, cellular studies can help elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving these changes. However, conducting cellular studies in microgravity poses significant challenges. Indeed, carrying out these experiments in space requires extensive planning, specialized equipment, and logistical coordination. Ground-based methods range from experiments simulating microgravity in the laboratory to specific environments creating transient phases of microgravity, such as in rockets, drop towers or parabolic flights. They offer shorter experiment durations, ranging from a few seconds (drop towers, parabolic flights) to a few minutes (rockets)6. Experiments on the International Space Station (ISS) or in automated space laboratories such as Cube Sats offer the possibility of longer-term studies but require the development of dedicated tools allowing the manipulation of living cells in a weightless environment7,8. Although cell biology experiments are regularly carried out in a constrained environment such as the ISS, they are limited due to technical difficulties. Most cellular studies rely on the use of a flat 2D environment, which allows observation by microscopy. However, the evaluation of biological processes in more complex models such as 3D organoíds cultures is limited to analysis on Earth, before and after the stay in space. Due to the lack of sedimentation that allows cell culture on Earth, manipulating objects, including biological models, in microgravity presents unique challenges and opportunities. Indeed, in the absence of gravity, objects float in the environment and can drift at the slightest contact, thus complicating precise positioning. Additionally, it is difficult to maintain the orientation and position of tools and objects without a stable reference point6,9.

To solve these problems, several methods have been proposed, from trapping cells in bioactive gels10 to magnetic labeling of living cells allowing trapping in a paramagnetic liquid cell culture medium11. Acoustic trapping is another technology that allows the manipulation of microscopic objects such as cells or particles without interactions with a substrate. It has already been used during parabolic flights to study the self-propulsion of acoustically levitating metal nanorods in microgravity12 and has repeatedly proven to be a useful tool for the handling and cultivation of biological systems, particularly spheroids13,14 or cyanobacteria15. Acoustic trapping relies on the creation of a stationary acoustic wave (ASW) inside a resonant cavity. Variations in acoustic pressure lead to the creation of an acoustic radiation force (ARF). The axial component of the ARF pushes all suspended objects toward the acoustic pressure nodes (minimum pressure regions) of the ASW. Objects can then be trapped in pressure nodes, also called acoustic trapping planes or acoustic levitation planes, where they can be held for as long as necessary in stable positions. The magnitude of the ARF depends on the acoustic wave properties (frequency, amplitude) as well of the properties of the objects and the surrounding environment (acoustic contrast). Objects can also be trapped radially by the transverse component of the ARF depending on the geometry of the cavity16,17 and of the shape and design of the acoustic source18.

It has been demonstrated that BAW resonators can be used to structure19,20, assemble21, stimulate22, levitate and culture mammalian cells23–25 or pattern in hydrogels26. They can also enable the self-organization of large cellular spheroids27–29, in a dynamic manner30–32 making acoustic levitation a powerful tool for tissue engineering33 and an interesting alternative for manipulating and growing cells in microgravity.

In this study, a dedicated experimental platform developed for previous parabolic flight campaigns by our team12,34 has been modified to host an acoustofluidic chip allowing the acoustic trapping of large cellular spheroids composed of active neurons. It allows the manipulation and observation of 3D neuronal networks (spheroids), which is a major difference with our previous study dedicated to 2D neuron networks grown in microfluidic chips. It has been validated by recording the neuronal activity of acoustically trapped neuronal spheroids during a parabolic flight organized by Novespace in Mérignac Airport (France). The efficient trapping of large active living objects in a microgravity environment is demonstrated, thereby enabling the study of microgravity-induced variations in neuronal activity of 3D neuronal spheroids. The experimental measurements indicate that the microgravity environment modifies the calcium activity of neuronal networks. They also show that acoustic trapping could prove to be a particularly interesting tool for all biological experiments in microgravity involving 3D cell cultures such as cellular spheroids or organoids.

Methods

Acoustic levitation

Performing microscopy imaging during parabolic flights presents unique challenges due to abrupt gravity changes and high noise and vibration generated by aircraft engines and aerodynamic fluid-structure interactions. These conditions can interfere with such sensitive imaging equipment and make the data collection difficult. In order to consider live imaging of active cellular objects such as neuronal spheroids in such a harsh environment, efficient and robust trapping of the living cellular objects to be imaged is required. In microgravity, while traditional methods of holding and manipulating delicate structures may fail, acoustic trapping could prove an well-fitted way to hold living objects in a stable position in a liquid culture medium, allowing precise observations and interventions without damaging the objects by mechanical contacts. To be used in such an environment, the acoustic radiation force should be able to counterbalance the weight of the object in hypergravity as well as to facilitate the handling in microgravity.

The first step consists of generating an Acoustic Standing Wave (ASW) inside a resonant cavity. This cavity consists of two opposite walls: one transmitting the acoustic wave emitted by a transducer placed outside the cavity, the other reflecting the acoustic wave (Fig. 1a). The resonance condition is satisfied if the cavity height h is a multiple of half the acoustic wavelength λac (h=nλac2). In this case, every particle in suspension in the fluid medium will be moved towards the acoustic pressure nodes by the axial component of the ARF, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Once the object has reached the acoustic pressure node (also called the acoustic levitation plane), it can be maintained in acoustic levitation as long as necessary.

The acoustic contrast factor depends on the density and compressibility differences between the particles and the medium. In general, the density is the critical parameter: if the particles are denser than the fluid (respectively less dense) then the acoustic contrast factor is positive (respectively negative) and the particles go to the closest acoustic pressure node (respectively to the closest pressure antinode).

It is important to emphasize that this expression of the ARF is valid for small homogeneous spheres. More refined models are needed to deal with large spheres37,38 or non-spherical objects17,39,40. It is noteworthy that the objects that will be handled in these experiments are spheroids which are not small compared to the acoustic wavelength. Indeed, the average diameter of a spheroid is ~300 μm, which is close to the half of the acoustic wavelength (λac/2 ≈ 375 μm for an acoustic frequency of λac = 2 MHz). Nevertheless, it has been shown in previous studies that it is possible to maintain large particles in acoustic levitation38,42 and even culture large spheroids in acoustic levitation for hours or even days28,29,31,43. Still, preliminary experiments were needed to ensure that it is possible to keep such large objects in acoustic levitation during both micro and hyper-gravity.

Acoustofluidic setup

In a previous study34 the influence of gravity variations on 2D neuronal networks was carried out in 2D microfluidic chips. In the present study, a dedicated acoustofluidic chip is used to trap large spheroids of neurons (3D neuronal network) in acoustic levitation. This involves setting up an experimental system dedicated to the emission of ultrasonic waves and the design of the acoustofluidic chip which consists of a PDMS (polydimethylsiloxane) body bonded to a microscope cover glass. The PDMS material was chosen because it is biocompatible and gas permeable, making it ideal for cell culture44. It is also transparent, allowing optical access, and is easy to process. A 2 MHz ultrasonic transducer is in direct contact with the PDMS body, closing the cylindrical cavity (Fig. 1). The microscope cover glass, facing the transducer, acts as an efficient acoustic reflector and allows optical access to visualize objects inside the resonant cavity using an inverted fluorescent microscope.

Two acoustofluidics chips and two different setups were used. The first is a 5 mm high multinode chip, which allows simple observation from the side and an easy tracking of the objects acoustically trapped in the acoustic pressure nodes. It will be used to validate the acoustic trapping of large objects even during gravity changes. The second is a 700 μm high single-node chip. It allows an easy observation from under the chip and is then well adapted to fluorescence microscopy with an inverted microscope.

The microchips were composed of a PDMS (Polydimethylsiloxane) layer bonded on top of a microscope cover-glass and further connected to a mono-element ultrasonic transducer.

The design of the microfluidic chips was coded on a brass mold generated with a micro-milling machine. To fabricate the chips, a mixture of PDMS elastomer and curing agent in a ratio of 9:1 was degassed under vacuum to remove air bubbles. The PDMS mixture was then poured onto a mold and baked for 4 h at 70 °C. The PDMS microfluidic chips were peeled off from the mold, and the PDMS surface was cleaned with adhesive tape. The PDMS buffers were then stuck on glass slides, previously cleaned with isopropanol as well as using a plasma cleaner (power 100%, 0.6 mBar in O2, Diener Electronic). The chamber was immediately filled with distilled water maintaining the hydrophilicity of glass and PDMS acquired during plasma treatment. Finally, the microfluidic chips were sterilized under UV for 30 min.

The geometry of the PDMS chip is designed to allow insertion of the ultrasonic transducer on top of the PDMS body. The transducer closes the acoustofluidic cavity. One side of the resonant cavity is then the ultrasound emitter, while, on the other side, the microscope cover glass acts as an effective acoustic reflector while allowing an optimal optical access. Ultrasound waves were generated by an ultrasonic transducer (SignalProcessing™) driven by an USB compact arbitrary waveform generator (Handyscope HS5, Tiepie Engineering™) monitored by a computer. A sinusoidal signal of amplitude 5Vpp and frequency 2 MHz is applied to the ultrasonic transducers. The acoustic wave parameters were chosen to optimize the trapping process while avoiding unwanted phenomena such as acoustic streaming or excessive heating.

Two characteristics are important to explain the robustness of the acoustofluidic chips. First, the use of a broadband ultrasonic transducer (operating around 2 MHz +/− 10%) allows for simple and efficient frequency modification to ensure a good acoustic trapping. Second, the multi-layering of the chip45, which, coupled with a broadband source, allows for the displacement of the acoustic levitation planes just by changing the acoustic frequency, as demonstrated in Dron et al.30 for single node chips and in Jeger-Madiot et al.31 for multinodes chips. Morevoer, working with a low voltage limits the heating of the fluid by the transducer to 1 °C, which is compensated by a reduction of the temperature of the incubator. It also avoids the acoustic streaming.

Cell culture of Hippocampal spheroids

Hippocampal neuronal cultures were generated from E18 SWISS mouse embryos by building upon standard cell-culture techniques, as follows. Animal handling was performed according to the U.S. National Institutes of Health publication no. 85-24, revised 1985, and European Committee Guidelines on the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. SWISS pregnant mice were purchased from Janvier (Le Genest Saint Isle, France). Animal care was conducted in accordance with standard ethical guidelines. The cell culture preparation was done in the Neurocentre Magendie laboratory in Bordeaux. E18 regnant mice were deeply anesthetized with Isoflurane and killed by cervical dislocation. E18 Embryons were removed and Hippocampi were micro-dissected from embryoes heads with all steps performed in cold Gey’s Balanced Saline Solution (Sigma G9779). Dissected structures were digested with papaín (50 U/mL, Sigma 76220) in DMEM (Thermofisher 31966 021). After papaín inactivation with FBS (100 U/mL, GE Healthcare), structures were mechanically dissociated with a pipette (plastic tip 1 mm diameter) in presence of DNAse type IV (20U/mL, Sigma D5025). Cells viability was determined by Trypan Blue exclusion assay. The resulting cells were centrifuged 7 min at 800 rpm and the pellet was resuspended in the culture medium containing DMEM, 10% FBS, 2% B27, 0.5 mM Glutamate and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco®) at a concentration of 20 million cells/mL. Cells were seeded in microwells (30,000 cells per well) of U-shaped low adhesion plates (Nunclon Sphera, Thermofischer). All cell cultures were kept in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Spheroids were cultured 14 days before transfering to the Novespace biological facility laboratory for the 3 days of the flight campaign. The typical diameter of the spheroids obtained was around 200 μm, which is a good compromise to have a large enough spheroid and avoid to grow a necrotic core.

Calcium imaging

The calcium dye Fluo4-AM was obtained from ThermoFisher Scientific® (ref F14201). It was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in a ratio of 1 μg/μL to obtain a stock solution of 1 mM. This stock solution was frozen in 2 μL aliquots. Ca2+ free wash buffer was made according to previous protocols46 and the stock solution of Fluo4-AM was added in order to load the cells with a final concentration of ~2 μM. Before the flights or before classical ground experiments, the spheroids were chosen, loaded onto a microfluidic chip with Fluo 4 AM and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 25 min. Just before the flights, all spheroids were then placed in culture medium (Brainphys, Stemcell Technologies) containing 50 μM bicuculline (Sigma-Aldrich ref 505875) to normalize the spontaneous activity of the spheroids and decrease the number of spontaneously inactive spheroids (~25%). For the 4-AP treatments, spheroids were similarly treated with 50 μM of 4-AP in bicuculline containing medium.

Finally, after sealing the inlet and outlet reservoirs with a PDMS membrane to mitigate fluid movement during flight, the chips were transported in a closed box (to protect the cells from light as well as temperature variations) On board the plane. The equipment is described in the results section below. Observation of the spheroids were made with an exposure time of 20 ms every 50 ms. The activity recorded was purely spontaneous for all measurements. For analysis, the signal is normalized using DF/F=F(t+δt)−F(t)Fm where F(t) is the spheroid intensity for a given time and Fm the average value of the intensity of the field of vision. From this normalized signal, only values exceeding 2 times the standard deviation are counted as bursts. Average periods of firing (i.e. the time between two distinct bursts) were extracted. Bursting is a phenomenon observed in neurons and is characterized by a brief increase in signal followed by a longer return to the initial point. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for statistical analysis. All analyzes and plots were generated using Python routines.

Experimental platform inside the AirZero-G plane

As mentioned previously, each parabola consists in a microgravity (0g) phase, preceded and followed by an hypergravity (1.8g) phase, with an accuracy of ±0.1g. This very specific environment necessitate to design a robust and practical experimental setup rescpecting all the security constraints imposed by Novespace. To perform calcium imaging of live cells spheroids in acoustic levitation in the Airbus AIR ZERO-G aircraft, the experimental platform developed for previous parabolic flights campaigns was used12,34. It was adapted to calcium imaging of 2D neuronal networks grown in microfluidic chip, without any acoustics34. The main new constraints for the setup were related to the manipulation and observation of live spheroids in acoustic levitation by fluorescence microscopy in a very confined environment. It meant adding an acoustic emission chain, from the signal generator to the acoustic transducer on top of the acoustofluidic chip. The rest of the platform was identical to the one used in Lecoq et al.34. Briefly, the platform is composed of three main modules. The first module is a monitoring rack designed to allow the remote control of every key experimental parameters as well as the data acquisition with two computers, each one recording images from a different experiment. The second module is the experimental setup (Fig. 2) which was designed to fit in a sealed Zarges box, for safety reasons. Acoustically levitated particles and spheroids were studied using a fully automated Zeiss Axio Observer inverted microscope equipped with a stage incubator (Tokai Hit™), a LED source (Coolled pE 4000) and a Hamamatsu Orca Fusion CMOS camera. The snapshots were streamed in real-time from the camera to a workstation through a CoaxPress card.

Fixation, immunostaining, and cryosections

Spheroids were fixed in PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Science 15714S) and 4% sucrose (Sigma S9378) for 30 min. Spheroids were permeabilized for 5 min with 0.25% Triton (Sigma X100) and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Thermofisher 15561020) in PBS and washed with 10% BSA in PBS for one hour. Primary antibodies were added in PBS with 5% BSA and the samples incubated at 4 °C overnight. Samples were then rinsed three times for 5 min with PBS and further incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies in PBS with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. After three washing with PBS, slides were mounted using Mowiol (475904-M, Sigma) and stored at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: DAPI (1050-A, Euromedex, 1/20,000), MAP2 (M4403, Sigma, 1/500), GFAP, B3tubulin. Species-specific secondary antibodies coupled to Alexa 350, 488, 555 or 633 were used (1/1000, Invitrogen) to visualize bound primary antibodies. In order to be sliced, the spheroids were put in 15% sucrose for one hour and 30% sucrose for 12 h minimum. Later, They were frozen at −30 °C and stored at −80 °C. A Cryostat (Leica Biosystems) was then used to cut them in thin slices of 25 μm, en then placed on microscopic glass slides for later imaging.

Results

Acoustic trapping of large particles

The robustness of acoustic trapping was first tested using large beads of diameter dp = 300 μm, close to the dimension of a cell spheroid. The multi-node acoustofluidic chip was used with an acoustic frequency of 2 MHz and a voltage amplitude of 5 V. To study the motion of large scale objects during the parabola, the displacements in the X/Z plane (vertical plane, aligned with the axis of propagation of acoustic waves) of 10 beads trapped in ten different pressure nodes were recorded in the reference frame of the plane.

A typical result is shown in Fig. 3. The Fig. 3A shows the full trajectory of a bead during gravity changes and the evolution of the corresponding X and Z position as a function of time. The first observation is that the displacements along the Z direction are ten times smaller than along the X direction. This result is confirmed when looking at the averaged maximal displacements for ten different particles shown in Fig. 3B. The maximum displacement along the X axis is at most a third of the diameter of the bead while it is less than 3% of the bead diameter for the Z displacement. This result can be explained by the fact that the axial component of the ARF is indeed one order of magnitude larger than the transverse component, as mentioned in a previous section. It confirms that the acoustic trapping is indeed strong enough to maintain large and dense objects in acoustic trapping even during the hyper-gravity phase.

These results confirm that the acoustofluidic chip can indeed be used to levitate and trap even large cell spheroids and should subsequently enable live recording of fluorescence microscopy of living cell spheroids.

Monitoring calcium activity of spheroids

Calcium imaging is a valuable technique for studying the influence of gravity variations on a neural network during parabolic flights. This method is particularly useful because it can capture rapid changes in neuronal activity, typically in ten seconds or less, making it ideal for the brief gravity phases of these flights. Calcium imaging tracks fluorescence fluctuations of many neurons simultaneously, with typical signal frequencies around a few Hertz47. It also allows live video recording for later offline analysis. For this parabolic flight campaign, the effect of microgravity was studied on neuronal spheroids derived from a hippocampal neural progenitor grown under differentiating conditions in non-adherent cell culture plates. This neuronal progenitors spontaneously self-organize into spheres containing ~70% of hippocampal neurons among which 25% are GABAergic neurons, and 30% of astrocytes (Fig. 4). The typical diameter of a spheroid is about 200 μm. These 3D networks were selected because they allow the observation of spontaneous and synchronized calcium transients, providing information on the overall behavior of the spheroids without requiring external stimulation.

It should be noted that low-intensity ultrasounds (LIU) are known to modulate neuronal excitability through the activation of ionic mechanoreceptors48,49, a process that depends on ARF amplitude. Even though our acoustic parameters lead to an acoustic pressure in the range of 0.3 Mpa, insufficient to trigger direct action potential firing, it remains conceivable that membrane potential is slightly increased by this pressure variation50. Therefore common molecular signals could theoretically be involved in an alteration from both gravity and ultrasound, bringing out effects on cell behavior.

Influence of gravity changes on calcium activity of spheroids in acoustic trapping during parabolic flights

The activity of the spheroids was recorded during the three gravity phases (standard gravity, hypergravity and microgravity). The bursting period was extracted for all gravity phases during 20 parabolas. The mean values and the distribution of the activity periods are plotted as violins in Fig. 6. It varies from 5.49 ± 3.51 s in 1g to 4.75 ± 3.34 s in 1.8g and 3.16 ± 2.56 s in microgravity. The first observation is that hypergravity does not induce a significant variation of the bursting period, only a small reduction of the mean period. On the contrary, microgravity causes a significant decrease in the mean bursting period. Moreover, the dispersion of the bursting periods is also reduced.

These first experiments demonstrate that acoustic trapping allows efficient trapping and rapid imaging of live neuronal spheroids during a 3-h parabolic flight. Our results clearly show that mature neuronal networks respond specifically to microgravity by accelerating their internal rhythms.

Changes in neuronal activity due to microgravity has been postulated to be caused by modification of membrane viscosity and/or excitability51. We therefore wondered whether increasing neuronal spheroids excitability through 4-Aminopyridine treatment, a non-selective voltage-dependent Kv+ channel blocker, would modify microgravity induced change in activity.

In a second set of experiments, 15 DIV hippocampal spheroids treated with 50 μM 4-aminopyridine (4-AP) were studied. As shown in Fig. 7, hippocampal spheroids exhibited faster bursting activity during the 1g phase compared to untreated spheroids. This is an expected result since it has been previously described that 4-AP significantly increases the bursting activity of 3D networks52.

In comparison with the untreated spheroids (Fig. 6), the hypergravity phase modifies the bursting period of the 4-AP-treated spheroids compared to the 1g phase (Fig. 7). Strikingly, the microgravity phases led to a specific and dramatic decrease in the bursting period of the spheroids. We also observed a significant modification of the envelope of the violin distribution. Indeed, the activity at 1g is slower than at 0g, where it seems to reach a plateau (maximum frequency around 10 Hz). The activity at 1.8g seems to be bimodal, with both average response times of 1g and 0g.

It has been shown that neural network oscillations occur through the fine-tuning of the excitatory/inhibitory balance, a process that occurs through a percolation scenario53. The result is that, at the time of sampling, some spheroids are spontaneously active while others are not (a phenomenon observed in both untreated and treated spheroids). Strikingly, in the latter cases, the spheroids woke up only during the microgravity phase. Two typical time series of calcium activity variations during the three phases of gravity are shown in Fig. 7 where the first one represents an always active spheroid while the second only bursts in microgravity.

Taken together, these data suggest that acoustically trapped 3D neuronal spheroids exhibit decreased bursting periods upon microgravity phase. Change in membrane excitability through 4-AP pretreatment further enhances microgravity effects.

Discussion

With advances in deep—space exploration technologies and the growing number of manned missions, space biotechnologies are becoming increasingly critical—both for developing sustainable, efficient strategies to improve astronauts’ well-being during long-duration flights and for enabling novel technologies that cannot be produced on Earth54,55. In this context, techniques for manipulating living cells, such as acoustic trapping, open the possibility of studying the effects of spaceflight conditions on biological samples that are otherwise difficult to investigate in microgravity. We have therefore designed and validated an experimental platform capable of capturing and levitating 3-D live structures-akin to organoids-using acoustic tweezers, thereby permitting real-time dynamic imaging of rapid biological responses. Below we outline the key motivations and potential advantages of employing acoustic trapping for space bioproduction.

Acoustic trapping is based on the creation of a standing wave pattern leading to an acoustic radiation pressure field which, without contact, acts on all objects suspended in a fluid medium. The acoustic forces act on particles by grouping and trapping them in disk-shaped layers17,56, located at the different pressure nodes of a resonant cylindrical cavity36. It has already been used to form and culture cell spheroids of MSCs (Mesenchymatous Stromal Cells) or hepatocytes for several days28,29. Once acoustically levitated, objects (cell sheets, spheroids) can be further manipulated by variations in the magnitude of the force or by changing the distance between the acoustic pressure nodes30,31,57,58. Acoustic trapping has been shown to maintain floating spheroids in a scaffold-free environment with precise control of axial position, enabling long-term, fast, inverted, fluorescent imaging of active neural spheroids. Significant, but limited, lateral movements of the spheroids were nevertheless observed during parabolic flights. These are caused by the acceleration of the X axis of the plane during the execution of the parabolas, and by the fact that the transverse ARF is much smaller than the axial component of the ARF. However, this did not prevent calcium analysis of the spheroids as tracking algorithms can easily cope with that issue.

It is important to note that drift in the trapping plane is limited to the specific conditions of parabolic flights during which hypergravity phases alternate with microgravity phases and is not supposed to occurs in constant microgravity environments such as the one observed in ISS. Importantly, the trapping efficiency can be easily controlled by changing the ARF amplitude. While this can mitigate the drift of objects in the trapping plane during parabolic flights, it could also be an interesting tool to modify the forces around levitating spheroids, and/or implement dynamic control of mechanical forces in cell culture experiments. In the present study, a simple, low-power, broadband piezoelectric transducer was used. Piezoelectric transducer arrays could be implemented in multiple directions, allowing for even more precise control of cells/spheroids in a 3D environment59.

As the manipulation of 3D cellular spheroids and organoids in weightlessness becomes a necessity60, the present study demonstrates that acoustic waves allow a very efficient trapping of large living objects (hundreds of μm) as they have already been used to trap nanorods in microgravity12. Only a few experimental methods permit manipulating 3D cellular objects in microgravity while allowing for their observation. Besides hydrogel trapping and/or microfluidic constraint, magnetic trapping has been proposed as an interesting alternative since it allows for moving cells in all 3 directions11. However, magnetic trapping requires the magnetization of cells by labeling them with nanoparticles and the use of paramagnetic cell culture media, a process that can be problematic for long-term studies61,62. In this context, acoustic trapping appears to be a promising alternative strategy, as it requires no cell labeling, no modification of the culture medium, and no mechanical contact.

Neuronal connectivity and brain dynamics are highly complex and interdependent processes, requiring detailed investigations to elucidate how microgravity and space conditions alter the functions of neural networks. This understanding is essential for addressing the neurological effects of spaceflight. Using these 3-D cellular spheroid/organoid handling platforms enabled the study of neuronal spheroids that mimic brain activity. At the cellular level, microgravity has consistently been shown to directly affect cells, inducing rapid cytoskeletal remodeling and changes in membrane physical properties; however, only a limited number of experiments have addressed these phenomena to date63–66.

In this study, a decrease in the period of spontaneous activity of neuronal oscillations was observed during variations in gravity. The time between spikes is specifically reduced in microgravity compared to 1g and 1.8g. These results apparently contrast with a previous study which showed a tendency to decrease the firing of hippocampal neurons cultured in 2D networks during the 0g and 1.8g phases34. Yet, the experiment was designed to study the impact of altered gravity on single, unconnected, immature neurons. It showed that only a subset of cells showed a reduction in their firing rate, while a separate silent population began to burst, providing evidence that the effect of microgravity on the neuronal subpopulation could be twofold. Here, we leveraged the use of neuronal spheroids that have specific advantages over immature brain organoids. Indeed, neuronal spheroids were fully mature and exhibited highly coordinated network activity, a result of their intrinsic excitatory/inhibitory balance67.

Furthermore, the presented results are consistent with other experiments monitoring the activity of mature human iPSC-derived neurons using microelectrode array (MEA) recording platforms; these studies reported a slight increase in network bursting frequency during the 0g and 1.8g phases in a drop-tower configuration68. These results suggest that microgravity can exert subtle effects on individual cells, leading entire networks to adapt toward hyperactivity. The data show that promoting network hyperexcitability with a Kv+ channel blocker dramatically increases rapid bursting events, especially during the 0g phases, consistent with the possibility that microgravity may affect membrane polarization. Although the exact molecular mediators remain unclear, one plausible explanation is that microgravity-induced changes in membrane tension and/or fluidity limit the activation of ion channels in neuronal membranes-a phenomenon uncovered by pharmacological hyperexcitability assays. Indeed, membrane deformation can alter the function of mechanoreceptors, some of which contribute to membrane polarization69.

Indeed, it is known that a single mechanical perturbation in the pico-Newton range can elicit a local and transient calcium response in cells70. It is tempting to speculate that microgravity may induce subthreshold activation of mechanosensitive ion channels, which-together with 4-AP—produces rapid depolarization. An alternative, though not exclusive, hypothesis is that the microgravity phase alters either presynaptic glutamate release probability or postsynaptic glutamatergic receptor density. Although this has yet to be formally tested in true microgravity, artificial gravity studies have revealed changes in glutamatergic pathways71–73. It should also be noted that untreated spheroids that were inactive at baseline (1g) were excluded from quantification, whereas inactive spheroids were retained in the 4-AP-treated group due to the limited number of recordings. This difference in inclusion criteria may influence the interpretation of comparisons between groups. Finally, since the spheroids were recorded under acoustic trapping, ultrasound may partially contribute to the observed effects. Low-intensity ultrasound is known to modulate neuronal excitability via activation of mechanosensitive ion channels-a process that depends on the ARF amplitude48,49. Although our acoustic parameters generate an ARF field in the range of 0.3 MPa-insufficient to elicit direct action potentials-it remains conceivable that this paradigm slightly depolarizes the membrane potential50. Consequently, shared molecular pathways may be perturbed by both changes in gravity and ultrasound, thereby modulating cellular behavior74. The inherently constrained setting of parabolic flight makes it difficult to tease apart the exact molecular and cellular mechanisms that drive the subtle impact of microgravity on neuronal membrane excitability and the subsequent self-adaptation of networks. Addressing this challenge will require higher-throughput experimental approaches.

With a growing need to investigate how the space environment affects human physiology—and to develop new strategies for ensuring survival in space, including bioproduction—manipulating complex cellular systems in microgravity has become increasingly important. Experimental tools are therefore required to manipulate and interrogate 3-D structures such as organoids75. Acoustic approaches, owing to their excellent biocompatibility and versatility, hold promise for contactless, dynamic manipulation of living cells both on Earth and in space28,29,76. By providing controlled constraints and mechanical trapping, acoustic levitation enables efficient confinement and precise observation of 3-D cellular objects in a weightless environment, yielding valuable data for space biology experiments conducted aboard platforms such as the International Space Station (ISS)11. Fine-tuning parameters that govern the acoustic radiation force—such as frequency—or employing transducer arrays to shape the spatial distribution of the field allow the fabrication and manipulation of complex 3-D cellular assemblies77,78. This technique can address several unique challenges associated with cell culture, bioproduction, and tissue engineering in microgravity and other harsh extraterrestrial environments.
