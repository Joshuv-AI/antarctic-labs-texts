A Systematic Review of Recent Developments in Platelet-Rich Plasma Therapy in Hip Osteoarthritis: Variability in Protocols and Clinical Outcomes (2019–2024)
Authors: Emily Awdish, Marlena Muszynska, Konrad Lautenschlager, Ali Hamade, Matthew Caid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHip osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, progressive degenerative joint disorder associated with significant morbidity and limited non-surgical treatment options. Over the past 20 years, Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has garnered increasing interest as a conservative intervention in the setting of OA; however, its clinical efficacy in hip OA remains inconclusive due to substantial heterogeneity in preparation protocols and administration methods. This systematic review aims to examine randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published between 2019 and 2024 that evaluated the efficacy of PRP in the management of hip OA, with specific criteria including the use of the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and/or the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for outcome measurement. Three RCTs met the eligibility criteria. While short-term improvements in pain and function were observed in patients with mild to moderate OA, long-term benefits were inconsistent. Considerable variability in PRP composition (leukocyte-rich versus leukocyte-poor), activation techniques, injection schedules, and follow-up durations was evident across studies. Particularly, one trial demonstrated no significant advantage of PRP over placebo. Methodological limitations, including lack of blinding, underreporting of PRP characteristics, and protocol heterogeneity, impeded the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Although PRP may provide short-term symptomatic relief in early-stage hip OA, the absence of standardized protocols highlights the need for rigorous, long-term randomized controlled trials that employ consistent classification systems (eg, PAW or MARSPILL) and stratified patient populations to inform evidence-based clinical guidelines.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) of the hip is a common condition that has long been recognized for its deleterious effects on patients and the healthcare system.1,2 At the tissue level, hip OA is understood as a whole-joint disease.3 The initial mechanical wear and tear destroy articular cartilage, triggering an imbalance between breakdown and repair facilitated by chondrocytes, followed by subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, synovial inflammation, and eventual joint deformity and pain.4 Despite advances in surgical treatments, various factors still create barriers for patients and cause them to not be suitable candidates for surgery. Additionally, some patients may have early-stage disease that does not warrant surgery, or anatomical variations that exclude them from surgery. As a condition characterized by chronic pain, as well as reduced quality of life and mobility, it has been observed that it is a leading cause of disability amongst the older population.1,2 The Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) scale, a widely used radiographic grading system, is commonly applied to classify the severity of OA. The KL scale ranges from Grade 0 (no radiographic signs) to Grade 4 (severe joint space narrowing, large osteophytes, and bone deformity). This standardized system which helps clinicians and researchers stratify disease progression and guide treatment decisions.5 With more patients plagued by the limitations of hip OA, there is a growing need for novel therapies that minimize symptoms and address the underlying pathology of the disease.

One such therapy may be platelet-rich plasma (PRP). PRP is an autologous concentrate of platelets; when injected into a joint, it releases growth factors implicated with proliferative, angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory capabilities from within its alpha-granules. These include Platelet-Derived Growth Factor (PDGF), Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β), Insulin-like Growth Factor (IGF-1), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF), and Basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF), which work synergistically to modulate inflammation, stimulate chondrocyte proliferation, and enhance extracellular matrix production.6 It attenuates joint inflammation by suppressing catabolic mediators (eg, interleukin-1β (IL‑1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)).6 It promotes a shift toward an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype, thereby allowing for the restoration of homeostasis within the joint.7 Some evidence suggests that many favorable outcomes can be achieved after PRP joint injections, including reduced pain, decreased inflammation, and the potential for delayed joint arthroplasty in patients with hip OA.4,5 Despite these promising results in the early phases, significant gaps exist regarding the optimal application of PRP. This underscores the need for standardized protocols to achieve a uniform outcome and optimal success for patients. There is a need for robust comparative studies that utilize standard outcome measures such as the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS).

The WOMAC is a patient-reported outcome measure used to assess pain, stiffness, and physical function in individuals with OA of the knee or hip.8 WOMAC is frequently utilized in clinical trials and observational studies to evaluate the effectiveness of treatments, such as PRP injections. The VAS is another commonly used tool that measures subjective pain intensity.9 It consists of a 10 cm horizontal line anchored by “No Pain” at one end and “Worst Pain Imaginable” at the other. Patients are asked to indicate their current pain level by marking a point along the line, which is then measured in millimeters from the left anchor. This value can then be expressed on a simplified 0–10 numerical scale. Additionally, to aid understanding, severity can be paired with facial expressions and colors, ranging from a green smiling face (minimal symptoms) to light green (mild pain), yellow (neutral), orange frowning (severe pain), and red tearful (extreme pain). VAS is valued for its simplicity, sensitivity, and responsiveness to change, and is frequently used in conjunction with WOMAC to assess pain reduction and treatment efficacy in both clinical practice and research on hip and knee OA.8,9

An additional factor that needs to be considered is the preparation of PRP. There are two main preparations: leukocyte-rich (LR-PRP) and leukocyte-poor (LP-PRP). LR-PRP incorporates a high concentration of white blood cells, which has been noted to cause greater variability in growth factor concentrations and, ultimately, a more pronounced inflammatory response.10,11 While this inflammatory property has proven beneficial with tendinopathies given its ability to stimulate remodeling, it’s believed to be more problematic when attempting to address an already inflammatory osteoarthritic disease process.12 That said, LP-PRP is increasingly used to help manage the inflammatory component of OA. By isolating the growth factors and extracellular matrix molecules without introducing the inflammatory cytokines, LP-PRP is able to promote healing without exacerbating an already inflammatory state which is harmful to the cartilage.13 Even with these different formulations of PRP, the optimal dosage of these various components remains unclear, as current studies have shown conflicting results depending on the clinical application and type of injury.

While numerous studies have suggested pain reduction and functional improvement when PRP is administered in knee OA, the hip joint has distinct cartilage structure, function, and load mechanics. As a result of these differences, it is essential not to generalize these results to other joints, making the study of PRP specifically in hip OA a valuable endeavor.14

With specific inclusion criteria focusing on published studies between 2019 and 2024 that included WOMAC and VAS scoring outcomes, this paper aims to critically evaluate recent findings on PRP treatment for hip OA. By reviewing this literature, we attempt to identify limitations in study design and propose recommendations for future research on PRP to lead to optimal outcomes for patients.

Material and Methods

A systematic approach was used to gather and analyze existing literature on PRP as a treatment modality for hip OA. A structured and targeted search of the literature was conducted using PubMed, where specific search terms and filters were applied to identify relevant studies published in recent years as delineated below in Figure 1. PubMed was selected as the primary database due to its comprehensive coverage of biomedical literature and the ability to filter for freely accessible full-text articles, aligning the focus on openly available research. Following the search, studies were screened for eligibility based on clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, as outlined below, to ensure that only high-quality and relevant articles were considered for review. Then, to ensure relevance and capture only the most recent advancements in this rapidly evolving area, the search was limited to studies published between 2019 and 2024. Key data were systematically extracted from the selected studies to facilitate meaningful comparisons across trials and to assess the clinical effectiveness of PRP in managing hip OA. Outcome measures were standardized between these studies using the WOMAC and the VAS, two validated tools commonly used in OA research. WOMAC assesses pain, stiffness, and physical function, while VAS provides a simple, subjective measure of pain intensity. The consistent use of these metrics across studies enabled a reliable synthesis of therapeutic outcomes.

This methodology was designed in accordance with established guidelines for conducting medical literature reviews, promoting transparency, reproducibility, and rigor. By focusing on studies that utilized standardized outcome measures and clinically relevant endpoints, this review aims to provide a clear and evidence-based evaluation of PRP as a non-surgical treatment option for patients with hip OA.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted on PubMed using the terms “(PRP Hip Osteoarthritis) AND (VAS) AND (WOMAC),” with filters selected for free full-text articles published from 2019 to 2024.

Data Extraction

The following variables were extracted from each study: author and year of publication, sample size, patient demographics, OA grade, characteristics of the PRP used (including platelet and leukocyte concentrations and activation method), injection schedule, duration of follow-up, and reported outcomes based on WOMAC and VAS scores.

Scoring and Interpretation

Womac

The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index is a validated instrument used to assess pain, stiffness, and physical function in individuals with OA. The scale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity across diverse populations with OA.15 It exhibits high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha typically > 0.80 for each subscale) and good test-retest reliability.16 Construct and criterion validity have been established through correlations with clinical measures of pain, function, and stiffness, supporting its use in both clinical and research settings. In the Likert version, each item is rated on a 5-point ordinal scale: none (0), mild (1), moderate (2), severe (3), and extreme (4).

Scores are summed within each of the three subscales to yield the following possible score ranges: ●Pain: 0–20●Stiffness: 0–8●Physical Function: 0–68

Ultimately, higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. A total WOMAC score may be calculated by summing all item scores across subscales and dividing by the maximum possible total (96) to yield a normalized score.

Vas

The VAS assesses the same domains using a 100 mm horizontal line for each item, anchored by “no pain/stiffness/difficulty” on the left and “extreme pain/stiffness/difficulty” on the right.17 Participants mark a point along the line to indicate the severity of their symptoms. Each response is measured in millimeters from the left anchor, resulting in a score ranging from 0 to 100 per item. However, the 100 mm VAS is often simplified to a 0–10 numerical scale, allowing for easier scoring and interpretation while preserving the scale’s sensitivity to symptom severity.18 Scale scores are obtained by summing the individual item scores within each domain. A total score may be derived by summing all items and expressing the result as a percentage of the maximum possible score.

The scale demonstrates high test-retest reliability and strong responsiveness to clinical changes over time. Significant correlations with other established measures of symptom severity and patient-reported outcomes support its validity.

Kraeutler et al (2021): This double blind, randomized controlled pilot study, although limited by sample size of 31 patients and 33 hips, provided the longest follow-up period of the included trials, which extended up to 24 months. The use of LP-PRP activated with calcium chloride was compared to injections of low-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid among patients with symptomatic hip OA. In this study design, patients received three weekly injections of either low-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid (LMW-HA) or Leukocyte poor platelet-rich plasma (LP-PRP). WOMAC scores improved significantly in the LP-PRP group, compared to the hyaluronic acid, by the 6-week mark and remained stable throughout the 6-month follow-up, suggesting possible mid- to long-term benefits in symptom management. Hip internal rotation was found to have improved in the LP-PRP group at the 6-month follow-up, while the LMW-HA group showed a decrease in hip internal rotation. However, the study did not include VAS as an outcome measure, limiting its comparability to the others. Additionally, despite the double-blind design, the small cohort size and lack of stratified outcomes (eg, by age, BMI, or OA severity) constrain the broader applicability of these results. This study found that within an average time of less than 1.5 years after the first injection, 50% of patients in the LMW-HA group were lost to surgery as compared to the LP-PRP group, which only 15.8% later underwent surgery.

Nouri et al (2022): This study had the largest sample size (n = 105) and was the only one to evaluate the combination of PRP and high-molecular-weight sodium hyaluronate (PRP+HA) versus each treatment alone. The two-injection protocol, administered two weeks apart, consisted of an intra-articular injection of either PRP, HA, or a combination of PRP and HA. They found that the PRP and PRP+HA groups showed the most pronounced improvements in both WOMAC and VAS scores at 6 months, indicating that HA added no additional benefit beyond PRP alone. By the 6-month follow-up, the HA group had lost the therapeutic benefits from the injections, while the PRP and PRP+HA groups-maintained benefits. However, the trial lacked blinding, which introduced potential performance and detection biases. This study failed to implement a placebo control group in its study design. Additionally, although early outcomes favored PRP+HA, the study did not assess whether these benefits persisted beyond 6 months. The lack of detail regarding PRP composition (eg, leukocyte or platelet concentration) further limits reproducibility and comparison with other studies.

Topaloglu et al (2024): This is the only placebo-controlled trial that met our inclusion criteria. This study employed a double-blind design to compare PRP with saline in patients with hip OA (KL grade 2 or 3). Patients received an intra-articular injection once a week for three weeks and followed an individualized rehabilitation program. Significant improvements were observed for both groups in WOMAC and VAS scores at both 1 and 6 months. No significant difference was found between the PRP and saline groups; however, the placebo group showed improvements in physical role functioning and general health at both 1 and 6 months. In this study, intra-articular PRP injections did not show a greater improvement in reducing pain, improving function, or enhancing quality of life over six months as compared to a placebo. This may be due to several limiting factors of this study, including an underpowered sample size, inadequate PRP characterization, or potential variability in patient OA severity despite using a KL grade 2–3 as the inclusion criterion. The study did not specify whether leukocyte-rich or leukocyte-poor platelet-rich plasma was used. Without full disclosure of the PRP preparation technique, interpreting the results and understanding the lack of efficacy becomes challenging.

Cross-Study Comparisons and Interpretation

Across the three studies, all used validated outcome measures (WOMAC and/or VAS), but timing and reporting varied significantly. The injection frequency ranged from two to three sessions, and the PRP activation methods (calcium chloride vs none) differed. Only one study incorporated a placebo arm, and none utilized stratified randomization based on demographic or disease severity subgroups.

Across two studies, a trend emerged suggesting that leukocyte-poor PRP and/or combined therapies (such as PRP+HA) may be more effective in the short term for patients with mild to moderate OA.14,19 However, inconsistencies in PRP characterization—such as platelet concentration, leukocyte presence, and activation status—complicate comparisons and limit the generalizability of findings.

These findings underscore the importance of standardized PRP classification systems, transparent reporting, and longer-term trials that incorporate subgroup analyses. While the evidence remains promising, the heterogeneity of methodologies highlights the current gap between emerging research and practical clinical application.

Discussion

Consistent with prior meta-analyses, the studies included in this review suggest that PRP may be an effective treatment for early-stage hip OA.21,22 Most patients experienced improvement in WOMAC and/or VAS scores within 3 to 6 months following injection.14,19 However, the outcomes were not consistent as the trials lacked standardization in PRP preparation or had insufficient blinding.

Methodologies vary widely when preparing PRP, and this plays a significant role in the varying outcomes of PRP effectiveness.22–25 Heterogeneity in the preparation method of PRP results in a unique composition of PRP specific to that laboratory, creating a challenge in characterizing and comparing it.23 Our review highlights that protocol variability remains one of the most significant barriers to the broader clinical adoption of this approach. Differences in leukocyte concentration may influence post-injection inflammation, and discrepancies in platelet dosing and activation methods limit the ability to replicate and compare findings. Leukocytes function in the immune and host defense system and lay a large role in tissue repair and regeneration.26 Without knowledge of the concentration of what is in the PRP such as leukocytes, lymphocytes, macrophages, and monocytes it is difficult to standardize for future use.

There has been ongoing debate regarding the ideal composition of PRP for OA, particularly the choice between leukocyte-rich and leukocyte-poor formulations.4,24,26 Over the years there has been great debate on what defines PRP and multiple classification systems have been suggested each focusing on different parameters.26 One such framework, the PAW system, highlights fundamental prerequisites that are necessary in order for PRP to be a safe and effective treatment in orthopedic disorders.25 The PAW classification is based on parameters regarding platelet, activation, and white blood cells.25 Another system proposed is the MARSPILL framework which categorizes PRP formulations according to eight critical factors: method of preparation, activation status, red blood cell content, spin technique, platelet concentration, image guidance, leukocyte content, and light activation.24 This approach offers a more comprehensive and standardized means of characterizing PRP products across studies. However, these classification systems as well multiple others that have been proposed throughout literature, all failed to standardize PRP in a way that can successfully be used by all clinicians. As PRP technology continues to expand, incorporating a standardized classification systems into future research may improve transparency and facilitate meta-analyses by allowing more accurate comparisons across heterogeneous protocols; leading to a standard methodology than clinicians can confidently use.25–27

Moreover, long-term outcome data are sparse, and few studies stratify results based on OA severity, patient age, or comorbid conditions.28 As surgery is not a viable option for every patient, conservative measures, such as injections, are needed as a treatment option. Standardized methodologies are crucial for comparing outcomes across clinical trials and identifying which patient populations are most likely to benefit from a particular treatment.

Therefore, addressing these challenges and creating a standard of care for clinicians may enhance therapeutic outcomes and delay the need for total joint reconstruction for a large population of people facing the detrimental effects of hip OA.

Conclusion

PRP shows promise as a minimally invasive treatment for hip OA, providing a sustainable alternative to conventional therapies. However, variability in PRP preparation protocols and administration techniques continues to limit its widespread clinical adoption and the generalizability of the research findings. In order to advance this field and optimize patient outcomes, there is a critical need for standardized PRP preparation guidelines, long-term clinical trials that follow patients over several years to assess durability of intervention, and well-designed subgroup analyses to identify patient populations most likely to benefit from this minimally invasive approach. Future research addressing these gaps will be instrumental in establishing the role of PRP in managing hip OA and in guiding evidence-based clinical practice.
