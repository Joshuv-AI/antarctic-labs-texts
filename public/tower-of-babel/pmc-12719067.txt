Cartilage organoids from basic research to clinical translation
Authors: Wenyi Zhang, Lin Chen, Yuzhen Li, Di Zhang, Nan Wang, Xueyong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCartilage organoids (COs) represent an emerging model system that advances joint repair and tissue regeneration research. These three-dimensional models overcome the limitations of traditional monolayer culture by preserving chondrocyte phenotype and metabolic function, while also modeling pathological microenvironments like osteoarthritis. This capability positions COs as powerful tools for investigating disease mechanisms and screening therapeutic agents. Furthermore, when integrated with microfluidics, bioprinting, and mechanical loading systems, COs exhibit significant potential for applications in personalized tissue engineering. This review synthesizes recent progress in the CO field, critically assesses its translational promise for biomedical applications, and outlines the prevailing challenges and key future research directions.Subject areas: Health sciences, Health technology

Introduction

The musculoskeletal system faces growing challenges from aging populations, sports injuries, and lifestyle factors. Musculoskeletal diseases, such as cartilage disorders like osteoarthritis (OA), are becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide. As a specialized connective tissue, cartilage coats joint surfaces with its extracellular matrix (ECM) and embedded chondrocytes, providing friction reduction and shock absorption. Damage causes pain, swelling, and impaired mobility, severely impacting quality of life. Understanding cartilage development, disease mechanisms, and therapies demands improved models. Current animal studies and two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures lack physiological spatial organization, ECM interactions, and cellular crosstalk, making them poor mimics of real tissue complexity. This gap underscores the need for advanced three-dimensional (3D) culture systems.

Recent advances in developmental biology and stem cell research have revealed ways to control the behavior of stem and progenitor cells at the molecular level. Regenerative medicine studies have demonstrated that these cells can differentiate into functional mature cell types. Building on these insights, cartilage organoids (COs) have emerged as self-organizing, 3D structures derived from stem cells, which closely mimic the characteristics of native cartilage.1 Modern COs now mirror native tissue architecture and function, modeling growth, repair, and disease processes in vitro.

This review explores cartilage homeostasis, organoid generation methods, and their applications in disease modeling and regenerative therapies. We highlight current limitations and propose strategies to harness their full potential for transforming cartilage research and treatment.

Cartilage development, homeostasis, and regeneration

Articular cartilage (AC), which is primarily composed of chondrocytes and an ECM rich in collagen fibers and proteoglycans, has a stratified architecture that includes the superficial, transitional (middle), deep (radial), and calcified zones. The tidemark demarcates the boundary between non-calcified and calcified layers.2 Chondrocyte size increases progressively from the articular surface toward the subchondral bone, culminating in calcified, vascularized hypertrophic cells.3 AC maintains its avascular and aneural nature through anti-angiogenic factors such as thrombospondin-1 (TSP1), chondromodulin-I, collagen type XVIII, secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine, N-terminal propeptide of type Ⅱ collagen, and tissue inhibitor of metalloprotease-2.4,5,6 Nutrient and oxygen exchange occurs solely via diffusion due to this vascular exclusion. While AC exhibits limited regenerative capacity under normal conditions,7 minor injuries may trigger local chondrocyte proliferation and matrix synthesis. Severe damage often leads to the formation of fibrocartilaginous repair tissue, which is a mechanically inferior substitute lacking native structural organization.

During embryogenesis, cartilage precursors originate from mesenchymal condensations in skeletal-forming regions. Postnatal studies of bovine and murine articular surfaces identified clonogenic cells expressing mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) markers.8,9 These transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)-responsive progenitors upregulate proteoglycan 4 (PRG4), aggrecan, and collagen type Ⅱ (COL II) during chondrogenic differentiation.10,11 Joint development begins at embryonic week 6, driven by the differentiation of chondrocytes mediated by the SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9). This process is further regulated by the Indian Hedgehog protein, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs).12,13 Developmental regulation involves coordinated upregulation of wingless-type MMTV integration site family member 9A, growth differentiation factor 5 (GDF5), ETS-related gene (Erg), GLI family zinc finger 3, cluster differentiation 44, and COL II.14,15,16,17 Lineage tracing reveals GDF5+ cell populations with chondrogenic potential marked by doublecortin expression in E12.5 mice.18 The key stages of cartilage development and the structural characteristics of mature AC mentioned are visually illustrated in Figure 1.

Embryonic COs have emerged as powerful models for dissecting developmental and pathological mechanisms. To faithfully recapitulate in vivo cartilage development, recent research has embraced multi-faceted strategies centered on three core aspects: controlling cellular ontogeny and lineage commitment, reconstructing the biochemical and physical microenvironment, and dynamically modeling developmental and transformative processes. First, by efficiently directing human pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) to differentiate into SOX9+ sclerotomal progenitors, researchers have established models that mirror the cellular origins of embryonic cartilage. These models spontaneously form growth plate-like structures and recapitulate the complete endochondral ossification sequence.19 Concurrently, the molecular signaling pathways governing this process are being decoded. Single-cell analyses, for instance, implicate ten-eleven translocation 2/3 (TET2/3) in pharyngeal cartilage development via BMP signaling modulation.20 Likewise, FGF-8 orchestrates chondrocyte proliferation and migration not only via the RAS/MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways but also by enhancing gap junction intercellular communication.21 Second, the preservation of cartilage homeostasis is modeled by interrogating key regulatory nodes within organoids. The phosphatase SCP4/CTDSPL2 maintains cartilage homeostasis by regulating the dephosphorylation of forkhead box O3. Its deficiency disrupts the organization of the growth plate and delays endochondral ossification.22 In summary, the integration of these complementary approaches offers a robust and comprehensive framework for simulating the intricate process of in vivo cartilage development using organoid models.

Collectively, engineering cartilage organ tissues not only mimics disease states but also informs regenerative strategies.23 Current efforts are focused on the development of robust in vitro and in vivo regeneration platforms using cartilage hematopoietic stem cells, MSCs, and reprogrammed chondrocytes. These advances aim to decode cartilage repair mechanisms and accelerate therapeutic innovation for cartilage-related conditions. However, to truly achieve these goals, research models capable of more accurately simulating the complex structure and function of cartilage are urgently needed. In this context, CO technology has emerged, which by guiding cellular self-organization into 3D structures provides an unprecedented platform for deciphering developmental processes, pathological mechanisms, and regenerative potential in a controlled in vitro environment.

Cartilage organoid derivation

The development of appropriate tissues requires multifaceted capabilities, which not only involve acquiring specific cellular phenotypes but also encompass the ability to regulate ECM composition and production, angiogenesis, neurogenesis, growth factors, molecular signaling, as well as the capacity to respond to stress stimuli.24 The development and formation of COs rely on the self-organization of chondrocytes, a process that can be divided into two stages: self-patterning and morphogenetic rearrangement.25 Initially, a unique pattern of chondrocyte differentiation is established within a homogeneous system. Subsequently, this process stems from the sorting of chondrocyte subtypes and the reorganization of tissue architecture, which are mutually facilitated by cell-cell physical interactions and maintained by intrinsic mechanical forces to form complex 3D structures. The key to successfully deriving COs lies in recapitulating the self-organization process, which necessitates a comprehensive consideration of the physical properties of the culture environment, signaling requirements, as well as the starting cell type and system conditions. Construction of cartilage organoids

The cells used to culture cartilage organ tissues are primarily stem cells, including ESCs, iPSCs, MSCs, and HPDCs, as well as chondrocytes from tissue digestion. These cells are induced and differentiated by various differentiation and growth factors including BMP2, TGF-β3, SOX9, and IGF-1 and then self-assembled into organ tissues in scaffold-based (common ECMs are HA, DM, collagen, etc.) or scaffold-free cultures.

Cell origins for cartilage organoids

Cells can be obtained from two major sources: they can either be isolated and extracted from primary tissues through digestion or differentiated from stem cells that have been isolated and cultured.

Isolated and cultured stem cells

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are a type of cells isolated from the early embryos or primordial gonads,26 characterized by their ability to proliferate indefinitely, self-renew, and undergo multilineage differentiation in vitro. Both in vitro and in vivo, ESCs can be induced to differentiate into nearly all cell types required by the organism. For instance, the sequential addition of activin A and BMP4 can induce their differentiation into cardiomyocytes.27 If BMP2 is used instead of BMP4, it can rapidly induce the expression of the SOX9 gene, thereby promoting increased expression of chondrogenesis-related genes such as SOX5, ACAN, and COL2A1. This process enhances the formation of chondrogenic cell aggregates and leads to substantial deposition of COL II. Ultimately, ESCs differentiate into chondrocyte progenitor cells, successfully inducing chondrocytes and forming cartilage tissue.28 Alternatively, ESCs can first be differentiated into other types of stem cells, which are then further differentiated and self-assembled into cartilage-like tissues.29 However, the application of human ESCs is constrained by legal, ethical, scientific, and safety considerations.

MSCs, primarily derived from bone marrow, adipose tissue, muscle tissue, and umbilical cord blood,30 possess the potential to differentiate into osteoblasts, adipocytes, or chondrocytes during in vitro culture, thereby contributing to the ongoing advancements in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Members of the TGF-β superfamily serve as key mediators in the chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs. Bian et al. demonstrated that local delivery of TGF-β3 could induce MSCs to form cartilage-like tissue both in vitro and in vivo.31 Compared to other sources, adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) offer several advantages, including ease of tissue harvest, robust self-renewal potential, and a rapid population doubling time. Leveraging these characteristics, a study in 202232 established the first miniaturized 3D in vitro skeletal model using pre-differentiated ADSCs embedded within a hydrogel-collagen matrix, along with a preliminary safety assessment, laying the foundation for personalized bone tissue engineering applications. Notably, research has indicated33 that ADSCs may represent a more controllable source of stem cells, potentially better adapted to survive in the hypoxic joint cavity niche and exhibiting advantages in modulating inflammation.

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) represent a cell type that can be reprogrammed from terminally differentiated somatic cells into PSCs through the introduction of specific transcription factors.34 This groundbreaking technology was first pioneered by a laboratory in Kyoto, Japan,35 which discovered that the introduction of four specific genes, collectively termed the Yamanaka factors (Myc, Oct3/4, Sox2, and Klf4), encoding transcription factors, could transform somatic cells into PSCs. These iPSCs exhibit morphological and characteristic similarities to ESCs and possess the ability to differentiate into various tissue types. Driven by TGF-β and BMP, iPSCs have been successfully differentiated into chondrogenic and osteogenic lineages and are widely applied in osteochondral tissue engineering.36,37 Li et al. successfully induced the differentiation of iPSCs into cartilage-like tissues in vitro and characterized them, thereby supporting the potential of iPSCs as progenitor cells for CO tissues.38 Furthermore, a study demonstrated that by differentiating iPSCs into the mesodermal lineage, corpus callosum organoids could be formed, which then underwent self-organization in the absence of scaffolds and subsequently differentiated into chondrocytes, forming COs.39 Consequently, iPSCs have emerged as a powerful source of cells for constructing cartilage-like tissues, but iPSC derivation is usually a slow and inefficient process that remains limited by technical aspects of extraction induction and transformation.

Digest and extract from the corresponding tissue

Human periosteal-derived cells (HPDCs) are primarily sourced from the periosteal tissue of the human body. The periosteum is a thin membrane that adheres closely to the bone surface and is rich in blood vessels, nerves, and fibroblasts, among other components. It plays a pivotal role in both intramembranous and endochondral ossification.40 Under specific conditions, these cells can proliferate and differentiate into various cell types, including osteocytes and chondrocytes. Activin A directly stimulates myofibroblast differentiation, chondrogenesis, and osteogenesis in cultures of periosteal mesenchymal progenitor cells.41 Although periosteal cells and MSCs originate from the same embryonic mesenchymal lineage, periosteal cells exhibit higher regenerative capacity than MSCs due to the abundance of skeletal stem cells within the periosteum.42 When cultured under laboratory conditions, periosteal cells (extracted from the femoral neck of arthritis patients) and bone marrow stromal cells displayed striking similarities in proliferation rate, morphological appearance, and surface receptor expression profiles.43 This observation underscores the multipotent potential of PDCs, opening new avenues for research in the field of tissue engineering. Further studies have demonstrated that cartilage-like microtissues cultivated from HPDCs can further develop into bone tissue in vivo and exhibit the capability to repair skeletal defects.44 Additionally, when these callus-like organoid tissues formed from HPDCs are combined with cartilage microtissues constructed from iPSCs, they can sequentially differentiate into bone and cartilage tissues in vivo, respectively.45 This discovery offers novel strategies and hope for the repair of bone and cartilage injuries using cell therapy and tissue engineering techniques.

In addition to the aforementioned sources, chondrocytes can also be derived from autologous digestive origin. Abraham et al.46 employed enzymatic digestion to directly process cartilage tissue donated by pediatric donors, successfully generating COs. To more accurately mimic joint development and disease states, the research team also subjected rib tissue, which contains cartilage components, to enzymatic digestion. The resulting cells were then subjected to chondrogenic differentiation, leading to the creation of miniature joint spheres containing cartilage tissue. Autologous chondrocyte implantation stands as the only cell-based surgical procedure approved in the United States for treating cartilage defects. However, this approach has limitations due to the dedifferentiation of articular chondrocytes during in vitro expansion. When the initial population of articular chondrocytes is co-cultured with MSCs, dedifferentiation can be mitigated. MSCs not only undergo chondrogenesis themselves but also support chondrocyte viability.47

Biomaterials mimicking the ECM and methods for assembling COs

CO assembly primarily involves two strategies: scaffold-free approaches and scaffold-based systems. Scaffold-free methods eliminate reliance on exogenous ECM analogs through techniques such as suspension culture, microgravity bioreactors, rotating wall vessels, or hollow fiber systems, thereby inducing cellular self-aggregation into spheroids.48 A key advantage of this approach is its close recapitulation of the mesenchymal condensation process in embryonic development, promoting the secretion of a purely autologous, native-like ECM. However, these methods often face challenges in controlling the size, shape, and structural integrity of the resulting organoids, which can limit their reproducibility and application in modeling complex tissue architectures. Conversely, scaffold-based systems employ biomaterial matrices to mimic native ECM environments, providing critical mechanical support and biochemical cues that significantly enhance CO growth, maturation, and structural consistency. The primary drawback of such systems is the potential introduction of non-physiological signals from the scaffold material itself, which may interfere with the innate self-organization and signaling pathways of the cells.

The ECM plays a dual role in biological systems. It not only provides structural support as a scaffold but also actively regulates cellular processes, including adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and function. This regulation is achieved through mechanisms such as growth factor sequestration and cytokine presentation. Within cartilage tissue, collagen stands out as the predominant component of the ECM. It is renowned for its excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and inherent bioactivity. Specifically, type I collagen contains unique recognition motifs that facilitate enhanced chondrocyte adhesion, proliferation, and matrix synthesis, as evidenced by relevant studies.31 Another vital component of the native ECM is hyaluronic acid (HA), a linear glycosaminoglycan that is ubiquitous in various tissues. HA supports cellular migration and maintains matrix hydration, contributing to tissue integrity and function. Additionally, it is biodegradable, breaking down into absorbable metabolic by-products that can be safely eliminated from the body. Chitosan, a polysaccharide derivative, also merits attention for its biological properties. It exhibits inherent hemocompatibility and antimicrobial effects, making it a valuable material in biomedical applications. Moreover, chitosan demonstrates synergistic effects when combined with other biomaterials, enhancing their overall performance and suitability for specific therapeutic purposes.49

The use of these cellular ECM components in combination with hydrogel,50,51 gelatin,52 or agarose53 can be constructed into cellular scaffolding complexes, which enhances cell proliferation, adhesion, and chondrogenic differentiation, making them ideal for constructing and culturing cartilaginous organs over time.31,49 Beyond these conventional materials, emerging biomimetic systems show enhanced regulatory capabilities. Functionalized nanoparticles not only serve as effective delivery vehicles for growth factors but also can actively modulate cellular behaviors such as adhesion and migration through surface modifications, thereby enabling the construction of more complex tissue models.54 Similarly, stimuli-responsive hydrogels can mimic the dynamic properties of the native ECM, allowing real-time and precise regulation of the organoid developmental microenvironment in response to external cues like light, temperature, or pH, ultimately yielding more mature and physiologically relevant organoids.55,56 Notably, a recent advance involves the use of adaptable supramolecular hydrogels, which not only support the rapid enlargement of COs but also actively reprogram the cellular “glycolysis-lactylation” metabolic trajectory. This metabolic rewiring enhances chondrogenic maturation and functionally links CO maturity to specific metabolic fingerprints, offering a novel biomaterial-driven strategy for controlling cartilage development and quality.57 Additionally, the adoption of well-defined, biocompatible synthetic polymers represents a crucial strategy to overcome the batch-to-batch variability and compositional uncertainty associated with conventional matrices like Matrigel, which is essential for standardizing organoid technology and facilitating its clinical translation.58 Decellularized matrix (DM), a biomaterial that retains the original structure and functional matrix proteins, is also widely used in tissue engineering due to its excellent biocompatibility and lack of immune rejection. These materials can be used individually or in combination to mimic the natural environment of cartilage ECM and promote the growth and development of cartilage-like organs. Meanwhile, the development of novel materials such as silicon-based bioceramic scaffolds offers promising strategies for the regeneration of osteochondral defects.59

Scalability, cost, and throughput analysis

Currently, the widespread application of organoids, organs-on-a-chip, and 3D bioprinting technologies remains constrained by three core challenges: scalability, cost, and high-throughput capacity.

In terms of scalability, traditional manual production of organoids leads to significant batch-to-batch variability and low reproducibility, severely limiting their large-scale application. To address this challenge, researchers are actively developing automated biofabrication strategies. For instance, 3D bioprinting60,61 and microfluidic technology62,63 enable precise, controlled deposition of cells and hydrogels in a reproducible manner, facilitating the construction of complex tissues with consistent architecture. This significantly enhances the standardization and scaling potential of organoid production. Particularly, unconventional bioprinting modalities (such as acoustic-, magnetic-, or electric field-driven approaches) show breakthrough promise in current resolution and speed limitations, thereby further enhancing scalability.64

Regarding cost control, these technologies demonstrate value through multiple avenues. On one hand, they provide more physiologically relevant disease models and drug screening platforms capable of earlier and more accurate prediction of drug efficacy and toxicity, thereby reducing the substantial costs associated with late-stage drug development failures.65 On the other hand, these technologies may reduce reliance on expensive and ethically controversial animal testing, indirectly lowering overall costs.66

In the context of high-throughput capacity, the integration of microfluidic technology and bioprinting serves as a key driving force. Microfluidic platforms can generate uniform hydrogel microspheres or microfibers in a high-throughput manner, and these modular units form the foundation for rapid, large-scale assembly of 3D tissues.63 When combined with automated imaging and analysis technologies, such integrated systems enable high-throughput drug sensitivity testing and toxicity assessment.67

In summary, the synergistic integration of technologies such as 3D bioprinting and microfluidics is systematically addressing these bottlenecks. This multi-technology convergence strategy not only accelerates their practical applications but also paves the way for their eventual clinical translation.

Applications of cartilage organoids

Disease modeling

Hereditary cartilage dysplasias

Achondroplasia is caused by gain-of-function mutations in the FGFR3 gene, which lead to the obstruction of endochondral ossification, manifesting as restricted long bone growth. In recent years, COs differentiated from patient-specific iPSCs have successfully simulated the hypertrophy of growth plate chondrocytes and abnormal matrix mineralization caused by excessive activation of the FGFR3 signaling pathway. For example, FGFR3-mutant COs constructed using CRISPR-Cas9 technology can reproduce clinical pathological features (such as small hypertrophic chondrocytes) and are used to evaluate the therapeutic effects of FGFR inhibitors.68,69 Such models provide a high-fidelity research platform for exploring the dynamic relationship between gene mutations and cartilage development.

The deficiency of SOX9, a core transcription factor regulating cartilage formation, leads to abnormal growth plate closure and early-onset OA. Through CO models with SOX9 knockout, researchers have found that this defect promotes the transdifferentiation of chondrocytes into osteoblasts, accompanied by an imbalance in the TGF-β/BMP signaling pathway.13 In addition, COs with SOX9 gene delivery based on viral vectors can significantly restore the expression of COL2A1 and ACAN, providing an in vitro verification system for gene therapy.70 These models not only reveal the crucial role of SOX9 in maintaining cartilage homeostasis but also lay the foundation for the optimization of regenerative medicine strategies.

Mutations in the COMP gene cause endoplasmic reticulum stress and necroptosis of chondrocytes, leading to severe dwarfism and joint degeneration. Studies using COs derived from iPSCs of patients with pseudoachondroplasia have confirmed that the mutant COMP protein is abnormally retained in the endoplasmic reticulum, accompanied by an upregulation of oxidative stress markers.71 This model has further been used to screen the therapeutic effects of antioxidants, showing that melatonin can improve cell survival by alleviating calcium overload and endoplasmic reticulum stress.72 Organoid technology provides an efficient tool for the development of precision therapies targeting COMP pathology.

The sulfate transport defect caused by SLC26A2 mutations leads to insufficient sulfation of glycosaminoglycans, resulting in diastrophic dysplasia. Studies on COs derived from patients have revealed disorders in collagen orientation and distortion of the joint protective layer.73 Intervention with melatonin can alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress and calcium overload in organoids, suggesting the potential therapeutic value of melatonin in such diseases. Such models provide a reliable platform for the mechanism research and drug screening of metabolic chondropathies.

Secondary cartilage pathologies

The core pathological features of OA include chondrocyte hypertrophy, ECM degradation (accompanied by the upregulation of catabolic enzymes such as MMP13 and ADAMTS5), and catabolic processes driven by inflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin [IL]-1β). Engineered OA COs constructed from MSCs can accurately mimic the cartilage degeneration characteristics induced by IL-1β and are used to evaluate the therapeutic effects of IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra).74 A study utilizing 3D tissue-engineered cartilage derived from mouse iPSCs demonstrated that the inflammatory cytokine IL-1 disrupts the circadian rhythm in chondrocytes, leading to a dampened amplitude and prolonged period of circadian oscillator genes; furthermore, genetic engineering approaches, such as IL-1 receptor 1 knockout (IL-1R1-KO) or the introduction of IL-1Ra, were shown to protect the circadian clock from this IL-1-induced disruption and prevent cartilage matrix degradation.75 In addition, abnormal mechanical signal transduction mediated by TRPV4 channels plays a crucial role in OA progression. Studies using TRPV4 gene knockout COs have confirmed that the absence of this channel can significantly reduce the severity of aging-related OA.76

It is increasingly recognized that the synovium, as an integral part of the joint microenvironment, actively contributes to OA progression through the release of inflammatory mediators. In this context, recently developed synovial organoids that incorporate fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells within a 3D architecture have emerged as valuable tools. These models replicate key aspects of synovitis and have been used to dissect the role of specific immune pathways and to screen anti-inflammatory compounds.77,78,79 A compelling future direction will be to integrate these synovial models with evolving osteochondral organoids. Such integration will be essential for uncovering the systemic pathophysiology of joint diseases and for developing truly effective regenerative and anti-inflammatory therapies. These organoid models encompassing both osteochondral and synovial tissues provide a dynamic research platform for elucidating the pathogenesis of OA and developing targeted drugs.

Abnormal mechanical loading, such as meniscus injury, triggers the transient differentiation of chondrocytes and ECM degradation. COs constructed based on silk fibroin (SF) hydrogels, combined with mechanical stimuli such as cyclic stretching, can effectively simulate the injured microenvironment and promote the differentiation of MSCs into chondrocytes.58 For example, SF-DNA hydrogel microspheres (RSD-MS) loaded with bone MSCs can enhance chondrogenesis by activating the integrin signaling pathway and promoting glycosaminoglycan synthesis.80 This biomaterial-organoid composite system offers a biomimetic solution for traumatic cartilage repair.

The excessive activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway accelerates chondrocyte hypertrophy and OA progression. Research using CRISPR-edited COs has revealed that the Wnt signaling pathway accelerates cartilage matrix mineralization by upregulating Runx2 gene expression.81 Wnt pathway-targeted inhibitors can effectively reverse pathological phenotypes in organoid models, providing new insights into the development of disease-modifying osteoarthritis drugs.82

In conclusion, COs provide innovative tools for disease mechanism research, drug screening, and the development of regenerative medicine by simulating pathological processes induced by genetic mutations, mechanical stress, or metabolic abnormalities. In the future, the integration of 3D bioprinting, multi-omics analysis, and dynamic mechanical stimulation technologies is expected to further promote the precision treatment of cartilage diseases.83,84

Therapeutic application of cartilage organoids

Drug discovery and toxicity assessment

CO as an innovative tool provides a new approach for drug screening and toxicity assessment of degenerative diseases such as OA by constructing highly realistic pathological models. Cos can precisely mimic the pathological features of OA, such as chondrocyte hypertrophy, matrix mineralization, and enhanced catabolism and can dynamically analyze the specific mechanisms of drug intervention.

For example, OA organoid models constructed based on human mesenchymal stromal cells have confirmed that IL-1Ra can effectively reverse the process of cartilage degradation by inhibiting the signaling pathway of the transcription factor C/EBPβ.74 Single-cell sequencing technology has further revealed the role of Col11a1-HIF1α-mediated glycolytic dysregulation in the senescence of chondrocytes. By using Col11a1-overexpressing organoids, it is possible to target and regulate metabolic homeostasis, thus providing new targets for drug development.85

From a technical perspective, the organoid platform integrated with hydrogels (such as RGD-SF-DNA microspheres) can, by activating the integrin-mediated adhesion pathway and the glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis pathway, not only promote the differentiation of chondrocytes but also serve as a standardized system for high-throughput drug screening and toxicity testing.80,83 In addition, the organoid-on-a-chip system, by integrating the multi-tissue microenvironment of bone/cartilage, enables the dynamic monitoring and precise evaluation of the effects of drugs on tissue interactions.86

These COs have broken through the limitations of traditional 2D culture in simulating pathological complexity and analyzing multi-dimensional signals, laying an efficient and reliable research foundation for in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms of OA, screening of targeted drugs, and optimization of personalized treatment regimens.

Personalized medicine

COs demonstrate great potential in constructing personalized disease models and implementing precise interventions. Take the mouse model of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) as an example. With the aid of single-cell sequencing technology, researchers have discovered that the glycolytic imbalance mediated by the Col11a1-HIF1α pathway is a crucial mechanism leading to cartilage degeneration. Based on this significant finding, researchers have successfully developed synovial MSC organoids with overexpression of Col11a1. By intra-articular injection of these organoids, the metabolic homeostasis of chondrocytes can be effectively restored, thus significantly improving the cartilage degeneration in DDH mice.85,87

Similarly, COs differentiated from patient-specific iPSCs can highly mimic the pathological features of hereditary skeletal diseases such as achondroplasia. Moreover, potential therapeutic targets can be verified through gene editing techniques.25,88 It is worth noting that iPSC-derived COs have been successfully applied to the repair of joint defects in primates. After transplantation, these organoids can integrate well with the host cartilage and express the lubricin protein PRG4, thereby significantly delaying the progression of OA.89

When single-cell sequencing technology is combined with organoid models, it is possible to deeply analyze the dynamic changes of chondrocyte subpopulations during the occurrence and development of diseases, which provides a solid and reliable basis for the design of personalized treatment strategies.85

Regenerative medicine

With their unique 3D biomimetic properties and strong functional plasticity, COs demonstrate great clinical application potential in the fields of tissue repair and functional reconstruction. COs differentiated by means of iPSC technology have successfully achieved long-term repair of cartilage defects in the knee joints of primates. After transplantation, these COs can seamlessly integrate with the host cartilage, can stably express marker proteins such as PRG3, and do not trigger any immune rejection reactions.89,90

Joint biomimetic organoids constructed through 3D bioprinting technology, combined with a targeted hydrogel delivery system loaded with BMP, can precisely repair critical-sized bone defects and effectively promote the functional regeneration of the bone-cartilage interface.39,91 In the field of craniofacial repair, elastic COs can regulate cell fate through autocrine ECM signals. Meanwhile, combined with the paracrine network revealed by organoid models derived from neural crest stem cells (NCSCs), it provides a new strategy for the regenerative treatment of complex defects in the maxillofacial region.25,92 It is important to note that craniofacial cartilage and AC are fundamentally distinct, particularly in their embryonic origins; the former is predominantly derived from the neural crest, while the latter originates from the mesoderm. This lineage divergence results in distinct signaling dependencies and cellular behaviors. Consequently, caution must be exercised when extrapolating findings from one cartilage type to the other. Embracing this lineage-specific approach is crucial for advancing personalized regenerative medicine for the entire spectrum of cartilage disorders.

In addition, iPSCs can be directionally differentiated into AC or growth plate chondrocytes through 3D particle culture. This process not only successfully simulates the process of endochondral ossification but also builds an important model foundation for the personalized treatment of hereditary skeletal diseases.88

These research progresses fully demonstrate that COs, by organically integrating biomaterial engineering and stem cell technology, are gradually breaking through the inherent limitations of traditional repair methods and strongly promoting the significant advancement of regenerative medicine toward the direction of precision and functionality.

Decoding the mechanical microenvironment and developmental signaling

COs have established an innovative platform for probing the fundamental mechanisms of cartilage development and disease, with particular strength in elucidating developmental signaling pathways and the critical role of the mechanical microenvironment.

Recapitulating and probing the mechanical microenvironment

The mechanical microenvironment, which includes factors such as matrix stiffness, fluid shear stress, and compressive loads, serves as a cornerstone of cartilage biology by governing chondrocyte fate, metabolism, and pathology. COs integrated with mechanically adaptable hydrogels establish stable composite systems that markedly improve the mechanical stability of cellular aggregates. This integration allows organoids to retain their three-dimensional architecture when subjected to external dynamic stimuli, such as shock waves93 and cyclic loading, thereby effectively overcoming the experimental limitations associated with traditional 2D culture systems, where unstable substrate interfaces often introduce significant artifacts. This unique characteristic provides a new 3D research paradigm for in-depth analysis of the effects of mechanical factors such as ECM stiffness and fluid shear force on the maintenance of chondrocyte phenotypes and metabolic regulation, making up for the deficiencies of the single-layer culture system in simulating the complex mechanical microenvironment in vivo. Furthermore, the combination of hydrogel technology and microfluidic systems not only efficiently construct organoids but also maintain their biological functions for a long time. Meanwhile, the introduction of biomaterials such as alginate and silk fibroin has optimized the mechanical properties and biocompatibility of organoids,80,94 laying a solid technical foundation for their clinical translation.

Investigating developmental and regenerative signaling pathways

Beyond mechanical cues, COs have profoundly advanced our understanding of developmental biology. The cartilage-osteoblast organoid system differentiated from iPSCs has clearly revealed the expression profiles of key genes governing cartilage maturation, hypertrophy, and transformation into osteoblasts, successfully simulating the complex process of endochondral ossification.88 Craniofacial COs derived from NCSCs, with the help of single-cell sequencing technology, have thoroughly elucidated the paracrine signaling network between mesenchymal cells and nascent chondrocytes, as well as the regulatory mechanism of this network on cell fate determination.25 Additionally, the “mini-joint” organoids constructed based on LGR5+ joint progenitor cells have become a high-fidelity model for studying embryonic joint development and the pathological mechanisms of OA.95

These above-mentioned research progresses have not only deepened our understanding of the mechanisms of cartilage development and degeneration but also opened up multi-dimensional technical paths for research in tissue engineering and mechanobiology, bringing new development opportunities. From organoids to functional osteochondral constructs

Although COs show remarkable potential in modeling cartilage biology and pathology, existing models largely concentrate on regenerating the non-calcified cartilage region. However, successful clinical repair of full-thickness osteochondral defects hinges on reconstructing the complete interfacial architecture, such as the calcified cartilage zone and subchondral bone, and restoring biomechanical function and lubrication mechanisms. These challenges cannot be resolved by organoids alone and require the integration of advanced biomaterial engineering strategies.

Biphasic/triphasic hydrogels with micro-/nanoscale gradients have emerged as promising approaches for osteochondral regeneration. The calcified zone, crucial for mechanical integration, can be reconstructed using compositionally graded hydrogels that promote COL X expression and mineralization. Stiffness gradients further direct MSC migration and calcified cartilage formation, while growth factors like GDF-5 simultaneously enhance chondrogenesis and alkaline phosphatase activity. These processes can be monitored through COL X staining,105 mineralization assays, and UTE-MRI.106 PRG4 is a key protein for maintaining low friction and wear resistance in joints.107 For functional lubrication, PRG4 adsorption on hydrogels is substrate dependent, but surface modification with chemical groups or polymer brushes can optimize lubricin layer stability and significantly reduce friction coefficients. The integration strength between engineered constructs and host tissue is substantially improved by incorporating a calcified zone and continuous gradients, which facilitate smoother load transfer compared to abrupt biphasic designs. Interfacial shear strength testing provides the gold standard for evaluating this integration.108

Thus, combining COs with strategically designed gradient hydrogels represents an essential direction for developing functional osteochondral tissues.

Prospects and challenges

As a remarkable breakthrough in the domains of regenerative medicine and disease models, COs offer a highly authentic biomimetic research platform for comprehensively analyzing the pathogenesis of cartilage-related diseases, formulating targeted drugs, and implementing regenerative therapies. The primary task is to construct suitable COs. Beyond conventional materials like hydrogels94,95,109 and collagen, functionalized nanoparticles can serve as carriers to deliver growth factors or drugs to specific cellular subpopulations, enabling precise regulation. Nanosensors can monitor microenvironmental parameters such as pH and oxygen concentration within organoids in real time, providing data to optimize culture conditions. Additionally, nano-hydroxyapatite can mimic the inorganic components of native cartilage matrix, enhancing the mechanical properties and bioactivity of grafts. These nanocomposite scaffolds facilitate the construction of more functional grafts, further augmenting the capabilities of organoid models. COs are often integrated with single-cell omics and metabolic regulation studies to dynamically analyze molecular networks during cartilage development and degeneration. Leveraging bioprinting technologies and gene editing methods, they strongly drive the advancement of personalized precision medicine.

Despite these advantages, COs face numerous challenges in clinical translation. A primary limitation is the issue of scalability and structural fidelity. Current self-assembly protocols often yield organoids of millimeter scale or smaller, which lack the robust mechanical properties and complex zonal architecture of native AC, thereby hindering their application in repairing large osteochondral defects. Functionally, existing COs face two primary challenges: replicating the native mechanical stress microenvironment and ensuring sufficient nutrient diffusion within their avascular, hypoxic core. From a manufacturing and safety standpoint, achieving batch-to-batch consistency is a critical hurdle. The incorporation of nanomaterials, while beneficial, increases process complexity, with the preparation of standardized nanobiological inks and safety assessments of nanoscale residues becoming key bottlenecks. Moreover, systematic evaluation of the long-term metabolic behavior of nanomaterials and the potential off-target effects of gene-edited organoids is required.

To overcome these barriers, several promising strategies are emerging, many of which leverage advanced bioengineering and nanotechnology. The integration of biofabrication techniques, such as 3D bioprinting, with organoid technology is pivotal for creating larger, anatomically shaped constructs. Looking ahead, four-dimensional bioprinting technology, combined with nanostructural control, promises to enable dynamic tissue construction. Here, functionalized nanoparticles can serve as carriers to deliver growth factors with high precision, while nanosensors can monitor microenvironmental parameters in real time to optimize culture conditions. The development of advanced bioreactors that provide dynamic mechanical stimulation remains crucial for promoting functional maturation. Furthermore, nanocomposite scaffolds, such as those incorporating nano-hydroxyapatite to mimic the inorganic components of the native matrix, are being explored to enhance the mechanical properties and bioactivity of grafts.

Finally, establishing standardized, safe, and reproducible manufacturing protocols is imperative for clinical translation. This involves implementing rigorous quality control measures, including single-cell RNA sequencing to monitor cell fate. The integration of AI with nanoscale high-throughput screening platforms can significantly accelerate the screening and optimization of drug-nanocarrier complexes, thereby enhancing the practicality and predictive power of organoid models. Through the profound integration of bioengineering, nanotechnology, and AI, and by establishing a standardized research framework, COs are poised to transcend current limitations and become a core tool in cartilage regenerative medicine, accelerating the translational leap from laboratory research to clinical application.

Limitations of the study

While this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of CO technology, certain limitations should be acknowledged. There remains a possibility of publication bias, as our literature search, though extensive, may have missed relevant studies, particularly those in non-peer-reviewed formats. Additionally, as a narrative review, the interpretation and prioritization of evidence rely on scholarly judgment rather than strictly systematic methods, which may introduce an element of subjectivity. Finally, the future prospects and directions discussed are inherently speculative and have yet to be validated through ongoing research. It is our hope that this review will, nevertheless, serve as a guide and facilitate further progress in the rapidly evolving field of CO technology.
