Biomaterials for vertebral body repair: interior augmentation and total replacement—a literature review of bone fusion outcomes in clinical studies
Authors: Diogo Lino Moura, Diogo Casal, Paulo Lourenço, Carlos Jardim, Fernando Fonseca, José Casanova, António Bernardes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveVertebral body repair comprises surgical procedures aimed at restoring height and stability of injured vertebrae, thereby re-establishing spinal structural integrity and load-bearing function. Bone fusion and biomaterial incorporation or integration are key determinants of long-term clinical outcomes, providing biological stability. The ideal biomaterial for vertebral body repair remains undefined. Despite the increasing number of available materials, achieving an optimal balance between mechanical load-bearing capacity and biological potential for progressive bone substitution—key requirements for restoring spinal biomechanics—remains a major challenge. Therefore, this narrative review examines biomaterials used in vertebral body repair, with emphasis on bone fusion outcomes.MethodsA literature review of MEDLINE/PubMed was performed using two separate searches: one on vertebral body augmentation and another on total vertebral body replacement. Original clinical studies involving the mobile spine reporting bone fusion outcomes were included, whereas studies with unclear methods for assessing bone healing were excluded.Key Content and FindingsAmong the vertebral augmentation fillers analyzed, the most notable were bioactive bone cements, including calcium phosphate (16 studies), calcium sulfate (6 studies), and composite cements (3 studies). Regarding total vertebral body replacement, the most commonly reported implants were titanium mesh cages (85 studies), with bone fusion rates (BFRs) of 70–100%, and expandable titanium cages (58 studies), with BFRs ranging from 23.5% to 100% across various conditions. Other notable materials included bioactive ceramics (4 studies, 95–100%), carbon fiber (3 studies, 80–97.5%), thermoplastic polymers (8 studies, 70–100%), and carbon fiber-reinforced polymers (CFRPs) (2 studies, 100%). Porous metallic static cages (8 studies) exhibited BFRs of 76.9–100%, whereas nanohydroxyapatite/polyamide-66 (HA/PA66) cages (13 studies) showed BFRs ranging from 72.7% to 100%. Benefits and limitations of each biomaterial used in vertebral body repair were summarized and discussed.ConclusionsCurrent biomaterials for vertebral body repair demonstrate high BFRs; however, challenges remain concerning mechanical load-bearing capacity and biological interactions, including their potential for progressive bone substitution. Composite biomaterials that combine the most desirable properties of multiple materials represent promising solutions. Future studies on the development of optimal biomaterials are essential to achieve accurate vertebral body reconstruction from both biomechanical and biological perspectives.

Introduction

A biomaterial is defined as a material intended to interface with biological systems to evaluate, treat, augment, or replace tissues, organs, or bodily functions (1). In the spine, vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs bear approximately 80% of axial compressive loads, and disruption of vertebral body morphology—most commonly kyphotic collapse—alters load distribution, shifts the load-bearing axis anteriorly, increases compressive forces on the injured vertebra and tensile forces on posterior elements, and leads to progressive biomechanical instability (2-5). For this reason, there is a growing consensus on the importance of restoring the anatomical structure of the vertebral body following injury, similarly to principles applied to other joints in the human body, with the aim of re-establishing its load-bearing capacity and rebalancing the vertebral functional unit (2-19).

Thus arises the concept of vertebral body repair, which consists of surgical procedures aimed at restoring the height and stability of the injured vertebral bodies. There are two main methods of vertebral body repair. The first method is interior vertebral body replacement, or augmentation, which requires preservation of the vertebral body’s peripheral shell—namely, the cortical ring and endplates—to contain the filler (Figures 1,2). If the cortical ring and vertebral endplates are compromised, a filler cannot be contained within the vertebral body, so the second method of vertebral body repair, total vertebral body replacement, must be used (Figure 3) (2-5). The damaged vertebral body and adjacent intervertebral discs are removed (corpectomy) and replaced with a solid biomaterial, sacrificing the discs since they cannot yet be reconstructed, with the goal of achieving solid fusion between the implant and adjacent vertebrae to accomplish anterior interbody arthrodesis (2-5).

The ideal biomaterial for use in both techniques of vertebral body repair should incorporate two essential properties. First, a biological component is required, meaning the material must be biocompatible, bioactive, and porous, in order to promote rapid and effective bone incorporation, integration, and bone fusion. The importance of achieving solid bone fusion (definitive biological stability, Figure 4) in vertebral body reconstruction is well recognized, as it is believed that bony healing may represent the most critical determinant of the patient’s long-term clinical outcome. This is due to the fact that it is the only factor capable of providing sustained dynamic stability to the reconstructed vertebral segment (2-5,20,21). Second, the biomechanical properties of the material are equally critical: it must have sufficient mechanical strength and stiffness—ideally with an elastic modulus similar to native bone—to support the complex, repetitive load-bearing patterns of the spine (provisional synthetic stability, Figure 4) and allow early patient mobilization and ambulation prior to full osseointegration or fusion, which are essential for promoting recovery and reducing postoperative complications (22-25). Achieving the ideal balance between mechanical load-bearing capacity and biological potential for progressive bone substitution remains one of the greatest challenges in the development of biomaterials for vertebral body repair. Nonetheless, this balance is essential, as the spine represents the most important load-bearing system in the human body, serving as the central axis; thus, the mechanical properties of the biomaterial are of crucial importance compared to other anatomical regions (26-42).

Diagrammatic representation of the factors responsible for provisional and definitive vertebral segment stability during the post-operative period of total or interior vertebral body replacement. Vertebral segment stability is initially provided temporarily either by the implant fixation systems in total vertebral replacement or by the strength properties of the vertebral filler or intravertebral implants in interior vertebral body replacement or vertebral augmentation. The osseointegration process begins and progresses gradually, always protected by the provisional fixation systems from excessive loads that could compromise the biological process of bone regeneration. At 6–8 weeks, if the process progresses favorably, the bone fusion will begin to play a role similar to that of the previous provisional fixation methods in load-bearing, relieving and sharing the load-bearing function with them. From this point forward, the role of the provisional fixation methods decreases, while that of bone fusion increases, and it is typically at 6 months post-operation that complete osteointegration occurs, with bone fusion becoming almost fully responsible for the stability of the vertebral segment, as the bone has incorporated the implant or replaced the vertebral filler.

Globally, the most commonly used biomaterials for vertebral body repair are polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) for vertebral augmentation and metallic cages for total replacement. Despite favorable and well-documented clinical outcomes, particularly regarding mechanical stability, both materials have biological limitations. They remain foreign bodies with excessive stiffness within the vertebral body, lacking biodegradability and bone substitution capacity, which compromises their ability to biomechanically and biologically replicate the native vertebra, leading to complications such as stress shielding, subsidence, implant migration, adjacent-level fractures, and increased infection risk (7,11,15,24,26,27,33-37,39-41). Furthermore, metallic implants generate imaging artifacts that hinder tumor follow-up and radiotherapy planning, highlighting the need for non-metallic, radiolucent implants that ensure stable reconstruction in oncologic patients (24,34-37). Understanding the current status of available biomaterials for vertebral body repair—including their outcomes, advantages, and limitations regarding bone fusion, incorporation, and integration—is therefore essential for selecting the most appropriate material for vertebral reconstruction. Our objective is to review and compile all relevant, up-to-date information on bone healing outcomes associated with vertebral body repair techniques applied in humans. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2025-1-250/rc).

Methods

A narrative review incorporating systematized elements of the literature search and study selection process was conducted by searching the MEDLINE/PubMed database. Two independent searches were conducted (Figures 5,6), the first analyzing the use of biomaterials in interior vertebral body replacement or augmentation and the second focusing on total vertebral body replacement (43). Discussion

Of all included studies (Tables 2-5), by descending level of evidence, 4.4% were Level II (prospective randomized controlled trials, prospective cohorts, and case-control studies), 25.3% were Level III (comparative cohorts and retrospective case-control studies), 69.8% were Level IV (case series), and 0.4% were Level V (technical notes). No Level I studies were identified on this review topic. Most studies were retrospective (72.9%) and case series (69.8%), highlighting the need for readers to remain aware of inherent limitations, including selection bias, information bias, and unmeasured confounding, which should be considered when interpreting each study (Tables 2-5) (250).

In the present review, 20.9% of studies relied solely on plain radiographs to assess bone fusion. while the remainder combined radiographs with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to improve evaluation of bone bridging (Tables 2-5). Imaging indicators of fusion include healing of simple fracture lines, callus formation, trabecular bone bridging between native bone and biomaterial, and bone ingrowth or formation within implants or at sites of resorbed cement. However, imaging assessment of bone callus formation can be challenging due to metal artifacts that mostly hinder the evaluation of fusion bridges on CT. Consequently, many studies rely on indirect criteria of fusion rather than direct assessment of bone bridging, including absence of implant failure, no motion on dynamic radiographs, and no radiolucent lines at the implant-endplate interface (33-42). Correlations between imaging findings and true bony fusion remain poorly understood, highlighting the need in this review to consider how each study defines and assesses bone fusion (Tables 2-5). Ultimately, only biopsy and histological analysis can definitively confirm bone healing, although their invasive nature limits their use; only two included studies employed this method (28,59,196,251-257). The variability in definitions of bone fusion, the different methods used to assess it, and the heterogeneity in underlying pathologies across the included studies represent important limitations of this review and should be considered when interpreting each study.

Vertebral body repair for tumoral disease requires special considerations. In many cases, given limited life expectancy, the goal is not bony fusion but immediate mechanical stabilization, often using the bioinert cement PMMA, allowing early mobilization and preservation of quality of life (132,135,153,165,171,179,184,187,191,198-201,203-205,210). These patients frequently have impaired bone healing due to prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, steroid use, poor bone quality, local necrosis, reduced vascularity after wide tumor resection, malnutrition, and other factors, increasing the risk of non-union, pseudoarthrosis, and implant failure (21,34,82,84,85,88,93,94,96,98,108,111,114,117,125,128,150). Therefore, their specific characteristics must be considered, and caution is warranted when comparing bone fusion rates (BFRs) with patients undergoing vertebral body repair for traumatic, degenerative, or deformity-related etiologies. Nevertheless, in oncologic patients with longer expected survival, achieving solid bone fusion is essential for long-term stability and reducing reconstruction failure. Treatment decisions should be guided by oncologic staging and survival prognosis, balancing biological fusion with immediate mechanical stabilization (211,213,216,217,219,220,222,227,230,233,235,236,247,248,258,259).

Interior vertebral body replacement techniques, also referred to as vertebral augmentation procedures, include vertebroplasty, kyphoplasty, and transpedicularly inserted static or expandable implants, the latter being also known as armed kyphoplasty (2-4,260). Three main biomaterial categories were identified for these procedures (Table 2): bioinert injectable cements (PMMA), bioactive injectable cements (CPC and CSC), and solid intravertebral implants used with cement or bone graft. This analysis included only 11.9% of Level II studies and 14.3% of Level III studies, with the majority being Level IV studies (73.8%). Within this group, most studies were prospective (59.5%) (Table 2) (250). Traditionally, for approximately 20 years, fractured vertebral bodies were augmented using morselized cancellous bone graft inserted through a posterior transpedicular approach to fill the vertebral body following disruption of its trabecular network. However, negative outcomes such as vertebral height loss, necrosis, and resorption of the graft led surgeons to abandon intracorporeal bone graft augmentation (253,261-278). More recent studies, however, have demonstrated improved outcomes when cancellous bone grafting is combined with contemporary pedicle screw fixation, kyphoplasty, or expandable intravertebral implants, which protect the graft and maintain vertebral body height until bone healing occurs; nevertheless, the evidence supporting these approaches remains preliminary (3,4,260,266,279-286).

Even so, following the decline in the use of intrasomatic bone grafting, vertebral augmentation techniques using synthetic fillers have expanded rapidly, with bone cements now representing the most widely used option. PMMA cement remains, by far, the most widely used biomaterial for vertebral body augmentation due to its provision of immediate mechanical stability (44,45). However, it is well established that PMMA, although effective in load-bearing applications, is far from being the ideal intravertebral filler. PMMA is biologically inert, non-resorbable, and lacks osteoconductive or osteointegrative properties. Histological studies consistently demonstrate a fibrous interface between PMMA and bone, indicating the absence of true osseointegration (27,44,45,64,253,255,256). PMMA also exhibits excessive stiffness relative to cancellous bone, contributing to stress shielding, bone resorption, and adjacent vertebral fractures. Additional drawbacks include thermal osteonecrosis and potential toxicity of monomers and additives (255,256,286). Therefore, PMMA is generally reserved for elderly patients or those with metastatic disease, where immediate mechanical stability is prioritized over long-term biological reconstruction. Although a vast number of studies have investigated PMMA cementoplasty, since the primary goal is often merely to achieve vertebral stabilization rather than true bone healing, the union status of fractured vertebrae after the procedure has not been thoroughly addressed. Consequently, the only studies identified reporting bone healing outcomes following PMMA cementoplasty comprise 9 studies (Table 2), in which the reported BFR ranged from 58.5% to 100% (44-46,48,50). However, caution is warranted when interpreting such high BFRs in the context of a biologically inert biomaterial, which by definition is incapable of bone incorporation even when derived from a prospective randomized clinical study (49). These elevated rates are closely related to the definition of union adopted in each study, primarily based on indirect imaging criteria (44,47-49). Most of the included PMMA studies define union as the presence of osseous bridging between adjacent vertebral bodies, absence of vertebral mobility, or intravertebral clefts—criteria that do not necessarily reflect true biological bone fusion (46-48). Accordingly, some of these studies report persistent residual mobility in 85.71% of cases and a BFR of 58.5% in vertebral nonunion cases, indicating limited bone healing (45,46). Nevertheless, several studies have evaluated trabecular bone bridging across the interspace between the PMMA and the adjacent vertebral body, as well as the disappearance of fracture lines on CT imaging, suggesting that bone healing may occur around the PMMA. In such cases, the PMMA subsequently remains as an inert spacer within the healed vertebral body (44,45,48,51).

Interest subsequently shifted toward bioactive ceramic cements, particularly CPC and CSC. These materials are biodegradable, osteoconductive, and allow gradual replacement by bone tissue (26-32,53-67). This approach results in a vertebral body with a biological reconstruction, meaning it is primarily composed of bone that has partially replaced the bone cement, thereby allowing biomechanical load transmission that closely resembles that of the native pre-injury vertebra. For this reason, it is regarded as a more physiological and biologically dynamic option for younger patients, who are likely to place greater long-term mechanical demands on the spine. The goal is to restore the anatomy and biomechanics of the spine as accurately as possible, thereby minimizing the risk of early degenerative changes as sequelae of the fracture. Furthermore, the formation of a fully osseous vertebral body at a young age promotes proper vascularization of its tissue, which facilitates responsiveness to systemic treatments such as antibiotics and chemotherapy—an outcome that would not be possible if the vertebra contained an avascular foreign material like PMMA (26-32,53-67). CPC exhibits porosity, which enhances osteoconduction through improved cellular adhesion and vascular invasion. Its elastic modulus is similar to that of cancellous bone, which enables a more physiological load transfer, thereby minimizing the risk of stress shielding and reducing the likelihood of fractures in adjacent vertebral bodies (26-32,53-67). CPC resorption is slow and often incomplete, with reported rates of 2–20.3% per year and approximately 22.9% at 10 years, while BFR can reach 100% (26-32,53-64). However, concerns remain regarding early mechanical stability, cement cracking, unpredictable remodeling, and risk of vertebral collapse if resorption outpaces bone formation (27-30,56,65,66,254,287-291). The key to maintain vertebral body height lies in achieving a balance between cement resorption and bone healing rates. If cement is resorbed more rapidly than new bone can form, the resulting bone defect will be filled with avascular fibrous tissue, leading to a reduction in the mechanical strength of the vertebral body, which will progressively collapse under load. In light of this, some authors do not recommend the use of CPC or limit its application in anatomical regions subjected to repetitive loading, such as the vertebrae (27-30,56,65,66,254,287-291). Meanwhile, CSC demonstrates faster resorption and complete replacement by bone but is associated with progressive vertebral height loss and kyphotic deformity, likely due to excessive resorption rates (30-32,65-67). Reported BFR ranges from 75% to 100% (Table 2). The disadvantages associated with PMMA, CPC, and CSC bone cements have sustained ongoing research into the development of an ideal filler for vertebral body augmentation, leading to the emergence of composite bone cements. These aim to combine the advantageous properties of existing biomaterials, particularly the mechanical strength of acrylic cements with the biodegradability of biologically active cements, to alleviate the previously mentioned disadvantages. The objective is either to endow bioactive cements with sufficient mechanical resistance or to render PMMA biologically active. Experimental formulations include PMMA combined with ceramics, bioglass, collagen, and other bioactive compounds (68-70,292-301). Two clinical studies comparing PMMA with MC-PMMA composites demonstrated improved osseointegration, with disappearance of the bone-cement interface and regenerated trabecular bone in the composite group (68,69). Despite these promising results, the ideal intravertebral filler remains undefined.

However, not only semi-liquid or pasty injectable fillers are used for intravertebral body augmentation, but also solid intravertebral implants, which may be either static or expandable devices, both applied via a posterior transpedicular approach. Intravertebral implants eliminate the risk of leakage associated with the semi-liquid phase application of biomaterials, as they are solid in nature. Static intravertebral devices commonly feature a porous morphology that promotes osteointegration through cell adhesion, invasion, and bone ingrowth, thereby facilitating the establishment of definitive late-stage secondary biological stability. Clinical studies on transpedicular hydroxyapatite blocks have reported BFR ranging from 95% to 100% (71-74). Porous titanium spacers demonstrated stability but were associated with height loss and adjacent fractures (302-307). Other devices, including HA/PA66 implants and interbody cages, showed favorable outcomes, although BFR was rarely reported (308-315). Porous titanium microspheres have shown early partial fusion, combining mechanical support with osteoconductivity (75).

Expandable intravertebral implants, such as vertebral body stent® (VBS®), SpineJack®, Osseofix®, and others, are widely used for vertebral augmentation, also known as “armed kyphoplasty” (2-4,51,76-78,260,281,316-333). Through their expansion mechanism, these implants enable direct fracture reduction, and the metal-cement or metal-bone graft combination serves as internal support for the vertebral endplates, theoretically allowing more effective long-term preservation of vertebral body height compared to balloon kyphoplasty without intrabody implants (2,51,76-78,260,281,316-333). Two main designs exist (Figure 2): space-occupying cavitary implants, such as VBS®, Osseofix®, and reduction implants, such as SpineJack® (2-4). Space-occupying implants (Figure 2A) create cavities filled with cement or bone graft, while reduction implants (Figure 2B) mainly improve vertebral body height without relevant occupied space, so they preserve trabecular structure and require less filler. Despite their highly promising results in the scientific literature, particularly due to their capacity to restore and maintain vertebral body height through a posterior percutaneous transpedicular approach, there are currently few studies addressing the status of bone fusion following vertebral stabilization with these implants, particularly considering that they are most frequently applied alongside PMMA bone cement (64,76). One study demonstrated trabecular bone formation on CT when bioactive composite fillers—calcium sulfate/calcium phosphate and hydroxyapatite/collagen—were used in combination with intracorporeal stents (64). Cementless Osseofix® implants achieved 100% BFR at 6–7.5 months (76,77).

Regarding vertebral body total replacement techniques (Figure 3), the choice of spacer to be placed in the vertebral body defect following corpectomy has varied in the literature. Given the limitations of structural bone graft spacers, including donor-site morbidity in autografts and the risks of delayed incorporation, non-incorporation, or infection with allografts, advances have led to the development of synthetic implants made from various biomaterials (334-350). Three main implant types are used: cavitary mesh cages, static solid implants, and expandable cages (Figure 7) (25,33-42). Cavitary-type implants (Figure 7) are usually shaped as hollow cylinders (mesh cage), designed to replicate the strong cortical ring of the vertebral body, and are subsequently filled with a material that simulates the cancellous bone of the trabecular network inside the vertebral body (351). The earliest biomaterials used for total vertebral body replacement emerged in the 1950s and 1960s and primarily involved PMMA bone cement. PMMA was not employed as a true implant but rather as a space-filling material, often combined with metallic elements such as Steinmann pins, screws, or Kirschner wires to provide mechanical anchorage (352-365). However, significant technical limitations were identified, including difficulties in molding PMMA to the corpectomy defect, poor bone–cement contact, risks related to handling, and thermal injury to adjacent neurological structures during polymerization (357-359,362,366-369). Consequently, PMMA as a space-filling mass rather than a true implant was excluded from this review. An exception was made for studies by Chen et al., who developed rigid PMMA implants with a hollow cylindrical design allowing insertion of bone graft. In total cervical vertebral body replacement, these implants achieved BFRs (as implant-endplate bone bridging and absence of segmental motion) of 80.4–85.71% during the first postoperative year and 100% in the second year (Table 3) (79,80). The authors suggested that the combination of rigid PMMA and internal bone graft resulted in an elastic modulus closer to native bone, promoting fusion and reducing stress shielding compared with titanium implants, while also generating fewer imaging artifacts and lower costs. Nonetheless, the presence of a biologically inert PMMA component raises concerns regarding long-term osseointegration (79,80).

Types of cages in total vertebral body replacement: mesh cage, a cavitary implant, which is hollow in order to accommodate bone graft, static solid cage, and expandable solid cage.

Subsequently, rigid monoblock implants—commonly referred to as cages—were developed for total vertebral body reconstruction. These nonbony load-bearing spacers evolved from early metallic designs made of stainless steel, cobalt-chromium, and titanium (25,33-42). Initially, these implants provided mechanical stability but lacked biological activity and were often combined with PMMA. Later generations incorporated intrinsic stabilization features and were used in combination with cancellous bone graft, with the implant providing early load-bearing support while protecting the graft until fusion occurred (370-383). Ceramic implants were also explored, initially using bioinert ceramics such as alumina and zirconia, followed by bioactive ceramics including calcium phosphate and bioglass (20,81-83,372,384,385). Bioinert ceramics demonstrated high biocompatibility but lacked osseointegration, limiting long-term stability, and were prone to brittle fracture under load (372,384,385). In contrast, bioactive ceramics promoted osseointegration and achieved BFRs of 95–100%, with complete resorption and replacement by bone within 1 to 3 years (20,81-83). Despite these advantages, brittleness remained a limitation.

Returning to metallic implants, in 1986, J. Harms introduced a non-expandable cylindrical cavitary implant made of metallic mesh, known as a mesh cage (Figure 7), which became and remains one of the most commonly selected implants for total vertebral body reconstruction (115,386). In this review, 85 studies employing metallic mesh cages were included, reporting BFRs ranging from 70% to 100%. The most frequently reported value was a BFR of 100%, while the lower rates occurred, as expected, in patients with tumor-related etiologies (Table 4). This group analysis included only 4.7% Level II studies, with the majority being Level III (32.9%) and Level IV (62.4%) studies. Most studies in this group were retrospective (85.9%) (250). The mesh cage design combines a rigid metallic periphery mimicking the cortical ring with a cancellous bone graft-filled interior replicating trabecular bone, aiming to create a biologically active neo-vertebral body, thereby enabling a reconstruction that biomechanically replicates the native vertebral body in terms of load absorption and distribution. This is particularly important for achieving definitive long-term secondary stability and for the harmonious load-sharing with adjacent levels, theoretically restoring the physiological biomechanics of the original spinal column more faithfully than implants based primarily on synthetic materials rather than bone tissue (3,4,115,116,260,281,351,386). The mesh cage provides effective early structural support (temporary primary stability) due to its robust metallic framework, while the serrated cutting edge ensures secure fixation to the endplates of the adjacent vertebral bodies, enhancing its resistance to translational and torsional shear forces. Additionally, its cavitary design allows for the containment of a considerable amount of bone graft that is subjected to compressive forces within the implant, forces that act directly on the bone graft rather than solely on the implant itself, which is beneficial as it promotes bone fusion (352,387). Despite satisfactory BFRs, high subsidence rates—sometimes exceeding 50% and reaching up to 93.3%—remain a major drawback (21,34-37,39-41,119-122,169-171,181,182,185-187,191,192,198,388). Subsidence is largely attributed to excessive implant stiffness, with an elastic modulus much higher than that of bone, and to a limited cage-endplate contact area, resulting in stress concentration at this interface. This can result in stress shielding, bone resorption, implant loosening, mechanical failure, implant migration, and an increased risk of fractures of the endplate and adjacent vertebrae. Although endcaps were introduced to increase the contact area, suboptimal adaptation to endplate geometry persists, potentially compromising fusion (21,34-37,39-41,97,107,113,116,117,125,126,129,134,137,150,163,173,175,177-178,388).

Solid static metallic implants subsequently emerged, particularly those made of porous metals, particularly titanium or tantalum. Their porous morphology is engineered to serve as an osteoconductive scaffold that promotes bone ingrowth, offering sufficient elasticity to accommodate micromovements and strains that return to their original state upon load release. This behavior stimulates bone formation while maintaining stable and robust mechanical support for the spine (95-102). We included in our review eight studies involving solid static metallic implants (Table 3), comprising five studies using porous titanium with BFR ranging from 76.9% to 100% and three studies using tantalum with BFR between 98.11% and 100% (95-102). All are retrospective case series (Level IV), except for one prospective randomized cohort study (Level II) (Table 3). Compared with mesh cages, porous titanium implants demonstrated reduced height loss and lower severe subsidence rates (97). However, high overall subsidence rates (up to 83%) persist, along with concerns related to excessive stiffness, stress shielding, corrosion, infection risk, and imaging artifacts (33,95-102). In summary, regarding metallic implants and considering the ideal characteristics of a biomaterial for vertebral body total replacement, these devices provide adequate biomechanical stability but exhibit limited biological performance. However, due to the mismatch in elastic modulus with native bone, they demonstrate excessive stiffness and consequently transmit increased loads to the surrounding bone, leading to higher risks of subsidence, migration, and stress shielding, while failing to promote a balanced load distribution conducive to bone fusion. Biologically, they remain essentially bioinert and non-resorbable foreign bodies, with bone fusion occurring primarily through bone bridging within and around the cage, as well as through osseointegration via bone ingrowth into the porous structure of the implant. Their long-term stability depends on the formation of these bone bridges; in their absence, persistent implant mobility inevitably leads to mechanical failure (33,95-102). Therefore, significant biomechanical and biological limitations remain in what are currently among the most widely used implants for total vertebral body replacement, driving the development of new biomaterials.

To address imaging limitations and radiotherapy planning challenges—particularly in oncologic patients—radiolucent implants were developed. Carbon fiber cages exhibit elastic modulus similar to cortical bone and produce minimal imaging artifacts, facilitating postoperative assessment and improving radiotherapy accuracy (24,84-86,387-394). However, their bioinert nature limits osseointegration, making them suboptimal for long-term reconstruction due to the lack of favorable biological properties. Three studies included in this review reported BFRs ranging from 80% to 97.5%, all of which were retrospective case series (Level IV) (Table 3) (84-86).

Initially, some authors employed solid plastic implants, such as polyethylene, for vertebral body reconstruction; however, these early attempts were unsuccessful and subsequently not pursued further (395). Much later, plastic polymers re-emerged as viable alternatives for anterior reconstruction following total vertebral body replacement, particularly polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and polyetherketoneketone (PEKK). These implants are primarily static and hollow and can often accommodate bone grafts or their substitutes within their interior. More recently, expandable cages made from this material have also become available (33,34,87-94). Despite extensive experience with interbody fusion between two vertebral bodies following discectomy, studies on the use of PEEK cages for vertebral body reconstruction after corpectomy remain relatively scarce. Eight studies included in this review reported BFRs of 70–100% (33,87-93), comprising five retrospective case series (Level IV), one prospective case series (Level IV), and two retrospective comparative cohort studies (Level III) (Table 3) (250). These radiolucent materials possess elastic properties closer to cancellous bone, reducing stress shielding and subsidence. Biomechanical studies have shown lower endplate pressures and greater load transfer through bone graft compared with titanium cages (396). However, concerns remain regarding the use of these biomaterial implants for reconstruction after corpectomy, due to their relatively low mechanical strength for structural load-bearing in a mechanically demanding region such as the vertebral body. This limitation stems from their elastic modulus being closer to that of cancellous rather than cortical bone, increasing the risk of cage fracture under load, particularly in cases of delayed or non-union, where the implant continues to bear the load in the absence of bone fusion (Figure 4). Additionally, thermoplastic biomaterials, although biocompatible, are biologically inactive; like metallic and carbon fiber implants, they remain foreign bodies within the vertebral body and do not undergo osseointegration, potentially compromising long-term dynamic stability, particularly if interbody bone bridges around or within the implant fail to adequately support the reconstructed segment (33,87-93). In this context, CFRP cages were developed to enhance mechanical strength while maintaining radiolucency. This combination allows for the production of cages with an elastic modulus closer to that of cortical bone, theoretically enabling the transmission of more physiological mechanical forces to the endplate bone and within the implant, thereby reducing stress shielding and favoring fusion (34,94). Only two studies assessing BFRs for this biomaterial were included: one technical note (Level V) and one retrospective comparative cohort study (Level III), which limits the strength of the conclusions, particularly regarding BFRs of up to 100% and significantly lower subsidence rates compared with titanium mesh cages (34,94,250).

More recently, expandable implants have been developed, characterized by their intrinsic capacity for expansion and in situ adaptation to the bone defect resulting from corpectomy. These expandable implants are usually modular and most commonly made of titanium, although implants made of PEEK are also available (Tables 3,5) (42,93,194,196,204,212,397). In this review, we included 58 studies involving titanium expandable cages and one study using an expandable PEEK cage, with BFR ranging from 23.5% to 100% (Tables 3,5). The most frequently reported BFR was 100%, with, as expected, lower fusion rates observed in tumor-related cases for the reasons discussed earlier in this section. Regarding titanium expandable cages (Table 5), none of the included studies were Level II, with 20.7% classified as Level III and 79.3% as Level IV studies (250). Also, most studies in this group were retrospective (72.4%), which limits the robustness of the findings. The main advantage of expandable implants lies in their initially smaller dimensions, which allow insertion through less invasive approaches, such as posterior access, reaching their final size only once positioned at the site of the bone defect. Moreover, they theoretically enable a more precise in situ adaptation to the corpectomy defect compared to static implants, thereby theoretically reducing the risk of becoming loose or generating excessive compression. Additionally, their expansion capability allows the application and control of compressive forces on the adjacent endplates, theoretically providing a mechanical stimulus that promotes intervertebral bone fusion (42,93,194,196,204,212,397). The design of these implants has undergone continuous refinement. Both the expansion mechanism and the locking system, which ensures the implant stays in the desired position, have been enhanced. Additionally, the fixation system to the endplates, now offering multiple angulation options for the terminal plates and fixation pegs, has become more secure. Implants have also been developed to internally accommodate bone graft material and to expand the central cavity of the vertebral endplates, thereby increasing the contact area between the graft and the endplate and enhancing the likelihood of more effective intervertebral bone fusion (204).

Despite these developments and the excellent BFR rates reported in the included studies, the use of expandable versus static implants remains controversial in the scientific literature, particularly regarding their respective mechanical advantages and disadvantages (6,93,194,196,204,212,352,398). Static cages require precise sizing, which can sometimes be difficult to achieve, and may lead to endplate damage or subsidence if improperly fitted. In contrast, expandable cages allow intraoperative height adjustment and improved endplate contact, but carry risks of overdistraction, with consequent subsidence and mechanical failure (93,194,204,212,352). Additionally, although expandable designs offer advantages in alignment correction and minimally invasive placement, their impact on fusion remains debated, particularly regarding load transmission through the contained bone graft. Some authors suggest that the graft may not receive sufficient mechanical stimulation, as load transfer may occur primarily through the expanded cage, which may not favor interbody fusion (93,194,204,212,352). Supplementary fixation, such as anterior plating, significantly improves BFR and reduces subsidence; nevertheless, the development of more effective integrated fixation systems within the cages themselves may eventually eliminate the need for additional plate application (247).

To enhance osseointegration, metallic, ceramic, carbon-based, and thermoplastic polymer implants are increasingly combined with biologically active compounds, creating composites that merge high structural performance with strong biological properties to leverage the strengths of each component while minimizing their limitations. Coated implants are currently available to optimize surface topography by increasing its roughness, which promotes better cellular adhesion and invasion, ultimately improving osseointegration (35-41,103-108). Hydroxyapatite-based composites, particularly HA/PA66 as a hollow cylindrical implant designed to be filled with bone graft material, have demonstrated promising results (31-37,95-100,399-403). This composite mimics natural bone composition and mechanical properties, promotes osteoconduction and osseointegration, and is radiolucent. Thirteen studies included in this review reported BFRs ranging from 72.7% to 100%, most exceeding 90% (Table 3) (35-41,103-108). None of the included studies were Level II, with 53.8% classified as Level III and 46.2% as Level IV studies (250). Also, most studies in this group were retrospective (84.6%), which weakens the validity of the conclusions. Comparative studies consistently demonstrated higher BFRs and lower subsidence rates for HA/PA66 compared with titanium mesh cages (35-41,107). Despite its favorable biological and mechanical properties and promising outcomes, further experience and higher-level evidence studies are needed to better define the advantages of this biomaterial compared with conventional options.

Three-dimensional (3D) printing has advanced vertebral body reconstruction by enabling patient-specific implants that replicate native bone macro- and microarchitecture, with controlled porosity that enhances osteoconduction, osseointegration, and mechanical properties closer to those of native bone, thereby reducing stress shielding and subsidence (41,92,95-99,172,257). Based on preoperative imaging integrated into computer-aided design, fully customized implants can be produced to accurately match corpectomy defects and endplates, homogenize load distribution, improve primary stability, maximize bone-implant contact, and reduce surgical time and complexity (22,23,41,98,150,259,404-410). Seven studies included in this review reported 100% BFR with 3D-printed implants (Tables 3,4) (41,92,95-99,172).

Following the analysis of the included articles, it becomes clear that we are still far from a biomaterial with ideal properties for partial or total vertebral body repair. Table 6 summarizes the main characteristics of the currently available biomaterials for partial or total vertebral body replacement, organized according to their respective advantages and disadvantages. Finally, it is important to emphasize that, beyond selecting an appropriate vertebral body replacement implant with suitable material properties, proper surgical technique—including precise centering and angular adaptation of the implant on prepared endplates and careful preservation of subchondral cortical bone—is essential to maximize bone-implant contact, promote osseointegration, ensure durable fixation, and minimize the risks of subsidence, migration, and mechanical failure (22,23,35,36,87,150,193,244,406,407).

This review has several limitations. First, there is considerable heterogeneity in how bone fusion outcomes are defined and measured, as well as in the underlying pathologies across studies, which likely affects the comparability of results. Most included studies are retrospective and of Level IV evidence, introducing potential bias and limiting the strength of the conclusions. Finally, follow-up periods were generally short to intermediate, restricting the generalizability of findings to long-term outcomes. However, to date, this is, to the best of our knowledge, the most comprehensive review of bone fusion outcomes for biomaterials used in vertebral body repair, covering both interior and total replacements.

Conclusions

Current biomaterials for vertebral body repair achieve BFRs and effective osseointegration; however, no currently available biomaterial or implant fully replicates the biological and mechanical properties of the native vertebral body, due to the challenge of balancing mechanical load-bearing capacity with the biological potential for progressive bone substitution. Instead, a wide range of biomaterials is available, each with specific characteristics that should be carefully matched by the surgeon to the requirements of vertebral body reconstruction in a given injury and patient. Composite biomaterials that combine the most desirable properties of multiple materials, along with 3D-printing technology, offer promising strategies to enhance osseointegration and fusion. Further high-quality research, particularly multicenter and long-term studies, is needed to optimize biomaterials that combine immediate mechanical stability with long-term biological integration and fusion, aiming for a more physiologically balanced spine that closely resembles its pre-injury state. Stratification by underlying disease, together with standardized and rigorous assessment of bone fusion outcomes—for example, using quantitative CT analysis—is also important and recommended to guide the selection of the most appropriate biomaterial for each indication. Achieving this goal requires close interdisciplinary collaboration among medicine, surgery, and tissue engineering to expand the availability of high-quality solutions for spinal repair.
