Evolutionary signals in metabolic networks of insect endosymbionts revealed through comparative topological modeling
Authors: Mariana Reyes-Prieto, David José Martínez-Cano, Mercè Llabrés, Pere Palmer-Rodríguez, Carlos Vargas-Chávez, Luis Delaye, Rosario Gil, Andrés Moya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the organization and evolution of metabolic networks is essential for uncovering how organisms adapt to changing environments. Whereas free-living bacteria typically maintain robust and redundant metabolic systems, endosymbiotic bacteria undergo extreme genome reduction during their adaptation to intracellular life. This process results in highly streamlined and interconnected metabolic networks, in some cases smaller than the theoretical minimum required for sustaining independent cellular function.ResultsUsing a large-scale comparative framework, we analyzed 101 genomes of insect endosymbiotic bacteria by computing two metabolic network models: metabolite- and reaction-based. We found strong correlations between genome size and key topological properties, including clustering coefficient, network diameter, and number of nodes, indicating that genome reduction directly constrains metabolic network architecture. Despite extensive gene loss, endosymbiotic metabolic networks retain scale-free organization, suggesting the preservation of essential connectivity and robustness. Furthermore, clustering analyses revealed that network topology reflects phylogenetic relationships across bacterial taxa, demonstrating that metabolic organization retains evolutionary signals even in the most reduced genomes.ConclusionsOur findings show that the metabolic networks of insect endosymbiotic bacteria preserve clear evolutionary imprints, revealing a deep connection between genomic reduction, network structure, and phylogenetic history. The complementary use of metabolite- and reaction-based models provide a powerful framework for exploring how symbiotic evolution reshapes metabolic systems while maintaining essential biological organization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12869-7.Keywords: Metabolic networks, Symbiosis, Endosymbionts, Genome evolution, Genome reduction, Metabolic building blocks, MetaDAG

Background

Bacteria are among the most diverse and abundant organisms on Earth, with billions of species occupying nearly every conceivable niche. Estimates suggest that around 60% of bacterial species are associated with a host, either as transient residents or long-term symbionts, highlighting the importance of bacteria-host interactions as a fundamental ecological and evolutionary process [1]. Within humans alone, approximately 39 trillion bacterial cells reside on or within the body, outnumbering human cells and forming a dynamic ecosystem known as the microbiome [2]. These bacterial communities engage in a wide spectrum of symbiotic relationships, ranging from mutualistic (beneficial to both partners) to commensal (beneficial to bacteria without harming the host) to pathogenic (harmful to the host), that influence host development, health, and survival. Host-associated bacteria collectively account for a significant fraction of Earth’s global bacterial biomass, underlining their ecological significance [3].

Within this vast pool of symbiotic relationships, endosymbiotic bacteria of insects have emerged as a particularly intriguing group due to their intimate associations with hosts and profound evolutionary adaptations. Endosymbiotic bacteria reside within host cells or tissues, often in specialized organs called bacteriomes. These bacteria play essential roles in the physiology and ecology of their hosts, particularly in nutrient-poor or specialized environments. For example, endosymbionts can provide their hosts with novel phenotypic traits, such as synthesizing essential nutrients (amino acids or vitamins) that the host cannot obtain from its diet, breaking down plant polymers, detoxifying harmful compounds, and even protecting the host from environmental stressors and natural enemies [4–6]. Such functional contributions enable insect hosts to exploit new ecological niches, driving their diversification and evolutionary success. Insects, the most species-rich group among animals, owe part of their remarkable evolutionary radiation to these microbial partnerships, which are considered major drivers of evolutionary innovation [6–9].

The availability of endosymbiont genomes has opened new avenues for exploring the mechanisms underpinning their evolutionary integration within host cells. Understanding the processes of symbiotic integration, genomic reduction, and functional specialization requires genomic and metabolic analyses that go beyond gene-level studies. One powerful approach for addressing these questions is the reconstruction of metabolic networks—a systems-level representation of an organism’s complete set of biochemical reactions—based on genomic data [10]. This methodology is commonly used within the reverse ecology framework, where reconstructed metabolic networks are analyzed to infer ecological and evolutionary properties [11].

Metabolic networks are not only central to understanding the biochemical capabilities of an organism but also offer unique insights into its evolutionary history. These networks consist of nodes (metabolites) and edges (reactions), whose organization reflects the selective pressures and environmental constraints acting upon the organism over time. Variations in network topology—such as clustering coefficient, network diameter, and node connectivity—are subject to different evolutionary forces, leading to identifiable “signatures” that can reveal the evolutionary pathways and ecological contexts in which the networks evolved. For instance, interactions with the host environment or with coexisting symbiotic partners can impose constraints on metabolic networks, shaping their structure and functionality [12].

Endosymbiotic bacteria of insects represent an extreme case of genomic and metabolic reduction, where gene loss is driven by the transition to an obligate intracellular lifestyle [13]. In some cases, these bacteria undergo such drastic genome reduction that they reach a state referred to as “symbionelle”, in which their genomes are smaller than what is theoretically considered sufficient for sustaining minimal cellular life [14]. In these highly reduced systems, the metabolic capabilities of endosymbionts are often complemented by the metabolic context of the host or by the presence of other co-symbionts. For example, bacterial consortia such as Buchnera and Serratia, Tremblaya and Moranella, or Sulcia and Baumannia collectively fulfill the host’s metabolic needs, forming a tightly integrated symbiotic system [11].

Such associations introduce unique constraints on the structure and evolution of metabolic networks, leading to highly interconnected but simplified systems. These networks likely exhibit distinctive topological signatures that reflect the selective pressures and functional dependencies inherent to obligate symbiosis. Furthermore, the availability of closely related bacterial genomes—subject to similar evolutionary pressures—provides an opportunity to investigate metabolic evolution within a phylogenetic framework. By comparing the metabolic networks of endosymbiotic bacteria across taxonomic groups, it becomes possible to trace evolutionary patterns and disentangle the processes shaping these reduced yet functionally critical systems [15].

Historically, studies on metabolic networks have mainly focused on small-scale analyses, typically examining one or a few organisms at a time or emphasizing specific pathways within defined biochemical environments [16–18]. However, large-scale comparative approaches that integrate genomic data across multiple species are gaining ground, especially with the availability of more powerful computational resources and artificial intelligence [19–21].

A typical metabolic network reconstruction study includes harvesting metabolic information from different resources. KEGG [22–24] is a commonly used, manually curated, and high-quality database that includes metabolic information of genomes. Researchers commonly perform additional validation when reconstructing networks from KEGG-derived data; they are often used as initial drafts and may be refined through manual curation, integration of organism-specific literature, or comparison with experimental data. The extent of validation typically depends on the intended application of the model. For predictive or quantitative analyses, further validation, such as flux simulations or gene essentiality testing, it is standard practice. However, in comparative or exploratory studies using well-curated genomes, KEGG-based reconstructions are commonly used with minimal additional validation, as the underlying data already meet high-quality standards.

In this work, we present a large-scale evolutionary analysis of the metabolic networks of 143 bacteria, including 109 endosymbiotic bacteria of insects and 34 closely related bacteria considered as outgroups, by computing two metabolic network reconstruction models, a metabolite-based model and a reaction-based model [25, 26], from KEGG-derived data. Furthermore, we compared them to a theoretical minimal network derived from the minimal gene set proposed to sustain cellular life [27].

Our results confirm that insect endosymbionts possess some of the smallest metabolic networks inferred to date, in some cases even smaller than theoretical minimal metabolic networks [28]. To investigate patterns of metabolic evolution, we analyzed the network topology within taxonomic groups at the genus level, constructed a “pan metabolic network” (analogous to the concept of a pangenome [29, 30]) representing all pathways across species, calculated their core metabolic network, and analyzed the networks within and between both the metabolite-based and the reaction-based models. In the metabolite-based approach, we detected significant correlations between key network parameters, such as clustering coefficient, network diameter, and node number within taxonomic groups. In the reaction-based approach, we calculated Metabolic Building Blocks (MBBs) [25] and identified correlations between metabolic distances and taxonomic distances derived from molecular phylogenies.

Our findings provide strong evidence that evolutionary traces (i.e., conserved topological features of metabolic networks that persist despite extensive genome reduction) are embedded in the metabolic networks of endosymbiotic bacteria of insects. These results underscore the utility of large-scale metabolic network analyses in revealing the evolutionary forces and constraints that shape symbiotic systems. By identifying topological and functional patterns across phylogenetically diverse bacteria, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of how metabolic evolution influences the adaptation of organisms to specialized ecological niches.

Materials and methods

Genomes of endosymbiotic bacteria of insects and their outgroups

To investigate the genomic and metabolic evolution of endosymbiotic bacteria of insects, we utilized publicly available genomic data from two well-established databases. The genomic information for insect-associated endosymbiotic bacteria was obtained from the SymGenDB database [31, 32], a curated resource dedicated to symbiotic bacterial genomes. In parallel, we sourced genomic data from the Microbial Genome Database for taxonomically closely related free-living/non-symbiont bacterial species, which were used as comparative outgroups to root the evolutionary analyses [33, 34].

Our initial search identified a total of 169 bacterial genomes associated with insects in SymGenDB. However, not all genomes corresponded to endosymbionts. To ensure the accuracy and specificity of our dataset, we systematically filtered out bacterial genomes that were associated with insects but did not meet the criteria for being endosymbiotic, such as those representing transient, non-obligate, or non-intracellular bacteria. This refinement process resulted in a final selection of 109 genomes of endosymbiotic bacteria for further analysis.

To provide a robust evolutionary context, we included 34 genomes of free-living/non-symbiont bacteria chosen to represent phylogenetically related bacterial lineages. These bacteria served as important reference points (or outgroups) for rooting the evolutionary trajectories and patterns observed in endosymbiotic bacteria. Together, the final dataset used for this study consisted of a total of 143 bacterial genomes (Supplementary Table ST1).

The selected endosymbiotic bacteria span multiple bacterial phyla, reflecting the diversity of lineages that have transitioned into intracellular, host-dependent lifestyles. Specifically, these genomes were distributed across the following taxonomic phyla: Actinomycetota (3 genomes), Pseudomonadota (79), Bacteroidota (20), Elusimicrobiota (1), Fusobacteriota (1), Mycoplasmatota (2), and Spirochaetota (3). To explore evolutionary signal at different scales, we applied Bayesian inference at two independent levels. First, a Bayesian phylogenetic analysis was performed on the complete dataset to examine overall relationships among the endosymbionts considered (Fig. 1). Second, the same approach was applied separately within major taxonomic groups, using their respective free-living/non-symbiont relatives as outgroups, in order to assess coevolutionary patterns between taxonomic affiliation and metabolic network topological parameters. This grouping strategy allowed us to examine patterns of metabolic network evolution and genomic reduction within and across taxonomic groups (Supplementary Table ST1 and Fig. 1).

Metabolic networks and metabolic directed acyclic graphs

For each genome, the number of metabolic pathways was estimated based on KEGG pathway annotations associated with the reactions encoded in that genome. Specifically, reactions inferred from KEGG orthology assignments were mapped to KEGG pathway maps, and the total number of distinct pathway maps represented was used as a proxy for metabolic functional breadth. Although KEGG pathways are curated functional groupings rather than discrete biological entities, this metric provides a standardized and widely used summary of metabolic coverage.

We implemented two different models of metabolic networks: (i) the metabolite-based model, where nodes are metabolites, and (ii) the reaction-based model, where nodes are reactions. For these analyses, all metabolic pathways, metabolic reactions, and compound elements of each bacterial species present in our data set were retrieved from the KEGG database [22–24]. In the metabolite-based model, for each organism, we considered the compounds present in each of its pathways to obtain the set of compounds of our metabolite-based network. So, for each MN, the metabolites (and only the metabolites) are the nodes, while the edges represent the metabolic reactions in the following way: there is a direct link from metabolite A to metabolite B if there is a reaction that consumes A and produces B. When a reaction is reversible, there are two edges between the involved nodes, one in each direction.

In the reaction-based model, we constructed a reaction graph as follows: the nodes are the metabolic reactions, and there is a directed edge from a reaction R to a reaction R’ if, and only if, some product metabolite of R is also a substrate of R’. That is, the product of R and the substrate of R’ have some metabolite in common. In this reaction-based model, following the methodology introduced by Alberich et al. [25], we also constructed the metabolic Directed Acyclic Graphs (m-DAG for short). An m-DAG is obtained from a reaction graph by collapsing every strongly connected component in the reaction graph into a single node, called a Metabolic Building Block (MBB for short). More precisely, the strongly connected components in the reaction graph are those pairs of reactions, R and R’, such that there is a path from R to R’ and there is also a path from R’ to R in the reaction graph, i.e., generating a cycle. As a result of collapsing these biconnected reactions into a single node, we obtain a directed graph with no cycles, which in the graph theory setting is called a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). Hence, we constructed the corresponding m-DAG from every reaction graph.

The two models considered here are essentially different. In the metabolite-based model, we obtain a huge and dense MN that deeply describes the metabolism, but it is difficult to handle, and only the topological parameters of these networks have been determined. In the reaction-based model, we reduce the size of the network, and we deeply describe the relationships among the reactions present in the MN. In addition, by considering the m-DAGs, we were able to easily analyze and compare in detail groups of MNs of interest.

Metabolic networks were constructed directly from KEGG-annotated reactions without removing low–molecular-weight or highly connected currency metabolites (e.g. H₂O, H⁺, CO₂, ATP/ADP, NAD(P)H). Although such compounds can generate highly connected nodes, retaining them ensures a consistent representation of reaction connectivity across all genomes and allows for unbiased comparative topological analyses. Note that, for both metabolic models, the organism-specific gene content was used to constrain these KEGG-derived networks. Only reactions associated with annotated genes in each genome were included, and full pathways were not added indiscriminately.

Topological parameters of the MNs

The topological parameters and centrality measures of each metabolite-based MN were calculated with the Cytoscape Network Analyzer [35]. We used 13 quantitative descriptors that have been effectively applied in other analyses [36, 37], which include (1) number of nodes; (2) number of edges; (3) clustering coefficient (CI) for each MN, which is the average of the clustering coefficients for all nodes in the network, measured as the degree of interconnectivity in the neighborhood of a node. The average number of neighbors is calculated as the sum of in-degree and out-degree (i.e., total degree) for each node, averaged across the network; (4) connected components, where each connected component contains any pair of nodes connected by a path within the component; (5) largest connected component (size, i.e., the number of nodes of the biggest connected component); (6) mean of the size of the connected components; (7) shortest paths percentage, considering that the length of a path is the number of edges included in it (there can be multiple paths connecting any two given nodes, so this parameter gives the percentage of paths with the minimum length); (8) characteristic path length, which gives the expected distance between two connected nodes (the distance between two nodes is the length of the shortest path connecting them); (9) network diameter, which indicates the largest distance between two nodes; (10) the average number of neighbors, which indicates the average connectivity of a node in a network; (11) isolated nodes; (12) self-loops; and (13) multi-edge node pairs, which indicates how often neighboring nodes are linked by more than one edge [12]. In this model, if a reaction is reversible, a multi-edge occurs between all substrates and products.

Comparative evolution of the genome and metabolism

Three different procedures were employed to compare genomic data with metabolic processes by using both genomic and metabolic data to reconstruct the evolutionary history of endosymbionts. In the first one, we constructed a phylogenetic tree with PhyloPhlAn v3.0 [38, 39], software that takes advantage of whole-genome sequence data and uses its curated database of 400 universal proteins to build a highly accurate and resolved phylogenomic tree. This integrated pipeline employs MUSCLE version 3.8 [40] for the alignment of amino acids and FastTree version 2.1 [41] to build the tree, which is generated using the ‘minimum-evolution interchanges and subtree-pruning-regrafting’ and approximate maximum likelihood joining (JTT + CAT model), as recommended by the authors for large datasets.

In the second procedure, we considered the outcome from the topological analysis and normalized each metric to derive a hierarchical clustering with the default parameters of the hclust function in R [42]. To establish which MN metric correlates with bacterial evolution, we used the software BayesTraits V3 [43]. We first employed the methodology for the complete data set and later by taxonomic groups at the genus level, where every tree was rooted by an output with a closely related free-living organism, to have a clear and robust identification of the evolutionary relationship of the organisms under study. BayesTraits works by comparing two different models on the same data, one in which a correlation is assumed and another one where the correlation is set to zero. We used the Continuous Random Walk model and Markov Chain Monte Carlo analysis to estimate the marginal likelihood of this comparison. Then, we computed the log Bayes Factor (BF) between the two runs, where the model that estimated the correlation was the complex model (the one where the correlation is not assumed). The BF interpretation is as follows: if the logBF is < 2, there is weak evidence of a correlation between traits; if it is ≥ 2, there is positive evidence, and the higher the number, the stronger the evidence. BayesTraits software takes as input at least 50 phylogenetic trees, the result of sampling the process of the reconstruction of a Bayesian phylogeny every 100th tree, as well as a text file with the matrices of the trait data to calculate the marginal likelihood. This Bayesian phylogeny was calculated using MrBayes software [44, 45]. This software was also used to construct the phylogenies (and their sampling) of each taxonomic group under study, since they were also needed for the evolutionary tests. The alignment file produced by PhyloPhlan was used as an input for the Bayesian phylogenetic reconstruction using the GTR model [46] as suggested by JModelTest2 [47]. Bayesian phylogenetic inference was conducted using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to approximate the posterior distribution of trees and model parameters. Two independent runs of 500,000 iterations were performed, with every 100th iteration sampled to reduce autocorrelation. The first 7,500 sampled trees were discarded as burn-in. Posterior probabilities were calculated as the proportion of sampled trees containing each clade and summarized on a 50% majority-rule consensus tree, interpreted as nodal support; these values represent posterior probabilities rather than bootstrap support. Finally, we used the traits from the BayesTraits tests that resulted in positive evidence of coevolution to infer the metabolic distances between our dataset by applying a hierarchical clustering analysis, where we first standardized all the parameters individually.

For the third procedure, distances between all the reaction graphs were calculated based on the distances between the corresponding m-DAGs with the software MetaDAG [26]. The distance between two m-DAGs described in Alberich et al. [25] is calculated by computing the similarities between their nodes, that is, their MBBs. Namely, the similarity between two MBBs is calculated using a maximum weighted bipartite matching of their reactions, where edge weights represent reaction similarity. This score is normalized by the size of the larger MBB. To compare two m-DAGs, the same approach is applied: a bipartite graph is constructed between their MBBs using MBB similarity scores, matched optimally, and normalized by the size of the larger m-DAG. Because the presence or absence of specific reactions is directly determined by the genomic content, the topology of the m-DAG reflects the organism’s metabolic potential. Consequently, distances between m-DAGs—calculated through structural comparisons of these graphs—quantify differences in metabolic organization that arise from underlying genomic variation.

To identify conserved and variable metabolic features, we defined the core m-DAG and the pan m-DAG of all endosymbiotic bacteria in the dataset. First, the core m-DAG, which includes the MBBs shared across all organisms. That is, we first consider the set of reactions present in all bacteria in our dataset; from this set of reactions, we construct the corresponding reaction graph (core reaction graph) and associated m-DAG. Next, we obtained the pan m-DAG, defined by the representation of the union of all reactions across the dataset, for which we construct the corresponding reaction graph (pan reaction graph) and the associated m-DAG. This approach parallels the concepts of the core genome and pangenome [29, 30].

Furthermore, an analysis of the connectivity of all constructed networks was conducted with the poweRlaw package in R [48] to assess if they follow a power law distribution (Supplementary Table ST2, with the mathematical methodology fully explained). This analysis was performed to determine whether network connectivity exhibits scale-free behavior, which would indicate non-random, hub-dominated organization with implications for robustness, functional integration, and evolutionary constraints. In this context, a scale-free network refers to a network in which most nodes have few connections, while a small number of nodes act as highly connected hubs, a topology commonly observed in biological systems. Because the networks are directed, node connectivity was decomposed into in-degree, out-degree, and total degree. In-degree captures the extent to which nodes act as convergence points receiving interactions, whereas out-degree reflects their influence as sources of interactions. Total degree provides a direction-agnostic measure of overall connectedness. Analyzing these components separately allows detection of asymmetric connectivity patterns that would be obscured by total degree alone.

Finally, to assess the structural similarity between the metabolite-based metabolic networks (MNs) and phylogenetic relationships, we computed the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) between two dendrograms derived from different data sources: one based on genetic data (the phylogenetic tree resulting from PhyloPhlan) and the other from hierarchical clustering of the MN parameters resulting from Cytoscape. We calculated NMI values across a range of cluster counts (1 to 60), quantifying how consistently taxa were grouped in both trees. This approach allowed us to evaluate the degree of convergence between metabolic and evolutionary signals at varying levels of taxonomic resolution.

Results

Evolutionary patterns in the metabolic networks of insect endosymbionts

To investigate the evolutionary imprints within the metabolic networks (MNs) of insect endosymbiotic bacteria, we performed coevolution analyses using BayesTraits. Our goal was to determine whether the extensive genome reduction observed in these bacteria is mirrored by structural transformations in their MNs.

The complete Bayesian phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) depicts the dataset used in this study and shows the bacterial lineages analyzed, highlighting groups tested for correlated evolution using Bayes Factor statistics (see Supplementary Table ST3). We then repeated this analysis at every genus level, restricting comparisons to endosymbionts and their corresponding outgroup relatives, to specifically test for coevolutionary signal between taxonomic groups and metabolic network topology. Evidence for coevolution was assessed using the Bayes Factor, with logBF > 2 interpreted as positive support for correlated evolution, fully described in Materials and Methods. As anticipated, genome size exhibited a strong positive relationship with two key topological properties of metabolic networks—network diameter (the longest path between nodes) and number of nodes (representing metabolites or reactions). These parameters co-evolved with genome size across most lineages, consistent with the direct influence of gene loss on network topology during endosymbiotic evolution.

Notably, a positive coevolutionary signal was also detected for the clustering coefficient in six genera. The clustering coefficient quantifies the degree of interconnectivity among neighboring nodes and reflects local redundancy in biochemical processes. High clustering implies local robustness, whereas reduced clustering indicates fragmentation and loss of redundancy, hallmarks of metabolic streamlining. Thus, the correlation between clustering coefficient and genome size underscores a profound structural reorganization of metabolic networks accompanying genome reduction. Detailed topological parameters for all species are reported in Supplementary Table ST4.

Nearly all MNs examined displayed power-law degree distributions (Supplementary Table ST2), indicating that these systems are scale-free networks. Such organization, characterized by a few highly connected hubs and many weakly connected nodes, is typical of biological systems and suggests evolutionary optimization for robustness and efficiency. In practical terms, this indicates that despite extensive genome reduction, endosymbiont metabolic networks retain a core of highly connected metabolites and reactions. Therefore, endosymbiotic bacteria have preserved these topological hallmarks, implying that even minimal metabolic systems maintain efficient connectivity patterns.

This finding carries both biological and methodological implications. It is consistent with previous studies showing that genome reduction in endosymbiotic bacteria does not lead to a random collapse of metabolic organization, but instead preserves a core set of highly connected reactions and metabolites essential for cellular function [6, 9, 12, 49–51]. Biologically, it reinforces that natural selection favors network architectures, ensuring essential metabolic flow despite gene loss. Methodologically, scale-free topology enhances analytical robustness, as power-law distributions are relatively insensitive to missing or noisy data. In this context, power-law behavior refers to a degree distribution in which most nodes have few connections, whereas a small number of nodes act as highly connected hubs. In metabolic networks, such distributions indicate that a limited set of metabolites or reactions play a central role in maintaining connectivity, even as peripheral components are lost. The persistence of this pattern in highly reduced genomes suggests that core metabolic connectivity is retained despite extensive genome erosion. Consequently, power-law behavior not only captures evolutionary signatures but also validates the use of network-based methods for studying reductive evolution.

Correlation between genome size and pathway complexity

To validate these evolutionary trends, we examined the correlation between genome size and number of annotated pathways per organism. Following the approach of Mazurie et al. [52], we computed the relationship between the number of pathways and the logarithm of genome size. The analysis revealed a strong linear correlation (r² = 0.95, p = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶), indicating that genome reduction proportionally constrains metabolic complexity. Here, the number of pathways refers to the count of distinct KEGG pathway maps associated with the reactions encoded in each genome, and should be interpreted as a measure of metabolic functional diversity rather than as discrete biochemical units.

Figure 2 illustrates this relationship for both the reaction-based and metabolite-based models. In both frameworks, endosymbiotic bacteria exhibit markedly smaller MNs than free-living relatives, consistent with extensive metabolic erosion during adaptation to intracellular environments. These results support the idea that genome minimization is accompanied by proportional contraction of biochemical capabilities, reflecting the increasing metabolic dependence of symbionts on their hosts. Although Fig. 2 depicts the relationship between genome size and the number of nodes in metabolite-based and reaction-based networks, this metric is conceptually related to pathway coverage. The number of nodes reflects the total set of metabolites or reactions encoded by a genome, which in turn constrains the number of KEGG pathway maps that can be represented. Thus, reductions in node number and pathway count both capture complementary aspects of metabolic simplification associated with genome erosion. In this context, node number provides a topological measure of metabolic complexity, whereas pathway counts summarize functional annotation breadth.

Comparison with a theoretical minimal metabolic network

To contextualize network reduction, we compared each endosymbiont MN with a theoretical minimal metabolic network (MNₘiₙ), reconstructed from the minimal gene set required for independent cellular life [28]. The minimal metabolic network shown here represents a theoretical reference model, and does not correspond to an obligate endosymbiotic taxon. This theoretical model comprises 80 metabolites and 98 reactions, corresponding to an estimated genome size of ~ 204 kb, assuming approximately 1 kb per gene [53]. Surprisingly, several endosymbiotic bacteria displayed MNs even smaller than this theoretical limit. In the metabolite-based model, the smallest networks corresponded to Nasuia NAS-ALF, Hodgkinia TETUND1 and TETUND2, and Tremblaya princeps PCVAL. In the reaction-based model, two additional Hodgkinia strains (Dsem and TETULN) also fell below this threshold. Nasuia NAS-ALF consistently exhibited the smallest MN in both modeling frameworks. Convergence of metabolite- and reaction-based models

To assess the consistency of our modeling approaches, we compared the dendrograms derived from reaction-based and metabolite-based distance matrices. For the metabolite-based model, distances reflected network topology, whereas for the reaction-based model, they were derived from reaction graphs generated during the construction of metabolic directed acyclic graphs (m-DAGs; see Materials and Methods). Remarkably, the resulting dendrograms showed strong convergence with known phylogenetic relationships (Figs. 3 and 4). Normalized Mutual Information (NMI; Fig. 3) analyses revealed that the alignment between network-based and phylogenetic clustering was highest around 50 clusters, approximately corresponding to the number of bacterial genera represented. This suggests that MN structure retains sufficient information to recapitulate phylogenetic signals at the genus level. As the number of clusters decreased beyond this point, NMI values declined, indicating reduced correspondence at higher taxonomic levels. Nevertheless, the persistence of evolutionary signatures in network topology underscores the potential of MN metrics to capture deep evolutionary constraints, even within highly reduced genomes.

Figure 4 illustrates the parallelism between distance trees derived from the reaction-based and metabolite-based analyses. In both representations, closely related taxa tend to cluster together, indicating that major phylogenetic relationships are preserved across modeling approaches. While deep taxonomic groupings are consistently recovered, finer-scale relationships show greater variability, reflecting lineage-specific differences in metabolic network organization. This comparison highlights which aspects of metabolic structure are conserved across evolutionary scales and which are more sensitive to network reduction.

Core and pan m-DAG architecture of endosymbiotic bacteria

We next characterized the core and pan metabolic directed acyclic graphs (m-DAGs) across all endosymbiotic bacteria. The core m-DAG, representing universally conserved reactions, revealed that insect endosymbionts retain only a minimal subset of biochemical processes—chiefly those required for DNA and RNA synthesis. As shown in Fig. 5, the core metabolic network shared by all endosymbionts is extremely reduced and dominated by reactions associated with nucleic acid metabolism, which collapse into a small number of conserved metabolic building blocks in the corresponding m-DAG. Notably, purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis pathways, though essential for nucleotide cofactors, were absent from this conserved core. These findings align with prior observations that universal metabolic functions—such as purine nucleotide and redox coenzyme biosynthesis—are conserved across microorganisms but often utilize alternative reaction routes [27]. Random gene losses in different lineages appear to have rendered distinct sets of genes essential to maintain equivalent functions.

In contrast, the pan m-DAG, integrating reactions from all symbionts, consisted of 2,964 reactions (598 reversible) and 1,883 metabolites (from its reaction graph depicted in Supplementary Figure SF1), which condensed into 1,081 MBBs after simplification (Supplementary Figure SF2). Although complex, the pan m-DAG serves as a comprehensive reference of conserved and lineage-specific metabolic capacities across endosymbiotic bacteria.

We identified 35 MBBs present in the minimal MN but absent in the smallest endosymbionts. These missing components encompass pathways for non-essential amino acids (cysteine, glycine), essential amino acids (methionine, threonine), glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycolysis, and the biosynthesis of several vitamins and cofactors (B₁, B₂, B₅, B₆, nicotinamide, and CoA). Their absence reflects the metabolic outsourcing that defines symbiotic consortia, wherein host and partner bacteria collectively form a complete functional system.

Conversely, the largest MNs, such as Nocardiopsis alba ATCC BAA-2165 and Bifidobacterium longum NCC2705, retained extensive connectivity resembling that of free-living relatives (Supplementary Figures SF3 and SF4). Its network contained 1,220 nodes with a dominant connected component corresponding to central metabolism, consistent with partial autonomy and limited host dependence. Notably, several pathways associated with antibacterial metabolite production [54, 55] were also detected, illustrating the analytical versatility of the MetaDAG framework across genomes of varying complexity.

Metabolic complementation in insect–bacteria consortia

To explore metabolic complementarity within insect–microbe symbiotic systems, we performed two multispecies network-based analyses using the MetaDAG framework. The analyses focused on two well-characterized insect–bacteria systems: (i) the pea aphid Acyrthosiphon pisum and its primary endosymbiont Buchnera aphidicola APS, and (ii) the glassy-winged sharpshooter Homalodisca vitripennis and its dual symbionts Sulcia muelleri GWSS and Baumannia cicadellinicola Hc. The selection of these models was guided by the availability of complete genomic data in the KEGG database, allowing for detailed metabolic reconstruction.

Analysis of the pan m-DAGs for both systems revealed extensive and complex metabolic architectures typical of integrated biological systems. Each exhibited the hierarchical topology characteristic of MetaDAG outputs, comprising a dominant large MBB representing general metabolism and numerous smaller MBBs contributing to specialized functions. The pan m-DAG for the A. pisum–Buchnera system contained 728 MBBs (Supplementary Figure SF5), while the H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia system contained 834 MBBs (Supplementary Figure SF6). This complexity reflects highly modular and interconnected metabolic landscapes, consistent with the intimate metabolic integration observed in obligate symbioses.

The core m-DAGs, representing reactions conserved across all partners within each system, highlighted the essential shared metabolic foundation. The A. pisum–Buchnera core contained 94 MBBs, encompassing central functions for cellular maintenance and host–symbiont integrity. In contrast, the H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia core comprised 24 MBBs, reflecting a reduced but functionally critical metabolic core associated with core energy metabolism and precursor biosynthesis.

A key finding was the emergence of novel MBBs (Figs. 8 and 9) when the symbiotic partners of each host–symbiont system were analyzed together, rather than individually, as an integrated metabolic network. These newly formed modules are indicative of metabolic complementation, where fragmented or incomplete pathways in individual organisms are restored through host–symbiont integration. Seven new MBBs were detected in the A. pisum–Buchnera system and nine in the H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia system, demonstrating the ability of MetaDAG to reveal interspecies cooperation.

In the A. pisum–Buchnera association, one newly formed MBB (0.7.2) consisted of 887 reactions, whereas the remaining six ranged between 4 and 20 reactions (Fig. 8; Supplementary Figure SF5). These modules revealed Buchnera-specific reactions involved in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) metabolism, as well as NAD⁺-dependent protein deacetylation. The exclusive presence of these reactions in Buchnera when compared to the host metabolic reconstruction, underscores its central role in maintaining redox balance and energy metabolism within the symbiotic system.

In the H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia system, the newly formed MBBs exhibited greater diversity in size and composition. One large MBB (0.15) comprised 975 reactions, while others contained between 1 and 12 reactions (e.g., 0.2.117, 0.0.43.60.10, and 9.177) (Fig. 9; Supplementary Figure SF6). These modules collectively point to a distributed network of metabolic cooperation, with Sulcia and Baumannia contributing complementary enzymatic capabilities.

Detailed analysis of these reaction sets revealed that the symbionts harbor several key biosynthetic reactions absent from the host. In Sulcia and Baumannia, these include R00226 (acetolactate synthase), R01071 (histidine biosynthesis initiation), R01714, R01715, and R01826 (shikimate pathway for aromatic amino acids), and R01777 (homoserine O-succinyltransferase, methionine biosynthesis). Additional symbiont-exclusive reactions—such as R01226 (pantothenate/CoA biosynthesis), R01404 (fatty acid biosynthesis), and R00481 (NAD biosynthesis)—highlight their indispensable roles in vitamin, cofactor, and fatty acid production.

A particularly significant finding was the shared occurrence of MBB 0.2.117 in both symbiotic systems. This module encompasses reactions integral to the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway, including R01403, R01404, R02767, R02768, and R10208 (and their reversible counterparts), which collectively mediate the cyclic elongation of acyl chains attached to acyl carrier proteins. The recurrence of this MBB across phylogenetically distinct host–symbiont systems indicate a conserved role for fatty acid biosynthesis in symbiotic nutrition. Although fatty acid biosynthesis is indispensable in most free-living bacteria due to its role in membrane formation, the retention of this pathway in obligate endosymbionts suggests that it is not fully compensated by the host or its diet and therefore remains essential within the symbiotic system analyzed here.

These results collectively support that both the A. pisum–Buchnera and the H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia systems exhibit highly integrated metabolic architectures, characterized by modular specialization, metabolic complementarity, and the conservation of essential biosynthetic pathways across evolutionarily distant lineages. While some of these patterns are consistent with previously reported properties of metabolic network erosion, the present analysis extends these observations by revealing phylogenetic and coevolutionary signals encoded in metabolic network topology across a large comparative dataset.

Discussion

Evolutionary reduction and network robustness

Our results reveal that genome reduction in insect endosymbiotic bacteria is closely mirrored by the simplification of their metabolic networks. Despite severe gene loss, the persistence of scale-free topology indicates that these minimal systems retain evolutionary signatures of robustness and efficiency. This architectural resilience likely reflects strong selective pressure to preserve essential metabolic connectivity while discarding redundancy.

We note that currency metabolites were retained in network reconstructions, which may introduce indirect connections between reactions that do not strictly follow carbon backbone transformations. However, because our analyses are comparative and all networks were generated using identical criteria, these effects are systematic and unlikely to affect relative patterns of network erosion, phylogenetic signal, or conserved topological features.

The observed coevolution between genome size and network parameters—particularly network diameter, clustering coefficient, and number of nodes—supports the hypothesis that endosymbiotic genome streamlining does not occur randomly. Instead, it follows predictable trajectories shaped by functional constraints and the necessity to maintain viable metabolic flow. The retention of hub metabolites and reactions suggests that, even at minimal genome sizes, evolutionary forces favor maintaining a core set of biochemical routes indispensable for survival in nutrient-limited intracellular environments. These findings reinforce prior work highlighting that genome reduction does not equate to functional collapse but rather to the optimization of network efficiency under host-provided nutrient regimes [49, 50].

Minimal networks and the lower limits of cellular autonomy

The comparison between endosymbiotic MNs and a theoretical MN provides insights into the lower boundary of metabolic viability. Several taxa—including Nasuia, Tremblaya, and Hodgkinia—display MNs smaller than this theoretical minimum, emphasizing the extent to which endosymbionts can offload functions to their host or to co-symbionts. The missing reactions in these organisms encompass key biosynthetic pathways for amino acids, vitamins, cofactors, and lipid precursors. The absence of these reactions implies a metabolic dependence that surpasses previously accepted minimal genome models, suggesting that evolutionary minimalism in symbiosis transcends cellular autonomy. These bacteria effectively outsource essential metabolic functions to their environment—namely, to host cells and co-resident symbionts [50, 51, 56, 57]. Such systems redefine the notion of the “minimal cell” and reinforce the “symbionelle” concept as context-dependent: in an intracellular symbiosis, metabolic viability arises from the collective network rather than the individual genome.

Phylogenetic signal in network structure

The convergence observed between reaction- and metabolite-based models, particularly their alignment with bacterial phylogeny, indicates that metabolic network architecture encodes evolutionary information. Despite massive genome erosion, MN topology still reflects lineage-specific traits. This pattern parallels findings in other comparative systems biology studies [58], suggesting that metabolic architecture is a robust evolutionary trait shaped by both historical contingency and functional necessity. The high correspondence between network-based dendrograms and phylogenetic trees at the genus level implies that endosymbiont metabolism evolves under both vertical inheritance and selective pruning, maintaining a structural “memory” of its ancestry. Thus, even in the face of extreme genome reduction, the metabolic phenotype retains phylogenetic coherence—an observation that further supports the evolutionary stability of metabolic modularity.

The core and pan m-DAG: conserved functions and adaptive flexibility

The core and pan m-DAG analyses underscore the balance between conservation and innovation in endosymbiont evolution. The small core m-DAG, dominated by reactions for nucleic acid synthesis, highlights the universal requirement for maintaining replication and transcriptional capacity. Its simplicity aligns with the concept of an irreducible biochemical core conserved across all life forms [27, 58]. By contrast, the expansive pan m-DAG demonstrates substantial variability among lineages, capturing both the legacy of ancestral metabolic diversity and the adaptive responses to distinct host environments. This duality suggests that genome reduction proceeds through lineage-specific trajectories, yet converges on a shared minimal architecture that supports essential life functions.

Functional fragmentation and symbiotic integration

The extreme fragmentation of metabolic networks in the smallest endosymbionts, such as Nasuia, Tremblaya, and Hodgkinia, highlights an evolutionary endpoint in which individual metabolic autonomy is replaced by consortial interdependence. These species exhibit disjointed networks lacking continuity across major biosynthetic pathways, consistent with their roles in multi-partner symbioses where metabolic complementation restores functionality at the community level [59, 60]. For example, Nasuia coexists with Sulcia in the leafhopper Macrosteles quadrilineatus, and together they synthesize the full set of essential amino acids required by the host. Similarly, Tremblaya princeps and Moranella endobia form a nested endosymbiosis in mealybugs, while Hodgkinia participates in dual associations with Sulcia in cicadas. In all these cases, endosymbiont metabolism cannot be understood in isolation; instead, the functional unit is the composite network formed by multiple genomes. The absence of key pathways such as glycolysis, lipid synthesis, and vitamin metabolism reflects extensive functional outsourcing to the host cytoplasm, which provides both metabolites and, in some cases, imported proteins [61–65].

Metabolic complementation and evolutionary integration in symbiotic systems

The results of our comparative MetaDAG analyses reveal that metabolic complementation is a central feature of insect–bacteria symbioses, emerging as a direct evolutionary response to host dietary constraints. Nutrient-deficient diets, such as the phloem and xylem sap consumed by aphids and sharpshooters, lack essential amino acids, vitamins, and lipids. This ecological limitation imposes selective pressure that drives the evolution of integrated metabolic architectures, where symbionts collectively compensate for missing biosynthetic capacities. The modular complexity observed in both the A. pisum–Buchnera and the H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia systems illustrates how genomic reduction can foster, rather than hinder, metabolic functionality by promoting division of labor and biochemical interdependence among partners.

The emergence of novel MBBs in joint symbiotic analyses provides strong evidence of pathway reconstruction through cooperation. These modules, absent in the isolated networks, arise when enzymatic reactions from different organisms combine to complete otherwise fragmented metabolic routes. This phenomenon represents a hallmark of evolutionary metabolic integration, where the collective system restores essential biochemical cycles that no single genome can sustain independently. Their detection in both systems—seven in A. pisum–Buchnera and nine in H. vitripennis–Sulcia–Baumannia—demonstrates how the MetaDAG framework effectively captures these emergent functional interactions.

Within the aphid–Buchnera consortium, Buchnera’s unique contribution to NAD⁺ and NADP⁺ metabolism and NAD⁺-dependent deacetylation highlights its critical role in maintaining redox balance and energy flow. Previous studies have shown that Buchnera contributes to redox homeostasis and central energy metabolism in aphids through the retention of NAD/NADP-related reactions and other core metabolic functions essential for host–symbiont integration [4, 53, 61, 66]. These functions extend beyond amino acid biosynthesis, revealing a broader symbiotic contribution to the host’s cellular homeostasis. In the sharpshooter’s tripartite symbiosis, the extensive novel MBBs uncovered suggest even greater biochemical complexity. Sulcia primarily contributes to essential amino acid biosynthesis, whereas Baumannia complements with pathways for vitamins, cofactors, and lipids, particularly through its involvement in fatty acid biosynthesis. This distributed organization exemplifies functional compartmentalization, where metabolic roles are partitioned among symbiotic partners to achieve systemic efficiency.

The identification of MBB 0.2.117, a fatty acid biosynthesis module, shared by both systems is particularly revealing. Its recurrence across phylogenetically distant host–symbiont consortia underscores convergent evolution in response to similar ecological pressures. Both aphids and sharpshooters rely on diets poor in lipids and essential fatty acids, making symbiont-derived fatty acid synthesis indispensable. This convergence illustrates how nutritional ecology shapes the trajectory of symbiotic evolution: similar selective constraints yield parallel metabolic adaptations across independent lineages.

These observations reinforce the idea that obligate mutualism emerges not solely from gene loss but from a reorganization of metabolic capacity across multiple genomes. Each partner retains a subset of reactions optimized for its ecological niche within the consortium, while collective integration restores metabolic completeness. This distributed metabolism enhances host survival on nutritionally imbalanced diets and likely drives the long-term stability of the symbiotic relationship. Moreover, the hierarchical topology and modularity detected in both systems reveal that scale-free organization and functional redundancy persist even in reduced networks. Such structural resilience suggests that, despite extensive genome erosion, the resulting metabolic systems remain evolutionarily optimized for stability and efficiency. These principles parallel the patterns observed in free-living bacterial networks, extending the concept of network robustness into the realm of obligate symbiosis.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that metabolic complementation represents an advanced stage of coevolution, where genome reduction is counterbalanced by systemic integration. Through modular specialization, pathway sharing, and cross-genome cooperation, these insect–microbe consortia achieve a level of biochemical coherence that sustains life under extreme nutrient constraints. The application of MetaDAG highlights this complexity by providing a systems-level view of how symbiotic interactions reshape metabolic landscapes, preserving functional integrity across evolutionary time. Importantly, MetaDAG is used here not as a validation exercise, but as an analytical framework to investigate evolutionary patterns in metabolic network organization, enabling comparative inferences that go beyond descriptive pathway analyses.

Ecological and evolutionary implications

The findings presented here have broad implications for understanding the genomics of symbiosis. Endosymbiotic systems represent natural experiments in minimal evolution, demonstrating how metabolic functions can be distributed across multiple partners to sustain collective viability.

At a systems level, metabolic complementarity reflects the optimization of nutrient exchange within ecological networks, bridging genomic erosion and adaptive success. Evolutionarily, these patterns illustrate that the loss of genes is not solely degenerative but can be an adaptive response promoting cooperative stability within host–symbiont systems.

By combining comparative genomics with network topology, this study highlights that evolutionary streamlining can preserve complexity at higher organizational levels, even as individual genomes approach minimality. The persistence of scale-free properties, modular structure, and phylogenetic coherence across reduced MNs underscores that evolutionary and functional order can emerge from loss-driven processes. This persistence in reduced metabolic networks should not be interpreted as direct evidence of selection acting specifically on network architecture. Previous theoretical and empirical work has shown that scale-free networks can retain their degree distributions under substantial levels of random node loss, indicating that neutral erosion alone may contribute to the maintenance of this property. Accordingly, the conservation of scale-free topology observed here is best interpreted as compatible with, but not uniquely attributable to, functional constraint. In this context, our results suggest that the evolutionary trajectories of endosymbiont metabolic networks likely reflect an interplay between neutral genome erosion and constraints imposed by the need to preserve essential metabolic connectivity.

Ultimately, this work expands the paradigm of genome reduction into one of systems-level reorganization, where symbiotic networks—not individual genomes—constitute the enduring units of evolutionary innovation.

Conclusions

Our findings reveal that the highly reduced yet interconnected metabolic networks of insect endosymbiotic bacteria form a distinct evolutionary signature shaped by extreme metabolic constraints. Genome reduction favors the loss of redundancy while preserving essential reactions that sustain cooperation within host-dependent systems.

Through reaction- and metabolite-based analyses, we show that network topology co-evolves with genome size and phylogeny, maintaining a scale-free architecture even in the smallest genomes. This persistence of structural order demonstrates that the principles of biological organization and robustness extend to the lower limits of genome evolution.

Moreover, our results highlight the fragmentation and complementarity characteristic of endosymbiotic metabolism, where collective biochemical networks across multiple partners restore essential pathways. Together, these insights position metabolic networks as powerful models for understanding how genomic streamlining drives evolutionary innovation and the emergence of interdependent life.
