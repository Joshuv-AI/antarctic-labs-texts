Global immunisation planning and economic development potential: a panel data analysis for the macroeconomic impact of hepatitis B vaccination from 2000 to 2018
Authors: Linzheng Quan, Ya Fang, Liangwen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Immunisation Agenda 2030, led by the World Health Organisation (WHO), outlines a ten-year strategy to ensure universal access to vaccines worldwide to improve health and well-being. The hepatitis B virus (HBV) vaccine, included in the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunisation (Gavi) pentavalent vaccine, has been successfully introduced in many low-income countries since 2000. Despite its recognised cost-effectiveness in enhancing health capital, the economic pathways through which vaccination rate affects development remain underexplored. This study aims to examine the effect of vaccination rate on gross domestic product (GDP) growth and its underlying mechanisms after the launch of Gavi.MethodsData were mainly obtained from WHO, World Development Indicators (WDI), Penn World Table 10.0 (PWT 10.0), WHO and UNICEF Estimates for National Immunisation Coverage (WUENIC). A conditional convergence model was used to evaluate the association between the three-dose vaccine of HBV (HBV3) vaccination rate and GDP growth from 2000 to 2018 across countries. Guided by Grossman’s health capital theory, the study further explored mediation pathways via the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged. Income-group-based heterogeneity analyses were also conducted.ResultsHBV3 vaccination rates rose globally during the study period, with the rate gap across income groups narrowing from over 20% to around 10%. A 1% increase in HBV3 vaccination rate was associated with a 1.17% GDP per capita convergence rate per year (p < 0.01). Vaccination rate was negatively associated with the under-5 mortality rate (β=-0.104, p < 0.01) and positively with the number of persons engaged (β = 6.935, p < 0.01), though mediation was only partial. Heterogeneity analyses by income group revealed that the economic impact of vaccination rate was significantly concentrated (β = 0.0164, p < 0.01) in lower-middle-income countries (LMICs).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that improved HBV3 vaccination rate promotes GDP growth through enhanced health capital, especially in LMICs. Therefore, sustained investments in routine immunisation are crucial for maximising economic and health returns and addressing vaccine inequities. However, the limitations of the analytical framework and data accessibility hinder solid conclusions regarding the long-term economic impacts driven by delayed health benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04930-9.Keywords: Hepatitis B vaccine, Global health, Health economics, Health capital, Vaccine inequity

Background

In 2020, the World Health Organisation (WHO) launched the Immunisation Agenda 2030 (IA 2030), outlining a ten-year vision to ensure that everyone, everywhere, at every age, fully benefits from vaccines to achieve optimal health and well-being by 2030. In 2023, the General Assembly of the United Nations identified immunisation as one of the most effective strategies for advancing health for all. During the “Decade of Vaccines” (2011–2020), the Global Vaccine Action Plan (GVAP) was formulated through extensive international collaboration, setting ambitious targets including disease elimination and eradication. These efforts have led to substantial progress in expanding immunisation coverage. The Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunisation (Gavi) was established in 2000 to provide technical and financial assistance to supported countries to enhance the equity and sustainability of vaccinations. The hepatitis B virus (HBV) vaccine, included in the Gavi-supported pentavalent vaccine [1], has been successfully introduced since 2001, with global coverage increasing from just 30% in 2000 to 85% by 2019 [2], enabling the world’s poorest countries to access immunisation.

HBV, one of the most prevalent chronic viral infections worldwide, has infected an estimated 2 billion individuals globally, with over 350 million living with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) [3]. Although acute HBV infection is typically self-limiting in immunocompetent adults, chronic infection can progress to liver cirrhosis (LC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), or other liver diseases [4], causing more than 1 million HBV-related deaths each year [5], and was once ranked among the world’s top health priorities [6]. In 1992, the World Health Assembly recommended the inclusion of HBV vaccination in the national immunisation schedules of all countries [7]. In 2016, WHO launched a global strategy to eliminate HBV by 2030, designating HBV as a major public health threat and calling for a substantial increase in postnatal vaccination rate alongside expanded measures to prevent mother-to-child transmission, to achieve a 90% reduction in new HBV infections [8]. As a result of these global initiatives, the overall burden of HBV has declined, with the global age-standardised incidence rate (ASIR) of HBV among children and adolescents falling from 1385.2 per 100,000 in 1990 to 418.7 per 100,000 in 2021 [9]. However, the absolute number of cases of HBV-related advanced liver disease, such as LC and HCC, has been increasing in certain regions. The burden of HBV-associated disease also remains high [10], particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where over 95% of infections and 99% of cases among children aged 5 years and younger occur [11]. For this population, out-of-pocket expenses for health care remain a substantial financial burden, and severe or chronic diseases may result in catastrophic health expenditure or illness-induced impoverishment.

The HBV vaccine, introduced into clinical practice in 1982 [12], was the first vaccine proven to prevent a human cancer [13]. According to WHO, a full three-dose schedule of HBV vaccination offers over 95% protection against all HBV genotypes [14], is immunogenic for people of all ages, and lasts for at least 20 years, potentially lifelong [15]. For example, in Taiwan, China, where neonatal HBV vaccination has been implemented since 1984, the prevalence of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity declined markedly from 9.8% in 1984 to 0.6% in 2004 [2]. Additionally, long-term antiviral therapy can reverse cirrhosis and lower the incidence of HCC [16], improving clinical prognosis. Currently, substantial disparities in HBV vaccination rates persist across global regions, with the Western Pacific (90%), Americas (89%), and Southeast Asia (87%) regions currently above the global average, and the European Region (81%), Eastern Mediterranean Region (80%) and African Region (75%) have lower coverage rates [2]. In many low- and middle-income countries, widespread vaccination remains challenging [17] due to poor adherence and logistical constraints [18], and infections occur even before vaccination is available. Whereas in high-income countries, reaching vulnerable populations, such as intravenous drug abusers and homeless people, remains difficult [19]. Moreover, due to rapid transportation and economic development, population mobility occurs at an unprecedented pace, including many migrants from areas with high HBV prevalence to those with lower prevalence. To establish a widespread immunisation barrier, countries should actively engage in regional vaccine action plans.

Effective vaccine uptake at the national level requires increased resource allocation, including robust immunisation programmes, continuous disease surveillance, and efforts to reduce costs. Consequently, gross domestic product (GDP) growth is widely recognised as a contributing factor to vaccination rate improvements. For instance, one study highlighted the association between economic development and vaccine coverage and supply, using COVID-19 vaccination as an example [20]. Cost–benefit analyses have also been conducted for vaccines such as HBV [21] and the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) [22]. Bhargava’s study found that a 1% increase in the age-standardised rate (ASR), a key health indicator, was associated with a 0.05% increase in GDP growth [23]. However, empirical studies examining the effect of vaccination rate on GDP growth remain limited, particularly across countries of different regions and income levels based on health capital theory. On the one hand, the impact of vaccination rate on economic growth often lags, meaning that productivity gains may only become apparent years or even decades later. Consequently, short-term vaccine investments may not yield immediate returns [24]. On the other hand, the health capital theory proposed by Grossman posits that the demand for medical care shouldn’t be emphasised at the expense of the demand for health [25], and human capital accumulated in the form of health positively influences economic growth [26]. Based on health capital theory, this study aims to assess the impact of HBV vaccination on economic development across countries with different regional and income contexts following the launch of Gavi, using country-level data from 2000 to 2018, thereby providing empirical evidence to inform immunisation policymaking.

In light of the economic challenges posed by the post-pandemic era and global population ageing, many countries need to pursue sustainable investment strategies to balance short-term and long-term economic growth. The HBV vaccine exemplifies how immunisation can be one of the most cost-effective health interventions, highlighting that rational allocation of health investments on immunisation is an ideal intervention perspective. We further examine how vaccination improvements influence economic output through the population quality and workforce quantity in health capital, with recommendations for countries across income levels optimising investments in health and education, responding to the health equity goals of the IA 2030 initiative, to promote the health benefits of vaccination for all.

Methods

Data sources

We combined representative publicly available national data on vaccination rates, GDP, and their influencing factors. The data used in this study were obtained in 2024 from World Development Indicators (WDI) [27], WHO and UNICEF Estimates for National Immunisation Coverage (WUENIC) [28], Penn World Table 10.0 (PWT 10.0) [29], Polity IV [30], and the International Country Risk Guide (ICRG) [31].

The core variables included the GDP per capita (constant 2015 US$), average per capita GDP growth rate, and under-5 mortality rate (U5MR) from WDI, the three-dose-vaccine of HBV (HBV3) vaccination rate from WUENIC, and the number of persons engaged from PWT 10.0. A summary of data sources and corresponding indicators is presented in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Income classifications for 85 countries were obtained from the WDI updated on July 1, 2023, which reflect Gross National Income (GNI) per capita from the preceding calendar year, using Atlas method-based conversion factors and expressed in US dollars [32]. It divided global economies into four categories (list in Additional file 1: Table S2): high-income (n = 27 countries), upper-middle-income (n = 23 countries), lower-middle-income (n = 21 countries), and low-income (n = 14 countries).

Model structure

The conditional convergence model proposed by Barro and Sala-i-Martin [33] is often used as a standard framework for measuring economic growth. It posits that GDP growth rates across countries tend to converge towards a stable long-run equilibrium growth rate. Conditional on covariates including life expectancy, fertility, rule of law, investment, education (indicated by the female elementary school graduation rate and the male elementary school graduation rate), government consumption, openness to markets, trade, democracy (reflects political stability), inflation, and time-fixed effects, it assessed how differences in initial economic conditions influence the rate at which countries with lower GDP per capita converge with those at higher levels. Specifically, Barro’s conditional convergence model [34] uses the logarithm of five-year lagged GDP per capita as the key explanatory variable, whose coefficient captures the relationship between initial GDP and the growth rate. This study modified this standard conditional convergence model to estimate the impact of vaccination rate on macroeconomic growth and its mechanism.

Grossman’s health capital theory conceptualised health as a durable capital stock that generates healthy time as its output [30]. Building on this theory, the study hypothesised that higher vaccination rates promote GDP growth by enhancing the stock of health capital. Health capital was primarily measured using indicators that capture population quality and workforce quantity.

Informed by the theoretical framework, previous literature, and data availability, the following variables were selected. The under-5 mortality rate, which is often used to evaluate the health level of a country, was used as a proxy for population quality in this study, while the number of persons engaged was used as an indicator of the quantity of the workforce. The HBV vaccine, one of the Gavi-supported pentavalent vaccines, had been incorporated into national immunisation programmes covering 97% of countries worldwide [2]. Among these, the three-dose HBV (HBV3) regimen remains the most widely used and effective full-dose schedule [18]. Accordingly, the HBV3 vaccination rate was adopted as the primary independent variable.

Impact and its mechanism analysis

This study analysed data from 2000 to 2018, aiming to investigate the impact of vaccination rate on GDP following the launch of Gavi in 2000, while avoiding confounding effects from the COVID-19 pandemic, which began spreading widely at the end of 2019. Regressions were conducted using a conditional convergence framework, where the average GDP growth rate over five-year periods from 2000 to 2019 served as the dependent variable, aiming to replicate Barro’s results and assess whether the vaccination rate constituted a key determinant of GDP growth. The analysis then examined how vaccination rate influenced the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged, to elucidate the mechanisms through which vaccination contributed to GDP convergence, in terms of both population quality and workforce quantity.

First, model Ⅰ replicated Barro’s conditional convergence analysis [34]. Second, Model II incorporated the HBV vaccination rate with a four-year lag to plausibly account for its delayed effects on economic output.

Next, models Ⅲ and Ⅳ were developed to assess the impact of vaccination rate on the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged respectively.

Finally, the four-year lagged HBV vaccination rate, the under-5 mortality rate, and the number of persons engaged were simultaneously included in model Ⅴ to examine potential mediating effect.

In Model V, the coefficient denotes the relationship between the initial GDP and the growth rate after the inclusion of the vaccination rate, denotes the effect of the vaccination rate on the convergence rate of GDP, indicates the effect of the under-5 mortality rate on the convergence rate of the GDP, and measures the effect of the number of persons engaged on the convergence rate of GDP.

Heterogeneity and robustness analysis

Heterogeneity analyses based on time-fixed effects were conducted across income-level groups for each of the five regression models described above. To address potential endogeneity among key variables and ensure model validity, a series of two-by-two regression analyses was conducted to assess the correlations between HBV vaccination rates, GDP per capita, and health expenditure per capita.

To test the robustness of the coefficient estimates, additional regression analyses were performed. Non-significant variables were excluded from the model to evaluate their influence on the main results. Moreover, to address concerns regarding variable specificity, all five regression models were re-estimated by replacing theHBV3 with vaccination rate of the three-dose diphtheria tetanus toxoid and pertussis vaccine (DPT3). This alternative specification was used to assess the robustness of the estimated effects on GDP convergence and to compare coefficient estimates across vaccines.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 17.0.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analysis of the main variables (Additional file 1: Table S3), correlation analysis (Additional file 1: Table S4), and multicollinearity testing (Additional file 1: Table S5) were conducted. The mean variance inflation factor (VIF) across all variables was 4.29, indicating that multicollinearity did not pose a significant concern.

As illustrated in Fig. 1 (A), HBV3 vaccination rates showed a positive correlation with income levels between 2000 and 2018. HBV3 vaccination rates rose steadily in high- and upper-middle-income countries. From 2000 to 2003, lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) experienced a rapid increase of more than 20%, while low-income countries saw an approximately 30% increase between 2002 and 2006. Thereafter, all income groups showed relatively stable and comparable growth rates. By 2018, the average HBV3 vaccination rate had reached 93% in high-income countries, and 83% in low-income countries, narrowing the vaccination rate gap across income groups to approximately 10%.

Second, regression including a four-year lag of HBV vaccination rate suggested that a 1% increase in the HBV3 vaccination rate was associated with an approximate 1.17% GDP convergence rate annually (p < 0.01), providing an estimation of the delayed effect on macroeconomic outcomes.

Next, the effects of HBV vaccination rate on the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged were examined separately to elucidate how vaccination rate influenced the speed of GDP convergence through improvements in population quality and workforce quantity.

As shown in Table 2, the coefficient estimate for the under-5 mortality rate was − 0.104, statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating that an increase in vaccination rate statistically reduced the under-5 mortality rate, thereby improving population quality in the long term and contributing to GDP growth.

The coefficient estimate of the number of persons engaged was 6.935, also significant at the 1% level, indicating increased vaccination rates are likely associated with a higher number of persons engaged, contributing to GDP growth through the expansion of workforce quantity. These findings were consistent with the proposed hypothesis.

Finally, the 4-year lagged HBV vaccination rate, under-5 mortality rate, and number of persons engaged were simultaneously included in the regression model to assess mediating effects. As shown in Table 2, the estimated coefficients were 0.0219 for the under-5 mortality rate and 0.0000575 for the number of persons engaged, both significant at the 1% level. However, the effect of the under-5 mortality rate on GDP was statistically inconsistent with the initial hypothesis, suggesting that the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged had a partially mediating effect on the impact of vaccination rate on GDP growth.

Heterogeneity Analysis

Building on the five regression models, heterogeneity analyses by income level revealed the impact of vaccination rate on GDP growth and its underlying mechanisms that were most pronounced in middle-income countries.

Specifically, in model Ⅰ (Table 3), the convergence rate for high-income countries was − 0.21%, though it was statistically insignificant. The rate of convergence for upper-middle-income countries was 1.25% (p < 0.1) and for low-income countries was 1.02% (p < 0.05), both statistically significant, validating the conclusions presented by Barro in his model - GDP in rich countries grows at a slower rate, while GDP in poor countries grows at a more rapid rate.

Robustness check

To assess the robustness of the coefficient estimates in the aforementioned models, the following regression analyses were conducted.

First, non-significant variables were excluded from the model, and the results (Additional file 1: Table S6) indicated that these variables did not materially affect the primary findings. Second, the five regression models were re-estimated using the DPT3 vaccination rate in place of the HBV3 rate, and the results (Additional file 1: Table S7) showed that the effects of DPT3 vaccination rate on GDP growth and its underlying mechanisms were broadly consistent with those of HBV3 in both direction and magnitude, thereby supporting the robustness of the analytical framework employed in this study. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed by replacing the 4-year lagged HBV3 vaccination rate variable with 2-year and 6-year lagged variables. The results (Additional file 1: Table S8) indicated that, in the short term, the impact of vaccination rate on GDP was most pronounced 2 years after vaccination, while the effect diminished or became statistically insignificant with longer lags. This finding suggests that incorporating the 4-year lagged HBV3 vaccination rate in the regression helps control for confounding factors and objectively reveal its effect on GDP.

Discussion

This study used panel data from 85 countries between 2000 and 2018 and found that vaccination rates increased overall in the context of global immunisation initiatives. Higher vaccination rates were associated with GDP growth, mainly through improvements in the health capital of population quality and workforce quantity, supporting the initial hypothesis and the health capital theory. These findings have important implications for public health, particularly in advancing the IA2030 Global Strategy to reduce vaccination inequities and strengthen investment in vaccines.

Explaining the impact mechanism of vaccination rate: the contribution of health capital to GDP

Repeating the conditional convergence model, we found that GDP converged at a rate of 1.1%, slightly lower than the 1.7% reported by Barro [34]. Previous research suggests that slower cross-country economic convergence [35] is linked to rising global income inequality, as embodied in the unequal distribution of countries across income groups in this study. Additionally, recent studies indicate significant improvements in health inequality and accessibility, with increased life expectancy per capita contributing to a decline in overall inequality [36]. Global political instability and macroeconomic conditions also affect national economic growth and might further slow GDP convergence.

This study confirmed that a higher vaccination rate contributes to GDP growth, as evidenced by its inclusion in the conditional convergence regression. Improved health, viewed as a form of capital stock, enhances economic output by improving both the population quality and quantity of the workforce. The influence of vaccination rate on GDP growth may operate through two distinct mechanisms: in the short term, it may be interrelated to shifts in investment allocation aimed at increasing vaccination rate [24], or it may boost growth prospects, as observed with the widespread adoption of the COVID-19 vaccine, which contributed to economic recovery by restoring activity [37], as well as reducing inflation and unemployment pressures [38]. In the long term, improved population health is expected to enhance societal productivity. According to the health capital theory [25], health functions as a long-term capital stock, yielding increased output through extended healthy life spans.

Specifically, the impact of vaccination rate on population quality and workforce quantity was reflected in a lower under-5 mortality rate and an increased number of persons engaged, respectively. Higher vaccination rate helps establish a population-wide immunisation barrier, thereby reducing infectious disease incidence and increasing workforce numbers, ultimately enhancing overall societal productivity. Most childhood vaccinations are recommended before the age of two, with the WHO explicitly advising that the HBV vaccine be administered within 24 h of birth [39], to minimise the risk of infection before the introduction of vaccines. Vaccination during the “first 1000 days of life”, a critical period of growth and development, is highly effective in preventing diseases caused by HBV infection or mother-to-child transmission, reducing neonatal deaths or disability, and lowering family health expenditure. Vaccination also reduces opportunity costs for newborn parents and prevents economic losses from sick leave or medical expenses, contributing to broader socioeconomic development.

Except for these short-term implications, previous studies have shown that the effects of vaccination rate are more pronounced over longer periods. Health effects accumulate across the life cycle [40], with a correlation between better child health and greater adult social capital [41]. A generation with widespread vaccination is expected to have substantial impacts on social productivity in adulthood. For example, researchers evaluated the costs of immunisation alongside the lifetime costs saved for patients across 10 different vaccines, finding that immunisation yielded an average net return of about 26.1 times between 2011 and 2020, and about 19.8 times between 2021 and 2030 [42]. Similarly, there is an estimation that an adult immunisation program covering seven vaccine-preventable diseases would generate over 500 million euros in returns for Dutch taxpayers over the lifetime of a 50-year-old cohort, representing a fourfold investment return [43]. As populations age, with the WHO estimating that the world’s population aged 60 years or older will double to 2.1 billion by 2050, current vaccination efforts will be crucial in meeting the future demand for a healthy workforce.

Country Heterogeneity Analysis by Income Group

Divergent national vaccination policies may result in substantially different economic growth trajectories. Heterogeneity analysis revealed that the effects of vaccination rate on GDP growth and the underlying mechanisms were most pronounced in middle-income countries, whether considering susceptibility to infectious diseases or the accessibility of vaccines.

High-income countries, owing to greater financial resources and health awareness [44], rapidly integrated vaccination into primary health care (PHC) following clinical introduction. Over time, the marginal impact of vaccination rate on GDP growth may have declined. In some high-income countries, including Switzerland and Sweden, early vaccine uptake has led to persistently low prevalence, and vaccination is now targeted at risk groups rather than universally promoted. Notably, at least 30% of acute HBV infections occur in individuals without identifiable risk factors, limiting the protective reach of targeted vaccination [2]. This may explain the lack of significant associations between the vaccination rate and the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged in high-income group analysis.

In some middle-income countries, vaccination programmes have been largely supported financially and technically by Gavi since its establishment in 2000, resulting in a continued positive effect on economic growth. Health capital, integrating the value of education and health in production, remains a key determinant of economic growth [26], and its economic returns are proven to vary by country income level. In high-income OECD countries, health capital had limited contributions to long-term growth [45], while health in low- and middle-income countries was positively and significantly associated with GDP growth [46]. Some developed countries, such as Singapore, also showed positive growth effects, with rapid long-term adjustments to equilibrium facing short-term economic shocks [47]. However, for individuals, greater access to vaccines and education may shift expenditure allocation, as people tend to invest in vaccinations before disease occurs rather than in more expensive disease-related expenditures after disease occurs. Previous studies show that screening pregnant women followed by HBV vaccination is cost-effective in some low- and middle-income countries [48]. Meanwhile, age-related declines in innate health stock can be offset by investment, and rising health value may reduce health demand while increasing the need for medical care. All of these may account for the concentration of vaccination impact on GDP in lower-middle-income groups.

Low-income countries face slow growth in vaccination rates, with increases in disease burden due to migration, non-inclusion in immunisation programmes, or other factors [49]. The insignificant statistical results of vaccination impact on GDP may be caused by the lack of manpower or budget for an effective disease control system surveillance, according to IA 2030. Moreover, the under-5 mortality rate and the number of persons engaged in these countries might be more influenced by broader structural determinants, which reflect social perceptions and health levels, instead of the vaccination rate. The Social Determinants of Health (SDH) framework posits that societal, economic, and environmental factors are interdependent. Beyond income and education [50], housing safety and food security are key drivers of health inequities [51]. In this regard, achieving an initial vaccine rate from zero requires substantial investment, but policymakers should note that the long-term economic returns of such efforts would be considerable. For instance, implementing cold-chain vaccine logistics and mobile health clinics can generate employment, improve immunisation rates in remote areas, and enhance health literacy among underserved populations. For high-income countries, examples include EU member states, which combine mandatory vaccination requirements with recommendations and professional guidelines. France and Germany require proof of childhood vaccination for school entry, while Italy and Germany (for measles) impose fines for non-compliance [52]. Since 2017, France, Italy, and Germany have expanded mandatory vaccines to boost coverage. Colombia, as an upper-middle-income country, has inclusive COVID-19 programs covering Venezuelan refugees and immigrants, supported by clear communication, reduced vaccine inequality, and raising uptake among irregular migrants by 26.2% in four months [53]. These targeted policies correspond with lower disease incidence and reduced mortality, confirming the practical significance of this study.

Bidirectional facilitation of GDP and vaccination

Previous studies have demonstrated that GDP growth can facilitate higher vaccination rates. Countries with higher GDP typically have more robust fiscal capacity, enabling greater investment in healthcare, including vaccine procurement, distribution, and administration [54]. For instance, such countries have invested in vaccine research and development (R&D), supply chain infrastructure, and immunisation site construction [55], thereby expanding the vaccination rate. Especially in the post-COVID-19 era, these countries could establish more comprehensive public health systems, facilitating universal accessibility and equitable distribution of vaccines [56], ensuring vaccination for domestic low-income populations and those in remote communities. Meanwhile, financial investment is often supported by an enabling policy environment, such as subsidies, national immunisation programmes, and health education investments [57], aimed at reducing vaccine hesitancy and increasing vaccination rate [58], thus creating a virtuous cycle of improved health investment and optimised resource allocation.

Limitations

First, data on vaccination rates are second-hand data, primarily relying on national disease surveillance systems, and the reliability and completeness are limited. In Gavi-supported countries, economic incentives may lead to overreporting of immunisation coverage [59]. These factors might introduce reporting bias, particularly in low-income countries with high early vaccination rates. Second, limitations in the analytical framework precluded a detailed examination of the interaction between population quantity and workforce quality. For instance, developed countries may have a smaller workforce but higher levels of education and human capital, resulting in a greater proportion of skilled workers and R&D personnel, thereby exerting scale effects on economic growth. In this regard, endogeneity and robustness tests conducted in the study helped validate the findings.

To assess the impact of vaccination rate on GDP following the launch of Gavi and to mitigate data availability issues, this study included data from 2000 to 2018. While this timeframe avoided the confounding effects of COVID-19-related shocks, it might underestimate the importance of vaccination in the post-pandemic context. Furthermore, in clinical practice, distinctions of efficacy exist between the birth dose and subsequent doses of HBV vaccination, with the latter typically administered as part of pentavalent or hexavalent combination vaccines that include DPT components. Given data and study design constraints, we were unable to further explore heterogeneity between HBV vaccination and DPT-containing vaccines (excluding the birth dose). Consequently, the potential synergistic effects of combination vaccines cannot be fully ruled out. As such, the findings primarily capture short-term associations, whereas the longer-term macroeconomic effects driven by lagged health improvements warrant ongoing investigation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that vaccination rate contributed to economic growth between 2000 and 2018, primarily by improving population quality and expanding the workforce, with particularly pronounced effects in LMICs. The findings underscore the importance of regional vaccine action plans within the framework of the IA2030 global strategy. In LMICs, sustained and enhanced investment in routine immunisation-programmes vaccines remains highly cost-effective.

As recommended by WHO, addressing health equity concerns requires the development of robust national immunisation systems that are integrated with PHC services, enabling efficient and adaptable planning to reduce mortality and morbidity from vaccine-preventable diseases across the life course. Simultaneously, improving the accessibility of health services and ensuring equitable and stable allocation of both technical and financial resources across and within countries will enhance the effective impact of GVAP in each country.

Overall, the strategic imperative lies in shifting from “health in all policies” to “health for all policies”, emphasising the cross-sectoral benefits of improved health policies and outcomes, thereby fostering economic and social development through public health and education. Aligning with the health equity goals of the IA2030 initiative, the overarching aim of “a world where everyone, everywhere, at every age, fully benefits from vaccines to improve health and well-being” (SDG-3) can be progressively realised.
