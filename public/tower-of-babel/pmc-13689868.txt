Cardioneuroablation for Pediatric Vasovagal Syncope Guided by Peak Frequency Mapping

Published: 2026

Vasovagal syncope (VVS) is the most common subtype of reflex syncope. The management of pediatric VVS presents a particular therapeutic dilemma, especially for those with drug-refractory VVS. While permanent pacemaker implantation is an option, it entails lifelong device dependency and lifestyle limitations, which are especially burdensome for this population. As an emerging therapeutic approach, cardioneuroablation (CNA) aims to achieve permanent cardiac vagal denervation by precisely ablating the epicardial ganglionated plexi (GPs) of the cardiac autonomic nervous system.1 Although CNA has emerged as a promising strategy in adults, its application in younger patients constitutes a genuinely underexplored area, with scarce data on the safety, efficacy, and optimal technique of this method. This evidence gap highlights the critical need for detailed case reports and methodological exploration in younger patients.

However, the clinical application of CNA faces significant challenges, primarily reflected in the lack of standardization at the technical level, especially concerning GP localization techniques.1 Existing methods such as high-frequency stimulation, a fractionated electrogram-guided strategy, and an empirical anatomic approach each have their own limitations. Real-time automated spectral analysis with continuous-wavelet transform is a novel mapping algorithm that enables the generation of intuitive peak frequency (PF) maps.2 This study provides a detailed report on the stand-alone application of this real-time PF mapping method to guide CNA in a young patient with mixed-type VVS.

History of Presentation

A 12-year-old girl was admitted to our hospital because of 3 episodes of recurrent syncope within 1 year. During head-up tilt testing, she developed profound bradycardia with a minimum heart rate of 29 beats/min.

Past Medical History

The patient’s medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no relevant past medical interventions.

Differential Diagnosis

Structural heart disease was excluded by echocardiography and other appropriate investigations. Other causes of syncope, including arrhythmic, neurological, and metabolic etiologies, were ruled out. Given the clinical presentation and tilt-test findings, the diagnosis of VVS (mixed type) was established.

Investigations

The basal electrophysiological (EP) study under general anesthesia exhibited a PP interval of 1,159 ms (heart rate: 52 beats/min) and a sinus node recovery time (SNRT) of 2,519 ms. Concurrently, evidence of atrioventricular (AV) nodal suppression was observed, manifested as AV Wenckebach conduction during right atrial pacing at an S1S1 cycle length of 610 ms. Both the PR interval (154 ms) and the HV interval (40 ms) were within normal limits.

Management

Considering her young age, CNA was discussed with the patient and her family as a potential therapeutic alternative to permanent pacemaker implantation.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Detailed electroanatomic maps of both the right atrium (RA) and left atrium (LA) were reconstructed by a multipolar mapping catheter (Advisor HD Grid, Abbott), with simultaneous acquisition of activation maps, voltage maps, and the PF maps (Figure 1). Using the omnipolar technology near field (OTNF) algorithm (Abbott) integrated into the EnSite X EP system (Abbott), PF mapping was performed to identify the location of the GP by adjusting the frequency window to maximize the isolation of PF areas. The mapping procedure employed a PF window of >550 Hz for the RA and >600 Hz for the LA. Based on these parameters, the right superior GP (RSGP), right inferior GP (RIGP), posteromedial left GP (PMLGP), and left inferior GP (LIGP) were identified (Figures 1C to 1F). In alignment with the anatomical locations of the GP sites, radiofrequency ablation was delivered using a 3.5-mm-tip TactiCath catheter (Abbott) targeting the PF highlighted regions of the RSGP, RIGP, and PMLGP. Ablation was performed at the corresponding anatomical sites through the LA and RA (Figure 2). Ablation was guided by the lesion index and local potential elimination. As the area highlighted on PF mapping at the RIGP anatomical site, situated between the ostium of the right inferior pulmonary vein and the RA, was very small, ablation was performed mainly under anatomical guidance. During ablation of the RSGP, the heart rate immediately increased from 60 to 93 beats/min. Outcome and Follow-Up

The postablation EP study revealed a significant reduction in the RR interval to 575 ms and in the SNRT to 804 ms, with AV Wenckebach conduction occurring during right atrial pacing at an S1S1 cycle length of 350 ms. At the 1-week postoperative follow-up, repeat head-up tilt testing failed to induce bradycardia. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained free of syncopal episodes. A 24-hour Holter monitor demonstrated sinus rhythm with a mean heart rate of 95 beats/min, minimum heart rate of 75 beats/min, and longest RR interval of 0.81 seconds.

Discussion

This study presents a detailed report on the successful guidance of CNA in a young patient with VVS, exclusively using PF mapping. The procedure employed frequency thresholds of >550 to 600 Hz to precisely localize the GPs. During ablation of the RSGP, an immediate real-time increase in heart rate was observed. Following the ablation, key EP parameters, including heart rate, SNRT, and AV Wenckebach conduction, showed marked improvement. This case provides valuable experiential data supporting the stand-alone use of OTNF-based PF mapping in a pediatric patient with VVS.

The management of drug-refractory VVS in children and adolescents presents a distinct therapeutic challenge. Current pediatric syncope guidelines primarily recommend lifestyle modification, pharmacotherapy, and permanent pacemaker implantation in severe cases. Notably, CNA is not addressed in these management recommendations, highlighting a significant evidence gap regarding its safety, efficacy, and optimal technique in the pediatric population. A recent study of 20 children with malignant VVS demonstrated that CNA was safe and effective.3 Furthermore, CNA has been shown to be effective in pediatric patients with functional AV block.4 However, these studies relied on high-frequency stimulation, fractionated electrograms, and/or anatomic landmark mapping techniques, which have limits in sensitivity and specificity. The present case of a 12-year-old girl demonstrates that CNA, guided by the novel and precise PF spectral mapping technique, is not only feasible but can result in immediate procedural success and significant EP improvement. Therefore, this report provides preliminary evidence that PF mapping–guided CNA could also be considered as a potential therapeutic strategy for young patients with refractory VVS. It underscores the need for further systematic investigation to evaluate the long-term outcomes and establish the procedural standards for CNA in a younger age group.

Farwati et al5 initially used fast Fourier transformation to convert intracardiac electrograms into frequency spectra for localizing GPs. The electrical signals at these sites exhibited a fragmented spectrum, and spectral analysis revealed a characteristic increase in high-frequency components, presenting a fibrillar pattern characterized by a wide spectrum of frequencies with multiple components >100 Hz.5 In contrast, normal myocardial tissue displayed a sharp spectrum of frequencies, with a main component of approximately 40 Hz.5 However, because its operation requires specialized software for spectral analysis, its adoption has been limited within the broader electrophysiology community. Consequently, various alternative methods have been adopted, such as high-frequency stimulation, a fractionated electrogram-guided strategy, and an empirical anatomic approach, each of which possesses certain limitations.3,5,6

High-frequency stimulation is a functional verification method that applies high-frequency electrical pulses at specific endocardial sites to observe whether a clear vagal response can be elicited.5 However, it has relatively low sensitivity and specificity. Fractionated electrogram mapping is an indirect inference method based on characteristics of endocardial electrograms. GP regions exhibit low-amplitude, multicomponent-complex electrograms.6 However, definitions of fractionation vary across studies, and this approach lacks specificity in diseased atrial myocardium or areas of anatomical overlap.1 Anatomically guided ablation is an empirical method based on anatomical structures, relying on the assumption that GP distribution is similar among individuals, but it fails to account for the functional status, size, and precise boundaries of GPs in specific patients, and it is unable to differentiate between active and inactive GPs.1

This study employed the OTNF algorithm integrated into the EnSite X EP system. This algorithm uses continuous-wavelet transform to perform real-time, automated spectral analysis of the raw intracardiac electrogram, directly measuring and annotating the PF at each site while preserving temporal information, then generating a visual PF map.2 It has been observed that GP regions exhibit significantly higher peak frequencies compared to ordinary myocardium. Consequently, a specific threshold is set to highlight these high-frequency zones on the map, thereby guiding ablation. Numerous studies have demonstrated that compared to fractionated potential maps, PF maps can more precisely delineate the ablation area, show high correlation within GP regions, and may even identify targets missed by the former approach.1,6,7 Giomi et al2 reported the first use of OTNF-based real-time spectral analysis for CNA in an adult patient, though they concurrently employed fractionation mapping. Sobue et al8 recently applied high-resolution fractionation mapping combined with PF analysis to identify autonomic regions, demonstrating spatial concordance in an adult population with sinus node dysfunction and atrial fibrillation. By contrast, our case used an isolated PF-mapping approach for GP localization in a pediatric patient, supporting the feasibility of a pure PF-mapping strategy.

The optimal cutoff value for PF mapping of target GPs remains undefined. Prior studies have employed varying criteria: One report combining fractionated potential mapping with PF analysis identified 600 Hz as the most accurate threshold for GP definition and reduction of unnecessary ablation area7; a retrospective study selected >550 Hz for its favorable spatial concordance with fractionated potential regions2; and Sobue et al8 empirically set the threshold at 400 Hz to enhance detection specificity for near-field autonomic signals. In the present case, we did not apply a preset threshold; instead, after completing the mapping, we progressively adjusted the cutoff frequency based on real-time PF maps to identify the smallest regions with the highest frequencies corresponding to anatomical GP locations, ultimately adopting thresholds of 550 Hz for the RA and 600 Hz for the LA. The differential thresholds were selected post hoc as an empirical observation from the real-time PF maps in this single patient. While we observed that the RA exhibited lower peak frequencies at anatomical GP sites compared to the higher frequency signatures in the LA, we hypothesize that these chamber-specific PF distribution patterns were attributable to differential autonomic innervation density. Given the marked spatial heterogeneity of PF distribution and the potential influence of catheter contact on this parameter,6 absolute values should be interpreted with caution. We postulate that the optimal frequency threshold may vary among individual patients. This tailored approach aligns with the concept of individualized ablation strategy, aiming for precise targeting while minimizing unnecessary ablation.

In adult patients, individualized GP ablation strategies have demonstrated comparable efficacy to approaches targeting all GPs.9 However, no consensus recommendation currently exists for GP ablation in pediatric patients.3,4,10 For patients with cardioinhibitory syncope, ablation of GPs innervating the sinoatrial (SA) and AV nodes is indicated. We performed paraseptal GP ablation to achieve vagal denervation of the SA and AV nodes, primarily targeting the RSGP, RIGP, and PMLGP from the bilateral atria. The aorta–superior vena cava GP was not ablated given concerns regarding injury to the SA nodal artery. Ablation targets were identified by combining anatomical localization with PF mapping. Both acute procedural outcomes and follow-up results demonstrated favorable efficacy following ablation of these 3 GPs. The LIGP was identified on PF mapping; however, as ablation of all GPs is not mandatory for procedural efficacy,9 and given the risk of thermal esophageal injury at this site, no further ablation was performed.

For the assessment of the treatment endpoint, the atropine challenge was not employed in this study because of its prolonged autonomic effect, which has an average half-life of 4.1 hours and could hinder immediate postprocedural evaluation. Instead, we used a composite endpoint to determine immediate procedural success. During the procedure, ablation of the RSGP resulted in an immediate increase in heart rate, which was regarded as direct physiological evidence of vagal inhibition release. Furthermore, postprocedural evaluation showed prompt and marked improvement in key EP parameters, including heart rate, SNRT, and AV Wenckebach conduction, and repeated head-up tilt testing failed to induce bradycardia during follow-up, collectively confirming the success of the ablation.

This report has several limitations. First, as a single-center, single-case report, its findings require validation in prospective cohort studies. Second, the procedural endpoint assessment did not include an atropine challenge to confirm complete vagal denervation, relying instead on a composite of acute heart rate and EP responses. Finally, only short-term follow-up results are presented; the long-term stability of the ablated GP and the potential risk of reinnervation require extended monitoring. Future multicenter studies are needed to further optimize PF mapping parameters and compare it with other mapping techniques, thereby promoting comprehensive standardization of mapping methodologies in CNA.

Conclusions

This case report demonstrates that CNA guided by stand-alone PF (>550-600 Hz) mapping is a feasible, precise, and effective therapeutic strategy for young patients with VVS. This technique allows for intuitive identification of GPs, enabling targeted ablation and significant improvement in cardiac autonomic function.
