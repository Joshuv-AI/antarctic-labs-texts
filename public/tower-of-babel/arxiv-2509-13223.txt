Geometry, Energy and Sensitivity in Stochastic Proton Dynamics
Veronika Chronholm, Tristan Pryer

Abstract
. We develop numerical schemes and sensitivity methods for stochastic models of proton transport that couple energy loss, range straggling and angular diffusion. For the energy equation we introduce a logarithmic Milstein scheme that guarantees positivity and achieves strong order one convergence. For the angular dynamics we construct a Lie-group integrator. The combined method maintains the natural geometric invariants of the system. We formulate dose deposition as a regularised path-dependent functional, obtaining a pathwise sensitivity estimator that is consistent and implementable. Numerical experiments confirm that the proposed schemes achieve the expected convergence rates and provide stable estimates of dose sensitivities.



1.
Introduction

Stochastic models of proton transport play an important role in
high-precision applications such as proton therapy, where accurate
characterisation of energy loss and angular scattering is essential to
treatment planning and dose delivery. Deterministic formulations,
based on the continuous slowing down approximation or transport
equations, capture average behaviour and admit analytic
approximations, but they neglect statistical fluctuations

[
BLP23
,
AHP25
]
. Stochastic formulations, by contrast,
describe individual particle paths, accounting for both deterministic
energy loss due to inelastic interactions and random angular
deflections from Coulomb scatter, with additional variability
introduced by range straggling

[
Cro+24
,
KPP25
]
. Analytic models of
either type provide reduced-order descriptions of dose, while
numerical approaches range from PDE solvers and pencil-beam algorithms
to Monte Carlo and SDE-based simulations

[
Ash+25
]
. For physical observables such as energy
deposition, fluence and dose
[
NZ15
]
, it is the
stochastic, numerically approximated models that are required to
capture fluctuations and uncertainty at clinically relevant accuracy.

In radiotherapy and related fields, reliable computation of dose and
fluence demands numerical approximations that faithfully capture the
geometry of particle transport and the stochastic nature of energy
loss. For protons, this means maintaining positivity of the energy,
ensuring that directional vectors remain on the unit sphere and
correctly representing the randomness inherent in multiple scattering
and range straggling. Conventional numerical schemes, even in the
deterministic case, often violate one or more of these requirements

[
HLW06
]
. The energy variable may become negative
under naive discretisation, or angular updates may drift off the
sphere. Moreover, many practical tasks, such as machine-learning
guided treatment optimisation, uncertainty quantification, or
calibration to experimental data, depend not just on forward
simulations but also on sensitivities of dose and fluence with respect
to model parameters (e.g. stopping-power coefficients or straggling
parameters). Finite-difference estimates of these sensitivities are
routinely employed in clinical settings, yet they tend to be biased
and have large variance when applied to stochastic systems, making
them computationally inefficient for high-accuracy work. There is a
clear need for numerical methods that simultaneously respect the
physical constraints of proton transport and provide unbiased, low
variance sensitivity information in a unified framework.

In this work we develop a structure-preserving framework for the
simulation and sensitivity analysis of a stochastic proton transport
model with continuous energy loss and angular diffusion. Our main
contributions are:

(1)

We reformulate the dynamics using a logarithmic transformation
of the energy and an exponential map for angular updates. This
guarantees exact positivity of energy and preservation of the
unit-norm constraint on direction at the discrete level. On this
basis we construct (i) a geometric Euler scheme of strong order

1/2
and (ii) a higher-order scheme that couples a Milstein
discretisation of the log-energy SDE with a geometric integrator for
angular evolution, achieving strong order
1
.

(2)

We derive and discretise forward (pathwise) sensitivity
equations for the stopping-power and straggling parameters. These
sensitivities satisfy linear SDEs driven by the same Brownian motion
as the state, so that the coupled state-sensitivity scheme achieves
strong order
1
convergence. This provides unbiased gradient
estimates of path-dependent observables such as dose, in contrast to
the high-variance estimates produced by finite differences.

(3)

We present numerical experiments that demonstrate the efficacy
of the proposed methods. The geometric discretisations preserve key
physical features of proton transport, including Bragg peaks and
angular spreading, while the pathwise sensitivities converge with
lower variance than finite-difference benchmarks. We further show
how incorporating energy-dependent angular diffusion (Molière
theory) modifies dose distributions and sensitivities, highlighting
the flexibility of the framework.

Numerical simulation of stochastic differential equations is a well
developed area, with classical treatments of strong approximation
theory and positivity-preserving schemes appearing in

[
Klo+92
,
MT04
]
. Recent work has
extended this theory to include tamed and truncated schemes for SDEs
with super-linear coefficients and positivity constraints

[
Den+24
,
Sab13
]
. The use of geometric
integrators, including exponential maps and Lie group methods, has
gained attention for systems constrained to manifolds, particularly in
mechanics and molecular dynamics
[
MT04
,
HS24
]
. Structure-preserving stochastic integrators
for manifold-valued systems have also been developed in the context of
matrix Lie groups, with Runge-Kutta-Munthe-Kaas methods recently
extended to SDEs in
[
Mun+22a
]
. The approach adopted
here draws on these developments, combining log-transformed variables
with manifold-aware angular updates to ensure structural fidelity in a
proton transport setting.

In proton therapy, dose modelling is typically performed using
deterministic or Monte Carlo methods
[
Bor97
,
Pag18
,
Fad+20
,
Kus+23
]
, with recent
machine learning and dynamical low-rank solvers for proton transport

[
Sta+24
,
Sta+25
]
. These approaches
generally decouple model simulation from parameter sensitivity
analysis, or rely on finite difference estimators that suffer from
the tradeoff between bias and high variance, as well as numerical instability.
Recent work by

[
Ash+25
]
applied sensitivity analysis to an analytic
model of proton transport, while a more complex stochastic model
incorporating jump processes was introduced in

[
Cro+24
]
. An MCMC-based framework for inverse inference
of dose delivery from experimental data has also been proposed in

[
Cox+24
]
. Pathwise sensitivity methods are
well established in the context of financial mathematics (see
e.g.
[
GG06
,
BG96
,
CG23
]
,
or Chapter 7 of
[
Gla04
]
) and stochastic simulation.
Their application in radiation transport has been limited, though
early examples include physical simulation contexts such as chemical
kinetics and photon propagation
[
SRK12
]
. The
present work adapts these techniques to a geometric SDE model of
proton motion, coupling structure-preserving integrators with forward
sensitivity equations to enable efficient, unbiased gradient
computation for physically meaningful observables such as dose.
Connections to Feynman-Kac representations for energy deposition
problems are discussed in light of recent work on stochastic particle
transport
[

,

]
.

Physical Model and Stochastic Formulation

The motion of a proton undergoing interactions in a medium is governed
by a combination of deterministic energy loss and stochastic angular
deflections. The proton moves along a trajectory described by its
position
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, direction
\Omega_{t}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and energy
E_{t}>0
. In the clinical energy range
(50–150 MeV), the dominant interactions influencing proton transport
are illustrated in Figure
1
. These include inelastic
collisions with electrons, elastic Coulomb scattering with nuclei,
and, less frequently, inelastic nuclear reactions.

Inelastic collisions with electrons lead to gradual energy loss,
typically described deterministically through the Bragg-Kleeman or
Bethe-Bloch equation
[
Int93
]
, and are responsible for the sharp
rise and falloff of the Bragg peak. However, due to the stochastic
nature of these collisions, protons of the same initial energy do not
all stop at the same depth, leading to an inherent longitudinal spread
known as range straggling
[
Bor97
]
. Angular
deflections arise primarily from elastic Coulomb interactions with
nuclei and are typically small but frequent, resulting in lateral beam
broadening through multiple scattering
[
Got+93
]
. These
deflections are well described by Brownian motion on the
sphere. Occasional inelastic nuclear interactions produce discrete
energy losses and secondaries such as neutrons, contributing to the
dose halo and distal falloff
[
SPL02
]
, but are not
included in the present model, although further details can be found
in
[
Cro+24
]
.

Our focus is therefore on a simplified yet representative regime. This
leads to a coupled system for
(X_{t},E_{t},\Omega_{t})
that captures the
dominant physics while remaining analytically and numerically
tractable.

The dynamics are described by the stochastic differential equations

(1)

\begin{split}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\Omega_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}&=-S(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}&=\sqrt{2\epsilon(E_{t})}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{S}.\end{split}

The first equation expresses free streaming with unit velocity in the
direction
\Omega_{t}
. The second describes the deterministic decrease
in energy, governed by the stopping power
S(E)
. The third models
angular diffusion, where
W_{t}^{S}
is a standard Brownian motion on the
unit sphere and
\epsilon(E)
determines the scattering strength. In
the simplest case,
\epsilon(E)=\epsilon_{0}
may be taken constant,
though in practice it is energy dependent.

The angular SDE is interpreted in the Stratonovich sense, consistent
with Brownian motion on the sphere. This avoids explicit curvature
drift terms and preserves the uniform distribution as the angular
equilibrium law.

The stochastic description is naturally linked to a forward PDE for
the particle density
\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\omega},e)
in phase
space. This fluence satisfies

(2)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi-\frac{\partial}{\partial e}\!\left({S(e)\psi}\right)-\epsilon(e)\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi=0,

with inflow boundary condition

(3)

\psi=g\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{-},

where

(4)

\Gamma_{-}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\omega},e)\in\partial D\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}:\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})<0\}

and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
the sphere. By the Feynman-Kac formula

[
Del04
,
KPP25
]
,
\psi
admits a
probabilistic representation in terms of expectations over
realisations of the stochastic process (
1
).

2.1 Remark

(Track-length parametrisation)
.

Equation 1
formulates the dynamics with respect to track
length rather than physical time. Under a physical-time
parametrisation, the transport equation for
X_{t}
would involve a
velocity factor depending on the current energy
E_{t}
, rather than
the unit speed assumed here. The two formulations are related by a
time-change transformation, so the choice of parametrisation does
not alter the underlying physics but simplifies the mathematical
structure.

2.1.
Stopping Power Models

The stopping power
S(E)
characterises the mean rate of energy loss
per unit path length. Microscopically it is described by the
Bethe-Bloch equation
[
Int93
]
, which accounts for inelastic
collisions with atomic electrons. For reduced models, the
Bragg-Kleeman law provides a simpler approximation,

(5)

S(E)=\frac{1}{p\alpha}E^{1-p},

where
\alpha>0
and
p\in[1,2]
are material-dependent
parameters. This form captures the essential energy dependence of
proton stopping power and is widely used in analytic descriptions of
range and dose
[
Ash+25
]
.

2.1.
Moliére Theory

The angular diffusion coefficient
\epsilon(E)
in (
1
)
reflects the cumulative deflections from multiple small-angle Coulomb
scatterings. Physical theory and empirical evidence both support its
dependence on the proton’s energy. In particular, Moliére’s theory
of multiple Coulomb scattering
[
Mol48
]
predicts that
the scattering power is inversely related to the square of the
particle’s momentum.

In the small-angle limit, the mean squared scattering angle per unit
path length satisfies

(6)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}x}\langle\theta^{2}\rangle\propto\frac{1}{\beta^{2}p^{2}},

where
\beta=v/c
is the dimensionless velocity and
p
is the
momentum. For protons in the therapeutic energy range, a
non-relativistic approximation gives
p\sim\sqrt{2mE}
and
\beta^{2}\sim E/m
, yielding

(7)

\epsilon(E)\propto\frac{1}{E^{2}}.

This energy dependence implies that angular diffusion intensifies at
lower energies, consistent with the observed lateral beam broadening
in the distal region of a proton beam.

An empirical parametrisation inspired by the Highland
formula
[
Hig75
]
is

(8)

\epsilon(E)=\frac{\bar{\epsilon}}{E^{2}+\epsilon_{c}^{2}},

where
\bar{\epsilon}
is a reference scattering strength and

\epsilon_{c}
is a regularising constant, typically in the range 5-10
MeV, calibrated to experimental beam spread.

2.1.
Range Straggling

The energy loss of a charged particle in a medium is subject to
statistical fluctuations due to the discrete nature of ionisation
interactions. This variability, known as
range straggling
,
leads to deviations in the stopping range of particles with the same
initial energy. The function
T(E)
characterises the variance of the
energy loss per unit path length and is derived from statistical
models of energy deposition.

The first statistical description was provided by Bohr

[
Boh13
]
, who showed that for many small, independent
collisions, the variance of energy loss grows proportionally to the
stopping power. Landau
[
Lan44
]
refined this picture by
accounting for the asymmetric tail of the energy-loss distribution. In
the thick-target regime relevant to therapy beams, a standard
modelling choice is

(9)

T(E)=\kappa S(E)E,

where
\kappa
is a medium-dependent coefficient. For the
Bragg-Kleeman form of
S(E)
, one obtains

(10)

T(E)=\frac{\kappa}{p\alpha}E^{2-p}.

The range variance then follows from

(11)

\operatorname{Var}(R)=\int_{0}^{E_{0}}\frac{T(E)}{S(E)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}E=\kappa p\alpha\int_{0}^{E_{0}}E^{p}\,\mathrm{d}E=\kappa p\alpha\frac{E_{0}^{p+1}}{p+1}.

Solving for
\kappa
gives

(12)

\kappa=\frac{(p+1)\operatorname{Var}(R)}{p\alpha E_{0}^{p+1}}.

Based on the measured distal
90\%-10\%
falloff width of
1.7
mm in
water for a
62
MeV beam at Clatterbridge
[
Bon+93
]
, the
standard deviation in proton range can be estimated as
\sigma_{R}\approx 0.072
cm. Substituting
\sigma_{R}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(R)

with
p=1.77
,
\alpha=2.2\times 10^{-3}
,
E_{0}=62
and

\sigma_{R}=0.072
, we obtain

(13)

\kappa\approx\frac{(0.072)^{2}\cdot(1.77+1)}{1.77\cdot(2.2\times 10^{-3})\cdot 62^{2.77}}\approx 4\times 10^{-5}.

In the distal falloff of the Bragg peak, nuclear interactions also
contribute to broadening the range distribution. While we do not model
these effects here, a description of their impact is given in

[
Cro+24
]
.

2.1.
Modification of the SDE to Account for Range Straggling

The deterministic energy loss model in (
1
) neglects
fluctuations arising from the stochastic nature of energy-depositing
collisions. These fluctuations lead to
range straggling
, where
protons of the same initial energy exhibit a spread in stopping
distance. To account for this, we introduce a stochastic term in the
energy equation, with variance governed by the empirical straggling
function
T(E)
. The resulting stochastic differential equation reads

(14)

\begin{split}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\Omega_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\\
\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}&=-S(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(E_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E},\\
\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}&=\sqrt{2\epsilon(E_{t})}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{S},\end{split}

where
W_{t}^{E}
is a standard Brownian motion in energy. This choice
ensures that the energy variance grows in accordance with experimental
observations of range straggling in water phantoms. The deterministic
term continues to govern the average energy loss, while the stochastic
term introduces variability that accounts for deviations in stopping
range between different protons.

As a result of this modification, the corresponding transport equation
includes a second derivative in energy, leading to

(15)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi-\frac{\partial}{\partial e}\!\left({S(e)\psi}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial e^{2}}\!\left({T(e)\psi}\right)-\epsilon(e)\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\psi=0.

This additional term captures the spread in energy due to range
straggling and contributes to the overall uncertainty in proton range.
Its inclusion is particularly relevant in clinical proton therapy,
where range uncertainties impact treatment planning and dose
deposition.

We note that the coefficients
S(E)
,
T(E)
and
\epsilon(E)
are
smooth and locally Lipschitz on
(0,\infty)
, ensuring well-posedness
of the SDE system up to the point where
E_{t}\to 0
. One way of
handling the delicate behaviour near zero is to impose a killing
boundary at some small
E=E_{\min}>0
. By imposing a killing boundary
at
E=E_{\min}
, we need only consider the process on the domain

[E_{\min},\infty)
, on which all coefficients are globally Lipschitz.

The form (
9
) neglects the asymmetric Landau tail in
the energy-loss distribution and is most accurate in the thick-target
regime where many small collisions dominate. Alternative
parameterisations, such as those based on Vavilov theory

[
NB12
]
, provide higher fidelity when rare
large energy losses are important.

2.1.
Stopping Time and Particle Killing

We define the stochastic process

(16)

\{\!\left({X_{t},\Omega_{t},E_{t}}\right):t\geq 0\},

where
X_{t}\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\Omega_{t}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and

E_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
denote the proton’s spatial position, direction and
energy at time
t
, respectively. The process models the evolution of
an individual proton trajectory in the phase space
D\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The process is defined up to a random stopping time
\tau
, given
by

(17)

\tau:=\inf\{t>0:E_{t}={E_{\min}}\text{ or }X_{t}\notin D\},

which corresponds to the track length at which the proton either exits
the spatial domain or loses all its energy. The stopping time
\tau

is a random variable that depends on the initial configuration
(X_{0},\Omega_{0},E_{0})
, though we suppress this dependence to simplify
notation.

After the stopping time
\tau
, the proton is considered to have been
absorbed or exited the domain and no longer evolves. To formalise
this, we extend the state space by adding a cemetery state
\dagger
,
and define the process over
[0,\infty)
by

(18)

\!\left({X_{t},\Omega_{t},E_{t}}\right):=\begin{cases}\!\left({X_{t},\Omega_{t},E_{t}}\right),&t<\tau,\\
\dagger,&t\geq\tau.\end{cases}

This extension ensures that the particle is well defined for all
t\geq 0
, even after the proton has exited the physical system. The
cemetery state
\dagger
is absorbing, i.e. if the process enters

\dagger
at time
\tau
, then
(X_{t},\Omega_{t},E_{t})=\dagger
for
all
t\geq\tau
. For consistency, we define all test functions
f:(D\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\times[E_{\min},\infty))\cup{\dagger}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\dagger)=0
. In this way, the state

\dagger
contributes nothing to integrals, expectations or
occupation measures. This convention allows us to work on the
extended domain while effectively restricting calculations to the
live configuration space
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\Omega_{t}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

2.1.
Logarithmic Transformation for Energy Positivity

In modelling proton transport, the energy variable
E_{t}
must remain
strictly positive. Direct numerical simulation of its stochastic
dynamics can nevertheless produce negative values due to
discretisation error, especially when the diffusion coefficient is
state-dependent.

Even in the deterministic case without range straggling, the energy
equation exhibits a finite stopping time. For
p\in(1,2)
, the
solution

(19)

E(t)=\bigl(E_{0}^{p}-t/\alpha\bigr)^{1/p}

is strictly positive only up to the critical time
T=\alpha E_{0}^{p}
. As
t\to T^{-}
, the solution remains continuous but becomes
non-differentiable as
E\to 0^{+}
. This illustrates the delicate
behaviour near
E=0
, even without stochasticity.

When range straggling is included, the SDE for
E_{t}
contains both a
degenerate diffusion coefficient and a singular drift. Neither is
locally Lipschitz at
E=0
, violating standard conditions for
existence and uniqueness of strong solutions. While the probability of

E_{t}
becoming negative may remain small for moderate times, global
well-posedness is not guaranteed.

To regularise the problem and enforce positivity, we reformulate the
dynamics in terms of a log-transformed energy variable. Such
transformations are standard in the SDE literature as a means of
stabilising dynamics and constraining state variables to their
physical domains (see, e.g.,
[
YHZ21
]
).

We define the logarithmic variable

(20)

Y_{t}=\log E_{t}.

Applying Itô’s lemma, we compute

(21)

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\frac{1}{E_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{E_{t}^{2}}(\,\mathrm{d}E_{t})^{2}.

Substituting the SDE for
E_{t}
,

(22)

\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}=-S(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(E_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E},

we obtain

(23)

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\frac{1}{E_{t}}\!\left({-S(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(E_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{E_{t}^{2}}T(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
, we rewrite the equation entirely in terms of
Y_{t}
:

(24)

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\!\left({-S(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{t}}\right)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E}.

This reformulation ensures that
Y_{t}
evolves naturally in

\mathbb{R}
, so its numerical approximation cannot lead to negative
values of
E_{t}
when transformed back via

(25)

E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right).

2.1.
Structural Properties of the Model

The system evolves in terms of
(X_{t},Y_{t},\Omega_{t})
, and is given by

(26)

\begin{split}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\Omega_{t}\,\,\mathrm{d}t,\\
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}&=\!\left({-S(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{t}}\right)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E},\\
\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}&=\sqrt{2\epsilon(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{S},\end{split}

with initial conditions

(27)

X_{0}=x_{0}\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\Omega_{0}=\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\quad Y_{0}=\log E_{0},\quad E_{0}>0.

2.2 Proposition

(Energy positivity under the log transform)
.

Let
(X_{t},Y_{t},\Omega_{t})
solve (
26
) with
E_{0}>0
and
define
E_{t}:=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
for
t<\tau
, where
\tau
is the killing time
from Section
2.1
. Then
E_{t}>0
almost surely
for all
t<\tau
.

Proof.

By construction
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
with continuous sample paths for

t<\tau
(the coefficients of (
26
) are locally Lipschitz
on the live domain), hence
E_{t}=\exp(Y_{t})
is well defined and
strictly positive for all
t<\tau
, almost surely. After
\tau
the
process is sent to the cemetery state and
E_{t}
is not a physical
variable.
∎

2.3 Proposition

(Monotone decay of the expected energy)
.

Let
(X_{t},Y_{t},\Omega_{t})
solve (
26
) with killing time

\tau
and set
E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
for
t<\tau
. Suppose
S(E)>0
for
all
E>0
and
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,S(E_{t})\,\right]<\infty
for

t<\tau
. Then for all
t<\tau
,

(28)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[E_{t}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{-S(E_{t})}\right]<0

with strict inequality whenever
\mathbb{P}(t<\tau)>0
.

Proof.

Applying Itô’s formula to
E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
, we have

(29)

\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)(\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t})^{2}.

Substituting the SDE for
Y_{t}
from (
26
), we
write

(30)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\beta_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E},

(31)

\displaystyle\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=-S(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{t}}\right),

(32)

\displaystyle\beta_{t}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right).

Then the drift of
E_{t}
becomes

(33)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[E_{t}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)\mu_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)\beta_{t}^{2}}\right].

Substituting the expressions for
\mu_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
, we
compute

(34)

\displaystyle\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=-S(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))-\tfrac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right),

(35)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)\beta_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right),

so the
T
terms cancel and we obtain

(36)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[E_{t}]=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[{S(E_{t})}\right],

which is strictly negative for all
t
as
S(E_{t})>0
.
∎

2.4 Proposition

(Stationarity and exponential mixing for constant angular diffusion)
.

Let
\Omega_{t}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
solve

(37)

\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{S},

with constant
0<\epsilon_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
W_{t}^{S}
is Brownian
motion on the sphere. Let
\sigma
denote the uniform probability
measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then
\sigma
is invariant and
ergodic. Furthermore, writing the Markov semigroup as

(38)

(P_{t}f)(\boldsymbol{\omega}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left({f(\Omega_{t})\ |\ \Omega_{0}=\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right),

we have, for all
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\sigma)
with

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=0
,

(39)

\left\|P_{t}f\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\sigma)}\leq\exp\!\left({-2\epsilon_{0}t}\right)\left\|f\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\sigma)},

so the law of
\Omega_{t}
converges exponentially fast to
\sigma
as

t\to\infty
in
\operatorname{L}^{2}
. Equivalently, if
\rho_{t}
is the density of
the law of
\Omega_{t}
with respect to
\sigma
, then

(40)

\left\|\rho_{t}-1\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\sigma)}\leq\exp\!\left({-2\epsilon_{0}t}\right)\left\|\rho_{0}-1\right\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\sigma)}.

Proof.

The Stratonovich dynamics have generator

(41)

L=\epsilon_{0}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

on defined on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, which is dense in

L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\sigma)
. Integration by parts shows that

\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is symmetric with respect to the

L^{2}(\sigma)
inner product and since
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}1=0
,
the uniform measure
\sigma
is invariant under the semigroup

\exp\!\left({tL}\right)
. Now, the spectrum of
-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
on

\mathbb{S}^{2}
is
\{\ell(\ell+1):\ell=0,1,2,\dots\}
with
eigenspaces given by spherical harmonics and the first non-zero
eigenvalue being
2
. Therefore, on the mean-zero subspace, the
semigroup
P_{t}=\exp\!\left({tL}\right)
contracts as
(
39
). Compactness and ellipticity imply uniqueness
of the invariant measure and ergodicity.
∎

2.5 Remark

(Energy-dependent scattering)
.

When the angular coefficient depends on energy, the joint process obeys

(42)

\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}=\sqrt{2\epsilon(E_{t})}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{S},

so the
marginal
dynamics of
\Omega_{t}
are time-inhomogeneous
and generally non-Markovian. For each frozen energy level
e
, the
uniform law on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is invariant for the generator

\epsilon(e)\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
(since
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}1=0
), but
there need not exist a stationary measure for the marginal of

\Omega_{t}
when coupled to
E_{t}
. If
\epsilon(E)
grows as

E\downarrow 0
(as suggested by Moliére theory), the instantaneous
angular mixing rate increases along paths as the energy decreases and,
informally, conditional on survival, the angular distribution becomes
rapidly isotropic.

2.6 Remark

(Long-time behaviour prior to killing)
.

Let
\tau
be the killing time from §
2.1
. On

t<\tau
, the components of (
26
) exhibit distinct
trends. The energy decreases on average and, under mild regularity,

E_{t}\to 0
almost surely as
t\uparrow\tau
. If
\epsilon(E)

increases as
E\downarrow 0
, the angular motion mixes faster and the
conditional law of
\Omega_{t}
approaches isotropy before killing. The
position exhibits increasing lateral spread due to angular diffusion
with killing at
E_{\min}>0
,
X_{t}
is only defined on
[0,\tau)
,
after which the process is sent to the cemetery state.

3.
Euler–Maruyama Discretisation

To solve the stochastic system numerically, we begin by applying the
Euler–Maruyama method, an explicit scheme for approximating solutions
to SDEs. While straightforward to implement, this scheme fails to
preserve key structural properties of the continuous dynamics,
motivating the development of geometric alternatives in the next
section.

3.0.
Time Discretisation

Let
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
be a uniform discretisation of the time
interval
[0,T]
with step size
h
, where

(43)

t_{n}=nh,\quad\text{for }n=0,1,\dots,N,\quad\text{with }h=\frac{T}{N}.

We denote the discrete approximations of
X_{t}
,
Y_{t}
and
\Omega_{t}

at time
t_{n}
by
X_{n}
,
Y_{n}
and
\Omega_{n}
, respectively. The
corresponding energy variable is given by
E_{n}=\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right)
.

3.0.
Euler–Maruyama Approximation

Applying the Euler–Maruyama scheme to the system (
26
),
we obtain the updates

(44)

\begin{split}X_{n+1}&=X_{n}+h\Omega_{n},\\
Y_{n+1}&=Y_{n}-hS(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)-\tfrac{h}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{n}}\right)+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)\xi_{n}^{E},\\
\Omega_{n+1}&=\!\left({1-\epsilon_{0}h}\right)\Omega_{n}+\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}\xi_{n}^{S}.\end{split}

Here,
\xi_{n}^{E}
denotes a Gaussian increment with distribution

\mathcal{N}(0,h)
and the angular increment
\xi_{n}^{S}
is
constructed by projecting standard Gaussian noise onto the tangent
space at
\Omega_{n}
, that is,

(45)

\xi_{n}^{S}=\!\left({I-\Omega_{n}\Omega_{n}^{\top}}\right)\eta_{n},\quad\eta_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,hI_{3}).

3.0.
Properties and Convergence Notes

The Euler–Maruyama scheme is a first-order method that achieves
strong convergence of order
O(h^{1/2})
for SDEs with globally
Lipschitz coefficients
[
Klo+92
]
. In our setting,
the energy equation involves nonlinear drift and diffusion terms that
are not globally Lipschitz in
E_{t}
, due to their power-law form. This
precludes direct application of standard convergence theory.

Imposing a killing boundary at
E=E_{\min}>0
restricts the state
space to a compact subdomain on which the coefficients are Lipschitz,
ensuring existence and uniqueness and retaining the
O(h^{1/2})

convergence rate in practice. Whether one works directly with
E_{t}
or
with the log-energy variable
Y_{t}=\log E_{t}
, this restriction makes
the coefficients Lipschitz on the relevant domain. In the
log-transformed setting they are globally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
,
so the standard Euler–Maruyama convergence theory applies directly.

3.1 Remark

(Loss of Structure under Euler Discretisation)
.

While Euler–Maruyama is consistent, it does not preserve key
geometric properties of the SDE system (
26
).

For the angular dynamics, the update does not maintain a unit-norm
constraint, so the iterates drift off the sphere. Post hoc
projection can restore the norm constraint, but this introduces bias
and destroys the invariant measure structure of the continuous
angular diffusion. In particular, for constant diffusion, the
uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is invariant under the
continuous dynamics (Proposition
2.4
) but not under
the discretised scheme. These discrepancies accumulate in long-time
simulations and compromise the fidelity of angular marginals.

For the energy dynamics, direct discretisation of
E_{t}
can also
produce negative energies, since the Gaussian increment may outweigh
the deterministic drift. This violates the strict positivity of
physical energy and can lead to instability. The log-transformed
formulation avoids this issue by evolving
Y_{t}=\log E_{t}
in

\mathbb{R}
, ensuring that
E_{n}=\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right)
remains strictly
positive at all times.

3.1.
Geometric Angular Update

A common workaround for norm-constraint violation is to apply an
explicit normalisation after each Euler update, that is post-process

(46)

\Omega_{n+1}=\!\left({1-\epsilon_{0}h}\right)\Omega_{n}+\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}\xi_{n}^{S},\quad\Omega_{n+1}\leftarrow\frac{\Omega_{n+1}}{\left|\Omega_{n+1}\right|}.

While this projection enforces norm preservation, it introduces a
statistical bias as the renormalisation step distorts the distribution
of
\Omega_{n}
and breaks consistency with the continuous SDE.

A more consistent approach is to update
\Omega_{n}
using the
exponential map on the sphere

(47)

\Omega_{n+1}=\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right),

where
\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}
denotes the Riemannian exponential map on the
sphere. This operation transports
\Omega_{n}
along the geodesic
determined by
\xi_{n}^{S}
, ensuring that the update remains on the
sphere while preserving the correct diffusion behaviour.

3.2 Remark

(Exact Simulation of Angular Process with Constant
\epsilon
)
.

When
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}
is constant the process admits a symmetry
reduction that allows exact sampling from the transition
law
[
MMB20
,
BR05
]
. Specifically, by
rotating the coordinate system so that the initial direction aligns
with the north pole, the process reduces to a time-homogeneous
Wright–Fisher diffusion for the polar angle. This permits exact
simulation of
\Omega_{t}
by drawing from known distributions on

\mathbb{S}^{2}
, conditioned on the elapsed time and initial
orientation. This method provides exact marginal samples and
bypasses the need for time stepping when only the angular position
at fixed time is required
[
Cro+24
]
. For
energy-dependent
\epsilon(E)
, in general no such reduction is
available and time discretisation is necessary.

3.3 Theorem

(Strong Convergence of the Geometric Euler Scheme)
.

Let
(X_{t},Y_{t},\Omega_{t})
be the solution to the log-transformed
SDE system (
26
), with
E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
. Let
(X_{n},Y_{n},\Omega_{n})
denote the numerical approximation given by the geometric
Euler scheme

(48)

\begin{split}X_{n+1}&=X_{n}+h\Omega_{n},\\
Y_{n+1}&=Y_{n}-hS(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)-\tfrac{h}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{n}}\right)+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)\xi_{n}^{E},\\
\Omega_{n+1}&=\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right),\end{split}

where
\xi_{n}^{E}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h)
is a scalar Gaussian increment
for the energy noise, and
\xi_{n}^{S}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,hI_{3})
is a
tangent vector sampled in
T_{\Omega_{n}}\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The map

\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}
denotes the exponential map on the sphere. Then the
scheme satisfies the strong convergence estimate

(49)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\!\left({\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}+\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|^{2}+\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right)}\right]\leq Ch,

where
E_{n}=\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right)
and
C
is a constant independent of
h
. That
is, the method converges strongly with order
O(h^{1/2})
.

Proof.

We begin with the energy variable. The drift and diffusion
coefficients in the
Y_{t}
SDE are given by smooth compositions of
the form
S(\exp\!\left({y}\right))\exp\!\left({-y}\right)
and
T(\exp\!\left({y}\right))\exp\!\left({-2y}\right)
, which are smooth and
locally Lipschitz for
y\in\mathbb{R}
provided
T>0
since

E_{min}>0
. Although they do not satisfy global linear growth
conditions due to their exponential decay as
y\to-\infty
, the
solution
Y_{t}
remains bounded with high probability on finite time
intervals, since the energy process
E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
decays over
time.

Therefore, the Euler–Maruyama scheme applied to
Y_{t}
converges
strongly with order
O(h^{1/2})
, by standard localisation arguments
(see
[
Klo+92
, Thm 10.2.2]
). We obtain

(50)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|Y_{n}-Y(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch,

and thus, by smoothness of the exponential,

(51)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch.

For the angular variable we proceed by estimating the error via the
identity

(52)

\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega(t_{n+1})=\!\left[{\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon(E_{n})}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right)-\text{Exp}_{\Omega(t_{n})}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon(E(t_{n}))}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right)}\right]+\delta_{n},

where
\delta_{n}=\text{Exp}_{\Omega(t_{n})}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon(E(t_{n}))}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right)-\Omega(t_{n+1})
denotes the local discretisation
error. The first term is controlled via Lipschitz continuity of the
exponential map in both base point and argument

(53)

\left|\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon(E_{n})}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right)-\text{Exp}_{\Omega(t_{n})}\!\left({\sqrt{2\epsilon(E(t_{n}))}\xi_{n}^{S}}\right)\right|\leq C\!\left({\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|+\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|}\right)\left|\xi_{n}^{S}\right|.

Taking expectations and using
\left|\xi_{n}^{S}\right|=O(h^{1/2})
in mean
square, and
\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|=O(h^{1/2})
, we obtain

(54)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega(t_{n+1})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq(1+Ch)\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]+Ch^{2}.

By Grönwall’s inequality, this implies

(55)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch.

Finally, the position update is deterministic and, upon noting

(56)

X(t_{n+1})=X(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\Omega(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

subtracting and taking norms,

(57)

\left|X_{n+1}-X(t_{n+1})\right|\leq\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|+h\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|+\left|\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\!\left({\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|.

The last term is
O(h^{3/2})
in mean square due to Hölder
continuity of
\Omega_{t}
. Using the previous bound on the angular
error, we conclude

(58)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2},

completing the proof.
∎

3.4 Remark

(Structure preservation under the geometric Euler scheme)
.

The log-transformed geometric Euler scheme (
48
)
retains the main structural features of the continuous dynamics
(
26
). The logarithmic variable guarantees that the
numerical energy iterates satisfy
E_{n}>0
for all
n
, without
requiring projection or truncation. The exponential-map update
enforces unitary angle at every step, so the angular trajectory
remains on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
by construction.

When diffusion is constant, the angular dynamics coincide with
Brownian motion on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and the scheme is consistent with
convergence to the uniform distribution (cf. Proposition

2.4
). When
\epsilon=\epsilon(E_{t})
depends on the
energy, the angular update still preserves the sphere constraint,
but stationarity with respect to the uniform law is lost. In this
regime, the long-time distribution of
\Omega_{n}
reflects the
coupling between energy loss and angular diffusion rather than an
invariant measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

3.4.
Illustrative Numerical Example - Angular Drift in Two Dimensions

To highlight the structural deficiencies of the naive Euler scheme, we
consider angular diffusion on the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
discretised over a long time interval. We compare the standard Euler
update

(59)

\Omega_{n+1}=\Omega_{n}\!\left({1-\epsilon h}\right)+\sqrt{2\epsilon h}\xi_{n},\quad\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2}),

applied both with and without explicit renormalisation, against the
structure-preserving update based on the exponential map. In
two dimensions, this corresponds to a rotation

(60)

\Omega_{n+1}=\operatorname{Rot}(\theta_{n})\Omega_{n},\quad\theta_{n}=\sqrt{2\epsilon h}\,\lambda_{n},\quad\lambda_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The naive Euler scheme accumulates norm error and introduces bias in
the angular distribution, particularly in long-time simulations. In
contrast, the exponential map method preserves the unit-norm
constraint exactly and reproduces the expected uniform distribution in
angle.

Figure
2
shows representative trajectories and
histograms of sampled angles after
N=10^{5}
steps with time step
h=10^{-2}
. The difference in long-time behaviour is clearly visible.

4.
Higher-Order Structure-Preserving Schemes

For applications requiring higher accuracy, we now construct
second-order structure-preserving schemes for both the angular and
energy components. For the energy dynamics, we apply a Milstein
discretisation to the log-transformed variable
Y_{t}=\log\!\left({E_{t}}\right)
,
ensuring positivity while achieving strong
O(h)
convergence. The
angular integrator is then based on the Runge–Kutta–Munthe–Kaas
(RKMK) framework, which lifts the dynamics into the Lie algebra,
performs a higher-order update and maps back to the sphere via the
exponential map
[
Mun+22
]
. These two components are
combined into a fully structure-preserving scheme for the coupled
system.

4.0.
Log-Energy Milstein Scheme

The evolution of energy follows the stochastic differential equation

(61)

\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}=-S(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(E_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E}.

To improve numerical accuracy while preserving the positivity of
energy, we apply a second-order Milstein discretisation to the
logarithmic variable
Y_{t}=\log\!\left({E_{t}}\right)
. This approach avoids the
need for artificial projection and ensures that the reconstructed
energy
E_{t}=\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right)
remains strictly positive.

Applying Itô’s lemma, the log-transformed SDE takes the form

(62)

\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\!\left({-S(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{t}}\right)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{t}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{t}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E}.

Applying the Milstein scheme to this SDE yields the update

(63)

\begin{split}Y_{n+1}&=Y_{n}-hS(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)-\tfrac{h}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{n}}\right)+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)\xi_{n}^{E}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\!\left({\frac{T^{\prime}(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)}{2}-T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{n}}\right)}\right)\!\left({(\xi_{n}^{E})^{2}-h}\right),\end{split}

where
\xi_{n}^{E}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h)
.

4.1 Lemma

(Positivity and strong order
1
for the log–energy Milstein scheme)
.

Let
E_{t}
solve (
61
) on
[0,T]
with initial

E_{0}>E_{\min}>0
and impose killing at
E_{\min}
. Assume
S,T\in C^{2}([E_{\min},\infty))
,
T(E)\geq c_{0}>0
on
[E_{\min},\infty)
. Let

Y_{t}=\log E_{t}
and let
Y_{n}
be defined by the Milstein update
(
63
) with stepsize
h=T/N
, and set

E_{n}=\exp(Y_{n})
. Then,
E_{n}>0
for all
n
, and there exists a
constant
C
(independent of
h
) such that

(64)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}.

In particular,
E_{n}
converges to
E(t_{n})
with strong order
1
.

Proof.

Positivity is immediate since
E_{n}=\exp(Y_{n})
. Write the log-energy
SDE as

(65)

\begin{split}\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}&=\mu(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+b(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E},\\
\mu(y)&=-S(\exp\!\left({y}\right))\exp\!\left({-y}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}T(\exp\!\left({y}\right))\exp\!\left({-2y}\right)\\
b(y)&=\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({y}\right))}\,\exp\!\left({-y}\right).\end{split}

Because
E_{t}\in[E_{\min},\infty)
up to the killing time and
S,T\in C^{2}([E_{\min},\infty))
with bounded derivatives, the compositions

\mu,b\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(globally Lipschitz with bounded
first and second derivatives). Hence the classical Milstein
convergence theorem (see, e.g.,

[
Klo+92
, Thm. 10.6.2]
) applies to the scheme
(
63
), giving

(66)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|Y_{n}-Y(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}.

To transfer this estimate to
E=\exp(Y)
, use the mean value
theorem. For each
n
there exists
\zeta_{n}
between
Y_{n}
and

Y(t_{n})
such that

(67)

\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|=\left|\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right)-\exp\!\left({Y(t_{n})}\right)\right|=\exp\!\left({\zeta_{n}}\right)\left|Y_{n}-Y(t_{n})\right|.

Since
E(t)\geq E_{\min}
up to killing and
E_{n}=\exp(Y_{n})>0
, we
have
\exp\!\left({\xi_{n}}\right)\leq C
uniformly on
[0,T]
(the constant depends
on
E_{\min}
and on a moment bound for
E
but not on

h
). Squaring and taking the supremum over
n
,

(68)

\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|^{2}\leq C\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|Y_{n}-Y(t_{n})\right|^{2}.

Taking expectations and combining with the Milstein bound for
Y

yields the desired result.
∎

4.1.
Lie-Group Angular Integrator

At each
\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, choose an oriented orthonormal frame

\{u_{1}(\Omega),u_{2}(\Omega)\}
of the tangent plane

T_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}^{2}
(with
u_{1}(\Omega)\times u_{2}(\Omega)=\Omega
)
and define the tangent vector fields

(69)

\sigma_{i}(\Omega):=u_{i}(\Omega)\times\Omega,\qquad i=1,2.

The particular smooth frame chosen does not affect the law of the
process, all such choices generate the same operator on functions of

\Omega
.

With this notation, the angular dynamics are the Stratonovich SDE

(70)

\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}=\sqrt{2\epsilon(E_{t})}\!\left({\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{t})\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{(1)}+\sigma_{2}(\Omega_{t})\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{(2)}}\right),

where
W^{(1)},W^{(2)}
are independent standard Brownian motions.
The fields
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
are smooth, tangent and satisfy the
commutator identity

(71)

[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}](\Omega)=-\,\Omega,

so they do not commute. This bracket (a rotation about the normal
axis) is the source of the Lévy–area correction in strong
order–
1
integrators.

Writing
\Omega=(\operatorname{sin}\left(\theta\right)\operatorname{cos}\left(\phi\right),\operatorname{sin}\left(\theta\right)\operatorname{sin}\left(\phi\right),\operatorname{cos}\left(\theta\right))
, a
standard orthonormal frame (smooth away from the poles) is

(72)

u_{1}(\Omega)=(\operatorname{cos}\left(\theta\right)\operatorname{cos}\left(\phi\right),\operatorname{cos}\left(\theta\right)\operatorname{sin}\left(\phi\right),-\operatorname{sin}\left(\theta\right)),\qquad u_{2}(\Omega)=(-\operatorname{sin}\left(\phi\right),\operatorname{cos}\left(\phi\right),0).

Over one step
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
we first approximate the diffusion clock increment

(73)

\Delta\gamma_{n}:=h\big(\epsilon(E_{n})+\epsilon(E_{n+1})\big)\approx 2\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\epsilon(E_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s=:\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}

which acts as the process’s internal time. Careful discretisation
ensures consistency across components and prevents numerical time
paradoxes.

Now draw
(\Delta W_{n}^{(1)},\Delta W_{n}^{(2)})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta\gamma_{n}I_{2})
and a scalar Lévy area

(74)

A_{n}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\big(\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{(2)}-\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{(1)}\big),

using, e.g., the Wiktorsson method. For two driving dimensions,
A_{n}

has mean
0
, variance
\Delta\gamma_{n}^{2}/12
and must be simulated

jointly
with
(\Delta W_{n}^{(1)},\Delta W_{n}^{(2)})
.

Define the Lie algebra increment

(75)

\xi_{n}:=\Delta W_{n}^{(1)}u_{1}(\Omega_{n})+\Delta W_{n}^{(2)}u_{2}(\Omega_{n})-A_{n}\Omega_{n}.

Finally, perform the angular update as a rotation

(76)

\Omega_{n+1}=\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}(\xi_{n}).

4.2 Lemma

(Angular strong order
1
)
.

Assume the conditions of Lemma
4.1
hold.
Suppose the diffusion clock (
73
) is approximated by
an adapted quadrature based on the Milstein energy such that

(77)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Delta\gamma_{n}-\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}\right|^{2}}\right]=O(h^{3}).

With the Lie Algebra element defined by (
75
) and the
update (
76
), there exists
C>0
independent of
h

such that

(78)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}.

Proof.

Define the clock
\gamma(t)=2\int_{0}^{t}\epsilon(E_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s
and let

s_{n}=\gamma(t_{n})
, so

\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}=s_{n+1}-s_{n}
. Consider the
time-changed process
\widetilde{\Omega}_{s}:=\Omega_{\gamma^{-1}(s)}
.
By the time-change theorem for Stratonovich SDEs,
\widetilde{\Omega}

solves on
[s_{n},s_{n+1}]

(79)

\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\Omega}_{s}=\sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{s})\circ\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{(1)}+\sigma_{2}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{s})\circ\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{(2)},

where
B^{(1)},B^{(2)}
are independent Brownian motions in the
clock
s
. Let
\Delta B_{n}^{(i)}=B_{s_{n+1}}^{(i)}-B_{s_{n}}^{(i)}

and
A_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{s_{n}}^{s_{n+1}}\big(\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{(1)}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{(2)}-\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{(2)}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{(1)}\big)
. The
Stratonovich stochastic Taylor expansion for smooth vector fields

\sigma_{i}
on the compact manifold
\mathbb{S}^{2}
yields

(80)

\widetilde{\Omega}_{s_{n+1}}=\widetilde{\Omega}_{s_{n}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sigma_{i}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{s_{n}})\Delta B_{n}^{(i)}+[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}](\widetilde{\Omega}_{s_{n}})A_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}+R_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}},

with a remainder satisfying

(81)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|R_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}\right|^{2}}\right]\leq C(\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}})^{3}.

Here all coefficients and their derivatives are bounded because

\mathbb{S}^{2}
is compact and terms of weight
\geq 3/2
in the
Stratonovich hierarchy contribute

O\big((\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}})^{3/2}\big)
in RMS, hence

O\big((\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}})^{3}\big)
in mean
square. Moreover, with the oriented frame
u_{1}\times u_{2}=\Omega
one
has
[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}](\Omega)=-\Omega
.

Now, freeze the frame at
\Omega_{n}
and form

(82)

\xi_{n}=\Delta W_{n}^{(1)}u_{1}(\Omega_{n})+\Delta W_{n}^{(2)}u_{2}(\Omega_{n})-A_{n}\Omega_{n},

with
(\Delta W_{n}^{(1)},\Delta W_{n}^{(2)},A_{n})
sampled over duration

\Delta\gamma_{n}
. For small
\xi
, the Riemannian exponential on

\mathbb{S}^{2}
admits the expansion

(83)

\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}(\xi)=\Omega_{n}+\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{n})\Delta W_{n}^{(1)}+\sigma_{2}(\Omega_{n})\Delta W_{n}^{(2)}+[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}](\Omega_{n})A_{n}+\widehat{R}_{n},

where
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\widehat{R}_{n}\right|^{2}}\right]\leq C(\Delta\gamma_{n})^{3}
. This follows by writing the update as the
rotation
\exp([\xi_{n}]_{\times})\Omega_{n}
(with
[\cdot]_{\times}
the
skew matrix), expanding the flow map in the Lie algebra via the
Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series and using

\left|\xi_{n}\right|=O\!\left({(\Delta\gamma_{n})^{1/2}}\right)
in RMS and the bracket
identity
[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}](\Omega_{n})=-\Omega_{n}
. The cubic bound
on
\widehat{R}_{n}
in mean square is a consequence of the smoothness
of the exponential map on the compact manifold and the boundedness
of the fields and their derivatives.

We now compare the exact clock increments
(\Delta B_{n}^{(i)},A_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}})
over
\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}

with the simulated increments
(\Delta W_{n}^{(i)},A_{n})
over

\Delta\gamma_{n}
. By construction

(84)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Delta W_{n}-\Delta B_{n}\right|^{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Delta\gamma_{n}-\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}\right|^{2}}\right]=O(h^{3/2})

in RMS, hence
O(h^{3})
in mean square. Similarly,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|A_{n}-A_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}\right|^{2}}\right]=O(h^{3})
because

\operatorname{Var}(A)=\Delta\gamma^{2}/12
and the variance error is

O(h^{3})
. Since the coefficients
\sigma_{i}
and the bracket are
bounded, replacing
(\Delta B_{n}^{(i)},A_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}})
by

(\Delta W_{n}^{(i)},A_{n})
in the expansions (
80
) and in the
numerical update produces a mean-square perturbation of order

O(h^{3})
.

Combining the above and identifying

\widetilde{\Omega}_{s_{n}}=\Omega(t_{n})
, the one-step error in clock
time satisfies

(85)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega(t_{n+1})\right|^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{n}}}\right]\leq(1+Ch)\|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\|^{2}+Ch^{3},

since
\Delta\gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{ex}}=O(h)
and all Lipschitz constants are uniform
(on the compact manifold). Taking expectations and iterating yields

(86)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega(t_{n+1})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq(1+Ch)\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]+Ch^{3}.

By the discrete Grönwall lemma this gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}
uniformly in

n
. A standard Doob-Kolmogorov argument then yields the desired result.
∎

4.3 Theorem

(Strong convergence of the full scheme)
.

Suppose the conditions of Lemmata
4.1
and

4.2
hold. Let the tuple
(X_{t},Y_{t},\Omega_{t})
solve
the SDE system (
26
) on
[0,T]
, with killing at

E_{\min}>0
. Let
(X_{n},Y_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n}
be the numerical approximation
given by

(87)

\begin{split}X_{n+1}&=X_{n}+h\Omega_{n},\\
Y_{n+1}&=Y_{n}-hS(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)-\tfrac{h}{2}T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{n}}\right)+\sqrt{T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))}\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)\xi_{n}^{E}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\!\left({\frac{T^{\prime}(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-Y_{n}}\right)}{2}-T(\exp\!\left({Y_{n}}\right))\exp\!\left({-2Y_{n}}\right)}\right)\!\left({(\xi_{n}^{E})^{2}-h}\right),\\
\Omega_{n+1}&=\text{Exp}_{\Omega_{n}}(\xi_{n}),\end{split}

with
\xi_{n}
prescribed by (
75
) and
E_{n}=\exp{Y_{n}}
.

Then, there exists
C>0
independent of
h
such that

(88)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\!\left({\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}+\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|^{2}+\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right)}\right]\leq Ch^{2}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
we have

(89)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|E_{n}-E(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2},

and by Lemma
4.2
,

(90)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}.

It remains to bound the spatial error. Write

(91)

X_{n+1}-X(t_{n+1})=\!\left({X_{n}-X(t_{n})}\right)+h\!\left({\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})}\right)+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\!\left({\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Taking norms, squaring and using
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
,

(92)

\left|X_{n+1}-X(t_{n+1})\right|^{2}\leq 3\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}+3h^{2}\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}+3\left|\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}.

For the last term, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

(93)

\left|\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\leq h\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\left|\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since
\Omega
is a diffusion with smooth coefficients on the compact
manifold
\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s)\right|}\right]^{2}\leq C\left|s-t_{n}\right|
. Hence

(94)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(\Omega(t_{n})-\Omega(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}(s-t_{n})\,\mathrm{d}s=Ch^{3}.

Taking expectations in the recursive bound and summing, we get

(95)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|X_{n+1}-X(t_{n+1})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq(1+Ch)\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]+Ch^{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]+Ch^{3}.

Using
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{n}\left|\Omega_{n}-\Omega(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}
from
Lemma
4.2
and applying the discrete Grönwall lemma
yields

(96)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sup_{0\leq n\leq N}\left|X_{n}-X(t_{n})\right|^{2}}\right]\leq Ch^{2}.

Combining the three componentwise bounds proves the stated estimate.
∎

4.4 Remark

(Structure preservation under the higher–order scheme)
.

The structure preserving features of the geometric Euler method are
retained by the higher accuracy scheme. In energy, the Milstein
update for the log variable preserves positivity exactly and
improves pathwise accuracy to strong order
1
without any
projection. In angle, the update is performed intrinsically on

\mathbb{S}^{2}
via the exponential map, so the unit-norm constraint
is preserved exactly at every step. For the angular accuracy
incorporating the Lévy-area terms yield a Milstein/Castell-Gaines
integrator of strong order
1
.

These refinements do not alter the geometric or physical constraints
of the model, but they do improve convergence rates while
maintaining fidelity to the manifold structure. The full scheme
therefore remains confined to the physically admissible state space
and is well suited to long time simulation and to sensitivity
computations.

5.
Sensitivity of quantities of interest

The energy dynamics in (
26
) depend on the parameters

\alpha
,
p
and
\kappa
, which enter through the Bragg–Kleeman
stopping power law and the straggling variance. In practice, these
parameters are often uncertain, require calibration, or must be
analysed for their influence on physically relevant observables. It is
therefore natural to study the sensitivity of such quantities of
interest with respect to variations in
\alpha
,
p
and
\kappa
.

5.0.
Introducing quantities of interest

In proton transport, am important observable is the expected dose deposited,
defined by

(97)

\displaystyle D(x;\theta):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau(\theta)}S(E_{t})\varphi(X_{t}-x)\,\mathrm{d}t}\right],

where
\varphi
is a spatial kernel that localises energy deposition
near
x
and
S(E_{t})
is the stopping power. This functional depends
on parameters
\theta\in\{\alpha,p,\kappa\}
through the energy
path
E_{t}=\exp(Y_{t})
. The stopping time
\tau(\theta):=\inf\{t\geq 0:E_{t}\leq E_{\min}\}
also depends on
\theta
via
E_{t}
. As
before, we impose a killing boundary at
E_{\min}>0
so that the
process is terminated when the energy drops below this threshold.

We are interested in the sensitivity of
D(x;\theta)
with respect to

\theta
. Under sufficient regularity conditions, the derivative can
be taken inside the expectation, giving

(98)

\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\theta}D(x;\theta)&=\mathbb{E}\bigg[S(E_{T\wedge\tau(\theta)})\varphi(X_{T\wedge\tau(\theta)}-x)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\theta}(T\wedge\tau(\theta))\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau(\theta)}\!\left({\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta}(E_{t})+\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}(E_{t})\partial_{\theta}E_{t}}\right)\varphi(X_{t}-x)\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg].\end{split}

5.1 Remark

(Differentiability of the stopping time)
.

The representation (
98
) is valid when the parameter
derivative may be exchanged with expectation

[
Gla04
, Section 7.2.2]
and in particular requires
that the stopping time
\tau(\theta)
is sufficiently smooth in

\theta
. This condition holds for
\alpha
and
p
when
\kappa=0
,
since the energy trajectory is then deterministic and a closed-form
expression for
\tau(\theta)
is available. However, when
\kappa\neq 0
, stochastic fluctuations in the energy may cause small
parameter perturbations to induce large changes in the hitting time
of
E_{\min}
. In this case,
\tau(\theta)
fails to be
differentiable, and a modification of the dose definition is
required to obtain pathwise sensitivities; see

subsection 5.1
.

5.1.
Deterministic case (
\kappa=0
)

When
\kappa=0
, the energy evolution becomes deterministic and satisfies the ODE

(99)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}E}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=-S(E),\qquad E(0)=E_{0}.

With
S(E)=\!\left({p\alpha}\right)^{-1}E^{1-p}
, the closed-form solution is

(100)

E(t)=\bigl(E_{0}^{p}-t/\alpha\bigr)^{1/p},

which is strictly decreasing and well defined up to the critical time

T_{c}=\alpha E_{0}^{p}
at which
E(t)
vanishes.

The deterministic stopping time
T(\theta)
, defined by

E(T(\theta))=E_{\min}
, is therefore

(101)

T(\theta)=\alpha\bigl(E_{0}^{p}-E_{\min}^{p}\bigr).

The dose functional reduces to

(102)

D(x;\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T(\theta)}S(E_{t})\,\varphi(X_{t}-x)\,\,\mathrm{d}t\right],

where randomness remains only through the spatial trajectory
X_{t}
and
angular component
\Omega_{t}
.

Differentiating with respect to
\theta\in\{\alpha,p\}
gives

(103)

\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\theta}D(x;\theta)&=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[S(E_{T(\theta)})\varphi(X_{T(\theta)}-x)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}T}{\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\\
&\qquad+\int_{0}^{T(\theta)}\!\left({\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta}(E_{t})+\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}(E_{t})\,\partial_{\theta}E_{t}}\right)\varphi(X_{t}-x)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg],\end{split}

where
E_{T(\theta)}=E_{\min}
by construction. The stopping-time
sensitivities are

(104)

\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}T}{\,\mathrm{d}\alpha}&=E_{0}^{p}-E_{\min}^{p},\\
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}T}{\,\mathrm{d}p}&=\alpha\!\left({\log(E_{0})E_{0}^{p}-\log(E_{\min})E_{\min}^{p}}\right).\end{split}

Since
E(t)
is known in closed form, its parameter derivatives can also be computed explicitly

(105)

\begin{split}\partial_{\alpha}E(t)&=\frac{t}{\alpha^{2}p}\!\left({E_{0}^{p}-t/\alpha}\right)^{1/p-1},\\
\partial_{p}E(t)&=\frac{E_{0}^{p}\log(E_{0})}{p}\!\left({E_{0}^{p}-t/\alpha}\right)^{1/p-1}-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\log\!\left({E_{0}^{p}-\tfrac{t}{\alpha}}\right)\!\left({E_{0}^{p}-t/\alpha}\right)^{1/p}.\end{split}

5.1.
Regularisation under stochastic straggling

When
\kappa\neq 0
, the energy path is stochastic and the stopping
time
\tau(\theta)
is no longer differentiable with respect to
parameters. Small parameter changes may cause large fluctuations in

\tau(\theta)
due to early crossings of the boundary
E_{\min}
,
making sensitivities calculated according to
Equation 98
ill-posed.
To address this, we introduce a regularised formulation of dose.

First, we replace the random stopping time by an indicator function
inside the time integral

(106)

D(x;\theta)\approx\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\int_{0}^{T}S(E_{t})\varphi(X_{t}-x)\mathbf{1}_{\{E_{t}>E_{\min}\}}\,\mathrm{d}t}\right].

This modification has little effect on the numerical value of the
dose, since near
E_{\min}
the deterministic drift dominates the
diffusion and re-entry above the threshold is unlikely.

However, the discontinuous indicator function still prevents
differentiability with respect to parameters. We therefore introduce a
smoothed approximation

(107)

\mathbf{1}_{\{E_{t}>E_{\min}\}}\approx\mathcal{I}(E_{t},E_{\min}):=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left({1+\tanh\!\left({\tfrac{E_{t}-E_{\min}}{\delta}}\right)}\right),

where
\delta>0
controls the smoothing scale. This mollification
ensures that the integrand is differentiable in both
E_{t}
and the
parameters
\theta
.

With this modification, the pathwise derivative of the dose is

(108)

\frac{d}{d\theta}D(x;\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\int_{0}^{T}\!\left({\!\left({\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta}(E_{t})+\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}(E_{t})\partial_{\theta}E_{t}}\right)\mathcal{I}(E_{t},E_{\min})+S(E_{t})\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\mathcal{I}(E_{t},E_{\min})}\right)\varphi(X_{t}-x)\,\mathrm{d}t}\right],

with

(109)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\mathcal{I}(E_{t},E_{\min})=\frac{\partial\mathcal{I}}{\partial E}(E_{t},E_{\min})\,\partial_{\theta}E_{t}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}E_{t}}{2\delta\,\cosh^{2}\!\bigl(\tfrac{E_{t}-E_{\min}}{\delta}\bigr)}.

6.
Pathwise Sensitivities and Observable Gradients

Building on
section 5
, we now turn to derivatives of
path-dependent observables with respect to the parameters

\alpha,p,\kappa
that govern the energy dynamics in

Equation 26
. Such derivatives are known as
pathwise
or

forward
sensitivities. They are obtained by differentiating the
stochastic system with respect to the parameters, leading to an
augmented system of SDEs in which the sensitivities evolve alongside
the original state variables.

In what follows we derive the sensitivity equations for the energy,
discuss their discretisation, and illustrate their behaviour under
parameter variation.

6.0.
Sensitivity Equations

For pathwise parameter sensitivities we work with the model in

Equation 14
but with constant angular diffusion

(110)

\begin{split}\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\Omega_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\\
\,\mathrm{d}E_{t}&=-S(E_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{T(E_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E},\\
\,\mathrm{d}\Omega_{t}&=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{S},\end{split}

where
W^{E}
and
W^{S}
are independent Brownian motions. We are
interested in the energy sensitivities with respect to

\theta\in\{\alpha,p,\kappa\}
, defined by

(111)

J_{t}^{\theta}:=\partial_{\theta}E_{t},\qquad J_{0}^{\theta}=\partial_{\theta}E_{0}(=0\text{ if }E_{0}\text{ is fixed}).

Differentiating the energy SDE with respect to
\theta
yields the
linear SDE

(112)

\,\mathrm{d}J_{t}^{\theta}=-\Big(\partial_{\theta}S(E_{t})+\partial_{E}S(E_{t})J_{t}^{\theta}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\Big(\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{T(E_{t})}+\partial_{E}\sqrt{T(E_{t})}J_{t}^{\theta}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{E}.

For each
\theta
this SDE is driven by the
same
Brownian
motion
W^{E}
as the primary energy process and can be evolved in
parallel to compute sensitivities of observables depending on

E_{t}=\exp(Y_{t})
.

6.0.
Explicit Derivatives of Drift and Diffusion Coefficients

Since only the energy dynamics depend on the parameters

\alpha,p,\kappa
, it suffices to compute derivatives of the drift

S(E)
and diffusion
\sqrt{T(E)}
with respect to
E
and the
parameters. These determine both the coefficients of the sensitivity
equations and the inhomogeneous forcing terms.

Derivatives with respect to the energy variable are

(113)

\begin{split}\partial_{E}S(E)&=\frac{1-p}{\alpha p}E^{-p}=\frac{(1-p)}{E}S(E),\\
\partial_{E}\sqrt{T(E)}&=\Bigl(1-\tfrac{p}{2}\Bigr)\sqrt{\tfrac{\kappa}{\alpha p}}E^{-p/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{T^{\prime}(E)}{\sqrt{T(E)}}.\end{split}

Derivatives with respect to the model parameters are as follows. With
respect to
\alpha

(114)

\begin{split}\partial_{\alpha}S(E)&=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}S(E),\\
\partial_{\alpha}\sqrt{T(E)}&=-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\sqrt{T(E)}.\end{split}

With respect to
p

(115)

\begin{split}\partial_{p}S(E)&=-\tfrac{1}{p}S(E)-(\log E)S(E)=-\tfrac{E^{1-p}}{\alpha p^{2}}-\tfrac{\log E}{\alpha p}E^{1-p},\\
\partial_{p}\sqrt{T(E)}&=-\tfrac{1}{2p}\sqrt{T(E)}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\log E)\sqrt{T(E)}.\end{split}

With respect to
\kappa

(116)

\begin{split}\partial_{\kappa}S(E)&=0,\\
\partial_{\kappa}\sqrt{T(E)}&=\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}\sqrt{T(E)}.\end{split}

These expressions enter directly into the sensitivity SDEs and define
the forcing terms that drive the evolution of
J_{t}^{\theta}
.

6.1 Remark

(Structure of the Sensitivity System)
.

Unlike the original stochastic system, the sensitivity equations for

J_{t}^{\theta}=\partial_{\theta}E_{t}
evolve in the full real line with
no positivity or geometric constraints. They admit neither an
invariant measure nor a conserved structure.

Nevertheless, the discretisation of
J_{t}^{\theta}
must be coupled to
that of the other variables in a consistent manner. In particular,
the same Brownian increments must be used and the scheme should match
the order of the primary integrator. For example, when
E_{t}
is
simulated using a Milstein scheme, the sensitivities should also be
discretised with a Milstein method. This coupling guarantees strong
convergence of the joint system at a consistent rate of convergence.

6.1.

To simulate sensitivities for
\theta\in\{\alpha,p,\kappa\}
we add

Equation 110

through the energy equation and evolves independently of the others.

Euler–Maruyama discretisation

Given

and

at time

7.3.
Sensitivity computations

We now present a series of numerical experiments illustrating the
computation of pathwise sensitivities using the structure-preserving
Milstein discretisation developed in the preceding sections. These
experiments demonstrate the utility of the method for quantifying
parameter dependence of physical observables and for verifying the
convergence and variance reduction properties of the approach. Our
focus is on the expected dose observable defined in

section 5
.

7.4 Remark

(Interpretation of sensitivities)
.

The sensitivity profiles quantify how the expected dose distribution
shifts in response to perturbations of the stopping power
parameters. A negative sensitivity means that increasing the
parameter decreases dose at that spatial location, while a positive
sensitivity indicates dose amplification. For example, the negative
region on the proximal side and the positive region on the distal
side of the Bragg peak show that increasing
\alpha
or
p
shortens
the particle range and moves the peak towards the entrance, while
decreasing them lengthens the range.

These profiles provide local information on where uncertainties in
physical parameters most strongly affect dose deposition. Regions
with large sensitivity magnitude are those where parameter
uncertainty is most critical for treatment planning and robustness
analysis, while regions with near-zero sensitivity are less
affected. Thus, the sensitivity plots can be read as maps of dose
susceptibility to modelling error in the stopping power law.

To assess the accuracy of the pathwise method, we compare it with
central finite difference estimates. For a fixed observable

(122)

\Phi=\int_{0}^{T}S(E_{t})\varphi(X_{t}-x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t,

the finite difference gradient estimator is

(123)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\mathbb{E}[\Phi]\approx\frac{\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\theta+\Delta\theta)]-\mathbb{E}[\Phi(\theta-\Delta\theta)]}{2\Delta\theta}.

Unlike the pathwise approach, which is unbiased and enjoys reduced
variance, the finite difference estimator is biased except in the
limit
\Delta\theta\to 0
and the bias-variance tradeoff makes it
less efficient in practice.

The finite difference estimator involves an intrinsic
tradeoff. Decreasing the stepsize
\Delta\theta
reduces bias but
increases variance, requiring more Monte Carlo samples to control
noise. For the central difference estimator, the optimal balance
between
\Delta\theta
and sample size

yields a slow asymptotic
rate of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-2/5})
(see table 7.1 of

[

]

[

,
FG89
]
). This is
significantly worse than the
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})
variance
reduction achievable with the pathwise method.

8.
Conclusion

We have developed a structure-preserving framework for stochastic
proton transport that incorporates continuous slowing down and angular
diffusion in a geometrically consistent manner. The scheme enforces
positivity and manifold constraints exactly while remaining compatible
with standard SDE discretisations through a logarithmic transformation
for energy with a Lie-algebraic exponential map for direction.

The framework admits a Milstein extension that achieves strong
O(h)

convergence and enables efficient pathwise sensitivity analysis with
respect to physical parameters, including the Bragg–Kleeman
coefficients and the straggling strength. This provides a rigorous
basis for gradient-based parameter studies, uncertainty quantification
and calibration of transport models. Numerical experiments demonstrate
that pathwise estimators outperform finite difference methods in both
accuracy and variance, while remaining unbiased. The strong
convergence properties also open the way for advanced variance
reduction strategies, such as multilevel Monte Carlo

[
Gil08
,
Gil24
]
.

Future work will focus on extending the present framework to include
discrete inelastic events, coupling with heterogeneous media and
sensitivity analysis for rare low-energy events. Integration into
optimisation and Bayesian inference pipelines will require further
advances, particularly in handling discontinuous or non-smooth
observables.
