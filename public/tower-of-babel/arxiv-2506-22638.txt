Layer Importance for Mathematical Reasoning is Forged in Pre-Training and Invariant after Post-Training
Aadim Nepal, Safal Shrestha, Anubhav Shrestha, Minwu Kim, Keith Ross

Abstract
Large language models can exhibit improved mathematical reasoning capabilities following post-training with instruction tuning, reinforcement learning, or knowledge distillation. However, it remains unclear whether these improvements are driven by major changes in transformer layers or from minor adjustments that leave the relative layer importance structures of the base model largely unchanged. We investigate this question through systematic layer-wise ablation experiments, examining base, instruction-tuned, knowledge-distilled, and reinforcement learning variants on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Our findings show that mathematical reasoning gives rise to a specific layer importance structure, and this structure persists across all post-training paradigms. Removal of such layers causes accuracy drops of up to 80%. In contrast, non-mathematical tasks like factual recall exhibit no critical layers. This distinction suggests that mathematical reasoning requires specialized layers that emerge during pre-training, while other non-reasoning tasks do not. From an information-theoretic perspective, we also observe that these critical layers are the same layers where major representational transformation occurs. 1 1 1 Code available at https://github.com/AadimNepal/Layer-Ablation



1
Introduction

The capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have been improved through different post-training methods such as instruction tuning and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, knowledge distillation from a teacher model
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
, and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)
(
Lambert et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
.
Notably, these training methods have boosted mathematical reasoning performance across diverse benchmarks
(
Bai et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
OpenAI 2025
)
. Yet, the following question remains unresolved:

Do post-training methods primarily enhance the existing layer importance structure of transformers, or do they fundamentally change how models process mathematical problems?

This question carries important implications for model development and analysis. If post-training mainly strengthens existing representational structures within the transformer, efforts to improve reasoning may be directed toward enhancing these structures. On the other hand, if new structures are formed, it would indicate that post-training significantly reconfigures model’s internal representations to support novel reasoning capabilities.

In this paper, we investigate this question through an in-depth analysis of the internal representational structure of LLMs.
We conduct this analysis on two model families, Llama-3.1-8B
(
Meta 2024
)
and Qwen-2.5-7B
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, examining four variants for each: the pre-trained base model, an instruction-tuned model, a knowledge-distilled model, and an RLVR-trained model.
We evaluate these models on two mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
and MATH500
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
). We find that post-training largely preserves the layer importance structure in mathematical reasoning: the set of critical layers—those whose removal leads to substantial performance drop—remains largely invariant across all four model variants.

Additionally, we conducted the same experiment on the TriviaQA factual recall task
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
. Here, we observed a different layer importance pattern: no specific critical layers were found. Instead, removing any layer results in a smaller, more consistent drop in performance. Notably, this structure does not change across the different post-training methods, mirroring the stability we found for the mathematical reasoning task.

To begin to explore the purpose of the critical layers for mathematical reasoning, we also carry out a Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) analysis relative to the initial layer to measure how token representations change throughout the network. We present this analysis as preliminary and exploratory while acknowledging its limitations. We find that the most dramatic changes occur at the critical layers, as defined by our layer ablation study. Moreover, for the factual recall task, we observe relatively little changes in the NMI, again mirroring our results for layer ablation.

Our study presents several key findings:

•

Mathematical reasoning relies on specific critical layers, which upon removal lead to substantial performance drops.

•

The critical layers identified for mathematical reasoning maintain their importance after various post-training methods.

•

Unlike reasoning tasks, non-reasoning tasks like factual recall do not have critical layers; removal of any one layer only results in a small performance drop.

•

For mathematics tasks, the greatest divergence in how tokens are clustered occurs near the critical layers, providing insight into why these layers could play an important role in reasoning.

These findings suggest that mathematical reasoning capabilities are concentrated in specific layers that persist across training paradigms, offering new perspectives on how specialized reasoning abilities emerge and are maintained in LLMs.

Related Work

Identifying Critical Layers with Ablation.

Recent studies show that transformer models behave differently when their layers are changed. Some work shows that models can be robust to removing or reordering layers for certain tasks
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
, but are very sensitive for others, like multi-step reasoning
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
. This suggests that some parts of the model are more important than others. For example,
Zhang et al. 2024
used layer ablation to find ”cornerstone layers,” where removing just one layer causes the model’s performance to collapse. Similarly,
Yu et al. 2025
found that a very small set of parameters, or ”super weights,” are critical for model fluency.
Teplica et al. 2025
used zero ablation to show that uncertainty and factuality originate from the same shared circuits. Early work on BERT also showed that different layers specialize in different functions, acting like a classical NLP pipeline
(
Tenney et al. 2019
;
Van Aken et al. 2019
)
.
(
Lad et al. 2024
)
used layer ablation to demonstrate LLM robustness to layer deletion and applied attention analysis, logit lens probing, and neuron classification to identify four distinct stages of inference across model depth. Most studies focus on component-level analyses—ablating or probing individual attention heads, neurons, or MLP sub-blocks—to assess their contributions
(
Meng et al. 2022
;
Michel et al. 2019
;
Voita et al. 2019
;
Pochinkov et al. 2024
;
Elhage et al. 2021
)
.

While prior work has predominantly used zero-ablation to analyze isolated transformer components—such as neurons, attention heads, and sublayer circuits—our research employs systematic layer-wise ablation to show that mathematical reasoning relies heavily on critical layers that are established during pre-training. And most importantly, we further study the effects of post-training on the critical layers. This complements work such as
(
Du et al. 2025
)
that show post-training preserves knowledge storage locations and truthfulness directions, but do not examine layer-level stability through ablation and distinguish between reasoning and non-reasoning tasks.

Representational Analysis with NMI.

To understand
why
certain layers are important, researchers analyze the model’s internal representations. An information-theoretic approach can measure how these representations change across layers. Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) is a standard method to compare clustering results
(
Strehl & Ghosh 2002
)
, which makes it useful for comparing how tokens are grouped together at different layers. For example,
Jawahar et al. 2019
used various probing techniques, including comparing clusterings using NMI, to show that BERT learns a hierarchy of linguistic features, from surface-level features in early layers to more complex semantic features in deeper layers. Other work has qualitatively shown that token representations cluster differently at different stages of a task, such as question answering
(
Van Aken et al. 2019
)
. This connects to the idea that intermediate layers contain a rich representation
(
Skean et al. 2025
)
, and that the geometry of these representations is key to model capabilities
(
Gurnee & Tegmark 2023
)
.

Our work applies NMI analysis in a novel way to offer preliminary hypothesis as to why some of the layers we identified are critical. While others have used it to probe for specific linguistic properties, we use it to measure the representational divergence of each layer from the initial layer’s structure. This allows us to test if the largest representational transformations occur in the same layers identified as critical by our ablation studies.

3
Methodology

Our methodology is composed of two main parts. First, we use a zero-ablation technique to identify critical layers for mathematical reasoning.
Second, to understand why these layers matter, we introduce Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) analysis to measure how internal representations evolve across layers, revealing where key transformations occur that support task-specific computation.

3.1
Zero-Ablation of Transformer Layers

As illustrated in Figure
1
, our zero-ablation procedure systematically nullifies all parameters within a target layer while preserving the overall model architecture through its residual connections. For each target layer
\ell
in a given model, we set the weight matrices and bias vectors in both the multi-head attention and MLP sublayers to zero. Specifically, for the multi-head attention mechanism, we ablate:

\displaystyle W_{Q}^{(\ell)},W_{K}^{(\ell)},W_{V}^{(\ell)},W_{O}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle b_{Q}^{(\ell)},b_{K}^{(\ell)},b_{V}^{(\ell)},b_{O}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

For the MLP component, we ablate:

\displaystyle W_{\text{gate}}^{(\ell)},W_{\text{up}}^{(\ell)},W_{\text{down}}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle b_{\text{gate}}^{(\ell)},b_{\text{up}}^{(\ell)},b_{\text{down}}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

Here,
W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}
are the query, key, and value weight matrices;
W_{O}
is the output projection; and
W_{\text{gate}},W_{\text{up}},W_{\text{down}}
are the gating, up-projection, and down-projection matrices in the MLP.

When the self-attention and MLP sub-layers are zeroed out, the operation of layer
\ell
is reduced to its residual connections and normalization steps. The output of the ablated layer,
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}
, becomes a direct transformation of the previous layer’s output,
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}
:

\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}=\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)})

We conduct this analysis on two model families, Llama-3.1-8B
(
Meta 2024
)
and Qwen-2.5-7B
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, examining four variants for each:
(1) the pre-trained base models,
(2) instruction-tuned models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(
Meta 2024
)
and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
,
(3) knowledge-distilled models: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
, and
(4) RLVR-trained models: Llama-3.1-8B-SimpleRL-Zoo
(
Zeng et al. 2025
)
and Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B
(
Hu et al. 2025
)
.
Our evaluation spans mathematical reasoning (GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
, MATH500
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
) and factual recall (TriviaQA
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
) to distinguish task-specific processing patterns.

3.2
Representational Analysis via Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)

To understand
why
certain layers are critical, we analyze the evolution of the model’s internal representations using Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)
(
Strehl & Ghosh 2002
)
. While prior work has qualitatively described how token representations cluster across transformer layers
(
Van Aken et al. 2019
)
, our approach introduces a quantitative measure. We compute the NMI between the clustering of hidden states at layer 0 and the clustering of hidden states at each subsequent layer
\ell
. This enables us to track how far each layer’s cluster structure has diverged from the initial structure. See Appendix
6.5
for more details on the prompts used.

Our procedure is as follows:

1.

Extract Hidden States:
For a given task input, we perform a single forward pass and store the hidden state representations for each layer. For each layer
\ell
, we have a sequence of
T
token representations,
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}=(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{T})
, where each
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
d
is the dimension of the hidden state.

2.

Establish Baseline Clustering:
We apply K-Means clustering to the
T
vectors from layer 0,
(\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{T})
, to establish a set of baseline clusters,
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}=\{\mathbf{C}^{(0)}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}^{(0)}_{K}\}
. Note that we use zero-indexing, so layer 0 refers to the output of the first transformer layer.

3.

Cluster Subsequent Layers:
For each subsequent layer
\ell>0
, we apply K-Means with the same number of clusters,
K
, to its hidden state vectors
(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{T})
to obtain a new set of clusters,
\mathbf{C}^{(\ell)}=\{\mathbf{C}^{(\ell)}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{C}^{(\ell)}_{K}\}
.

4.

Calculate NMI:
We measure the similarity between the baseline clustering
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
and each subsequent layer’s clustering
\mathbf{C}^{(\ell)}
. Given two clusterings
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{D}
, the NMI is formulated using the arithmetic mean for normalization:
(
Strehl & Ghosh 2002
)

\text{NMI}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})=\frac{I(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})}{(H(\mathbf{C})+H(\mathbf{D}))/2}

This corresponds to the default method in the
sklearn.metrics.normalized_mutual_info_score
function used in our analysis. A detailed breakdown of each term—Mutual Information (
I
) and Entropy (
H
)—is provided in Appendix
6
.

A high NMI score between Layer 0 and Layer
\ell
indicates that the layer has preserved the initial, context-free semantic structure. Conversely, a low NMI score signals that the layer has performed significant transformation, reorganizing token relationships to build the abstract structures required for complex reasoning.

4
Results

4.1
Ablation studies reveal stable critical layers

To identify the critical layers for mathematical reasoning, we conduct layer ablation studies across the Qwen and Llama models on the GSM8K and MATH500 benchmarks. In addition, to assess the effect of post-training on the models, we perform the same zero-ablation studies on the instruction-tuned, distilled, and RLVR-trained variant of each model.

Mathematical Reasoning is Concentrated in Specific Critical Layers
Our analysis of the qwen and llama models shows that they handle step-by-step reasoning in distinct ways, with both concentrating this processing in specific layers. As shown in Figure
2
, the Qwen models exhibits strong performance degradation at layer 23 and, to a lesser extent, in the vicinity of layer 23. As shown in Appendix
6.4
, although zeroing out layer 23 causes a severe performance drop, the model’s linguistic fluency remains largely intact. The Llama models also show performance drops at specific layers; however, the drops occur at different places, mostly, at layers 15 and 18. The results, shown in Figure
2
, indicate that for most of these models, mathematical reasoning ability in the layers is not distributed broadly across the network. Instead, it is concentrated in specific layers established during pre-training.

Critical Layers are Invariant to Post-Training.

As shown in Figure
2
, the same layers that we identify as critical for math reasoning in the base model maintain their importance even after applying different post-training techniques. Furthermore, the layer importance structure remains largely unchanged across all training paradigms we examined regardless of problem difficulty(Figure
3
) or category (Figure
7
). This consistency suggests that the fundamental mechanisms for mathematical reasoning are established during pre-training and remain relatively stable. Subsequent post-training techniques refine the model’s behavior but do not appear to restructure these core reasoning layers.

Vulnerabilities Exist at Network Extremes.

Our analysis also confirms important functional roles for layers at the transformer’s boundaries. In alignment with recent work on ”super-weights”
Yu et al. 2025
, the earliest layers are important for basic semantic processing. Ablating Layer 1 in Qwen models, or the first two layers in Llama models, results in failure to generate coherent text. Some vulnerabilities also appear in the final layers of Llama models, highlighting further architectural distinctions.

4.2
Mathematical Reasoning has Greater Sensitivity to Layer Ablation than Factual Recall

To evaluate whether the observed pattern also holds for non-mathematical tasks, we conduct an identical layer ablation experiment on TriviaQA, a benchmark focused on factual recall. As shown in Figure
4
, although there is some small fluctuations among the layers, the performance drops are relatively small, with the maximum performance drop ranging in only 10-30%.
This stands in contrast to the mathematical benchmarks (Figure
2
), where performance is highly sensitive to the removal of specific layers. For mathematical reasoning, removing critical layers causes performance to drop substantially by 60-80% from the baseline, with the removal of some layers reducing accuracy to near-random levels.
This difference in behavior as a result of layer ablation suggests that mathematical reasoning takes place in specific layers of the transformer, and those layers can be identified by layer-ablation.

4.3
Representational Analysis

Figure 5
:
The plots show the NMI score (Y-axis) at each transformer layer (X-axis), calculated relative to the token clusters at Layer 0. The analysis covers the Qwen (left) and Llama (right) model families. The math task (top) shows a notable drop in NMI, with shifts concentrated in the critical regions observed with layer ablation. In contrast, the TriviaQA task (bottom) shows high NMI across all layers. The observed trends are robust to the number of clusters (k) used for the analysis, with similar results for k-values between 10 and 70. The choice of 50 here is arbitrary.

To gain some preliminary insight into the mechanisms of the critical layers, we performed a representational analysis using the procedure detailed in
subsection 3.2
. To conduct this analysis, we constructed a single, large-context prompt for each task. For mathematical reasoning, the prompt concatenates 14 diverse problems and their solutions from the MATH500 dataset. A similar approach was used for TriviaQA with 20 question-answer pairs. This specific methodology is further detailed in Appendix
6.5
.

Mathematical Reasoning.
Our NMI analysis in Figure
5
shows a clear pattern for the math tasks. The NMI score starts high and then drops sharply in the middle-to-late layers, forming an ’elbow’ region. For Qwen models, this elbow is in the 20-25 layer region. For Llama models, it is between layers 13 and 18.

We use Layer 0 as our baseline because prior work on BERT shows that initial layers groups similar tokens together
(
Van Aken et al. 2019
;
Jawahar et al. 2019
)
. This means tokens are grouped by their type; for example, in a math problem, all numbers might form one family, while all mathematical operators (+, -, ) form another. The NMI score simply measures how similar the token groupings at any other layer are compared to this baseline. A low NMI score, therefore, means a layer is no longer keeping tokens in their simple families; instead, it is actively mixing different types of tokens together.

Our hypothesis is as follows: At the elbow region, we observe that the clusters are the most dissimilar from the initial layer’s clusters, as indicated by the low NMI. We also note that prior literature shows that the first layer clusters tokens from the same family together
(
Van Aken et al. 2019
;
Jawahar et al. 2019
)
. This means that the clusters in the elbow layers mix tokens from different families. We conjecture that this elbow region is important for reasoning because to solve a reasoning task, one needs to learn the relationships between tokens of different families. We observe that the critical layers for both Llama and Qwen lie in this elbow, which leads to our conjecture as to why these layers might be critical.

Factual Recall.
A similar NMI analysis for TriviaQA exhibits minimal NMI decline, with values remaining relatively consistent across all layers. This suggests that token clusters change little from the Layer 0 baseline. This is likely because TriviaQA prompts have less token diversity than math problems. With fewer types of tokens to begin with, the model does not need special layers for complex mixing. This would explain both the flat NMI line and why no single layer was critical for the TriviaQA task

Limitations
We must emphasize that this NMI analysis is exploratory, intended only to generate an initial hypothesis. Our choice to use a single, large-context prompt was made to ensure a uniform basis for comparison across all models and to observe representations in a rich context. However, this methodological choice has limitations. The results may be specific to this prompt format, and the effect of mixing distinct problems could lead to more complexity.

A valuable next step is to analyze how the clusters change as a model generates a step-by-step, Chain-of-Thought solution, instead of just using one forward pass on a static prompt. This would require developing a new technique to perform a uniform analysis across different models, since each may generate unique reasoning steps. Such work could build on our initial findings, which represents an attempt to provide a layer-centric view of what might be happening inside these critical layers.

Sub-component analysis reveals different contribution patterns.
To further understand the source of these representational changes, we conducted sub-layer ablations targeting MLP blocks and attention heads separately (Figure
6
). For Qwen’s Layer 23, both components contribute cumulatively to performance drops. Llama shows similar cumulative effects at Layer 15, but at Layer 18, attention head removal produces minimal impact while MLP ablation causes performance degradation comparable to full layer removal. Additionally, removing MLP blocks from the initial layers (Layer 1 for Qwen, Layer 2 for Llama) results in complete failure, while attention head removal produces smaller effects. This pattern suggests that MLP blocks play a particularly important role in early semantic processing, consistent with recent findings on transformer component specialization
(
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Dong et al. 2025
)
.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

Our work demonstrates that mathematical reasoning in LLMs relies on a specific set of layers. Through systematic ablation experiments on Llama and Qwen model families, we find that this layer importance structure is established during pre-training and remains largely unchanged for post-training methods, including instruction tuning, knowledge distillation, and reinforcement learning.
This finding of critical layers for math contrasts with the the lack of highly critical layers for non-reasoning tasks like factual recall.

Figure 6
:
The plots compare the accuracy impact of ablating the full layer (blue) versus only the multi-head attention (red) or MLP (green) components for the Instruct models. The results show that in some important layers, such as Qwen’s layer 23, both components contribute significantly to performance. In other cases, the performance drop is isolated to a single component; for example, at Llama’s layer 18, the drop is almost entirely caused by ablating the MLP block, while removing the attention block has little effect.

Our information-theoretic analysis provides a mechanism to begin to explain these results
(
Gu 2021
)
. Using Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) with the output of the first layer as a baseline—a novel approach to our knowledge—we find that these critical layers are precisely the layers with the largest representational transformation. For mathematical reasoning, the NMI profile declines steadily in these critical regions but remains largely constant for factual recall, and this transformation profile is also preserved through post-training. This suggests that it is in the critical layers where the model learns relationships between different token types - a process important for reasoning.

Taken together, these findings indicate that post-training does not create new reasoning layers but rather enhances the importance structure forged during pre-training. This has potential implications for model development, suggesting that efforts to improve reasoning may be better focused on refining these existing structures.

While our study provides useful insights, we acknowledge its limitations. Our NMI analysis uses a specific single-prompt methodology (see Appendix
6.5
). Additionally, our ablation is performed at the layer and component level, not on finer-grained circuits or specific sets of parameters within layers. Our investigation is also limited to two model families at the 7B–8B scale and focuses primarily on accuracy metrics. Future work could explore multi-layer interactions, investigate these finer-grained circuit-level dynamics, extend analysis to different architectural families and parameter scales, and incorporate more nuanced evaluation approaches
(
Elhage et al. 2021
;
Lightman et al. 2024
)
.

Ultimately, our work reveals mathematical reasoning is tied to specific, persistent layers that are established during pre-training and persist through subsequent post-training, offering a new perspective on how reasoning emerges in large language models.

2. Determine the number of minutes it takes to read 120 pages: Since Joy reads 0.4

Our main goal for this analysis was to see how the model organizes its representations when given a prompt with a wide variety of tokens and concepts. To achieve this diversity, we concatenated multiple question-and-solution pairs into a single prompt. We used a single forward pass on this long prompt because of the unique challenge it presents. While the self-attention mechanism allows every token to see every other token, the need to form a coherent representation for each distinct problem creates a strong incentive for the model to learn to differentiate between their contexts. This is a more demanding task than processing a simple, isolated prompt, and it gives us a better view of how the model uses its representations to manage and insulate different contexts. While ideally, we might use a single, extremely long problem, our approach is a practical way to achieve the high diversity needed for this analysis.

Detailed NMI Calculation

This section provides a detailed breakdown of the terms used to calculate the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) score, as referenced in
subsection 3.2
. The calculation follows the default implementation in the
scikit-learn
package. The final formula uses arithmetic mean normalization:

\text{NMI}(C,D)=\frac{I(C,D)}{(H(C)+H(D))/2}

The calculation begins by defining the properties of the clusterings. Let
C=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{|C|}\}
and
D=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{|D|}\}
be two different clusterings of the same
N
tokens. The probability of a token belonging to a specific cluster
c_{i}\in C
is
P(i)=\frac{|c_{i}|}{N}
, and for a cluster
d_{j}\in D
it is
P(j)=\frac{|d_{j}|}{N}
. The joint probability of a token belonging to both cluster
c_{i}
and cluster
d_{j}
is
P(i,j)=\frac{|c_{i}\cap d_{j}|}{N}
.

Using these probabilities, we first calculate the entropy of each clustering independently. The entropy
H(C)
measures the uncertainty or disorder of a single clustering and is defined as:

H(C)=-\sum_{i=1}^{|C|}P(i)\log(P(i))

The entropy
H(D)
is calculated identically for the second clustering.

Next, the mutual information,
I(C,D)
, measures the information shared between the two clusterings. It quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about one clustering given knowledge of the other. It is defined as:

I(C,D)=\sum_{i=1}^{|C|}\sum_{j=1}^{|D|}P(i,j)\log\left(\frac{P(i,j)}{P(i)P(j)}\right)

These components are then combined in the final formula to produce the normalized score, which provides a robust measure of similarity between cluster structures on a scale from 0 to 1. Note that the
scikit-learn
implementation uses the natural logarithm.
