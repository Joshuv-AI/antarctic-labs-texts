Functional inequalities for Boolean entropy
Guillaume Cébron, Kewei Pan

Abstract
. Building on the recently introduced notion of Boolean entropy, we define the corresponding Boolean Fisher information via a de Bruijn identity. We study the monotonicity of this Fisher information in the Boolean Central Limit Theorem and establish several functional inequalities involving these quantities, including a logarithmic Sobolev inequality. We also develop Non-microstate counterparts and prove the associated functional inequalities. In addition, we introduce a notion of Stein discrepancy in the Boolean setting, which leads to new Berry–Esseen type bounds in the Boolean central limit theorem.



Introduction

Boolean entropy and Fisher information.

In
[
30
]
, the second author introduced a notion of
Boolean entropy
analogous to Voiculescu’s free entropy. This Boolean entropy arises as the rate function in large deviation principles for the empirical spectral distribution of certain random matrix models. More precisely, two models were investigated: the GUE conditioned to have a majority of zero eigenvalues, and the GUE conditioned to have a majority of zero entries. For both models, a large deviation principle is established for the empirical distribution of the nonzero eigenvalues. The corresponding rate functions coincide (up to a multiplicative constant) and lead to the following definition. For any probability measure
\mu
on the real line with finite second moment, the
Boolean entropy
of
\mu
is defined by the logarithmic integral

\Gamma(\mu):=\int\log|x|\,d\mu(x)\in[-\infty,+\infty).

The terminology Boolean entropy reflects the fact that the two random matrix models arise from earlier works
[
9
,
26
]
in which asymptotic Boolean independence (as defined in
[
36
]
) naturally emerges, just as free entropy is defined by Voiculescu from the GUE model
[
3
,
40
]
, which is an instance of asymptotic free independence. Boolean independence forms the third component of the triptych —
classical statistical independence / free independence / Boolean independence
— put forward by Speicher
[
37
]
as the only three universal independences in noncommutative probability. The definition of Boolean entropy is thus the first stone of the development of a full theory for Boolean entropy, paralleling the classical and the free settings. In
[
30
]
, it is proved that the Boolean entropy satisfies two properties analogous to those of classical and free entropy: it remains monotone along the Boolean central limit theorem; and it is maximized by the limiting distribution, namely the Rademacher distribution
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{+1}
(the symmetric Bernoulli distribution).

In this article, we introduce the
Boolean Fisher information

\Psi(\mu)
by mimicking the de Bruijn identity: starting from a symmetric measure, and differentiating the Boolean entropy along the Boolean convolution semigroup generated by
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
, we obtain

\Psi(\mu)=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

Starting from a non-symmetric measure leads to a different expression for the associated Fisher information, which appears significantly more difficult to analyze. For this reason, we systematically work with symmetric probability measures in the rest of this paper and leave the investigation of the non-symmetric Fisher information for future work.

We prove that the Boolean entropy and the Boolean Fisher information satisfy the Boolean analogues of the Shannon-Stam inequality, the Blachman-Stam inequality, the Cramér-Rao inequality, the entropy-power inequality and the Voiculescu-Stam inequality, which concern sums of Boolean independent random variables. Moreover, we establish the monotonicity of Boolean Fisher information, which provides another perspective of proving the monotonicity of Boolean entropy. These results show that these quantities are well adapted to Boolean independence.

Functional inequalities.
Then, we consider
\Gamma(\mu)
and
\Psi(\mu)
with respect to the equilibrium measure
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
. The
Boolean entropy relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
is defined, for any symmetric probability measure
\mu
, by

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\frac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-\int\log|x|\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2}\in[0,+\infty].

This is in fact the full rate function mentioned earlier — when the potential term is included — and which is minimized at
\Gamma(\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=0
as we will see (see also
[
30
]
). We define also the
Boolean Fisher information
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
as the derivative of
\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
along the Boolean Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which admits the following explicit representation: for any symmetric probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
,

\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)+\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2\in[0,+\infty].

The positivity of the Boolean Fisher information is not trivial, and implies the monotonicity of the Boolean entropy along the Boolean Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.

Following Voiculescu, the two quantities introduced above can be qualified as
Microstates
, since they arise from random matrix approximations.
The Non-microstates approach to free entropy, introduced by Voiculescu
[
41
]
, defines free entropy without relying on Microstates. In the Boolean case, this intrinsic approach allows us to introduce an alternative version of the Fisher information. The
Non-microstates Boolean Fisher information relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
is defined, for any symmetric probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, by

\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)+\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2\in[0,+\infty].

Integrating this quantity along the Boolean Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process yields the
Non-microstates Boolean entropy relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
, given, for any symmetric probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, by

\Gamma^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2}\in[0,+\infty].

Finally, in parallel with Stein’s method developed
in
[
25
,
16
]
for the classical and free settings respectively, we define the
Boolean Stein discrepancy

D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
. It is given, for any symmetric probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, by

D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\left(\int x^{4}\,d\mu(x)-2\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)+1\right)^{1/2}\in[0,+\infty].

Moreover, we are able to prove two refined versions of the diagonal log-Sobolev inequality
2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
: the direct Boolean analogue of the Otto–Villani HWI inequality, namely the
Boolean HWI inequality
(Thm.

2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq 2W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\,\sqrt{\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}-W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

and the direct Boolean analogue of the Ledoux-Nourdin-Peccati HSI inequality, namely the
Boolean HSI inequality
(Thm.
3.9
)

2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}\cdot\log\left(1+\frac{\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}\right)\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Unlike the one-dimensional free case, we observe a difference between the Microstates entropy and the Non-microstates one.

Starting from a not necessarily symmetric measure
\mu
, all the quantities involved but
W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}
are invariant under taking the symmetrization
\mu^{\mathrm{s}}(B):=\frac{1}{2}\mu(B\cup(-B))
of
\mu
. Consequently, replacing
W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}
by
W_{2}(\mu^{\mathrm{s}},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=\int||x|-1|^{2}d\mu(x)
, the eight inequalities above remain true for all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, not necessarily symmetric.

Berry-Esseen inequalities.
The WS inequality provides a direct control of the Wasserstein distance and yields a new quantitative estimate in the Boolean central limit theorem for the Boolean convolution
\uplus
. For any measure
\mu
, we denote by
m_{k}(\mu)
(with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
) the
k
-th moment
\int x^{k}d\mu(x)
of
\mu
whenever it exists. Let
\mu
be a probability measure such that the first four moments exist with
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
(
\mu
is centered and normalized),
and
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
. We define
\mu_{n}
to be the dilation of
\mu^{\uplus n}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, i.e.
\mu_{n}(B)=\mu^{\uplus n}(\{x:\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\in B\})
. We prove that, whenever
\mu
is symmetric, for all
n\geq 1
, we have

W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}.

This follows directly from the transport control provided by the WS inequality.
The rate of convergence
O(n^{-1/2})
is the usual one in Berry-Esseen inequalities for the classical central limit theorem
[
6
,
15
]
and for the free one
[
12
,
24
]
. In the Boolean case, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are only four existing Berry-Esseen type results: the rate of convergence
O(n^{-1/3})
was obtained for the (weaker) Lévy distance under the same assumptions
[
1
]
, the rate of convergence
O(n^{-1/2})
was obtained for the Lévy distance under the (stronger) assumption of existence of the
6
-th moment
[
33
]
, the rate of convergence
O(n^{-1/2})
was obtained for an
ad-hoc
distance under the (weaker) assumption of existence of the
3
-th moment
[
20
]
, and the rate of convergence
O(n^{-1/2})
was obtained for a coupling distance for measures with bounded support
[
22
]
. The assumption of a
symmetric
measure is not needed in the four works cited, and since the Stein discrepancy directly controls the Wasserstein-
1
distance
W_{1}
without symmetry assumptions we are able to remove this assumption as well :
for all
n\geq 1
, we have

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}.

Beyond metric convergence, one may also study information-theoretic convergence. In the classical (and also free) central limit theorem, it is also possible to quantify the convergence of the Fisher information and the convergence of the relative entropy. The optimal rate is
O(n^{-1})
under the appropriate conditions
[
5
,
13
,
23
]
. However, in the free and classical case, the Stein method allows to reach quite directly the (almost optimal) rate
O(\log(n)/n)
under very weak conditions
[
25
,
16
]
. The Boolean Stein’s method developed in this paper allows to get the following quantitative entropic central limit theorems.
Let
\mu
be a symmetric probability measure with the existing moments
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
,
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
and
m_{-2}(\mu)<+\infty
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\Gamma(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=O\left(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \Psi(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Notice that, under the same conditions,
\Gamma^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
and
\Psi^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
remain constant as
n
increases, which reveals a structural difference in behavior between the Microstates and Non-microstates quantities.

\Gamma(\mu)
and the Boolean Fisher information
\Psi(\mu)

\Gamma(\mu)
and

In Section

In Section
1.3
, we introduce the relative Boolean entropy
\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
and relative Boolean Fisher information

, and we prove the Boolean Talagrand transport inequality, the Boolean log-Sobolev inequality, and the exponential decay of relative entropy along the Boolean Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

In Section

, we introduce the Non-microstates relative Boolean entropy

In Section

, we adapt Stein’s method to the Rademacher target

In Section

Let us denote by

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
and by
\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
the subset of symmetric probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. The Cauchy transform of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by

G_{\mu}(z)=\int\frac{1}{z-x}\,d\mu(x),\quad z\in\mathbb{H}_{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im z>0\}.

Writing

with
\epsilon>0
, we have the decomposition
G_{\mu}(x+i\varepsilon)=\pi(Q_{\epsilon}\ast\mu(x)-iP_{\epsilon}\ast\mu(x)),

where

P_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\epsilon}{t^{2}+\epsilon^{2}},\quad Q_{\epsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{t}{t^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}

are respectively the Poisson kernel and the
conjugate
Poisson kernel. Recall that
P_{\epsilon}\ast\mu
weakly converges to
\mu
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
. Moreover, for any
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
P_{\epsilon}\ast f(x)\longrightarrow f(x)
for every Lebesgue point of
f
, and the convergence is uniform if
f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. The self-energy (or reciprocal Cauchy transform, also called the
K
-transform) of
\mu
is given by
K_{\mu}(z):=z-1/G_{\mu}(z)
for
z\in\mathbb{H}_{+}
. For

, the Boolean convolution introduced by Speicher and Woroudi
[
36
]
is the unique measure
\mu\uplus\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
such that

K_{\mu\uplus\nu}(z)=K_{\mu}(z)+K_{\nu}(z),\ \forall z\in\mathbb{H}_{+}.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, we denote by
m_{k}(\mu)
(with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
) the
k

\int x^{k}d\mu(x)
of
\mu
whenever it exists. For
k\geq 1
, the Wasserstein-
k
distance (or Kantorovich–Rubinstein metric) between
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by

W_{k}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi}\left(\iint|x-y|^{k}\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{k}},

where the infimum is over all couplings of
(\mu,\nu)
. We will not use the Lévy distance
d_{\text{Lévy}}
in this article, but if one wants to transfer our result to the Lévy distance, let us mention that

\sqrt{d_{\text{Lévy}}(\mu,\nu)}\leq W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ d_{\text{Lévy}}(\mu,\nu)\leq W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

If
X
and

\mu

\nu

W_{1}(X,Y):=W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
, and the Kantorovich duality yield

W_{1}(X,Y)=\sup_{\varphi\ 1-\text{Lipschitz}}|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)]|.

We will consider Boolean independent variables. Boolean independence is a non-unital

is a von Neumann algebra

with a nondegenerate normal state

are called

. For any self-adjoint

Two self-adjoint variables

\tau(X^{k_{1}}Y^{k_{2}}X^{k_{3}}\cdots)=\tau(X^{k_{1}})\tau(Y^{k_{2}})\tau(X^{k_{3}})\cdots

and in this case, the law of the sum is given by the Boolean convolution:

\mathcal{L}(X+Y)=\mathcal{L}(X)\uplus\mathcal{L}(Y).

In the symmetric case, i.e. whenever

1.
The Microstates approach

1.1.
Boolean entropy and Fisher information

In this section, we introduce a notion of Boolean Fisher information and show that it interacts with Boolean entropy via an integral formula that mirrors the classical de Bruijn identity. This provides a differential characterization of Boolean entropy along a Boolean analogue of a heat flow. Let us first recall the definition of Boolean entropy.

Definition 1.1
.

let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
. The
Boolean entropy

\Gamma(X)
of
X
is defined as

(1.1)

\Gamma(X):=\int\log|x|\,d\mu(x)\in[-\infty,+\infty).

More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the
Boolean entropy

\Gamma(\mu)
of
\mu
as

\Gamma(\mu):=\int\log|x|\,d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int\log x\,d\mu^{(2)}(x)\in[-\infty,+\infty)

provided that
\int_{1}^{+\infty}\log x\,d\mu^{(2)}(x)<+\infty
. Recall that
\mu^{(2)}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is the push forward measure of
\mu
by the map
x\mapsto x^{2}

Throughout this section, unless explicitly stated otherwise, all probability measures are assumed to be symmetric. In the Boolean case, the heat semigroup is given by the Boolean convolution with a scaled Rademacher variable: starting with a self-adjoint operator
X
of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
whose law is symmetric, we consider the variable
X+\sqrt{t}B
where
B\in\mathcal{M}
is Boolean independent from
X
and distributed according to the Rademacher law
\mathrm{b}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}.
Our first result, Theorem
1.3
, establishes the fundamental link between the Boolean entropy and the Boolean Fisher information, in the form of a Boolean de Bruijn identity. It expresses the increment of Boolean entropy along the Boolean convolution semigroup
(\mathcal{L}(X+\sqrt{t}B))_{t\geq 0}
generated by the Rademacher variable in terms of a quantity that we introduce now as the Boolean Fisher information.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
. The
Boolean Fisher information

\Psi(X)
of
X
is defined as

(1.2)

\Psi(X):=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\in(0,+\infty]

understood with the continuous extension on the diagonal.
More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the
Boolean Fisher information

\Psi(\mu)
of
\mu
as

\Psi(\mu):=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}(x)d\mu^{(2)}(y)\in(0,+\infty].

Classically, Fisher information arises as the derivative of entropy along the Brownian motion, a relation known as the de Bruijn identity. Voiculescu
[
40
]
extended this framework to free probability, introducing the notions of free entropy and free Fisher information. In the Boolean setting, an analogous de Bruijn identity can also be established.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
, with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, and
B\in\mathcal{M}
be Boolean independent from
X
and distributed according to
\mathrm{b}
. Fix
t\geq 0
such that
\Gamma(X+\sqrt{s}B)<\infty,\ \Psi(X+\sqrt{s}B)<+\infty
for any
s\in[0,t].
Then we have

(1.3)

\Gamma(X+\sqrt{t}B)-\Gamma(X)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\Psi(X+\sqrt{s}B)\,ds.

In particular,
\Gamma(X+\sqrt{t}B)
is increasing with respect to
t
.

It is worth noting that the Fisher information
\Psi
solely depends on the push forward probability measure
\mu^{(2)}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
by
x\mapsto x^{2}
. Therefore, without altering the expression of
\Psi
, the Fisher information can be defined on
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
. However, the de Bruijn identity generally fails under this naïve extension. Indeed, if the law of
X
is not symmetric, we have another explicit expression for the Fisher information satisfying the above de Bruijn relation, given by

\Psi(X)=\Psi(\mu):=\iint\frac{\frac{\log x^{2}}{x}-\frac{\log y^{2}}{y}}{x-y}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\in[-\infty,+\infty],\quad\text{if}\ X\sim\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}).

We emphasize that unlike the classical and free Fisher information, this quantity is not necessarily nonnegative and the translation invariance, scaling property (i.e.
\Psi(\lambda X+m)=\lambda^{-2}\Psi(X)
for any
\lambda,m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
) are no longer satisfied. Nevertheless, in the case where
\mu
is symmetric, it can be shown to reduce exactly to the Fisher information defined in (
1.2
). Moreover, the symmetric framework provides substantially better analytical properties, under which many results from the classical theory, as well as their free analogues, can be recovered. For this reason, we work from now on in the symmetric framework unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Proof.

Since

X
and
B
are self-adjoint elements of a von Neumann algebra, they are bounded operators. Hence the spectral measure of
X
and of
X+\sqrt{t}B
are compactly supported. Thanks to (
0.1
), we know that
\mathcal{L}((X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2})=\mathcal{L}(X^{2}+tB^{2})
, and we can compute

\displaystyle G_{(X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{X^{2}+tB^{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-K_{X^{2}+tB^{2}}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-K_{X^{2}}-K_{tB^{2}}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1/G_{X^{2}}(z)-K_{tB^{2}}(z)}.

Now, since
\mathcal{L}(tB^{2})=\delta_{t}
, we have

K_{tB^{2}}(z)=z-\frac{1}{G_{tB^{2}}(z)}=z-\frac{1}{(z-t)^{-1}}=t,

which yields

(1.4)

G_{(X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{1/G_{X^{2}}(z)-t}=\frac{G_{X^{2}}(z)}{1-tG_{X^{2}}(z)}.

In particular, we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{(X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2}}(z)=(G_{(X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2}}(z))^{2}.

To abbreviate the notation, we denote by
\mu(t,\cdot)=\mathcal{L}(X+\sqrt{t}B)
and
G(t,z)=G_{(X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2}}(z)
so that
G(t,z)
is the Cauchy transform of
\mu^{(2)}(t)
. Put
u(t,z)=\pi^{-1}\Re G(t,z),\ v(t,z)=-\pi^{-1}\Im G(t,z)
, By the above relation, for
z=x+i\epsilon
we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v(t,z)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im(G(t,z))^{2}=2\pi u(t,z)v(t,z).

Denote by
f_{\delta}(x)=\log|x+i\delta|
for any
\delta>0
and

\Gamma_{\delta}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{\delta}(x)\,d\mu(x),\quad\Psi_{\delta}(\mu)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\frac{f_{\delta}(x)-f_{\delta}(y)}{x-y}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

For any
0\leq s\leq t
and fixed
\epsilon>0
, since
\mu^{(2)}(s)
is compactly supported, we have
|\frac{\partial}{\partial s}v(s,x+i\epsilon)|\leq|(G(s,x+i\epsilon))^{2}|\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{1+|x|^{2}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\Gamma_{\delta}(v(s,\cdot+i\epsilon))

\displaystyle=\int f_{\delta}(x)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial s}v(s,x+i\epsilon)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\int f_{\delta}(x)\cdot(G(s,x+i\epsilon))^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\int f_{\delta}(x)\int\frac{1}{x+i\epsilon-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}(s,y)\int\frac{1}{x+i\epsilon-y^{\prime}}\,d\mu^{(2)}(s,y^{\prime})\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\Im\iint\frac{1}{y-y^{\prime}}\left[\int\left(\frac{f_{\delta}(x)}{x+i\epsilon-y}-\frac{f_{\delta}(x)}{x+i\epsilon-y^{\prime}}\right)\,dx\right]\,d\mu^{(2)}(s,y)d\mu^{(2)}(s,y^{\prime}).

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{1}{y-y^{\prime}}(P_{\epsilon}\ast f_{\delta}(y)-P_{\epsilon}\ast f_{\delta}(y^{\prime}))\,d\mu^{(2)}(s,y)d\mu^{(2)}(s,y^{\prime}).

\Gamma_{\delta}(v(t,\cdot+i\epsilon))=\int\log|x+i\delta|\cdot v(t,x+i\epsilon)\,dx=\int\log|x+i(\delta+\epsilon)|\,d\mu(t,x)=\Gamma_{\delta+\epsilon}(\mu(t)),

hence, we have

(1.5)

\Gamma_{\delta+\epsilon}(\mu^{(2)}(t))-\Gamma_{\delta+\epsilon}(\mu^{(2)})=\int_{0}^{t}\iint\frac{P_{\epsilon}\ast f_{\delta}(y)-P_{\epsilon}\ast f_{\delta}(y^{\prime})}{y-y^{\prime}}\,d\mu^{(2)}(s,y)d\mu^{(2)}(s,y^{\prime})\,ds,

Let
\epsilon\to 0^{+},

\Psi_{\delta}(\mu(t))
due to the fact that
P_{\epsilon}\ast f_{\delta}\longrightarrow f_{\delta}
uniformly on the support of
\mu^{(2)}(s)
. To conclude, we get
\Gamma_{\delta}(\mu^{(2)}(t))-\Gamma_{\delta}(\mu^{(2)})=\int_{0}^{t}\Psi_{\delta}(\mu^{(2)}(s))\,ds
. Finally let

, and the monotone convergence theorem yields (
1.3
), where the factor

comes from the definition
1.1
.

From the definitions of
\Gamma(X)
and
\Psi(X)
, we have the following scaling property.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
We have

\Gamma(\lambda X)=\Gamma(X)+\log|\lambda|;\ \Psi(\lambda X)=\lambda^{-2}\Psi(X).

The scaling property still holds if we extend the definition of
\Psi
to
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
without altering the expression. However, the translation invariance of
\Psi
fails.

Now let us consider the variables
X_{t}=e^{-t}X+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}B
, which has the same distribution as the variables in the Boolean Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. By the scaling property of
\Gamma
, we know that

\Gamma(X_{t})=\Gamma\left(X+\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}B\right)-t.

Thus, with the de Bruijn identity (
1.3
) and the scaling property of
\Psi
, we have

(1.6)

\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(X_{t})=e^{2t}\Psi\left(X+\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}B\right)-1=\Psi(X_{t})-1.

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose that
\Gamma(X+\sqrt{s}B)<\infty
and
\Psi(X+\sqrt{s}B)<+\infty
for all
s\in[0,+\infty)
. Then we have

(1.7)

\Gamma(X)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{s+1}-\Psi(X+\sqrt{s}B)\right)\,ds

Proof.

By the previous computation, and clearly
\lim_{t\to 0}\Gamma(X_{t})=\Gamma(X),\ \lim_{t\to+\infty}\Gamma(X_{t})=\Gamma(B)
, thus we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(B)-\Gamma(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[e^{2t}\Psi\left(X+\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}B\right)-1\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[(s+1)\Psi(X+\sqrt{s}B)-1\right]\frac{1}{2(s+1)}\,ds,

where the second equality is derived from change of variable
s\mapsto\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}
. Therefore, with the fact that
\Gamma(B)=0
, we complete the proof.

The monotonicity of classical entropy originates in the work of Shannon (see
[
34
]
,
[
38
]
,
[
27
]
) and was settled in
[
4
]
. Adapting a similar strategy, Shlyakhtenko established the free counterpart (see
[
35
]
). This result was later revisited by Dadoun and Youssef (see
[
14
]
) through the maximal correlation method. We emphasize that the proofs of all these results are through the monotonicity of Fisher information, and the key is the
L^{2}
-structure of the Fisher information (i.e. the conjugate variable). In
[
30
]
, we established the Boolean analogue of this monotonicity phenomenon. However, the argument does not involve the Fisher information, but instead relies purely on the analytical structure of the Boolean convolution. For the sake of completeness, we now turn to the monotonicity of the Fisher information itself, but as the quantity
\Psi
does not yields a nice
L^{2}
-structure, the way we prove the monotonicity shall be different from the classical and free case.

Theorem 1.6

(Monotonicity of the Fisher information)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(\mathcal{M},\tau)
be a sequence of Boolean independent and identically distributed self-adjoint random variables with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
. Define

S_{n}:=\frac{X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Then, for any integers
n\geq m\geq 1
, we have

\Psi(S_{n})\leq\Psi(S_{m}),

with equality if and only if

X_{1}\sim\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{-a}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{a}

for some
a\neq 0
.

Proof.

Since the
variables are self-adjoint elements of a von Neumann algebra, they are bounded operators and their spectral measures are compactly supported.
We begin with the monotonicity of
\Psi
. Thanks to (
0.1
), we have

S_{n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{n}^{2}}{n}.

Recall that
\mu_{n}
is the dilation of
\mu^{\uplus n}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, thus we denote by
\mu^{(2)}_{n}
the law of
S_{n}^{2}
. Via the change of variable
x\mapsto x^{2}
, one can reformulate the Fisher information as follows:

\Psi(S_{n})=\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(y)=\int\frac{1}{x}\left(\int\frac{\log(y/x)}{y/x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(y)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x).

It therefore suffices to show that for any
X_{1}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
and any
n\geq m
,

(1.8)

\int\frac{\log x}{x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x)\leq\int\frac{\log x}{x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(x).

Indeed, the above inequality (if true for any symmetric law, and in particular for
X_{1}/\sqrt{x}
with fixed
x>0
) implies that we have

\int\frac{\log y/x}{y/x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(y)\leq\int\frac{\log y/x}{y/x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(y),

and allows to write

\displaystyle\Psi(S_{n})=\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(y)=\int\frac{1}{x}\left(\int\frac{\log(y/x)}{y/x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(y)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{1}{x}\left(\int\frac{\log(y/x)}{y/x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(y)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x)=\int\frac{1}{y}\left(\int\frac{\log(x/y)}{x/y-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{n}(x)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{1}{y}\left(\int\frac{\log(x/y)}{x/y-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(x)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(y)=\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{m}(y)=\Psi(S_{m}).

To establish (
1.8
), we adopt the same approach as in the proof of the monotonicity of Boolean entropy (Proposition 4.2 in
[
30
]
). In view of the definition of
\mu^{(2)}_{n}
, recall that for any
\alpha>0
, it can be checked that
G_{\alpha X}(z)=\alpha^{-1}G_{X}(\alpha^{-1}z)
and
K_{\alpha X}(z)=\alpha K_{X}(\alpha^{-1}z)
, then by the additivity of the self energy
K
under Boolean convolution, the Cauchy transform of
\mu^{(2)}_{n}
is given by

G_{\mu^{(2)}_{n}}(z)=\frac{1}{z-K_{X_{1}^{2}}(nz)}.

Observe that the discrete family
(\mu^{(2)}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
admits a natural extension to a continuous family
(\mu^{(2)}_{t})_{t\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
defined by

(1.9)

G_{\mu^{(2)}_{t}}(z)=\frac{1}{z-K_{X_{1}^{2}}(tz)},

and we aim to show a stronger version of the monotonicity:

(1.10)

\Psi(\mu^{(2)}_{t})\leq\Psi(\mu^{(2)}_{s}),\ \forall t\geq s\geq 1.

In fact, it follows directly from the definition that
\mu^{(2)}_{t}

satisfies the multiplicative semigroup property:
(\mu^{(2)}_{t})_{s}=\mu^{(2)}_{ts}
. Consequently, if
\Psi(\mu^{(2)}_{t})=+\infty
for all
t\geq 1
, the inequality is trivial. Otherwise, suppose that there exists
t_{0}\in[1,+\infty)
such that
\Psi(\mu^{(2)}_{t_{0}})<+\infty
, while
\Psi(\mu^{(2)}_{s})=+\infty
for all
s\in[1,t_{0})
. By the semigroup property, we may shift the parameter and assume without loss of generality that
t_{0}=1
, that is,
\Psi(X)<+\infty
. Moreover, to establish the monotonicity for all
t\geq 1
, it suffices to verify the negativity of the derivative with respect to
t
for an arbitrary
X_{1}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
,

\frac{d}{dt}\int\frac{\log x}{x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{t}(x)\leq 0.

To proceed, let
z=x+i\epsilon
and write
G_{\mu^{(2)}_{t}}(z)=\pi\bigl(u_{t}(z)-iv_{t}(z)\bigr)
. Taking partial derivative with respect to
t
on both sides of
(
1.9
)
at
t=1
, we obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=1}G_{\mu^{(2)}_{t}}(z)=z(G_{\mu^{(2)}_{1}}(z))^{2}+zG_{\mu^{(2)}_{1}}^{\prime}(z),

which gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=1}v_{t}(z)=x[2\pi u_{1}(z)v_{1}(z)+v_{1}^{\prime}(z)]-\epsilon[u^{\prime}_{1}(z)+\pi(u_{1}^{2}(z)-v_{1}^{2}(z))].

Let
L(x)=(x\log x)/(x-1)
, it can be checked that
L
is monotone increasing with
\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}L(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to+\infty}L(x)=+\infty
. Moreover, the singularity of
L
at
x=1
can be removed so that
L\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. In particular,
L
is bounded in any compact set. For sufficiently large
K>0
, we denote
g_{K}(x)=\operatorname{sign}(\log x)\cdot K\wedge|\log x|/(x-1)
and
L_{K}(x)=xg_{K}(x)
. Note that
|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|_{t=1}v_{t}(z)|\leq|z(G_{\mu_{1}^{(2)}}(z))^{2}|+|zG^{\prime}_{\mu_{1}^{(2)}}(z)|\leq\frac{c_{\epsilon}}{1+|x|}
because
\mu^{(2)}_{1}
is compactly supported, hence we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=1}\int g_{K}(x)v_{t}(x+i\epsilon)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int g_{K}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=1}v_{t}(x+i\epsilon)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int L_{K}(x)[2\pi u_{1}(x+i\epsilon)v_{1}(x+i\epsilon)+v_{1}^{\prime}(x+i\epsilon)]\,dx+o(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\iint\left(\frac{P_{\epsilon}\ast L_{K}(x)-P_{\epsilon}\ast L_{K}(y)}{x-y}\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{1}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\quad-\int L^{\prime}_{K}(x)v_{1}(x+i\epsilon)\,dx+o(\epsilon).

\displaystyle:=J_{K}(v_{1}(\cdot+i\epsilon))

Therefore, by the multiplicative semigroup property of
\{\mu^{(2)}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int g_{K}(x)v_{t}(x+i\epsilon)\,dx=\frac{1}{t}J_{K}(v_{t}(\cdot+i\epsilon)),

and consequently

\int g_{K}(x)v_{t}(x+i\epsilon)\,dx-\int g_{K}(x)v_{0}(x+i\epsilon)\,dx=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{s}J_{K}(v_{s}(\cdot+i\epsilon))\,ds.

Note that as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, the weakly convergence of
v_{s}(\cdot+i\epsilon)
to
\mu_{s}
and the uniform convergence of
P_{\epsilon}\ast L_{K}
to
L_{K}
on compact sets yields

\int g_{K}(x)\,d\mu_{t}(x)-\int g_{K}(x)\,d\mu_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{s}\iint\left(\frac{L_{K}(x)-L_{K}(y)}{x-y}-L^{\prime}_{K}(x)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{s}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{s}(y)\,ds.

Finally, let
K\to+\infty
along with the monotone convergence theorem, we conclude that

\int\frac{\log x}{x-1}\,d\mu_{t}(x)-\int\frac{\log x}{x-1}\,d\mu_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{s}\iint\left(\frac{L(x)-L(y)}{x-y}-L^{\prime}(x)\right)\,d\mu^{(2)}_{s}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{s}(y)\,ds

so that

(1.11)

\frac{d}{dt}\int\frac{\log x}{x-1}\,d\mu^{(2)}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{t}\iint\left(\frac{L(x)-L(y)}{x-y}-L^{\prime}(x)\right)d\mu^{(2)}_{t}(x)d\mu^{(2)}_{t}(y).

Since

\frac{L(x)-L(y)}{x-y}=\int_{0}^{1}L^{\prime}(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\,d\theta.

The proof is immediate if
L^{\prime}
is convex on
(0,\infty)
. Hence, it remains to verify the convexity of
L^{\prime}
. A direct computation yields

L^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2\bigl[(x-1)-\log x\bigr]}{x(x-1)^{4}}\geq 0.

Since
L^{\prime}(x)
admits a continuous extension at
x=1
, we conclude that
L^{\prime}
is convex on
(0,+\infty)
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\mu^{(2)}_{t}
is a Dirac mass
\delta_{a}
for some
a\in(0,+\infty)
. In view of the definition of
\mu^{(2)}_{t}
, it is clear that if
\mu^{(2)}
is not a one point Dirac mass, then
\mu^{(2)}_{t}
can not be a one point Dirac mass for all
t
. Therefore,
\Psi(S_{n})=\Psi(S_{m})
for some
n\neq m\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if
\mu
is a scaled Rademacher distribution. This completes the proof of the monotonicity of the Fisher information
\Psi
.
∎

\Gamma

(Monotonicity of entropy)
.

For a sequence of self-adjoint random variables
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

(1.12)

\Gamma(S_{n})\geq\Gamma(S_{m}).

1.2.
Information-theoretic properties

The Cramér–Rao inequality and Stam inequality (
[
38
]
) are fundamental in classical information theory. It turns out that these results are consistent with Voiculescu’s free entropy and free Fisher information (
[
41
]
). Motivated by these parallels, we aim to establish corresponding inequalities in the Boolean setting. Before that, we shall point out some properties of the Boolean entropy and Boolean Fisher information, some of these properties will be used in subsequent sections.

Proposition 1.8
.

The Boolean entropy
\Gamma
and Fisher information
\Psi
satisfy the following properties (where we assume that all appearing variables are self-adjoint in some non-commutative probability space and have symmetric laws):

(1)

We have the bound for
\Psi
as follows:

(1.13)

\tau\left(\frac{2}{X^{2}+\tilde{X}^{2}}\right)\leq\Psi(X)\leq\tau\left(\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right),

where
\tilde{X}
is a classically independent copy of
X
. If
X
is not invertible, the above formulas should be understood as
\tau\left(\frac{2}{X^{2}+\tilde{X}^{2}}\right)=\int 2/(x^{2}+y^{2})d\mu(x)d\mu(y)
and
\tau\left(\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right):=\int x^{-2}d\mu(x)
with
\mu=\mathcal{L}(X)
.

(2)

\Gamma
is upper semicontinuous with respect to the weak topology of probability measures with compact support, and
\Psi
is lower semicontinuous. In particular, let
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables such that
X_{k}\to X
in the sense of operator norm, then

\Gamma(X)\geq\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\Gamma(X_{k});\ \Psi(X)\leq\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\Psi(X_{k}).

(3)

If
X
and
Y
are Boolean independent, then we have the Boolean analogue of Shannon-Stam inequality and Blachman-Stam inequality: that is, for any
\theta\in[0,1]
,

(1.14)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\sqrt{\theta}X+\sqrt{1-\theta}Y)\geq\theta\Gamma(X)+(1-\theta)\Gamma(Y);

(1.15)

\displaystyle\Psi(\sqrt{\theta}X+\sqrt{1-\theta}Y)\leq\theta\Psi(X)+(1-\theta)\Psi(Y).

Proof.

To show the
lower bound
in (
1.13
), it suffices to prove the following inequality

(1.16)

\frac{\log t-\log s}{t-s}\geq\frac{2}{t+s}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
t\geq s
so that we can rewrite the inequality into

\left(\frac{t}{s}+1\right)\cdot\log\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)-2\left(\frac{t}{s}-1\right)\geq 0.

Here we denote by
F(\zeta):=(\zeta+1)\log\zeta-2(\zeta-1)\geq 0
for
\zeta\geq 1.
Observe that
F(1)=0
and
F^{\prime}(\zeta)=\log\zeta+\frac{1}{\zeta}-1\geq 0,
we deduce that
F(\zeta)\geq 0
when
\zeta\geq 1
as wanted.

For the
upper bound
, we note that

(1.17)

\Psi(X)=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=\iint\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2}}\,dt\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y),

where
\mu
is the law of
X
.
The convexity of
1/x
and Jensen’s inequality yields the bound.

To prove
(2)
, assume that
\{(\mu_{k})_{k\geq 1},\mu\}
are compactly supported probability measures such that
\mu_{k}\to\mu
in the weak topology. Note that
\Gamma(\mu)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\mu)
, where

\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\mu)=\int\log(|x|+\epsilon)\,d\mu(x).

Therefore, for any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\Gamma(\mu_{k})\leq\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\mu_{k})=\Gamma_{\epsilon}(\mu),

then let
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
on the right hand side yields the upper semicontinuity. Similarly, we can show that
\Psi
is lower semicontinuous.

For
(3)
, due to the equation (
1.7
) and divisibility of Rademacher distribution under Boolean convolution, it suffices to show the Blachman-Stam inequality (
1.15
).

Let us denote
\mu^{(2)},\nu^{(2)}
as the laws of operators
X^{2},Y^{2}
, respectively, and let
m^{(2)}_{\theta}
represent the law of the operator
\theta X^{2}+(1-\theta)Y^{2}
. For the sake of simplifying the notation, we still write
\Psi({\mu^{(2)}}):=\Psi(X)=\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}(x)d\mu^{(2)}(y)
(the same for
\nu^{(2)}
and
m^{(2)}_{\theta}
). Thanks to (
0.1
), the Blachman-Stam inequality becomes:

\Psi(m^{(2)}_{\theta})\leq\theta\Psi(\mu^{(2)})+(1-\theta)\Psi(\nu^{(2)}).

If
\Psi(X)=+\infty
or
\Psi(Y)=+\infty
, we automatically have the inequality. Hence, we may assume that
\Psi(X)
and
\Psi(Y)
are finite. Moreover, there is nothing to prove when
\theta=0
or
\theta=1
. To proceed, for
\theta\in(0,1)
, we begin by analyzing the Cauchy transform of
m^{(2)}_{\theta}
. By the scaling of the Cauchy transform and the additivity of the self energy
K
under Boolean convolution, we have,

(1.18)

\displaystyle G_{m^{(2)}_{\theta}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-K_{m^{(2)}_{\theta}}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-\theta K_{\mu^{(2)}}(\theta^{-1}z)-(1-\theta)K_{\nu^{(2)}}((1-\theta)^{-1}z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta\bigl(z-K_{\mu^{(2)}}(\theta^{-1}z)\bigr)+(1-\theta)\bigl(z-K_{\nu^{(2)}}((1-\theta)^{-1}z)\bigr)}

(1.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta\,\frac{1}{G_{\mu^{(2)}_{\theta}}(z)}+(1-\theta)\,\frac{1}{G_{\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}}(z)}},

where the Cauchy transform of
\mu^{(2)}_{\theta},\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}
are defined via

G_{\mu^{(2)}_{\theta}}(z):=\frac{1}{z-K_{\mu^{(2)}}(\theta^{-1}z)};\quad G_{\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}}(z):=\frac{1}{z-K_{\nu^{(2)}}((1-\theta)^{-1}z)}.

In view of the expression
(
1.9
)
, thus
\mu^{(2)}_{\theta}
coincides with the continuous extension of the law of
S_{n}^{2}
evaluated at
n=\theta^{-1}
(this is well defined since
\theta\in(0,1)
), and the same constructions applies to
\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}
. Furthermore, recall that
\mu^{(2)}_{\theta},\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}
are supported on the positive real line. Now, the main observation is the following: assume that
x,y>0
, then

\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(x+t)(y+t)}\,dt,

hence, by Fubini’s theorem, for any probability measure
\mu
supported on the positive real line, we have

\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\int\frac{1}{x+t}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{2}\,dt=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(G_{\mu}(-t)\right)^{2}\,dt.

Since
\mu
is supported on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the Cauchy transform
G_{\mu}(-t)
is well defined for
t>0
and we can extend the equality (
1.18
) to
z\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
. Therefore, using the Jensen’s inequality twice, we get

\displaystyle\Psi(m^{(2)}_{\theta})=\int_{0}^{+\infty}(G_{m^{(2)}_{\theta}}(-t))^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\theta\frac{1}{G_{\mu^{(2)}_{\theta}}(-t)}+(1-\theta)\frac{1}{G_{\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}}(-t)}}\right)^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\theta G_{\mu^{(2)}_{\theta}}(-t)+(1-\theta)G_{\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}}(-t)\right)^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[\theta(G_{\mu^{(2)}_{\theta}}(-t))^{2}+(1-\theta)(G_{\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta}}(-t))^{2}\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi(\mu^{(2)}_{\theta})+(1-\theta)\Psi(\nu^{(2)}_{1-\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\theta\Psi(\mu^{(2)})+(1-\theta)\Psi(\nu^{(2)}).

In the last inequality, we use the monotonicity of the Fisher information
\Psi
, see (
1.10
).
∎

As a direct consequence of this proposition, the Boolean counterparts of the information inequalities can be easily deduced.

Corollary 1.9

(Cramér-Rao inequality)
.

Let
X\in(\mathcal{M},\tau)
be a self-adjoint random variable with a symmetric law. We have

(1.20)

\Psi(X)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(X^{2})}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\Psi(X)\tau(X^{2})\geq 1
. By the lower bound of
\Psi
in (
1.13
), along with Hölder inequality, we have

\Psi(X)\tau(X^{2})\geq\tau\left(\frac{2}{X^{2}+\tilde{X}^{2}}\right)\cdot\tau\left(\frac{X^{2}+\tilde{X}^{2}}{2}\right)\geq 1.

∎

Corollary 1.10

(Boolean Stam inequality)
.

Let
X,Y
be self-adjoint random variables of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with symmetric laws and assume that
X,Y
are Boolean independent. We have

(1.21)

\frac{1}{\Psi(X+Y)}\geq\frac{1}{\Psi(X)}+\frac{1}{\Psi(Y)}.

In particular, if
\Psi(X+Y)=+\infty
, then
\Psi(X)=\Psi(Y)=+\infty
.

Proof.

First we assume that
\Psi(X),\Psi(Y)
are finite, and by the Blachman-Stam inequality (
1.15
) and the scaling property of Fisher information
\Psi
, for any
\theta\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi(X+Y)

\displaystyle=\Psi\left(\sqrt{\theta}\frac{X}{\sqrt{\theta}}+\sqrt{1-\theta}\frac{Y}{\sqrt{1-\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\theta\Psi\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{\theta}}\right)+(1-\theta)\Psi\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{1-\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi(X)+(1-\theta)^{2}\Psi(Y).

Let
F(\theta)=\theta^{2}\Psi(X)+(1-\theta)^{2}\Psi(Y)
, since the above inequality is valid for all
\theta\in[0,1]
, we deduce that

\Psi(X+Y)\leq\inf_{\theta\in[0,1]}F(\theta).

Set
F^{\prime}(\theta)=0
gives a unique solution
\theta_{0}=\Psi(Y)/(\Psi(X)+\Psi(Y))\in[0,1]
and it is easy to see that this is a minimum point. Finally, one can check that

F(\theta_{0})=\frac{\Psi(X)\Psi(Y)}{\Psi(X)+\Psi(Y)},

which gives the desired inequality.

If
\Psi(X)=\Psi(Y)=+\infty
, the equality is obvious. Else if either
\Psi(X)=+\infty
or
\Psi(Y)=+\infty
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\Psi(X)<+\infty
and
\Psi(Y)=+\infty
, then (
1.21
) reduces to
\Psi(X+Y)\leq\Psi(X)
. Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
be the law of
X,Y
respectively, note that there exists a sequence of compactly supported symmetric probability measures
\{\nu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\Psi(\nu_{n})<+\infty
and
\nu_{n}
weakly converge to
\nu
. Since Boolean convolution preserve the weak convergence i.e.
\mu\uplus\nu_{n}\longrightarrow\mu\uplus\nu
(because the Cauchy transform converges). Thus, by the lower semicontinuity of
\Psi
, we have

(1.22)

\Psi(X+Y)=\Psi(\mu\uplus\nu)\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\Psi(\mu\uplus\nu_{n})\leq\Psi(\mu)=\Psi(X),

the last inequality holds because
\Psi(\nu_{n})<+\infty
and goes back to the finite case with
\theta=1
, hence we complete the proof.
∎

Corollary 1.11

(Entropy power inequality)
.

Let
X,Y
be self-adjoint random variables of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with symmetric laws and assume that
X,Y
are Boolean independent. We have

(1.23)

\exp(2\Gamma(X+Y))\geq\exp(2\Gamma(X))+\exp(2\Gamma(Y)).

In particular, if
\Gamma(X+Y)=-\infty
, then
\Gamma(X)=\Gamma(Y)=-\infty
.

Proof.

If
\Gamma(X)=\Gamma(Y)=-\infty
, then the inequality is trivial, hence we may assume without loss of generality that
\Gamma(X)>-\infty
and
\Gamma(Y)=-\infty
. Following the same argument as the preceding proof for Stam inequality, it can also be checked that
\exp{2\Gamma(X+Y)}\geq\exp{2\Gamma(X)}
. Now if
\Gamma(X),\Gamma(Y)
are finite, by the Shannon-Stam inequality (
1.14
), and the scaling property of entropy
\Gamma,
for any
\theta\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(X+Y)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left(\sqrt{\theta}\frac{X}{\sqrt{\theta}}+\sqrt{1-\theta}\frac{Y}{\sqrt{1-\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\theta\Gamma\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{\theta}}\right)+(1-\theta)\Gamma\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{1-\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta\left(\Gamma(X)-\frac{1}{2}\log\theta\right)+(1-\theta)\left(\Gamma(Y)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\theta)\right).

Now taking
\exp 2(\cdot)
on both sides gives

\exp(2\Gamma(X+Y))\geq\left(\frac{\exp(2\Gamma(X))}{\theta}\right)^{\theta}\cdot\left(\frac{\exp(2\Gamma(Y))}{1-\theta}\right)^{1-\theta}.

Notice that the choice of

\theta=\frac{e^{2\Gamma(X)}}{e^{2\Gamma(X)}+e^{2\Gamma(Y)}}

yields the result.
∎

As observed from the proof, the entropy power inequality (
1.23
) (Stam inequality (
1.21
), respectively) is equivalent to Shannon-Stam inequality (
1.14
) (Blachman-Stam inequality (
1.15
), respectively).

Transportation cost inequalities play an important role in optimal transport, which estimate the square of the 2-Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
and relative entropy between a pair of probability measures. The classical one was first obtained by Talagrand
[
39
]
in 1996. Later on, the free analogue in one dimension was introduced by Biane and Voiculescu
[
8
]
, and it was extended to multivariate case by Hiai and Ueda
[
21
]
. In the following, we present the Boolean version of such inequalities in one dimension.

Theorem 1.13

(Boolean Talagrand Transport Inequality)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

(1.25)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq 2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Proof.

Since the target measure has two atoms, optimal couplings reduce to mass splitting: any joint law
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with marginal
\mu
and
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
can be decomposed into
\mu_{+}\otimes\delta_{1}+\mu_{-}\otimes\delta_{-1},
where
\mu_{-},\mu_{+}
are positive measures such that
\mu_{-}+\mu_{+}=\mu
and
\mu_{-}(\mathbb{R})=\mu_{+}(\mathbb{R})=\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, the optimal coupling is when
\mu_{+}
is supported on
[0,+\infty)
and
\mu_{-}
is supported on
(-\infty,0]
, then

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|x-1|^{2}\,d\mu_{+}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}|x+1|^{2}\,d\mu_{-}(x)=\int(|x|-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x).

Thus, using the inequality that
-2|x|\leq-2\log|x|-2
gives

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

\displaystyle=\int(|x|-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int(x^{2}-2\log|x|-1)\,d\mu(x)=2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

∎

Let us come back to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Starting from a self-adjoint operator
X
of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
whose law is symmetric, we consider the variable
X_{t}:=e^{-t}X+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}B
, where

is Boolean independent from
X
and distributed according to the Rademacher law
\mathrm{b}.
We denote by
\mu_{X_{t}}
the law of
X_{t}
. We have

\tau(X_{t}^{2})=e^{-2t}\tau(X^{2})+1-e^{-2t}.

We define our relative Boolean Fisher information via taking derivative with respect to
t
of
-\Gamma(X_{t}|B)
. Using the equation (
1.6
) we get

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Gamma(X_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\tau\left(X_{t}^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu_{X_{t}}(x)d\mu_{X_{t}}(y)-1+e^{-2t}\int x^{2}\,d\mu_{X_{0}}(x)-e^{-2t}

(1.26)

\displaystyle=\Psi(X_{t})+\tau(X_{t}^{2})-2

which motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.14
.

let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
. The
Boolean Fisher information

\Psi(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of

relative to

(1.27)

\Psi(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)+\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2\in[0,+\infty].

More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})

Boolean Fisher information
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
\mu
relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}

\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)+\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2\in[0,+\infty].

A priori, it is not obvious that this expression should be nonnegative. Nevertheless, the following result shows that Boolean Fisher information retains the fundamental positivity property known from classical and free settings.

Proposition 1.15
.

The relative Boolean Fisher information
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
is non-negative for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
and
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=0
if and only if
\mu=\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
.

Proof.

From the explicit expression above,

(1.28)

\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\iint\left(\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{2}y^{2}-2\right)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

To prove that
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\geq 0
for all symmetric probability measures
\mu,
we intend to show that the integrant is almost everywhere non-negative. By the inequality (
1.16
), together with the inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab
yield

\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\geq\iint\left(\frac{2}{x^{2}+y^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})-2\right)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\geq 0.

This completes the first part of the proof. Based on the previous argument, it is clear that the equality holds if and only if
\mu=\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}.

∎

The log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) is a fundamental tool in analysis and probability to study convergence to equilibrium, concentration phenomenon, namely the relative entropy is bounded by the relative Fisher information. We aim to establish the Boolean counterpart, which mirrors the celebrated classical log-Sobolev inequality of Gross
[
19
]
, and the free analogue was first discovered by Voiculescu
[
41
]
and recovered by Biane and Speicher
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.16

(LSI)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
with finite variance, we have

(1.29)

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Proof.

If
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=+\infty
, we automatically have the inequality. Now assume that
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})<+\infty
, and since
\mu
has finite variance, in view of the expression of
\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
and
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
, we can reduce the inequality to the following:

\int\log|x|\,d\mu(x)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\iint\frac{\log x^{2}-\log y^{2}}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)-1\right)\geq 0.

As the first step, observe that the map
x\mapsto x^{2}
gives a bijection between symmetric probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
and probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Therefore, the above is equivalent to showing that for any
\mu^{(2)}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,

\int\log x\,d\mu^{(2)}(x)+\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\mu^{(2)}(x)d\mu^{(2)}(y)-1\geq 0.

We may rewrite the left-hand side as the following functional:

F(X):=\mathbb{E}[\log X]+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\log X-\log\tilde{X}}{X-\tilde{X}}\right]-1,

where
X,\tilde{X}
are classical i.i.d random variables with law
\mu^{(2)}
. For any
\lambda>0
, consider the dilation function

f_{X}(\lambda):=F(\lambda X)=\log\lambda+\mathbb{E}[\log X]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\log X-\log\tilde{X}}{X-\tilde{X}}\right]-1.

To ensure
f_{X}(\lambda)\geq 0
for all
X>0
, it suffices to check that the global minimum of
f_{X}(\cdot)
is nonnegative. Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
and setting the derivative equal to zero gives
\lambda=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\log X-\log\tilde{X}}{X-\tilde{X}}\right]
, which is the unique minimizer of
f_{X}
. Evaluating
f_{X}(\cdot)
at this point, thus it reduces to proving that for any positive random variable
X\sim\mu^{(2)}
and its classical i.i.d copy
\tilde{X}
,

(1.30)

\mathbb{E}[\log X]+\log\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\log X-\log\tilde{X}}{X-\tilde{X}}\right]\right\}\geq 0.

Using the lower bound from (
1.13
), it is enough to show that

\mathbb{E}[\log X]+\log\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{X+\tilde{X}}\right]\right\}\geq 0,

To proceed, assume
\mu^{(2)}
is discrete of the form
\mu^{(2)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}},
where
x_{i}>0
fixed for all
i=1,\ldots,n.
We aim to show that the above inequality holds for such discrete
\mu^{(2)}
. Plugging it into the left hand side of the above inequality gives

\displaystyle LHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\log(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})+\log\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{i}}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{2}{x_{i}+x_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left\{\sqrt[n]{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{i}}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{2}{x_{i}+x_{j}}\right)\right\}.

Hence it remains to show that for all
x_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n,

\sqrt[n]{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{i}}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{2}{x_{i}+x_{j}}\right)\geq 1.

By homogeneity, we may assume
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}=1
. Under this assumption, the inequality follows from the lemma stated below, which we shall prove later.

Lemma 1.17
.

Fix
x_{i}>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n,
such that
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}=1.
Then for any integer
n\geq 1,
we have

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{i}}+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{2}{x_{i}+x_{j}}\geq 1,

and the equality holds if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n}=1.

With the above lemma, we have shown that the inequality (
1.30
) is valid for all discrete probability measures in the set
\mathcal{D}:=\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}\ |\ x_{i}>0,\ \forall i=1,\ldots,n,\ n\geq 1\}.
Since
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
is dense with respect to the weak topology, using the dominated convergence theorem, we deduce that the inequality holds for all probability measures with support
[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]
for any
0<\epsilon\leq 1.
Furthermore, for a general probability measure
\nu
supported on
\mathbb{R}_{+},
we define

\nu_{\epsilon}(A):=\frac{1}{\nu([\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}])}\nu(A\cap[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}])\ \text{for any}\ A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

as the truncated measure of
\nu
. Clearly, we have
\nu([\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}])\to 1
as
\epsilon\to 0.
In addition, the fact that the function
\log(\cdot)
is monotone on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
implies that
(\log x-\log y)/(x-y)
is non-negative. Thanks to the monotone convergence theorem, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log X\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]}]

\displaystyle\overset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}[\log X],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\log X-\log\tilde{X}}{X-\tilde{X}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\log X-\log\tilde{X}}{X-\tilde{X}}\right].

Therefore, these yield

\displaystyle\int\log x\,d\nu_{\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\overset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\int\log x\,d\nu(x),

\displaystyle\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\nu_{\epsilon}(x)d\nu_{\epsilon}(y)

\displaystyle\overset{\epsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\iint\frac{\log x-\log y}{x-y}\,d\nu(x)d\nu(y),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof.

(Sketch of proof of Lemma

)
This is a minimization problem with constraints. Therefore, we just apply Lagrange multiplier method and we define the following function in the domain
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}:

L(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\lambda):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{i}}+2\cdot\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{2}{x_{i}+x_{j}}+\lambda(x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}-1).

Setting all partial derivatives equal to zero yields the system:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{i}}=-\frac{1}{x_{i}^{2}}-2\cdot\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}}+\lambda\prod_{j\neq i}x_{j}=0\ i=1,\ldots,n;

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}-1=0.

By symmetry, it is clear that all
x_{i}
must be equal. Imposing the constraint
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}=1
, we obtain the unique solution
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n}=1
. It is straightforward that this critical point is indeed a minimum.
∎

Remark 1.18
.

In fact, if
\mu
is compactly supported and let

, the left and the right hand side of (

) are equal. Hence we deduce that

\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\Gamma(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}e^{-2t}\Gamma(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}),

which just gives back the LSI.

Proof.

By the de Bruijn identity (
1.3
), with the LSI, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=-\Psi(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq-2\Gamma(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Then Gronwall’s lemma yields the exponential decay.
∎

We emphasize that the assumption of symmetry in Theorem
1.16
can be removed because the quantity involved only depends on the push forward measure
\mu^{(2)}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. However, the above equivalences fail under such extension, as the argument relies on the de Bruijn identity. The following result is direct from Theorem
1.13
and Theorem
1.16
.

Corollary 1.20
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
,we have the bound
W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

2.
The Non-microstates approach

2.1.
The Boolean Schwinger-Dyson equation

Let us recall informally the notion of classical and free Fisher information in order to motivate our upcoming definition. In particular, we will not explore the minimal regularity of the test-functions
f
occurring for the equations to hold. In the classical case, we have the integration by parts formula

\int x\cdot f(x)\,d\gamma(x)=\int f^{\prime}(x)\,d\gamma(x)

satisfied by the standard Gaussian distribution
\gamma
. The Fisher information of any measure
\mu
, if it exists, is given as the norm
\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}
of the
score function

\xi
, a function which allows to write the following deformation of the integration by part formula:

\int\xi(x)\cdot f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int f^{\prime}(x)\,d\mu(x).

Similarly, in the free case, we have the following Schwinger-Dyson formula

\int x\cdot f(x)\,d\sigma(x)=\iint\partial f(x,y)\,d\sigma(x)d\sigma(y)

satisfied by the standard semicircular distribution
\sigma
, where the
\partial f
is the non-commutative derivative
\partial f(x,y)=(f(x)-f(y))/(x-y)
. The free Fisher information of any measure
\mu
, if it exists, is given as
\|\xi\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}
for the
conjugate variable
, a function which allows to write the following deformation of the Schwinger-Dyson formula:

\int\xi(x)\cdot f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\iint\partial f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

If we want to follow the same path in the Boolean case, the first step is to find a similar formula satisfied by the Rademacher variable
\mathrm{b}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{+1}
. Let us define
D^{\downarrow}f(x):=\frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x}
for functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which are differentiable at
0
. For
\mathrm{b}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{+1}
, we have

\int x\cdot f(x)\,d\mathrm{b}(x)=\int D^{\downarrow}f(x)\,d\mathrm{b}(x)

since
x=1/x
on the support of
\mathrm{b}
.
The operator
D^{\downarrow}
will be useful in Section
3.1
in order to use Stein’s method in the Boolean setting.
For symmetric measures
\mu
, we can say that
x\mapsto 1/x
plays the role of the score function, or of the conjugate variable, in the Boolean case. Indeed, if
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, for all measurable functions
f
which are differentiable at
0
, we have

\int\frac{1}{x}\cdot f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int D^{\downarrow}f(x)\,d\mu(x)

whenever both sides are integrable.

2.2.
Boolean entropy and Fisher information

Since the function
x\mapsto 1/x
plays the role of a Boolean score function
in the Boolean Schwinger–Dyson identity, we take
\|1/x\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}
as a definition for the Non-microstates Boolean Fisher information.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space

(2.1)

More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the (Non-microstates)
Boolean Fisher information

\Psi^{*}(\mu)
of
\mu
as

\Psi^{*}(\mu):=\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)\in(0,+\infty].

If we want to define the Non-microstates Boolean entropy, we need to integrate this Fisher information
\Psi^{*}
along the Boolean heat semigroup: starting from a self-adjoint operator
X
of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
whose law is symmetric, we consider the variable
X+\sqrt{t}B
where
B\in\mathcal{M}
is Boolean independent from
X

\mathrm{b}.
The derivative of
\Psi^{*}(X+\sqrt{t}B)
will produce the following quantity (see Theorem

Definition 2.2

Let

with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})

Boolean entropy

\Gamma^{*}(X)
of

(2.2)

\Gamma^{*}(X):=-\frac{1}{2}\log\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)\in]-\infty,+\infty].

More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})

Boolean entropy

\Gamma^{*}(\mu)
of
\mu
as

\Gamma^{*}(\mu):=-\frac{1}{2}\log\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)\in]-\infty,+\infty].

In contrast with the Microstates framework, the Boolean heat flow admits a
closed-form evolution for
\Psi^{*}
; this makes the following de Bruijn identity a
direct computation rather than a variational statement.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
, with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, and
B\in\mathcal{M}
be Boolean independent from
X
and distributed according to
\mathrm{b}
. Fix
t\geq 0
such that
\Gamma^{*}(X+\sqrt{s}B)<\infty,\ \Psi^{*}(X+\sqrt{s}B)<\infty
for any
s\in[0,t].
Then we have

(2.3)

\Gamma^{*}(X+\sqrt{t}B)-\Gamma^{*}(X)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\Psi^{*}(X+\sqrt{s}B)\,ds.

Proof.

By (
1.4
), we know that

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(X+\sqrt{t}B)

\displaystyle=-G_{(X+\sqrt{t}B)^{2}}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{G_{X^{2}}(0)}{1-tG_{X^{2}}(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Psi^{*}(X)}{1+t\Psi^{*}(X)}.

In particular, we can compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\Psi^{*}(X+\sqrt{s}B)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+t\Psi^{*}(X))-\frac{1}{2}\log(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\Psi^{*}(X)}{1+t\Psi^{*}(X)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log(\Psi^{*}(X))

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{*}(X+\sqrt{t}B)-\Gamma^{*}(X).

∎

From the definitions of
\Gamma^{*}(X)
and
\Psi^{*}(X)
, we have the expected scaling properties.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

We have
\Gamma^{*}(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(\Psi^{*}(X));\ \Psi^{*}(\lambda X)=\lambda^{-2}\Psi^{*}(X)
and
\Gamma^{*}(\lambda X)=\Gamma^{*}(X)+\log|\lambda|
.

Following the computations of the previous section for
\Gamma(X)
and
\Psi(X)
, we also get the following.

Corollary 2.5
.

We have

(2.4)

\Gamma^{*}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{s+1}-\Psi^{*}(X+\sqrt{s}B)\right)\,ds

2.3.
Information-theoretic properties

In the Non-microstates case, a lot of inequalities of the previous section become equalities.

Proposition 2.6

(Boolean Stam equality)
.

Let
X,Y
be self-adjoint operators of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with symmetric laws and which are Boolean independent. We have

(2.5)

\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(X+Y)}=\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(X)}+\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(Y)}.

Proof.

For all
z\in\mathbb{H}_{+}
, we have

G_{X^{2}+Y^{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{z-K_{X^{2}+Y^{2}}(z)}=\frac{1}{1/G_{X^{2}}(z)+1/G_{Y^{2}}(z)-z}

which extends to
z
negative real numbers and yields : for
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\tau\left(\frac{1}{-\varepsilon-X^{2}-Y^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau\left(\frac{1}{-\varepsilon-X^{2}}\right)}+\frac{1}{\tau\left(\frac{1}{-\varepsilon-Y^{2}}\right)}+\varepsilon},

or equivalently

\tau\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+X^{2}+Y^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+X^{2}}\right)}+\frac{1}{\tau\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+Y^{2}}\right)}-\varepsilon}.

We conclude using monotone convergence:

\Psi^{*}(X+Y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tau\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+X^{2}+Y^{2}}\right),\ \Psi^{*}(X)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tau\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+X^{2}}\right)\ \text{ and }\ \Psi^{*}(Y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tau\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon+Y^{2}}\right).

∎

Thus, many information-theoretic inequalities from the Microstates approach
collapse here into exact identities, reflecting the linear behaviour of
Non-microstates quantities under Boolean convolution. Because
\Gamma^{*}(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\log\Psi^{*}(X)
, we get the following.

Corollary 2.7

(Entropy power equality)
.

Let
X,Y
be self-adjoint operators of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with symmetric laws and which are Boolean independent. We have

(2.6)

\exp(2\Gamma^{*}(X+Y))=\exp(2\Gamma^{*}(X))+\exp(2\Gamma^{*}(Y)),

Proof.

From the Boolean Stam equality, we have

\exp(2\Gamma^{*}(X+Y))=\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(X+Y)}=\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(X)}+\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(Y)}=\exp(2\Gamma^{*}(X))+\exp(2\Gamma^{*}(Y)).

∎

Except for the proof of the Blachman-Stam inequality and the proof of the Cramér-Rao inequality, all the following properties are proved following verbatim the computations of the previous section for
\Gamma(X)
and
\Psi(X)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The Boolean entropy
\Gamma^{*}
and Fisher information
\Psi^{*}
satisfy the following properties (where we assume that all appearing variables are self-adjoint and have symmetric probability measures in some non-commutative probability space):

(1)

We have the bound for
\Psi^{*}
as follows:

(2.7)

\Psi(X)\leq\Psi^{*}(X).

(2)

\Gamma^{*}
is upper semicontinuous with respect to the weak topology of probability measures with compact support, and
\Psi^{*}
is lower semicontinuous.

(3)

If
X
and
Y
are Boolean independent, then for any
\theta\in[0,1]
we have the corresponding Shannon-Stam inequality and Blachmann-Stam inequality:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{*}(\sqrt{\theta}X+\sqrt{1-\theta}Y)\geq\theta\Gamma^{*}(X)+(1-\theta)\Gamma^{*}(Y);

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(\sqrt{\theta}X+\sqrt{1-\theta}Y)\leq\theta\Psi^{*}(X)+(1-\theta)\Psi^{*}(Y).

Proof.

We just need to justify the proof of the Blachmann-Stam inequality, as the other proofs are mutatis mutandis the same proof as for
\Psi
and
\Gamma
. We use Voiculescu-Stam equality, the scaling property of
\Psi^{*}
and Jensen inequality applied to the inverse function:

\Psi^{*}(\sqrt{\theta}X+\sqrt{1-\theta}Y)=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(\sqrt{\theta}X)}+\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(\sqrt{1-\theta}Y)}}=\frac{1}{\theta\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(X)}+(1-\theta)\frac{1}{\Psi^{*}(Y)}}\leq\theta\Psi^{*}(X)+(1-\theta)\Psi^{*}(Y).

∎

Corollary 2.9

(Cramér-Rao inequality)
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with a symmetric law. We have

(2.10)

\Psi^{*}(X)\geq\frac{1}{\tau(X^{2})}.

Proof.

Because
\Psi^{*}(X)\geq\Psi(X)
, it follows from the Cramér-Rao inequality for
\Psi(X)
.
∎

We have seen that in contrast with the Microstates framework, the Non-microstates
quantities behave linearly under Boolean convolution. This leads to the following result.

Theorem 2.10

(Constancy in the Central Limit Theorem)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset(\mathcal{M},\tau)
be a sequence of i.i.d. (in the Boolean sense) random variables with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
. Define

S_{n}:=\frac{X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Then, for any
n\geq 1
, one has

\Gamma^{*}(S_{n})=\Gamma^{*}(X_{1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi^{*}(S_{n})=\Psi^{*}(X_{1}).

Proof.

Since
\Gamma^{*}(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(\Psi^{*}(X))
, we just need to check it for the Fisher information: it is a consequence of Voiculescu-Stam equality combined with the scaling property of
\Psi^{*}
.
∎

Overall, the Non-microstates framework exhibits a striking rigidity: many
functional inequalities that are genuinely analytic in the Microstates
setting become explicit algebraic identities or elementary bounds.

2.4.
Functional inequalities

In classical and in free probability, the Fisher information
relative
to the quadratic potential is equal to
\|\xi(x)-x\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}
where
\xi
is the score function in the classical case and
\xi
is the conjugate variable in the free case. Following the same idea, and because
x\mapsto 1/x
is the score function in the Boolean framework, we define now the Boolean Fisher information relative to the Rademacher variable as
\|1/x-x\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\|1/x\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}+\|x\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}-2
.

Definition 2.11
.

let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
. The (Non-microstates)
Boolean Fisher information
\Psi^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
X
relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
is defined as

(2.11)

\Psi^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)+\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2\in[0,+\infty].

More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the (Non-microstates)
Boolean Fisher information
\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
\mu
relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
as

\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)+\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2\in[0,+\infty].

We would like to define the relative Boolean entropy as in the Microstates case, by
\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=-\Gamma^{*}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\tau\left(X^{2}\right)-1/2
, so that its derivative along the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck flow equals minus the relative Fisher information. Let us see how it behaves with respect to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Starting from a self-adjoint operator
X
of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
whose law is symmetric, we consider the variable
X_{t}:=e^{-t}X+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}B
where
B\in\mathcal{M}
is Boolean independent from
X
and distributed according to the Rademacher law
\mathrm{b}.
We denote by
\mu_{X_{t}}
the law of
X_{t}
. By the scaling property of
\Gamma
, we know that

\Gamma^{*}(X_{t})=\Gamma^{*}\left(X+\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}B\right)-t.

Thus, using the scaling property of
\Psi^{*}
, we have

(2.12)

\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma^{*}(X_{t})=e^{2t}\Psi^{*}\left(X+\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}B\right)-1=\Psi^{*}(X_{t})-1.

We get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(-\Gamma^{*}(X_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\tau\left(X_{t}^{2}\right)-1/2\right)

\displaystyle=-\Psi^{*}(X_{t})+1-e^{-2t}\int x^{2}d\mu(x)+e^{-2t}

(2.13)

\displaystyle=-\Psi^{*}(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

This justifies the following definition.

Definition 2.12
.

let
X
be a self-adjoint operator of a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
. The
(Non-microstates) Boolean entropy
\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
X
relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
is defined as

(2.14)

\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\frac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)+\frac{1}{2}\log\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2}\in[0,+\infty].

More generally, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the
(Non-microstates) Boolean entropy
\Gamma^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
\mu
relative to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
as

\Gamma^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\frac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)+\frac{1}{2}\log\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2}\in[0,+\infty].

Let us compare the relative micro-state entropy with the Non-microstates one, showing the positivity of
\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
.

Proposition 2.13

(Microstates vs Non-microstates)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\Gamma(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \Psi(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Psi^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

In particular,
\Gamma^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
provides a universal upper bound
for the Microstates relative entropy, hence positivity of the Non-microstates
functional is automatic once the Microstates one is known.

Proof.

The first inequality follows from Jensen inequality:

-\int\log x^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int\log 1/x^{2}\,d\mu(x)\leq\log\int 1/x^{2}\,d\mu(x).

For the second one, it is a consequence of
\Psi(X)\leq\Psi^{*}(X)
.
∎

We have already proved the logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI) in the Microstates
framework. We now show that the same principle holds in the Non-microstates
setting.

Theorem 2.14

(LSI)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Psi^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

Note that this inequality is sharp, with equality at the Rademacher law. As in the Microstates case, following the same proofs, a direct consequence is the exponential decay of relative entropy along the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
X_{t}
:

\Gamma^{*}(X_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq e^{-2t}\Gamma^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Proof.

In contrast with the Microstates case, the logarithmic Sobolev inequality
here reduces to an elementary convexity inequality. It is just the inequality

\frac{1}{2}\log\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)+\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{1}{x^{2}}\,d\mu(x)-1.

∎

Combining the two previous results, we get a log-Sobolev inequality which connects the Microstate entropy
\Gamma
with the Non-microstate Fisher information
\Psi^{*}
:

\Gamma(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\Psi^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

One can refine this log-Sobolev inequality by interpolating entropy with transport and Fisher information. This leads to a Boolean HWI inequality, in the spirit of Otto–Villani, from which the log-Sobolev inequality above follows immediately.
In the classical
[
29
]
and free settings
[
31
]
, HWI inequalities takes the exact same form (with the corresponding entropy and Fisher information). They play a central role in connecting functional inequalities to transportation estimates.

Theorem 2.15

(HWI inequality)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
be a symmetric probability measure with
m_{2}(\mu)<\infty
.
Then one has the following HWI inequality:

(2.15)

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\,\sqrt{\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}-\frac{1}{2}\,W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}.

By the elementary inequality
ab-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}b^{2}
, the HWI inequality implies

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Proof.

If
\int x^{-2}\,d\mu(x)=+\infty
, then
\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=+\infty
and there is nothing to prove. We may therefore assume
\int x^{-2}\,d\mu(x)<+\infty
, which also implies
\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})<+\infty
and
\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})<+\infty
.

Since
\mu
is symmetric, the optimal coupling with
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
gives

W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=\int\bigl(|x|-1\bigr)^{2}\,d\mu(x).

The two other quantities appearing in (
2.15
) are

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\,d\mu(x)-\int\log|x|\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})

\displaystyle=\int\bigl(x^{2}+x^{-2}-2\bigr)\,d\mu(x).

For
r>0
define the potential

V(r):=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}-\log r-\frac{1}{2}.

Then, setting
r=|x|
,

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\int V(|x|)\,d\mu(x).

It is straightforward to verify the pointwise inequality

V(r)\leq\Bigl(r-\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)(r-1)-\frac{1}{2}(r-1)^{2},\qquad r>0,

because

\Bigl(r-\frac{1}{r}\Bigr)(r-1)-\frac{1}{2}(r-1)^{2}-V(r)=\log r+\frac{1}{r}-1\geq 0

Integrating with respect to
\mu
,

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\int\Bigl(|x|-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)\bigl(|x|-1\bigr)\,d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{2}\,W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\int\Bigl(|x|-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)\bigl(|x|-1\bigr)\,d\mu(x)\leq\Bigl(\int\bigl(|x|-1\bigr)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\int\Bigl(|x|-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\Bigr)^{1/2}.

The first factor on the right is exactly
W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
. For the second,

\int\Bigl(|x|-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int\bigl(x^{2}+x^{-2}-2\bigr)\,d\mu(x)=\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Therefore the integral is at most

W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\sqrt{\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}.

Substituting back yields the desired HWI inequality (
2.15
).
∎

3.
Stein’s method for Boolean independence

In this section we do not work inside a non-commutative probability space.
Instead, we develop a Stein-type method for the Rademacher distribution on a classical probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, which will later be applied to Boolean convolution.

3.1.
Stein’s method

First of all, let us briefly recall Stein’s method
[
11
,
18
,
32
]
, which allows one to bound distances of the form

\sup_{\varphi}|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(B)]|

for a reference variable
B
. The steps are as follows:

•

Find an operator
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f(B)]=0
. It is called the
Stein operator
of
B
.

•

Solve the Stein equation

\mathcal{D}f(x)=\varphi(x)

for a fixed
\varphi
. Writing
f_{\varphi}
the solution of
\mathcal{D}f(x)=\varphi(x)-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(B)]
, we have

\sup_{\varphi}|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(B)]|=\sup_{\varphi}|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f_{\varphi}(X)]|

where the supremum is taken over a certain class of functions.

•

It remains to bound the quantities

|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f_{\varphi}(X)]|
.

We now specialize Stein’s method to the Rademacher distribution.

Step 1.

Let
B
be a random variable with Rademacher distribution
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
.
Since
B^{2}=1
, we have
B=1/B
almost surely. As a consequence, if we define
D^{\downarrow}f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x}
for measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which are differentiable at
0
(with the continuous extension
D^{\downarrow}f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)
),
we have

\mathbb{E}[B\cdot f(B)]=\mathbb{E}[D^{\downarrow}f(B)]

whenever all the terms are integrable.
We define the Stein operator
\mathcal{D}
on measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which are differentiable at
0
by

\mathcal{D}f(x)=D^{\downarrow}f(x)-xf(x),

in such a way that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f(B)]=0
.

Step 2.
Let us solve the Stein equation in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any measurable function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which is continuous at
0
and such that
\varphi(1)=\varphi(-1)=0
, there exists a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
f(0)=0
and such that

\varphi=\mathcal{D}f.

Furthermore, whenever
\varphi
is
1
-Lipschitz, we have

\|D^{\downarrow}f\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Proof.

It suffices to set
f(1)=f(-1)=0
and
f(x)=x\frac{\varphi(x)}{1-x^{2}}
if
x\neq\pm 1
. The assumption that

\varphi
is continuous at
0
ensures that

f
is differentiable at

0
. The bound on
D^{\downarrow}f
is because

|D^{\downarrow}f(x)|=\left|\frac{1}{1+x}\right|\left|\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(1)}{x-1}\right|\leq 1

if
x\geq 0
and

|D^{\downarrow}f(x)|=\left|\frac{1}{1-x}\right|\left|\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(-1)}{x-(-1)}\right|\leq 1

if
x<0
.
∎

Step 3.
We now apply the Stein equation to control
the
1
-Wasserstein distance
W_{1}
between a random variable
X\in L^{1}
and
B
. By the Kantorovich duality, we have

W_{1}(X,B)=\sup_{\varphi\ 1-\text{Lipschitz}}|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(B)]|.

Asume that
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
. Since
B
is centered, we can write

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(B)]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\varphi}(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\varphi}(B)]

for any
\tilde{\varphi}
such that
\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}
is affine. In particular, we can choose
\varphi
such that
\varphi(1)=\varphi(-1)=0
and write

W_{1}(X,B)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\ 1-\text{Lipschitz}\\
\varphi(1)=\varphi(-1)=0\end{subarray}}|\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]-\mathbb{E}[\varphi(B)]|.

Using the solution of the Stein equation yields the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any variable
X\in L^{1}
such that
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
, we have

W_{1}(X,B)\leq\sup_{f}|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f(X)]|

where the supremum is taken over measurable functions
f
which are differentiable at
0
, such that
f(0)=0
and such that
\|D^{\downarrow}f\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

We obtain the following bound on the Wasserstein distance.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any variable
X\in L^{2}
, we have

W_{1}(X,B)\leq|\mathbb{E}[X]|+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^{2}-1\right|\right].

In particular, if
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
,

W_{1}(X,B)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X^{2}-1\right|\right].

Proof.

Because
W_{1}(X,B)\leq W_{1}(X,X-\mathbb{E}[X])+W_{1}(X-\mathbb{E}[X],B)=|\mathbb{E}[X]|+W_{1}(X-\mathbb{E}[X],B)
, it suffices to prove the inequality in the case where
X
is centered.

Whenever
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
, we have

W_{1}(X,B)\leq\sup_{f}|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f(X)]|

where the supremum is taken over measurable functions
f
which are differentiable at
0
, such that
f(0)=0
and such that
\|D^{\downarrow}f\|_{\infty}\leq 1.
For such a function
f
, we have

\mathcal{D}f(X)=(1-X^{2})\,D^{\downarrow}f(X),

which allows to write

|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}f(X)]|=|\mathbb{E}[(1-X^{2})\cdot D^{\downarrow}f(X)]|\leq\|1-X^{2}\|_{L^{1}}\cdot\|D^{\downarrow}f\|_{\infty}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X^{2}-1\right|\right]

which implies that
W_{1}(X,B)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X^{2}-1\right|\right]
.
∎

We may now restate the previous bound at the level of laws, and get a quantitative version of the Boolean fourth moment theorem in
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Corollary 3.4

(Quantitative fourth moment theorem)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
such that
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
, and
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
. We have

W_{1}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}.

Proof.

Let
B
be a random variable with Rademacher distribution
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
and
X
be a random variable with distribution
\mu
. We have

W_{1}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=W_{1}(X,B)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X^{2}-1\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right]}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]-1}.

∎

Using (
0.2
), we obtain the following Berry-Esseen bound.

Corollary 3.5

(Berry-Esseen bound)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
such that
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
,
and
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
. We define
\mu_{n}
to be the dilation of
\mu^{\uplus n}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, i.e.
\mu_{n}(B)=\mu^{\uplus n}(\{x:\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\in B\})
. We have

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}.

3.2.
Stein discrepancy

The notion of Stein kernel plays a central role in classical and free probability. We now introduce its Boolean analogue.
In the classical setting, a Stein kernel
[
25
]
with respect to the Gaussian distribution is a function
A\in L^{2}(\mu)
such that

\int x\cdot f(x)d\mu(x)=\int A(x)\cdot f^{\prime}(x)d\mu(x).

In other words, the score function is obtained by replacing
x
by
\xi
on the left side in the integration by parts (see Section
2.1
), and the Stein kernel is obtained by replacing
1
by
A(x)
on the right side of the equality. Similarly, a free Stein kernel
[
16
]
with respect to the semicircular distribution is any
A\in L^{2}(\mu\otimes\mu)
such that the following deformation of the Schwinger-Dyson formula holds:

\int x\cdot f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\iint A(x,y)\cdot\partial f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

Following the same philosophy, it is natural to interpret the map
x\to x^{2}
as a Boolean Stein kernel (with respect to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
) of any centered measure
\mu
,. Indeed, for any function which is differentiable at
0
, we have the deformation of the Boolean Schwinger-Dyson identity

\int x\cdot f(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int x^{2}\cdot D^{\downarrow}f(x)\,d\mu(x).

This leads to the definition of the Boolean Stein discrepancy, in parallel to the classical Stein discrepancy
\|T-1\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
(see also the free one).

Definition 3.6
.

Let
X
be a real random variable with law
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
. The
Boolean Stein discrepancy

D^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
X
with respect to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
is defined as

D^{*}(X|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\left(\int(x^{2}-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}\in[0,+\infty].

Similarly, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the
Boolean Stein discrepancy

D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
of
\mu
with respect to
\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
as

D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}):=\left(\int(x^{2}-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}\in[0,+\infty].

The Stein discrepancy has already shown up in the previous Section
3.1
, where it was shown that whenever
\mu
is centered, we have

W_{1}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

We can strengthen this result to the 2-Wasserstein distance as follows. It is the Boolean analogue of the classical and free WS inequalities in respectively
[
25
,
10
]
.

Proposition 3.7

(WS inequality)
.

For any measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\left(\int(|x|-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

In particular, if
\mu
is symmetric,

W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Proof.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(|x|-1)^{2}\leq(|x|-1)^{2}(|x|+1)^{2}=(|x|^{2}-1)^{2}.

Therefore,

\left(\int(|x|-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\int(|x|^{2}-1)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}=D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

For
\mu
symmetric, the equality
W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\left(\int(|x|-1)^{2}d\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}

was established in previous sections.
∎

As a consequence, inequalities involving the Boolean Stein discrepancy can be turned into Berry-Esseen results using (
0.2
).

Proposition 3.8

(Berry-Esseen bound)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
such that
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
,
and
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
. We define
\mu_{n}
to be the dilation of
\mu^{\uplus n}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, i.e.
\mu_{n}(B)=\mu^{\uplus n}(\{x:\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\in B\})
. We have

W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}.

Proof.

It suffices to observe that
m_{2}(\mu_{n})=1
, so we can compute

\displaystyle D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int(|x|^{2}-1)^{2}d\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=m_{4}(\mu_{n})-2m_{2}(\mu_{n})+1

\displaystyle=r_{4}(\mu_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}r_{4}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}(m_{4}(\mu)-1),

where we used (
0.2
).
∎

3.3.
Entropic CLT

To turn the convergence of the Stein discrepancy into entropic convergence in the Boolean central limit theorem, we establish a Boolean HSI inequality.
This inequality is the exact analogue, at the level of its functional form, of the HSI inequalities in the classical and free settings
[
25
,
16
]
.

Theorem 3.9

(HSI inequality)
.

For any measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\frac{1}{2}D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}\cdot\log\left(1+\frac{\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}\right).

Using
\log(1+x)\leq x
, we recover the inequality
2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu|b)
which is the log-Sobolev inequality between
\Gamma
and
\Psi^{*}
.

Proof.

The proof relies on the pointwise inequality

u-\log u-1\leq\delta\!\left(u+\frac{1}{u}-2\right)+(-\log\delta+\delta-1)(u-1)^{2}

for all
u>0
and
\delta\in(0,1]
, and then integrate and optimize over
\delta
.

Let

F(u,\delta)=\log u-u+1+\delta\!\left(u+\frac{1}{u}-2\right)+(-\log\delta+\delta-1)(u-1)^{2}.

Note that
F\geq 0
is equivalent to the desired inequality.
Fix
u>0
and minimize with respect to
\delta\in(0,1]
. Differentiating gives

\partial_{\delta}F(u,\delta)=\left(u+\frac{1}{u}-2\right)+\left(-\frac{1}{\delta}+1\right)(u-1)^{2}.

Using
u+1/u-2=(u-1)^{2}/u
, this becomes

\partial_{\delta}F(u,\delta)=(u-1)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{u}-\frac{1}{\delta}+1\right).

For
u\neq 1
the critical point is
\delta_{*}(u)=u/(u+1)\in(0,1)
. The second derivative

\partial_{\delta}^{2}F(u,\delta)=(u-1)^{2}/\delta^{2}>0

shows it is a minimum. For
u=1
,
F(1,\delta)=0
for all
\delta
. Substituting
\delta=u/(u+1)
yields

\min_{\delta\in(0,1]}F(u,\delta)=H(u):=\log u-u+1+(u-1)^{2}\log\!\frac{u+1}{u}.

It remains to show
H(u)\geq 0
for all
u>0
. Clearly
H(1)=0
. A direct computation gives

H^{\prime}(u)=2(u-1)\left(\log\frac{u+1}{u}-\frac{1}{u+1}\right).

The function
\phi(u)=\log((u+1)/u)-1/(u+1)
satisfies
\phi(u)>0
for all
u>0
(since
\phi(1)>0
,
\phi(u)\to 0^{+}
as
u\to\infty
, and
\phi^{\prime}(u)<0
). Thus
H^{\prime}(u)<0
on
(0,1)
and
H^{\prime}(u)>0
on
(1,\infty)
, so
H
attains its global minimum at
u=1
, where
H(1)=0
. Hence
H(u)\geq 0
.

Consequently
F(u,\delta)\geq 0
for all
u>0
and
\delta\in(0,1]
, which rearranges to the desired pointwise inequality. Integrating with respect to
\mu
gives

2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\delta\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})+(-\log\delta+\delta-1)D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}.

The right-hand side, seen as a function of
\delta
, is minimized at

\delta=\frac{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}+\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}.

Substituting this optimal value yields exactly

2\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}\cdot\log\left(1+\frac{\Psi^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

In particular, the Boolean central limit theorem holds at the entropic level, from the convergence of the Stein discrepancy via the HSI inequality.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
such that
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
,
and
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
. We define
\mu_{n}
to be the dilation of
\mu^{\uplus n}
by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, i.e.
\mu_{n}(B)=\mu^{\uplus n}(\{x:\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\in B\})
. We have, as
n\to\infty
,

\Gamma(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=O\left(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.10
, the quantity
\Psi^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
is constant and Proposition
3.8
gives the rate
D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=O(1/n)
. Thus

D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}\cdot\log\left(1+\frac{\Psi^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}{D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right),

and the HSI inequality allows to conclude.
∎

We can also derive a quantitative bound on the Boolean Fisher information along the Boolean central limit theorem. We do not expect the rate below to be optimal.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
such that
m_{1}(\mu)=0
,
m_{2}(\mu)=1
and
m_{4}(\mu)<+\infty
.
Let
\mu_{n}
be the dilation of
\mu^{\uplus n}
by
1/\sqrt{n}
, i, i.e.
\mu_{n}(B)=\mu^{\uplus n}(\{x:\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\in B\})
.
Then

\Psi(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu_{n})\,D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,m_{-2}(\mu)\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}.

Proof.

We first prove the general bound

|\Psi(\mu)-1|\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu)\,D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Using (
1.17
), we write

\Psi(\mu)=\iiint\frac{1}{tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2}}\,dt\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

Hence

|\Psi(\mu)-1|=\left|\iiint\frac{1-(tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2})}{tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2}}\,dt\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\right|.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz with respect to the product measure

dt\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y)
gives

|\Psi(\mu)-1|\leq\left(\iiint\frac{1}{(tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\iiint(1-(tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2}))^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For the second factor,

\iiint(1-W_{t})^{2}\,dt\,d\mu\,d\mu=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Var}(W_{t})\,dt,

where
W_{t}=tX^{2}+(1-t)Y^{2}
. Since
\mathrm{Var}(W_{t})=[t^{2}+(1-t)^{2}]D^{*}(\mu|\mathrm{b})^{2}
and
t^{2}+(1-t)^{2}\leq 1
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Var}(W_{t})\,dt\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathrm{b})^{2},

so the second factor is at most
D^{*}(\mu|\mathrm{b})
.

The first factor equals
\Psi^{*}(\mu)
, because

\iiint\frac{1}{(tx^{2}+(1-t)y^{2})^{2}}=\iint\frac{1}{x^{2}-y^{2}}\!\left(\frac{-1}{x^{2}}-\frac{-1}{y^{2}}\right)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=\Psi^{*}(\mu)^{2}.

Thus

|\Psi(\mu)-1|\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu)\,D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Since

\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\Psi(\mu)+m_{2}(\mu)-2,

we obtain

\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\Psi^{*}(\mu)\,D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})+|m_{2}(\mu)-1|.

Applying this to
\mu_{n}
, we have
m_{2}(\mu_{n})=1
, while

\Psi^{*}(\mu_{n})=\Psi^{*}(\mu)
by Theorem
2.10

and

D^{*}(\mu_{n}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{m_{4}(\mu)-1}

by Proposition
3.8
.
This yields the claimed bound.
∎

Beyond its role in the entropic central limit theorem, the HSI inequality above fits into a broader family of interpolation inequalities relating transport, entropy and Fisher information.
In the classical and free settings, the HSI inequality implies a WSH inequality
[
25
,
10
]
, which refines the Talagrand inequality.
We now establish the corresponding Boolean WSH inequality, thereby completing the parallel between the classical, free and Boolean frameworks.

Theorem 3.12

(WSH inequality)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{sym}(\mathbb{R})
with
m_{2}(\mu)<+\infty
.
Then

(3.1)

W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\,\arccos\!\left(\exp\!\left\{-\frac{\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}\right\}\right).

Observe that for
x\geq 0
one has

\arccos(e^{-x})\leq\sqrt{2x}.

Applying this with

x=\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})/D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}

in (
3.1
), we obtain the Boolean Talagrand inequality

W_{2}(\mu,\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}\leq 2\,\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable whose law is
\mu
and, for
t\geq 0
, let
X_{t}
be a symmetric random variable such that

X_{t}^{2}=e^{-2t}X^{2}+(1-e^{-2t}).

We warn the reader that the semigroup
(X_{t})_{t}
is not the one given by the Boolean Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semi-group as considered in the previous sections.
However, setting
Y_{t}=X_{t}^{2}
, we have
Y_{t}\to 1
as
t\to+\infty
, hence

X_{t}\to B
in distribution.

Since
X_{t}
is symmetric,

W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=\mathbb{E}\big[(|X_{t}|-1)^{2}\big].

Differentiating with respect to
t
gives

\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(|X_{t}|-1)\frac{d}{dt}|X_{t}|\right].

Because
|x|=\sqrt{x^{2}}
and
X_{t}^{2}=e^{-2t}X^{2}+1-e^{-2t}
,

\frac{d}{dt}|X_{t}|=\frac{1}{2|X_{t}|}\frac{d}{dt}X_{t}^{2}=-\frac{X_{t}^{2}-1}{|X_{t}|}.

Hence

\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=-2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(|X_{t}|-1)\frac{X_{t}^{2}-1}{|X_{t}|}\right].

Since

\frac{X_{t}^{2}-1}{|X_{t}|}=|X_{t}|-\frac{1}{|X_{t}|},

we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}=-2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(|X_{t}|-1)\left(|X_{t}|-\frac{1}{|X_{t}|}\right)\right].

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

-\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|X_{t}|-\frac{1}{|X_{t}|}\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

Since

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(|X_{t}|-\frac{1}{|X_{t}|}\right)^{2}\right]=\Psi^{*}(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}),

this yields

(3.2)

\frac{d^{+}}{dt}W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq\sqrt{\Psi^{*}(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}.

We now compute the evolution of the entropy along the same flow.
By definition,

\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}-\frac{1}{Y_{t}}\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{Y_{t}}\right)\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}\right].

Since
\frac{d}{dt}Y_{t}=-2(Y_{t}-1)
,

\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=-\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{Y_{t}}\right)(Y_{t}-1)\right].

But

\left(1-\frac{1}{Y_{t}}\right)(Y_{t}-1)=\frac{(Y_{t}-1)^{2}}{Y_{t}}=Y_{t}+\frac{1}{Y_{t}}-2.

Therefore

-\frac{d}{dt}\Gamma(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_{t}+\frac{1}{Y_{t}}-2\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{X_{t}^{2}}-2\right]=\Psi^{*}(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}).

Applying Theorem
3.9
to
\mu_{t}
and using

D^{*}(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\leq D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
, we obtain

\sqrt{\Psi^{*}(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}\leq-\frac{d}{dt}\left[D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})\arccos\!\left(\exp\!\left\{-\frac{\Gamma(\mu_{t}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})}{D^{*}(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})^{2}}\right\}\right)\right].

Combining this with (
3.2
) and integrating on
[0,+\infty)

gives (
3.1
), since
\mu_{t}\to\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}
and

\Gamma(\mathbb{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})=0
.
∎

Conclusion and outlook

In this paper, we developed a Boolean information-theoretic framework paralleling the classical and free settings, by introducing Microstates and Non-microstates Boolean Fisher informations and relating them to Boolean entropy through de Bruijn-type identities. We established Boolean analogues of the main functional inequalities, including logarithmic Sobolev, HWI, HSI, Talagrand and WSH-type inequalities, and complemented this approach with a Boolean Stein method, yielding quantitative Berry–Esseen bounds and entropic convergence rates. Taken together, these results complete the transport–entropy–information picture in the Boolean setting and highlight its analogies with the classical and free frameworks. We also note that the recent work
[
17
]
develops an information-theoretic framework for another branch of non-commutative probability, namely finite free probability.

Several natural questions remain open. Wande restricted ourselves to symmetric measures, and we postpone both the non-symmetric theory and multivariate extensions to future work. Moreover, we expect that a genuine HWI-type inequality relating the Microstates Boolean entropy
\Gamma(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
and the Microstates Boolean Fisher
\Psi(\mu|\mathbb{\mathrm{b}})
information should hold. These directions will be investigated in subsequent works.
