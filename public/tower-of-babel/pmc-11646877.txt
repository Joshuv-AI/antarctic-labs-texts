Aortic Thrombus Formation in the Mixing Zone of a Patient With Acute Myocarditis
Authors: Mikio Shiba, Takaharu Hayashi, Yuma Hamanaka, Yasuhiro Ichibori, Yoshiharu Higuchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 66-year-old man presented with chills, exertional dyspnea, and palpitations; he later developed a fever. Because of his elevated cardiac enzymes and electrocardiography and coronary angiography findings, he was diagnosed with acute myocarditis. Given his unstable hemodynamics, an intra-aortic balloon pump was inserted; however, he experienced a hemodynamic collapse due to refractory ventricular fibrillation. He was then cannulated with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and microaxial flow pump and underwent steroid pulse therapy. His lactate levels were elevated, and computed tomography imaging revealed an aortic thrombus possibly formed in the mixing zone of the venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and microaxial flow pump. Based on the antibody test results, he was diagnosed with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia; thus, heparin was replaced with argatroban. Surgical intervention restored the arterial flow. The patient was stabilized and discharged on day 47.Key Words: acute myocarditis, aortic thrombus formation, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, microaxial flow pump, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation

Case Presentation

A 66-year-old man presented with a 2-day history of chills, exertional dyspnea, and palpitations. He had developed a fever the day before hospital admission. On admission, his blood pressure was 98/63 mm Hg, heart rate was 107 beats/min, and temperature was 37.5 °C. Physical examination revealed mild peripheral coldness and lower leg edema. Electrocardiography demonstrated a QS pattern and ST-segment depression in leads V1 to V3 and V5 to V6, respectively, and cardiac enzymes levels were elevated. Coronary angiography did not reveal any significant stenosis. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 25%, and acute myocarditis was clinically diagnosed.

An intra-aortic balloon pump was inserted because of hemodynamic instability, and intravenous administration of heparin was initiated. On day 3, the patient experienced a ventricular fibrillation storm. Immediately, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was introduced, and the intra-aortic balloon pump was removed and replaced with a microaxial flow pump (Impella CP, Abiomed); both devices were necessary because of the patient’s severely impaired cardiac function. Given the limited treatment options, a 3-day methylprednisolone (1,000 mg/d) course was initiated based on its reported efficacy in myocarditis. By day 5, blood gas analysis revealed a sudden elevation of lactate levels to 7.01 mmol/L (normal range, 0.50-1.99 mmol/L). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed an aortic thrombus that was dislodged from the renal artery to the right external iliac artery (Figure 1A). Aortography confirmed the presence of occlusions in the right renal artery and both common iliac arteries (Figures 1B and 1C, Videos 1 and 2). After consulting with a cardiovascular surgeon, we decided to salvage the limb by performing bypass surgery. Axillo-bifemoral artery bypass surgery restored the blood flow to the common femoral and right internal iliac arteries (Figure 1D, Video 3). The patient experienced a rapid decline in platelet count from day 5. We suspected that the onset of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and blood flow collision by the mechanical circulatory devices caused thrombus formation. The VA-ECMO was weaned to avoid nonphysiological collision of blood flow, which contributed to thrombus formation, and the microaxial flow pump was upgraded (from Impella CP to Impella 5.5 [Abiomed]) for more robust left ventricular support (Figure 1E).

Mixing Zone of the Microaxial Flow Pump and VA-ECMO

(A) A mixing zone forms at the point where the microaxial flow pump and venoarterial-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) flows converge, and thrombotic occlusion is observed at the same site on contrast-enhanced computed tomography. Images captured when VA-ECMO is turned off (B) and on (C). When turned on, a to and fro phenomenon is observed, where flows converge and correspond to the radiolucent thrombus formation site. (D) Postoperative angiographic image. Blood flow to the lower limbs secured via an axillo-bifemoral artery bypass. (E) Schematic illustrations before and after surgery.

Heparin was subsequently replaced with argatroban after suspecting HIT. After 1 week, the biochemical test results for the HIT antibody were positive. Other thrombocytopenia-associated diseases were ruled out through examination. Accordingly, hemodynamic stability was achieved, and the microaxial flow pump was removed on day 14. The patient was discharged on day 47.

The diagnosis of myocarditis was not definitive because a myocardial biopsy could not be performed. However, we confirmed this diagnosis clinically based on cardiac function, which was depressed with myocardial impairment at admission and improved over the course of treatment. Circulatory support with VA-ECMO and microaxial flow pump may trigger nonphysiological blood flow stagnation.1 The displacement stress created when blood collides because of the conflicting flow of mechanical circulatory support devices activates platelets, possibly forming a blood clot. In addition, the onset of HIT may have contributed to thrombus formation in the present case, leading to critical thrombosis. HIT should be suspected in cases of assisted circulation with decreased platelet counts and abnormally elevated D-dimer levels. As a precaution, administration of heparin must be immediately suspended and changed to argatroban to prevent HIT-related thrombosis.
