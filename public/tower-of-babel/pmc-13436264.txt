CD49a expression defines a metabolically robust, cytokine‐biased liver NK cell subset in rhesus macaques during lentivirus infection
Authors: Andrew Hudson, Melissa Xin Yu Wong, Michael Verdolin, R Keith Reeves, Cordelia Manickam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver‐resident natural killer (LrNK) cells are critical regulators of hepatic immune homeostasis and demonstrate antiviral activity, yet their role in lentivirus infections remains poorly defined. Using a rhesus macaque (RM) model of Simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) and Simian‐human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV) infections, we investigated the phenotypes, metabolic profiles and functional responses of hepatic NK cells across naïve, acutely SIV‐infected and chronically SHIV‐infected RM. We identified liver‐resident (Lr) NK cells as CD3−CD159a/c+CD49a+, whereas CD3−CD159a/c+CD49a− NK cells include circulating NK cell subsets in the liver. Across infection states, CD49a+ LrNK cells exhibited elevated expression of activation markers, including NKG2D, NKp30, NKp46 and CD69, as well as increased expression of metabolic markers, including CD98, Glut1 and MitoTracker. In contrast, Ki‐67 expression was reduced in CD49a+ LrNK cells compared with their CD49a− counterparts. Unsupervised clustering revealed that CD49a+ LrNK cells were also marked by high CD69 expression. While total NK cell cytokine secretion was elevated in acute SIV‐infected animals, CD49a+ LrNK cells exhibited robust polyfunctional cytokine responses and reduced expression of the cytotoxic marker CD107a upon mitogen stimulation in comparison with CD49a− LrNK cells regardless of infection status. These findings reveal distinct metabolic and functional specialization of hepatic NK cell subsets, underscoring the unique phenotype and functions of CD49a+ LrNK cells in RM, which may offer novel insights and therapeutic opportunities for treating liver inflammation in HIV infection and other chronic liver diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Natural killer (NK) cells are a critical component of antiviral and antitumor immunity. In the liver, up to 50% of lymphocytes are NK cells, the highest proportion in any tissue in the human body. 1 However, the hepatic NK cell population is heterogeneous and comprises both circulating NK cells that have trafficked to the liver and tissue‐resident NK cells, which are largely delineated by CD49a expression. 2 Liver‐resident NK (LrNK) cells are typically long‐lived with distinct developmental pathways and phenotypes compared with circulating NK cells. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Indeed, the liver has a unique immune environment, which includes a mix of tolerogenic and inflammatory responses 7 and serves as a preferential site for multiple NK cell biologic niches, including adaptive, antigen‐specific and memory‐like NK cell subsets that provide different forms of immunosurveillance and defense against HIV, CMV, HCV and other viral infections. 8 , 9 In addition, hepatic NK cells play a critical role in tissue remodeling and regeneration, and the liver is a secondary site of NK cell education and development. 10 , 11 In addition, LrNK cells are critical for hepatic homeostasis through their interactions with hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). Due likely, in part, to their heterogeneity, the full scope of LrNK cell modulation of health and disease remains undefined.

HIV infection remains a global health burden, and 30–50% of people living with HIV (PWH) develop liver complications ranging from steatosis, fibrosis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Liver disease in PWH accounts for the second leading cause of non‐AIDS mortality and drives higher healthcare costs and hospitalizations. 12 , 13 , 14 While antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved survival, it may also contribute to hepatotoxicity, metabolic dysfunction or accelerated liver injury. However, intrahepatic immune responses during HIV infection remain incompletely understood, as HIV research has largely focused on mucosal sites of infection, viral reservoirs and peripheral immunity. A deeper understanding of liver‐specific immune populations is therefore critical to defining mechanisms of hepatic inflammation and tissue injury in HIV.

To investigate hepatic NK cell biology in the context of lentiviral infection, we utilized a rhesus macaque (RM) model, a critical preclinical model with anatomical, physiological and immunological similarities to humans and that allows access to tissue compartments that are otherwise challenging to obtain from humans. Previous studies have shown that 5–30% of NK cells in the liver are CD49a+ in RM and indicate a tissue‐resident subset. 2 , 5 , 6 These CD49a+ LrNK cells exhibit unique phenotypic and functional properties compared with CD49a− cells. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 While NK cell effector functions are tightly linked to metabolic state, particularly mitochondrial fitness and nutrient‐sensing pathways, 15 , 16 the metabolic profiles of LrNK cells, including mitochondrial parameters and nutrient receptors, remain incompletely understood. Therefore, to define the metabolic properties and functional specialization of LrNK cells during lentiviral infection, we performed multiparametric flow cytometry to assess proliferation, metabolic fitness and effector function of CD49a+ LrNK cells. Using liver mononuclear cells (LMNC) from naïve, acute SIV‐infected and chronic SHIV‐infected RM, we demonstrate in this study that CD49a+ LrNK cells exhibit high metabolic activity irrespective of infection, an elevated proinflammatory cytokine profile and decreased cytotoxicity compared with CD49a− NK cells, altogether suggesting a metabolically robust, polyfunctional tissue‐resident population that is potentially important effectors in hepatic immune regulation.

RESULTS

CD49a+ Lr NK cells exhibit a unique phenotype and metabolic profile

We analyzed LMNC from naïve, acutely SIV‐infected and chronically SHIV‐infected RM using multiparametric flow cytometry to assess phenotypic and metabolic markers. NK cells were defined as live CD14‐CD20‐CD3‐CD159a/c+ cells as in the representative flow gating strategy (Figure 1a). Our initial analysis of liver NK cells showed that there were no significant differences in median total NK cell frequency, CD49a+ NK cell frequency and major phenotypic markers across naive, acute SIV and chronic SHIV groups (Supplementary Figure 1). To focus on shared features of LrNK cell biology, we stratified bulk liver NK cells as CD49a+ LrNK cells and CD49a− hepatic NK cells and analyzed all samples collectively regardless of infection. Interestingly, the phenotypes of CD49a+ and CD49a− cells were very distinct and strongly bifurcated. While CD49a‐ hepatic NK cells were enriched for the CD16‐CD56‐ (double negative, DN) subset (Figure 1b), their CD49a+ counterparts had higher frequencies of CD16+CD56‐, CD16‐CD56+ and CD16+CD56+ subsets (Figure 1c–e). In addition, CD49a+ NK cells exhibited higher expression of activation receptors, including CD69, NKG2D, NKp30 and NKp46, than CD49a− NK cells, though the proliferation marker Ki‐67 displayed higher expression in the CD49a− population(Figure 1f–j). This suggests that CD49a+ LrNK cells express a repertoire of activation receptors, consistent with what has been described for mature, educated and adaptive NK cell subsets 17 , 18 , 19 and may help regulate both immune defense and liver homeostasis.

Given the distinct phenotype of CD49a+ LrNK regardless of infection status, we further assessed the expression of metabolic indicators, specifically Glut1, CD98 and MitoTracker Red. Glut‐1 is critical for NK cell function and corresponds to NK cell activation, 16 , 20 while CD98 plays a vital role in NK cell metabolic remodeling by transporting amino acids that trigger MTORC1, a master regulator of cell growth and metabolism. 21 , 22 MTORC1 signaling is implicated in a wide range of intracellular changes in NK cells, such as the promotion of glycolysis and mitochondrial biogenesis. 22 Both Glut1 and CD98 were upregulated in RM CD49a+ NK cells compared with the CD49a− population (Figure 1k, l). In addition, we employed MitoTracker Red, a fluorescent dye that binds and accumulates in functional mitochondria. MitoTracker staining was enriched in CD49a+ cells compared with CD49a− cells (Figure 1m), demonstrating mitochondrial fitness. Altogether, CD49a+ LrNK cells are suggestive of a more robust metabolic profile than CD49a−hepatic NK cells.

CD69 expression delineates an activated CD49a+ LrNK profile

As a next step, we performed unsupervised dimensionality reduction to visualize liver NK cells and identify clusters with differential phenotypic, tissue‐resident and metabolic markers. We identified discrete regions enriched for CD49a expression that spatially co‐localized with high CD69 expression (Figure 2a), and both CD49a and CD69‐expressing neighborhoods also co‐expressed high levels of Glut1, CD98 and MitoTracker dye (Supplementary Figure 2). CD69 is both a marker of activation and tissue retention, 23 , 24 and therefore, CD69‐based stratification can provide higher resolution for discrimination of phenotypically distinct liver NK cell subsets. Thus, we stratified CD49a+ and CD49a− hepatic NK cells by CD69 expression to identify heterogeneity within the liver NK cell subsets. Our analyses indicated that CD49a+CD69+NK cells showed the highest expression of the activating receptors NKG2D, NKp30 and NKp46 (Figure 2b–d) among the three subsets. Interestingly, the metabolic markers CD98, Glut1 and MitoTracker were significantly elevated in CD49a+ vs CD49a− cells but generally did not differ between CD69a+ and CD69a− subsets. Altogether, these data indicate that CD69 expression delineates subsets with augmented activation receptor profile in hepatic NK cells, with CD49a+CD69+ LrNK cells displaying enrichment of both activating and metabolic markers (Figure 2e–g).

CD49a+ LrNK cells exhibit a robust polyfunctional cytokine response upon activation

To assess how CD49a expression influences hepatic NK cell function, LMNCs from naïve, acutely SIV‐infected and chronically SHIV‐infected RM were stimulated with mitogen (phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate and ionomycin; PMA/I) to induce effector responses. NK cell degranulation was assessed by CD107a expression, and cytokine secretion was evaluated as interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), macrophage inflammatory protein‐ beta (MIP‐1β) and tumour necrosis factor‐ alpha (TNF‐α) expression by ICS assay. Although liver NK cell frequency and phenotype were not affected by infection (Supplementary Figure 1), their functions were significantly altered by infection status. NK cells from acute SIV‐infected RM exhibited greater expression of IFN‐γ and MIP‐1β compared with NK cells from naïve RM upon PMA/I stimulation (Figure 3a). These findings suggest that lentiviral infection, particularly in the acute phase, primes liver NK cells for heightened effector responses, likely reflecting systemic immune activation and inflammatory conditioning in the liver during HIV infection.

We next examined the functional characteristics of CD49a+ LrNK cell responses across all infection states by comparing them with those of their CD49a− NK cell counterparts. Interestingly, the functional profile of CD49a+ LrNK cells was characterized by reduced CD107a and elevated IFN‐γ, MIP‐1β and TNF‐α expression compared with CD49a− NK cells (Figure 3a). This pattern indicates that CD49a+ LrNK cells are preferentially polarized toward cytokine secretion rather than cytotoxic effector function upon PMA/I stimulation. This functional profile of CD49a+ LrNK cells is consistent with prior reports in humans and NHP, 2 , 4 , 5 , 25 , 26 suggesting that CD49a+ LrNK cells are specialized for robust cytokine production and immunoregulatory activity rather than direct cytotoxicity.

To further define the functional complexity of hepatic NK cells, we performed polyfunctionality analysis using the SPICE platform. Permutation testing revealed a significant difference in overall polyfunctional profiles between CD49a+ and CD49a− hepatic NK cell populations post stimulation (Figure 3b). The CD49a+ LrNK cell subset was enriched for populations simultaneously expressing multiple cytokines (IFN‐γ, MIP‐1β and TNF‐α) while lacking CD107a expression, confirming a strongly cytokine‐biased functional phenotype, while CD49a− hepatic NK cells showed broader distribution of functional combinations, including elevated degranulating subsets. CD49a expression potentially corresponds to robust metabolism and cytokine response, on aggregate across all three defined CD56/CD16 subsets, with the DN population being the greatest driver of differences in cytokine secretion between CD49a+ and CD49a− populations (Supplementary Figure 3). Overall, our findings suggest that the CD49a+ LrNK cell subset is characterized by reduced cytotoxicity and elevated cytokine polyfunctionality, thus indicating that the LrNK cells are primarily cytokine‐secreting immunoregulatory effectors within the hepatic immune environment.

DISCUSSION

Tissue‐resident NK cells, including those in the liver, play critical roles in immune defense and in maintaining tissue homeostasis. However, difficulties in accessing human tissue samples have limited their study. LrNK cells are crucial effectors of liver immunology due to their multifaceted roles, which include unique memory and memory‐like functions against pathogens and their homeostatic interactions with hepatocytes, HSCs and other liver immune cells. 1 , 27 , 28 In mice, CD49a is the primary marker of liver residency, whereas conventional NK cells are CD49a−; these two subsets are known to have distinct origins, development, phenotypes and immune functions. 6 , 29 Human LrNK cell identification is more controversial, with some defining LrNK cells as CD49a+, CD56bright and CD49e− in addition to other characterizations. 3 , 30 Previously, we identified CD49a+CD49e− hepatic NK cells in an NHP model. 5 In this study, we expand current knowledge of RM LrNK cells by identifying phenotypic and metabolic features that are conserved across naïve, acute SIV and chronic SHIV infection, highlighting core characteristics of the LrNK cell compartment independent of infection status. We observed that RM CD49a+ LrNK cells demonstrate high expression of activating receptors, enhanced metabolism and a distinct functional profile skewed toward high cytokine secretion.

Although human LrNK cells nearly all belong to the CD56brightCD16‐ subset, 2 we have found that CD49a+ NK cells in the RM liver represent a heterogeneous population comprising cell subsets that are CD56+CD16−, CD56−CD16+ and CD56−CD16− DN. Based on receptor expression and transcriptomics, the CD56+CD16− NK cells in RM correspond to CD56brightCD16− NK cells in humans, which are characterized by high cytokine production and low degranulation and associated with long‐term tissue residency, while CD56− CD16+ cells correspond to the mature, shorter‐lived, highly cytotoxic CD56dim CD16+ subset abundant in the periphery. 31 , 32 The DN NK cell population in RM has been reported at high levels in liver, spleen and lymph nodes in normal animals, elevated in SIV‐infected animals but overall is poorly understood. 33 , 34 , 35 Notably, in a RM study using barcode clonal tracking of tissue immune cells, highly expanded peripheral clones in the DN NK cell subset gave rise to DN and CD16+ NK cells in tissue sites, suggesting that the DN subset may represent an intermediate subset in the developmental pathway to tissue‐resident CD16+ NK cells. 34 Although composed of populations representing different stages of NK cell development, RM CD49a+ LrNK cells exhibited surprisingly consistent metabolic and functional characteristics.

For effective immune cell effector function, cellular access to nutrients and their associated metabolic pathways is critical. In this study, we demonstrate that CD49a+ LrNK cells display a more robust metabolic profile than CD49a− cells, with higher expression of GLUT1 and CD98. These transporters provide substrates for glycolysis and aminolysis, respectively, with the amino acids taken up by CD98 also inducing critical activating signals through MTOR. NK cells are known to rely on the MTOR pathway for metabolic reprogramming upon IL‐15 activation, which in turn leads to elevated glycolysis necessary for secretion of effector molecules, including IFN‐γ and granzyme B. 22 , 36 Furthermore, adaptive NK cell subsets including cytokine‐induced memory‐like NK cells exhibit elevated CD98, GLUT1, glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial membrane potential, 37 , 38 , 39 representing cells primed for robust activity and resistance to metabolic stress. NK cells expressing CD49a exhibited mitochondrial fitness, which is strongly linked to NK cell function/exhaustion in the context of HIV and the cancer microenvironment. 40 , 41 Our findings provide initial evidence that CD49a+ LrNK cells are metabolically robust in an NHP model and align with other research that indicates that both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are vital metabolic axes for cytokine‐secreting NK cells. 20 , 42 This metabolic robustness could be particularly important for tissue‐resident populations in the liver due to their chronic exposure to inflammatory cues and antigenic stimulation and may reflect their functional specialization to adaptive and antigen‐specific subsets which are highly enriched in the liver.

RM CD49a+ LrNK cells also expressed elevated levels of activating receptors, with NKp46, NKp30 and NKG2D more prevalent on CD49a+ cells than on their CD49a− counterparts. NKp46 and NKG2D, in particular, are critical for the homeostatic role of LrNK cells, as these molecules activate NK cells and direct their killing of senescent and pro‐fibrotic HSCs to suppress inflammation. 43 The activating receptor CD69, which also serves as a marker of tissue residency, 44 correlates with higher expression of NKp46, NKp30 and NKG2D. CD49a+CD69+ NK cells possessed the highest expression of these markers, suggesting that they may represent a population potentially primed for rapid and localized immune surveillance. Indeed, this subset may play critical roles in immune control and the modulation of inflammatory outcomes, thereby contributing toward maintenance of tissue homeostasis and favoring anti‐fibrotic functions in the hepatic microenvironment.

Our finding that RM CD49a+ LrNK cells display higher cytokine secretion and decreased degranulation in response to PMA+ ionomycin agrees with what is known for human CD49a+ resident NK cells. 2 , 4 , 25 , 45 LrNK cells have been described as less cytotoxic against malignant cells 46 and associated with hepatocellular carcinoma progression. 47 Furthermore, CD49a deficiency and CD49a antibody blockade enhanced antitumor efficacy in mouse models. 48 , 49 , 50 In the context of viral infections, multiple studies 51 , 52 , 53 suggest that LrNK cells have important roles in antiviral immunity. CD49a+CD16− LrNK cells isolated from liver samples of hepatitis A and/or hepatitis B vaccinated human individuals demonstrated antigen‐specific recall responses by recognizing and lysing autologous target cells pulsed with hepatitis A or hepatitis B antigens and was associated with hyperresponsiveness to IL‐12/IL‐15 stimulation and high chromatin accessibility of effector genes. 53 In a human tonsillar tissue model, resident NK cells (CD49a+CD69+CD103+NKG2C+) demonstrated in vitro cytotoxic activity against HIV‐infected CD4+ cells and was associated with reduced viral burden. 44 In HCMV seropositive individuals, adaptive LrNK cells were observed to be protective against multiple viruses, including HCMV variants and alphaherpesvirus HSV‐1 in vitro and were associated with HCMV viral control and better outcomes post‐transplant. 54 Altogether, this suggests that RM CD49a+ LrNK cells could play an antiviral role, with heightened cytokine responsiveness but restrained cytotoxicity, and are positioned to contribute to immune surveillance and immune defense while limiting excessive tissue damage. More studies are needed to fully characterize the definitive role of CD49a+ LrNK cell and its functional plasticity in different inflammatory conditions, including the immunosuppressive and pro‐fibrotic liver microenvironment mediated by TGFb as well as in the setting of recurrent viral hepatitis following liver transplantation where LrNK cells may exhibit exhaustion or overactivation.

The mechanisms of LrNK cells development and maintenance are not completely understood, though it is known that their replenishment is promoted locally in the liver by IFN‐γ‐dependent self‐amplified feedback loops and is potentially regulated through the gut‐liver axis and microbiota interactions. 55 , 56 In a maternal antibiotic treatment mouse model, early‐life antibiotic treatment resulted in defective LrNK cell maturation in adulthood through impaired butyrate/IL‐18 signaling while conventional NK cells in the liver and spleen remained unaffected, though dietary supplementation of Clostridium butyricum restored LrNK cell maturation and functions. 56 These studies indicate a unique sensitivity of the LrNK cell subset to metabolic and inflammatory cues in the liver microenvironment. In the context of chronic viral infections, including HIV, where microbial translocation, metabolic dysregulation, overt inflammation and viral reservoirs are prominent features, tissue specific metabolic programming and resultant modulation of resident NK cell populations could further exacerbate liver inflammation. However, this study is limited by the small sample size of the infected cohorts, which may be insufficient to identify major impacts of infection on LrNK cells. The lack of specific antibodies that distinguish between RM NKG2A and NKG2C, which are important inhibitory and adaptive NK cell markers, limits our ability to assess memory‐like populations. Despite these constraints, RM represents a far more faithful model to human anatomy and immunology than current murine or organoid systems.

This study has furthered our understanding of LrNK cells in this nonhuman primate model, providing evidence of metabolic fitness and cytokine secretion in a heterogeneous CD49a+ LrNK cell population. Future studies dissecting how environmental cues shape LrNK cell function will be critical for defining their contributions to liver pathogenesis and metabolic dysfunctions, and their potential as therapeutic targets. As the clinical relevance of tissue‐resident NK cells becomes increasingly apparent, our study findings provide a foundation to inform future research and therapeutic strategies in liver disease associated with infections, cancer, transplant and autoimmunity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

Indian‐origin RM (Macaca mulatta) were housed at Biomere Inc (Worcester, MA,USA), New Iberia Research Center (NIRC, New Iberia, LA, USA), or the New England Primate Research Center (Southborough, MA,USA) in accordance with the rules and conditions of the Committee on the Care and Use of Laboratory Animal Resources. All procedures were approved by the local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Animals were fed a standard monkey chow diet and were supplemented daily with fruit and vegetables and water ad libitum. Social enrichment was delivered and overseen by veterinary staff, and animal health was monitored daily. Animals showing signs of weight loss, disease or distress were clinically evaluated and provided dietary supplementation, analgesics and/or therapeutics as necessary. Animals were euthanized by an overdose of pentobarbital, and tissues were collected following necropsy.

Animal samples

Liver samples collected from animal necropsies were rinsed with RPMI media containing 5% fetal bovine serum to minimize peripheral blood contamination and processed using a previously optimized protocol 5 to obtain LMNC, which were counted and frozen in liquid nitrogen for later use. The cryopreserved LMNC aliquots from experimentally naïve animals (n = 4), animals acutely infected with SIVmac239X for 7–14 days (n = 4) and animals chronically infected with SHIVSF162P3 or SHIV1157(QNE)Y173H (n = 8) were thawed and counted for use in all assays as previously described. 5 , 57 , 58

Flow cytometric staining of LMNC

After thawing, LMNC were rested for 2 h at 37°C prior to flow cytometric staining and were then split into a phenotypic panel and a metabolic panel, each containing 2 million cells. All antibodies were sourced from BD Biosciences, Woburn, MA, USA, unless specified. For the phenotypic panel, LMNC were stained with the surface staining master mix which included CD14‐BUV395 (clone MΦP9), CD16‐BUV496 (3G8), CD49a‐BUV563 (SR84), CD45‐BUV615 (D058‐1283), CD3‐BUV737 (SP34‐2), CD20‐BUV805 (2H7), CD69‐BV421 (FN50), CD4‐BV480 (L200), CD56‐BV605 (NCAM16.2), CD337‐BV711 (p30‐15), Ki‐67‐FITC (B56), CD195‐BB700 (3A9), CD314‐PE/Dazzle™ 594 (1D11, Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), CD335‐PC5 (BAB281, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), CD159a/c‐PE‐Cy7 (Z199, Beckman Coulter), CD8‐AF700 (RPA‐T8). For the metabolic panel, the staining master mix included CD14‐BUV396 (MΦP9), CD20‐BUV396 (2H7), CD3‐BUV396 (SP34‐2), CD16‐BUV496 (3G8), CD49a‐BUV615 (SR84), CD45‐BUV615 (D058‐1283), CD69‐BV421 (FN50), CD56‐BV605 (NCAM16.2), CD98‐BV786 (UM7F8), MitoSOX Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), CD195‐BB700 (3A9), CD89‐PE (A59), MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Thermo Fisher Scientific), CD159a/c‐PC7 (Z199, Beckman Coulter) and GLUT1‐AF647 (202915). For all panels, LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Near‐IR Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) staining was done concurrently with the master mix to identify live cells. After staining, the cells were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde, acquired and recorded using BD FACSymphony, and data analyzed using the FlowJo™ software (v10).

Intracellular cytokine staining assay

Thawed LMNC were cultured with or without 1X cell stimulation mix (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA), which contains PMA and Ionomycin in the presence of Brefeldin A and Monensin. Degranulation marker CD107a‐BV605 (H4A3; BioLegend) was added at the start of incubation. After 6 hours of incubation, the cells were subjected to flow cytometric staining. Briefly, cells were stained with an NIR live/dead dye and a surface marker master mix (CD3, CD20, CD14, CD16, CD49a, CD45, CD56, CD98, CD159a, Glut1, Mitotracker) for 20 minutes and then washed. The cells were then permeabilized using Perm A solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min and washed. Following this, the cells were incubated with a master mix containing IFN‐γ‐FITC (B27), MIP‐1β‐BV421 (D21‐1351) and TNF‐α AF700 (Mab11) with Perm B (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min. The cells were washed, fixed with 2% PFA and analyzed on the BD FACSymphony flow cytometer followed by the FlowJo™ software.

Statistical methods

For UMAP analysis, metabolic flow cytometry data were compiled from naïve (n = 4), acute SIV (n = 4) and chronic SHIV (n = 7) infected animals. Data were down‐sampled in FlowJo™ to 9000 events per sample and pooled by infection group using the concatenate function with compensation parameters retained for each sample. UMAP dimensionality reduction was performed on the NK cell population with the FlowJo™ UMAP plugin using Euclidean distance, 35 nearest neighbors and a minimum distance of 0.2. 59 Infection context was preserved by sample ID labeling prior to embedding, and heatmaps were generated using FlowJo™ Cluster Explorer with intensity scaled to the global pooled expression range.

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (v10.4.2). Unless otherwise specified, all data are presented as matched plots or medians. For comparisons between CD49a+ and CD49a− NK cell subsets, Student's t‐test and two‐way ANOVA/mixed‐effects model with multiple comparison tests were used, and for comparison between infection groups, one‐way ANOVA was used. Statistical significance was defined as having a *P‐ or adjusted P‐value <0.05 **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.
