Pitch-level college baseball data captured by optical tracking technology
Authors: N. David Pifer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Since gaining popularity via the Moneyball story near the turn of the century, data analytics has become a common feature of high-level baseball. In recent years, optical tracking technologies have allowed data to move beyond basic box-score measures of performance (e.g., strikeouts and batting averages) to high-detail measures of ball movement following a pitch (e.g., spin rates, release speeds, and ending locations) or hit (e.g., exit velocity and launch angle). Such information has provided analysts with new ways to measure the game and communicate complex results via helpful visualizations. The data described in this article were recorded by an optical tracking unit stationed on a collegiate baseball field over a series of 21 exhibition matches. The technology was monitored by a trained individual who confirmed, cleaned, and compiled the data after every game. Each observation (n = 3,344) corresponds to a pitch that was thrown during a game, and the associated columns provide a variety of descriptive information related to the pitch and its eventual outcome. Data of this detail at the collegiate level of play can be difficult to acquire, and the high level of granularity allows for a variety of analyses and visualizations to be conducted for baseball and non-baseball purposes.

Value of the Data

•Granularity. The data in this sample were captured using advanced optical tracking technologies, meaning the level of detail surpasses the publicly available metrics in traditional box scores and play-by-play records. Information related to the amount and orientation of spin on the ball, the speed and trajectory of the ball while in motion, as well as locational coordinates of the ball at various points following a pitch or hit were captured for each observation. Such detail could allow for more thorough analyses of the processes and actions underlying higher-level outcomes to be conducted.•Visualization & Education. Data visualizations constitute an important part of the sports analytics process at the academic and practical levels [4]. Current and aspiring analysts can use the provided data to create data visualizations that assist baseball managers and other sports personnel in their efforts to communicate complex data simplistically (see Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Given the ability of sports to break through educational barriers in ways that other subject material cannot [5], the data might also be useful as an instructional tool for data science and data visualization courses.•Rarity. Publicly available tracking data outside the professional ranks of sport are often difficult to acquire. In making this sample available to the public, researchers and analysts will be able to analyze data from the frequently overlooked realm of college baseball.•Efficiency. While some statistical programs have methods for acquiring tracking data from professional baseball, such programs require a knowledge of/expertise in that program and its associated functions to obtain the data [6]. By making this dataset accessible in common formats through a public repository, users of all skill levels can acquire and analyze the data more quickly and efficiently using their program of choice.•Synchronization. Baseball organizations, including Major League Baseball (MLB) teams, use technologies (e.g., Statcast) similar to Trackman to collect tracking data for their players and teams. However, subtle nuances across systems mean similar metrics can be recorded on different scales or in different formats. By making a sizable sample of Trackman data available to the public, we aim to assist analysts and researchers in their efforts to better synchronize data collection processes and analyses across platforms.•Generalizability. Prior studies have used baseball tracking data to explore biomechanical, statistical, and economic phenomena [[8], [9], [10]], so this dataset offers researchers an opportunity to experiment with, and better understand, the utility of sports tracking data in different contexts.

Background

While the primary motivation for compiling this dataset centered on providing access to detailed, ball-tracking data from an overlooked realm of competitive baseball, it is important to recognize areas outside of sports analytics (i.e., the utilization of sports data for purposes of generating competitive advantages) that can benefit from granular sports data. Exercise science and economics are two areas that frequently take advantage of tracking data, with prior studies using similar data to fine-tune motion capture technologies and machine learning algorithms [8], monitor employee shirking [9], and assess biomechanical relationships between pitching kinematics and pitch location consistency [10]. These uses give the data generalizable merit beyond its role as a toolbox for analyzing and visualizing baseball performance.

Data Description

Within the linked repository [[1], [2]], the file containing the data is labeled Track_Combo.csv. There is also an alternative version stored in a text format, labeled Track_Combo.txt. The data in both files are arranged in a wide format where each row corresponds to a recorded pitch observation and each column provides information related to that pitch, including the ball-tracking measures and information associated with possible outcomes (e.g., hits, strikes, etc.).

The included observations are derived from a series of college baseball scrimmages that took place at a United States university baseball stadium post-2020. Data associated with 3,344 pitches from 21 distinct scrimmages are included. The PitcherId, BatterId, and CatcherId columns allow for potential filtering or grouping of ball-tracking information by player. The PitcherTeam and BatterTeam columns allow users to distinguish between the involved teams. Note that the length, in innings (Inning), of these exhibitions frequently varies from the traditional 9 innings played in college baseball games.

Across the dataset, some columns contain situational/informational data such as the number of outs, strikes, and balls at the time of the pitch, anonymous player and team IDs, and what inning the pitch was thrown in. Experimental design, materials and methods

The data were acquired by a TrackMan V3 Game Tracking unit installed in the stadium where the 21 exhibition games were played. The unit, which is 17.7 inches wide, 17.7 tall, and 3.3 inches deep [12], offers full-field in-stadium coverage, including full ball-flight tracking [3]. It was subtly positioned in a way that did not interfere with gameplay, and participating athletes were accustomed to being monitored by such devices. This technology is currently used in more than 500 baseball stadiums worldwide, making it the data provider of choice for many teams [3]. The technology's accuracy has been validated and fine-tuned over the more than 160,000 games it has tracked [13]. Many of the actions measured in this dataset were only capturable by the optical tracking technology, providing users with a level of detail that could not have been observed through the naked eye.

At a technical level, the V3 uses dual (short-range and long-range) radar tracking systems enhanced by synchronized high-speed optics and a two-piece, 4k camera to capture ball movement metrics at release (from the pitcher's hand), during flight, and as the ball crosses the strike zone around home plate. If hit, additional metrics such as the exit speed, launch angle, and distance of the ball are also estimated and recorded. Information related to the catcher's actions around home plate is also recorded when applicable. Data are then relayed to an operator's interface via a 1 GB optical/ethernet connection. Full specifications for the technology can be found on TrackMan's official website [12].

This dataset also contains pitch and hit tags that were manually assigned by a trained operator. For each pitch and hit, the operator manually labeled (tagged) the type of pitch (hit) that was thrown (hit) based on their professional judgment. This led to the insertion of values in the TaggedPitchType and TaggedHitType columns; note that the values in these columns may vary from the tags assigned by the V3’s automated taggers, AutoPitchType and AutoHitType.

After the final scrimmage, the operator manually downloaded and combined the raw data from each scrimmage file into a single dataset. The player ID values were then anonymized, and columns containing values that could be used to identify the involved teams and individuals were removed. The actual game dates (Date)—while consistent across the observations from a specific game—were altered as an additional means of de-identification. The resulting dataset was then exported in comma-separated value (.csv) and text (.txt) formats. These files are now stored in the linked repository as Track_Combo.csv and Track_Combo.txt, respectively [2].

The first row of data from Track_Combo.csv can be used to illustrate how the technology recorded the raw data. During this play, the ball was thrown on the first pitch of the game (PitchNo = 1) by the pitcher (1000036206) on team T_A to the batter (1000102964) on team T_B. Limitations

The provided data are limited in that they are only representative of exhibition matches (i.e., scrimmages) and not matches that counted toward a team's official record. As such, users are cautioned against extrapolating the data to conditions that might not hold in official matches. In addition, the sample size provided by optical tracking technologies over the course of an entire season far exceeds the number of observations provided here. The tagged pitch and hit type are also subject to the judgment of the trained individual overseeing the technology during data collection. Lastly, though efforts were taken to ensure the cleanliness and accuracy of the collected data, potential inaccuracies or missing observations may be present in the data due to technical errors.
