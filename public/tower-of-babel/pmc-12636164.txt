Metabolic score for visceral fat and atrial fibrillation risk: a prospective study
Authors: Chenglin Duan, Wenjing Zhang, Jingjing Shi, Yihan Yang, Shuai Shi, Yuguang Chu, Yuanhui Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF), which integrates measures of visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and demographic variables, serves as a composite indicator of metabolic health. However, the association between METS-VF and atrial fibrillation (AF) risk is unclear. This study aimed to investigate the prospective association between METS-VF and incident AF within the UK Biobank cohort.MethodsA total of 400,668 participants from the UK Biobank were included in the analysis. Flexible parametric survival models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between METS-VF and AF incidence. Additional subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the results.ResultsOver a cumulative follow-up of 62,789,030 person-months, 28,764 individuals were diagnosed with AF. In multivariable-adjusted models, elevated METS-VF levels were significantly associated with a heightened risk of AF, with an adjusted HR of 1.38 (95% CI, 1.34–1.42). When stratified by METS-VF quartiles, the adjusted HRs for the second, third, and fourth quartiles were 1.08 (95% CI, 1.03–1.12), 1.14 (95% CI, 1.09–1.19), and 1.44 (95% CI, 1.37–1.50), respectively (P for trend < 0.001). The association remained stable across multiple sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsElevated METS-VF is independently linked to a greater risk of incident AF. These findings underscore the potential utility of METS-VF as a clinical marker for early identification of individuals at elevated AF risk, supporting its role in targeted prevention and personalized cardiovascular risk management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02083-z.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Flexible parametric survival model, Insulin resistance, UK Biobank, Visceral adipose tissue

Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common supraventricular arrhythmia marked by disordered atrial electrical conduction and the presence of irregular, chaotic fibrillatory activity [1]. AF is associated with multiple adverse clinical outcomes, including heart failure, ischemic stroke, cognitive decline, and higher all-cause mortality [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study, approximately 52.55 million individuals worldwide were living with AF in 2021, and both its prevalence and incidence have continued to increase [2]. Several studies suggest that the rising prevalence of metabolic disorders, including excess adiposity and abnormal glucose regulation, may significantly contribute to the global increase in AF [2–4]. Considering the significant impact of AF on public health and the central involvement of metabolic disturbances in its development, timely identification and intervention targeting these risk factors are essential for reducing the incidence of AF.

Accumulation of visceral adipose tissue and the presence of insulin resistance are widely regarded as central indicators of metabolic dysfunction. insulin resistance is commonly connected with the dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as with the accumulation of adipose tissue, particularly in the abdominal region, which are closely interrelated [5]. The American Heart Association has highlighted excessive visceral fat as an independent and clinically significant determinant of AF [6]. Evidence from experimental studies in insulin-resistant rat models indicates that impaired insulin sensitivity increases vulnerability to AF by promoting structural remodeling of the atria and disrupting calcium homeostasis [7]. Therefore, accumulation of visceral fat and impaired insulin sensitivity may represent critical metabolic drivers in the development of AF.

Although multiple parameters are available to evaluate visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, most prior studies have examined these measures in isolation, with limited efforts to integrate them into a comprehensive framework. Body fat distribution is a standard approach for estimating visceral fat, commonly assessed by waist circumference, the weight-adjusted waist index, the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and body mass index (BMI) [8, 9]. Non–insulin-based indices of insulin resistance, such as the triglyceride-glucose index, the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), and the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, are also widely applied in epidemiological studies [10–12]. The metabolic score for visceral fat (METS-VF) integrates insulin resistance indicators (METS-IR), a visceral adiposity metric (WHtR), and demographic variables including sex and age, thereby offering a broader assessment of metabolic status [13]. Therefore, simultaneous consideration of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity is essential for evaluating the contribution of metabolic burden to AF risk at the individual level.

Assessment of AF risk based solely on insulin resistance or visceral adiposity does not provide a sufficiently comprehensive measure of metabolic burden. To address this limitation, the present study investigates the association between METS-VF and incident AF in the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort. Flexible parametric survival models (FPSM) were employed, and additional analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results.

Materials and methods

Research design

The UK Biobank is a large, population-based prospective cohort designed to investigate the relationships between diverse health determinants and a wide range of diseases. From 2006 to 2010, more than 500,000 participants aged 40 to 69 years were recruited through 22 assessment centers located in England, Scotland, and Wales. At baseline, participants completed extensive assessments that included medical history, lifestyle questionnaires, and standardized physical examinations. In addition, blood and urine specimens were obtained under a standardized protocol. Detailed descriptions of the study design and data collection procedures have been published previously [14, 15]. The study was approved by the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee, which authorized the use of these data without the need for further ethical approval (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/learn-more-about-uk-biobank/about-us/ethics). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study population

The UK Biobank initially recruited 502,129 participants at baseline. To enhance data quality and ensure validity, exclusions were made for the following reasons: (i) incomplete information on waist circumference, height, BMI, glucose, triglycerides, or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (n = 75,110); (ii) documented AF prior to baseline or AF identified outside the follow-up period (n = 7,166); and (iii) individuals with outlier values for METS-VF (n = 19,185). Consequently, 400,668 participants were retained for the final analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Calculation of exposure

Sociodemographic data, anthropometric parameters, and blood samples were obtained during the baseline survey (2006–2010) through structured questionnaires (https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/rectab.cgi?id=935) and standardized protocols. Age and sex were recorded at baseline using structured questionnaires. Weight, height, and waist circumference were assessed at 22 assessment centers with the Tanita BC-418 MA body composition analyzer, the Seca 202 stadiometer, and the Wessex non-stretchable sprung tape measure, respectively. Fast plasma glucose, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were analyzed with the Beckman Coulter AU5800 platform. BMI, WHtR, METS-IR, and METS-VF were derived according to validated approaches described in prior publications [8, 11, 13].

Ascertainment of follow-up time and outcome

Follow-up duration was defined as the interval between the date of baseline assessment and the earliest occurrence of AF diagnosis, loss to follow-up, death, or the administrative censoring date (31 October 2022 for England, 31 August 2022 for Scotland, and 31 May 2022 for Wales) [16].

Incident AF was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code I48. AF diagnoses were obtained from health outcome records classified under circulatory system disorders [17]. Consistent with prior UK Biobank investigations, AF diagnoses were primarily ascertained from death registries, primary care data, and hospital admission records [17, 18].

Covariates

Covariates were grouped into three categories, informed by prior studies of AF risk factors in the UK Biobank and established clinical practices [17–19]. The first category included sociodemographic variables: sex, age, and the Townsend deprivation index, an established indicator of socioeconomic status.

The second category comprised lifestyle factors, physical examination, and laboratory biomarkers. Lifestyle variables included smoking status and alcohol consumption. Smoking status was classified according to UK national guidelines as non-smokers (never smokers) and smokers (current or former smokers) [20]. Alcohol consumption may have beneficial effects when consumed in moderation, moderate alcohol consumption was defined as 0–14 g/day for female and 0–28 g/day for male [21, 22]. Physical examination data included resting pulse rate, measured twice with the Omron HEM 7015-T automated device, and the mean of the two readings was used in the analyses. Laboratory analyses included C-reactive protein and cystatin C, which served as indicators of systemic inflammation and renal function, respectively.

The third category covered medical history, specifically hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Information on these diagnoses was obtained from death registries, primary care data, and hospital admission records in the UK Biobank. Hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus were coded as I10 and E11, respectively, according to International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were analyzed by dividing participants into quartiles (Q1–Q4) according to METS-VF levels. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Group differences were assessed using ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, depending on distribution, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Missing values were imputed with the random forest approach to minimize bias and maintain statistical efficiency.

Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to estimate the cumulative incidence of AF, with group comparisons assessed by log-rank tests. FPSM were applied to examine the effect of METS-VF on AF risk, thereby addressing limitations of the proportional hazards assumption in Cox models and restrictive distributional assumptions of traditional parametric survival approaches [23]. Three survival models were developed using FPSM to evaluate the association between METS-VF and AF. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each model. METS-VF was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable (quartiles). Model 1 was an unadjusted model. Model 2 adjusted for sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, and physical examination results. Model 3 further adjusted for C-reactive protein, cystatin C, and medical history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Trend tests were performed using the median METS-VF value within each quartile.

To evaluate robustness, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed: (I) subgroup analyses examined potential effect modification by age (< 60 or ≥ 60 years), sex (female or male), smoking (non-smokers or smokers), alcohol consumption (non-moderate or moderate), hypertension (yes or no), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (yes or no), with interactions assessed by likelihood ratio tests; (II) E-values were estimated to assess the potential influence of unmeasured confounding on the observed effects [24]; (III) participants with AF onset within the first two years of follow-up were excluded to minimize reverse causation; (IV) analyses were repeated with METS-VF categorized into quintiles, and linear trends were tested; (V) participants with missing values were excluded; (VI) participants with baseline cancer were excluded; (VII) models were further adjusted for total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol; (VIII) models were further adjusted for antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic medications; (IX) models were further adjusted for coronary artery disease, heart failure, thyroid dysfunction, and sleep apnea; (X) models were further adjusted for diet quality, physical activity, and sleep duration; and (XI) a Fine–Gray competing risk model was applied to account for mortality as a competing event.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.4.0. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline information of study participants

Baseline sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, physical examination, laboratory biomarkers, and medical history were summarized across quartiles of METS-VF (Table 1). A total of 400,668 participants were included, with a median age of 58.0 years (interquartile range, 50.0–63.0); 52.3% were female. Participants in the upper METS-VF quartiles tended to be older, more frequently male, have lower socioeconomic status, and report a higher prevalence of smoking. Higher METS-VF quartiles were also characterized by higher BMI, WHtR, METS-IR, waist circumference, height, pulse rate, glucose, triglycerides, cystatin C, and C-reactive protein, accompanied by lower levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. In addition, participants with higher METS-VF values demonstrated a greater prevalence of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

METS-VF and risk of incident AF

During 62,789,030 person-months of follow-up, 28,764 participants developed AF. Kaplan–Meier curves showed progressively higher AF incidence across METS-VF quartiles, with significant differences between groups (P for log-rank test < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Kaplan-Meier curves for METS-VF on AF risk using UK Biobank 400,668 participants. AF, atrial fibrillation; METS-VF, metabolic score for visceral fat. P value calculated with log-rank test

FPSM indicated that higher METS-VF values were significantly linked to greater AF risk, with an adjusted HR of 1.38 (95% CI, 1.34–1.42) (Table 2). In crude models, compared with the lowest quartile, HRs for the second, third, and fourth quartiles were 1.46 (95% CI, 1.40–1.52), 2.02 (95% CI, 1.94–2.10), and 3.89 (95% CI, 3.75–4.04), respectively, with a significant trend across quartiles (P for trend < 0.001) (Table 2). After adjustment for all covariates, the associations were attenuated but remained significant, with HRs of 1.08 (95% CI, 1.03–1.12), 1.14 (95% CI, 1.09–1.19), and 1.44 (95% CI, 1.37–1.50) for the second, third, and fourth quartiles, respectively (P for trend < 0.001) (Table 2).

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis indicated that METS-VF had superior ability to identify individuals at risk of AF, with an AUC of 0.65 (95% CI, 0.65–0.66) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Its predictive performance was higher than that of BMI, WHtR, and METS-IR.

Sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses consistently demonstrated a positive linear relationship between METS-VF and AF risk in all subgroups (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Interaction analyses indicated significant effect modification by age, smoking status, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus on the association between METS-VF and AF.

To further examine the robustness of these findings, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed. Excluding participants who developed AF within the first 24 months (Supplementary Table 1), categorizing METS-VF into quintiles (Supplementary Table 2), analyzing complete-case datasets without imputation (Supplementary Table 3), and excluding individuals with cancer at baseline (Supplementary Table 4) did not materially change the relationship between METS-VF and AF risk. Furthermore, additional adjustments for total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (Model I), medication use including antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic agents (Model II), history of coronary artery disease, heart failure, thyroid dysfunction, and sleep apnea (Model III), and lifestyle factors including diet quality, physical activity, and sleep duration (Model IV) did not materially alter the association (Supplementary Table 5). The calculated E-value was 2.10, with a lower 95% CI bound of 2.01, suggesting that the observed relationship between METS-VF and AF risk is unlikely to be explained by unmeasured confounding. We applied Fine-Gray model to rule out the competing risk of mortality, the result remain robust (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study from the UK Biobank, higher METS-VF levels were independently linked to greater AF risk. Subgroup analyses demonstrated effect modification by age, smoking status, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus on the relationship between METS-VF and AF. Multiple sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of the findings. These results suggest that METS-VF may serve as a practical early indicator for AF risk. Routine monitoring of METS-VF could help identify individuals at elevated AF risk and support targeted prevention strategies.

Metabolic burden is increasingly recognized as a major contributor to the rising incidence of non-communicable diseases [25, 26]. METS-VF, developed by Bello-Chavolla and colleagues, integrates obesity indices, insulin resistance measures, sex, and age, and was originally validated in a Mexican population. Their work established METS-VF as a novel and reliable metric for evaluating cardiometabolic risk [13]. Subsequent studies confirmed that METS-VF reflects elevated metabolic burden and is applicable across different populations. In the Rural Chinese Cohort Study, METS-VF predicted the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension, supporting its value as a marker of metabolic burden [27, 28]. A multi-ethnic cohort in southwestern China also demonstrated that METS-VF independently predicted hypertension risk [29]. In a retrospective study of 16,058 non-obese participants, METS-VF was linked to hyperuricemia, with stronger associations observed among female [30]. Collectively, these findings indicate that METS-VF is a reliable indicator of cardiometabolic risk and a robust predictor of metabolic burden across populations.

More recent studies have expanded the use of METS-VF to evaluate metabolic burden in relation to cardiovascular outcomes in both general and specific populations. For instance, Tan et al., using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, showed that METS-VF was associated with higher cardiovascular disease risk, including heart disease and stroke [31]. A cross-sectional study in Taiwan (n = 1,401) also demonstrated a significant relationship between METS-VF and coronary artery calcification [32]. In the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4 C) Study, METS-VF predicted cardiovascular disease risk and all-cause mortality across glucose tolerance categories and outperformed other obesity and insulin resistance indices [33]. While these studies underscored the predictive utility of METS-VF for cardiovascular disease, none specifically examined its relationship with AF, a distinct cardiovascular condition. In a Korean community study, insulin resistance assessed by the homeostasis model was associated with AF risk, mediating the relationship between abdominal obesity and AF [34]. Similarly, our study demonstrated that METS-VF, derived from insulin resistance and adiposity measures, was positively related to AF risk. This relationship persisted after extensive sensitivity analyses.

Elevated METS-VF values may contribute to AF development through several mechanisms, although the exact pathways remain incompletely defined. As a metabolic composite index, METS-VF reliably reflects visceral fat accumulation [13]. Visceral adiposity, particularly pericardial and epicardial fat, is increasingly recognized as pivotal in AF pathogenesis. Proposed mechanisms include local inflammation, oxidative stress, and autonomic imbalance. Pericardial fat secretes pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic mediators that trigger oxidative stress, alter atrial myocyte structure and electrophysiology, and facilitate fibrosis and conduction abnormalities [35, 36]. Excess pericardial fat may also disrupt sympathetic-parasympathetic balance, enhance vagal tone, and increase AF susceptibility [37]. Epicardial fat, as a metabolically active visceral fat depot, contains adipocytes, immune cells, and an extensive microvascular network. It releases mediators such as interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α, which drive atrial inflammation, remodeling, and conduction abnormalities [38, 39]. Imaging studies have shown that increased epicardial fat volume and reduced attenuation around the left atrium are closely linked to low-voltage areas, markers of atrial fibrosis [40]. Patients with recurrent AF after ablation had greater epicardial fat thickness and volume than those without recurrence, suggesting a role in AF persistence [41].

Metabolic dysfunction, particularly insulin resistance, may also contribute through altered calcium signaling. Insulin resistance increases CaMKIIδ oxidation and enhances phospholamban and RyR2 phosphorylation, resulting in calcium overload, atrial remodeling, and greater AF vulnerability [7]. Animal models of insulin resistance support these mechanisms, showing prolonged atrial calcium transients, slowed conduction, and enhanced ectopic activity [7]. In summary, METS-VF, as a composite score integrating visceral adiposity and metabolic dysfunction, may increase AF risk through structural remodeling, electrical abnormalities, calcium dysregulation, and autonomic imbalance.

Interaction analyses indicated that age, smoking, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus significantly influenced the relationship between METS-VF and AF risk. Nevertheless, the association remained statistically significant across all subgroups, underscoring the robustness of METS-VF as a risk marker in diverse populations. Notably, age is incorporated into the METS-VF algorithm, part of the observed association with AF may reflect this inherent structural coupling. In addition, the stronger effect observed among smokers, individuals with hypertension, and those with type 2 diabetes suggests that elevated metabolic burden in these groups may amplify the impact of METS-VF on AF risk. These observations are consistent with previous reports showing that METS-VF predicts the risk of both type 2 diabetes and hypertension [27, 28]. Therefore, these findings imply that under conditions of metabolic dysfunction, METS-VF may serve as an integrated indicator of visceral adiposity and metabolic disturbance, providing enhanced predictive value in high-risk subgroups.

This is the first study to evaluate the relationship between baseline METS-VF and AF using a large-scale population dataset. Strengths include the prospective design, large sample size, and standardized data collection. Additionally, the robustness of the results was confirmed through multiple sensitivity analyses. Data collected from 22 centers across the UK enhanced the generalizability of the findings. The METS-VF score, integrating obesity, insulin resistance, sex, and age, may aid in early identification of individuals at increased AF risk and guide precise risk management.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, causal inference between METS-VF and AF cannot be established. Second, although multiple confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. However, the calculated E-value suggested that their impact on the primary findings was likely minimal. Third, METS-VF was measured only at baseline, and changes during follow-up were not captured. Future studies should investigate whether longitudinal changes in METS-VF influence AF risk. Finally, as the study population was predominantly White British, generalizability to other ethnic groups should be considered with caution.

Conclusion

In this large prospective cohort from the UK Biobank, higher METS-VF values were independently linked to an increased risk of AF. The relationship remained consistent across analyses, supporting METS-VF as a reliable indicator of AF risk. As a composite index that incorporates adiposity, insulin resistance, sex, and age, METS-VF offers a comprehensive measure of metabolic health. Monitoring METS-VF may aid in the early identification of individuals at elevated AF risk and support more precise strategies for clinical risk stratification and prevention.
