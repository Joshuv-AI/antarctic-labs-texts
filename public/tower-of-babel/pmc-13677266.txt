Dynamics of ventriculo-arterial coupling and peripheral tissue perfusion during early resuscitation of septic shock
Authors: Juan Daniel Caicedo Ruiz, Gustavo García Gallardo, Jose Luis Aldana, Eduardo Kattan, Ricardo Castro, Glenn Hernandez, Michael Pinsky, Jihad Mallat, Gustavo Adolfo Ospina-Tascón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Resuscitation of septic shock relies on the assumption that optimizing macrohemodynamics will reverse tissue hypoxia and the metabolic derangements of shock [1]. However, cardiovascular dysfunction in septic shock results from a variable combination of pathological vasodilation, relative or absolute hypovolemia, microvascular flow misdistribution, and cardiac dysfunction [2,3], implying that resuscitation should be individually adjusted.

Conventional modeling of cardiovascular function emphasizes cardiac contractility and venous return as the primary determinants of stroke volume [4,5], but this approach does not capture the influence of arterial load on flow generation. Left ventriculo–arterial coupling (LVAC), the ratio between arterial elastance (Ea) and left ventricular end-systolic elastance (Ees), describes this interaction between arterial load and ventricular contractility [6], reflecting the efficiency with which mechanical energy generated by the heart is transferred to the arterial system [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Under normal conditions LVAC is below 1.0 (Ea < Ees), indicating that ventricular contractility exceeds effective arterial load. Nevertheless, during abnormal conditions such as septic shock, altered arterial load and myocardial contractility frequently increase LVAC, a state termed ventriculo–arterial uncoupling [10], in which increased myocardial oxygen consumption is not effectively converted into external mechanical work. Evolving from high to normal LVAC has been associated with more favorable outcomes in chronic heart failure [11], while high LVAC during stress echocardiography has predicted adverse outcomes [12], and LVAC status may modify the hemodynamic response to norepinephrine, fluid loading, or inotropes in septic shock [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Nevertheless, the clinical impact of assessing LVAC during resuscitation of septic shock remains unclear.

Capillary refill time (CRT) reflects the kinetics of tissue perfusion during shock resuscitation; CRT-targeted resuscitation has been associated with lower mortality and faster resolution of organ dysfunction than lactate-targeted resuscitation [17,18], and the recent ANDROMEDA-SHOCK 2 trial showed that personalized, CRT-guided resuscitation was superior to usual care for a hierarchical composite outcome of 28-day mortality, duration of vital support, and hospital length of stay [19].

Restoration of macrohemodynamics is frequently not associated with improved microcirculatory flow [20,21], and the same dissociation could occur with LVAC restoration. Since it remains unknown whether optimizing energy transmission from the left ventricle to the proximal arterial tree translates into better peripheral perfusion, we evaluated the dynamic relationship between LVAC and CRT, as a surrogate of peripheral perfusion, during the very early resuscitation of septic shock.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted in a 114-bed mixed medical–surgical intensive care unit (Fundación Valle del Lili, Cali, Colombia), over a 15-month period (October 2024 to February 2025). The study protocol was approved by the local Ethical and Biomedical Research Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (protocol No. 2023.096, approval No. 25/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized representatives. Consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted with septic shock were considered eligible. Sepsis was defined as a suspected or confirmed infection, and septic shock was defined as the need for vasopressor support to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mm Hg after an intravenous fluid bolus of 1000 mL and a serum lactate ≥2 mmol/L [19]. Eligible patients were enrolled within 4 h of meeting these criteria. Exclusion criteria included patients < 18 years old, pregnant women, active bleeding, advanced cirrhosis, irregular heart rhythm, under extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, permanent pacemaker, bundle branch block, or moderate-to-severe mitral or aortic valvular disease, those with do-not-resuscitate orders, and those with > 4 h after onset of septic shock. Of the 84 patients included in this physiological sub-study, 78 were enrolled participants of the ANDROMEDA SHOCK 2 trial (TRIAL REGISTRATION ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05057611) [19]; the remaining 6 patients were screened for ANDROMEDA-SHOCK 2 but were ultimately not enrolled in that trial, while still meeting the eligibility criteria for the present physiological study. A STROBE statement checklist for observational studies is provided in the ESM.

General management

All potentially eligible patients admitted from the emergency department or medical–surgical wards were screened by the intensive care unit (ICU) rapid response team, which was available 24 h a day, seven days a week. Patients included in this study received a personalized hemodynamic resuscitation protocol targeting normalization of CRT [19] adjusting interventions according to individualized components of cardiovascular dysfunction including fluid responsiveness status, arterial tone, and myocardial function. The protocol included the iterative evaluation of peripheral perfusion using a standardized method to assess CRT, considering a CRT > 3.0 s as abnormal. The personalized CRT-guided resuscitation algorithm was based on four fundamental pillars: (a) CRT normalization as hemodynamic resuscitation target; (b) Individual identification of hemodynamic patterns: vasoplegia, persistent hypovolemia, and cardiac dysfunction, followed by specific interventions and CRT re-assessment; (c) systematic assessment of fluid responsiveness before any fluid loading; and, (d) two acute hemodynamic tests, when CRT remained abnormal despite previous interventions: a MAP test consisting of a transitory increase in mean arterial pressure (MAP) target (in those patients with previous chronic arterial hypertension), and an inodilator test consisting of a transitory trial of low-fixed dose of dobutamine, both followed by CRT reassessment within the next hour. Fluid boluses were infused over 30 min, with the post-intervention hemodynamic and echocardiographic reassessment performed at 1 h from the start of the infusion; dobutamine was titrated at initiation, with the post-intervention reassessment likewise performed at 1 h. By design, each patient received only one type of intervention (fluid bolus or dobutamine) at a time, following this stepwise algorithm. If CRT was normalized or decreased at least 25% from the initial value, the respective intervention was maintained for at least the 6 h of the study protocol. Otherwise, previous MAP target was maintained or dobutamine was stopped. In addition, dobutamine was stopped in cases of dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction or development of threatening arrhythmias.

All patients were equipped with a central venous catheter and an arterial line (radial or femoral position) for arterial pressure monitoring. Advanced cardiac output monitoring was used at the discretion of the attending physician. Other resuscitation parameters such as central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2), venous-to-arterial carbon dioxide difference (Pv-aCO2), and lactate levels were also allowed and evaluated within the individual resuscitation context. Norepinephrine was the first-choice vasopressor, titrated to achieve MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, while vasopressin was allowed up to 0.04 IU/min to increase MAP when norepinephrine base dose was ≥0.15 µg kg−1 min−1. Norepinephrine dose was increased in those patients eligible for MAP test as previously mentioned. Meanwhile, dobutamine was used within individual context when left ventricular dysfunction was detected or during the inodilator test previously described.

Invasive mechanical ventilation support was provided following protective strategies, under sedation (midazolam or propofol) and analgesia (fentanyl or hydromorphone), and sometimes under neuromuscular paralysis according to local protocols. In other cases, respiratory support was provided using non-invasive mechanical ventilation, high-flow oxygen therapy, or conventional oxygen therapy at the discretion of the attending physician. General management of sepsis, including source control, antibiotics/antimicrobials, and additional therapies, such as glycemic control, red blood cell transfusions, stress ulcer prophylaxis, steroids, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, renal replacement therapy, and rescue therapies, followed local protocols and recognized international recommendations [22].

LVAC assessment

Quantification of LVAC requires the arterial elastance (Ea) and left ventricular end-systolic elastance (Ees). Ea was calculated as the ratio of end-systolic pressure (Pes) to stroke volume (SV), with Pes estimated as 0.9 × systolic arterial pressure [23]. Ees was derived noninvasively from transthoracic echocardiography using the single-beat method of Chen et al. [24]; a complete, step-by-step worked example of this calculation in a representative patient, including all required echocardiographic and Doppler measurements, is provided in the ESM Appendix A. LVAC was expressed as the Ea/Ees ratio. Left ventricular efficiency (LVeff), illustrated in ESM Figure S1, was calculated as SW/PVA, where PVA = SW + potential energy (PE) and PE = 0.5 x Pes^2/Ees (obtained algebraically from Pes and Ees, without an independent estimate of the ventricular volume-axis intercept, V0) [11]. All LVAC estimations were performed at baseline and 6 h, and before/after fluid bolus or inodilator interventions within this window. Patients were classified as uncoupled when LVAC > 1.36, the upper boundary of the physiological LVAC range (approximately 0.8–1.36) [6,13]; by contrast, LVAC = 1.0 marks the loading condition of maximal external stroke work rather than maximal efficiency, since efficiency is in fact maximal at Ea/Ees ratios below unity [7,8]. All echocardiographic studies were performed by two trained operators, who were not blinded to CRT or intervention status.

Other measurements

Arterial and central venous blood samples were drawn for blood gases analysis (ABL300, Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark) at baseline and 6-h after. Macro hemodynamics and respiratory parameters, vasopressor and inodilator doses (when already under inodilator therapy), and resuscitation fluid volumes were simultaneously recorded at baseline and 6-h after, and before – after specific hemodynamic interventions (i.e., fluid loading and dobutamine start). Organ dysfunction was assessed at baseline, 24–, 48–, and 72–h after inclusion using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score. Vasopressor doses and other co-interventions (sedation, ventilator settings, temperature) remained stable at the time of the fluid-loading or dobutamine intervention itself.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as median [IQR] and categorical variables as counts (%). Sample size calculation is described in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

Associations between percentage changes in LVAC and CRT from baseline to 6 h were assessed using Spearman's rho and the coefficient of determination (R2). Variations in hemodynamic, echocardiographic, stroke-work, LVeff, and CRT variables among the four LVAC evolution groups were compared using ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis testing, with post-hoc comparisons by Tukey's test or Dunn's test (Holm-corrected), respectively. The association between LVAC status and CRT response at 6 h (LVAC dichotomized at 1.36; CRT responder defined as CRT < = 3 s and/or > = 25% relative decrease from baseline) was assessed using Fisher's exact test, and a multivariable logistic regression model – with covariates selected a priori for physiological relevance (baseline CRT, arterial lactate, and norepinephrine-equivalent vasopressor dose), calibration assessed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and discrimination by the AUC – was used to explore independent predictors of CRT response. Robustness and temporal stability of the LVAC-CRT relationship were further examined through five complementary ESM analyses: diagnostic concordance (Cohen's κ), within-group dispersion of Δ%CRT, an outlier-sensitivity regression, extension of the concordance analysis to 24 h, and a parallel analysis using lactate clearance.

Effects of fluid loading and dobutamine on CRT, SV, SW, LVeff, and MAP were analyzed with paired Wilcoxon tests stratified by pre-intervention LVAC status. As a sensitivity analysis, the primary LVAC-trajectory-versus-CRT comparison was repeated using two alternative LVAC classification schemes (cutoff of 1.0; a physiological “coupled” band of 0.80–1.36), with Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn's post-hoc testing (Bonferroni-corrected). Analyses used R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria); a two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 84 patients with early septic shock were included. Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics according to LVAC status are summarized in Table 1 and ESM Table S1 (sensitivity analysis using worst outcome imputation). SOFA score at inclusion was 9 [IQR 7–12]. According to LVAC status, 56 patients were classified as coupled (LVAC ≤ 1.36) and 22 as uncoupled (LVAC > 1.36) at 6-h (Table 1). Among those coupled at 6-h, 20 (36%) were CRT responders (CRT normalization, ≤3.0 s, and/or a relative decrease ≥25% from baseline), while only 5 (23%) of the uncoupled at 6-h were CRT responders (Table 1; ESM Figure S3). A total of 40 patients (48%) had normal CRT at inclusion and remained normal during the entire 6-h of the resuscitation protocol, whereby they did not receive further hemodynamic interventions (i.e., additional resuscitative fluid loading, inodilator support, and MAP or dobutamine test). Five of these 84 patients died before the 6-h assessment and could not have their CRT or LVAC measured at that time-point; one additional patient, alive at 6 h, could not have LVAC measured for technical reasons unrelated to death (true missing, cannot be imputed). These six patients remain excluded from the 6-h LVAC classification, leaving 78 of the 84 patients with a valid 6-h LVAC status (56 coupled + 22 uncoupled). A CONSORT-style patient flow diagram detailing all denominators used in this manuscript is provided in the ESM (Fig. S7). A total of 10 patients were subjected to MAP test because persisting CRT prolongation; six of them normalized CRT during the test and high MAP target was maintained by at least the initial 6-h study period.

Hemodynamic and CRT variations according to LVAC evolution

Complete hemodynamic, echocardiographic–derived, and tissue perfusion variables according to the LVAC evolution group between baseline and 6-h are summarized in Table 2. The relationship between percentual variations of LVAC and CRT from baseline to 6-h was not statistically significant (Spearman Rho = 0.17, p = 0.142) (Fig. 1, Panel A). Persistently coupled and uncoupled patients from baseline to 6-h exhibited heterogeneous CRT variations; remarkably, a subset of persistently coupled patients showed prolongation of CRT, whereas most of the persistently uncoupled patients showed a decrease in CRT (Fig. 1, Panel B). Patients transitioning from uncoupled to coupled LVAC status exhibited a decrease in CRT (−33.8 [−53.1 to −1.2]%, p = 0.013) (Fig. 1, Panel B), and lactate (−31.9 [−41.2–2.4]%, p = 0.020) (Fig. 2, Panel A), along with significant increases in SV (29.4 [5.1–50.3]%) (Fig. 2, Panel B), LVeff (16.8 [10.3–21.5]%) (Fig. 2, Panel C), SW (45.5 [7.4–53.8]%) (Fig. 2, Panel D), and LVEF (56.9 [14.7–83.4]%) (Fig. 2, Panel E) (all p ≤ 0.01). In contrast, patients transitioning from coupled to uncoupled LVAC status showed a predominant CRT prolongation (17.5 [7.1–31.31] % p = 0.013) (Fig. 1, Panel B), and increasing lactate levels (27.7 [−26.0–63.1]%, p = 0.020) (Fig. 2, Panel A); these changes were accompanied by a significant decrease in SV (−16.0 [-30.8–8.3] %) (Fig. 2, Panel B), LVeff (−19.4 [−29.2–−15.5]%) (Fig. 2, Panel C), SW (−17.9 [−40.1–−8.5]%) (Fig. 2, Panel D) and LVEF (−24.3 [−28.6 to −20.1]%) (Fig. 2, Panel E) (all p ≤ 0.05). There were no significant changes in MAP (Fig. 2, Panel F) among the predefined LVAC groups.

At 6-h, 56 patients achieved a coupled LVAC status and 22 remained uncoupled. The unadjusted proportion of CRT responders was numerically higher among coupled patients (36%) than uncoupled patients (23%), but this crude association was not statistically significant (OR 1.89, 95% CI 0.61–5.89, p = 0.27). After adjusting for baseline APACHE II and SOFA scores in addition to the original covariates, a multivariable logistic regression showed that a coupled status at 6 h was independently associated with being a CRT responder (adjusted OR 4.71, 95% CI 1.48–15.76, p = 0.021; AUC 0.77). This model should be interpreted as exploratory, given the possibility of reverse causation (CRT improvement driving more favorable coupling, rather than the reverse) and residual confounding by severity.

Responses of macro hemodynamics and CRT to dobutamine

Dobutamine was started in thirty-two patients within the 6-h of resuscitation period. Dobutamine use was related to a significant increase in HR, systolic arterial pressure, SV, SW, LVEF, LVeff, and Ees (Table 3); furthermore, it was also related to a significant decrease in diastolic pressure, CVP, Ea, and LVAC (Table 3).

Among 24 initially uncoupled patients, 14 (58.3%) became coupled after dobutamine infusion. When stratified by pre-intervention LVAC status, SV significantly increased among those initially uncoupled (49.4 [42.9–55.8] to 57.0 [52.1–61.7], delta: 14.2%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4, Panel A), as well as increases in LVOT-VTI, SW, Ees, LVeff and LVEF (ESM Table S7–Fig. S5). Systolic and diastolic pressures, Ea and CVP significantly decreased in those initially uncoupled (ESM Table S7). Meanwhile, no significant variations in macro hemodynamics were observed in initially coupled patients after dobutamine infusion (ESM Table S7).

Discussion

Our study has three main findings. First, LVAC improvement is more likely to accompany a clinically meaningful recovery of peripheral perfusion during early resuscitation of septic shock, while LVAC deterioration more often accompanies CRT prolongation; however, the unpredictable CRT behavior observed in persistently coupled and uncoupled patients suggests that peripheral perfusion frequently varies independently of the energy transmission from the heart to the proximal arterial tree. Second, significant CRT improvement or worsening sometimes occurred without concurrent changes in macro hemodynamics. Third, despite favorable effects of fluids and dobutamine on LVAC, their impact on peripheral perfusion was variable, suggesting some dissociation between macro- and microcirculatory domains during early resuscitation of septic shock.

It is important to note, however, that LVAC as a determinant of ventricular-to-arterial energy transfer follows directly from the pressure-volume area (PVA) framework established by Suga and Sagawa [7,8]: total mechanical energy generated per beat equals external stroke work (SW) plus potential/elastic energy (PE), and the Ea/Ees ratio [6] determines how this energy is partitioned between useful external work and energy not transmitted to the arterial system; since ejection fraction relates to this ratio as EF≈1/(1+Ea/Ees), an Ea/Ees of 1.0 corresponds to an EF of approximately 50% (Ea/Ees = 1 ≈ LVEF 50%), rather than to maximal efficiency. We also note that because Ea is calculated as Pes/SV, LVAC is mathematically coupled to stroke volume; an apparent LVAC change after an intervention that alters SV (e.g., fluid loading) may therefore partly reflect this mathematical relationship rather than a distinct physiological signal, a caveat revisited below.

LVAC has been proposed as an integrative descriptor of cardiovascular function linking myocardial performance and arterial load, whereby its assessment could theoretically help guide resuscitation [[25], [26], [27]]; preliminary observations have described the effects of common interventions on LVAC during early septic shock resuscitation [13]. However, the impact of LVAC variations on peripheral perfusion after cardiovascular interventions has not been widely explored. Consistent with this construct, uncoupled patients received vasopressin more frequently (73% vs 45%, p = 0.043), and had higher arterial elastance (Ea 2.28 vs 1.82 mmHg/mL, p = 0.043) and lower ejection fraction (LVEF 44% vs 58%, p = 0.001) than coupled patients at baseline (Table 1), reflecting the combined afterload- and contractility-related origin of ventriculo-arterial uncoupling in septic shock. Our data show important discordances between CRT and LVAC variations: LVAC improvement was more often related to a positive CRT response, and LVAC deterioration to CRT prolongation, but the highly variable CRT behavior among persistently coupled or uncoupled patients suggests that CRT frequently varies independently of cardiac-to-arterial energy transmission. This variability is consistent with the complex regulation of the microcirculation in septic shock, where alterations in endothelial function, regional flow distribution, and microvascular derangements may persist despite normalized systemic hemodynamics, limiting effective oxygen delivery even when SV and MAP are optimized [[28], [29], [30], [31]].

An important caveat applies to the intervention analyses (fluid loading, dobutamine) and the multivariable model: because patients were managed within the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK 2 personalized, CRT-guided algorithm, these analyses could be subjected to confounding by indication and should be read as exploratory associations rather than evidence of a treatment effect. Nevertheless, the central LVAC-vs-CRT concordance analysis is less affected because the protocol titrated interventions to CRT normalization, and those interventions plausibly also modify LVAC; thus, any resulting bias would be expected to inflate rather than diminish the apparent concordance. The weak concordance we observed is therefore, if anything, a conservative estimate of the true dissociation between these variables.

Supplementary analyses supported fair concordance between LVAC status and CRT response at 6 h (Cohen's κ = 0.33) and 24 h (κ = 0.29) (ESM Table S8), but the dispersion of Δ%CRT remained wide across all LVAC groups (ESM Table S9), and the ΔLVAC-Δ%CRT association was not robust to exclusion of a single influential observation (ESM Table S10). LVAC coupling status was dynamic over time, with frequent transitions even after adjusting for severity (ESM Table S11), and extending the analysis to 24 h did not strengthen the LVAC-CRT association (ESM Tables S11-S12). A parallel analysis using lactate clearance showed a similar pattern of modest, inconsistent concordance (ESM Table S13). Collectively, these findings reinforce that changes in ventriculo-arterial coupling and peripheral perfusion are only partially aligned during early resuscitation of septic shock.

The SV increase after fluid loading without CRT improvement in initially uncoupled patients warrants careful interpretation. Because arterial elastance is mathematically coupled to stroke volume (Ea = Pes/SV), an SV increase mechanically reduces Ea and can improve LVAC even without a true change in arterial tone or ventricular contractility [27,32]; the apparent recovery of LVAC after fluid loading may therefore partly reflect this mathematical relationship rather than genuine physiological optimization. The absence of significant CRT improvement after fluid loading in our cohort supports the view that preload responsiveness and SV increases do not necessarily translate into better peripheral perfusion, as proposed by others [[28], [29], [30], [31]].

In contrast, dobutamine was associated with a significant SV increase and simultaneous CRT improvement in initially uncoupled patients. While fluids primarily act on preload, inodilators enhance contractility and may reduce effective arterial load, improving ventriculo-arterial interaction [13]. Experimental and clinical data also indicate dobutamine may improve microcirculatory perfusion through mechanisms beyond macrohemodynamics, including improved functional capillary density and reduced microvascular flow heterogeneity, even without changes in cardiac output or systemic oxygen delivery [[33], [34], [35]]. Because this CRT improvement was seen predominantly in initially uncoupled patients, the underlying ventriculo-arterial state may determine whether dobutamine's hemodynamic effects translate into improved tissue perfusion.

Our study has several limitations. First, LVAC was estimated noninvasively via the single-beat method, an indirect approximation of ventricular elastance that may be influenced by loading conditions and measurement variability [24,36]. Second, central-to-peripheral pressure decoupling may also have affected the arterial elastance estimate, though this limitation applied systematically across all patients and time points [37,38]; because peripheral pulse pressure is typically amplified relative to central aortic pressure, particularly at radial sites, the 0.9×systolic arterial pressure approximation of Pes would be expected to overestimate Ea and LVAC, biasing classification toward the uncoupled category [38]. Third, cardiac output was not continuously measured, which may have limited detection of transient macrohemodynamic changes. Fourth, peripheral perfusion assessment relied exclusively on CRT, which introduces inherent limitations. From a physiological standpoint, CRT is a complex variable, possibly reflecting vasoreactivity of the microcirculation, and a single time-point assessment cannot granularly detail variations in regional blood flow components. In addition, CRT can be influenced by skin pigmentation, ambient temperature, and interobserver variability [39]; however, CRT correlates closely with skin microvascular blood flow on laser Doppler flowmetry [40], and CRT-guided resuscitation has been associated with improved clinical outcomes in septic shock [17,19], supporting its validity as a bedside perfusion target. Fifth, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, which may limit external validity, and findings were restricted to septic shock, although LVAC appears relevant in other clinical conditions as well [26,41,42]. Additional limitations include the small number of patients in the groups showing the largest CRT changes (uncoupled-to-coupled, n = 12; coupled-to-uncoupled, n = 8); restriction of the single-beat method to sinus rhythm, limiting generalizability to patients with arrhythmias; the fact that the ANDROMEDA-SHOCK 2 protocol targeted CRT rather than LVAC, so the intervention analyses do not test an LVAC-guided strategy; the absence of operator blinding and of formal inter-/intra-observer reproducibility data for SV, Ees, Ea, and LVAC; and the inability to report the radial-versus-femoral catheter distribution or a sensitivity analysis by catheter site.

In conclusion, although LVAC improvement was associated, in some patients, with recovery of peripheral perfusion, and its deterioration with CRT prolongation in others, this relationship was overall weak: the inconsistent CRT behavior among persistently coupled or uncoupled patients indicates that peripheral perfusion frequently evolves independently of cardiac-to-arterial energy transmission. Despite consistent gains in stroke volume and cardiac efficiency, the peripheral perfusion response to fluids and dobutamine remained highly variable, reinforcing a partial dissociation between macro- and microcirculatory domains during early resuscitation of septic shock. These findings warrant caution, as single-beat LVAC estimation may be inaccurate in vasodilatory states; larger, purpose-designed studies are needed to determine whether LVAC assessment can meaningfully guide perfusion-oriented resuscitation.
