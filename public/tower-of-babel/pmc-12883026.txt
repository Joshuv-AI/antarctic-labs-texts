Primary large B-cell lymphoma of the central nervous system mimicking inflammatory myelitis at initial presentation
Authors: Satoshi Ichikawa, Katsuki Imanishi, Hikaru Kumagai, Shunsuke Hatta, Yoko Okitsu, Masahiro Kobayashi, Narumi Okajima, Dai Ichikawa, Fumiyoshi Fujishima, Junichi Kameoka, Tomonori Ishii
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Primary central nervous system large B-cell lymphoma (PCNS-LBCL) is a rare extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma that typically affects the brain parenchyma. Spinal cord involvement as an initial manifestation is extremely uncommon and poses considerable diagnostic challenges, often mimicking inflammatory demyelinating diseases. We report the case of a man in his late 50s who initially presented with steroid-responsive cervical myelitis, resulting in a provisional diagnosis of multiple sclerosis. Despite treatment with corticosteroids and ofatumumab, the patient experienced neurological deterioration and developed cerebellar lesions. Stereotactic brain biopsy confirmed PCNS-LBCL. Although the initial spinal lesion may have represented a definite lymphoma, it was also considered a sentinel lesion, a non-neoplastic inflammatory precursor occasionally observed in PCNSL. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of PCNS-LBCL presenting with isolated spinal involvement and steroid responsiveness, both of which may delay appropriate treatment. Notably, cerebrospinal fluid analysis at the time of initial presentation of spinal lesions revealed elevated interleukin-10 levels, which declined substantially following chemotherapy, correlating with clinical improvement. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for central nervous system lymphoma in cases of relapsing myelitis unresponsive to conventional immunotherapy and consider timely biopsy when clinically feasible. Heightened awareness of sentinel lesions may facilitate early recognition and improve outcomes in patients with PCNSL.

INTRODUCTION

Malignant lymphoma confined to the central nervous system (CNS)—including the brain, spinal cord, eyes, and leptomeninges—without systemic involvement at diagnosis, is referred to as primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL). This rare extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma accounts for 2% of all primary CNS tumors.1,2 The vast majority of cases (>90%) are histologically classified as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma,2,3 also referred to as primary CNS large B-cell lymphoma (PCNS-LBCL). There is often a long interval between symptom onset and diagnosis, with studies reporting diagnostic delays of several weeks to months.2 This delay is particularly problematic because PCNSL requires the immediate initiation of appropriate chemotherapy to prevent irreversible neurological damage. The disease typically presents with focal neurological deficits, cognitive changes, or seizures related to deep-seated brain parenchymal lesions; however, presentation solely with myelitis is uncommon.2,4-6 These atypical cases pose a considerable diagnostic challenge, as they are often initially diagnosed as neuroinflammatory diseases such as multiple sclerosis (MS) or neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).7,8 Furthermore, PCNSL is occasionally preceded by inflammatory non-neoplastic sentinel lesions that may respond transiently to steroid therapy, further mimicking demyelinating diseases.9-11 Here, we present a case of PCNS-LBCL in which myelitis was the initial manifestation, highlighting the diagnostic difficulties and the importance of considering lymphoma in atypical neuroinflammatory presentations.

CASE PRESENTATION

A man in his late 50s presented with a 1-month history of paresthesia in his extremities. The patient was afebrile, and no other systemic symptoms were observed. Blood test results revealed no abnormalities. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no abnormal findings, whereas spinal MRI with T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) revealed a high-intensity lesion in the cervical cord at the C2 level (Figure 1a). Only slight contrast enhancement was observed on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images, and no ring enhancement pattern was observed. Diffusion-weighted imaging showed slightly hyperintense signals in the lesions. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed mild mononuclear pleocytosis (19/µL), elevated protein (83 mg/dL), and normal glucose (49 mg/dL). Tests for anti-aquaporin-4 and anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies were negative, and the CSF soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was not elevated (131 U/mL). In the absence of evident tumor structures and given the localized nature of the lesion, an inflammatory demyelinating disorder was considered more likely than a tumor or infection. The patient’s condition improved after intravenous methylprednisolone pulse (IVMP) therapy. Oligoclonal bands were detected in the initial CSF samples and later confirmed. After one month, a follow-up spinal MRI revealed a new T2 high-intensity lesion at the C4/C5 level (Figure 1b). Although several atypical features were observed, the temporal and spatial multiplicity of the inflammatory lesions suggested MS. Following another course of IVMP, treatment with ofatumumab was initiated.

Figure 2 illustrates the timeline of the patient’s clinical course. One month after initiating ofatumumab treatment, the patient developed progressive dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. Brain MRI revealed multiple new ring-enhancing lesions in the bilateral cerebellar hemispheres and right temporal lobe on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted imaging, with corresponding high signals on T2WI (Figure 1c–e). No spinal lesions were detected at this time. His neurological status deteriorated rapidly, with impaired consciousness and motor deficits, including right lower limb paralysis and considerable ataxia, leaving him bedridden. Symptoms did not improve despite repeated IVMP therapy. A complete blood count revealed a normal white blood cell count of 6,600 cells/μL, with a decreased percentage of lymphocytes (4%) and maintained proportions of neutrophils (91%) and monocytes (5%). The patient also had slight anemia (hemoglobin level, 12.7 g/dL) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count, 115,000 cells/μL). Serum lactate dehydrogenase and sIL-2R levels were within normal limits (209 U/L and 248 U/mL, respectively). Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) serology indicated a past infection. Repeat CSF analysis revealed mononuclear pleocytosis (26/µL), markedly elevated protein (206 mg/dL), and elevated sIL-2R (462 U/mL) levels, whereas CSF glucose remained normal (53 mg/dL). CSF cytology revealed numerous lymphocytes and histiocytes, but no definitive malignant cells were identified. Ophthalmological examination findings were normal. The staging workup, including whole-body computed tomography, showed no evidence of systemic lymphadenopathy or organ involvement except for mild enlargement of the left adrenal gland. Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy demonstrated a normocellular marrow with no evidence of lymphoma infiltration. Histopathological examination of a stereotactic biopsy specimen from the right cerebellar lesion showed diffuse infiltration of large atypical lymphoid cells with perivascular cuffing (Figure 3a). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that abnormal cells were CD45+, CD19+, CD20+, and CD10– and lacked surface immunoglobulin kappa and lambda light chains (Figure 3b). Immunohistochemistry (Figure 3c–h) confirmed that they were CD20+, CD3–, CD5+, CD10–, BCL2+, BCL6+, MUM1+, and c-myc+, with a high proliferation index demonstrated by Ki-67 staining (80–90%). EBV-encoded RNA in situ hybridization results were negative (Figure 3i). Collectively, these findings confirmed a diagnosis of PCNS-LBCL.

Immediately after brain biopsy, the patient’s level of consciousness deteriorated rapidly and did not improve despite IVMP or dexamethasone therapy. Six days later, treatment with the R-MPV regimen (rituximab 375 mg/m2, methotrexate 3.5 g/m2, procarbazine 100 mg/m2/day for 7 days, and vincristine 2 mg/body) was initiated. Procarbazine was omitted from the first cycle owing to difficulties with oral administration, but was included in the second cycle. The patient’s level of consciousness gradually improved during the first cycle. After two cycles of R-MPV, follow-up brain and spinal MRI revealed a substantial reduction in the size and enhancement of brain lesions, indicating a partial response (Figure 4). Clinically, the patient’s neurological status also gradually improved, and CSF parameters normalized (sIL-2R 61 U/mL, mononuclear cells 3/µL). Retrospective analysis of stored CSF samples from the initial presentation (myelitis onset) and immediately before biopsy revealed markedly elevated interleukin-10 (IL-10) levels (124 and 145 pg/mL, respectively), which decreased significantly to 41 pg/mL after the second cycle of R-MPV. The patient was transferred to another hospital for further treatment.

DISCUSSION

The ability of PCNSL to masquerade as an inflammatory demyelinating disease represents one of the most challenging diagnostic pitfalls in neurooncology. This phenomenon, referred to as “sentinel lesions”,12,13 can precede the development of overt lymphoma by months to years, predominantly exhibits T-cell infiltrates with rare B cells, and may be histologically indistinguishable from MS or acute disseminated encephalomyelitis.10 The clinical presentation further complicates the diagnosis, as PCNSL and demyelinating diseases exhibit relapsing-remitting patterns with an excellent initial response to corticosteroid therapy.13 This steroid responsiveness, typically characteristic of inflammatory demyelinating diseases, also occurs in PCNSL owing to the transient regression of lymphomatous tissue, potentially creating false reassurance for clinicians.14 Oligoclonal bands in the CSF, traditionally associated with MS, can also be found in CNS lymphoma, representing intrathecal immune responses rather than a specific demyelinating pathology.15,16 The current case illustrates this diagnostic complexity: the patient’s initial spinal cord lesions responded favorably to corticosteroid therapy and were clinically interpreted as an inflammatory demyelinating disease, yet they may have represented sentinel lesions heralding the subsequent development of frank lymphoma. However, the possibility of a lymphoma lesion could not be excluded.

The diagnosis of PCNSL poses considerable challenges owing to its nonspecific clinical presentation and variable imaging characteristics, frequently leading to diagnostic delays that adversely affect patient outcomes.17 Histopathological examination of specimens obtained by stereotactic biopsy remains the gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of PCNSL2; however, this invasive procedure is associated with substantial risks and may not always be feasible. Moreover, corticosteroid therapy administered prior to biopsy can obscure histological findings; studies have shown that steroid treatment before biopsy may render accurate diagnosis difficult.2,18 Alternative diagnostic modalities, including CSF cytology, have low sensitivity, with tumor cells detected in only 16% of cases.19 Recent advances in understanding the pathophysiology of PCNSL have identified several promising CSF biomarkers that could aid in early diagnosis. Elevated CSF IL-10 levels have emerged as a highly sensitive and specific diagnostic marker for PCNSL, with studies reporting sensitivity rates of 88.6–95.5% and specificity rates of 88.9–100% when appropriate cutoff values are employed.20 The IL-10/IL-6 ratio demonstrated even greater diagnostic accuracy, with a specificity approaching 100%.20 Additionally, the MYD88 L265P mutation, found in approximately 70% of PCNSL cases, represents a promising disease-specific molecular marker.21 However, this analysis is currently recommended only for freshly biopsied specimens, which can be challenging to obtain. CSF testing for MYD88 mutations can serve as an effective supplementary or alternative method when performed with a high-sensitivity technique, such as digital droplet polymerase chain reaction.22 In the present case, retrospective analysis revealed elevated CSF IL-10 levels at the initial presentation of myelitis; however, MYD88 mutation testing was unavailable. Although chemotherapy could not have been initiated based solely on this finding, it may justify careful follow-up with lymphoma in mind. In patients presenting with atypical myelitis, assessing CSF IL-10 levels, the IL-10/IL-6 ratio, and MYD88 L265P mutation as part of the initial diagnostic workup may facilitate earlier diagnosis of lymphoma, enabling prompt treatment and potentially improving neurological prognosis.

Spinal cord involvement as the initial presentation of PCNSL is exceedingly rare and presents with spinal lesions that closely mimic NMOSD, transverse myelitis, or other inflammatory myelopathies.4,9,23,24 However, clinical and radiological findings are atypical in most reported cases. Owing to the risks associated with spinal biopsy, the rarity of the disease, and the difficulty in distinguishing it from other diseases, there is a long time lapse from symptom onset to diagnosis, and the rate of sequelae after treatment is high.23,25 CSF analysis may reveal pleocytosis and elevated protein levels; however, standard cytological examination is often negative. In cases of spinal cord infiltration, steroid therapy is ineffective, and a spinal cord biopsy is required to establish a diagnosis.4,26-28 Some patients require multiple biopsies, including biopsies of spinal cord lesions.25 In the present case, the initial spinal cord lesions were multiple and confined to the spinal cord, an atypical pattern for MS. In patients with myelitis with particularly atypical features or a clinical course, careful follow-up and timely biopsy are essential, given the possibility of lymphoma that may become apparent later.

In summary, this case underscores the diagnostic complexity of PCNSL, particularly when it presents with atypical features—such as spinal cord involvement and excellent steroid responsiveness—that mimic inflammatory demyelinating diseases. The delayed diagnosis in this case highlights the critical need for heightened clinical awareness of PCNSL as a potential mimic of other common neurological conditions. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for PCNSL in patients presenting with atypical features of presumed inflammatory demyelinating diseases.
