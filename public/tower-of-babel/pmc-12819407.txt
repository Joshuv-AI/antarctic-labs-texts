miR-193a-5p–mediated Inhibition of the METTL1/COX-2 axis is critical for Astragalin-induced apoptosis in cervical cancer
Authors: Yuchan Lee, Su-Yeon Park, Bum-Sang Shim, Bonglee Kim, Sung-Hoon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Astragalin (kaempferol-3-O-glucoside), a flavonoid from Astragalus membranaceus, has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, neuroprotective, and antitumor activities. However, its molecular mechanisms in cervical cancer remain largely undefined. In this study, we investigated the apoptotic effects of Astragalin in association with methyltransferase-like protein 1 (METTL1)/cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) signaling. Astragalin significantly reduced cell viability in SiHa, CaSki, and HeLa cervical cancer cells, increased the sub-G1 population and TUNEL-positive cells, and decreased pro-poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (pro-PARP) and procaspase-3 expression in SiHa and CaSki cells. Notably, Astragalin downregulated METTL1 and COX-2 expression, consistent with TCGA data linking METTL1 overexpression to poor prognosis in cervical cancer. Cycloheximide chase assays demonstrated stronger inhibition of METTL1 than COX-2 protein stability, while immunoprecipitation revealed METTL1 binding to COX-2 (a weak but significant positive correlation (r = 0.16), which was disrupted by Astragalin. Mechanistically, Astragalin upregulated miR-193a-5p, and its mimic suppressed METTL1 and COX-2 expression in SiHa cells, whereas its inhibitor restored. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Astragalin induces apoptosis through miR-193a-5p–mediated inhibition of the METTL1/COX-2 signaling axis, highlighting its potential as a promising antitumor candidate for cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32320-3.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the third most common gynecologic malignancy in young adults worldwide according to GLOBOCAN 20221. Despite progress in prevention through human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and chemotherapy with agents such as cisplatin, paclitaxel, gemcitabine, and carboplatin, drug resistance remains a major hurdle in improving patient outcomes2–4. Consequently, natural compounds and medicinal plants have attracted attention for their ability to selectively induce apoptosis in cervical cancer cells while sparing normal tissues. For example, curcumin5, Beta vulgaris6, Calystegia silvatica7 and Prosopis laevigata8 have demonstrated anticancer effects9.

In addition to phytochemicals, molecular targeted therapies are increasingly recognized as promising strategies in cervical cancer. Recent studies have identified potential targets such as integrin α3 (ITGA3)10, NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit B3 (NDUFB3)11, HPV oncoprotein E612 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-induced protein 1 (MCPIP1).

Methyltransferase-like protein 1 (METTL1), also known as TRMB (tRNA guanine-N7-methyltransferase), has been implicated in cancer progression through RNA modification. METTL1 was reported to promote resistance to the tyrosine kinase inhibitor lenvatinib13,14. Consistently, TCGA analysis reveals METTL1 overexpression in multiple cancers, including leukemia, lung cancer, lymphoma, bladder cancer, colorectal cancer, and sarcoma15,16.

Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), another cancer-associated molecule, promotes tumor development via prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which drives proliferation, angiogenesis, and invasion in solid malignant tumors17 including cervical cancer18. Together, these findings highlight METTL1 and COX-2 as potential oncogenic drivers and therapeutic targets.

Natural compounds have been increasingly investigated as chemopreventive agents for cervical cancer due to their safety and multi-targeted effects19. For example, berberine exhibits both anti-HIV and anticancer activities in cervical cancer20, while kaempferol induces apoptosis and autophagy in KB cervical cancer cells21.

Astragalin (kaempferol-3-O-glucoside), a flavonoid isolated from Astragalus membranaceus, exhibits anti-inflammatory22, antioxidant23 and neuroprotective24 effects, along with anti-tumor property in gastric25, colon26, melanoma27, lung28 cancers. However, its underlying mechanisms in cervical cancer remain poorly defined. In the present study, we investigated the antitumor potential of Astragalin, focusing on its role in apoptosis via METTL1/COX-2 signaling axis.

Results

Cytotoxic effect of Astragalin in human cervical cancer cells

To assess the cytotoxic effect of Astragalin (Fig. 1A), cell viability was evaluated in SiHa, CaSki, and HeLa cervical cancer cells using the MTT assay. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of Astragalin (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM) for 24 h. Astragalin treatment significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner across all tested cell lines (Fig. 1B), indicating its potential anti-proliferative effect on cervical cancer cells.

Astragalin increases sub-G1 population and induces apoptosis in SiHa and CaSki cells

To further confirm the apoptotic effect of Astragalin, cell cycle analysis and Western blotting were performed in Astragalin-treated SiHa and CaSki cells. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that Astragalin treatment increased the proportion of cells in the sub-G1 phase, indicative of apoptotic DNA fragmentation (Fig. 2A). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that Astragalin decreased the expression of pro-PARP, procaspase-3 and cleaved caspase-3 in both SiHa and CaSki cells (Fig. 2B), supporting the induction of apoptosis by Astragalin.

Astragalin increases the number of TUNEL-positive cells in SiHa cells

To further verify that the cytotoxic effect of Astragalin was associated with apoptosis, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay was performed in SiHa cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, Astragalin treatment markedly increased the number of TUNEL-positive cells displaying green fluorescence compared with untreated controls, confirming its apoptotic effect.

Astragalin suppresses the expression of METTL1 and COX-2 in cervical cancer cells

To investigate the potential role of METTL1 and COX-2 in cervical cancer, TCGA data from the cBioPortal database were analyzed. METTL1 expression was significantly elevated in cervical cancer tissues and was associated with poorer overall survival compared with normal controls (Fig. 4A,B). Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed that Astragalin treatment attenuated the expression of METTL1 and COX-2 in both SiHa and CaSki cells (Fig. 4C).

Astragalin reduces METTL1 and COX-2 protein stability in SiHa and Caski cells

To further examine whether Astragalin regulates the stability of METTL1 and COX-2 proteins, a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay was performed in SiHa and Caski cells. Treatment with Astragalin markedly accelerated the degradation of METTL1 and COX-2 proteins compared with CHX-treated controls, indicating that Astragalin suppresses their protein stability (Fig. 5A).

Astragalin disrupts the interaction between METTL1 and COX-2 in cervical cancer cells

To evaluate the relationship between METTL1 and COX-2, cBioPortal database analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between their expression, with a Spearman’s correlation coefficient of 0.16 (Fig. 6A). Consistently, co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that METTL1 physically interacts with COX-2 in SiHa and Caski cells, and this interaction was markedly suppressed by Astragalin treatment (Fig. 6B). Also, mRNA expression levels of METTL1 and COX-2 were measured in SiHa cells treated with siMETTL1 and/or Astragalin. While siMETTL1 alone reduced the mRNA levels of METTL1 and COX-2, Astragalin treatment enhanced the effect METTL1 depletion at mRNA level in SiHa cells (Fig. 6C). Functionally, overexpression of METTL1 attenuated the ability of Astragalin to reduce METTL1 and COX-2 expression (Fig. 6D), whereas METTL1 depletion enhanced the suppressive effect of Astragalin on both proteins (Fig. 6E). These findings indicate that Astragalin exerts its antitumor effect, at least in part, by disrupting the METTL1–COX-2 interaction.

Astragalin upregulates miR-193a-5p expression in SiHa cells

To investigate the role of miR-193a-5p in Astragalin-induced apoptosis, bioinformatic prediction was first performed using the TargetScan database, which revealed a putative binding site for miR-193a-5p in the 3′-untranslated region (UTR) of METTL1 (Fig. 7A). Consistently, Astragalin treatment significantly increased the mRNA level of miR-193a-5p in SiHa cells compared with untreated controls (Fig. 7B).

To further examine its functional role, miR-193a-5p mimic or inhibitor was transfected into SiHa cells and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. The miR-193a-5p mimic significantly reduced cell viability, whereas the inhibitor had little effect on cytotoxicity (Fig. 7C). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the miR-193a-5p mimic suppressed METTL1 and COX-2 protein expression (Fig. 7D), while qRT-PCR confirmed downregulation of METTL1 and COX-2 mRNA levels (Fig. 7E). Conversely, the miR-193a-5p inhibitor enhanced METTL1, COX-2, and pro-PARP expression relative to untreated controls (Fig. 7F).

These results suggest that Astragalin induces apoptosis, at least in part, through the upregulation of miR-193a-5p, leading to the suppression of METTL1 and COX-2 expression.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the apoptotic mechanism of Astragalin, a major flavonoid from Astragalus membranaceus, in cervical cancer cells with a focus on METTL1 and COX-2 signaling. Astragalin significantly suppressed the viability of SiHa, CaSki, and HeLa cells, supporting its cytotoxic potential in cervical cancers, while Astragalin did not show any toxicity in human colon epithelial cell lines NCM460 cells26. Cell cycle analysis and Western blotting further demonstrated that Astragalin increased the sub-G1 population and reduced the expression of procaspase-3 and pro-PARP in SiHa and CaSki cells, confirming that its cytotoxicity is mediated by apoptosis. These results are consistent with previous studies showing that Astragalin inhibited aerobic glycolysis and proliferation in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells29 and induced apoptosis in A549 lung cancer cells via caspase activation, ROS production, and JAK/STAT3 inhibition. Also, Xu et al.30 demonstrated the apoptotic effect of Astragalin in A549 lung cancer cells via caspase activation, ROS production and inhibition of JAK/STAT3 signaling.

Molecular targeting approaches have gained increasing attention in cervical cancer, including inhibition of ITGA310, NDUFB311, HPV oncoprotein E612 and MCPIP1 in cervical cancers. In line with this, our findings highlight METTL1 and COX-2 as critical oncogenic factors. METTL1 forms a complex with WDR4 to regulate tRNA m^7G methylation31, functioning as an oncogene in liver cancer14 and bladder cancer32, while WRD4 mutations destabilize METTL133. Also, COX-2, a well-known pro-tumorigenic enzyme is overexpressed in cervical cancer at levels 7.4-fold higher than in normal tissue, mediated through EGFR signaling34.

As expected, Astragalin significantly suppressed METTL1 and COX-2 expression in cervical cancer cells. TCGA analysis further confirmed that high METTL1 expression correlates with poor survival, underscoring its clinical relevance. Moreover, cycloheximide chase assays revealed that Astragalin reduced METTL1 protein stability more strongly than COX-2, while co-immunoprecipitation demonstrated a direct interaction between METTL1 and COX-2 (correlation coefficient r = 0.16), which was disrupted by Astragalin.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate the majority of protein-coding genes and function as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors in cancer35. Among these, miR-193a-5p has been identified as a tumor suppressor36,37. Notably, Astragalin upregulated miR-193a-5p in SiHa cells, and functional assays revealed that its mimic suppressed METTL1 and COX-2 expression, while its inhibitor enhanced their expression along with pro-PARP. These findings indicate that Astragalin induces apoptosis at least in part through miR-193a-5p–mediated inhibition of the METTL1/COX-2 axis. Likewise, Huang et al. reported miR-193a-5p was downregulated in cervical cancers, which was suppressed by FBXL19-AS138. However, although METTL1 and COX-2 appear to play pivotal roles in Astragalin-induced apoptosis in cervical cancers, additional investigations are warranted to substantiate these mechanistic findings in vivo and to comprehensively assess the efficacy, safety, and bioavailability of Astragalin, including, luciferase reporter assay with the METTL1 3′UTR, time-course analysis and therapeutic synergy with cisplatin in the near future.

Conclusion

In summary, Astragalin exhibited strong cytotoxic effects in cervical cancer cells, increasing the sub-G1 population, promoting TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells, and downregulating procaspase-3 and pro-PARP. Astragalin disrupted the METTL1–COX-2 interaction, reduced their protein stability, and upregulated miR-193a-5p, which in turn suppressed METTL1 and COX-2 expression. These findings establish Astragalin as a promising antitumor candidate that induces apoptosis in cervical cancer through miR-193a-5p–mediated inhibition of the METTL1/COX-2 axis. However, although our data clearly support the pro-apoptotic effects of Astragalin in vitro, further preclinical studies addressing its safety, pharmacokinetics, and therapeutic efficacy in animal models and clinical settings are warranted.

Methods

Cell culture and Astragalin preparation

Human SiHa, CaSki, and HeLa cervical cancer cell lines were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Korea). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Welgene, Inc., Gyeongsan, South Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic solution at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Astragalin (CAS: 79851), purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), was dissolved in 0.01% DMSO and was applied to the cells in next experiments.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was measured using the MTT assay. Briefly, SiHa, CaSki, and HeLa cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with various concentrations of Astragalin for 24 h. Cells were then incubated with MTT solution (1 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h, followed by dissolution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 20 min. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), and viability was expressed as the percentage relative to untreated controls.

Cell cycle analysis

SiHa and CaSki cells (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were treated with Astragalin (0, 10, or 20 µM) for 24 h. Cells were harvested, incubated with RNase A (10 mg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C, and stained with propidium iodide (50 µg/mL) for 30 min in the dark. DNA content was analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with CellQuest software.

TUNEL assay

SiHa cells were treated with Astragalin for 24 h, washed with cold PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% sodium citrate and 0.1% Triton X-100, then incubated with a TUNEL assay kit (Roche Molecular Biochemicals, Mannheim, Germany). TUNEL-positive cells were visualized using a FLUOVIEW FV10i confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cycloheximide chase assay

SiHa cells were treated with Astragalin for 24 h, followed by exposure to cycloheximide (CHX, 50 µg/mL; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for 120–240 min. Cells were then subjected to Western blotting for METTL1 and COX-2 expression.

RNA interference and plasmid transfection

METTL1 siRNA plasmids and control vectors (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) were transfected into SiHa cells using Turbofect transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Radnor, PA, USA). Cells were cultured for 48 h post-transfection before subsequent experiments.

Western blotting

Following the protocol of Sim et al.39, SiHa and CaSki cells were treated with Astragalin for 24 h, lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using the RC DC protein assay kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 4–12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris gels and transferred onto Hybond ECL membranes. Membranes were probed with antibodies against PARP (#9542; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), caspase-3 (#9662; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), METTL1 (14994-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), and COX-2 (#4842; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), with β-actin (A1978; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) as a loading control.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Based on Park et al.40, SiHa cells were lysed in IP buffer and incubated with METTL1 or COX-2 antibodies overnight. Protein A/G sepharose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) were added to capture complexes. Precipitated proteins were washed, eluted, and analyzed by Western blotting with METTL1 and COX-2 antibodies.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated at least three times independently. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SigmaPlot version 12 software (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Differences among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the student’s t-test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
