Black diaphragm intraocular lens implantation in complex aphakia with extensive iris loss: An Indian experience
Authors: Purendra Bhasin
Published: 2026

Extensive iris defects, whether congenital or acquired following trauma, represent a significant rehabilitative challenge for ophthalmic surgeons. In addition to aphakia or lens instability, these eyes frequently experience severe photophobia, glare, reduced contrast sensitivity, and cosmetically disfiguring appearances, resulting in substantial impairment of quality of life. Management becomes particularly complex when such eyes are associated with secondary glaucoma, corneal scarring, retinal pathology, optic nerve damage, or prior vitreoretinal surgery—features commonly encountered in clinical practice, especially in trauma-related cases.

Black diaphragm intraocular (BDI) lenses were developed to address the dual problem of aphakia and aniridia by providing optical correction along with effective photic control. Since their initial description by Sundmacher et al.[1] in congenital aniridia, BDI lenses have been employed in both congenital and post-traumatic settings. However, published outcomes have been heterogeneous, with reported complications including secondary glaucoma, corneal decompensation, and intraocular lens (IOL) instability.[23456] In this context, the present Indian experience offers valuable insight into the performance of Indian-manufactured BDI lenses in eyes with extensive iris loss complicated by multiple ocular comorbidities.

A key strength of this report lies in its realistic representation of patients undergoing BDI lens implantation in the Indian setting. The majority of eyes were post-traumatic and frequently associated with secondary glaucoma, corneal pathology, retinal disease, strabismus, or optic nerve compromise. Such complexity inherently limits visual potential, making conventional visual acuity outcomes alone insufficient to assess surgical success. Accordingly, subjective improvement in glare and photophobia assumes particular importance. Despite these limitations, most eyes demonstrated improvement in uncorrected distance visual acuity, with the best-corrected visual acuity reaching logMAR 1.0 or better in a majority. More importantly, patients consistently reported marked reduction in photopic symptoms, highlighting the functional value of restoring an effective pupillary aperture—an outcome consistent with prior reports on BDI lens implantation.[34567]

The authors also emphasize a modified surgical approach tailored to fragile, traumatized eyes. Conventional scleral-fixated techniques for large, rigid lenses often require extensive tissue dissection, multiple scleral flaps, and large incisions, which may aggravate limbal stem cell loss and compromise corneal health. The described technique, using two opposing reverse scleral tunnels with strategic burial of 9-0 polypropylene sutures, appears to reduce surgical manipulation while maintaining lens centration and stability [Fig. 1]. Preservation of limbal architecture is particularly relevant in aniridic and post-traumatic eyes, which are predisposed to ocular surface instability and progressive corneal decompensation.[56]

Accurate IOL power calculation remains a persistent limitation in eyes with irregular corneas, prior trauma, or multiple intraocular procedures. Similar challenges have been acknowledged in earlier series, where residual refractive error was considered acceptable given that the primary goal of surgery is functional rehabilitation rather than refractive precision.[348] Careful preoperative counseling regarding realistic visual expectations therefore remains critical to patient satisfaction and should be emphasized in all such cases.

Postoperative complications following BDI lens implantation—particularly secondary glaucoma and corneal decompensation—are well documented.[1234569] The relatively low incidence of uncontrolled intraocular pressure elevation and the limited number of eyes developing late corneal decompensation in this cohort underscore the importance of careful patient selection, meticulous preoperative control of inflammation and intraocular pressure, reduced surgical manipulation, and close long-term follow-up. Furthermore, successful rehabilitation in eyes requiring staged procedures, such as glaucoma drainage devices or optical keratoplasty, supports the concept of BDI lens implantation as part of a comprehensive, multistaged management strategy rather than a single definitive intervention.

From a broader perspective, this experience addresses a relevant gap in the literature regarding Indian-manufactured BDI lenses in Indian eyes. Patients in this setting often present with severe trauma, delayed referrals, and multiple coexisting ocular morbidities, all of which influence outcomes. Data reflecting these real-world challenges are essential for guiding clinical decision-making and patient counseling in similar populations.

In conclusion, BDI lens implantation can provide meaningful functional and cosmetic rehabilitation in aphakic eyes with extensive iris defects. Favorable outcomes depend on judicious patient selection, careful management of associated ocular comorbidities, and refinement of surgical techniques to minimize additional tissue trauma. In complex traumatic eyes, a staged, multidisciplinary approach remains essential for achieving sustainable visual rehabilitation.
