Soft Prompts for Evaluation: Measuring Conditional Distance of Capabilities
Ross Nordby

Abstract
To help evaluate and understand the latent capabilities of language models, this paper introduces an approach using optimized input embeddings, or ‘soft prompts,’ as a metric of conditional distance between a model and a target behavior. The technique aims to facilitate latent capability discovery as a part of automated red teaming/evaluation suites and to provide quantitative feedback about the accessibility of potentially concerning behaviors in a way that may scale to powerful future models, including those which may otherwise be capable of deceptive alignment. An evaluation framework using soft prompts is demonstrated in natural language, chess, and pathfinding, and the technique is extended with generalized conditional soft prompts to aid in constructing task evaluations.

Introduction

As machine learning models become more powerful,
the need to monitor their progress and the robustness of their behavior for safety increases.
The types of potentially concerning behavior range from present issues like jailbreaking
large language models
(
Shen et al., 2023
)
to future threats
like self-replication
(
Kinniment et al., 2023
)
and catastrophic outcomes.

The following desiderata guided the approach taken in this paper:

1.

Scalable
: The evaluation framework should continue to work on
future models without being invalidated by their greater capabilities.

2.

Generalizable
: The evaluation framework should be
easily applied to a wide range of tasks.

3.

Quantitative
: Where possible, the evaluation should be quantitative,
allowing for comparisons across models and model scales.

4.

Misuse Resistant
: Red teaming tools are dual-use.
Ideal techniques would help build robust systems without providing a blueprint for misuse.

Soft prompts
(
Lester et al., 2021
)
offer a promising foundation to evaluating capabilities
while satisfying these targets:

Scalable

Sufficiently strong models can pose additional challenges for evaluations.
Manually prompting a model to elicit a capability can demonstrate that the capability exists,
but failing to elicit a particular capability does not necessarily imply that the model
lacks the capability if given a different prompt. Strengthening and automating
the prompting process should reduce the likelihood of missing the presence of a capability
as the space of possible capabilities grows.

Further, strong models could be able to conceal their own capabilities.
2
2

2

While current pretrained models do not obviously exhibit any inclination
towards unprompted deception, and there is no clear path for the pretraining objective
to induce such behavior, the threat model is broader than just runaway autonomous agents.
A user could accidentally prompt or fine-tune a model in a way that implies unforeseen
consequences.

Attempting to discover this while treating the model as a black box could be extremely difficult.

While a model may be a black box with respect to a human observer’s intuition,
any end-to-end differentiable system trained by gradient descent is necessarily transparent to the optimizer
that trained it. Soft prompts benefit from backpropagation’s strong credit assignment and directly
exploit the model’s internal machinery; the fact that the model could be trained in the first place
implies that a differentiable model cannot hide features of its implementation from soft prompts.

Generalizable

Traditional soft prompts are already highly flexible and, in principle, can be trained
to condition the model to do anything that could be conveyed to the model through prompts.
Augmenting soft prompts with conditions allows them to be more easily applied across a wide
range of tasks with a finer degree of control over runtime behavior.
Conditioned soft prompts can also subsume reinforcement learning within predictive training
using an approach similar to decision transformers
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
.

Quantitative

The original paper noted that the number of optimized token embeddings necessary
to fine-tune a model tended to decrease as the model increased in size.
While not used for this purpose at the time, this directly offers a quantitative proxy metric
for the amount of information—represented as conditions in the prompt—necessary to achieve a given behavior.

Misuse Resistant

Optimizing soft prompts with gradient descent requires access to both model parameters
and input embeddings. Unless a model is deployed with a mechanism to allow for this,
this technique is not a realistic attack vector.
This paper’s proposal is most useful within the labs developing the models themselves and
in sanctioned third party evaluations.

2
Related Work

In parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), the goal is to cheaply elicit
a behavior from a model. Early versions include adapter modules
(
Houlsby et al., 2019
)

optimized to perform tasks. More recent PEFT techniques like
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
in
Hu et al. (2021)

eliminate deployment overhead by fine-tuning adaptations to be baked into
the model’s existing parameters. However, both techniques modify the internal
operation of the model, potentially introducing new capabilities
which may not have been present in the original model and making them
less useful for evaluating what capabilities could be elicited by input conditions alone.
Further, the performance advantages of other PEFT techniques are not significant in evaluations.

Soft prompts
(
Lester et al., 2021
)
were originally proposed
as a form of prompt tuning in the context of PEFT alongside other techniques like

prefix tuning

(
Li & Liang, 2021
)
and
p-tuning v2

(
Liu et al., 2022
)
.
Targeting a wider scope of internal layers allowed the latter techniques to
often demonstrate better fine-tuning performance, but it also made them less suitable for
evaluating condition-elicitable capabilities for the same reason as LoRA.

Adversarial prompting in the form of “jailbreaks” has been explored
in
Do Anything Now
,
(
Shen et al., 2023
)
, which demonstrates the ability to
elicit a wide range of undesirable behaviors from available large language models.
Jailbreaks are also examined as a part of GPT-4’s evaluations in
OpenAI (2023)
.
Developing jailbreaks in this fashion has often been a manual process that is
difficult to scale in an evaluations framework.

More scalable automatic approaches for generating jailbreaks, like

RLPrompt

(
Deng et al., 2022
)
,
Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement

(
Chao et al., 2023
)
, and
Tree of Attacks with Pruning

(
Mehrotra et al., 2023
)
have been developed but are limited to the more
challenging problem of generating discretely tokenized prompts.

Augmenting soft prompts with conditions has been explored in vision-language models as part of

Conditional Context Optimization (CoCoOP)

(
Zhou et al., 2022
)
and for
knowledge graph completion as part of
Conditional Soft Prompts
for KGC (CSProm-KG)

(
Chen et al., 2023
)
. This paper applies same simple underlying concept
to assist in constructing flexible task evaluations trained with supervised training objectives.

Decision Transformers

(
Chen et al., 2021
)
demonstrate how to subsume the generality of
reinforcement learning within prediction tasks. This paper applies a similar concept to optimizing soft prompts,
but does not use explicit reward signals when other sufficient conditions are readily available.

Kinniment et al. (2023)
builds a framework of agentic scaffolding targeting a suite of tasks
to monitor for the development of concerning capabilities. This paper’s use of soft prompts are
intended to serve as another tool to be deployed in similar evaluations.

Bailey et al. (2023)
suggests that soft prompts can be thought of as adversarially
exploitative. Fortunately, that is the goal of this paper: to exploit every lever exposed by the
model’s input interface to elicit a behavior, and to observe how much prompting it takes to do so.

3
Methods

The core approach to training the soft prompts mirrors the original paper
(
Lester et al., 2021
)
.:

1.

Insert soft prompt embeddings into the input token sequence of the model.

2.

Run the model’s forward pass and evaluate the output against a loss function.

3.

Holding all other model parameters fixed, backpropagate the loss gradient through the model
to the soft prompt embeddings and optimize them with gradient descent.

The manner in which loss is computed is flexible and depends on the task. Experiments in this
paper use the same cross-entropy loss used by the Pythia suite models but sometimes combine it
with twists applied to output labels.

All experiments run on a 4090 GPU with 24GB of memory.

3.1
Tokens as Distance Metric

In autoregressive language models, the model’s output is a probability distribution over the next token
for each position in the sequence. The output probability distribution for a given sequence position
is a function of the current and previous tokens. In other words, the conditional probability
that a token
t
appears following an observed sequence
s
is refined as additional tokens—
additional conditions—are added to
s
.

Under normal operation,
s
is composed of tokens of discrete types.
The embeddings associated with those token types are typically optimized to serve the model’s
default predictive task and so will tend to be far less information-dense with respect to the
arbitrary tasks aimed at by soft prompts. Soft prompts also insert additional tokens into
s
,
but they have continuous embeddings optimized to exploit the machinery of the model as much as possible
to serve the target task.

The utility of soft prompts as a metric arises from that optimization pressure squeezing the
soft prompt token embeddings:
once the optimizer reaches a minimum in the loss landscape for a given set of token embeddings
across a training distribution, that number of tokens represents the amount of

information-as-conditions
necessary to elicit the achieved behavior
for that model in that distribution.
3
3

3

The information density of the soft prompt embeddings
with respect to the model also depends on the optimizer’s ability to exploit the model.
For example, not all hyperparameters are equally effective at optimizing soft prompts,
and training may exhibit path dependence.

If a model achieves the target behavior with a single token soft prompt,
the behavior is trivially accessible and “natural” to the model. If it takes many tokens,
the behavior is distant, and if no number of tokens is sufficient to induce the target behavior,
the behavior may not be supported by the interface of the model exposed to the input embeddings.

Given a task performance threshold
T
(here expressed in terms of a loss function
L
),
a task training distribution
D
, a model
M
, the
conditional distance

C

in terms of the number of tokens
n
is defined as:

\text{C}(M,D,T)=\min_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{n\mid\text{L}(M,D,n)\leq T\}

(1)

In the absence of an obvious threshold,
monitoring the performance with respect to additional soft prompt tokens can
reveal a natural threshold where additional tokens do not meaningfully improve
performance. This will be referred to as the distance to
conditional saturation
,
S
:

\text{S}(M,D)=\min_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{n\mid\text{L}(M,D,n+1)-\text{L}(M,D,n)\leq\epsilon\}

(2)

where
\epsilon
is a chosen threshold for a meaningful improvement.

Given the lack of meaningful binary thresholds in the tested tasks,
conditional saturation will be the primary metric used.

3.2
Conditional Soft Prompts

Conditional soft prompts are a simple extension of the standard soft prompt.
Rather than directly optimizing the soft prompt embeddings, the embeddings are the product
of an input model which maps task-relevant conditions to soft prompt embeddings.
The parameters of the input model are then optimized.
As an example, contrast
Figure 1
and
Figure 2
.

Figure 1:
Unconditional soft prompt embeddings are provided to the model directly,
bypassing input token ids and the usual embedding process. The parameters composing
the soft prompt are optimized directly. Here, the soft prompt’s embeddings are inserted in the middle of the
sequence between tokens 1 and 2, but in principle they can appear anywhere.

Figure 2:
Conditional soft prompt embeddings are created from an input model that maps
task-relevant conditions to soft prompt embeddings. The input model’s parameters are
optimized.

3.2.1
Input Model Architecture

The input model used for all experiments is a simple feedforward network with a single hidden layer, as follows:

1.

A linear layer with input dimension equal to the number of conditions and output dimension
equal to the size of the hidden layer. In all experiments, the hidden layer size is 128.

2.

A Mish
(
Misra, 2020
)
activation function.

3.

A linear layer with input dimension equal to the hidden layer and output
dimension equal to the size of an input embedding.

Each soft prompt token has a unique input model.

3.2.2
Prompt Equivalence

Conditional soft prompts can often be emulated by the combination of an unconditional soft prompt
and some additional standard input tokens in the same way that, in principle, some discrete prompt
often exists that elicits behavior similar to that observed with a soft prompt.
This paper does not investigate the relative effectiveness of the two approaches for fine-tuning behavior;
instead, the primary goal of conditional soft prompts as proposed is to make it easier to build evaluations
with arbitrary conditions without needing to format them as a traditional prompt.

3.4
Tasks

The following section enumerates the tasks used in this paper’s evaluations.

3.4.1
Autoregressive Prediction

Exactly mirroring autoregressive language model training, the autoregressive prediction task
optimizes a soft prompt to minimize the loss of the model’s future token predictions.

This task is intended to demonstrate a case where soft prompting
cannot elicit improvements in performance. In other words, because the model has already been
aggressively optimized for the exact same task, it may be surprising if there
existed an accessible prompt that improved performance.

Some paths by which soft prompts could improve autoregressive performance include:

•

Most commonly, the model’s original training distribution and the
soft prompt’s training distribution could be different. The soft prompt could
condition the model to match the new distribution.

•

The model may contain machinery that is able to make use of the additional tokens
as computational space.
4
4

4

Lanham et al. (2023)
previously examined the
effect of filler tokens on model performance and found no performance improvements.
By adversarially optimizing against the model, soft prompts may be more effective in
some architectures that have reason to contain such machinery.

•

The model may not, by default, perform autoregressive prediction as well as it could.
This may arise for mundane reasons like fine-tuning, but in the limit of powerful systems
and without an alternative explanation, it could be an extremely concerning red flag:
the model may have learned a subtly different task than autoregressive prediction.

Given that all goal-oriented behavior within a distribution not aligned with an
autoregressive objective necessarily manifests as a bias in the model’s outputs
relative to that distribution, soft prompts’
ability to adversarially force a model to reveal more of its autoregressive capabilities
could be a useful tool for detecting or characterizing the divergence.
5
5

5

It is unclear how an autoregressive language model with a
current
architecture
could persist in “sandbagging” prediction without the behavior being optimized
away during training. The requisite degree of resistance to adversarial optimization—
that is, the autoregressive gradients fighting the misaligned implementation—may
not actually be possible.
If such a thing were possible, it is also possible that soft prompts trained with
the same approach could fail to optimize.

3.4.2
Repetition

Language models often implement basic circuitry to detect and repeat trivial patterns,
like induction heads
(
Olsson et al., 2022
)
. To demonstrate a case where soft prompts
should be able to easily harness even a small model’s machinery with few tokens,
the repetition task prompts the model to repeat a sample string indefinitely.
Strings of varying length are tested to check for differences in conditional distance.

3.4.3
Token Skipping

Instead of predicting the next token in the sequence, the token skipping task prompts the model
to predict the token
N
steps ahead.

3.4.4
Reverting Fine-tuning

As a proxy for jailbreaking a model, a soft prompt is trained to revert a model
fine-tuned for chat back to its original pure autoregressive prediction behavior.

TinyLlama

(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
is used for this task. To adjust for the shift
in distribution between the original training set and RedPajama v2, both the pretrained and
chat-tuned models are trained on the autoregressive task with RedPajama v2.
The performance of the soft prompted pretrained model is then compared against the
soft prompted chat-tuned model.

Unlike the other Pythia-only unconditional tasks, this task’s use of TinyLlama requires
randomly initialized soft prompt embeddings:
each soft prompt token has values drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 0.1.

3.4.5
Pathfinding

The pathfinding task requires predicting a sequence of tokens corresponding to the moves from point
A

from point
B
. The model is provided the tokenized board state as input, and the soft prompts are
given the number of extra steps and invalid moves as conditions.

A small example sequence the model may train on could look like:

A O O
O B O
O O X
Moves:
E W N E S D

where ”O” represents empty space and ”X” represents a blocker.
The move sequence is given in directions
E
,
W
,
S
, and
N
,
representing cardinal directions.

D
is output when
B
is reached. Here, the turn sequence includes three wasted turns and one attempt
to move off the game board; the wasted turn count and invalid move count will be given to the soft prompt
as input conditions.

Another example:

O O A
X X X
B X O
Moves:
U

Here, there is no way to reach
B
from
A
, so the output is correctly
U
for “unsolvable”.

This test is similar to using offline reinforcement learning to train pathfinding,
but breaks down the reward signal to offer finer control.

Success on this task implies that the model has sufficient latent world modeling capabilities
that it can track the state of the game board over the course of moves.

3.4.6
Chess Prediction

The chess prediction task is a simple autoregressive prediction task on a chess dataset.
Sequences are pulled from the Lichess database
(
Lichess, 2023a
)
and converted to
long algebraic notation (e.g.
e2e4 e7e6 d2d4 f8e7
) before being handed to the model.

Player Elos are used as conditions to the soft prompt.

3.5
Evaluation

For simplicity, prompts are trained with shared hyperparameters:

Learning rate:

0.001

Weight decay:

0.0001

Batch size:

32 or 256

All tasks except for repetition use 32 batch lanes. Repetition uses 256 batch lanes.

Target token counts per batch lane vary between tasks.
These values are not optimal for all models and tasks, but provide sufficient stability for training.

Training durations vary between tasks. No task runs for even one full epoch; the datasets are large.

Results will be reported in terms of losses and token counts to find an approximate point
of conditional saturation. Losses are computed in a post-training evaluation on parts
of the dataset not seen during training. All tasks will be evaluated for 256 batches.

5
Conclusion

The results demonstrate the usefulness of soft prompts in quantifying the
accessibility of capabilities at small scales, and show promise for larger scales
with greater compute resources.

Even within the limitations of this paper, tasks with dramatically different distances
to conditional saturation were identified. The simpler cases, like repeating a single
token, were shown to be easily accessible to the model while more complex tasks
(with respect to the model), like chess, continued to benefit from hundreds of tokens.

A partially successful reversion of fine-tuning also further supports the idea that
fine-tuning is often a shallow process that can be removed by mere input without
modifying any model weights. Soft prompts seem to be a promising tool for
characterizing the defensive robustness of models.

Soft prompts as demonstrated in this paper are not a panacea for evaluating the
capabilities of language models; they have limitations that often require researcher
correction or interpretation.

1.

Training can be sensitive to hyperparameters, and the necessary hyperparameters
may vary with task and prompt length.

2.

Tasks which may require a very large soft prompt to elicit a behavior depend
on the model being able to attend to all the soft prompt tokens with high fidelity.

3.

Longer prompts can require longer training times to converge;
early stopping can be misleading, and it isn’t always clear how much more room
to improve a prompt has.

4.

Confounders, like the distributional shift between datasets,
can sometimes make it difficult to interpret the results of a soft prompt evaluation.

In practice, while preparing experiments for this paper, compute limitations were the most
significant obstacle to obtaining results. Most tasks could not be trained to
convergence, and hyperparameters remained underexplored.

Ideally, organizations with the resources to train frontier models could integrate
soft prompt evaluations or similar strategies into a broader suite of evaluations
to monitor model robustness and capabilities.

6
Impact Statement

This research introduces a method using soft prompts to evaluate the conditional distance
between AI models and target capabilities, potentially including extreme cases like
self-replication and deception. The findings contribute to the field of AI safety and
offer a tool for assessing both the robustness of models against jailbreaking and
the accessibility of more dangerous capabilities in AI systems.
This work could enhance AI safety and ethical practices as it provides a means to identify
potential risks in AI development.
The methodology presented could be used in a broader suite of evaluations and act as a lever
for policymakers and AI developers in creating safer, more reliable AI systems.
