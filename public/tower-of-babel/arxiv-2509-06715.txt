On the Schur Stability of Some Image Reconstruction Operators
Debraj Banerjee, Kunal N. Chaudhury

Abstract
We investigate an open problem arising in iterative image reconstruction. In its general form, the problem is to determine the stability of the parametric family of operators P ⁡ ( t ) = W ⁡ ( I − t ​ B ) P(t)=W(I-tB) and R ⁡ ( t ) = I − W + ( I + t ​ B ) − 1 ​ ( 2 ​ W − I ) R(t)=I-W+(I+tB)^{-1}(2W-I) , where W W is a stochastic matrix and B B is a nonzero, nonnegative matrix. We prove that if W W is primitive, then there exists T > 0 T>0 such that the spectral radii ϱ ⁡ ( P ⁡ ( t ) ) \varrho(P(t)) and ϱ ⁡ ( R ⁡ ( t ) ) \varrho(R(t)) are strictly less than 1 1 for all 0 < t < T 0<t<T . The proof combines standard perturbation theory for eigenvalues and an observation about the analyticity of the spectral radius. This argument, however, does not provide an estimate of T T . To this end, we compute T T explicitly for specific classes of W W and B B . Building on these partial results and supporting numerical evidence, we conjecture that if B B is positive semidefinite and satisfies certain technical conditions, then ϱ ⁡ ( P ⁡ ( t ) ) , ϱ ⁡ ( R ⁡ ( t ) ) < 1 \varrho(P(t)),\,\varrho(R(t))<1 for all 0 < t < 2 / ϱ ⁡ ( B ) 0<t<2/\varrho(B) . As an application, we show how these results can be applied to establish the convergence of certain iterative imaging algorithms.

Introduction

We study a linear algebra problem motivated by iterative image reconstruction. We begin by stating the problem, highlighting the main difficulties, and explaining why existing results do not apply. We then present our results along with a conjecture. Finally, we explain how these findings connect to the convergence of iterative algorithms.

Throughout, we work in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some fixed
n\geqslant 1
. We let
I\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the identity matrix,
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the all-ones vector, and
\varrho(M)
the spectral radius of
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,

\varrho(M)=\max\big\{|\lambda|:\ \lambda\in\sigma(M)\big\},

where
\sigma(M)
denotes the spectrum (set of eigenvalues) of
M
. We say
M
is Schur stable (or simply
stable
) if
\varrho(M)<1

[
8
]
. For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
v\geqslant 0
if all components of
v
are nonnegative, and
v>0
if all components are strictly positive. For
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we write
M\geqslant 0
if all its entries are nonnegative.

We call
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
row-stochastic (or simply stochastic) if
W\geqslant 0
and
We=e
, and doubly stochastic if, in addition,
e^{\top}W=e^{\top}
. We say
W
is irreducible if, for every
i,j
, there exists
m\geqslant 1
such that
(W^{m})_{ij}>0
, and primitive if a single such
m
works for all
i,j
. If
W
is an irreducible stochastic matrix, then it follows from the Perron-Frobenius theorem that
\varrho(W)=1
and
1
is a simple eigenvalue. The vector
e
is a right eigenvector, and there exists a positive left (Perron) eigenvector
\pi
such that
\pi^{\top}e=1
. Moreover, if
W
is primitive, then
|\lambda|<1
for
\lambda\in\sigma(W),\lambda\neq 1
(e.g., see,
[
15
]
).

Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a stochastic matrix, and let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a nonzero matrix. For
t\geqslant 0
, consider the family of matrices

P(t)=W(I-tB),

(1)

and

R(t)=I-W+(I+tB)^{-1}(2W-I),

(2)

where we assume that the inverse
(I+tB)^{-1}
exists. The problem is to estimate
\varrho(P(t))
and
\varrho(R(t))
for different values of
t
, subject to additional assumptions on
W
and
B
.

Problem 1.1
.

Under what assumptions on
W
and
B
, do there exist
t_{1},t_{2}>0
such that
P(t_{1})
and
R(t_{2})
are stable? More generally, can we find
T>0
such that
P(t)
and
R(t)
are stable for all
0<t<T
?

Standard techniques for estimating the spectral radius include bounds based on matrix norms, Gershgorin’s theorem, Gelfand’s formula, the Collatz–Wielandt formula, and the Bauer–Fike theorem
[
15
]
. However, these tools are difficult to apply to matrix products. For instance,
W
and
I-tB
can each have spectral radius
1
, yet their product can still be stable. Conversely, even if both
W
and
I-tB
are stable, their product need not be stable. In what follows, we collect some observations relevant to
1.1
.

Remark 1.1
.

The problem of bounding the spectral radius of matrix products has been studied in the literature, but the existing results are limited and do not directly apply to
1.1
. For instance,
[
2
,
12
]
addresses only nonnegative matrices. While
W\geqslant 0
,
I-tB
may contain negative components. There are also Varga-type theorems on the stability of matrix products
[
20
,
17
]
. In particular, we can rewrite (
1
) as

P(t)=-FV^{-1},\quad F:=W,\,V=-(I-tB)^{-1},

where
(I-tB)^{-1}
exists and is nonnegative for small
t
. Varga’s theorem guarantees the stability of
-FV^{-1}
provided that both
F+V
and
V
are Metzler (i.e., have nonnegative off-diagonal components), and if
V
is Hurwitz. However, in our case, neither
V
nor
F+V
is Metzler, so Varga’s criterion does not apply. Similarly, (
2
) can be expressed as

R(t)^{\top}=-FV^{-1}\,,\qquad F:=(I-W^{\top})(I+tB^{\top})+(2W^{\top}-I),\ V:=-(I+tB^{\top}).

In this case, V also fails to be Metzler.

Remark 1.2
.

Since
P(0)=R(0)=W
and
\varrho(W)=1
, we cannot use the continuity of the spectral radius to conclude that
P(t)
and
R(t)
are stable in a neighbourhood of
0
. On the other hand,
P(t)
is not stable for large
t
. Indeed, if
WB
is not nilpotent, we have from the continuity of
\varrho
that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\>\varrho(P(t))=\lim_{t\to\infty}\>t\,\varrho\left(\frac{1}{t}W-WB\right)=\infty.

As for
\varrho(R(t))
, predicting its behavior at infinity is not possible without further assumptions on
B
.

Remark 1.3
.

Since
We=e
, it follows from (
1
) that
P(t)\,e=e
if
Be=0
.
Consequently,
\varrho(P(t))\geqslant 1
for all
t>0
. Thus, it is necessary that
Be\neq 0
for
P(t)
to be stable for some
t>0
. Moreover, even if
Be\neq 0
,
P(t)
may fail to be stable if
W
is not primitive. For example, consider the stochastic matrix

W=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

This is not primitive. If we choose
B=W
, then

P(t)=W(I-tW)=W-tW^{2}=W-tI.

A simple calculation shows that the eigenvalues of
P(t)
are
-(1+t)
and
1-t
; hence,
P(t)
is not stable for any
t>0
.

The condition
Be\neq 0
is also necessary for the stability of
R(t)
. Indeed, if
Be=0
, then
(I+tB)^{-1}e=e
, which implies
R(t)\,e=e
; hence,
R(t)
cannot be stable. As with
P(t)
, if
W
is not primitive, stability cannot be guaranteed. For instance, let
W
be as in (
3
) and take

B=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

In this case,

R(t)=\frac{1}{2(t+1)}\begin{pmatrix}-t&t+2\\
t+2&-t\end{pmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
-1
and
1/(t+1)
. Hence,
R(t)
is not stable for any
t>0
.

Our main result is that, in addition to the stochasticity of
W
, the above necessary conditions on
W
and
B
are (almost) sufficient to resolve
1.1
. This comes as a consequence of a more general result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
W
be primitive and stochastic, and let
B
satisfy
\pi^{\top}\!Be>0
, where
\pi
is the left Perron eigenvector of
W
. Then there exists
T>0
such that

P(t)
and
R(t)
are stable for all
0<t<T
.

We have the following corollaries of
Theorem

1.1
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
W
be primitive and stochastic, and let
B
be such that
Be\geqslant 0
and
Be\neq 0
. Then there exists
T>0
such that
P(t)
and
R(t)
are stable for all
0<t<T
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
W
be primitive and stochastic, and let
B\geqslant 0
with
B\neq 0
. Then there exists
T>0
such that

P(t)
and
R(t)
are stable for all
0<t<T
.

Theorem

1.1
is proved in
Section

3
using complex analytic techniques, specifically the perturbation theory of eigenvalues
[
11
]
. That complex analysis comes up is not surprising since
P(t)
and
R(t)
are generally nonsymmetric and can have complex eigenvalues.

Remark 1.4
.

We illustrate the importance of the condition
\pi^{\top}Be>0
in
Theorem

1.1
. Let

W=\frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix}7&3\\
6&4\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B=\frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix}34&-65\\
-65&126\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

The matrix

is primitive and stochastic, with
\pi^{\top}=(2/3\ \ 1/3)

, and we find that
\varrho(P(t))>1
and
\varrho(R(t))>1
for all

.

Remark 1.5
.

Perturbation theory does not provide an estimate for

To get an idea about how large

W=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0.7\\

Clearly,

Theorem 1.2

(

Let

In fact, a stronger result was established in

Theorem

A natural question is whether symmetry is necessary in

1.2
. Note that if

is symmetric and stochastic, it is automatically doubly stochastic. In fact,

1.2
can be formulated under this weaker assumption.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
W
be doubly stochastic such that both
W^{\top}W
and

are irreducible, and let
B
be positive semidefinite with

. Then
P(t)
and

are stable for all

Note that

1.2

1.3

is doubly stochastic, and

Theorems

1.2

Proposition 1.1
.

Let

Remark 1.6

Under the assumptions of

2
Application

We now apply the stability results from the previous section. As observed in
[
1
]
, these results provide convergence guarantees for certain iterative algorithms in image reconstruction. For completeness and to place the stability results in a broader context, we briefly outline the main ideas.

The operators (
1
) and (
2
) come from the plug-and-play method, which uses smoothing operators to regularize ill-posed imaging problems
[
19
]
. The plug-and-play framework itself is grounded in convex optimization algorithms, such as proximal gradient descent (PGD) and the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
3
]
. The origins of these algorithms can, in turn, be traced to operator splitting methods
[
9
,
14
]
and, further still, to the numerical solution of partial differential equations
[
7
,
18
]
.

The operators
P(t)
and
R(t)
are derived from PGD and ADMM in the setting of linear inverse problems
[
5
]
, namely problems of the form
Ax=b
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the reconstructed image,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the forward operator (or imaging model), and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the observed image. Since
b
is typically noisy and
A
is ill-conditioned, the inversion is performed by solving the regression problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\>J(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|^{2},

(9)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a quadratic loss function.

Remark 2.1

(properties of
A
)
.

Depending on the application, the forward operator
A
takes different forms
[
5
]
. In inpainting,
A
is a diagonal matrix with components in
\{0,1\}
; in deblurring,
A=H
is a circulant stochastic matrix; and in superresolution,
A=SH
, where
H
is as in deblurring and
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a subsampling matrix formed by selecting
m
rows from the identity matrix
I\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. In particular,
A
may be assumed to be nonzero in these applications.

The regression problem (
9
) can be solved using gradient descent, with iterations given by

x^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}-t\,\nabla\!J(x^{(k)}),\ k\geqslant 0,

(10)

where
t>0
is the step size. Substituting the loss function from (
9
) yields

x^{(k+1)}=(I-tB)\,x^{(k)}+tA^{\top}b\qquad(B:=A^{\top}\!A).

Remark 2.2

(properties of
B
)
.

The matrix
B=A^{\top}\!A
is always positive semidefinite. For inpainting, deblurring, and superresolution, it follows from
Remark

2.1
that
A\geqslant 0
, and consequently so is
B
. Furthermore,
Be\neq 0
, since
Be=0
would imply
Ae=0
, which is not possible as the measurement of a constant-intensity image (represented by
e
) cannot be zero. Thus,
B
satisfies the assumptions in the results in
Section

1
.

It is well known that the reconstruction obtained through linear regression is typically of poor quality. Thus, some form of
regularization
is required to obtain high-quality images. The plug-and-play framework achieves this by applying a denoiser after each gradient step. Specifically, if a linear denoiser
W
is used, the iterations (
10
) take the form

x^{(k+1)}=W\left(x^{(k)}-t\,\nabla\!J(x^{(k)})\right)=P(t)\,x^{(k)}+tWA^{\top}b,

(11)

where
P(t)
is as defined in (
1
). A well-known result is that the sequence
\{x^{(k)}\}
generated using (
11
) converges if
P(t)
is stable
[
15
]
. This explains the origin of
1.1
and its connection to the stability considerations in
Section

1
. It was demonstrated in
[
19
]
that excellent results are obtained if the linear denoiser
W
is modeled on the non-local means filter
[
6
]
. We briefly remark on the construction of
W
and its mathematical properties, which motivate the specific assumptions on
W
in
Section

1
.

Remark 2.3

(kernel denoiser)
.

The linear denoiser
W
is constructed from a kernel matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
K_{ij}
quantifies the proximity between pixels
i
and
j
. The precise form of
K
is not essential in the present context; we only require that
K
is nonnegative, primitive, and positive semidefinite. The denoiser
W
is obtained by row-normalizing
K
, producing a stochastic, primitive matrix. However, the normalization typically makes
W
nonsymmetric. We refer to this construction as a kernel denoiser, which falls within the class of
W
considered in
Section

1
.

A procedure for constructing a symmetric kernel denoiser from
K
was proposed in
[
19
]
, resulting in a matrix
W
that is symmetric, stochastic, and positive semidefinite. In this case,
W
can be interpreted as the proximal operator of a convex potential
[
16
]
, and the convergence of (
11
) then follows directly from standard optimization theory
[
3
]
. However, this argument does not yield a convergence rate. It was later shown in
[
1
]
that linear convergence can be established since the operator
P(t)
in (
11
) is contractive in this setting.

To our knowledge, establishing convergence for a nonsymmetric
W
is an open problem. This also has computational implications, as constructing the symmetric form of
W
in
[
19
]
is more expensive, even though it delivers comparable reconstruction quality.
Importantly, such symmetrizations can result in worse reconstructions in some cases.
These considerations motivated us to revisit the problem of establishing convergence without requiring
W
to be symmetric.

Building on the above discussion, we state the convergence results for the plug-and-play iterations in (
11
).

Corollary 2.1
.

Suppose
W
is a kernel denoiser and
A

3.1
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

In this part, we work with complex-valued matrices and vectors and follow the notation in
[
10
]
. We write
v^{*}
for the complex conjugate of
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. If
\lambda\in\sigma(M)
is simple (algebraic multiplicity
1
), then there exist
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
(left eigenvector) and
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
(right eigenvector) such that
v^{*}M=\lambda v^{*},\,Mu=\lambda u,
and
v^{*}u=1
. We call
\lambda\in\sigma(M)

dominant
if
\varrho(M)=|\lambda|
and if
|\mu|<\varrho(M)
for all
\mu\in\sigma(M),\,\mu\neq\lambda
.

We denote by
D_{r}(0)
the open disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ |z|<r\}
around the origin. A map
M:D_{r}(0)\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is said to be
analytic
if each component
M_{ij}(z)
is analytic (complex differentiable) on
D_{r}(0)

[
11
]
.

To apply perturbation theory of eigenvalues, we extend the domain of (
1
) and (
2
) by replacing the real parameter
t
in (
1
) and (
2
) with a complex parameter
z
. Specifically, we define the maps
P:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
R:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
by (reusing the same notation for simplicity)

P(z)=W(I-zB),\qquad R(z)=I-W+(I+zB)^{-1}(2W-I),

(12)

where
R
is defined in a neighborhood of the origin. We can view (
1
) and (
2
) as restrictions of (
12
) to the real line.

Proposition 3.1
.

The functions in (
12
) are analytic around the origin, and

P^{\prime}(0)=-WB,\qquad R^{\prime}(0)=-B(2W-I).

(13)

Proof.

The function
P(z)=W-zWB
is analytic on the entire complex plane, with derivative
P^{\prime}(z)=-WB
. On the other hand, we have

R(z)=I-W+G(z)^{-1}(2W-I),\qquad G(z):=I+zB.

The function
G(z)
is analytic with
G^{\prime}(z)=B
. Since
G(0)=I
is invertible, there exists
r>0
such that
G(z)
is invertible on
D_{r}(0)
. Moreover, its inverse
G(z)^{-1}
is analytic on
D_{r}(0)
with derivative (e.g., see
[
13
, Chapter 9]
)

\frac{d}{dz}G(z)^{-1}=-G(z)^{-1}G^{\prime}(z)\,G(z)^{-1}=-(I+zB)^{-1}B\,(I+zB)^{-1}.

(14)

It follows that
R(z)
is differentiable on
D_{r}(0)
, with derivative

R^{\prime}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}G(z)^{-1}(2W-I).

Combining this with (
14
), we get the formula for
R^{\prime}(0)
.
∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
M(z)
be a
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
-valued map that is analytic in a neighborhood of the origin. Suppose
M(0)
has a simple, dominant eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
with left and right eigenvectors
v
and
u
such that
v^{*}u=1
. Then there exists a neighborhood of the origin on which the function
\varrho(z)=\varrho(M(z))
is analytic, and

\varrho^{\prime}(0)=v^{*}M^{\prime}(0)\,u.

(15)

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{0}
is a simple eigenvalue of
M(0)
, there exists
r>0
and an analytic function
\lambda:D_{r}(0)\to\mathbb{C}
such that (e.g., see
[
10
, Theorem 1]
)

\lambda(0)=\lambda_{0},\quad\lambda^{\prime}(0)=v^{*}M^{\prime}(0)u,

(16)

where
u
and
v
are as defined in
Lemma

3.1
. Moreover, we know that
\varrho(0)=\lambda_{0}
, where
\lambda_{0}
is an isolated eigenvalue of
M(0)
. Since the eigenvalues of
M(z)
depend continuously on the components of
M(z)
and since
M(z)
is continuous, the eigenvalues depend continuously on
z
. Consequently, by shrinking
r
if necessary, we have
\varrho(z)=\lambda(z)
for all
z\in D_{r}(0)
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Note that
P(z)
and
R(z)
in (
12
) satisfy the assumptions of
Lemma

3.1
. Moreover,
P(0)=R(0)=W
, where
W
is assumed to be primitive and stochastic. Consequently,
\lambda_{0}=1
is a simple, dominant eigenvalue of both
P(0)
and
R(0)
, with corresponding left and right eigenvectors
v=\pi
and
u=e
such that
\pi^{\top}\!e=1
. It then follows from
Lemma

3.1
that the functions
\varrho(P(z))
and
\varrho(R(z))
are analytic in a neighborhood of
0
. Also as
\pi^{\top}Be>0
, we obtain from
Proposition

3.1
that

\frac{d\varrho(P(z))}{dz}\,\bigg|_{z=0}=\pi^{\top}P^{\prime}(0)\,e=-\pi^{\top}WBe=-\pi^{\top}\!Be<0,

and

\frac{d\varrho(R(z))}{dz}\,\bigg|_{z=0}=\pi^{\top}R^{\prime}(0)\,e=-\pi^{\top}B\,(2W-I)\,e=-\pi^{\top}\!Be<0.

In particular, this implies that there exists
T>0
such that
\varrho(P(t))
and
\varrho(R(t))
are strictly decreasing on the interval
(-T,T)
. Since
\varrho(P(0))=\varrho(R(0))=1
, the claim follows.
∎

3.2
Proof of
Theorem

1.3

In this section,
\|v\|_{2}
denotes the standard Euclidean norm of
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and
\|M\|_{2}
denotes the spectral norm of
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. We know that
\varrho(M)\leqslant\|M\|_{2}
for any
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, with equality if
M
is Hermitian
[
15
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be positive semidefinite,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
, and
t>0
. Then
C:=\lambda I+t(\lambda-1)B
is invertible and
\|C^{-1}\|_{2}\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

The eigenvalues of
C
are of the form
\lambda+t(\lambda-1)\mu
for some
\mu\in\sigma(B)
. Since
B
is positive semidefinite,
\mu\geqslant 0
. If
C
were singular, we would have
\lambda+t(\lambda-1)\mu=0
for some
t>0
. However, this implies
\lambda=t\mu/(1+t\mu)
, so that
|\lambda|=\lambda<1
, contradicting our assumption. Hence,
C
must be invertible.

Since
C^{-1}
is Hermitian, we have
\|C^{-1}\|_{2}=\varrho(C^{-1})
. The eigenvalues of
C^{-1}
are of the form
1/(\lambda(1+\mu t)-\mu t),\,\mu\in\sigma(B)
, and

|\lambda(1+\mu t)-\mu t|\geqslant|\lambda|(1+\mu t)-\mu t\geqslant 1+\mu t-\mu t=1.

(17)

Hence,
\varrho(C^{-1})\leqslant 1
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
W
be doubly stochastic. Then
\|Wv\|_{2}\leqslant\|v\|_{2}
for all
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Moreover, if
W^{\top}W
is irreducible, then equality holds only if
v
is a scalar multiple of
e
.

Proof.

Since both
W^{\top}
and
W
are stochastic, we have
\varrho(W^{\top}W)\leqslant\varrho(W^{\top})\varrho(W)=1
. Hence, for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\|Wv\|_{2}^{2}=v^{*}(W^{\top}Wv)\leqslant\varrho(W^{\top}W)\|v\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\|v\|_{2}^{2}.

Now, suppose
\|Wv\|_{2}=\|v\|_{2}
for some
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We can write that is

v^{*}Sv=v^{*}v,\qquad S:=W^{\top}W.

(18)

Since
S
is positive semidefinite and
\varrho(S)\leqslant 1
, we have
\sigma(S)\subset[0,1]
. Combining this with (
18
) and using the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices, we can deduce that
Sv=v
.
Finally, because
S
is stochastic and irreducible, it follows that
v=ce
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Since
I-tB
is symmetric and
B
is positive semidefinite, we have for all
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
,

\|I-tB\|_{2}=\varrho(I-tB)\leqslant 1.

(19)

Combining this with
Lemma

3.3
, it follows that for all
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
,

\varrho(P(t))\leqslant\|P(t)\|_{2}\leqslant\|W\|_{2}\|(I-tB)\|_{2}\leqslant 1.

To complete the proof, we must show that if
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
, then
|\lambda|<1
for all
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
.
Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
with
|\lambda|=1
. Then, for some left eigenvector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\lambda^{*}v=P(t)^{\top}v=(I-tB)W^{\top}v.

(20)

Taking norms on both sides and using (
19
), we get
\|v\|\leqslant\|W^{\top}v\|
. Since
W^{\top}
is doubly stochastic and
WW^{\top}
is assumed to be irreducible, it follows from
Lemma

3.3
that
v=ce
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Substituting this into (
20
) yields

\lambda^{*}e=(I-tB)W^{\top}e=e-tBe.

(21)

Thus,
(1-\lambda)/t\in\sigma(B)
. Since
B
is symmetric, the only possibilities are
\lambda=\pm 1
. However, if
\lambda=1
, we have
Be=0
from (
21
), which contradicts our assumption. On the other hand,
\lambda=1
yields
Be=(2/t)e
, so that
2/t\leqslant\varrho(B)
. This contradicts the condition
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
. Thus, we must have
|\lambda|<1
for all
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
.

We will prove using contradiction that
\varrho(R(t))<1
whenever
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
. Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists
\lambda\in\sigma(R(t))
with
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
. Then there exists a right eigenvector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\lambda v=(I-W)v+(I+tB)^{-1}(2W-I)v,

(22)

where the inverse exists for
t>0
. Rearranging gives
(I-tB)Wv=Cv
, where
C
is as defined in
Lemma

3.2
. Since
t>0
,
Lemma

3.2
guarantees that
C^{-1}
exists, so that

v=C^{-1}(I-tB)Wv.

(23)

Taking norms on both sides and using (
19
) together with the bound
\|C^{-1}\|_{2}\leqslant 1
from
Lemma

3.2
, we obtain

\|v\|_{2}\leqslant\|Wv\|_{2}
. Since
W
is doubly stochastic, it follows from
Lemma

3.3
that
\|v\|_{2}=\|Wv\|_{2}
. Moreover, as
W^{\top}W
is irreducible,
Lemma

3.3
implies that
v=ce
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Substituting this into (
22
) yields

e=\lambda e+t\lambda Be.

(24)

The rest of the argument is similar to that for
P(t)
. It follows from (
24
) that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, so that
\lambda=\pm 1
. However,
\lambda=1
yields
Be=0
, and
\lambda=-1
yields
\varrho(B)\geqslant 2/t
, both of which contradict our assumptions. Therefore, we must have
|\lambda|<1
for all
\lambda\in\sigma(R(t))
.
∎

3.3
Proof of
Proposition

1.1

We need the following notations. For
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we define
|v|\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
|M|\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
by taking absolute values componentwise. For
M,N\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we write
M\leqslant N
to mean
M_{ij}\leqslant N_{ij}
for all
i,j
. Finally, for
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we write
\mathrm{diag}(v)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
for the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the components of
v
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
W\geqslant 0
be irreducible with
\varrho(W)=1
. Suppose
p\geqslant 0
satisfies
p\leqslant Wp
. Then
p=Wp

and
p>0
.

Proof.

See
[
4
, Chapter 2]
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
W\geqslant 0
be irreducible with
\varrho(W)=1
. Let
D\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a diagonal matrix with
|D|\leqslant I
. Suppose there exists
v\neq 0
such that either
WDv=v
or
DWv=v
. Then necessarily
|D|=I
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists
v\neq 0
such that
WDv=v
. Let
p=|v|
. Since
|D|\leqslant I
, we have

p=|v|=|WDv|\leqslant W|D|p\leqslant Wp.

(25)

Because
p\geqslant 0
, it follows from
Lemma

3.4
that
p=Wp
and
p>0
. Substituting this into (
25
) yields

p=W|D|p.

(26)

Multiplying (
26
) by the left Perron vector of
W
gives
\pi^{\top}p=\pi^{\top}W|D|p=\pi^{\top}|D|p
, so that
\pi^{\top}(I-|D|)p=0
. As
\pi,p>0
and
|D|\leqslant I
, this forces
|D|=I
. An identical argument applies if
DWv=v
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

1.1
.

Suppose there exists
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
with
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
, so that
\lambda v=P(t)v
for some nonzero
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Since
\lambda\neq 0
, we can write this as

v=WDv,\qquad D:=\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-tB).

Let
B=\mathrm{diag}(b)
, where
b\geqslant 0
and
b\neq 0
by assumption. Because
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
, we have
|D|\leqslant I
whenever
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
. By
Lemma

3.5
, this forces
|D|=I
. Since
b\neq 0
, there exists
i
such that
b_{i}>0
. For this
i
, we cannot have
|1-tb_{i}|=|\lambda|
, because
|1-tb_{i}|<1
for
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
. This shows we cannot have
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
for any
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
.

Similarly, suppose
\lambda\in\sigma(R(t))
with
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
, and
v\neq 0
so that (
23
) holds. Since
B=\mathrm{diag}(b)
, we can write

v=DWv,\qquad D:=\mathrm{diag}(d),\>d_{i}=\frac{1-tb_{i}}{\lambda+t(\lambda-1)b_{i}}.

(27)

From (
17
), it follows that for
t>0
,

|\lambda+t(\lambda-1)b_{i}|\geqslant 1.

(28)

Moreover if
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
, then
|1-tb_{i}|\leqslant 1
. Hence, from (
27
), we obtain
|D|\leqslant I
. By
Lemma

3.5
, this forces
|D|=I
. However, we know that
b_{i}>0
for some
i
. For such as
i
,

|\lambda+t(\lambda-1)b_{i}|=|1-tb_{i}|<1,

(29)

which contradicts (
28
). Thus, we must have
|\lambda|<1
for any
\lambda\in\sigma(R(t))
.
∎

3.4
Proof of
Proposition

1.2

We begin by noting that if
B=\alpha I+\beta E
, then
\alpha,\alpha+n\beta\in\sigma(B)
. Since
B
is positive semidefinite and
Be\neq 0
, it follows that
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\alpha+n\beta>0
. Hence,

\varrho(B)\geqslant\max\big(\alpha,\alpha+n\beta\big).

(30)

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
. Then there exists a left eigenvector
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\lambda u^{*}=u^{*}W(I-tB).

(31)

Multiplying on the right by
e
gives
\lambda u^{*}e=(1-\alpha t-n\beta t)\,u^{*}e
. Thus, either
\lambda=1-t(\alpha+n\beta)
or
u^{*}e=0
. In the first case, it follows from (
30
) that
|\lambda|<1
for all
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
. In the second case, since
EW^{\top}u=0
, taking the complex conjugate of (
31
) yields

\lambda^{*}u=(I-tB)W^{\top}u=(1-\alpha t)W^{\top}u.

(32)

If
1-\alpha t=0
, then trivially
|\lambda|<1
. Otherwise,
\lambda^{*}/(1-\alpha t)\in\sigma(W^{\top})
. We consider the following cases:

•

If
\alpha>0
, then as
\varrho(W^{\top})\leqslant 1
, it follows from (
30
) that
|\lambda|\leqslant|1-\alpha t|<1
for all
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
.

•

If
\alpha=0
, then (
32
) reduces to
\lambda u^{*}=u^{*}W
. Suppose
|\lambda|=1
. Because
W
is stochastic and primitive, this forces
u=c\pi,c\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\pi
is the left Perron vector of
W
. However, since
u^{*}e=0
and
\pi^{\top}e>0
, we get
c=0
, contradicting the assumption that
u\neq 0
.

Thus, in all cases,
|\lambda|<1
. Since this holds for any
\lambda\in\sigma(P(t))
, we conclude that
\varrho(P(t))<1
.

Now, let
\lambda\in\sigma(R(t))
. Then there exists a nonzero
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\lambda\,u^{*}=u^{*}R(t)=u^{*}(I-W)+u^{*}(I+tB)^{-1}(2W-I).

(33)

Right-multiplying (
33
) by
e
gives

\lambda\,u^{*}e=(1+\alpha t+n\beta t)^{-1}\,u^{*}e.

Hence, either
\lambda=1/(1+(\alpha+n\beta)t)
or
u^{*}e=0
. In the first case, since
\alpha+n\beta>0
, we have
|\lambda|=\lambda<1
for all
t>0
. In the second case, taking the complex conjugate of (
33
) yields

\lambda^{*}u=(I-W^{\top})u+(2W^{\top}-I)(I+tB)^{-1}u.

Since
u^{*}e=0
, we have
(I+tB)^{-1}u=u/(1+\alpha t)
. Substituting this, we obtain

\lambda^{*}u=\frac{1}{1+\alpha t}\big(\alpha t\,I+(1-\alpha t)W^{\top}\big)u.

From this, we conclude that

\lambda=\frac{\alpha t+(1-\alpha t)\mu^{*}}{1+\alpha t}

for some
\mu\in\sigma(W^{\top})
. As before, since
W
is primitive and stochastic and
u^{*}e=0
, we must have
|\mu|<1
. If
\alpha=0
, then
\lambda=\mu^{*}
, so that
|\lambda|<1
. On the other hand, if
\alpha>0
, then for any
0<t<2/\varrho(B)
we have from (
30
) that
0<\alpha t<2
. Thus,

|\lambda|=\frac{|\,\alpha t+(1-\alpha t)\mu\,|}{1+\alpha t}\ \leqslant\ \frac{\alpha t+|1-\alpha t|\,|\mu|}{1+\alpha t}\ <\ \frac{\alpha t+|1-\alpha t|}{1+\alpha t}<1.

Therefore, in all cases,
|\lambda|<1
. Since this holds for any
\lambda\in\sigma(R(t))
, we conclude that
\varrho(R(t))<1
.
