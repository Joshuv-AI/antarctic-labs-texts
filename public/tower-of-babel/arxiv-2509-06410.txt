Verifying Sampling Algorithms via Distributional Invariants
Kevin Batz, Joost-Pieter Katoen, Tobias Winkler, Daniel Zilken

Abstract
This paper develops a verification framework aimed at establishing the correctness of discrete sampling algorithms. We do so by considering probabilistic programs as distribution transformers. Inspired by recent work on distributional verification of Markov models, we introduce the notion of (inductive) distributional loop invariants for discrete probabilistic programs. These invariants are embedded in a Hoare-like verification framework that includes proof rules for total and partial correctness. To illustrate the applicability of our framework, we prove the correctness of two discrete sampling algorithms: the Fast Dice Roller and the Fast Loaded Dice Roller.

For

For a sub-distribution

\mu

Fixed Point Theory.

A sequence of sub-distributions
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
ascending
if
\mu_{n}<\mu_{n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a sequence is called an
\omega
-chain
if
\mu_{n}\leq\mu_{n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The relation
\leq
is to be understood componentwise, i.e.
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}
holds iff
\mu_{1}(\sigma)\leq\mu_{2}(\sigma)
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
. Similarly, the
supremum
of an
\omega
-chain is to be understood componentwise, i.e.,
(\sup(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})(\sigma)=\sup\{\mu_{n}(\sigma)~|~n\in\mathbb{N}\}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(\sigma)
.

A
fixed point
of a function
f\colon D\rightarrow D
is an element
d\in D
such that
f(d)=d
. A
least
fixed point is a fixed point
d
of
f
with
d\sqsubseteq d^{\prime}
for any fixed point
d^{\prime}
of
f
, with respect to a partial order
\sqsubseteq
on
D
. Recall that a partial order is a reflexive, transitive and antisymmetric binary relation. A subset
S\sqsubseteq D
is called a
chain
if, for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in S\colon s_{1}\sqsubseteq s_{2}
or
s_{2}\sqsubseteq s_{1}
. An element
d\in D
is called an upper bound of
S
if
s\sqsubseteq d
for all
s\in S
. The least upper bound (supremum) of
S
, denoted
\bigsqcup S
or
\sup S
, is an upper bound
d
of
S
such that for every upper bound
d^{\prime}
of
S
, we have
d\sqsubseteq d^{\prime}
. A partial order on
D
is called
chain complete
(CCPO) if each of its chains has a least upper bound in
D
.

A function
F\colon D\rightarrow D^{\prime}
between partial orders
D
and
D^{\prime}
respectively is called monotonic if, for every
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
with
d_{1}\sqsubseteq d_{2}
it holds that
F(d_{1})\sqsubseteq^{\prime}F(d_{2})
. A function
F
is called continuous
[
38
]
if, for every non-empty chain
S\subseteq D
, it holds that
F(\bigsqcup S)=\bigsqcup F(S)
. The fixpoint theorem by Kleene gives us insights about the least fixed point of a function.

Theorem 2.1
(Kleene,
[
38
]
)

Let
(D,\sqsubseteq)
be a CCPO and
F\colon D\rightarrow D
continuous. Then the least fixed point of
F
is

\mathsf{lfp\,}F~\coloneqq~\bigsqcup\{F^{n}(\bigsqcup\emptyset)~|~n\in\mathbb{N}\}~.

Reachable Distributions.

Distributional invariants for probabilistic loops are defined (formally in
Section

4
) as overapproximations of
“all the reachable distributions”
.
Intuitively, this is the collection of all distributions that are reached after performing some finite number of loop iterations on some initial distribution.
Formally, the set of all reachable distributions in a probabilistic loop is given by the
\mathsf{reach}
-transformer.
The
\mathsf{reach}
-transformer maps a set of initial (sub-)distributions
M
to the set of all (sub-)distributions reachable from
M
.

Definition 4
(Reachable Distributions)

Let

be a loop and
M\subseteq{\Delta_{\leq 1}\Sigma}
.
The distributions

in
C
from
M
are defined as

\mathsf{reach}\llbracket C\rrbracket(M)~\coloneqq~\mathsf{lfp\,}X.~\,M~\cup~\mathsf{sp}\llbracket\textnormal{{if}}\,\textnormal{{(}}\varphi\textnormal{{)}}\,\textnormal{{\lx@text@lbrace}}B\textnormal{{\lx@text@rbrace}}\rrbracket(X)~.

Intuitively, the
\mathsf{reach}
-transformer
collects all distributions reached from some initial distribution in
M
after some finite number of loop iterations.
By Kleene’s fixed point theorem (

), we obtain an alternative characterization of the
\mathsf{reach}
-transformer:

Corollary 1

For every loop

Towards Practical Invariant Verification

Before we apply our verification techniques that we developed in the previous sections to some practical examples, the goal of this section is to summarize how schematic verification with our techniques works and to give some further techniques that ease the verification.

When given a probabilistic loop
C=\textnormal{{while}}\,\textnormal{{(}}\varphi\textnormal{{)}}\,\textnormal{{\lx@text@lbrace}}B\textnormal{{\lx@text@rbrace}}
and a set
M
of initial distributions, the first step towards verifying the output distributions of
C
wrt.
M
is in general to find a candidate set
I
of sub-distributions and then to prove
I
to be a distributional invariant wrt.
C
and
M
. One practical way to prove
I
to be an invariant is, to show that 1)
M\subseteq I
, and that 2)
I
is inductive, that is, for all
\mu\in I
, it holds that
\mathsf{D}\llbracket\textnormal{{if}}\,\textnormal{{(}}\varphi\textnormal{{)}}\,\textnormal{{\lx@text@lbrace}}B\textnormal{{\lx@text@rbrace}}\rrbracket(\mu)\in I
. Regarding 2), in practice it is often hard to find a simple and intuitive expression for the post distribution
\mathsf{D}\llbracket\textnormal{{if}}\,\textnormal{{(}}\varphi\textnormal{{)}}\,\textnormal{{\lx@text@lbrace}}B\textnormal{{\lx@text@rbrace}}\rrbracket(\mu)
.

Injective Assignments.

In the following, we present a simplification technique that can be applied to
“injective”
assignments. These are assignments
x\,\textnormal{{:=}}\,E
, such that for two different program states
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
, it always holds that
\sigma[x/E(\sigma)]\neq\sigma^{\prime}[x/E(\sigma)]
.

Substitution.

We define a
substitution
operator for sub-distributions.

Definition 12
(Substitution)

Let
E\colon\Sigma\dashrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a partial function and let
x\in\mathit{Var}
.
For a function
f\colon\Sigma\to[0,1]
, we define the
substitution

f[x/E]~\coloneqq~\lambda\sigma.\begin{cases}f(\sigma[x/E(\sigma)])&\textnormal{if }E(\sigma)\textnormal{ is defined}\\
0&\textnormal{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It follows from the definition that substitution preserves pointwise
\cdot
and
+
.
Notice that substitution is not only defined for distributions but also for functions of the form
[\varphi]
where
\varphi
is a predicate, in which case it holds that
[\varphi][x/E]=[\varphi[x/E]]
where
\varphi[x/E]
results from
\varphi
by
syntactically replacing
6
6

6

Assuming a concrete syntactic form of
\varphi
(e.g., as a first-order formula) is given.
all free occurrences of
x
in
\varphi
by
E
.
If
\mu\in{\Delta_{\leq 1}\Sigma}
, then
\mu[x/E]
is a distribution assigning to state
\sigma
the probability that
\mu
assigns to the updated state
\sigma[x/E(\sigma)]
(if
E(\sigma)
is defined; otherwise
\mu[x/E]
assigns
0
to
\sigma
).
For example, for the Dirac distribution
\delta_{x\mapsto 1}
we have
\delta_{x\mapsto 1}[x/x+1]=\delta_{x\mapsto 0}
.
Substitution is not mass preserving:
For instance,
\delta_{x\mapsto 1}[x/2x]=0
because there is no
x\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(\frac{a_{1}}{m},\dots,\frac{a_{n}}{m})
.

Purposely, the FLDR does not achieve an optimal runtime (= number of coin flips): the key motivation behind the FLDR is to eliminate the exponential memory needs that a standard optimal-runtime sampler has. The construction of the FLDR is based on the observation that an optimal-runtime algorithm
is
always memory efficient, in the case where
m
is a power of
2
. This observation is exploited by performing rejection sampling on the proposal distribution
(\frac{a_{1}}{2^{k}},\dots,\frac{a_{n}}{2^{k}},1-\frac{m}{2^{k}})
where
k=\lceil\log(m)\rceil
. In words, the FLDR adds an
(n+1)
-th side to the die and the probability to roll an
n+1
is the probability missing to reach the next power of
2
. If the
(n+1)
-th side is rolled, the program rejects it and simply returns to its starting state.

First, a preprocessing is done (
Figure

and a matrix

(an intuitive explanation of these is given in the next paragraph and an illustration is provided in

. Our given FLDR algorithm is a slightly rewritten variant of the FLDR as described by

]
that 1) obeys our precise syntax for probabilistic programs and 2) contains less branches inside the loop-body, making it more practical for our verification purposes. The while condition makes use of the short-hand notation
\phi_{h}(c)\coloneqq 2^{c}-\sum_{j=0}^{c}h[j]\cdot 2^{c-j}
, which intuitively represents the first position in row
c
of matrix
H
that contains a number other than
0
.

The FLDR is a direct implementation of the binary tree that underlies its proposal distribution
(\frac{a_{1}}{2^{k}},\dots,\frac{a_{n}}{2^{k}},1-\frac{m}{2^{k}})
. This is the tree that contains a leaf labelled with
i
in row
c
, iff the
i
-th entry of the tuple
(\frac{a_{1}}{2^{k}},\dots,\frac{a_{n}}{2^{k}},1-\frac{m}{2^{k}})
written as a binary number has a
1
at position
c
behind the comma (see
Example

10
). The leafs are then ordered from the largest to the smallest number (left to right). Based on the binary tree, the data structures

and

that result from the preprocessing algorithm can be explained intuitively:
h[c]
stores the number of leaves in row
c
of the binary tree and
H[d,c]
stores the output number
j
if in row
c
of the binary tree, the
d
-th node is
j
. Otherwise,

equals
0
.

Related Work

Finite-state Markov Models.

As mentioned earlier, this paper was primarily inspired by recent works viewing probabilistic models as transformers of probability distributions
[
4
,
2
,
3
,
1
]
.
However, these papers only consider
explicit, finite-state
Markov chains and extensions thereof, whereas the programs we study may generate
countably infinite Markov chains
or
infinite families
as in our case studies in
Section

7
.
Another technical difference is that we focus on the Markov chains’
limiting
distributions over terminal states and do not aim to verify e.g., safety or temporal properties over the stream of reachable distributions.

Probabilistic Hoare Logics.

Hoare rules for probabilistic loops based on distributional invariants have been previously studied
[
21
,
7
]
.
The key technical difference between our rules (
While-P
) and (
While-T
) from
Section

5
and those in prior work lies in the treatment of closedness: rather than requiring the
invariant
to satisfy a closedness condition, we apply a closure operation to the resulting
postcondition
.
Although this difference may appear minor from a mathematical standpoint, our formulation more closely aligns with the actual reasoning process:
Intuitively, the closure is necessary only because the semantics of a loop—required to satisfy the postcondition of a sound Hoare rule—is a
limit
of the reachable distributions (
Theorem

3.1
).
When reasoning about the reachable distributions themselves, no closedness condition is needed.
In this way, our
While
rules provide a meaningful and practical extension to existing distributional probabilistic Hoare logics.

Further Related Work.

Several other theoretical frameworks exist that are—at least in principle—applicable to random sampler verification, i.e., they address challenges
1
to
3
on page
1
.
A prominent example is the
weakest pre-expectation
(
wp
) calculus
[
30
,
24
,
27
]
, a generalization of Dijkstra’s classic weakest pre
condition
calculus
[
16
,
17
]
.
Using
wp
, the correctness of e.g., a uniform sampling program
C
is expressed as (variable
res
stores the result)

\textsf{wp}\llbracket C\rrbracket([res=i])~=~\tfrac{1}{n}\cdot[0\leq i<n]~.\quad\begin{minipage}[169.0519pt]{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\ltxml@oqmark@open\textquotedblleft\penalty\hskip-0.0002pt\hskip 0.0002ptOn termination, \\
\phantom{``}$\textnormal{Pr}[res=i]=\tfrac{1}{n}$ if $0\leq i<n$, else $0$.\textquotedblright\ltxml@oqmark@close{}
}
\end{minipage}

However, proving this equality requires guessing and verifying an
exact
least fixed point, which is notoriously difficult.
To our knowledge, no published
wp
-based proof exists for either of our two case studies.
A program analysis using
probability generating functions
(PGF) was proposed in
[
25
]
, but its application to our case studies appears highly challenging.
Indeed,
comparisons
between program variables (e.g.,
v<n
in the loop guard of the FDR algorithm) were identified as a fundamental obstacle to effective PGF-based reasoning
[
13
]
.
On the more practical side,
[
5
]
describes and implements
Zar
, a tool that automatically generates formally verified executable samplers in the random bit model from high-level probabilistic program specifications.

Dahlqvist, Silva, and Smith
[
15
]
propose a
type system
for verifying sampling algorithms built from a given set of pre-defined simpler deterministic samplers based on infinite streams.
It appears promising to investigate whether, e.g., the Fast Dice Roller (
Section

7.1
) can be verified assuming a pre-defined sampler for fair coin flips.
However, we conjecture that one would have to extend the type system of
[
15
]
by some form of unbounded iteration, which is currently not supported.

The related problem of characterizing the
posterior distribution
of probabilistic programs with
conditioning
constructs has also been extensively studied.
For instance, the tool
PSI

[
19
]
computes exact expressions for density functions but does not support unbounded while-loops
[
19
, Sec. 3.6]
, which are required by the F(L)DR algorithms.

On the other hand,
[
37
,
10
]
support such loops but only provide
approximations
(with precision guarantees) for the final distribution.

Additional related work includes methods for
testing
uniform random samplers with statistical guarantees
[
11
]
and a recent
equivalence refutation
technique for probabilistic programs, which can be used to prove
incorrectness
, i.e., the presence of a bug in a given sampling algorithm
[
12
]
.

9
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented a framework for verifying quantitative properties of probabilistic programs based on viewing programs semantically as distribution transformers. We introduced inductive distributional invariants, showed their correspondence to similar notions defined for Markov chains, and employed them in proof rules for total and partial correctness. We believe that our framework is particularly well-suited to prove the correctness of discrete sampling algorithms, which are key to a broad range of applications. Indeed, we provide formal correctness proofs for efficient samplers for discrete uniform distributions (FDR) and, more generally, arbitrary discrete finite-support distributions (FLDR).

For future work, we plan to investigate assertion languages for distributions which render reasoning with distributional invariants more syntactic and thus more amenable to automation. A particularly promising direction is to leverage the assertion language provided by Barthe et al.
[
7
]
, who also provide a weakest pre-condition-style approach for reasoning about reachable distributions. Additionally, detailed proofs for the almost sure termination of the FDR and the FLDR w.r.t. their respective invariants are left for future work, and would support the verification process of these two programs.
