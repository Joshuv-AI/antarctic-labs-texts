Machine Learning-Assisted Designing and Screening of Polymers with a High Melting Point: Database Visualization and Synthetic Feasibility Assessment
Authors: Zaheer
Ahmed Dayo, Jiang Guosong, Mohamed A El-Tayeb, Syed Shoaib Ahmad Shah, Sumaira Naeem
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, a novel approach leveraging machine learning (ML) techniques for the design and screening of polymers with high melting points is introduced. More than 40 ML models are trained for the prediction of the melting point. One best model is selected for further analysis. 10,000 polymers are generated using an automatic approach. The generated database of polymers is visualized and analyzed to find the hidden trends. Synthetic feasibility assessment is conducted to prioritize candidate polymers for future experimental work. Chemical similarity of chosen polymers is analyzed using cluster analysis and a heatmap. This research contributes to the advancement of polymer design methodologies, offering insights into the development of heat-resistant polymers for a wide range of industrial applications.

Introduction

The melting point holds significant importance in the realm of materials science, particularly in constructing phase diagrams and in the development of high-performance refractory materials.1 These materials play critical roles in various applications. However, unlike certain material properties that can be determined through straightforward calculations at absolute zero, determining the melting temperature is a complex and costly process. It involves intricate computations, particularly as it necessitates modeling the liquid phase and extensive configuration sampling. Consequently, calculating the melting temperature poses significant challenges, and our current capabilities within the computational community remain limited.

The prediction of material properties plays a pivotal role in various fields, ranging from pharmaceuticals to materials science, where accurate forecasts facilitate the design and optimization of novel materials with desirable characteristics.2,3 Among these properties, the melting point stands out as a fundamental thermodynamic parameter, influencing the material behavior across a spectrum of applications.

Conventional methods for predicting melting points often rely on empirical correlations, physical models, or experimental measurements.4 While these approaches have provided valuable insights, they can be time-consuming and resource-intensive and may lack accuracy, particularly for complex materials with intricate structures.

Recently, machine learning (ML) is revalorizing the field of material science by offering efficient and accurate alternatives for property prediction.5,6 ML algorithms, trained on data sets comprising structural features and corresponding melting points of diverse materials, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in capturing intricate relationships between molecular structures and material properties.

ML is useful for the design of polymers for material science. Xu et al. used ML to design thermosetting polymers with desirable properties.7 Ignacz et al. used ML to study the solvent effects in polyimide organic solvent nanofiltration membranes.8 Ignacz et al. used artificial intelligence for the prediction of solute rejection in organic solvent nanofiltration.9 Tao et al. used high-throughput screening and ML to find multifunctional polyimides.10 They created a database of 8 million hypothetical polyimides. Martí and co-workers predicted the glass transition temperature of biopolymers using ML.11 The ML model is trained on a data set of 546 polymers that are generated using molecular dynamics simulations, and reasonable accuracy is obtained.

The current research explores various ML models to forecast the melting points of polymers. Among these models, the extra-tree regressor demonstrates superior performance. A comprehensive polymer database is constructed, and rapid ML models are utilized for predicting melting points. Database visualization is done by using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and structure activity landscape index (SALI) analysis. Additionally, the synthetic accessibility score of the designed polymers is computed.

Computational Methods

Machine Learning Analysis

The data of the melting point of organic compounds are taken from a published paper.12 Data contain over 340 data points. The distribution of the melting point (Tm) is depicted in Figure 1. RDKit is employed to compute 200 descriptors.13 RDKit descriptors include several types of molecular descriptors such as constitutional descriptors, which provide basic information such as atom count, molecular weight, and the number of bonds. Topological descriptors capture the molecule’s connectivity and structure, like the Wiener index and various shape indices. Electronic descriptors describe the distribution of charges within the molecule, such as partial charges and the polar surface area. Finally, geometrical descriptors offer insights into the 3D structure of molecules, although RDKit primarily focuses on 2D descriptors for most cheminformatics tasks. To select the descriptors for model training, various actions were taken, including the exclusion of descriptors with zero values and employing univariate regression analysis to identify the most relevant descriptors. Python-based packages such as Seaborn, Pandas, Numpy, Matplotlib, and Scikit-learn were employed for analysis and visualization. The data set was split into training and test sets using a 70:30 ratio, respectively.

Distribution of Tm (K) in the data set.

Monomer Design and Similarity Analysis

The material design using theoretical methods such as density functional theory calculations allows to design a limited number of compounds. A large number of compounds can be designed using a ML-assisted framework. For this purpose, RDKit, a suite of cheminformatics tools, can be used with ML. A tool in RDKit named as breaking retrosynthetically interesting chemical substructures (BRICS)14 is very helpful. It disconnects the complex chemical structures into simpler fragments. Then, these fragments were used to build more compounds. In the present study, polymers from a published paper15 are used as input. 10,000 polymers are generated. The generated database is analyzed to study hidden trends in the data. The melting points of generated polymers are predicted using the best ML model. Thirty polymers having high meting points are selected, and their synthetic accessibility is determined. Their chemical structural behavior is studied using a cluster plot and heatmap.

Regression Analysis

Regression analysis is a statistical method used to examine the relationship between one or more independent variables and a dependent variable.18,19 It aims to model the relationship and make predictions based on the observed data. Linear regression is the most basic form of regression analysis, based on the assumption of a linear relationship between the variables. However, other forms, such as polynomial regression and multiple regression, allow for more complex relationships to be captured. Various ML algorithms have unique strengths and weaknesses, excelling with certain types of data or under specific conditions. Trying multiple algorithms allows one to find the one that is most suitable for particular problem.

Lazy Predict (a Python library) is very helpful for the selection of ML models. It offers a convenient way for users to quickly assess the performance of multiple ML algorithms without the need for extensive manual coding or model configuration. With LazyPredict, users can automate the training, testing, and evaluation of a wide range of ML models. The results for tested ML model are depicted in Figure 3. From these results, extra trees regressor have appeared as best model. This model is chosen for further analysis (Figures 4 and 5).

Comparative assessment of the performance of diverse ML models on the test set. Residual plot for extra trees regressor is given in Figure 4. A residual plot in ML is a graphical representation of the differences between the observed values and the predicted values generated by a regression model. These differences, known as residuals, are the vertical distances between the actual data points and the regression line or curve fitted to the data. R2 values for training and test set are 0.979 and 0.860, respectively. Residual values range from –100 to about 200. A majority of points are near to the zero line. Figure 5 depicts a scatter plot comparing calculated and predicted values, showing points clustered closely around the standard line. When data points align closely with the line of best fit, it suggests that the model is likely to perform effectively, yielding predicted values that closely resemble the true values.

Designing New Polymers

Polymers wield significant influence over our daily lives, playing a pivotal role in various applications.20,21 While many polymers are naturally occurring, a multitude of synthetic variants have been developed in laboratories to capitalize on their diverse and beneficial physical attributes. Traditionally, the process of polymer research adhered to a consistent sequence: scientists would synthesize a novel polymer in the lab and subject it to testing to ascertain its physical characteristics such as melting point, elasticity, and tensile strength.22−24

The BRICS technique is employed in polymer design.14 Utilizing BRICS involves breaking down polymers into fragments, which are subsequently recombined according to predetermined rules to form new polymers. The process takes input in the form of SMILES notation extracted from the cited paper.15 A total of 10,000 polymers are generated, and a pretrained ML model is deployed to forecast the melting point of these generated polymers.

A t-SNE plot serves as a scatter plot used to visualize complex data in a lower-dimensional space, aiding in the identification of patterns and clusters within the data set and revealing its underlying structure. Widely employed in fields such as data science and ML, t-SNE is a powerful technique for reducing the dimensionality of data sets while retaining significant original information. Particularly valuable for analyzing and visualizing intricate data sets, t-SNE enables the projection of data into a lower-dimensional space, which is particularly useful for handling larger or more intricate data sets. The t-SNE plot is depicted in Figure 7. Polymers with lower, medium, and higher values are grouped together, which indicates that the melting point strongly depends on the chemical structure.

t-SNE plot.

The SALI provides a quantitative measure of activity cliffs within the structure–activity relationship.25 Activity cliffs denote pairs of closely related polymers with significant differences in activity, indicating that even minor structural changes can profoundly affect their activity. SALI operates under the assumption that activity cliffs appear as regions of increased local density in the structure–activity relationship landscape surrounded by regions of lower density. In this study, 1000 polymers with the highest melting point are used to generate the SALI plot. Figure 8 presents the SALI plot, highlighting substantial variations in the melting point with structural modifications.

Synthetic Accessibility

Synthetic accessibility refers to the ease with which a given chemical compound can be synthesized or manufactured in a laboratory setting. It encompasses various factors such as the availability of starting materials, the complexity of the synthetic route, the efficiency of chemical reactions involved, and the feasibility of purification processes.26,27 Synthetic accessibility is crucial before experimental synthesis because it assesses how feasible it is to produce a molecule using the available techniques and resources. By evaluating the complexity of a compound and its potential synthetic routes, researchers can prioritize compounds that are easier and less costly to synthesize, saving time and effort. This helps to avoid pursuing molecules that may be theoretically promising but impractical to create in a lab setting, ensuring a more efficient and cost-effective experimental process. A compound with a low synthetic accessibility score can be produced with relative ease, using readily available reagents and straightforward synthetic methods, while a compound with a high synthetic accessibility score may require more complex or labor-intensive procedures, limiting its practicality for large-scale synthesis. Assessing synthetic accessibility is crucial in drug discovery, materials science, and other fields, in which the efficient synthesis of new polymers is essential for research and development. Similarity Analysis

The link between chemical similarity and structure–activity relationship (SAR) lies in the understanding that structurally similar polymers often exhibit similar biological or physicochemical properties.28,29 SAR studies aim to elucidate how changes in the chemical structure of a compound influence its activity, such as its potency, efficacy, or toxicity. Chemical similarity metrics, such as the Tanimoto coefficient, Dice coefficient, or cosine similarity, quantify the likeness between two chemical structures based on their molecular fingerprints or descriptors.

By analyzing the SAR within a data set of structurally similar polymers, researchers can identify patterns and trends that reveal relationships between structural features and activity.30,31 This knowledge enables the prediction of the biological or physicochemical properties of new polymers based on their structural characteristics. Furthermore, SAR studies help in rational drug design by guiding the modification of chemical structures to optimize desired properties while minimizing undesired effects.32 Results of cluster analysis are given in Figure 11. Polymers are divided in several clusters. One cluster has a large number of polymers, and other clusters have a smaller number of polymers. All the clusters are joined after the distance of 0.4. This indicates that the selected polymers are structurally diverse in nature. The other way to represent the similarity analysis is a heatmap. The heatmap of similarity is given in Figure 12. Similarity values are between 0.65 and 1. This is long-range. This indicates that polymers are structurally diverse. So, high Tm values can be obtained from any structural entity.

Here, it is important to note that ML models may not achieve the same level of accuracy as experimental methods due to limitations in the training data and model assumptions. The availability of more high-quality experimental data can significantly improve the accuracy of ML models by allowing them to better capture the complex relationships between polymer structures and properties.

Conclusions

ML can be helpful for fast discovery of efficient materials. 42 ML models are trained for the prediction of the melting point. Extra trees regressor has appeared as best model with R2 values of 0.979 and 0.860 for training and test set, respectively. A database of 10,000 polymers is generated, and melting points of these polymers are predicted using a pretrained ML model. To explore the structural diversity of these polymers, various data visualization techniques are utilized. Evaluations of synthetic accessibility for the chosen polymers demonstrate their feasibility for synthesis. Through utilization of chemical similarity-based clustering, a significant level of structural diversity is identified among the selected polymers. This proposed framework serves as a valuable resource in the material design process, streamlining the identification of optimal polymers by integrating ML predictions, the exploration of structural diversity, and assessments of synthetic feasibility.
