Seasonal moderators of maternal CRP levels during pregnancy
Authors: Cindy X.W. Zhang, Justin Levinsky, Daphne Korczak, Patrick McGowan, Mark Wade, Julia A. Knight, Rayjean Hung, Kashtin Bertoni, Kellie Murphy, Stephen Lye, Stephen G. Matthews, Robert D. Levitan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe human immune system is highly susceptible to seasonal changes, however it is unknown whether this holds true during pregnancy. This study assessed whether C-Reactive Protein (CRP) levels are moderated by season of sample collection and/or a person's tendency to have low mood during the fall-winter months (Seasonal Affective Disorder or SAD) in a Canadian pregnancy and birth cohort. As overweight/obesity has been associated with both elevated CRP and fall-winter SAD, the moderating effect of overweight/obesity on these seasonal effects was also examined.MethodsMaternal plasma CRP was measured at weeks 11-19 and 24-30 of pregnancy. Separate Generalized Linear Models were used to predict these two CRP levels using a 2 season of collection (fall-winter vs. spring-summer) by 2 seasonal group (yes/no for lifetime SAD) by 2 pre-pregnancy BMI group (healthy weight vs. overweight/obese) design.ResultsA significant 3-way interaction predicted CRP levels at pregnancy weeks 24-30 (F = 8.49, df = 1, 353, p = .004), with the highest levels in mothers with the combination of fall/winter plasma sampling, meeting screening criteria for lifetime SAD, and being overweight/obese. The respective 2-way season of sampling by BMI group interaction was also highly significant (F = 8.18, df = 1, 353; p = .004). Unexpectedly, these various results were independent of state depression and maternal anti-depressant use. No seasonal differences were found for early pregnancy CRP levels.ConclusionsOverweight/obese mothers had greater fall/winter increases in later pregnancy CRP levels than did healthy weight mothers, further boosted by a maternal history of fall-winter SAD. Both environmental and individual-level seasonality may be critical considerations for future research on gestational inflammation.

Introduction

Pre-clinical research has shown that maternal immune activation in pregnancy induces epigenetic changes in the fetal brain (Woods et al., 2026; Ebenezer et al., 2026), and can cause structural brain changes of potential relevance to human neuropsychiatric disorders (Estes and McAllister, 2016; McEwan et al., 2023). In humans, high inflammation during pregnancy can increase the risk for neuro-psychiatric morbidity over the lifespan (Canetta et al., 2014; van der Burg et al., 2016; Gumusoglu and Stevens, 2019; Han et al., 2021; Suleri et al., 2025). Identifying both environmental and individual-level risk factors that contribute to maternal inflammation is thus a high priority for developmental research.

One basic environmental factor that is likely to influence inflammation during pregnancy is the change of seasons. In the general population, Dopico et al. (2015) showed that the human immune system has a profound pro-inflammatory transcriptomic profile during European winter, with the opposite pattern in the Southern Hemisphere. To date, only one study has examined whether inflammation changes across the seasons during pregnancy. In 323 pregnant European women over-sampled for depressive symptoms, Henriksson et al. (2019) found robust seasonal changes in 23 inflammatory markers sampled at week 38 of pregnancy, with most peaks occurring in the spring. Henriksson et al. noted that only one inflammatory marker showed seasonal variation in the post-partum period, suggesting that seasonal effects on the immune system may be amplified or unmasked during pregnancy itself.

If seasonality has an effect on inflammation during pregnancy, as suggested by Henriksson et al.’s findings, it would be important to know which particular mothers are most sensitive to these changes. Identifying individual level risk related to seasonality could inform novel interventions for at least a subset of pregnancies. Of note, in temperate northern climates such as Canada, many women experience one or more symptoms of Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD), a mood syndrome triggered by low light availability and characterized by low energy, increased appetite, hypersomnia and weight gain in the fall-winter months (Rosenthal et al., 1984a, 1987; Levitt et al., 2000). It has been suggested that SAD may be a vestigial evolutionary strategy to conserve energy during anticipated periods of low food availability (Levitan et al., 2006). This might have particular relevance to pregnancy given the high energy demands of gestation and childbirth (Eagles, 2004; Davis and Levitan, 2005). In support of this model, unique seasonal patterns of pregnancy and childbirth have been found in SAD (Pjrek et al., 2004). Our group has recently proposed a model whereby seasonal effects on pregnancy and fetal neurodevelopment are exaggerated in women meeting lifetime criteria for fall-winter SAD (Levitan et al., 2025). A key prediction of the model is that highly seasonal mothers will have greater seasonal changes in biomarkers relevant to fetal neurodevelopment than their non-seasonal peers, including inflammatory changes. To test this model empirically, the current study examined whether there are seasonal differences in maternal CRP levels across pregnancy in a Canadian birth cohort, and if such differences are exaggerated in mothers meeting lifetime screening criteria for fall-winter SAD. As increased body mass has been associated with both elevated CRP levels (Rawson et al., 2003; Choi et al., 2013), and fall-winter SAD (Rosenthal et al., 1987; Kräuchi et al., 1997), the potential moderating effects of high BMI on these seasonal variables was also examined.

To accomplish this, we used longitudinal data from the Ontario Birth Study, a naturalistic pregnancy and birth cohort in Toronto, Ontario, Canada (Anderson et al., 2018). The main study questions were as follows:1.Does season of sample collection influence maternal CRP levels during pregnancy, as it does in the general population (Dopico et al., 2015) ?2.If so, are these seasonal differences amplified in mothers meeting lifetime criteria for fall-winter SAD ?3.Are these putative seasonal effects on CRP expressed differently in overweight/obese vs normal weight mothers ?

Materials and methods

Study sample

The Ontario Birth Study (OBS) was initiated in 2013 as a prospective pregnancy and birth cohort focused on maternal and fetal health. Inclusion criteria include maternal age of at least 18 years, up to 17 weeks of gestation at study intake, ability to speak and understand English and having antenatal care at a participating OBS-affiliated hospital or obstetrical clinic. Exclusion criteria include an inability or unwillingness to provide informed consent, carrying a non-viable fetus or the presence of significant fetal abnormalities. Women who consent to be part of the OBS have biospecimen samples, lifestyle questionnaires and clinical data collected during their antenatal visits. Additionally, women have the option to enrol their infant into a longitudinal post-natal follow up study called OBS-Kids. A key goal of the OBS is to link maternal risk factors during pregnancy, such as high inflammation, with long term child development.

As part of their initial study intake, participants are asked to complete a lifestyle questionnaire that includes the Seasonal Pattern Assessment Questionnaire (SPAQ) (Rosenthal et al., 1984b) to assess maternal trait seasonality. Blood samples and state depression scores are collected in early and later pregnancy. The current sample consisted of 363 mothers who provided blood samples between January 2013 and January 2018 with a) an initial blood sample collected between 11 and 19 weeks of gestation (mean 14.4 ± 1.9 weeks) b) a later pregnancy blood sample collected between 24 and 30 weeks (mean 27.0 ± 1.3 weeks) and c) complete data available for pre-pregnancy body mass index, maternal trait seasonality and depressive symptoms in early and later pregnancy.

The current study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Boards of Mount Sinai Hospital and the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health, both located in Toronto, Ontario, Canada.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes for the current study were log transformed maternal C-reactive protein (CRP) levels sampled at pregnancy weeks 11-19 (earlier sample) and 24-30 (later sample).

Venous whole blood was collected into 10 mL EDTA vacutainer tubes and samples were centrifuged at 2000 RPM with low brake at 4°C for 10 min. Following centrifugation, the plasma layer was aliquoted into 1.8 mL vials using a Tecan Freedom EVO automated aliquoting system and subsequently stored at −80°C until assay. High-sensitivity plasma CRP concentrations were measured in duplicate using solid-phase sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). These data were collected on a fee-for-service basis offered at the Analytical Facility for Bioactive Molecules at The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Ontario, Canada. The Human C-Reactive Protein/CRP Quantikine ELISA Kit from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used due to its specificity to natural and recombinant human CRP, sensitivity (0.02 ng/mL), assay range (0.8-50 ng/mL), and <0.5% cross-reactivity with related molecules.

CRP was chosen based on its relatively long half-life compared to most cytokines and its reliability as an indicator of inflammation even when the interpretation of other proinflammatory markers is unclear (Karadag et al., 2008; Keane et al., 2021). Log transformed levels were used to correct for the left skewed nature of this data.

Independent variables

Sampling season

In a Canadian context, fall and winter are the highest risk seasons for both seasonal affective changes (Levitt et al., 2000) and for increased CRP levels (Dopico et al., 2015). Based on this, and to be consistent with our prior study of maternal SAD (Levitan et al., 2025), we merged fall and winter into a single putative “high risk” season running from October 1 through March 31, and spring and summer into a putative “low-risk” season running from April 1 through September 30.

Maternal seasonality

OBS mothers complete the Seasonal Pattern Assessment Questionnaire (SPAQ) (Rosenthal et al., 1984b) as part of an early pregnancy questionnaire. The SPAQ assesses long term seasonal changes (from 0 = “not at all” to 4 “extreme changes”) in six items (sleep length, social activity, mood, weight, energy level and appetite), generating a summed global seasonality score ranging from 0 to 24. The SPAQ further establishes at what time of year symptoms are most severe (Fall, Winter, Spring or Summer) and whether the participant considers them a problem. Based on these various items, lifetime case-finding criteria for both fully syndromal and sub-syndromal SAD have been established (designated SAD and s-SAD respectively- Kasper et al., 1989). While many seasonal patterns can be identified using this instrument, we limited our focus to women meeting established criteria for fall-winter SAD or s-SAD. This is the approach used in our recent paper based on this same cohort (Levitan et al., 2025). It is important to note that while the SPAQ assesses long term seasonal changes in symptoms, it was not designed to measure current mood symptoms per se, i.e., it is possible to meet screening criteria for lifetime SAD without having current depression.

As SAD and s-SAD are thought to exist along a single pathophysiological dimension (Kasper et al., 1989; Young et al., 1991), and based on our prior research on maternal seasonality in pregnancy (Levitan et al., 2020, 2025), we combined mothers with SAD and s-SAD into a single “SADS” group for all analyses.

Maternal BMI grouping

Maternal pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated from self-reported weight and height on the questionnaire administered in early pregnancy. Mothers with a pre-pregnancy BMI of 18.5-24.99 were designated as normal weight while mothers with a BMI of 25 or higher were designated as overweight/obese. To limit the heterogeneity of the sample, and following from both Lutsiv et al. (2015) and our own work (Levitan et al., 2025), we excluded 33 mothers who were designated as underweight (BMI <18.5). A preliminary analysis found that the standard error for later pregnancy CRP levels was twice as high in these underweight mothers compared to the other mothers, adding empirical support for their exclusion.

Maternal state depression as a potential mediator

At a conceptual level, seasonality and depression are overlapping but distinct phenomena (Young et al., 1991; Lam et al., 2001). To assess current maternal depression in the OBS protocol, the 2-item depression subscale of the 4-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4; Kroenke et al., 2009) is used. The PHQ-4 includes two items assessing depressive symptoms and two items assessing anxiety symptoms. The current analyses focused on the PHQ-2 depression subscale because the relevant clinical question was whether associations involving lifetime SADS were independent of concurrent depressive symptoms during pregnancy.

The 2 depression items assessed by the PHQ-2 are “feeling down, depressed or hopeless” and “little interest or pleasure in doing things”. The lead-in question for each item is: “Over the last two weeks, how often have you been bothered by the following?”. The possible responses are “not at all”, “several days”, “more than half the days” or “nearly every day” (scored as 0, 1, 2 and 3 respectively). These two depression scores are summed to establish a total score ranging from 0 to 6. For the current analyses, PHQ-2 total scores collected at weeks 10–19 and 28–32 of pregnancy were entered as covariates in sensitivity models to test whether associations between seasonality-related variables and CRP were independent of concurrent depressive symptom burden.

Statistical method

Descriptive variables and selection of potentially confounding covariates

To describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of the mothers, means with standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables (maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI and PHQ-2 depression scores) and percentages for categorical variables (parity, maternal education, marital status, and maternal anti-depressant use).

Each of the descriptive variables listed above (other than BMI group, PHQ-2 depression scores and maternal anti-depressant use, which were part of the planned analyses as described below) was considered as a further potential confounding covariate for the GLM analyses. To determine which covariates to control for in the final models, a series of preliminary univariate analyses using one potential covariate at a time were performed using the two CRP measures as separate dependent measures. Pearson correlations were used for continuous variables while ANOVA was used for categorical variables. Potentially confounding variables that were significantly associated with either CRP level in these preliminary analyses were included as co-variates in all subsequent analyses, for both CRP outcomes. We used this approach to best compare the regression results obtained for the two CRP measures.

Main analyses

Overall Analytical Design: To assess the association of seasonality with maternal CRP levels, we used generalized linear models that included both season of sample collection (fall-winter versus spring-summer) and maternal history of SAD (yes/no) as independent variables. Mothers who did not meet criteria for SAD were designated “Controls”. An important decision point was whether to include pre-pregnancy BMI as a third independent variable, generating interaction terms with the other independent variables, or simply control for it as a potentially confounding co-variate. Past research shows that BMI is itself influenced by both season of the year (Yanovski et al., 2000; Guerrero-Magaña et al., 2025) and by maternal seasonality (Rosenthal et al., 1987; Kräuchi et al., 1997). This suggests that maternal BMI is likely to be on one or more mechanistic pathways linking seasonality with CRP levels. To address this, it was decided a priori that BMI grouping (normal vs. overweight/obese) would be included as a third independent variable rather than as a potential confounder in our main analyses.

Because the design was unbalanced across SADS, BMI, and season-of-collection subgroups, all factorial models were estimated using Type III sums of squares. This approach tests each main effect and interaction after adjustment for the other terms in the model and is appropriate for unbalanced designs (Searle, 2006).

Given that season of sample collection will typically differ between early and late pregnancy samples, we ran separate models for the early and later maternal CRP levels. We also ran the models twice, first without controlling for state depression, and a second time controlling for both early and later maternal depression scores. The goal was to establish whether any putative effect of maternal SAD history on CRP levels was dependent on state depression scores during pregnancy since one can meet lifetime criteria for SAD without being currently depressed.

Sensitivity analysis based on maternal anti-depressant use

To assess whether maternal anti-depressant use influenced the current results, we repeated the main analyses above while adding maternal anti-depressant use (yes/no) to the model.

Sensitivity analysis based on maternal infections in late pregnancy

In the OBS protocol, common maternal infections during pregnancy are assessed at weeks 28-32 using separate yes/no questions for “sore throat”, “colds” and “flu”. To assess whether these confounded the main findings related to season of sample collection and/or maternal seasonality, a further sensitivity analysis was completed adding these three variables as covariates.

Selection of Co-variates

Preliminary univariate ANOVAs indicated that maternal education was significantly associated with both early and later pregnancy logCRP levels (F = 7.26, df = 2, 360; p < .001 and F = 3.63, df = 2360; p = .028 respectively). For early pregnancy, pairwise post-hoc testing indicated that mothers with a college degree or equivalent had higher levels compared to mothers with a bachelor's degree or higher (logCRP = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.67-0.94 vs. 0.54, 95% CI = 0.49-0.59 respectively, p < .001). A similar pairwise difference in the same direction was found at the later pregnancy time point (logCRP = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.62-0.88 vs. 0.56, 95% CI = 0.51-0.61 respectively, p = .007).

Single mothers had higher logCRP levels than did other mothers in early pregnancy (logCRP = 1.0, 95% CI = 0.69-1.35 vs. 0.57, 95% CI = 0.52-0.61 respectively; F = 7.13, df = 1, 361, p = .008) and in later pregnancy (logCRP = 1.1, 95% CI = 0.78-1.42 vs. 0.57, 95% CI = 0.53-0.62 respectively; F = 10.10, df = 1, 361, p = .002).

Based on these results, maternal education and relationship status were included as co-variates in all subsequent analyses.

Given the wide range of data collection times for the early pregnancy plasma sample (gestational weeks 11-19), which was due to pragmatic issues around participant availability, we ran a preliminary analysis to determine if week of gestation at the time of this sampling influenced maternal CRP levels. A moderate positive correlation between week of gestation and logCRP in early pregnancy was found (r = 0.11, p = .042). To address this, we controlled for week of gestation at sampling in the MANCOVA predicting logCRP early in pregnancy. There was no significant correlation between gestational week at sample collection and CRP levels for the late pregnancy sample. As a result, we did not correct for gestational week when analyzing the later pregnancy CRP levels.

Lack of mediation by maternal depression

As shown in Table 2, controlling for state depression scores in the main analysis did not change any of the significant findings described above. Inclusion of state depression scores did change the main effect of season of blood collection from borderline non-significant to borderline significant effect at p = .045.

Sensitivity analysis with maternal anti-depressant use

Preliminary univariate analysis revealed no significant association between maternal anti-depressant use and either early pregnancy logCRP levels (F = 0.12, df = 1,361, p = .73) or later pregnancy logCRP levels (F = 3.30, df = 1,361, p = .07). When anti-depressant use was included in the main MANCOVA analysis, the three-way season of collection by SADS group X BMI group interaction, and the two-way season of collection by BMI group interaction, remained significant predictors of late pregnancy logCRP levels (F = 8.25, df = 1352; p = .004 and F = 8.08, df = 1 352, p = .005 respectively).

Sensitivity analysis including maternal infections in late pregnancy

There were 143 mothers who reported sore throat, 229 who reported having a cold, and 39 reporting the flu in later pregnancy. Preliminary univariate analysis revealed no significant association between any of these three maternal conditions and later pregnancy logCRP levels (F = 2.69, df = 1,358, p = .10 for sore throat, F = 1.99, df = 1,358, p = .16 for cold, F = 0.19, df = 1,358, p = .89 for flu). When these maternal infections were included in the main MANCOVA analysis as potentially confounding co-variates, the three-way season of collection by SADS group X BMI group interaction, and the two-way season of collection by BMI group interaction, remained significant predictors of late pregnancy logCRP levels (F = 8.51, df = 1347; p = .004 and F = 7.75, df = 1 347, p = .006 respectively).

Discussion

Outside of pregnancy, the human immune system has a clear pro-inflammatory profile during European winter (Dopico et al., 2015). Using data from a naturalistic Canadian pregnancy and birth cohort, the current study assessed whether maternal CRP levels vary by season during pregnancy, and whether this is moderated by a maternal history of fall-winter SAD. As increased body mass has been associated with both elevated CRP levels (Rawson et al., 200; Choi et al., 2013), and fall-winter SAD (Rosenthal et al., 1987; Kräuchi et al., 1997), the potential moderating effects of high BMI on these seasonal variables was also examined.

Several significant findings related to season of sample collection and/or maternal SAD history were found. A significant 3-way interaction predicted CRP levels at weeks 24-30 of pregnancy, with the highest levels in fall/winter samples from mothers in the SAD group having a BMI >25. For mothers in the SAD group with a normal BMI, a different seasonal pattern was found i.e. for these mothers, spring-summer sampling was associated with higher CRP levels than found in the two control subgroups defined by season of sampling. This suggests that amongst mothers with lifetime SAD, the normal and high BMI subgroups may have different inflammatory phenotypes that may be driven by different mechanisms. The 2-way season of sampling by BMI interaction was also highly significant, showing that fall-winter sampling was associated with higher CRP levels only in higher BMI mothers. Unexpectedly, these various results were independent of maternal state depression and anti-depressant use during pregnancy, and no seasonal associations were found for early pregnancy CRP levels. Taken as a whole, these various results highlight the potential importance of considering both sampling season and maternal SAD history, in addition to maternal BMI, when studying inflammation during pregnancy.

While the significant 3-way interaction predicting later pregnancy CRP levels must be considered as preliminary due to the small number of participants in the highest risk group, it is noteworthy that the specific pattern of results is consistent with separate lines of research demonstrating increased inflammation in the fall/winter months (Dopico et al., 2015), in individuals with SAD (Leu et al., 2001), and in overweight/obese individuals (Choi et al., 2013). The one discrepancy (vs. Leu et al., 2001) is that the current association linking maternal history of SAD with higher CRP levels was independent of state depression. Of note, Henriksson et al. (2019) reported a seasonal rhythm in 11 inflammatory markers in 226 non-depressed mothers sampled in very late pregnancy. Taken together with the current results, this suggests that seasonal moderation of inflammation by both the time of year and maternal seasonality is not limited to mothers with current depression.

Prior research has consistently demonstrated a very strong effect of BMI on various measures of inflammation (Rawson et al., 2003; Choi et al., 2013), which remains robust during pregnancy (Sominsky et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). A separate line of work has shown that several inflammatory markers, including CRP, are significantly higher in the fall-winter months (Dopico et al., 2015). The current data suggest that these lines of work may be related mechanistically i.e., that the association between higher BMIs and CRP levels may be greater in the fall-winter months. More work is needed to confirm these initial results both within and outside the context of pregnancy, and at different geographic latitudes.

There are several potential mechanisms that might contribute to fall-winter increases in inflammation, both within and outside of pregnancy. Perhaps the two most important are seasonal infections, which are very common in a Canadian context, and photoperiodic mechanisms that drive circadian variation of the immune system to match predicted environmental conditions (Dopico et al., 2015). Of note in the current analysis, when we included common maternal infections as potential confounding variables for the week 24-28 logCRP levels, the main findings related to seasonal effects remained robust. This, and the well established link between SAD, weight gain and photoperiodism (Rosenthal et al., 1984a, 1987), suggests the latter as a potential mechanism for the seasonal effects on CRP found currently.

The main strength of the current study was the inclusion, for the first time, of an individual level variable (maternal SAD history) to identify mothers most likely to be affected by broader seasonal effects on inflammation. Going forward, this could help to identify higher risk pregnancies while informing novel interventions such as bright light therapy (Parry et al., 2023). The use of longitudinal measurement of maternal CRP at two distinct time points during pregnancy, which allowed us to demonstrate that only later pregnancy CRP levels were susceptible to seasonal effects, was a further strength. In terms of limitations, the overall sample size was modest, particularly to study interaction effects, and the unequal group sizes limits statistical power and the interpretation of subgroup analyses. The current sample was highly educated, and the majority of mothers were married or living common-law. While this might limit the confounding effects of family and socio-economic stress on maternal CRP levels, the current findings may not be generalizable to more vulnerable populations. Furthermore, as the current study focuses on seasonal patterns associated with European winter conditions, the findings may not be generalizable to other geographical regions. For pragmatic reasons we used CRP on its own as a marker of maternal inflammation, and further analysis of other inflammatory markers will be needed to further elucidate the pathways underlying our results.
