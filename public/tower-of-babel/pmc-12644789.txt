Neonatal seizure detection from EEG using inception ResNetV2 feature extraction and XGBoost optimized with particle swarm optimization
Authors: Nazanin Nemati, Saeed Meshgini, Tohid Yousefi Rezaii, Reza Afrouzian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal seizure detection is crucial for preventing neurological damage, as these seizures are linked to abnormal brain activity. While machine learning and deep learning show promise for EEG analysis, persistent challenges remain, including signal non-stationarity, class imbalance, and limited interpretability. We propose a hybrid deep framework for early neonatal seizure detection. The method uses discrete wavelet transform (DWT) to decompose EEG signals into subbands and short-time Fourier transform (STFT) to create time-frequency spectrograms. Inception-ResNetV2 extracts deep features from these spectrograms, which are then classified by an XGBoost model. This model is fine-tuned offline using particle swarm optimization (PSO) to enhance performance. This hybrid approach tackles key challenges by combining powerful deep representations with a fast, explainable classifier. This design specifically addresses signal non-stationarity and class imbalance while minimizing computational load and preventing overfitting. The framework’s performance was evaluated on the Helsinki Neonatal EEG Dataset, which contains data from 79 term neonates, including 460 expert-annotated seizures. Our model achieved an average accuracy of 98.75%, with 98.56% precision, 98.36% sensitivity, and 98.91% specificity, outperforming most existing methods. Using medium analysis windows further improved accuracy to 99.82%, with some cross-validation runs reaching 100%. Subband analysis highlighted the importance of theta and delta bands in the frontotemporal regions for early detection. The integration of Inception-ResNetV2 and PSO-optimized XGBoost provides significant performance gains while offering insights into the role of EEG subbands in seizure manifestation. This framework is a practical solution for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) monitoring, offering a robust foundation for a clinical decision-support system to enable rapid and accurate seizure detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25361-1.Keywords: Epilepsy, EEG signals, Neonatal seizures, Hybrid convolutional network, Deep learning, Spectrogram image, Inception-ResNet structure, Decision-making systemSubject terms: Biological techniques, Neuroscience, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Engineering

Introduction

Epilepsy affects more than 70 million people worldwide1, making it one of the most common serious brain diseases. Seizures can cause memory loss, loss of consciousness, hallucinations, or even loss of movement balance that affects the entire body2. A brief period of abnormal electrical activity occurs in the brain during an epileptic episode3. Genetic mutations may alter brain activity, transmission, or even decision-making in epileptic seizures. There are several potential causes of this disease, including head trauma and stroke4. Epileptic seizures can be treated more effectively if they are diagnosed early in the course of the disease. In spite of these interpretations, epilepsy and seizures have been studied in a wide range of people, although there have been few studies identifying epilepsy in infants5–7.

Epilepsy-related seizures in infants are caused by specific neurological disorders8,9. Many newborns exhibit epilepsy symptoms during the first few months of their lives10. Compared to children and adults, the average period of epilepsy occurrence in infants is clearly shorter. Epilepsy in infants can be diagnosed using analyses of multi-channel EEG signals and clinical evaluations, according to recent research11–13. Aside from the invasive nature of some diagnostic methods, accurate interpretation requires time, effort, and significant expertise, which are lacking in many neonatal intensive care units. In this way, an automatic method that analyzes brain signals can be used to diagnose seizures in infants in an accurate and highly reliable way. As the brain signal contains many complexities, and the proposed models in this field face various challenges in diagnosing epilepsy in infants14, diagnosing seizures in these neonates is challenging.

Even though machine learning and deep learning have been effective in the automatic analysis of brain signals, conventional methods are not as effective in identifying brain signals15. Recently, automatic machine learning methods have become increasingly popular for diagnosing epilepsy and related disorders in adults. As a result of their ability to improve classification accuracy, deep learning algorithms have been used in many applications. The use of machine learning algorithms significantly improved the accuracy of neonatal sleep stage detection, achieving high performance metrics with minimal manual intervention16,17. Nevertheless, each family of artificial intelligence methods comes with distinct trade-offs: convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transfer learning provide strong representation learning yet typically require large labeled datasets; recurrent/temporal models (e.g., LSTM/TCN) can capture temporal dependencies but may be computationally heavier; transformer/attention mechanisms improve long-range context modeling at the cost of increased training complexity; and classical learners (e.g., SVM/XGBoost) are data-efficient and more interpretable when fed with informative features, though they depend on effective feature extraction. These considerations are particularly relevant under neonatal constraints such as limited recording durations, class imbalance, and clinical need for explainability.

Considering the available signals in the field of infantile epilepsy analysis18, this research aims to detect epilepsy in infants early by using transfer learning methods. EEG signal windowing is used to overcome non-stationarity and the inherent complexity of the signal during recording. Using the important frequency component and the resulting changes in the signal, images of the signal power spectrum will be obtained. As a next step, the images created in the two-dimensional input format will enter the ResNet network and the Inception module of the transfer learning structure. It is due to ResNet’s residual connections—which mitigate vanishing gradients—and Inception’s parameter-efficient, multi-scale design—which helps curb overfitting—that these two are combined19.

First, a discrete wavelet transform (DWT) was used to decompose EEG signals into sub-bands. To enhance the accuracy of classification, the signals were windowed after decomposition to accommodate the non-stationary characteristics of EEGs. Short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is then applied to the windowed signals to construct time–frequency spectrograms, which serve as inputs to the deep feature extractor. The Inception-ResNetV2 decision-making approach is used to extract the most optimal features from the spectrogram image to enhance the fidelity of the EEG signal representation. In other words, STFT produces the spectrograms and Inception-ResNetV2 operates on these images via transfer learning to extract discriminative features; the network does not compute the STFT itself. Next, in order to enhance classification performance, non-linear and linear components are aggregated, incorporating frequency and time-space features. Finally, the extracted deep features are classified with a lightweight gradient-boosting model (XGBoost), and hyperparameters are tuned offline to improve generalization under class imbalance. Based on the deep characteristics of the proposed architecture, the concurrent diagnosis of neonatal seizure disorders was further assessed. Several methods have been found to produce spectrograms from windowed signals, but their performance varies. As a result, some methods may lose signal information, frequency resolution may be lost in short-length segmented signals, and even frequency-time changes may not be tracked properly. However, in the STFT method compared to other methods such as wavelet transform, the frequency resolution is somewhat better, the loss of information is less, and on the one hand, for frequency-time changes in the case of brain signals with a length greater than a few hundred milliseconds and smaller than one second, it produces a more responsive response20.

In comparison with alternative methodologies, the proposed model demonstrated its utility in evaluating binary classification. Furthermore, deep characteristics contribute to the differentiation and categorization of EEG data. Performance metrics such as F1-score, specificity, sensitivity, and overall detection accuracy were used to evaluate and analyze the proposed approach. The purpose of this study is to evaluate and propose the most effective integrated approach for detecting and diagnosing infantile epilepsy. To enhance clinical relevance, we report a comprehensive set of metrics beyond accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and AUC) and assess robustness across analysis window lengths.

Accordingly, this study is guided by the following research questions: (RQ1) Can a hybrid framework integrating Inception-ResNetV2 feature extraction with an optimized XGBoost classifier improve the robustness and accuracy of neonatal seizure detection compared to conventional deep models? (RQ2) How do different analysis window lengths and time–frequency representations (DWT + STFT) affect the stability and generalizability of classification results on neonatal EEG? (RQ3) To what extent can subband-level analysis provide clinically meaningful insights, particularly regarding the role of delta and theta activity in early seizure manifestation? Addressing these research questions is essential for bridging computational advances with clinical decision support in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). Moreover, based on RQ1–RQ3, we hypothesize that (H1) the proposed hybrid framework (Inception-ResNetV2 + PSO-optimized XGBoost) will outperform deep-only baselines with higher class-balanced performance (macro F1, Sensitivity, AUC) and lower variability across window settings; (H2) moderately medium windows combined with DWT + STFT spectrograms will improve stability and generalizability relative to shorter or single-stage representations, with macro metrics remaining robust under class imbalance; and (H3) subband-level analyses will reveal delta/theta activity as the most informative for early seizure manifestation, providing clinically interpretable insights consistent with neonatal seizure patterns.

Despite recent progress, gaps remain in: (i) neonatal-specific validation under limited recording duration and class imbalance; (ii) systematic reporting of clinically meaningful metrics beyond accuracy; (iii) balancing performance with computational efficiency and interpretability suitable for NICU workflows; and (iv) consistent, well-documented pipelines from raw EEG to time–frequency representations and downstream classifiers.

We address these gaps by: (1) combining DWT-based sub-banding with STFT spectrograms to stabilize non-stationary EEG; (2) leveraging transfer learning with Inception-ResNetV2 for robust feature extraction from spectrogram images; (3) employing a data-efficient, interpretable XGBoost classifier with offline hyperparameter tuning to mitigate imbalance while keeping inference lightweight; and (4) adopting comprehensive evaluation (sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, AUC) and analysis across window lengths to support clinical applicability.

Related work

EEG is widely used across many neurological and psychiatric conditions21–26. Within this broader landscape, automated seizure detection in infants is especially important for early intervention and risk reduction in the NICU. The problem remains challenging due to EEG non-stationarity, low SNR, inter-patient variability, artifacts, and limited or noisy labels. In the remainder of this section, we review recent machine-learning approaches for neonatal epilepsy, emphasizing time–frequency representations, transfer learning, and graph/attention mechanisms that have driven progress in detection and classification.

Gotman et al.27 developed a continuous discharge detector capable of identifying rhythmic discharges, spike accumulations, and highly slow rhythmic seizures. There were ten unique thresholds used in the algorithm.

Using a single spectrum analysis technique, Celka and Colditz28 compared the “required optimal model order” with the seizure detection threshold.

A study by Navakatikyan et al.29 presented nine discrete thresholds for seizure detection based on wave sequence analysis. In addition, the researchers employed a novel algorithm that replicates human EEG signals, a technique that has been clinically validated30–32. In this method, two parallel algorithms are used to detect both shock and oscillating convulsions. The thresholds and parameters for the aforementioned methods are established experimentally and often involve trial and error, which may result in suboptimal results.

In previous studies, data-driven techniques have been used to diagnose infantile epilepsy. These algorithms rely on this particular rule. The low and high frequency characteristics of continuous impulse signals were extracted by Hassanpour et al.33 using single value analysis and interval analysis. Afterwards, these characteristics are applied to the analysis of two separate artificial feeding counter neural networks, each with two hidden layers.

In previous works, machine learning has been used to detect seizures in infants, ranging from shallow to deep seizures34–38. It is not necessary to manually engineer features for deep neural networks. Kalif et al.38 introduced a new method for measuring signal complexity. Based on pursuit matching analysis, this method identifies the transition between two modes. A dataset of 30 EEG recordings of infants was analyzed using a novel metric, weighted structural complexity. To identify the transition between the two modes, this method uses pursuit matching analysis.

O’Shea et al.39,40 proposed the use of a single-channel CNN to detect neonatal seizures. As input for CNN, EEG signals with a duration of 8 s and a single channel were used. A variety of alternative methods for detecting epilepsy in newborns have also been explored40–44. Using a convolutional neural network, Raeisi et al.41 presented a novel deep learning model for automatically detecting neonatal convulsions using a deep learning model. Based on continuous EEG signals obtained from 39 neonates, the model is evaluated on a publicly available dataset. An evaluation is conducted by measuring the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC curve), focusing specifically on specificity values exceeding 90%.

A CNN algorithm was developed by Ansari et al.45 to identify infantile epileptic seizures. Epilepsy-related seizures in infants can be detected using the aforementioned algorithm. Unprocessed multi-EEG data is either classified as indicative of seizures or as not indicative of seizures. The method proposed by the authors does not require any preprocessing of EEG data, contrary to prevailing approaches in the literature. A further advantage of this approach is that it does not rely on manual feature extraction, but rather on an automated one.

The goal of the research conducted by Ansari et al.46 is to automate the process of feature selection and classification by employing deep CNNs and random forest algorithms. A network was trained using EEG recordings from 26 infants. The final five classification layers were replaced with random forest classification to improve performance. Based on the analysis of EEG recordings obtained from a cohort of 22 neonates exhibiting signs of potential seizure activity, there was a false alarm rate of 0.9 per hour, in addition to a seizure identification rate of 77%.

Ghembaza et al.47 introduced the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) classifier to effectively classify the three types of signals, which has been demonstrated to be highly accurate up to 95%. Binary classification has high accuracy, reaching 98%, identifying interictal or ictal events. According to them, EEG signals are commonly described as non-stationary and wide-sense random processes. For each signal, the Hurst Exponent and auto-regressive moving average were calculated in their work.

Tanveer et al.48 developed a method for classifying neonatal seizures based on EEG signals. Machine learning algorithms and manual feature extraction were used to identify seizure activity in unprocessed EEG signals. This study used a dataset that was annotated by three experts. As a result, three separate structures were trained using the individual annotations. The average accuracy of these models was 95.6%, 94.8%, and 90.1%, respectively, as well as an average level under the ROC curve.

To detect neonatal epilepsy, Caliskan and Rencuzogullari49 used a transfer learning approach. They employed pre-trained deep convolutional neural networks (p-DCNN) such as Alexnet, Resnet18, Googlenet, modified Densenet, and Resnet50.

Caro et al.50 presented a framework for modeling infant EEG data based on multi-output Gaussian processes. This study develops and validates a framework for analyzing neonatal EEG based on multi-output Gaussian processes. To attribute the observed data to specific sources or factors, we aim to experimentally validate the covariance kernel of a multi-output spectral mixture model in real-world newborn EEG data. A primary contribution of the authors is the development of multi-output spectral mixture metaparameters for EEG features and their application to seizure detection (classification) within a specific approach.

The study by Visalini et al.51 devised a machine learning architecture based on deep belief networks. This architecture classified episodes binaryally, distinguishing seizure occurrences from nonseizure occurrences. Annotated EEG recordings from 79 neonates collected at Helsinki University Hospital were used to train and evaluate the classifier. The architecture achieved 98.7% accuracy when applied to multichannel EEG recordings. In this dataset, the model had a sensitivity of 96.1% and a specificity of 99.2%, which are comparable to the performance of recent machine learning classifiers.

Using EEG data, the authors of the study52 developed a deep learning framework for diagnosing epileptic seizures in infants. Based on 79 meticulously selected neonatal EEG recordings, a comprehensive framework for seizure detection was developed using deep convolutional learning.

Abbasi et al.53 developed an innovative method combining traditional feature extraction with ensemble learning algorithms, such as Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and multilayer perceptron (MLP), achieving a mean kappa of 0.73 for 2-stage and 0.66 for 3-stage classifications. Tested on a dataset of neonatal EEG recordings, the approach provides semi-real-time classification with minimal manual intervention, showing high performance in a clinical setting. The main advantages are its accuracy and real-time capability, while its complexity and computational resource requirements are notable drawbacks.

Abbasi et al.54 also proposed a method for classifying sleep-wake states in neonates using a deep multilayer perceptron (DMLP) neural network. The innovation of this study lies in combining multiple EEG channel features into a single input vector, which the multilayer perceptron processes without requiring any post-processing, making it suitable for real-time applications. The method was tested on EEG recordings from 19 neonates, achieving a maximum mean accuracy of 83% for sleep-wake classification—the highest reported accuracy for this task to date. The primary advantage of this approach is its high accuracy and potential for real-time application, though a drawback is its reliance on multichannel EEG data, which may limit its use in settings with fewer available channels.

Fatma et al.55 developed a method for detecting epileptic seizures using EEG data by combining traditional feature selection with a CNN, achieving approximately 99% accuracy on a standard dataset. The approach’s main advantage is its ability to automatically learn and classify features with minimal manual intervention, though it requires substantial computational resources for training. Similarly, Siddiqui et al.56 compared four classifiers—SVM, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree, and an Ensemble method—for detecting seizures. The ensemble method outperformed others with high accuracy and a lower false detection rate, despite its higher complexity and computational demands.

The initial and paramount measure in managing neonatal seizures is a comprehensive clinical diagnosis. Feature extraction and categorization are widely utilized techniques in literary research. In the context of neonates, there exists a current deficiency in a cohesive, universally applicable, and understandable medical diagnostic approach that can consistently categories a broad spectrum of seizure disorders and consistently achieve a definitive diagnosis. Consequently, efforts were made to develop a diagnostic tool utilizing a deep learning algorithm and machine learning techniques to facilitate the identification of underlying issues. This study aimed to enhance a comprehensive clinical diagnosis framework for neurologists by employing hybrid feature extraction and classification methods. The objective was to enable automated, rapid, and precise diagnosis of seizure disorders in newborns.

Decomposition and signal windowing

Sub-band decomposition of EEG signals provides insight into the frequency content of the signal. This can help to detect changes in the brain caused by epilepsy. Early diagnosis is essential to reduce the risk of long-term complications. Moreover, sub-band analysis can help identify abnormal EEG patterns in infants with epilepsy. It can provide additional information about the type of seizure and the severity of the condition. This information can then be used to diagnose and treat epilepsy in infants. As a result, sub-bands analysis can also help detect early signs of epilepsy, which may prevent long-term complications. In addition to monitoring the effects of treatment over time, it can also be used to determine its effectiveness. Delta (0.5–4 Hz), Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha (8–13 Hz), Beta (13–30 Hz), and Gamma (30–48 Hz) are the most commonly used frequency bands for EEG classification of human brain activity. Based on the frequency conditions, Fig. 2 shows the breakdown of EEG signal into different sub-bands.

Shows the breakdown of EEG signal into different subbands based on frequency conditions.

In this study, we utilize the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) to decompose EEG signals into various sub-bands for epilepsy detection57,58. DWT enables us to hierarchically decompose the signal into different frequency bands, each containing significant information about brain activities. Moreover, we grouped electrodes into lobar regions (frontal: Fp1/Fp2/F3/F4/F7/F8; temporal: T3/T4/T5/T6; central: C3/C4; parietal: P3/P4; occipital: O1/O2).

For each window and sub-band, we aggregated SHAP/feature attributions over channels within each region and summarized distributions across subjects. Lobe-level statements in Sects. 4-4-4 are thus qualitative summaries of these channel-group attributions rather than claims of precise source localization.

Spectrogram

Spectrograms provide a compact, interpretable time–frequency view of neonatal EEG that helps discriminate normal from abnormal activity. When seizures occur, evolving rhythmic discharges emerge that concentrate power within specific bands (often delta/theta) and persist over time; on a spectrogram this appears as sustained, band-limited “ridges,” enabling visual distinction from background patterns and supporting alignment with expert-annotated onset/offsets. Because the spectrogram preserves both timing and frequency content, it can aid in estimating event duration and assessing band-specific evolution in line with the 1-s–resolution annotations by three experts described in the Dataset section. Additionally, ρ serves as a frequency parameter. Hence, the spectrogram representation (SP) can be formally described as and serves as the energetic interpretation of the STFT function.

In our workflow, raw multi-channel EEG sampled at 256 Hz is first segmented into short analysis windows to accommodate non-stationarity. STFT is then applied to each window to obtain time–frequency representations (spectrograms), which capture neonatal EEG bandwidths of interest without incurring unnecessary data burden. These spectrogram images provide a consistent input space for transfer-learned feature extraction and downstream classification, while remaining easily interpretable for clinical review (see Fig. 3).

Deep structure

When CNN structures are used for feature extraction, one of the problems is that the output is inexplicable features. Therefore, the Inception-ResNet architecture is able to capture the features of EEG signals associated with the classification of seizure and non-seizure EEG signals. These features include the frequency changes that can be seen in the spectrogram. By analyzing these features, the Inception-ResNet architecture is able to accurately classify the EEG signals into seizure and non-seizure categories. CNNs use convolution filters to extract relevant information from images. When determining which features to extract from an image, feature size is taken into account. Before the availability of Inception modules, network architects were responsible for selecting suitable filter sizes. Inception-ResNetV2 is a CNN that incorporates residual connections instead of filter concatenation, derived from the Inception architecture family ResNet’s architectural design facilitates enhanced differentiation between classes, resulting in a more comprehensive and inclusive feature set. It is possible, however, to consider that the preceding layers may not retain the essential characteristics when the sample rate is increased, especially if the feature mapping is sparse. A growing body of research suggests that this particular arrangement reduces labour requirements and enhances precision59. Based on the ResNet architecture, the hybrid primitive module in Inception-ResNetV2 was developed. Inception ResNet consists of two minor releases, Version 1 and Version 2. In both models, A, B, and C modules and reduction blocks are uniform, and Hyperparameters are the only variables. For determining the optimal values of hyperparameters, numerous strategies have been developed.

It is possible to argue that residual connections are generated when the complexity operation is applied to the initial module. For a residual to be incorporated into the analysis, the output and input of the convolution must have the same dimensions. By applying 1 × 1 convolutions after the initial convolutions, the depth is aligned with the depth of the original convolutions. While these networks share a common core, their stems and internal modules differ, with Inception-ResNetV2 being more closely aligned with Inception-v4. In Fig. 4, the convolutional blocks highlight the stem preprocessing pathway that prepares the raw 299 × 299 × 3 spectrogram input for the higher-level Inception-ResNet modules shown above. This sequence of 3 × 3 convolutions with controlled strides, combined with a 1 × 1 bottleneck convolution and a 3 × 3 max-pooling layer, progressively reduces spatial resolution while expanding channel depth—from 299 × 299 × 3 through 149 × 149 × 32, 147 × 147 × 64/80/192, and ultimately 35 × 35 × 256. This staged down-sampling increases the receptive field, suppresses local noise, and constructs efficient multi-scale representations. Consequently, the features passed into the subsequent Inception-ResNet A/B/C (see Fig. 5) and reduction modules (Fig. 6) are both spectrally and spatially refined, enabling more stable and computationally efficient deep feature extraction.

Figures 5 and 6 clarify the internal mechanics of the backbone used above the stem. The Inception-ResNet A/B/C modules (Fig. 5) implement multi-branch convolutions at different effective receptive fields—A emphasizes 1 × 1/3 × 3 paths, B factorizes larger kernels as 1 × 7/7 × 1 to expand context at lower cost, and C refines high-level features with 1 × 3/3 × 1 paths. In each module, branch outputs are concatenated, passed through a 1 × 1 projection, and residually added to the input before ReLU, which improves optimization stability and preserves information flow. The Reduction A/B blocks (Fig. 6) then perform structured down-sampling (strided 3 × 3 conv and/or max-pool) to shrink spatial dimensions while increasing channel depth, preparing compact, high-capacity feature maps for subsequent stages.

Experimental results

This section presents a comprehensive analysis of EEG data processing experimental results, as well as subsequent evaluations and debates. Further, the paper discusses the development of a novel model.

EEG signals

This study uses the publicly available Helsinki Neonatal EEG Dataset60, comprising multi-channel EEG from 79 term neonates admitted to the NICU at Helsinki University Hospital between 2010 and 2014 with clinical suspicion of seizures (see Supplementary Material). Infants were excluded if extremely/very preterm (< 32 weeks GA), if post-menstrual age at recording was outside 35–45 weeks, if clinical metadata were insufficient, or if EEG technical quality was poor. Recordings are de-identified. The median duration per recording is ~ 74 min (IQR 64–96 min), totaling ~ 112 h across the cohort60.

Signals were acquired with a Natus NicOne amplifier at 256 Hz using 19 Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned according to the 10–20 system with a midline reference. For visual review and labeling, data were arranged into the standard 18-derivation longitudinal bipolar (“double-banana”) montage, e.g., Fp2–F4, F4–C4, C4–P4, P4–O2, Fp1–F3, F3–C3, C3–P3, P3–O1, Fp2–F8, F8–T4, T4–T6, T6–O2, Fp1–F7, F7–T3, T3–T5, T5–O1, Fz–Cz, Cz–Pz. This sampling rate covers the neonatal EEG bandwidth while limiting data burden.

Each EEG was independently annotated by three experienced experts (blinded to clinical details) at 1-s resolution using the standard neonatal electrographic seizure definition (distinct rhythmic/spike activity with evolution ≥ 10 s). Annotations record onset time and duration considering all derivations; no localization labels are encoded. ECG was available to assist interpretation; video/polygraphy were not included. Inter-observer agreement on this dataset is substantial (e.g., Fleiss’ κ ≈ 0.77 with 95% CI). By subject-level consensus, 39 neonates had seizures and 22 were seizure-free; seizure presence was annotated by at least one reviewer in 57 infants, by ≥ 2 in 18, and by all 3 in 39. Across the corpus, each expert annotated on the order of ~ 460 seizures (dataset-level count per expert). Seizure frequency and duration vary markedly between infants.

EEG signals are stored as EDF files (referential montage, 256 Hz). Annotations are provided as CSV (A/B/C, one file per expert) and MAT (cell array: experts × time [1 Hz]) synchronized to the EEG start. Clinical spreadsheets include study ID, sex, birth-weight, GA, postnatal age at EEG, primary neurological diagnosis, and MRI/cranial-US summaries (de-identified via categorical fields). Exported EEGs have acquisition filters off; amplifier bandwidth defines frequency response (50 Hz notch and 0.5 Hz high-pass used for some QC analyses in the descriptor). Common NICU artefacts are present (e.g., recording pauses, mains noise, movement/muscle activity, > 500 µV excursions). Reported quality metrics include a median noise floor ≈ 0.208 µV²/Hz in 70–128 Hz and rBSI ≈ − 0.001 (IQR − 0.207–0.161), indicating good hemispheric symmetry overall.

Recordings were obtained as part of routine clinical care; ethics approval for public release of de-identified data was granted by the local committee at Children’s Hospital, Helsinki University Hospital. The dataset (EEG, annotations, clinical spreadsheets) is openly available via Zenodo, with the peer-reviewed descriptor in60. In line with the dataset’s tri-expert annotations, we generated per-window labels by majority vote (≥ 2/3) at 1-s resolution and derived subject-level status from the dataset’s consensus indicators. This choice balances annotation variability and class imbalance while preserving clinical realism; sensitivity analyses with alternative voting rules are reported in our Results.

Setting in implementation

All experiments were conducted on a 64-bit Intel® operating system with 16 GB RAM and an Intel® Core™ i7 processor. The task was formulated as a binary classification problem, distinguishing seizure from non-seizure EEG events using multi-subband representations. To prepare the data, EEG recordings were segmented into overlapping 3-second windows with a 1-second hop, consistent with prior neonatal seizure studies, and converted into spectrogram images of size 299 × 299 pixels. Each spectrogram was processed through Inception-ResNetV2, from which 1536 deep features were extracted prior to classification. Moreover, EEG was segmented into 1.5, 2, 3, and 4 s windows (50% overlap). We adopt 2–3 s at inference, balancing time–frequency trade-offs (Δf ≈ 0.5–0.33 Hz for δ/θ) against seconds-level latency suitable for NICU monitoring39,61. This choice is consistent with clinical definitions and the Helsinki annotation protocol, where seizures are ≥ 10 s; thus, our operating point allows several consecutive decisions within a single ictal event.

The Inception-ResNetV2 backbone incorporated standard layers such as batch normalization, scaling, addition, ReLU activations, global average pooling, and depth concatenation. Training used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a decay rate of 0.9. Other hyperparameters included sparse categorical cross-entropy loss, 400 epochs, 10 steps per epoch, 5 validation steps, and a batch size of 8. A regularization parameter of 0.001 was applied to mitigate overfitting. While preliminary experiments were run with alternative optimizers (SGD and RMSProp), the Adam configuration yielded the most stable convergence. Training required approximately 8–12 h on a single CPU.

For classification, deep features were passed to an XGBoost model, which was optimized offline using Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to identify robust hyperparameters. The key XGBoost parameters included: learning rate (0.005–0.2), subsample ratio (0.8–1.0), maximum number of leaves (10–200), maximum tree depth (5–30), γ (minimum loss reduction for partitioning, 0–0.02), column subsampling ratio (0.8–1.0), and minimum child weight (0–10)62. The use of XGBoost offered several advantages: efficient parallel processing, built-in regularization to prevent overfitting, flexibility in parameter tuning, and improved interpretability through feature importance analysis. These properties make the hybrid Inception-ResNetV2 + XGBoost framework both scalable and clinically meaningful for large-scale neonatal EEG analysis.

Discussion

Diagnostic techniques for neonatal epilepsy are crucial, and prior knowledge often influences both the interpretation of EEGs and the clinical decisions that follow. Automating seizure detection not only reduces diagnostic burden but also enables earlier interventions, which are vital for preventing long-term neurological damage63,64. In this context, our proposed hybrid approach integrating Inception-ResNetV2 feature extraction with an optimized XGBoost classifier addresses several persistent challenges in neonatal EEG analysis, including non-stationarity, class imbalance, and the need for interpretability.

The results support our initial hypotheses. Consistent with H1, the hybrid framework achieved higher class-balanced performance (macro F1 ≈ 99%) than deep-only baselines, while maintaining low variability across conditions, demonstrating robustness and generalizability. Regarding H2, analysis with medium windows produced more stable results, highlighting that slightly extended temporal contexts improve seizure detection in non-stationary neonatal EEG. Importantly, even under class imbalance, macro metrics remained strong, confirming that performance gains were not driven solely by the majority class. Finally, consistent with H3, subband-level analysis emphasized the clinical relevance of delta and theta activity, especially in frontotemporal regions, aligning with known seizure patterns in neonates and providing a degree of interpretability for clinical end-users.

Our proposed method addresses the class imbalance issue in epilepsy EEG data, which arises due to the short duration of seizures and long hours of EEG recordings65, by combining the Inception-ResNet deep network with the XGBoost classifier. This approach offers several key advantages that help overcome this challenge. Firstly, the deep network, utilizing Inception modules and residual connections, is capable of accurately extracting and analyzing complex and non-linear features in EEG signals.

Furthermore, the XGBoost classifier, with its parallel processing capabilities and algorithmic optimizations such as tree pruning and regularization, increases accuracy and reduces the false negative rate. This algorithm manages data imbalance by appropriately weighting different classes, especially the seizure class. To further enhance the model’s performance, we used the PSO algorithm to fine-tune the parameters of the XGBoost classifier and the network structure.

Additionally, the use of the signal windowing method is considered a form of data augmentation, generating a large number of signals in this way. Data augmentation helps in increasing the representation of the minority class and reduces overfitting. Moreover, employing k-fold validation has significantly helped address the class imbalance challenge. K-fold validation ensures optimal use of all available data and provides a robust evaluation of model performance. These approaches and techniques enable our model to effectively tackle the class imbalance in EEG data and identify seizures more accurately and swiftly.

Early methods, such as scalable machine learning pipelines66, achieved relatively modest performance (Acc ≈ 81%) and were constrained by feature-engineering bottlenecks and limited robustness to the non-stationary characteristics of neonatal EEG. With the introduction of deep architectures, particularly ResNet-1848 and graph CNNs41, classification accuracy and area under the curve (AUC) improved dramatically, often exceeding 98%. Models such as Deep Belief Networks51 and ensemble CNNs further demonstrated that deeper representations capture richer temporal–spectral dependencies in EEG, enabling sensitivity and specificity above 96%. More recent works in 2024–2025 (e.g., Wang et al.67, Tang et al.68, Tuncer et al.69, Zhou et al.70, Hogan et al.71 broaden the landscape by introducing semi-supervised autoencoders, multi-band graph neural networks, explainable models, lightweight CNNs, and large-scale scaled CNNs trained on tens of thousands of hours. These approaches mark a shift towards robustness, interpretability, and deployment feasibility, while simultaneously setting new performance benchmarks (AUC > 0.98 in most cases).

Against this backdrop, our proposed framework—Inception-ResNetV2 combined with PSO-optimized XGBoost—achieves competitive, and in many cases superior, performance. The headline cross-validated average yields Macro Accuracy/F1 ≈ 98.98%, Macro Sensitivity/Specificity ≈ 98.98%, and Micro metrics ≈ 99.29%, which is on par with or higher than most 2024–2025 baselines. Notably, in the best configuration (2–3 s or 3–4 s windows), our method achieves 99.82% accuracy, with some folds reaching 100%. This result places our model in the same echelon as the highest-performing graph-based and scaled CNN approaches, but with a crucial distinction: our pipeline retains a relatively lightweight structure. By decoupling feature extraction (Inception-ResNetV2 on spectrograms) from classification (XGBoost tuned via particle swarm optimization), we achieve both high accuracy and computational efficiency. Training times remain moderate compared to very deep CNNs, while inference is sufficiently fast for potential near real-time application. This dual advantage—state-of-the-art accuracy with minimized computational burden—positions our method as a practical alternative in clinical settings.

The comparative evidence in Table 6 also underscores the open challenges in neonatal seizure detection. Many existing approaches, despite high performance, are limited by overfitting risk, lack of explainability, or inadequate validation protocols (e.g., k-fold without subject-wise splits vs. LOSO or external testing). Some lightweight models trade accuracy for interpretability, while large-scale CNNs demand substantial computational resources and annotated data. Our method directly addresses these issues by integrating robust spectrogram-based representations with interpretable tree-based decision layers, offering a balance of precision, transparency, and deployability.

Clinical significance

Our findings have direct implications for neonatal care. The hybrid framework (Inception-ResNetV2 + PSO-optimized XGBoost) achieves near-ceiling, class-balanced performance while operating on medium-length windows, a choice that reduces latency and computational load—key requirements for bedside use in NICUs. High Sensitivity with preserved Specificity mitigates both missed seizures and alarm fatigue, supporting timely intervention and more efficient resource allocation. Subband-level insights (delta/theta prominence) provide clinically interpretable cues that can assist neurologists in reviewing events and refining treatment decisions. Together, these properties make the approach suitable for continuous monitoring, triage (flagging high-risk segments for expert review), and potential integration into clinical decision-support systems, thereby shortening time-to-diagnosis and improving the quality of neonatal seizure management.

Our proposed method is capable of effectively handling high-dimensional datasets. EEG data itself is quite complex and extensive, featuring multi-channel signals and numerous sub-bands, akin to Big Data. Although our method, like other similar methods, may not produce optimal results on weak hardware, it is important to note that utilizing suitable platforms and infrastructures such as Internet of Things (IoT) and edge computing can significantly enhance performance. With these advanced technologies, we can confidently assert that our method will deliver rapid responses even when dealing with Big Data.

Recent EEG studies stress that reporting computational profiles—FLOPs, runtime, and memory—is essential for fair comparison and clinical translation (see Table 7). Lightweight capsule-based or graph-based models have been proposed to achieve competitive accuracy with reduced complexity, echoing our design choice of pairing a strong but fixed feature extractor (Inception-ResNetV2) with a lightweight XGBoost head. Our reported FLOPs and model size show that the pipeline is demanding but manageable on modern CPUs, and freezing the backbone while tuning the decision layer further reduces cost and variance.

Advanced time–frequency methods (e.g., synchrosqueezing, VMD/HHT hybrids) deliver richer spectral resolution but at higher computational expense. Our choice of DWT + STFT balances resolution with efficiency: combined with Inception-ResNetV2, it yields near-ceiling accuracy while keeping FLOPs at ~ 15.1G per window, compatible with near real-time streaming. By quantifying model size, runtime, and FLOPs, we demonstrate that the proposed pipeline achieves state-of-the-art accuracy without prohibitive overhead—making it realistic for continuous NICU monitoring and scalable bedside deployment.

Moreover, main findings of this study demonstrate that the proposed hybrid framework (Inception-ResNetV2 + PSO-optimized XGBoost) achieved near-ceiling performance (Accuracy ≈ 99%, macro F1 ≈ 99%), robust across different window settings, and superior to deep-only baselines. Importantly, the method improved sensitivity without sacrificing specificity, ensuring balanced seizure detection under class imbalance. Subband analysis highlighted delta and theta activity in frontotemporal regions as the most clinically informative, providing interpretability beyond raw performance. The novelty of our work lies in combining DWT-based subband decomposition with STFT spectrograms for stable representation, leveraging transfer learning for deep feature extraction, and integrating a lightweight, interpretable gradient boosting classifier tuned with PSO. Together, these contributions bridge existing gaps in neonatal EEG research by simultaneously improving performance, robustness, and clinical interpretability, thus advancing the potential for real-world NICU deployment.

Limitations and future directions

Building on our neonatal EEG findings, several research avenues can strengthen clinical translation. First, connectivity-informed representations and transfer learning—shown effective in EEG-based psychiatric and emotion-recognition contexts—could be adapted to neonatal seizure phenotyping to enhance generalization and interpretability (e.g., dDTF/TE/PDC-based maps and pretrained CNN/Transformer backbones)58 Second, hybrid temporal–spectral models such as CNN–LSTM/attention architectures with explainable AI may improve robustness under non-stationarity and support clinician-facing explanations59. Third, broader surveys of EEG-AI pipelines underscore best practices for reporting clinically meaningful metrics and handling data imbalance, which we plan to incorporate in multi-center validations60. Finally, cross-domain insights from affective EEG (ensemble deep learning with effective connectivity fusion) suggest scalable strategies for multi-class neonatal event detection (e.g., seizure subtypes, artifacts), paving the way for richer decision-support in NICUs61.

Despite the promising results achieved by the proposed models, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the dataset used for training and validation, although carefully annotated and widely adopted in the literature, remains relatively small and limited to a single medical institution. This constrains the generalizability of the findings, as performance may vary across neonates with different demographics, genetic backgrounds, or comorbid neurological conditions. Furthermore, the restricted dataset size raises the possibility of overfitting, particularly in the case of complex hybrid architectures such as Inception-ResNetV2 + XGBoost, which typically require large-scale data to fully exploit their capacity. Addressing this limitation will require evaluation on larger and more diverse cohorts, ideally through multi-center collaborations that capture a wider spectrum of seizure presentations.

Second, the average EEG recording length of ~ 85 min per subject may not sufficiently capture rare or sporadic seizure events. While this duration aligns with routine clinical recordings, it restricts the temporal variability available for model training and may bias performance toward more frequently occurring seizure patterns. Longer-term monitoring (e.g., 24-hour EEG) would enable more comprehensive coverage of seizure and non-seizure states, improve the robustness of training data, and better approximate real-world neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) conditions, where seizures may occur unpredictably across day–night cycles. Future studies should validate the proposed framework under extended monitoring scenarios to ensure consistent performance.

Third, the reliance on predefined window lengths (LL, ML, HL) for EEG segmentation introduces another limitation. Although medium-length windows yielded the most stable and accurate results in our experiments, the choice of these intervals was somewhat arbitrary and may not represent the optimal temporal context for all patients or seizure types. Seizure dynamics are highly individual, and fixed segmentation may fail to capture subtle, subject-specific variations. This suggests the need for adaptive or learning-based windowing strategies, capable of tailoring temporal granularity to individual signal dynamics, thereby improving both performance and interpretability.

Fourth, the study was restricted to a binary classification framework—distinguishing between healthy and epileptic EEG segments. In clinical reality, neonatal seizures are heterogeneous in morphology, duration, and severity, and a binary approach may oversimplify this complexity. For practical deployment, a multi-class classification system or even real-time seizure prediction framework would be more valuable, enabling clinicians not only to detect seizures but also to characterize seizure types and anticipate onset. Expanding toward multi-class and predictive modeling is therefore an essential next step for clinical translation.

Finally, although the integration of DWT + STFT, Inception-ResNetV2, and PSO-optimized XGBoost balances accuracy, robustness, and interpretability, the computational requirements of deep learning architectures remain a practical consideration. While our hybrid design is lighter than purely deep end-to-end systems, resource-constrained NICU environments may still face challenges in deploying such models without appropriate hardware acceleration or edge-computing support. Future work should therefore explore further optimization of the pipeline for real-time, low-latency inference on embedded or IoT-based platforms to facilitate bedside monitoring at scale.

In summary, while our study demonstrates that the proposed hybrid framework can achieve state-of-the-art performance in neonatal seizure detection, its broader applicability will depend on addressing the above limitations. These include expanding dataset size and diversity, validating performance on longer recordings, developing adaptive preprocessing strategies, moving beyond binary classification, and optimizing deployment for real-world clinical environments. Tackling these challenges will be essential for bridging computational advances with routine clinical practice in neonatal care. In Addition, to avoid overfitting challenges in identifying more classes of neonatal epilepsy, we will combine feature extraction based on Inceptoion-ResNet with attention mechanisms72 in the future. Our research focuses on the interpretability of predictions made by an automatic infant seizure detector in real-world medical settings. Medical professionals must provide a logical justification for the presence or absence of seizures, based on the underlying neurological theory. Domain experts can interpret the decisions made by individual detectors based on optXGBoost hyperparameters, despite the fact that our approach does not aim to exceed the existing standard.

Conclusion

We introduced a clinically oriented hybrid pipeline for neonatal seizure detection that combines DWT-based subbanding and STFT spectrograms with Inception-ResNetV2 feature extraction and a PSO-optimized XGBoost classifier. On the Helsinki neonatal EEG cohort (79 neonates; ~112 h; 460 annotated seizures), our method consistently surpassed deep-only baselines while maintaining class-balanced performance. Using medium windows (2–3 s)—the optimal trade-off between temporal context and latency—macro metrics approached ~ 99% and micro performance reached ~ 99.92%, confirming robust detection beyond majority-class effects. Complementary analyses (t-SNE, SHAP) localized discriminative structure to delta/theta activity—often frontotemporal—providing physiologically plausible, clinician-interpretable evidence. The proposed design improves over end-to-end CNNs by (i) stabilizing non-stationary EEG via a DWT + STFT representation, (ii) leveraging multi-scale visual features from Inception-ResNetV2, and (iii) employing a lightweight, regularized decision layer (XGBoost) tuned with PSO. This combination delivers state-of-the-art sensitivity/specificity/F1 with lower variance across window settings and explicit interpretability anchored in subband physiology.

The 2–3 s operating point limits detection latency while preserving specificity, aligning with continuous NICU monitoring and the triage of high-risk segments for rapid expert review. The compact decision head and calibrated operating point help reduce alarm fatigue without missing short, evolving low-frequency events typical of neonatal seizures, supporting earlier intervention. The modular pipeline—frozen feature extractor + updatable XGBoost head—keeps computational overhead modest, facilitates on-site updating, and integrates with routine EEG acquisition/IT workflows. Windowed streaming at seconds-scale and vectorizable inference make edge/bedside deployment realistic for clinical decision support. Current limitations include single-center data with ~ 85-minute recordings per subject, fixed (non-adaptive) windowing, and a binary task formulation. Next, we will (i) validate on multi-center, longer-duration (e.g., 24 h) recordings to stress-test generalization across day–night cycles, (ii) develop adaptive/learning-based windowing and uncertainty-aware calibration for non-stationarity, (iii) extend to multi-class endpoints (seizure subtypes, artifacts) with prospective, real-time evaluation, and (iv) further optimize for embedded/edge hardware while preserving interpretability and clinician-facing explanations. Taken together, the framework couples high, balanced accuracy with transparent, time–frequency-grounded evidence, charting a practical path from computational modeling to reliable early diagnosis and bedside monitoring in neonatal intensive care.
