Colouring signed analogues of Kneser, Schrijver, and Borsuk graphs
Luis Kuffner, Reza Naserasr, Lujia Wang, Xiaowei Yu, Huan Zhou, Xuding Zhu

Abstract
The Kneser signed graph 𝐾𝑆 ⁡ ( n , k ) \mathit{KS}(n,k) , k ≤ n k\leq n , is the graph whose vertices are signed k k -subsets of [ n ] [n] (i.e. k k -subsets S S of { ± 1 , ± 2 , … , ± n } \{\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots,\pm n\} such that S ∩ ( − S ) = ∅ S\cap(-S)=\emptyset ). Two vertices A A and B B are adjacent with a positive edge if A ∩ ( − B ) = ∅ A\cap(-B)=\emptyset and with a negative edge if A ∩ B = ∅ A\cap B=\emptyset . We prove that the balanced chromatic number of 𝐾𝑆 ⁡ ( n , k ) \mathit{KS}(n,k) is n − k + 1 n-k+1 . We then introduce the signed analogue of Schrijver graphs and show that they form vertex-critical subgraphs of 𝐾𝑆 ⁡ ( n , k ) \mathit{KS}(n,k) with respect to balanced colouring. Further connection to topological methods, in particular, connection to Borsuk signed graphs is also considered.

One of the most noticeable differences between the two colouring schemes is that for balanced colouring, it is the unbalanced (equivalently, negative) cycles that create chromatic obstacles, while for zero-free colouring the same role is played by both positive odd cycles and negative even cycles.

As we shall show in Section
3

Lovász’s proof of Kneser conjecture initiated the application of topological methods in graph colouring. Presently, the study of topological bounds for graph parameters forms an important and elegant part of chromatic graph theory.

The goal of this paper is to generalize the concepts of Kneser graphs and Schrijver graphs to Kneser signed graphs and Schrijver signed graphs and to explore applications of topological methods in the colouring of signed graphs.

we conclude that
\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq d}A_{i}=S^{d}
. As each
A_{i}
is easily observed to be an open set, by
Theorem

10
, there is an
A_{i}
connecting two antipodal points
x_{0}
and
-x_{0}
of
S^{d}
. Thus, there exists a (simple) path
\gamma:[0,1]\to S^{d}
with
\gamma(0)=x_{0},\gamma(1)=-x_{0}
such that
\Gamma:=\gamma([0,1])\subseteq A_{i}
.

By definition,
x\in A_{i}
if and only if there is an alternating
k
-set
X\subset H_{x}
with
i\in c(X)
. We denote such a
k
-set by

(when there is more than one choice, pick one arbitrarily). Since

is an open hemisphere and

, there is an

such that the open neighbourhood
U_{x}:=\{y\in S^{d}:\dist(x,y)<\eta\}
of

X_{x}\subset H_{y}
for all

, where
\dist(\cdot,\cdot)

Thus
\{U_{x}:x\in I\}
covers

We claim that the alternating

i_{0}
with

2.2
A conjecture on the structure of Schrijver signed graphs

Given a signed graph
(G,\sigma)
the subgraph of
G
induced by the set of negative edges is denoted by
(G,\sigma)^{-}
. The following proposition, proved in
[
18
]
, connects the balanced chromatic number of a signed graph to the chromatic numbers of subgraphs induced by the set of negative edges among all switchings of it.

Proposition 12
.

For every signed graph
(G,\sigma)
,

\chi_{b}(G,\sigma)=\min_{\sigma^{\prime}\equiv\sigma}\chi((G,\sigma^{\prime})^{-}).

Recall that
\widehat{\mathit{KS}}(n,k)
and
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k)
are the subgraphs of
\mathit{KS}(n,k)
and
\mathit{SS}(n,k)
, respectively, induced by the vertices whose first nonzero element is positive with the signature inherited. We observe here that this standard signature is the one for which the equality of
Proposition

12
holds. We will need the following notation.

For
i\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{+}(n,k)=\{A\in\binom{[n]}{\pm k}:i\in A\}
. It is easily observed that
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{+}(n,k)
is an independent set of
\mathit{KS}(n,k)^{-}
.

Theorem 13
.

For all
n\geq k
,

\chi(\widehat{\mathit{KS}}(n,k)^{-})=\chi(\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k)^{-})=n-k+1.

Proof.

The lower bound follows from
Proposition

12
,
Theorem

6
, and
Theorem

8
. Hence it is enough to give an
(n-k+1)
-colouring for
\widehat{\mathit{KS}}(n,k)^{-}
(hence also for
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k)^{-}
). To that end, we observe that
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{+}(n,k)
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n-k+1
covers all vertices of
\widehat{\mathit{KS}}(n,k)
because the first nonzero element of each vertex is positive.
∎

Now, we turn to the colouring of
\mathit{KS}(n,k)^{-}
and
\mathit{SS}(n,k)^{-}
. Since
\mathit{KS}(n,k)
and
\mathit{SS}(n,k)
each contains two copies of
\widehat{\mathit{KS}}(n,k)
and
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k)
respectively, the upper bound for the chromatic number of their negative subgraphs is
2n-2k+2
. We show that
\mathit{KS}(n,k)
reaches this bound while
\mathit{SS}(n,k)
does not.

Theorem 14
.

For all
n\geq k
,

\chi(\mathit{KS}(n,k)^{-})=2n-2k+2,

\chi(\mathit{SS}(n,k)^{-})=n-k+2.

Proof.

The first claim is clear once we recall from the proof of
Theorem

6
that
\mathit{KS}(n,k)^{-}
contains
S(2n,k)
as a subgraph.

To see the second part, first notice that
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{+}(n,k),

i=1,2,\ldots,n-k+2
is an
(n-k+2)
-colouring of
\mathit{SS}(n,k)^{-}
, establishing the upper bound.

The lower bound is already implicitly proved along the way of proving
Theorem

8
, so we give a sketch of it. Using
Theorem

11
, we embed
\pm[n]
in
S^{n-k}
in such a way that
i
and
-i
are antipodal for each
i\in[n]
, and any open hemisphere contains an alternating
k
-set.

Suppose there is an
(n-k+1)
-colouring
c
of
\mathit{SS}(n,k)^{-}
, let

A_{i}:=\{x\in S^{n-k}:\exists X\in\mathcal{A}(n,k),X\subset H_{x},c(X)=i\}

for
i\in[n-k+1]
.

Since each hemisphere contains an alternating
k
-set,
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n-k+1}
gives an open cover of
S^{n-k}
. By the Borsuk-Ulam theorem (cf.
Theorem

17
), there is an
A_{i}
that contains a pair of antipodal points of
S^{n-k}
. However, this gives a pair of disjoint alternating
k
-sets in the colour class
c^{-1}(i)
. A contradiction.

∎

By
Proposition

12
, Theorem
8
is equivalent to saying that for any switching of
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k)
, the set of negative edges induces a graph of chromatic number at least
n-k+1
. Nevertheless, it seems that all these induced subgraphs are highly structured. This is presented in the following conjecture.

Conjecture 15
.

In any switching equivalent copy of
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k)
, the graph induced by the set of negative edges contains
S(n-1,k/2)
as a subgraph when
k
is even and
S(n,(k+1)/2)
when
k
is odd.

As
\chi(S(n-1,k/2))=\chi(S(n,(k+1)/2))=n-k+1
, Conjecture
15
would imply that
\chi_{b}(\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,k))\geq n-k+1
. It can be easily verified that Conjecture
15
holds for
k=1,n-1,
and
n
. Next, we prove that it holds when
k=2
.

Theorem 16
.

Any switching equivalent copy of
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,2)
contains
(S(n-1,1),-)
as a subgraph.

Proof.

Note that
S(n-1,1)=K_{n-1}
. We need to show that for any switching equivalent copy of
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,2)
, its negative subgraph has clique number at least
n-1
.

Let
B
be the bipartite graph with parts
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
\{-1,-2,\ldots,-n\}
, where
\{i,-j\}
is an edge if
i<j
. The vertices of
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,2)
are the edges of
B
, where two vertices (that is, the edges of
B
) are connected by a negative edge if they form a matching. Thus the clique number of the subgraph induced by the negative edges in
\widehat{\mathit{SS}}(n,2)
is the maximum size of a matching in
B
. To switch a vertex
\{i,-j\}
means to replace this edge by
\{j,-i\}
in
B
.

So we need to prove that for any subset
S
of
E(B)
, replacing each edge
\{i,-j\}\in S
with edge
\{j,-i\}
, the resulting bipartite graph
B^{\prime}
has a matching of size
n-1
. By Kőnig’s theorem, it suffices to show that the minimum size of a vertex cover of
B^{\prime}
is
n-1
.

Note that for any
i
,

d_{B}(i)=n-i
and
d_{B}(-i)=i-1
. So
d_{B}(i)+d_{B}(-i)=n-1
.
Replacing edge
\{i,-j\}
with edge
\{-i,j\}
does not change the sum of the degrees of
i
and
-i
.
So
d_{B^{\prime}}(i)+d_{B^{\prime}}(-i)=n-1
for
i\in[n]
.

Let
C
be a cover of
B^{\prime}
. If for some
i
,
C\cap\{i,-i\}=\emptyset
, then all neighbours of
i
and
-i
in
B^{\prime}
must be in
C
. Thus
|C|\geq n-1
. Otherwise,
C\cap\{i,-i\}\neq\emptyset
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, and hence
|C|\geq n
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
16
.
∎

3
Borsuk signed graphs

One of the original versions of the Borsuk-Ulam is the following.

Theorem 17
.

For any open cover
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots A_{d+1}
of
S^{d}
, one of the
A_{i}
’s contains a pair of antipodal points.

Given positive integer
d
and positive real number
\varepsilon<2
, the
Borsuk graph

B(d,\varepsilon)
has as its vertex set the points of
S^{d}
, where a pair
x,y
of points are adjacent if
\dist(x,-y)\leq\varepsilon
(again,
\dist(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
). Deciding the chromatic number of Borsuk graph for small values of
\varepsilon
turned out to be equivalent to the Borsuk-Ulam theorem, see
[
10
]
.

Theorem 18

(Reformulation of Borsuk-Ulam)
.

Given
d
, there exists an
\varepsilon_{d}
such that for every
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{d}
we have
\chi(B(d,\varepsilon))=d+2
.

It is mentioned by Lovász in his original proof of Kneser’s conjecture that this equivalence has been the motivation behind his work. It was shown in
[
15
]
that for
d=n-2k
, there is a suitable choice of
\varepsilon
such that the Borsuk graph
B(d,\varepsilon)
admits a homomorphism to the Schrjiver graph
S(n,k)
, implying that
\chi(S(n,k))\geq n-2k+2
.

Following this direction of thought, here we introduce Borsuk signed graphs and present the connection between their chromatic property and various extensions of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem.

Definition 19
.

The
Borsuk signed graph,

\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
, is the signed graph on the vertex set
S^{d}
, where for any
x,y\in S^{d}
, there is a positive edge joining
x
and
y
if
\dist(x,y)\leq\varepsilon
, and a negative edge joining them if
\dist(x,-y)\leq\varepsilon.

That
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
admits a balanced
(d+1)
-colouring for a small enough value of
\varepsilon
can be observed in various ways. One possible colouring is obtained as follows. For each element
\mathbf{e}_{i}
of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, let
{E_{i}}=h_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\cap S^{d}
where
h_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}
is the hyperplane perpendicular to
{\bf e}_{i}
containing the origin. Then for a small enough value of
\varepsilon
let
{B}_{i}
be the subset of
S^{d}
obtained by removing an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
{E}_{i}
. Observe that each
B_{i}
is a balanced set and that each point of
S^{d}
belongs to at least one
B_{i}
. So
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
admits a balanced
(d+1)
-colouring.

The following theorem explores the relations between chromatic properties of
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
and various extensions of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem. In particular, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the balanced chromatic number of
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
is determined.

Theorem 20
.

For every natural number
d
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

There exist an
\varepsilon_{d}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon
,
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{d}
, we have
\chi_{b}(\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon))=d+1
.

(b)

(
Theorem

10
, signed Borsuk-Ulam theorem (open form)) For every symmetric open cover
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{d}
of
S^{d}
, one of the
{A_{i}}
’s connects (hence path-connects) a pair of antipodal points.

(c1)

(Signed Borsuk-Ulam theorem (closed form 1)) For every symmetric closed cover
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{d}
of the sphere
S^{d}
, there is an
F_{i}
such that any open neighbourhood
U
of
F_{i}
connects a pair of antipodal points.

(c2)

(Signed Borsuk-Ulam theorem (closed form 2)) For every symmetric closed cover
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{d}
of
S^{d}
, one of the
F_{i}
’s connects a pair of antipodal points.

Proof of
(a)\Rightarrow(c1)
.

Assume that antipodally symmetric sets
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{d}
is a closed cover of
S^{d}
. Further, suppose for each
i\in[d]
there is an open neighbourhood
U_{i}\supset F_{i}
that does not connect any pair of antipodal points.

By compactness of the sphere, there is an
\varepsilon_{i}>0
such that
F_{i}\subset F_{i,\varepsilon_{i}}\subset U_{i}
where
F_{i,\varepsilon_{i}}
is the
\varepsilon_{i}
-neighbourhood of
F_{i}
. Being a subset of
U_{i}
,
F_{i,\varepsilon_{i}}
does not connect a pair of antipodal points either.
For every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, let
\varepsilon=\min_{i\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,d\}}\varepsilon_{i}.
We claim that
F_{i}
is a balanced set in
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
.

If
x,y\in F_{i}
belong to different connected components of
F_{i,\varepsilon_{i}}
, then
\dist(x,y)\geq 2\varepsilon_{i}>\varepsilon.
In that case
x
and
y
cannot be joined by a positive edge in
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
. On the other hand, if
x,y\in F_{i}
belong to the same component of
F_{i,\varepsilon_{i}}
, since
-x
is not in the same component as
x
, we have
\dist(-x,y)\geq 2\varepsilon_{i}>\varepsilon.
This shows that vertices in the same component cannot be joined by a negative edge.

We now claim that
F_{i}
is a balanced set of
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
. Being symmetric, if there is a negative cycle in
F_{i}
, there is a negative cycle, say
C
, with exactly one negative edge. In this cycle, the sequence of positive edges implies that all its vertices are in the same component of
F_{i,\varepsilon_{i}}
. But the two ends of the only negative edge must be on two different components.
The collection of
F_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
, then gives a balanced
d
-colouring of
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
, contradicting
(a)
.
∎

Proof of
(c1)\Rightarrow(b)
.

This is a consequence of the fact that for every open cover
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{d}
of
S^{d}
, there is a closed cover
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{d}
of
S^{d}
such that
F_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
for each
i=1,2,\ldots d
(see for example
[
1
]
). ∎

Proof of
(b)\Rightarrow(a)
.

For every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, suppose there is an
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
such that
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
is balanced
d
-colourable. Let
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{d}
be the colour classes of a balanced
d
-colouring for
\mathit{BS}(d,\varepsilon)
. We may assume that for each point
x
,
x
and
-x
are assigned the same colour. Thus each
F_{i}
is a symmetric set. Let
A_{i}
be the
(\varepsilon/8)
-neighbourhood of
F_{i}
for each
i
,
i=1,2,\ldots,d
. By
(b)
, there is a pair
x
and
-x
of antipodal points connected in some
A_{j}
. As
A_{j}
is an open set, these two points are path-connected. Thus there is sequence
x=x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}=-x
of vertices such that the distance between

and
x_{l+1}
is at most
\varepsilon/4
. By the choice of
A_{j}
, for each
x_{j}
, there is a vertex
x^{\prime}_{j}
in
F_{j}
at distance at most
\varepsilon/8
from
x_{j}
. Hence
x^{\prime}_{j}
and
x^{\prime}_{j+1}
are at distance at most
\varepsilon/2
. Moreover,
x^{\prime}_{1}
and
-x^{\prime}_{k}
have distance at most
\varepsilon/2
as well. So the vertices
\{x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k}\}
induce a negative cycle, contradicting the fact that

induces a balanced set. ∎

Proof of
(c1)\Leftrightarrow(c2)
.

The statement
(c2)
contains
(c1)
. For the other direction, suppose every
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
F_{i}
connects a pair of antipodal points. Let
x_{j}
and
-x_{j}
be a pair of antipodal points connected in the
(1/j)
-neighbourhood of
F_{i}
. Since
F_{i}
is a closed (and compact) set, there is a limit point
x\in F_{i}
of
\{x_{j}\}
. Then the antipodal pair
x
and
-x
of points are connected by
F_{i}
. ∎

Theorem

20

(b)
and
(c2)
first appear in a slightly different formulation in Philip Bacon’s paper

O_{n}(X)

Below we show a connection to yet another formulation of the signed Borsuk-Ulam Theorem.

Let

Theorem 21
.

The Lusternik-Schnirelmann category of the real projective space

is
d

of

.

Theorem

21

\Leftrightarrow

Theorem

20

(b)
.

A symmetric open cover

connects a pair of antipodal points of

[

∎

The Lusternik-Schnirelmann category can be equivalently defined with closed categorical covers, establishing the equivalence between
