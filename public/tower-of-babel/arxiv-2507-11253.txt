Second-Order Characterizations of Tilt Stability in Composite Optimization
Boris S. Mordukhovich, Peipei Tang, Chengjing Wang

Abstract
Tilt stability is a fundamental concept of variational analysis and optimization that plays a pivotal role in both theoretical issues and numerical computations. This paper investigates tilt stability of local minimizers for a general class of composite optimization problems in finite dimensions, where extended-real-valued objectives are compositions of parabolically regular and smooth functions. Under the weakest metric subregularity constraint qualification and other verifiable conditions, we establish unified neighborhood and pointbased characterizations of tilt stability via second-order generalized differentiation. The obtained results provide a rigorous theoretical foundation for further developments on variational stability and numerical algorithms of optimization and related topics.



Keywords:

variational analysis, generalized differentiation, composite optimization, tilt stability, second-order characterizations, parabolic regularity

Introduction

Variational analysis has unequivocally demonstrated that well-formulated stability concepts constitute fundamental pillars of optimization theory and its practical applications. Particularly important are Lipschitzian stability properties, which provide both qualitative characterization and quantitative measures that have been recognized to be indispensable for rigorous analysis and justification of numerical algorithms in problems of optimization and operations research.

This paper is devoted to studying the notion of
tilt stability
for local minimizers, which was introduced by Poliquin and Rockafellar
[
29
]
for optimization problems written in the unconstrained extended-real-valued format as follows. Given a function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}:=\mathcal{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and a point
x\in\operatorname{dom}f:=\{x\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\;|\;f(x)<\infty\}
, it is said that
x
is a
tilt-stable local minimizer
of
f
with modulus
\kappa>0
if there exists
\delta>0
such that the mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\delta}:u\mapsto\mathop{\operatorname{arg}\min}_{\|x^{\prime}-x\|\leq\delta}\{f(x^{\prime})-f(x)-\langle u,x^{\prime}-x\rangle\}

is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous with modulus
\kappa
on some neighborhood of
u=0
and that
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(0)=\{x\}
. The fundamental result of
[
29
]
shows that, for a broad class of prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous functions, the tilt stability of local minimizers is completely characterized by the positive-definitiness of the
second-order subdifferential/generalized Hessian
in the sense of Mordukhovich
[
19
]
. Over the years, a variety of second-order characterizations of tilt-stable minimizers for different classes of constrained optimization problems have been established and applied to qualitative and numerical aspects of optimization, control, and operations research. We refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
12
,
22
,
24
,
23
,
27
]
and the bibliographies therein for more details. The recent book
[
21
]
contains an extensive account of the major achievements on tilt stability, related properties, and their applications in finite and infinite dimensions. Quite new characterizations of tilt-stabile local minimizers for unconstrained extended-real-valued functions with prox-regularity but without subdifferential continuity requirements have been derived in the fresh papers
[
8
,
13
,
31
]
via quadratic bundles.

In this paper, we address tilt stability in composite optimization problem of the type

\displaystyle\mbox{minimize }\;f_{0}(x)+g(F(x))\;\mbox{ over }\;x\in\mathcal{R}^{n},

(1)

where
f_{0}:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
,
F:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}^{m}
are smooth mappings, and where
g:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
is a lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) convex function. The framework of (
1
) covers many particular classes of problems important in optimization theory and applications, e.g., nonlinear, second-order cone, and semidefinite programmings, where
g
is the indicator functions of the positive orthant, the second-order (Lorentz, ice-cream) cone, and the positive semidefinite cone, respectively.

The first characterization of tilt-stable local minimizers was obtained in
[
26
]
for problems of nonlinear programming (NLPs) in terms of Robinson’s strong second-order sufficient condition under the classical linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ). The results of
[
26
]
were given in the
pointbased form
, i.e., they were expressed entirely at the point in question, which is the most important for applications. Pointbased extensions of this characterization and related results for nonpolyhedral problems of conic programming (including second-order cone programs and semidefinite programs) were later established in
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
under certain
nondegeneracy conditions
, which are conic counterparts of LICQ.

More delicate characterizations of tilt stability for NLPs were obtained in
[
22
]
without imposing LICQ (and thus the uniqueness of Lagrange multipliers), but in a
neighborhood form
by invoking points near the reference one. A broad spectrum of pointbased characterizations of (as well as separate sufficient and necessary conditions for) tilt-stable minimizers in NLPs, involving only the point in question, were established in
[
9
]
without any nondegeneracy assumptions while under the novel
metric subregularity constraint qualification
(MSCQ). The latter qualification condition was further investigated and applied in many publications; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
16
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, the only paper providing pointbased characterizations of tilt-stable minimizers in nonpolyhedral optimization problems without nondegeneracy is
[
1
]
, where rather complicated conditions of this type are obtained for second-order cone programming (SOCP) with a single Lorentz cone under the SOCP counterpart of MSCQ. Up to now, the understanding of tilt stability for local minimizers in nonpolyhedral problems, particularly for structural composite problems of type (
1
), remains limited in the absence of nondegeneracy.

The main goal of this paper is to derive pointwise
no-gap second-order necessary and sufficient conditions
for tilt stability of local minimizers in a general class of nonpolyhedral problems of composite optimization (
1
) under MSCQ and other mild assumptions including
parabolic regularity
of
g
. To this end, we introduce a novel
second-order variational function
defined via the given data of (
1
). The established generalized differential properties of this function are of their own interest while eventually leading us to the desired pointbased characterizations of tilt stability in (
1
). Involving advanced matrix analysis and calculations allows us constructively implement the most challenging general assumption in the case of
spectral norm function
, which is particularly important for applications to machine learning and data science.

Basic Definitions and Preliminaries

In this section, we overview fundamental concepts of variational analysis and generalized differentiation that are broadly used in what follows; see the books
[
20
,
21
,
32
]
and further references below for more details. Basic assumptions on the data of (
1
) are also formulated below.

Given a set
C\subset\mathcal{R}^{m}
, the (Bouligand-Severi)
tangent cone
to
C
at
x\in C
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{C}(x):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{C-x}{t}=\left\{w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,\left|\,\exists\,t^{k}\downarrow 0,\;x^{k}{\xrightarrow{C}}x,\;\frac{x^{k}-x}{t^{k}}\rightarrow w\right.\right\}

(3)

in terms of the outer limit (
2
) of
G(t):=t^{-1}(C-x)
with
t\in\mathcal{R}
. The (Fréchet)
regular normal cone
to
C
at
x
can be defined equivalently by the equalities

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{C}(x):=\left\{y\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,\left|\,\limsup_{{x^{\prime}\xrightarrow{C}x}\atop{x^{\prime}\neq x}}\frac{\langle y,x^{\prime}-x\rangle}{\|x^{\prime}-x\|}\leq 0\right.\right\}=\mathcal{T}_{C}^{\circ}(x),

(4)

while the (Mordukhovich)
limiting normal cone
to
C
at
x
is given via (
2
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{C}(x):=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x^{\prime}\xrightarrow{C}x}\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{C}(x^{\prime}).

(5)

There are several equivalent representations of (
5
) that can be found in the aforementioned references. If
C
is convex, both regular and the limiting normal cones reduce to the normal cone of convex analysis, while (
5
) is often
nonconvex

even for simple sets on the plane; e.g., when
C
is the graph of the simplest nonsmooth convex function
f(x):=|x|
at
0\in\mathcal{R}^{2}
. Nevertheless, the limiting normal cone (
5
) and the associated generalized differentiation constructions for functions and multifunctions enjoy
full calculus
, which is much better than for their convex counterparts as, e.g., (
4
). This is based on
extremal/variational principles
of variational analysis.

The tangent and normal cones to graphs of set-valued (and hence single-valued) mappings generate the corresponding derivative-like constructions for them, which play a highly important role in variational analysis. For a set-valued mapping
S:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{m}
, consider its domain
\mbox{\rm dom}\,S:=\{x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\,|\,S(x)\neq\emptyset\}
, graph
\mbox{\rm gph}\,S:=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}^{n+m}\,|\,y\in S(x)\}
, and range
\operatorname{rge}S:=\{u\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,|\,\exists\,x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\,\mbox{with}\,u\in S(x)\}
. The inverse mapping
S^{-1}:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{n}
is defined by
S^{-1}(u):=\{x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\,|\,u\in S(x)\}
. Recall next the constructions of the graphical derivative and coderivatives of multifunctions generated by (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
), respectively.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{m}
with
x\in\operatorname{dom}S
. Then the
graphical derivative
of
S
at
x
for
u\in S(x)
is the mapping
DS(x,u):\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{m}
defined by

\displaystyle DS(x,u)(w):=\left\{z\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,\left|\,(w,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{gph}S}(x,u)\right.\right\},\quad w\in\mathcal{R}^{n}.

(6)

The
regular coderivative

\widehat{D}^{*}S(x,u):\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{n}
of
S
at
x
for
u\in S(x)
is defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{D}^{*}S(x,u)(w):=\left\{z\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\,\left|\,(z,-w)\in\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\operatorname{gph}S}(x,u)\right.\right\},\quad w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}.

(7)

The
limiting coderivative

D^{*}S(x,u):\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{n}
of
S
at
x
for
u\in S(x)
is the mapping

\displaystyle D^{*}S(x,u)(w):=\left\{z\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\,\left|\,(z,-w)\in\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{gph}S}(x,u)\right.\right\},\quad w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}.

(8)

When
S
is single-valued at
x
, we skip
u=S(x)
in the notations (
6
)–(
8
).

If
S
is single-valued and Fréchet differentiable at
x
with the Jacobian matrix
\nabla S(x)
, then we get
DS(x,u)(w)=\{\nabla S(x)w\}
and
\widehat{D}^{*}S(x,u)(w)=\{\nabla S(x)^{T}w\}
, where
A^{T}
indicate the matrix transposition. If
S
is strictly differentiable at
x
(in particular,
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth around this point), then
D^{*}S(x,u)(w)=\{\nabla S(x)^{T}w\}
. In general, all the mappings in (
6
)–(
8
) are positively homogeneous in
w
. It is easy to check that relationships

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} z\in DS(x,u)(w)\,&\Longleftrightarrow\,w\in D(S^{-1})(u,x)(z),\\
z\in D^{*}S(x,u)(w)\,&\Longleftrightarrow\,-w\in D^{*}(S^{-1})(u,x)(-z),\end{aligned}

(9)

and similarly for the regular coderivative of the inverse. For sums of set-valued mappings
S:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{m}
and
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth mappings
h:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}^{m}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{gph}(h+S)}(x,u)&=\big\{(w,\nabla h(x)w+z)\,\big|\,(w,z)\in\mathcal{T}_{\operatorname{gph}S}(x,u-h(x))\big\},\\
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\operatorname{gph}(h+S)}(x,u)&=\big\{(w-\nabla h(x)^{T}z,z)\,\big|\,(w,z)\in\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\operatorname{gph}S}(x,u-h(x))\big\},\end{aligned}

which readily yields the equality sum rules

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D(h+S)(x,u)(w)&=\nabla h(x)w+DS(x,u-h(x))(w),\\
D^{*}(h+S)(x,u)(z)&=\nabla h(x)^{T}z+D^{*}S(x,u-h(x))(z).\end{aligned}

(10)

When
S\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{R}^{m}
is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous on an open set
\Omega\subset\mathcal{R}^{n}
, yet another generalized derivative is used in what follows. By the classical Rademacher’s theorem, such a mapping
S
is differentiable almost everywhere in
\Omega
. Denoting by
\Omega_{S}\subset\Omega
be the set of points where
S
is differentiable, consider the collection of matrices

\displaystyle\partial_{B}S(x):=\left\{V\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times n}\,\left|\,\exists\,x^{k}\xrightarrow{\Omega_{S}}x,\,\nabla S(x^{k})\rightarrow V\;\mbox{ as }\;k\to\infty\right.\right\},

(11)

which is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathcal{R}^{m\times n}
known as the
B-subdifferential
of
S
at
x
. The convex hull of (
11
) is the (Clarke)
generalized Jacobian
of
S
at
x
denoted by
{\cal J}S(x)
.

To proceed further, recall that a multifunction
S:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{m}
is
calm
at
\bar{z}
for
\bar{w}
if there exist constants
\kappa\geq 0
and
\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that

S(z)\cap\mathbb{B}(\bar{w},\delta)\subset S(\bar{z})+\kappa\|z-\bar{z}\|\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m}}\;\mbox{ for all }\;z\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{z},\varepsilon).

(12)

It is said that
S:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{R}^{m}
is
metrically subregular
at
\bar{z}
for
\bar{w}
if

\operatorname{dist}(z,S^{-1}(\bar{w}))\leq\kappa\operatorname{dist}(\bar{w},S(z)\cap\mathbb{B}(\bar{w},\delta))

(13)

with the same choice of
z,\kappa,\varepsilon,\delta
as in (
12
). As well-recognized in variational analysis, the calmness and metric subregularity properties of mappings are equivalent up to the passing of their inverses. Based on (
13
), we now define the aforementioned qualification condition for general constraint systems that is employed below for problem (
1
).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G:\mathcal{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}^{m}
be a single-valued mapping, and let
C
be a subset of
\mathcal{R}^{m}
. The constraint system
G(x)\in C
satisfies the the
metric subregularity constraint qualification

(
MSCQ
)
at a feasible point
x
if the set-valued mapping
M(x):=G(x)-C
is metrically subregular at
x
for the origin, i.e., there exists a modulus
\kappa>0
along with
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)
we have the estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(x^{\prime},M^{-1}(0))\leq\kappa\,\operatorname{dist}(0,M(x^{\prime})).

(14)

It is worth emphasizing that the MSCQ from Definition
2.2
is significantly weaker than the standard
metric regularity
of
M(\cdot)
, which corresponds to (
14
) with replacing
\bar{z}=0
in both parts therein by all
z\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
with small norm.

Next we pass to extended-real-valued functions. Given
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, consider its domain and epigraph defined, respectively, by

\operatorname{dom}f:=\{x\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,|\,f(x)<\infty\},\quad\operatorname{epi}f:=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\times\mathcal{R}\,|\,x\in\operatorname{dom}f,\,f(x)\leq y\}

and associate with
f
the
Fenchel conjugate

\displaystyle f^{*}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{u\in\operatorname{dom}f}\left\{\langle x,u\rangle-f(u)\right\}.

We say
f
is proper if
f(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
and
\operatorname{dom}f\neq\emptyset
. Fixed
\sigma>0
, the
Moreau envelope

e_{\sigma f}
and the
proximal mapping

\operatorname{Prox}_{\sigma f}
corresponding to a proper l.s.c. function
f
are defined, respectively, by the formulas

\displaystyle e_{\sigma f}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{R}^{m}}\left\{f(u)+\frac{1}{2\sigma}\|u-x\|^{2}\right\},

(15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Prox}_{\sigma f}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\operatorname{arg}\min}\limits_{u\in\mathcal{R}^{m}}\left\{f(u)+\frac{1}{2\sigma}\|u-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(16)

If
f
is l.s.c. and convex, it is well known from convex analysis
[
30
]
that (
16
) is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous, and that (
15
) is convex and
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth with the gradient

\displaystyle\nabla e_{\sigma f}(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\big(x-\operatorname{Prox}_{\sigma f}(x)\big)=\operatorname{Prox}_{\sigma^{-1}f^{*}}(\sigma^{-1}x).

Addressing further subdifferentials of extended-real-valued functions, define them geometrically via the normal cones in (
4
) and (
5
), while observing that they admit various analytic representations; see
[
20
,
32
]
. In this way, the
regular subdifferential
and the
limiting subdifferential
of

at

are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle\widehat{\partial}f(x)

\displaystyle:=\big\{v\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,\big|\,(v,-1)\in\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(x,f(x))\big\},

\displaystyle\partial f(x)

\displaystyle:=\big\{v\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,\big|\,(v,-1)\in\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(x,f(x))\big\}.

The
singular subdifferential
of
f
at
x
is defined by

\displaystyle\partial^{\infty}f(x):=\left\{v\in\mathcal{R}^{m}\,\left|\,\exists\,t_{k}\downarrow 0,x^{k}\rightarrow x\;\mbox{with}\;f(x^{k})\rightarrow f(x),\;v^{k}\in\widehat{\partial}f(x^{k}),\;t_{k}v^{k}\rightarrow v\right.\right\}.

An l.s.c. function
f
is

at
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{dom}f
for
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
if there are
\varepsilon>0
and
\rho\geq 0
with

\displaystyle f(x^{\prime})\geq f(x)+\langle u,x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\frac{\rho}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}

for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\varepsilon)
such that

,
u\in\partial f(x)\cap\mathbb{B}(\bar{u},\varepsilon)
, and
f(x)<f(\bar{x})+\varepsilon
. If this holds for all
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
,
f
is said to be prox-regular at
\bar{x}
. We say that
f
is
subdifferentially continuous
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
if
f(x^{k})\rightarrow f(\bar{x})
whenever
(x^{k},u^{k})\rightarrow(\bar{x},\bar{u})
with

. If this holds for all
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
,

is called subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
. Any l.s.c. convex function is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at every point in its domain.

Consider now generalized directional derivatives of extended-real-valued functions. Given
f\colon\mathcal{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathcal{R}}
and

, the (first-order)

of

at

is defined by

\displaystyle df(x)(d):=\liminf_{{t\downarrow 0}\atop{d^{\prime}\rightarrow d}}\frac{f(x+td^{\prime})-f(x)}{t},\quad d\in\mathcal{R}^{m},

(17)

where we can put

if

is Lipschitz continuous around

. To define the second-order counterpart of (

), form the second-order difference quotient of
f
at
x\in\operatorname{dom}f

\displaystyle\Delta_{\tau}^{2}f(x,u)(d):=\frac{f(x+\tau d)-f(x)-\tau\langle u,d\rangle}{\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}}\ \ \mbox{ with }\ d\in\mathcal{R}^{m},\ \tau>0

and say that
d^{2}f(x,u):\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
is the
second subderivative
of
f
at
x
for

if

\displaystyle d^{2}f(x,u)(d):=\liminf_{{\tau\downarrow 0}\atop{d^{\prime}\rightarrow d}}\Delta_{\tau}^{2}f(x,u)(d^{\prime}),\quad d\in\mathcal{R}^{m}.

(18)

The function
f
is called
twice epi-differentiable
at
x
for
u
if
\Delta_{\tau}^{2}f(x,u)
epi-converges to
d^{2}f(x,u)
as
\tau\downarrow 0
. To proceed further, recall the notion of
parabolic subderivative
for a proper function
f
at
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
relative to a vector
d
for which
df(x)(d)
is finite. It is defined by

\displaystyle d^{2}f(x)(d,w):=\liminf_{{t\downarrow 0}\atop{w^{\prime}\rightarrow w}}\frac{f(x+td+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}w^{\prime})-f(x)-tdf(x)(d)}{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}},\quad w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}.

(19)

The function
f
is called
parabolically epi-differentiable
at
x

for
d
if
\operatorname{dom}d^{2}f(x)(d,\cdot)\neq\emptyset

and for every
w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
and every
t_{k}\downarrow 0
, there exists
w_{k}\rightarrow w
such that

\displaystyle d^{2}f(x)(d,w)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(x+t_{k}d+\frac{1}{2}t_{k}^{2}w_{k})-f(x)-t_{k}df(x)(d)}{\frac{1}{2}t_{k}^{2}}.

(20)

Next we define the fundamental second-order regularity concept introduced in
[
32
]
and comprehensively developed in the recent papers
[
18
,
16
,
17
]
, which revealed the fulfillment of this property for broad classes of functions appearing in variational analysis and optimization.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
with
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
and
u\in\partial f(x)
, we say that
f
is
parabolically regular
at
x
for
u
in the direction
d
if there is the connection

\displaystyle\inf_{w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}}\left\{d^{2}f(x)(d,w)-\langle u,w\rangle\right\}=d^{2}f(x,u)(d)

between the second subderivative (
18
) and the parabolic subderivative (
19
).

Now we are ready to formulate our basic general assumptions, which will be applied below to the initial data of the composite optimization problem (
1
).

Assumption 2.1
.

Given an l.s.c. proper convex function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, assume that
f
is
parabolically regular
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
for
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
and
parabolically epi-differentiable
at
\bar{x}
for every direction
d\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
satisfying the condition
df(\bar{x})(d)=\langle\bar{u},d\rangle
.

Assumption 2.2
.

Given an l.s.c. proper convex function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, assume that the subgradient mapping
\partial f
is
calm
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
for
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
.

Let us show that both Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
are mild indeed. To this end, recall that a closed convex
set

C\subset\mathcal{R}^{m}
is
{\cal C}^{2}
-reducible
to some
K\subset\mathcal{R}^{p}
at
x\in C
if there exist a neighborhood
U
of
x
and a
{\cal C}^{2}
-smooth mapping
\Xi:U\rightarrow\mathcal{R}^{p}
with the surjective derivative such that
C\cap U=\{x\in U\,|\,\Xi(x)\in K\}
and
\Xi(x)=0
. The reduction is
pointed
if the tangent cone
\mathcal{T}_{K}(\Xi(x))
is pointed. If in addition the set
K-\Xi(x)
is a pointed closed convex cone, it is said that
C
is
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
x
. We say that an l.s.c. convex
function

f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathcal{R}}
is
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible
at
x\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,f
if its epigraph is
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
(x,f(x))
. Moreover,
f
is said to be simply
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible if it is
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible at every point of the domain.

It follows from
[
16
, Theorem 6.2]
that the
{\cal C}^{2}
-reducibility of the function
f
yields its parabolic epi-differentiability at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{u}
in (
20
) as required by Assumption
2.1
, while the parabolic regularity of such
f
is a consequence of
[
2
, Propositions 3.103, 3.136]
. The subdifferential calmness of
{\cal C}^{2}
-reducible functions required in Assumption
2.2
is verified in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
be a proper convex
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible function, which is Lipschitz continuous around each point in
\operatorname{dom}f
. Given
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{dom}f
and
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
, the subgradient mapping
\partial f
is calm at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{u}
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{epi}f
is a closed convex set that is
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible at
(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))
, it follows from
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
that the normal cone mapping
\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}
is calm at
(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))
for each
(u,\gamma)\in\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))
. Furthermore, we get by
[
20
, Proposition 1.17]
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))=\big\{\lambda(v,-1)\,\big|\,v\in\partial f(\bar{x}),\;\lambda>0\big\}\cup\big\{(v,0)\,\big|\,v\in\partial^{\infty}f(\bar{x})\big\},

(21)

which yields
(\bar{u},-1)\in\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))
and therefore implies that the normal cone mapping
\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}
is calm at
(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))
for
(\bar{u},-1)
. The latter tells us that there exist numbers
\kappa,\varepsilon,\delta>0
such that for all
(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{B}((\bar{x},f(\bar{x})),\varepsilon)\cap\operatorname{epi}f
, we have the inclusion

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(x,\alpha)\cap\mathbb{B}((\bar{u},-1),\delta)\subset\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))+\kappa\|(x,\alpha)-(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))\|\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m+1}}.

Moreover, choosing
\varepsilon
and
\delta
to be sufficiently small leads us to

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}((\bar{u},-1),\delta)\cap\big(\big\{(v,0)\,\big|\,v\in\partial^{\infty}f(\bar{x})\big\}+\kappa\|(x,\alpha)-(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))\|\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m+1}}\big)=\emptyset.

(22)

Since
f
is continuous at
\bar{x}
, there exists
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\delta^{\prime})\cap\operatorname{dom}f
, we get

|f(x)-f(\bar{x})|\leq\varepsilon/2
. Letting
\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\min\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\delta^{\prime}\}
and taking any
x\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\varepsilon^{\prime})
ensure that
(x,f(x))\in\mathbb{B}((\bar{x},f(\bar{x})),\varepsilon)\cap\operatorname{epi}f
and that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(x,f(x))\cap\mathbb{B}((\bar{u},-1),\delta)\subset\mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))+\kappa\|(x,f(x))-(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))\|\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m+1}}.

Now we combine (
21
) and (
22
) to obtain the inclusions

\displaystyle\big\{\lambda(v,-1)\,\big|\,v\in\partial f(x),\lambda>0\big\}\cap\mathbb{B}((\bar{u},-1),\delta)\ \subset\ \mathcal{N}_{\operatorname{epi}f}(x,f(x))\cap\mathbb{B}((\bar{u},-1),\delta)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\subset\ \big\{\lambda(v,-1)\,\big|\,v\in\partial f(\bar{x}),\lambda>0\big\}+\kappa\|(x,f(x))-(\bar{x},f(\bar{x}))\|\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m+1}},

which readily allow us to conclude that

\displaystyle\partial f(x)\cap\mathbb{B}(\bar{u},\delta)

\displaystyle\subset\partial f(\bar{x})+\kappa\left(\|x-\bar{x}\|+|f(x)-f(\bar{x})|\right)\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m}}

\displaystyle\subset\partial f(\bar{x})+\kappa(1+L_{f})\|x-\bar{x}\|\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}^{m}},\;\mbox{for all}\;x\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\varepsilon^{\prime}),

where
L_{f}
stands for the Lipschitz constant of
f
around
\bar{x}
. This shows that the subgradient mapping
\partial f
is calm at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{u}
.
∎

Recall that the class of convex
{\cal C}^{2}
-cone reducible functions is fairly rich including, e.g., the
\ell_{p}
norm functions (for
p=1,2,\infty)
, the nuclear norm function, the spectral norm function, and indicator functions of standard cones in conic programming. This confirms that both Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
are pretty natural and unrestrictive.

The next assumption is more involved, but also covers a large territory in optimization theory and applications. In its formulation, we use the
B
-subdifferential (
11
) of the Lipschitzian proximal mapping (
16
) and the Moore-Penrose inverses of its elements.

Assumption 2.3
.

Given an l.s.c. proper convex function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
with
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{dom}f
and
\bar{u}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
, assume that there exists
\overline{W}\in\partial_{B}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(\bar{x}+\bar{u})
such that

\displaystyle\bigcup\limits_{V\in{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(\bar{x}+\bar{u})}\operatorname{rge}V=\operatorname{rge}\overline{W}

and for any
y\in\bigcup\limits_{V\in{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(\bar{x}+\bar{u})}\operatorname{rge}V
we have
y=\overline{W}\bar{z}
, where
\bar{z}
satisfies the equalities

\displaystyle\min\limits_{{z\in\mathcal{R}^{m},y=Vz}\atop{V\in{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(\bar{x}+\bar{u})}}\langle y,z-y\rangle=\langle y,\bar{z}-y\rangle=\langle y,(\overline{W}^{\dagger}-I)y\rangle.

Assumption
2.3
is a technical requirement, which is needed for our characterizations of tilt-stable minimizers in the general class of composite optimization problems (
1
). This assumption describes the properties that make such characterizations possible. Its verification is rather straightforward if the form of
{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(\bar{x}+\bar{u})
is known, which is often the case in typical problems of machine learning and statistics; see, e.g.,
[
12
]
. Due to the large size of the paper, we confine ourselves to the detailed verification of Assumption
2.3
and explicit calculations of all the data therein for the
matrix spectral norm function
whose great importance has been realized in various areas of numerical optimization, data science, and operations research; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
and the references therein. The aforementioned calculation results are presented in Appendix
A
.

3
The Second-Order Variational Function

In this section, we introduce and investigate the novel second-order variational function, which allows us to establish pointbased characterizations of tilt stability for local minimizers in the general composite optimization problem (
1
) obtained in Section
4
. We’ll see below that this function vanishes when (
1
) is a linear program in which case the obtained criteria reduce to those in
[
9
]
. Thus the second-order variational function can be viewed as a “measure of nonpolyhedrality”.

To begin with, recall that the
Lagrangian function
for problem (
1
) is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,u):=\inf_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{m}}\Big\{f_{0}(x)+g(F(x)+s)-\langle u,s\rangle\Big\}=L(x,u)-g^{*}(u),

where
L(x,u)=f_{0}(x)+\langle F(x),u\rangle
. The corresponding
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker
(KKT) conditions for (
1
) can be written as in the form

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}L(x,u)=0,\quad u\in\partial g(F(x)).

(23)

Given an extended-real-valued function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
with
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
and
u\in\partial f(x)
, define the
second-order variational function
(SOVF) associated with
f
by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{f}(x,u)(v):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{{d\in\mathcal{R}^{m},v=Vd}\atop{V\in{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)}}\langle v,d-v\rangle&\mbox{if}\ v\in\bigcup\limits_{V\in{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)}\operatorname{rge}V,\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and establish its generalized differential properties, which are of their own interest while being used below for deriving the main results of this paper. We first recall the following relationship that is proved in
[
33
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Given an l.s.c. proper convex function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
,
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
and
u\in\partial f(x)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\big\{d\,\big|\,df(x)(d)=\langle u,d\rangle\big\}^{\circ}=\big\{d\,\big|\,df^{*}(u)(d)=\langle x,d\rangle\big\}\cap\big\{d\,\big|\,d^{2}f^{*}(u,x)(d)=0\big\}.

Observe that if
f:=\delta_{C}
and Assumption
2.1
is satisfied at
x
for
u
, then Proposition
3.1
reduces to
[
15
, Corollary 3.1]
.

The next proposition provides a detailed description of the
SOVF domain
in (
3
).

Proposition 3.2
.

Given an extended-real-valued function
f:\mathcal{R}^{m}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, suppose that
f
satisfies Assumption
2.1
at some
x\in\operatorname{dom}f
for
u\in\partial f(x)
. Then
f
is properly twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
u
with
\operatorname{dom}d^{2}f(x,u)=\{d\,|\,df(x)(d)=\langle u,d\rangle\}
. Moreover, for any
v\in\operatorname{dom}d^{2}f(x,u)
and
z\in D(\partial f)(x,u)(v)
, we have the equalities

\displaystyle d^{2}f(x,u)(v)=d^{2}f^{*}(u,x)(z)=\langle v,z\rangle.

(26)

If in addition
f
satisfies Assumption
2.3
at
x
for
u
, then the domain of SOVF is described by

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}\Gamma_{f}(x,u)=\operatorname{aff}\big\{d\,\big|\,df(x)(d)=\langle u,d\rangle\big\}=\operatorname{dom}D^{*}(\partial f)(x,u).

(27)

Proof.

By Assumption
2.1
, we deduce from
[
18
, Theorem 3.8]
that the function
f
is properly twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
u
with the domain
\operatorname{dom}d^{2}f(x,u)
defined as
\left\{d\,\left|\,df(x)(d)=\langle u,d\rangle\right.\right\}
. Consequently,
[
5
, Lemma 3.6]
yields the equality
d^{2}f(x,u)(v)=\langle v,z\rangle
. Employing
[
32
, Theorem 13.21]
and
[
30
, Theorem 23.5]
leads us to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d^{2}f(x,u)(v)+\frac{1}{2}d^{2}f^{*}(u,x)(z)=\langle v,z\rangle,

which completes the verification of (
26
).

We know by the convexity of the l.s.c. function
f
that it is prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous at every point in
\operatorname{dom}f
. Taking into account the twice epi-differentiability of
f
and using (
9
), (
10
), Assumption
2.1
together with
[
32
, Theorem 13.57]
allows us
to conclude that

\displaystyle w\in z+D(\partial f)(x,u)(z)\subset z+D^{*}(\partial f)(x,u)(z)=D^{*}(I+\partial f)(x,x+u)(z)

for all
w\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
and
z\in D\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)(w)
. Consequently, we have

-z\in D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)(-w)
,
which implies in turn that

\displaystyle\operatorname{rge}D\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)\subset-\operatorname{rge}D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u).

Invoking now Assumption
2.3
and
[
33
, Lemma 4.2]
tells us that

\displaystyle\operatorname{aff}\operatorname{dom}D(\partial f)(x,u)=\operatorname{aff}\operatorname{rge}D\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)=\operatorname{rge}D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u).

Combining the latter with
[
11
, Proposition 2.4]
, and
[
32
, Corollary 13.53]
yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{rge}\overline{W}\subset\operatorname{rge}D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)\subset\bigcup\limits_{V\in{\cal J}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)}\operatorname{rge}V=\operatorname{rge}\overline{W},\end{aligned}

which justifies (

[

and thus completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Under the assumptions of the proposition above, we get the alternative formulation of the SOVF
\Gamma_{f}(x,u)
as follows, which greatly simplifies its calculation and applications.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f
satisfy all the assumptions of Proposition
3.2
.
Then for any
v\in\operatorname{dom}\Gamma_{f}(x,u)
, we have the representations

\displaystyle\Gamma_{f}(x,u)(-v)=\Gamma_{f}(x,u)(v),\quad\Gamma_{f}(x,u)(v)=\min_{z\in D^{*}(\partial f)(x,u)(v)}\langle v,z\rangle.

(28)

Proof.

The first formula in (
28
) follows immediately from definition (
3
). To verify the second one, we deduce from
Proposition
3.2
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{dom}\Gamma_{f}(x,u)=\operatorname{dom}D^{*}(\partial f)(x,u)=\operatorname{rge}D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u).

Picking any
v\in\operatorname{dom}\Gamma_{f}(x,u)
and
z\in D^{*}(\partial f)(x,u)(v)
gives us by (
9
) and (
10
) that

\displaystyle\langle v,z\rangle=\langle-v,w-(-v)\rangle\;\mbox{ with }\;w=-v-z,\,-v\in D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)(w),

which yields therefore the equality

\displaystyle\min_{z\in D^{*}(\partial f)(x,u)(v)}\langle v,z\rangle=\min_{-v\in D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)(w)}\langle-v,w-(-v)\rangle.

On one hand, it follows from
[
32
, Corollary 13.53]
the lower estimate

\displaystyle\Gamma_{f}(x,u)(-v)\leq\min_{-v\in D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)(w)}\langle-v,w-(-v)\rangle.

On the other hand,
[
11
, Proposition 2.4]
and Assumption
2.3
tell us that

\displaystyle\min_{-v\in D^{*}\operatorname{Prox}_{f}(x+u)(w)}\langle-v,w-(-v)\rangle\leq\langle-v,d-(-v)\rangle=\Gamma_{f}(x,u)(-v),\quad\mbox{with}\ -v=\overline{W}d,

which thus completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Observe that under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
, the function
\Gamma_{f}(x,u)
is
generalized quadratic
with its domain being a

. This holds, e.g., when

5
Conclusions and Further Research

The main achievements of this paper provide no-gap pointbased characterizations of tilt-stable local minimizers for a broad class of composite optimization problems without nondegeneracy assumptions. Just a few results have been obtained in this direction for problems of nonlinear programming and—partly—for second-order cone programming with a single Lorentz cone. Based on advanced tools of variational analysis and generalized differentiation, we develop here a general scheme to establish such pointbased no-gap characterizations of tilt stability without nondegeneracy under fairly mild assumptions that include the weakest metric subregularity constraint qualification. The obtained results involve a novel notion of the second-order variational function whose generalized differential properties play a crucial role in deriving our powerful characterizations of tilt-stable minimizers for nonpolyhedral and possible degenerate programs. Along with the conventional assumptions on the program data, we also impose a technical requirement (Assumption
2.3
) that is inherent in the class of composite optimization problems under consideration. This assumption is constructively verified in the appendix below for the spectral norm function in the composition, which is highly important for various applications.

Among topics of our future research, we mention revealing broader classes of composite optimization problems, where Assumption
2.3
and the pointbased no-gap characterizations of tilt stability in Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
can be explicitly calculated in terms of the given data. Moreover, we intend to incorporate the established no-gap pointbased characterizations of tilt stability into the design and justification of numerical methods of optimization; compare, e.g.,
[
12
,
21
,
27
]
for particular classes of problems in constrained optimization.
