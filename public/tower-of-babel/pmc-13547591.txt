Single‐Cell Transcriptome of Anaplastic Thyroid Cancer Reveals Immunosuppressive Tumor Microenvironment Remodeling
Authors: Saeko Tahara, Akiko Iguchi‐Manaka, Naoto Takeuchi, Tomohei Matsuo, Akira Shibuya, Kazuko Shibuya, Haruka Ozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) is a rare but extremely aggressive malignancy responsible for most thyroid cancer–related deaths. To elucidate molecular and immunological characteristics distinguishing ATC from aggressive papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing on three ATC and three lethal PTC samples. ATC tumor cells exhibited enhanced inflammatory and immune‐related gene expression, and co‐activation of MAPK and PI3K pathways. Notably, p53 pathway activity was suppressed in ATC, alongside activation of angiogenic and immune‐evasion programs, indicating a shift from proliferation‐driven to immune‐evasion–oriented signaling. Transcriptional network analysis revealed E2F family activation, including E2F1, E2F7, and E2F8, suggesting CDK–RB–E2F axis dysregulation drives tumor dedifferentiation and proliferation, whereby p53 suppression unleashes E2F‐dependent transcriptional reprogramming. Immunoprofiling revealed CD8+ T cells in ATC showed elevated exhaustion signatures and high expression of inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules, accompanied by enhanced Treg–CD8+ T cell interactions, indicating a dual‐layered immunosuppressive mechanism involving both T‐cell exhaustion and enhanced Treg‐mediated suppression. In addition, we identified antigen‐presenting CAFs with altered CD4+ T‐cell interactions. These findings delineate a comprehensive single‐cell landscape of ATC and demonstrate coordinated alterations in tumor‐intrinsic signaling and the tumor microenvironment during dedifferentiation, provide a foundation for developing rational combination immunotherapies tailored to the molecular and immunological landscape of thyroid cancer.

Introduction

Anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) is a rare type of thyroid cancer with an extremely poor prognosis, with a median overall survival (OS) of only 4 to 10 months after diagnosis [1]. In contrast, differentiated papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), the most common type of thyroid cancer, usually has a favorable course, with a 10‐year survival rate exceeding 90%. However, a subset of PTC cases develop local invasion, distant metastasis, or recurrence, becoming difficult to control with existing treatments and resulting in a poor prognosis. Such poor‐prognosis PTC can exhibit clinically similar refractory characteristics to ATC, underscoring the importance of elucidating the mechanisms underlying malignancy in these two entities to develop novel therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have attracted increasing attention in the treatment of thyroid cancers. A Phase II clinical trial combining lenvatinib and pembrolizumab reported promising efficacy in patients with ATC and poorly differentiated thyroid cancer (PDTC), suggesting a particularly high response rate in the ATC cohort [2]. Conversely, a Phase II trial evaluating the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab showed low response rates in radioiodine refractory (RAIR) differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) and medullary thyroid cancer (MTC), with a response signal observed only in the ATC group [3]. These findings suggest that while ATC may be responsive to ICI therapy, the efficacy of ICIs in DTC remains limited.

Recent studies on the immune microenvironment of ATC have revealed enrichment of immunosuppressive myeloid cells and fibroblasts within the tumors, as well as profound T‐cell exhaustion [4]. These observations imply that immune evasion contributes to the lethal clinical course of ATC. Nevertheless, direct comparative analyses of the immune landscapes of clinically poor‐prognosis PTC and ATC and comprehensively analyzing their immune environments remain scarce. Moreover, studies based on primary, patient‐derived ATC samples are still limited, largely due to the rarity and clinical inaccessibility of this tumor type.

In this study, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing analysis (scRNA‐seq) on surgical specimens from three patients with ATC and three patients with aggressive PTC that underwent surgery at our institution, enabling comprehensive characterization of tumor, immune, and stromal cell populations. Specifically, we analyzed signaling pathway dependency in tumor cells, immune cell states, and intercellular communication networks. Through this approach, we aimed to construct a single‐cell transcriptome analysis framework for understanding the immunosuppressive tumor ecosystem of ATC and to provide mechanistic insights useful for future immunotherapy strategies.

Materials and Methods

Human Tissues

Fresh surgical tissue was obtained from six thyroid cancer patients who underwent surgery at the University of Tsukuba Hospital between 2020 and 2021. Clinical and histological information was corrected from the medical record system and summarized in Table S1. Cancer classification was performed based on histopathological evaluation of H&E‐stained tissue sections, following the Eighth Edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors.

Tissue Dissociation and scRNA‐Seq

Fresh tissues were minced into 1 mm3 pieces and digested in medium containing Liberase TH Research Grade (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 h with gentle pipetting every 15 min. DNase I (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added 10 min before the end of the incubation period. The cell suspensions were washed with HBSS and filtered using a 70 μm cell strainer. Cells were cryopreserved, thawed prior to library preparation, and PI‐positive dead cells and debris were removed by MoFlo XDP (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) sorting. scRNA‐seq libraries were prepared using 10× Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X platform, and the raw data were processed into FASTQ files and analyzed to count unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) using Cell Ranger (v7.1.0) and reference data (GRCh38 (GENCODE v32/Ensembl 98); GRCh38‐2020‐A).

scRNA‐Seq Data Processing

The scRNA‐seq count matrices generated by Cell Ranger were processed with Seurat v5.0.1 (R 4.5.0). For each sample, we first computed quality‐control metrics, including the number of detected features (nFeature_RNA) and the fraction of mitochondrial transcripts (percent.mt). Low‐quality cells were removed by discarding the bottom and top 0.5% tails of the nFeature_RNA distribution per sample and cells with high mitochondrial content (percent.mt ≥ 80%). Doublets were identified with DoubletFinder (version 2.0.6). Normalization, scaling, and integration of the dataset were performed using the Seurat functions with the default parameters.

Cell Type Annotation From scRNA‐Seq Data

Automated cell‐type prediction was combined with manual annotation to precisely determine the cell types of single cells. R package scType (loading function by “source()” command) [5] and SingleR (version 2.10.0, “monaco_immune”, “2024‐02‐26” and “dice”, “2024‐02‐26”) [6] were applied to predict cell type annotation and validated by cell‐type‐specific markers in previous reports [4, 7].

Definitions of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

The “FindMarkers” function in Seurat was used to detect DEGs in the analysis of thyrocytes and fibroblasts. DEGs were required to be expressed in more than 5% of cells within at least 1 group [adjusted p‐value < 0.001 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1].

Identification of Differentially Activated Regulon (DAReg)

The Python package pySCENIC (version 0.12.1, Python 3.10.14) [8, 9] was applied to the thyrocytes scRNA‐seq dataset, and the regulon activities quantified by the area under the recovery curve (AUC) score were obtained (Table S4). The obtained regulon activities were processed in R using the Seurat package for downstream analyses. Differential regulon activity analysis was conducted with the “FindMarkers” function in Seurat using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with the Bonferroni correction (pseudocount.use = 1, default) referred to Nakata et al. [10]. Upregulated or downregulated regulons (differentially activated regulons; DAReg) were identified if the adjusted p‐value was less than 0.05 and log2FC was > 0.07 or < −0.07.

Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis

Gene ontology enrichment analyses were performed using EnrichR [11, 12, 13] (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) and Metascape [14] (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1). The identified DEGs were submitted to EnrichR and Metascape, and the results were re‐visualized by ggplot2 in R.

Enrichment Analysis of Exhausted T Cell Marker Genes

Exhausted T‐cell marker genes were defined as the 42 genes listed in Zhang et al. (Table S2) [15]. For each single cell, single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed, and an enrichment score for the exhausted T‐cell marker genes was computed using the “ssGSEA” function from the GSVA R package (v2.2.0) [16]. ssGSEA scores were then compared between ATC and PTC using a two‐sided Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (p‐value < 0.05) to assess differences in exhaustion‐signature enrichment.

Cell–Cell Interaction Analysis by CellChat

To infer cell–cell interactions, we applied CellChat (version 2.2.0) [17, 18] to our dataset. The human ligand–receptor pair repertoire was obtained from the CellChat package. p‐values and communication probabilities were calculated following the standard procedure, and the “netVisual_circle” function was used to visualize intercellular communication networks. For statistical evaluation, CellChat analysis was performed separately for each sample to estimate cell–cell interactions. The interaction strength (weight) of selected cell cluster pairs was compared between three ATC samples and three PTC samples using the Wilcoxon rank sum test (p < 0.05).

Immunohistochemistry for p53

Immunohistochemistry for p53 was performed on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissue sections using a monoclonal mouse anti‐human p53 antibody (clone DO‐7, Dako/Agilent Technologies). Staining was carried out on an automated staining platform (Autostainer Link 48, Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer's protocol, with heat‐induced antigen retrieval. Detection was performed using the EnVision FLEX system (Dako/Agilent Technologies).

Validation of Tumor‐Intrinsic and Immune Microenvironment Features Using External Datasets

Publicly available microarray datasets of anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) and papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) (GSE33630 and GSE29265) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and processed using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 platform (GPL570). Samples were classified as ATC or PTC based on metadata, yielding 20 ATC and 91 PTC samples after filtering. Probe‐level expression values were mapped to gene symbols, with the mean used for genes represented by multiple probes, and only genes shared across both datasets (12,345 genes) were retained. Missing values (NA) were excluded. Pathway activity scores for p53 and E2F were calculated as the mean expression of constituent Hallmark genes (HALLMARK_P53_PATHWAY and HALLMARK_E2F_TARGETS), while E2F family genes and proliferation markers were analyzed individually. Thyrocyte marker gene scores were computed as the mean expression of TG, TSHR, TPO, SLC5A5 (NIS), and PAX8. CD8+ T cell infiltration and T cell exhaustion were quantified using ssGSEA with a predefined CD8+ T cell signature (CD8A, CD8B, GZMB, PRF1) and a 42‐gene exhaustion signature reported by Zhang et al., respectively. Key immune checkpoint genes were analyzed individually. Comparisons between ATC and PTC were performed using two‐sided Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests, and p‐values were reported to three significant digits. Data visualization was performed in R (v4.2.3) using ggplot2. Analyses were conducted using the GEOquery, limma, GSVA, msigdbr, and ggplot2 R packages.

Results

ATC Was Characterized by a Higher Abundance of T and NK Cells Compared With Aggressive PTC

To investigate characteristics of ATC compared to aggressive PTC, we performed high‐throughput 3′ scRNA‐Seq (10× Genomics Chromium) on tumor samples from six patients with thyroid cancer, including ATC and PTC (Table S1, Figure 1A). The PTC samples included a solid variant with a poorly differentiated component associated with distant metastasis, an NRAS mutation‐positive follicular variant with distant metastasis, and a BRAF mutation‐positive poor prognosis case, indicating that these tumors represented aggressive PTCs (Figure S1A). After quality control and filtering (Section 2), a total of 68,419 cells were retained for downstream analyses. Integration of the six datasets followed by unsupervised clustering identified six major cell populations, visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Figure 1B). These clusters were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression (Figure 1C,D; Figure S1B) and automated annotation using scType [5], resulting in the identification of T and natural killer cells (T & NK cells) (33,248 cells), myeloid cells (15,688 cells), B cells (6953 cells), fibroblasts (2764 cells), endothelial cells (1864 cells), and thyrocyte‐derived tumor cells (thyrocytes) (7902 cells).

(B) UMAP visualization demonstrating six major clusters: T and natural killer cells (T & NK cells) (33,248 cells), myeloid cells (15,688 cells), B cells (6953 cells), fibroblasts (2764 cells), endothelial cells (1864 cells), and thyrocyte‐derived tumor cells (thyrocyte) (7902 cells). We next compared the proportion of each significant cell population across tumor samples of different histological subtypes. In ATC, T & NK cells were predominant (60%–88%), and their proportion was significantly higher than PTC (Welch's t‐test, Holm‐adjusted p < 0.05) (Figure 1E,F). In PTC, the cellular composition was heterogeneous among the three samples. In the BRAF mutation sample (TC015T), the cellular composition resembled that of ATC, whereas in TC006T and TC009T, thyrocyte‐derived tumor cells (~30%) or myeloid cells (~50%) were predominant, resulting in a cellular composition distinct from ATC.

Tumor‐Intrinsic Signaling and Regulome Analyses Identify MAPK/PI3K Co‐Activation, p53 Suppression, and E2F Activation in ATC

Upregulation of Genes Related to Inflammation and Immune Responses in ATC Tumor Cells

To investigate the molecular characteristics of tumor cells in ATC compared with aggressive PTC, we first confirmed that thyrocyte marker genes (TG, TSHR, TPO, and PAX8) were predominantly expressed in PTC (Figure 2A). In contrast, markers previously reported to be associated with ATC [19, 20], including TPM4, CDK6, TOP2A, UBE2C, and PBK, were highly expressed in ATC, validating the reliability of the tumor cell annotation (Figure 2A).

To further investigate the molecular features specifically associated with ATC, we identified DEGs between PTC and ATC tumor cells (PTC‐DEGs and ATC‐DEGs) (Section 2, Figure 2B; Table S3). PTC‐DEGs included the thyrocyte marker genes (TG, TSHR, TPO and PAX8), whereas these genes were largely absent in ATC, highlighting the dedifferentiated state of ATC tumor cells. Importantly, ATC‐DEGs were significantly enriched in immune response–related GO terms (e.g., regulation of leukocyte activation, positive regulation of immune response, and immune effector process) (Figure 2C), consistent with previous studies reporting that genes associated with inflammation and immune response are upregulated in ATC compared with PTC [4, 21, 22, 23]. In pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle3 [24] (Figure S2A), the expression of the thyrocyte markers progressively decreased along the inferred pseudotime axis toward ATC (Figure S2B). To identify genes dynamically regulated during dedifferentiation, we classified pseudotime‐dependent gene expression changes into 19 distinct modules (Figure S2C). Notably, a module with increased expression in late pseudotime (module 2) was enriched for immune response–related GO terms (e.g., adaptive immune response) (Figure S2D), consistent with ATC‐DEG analysis. These results suggest that immune‐related changes are associated with the process of dedifferentiation toward ATC. Because the pseudotime analysis was intended to characterize transcriptional changes along the established dedifferentiation axis from PTC to ATC, rather than to infer de novo lineage directionality, RNA velocity analysis was not performed.

Activation of MAPK and PI3K Pathways and Inhibition of the p53 Pathway in ATC

To investigate differences in pathway activation between ATC and aggressive PTC, we applied PROGENy [25] to infer pathway activities from gene expression profiles. Comparison between ATC and aggressive PTC revealed that the MAPK and PI3K pathways were more strongly activated in ATC, whereas the p53 pathway showed reduced activity (Figure 2D). At the individual sample level, the PTC samples showed distinct patterns depending on driver mutations: the BRAF‐mutant PTC sample (TC015T) exhibited strong dependence on the MAPK pathway, while the NRAS‐mutant PTC sample (TC009T) showed co‐activation of both the MAPK and PI3K pathways, consistent with previous studies [26]. In contrast, ATC samples (TC013T, TC008T, and TC016T) demonstrated activation of the MAPK and PI3K pathways regardless of the BRAF or RAS mutation status. Moreover, ATC samples relatively reduced dependence on MAPK signaling compared with BRAF‐ or NRAS‐driven PTC and instead exhibited activation of additional pathways, including those related to inflammation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion.

Immunohistochemical analysis of p53 revealed heterogeneous staining patterns among ATC and PTC samples (Figure S2E). Among the three ATC cases, two (TC008 and TC013) showed strong and diffuse nuclear staining (overexpression pattern), whereas one (TC016) exhibited weak and sporadic staining. In contrast, two PTC cases (TC006 and TC009) were negative for p53 expression, and one (TC015) showed sporadic staining. As both overexpression and complete absence represent aberrant (mutant‐type) patterns, whereas sporadic staining represents a wild‐type pattern, two of three cases in each group thus showed a mutant‐type and one a wild‐type pattern. However, because immunohistochemistry does not directly reflect p53 pathway activity, these patterns were interpreted separately from the transcriptomic analysis, which consistently indicated reduced p53 pathway activity in ATC.

Identification of the E2F Family as the ATC‐Specific Active Regulons

To identify gene regulatory networks specifically activated in ATC, we applied pySCENIC [8, 9] (Section 2) to the tumor cell dataset. Regulons showing higher activation in either tumor type relative to the other were defined as differentially active regulons (DARegs), and 86 ATC‐DARegs and 53 PTC‐DARegs were identified (Table S4; Figure 2E). Next, we examined the overlap between ATC‐DARegs and the genes of the upregulated pathways identified by PROGENy (Figure 2D). Among genes with increased expression in the MAPK pathway, 36 regulons overlapped with ATC‐DARegs, and 7 regulons overlapped with those in the PI3K pathway. Notably, genes in the E2F family—including E2F1, E2F7, and E2F8—were identified as ATC‐DARegs as well as components of the MAPK/PI3K pathways in PROGENy (Figure 2F).

External Validation of E2F Activation and p53 Pathway Suppression in ATC

To further validate these observations, we analyzed independent bulk transcriptomic datasets (GSE33630 and GSE29265). Consistent with our single‐cell analysis, ATC samples showed reduced thyrocyte marker gene expression, decreased p53 pathway activity and increased E2F pathway activity, together with increased expression of E2F family genes (E2F1, E2F7, and E2F8) and proliferation‐associated markers (MKI67 and TOP2A) (Figure S2F). These concordant results across independent datasets reinforce the robustness of p53 pathway suppression and E2F‐driven transcriptional reprogramming as key features of ATC dedifferentiation.

Identification of Lymphoid Immune Cell Subtypes Revealed Immunosuppressive Features in ATC

Re‐Clustering and Annotation of Immune Cells

To investigate the immunological characteristics of lymphoid cells in thyroid cancer, we performed re‐clustering analysis of T cells, NK cells, and myeloid cells. In T cells and NK cells, seven major clusters (Figure S3A), which were annotated as CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), NK cells, proliferating cells, and low‐quality cells were identified based on scType [5], SingleR [6], and canonical cell‐type specific marker expressions [4, 7]. The low‐quality cell cluster did not exhibit characteristic expression patterns of γδ T‐cell or Th17‐related marker genes, suggesting that misclassification of these subsets was unlikely. Moreover, the CD4+ T cells were classified into CD4_TCM (central memory CD4+ T cells) and CD4_TRM (resident memory CD4+ T cells), and the CD8+ T cells were classified into CD8_TEM (effector memory CD8+ T cells), CD8_TEF_TEMRA (effector/terminally differentiated effector memory CD8+ T cells), CD8_exhausted (exhausted CD8+ T cells), and CD8_TRM (resident memory CD8+ T cells) (Figure 3A) based on marker and functional gene expression patterns (Figure 3B). In myeloid cells, re‐clustering revealed 6 clusters: classical monocyte, non‐classical monocyte, cDC1 (classical dendritic cells 1), cDC2, pDC (plasmacytoid DC), and macrophages (Figure S3B), validated by marker gene expressions (Figure S3C). Comparison of the proportions of the lymphoid cell subpopulations across histological subtypes showed no significant difference between ATC and PTC (Figure 3C; Figure S3D).

Immunosuppressive Phenotype of CD8 + T Cells in ATC

We next investigated whether their functional state differs between ATC and PTC even though CD8+ T cells (CD8_TEM, CD8_TEF_TEMRA, CD8_exhausted, and CD8_TRM) were detected at comparable levels across samples. Using ssGSEA analysis per cells with the 42 exhausted T cell marker genes from Zhang et al. [15] (Section 2), we found that enrichment scores (ssGSEA scores) of the exhaustion markers were significantly higher in ATC than in PTC (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p < 2.2e‐16) (Figure 3D), indicating preferential enrichment of the exhaustion signature in ATC. Consistently, CD8+ T cells in ATC exhibited significantly higher expression of inhibitory checkpoint molecules—TIGIT, LAG3, HAVCR2 (TIM‐3), CTLA4, and PDCD1 (PD‐1)—than those in PTC (Figure 3E), a pattern consistent with functional exhaustion. These findings support an immunosuppressive microenvironment in ATC, characterized by increased impaired CD8+ T‐cell cytotoxicity.

External Validation of Immune Microenvironment Features in ATC

To externally validate these immunosuppressive features, we analyzed independent bulk microarray datasets (GSE33630 and GSE29265). Using ssGSEA analysis with the 42 exhausted T cell marker genes from Zhang et al., we found that the exhaustion score was significantly higher in ATC than in PTC (Figure S3E), indicating preferential enrichment of exhausted T cell–related transcriptional programs in ATC. Consistently, expression of key inhibitory checkpoint molecules—including TIGIT, LAG3, HAVCR2 (TIM‐3), CTLA4, PDCD1 (PD‐1), and CD274 (PD‐L1)—was significantly elevated in ATC compared with PTC (Figure S3E), mirroring our single‐cell analyses. These findings further support an immunosuppressive microenvironment in ATC, characterized by increased infiltration of functionally impaired CD8+ T cells.

Cancer‐Associated Fibroblast (CAF) Subtypes in Thyroid Cancer and Their Enrichment in ATC and PTC

In the analysis of fibroblasts, we examined the expression of CAF marker genes, including VIM, ACTA2 (αSMA), and COL1A1. Expression patterns of the eight CAF marker genes varied across cells, and almost all fibroblasts expressed at least one of these marker genes (Figure 4A), indicating that the fibroblasts analyzed here represent CAFs. To characterize the fibroblast subpopulation in thyroid cancer, we re‐clustered the fibroblast cells across all samples into 3 clusters (Figure 4B); myoCAFs (myofibroblastic CAFs), apCAFs (antigen‐presenting CAFs) [27] and uncCAFs (unclassified CAFs) based on the marker gene expression and gene ontology analysis using the DEGs specific to each cluster (Figure 4C,D, Table S5). The apCAFs overexpressed MHC Class II molecules and CD74 (Figure 4A) and were characterized by MHC Class II–related GO terms (Figure 4D, left), whereas the myoCAFs overexpressed myofibroblastic genes such as ACTA2, COL1A1, and THY1 (Figure 4A) and were characterized by collagen‐related and extracellular matrix–associated GO terms (Figure 4D, right), consistent with the previous report of apCAF and myoCAF [27]. The myoCAF population appeared to include two subpopulations: one characterized by high ACTA2 expression and another characterized by high COL1A1 expression (Figure 4A). Although the DEGs specific to the other cell cluster were enriched in the cytoskeleton and synapse (Figure S4), the function and phenotype of this cluster were not clearly defined, and we therefore termed it uncCAFs. While the proportions of CAF clusters did not differ significantly between ATC and aggressive PTC (Figure 4E), these findings highlight the heterogeneity of fibroblast populations in both tumor types.

Immunosuppressive Environment in ATC Demonstrated by Cell–Cell Interaction Analysis

To explore characteristics of cell–cell communication in ATC, we performed cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat [17]. In ATC, 452 ligand–receptor interactions were detected, whereas 510 interactions were identified in aggressive PTC (Figure 5A). To examine anti‐tumor immune responses against thyroid cancer cells, we first focused on interactions mediated by tumor cells. Interactions between tumor cells and other cell types were weaker in ATC compared with PTC (Figure 5B). In particular, interactions between tumor cells and CD8+ T cell subsets (CD8_exhausted, CD8_TEF_TEMRA, CD8_TEM, and CD8_TRM) as well as NK cells, which play a central role in anti‐tumor immune responses, were significantly weaker in ATC than in PTC (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p < 0.05) (Figure 5C). Notably, in PTC, interactions involving granzyme (GZMA) between NK cells or CD8_TEF_TEMRA cells and tumor cells were observed (Figure S5A), whereas such significant interactions were absent in ATC. These findings suggest that ATC establishes an immune‐evasive microenvironment.

The node colors represent cell clusters, and the size of the node is proportional to the total number of interactions (incoming and outgoing signals) associated with that cell cluster. The edges (connecting lines) indicate predicted ligand–receptor communications between cell clusters, and the thickness reflects the weight (strength) of communication. (B, D, F) Inferred cell–cell communication networks through thyrocytes (B), Tregs (D), and apCAFs (F) are shown. (C, E, G) Statistical analysis of interaction strength from thyrocytes to CD8+ T/NK cells (C), from Tregs to CD8+ T cells (E), and from apCAFs to CD4+ T cells (G) (Student's t‐test, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).

Interactions through Tregs tended to be stronger in ATC than in PTC (Figure 5D), and the strength of interactions between Tregs and CD8+ T cells was significantly greater in ATC compared with PTC (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p < 0.05) (Figure 5E). Even though the proportion of Tregs and the expression of CTLA4, ICOS, and CCR8 were not significantly different between ATC and PTC (Figure 3C; Figure S5B), Treg‐associated gene expressions differed; higher IL2RA expression in PTC and higher ENTPD1 and CCR4 expression in ATC (Figure S5B). These findings suggest that the distinct mechanism of immunosuppression by Tregs can contribute to the distinct immunosuppressive microenvironments in ATC.

ApCAFs have been reported to express MHC Class II molecules, suggesting their potential interaction with CD4+ T cells [27]. Indeed, interactions between apCAFs and CD4+ T cell subsets (TCM and TRM) mediated by MHC Class II molecules were detected by CellChat, and these interactions were significantly weaker in ATC compared with PTC (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p < 0.05) (Figure 5F,G; Figure S5C), suggesting attenuated immune activation and antitumor responses in ATC compared with PTC.

Discussion

ATC is an extremely aggressive malignancy that accounts for most of the thyroid cancer–related deaths despite its rarity. It is hypothesized that ATC arises from DTC, as suggested by the frequent clinical history of DTC and the histological coexistence of ATC and DTC in the same specimens [28]. DTCs are broadly classified into two major molecular subtypes—BRAF‐mutant and RAS‐mutant—which are largely mutually exclusive [29]. In contrast, ATCs frequently harbor additional mutations such as TP53 and TERT promoter mutations, combined with BRAF or RAS alterations [29]. These secondary or late‐acquired mutations are thought to drive dedifferentiation from DTC to ATC, which is accompanied by profound changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Previous studies using immunohistochemistry and bulk transcriptomic analyses have demonstrated that the TME of ATC is highly immunosuppressive compared with DTC [30]. To investigate the molecular and immunological changes underlying this dedifferentiation process, we compared ATC with aggressive papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), a differentiated but clinically lethal form of thyroid cancer, using primary tumor samples collected at our institution.

In this study, we performed scRNA‐seq on three ATC samples and three lethal PTC samples, revealing molecular and cellular differences between ATC and aggressive PTC (Figure 1). In the tumor cells, genes related to inflammation and immune responses were upregulated in ATC compared with aggressive PTC (Figure 2), suggesting that tumor dedifferentiation is accompanied by a profound remodeling of the immune microenvironment. Pathway activity analysis revealed that BRAF‐mutant PTC tumor cells exhibited strong dependency on the MAPK pathway, whereas ATC tumor cells showed relatively decreased MAPK dependency and co‐activation of the PI3K pathway. Notably, ATC tumor cells also showed suppression of the p53 pathway, along with activation of inflammatory, angiogenic, and immune‐escape–related signaling pathways, indicating a shift in pathway dependency from proliferation‐driven to immune‐evasion–oriented programs during dedifferentiation. Since ATC is thought to arise through a multistep dedifferentiation process [31], the observed reduction of p53 pathway activity in ATC is consistent with previous mouse model studies demonstrating that thyroid‐specific expression of Braf V600E combined with Trp53 deletion or mutation drives progression from PTC to PDTC/ATC and accelerates tumor aggressiveness [32]. In this context, our immunohistochemical findings provide orthogonal support for p53 pathway dysregulation: although strong and diffuse nuclear accumulation of p53 is generally regarded as a surrogate marker of TP53 mutation, the correlation between p53 immunohistochemistry and TP53 mutational status is imperfect, as certain mutations result in complete loss of expression [33]. While the predominance of overexpression patterns in ATC is broadly consistent with the high prevalence of TP53 alterations reported in these tumors [34]. The heterogeneous staining patterns observed in both ATC and PTC warrant cautious interpretation. Therefore, our conclusion regarding reduced p53 pathway activity in ATC is based primarily on transcriptomic pathway analysis, which was reproduced in independent external datasets, rather than on immunohistochemical assessment alone.

Moreover, 86 ATC‐specific DARegs were identified, and their overlap with genes upregulated in the MAPK and PI3K pathways suggested that E2F family genes (E2F1, E2F7, and E2F8) are the key regulators activated in ATC (Figure 2). The E2F family of transcription factors comprises eight members that regulate cell proliferation and cell cycle progression. In cancer, E2F transcription activity is elevated due to disruption of the CDK‐RB‐E2F axis [35]. Cross‐sectional analyses of multiple tumors using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data have reported that high expression of E2F family genes correlates with poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and lung cancer [36]. Furthermore, it has been shown that, except for E2F5, other E2F family genes are highly expressed in TP53‐mutated cancers compared with TP53 wild‐type cancers [36]. In normal cells, p53 induces E2F7 and E2F8 to suppress excessive E2F1 activity. However, under p53 deficiency, E2F7 and E2F8 are reported to be aberrantly overexpressed, thereby exerting tumor‐promoting functions such as angiogenesis and metastasis [35]. These findings suggest that disruption of cell cycle control due to p53 pathway suppression and E2F‐dependent transcriptional reprogramming may contribute to the dedifferentiation and the maintenance of proliferative capacity in ATC. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that E2F7 plays a critical role in driving anaplastic transformation in thyroid cancer [37]. External validation using independent bulk microarray datasets corroborated these findings, in which ATC samples showed reduced thyroid differentiation scores and p53 pathway activity together with increased expression of E2F1, E2F7, and E2F8 as well as proliferation‐associated genes, reinforcing the robustness of E2F‐driven transcriptional reprogramming as a hallmark of ATC dedifferentiation. Collectively, these findings suggest an association between reduced p53 pathway activity and E2F‐dependent transcriptional reprogramming in ATC. Such alterations may contribute to the proliferative and dedifferentiated phenotypes characteristic of ATC.

Lymphoid re‐clustering analysis revealed that CD8+ T cells in ATC exhibited markedly elevated expression of the exhaustion signature and inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules—including PD‐1, CTLA‐4, HAVCR2, LAG‐3, and TIGIT—compared with those in aggressive PTC (Figure 3). In addition, cell–cell interaction analysis identified granzyme‐mediated interactions between PTC cells and NK cells or effector/terminally differentiated effector memory CD8+ T cells, whereas such interactions were absent in ATC (Figure 5). Furthermore, interactions with Tregs were stronger in ATC, indicating the establishment of an immunosuppressive Treg–CD8+ T cell network. Specifically, although no significant differences were observed in the proportion of Tregs or the expression of standard markers between ATC and PTC, differences were noted in the Treg‐related gene expression profiles: ATC showed higher expression of ENTPD1 and CCR4, while PTC showed higher expression of IL2RA, suggesting qualitative differences in Treg suppressive phenotypes. These findings suggest that ATC harbors a dual‐layered immunosuppressive mechanism, characterized by both T‐cell exhaustion and functionally enhanced Treg‐mediated suppression. External validation using independent bulk microarray datasets further supported these findings, demonstrating significantly higher exhausted T‐cell signature scores and increased expression of inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules in ATC compared with PTC. These results reinforce the presence of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in ATC characterized by functionally impaired CD8+ T‐cell responses. Both factors may differentially affect responsiveness to ICIs: exhausted CD8+ T cells are the primary targets of ICI‐mediated reinvigoration, whereas Tregs, which suppress effector T‐cell activity, are also potential targets of ICI therapy. This immune landscape provides a biological basis for the distinct response to ICIs observed among thyroid cancer subtypes. Indeed, clinical trials have shown that ICI monotherapy exhibits limited efficacy in DTC [38], whereas ATC appears more responsive [39], likely reflecting ATC's higher immune signaling activity and checkpoint expression status. The presence of dual immunosuppressive mechanisms in ATC further supports the rationale that combination ICI therapy may achieve greater efficacy than monotherapy, as suggested by recent clinical studies [3].

Recently, Boruah et al. performed multiplex immunofluorescence analysis on a relatively large ATC cohort (n = 26) and reported that while PD‐L1 was positive in many ATCs, PD‐1 expression on CTLs was rarely detected, resulting in a “target‐missing” situation, an abundance of immunosuppressive cells such as MDSCs and Tregs, as well as exhausted CTLs, and that TIM‐3 and CTLA‐4 were frequently expressed on CTLs [40]. These findings are consistent with the results of this study, which showed that exhaustion‐related gene signatures were significantly enhanced in CD8+ T cells of ATC, with increased expression of multiple checkpoint genes, including HAVCR2. Furthermore, inhibitory gene signatures associated with Tregs and MDSCs were also predominant in ATC. In addition, single‐cell analysis allows for detailed visualization of cell–cell interactions of these immune cells, elucidating the molecular basis of the suppressive microenvironment. These complementary findings further support the necessity of combination immunotherapy involving multiple checkpoint inhibitors, as PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibition alone is insufficient.

CAFs have been reported to be associated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis in thyroid cancer [41, 42], and to play multiple important roles in tumor biology, exerting diverse functions [27]. Recent scRNA‐seq analyses of thyroid cancer have identified two major CAF subtypes, iCAFs and myoCAFs [4]. In our analysis, we identified the potential presence of apCAFs in the thyroid cancer microenvironment (Figure 4). The apCAFs have previously been described only in murine and human pancreatic, lung, breast, and gastric cancers, as well as in cholangiocarcinoma, and are considered to have distinct origins compared with iCAFs and myoCAFs [27, 43, 44]. Taken together, the higher expression of MHC Class II molecules in apCAFs than other CAF subtypes (Figure 4A,C,D) and the reduced apCAF–CD4+ T cell interactions observed in ATC (Figure 5F,G) suggest that immune activation and antitumor responses mediated through interaction with CD4+ T cells are attenuated in ATC compared with PTC. Although further studies will be required to elucidate the cellular origins and biological functions of apCAFs, previous reports have suggested that apCAFs can interact with CD4+ T cells to either induce Tregs or promote CD4+ T cell activation depending on the presence of co‐stimulatory molecules and activation status [27, 43, 44]. Such context‐dependent functions may also underlie the heterogeneous roles of apCAFs in thyroid cancer, highlighting the need for mechanistic studies to define their contributions to the tumor immune microenvironment.

This study has several limitations. First, the low percentage of thyrocytes observed in ATC samples may partly reflect technical limitations inherent to single‐cell RNA sequencing. ATC tissues are often characterized by extensive fibrosis and necrosis, and large, fragile, undifferentiated tumor cells may be preferentially lost during tissue dissociation, filtration, and cell sorting procedures. In addition, the dedifferentiated nature of ATC is associated with increased immune cell infiltration, which could further contribute to the relative enrichment of immune populations in these samples. Therefore, the cellular composition identified by scRNA‐seq may not fully reflect the histological tumor content. Second, the number of analyzed samples was limited, warranting cautious interpretation of the results. Notably, two of the three ATC cases were surgically resected and clinically belonged to a relatively better‐prognosis subset, which may not fully capture the molecular features of conventionally aggressive ATC. Third, the three PTC samples represented distinct molecular subtypes with different driver alterations (e.g., BRAF, NRAS), which may have influenced the tumor microenvironment and complicate direct comparisons. Finally, although activation of the E2F family was identified in ATC, the underlying mechanisms and functional roles of E2F signaling were not experimentally investigated in this study. Further studies with larger cohorts and functional analyses are required to validate and extend these findings.

Despite these limitations, recent studies have further characterized the molecular and immune microenvironmental features of ATC using single‐cell transcriptome [45, 46]. Our findings are broadly consistent with these reports while further extending current knowledge through an integrated analysis of tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cell populations in both ATC and aggressive PTC. In particular, our study highlights coordinated alterations involving E2F‐driven transcriptional reprogramming, suppression of thyroid differentiation and p53 signaling, exhausted CD8+ T‐cell states, Treg‐associated immunosuppression, and CAF heterogeneity during dedifferentiation toward ATC.

Given that most ATCs harbor BRAF mutations, combination therapy with kinase inhibitors with ICIs has demonstrated promising efficacy in recent clinical trials [47]. Considering the dual immunosuppressive mechanisms identified in this study, therapeutic strategies that combine ICIs with Treg‐targeting or other immune‐modulatory approaches may further enhance antitumor activity. For patients with aggressive PTC, personalized therapeutic strategies based on molecular biomarkers, such as driver mutations and immune checkpoint expression, should be pursued to optimize treatment efficacy and prognosis. Although further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies will be required, our findings provide a single‐cell transcriptomic framework for understanding the molecular and immunological landscape of ATC and support the development of rational combination immunotherapies tailored to the tumor microenvironment for this rare but highly lethal malignancy.
