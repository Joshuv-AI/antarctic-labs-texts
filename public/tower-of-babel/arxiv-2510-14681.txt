Hierarchical shot-noise Cox process mixtures for clustering across groups
Alessandro Carminati, Mario Beraha, Federico Camerlenghi, Alessandra Guglielmi

Abstract
Clustering observations across partially exchangeable groups of data is a routine task in Bayesian nonparametrics. Previously proposed models allow for clustering across groups by sharing atoms in the group-specific mixing measures. However, exact atom sharing can be overly rigid when groups differ subtly, introducing a trade-off between clustering and density estimates and fragmenting across-group clusters, particularly at larger sample sizes. We introduce the hierarchical shot-noise Cox process (HSNCP) mixture model, where group-specific atoms concentrate around shared centers through a kernel. This enables accurate density estimation within groups and flexible borrowing across groups, overcoming the density–clustering trade-off of previous approaches. Our construction, built on the shot-noise Cox process, remains analytically tractable: we derive closed-form prior moments and an inter-group correlation, obtain the marginal law and predictive distribution for latent parameters, as well as the posterior of the mixing measures given the latent parameters. We develop an efficient conditional MCMC algorithm for posterior inference. We assess the performance of the HSNCP model through simulations and an application to a large galaxy dataset, demonstrating balanced across-group clusters and improved density estimates compared with the hierarchical Dirichlet process, including under model misspecification.



Keywords:
Partial exchangeability; normalized random measures; dependent random measures; density estimation.

Introduction

Data arising from multiple related groups or populations, often referred to as
partially exchangeable data
, are common in numerous scientific and applied fields, including genetics and bioinformatics
(
Teh et al., 2006
;
Rodríguez et al., 2008
)
, neuroscience
(
Durante and Dunson, 2017
)
, linguistics and text modeling
(
Goldwater et al., 2011
;
Doyle and Elkan, 2009
)
, econometrics
(
Griffin and Steel, 2006
;
Bassetti et al., 2014
)
, and machine learning
(
Jordan, 2010
;
Orbanz and Teh, 2011
)
. These data typically exhibit heterogeneity across groups, reflecting inherent differences in underlying experimental or observational conditions, measurement processes, or environmental contexts, yet also often share some commonalities in their distributional structure. Proper statistical modeling of such partially exchangeable data is crucial to accurately borrow strength across groups, leveraging shared information while flexibly accounting for differences. Bayesian nonparametric (BNP) approaches have emerged as powerful tools to tackle these modeling challenges, due to their intrinsic flexibility and their capacity to adapt complexity to data without imposing restrictive parametric assumptions
(
Müller and Mitra, 2013
;
Hjort et al., 2010
)
.

BNP models for partially exchangeable data date back to the dependent Dirichlet process introduced by
MacEachern, 1999
; see
Quintana et al., 2022
for a recent review.
By partially exchangeable data from different groups, we mean that their law is invariant with respect to permutations that maintain the grouping structure. For infinite sequences of observations, de Finetti’s representation theorem
(
Regazzini, 1991
, see)
implies that partially exchangeable data are independent across groups and independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) within groups, conditioning on a set of group-specific random measures, often modeled with mixtures.
That is, if
Y_{\ell}=(Y_{\ell 1},\ldots,Y_{\ell n_{\ell}})
are data in group
\ell
, as
\ell=1,\ldots,g
, we assume

Y_{\ell i}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{\ell}\overset{iid}{\sim}\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(\cdot\,|\,x)\tilde{p}_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}x)
, where
(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{g})
is a vector of almost surely discrete random distributions.
The hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP,
Teh et al., 2006
) is a particularly notable and widely adopted example of prior for
(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{g})
, extensively employed in various applied contexts including topic modeling
(
Blei et al., 2010
)
, genetic clustering
(
Wang and Wang, 2013
)
, and image analysis
(
Sudderth et al., 2008
)
.
Other nonparametric priors include hierarchical NRMIs
(
Camerlenghi et al., 2019a
;
Argiento et al., 2020
)
, hierarchical species sampling processes
(
Bassetti et al., 2020
)
, the common atoms model of
Denti et al., 2023
, hierarchical nested models
(
Lijoi et al., 2023
)
, hierarchical finite mixture models
(
Colombi et al., 2025
)
, and the latent factor models of
Beraha and Griffin, 2023
.
Despite their methodological diversity, these models assume that the
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
’s are supported on the same atoms.
Alternative constructions, such as GM-dependent models
(
Lijoi et al., 2014
)
, latent nested models
(
Camerlenghi et al., 2019b
)
, and the semi-HDP
(
Beraha et al., 2021
)
allow for the possibility of group-specific atoms, but achieve borrowing of information across groups only through common atoms.

However, exact atom-sharing has proven successful in many scenarios
but imposes rigidity, potentially limiting flexibility when subtle differences across groups matter.
Data in different groups could be collected with different instruments or subject to slightly diverse experimental setups.
Such subtle differences can make clustering across groups less reliable if clusters are forced to depend on identical atoms. To illustrate this point, consider the following experiment: generate
500
observations in each of two groups from a mixture of two Gaussians, whose means are the dashed grey vertical lines in
Figure

1
. In the two groups of data, the cluster means differ only slightly, so we expect to identify only two clusters in the whole dataset.
When using the HDP mixture model to fit the data, we obtain an excellent density estimate, but we recognize four clusters (see the posterior similarity matrix in the bottom row of
Figure

1
), due to the exact atom sharing of the HDP, i.e., there is no borrowing of information across groups.

Motivated by these practical considerations, we propose a novel framework based on the shot-noise Cox process
(
Møller, 2003
)
to introduce a more flexible yet structured approach to modeling and clustering partially exchangeable data.
Our model, termed hierarchical shot-noise Cox process (HSNCP) mixture model, departs from traditional BNP models by allowing group-specific mixture distributions to have components that are not necessarily identical, but rather “close by” in a sense that will be made precise later.
This new model overcomes the limitations inherent to exact atom sharing: looking at
Figure

1
, it is clear that the HSNCP mixture recovers exactly two clusters (see the posterior similarity matrix in the top row) and estimates perfectly the true data generating density.

The HSNCP prior defines group-specific mixing distributions
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})
by normalizing (dependent)
completely random measures, in the same spirit of
James, 2002
;
Prünster, 2002
;
Regazzini et al., 2003
.
The dependence among the group-specific completely random measures is achieved by introducing a shared “mother” Poisson process which drives the borrowing of information across groups. The mother process’ points are shared centers around which the group-specific atoms lay down. This improves the HSNCP prior’s flexibility w.r.t. the HDP or hierarchical NRMIs, which, in contrast, constrain group-specific atoms to be identical.
In addition to improved flexibility, the HSNCP maintains attractive analytical and computational properties. Its structure ensures theoretical tractability, allowing for the explicit calculation of key statistical properties, such as expected values, correlation structures, and a priori clustering behavior.
Furthermore, posterior inference remains computationally manageable, facilitated by an efficient conditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section

2
defines our prior, and elucidates the induced clustering. In
Section

3
we derive several analytical properties of our model: a priori moments and correlation structure, the marginal distribution of a sample from the HSNCP, the posterior distribution of the HSNCP given such a sample, and the predictive distribution which allows for a simple interpretation in terms of a restaurant franchise process with thematic rooms.
Beyond shedding light on our prior, these theoretical results are the backbone of the sampling algorithm presented in
Section

4.2
.
Applications of our model to simulated data, including those of the illustrative example introduced above, are described in Section
5
, and to galaxies measurements are reported in Section
6
. We conclude with a discussion section. The supplementary materials collect all the proofs of our theoretical results, further simulation studies, and additional plots not reported in the main paper.

2
The normalized HSNCP and associated mixtures

An anticipated in the Introduction, we assume a mixture model for data in each group. Specifically, let
Y_{\ell i}
be the
i
-th observation,
i=1,\ldots,n_{\ell}
, in group
\ell=1,\ldots,g
, with each
Y_{\ell i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}))
be a Polish space equipped with the corresponding Borel
\sigma
-field, and
f(\cdot\,|\,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a probability density kernel, where
f(\cdot\,|\,x)
is a density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
, w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.
Then, we assume

Y_{\ell i}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{\ell}\overset{iid}{\sim}\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(\cdot\,|\,x)\tilde{p}_{\ell}(\mathrm{d}x),

(1)

where
\tilde{p}_{\ell}=\sum_{h\geq 1}S_{\ell h}\delta_{\phi_{\ell h}}
is the almost sure discrete mixing probability measure in the
\ell
-th group. Conditioning to
(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{g})
, data in different groups are assumed to be independent.
To discuss our construction for the prior of
(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{g})
we need some preliminaries.

2.1
Background on normalized random measures

A fruitful approach to constructing random probability measures is by normalization of completely random measures, i.e., setting
\tilde{p}(\cdot)=\tilde{\mu}(\cdot)/\tilde{\mu}(\mathbb{X})
, which was originally introduced in
Regazzini et al., 2003
.
A random measure
\tilde{\mu}
is a random element taking values in the space of locally finite measures over
\mathbb{X}
. The measure
\tilde{\mu}
is
completely random
if for any
n>1
and disjoint sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
, the random variables

\tilde{\mu}(B_{1}),\ldots,\tilde{\mu}(B_{n})
are mutually independent
(
Kingman, 1993
)
.
As customary in BNP, we restrict our attention to random measures of the kind
\tilde{\mu}(B)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times B}s\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}x)
where
\tilde{N}
is a Poisson process
(
Kingman, 1993
)
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{X}
with mean measure
\nu(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}x)=CG_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s
where
C>0
,
G_{0}
is a non-atomic probability measure on
\mathbb{X}
and
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a measurable function, henceforth the Lévy density. We write
\tilde{N}\sim\mathrm{PP}(\nu)
and
\tilde{\mu}\sim\mathrm{CRM}(\nu)
. In the context of
point process theory,
\nu
is the mean measure.
To ensure that the normalization of
\tilde{\mu}
, i.e.
\tilde{p}
, is well defined, one must require
0<\tilde{\mu}(\mathbb{X})<+\infty
almost surely.
As shown in
Regazzini et al., 2003
, sufficient conditions are

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s=+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\min\{1,s\}\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s<+\infty.

(2)

If (
2
) is enforced,
\tilde{\mu}
has an infinite number of support points almost surely.

2.2
The normalized hierarchical shot-noise Cox process

We now define the vector
(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{g})
by shifting our focus to the construction of a vector of random measures
(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{g})
, and then we set
\tilde{p}_{\ell}=\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}/\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}(\mathbb{X})
.
By the discussion above, this is equivalent to specifying the law of
(\tilde{N}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{N}_{g})
, where each
\tilde{N}_{\ell}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\delta_{(S_{\ell h},\phi_{\ell h})}
is a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{X}
. In the following, we will refer to the
S_{\ell h}
’s as the
jumps
and
\phi_{\ell h}
’s as the
locations
of
\tilde{N}_{\ell}
(and of
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
).

As in the HDP, we introduce borrowing of information across the
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
’s through a hierarchical formulation. Specifically, we assume that conditionally to a random measure
\eta_{\Lambda}
on
\mathbb{X}
, the
\tilde{N}_{\ell}
’s are i.i.d. Poisson processes with mean measure
\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s\eta_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}x)
where
\rho
is a Lévy density. However, contrary to the HDP, we do not assume that
\eta_{\Lambda}
is atomic, which would cause the same exact atom sharing property, but rather that it is almost surely diffuse.
Let
k(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a probability density kernel and
\Lambda
be a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Theta
shared across groups, with mean measure
\nu_{0}(\mathrm{d}\gamma,\mathrm{d}\tau)=\rho_{0}(\gamma)\mathrm{d}\gamma G_{0}(\mathrm{d}\tau)
where
\rho_{0}
is a Lévy density and
G_{0}
is diffuse. Then we set
\eta_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Theta}\gamma k(x,\tau)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}\gamma,\mathrm{d}\tau)\ \mathrm{d}x
.
Note that
\eta_{\Lambda}
is not a probability measure, but can be normalized since
\int_{\mathbb{X}}\eta_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty
by (
2
).

Spelling out
\Lambda=\sum_{j\geq 1}\delta_{(\gamma_{j},\tau_{j})}
, we observe that
\eta_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}x)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}k(\mathrm{d}x,\tau_{j})
. Hence, one can easily realize that, given
\Lambda
, the locations
\phi_{\ell,h}
’s of each
\tilde{N}_{\ell}

are i.i.d. with density proportional to
\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}k(\mathrm{d}x,\tau_{j})
so that they are generated from a mixture density.
It is worth noticing that this construction induces a random clustering of the locations of the
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
’s, while ensuring that the
\phi_{\ell h}
’s are all different almost surely, thus overcoming the drawbacks inherited by the exact-atom sharing of the HDP. Moreover, marginally, the locations of each
\tilde{N}_{\ell}
form a shot-noise Cox process as introduced in
Møller, 2003
, and the joint distribution of all locations can be seen as an instance of the multivariate shot-noise Cox process in
Møller and Waagepetersen, 2003
.

In the sequel, we will write
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot))
and
(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{g})\sim\mathrm{HSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot)
). For notational purposes, we also introduce a generalization of this prior, to the case where

\tilde{N}_{\ell}\,|\,\Lambda\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathrm{PP}(\rho_{\ell}(s)\mathrm{d}s\eta_{\Lambda}(\mathrm{d}x))
, being
\rho_{\ell}
a Lévy density on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,g
. In this case we write
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}((\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{g}),\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot))

The
\mathrm{normHSNCP}
prior shares similarities with several models previously considered.
First, letting
\mathbb{X}=\Theta
and
k(x,\tau)=\delta_{\tau}(x)
we recover the hierarchical random measures recently considered by
Catalano and Del Sole, 2025
, which encompass the HDP with a random total mass parameter.
This highlights the importance of the kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
in our model.
If instead we let
\rho_{0}(s)=\delta_{\gamma}(s)
(i.e., the jumps of the mother process are all equal), we obtain the shot-noise Cox process mixture recently considered in
Beraha et al., 2025
, who however consider a single group of data. Note that
\mathbb{X}
and
\Theta
will always be assumed different in the Gaussian HSNCP mixture model of Section
4.1
.

2.3
Clustering in the HSNCP mixture model

For the prior above, by exploiting the superposition property of Poisson processes it is possible to write
\tilde{N}_{\ell}\,|\,\Lambda=\sum_{j\geq 1}\tilde{N}_{\ell j}
, where, if
\Lambda=\sum_{j\geq 1}\delta_{(\gamma_{j},\tau_{j})}
, we have
\tilde{N}_{\ell j}\,|\,\Lambda\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathrm{PP}(\gamma_{j}\rho(s)\mathrm{d}sk(x,\tau_{j})\mathrm{d}x)
. That is to say,
\tilde{N}_{\ell}
is a
cluster point process
where
\tilde{N}_{\ell j}
’s locations are concentrated around the points
\tau_{j}
’s.
Equivalently,
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell j}
with
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell j}=\sum_{h\geq 1}S_{\ell jh}\delta_{\phi_{\ell jh}}
being the CRM associated with the Poisson process
\tilde{N}_{\ell j}
.
Then, the mixture model (
1
) with an HSNCP prior is equivalent to

Y_{\ell i}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{\ell}\overset{iid}{\sim}\tilde{f}_{\ell}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{S_{\ell\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\sum_{j\geq 1}S_{\ell j\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}\tilde{f}_{\ell j}(\cdot),\ \ \text{for }\tilde{f}_{\ell j}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{S_{\ell j\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\sum_{h\geq 1}S_{\ell jh}f(\cdot\,|\,\phi_{\ell jh}),

(3)

where
S_{\ell j\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}=\sum_{h\geq 1}S_{\ell jh}
and
S_{\ell\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}:=\sum_{j\geq 1}S_{\ell j\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}=\sum_{j,h\geq 1}S_{\ell jh}
.
That is, (
1
) is a mixture model where the mixture components
\tilde{f}_{\ell j}
are represented as nonparametric mixtures themselves.
If
g=1
, this is akin to the mixture of mixture model in
Malsiner-Walli et al., 2017
, who consider a fixed finite number of atoms in each mixture component. A similar model was also considered in
Aragam et al., 2020
in a frequentist setting, who establish consistency and identifiability properties.
Moreover, this two-level modeling bears similarities with the post-processing approach proposed by
Do et al., 2024
, and the fusing of localized densities method developed by
Dombowsky and Dunson, 2025
.

The HSNCP mixture model induces an interesting two-level clustering structure.
First, consider (
1
) with
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot))
and introduce variables
X_{\ell i}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{\ell}

\overset{iid}{\sim}\tilde{p}_{\ell}
, so that
Y_{\ell i}\,|\,X_{\ell i}\overset{ind}{\sim}f(\cdot\,|\,X_{\ell i})
.
Since
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
is almost surely discrete, with positive probability
X_{\ell i}=X_{\ell i^{\prime}}
for
i\neq i^{\prime}
. This induces the standard clustering in mixture models by the equivalence relation
Y_{\ell i}\sim Y_{\ell i^{\prime}}
if and only if
X_{\ell i}=X_{\ell i^{\prime}}
. Introducing latent cluster variables
c_{\ell i}
, this is indicated by
c_{\ell i}=c_{\ell i^{\prime}}
.
Next, let
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}=\sum_{h\geq 1}S_{\ell h}\delta_{\phi_{\ell h}}
and consider the double-summation formulation discussed in (
3
), and define latent indicator variables
t_{\ell h}
such that
t_{\ell h}=j
if and only if
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell j}(\phi_{\ell h})>0
, i.e., the
h
-th atom of
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
belongs to
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell j}
.
Then, the clustering in the HSNCP mixture model is defined by
(t_{\ell c_{\ell i}}\,|\,\ell=1,\ \ldots,g,\ i=1,\ldots,n_{\ell})
. That is, we say that two points
Y_{\ell i}
and
Y_{\ell^{\prime}i^{\prime}}
belong to the same cluster if
t_{\ell c_{\ell i}}=t_{\ell^{\prime}c_{\ell^{\prime}i^{\prime}}}
.
In particular, since
t_{\ell h}=t_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}
with positive probability even when
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, the HSNCP allows for clustering across groups of data.
See
Figure

2
for a stylized draw from the HSNCP mixture and the associated two-level clustering.

Figure 2
:
A (truncated) draw from a HSNCP mixture model for
g=2
. Each stick denotes an atom of
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
, with jumps given by the length of the sticks and locations denoted with
\phi_{\ell h}
’s. The diamonds represent the points of the mother process. The color of the sticks, the diamonds and the observations are coherent with the across-group clusters. The arrows represent the paths that obtain the association between two observations and the same point of the mother process.

In addition to allowing for clustering datapoints across groups, the HSNCP model also introduces a very flexible notion of cluster, whereby a cluster is not equivalent to a mixture component.
This is beneficial in applied settings, where the kernel
f(\cdot\,|\,x)
in (
1
) does not necessarily agree with the true data generating density. In such cases, standard nonparametric mixture models provide asymptotically consistent estimators for the density but not for the clustering. That is, we face a density-clustering trade-off when dealing with misspecified models, with standard mixture models favoring density estimation over clustering. See
Beraha et al., 2025
for a detailed discussion and
Guha et al., 2021
for asymptotic consistency results.
The normHSNCP model sidesteps this trade-off by relying on a different and more flexible notion of clustering. As we show in our simulations, even if we let
f(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
and
k(\cdot,\cdot)
be the Gaussian probability density function (defined on appropriate spaces), our model is able to correctly identify clusters also when the true data generating process is a mixture of skewed or heavy-tailed kernels, contrary to what happens with traditional mixture models.

3
Theoretical properties of the HSNCP prior

The direct study of model (
1
) under the joint normHSNCP prior is challenging. Therefore, as usually done in BNP and mentioned at the beginning of Section
2.3
, we introduce the latent variables
X_{\ell i}
for
\ell=1,\ldots,g
and
i=1,\ldots,n_{\ell}
, with values in
\mathbb{X}
, such that
Y_{\ell i}\,|\,X_{\ell i}\overset{ind}{\sim}f(\cdot\,|\,X_{\ell i})
, and study the model at the level of the latent variables. That is we consider the latent variable model

\displaystyle X_{\ell i}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\overset{ind}{\sim}\tilde{p}_{\ell},\ i=1,\ldots,n_{\ell},\textrm{ for any }\ell=1,\ldots,g

(4)

\displaystyle(\tilde{p}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{p}_{g})

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot)).

The study of (
4
) is useful for prior elicitation in the mixture model, by studying, e.g., prior moments and correlation structures.
Moreover, the marginal distribution of the
X_{\ell i}
’s and the posterior of
(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{g})
, given the
X_{\ell i}
’s, are the fundamental building blocks to design numerical algorithms for posterior inference in the full model.

We introduce some notation for ease of presentation. Let
\psi(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(1-\operatorname{e}^{-us})\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s
be the Laplace exponent of (the jumps of) the
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
’s and define
\kappa(u,n)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\operatorname{e}^{-us}s^{n}\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s
for
u\in\mathbb{R}_{+},n\in\mathbb{N}
. Define analogously
\psi_{0}
and
\kappa_{0}
starting from
\rho_{0}
.
For a sample
\bm{X}=(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{g})
,
\bm{X}_{\ell}=(X_{\ell 1},\ldots,X_{\ell n_{\ell}})
, of size
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{g}
from (
4
), ties occur within each
\bm{X}_{\ell}
with positive probability due to the almost-sure discreteness of the
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
’s. Let
X^{*}_{\ell 1},\ldots,X^{*}_{\ell K_{\ell}}
be the
K_{\ell}
unique values displayed in group
\ell
,
with value
X^{*}_{\ell h}
appearing exactly
\xi_{\ell h}
times.
Finally, for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}\in\mathbb{X}
let

m(\mathrm{d}x_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}x_{p})=\int_{\Theta}\prod_{i=1}^{p}k(x_{i},\tau)\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,G_{0}(\mathrm{d}\tau).

(5)

That is,
m(\cdot)
is the measure associated with the marginal distribution of a sample from the Bayesian hierarchical model
x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}\,|\,\tau\overset{iid}{\sim}k(\cdot,\tau)
with prior
\tau\sim G_{0}
.

3.1
Prior moments

Consider the marginal law of
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
. An application of the tower rule of conditional expectations and Proposition 1 in
James et al., 2006
yield for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
:

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)]=m(A)=\int_{A}\int_{\Theta}k(x,\tau)G_{0}(\mathrm{d}\tau)\mathrm{d}x,

(6)

for any choice of
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
.
An expression for the second moment of
\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)
is provided in
Section

\thechapter.A.2
of the Supplementary material.

Beyond the first (and second) moment of
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
, it is interesting to study the correlation between
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
and
\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}
for
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
. Indeed, such a correlation sheds light on the amount of borrowing of strength induced a priori, which is often of significant interest in complex models such as ours. See, e.g.,
Camerlenghi et al., 2019a
;
Denti et al., 2023
;
Beraha and Griffin, 2023
.
Other indexes to measure the dependence between
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
and
\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}
have recently been proposed in
Catalano et al., 2021
;
Catalano et al., 2024
, but their construction does not directly apply to our model.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot))
. Then, for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
and
\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1,\dots,g
with
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
,

\mathrm{Cor}(\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A),\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}(A))=\left\{\frac{\mathcal{I}_{1,2}}{\mathcal{I}_{1}}+\frac{m(A)-m(A)^{2}}{m(A,A)-m(A)^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{1,1}}{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\right\}^{-1},

(7)

where

is defined in (

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u\operatorname{e}^{-\psi_{0}(u)}\kappa_{0}(u,2)du
,
\mathcal{I}_{1,2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u\kappa(u,1)^{2}\kappa_{0}(\psi(u),2)\operatorname{e}^{-\psi_{0}(\psi(u))}du

\mathcal{I}_{1,1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u\kappa(u,2)\kappa_{0}(\psi(u),1)\operatorname{e}^{-\psi_{0}(\psi(u))}du

Clearly, the correlation increases as

increases and decreases as

or
\mathcal{I}_{1,1}

Considering two observations from distinct groups

(resp.
\mathcal{I}_{1,1}

X_{\ell i}\neq X_{\ell i^{\prime}}
(resp.
X_{\ell i}=X_{\ell i^{\prime}}
).

We specialize our treatment to two cases of interest, namely when

and

3.2
Marginal and posterior distributions

We now move to the Bayesian analysis of model (
4

1

Favaro and Teh, 2013

Recall the notation introduced at the beginning of this section.
With a slight abuse of notation, we introduce another set of latent variables

Theorem 3.2
.

Let

Next, we turn to the study of the posterior distribution. By a disintegration argument, the posterior law of the

3.3
The restaurant process and predictive distributions

Similarly to the HDP and hierarchical NRMI processes, the marginal distribution of
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{g}
can be expressed via a sequential allocation process known as the Chinese restaurant franchise
(
Teh et al., 2006
)

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{g}
be a sample of size

\displaystyle=\frac{U_{\ell}}{\Gamma(n_{\ell})}\sum_{h=1}^{K_{\ell}}\frac{\kappa(U_{\ell},\xi_{\ell h}+1)}{\kappa(U_{\ell},\xi_{\ell h})}\delta_{X^{*}_{\ell h}}(A)

\displaystyle\ +\frac{U_{\ell}}{\Gamma(n_{\ell})}\sum_{j=1}^{|\bm{T}|}\kappa(U_{\ell},1)\frac{\kappa_{0}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{g}\psi(U_{\ell}),\zeta_{j}+1\right)}{\kappa_{0}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{g}\psi(U_{\ell}),\zeta_{j}\right)}\int_{A}m(\mathrm{d}x\,|\,X^{*}_{\ell h}\colon T_{{\ell h}}=j)

The result in

suggests the following metaphor. Consider a restaurant franchise where tables are associated with dishes corresponding to the unique values

Tables are arranged into several

\ell

\tilde{p}_{\ell}

\ell

X_{1,1}

(iii)

4
Posterior inference on HSNCP mixtures

In this section, we describe posterior inference for the mixture model (
1
) with
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot))
.

4.1
Prior elicitation for Gaussian mixtures

For simplicity, we focus here on univariate data and Gaussian mixtures, and describe an empirical Bayesian approach to set the hyperparameters in a data-dependent fashion. Specifically, we assume that our data follow a nonparametric location-mixture with mixing measure
\tilde{p}_{\ell}
, where

\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}
,
f(\cdot\,|\,\phi)=\mathcal{N}(\cdot\,|\,\phi,\sigma_{f}^{2})
is the Gaussian density with mean
\phi
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, the kernel encoding the proximity of components across and within groups as

k(\cdot,\tau_{j})=\mathcal{N}(\cdot\,|\,\mu_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j})
for
\tau_{j}=(\mu_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j})\in\Theta=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
G_{0}(\mu_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j}\,|\,\mu_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})\times\mathrm{IG}(\sigma^{2}_{j}\,|\,\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
,
where
\mathrm{IG}(\cdot\,|\,\alpha,\beta)
is the inverse gamma density with mean
\beta/(\alpha-1)
.
We specialize the double-summation formulation of our model in (
3
) as follows

\displaystyle Y_{\ell i}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{\ell},\sigma_{f}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{iid}{\sim}\tilde{f}_{\ell}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{S_{\ell\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\sum_{j\geq 1}S_{\ell j\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}\tilde{f}_{\ell j}(\cdot),\ \ \text{for }\tilde{f}_{\ell j}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{S_{\ell j\mathbin{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}\sum_{h\geq 1}S_{\ell jh}\mathcal{N}(\cdot\,|\,\phi_{\ell jh},\sigma_{f}^{2})

(10)

\displaystyle(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{normHSNCP}(\rho,\rho_{0},G_{0},k(\cdot,\cdot))

\displaystyle\sigma_{f}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{IG}(\alpha_{f},\beta_{f}),

with
(\tilde{p}_{1},\dots,\tilde{p}_{g})
and
\sigma_{f}^{2}
a priori independent.
We first operate a centering of
G_{0}
with respect to the observed data, namely by setting
\mu_{0}
as the sample mean of all observations, and set
\sigma^{2}_{0}
so that
\max_{\ell,i}|Y_{\ell i}-\mu_{0}|=2\sigma_{0}
, which ensures that the cluster centers cover the span of the data.
Next, we focus on
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
. We take inspiration from the Bayesian distance clustering literature
(
Duan and Dunson, 2021
;
Natarajan et al., 2024
)
, and we consider the pairwise distances between datapoints. If there are well-separated clusters in the data, the kernel density estimate of the pairwise distances will be multimodal. The first (i.e., closest to zero) local minimum
d^{*}
of the density estimate gives a rough estimate of the maximal distance between two datapoints belonging to the same cluster.
This suggests that two atoms
\phi_{\ell jh},\phi_{\ell jh^{\prime}}
in (
10
) should be closer than
d^{*}
with high probability, as they are referring to the same clusters. Standard computations show that
|\phi_{\ell jh}-\phi_{\ell jh^{\prime}}|/\sqrt{(2\beta_{0}/\alpha_{0})}
is marginally distributed as a half-
t
distribution with
2\alpha_{0}
degrees of freedom. We impose two conditions. First,
\mathbb{P}(|\phi_{\ell jh}-\phi_{\ell jh^{\prime}}|\leq d^{*})=0.99
. Second, the expected distance between atoms
\phi_{\ell jh}
and
\phi_{\ell jh^{\prime}}
is set equal to a fixed fraction of the mean distance between observations in the same cluster (i.e., those within
d^{*}
). If we denote this mean value by
m
, the condition can be written as
\mathbb{E}[|\phi_{\ell jh}-\phi_{\ell jh^{\prime}}|]=\lambda m
, where
\lambda\in(0,1)
. In the remainder of this article, we fix
\lambda=0.7
.

To elicit the prior distribution of
\sigma_{f}^{2}
, we follow
Shi et al., 2019
and set
\alpha_{f}=3/2
and let
\beta_{f}
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{\ell i}\,|\,\sigma_{f}^{2},\sigma^{2}_{T_{\ell c_{\ell i}}}\right)\right]=s^{2}
, where
s^{2}
is the empirical variance of the data.

Finally, having fixed the parameters in
G_{0}(\mu_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j})
and
k(\cdot;\tau_{j})
, it is possible to exploit Theorem
3.1
to fix the hyperparameters in
\rho
and
\rho_{0}
(as in the examples in Section
3.1
) by considering the prior dependence between the random mixing measures.

4.2
The MCMC algorithm

We propose a Gibbs sampler to approximate the posterior in the HSNCP mixture model. The algorithm state consists of: the labels
\bm{c}=(c_{\ell i},\ell=1,\dots,g,\ i=1,\dots,n_{\ell})
and
\bm{T}
; the auxiliary variables
\bm{U}
; the mother process’ locations
(\tau_{j})_{j=1}^{|\bm{T}|}
; and the child processes
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
,
l=1,\ldots,g
. The key result to develop our algorithm is Theorem
3.3
, which describes the full conditional of

(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{g})
.

According to
(ii)
and
(iii)
of Theorem
3.3
, the full conditional distribution of the child processes contains the sum of
|\bm{T}|+1
CRMs, which in turn have an infinite number of jumps and locations. We approximate these infinite support random measures
with finite numbers of jumps and locations. Then we
adopt the Ferguson–Klass (FK) algorithm
(
Ferguson and Klass, 1972
)
to sample only the jumps whose magnitudes exceed a user-specified threshold
\varepsilon
and, then, of course, we also sample their corresponding locations. Implementation details are given in Section
\thechapter.B
of the Supplementary material. When
\varepsilon
is chosen sufficiently small, the truncation error is virtually negligible. See, e.g.,
Arbel and Prünster, 2017
,
Campbell et al., 2019
, and
Nguyen et al., 2024
. For a different truncation procedure see
Argiento et al., 2016
.

Therefore, we classify each child process’ jumps in different categories: the allocated jumps
\bm{S}_{\ell}^{(a)}
, i.e., the unique values in
(S_{\ell c_{\ell i}})_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}
;

7
Discussion

We have introduced the Hierarchical Shot-Noise Cox Process (HSNCP) mixture model, a novel Bayesian nonparametric approach for partially exchangeable data. Unlike traditional hierarchical models such as the HDP of
Teh et al., 2006
, which allow borrowing of information only through common atoms, the HSNCP allows group-specific components to concentrate around shared centers through a kernel-based construction. This results in more flexible across-group information sharing, especially in the presence of subtle structural heterogeneity.

The HSNCP retains desirable theoretical and computational properties: it admits closed-form expressions for key prior quantities, supports tractable posterior inference via a conditional MCMC algorithm, and induces a two-level clustering structure that improves both interpretability and robustness. Empirical results from simulations and real data demonstrate improved performance in density estimation and clustering, particularly in settings where traditional models are either too rigid or fail to identify shared structure.
Moreover, our model and the associated MCMC algorithm scale well as the total size is large, as the application shows.

The posterior characterization provided in this work lays the foundation for marginal algorithms that avoid sampling latent variables — a direction we leave for future research. Additionally, we are working on a nested extension of the HSNCP that enables clustering entire distributions based on their similarity, relaxing the requirement of exact equality imposed by standard nested processes.
Finally, extension to more complex dependence structures are of interest. For instance, for temporally evolving data, one option is to adopt the spatiotemporal shot-noise Cox process recently developed by
Bassetti et al., 2023
as prior distribution for the atoms in the mixing measures.

Exploiting Equation (
6
) and
Lemma

\thechapter.A.1
, we write the numerator as

\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A),\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}(A))=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)\,|\,\Lambda]\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}(A)\,|\,\Lambda]]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)]\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}(A)]=[m(A,A)-m(A)^{2}]\mathcal{I}_{1}.

Exploiting Equation (
6
) and
Lemma

\thechapter.A.2
, we write the denominator as

\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A))=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)]^{2}=[m(A)-m(A)^{2}]\mathcal{I}_{1,1}+[m(A,A)-m(A)^{2}]\mathcal{I}_{1,2}

and the result of the theorem follows.

Interpretation of the three integrals in Equation (
7
)

In this section we clarify the notation and interpretation of the three integrals appearing in Equation (
7
).

As shown in Equation (36) of
Pitman, 2003
, the integral
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is the marginal probability that two cluster allocation labels
t_{\ell h}
and
t_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}
are equal. The subscript 1 encodes that the corresponding partition of
\{X^{*}_{\ell h},X^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}\}
consists of a single block
\{X^{*}_{\ell h},X^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}\}
. In terms of the restaurant franchise metaphor in
Section

3.3
,
\mathcal{I}_{1}
corresponds to the probability that two tables are in rooms associated with the same theme.

Building on
Theorem

3.2
, we can prove that
\mathcal{I}_{1,2}
corresponds to the marginal probability that
X_{\ell i}\neq X_{\ell i^{\prime}}
and
T_{\ell c_{\ell i}}=T_{\ell c_{\ell i^{\prime}}}
. The first term in the subscript has the same interpretation as in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, indeed the partition of
\{X^{*}_{\ell c_{\ell i}},X^{*}_{\ell c_{\ell i^{\prime}}}\}
consists of a single block. The second term encodes that the partition of
\{X_{\ell i},X_{\ell i^{\prime}}\}
consists of two singleton blocks
\{X_{\ell i}\}
and
\{X_{\ell i^{\prime}}\}
. In terms of the restaurant franchise metaphor,
\mathcal{I}_{1,2}
corresponds to the probability that two customers in the same restaurant choose the same thematic room but sit at different tables.

By a similar argument,
\mathcal{I}_{1,1}
corresponds to the marginal probability that
X_{\ell i}=X_{\ell i^{\prime}}
. The notation follows the same convention as described for
\mathcal{I}_{1,2}
. In terms of the restaurant franchise metaphor,
\mathcal{I}_{1,1}
corresponds to the probability that two customers in the same restaurant sit at the same tables.

Here we report further details on the MCMC algorithm described in Section

.

Ferguson-Klass algorithm

Given a CRM
\tilde{\mu}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\gamma_{j}\delta_{\tau_{j}}
, the FK algorithm samples an approximate version of
\tilde{\mu}
by retaining only the jumps greater than a fixed threshold
\varepsilon
, along with their corresponding locations. The algorithm introduces a sequence of latent variables
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3},\ldots
such that the increments
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}-\eta_{1},\eta_{3}-\eta_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. exponential random variables with unit mean. The
j
-th jump
\gamma_{j}
is obtained by solving the equation
\nu([\gamma_{j},+\infty),\mathbb{X})=\eta_{j}
, where
\nu
is the mean measure associated to the CRM. This procedure ensures that the sampled jumps are in decreasing order, i.e.,
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\ldots
. The algorithm stops when
\gamma_{j}
falls below a positive threshold
\varepsilon
, thus keeping only the jumps bigger than
\varepsilon
. The corresponding locations are drawn independently from the base measure of the (homogeneous) CRM.

Supporting algorithm

Throughout the MCMC execution, we also run a supporting algorithm that maintains a valid and coherent state at all iterations. In particular, this step warrants no gaps in the labels
\bm{T}
by relabeling elements to obtain that
\zeta_{j}\geq 0\iff j\leq|\bm{T}|
. It also guarantees consistency between the labels
\bm{c}
and
\bm{T}
and the sets
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(a)}
,
\bm{\phi}_{\ell j}^{(na)}
,
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
and
\bm{S}_{\ell}^{(a)}
,
\bm{S}_{\ell j}^{(na)}
,
\bm{S}_{\ell}^{(na)}
, for all
j
,
\ell=1,\ldots,g
. For instance, in step 1., if an
h
is sampled such that
\phi_{\ell h}\in\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
, then the supporting algorithm moves
\phi_{\ell h}
to
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(a)}
and samples
T_{\ell h}
according to step 3. of the MCMC algorithm.

Details for step 3.

In step 3., we sample each element of
\bm{T}
using Algorithm 3 from
Neal, 2000
. In particular, the full conditional probabilities can be derived from (
9
) and are as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{\ell h}=j\,|\,-)

\displaystyle\qquad\propto\begin{cases}\frac{\kappa_{0}(\sum_{\ell=1}^{g}\psi(U_{\ell}),\zeta_{j}^{-\ell h}+1)}{\kappa_{0}(\sum_{\ell=1}^{g}\psi(U_{\ell}),\zeta_{j}^{-\ell h})}m(X^{*}_{\ell h}\,|\,X^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}:T_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}=j\wedge(\ell^{\prime},h^{\prime})\neq(\ell,h))&j\leq|\bm{T}|\\
\kappa_{0}(\sum_{\ell=1}^{g}\psi(U_{\ell}),1)m(X^{*}_{\ell h})&j=|\bm{T}|+1\end{cases}

where
\zeta_{j}^{-\ell h}
is the cardinality of the set
\{X^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}:T_{\ell^{\prime}h^{\prime}}=j\wedge(\ell^{\prime},h^{\prime})\neq(\ell,h)\}
. Whenever
j=|\bm{T}|+1
is chosen, we generate
\tau_{|\bm{T}|+1}
from its marginal prior. After this Gibbs step, the supporting algorithm discards all
\tau_{j}
’s such that
\zeta_{j}=0
.

Details and alternatives for step 5.

To sample each element of
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
and
\bm{S}_{\ell}^{(na)}
we exploit the FK algorithm twice. First, we use it to sample an approximate version of the mother process described in Theorem
3.3
(
iii
). Let
\bm{\gamma}^{(na)}
and
\bm{\tau}^{(na)}
be the finite dimensional vector of jumps exceeding the threshold
\varepsilon
and their corresponding locations. We then apply the FK algorithm the second time to sample
\bm{S}_{\ell}^{(na)}
and
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
. Specifically, the elements of
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
are drawn from a finite mixture model with mixing function
k
, weights obtained from the normalization of
\bm{\gamma}^{(na)}
, and locations
\bm{\tau}^{(na)}
.

As shown in Theorem
3.3
, the elements of
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
are generated according to a SNCP.
Therefore, an alternative approach to step 5, though we did not use it in the current MCMC, is to apply the FK algorithm only once, to sample
\bm{S}_{\ell}^{(na)}
and
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
. In this case, the locations
\bm{\phi}_{\ell}^{(na)}
can be sampled using simulation algorithms for SNCPs. See
Møller and Waagepetersen, 2003
for further details.

In this section, we present additional simulation studies to further characterize the HSNCP mixture model. We consider the model in (
10
) with prior hyperparameters as in Section
4.1
, compared with the HPD Gaussian mixtures, that is (
1
) with
\tilde{p}_{\ell}\,|\,\tilde{p}_{0}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathrm{DP}(b,\tilde{p}_{0})
and
\tilde{p}_{0}\sim\mathrm{DP}(b_{0},F_{0})
. The base measure
F_{0}
is a normal-inverse gamma distribution:
F_{0}(\mu_{j},\sigma^{2}_{j})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{j}\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{HDP}},\sigma^{2}_{j}/\lambda_{\mathrm{HDP}})\times\mathrm{IG}(\sigma^{2}_{j}\,|\,\alpha_{\mathrm{HDP}},\beta_{\mathrm{HDP}})
. To ensure a fair comparison with the HSNCP mixture model, we choose the HDP prior hyperparameters to match the two models. First, we set the total mass parameters
b
and
b_{0}
so that the correlation between
\tilde{p}_{\ell}(A)
and
\tilde{p}_{\ell^{\prime}}(A)
under the HDP matches that of the HSNCP when
A
is the interval containing all the observations. The expression of the correlation in the HDP case is given in Example 1 of
Camerlenghi et al., 2019a
. We match the prior distribution of the standard deviation for each mixture component in the HDP with the prior distribution of
\sigma_{f}+\sigma_{j}
in the HSNCP. In this way, the prior distribution for the standard deviation of each cluster is the same for both models. Then, to align the prior variance of the atoms’ mean, we set
\lambda_{\mathrm{HDP}}
so that the expected value of the prior variance of
\mu_{j}
is equal to
\sigma^{2}_{0}
, i.e.,
\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{HDP}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{HDP}}-1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{HDP}}}=\sigma^{2}_{0}
. Finally, we match the prior means by setting
\mu_{0}=\mu_{\mathrm{HDP}}
.

In every simulation we run each MCMC algorithm for 5,000 iterations, after a burn-in period of 1,000 iterations. For the HSNCP mixture, we use the algorithm described in Section
4
, while for the HDP model we run the sampler implemented in
https://github.com/alessandrocolombi/hdp
.
We apply the
salso
package
(
Dahl et al., 2022
, see)
to estimate clusters, minimizing the posterior expectation of the Variation of Information loss function.
