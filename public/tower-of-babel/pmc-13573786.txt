Vitamin D/VDR Signaling and Programmed Cell Death in Sepsis-Associated Organ Injury: Evidence from Sepsis and Related Disease Models
Authors: Guang-Tao Pan, Xiao Liu, Yu Deng, Yu-Han Wang, Lin-Rui Ma, Yu-Tong Li, Yi-Zhun Zhu, Pei-Yu Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection and is frequently complicated by multi-organ dysfunction. Current management relies primarily on timely antimicrobial therapy, source control, hemodynamic resuscitation, and organ support, whereas effective therapies that directly target programmed cell death (PCD) involved in sepsis-associated organ injury remain lacking. Recent evidence indicates that, beyond its classical role in mineral metabolism, vitamin D (VD) and the vitamin D receptor (VDR) are involved in the regulation of immune homeostasis, oxidative stress, and cellular fate. This review summarizes current advances in the protective effects of VD/VDR against organ injury through the modulation of PCD and discusses the mechanisms by which VD/VDR regulates PCD pathways in relevant non-septic disease contexts. We further synthesize the available evidence supporting the involvement of VD/VDR signaling in the regulation of major PCD pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, during sepsis-associated organ injury. Collectively, these findings suggest that VD/VDR signaling may serve as a systemic modulator of interconnected PCD networks and represent a potential adjunctive therapeutic target in sepsis. However, direct mechanistic evidence from clinically relevant sepsis models remains limited, and substantial preclinical and clinical investigation is still required before VD/VDR-targeted interventions can be established as a therapeutic strategy for sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis represents a systemic condition resulting from maladaptive immune activation in response to infection, typically progressing to acute organ impairment and carrying a substantial risk of fatality.1 Over the past decades, advances such as the widespread use of antibiotics and the development of multi-organ support strategies have contributed to a decline in sepsis-related deaths. Nevertheless, mortality remains unacceptably high.2 A 2017 global report estimated that sepsis accounted for approximately 11 million deaths, representing 18–21% of all annual fatalities.3 Beyond mortality, sepsis imposes a substantial healthcare and economic burden; for instance, patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) with sepsis face mortality rates as high as 35.5%.4 Analyses of sepsis-related deaths reveal diverse underlying causes, including infectious diseases, trauma, and non-communicable disorders.3 However, the pathophysiological mechanisms of sepsis are still incompletely understood. In particular, the cellular pathways leading to multiple organ dysfunction remain unclear.4 Conventional therapeutic approaches primarily focus on antimicrobial treatment and supportive care, while investigational strategies include immunomodulatory approaches. However, despite growing recognition of immune dysregulation, the precise contribution of distinct and interacting forms of programmed cell death to sepsis-associated organ failure remains insufficiently defined. Elucidating the specific forms of PCD that drive sepsis-associated organ dysfunction is therefore crucial. Such understanding may enable the recognition of potential therapeutic candidates and promote advancement in clinical studies.

The hormonally active metabolite of vitamin D (VD) is calcitriol [1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D3, 1,25(OH)2D3], which is generated through two sequential hydroxylation reactions of VD3. Calcitriol is fundamental in regulating calcium and phosphate balance.5,6 The major sources of VD are cutaneous synthesis induced by ultraviolet irradiation and dietary intake. Following hepatic hydroxylation by VD-25-hydroxylase to form 25-hydroxy VD, it undergoes further conversion into calcitriol in the kidneys and extra-renal sites. Acting through binding to the nuclear VD receptor (VDR), calcitriol regulates the transcriptional activation of calcium-binding protein genes, thereby sustaining mineral homeostasis and influencing immune function.7,8 Consequently, deficiency of VD or impaired VDR signaling may trigger immune dysregulation and metabolic imbalance. Under such conditions, inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6 can drive a cytokine storm, thereby aggravating sepsis-induced organ injury.9 Hu et al reported that VDR knockout mice exhibited increased levels of ferroptosis, leading to more severe acute kidney injury (AKI). Conversely, treatment with the VDR agonist paricalcitol alleviated cisplatin-induced acute renal injury.10 These findings uncover the regulatory role of VD/VDR signaling in ferroptosis of renal tubular cells. Similarly, Chen et al demonstrated in a prediabetic mouse model that VD improved renal morphology and function, potentially by suppressing ferroptosis through the Klotho/p53 pathway.11 Furthermore, VD is capable of protecting osteoblasts against ferroptosis in osteoporosis via activation of the Nrf2 pathway.12,13 In the context of obesity, VD may also promote apoptosis of adipocytes through calcium-dependent signaling pathways, thereby exerting anti-obesity effects.14 Beyond its classical endocrine functions, VD has emerged as a pleiotropic immunomodulator with the capacity to influence cellular fate decisions under inflammatory stress.

These findings indicate that VD is capable of modulating multiple forms of PCD across diverse disease models. These observations support the hypothesis that VD/VDR signaling may function as a nodal regulator of multiple PCD pathways implicated in sepsis-related tissue injury. A comprehensive synthesis of available studies examining the capacity of VD to modulate PCD and alleviate tissue injury could yield valuable implications for sepsis therapy. Such insights may not only contribute to improving patient quality of life but also hold promise for reducing sepsis-associated mortality.

Cell death serves both physiological and pathological functions.15 Apoptosis is a generally non-lytic, caspase-driven process characterized by cell shrinkage and orderly cellular dismantling; pyroptosis is an inflammatory, gasdermin-mediated lytic form of death initiated by canonical caspase-1 or non-canonical caspase-4/5/11 signaling; and ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by phospholipid peroxidation and failure of antioxidant defenses such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Although mechanistically distinct, these pathways can coexist and interact during sepsis-associated organ injury.16–18

Targeting a single programmed cell-death pathway in sepsis is challenging because pathway activation varies across pathogens, cell types, organs, and disease stages. Functional redundancy, compensatory signaling, and crosstalk among apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and other death programs may further limit the efficacy of single-target interventions. VD/VDR signaling is therefore of interest as a potential upstream modulator of several stress and cell-death pathways, but its relevance must be assessed separately at the mechanistic and clinical levels. Under normal conditions, apoptotic bodies are rapidly cleared by the phagocytic system, demonstrating its efficiency in maintaining tissue homeostasis. In pathological states, however, acute infections, chronic inflammation, or tissue injury can overwhelm the clearance capacity of the phagocytic system. As a result, the intracellular contents of dying cells may be released extracellularly, triggering intense immune responses and recruiting additional immune cells to participate in clearance and tissue repair.16 PCD can be initiated by genetically regulated self-destructive mechanisms, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, or can result from metabolic dysregulation, as in the case of ferroptosis.17 Among these, apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis have been most extensively studied. Accumulating studies highlight the pivotal contribution of ferroptosis to the pathogenesis and progression of sepsis.18–20 Taken together, this review will primarily focus on apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, with particular attention to how these PCD pathways are modulated by VD in the context of sepsis.

Mechanistic Insights from Non-Sepsis Tissue-Injury Models

Liver Injury

Although direct evidence from sepsis models remains limited, studies in metabolic, toxic, and ischemic liver injury provide mechanistic insights that may be highly relevant to sepsis-associated hepatic dysfunction.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a common chronic metabolic disorder.21 Evidence suggests that VD exerts hepatoprotective effects by suppressing inflammatory cell infiltration and vacuolation in liver tissue.22 Notably, VD supplementation suppresses ferroptosis through strengthening antioxidant capacity, limiting iron deposition, and regulating ferroptosis-related protein expression. Evidence from HepG2 steatotic cells and high-fat diet (HFD)-fed C57BL/6J mice confirmed its protective effect against ferroptosis and consequent hepatic injury.23

Ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury is another pathological condition often associated with hepatocyte apoptosis. Reports indicate that VDR reduces hepatocyte apoptosis during I/R by mediating autophagic processes.24 In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), VDR expression can be upregulated by inhibition of miR-125a-5p, which enhances apoptotic activity in hepatocytes.25 In earlier work, Zheng et al validated that activation of the VD/VDR axis in cholangiocytes could promote biliary repair and alleviate cholestatic liver injury.26 Subsequent studies from the same group revealed that VDR ameliorates cholestatic hepatocyte injury by attenuating autophagy-dependent apoptosis and blocking ROS-mediated activation of the ERK/p38 MAPK pathway.27 Similarly, studies in high-fat diet-induced rat models demonstrated that VD supplementation attenuated inflammation and suppressed hepatocyte apoptosis by lowering cytokine levels and modulating the expression of Bax and Bcl-2. The precise molecular mechanisms, however, have yet to be elucidated and merit further investigation at the cellular scale.28 Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a complex multifactorial disease, for which pioglitazone (PIO) is a commonly used therapeutic agent. Combined treatment with VD and antidiabetic drugs has been shown to better control diabetes-induced hepatitis and attenuate apoptosis by inhibiting Caspase-3 activity.29

Zhang et al investigated the effects of VD on pyroptosis and gut microbiota in NAFLD. Their findings demonstrated that VD alleviated liver injury induced by an HFD in vivo, as well as palmitic acid (PA)-lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced cellular injury in vitro. In both models, VD reduced lipid accumulation, suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and attenuated pyroptotic cell death, highlighting that VD may serve as a therapeutic target for modulating pyroptosis in NAFLD.30 Similarly, Abdelrahman et al combined an autophagy inhibitor with the active form of VD to explore the crosstalk between autophagy and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Their results indicated that VD suppressed inflammasome activation and pyroptosis through an autophagy-dependent mechanism.31 VDR has also been implicated in drug-induced liver injury. Evidence shows that treatment with a VDR agonist inhibited the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptosis pathway, thereby preventing dexamethasone-induced hepatic injury.32 Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of VD and its receptor in regulating pyroptosis within hepatocytes. By mitigating the severity of NAFLD and drug-induced liver injury, VD/VDR signaling emerges as a promising target for future research into pyroptosis-mediated liver pathology.

Beyond its role in regulating PCD, VD also exerts protective effects on the liver by modulating necrotic injury and it does not fall within the primary PCD category of this article. Studies have shown that VD functions as a potent antioxidant; its deficiency exacerbates hepatic oxidative stress (OS) and inflammation during acute liver injury in mice, ultimately leading to extensive hepatocellular necrosis.33 In models of acute liver failure, supplementation with calcitriol-the active metabolite of VD, was found to reduce hepatic lipid peroxidation, restore glutathione (GSH) reserves, and alleviate the extent of hepatocyte necrosis.34

In summary, VD possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties. Through the simultaneous regulation of inflammation, OS, and multiple modes of PCD, VD shows substantial potential to protect against liver injury across various disease models.

Kidney Injury

The onset and progression of kidney injury involve multiple forms of cell death, including ferroptosis, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necrosis.

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), recognized as a severe diabetic microvascular disorder, manifests predominantly through substantial tubular epithelial cell injury. Ferroptosis, as a newly identified form of regulated cell death, has been shown to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of DN. Studies demonstrate that VDR-knockout mice exhibit higher levels of ferroptosis and renal damage compared to wild-type mice.10 Conversely, activation of VDR reduces iron accumulation, elevates GSH activity, and decreases malondialdehyde (MDA) levels,35 thereby effectively suppressing tubular epithelial ferroptosis. Although the exact mechanisms remained unclear in earlier studies, Wang et al provided mechanistic insights by showing that VDR activation attenuates tubular ferroptosis in DN through regulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway.35 Similarly, Chen et al suggested that VD may inhibit hyperglycemia-induced ferroptosis via the Klotho/p53 axis.11 These findings imply that VD can suppress ferroptosis in diabetic kidney injury through multiple signaling cascades. In cisplatin-induced AKI models, activated VDR has also been shown to prevent renal injury by suppressing ferroptosis through trans-regulation of GPX4.10

In renal I/R injury, OS and apoptosis represent central pathogenic factors. Activation of the VD/VDR axis has been proven to provide protective effects, and treatment with the VDR agonist paricalcitol attenuates injury and apoptosis by repressing activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4).36 In addition, the VD/VDR axis has been reported to inhibit apoptosis through trans-regulation of renal glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3).37 Liang et al further identified VDR as a therapeutic target for mitigating I/R-induced renal injury. Their study demonstrated that DKS26, a compound enhancing VDR stability, promoted the interaction between VDR and phosphorylated NF-κB p65, thereby preventing its nuclear translocation. This mechanism suppressed downstream inflammatory gene transcription and consequently reduced OS, inflammation, and apoptosis.38 Beyond I/R injury, VD has also shown protective effects against drug-induced nephrotoxicity. For example, tamoxifen (TAMO), a chemotherapeutic agent for breast cancer, is known to cause renal toxicity during treatment. Experimental studies revealed that VD alleviated TAMO-induced renal damage by suppressing NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses and Caspase-3-dependent apoptosis.39

Emerging evidence highlights the role of pyroptosis in AKI progression. In wild-type mice, high-dose paricalcitol reversed cisplatin-induced renal dysfunction and cell death, as reflected by increased VDR expression and reduced levels of NLRP3, GSDMD-N, cleaved Caspase-1, and IL-1β. To further dissect the underlying mechanisms, researchers generated VDR knockout mice and tubular cell–specific VDR overexpression mice using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Compared with wild-type controls, VDR-deficient mice displayed more severe renal injury following cisplatin administration, whereas tubular cell–specific VDR overexpression markedly reduced renal damage and pyroptotic features, even under low-dose paricalcitol pretreatment.40,41 These findings suggest that the VD/VDR axis attenuates cisplatin-induced AKI at least in part by suppressing the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptosis pathway. Notably, in wild-type mice, low-dose paricalcitol showed minimal anti-pyroptotic effects, implying that simply increasing the dosage of VDR agonists does not necessarily confer renal protection. This observation may help explain why conventional VD supplementation fails to yield consistent clinical benefits in certain pathological settings.

Vancomycin, commonly prescribed for refractory infections, is clinically constrained by nephrotoxicity, which is frequently manifested as necrotic injury in renal tubular cells. Compared with untreated AKI models, VD supplementation markedly reduced tubular dilation, vacuolization, and necrotic lesions. Serum biochemical markers further confirmed that VD exerted antioxidant effects, thereby preventing vancomycin-induced nephrotoxicity.42 Interestingly, VD showed no significant protective effect against gentamicin-induced AKI in rats. No reduction in serum urea or creatinine levels was observed, suggesting that the discrepancy may be related to differences in the route of administration, treatment duration, or the distinct mechanisms underlying gentamicin-mediated nephrotoxicity.

Lung Injury

Recent studies indicate that VD mitigates lung injury primarily through the suppression of apoptosis and ferroptosis.

Acute lung injury (ALI) is defined by airway epithelial inflammation and OS, processes intricately governed by PCD. Ferroptosis, a lipid peroxidation-driven form of regulated cell death, has been identified in multiple pathological conditions.43,44 According to Ang et al, blocking lipid peroxidation mitigated ferroptosis in ALI. Their work further revealed that Sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1) negatively regulates airway epithelial ferroptosis via a VDR-driven autophagy mechanism, suggesting its promise as a therapeutic target in ALI.45 In a study on intestinal I/R-induced ALI, Li et al identified ferroptosis as a key contributor to lung injury and found that iASPP protected against this process through Nrf2/HIF-1/TF-mediated suppression of ferroptosis.43 Additional evidence indicates that VD prevents high-altitude pulmonary edema through inhibition of ferroptosis and ferritinophagy within alveolar epithelial cells.46 However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying VD-mediated regulation of ferroptosis and autophagy in this context remain to be elucidated.

In murine models of asthma, pathological deterioration of lung tissue is accompanied by increased fibrosis, elevated numbers of apoptotic cells, and enhanced autophagic activity. VD supplementation significantly ameliorated these pathological changes, reduced fibrosis, and markedly suppressed both apoptosis and autophagy. Mechanistically, investigators suggested that VD inhibited autophagy through the modulation of the HIF-1α/Notch1 signaling pathway.47 However, conflicting evidence exists regarding the role of autophagy in lung pathology. Some studies have indicated that autophagy can facilitate viral replication and proliferation within host cells, while simultaneously enhancing antigen presentation and promoting interactions between antigen-presenting cells and T cells, thereby contributing positively to adaptive immune responses. During viral infections, autophagy may also promote dendritic cell activation and maturation, functioning as a critical defense mechanism against viral invasion.48 These contradictory interpretations of autophagy likely reflect differences in viral specificity and the complexity of the host immune microenvironment.

Silicosis is among the most severe occupational diseases worldwide, characterized by immune cell infiltration and diffuse pulmonary fibrosis. Polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype represents a key protective mechanism to dampen inflammatory responses and preserve lung tissue integrity. Studies have shown that VD upregulates Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) expression in alveolar macrophages, which in turn enhances macrophage autophagy and protects them from silica particle-induced OS and apoptosis.49 Similarly, Yang et al reported that VD reversed crystalline silica-induced inflammatory cell infiltration and restored M2 macrophage levels through autophagy, thereby mitigating lung injury.50 VD has also been shown to restore natural killer (NK) cell activity in oleic acid (OA)-induced ALI, alleviating pulmonary edema and reducing apoptosis of alveolar type I and II epithelial cells.51 These findings suggest that VD indirectly protects lung tissue by modulating immune cell activity and apoptosis. More recently, Mu et al identified a distinct subset of M2 macrophages co-expressing MLPH and CD206. In pneumoconiosis models, this subset was activated and secreted TGF-β1, leading to alveolar structural damage, inflammation, and multiple forms of cell death, including apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis. Interestingly, VD was found to suppress pneumoconiosis-induced M2 macrophages, an effect potentially associated with attenuation of pulmonary fibrosis.52 Collectively, these studies suggest that different M2 macrophage subpopulations may possess diverse biological functions. Distinguishing these subsets will be critical for clarifying how VD modulates immune cell activity and PCD in lung injury.

Intestinal Injury

Cisplatin-induced intestinal injury is a common adverse outcome of chemotherapy. Liu et al established an animal model to assess VD intervention and observed that it preserved intestinal barrier integrity and improved morphology. The protective effects were linked to enhanced antioxidant capacity, decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS) and MDA levels, and reduced inflammation, leading to suppression of OS. Furthermore, VD inhibited ferroptosis, thereby protecting intestinal epithelial cells from cisplatin-induced injury.53

Protective roles of VD and VDR have also been demonstrated in maintaining the intestinal barrier during colitis.54 Sodium/hydrogen exchanger isoform 8 (NHE8), a multifunctional sodium/hydrogen exchanger, contributes to sodium transport, intracellular pH homeostasis, and cell volume regulation, and its downregulation is associated with worsening colitis.55 Evidence suggests that VD may upregulate NHE8 expression in UC through the NF-κB p65 pathway, thereby reducing inflammation and protecting the intestinal barrier.56 Dietary VD3 is closely associated with poultry intestinal health and foodborne salmonellosis. Adequate VD intake can maintain intestinal barrier function in chickens by regulating apoptosis and restoring the balance of gut microbiota.57,58 Similar to hepatic and renal injury, intestinal I/R induces epithelial apoptosis. Direct Evidence for VD/VDR Regulation of Programmed Cell Death in Sepsis-Associated Organ Injury71

Apoptosis

A limited number of preclinical studies have reported protective effects of VD/VDR signaling in sepsis-associated tissue injury.72–74 A recent study reported that serum levels of VD-binding protein (VDBP) were notably lower in patients with sepsis relative to healthy controls. Similarly, decreased VDBP expression was noted in the livers of septic mice. Overexpression of the VDBP gene reduced OS levels in hepatic tissue and inhibited activation of the JNK signaling pathway, indicating that JNK contributes to sepsis-induced liver injury. However, the study did not specify the exact mode of hepatocyte death regulated by VDR, despite the fact that the JNK pathway is generally considered to be closely associated with apoptosis.75

Sepsis-associated AKI is linked with impaired production of active VD. Experimental findings further showed that VD pretreatment attenuated LPS-triggered OS in the kidney and suppressed tubular epithelial cell apoptosis.76 In a separate investigation, astragalus polysaccharide treatment of LPS-induced septic mice markedly reduced tubular dilation and vascular congestion, as observed by HE staining. TUNEL assays further confirmed that renal cell apoptosis was significantly attenuated. These protective effects were attributed to activation of the VD axis, which functioned synergistically with anti-inflammatory signaling and gut microbiota modulation.77 Du et al also verified that VDR activation prevents tubular epithelial apoptosis in LPS-induced AKI.8 Mechanistically, LPS binds Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), activating the NF-κB pathway and markedly upregulating pro-apoptotic regulators.78 VD/VDR signaling counteracts this effect by disrupting the formation of the IKK complex or preventing nuclear translocation of p65/p50, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activation and suppressing tubular apoptosis.79 Beyond the kidney, VDR signaling has also been reported to inhibit intestinal epithelial apoptosis, thereby preserving gut barrier integrity during sepsis.80 Taken together, anti-apoptotic mechanisms appear to be a common pathway through which VD protects against sepsis-induced PCD. These findings indicate that suppression of excessive apoptosis represents a convergent mechanism through which VD/VDR signaling preserves organ integrity during sepsis.

Pyroptosis

Excessive release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) promotes pathological inflammatory responses linked to a variety of diseases. For example, S100 proteins modulate mitochondrial dynamics via the RAGE/ceramide pathway, thereby contributing to acute brain injury during sepsis.63 Importantly, VD intervention appears to interrupt this DAMP-inflammasome-pyroptosis amplification loop. Once released into the extracellular space, histones interact with multiple cell types and exert cytotoxic effects. Reports indicate that extracellular histones can activate inflammasomes and promote Caspase-1 cleavage, which in turn amplifies inflammation and induces pyroptosis.81 In models of sepsis-induced neurodegenerative disease, neuronal cells exposed to exogenous histones exhibited reduced expression of the pyroptotic marker GSDMD following VD treatment, underscoring that VD can attenuate pyroptosis and neuroinflammation in septic conditions.82

VD is classically recognized for its physiological role in regulating calcium-phosphate metabolism and maintaining skeletal homeostasis.83–85 More recently, emerging studies suggest that the VD/VDR axis is indispensable for the regulation of intestinal homeostasis.86 In sepsis, increased expression of miR-874 has been shown to promote intestinal barrier dysfunction. Notably, treatment with a miR-874-5p inhibitor alleviated intestinal barrier injury by reducing epithelial pyroptosis through the VDR/NLRP3 pathway.87 This study suggests that the protective effect of the miR-874-5p inhibitor is mediated by targeting VDR, inhibition of miR-874-5p increased VDR expression, suppressed NLRP3 activation, reduced caspase-1 activation and IL-1β release, and consequently attenuated intestinal epithelial pyroptosis; these protective effects were reversed by VDR downregulation.87

Non-canonical inflammasome signaling is particularly relevant to Gram-negative sepsis. In mice, cytosolic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) directly binds and activates caspase-11, whereas caspase-4 and caspase-5 serve related functions in humans.88 Activated caspase-11 cleaves GSDMD, generating a pore-forming N-terminal fragment that drives pyroptosis and potassium efflux; this process can secondarily activate the NLRP3-caspase-1 pathway and promote IL-1β and IL-18 maturation.89 HMGB1-mediated delivery of extracellular LPS into the cytosol has also been shown to amplify caspase-11-dependent pyroptosis and lethality in experimental sepsis.90 By contrast, direct evidence that VD/VDR signaling regulates caspase-11/4/5 in sepsis is currently lacking. The reported ability of VDR activation to restrain canonical NLRP3-caspase-1-GSDMD signaling suggests a possible indirect connection, but this remains a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism.40,87

Ferroptosis

The release of extracellular histones provokes pyroptosis and activates immune cells, which produce ROS and pro-inflammatory factors that accelerate ferroptosis and worsen cellular injury. Supporting this, Sun et al found that VD treatment elevated GPX4 levels in septic mouse brains and attenuated histone-induced ferroptosis.82

Previous studies have identified a close association between obesity and ferroptosis. In sepsis, ferroptosis contributes to the onset and progression of organ injury.19,91,92 The liver, as the primary iron-storage organ, is particularly susceptible. Accordingly, it is of significant research interest to determine whether VD can alleviate hepatic ferroptosis during septic injury under obese conditions. Findings revealed that VD treatment reduced hepatic iron accumulation, enhanced the GSH/reduced glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio, and attenuated liver ferroptosis.93 Mechanistically, these protective effects are thought to be mediated by VDR, since hepatic macrophages display the highest VDR expression among non-parenchymal liver cells.26 Moreover, experimental evidence confirms that VDR activation by VD regulates the expression of multiple genes in target cells, thereby exerting cell-specific biological functions.94 The VD/VDR axis may suppress ferroptosis in cisplatin-induced kidney injury.10 Therefore, further exploration of VDR signaling pathways and their downstream targets may reveal novel mechanisms by which VD inhibits ferroptosis. Additionally, determining whether the VD/VDR axis regulates other modes of PCD to protect hepatic tissue could provide valuable insights. Such investigations may facilitate the development of targeted inhibitors with enhanced clinical potential. Mechanistically, VD may suppress ferroptosis through coordinated regulation of iron metabolism, antioxidant defenses, and lipid peroxidation pathways in a tissue-specific manner. Given the extensive overlap between pyroptotic, apoptotic, and ferroptotic signaling, PANoptosis may represent a higher-order cell death program potentially influenced by VD/VDR signaling during sepsis.

Necrosis: A Non-Programmed form of Cell Death

VDBP, also known as Gc-globulin, is encoded by the group-specific component (GC) gene.95 VDBP plays a critical role in binding, solubilizing, and transporting VD and its metabolites, while also participating in the modulation of diverse pathophysiological processes. For example, during tissue injury, Gc-globulin contributes to the actin scavenger system, cooperating with gelsolin to clear necrotic cell debris. In addition, VDBP can be converted into Gc-macrophage activating factor (Gc-MAF) through enzymatic modification by immune cell–derived proteases, subsequently stimulating macrophages at the site of injury. Activated macrophages then promote apoptosis through cysteine proteases such as caspases. Moreover, Gc-globulin has been shown to reduce actin release from necrotic cells, thereby mitigating platelet aggregation.96,97 Taken together, as the primary binding protein for active VD, VDBP is closely linked with necrosis, influencing both the clearance of necrotic cells and the downstream inflammatory responses triggered by necrotic tissue.

Future Perspectives

It is important to emphasize that most forms of tissue injury are not mediated by a single type of cell death. For example, in NAFLD, hepatocytes exhibit multiple modes of death, including pyroptosis, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and necrosis.23,28,30,98 Similarly, in cisplatin-induced AKI, renal tubular epithelial cells undergo not only pyroptosis but also ferroptosis.10,40,41 Nevertheless, most studies tend to focus on one predominant mode of cell death when exploring the hepatoprotective effects of VD. Sepsis-associated tissue injury is likewise unlikely to be driven by a single form of cell death. Instead, necrosis, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis may occur simultaneously, with their interplay contributing significantly to disease progression. The crosstalk between these pathways can shape the underlying pathophysiology. For instance, it is well established that enhanced immune responses promote pyroptosis. In central nervous system diseases, neuroinflammation can trigger ferroptosis, while ferroptosis itself can further amplify neuroinflammation. This suggests a potentially complex and bidirectional interaction between ferroptosis and pyroptosis. Current studies investigating the protective effects of VD in sepsis-associated tissue injury have largely concentrated on single forms of cell death or the combined outcomes of multiple pathways, without thoroughly examining the interactions between them.82 The available evidence suggests that sepsis-associated organ injury is driven by a highly interconnected network of regulated cell death pathways rather than by a single mode of PCD. Ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and PANoptosis share extensive crosstalk through oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and mitochondrial dysfunction, while exhibiting distinct upstream regulatory mechanisms and tissue-specific characteristics. Although numerous molecular targets-including GPR81/H3K18la/METTL3/ACSL4, Nrf2, NF-κB, NLRP3, STING, AIM2, and GSDMD, witch have recently been identified, their interactions and hierarchical regulation during sepsis remain incompletely understood. Furthermore, current evidence is largely derived from isolated experimental models focusing on individual organs or single signaling pathways, limiting our understanding of systemic PCD regulation during sepsis. Since VD/VDR signaling simultaneously modulates oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, immune homeostasis, and mitochondrial function, it may function as an upstream coordinator of multiple PCD programs rather than merely regulating a single death pathway. However, this possibility has not yet been systematically validated in clinically relevant sepsis models and therefore represents an important direction for future investigation.

Unlike previous reviews that mainly focused on the immunomodulatory role of vitamin D in sepsis, this review specifically dissects how the VD/VDR axis orchestrates multiple forms of programmed cell death across different organs during sepsis. Importantly, we highlight the emerging crosstalk among apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis and discuss how VD may function as a systemic regulator rather than a single-pathway modulator.

The same mode of cell death may be regulated by multiple VD-mediated signaling pathways. For example, VDR agonists can attenuate renal epithelial apoptosis either by suppressing activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4)37 or by promoting VDR activation and binding to NF-κB, thereby inhibiting the transcription of downstream inflammatory genes, reducing inflammation, and alleviating apoptosis.38 Similarly, VD has been reported to inhibit hyperglycemia-induced ferroptosis through the Klotho/p53 pathway,11 while also suppressing ferroptosis via trans-regulation of GPX4.10 These findings suggest that the dominant regulatory pathway may differ depending on tissue type or disease stage. Thus, in the development of novel therapeutic strategies for sepsis, it is critical to account for the status of multiple signaling pathways in order to better predict treatment responses and potential resistance.

A single disease can induce injury in multiple organs, with infectious diseases in particular propagating damage through the circulatory system. As a systemic immune dysregulation syndrome, sepsis involves the dissemination of inflammatory mediators and pathogens via the bloodstream, placing multiple organs at risk and triggering PCD through shared or distinct signaling pathways. For instance, in septic patients, the intestinal barrier may fail due to inadequate perfusion, reperfusion-induced ischemia, inflammatory injury, and neuroendocrine dysregulation.99,100 Subsequently, sepsis-associated cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β impair intestinal cell proliferation and apoptosis, thereby directly disrupting barrier integrity. In addition, sepsis alters the intestinal microenvironment, exacerbating barrier dysfunction. Excessive inflammation and pathogenic microbes may then penetrate the hyperpermeable barrier into the lymphatic system,101 contributing to gastrointestinal dysfunction and potentially secondary liver injury.101,102 Recent evidence suggests that PANoptosis may represent an integrated cell death program in sepsis, yet whether VD modulates this coordinated pathway remains unexplored. Monitoring inter-organ interactions and the complex signaling networks underlying multi-organ involvement in sepsis is crucial for predicting the extent of organ damage and slowing disease progression. Within this context, future research should consider how VD therapy might influence organ crosstalk in septic models. Systematically dissect the temporal sequence and crosstalk among apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis in sepsis models, and determine whether VD exerts cell type-specific effects on programmed cell death across different organs.

However, the evidence base is uneven: most data are preclinical, several proposed mechanisms are inferred from non-sepsis-associate-injury models and direct sepsis-specific studies remain limited. VD interventions are heterogeneous and should not be discussed as a single treatment. Cholecalciferol is a nutritional precursor that requires sequential activation, calcitriol is the hormonally active metabolite, and paricalcitol is a synthetic VDR agonist; these agents differ in pharmacokinetics, target engagement, and safety considerations. Notably, the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines suggest against the use of VD specifically for sepsis treatment, although the certainty of evidence is very low and this recommendation does not apply to routine VD supplementation for other established indications.103 Consistent with this cautious position, the VIOLET trial found that early high-dose enteral VD3 did not reduce 90-day mortality in critically ill VD-deficient patients; importantly, however, this trial was not restricted to patients with sepsis.104 VD/VDR signaling should therefore be regarded as a biologically plausible regulatory axis and a candidate for further mechanistic investigation, rather than a validated therapeutic strategy. Future research should focus on investigating the interactions among PCDs in clinically relevant sepsis models and elucidating the underlying mechanisms of VD/VDR involvement, thereby providing additional evidence for the role of VD in the prevention and treatment of sepsis.

Conclusions

Current evidence supports a role for VD/VDR signaling in modulating apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis through mechanisms involving inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and cellular homeostasis. However, much of the mechanistic evidence is derived from non-sepsis disease models, whereas direct evidence in clinically relevant sepsis models remains comparatively limited. Emerging data suggest that VD/VDR may influence interconnected PCD networks rather than a single death pathway, but its role in non-canonical inflammasome signaling and PANoptosis remains largely unresolved. Importantly, current clinical evidence does not support vitamin D as an established treatment for sepsis. Future studies should systematically dissect the temporal sequence and crosstalk among apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis in clinically relevant sepsis models and determine whether VD/VDR signaling exerts cell type-specific effects across different organs.
