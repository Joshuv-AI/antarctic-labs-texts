qTaHa-5DL: another major QTL regulating wheat grain hardness
Authors: Xiangyun Fan, Peng Jiang, Yi He, Xu Zhang, Guihong Yu, Meixue Zhou, Peng Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrain hardness has important effects on grain quality and the end-use of wheat. In this study, a collection of 103 common wheat germplasms and a DH population of 194 lines were used to identify new quantitative trait loci (QTL) for grain hardness. Two stable genetic loci on chromosome 5D were detected under different locations and years with one of them being the Ha locus on 5DS where the major gene Puroindolines for wheat grain hardness is located. Another locus of qTaHa-5DL also showed a significant impact on grain hardness index (HI) with HI increasing from ~ 20 to ~ 45 and hardness type changing from soft to mixed when Puroindolines are wild type. A kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) marker for qTaHa-5DL was developed and the effectiveness of the QTL was confirmed in 184 breeding lines with the marker K-Ha5DL dividing wheat into two distinct categories. This new QTL can be effectively used to select soft or medium hard wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05731-z.Keywords: Common wheat, Grain hardness, GWAS, QTL, KASP marker

Introduction

Wheat is one of the major cereal crops cultivated worldwide and approximately 75% of wheat grain is used in food production (FAO dataset, http://faostat.fao.org/). In recent years, with the development of domestic wheat and food industries, the market of China has paid increasing attention to wheat grain quality (WGQ) [1]. Grain hardness is an important trait of WGQ as well as a crucial indicator for evaluation and classification of wheat [2].

Single Kernel Characterization System (SKCS) is used currently as the primary method for determining grain hardness in wheat due to its simplicity, fast detection, reliable data, and good repeatability [3]. Based on the value of hardness index (HI) measured by SKCS, wheat grains are classified into three types: soft (< 40), mixed (40–60) and hard (> 60) [4]. Grain hardness largely determines the amount of water for wheat tempering, flour extraction rate, particle size distribution of flour, and damaged starch content in flour, thereby affecting the processing of grains and the quality of flour as well as flour products [5]. Therefore, grain hardness is closely correlated with some other traits of WGQ, such as protein content, ash content, water absorption and pasting temperature [6–8].

The Puroindolines (Pins) consist of Puroindoline a (Pina) and Puroindoline b (Pinb) genes. They are located at the Ha locus on chromosome 5DS and are the major genes controlling grain hardness in common wheat [9]. The grains exhibit a soft texture when both Pina and Pinb are the wild type, while a mutation in either Pina or Pinb transforms the grain texture from soft to hard [9]. The wild type of Pina and Pinb was respectively named Pina-D1a and Pinb-D1a, while the mutant type of Pins was named Pina-D1b/ Pinb-D1b, Pina-D1c/Pinb-D1c, Pina-D1c/Pinb-D1c based on different types of mutations. There are about 10 and 20 allelic variation types in Pina and Pinb at present, and Pinb-D1b is the main allele detected in hard wheat varieties from the main wheat cultivation zone of China [4, 10]. Current studies on wheat grain hardness genetics and breeding primarily revolve around Pina and Pinb [10]. Based on the principal genotypes of mutation in Pina and Pinb, the corresponding functional molecular markers have been developed, encompassing those suitable for high-throughput genotyping like the KASP marker [11, 12]. However, population genetic studies have confirmed that grain hardness is a quantitatively inherited trait (heritability > 0.7) and polymorphism in Pins can only explain over 60% of the trait’s variation. Apart from the major-effect gene of Pins, multiple hardness-related loci have been identified on almost every chromosome in wheat [13]. Furthermore, grain hardness is affected by environmental factors such as soil, climate, diseases and nutrient levels [4]. Therefore, there is a dearth of further study to identify new QTL that exist stably in different environmental conditions for hardness to be used in breeding programs.

The middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River is the largest production area of soft wheat with weak gluten in China [14]. In the multi-year practice of marker-assisted selection (MAS) for soft wheat by utilizing the functional molecular markers of Pina and Pinb, we have observed that some wheat lines exhibited medium hard grain texture with the HI around 40—45 when both Pina and Pinb are wild type. We speculate that this phenomenon is due to the presence of other grain hardness genes in the genetic background. In this study, a natural population of wheat cultivars collected from local production area and a DH population were used to identify QTL controlling grain hardness, that can be used in breeding programs. The results could provide assistance for further improving wheat hardness in breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

A natural population [15] comprising 103 local wheat varieties released in the period of 1972–2016 in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River was used for GWAS. The 103 varieties were planted in the growing seasons of 2020–2021 at Guanyun and 2020–2021, 2021–2022 at Nanjing, China. A DH population [16] derived from H-020 × H-132 was used for QTL mapping. H-020 is a hard wheat variety, whereas H-132 is a soft wheat variety. The 194 DH lines and their parents were planted in the growing seasons of 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 at Nanjing, China, with a randomized complete block design and three replications. Each genotype was sown in a single row plot, 60 seeds per row with 1.6 m row length, 0.25 m row spacing. Agronomic management including fertilization, weed and disease control followed local practices.

Phenotyping and genotyping

The single kernel characterization system (SKCS) 4100 (Perten, USA) was used to measure grain hardness index according to AACC method 55–31. The natural population was genotyped using Afymetrix 50 K SNP assay (CapitalBio Technology, Beijing, China). The primer sequences of markers were used to BLAST for the physical positions at reference genome of cv. Chinese Spring in the IWGSC (International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium) RefSeq v1.0. TASSEL software v5.2.13 was used for quality control of the genotype data, and SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5% or heterozygosity rate > 10% as well as the SNP markers without positional information in the reference genome were discarded [15]. The DH population was genotyped using diversity array technology (DArT) (Canberra, Australia) (http://www.diversityarrays.com/dart-application-dartseq). The polymorphism markers (χ2-test, p < 0.05) with < 10% missing data were screened to construct genetic map [16].

Phenotypic analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis, frequency distribution analysis and T-test analysis were implemented using IBM SPSS Statistics 19.0 software.

Linkage and association mapping

The molecular map of this DH population has been published earlier [16] and the QTL linkage mapping followed the method described in a previous study by Fan et al. [17]. The GWAS was in accordance with the description in previous study of Jiang et al. [15].

Pins sequencing in the DH population

The sequencing of Pina and Pinb genes was according to Chen et al. [18]. The recovered PCR fragments were sequenced in BGI (Shanghai, China).

KASP marker development and validation analysis

KASP primers which contained two allele-specific forward primers and one common reverse primer were designed based on the sequences of the identified SNPs using PolyMarker [19] and synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. The KASP assays were performed in 384-well PCR plates with 2.5 μL KASP 2X Reaction Mix, 0.07 μL assay mix (12 μL each allele-specific forward primer [100 μM], 30 μL reverse primer [100 μM], and 46 μL TRIS [10 mM, pH 8.3]), and 2.43 μL 30 ng μL−1 genomic DNA. The PCR program was as follows: 94 °C for 15 min; 10 touchdown cycles of 94 °C for 20 s, 61 °C–55 °C for 60 s (decreasing by 0.6 °C per cycle); and 26 cycles of 94 °C for 20 s and 55 °C for 60 s. The PCR reactions was carried out with Hydrocycler 16 water instrument for PCR (LGC Genomics, Beverly, USA), and the PCR Fluorescence detection was performed using a PHERAstar (BMG LABTECH, Germany) microplate reader with known genotypes being included in the plate as controls to ensure the accuracy of the marker readings. KlusterCaller software (LGC Genomics, Beverly, USA) was used to analyze the data.

Results

Phenotype analysis

The hardness index (HI) of the DH population together with two parents and the natural population of 103 wheat cultivars are shown in Fig. 1 and Table S1. HI of the 103 wheat cultivars also showed a continuous distribution with two peaks, with the value varied from 10.78 to 69.56 (Fig. 1 and Table S1). In the DH population, the parent H-020 showed a higher HI (hard type) than H-132 (soft type) in both environments. The HI of DH lines ranged from 20.97 to 80.96 and 0.37 to 74.62, respectively, in the 2021/22 trial (NJ21) and the 2022/23 trial (NJ22) (Table S1). The HI of the DH also showed a continuous distribution with two peaks (Fig. 1). These results suggest that while a major gene primarily controls HI, minor effect genes also play a contributing role.

Linkage mapping

Two QTL were identified from the average HI values of the two environments, namely, qTaHa-5DS and qTaHa-5DL (Table 1, Fig. 2 and S1) from the DH population. qTaHa-5DS with the closest marker of D3956251-3D106, explaining 49.7% of the phenotypic variation, was located at the position of the Ha locus. The other QTL, qTaHa-5DL, was identified in 5DL with the closest marker of D3948927-5D138, explaining 10.3% of the phenotypic variation. These two major QTL were identified in both environments. Minor QTL were also identified from the average HI with only qTaHa-3B being significant from the NJ21 trial, determining only 3% of the phenotypic variation.

Association mapping

According to the population structure analysis, the population was divided into three distinct subgroups (△K = 3) and and a similar result was obtained from the kinship matrix analysis [15]. As shown in the quantile–quantile (Q-Q) plot (Fig. 3), the MLM model was suitable for this study. Fourteen SNP markers responsible for grain hardness index were detected used values from BLUPs by the threshold of P < 0.0001 (Table 2). These markers were all located at two major regions, 5DS and 5DL. Among those associated markers, AX-86170795 is located at the coding region of Pinb (G to A at 223bp) and is the functional marker for Pinb (Pinb-D1a vs Pinb-D1b). The functional marker AX-86185446 and AX-179477449 of this Afymetrix 50 K SNP assay for Pina showed non-polymorphism in the natural population with all the accessions being the wild type. Therefore, the QTL identified at 5DS is the Ha locus, differing in Pinb. The other region with the associated marker of AX-109987382 was located at a similar position to that identified in the DH population on 5DL. In the qTaHa-5DL region, the associated SNP maker AX-109846436 of GWAS (Table 2) was in the gene TraesCS5D02G549800 coding serine/threonine-protein phosphatase which could be a candidate gene.

Effects of qTaHa-5DL on grain hardness

According to the genotyping of two linked markers of D3956251-3D106 and D3948927-5D138 respectively for qTaHa-5DS and qTaHa-5DL, four combinations were grouped for the DH population (Fig. 4A). Significant differences were observed between different alleles of 5DS QTL with the mutant type allele (increasing HI) of 5DS (60.3 for A0B0 and 56.4 A0B1) being significantly higher than the wild type alleles (40.6 for A1B0 and 24.8 for A1B1). However, when 5DS are wild type, 5DL QTL showed a significant impact by increasing HI from 24.8 to 40.6 (Fig. 4A). The results confirmed that qTaHa-5DS is the major QTL for HI but qTaHa-5DL increased HI from soft type to mixed type when qTaHa-5DS allele is soft type.

To further clarify if Pina and Pinb are the candidate genes for the qTaHa-5DL, the Pina and Pinb genes of DH lines and two parents were sequenced. The results showed that the Pina gene were all wild type, while the Pinb gene had the mutant type with a deletion of base A at 210 bp (Pinb-D1p). Further grouping was conducted using the gene specific maker of Pinb gene and the marker for qTaHa-5DL. The use of the gene specific marker led to a much better grouping due to the recombination between the qTaHa-5DS marker and the candidate gene. The low HI lines in the A0 group (Fig. 4A) belonged to the A1 group and the high HI lines in the A1B0 group belonged to the A0 group (Fig. 4B). Within the mutant type of the Pinb gene, the impact of the 5DL QTL became significant, increasing HI from 60.8 to 63.7. Within the wild type of the Pinb gene, the HI increasing allele could increase the HI from 25.5 to 35.8 (Fig. 4B). Similar results were shown in the natural population (Fig. 4C) with an even greater impact of 5DL QTL on HI in the Pinb gene wild type groups (from 22.3 to 46.0).

Further validation of the KASP marker for qTaHa-5DL

Based on the qTaHa-5DL associated SNP marker AX-109987382, a KASP marker K-Ha5DL was developed through transformation (primer sequences: K-Ha5DL-F catcatgggtgtttctaagctct, K-Ha5DL-H catcatgggtgtttctaagctcc, K-Ha5DL-R cagaagttccgcaccatcct). Combined with the functional molecular markers of Pina (AX-86185446) and Pinb (AX-86170795), validation was carried out in another selected group of 184 wheat breeding lines. Furthermore, the known genotypes (cv. Ningmai9 with Pina-D1a/Pinb-D1a, cv. Ningmai23 with Pina-D1b/Pinb-D1a, cv. Ningmai24 with Pina-D1a/Pinb-D1b) were included in the plate as controls to ensure the accuracy of the marker readings. The results showed that all 184 lines had the wild-type Pina, while Pinb had 106 wild type and 53 mutant types. Among the 53 lines (mutant type of Pinb), K-Ha5DL (A0B0 and A0B1) showed no significant effects on HI, while among the 106 lines with wild-type Pinb, K-Ha5DL HI increasing allele significantly increased the HI from 28.7 to 48.6 (Fig. 4D). These results are consistent with the analysis in the natural population as well as the DH population, confirming the importance of the qTaHa-5DL in genotypes with wild-type Pinb.

Discussion

Wheat grain hardness is an important reference index for wheat quality. Grain hardness is primarily controlled by genetics, whereas the environment plays a negligible role [20]. The mutations of Pins determine the major grain hardness class of wheat. However, continuous variations in grain hardness have been observed within each class among natural populations or bi-parental segregating populations, implying the existence of other QTL [13]. Indeed, population genetic studies have confirmed that kernel hardness is a quantitatively inherited trait (heritability > 0.7) and that polymorphism in Pins can only explain about 60% of the trait’s variation [13]. Since the 1990s, many efforts have been made to uncover the genetic basis of wheat grain hardness. Campbell et al. [21] reported two grain hardness-associated QTLs using a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population with RFLP markers, including the major gene (Ha locus) and a minor QTL on chr. 3AS [21]. More QTL have been reported on 1A and 6D [22], 1AS, 1DL, 1BS, 5BL, 7AL and 7BS [23, 24] in bi-parental populations, and 1A, 3B, 4B, 7D through GWAS [25]. Among all the reported QTL, Ha locus on 5DS is the major one for wheat grain hardness with the candidate gene of Puroindoline genes. Other QTL for the HI–related traits reported on nearly all wheat chromosomes by using different mapping populations [13] play relatively minor roles. In our study, the associated marker of AX-86170795 identified by GWAS is in the coding region of Pinb gene. Pins are also within the mapping interval of the linkage mapping in the DH population. The Ha loci where the major gene Pins are located played a major role in regulating grain hardness in both mapping populations. In this study, we identified a new QTL, qTaHa-5DL on chromosome 5DL from both natural and bi-parental mapping populations. This new QTL is particularly important when the Pin genes are wild type. Sun et al. [24] had reported a QTL on 5DL for grain hardness measured by near-infrared reflectance (NIR) spectroscopy. However, this QTL was located in the middle rather than the tail of 5DL and the phenotypic variation was only about 3%. Further study is needed for this new QTL on its potential use in breeding programs targeting medium hardness grains.

The effect of Pins has already been proven that the grains exhibit a soft texture when both Pina and Pinb are the wild type while a mutation in either Pina or Pinb transforms the grain texture from soft to hard [9]. The functional molecular markers corresponding to the major mutation sites of Pina and Pinb have been developed, including KASP markers suitable for high-throughput genotyping, which have significantly advanced the molecular breeding efforts in wheat [12]. The quality of flour and flour products varies greatly among three different hardness types of wheat [26]. Generally, soft wheat is considered suitable for making cookies, while mixed wheat with medium hardness value is considered suitable for making noodles and steamed buns, and hard wheat is suitable for bread making [5, 27, 28]. In China, the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River is the advantage area of soft wheat. For MAS using the functional marker of Pins can be effectively used to screen wheat with hard grain texture (when one of Pins or both of Pins are the mutant type). However, the functional marker of Pins cannot clearly distinguish between soft type and mixed type. In this study, a QTL on 5DL for grain hardness, qTaHa-5DL, was identified through GWAS. qTaHa-5DL was also identified by linkage mapping from a DH population. When Pins are wild-type, qTaHa-5DL could change the hardness type from soft to medium hard with the HI rising from about 20 to 40. A KASP marker was developed and validated its effectiveness in 184 wheat breeding lines. Hence, qTaHa-5DL can be used together with Pins to select wheat varieties with soft or medium hardness grains.

In conclusion, functional markers of Pina and Pinb can be used to screen hard type wheat (Pina and Pinb are both mutant type), while the functional marker of qTaHa-5DL can be used to distinguish the soft and medium type wheat among the breeding materials with the wild type of Pina and Pinb. Further studies are needed to fine-mapp qTaHa-5DL, functional validation using CRISPR/Cas9, and cross-breeding with the specific wheat varieties to evaluate its effects in diverse genetic backgrounds.
