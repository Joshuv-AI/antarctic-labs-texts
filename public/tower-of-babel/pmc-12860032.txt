Association of unstimulated salivary parameters with dental caries in socioeconomically diverse Pakistani school children: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Asma Saher Ansari, Imtiaz Ahmed, Masood Anwar Qureshi, Zairah Muqaddas, Mehwish Hussain, Abdur Rehman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSaliva plays a vital role in maintaining oral health by facilitating mechanical cleansing, buffering acids, and providing essential minerals for remineralization. Alterations in salivary properties such as flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity are known to influence the development of dental caries. This study aimed to assess the association between unstimulated salivary parameters and dental caries in school-aged children in a sample of Pakistani population.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted on 516 schoolchildren aged 9–16 years, selected using multistage stratified random sampling from public and private schools in Karachi. Participants were categorized into caries-free (CF) and caries-active (CA) groups based on their dmft/DMFT scores. Unstimulated whole saliva was collected using the passive drool method. Salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity were measured and categorized as low, normal, or high. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v26.0, and comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U and Krusskal walis test. Logistic regression analysis was performed to find the risk predictor. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsMean salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity were significantly higher in the CF group compared to the CA group (p < 0.05). Girls in both groups showed higher mean values for all parameters than boys. Socioeconomic status was significantly associated with salivary pH and buffering capacity, particularly in the middle and lower SES groups.ConclusionUnstimulated salivary parameters are significantly associated with dental caries status in school-aged children. These findings support the potential use of salivary diagnostics as a non-invasive tool for early identification of high-risk individuals and formulation of targeted preventive strategies. Further longitudinal studies are recommended to validate these findings.Keywords: Buffer capacity, Dental caries, Flow rate, PH, Saliva, School children, Unstimulated salivary flow rate

Introduction

Saliva plays a fundamental role in maintaining oral health by facilitating mechanical cleansing, buffering plaque acids, and supplying essential minerals for tooth remineralization [1, 2]. As a complex biofluid composed of both organic and inorganic constituents, saliva contributes to the biological homeostasis of the oral cavity [3, 4]. In addition to protecting against dehydration, microbial invasion, and mechanical trauma, saliva contains antimicrobial peptides, enzymes, and immune components that help maintain oral and systemic health [5, 6].

Reduced salivary flow has been linked to increased dental caries risk, as it compromises the oral cavity’s natural cleansing mechanism and reduces the availability of protective factors [7]. Normal unstimulated salivary flow rates in healthy individuals typically range between 0.25 and 0.35 ml/min, though this may vary with age, sex, hydration status, and circadian rhythm [8].

Unstimulated saliva is particularly important in resting conditions and shows age- and gender-related variability [9, 10]. Studies have shown that females tend to have lower unstimulated flow rates, and age-related decline can further impact salivary defense mechanisms. Importantly, low flow rates can increase susceptibility to caries by lowering buffering capacity and slowing acid neutralization [11, 12].

Dental caries is a multifactorial disease influenced by a range of host, microbial, dietary, and environmental factors [13]. Salivary characteristics such as pH and buffering capacity are important biological factors that modulate the oral environment and influence caries risk [14]. Saliva’s buffering action—primarily through bicarbonate, phosphate, and protein systems—neutralizes plaque acids and stabilizes oral pH levels [15].

While previous studies have focused on microbial and dietary factors, there is increasing interest in evaluating salivary biomarkers and physicochemical properties as tools for caries risk assessment [16, 17]. Regional factors, including socioeconomic status (SES) and nutritional status, particularly protein malnutrition, can also influence salivary composition. Recent studies from Pakistan have shown that malnourished children have significantly altered salivary flow and pH, affecting their caries susceptibility [18, 19].

Given the high burden of dental caries in Pakistani school-aged children (20, 21) and the limited local data on salivary biomarkers, this study was uniquely stratified and designed to assess the relationship between salivary physiological factors and dental caries status. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on either biological salivary markers or general caries prevalence, this research integrates unstimulated salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity with age, gender, and socioeconomic status within a large, multistage-stratified sample. Such a combined assessment is rare in regional literature and provides population-relevant insights into how sociodemographic determinants interact with salivary physiology to influence caries susceptibility. By establishing locally relevant salivary profiles across diverse socioeconomic strata, this study fills a significant gap in the evidence base and supports the development of targeted, non-invasive caries-risk screening strategies for school-aged children.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted at the Oral Biology Department of Dr. Ishrat ul Ebad Khan Institute of Oral Health Sciences, Dow University of Health Sciences.

Sample selection

The sample size was calculated using the Open Epi online calculator (version 3.01) for comparing two independent means. Based on findings from a previous study [22], the expected mean salivary buffering capacity in the caries-free group was 1.0 (SD = 0.4) and in the caries-active group was 0.9 (SD = 0.4). Using a two-sided 95% confidence level, 80% power, and equal allocation between groups, the required sample size was estimated to be 504 participants, with 252 in each group. To account for potential dropouts or exclusions, the final sample size was increased to 516 participants.

Participants were recruited using a multistage stratified random sampling technique [23]. Using the City District Government’s town listing, three clusters of towns with similar socioeconomic status were identified [24]. One town from each cluster was randomly selected using the lottery method. Within each selected town, one public and one private school were randomly chosen using a number generator in MS Excel [25]. All recruited participants completed a structured questionnaire capturing sociodemographic indicators, including average household income, parental education level, and primary source of earnings or occupation, which were used to classify SES.

Although the sample size was calculated based on differences in salivary parameters between caries groups, the sampling technique employed multistage stratified random selection to ensure adequate representation across SES strata. This was necessary because SES was included as a potential explanatory variable in the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were healthy boys and girls aged 9 to 16 years from public and private schools who provided informed consent and were available on the day of data collection. Included participants had mixed and permanent dentition, with at least four permanent teeth in mixed dentition and at least 20 teeth in permanent dentition. Exclusion criteria included habitual smoking or betel nut chewing, wearing oral prostheses, having oral or systemic diseases, or undergoing radio- or chemotherapy. The age group 9–16 years was chosen because it captures the transition from mixed to permanent dentition as a period marked by high caries susceptibility and ongoing salivary gland maturation.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research and Ethics Committee of Hamdard University Dental Hospital, Hamdard University (Ref. No. HCM&D/HUDH/1102-10−21). Written permission was obtained from school principals, and informed assent/consent forms were signed by students and parents. Participants and school authorities were briefed about the study, and assent/consent forms were distributed one week before sample collection.

Data collection process

Data collection was conducted in two visits:

First visit to examine oral cavity for caries activity

A single trained, calibrated examiner conducted clinical examinations using a dental mirror. Caries detection followed WHO caries assessment criteria using the dmft/DMFT index [23]. To ensure consistency and reduce inter-observer bias, intra-examiner reliability was assessed prior to data collection using a duplicate examination of 10% of the sample, and a Cohen’s kappa value of > 0.85 was achieved. Participants were categorized into:

Caries Free (CF): dmft/DMFT score = 0.

Caries Free (CF): dmft/DMFT score = 0.

Caries Active (CA): dmft/DMFT score > 4.

Caries Active (CA): dmft/DMFT score > 4.

The threshold of DMFT > 4 was selected based on previous epidemiological data identifying this cut-off as reflective of moderate to high caries risk in pediatric populations [26].

Second visit for collection of unstimulated saliva

The Passive Drool Method [27] was used for saliva collection. To control for circadian rhythm variations, all saliva samples were collected between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM [28]. Participants were instructed to avoid food and beverages for at least one hour before sample collection. They were seated in a relaxed position with their heads slightly tilted downward and asked to allow saliva to dribble into a graduated collection tube placed near the lower lip. Saliva was collected for five minutes and immediately stored in sterile tubes on ice. Samples were transported in an icebox and stored at −20 °C until analysis within 24 h to preserve salivary integrity.

Salivary analysis

Salivary flow rate Estimation

The unstimulated salivary flow rate (US-SFR) was calculated by dividing the collected saliva volume by the collection time (ml/min). Flow rate classifications: Low: < 0.25 ml/min; Normal: 0.25–0.37 ml/min and High: > 0.37 ml/min within physiological limits [29].

pH measurement

A digital pH meter (Model 3510 Jenway) was used to measure salivary pH (S-pH). Each saliva sample was measured three times, and the mean value was recorded. pH classifications: Low: < 6.7; Normal: 6.7–7.9 and High: > 7.9.

Salivary buffering capacity measurement

The Ericsson method [30] was used to assess buffering capacity (S-BC). 0.5 ml of saliva was titrated with 1.5 ml of 5 mmol/L HCl (Merck), mixed, and centrifuged for one minute. After resting for 10 min, the pH of the mixture was measured using a digital pH meter. Buffering capacity classifications: Low: < 4.5; Medium: 4.5–5.5 and High: > 5.5.

Data management and analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Descriptive statistics: means and standard deviations were used for quantitative variables (age, US-SFR, S-pH, and S-BC), while qualitative variables (low, normal, and high ranges of saliva and age groups) were presented as frequencies and percentages Mann Whitney u and Kruskal Wallis test was used to compare the mean values of CF with CA groups, and salivary parameters among age, gender, and SES categories. Logistic regression analysis was done to find the risk predictor for dental caries. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the CF- group, no statistically significant differences were found in flow rate (p = 0.138) and pH (p = 0.355) across SES levels. Conversely, among the caries-active group, all three salivary parameters significantly varied with SES. Children from lower SES backgrounds had the highest mean ranks for flow rate (173.51), pH (179.32), and buffer capacity (182.10), with statistically significant differences across SES categories (p ≤ 0.001 for all). While these findings may appear paradoxical, the elevated salivary parameters in lower SES caries-active children could reflect a compensatory physiological response to cariogenic challenges or other unmeasured behavioral and environmental factors.

Logistic regression analysis

Table 4 summarize the Binary logistic regression to examine the influence of demographic variables (age and gender), salivary biomarkers (flow rate, pH, and buffer capacity), and socioeconomic status (SES) on the likelihood of a child being caries active. The overall model was statistically significant, χ² = 190.775, p < 0.001, indicating that the set of predictors reliably distinguished between caries-active and caries-free children. The model explained approximately 42% of the variance in caries activity (Nagelkerke R² = 0.418) and demonstrated good fit, as indicated by the non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 5.537, p = 0.699). The model correctly classified 80.0% of cases, improving on the baseline accuracy of 60.3%.

Among the predictors, age was significantly associated with caries activity. For each one-year increase in age, the odds of being caries-active increased by 27.2% (OR = 1.272, 95% CI: 1.149–1.407, p < 0.001). Salivary flow rate showed a very strong protective effect, with higher values substantially reducing the odds of caries activity (OR ≈ 0.000, p < 0.001). Similarly, buffer capacity was also protective (OR = 0.186, 95% CI: 0.090–0.388, p < 0.001).

Socioeconomic status (SES) was a significant predictor overall (p < 0.001). Children from upper SES backgrounds were over 11 times more likely to be caries-active compared to those from lower SES (OR = 11.419, 95% CI: 6.263–20.822, p < 0.001). The difference between middle and lower SES was not statistically significant (OR = 1.643, p = 0.059), though a trend toward increased risk was observed.

Gender and salivary pH, however, were not statistically significant predictors when controlling for other variables. Gender had no substantial effect (OR = 0.799, p = 0.347), and pH did not independently predict caries activity (OR = 0.573, p = 0.453).

These findings suggest that increasing age, low salivary flow rate, poor buffering capacity, and high SES status are independently associated with increased caries risk in this population. The surprising association of higher caries risk with upper SES warrants further investigation into potential confounding behavioral, dietary, or environmental factors.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the association between unstimulated salivary parameters—specifically flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity—and dental caries in a school-aged population, stratified by gender and socioeconomic status (SES). The findings confirm that children who were caries-free (CF) exhibited significantly higher values across all salivary parameters compared to caries-active (CA) counterparts. This is consistent with a large body of literature affirming that optimal salivary characteristics contribute to caries prevention by promoting mechanical cleansing, buffering acids, and facilitating enamel remineralization [11, 31–34].

A significant observation in this study was the inverse association between low salivary pH and increased caries activity, consistent with previous findings showing a higher risk in adolescents with reduced salivary pH are at higher caries risk [35, 36]. However, other literature highlights that pH alone may not reliably predict caries risk without considering buffering capacity and flow rate [37]; this study found that when buffering capacity was included, correlations with caries were stronger, supporting that buffering systems play a central role in acid neutralization and enamel preservation [33] The inverse relationship between salivary buffering capacity and caries activity observed in this study supports prior evidence that a robust buffering system is essential in maintaining pH homeostasis and resisting enamel demineralization [11].

A key finding in this study was the age‑related difference in salivary parameters, where older children (13–16 years) exhibited significantly higher mean unstimulated salivary flow, pH, and buffering capacity than younger children (9–12 years). This suggests that as children grow older, cumulative exposure to cariogenic challenges and perhaps lapses in preventive behaviors could lead to greater caries burden [11, 38, 39]. Such observations are supported by studies among adolescents which reported that low salivary parameters including flow, pH, and buffer capacity were significantly associated with higher DMFT scores, and these associations became stronger in older age groups (9,12,).

Gender-based analysis showed that girls had higher salivary flow, pH, and buffering capacity values than boys, particularly among CF individuals. This contrasts with some reports that either found no significant gender difference or higher values in boys, but supports the idea that hormonal, physiological, and behavioral factors may contribute to gender differences in oral health [38–40] as a significant modifier of salivary characteristics and caries status.

CF children from middle and lower SES groups showed significantly better salivary profiles than their CA counterparts. While a direct causal relationship between SES and salivary composition is difficult to establish, recent Pakistani research links poor nutrition and protein deficiency with altered salivary flow and composition [18, 20]. This suggests that dietary insufficiencies common in lower SES groups may indirectly increase caries risk by impairing salivary function [41].

Logistic regression analysis strengthened the evidence that low salivary flow, acidic pH, and reduced buffering capacity are predictive of caries risk. These findings reinforce the idea that a multifactorial salivary risk profile is more reliable than a single biomarker [16, 36, 39]. Importantly, this supports the integration of salivary diagnostics into routine dental screening as a non-invasive tool for identifying high-risk children [42, 43]. Moreover, our logistic regression analysis underscored the combined predictive power of salivary parameters alongside demographic factors (age, gender, SES), reinforcing the need to adopt a multifactorial risk model rather than relying on single biomarkers for caries prediction.

This is one of the few stratified assessments of salivary parameters and caries risk among socioeconomically diverse Pakistani schoolchildren. The novelty lies in its context specific approach, capturing both biological and sociodemographic influences on salivary function and caries susceptibility. By analyzing differences across SES and gender, this study offers nuanced insights into caries prevention strategies tailored to the local population.

Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, and reliance on unstimulated saliva alone, which may not capture dynamic responses. Additionally, external factors such as microbial and dietary variables including type of food and frequency of intake along with oral hygiene practices were not assessed. Future longitudinal studies incorporating microbial profiles and dietary assessments along with additional biochemical and clinical parameters are recommended.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms the significance of salivary flow rate, pH, and buffering capacity as protective factors against dental caries in children. These findings advocate for the inclusion of salivary diagnostics in school-based oral health programs, particularly in resource-limited settings. Interventions targeting hydration, diet, and oral hygiene practices may help optimize salivary parameters and reduce caries risk in vulnerable populations.
