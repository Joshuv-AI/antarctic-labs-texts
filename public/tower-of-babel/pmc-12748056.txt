Clinical Outcomes of Robot-Assisted “Asleep” Deep Brain Stimulation With Microelectrode Recording in Parkinson’s Disease
Authors: Kyung Won Chang, Jinyoung Youn, Ji Seon Jeong, Inje Jo, Yeajung Choi, Jin Whan Cho, Jung-Il Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep brain stimulation (DBS) is a well-established treatment for movement disorders, particularly Parkinson’s disease (PD), where precise electrode placement is critical for optimal outcomes. With advances in robotic systems and imaging technologies, robot-assisted asleep DBS combined with microelectrode recording (MER) has emerged as a promising technique. This study aimed to evaluate the surgical accuracy and clinical outcomes of robot-assisted asleep DBS with MER in PD patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of PD patients who underwent either robot-assisted asleep DBS or conventional manual frame-based DBS at a single center between August 2021 and August 2024. Targeting accuracy was assessed using radial error measured on intraoperative computed tomography. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), and the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) at baseline, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively.ResultsA total of 51 patients underwent robot-assisted asleep DBS. This group demonstrated significantly higher targeting accuracy than the manual frame-based group (P = 0.001). Both surgical methods resulted in significant improvements in UPDRS, LEDD, and PDQ-39 scores over time, with no significant differences between groups. MER was successfully performed under general anesthesia using remimazolam, maintaining high-quality recordings and patient safety.ConclusionRobot-assisted asleep DBS significantly improves targeting accuracy while maintaining comparable clinical outcomes to manual frame-based DBS. Asleep DBS with MER is a safe and effective approach, enhancing patient comfort without compromising efficacy. These findings support the broader adoption of robotic techniques in DBS for PD.Keywords: Robot-Assisted Deep Brain Stimulation, Asleep Deep Brain Stimulation, Manual Frame-based Deep Brain Stimulation, Parkinson’s Disease

INTRODUCTION

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a well-established treatment modality for movement disorders, including Parkinson’s disease (PD), essential tremor, and dystonia,1 In particular, for PD which is characterized by its long and progressive clinical course, the flexibility to adjust stimulation parameters over time represents a significant advantage of DBS.2,3

However, in order to achieve these benefits, accurate placement of electrodes within the stimulation target is crucial.4 Traditionally, the manual stereotactic frame has been considered the gold standard for achieving precision in DBS procedures. With advancements in technology and imaging modalities, robot-assisted techniques and intraoperative imaging have emerged as valuable tools for enhancing the accuracy of DBS targeting.5,6,7,8

The evolution of imaging technologies has also enabled direct targeting of stimulation sites, sparking ongoing debate about the necessity of microelectrode recording (MER).9 Historically, awake surgery was preferred to facilitate MER and to assess clinical symptoms intraoperatively.10 However, with the advent of intraoperative imaging and the challenges associated with anesthesia management, patient discomfort, and prolonged operation times, there has been increasing discussions about the comparative advantages of awake versus asleep DBS procedures.11

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of robot-assisted asleep DBS with MER recording in patients with PD, shedding light on its feasibility and potential advantages.

METHODS

Study design and patient selection

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent DBS for movement disorders using robotic assistance since the installation of the robotic system in August 2021. Data of patients with PD were extracted from a single-institution cohort spanning from August 2021 to August 2024. Patients who underwent DBS using the conventional Leksell frame during the same period were included as a control group. The baseline characteristics of each group are presented in Table 1. Since there is no established consensus on the selection criteria for surgical methods, the choice of procedure was based on patient preference. As both methods were approved, patients were given the option to choose their preferred approach after receiving detailed information about each technique.

Operation method

The operation methods used in this study were described in a previous paper.12 All DBS procedures were performed by a single surgeon. In summary, the patients were preoperatively stabilized using an O-shaped stereotactic robotic frame. A preoperative magnetic resonance imaging series was obtained and subsequently merged with an intraoperative computed tomography (CT) scan acquired after frame fixation. The robotic system (Kymero©; Koh Young, Seoul, Korea) was then attached to the operating table and the patient was positioned and securely stabilized to prevent any movement-related errors. The robotic system performed an initial self-error check which was further validated using an optical lens navigation system integrated into the robotic arm. Based on the trajectory planned via dedicated software, the robotic system guided the insertion path with precision.

For PD, the common stimulation targets were the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and the globus pallidus internus (GPi). In cases of tremor-dominant PD, the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus was targeted.

Commercially available DBS system were utilized, including Medtronic electrodes (3387, 3389, SenSight leads) paired with Activa RC or Percept PC implantable pulse generators (IPG), and Boston Scientific directional electrodes (DB2202) paired with IPG systems (Gevia, Vercise PC, Genus R16), and St. Jude system.

Most DBS procedures for PD were performed using the “Asleep” method under general anesthesia with intravenous (IV) anesthetics. During the early phase of this study, propofol and remifentanil were primarily used. However, maintaining the bispectral index (BIS) within the desired range proved challenging with propofol. Consequently, remimazolam was adopted as the primary anesthetic in combination with remifentanil allowing for better BIS control without interfering with MERs.

IV anesthetics were administered using the target-controlled infusion. The induction doses were as follows: remimazolam at 6–12 mg/kg/hr, remifentanil is 3 ng/mL, and propofol at 5 mcg/mL. Following induction, the BIS target was maintained within a range of 40 to 50 to ensure optimal conditions for clearly observing MER. It was particularly important to achieve correct BIS attachment and avoid the use of muscle relaxants whenever possible. The train-of-four level was maintained at 3 or 4 to minimize the muscle relaxants interference with MER.

The MER system used during the procedure was the Lead Point 4 (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). MER was routinely performed using a single trajectory on each side, with additional trajectory recordings conducted only when the initial trajectory recordings or responses to macrostimulation were deemed unsatisfactory. The MER settings were configured as follows: low-frequency filter/high-frequency filter: 100–500 Hz/3–5 kHz, sweep: 100 ms/div, amplitude: 10–50 µV. Microstimulation settings were configured with a duration of 0.5–1 second, a rate of 60–200 Hz, and an intensity of 0.5–5 mA.

Intraoperative electromyography was recorded during macrostimulation to identify stimulation-induced side effects and to relatively estimate the electrodes' distance from adjacent anatomical structures.

Surgical outcomes

Surgical outcomes, including target accuracy, surgical time, preparation time, and side effects, were evaluated. After electrode insertion, an intraoperative CT scan was performed to confirm any target errors, intracranial hemorrhage, or other unintended side effects. Target accuracy in this study was defined by radial error, a commonly used measure in other studies,13 which was determined by measuring the perpendicular distance between the planned trajectory and the center of the electrode from the CT scan. The detailed methodology has been described in our previous publication.12 Both the depth and radial errors of the implanted electrodes were assessed in the operating room. However, depth error was not considered a measure of surgical accuracy in this study, as we typically determine the depth of the electrodes with the assistance of MER, ensuring optimal placement to allow the use of as many contacts as possible for stimulation. Therefore, the primary focus of our measurements was radial error.

Clinical outcomes

For the clinical outcomes of DBS, symptoms of PD were assessed using the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) at baseline, 6 months postoperatively, and 1 year postoperatively. UPDRS scores were compared across these time points. Additionally, the levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) and the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) was measured at the same intervals. Patients with follow-up durations of less than 6 months were excluded from the statistical analysis.

Statistical method

A t-test was performed to compare the accuracy based on the stereotactic device, with a P value of < 0.01 considered statistically significant. Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using an independent t-test to assess differences in demographic and clinical factors. Also, a paired t-test was done to compare the clinical scores at baseline, 6 months postoperatively, and 1 year postoperatively. A linear mixed model was conducted to analyze whether the degree of clinical improvement over time differed depending on the surgical method used (robot-assisted DBS or manual frame-based DBS). Additionally, we performed repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to analyze clinical outcome results.

Ethics statement

This study was performed with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (approval No. 2022-11-059). Patient consent was not required since this study does not include personal identifiable data.

RESULTS

Surgical outcome

Fifty-one patients with PD underwent robot-assisted asleep DBS during the study period. A control group of 26 patients who underwent manual frame-based DBS during the same period was included in this study to compare the surgical and clinical outcomes (Table 1). The most commonly targeted structure for robot-assisted DBS was the STN (29/51, 56.9%), followed by the GPi (19/51, 37.3%) and the VIM (3/51, 5.9%). In the control group, the targeted structures were STN (17/26, 65.4%), GPi (8/26, 31%), and VIM (1/26, 3.8%) (Fig. 1).

The average preparation time for robot-assisted asleep DBS was 74 minutes, which included time for anesthetic induction, robotic installation, attachment of monitoring leads, insertion of a Foley catheter, and the intraoperative CT scan. The average surgical time was 2 hours and 26 minutes with the majority of cases being bilateral cases (50/51, 98.0%). In the control group, the average preparation time was 70 minutes, and the surgical time averaged 2 hours and 14 minutes.

Regarding the surgical complications of robot-assisted DBS, hemorrhage occurred in one trajectory of a bilateral GPi case, resulting in a hemorrhage rate of 1.96%. Fortunately, the patient recovered without any specific neurologic deficits. No postoperative infections were found.

When comparing accuracy based on the stereotactic device, the t-test revealed that the robot-assisted method resulted in statistically significant (P = 0.001) less radial error compared to the manual frame (Fig. 2). The robot-assisted method demonstrated a lower average radial error and a narrower range of error across surgeries compared to the manual frame. However, because the depth was determined based on MER, depth differences along the MER trajectory or Z-axis were observed in both groups. The average depth difference was 3.46 mm in the robot-assisted group and 3.47 mm in the manual group.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were measured using UPDRS scores, LEDD and PDQ-39 at baseline, 6 months post-DBS, and 1-year post-DBS, and were evaluated and compared (Fig. 4, Table 2). For LEDD, same pattern with UPDRS, a significant decrease was observed from baseline to both 6 months and 1 year. However, no significant differences were noted between 6 months and 1-year post-DBS (all groups: P = 0.968, robot-assisted group: P = 0.567, manual group: P = 0.774).

Regarding PDQ-39, a significant decrease was observed at both 6 months and 1 year for both the robot-assisted group and the overall group (all groups: P = 0.001 at 6 months, P = 0.039 at 1 year; robot-assisted group: P = 0.001 at 6 months, P = 0.032 at 1 year). DISCUSSION

The integration of intraoperative imaging and robotic systems in DBS procedures has the potential to enhance targeting accuracy and optimize electrode placement—both of which are critical for minimizing complications and improving outcomes. In our study, robot-assisted DBS demonstrated significantly better targeting accuracy than manual frame-based DBS, as evidenced by reduced radial error. However, this technical advantage did not translate into statistically significant differences in clinical improvement between the 2 groups. This suggests that while robotic systems improve surgical precision, the therapeutic efficacy of DBS in PD remains consistently high regardless of the surgical approach.

Beyond the clinical outcomes, robotic-assisted DBS offers several practical advantages. Robotic systems enable submillimeter-level adjustments, allowing for highly precise and reproducible trajectory alignment. These refinements improve surgical consistency and confidence, even if they do not always lead to immediate clinical benefit. In addition, robotic systems help minimize mechanical deformation of the stereotactic frame and reduce the risk of human error in coordinate interpretation. The automation of trajectory alignment also reduces the number of manual adjustments needed during surgery, lowering the risk of contamination from repeated manipulation of the frame.

MER remains a valuable tool in DBS surgery, even under general anesthesia in “asleep” procedures. Our findings suggest that MER is still necessary to guide the ideal depth of electrode placement and occasionally refine the radial trajectory by identifying paths with better neural activity recordings. Notably, the use of MER in our study did not significantly increase the risk of bleeding or infection, corroborating findings from other studies and our prior results. Though there are still remaining concerns regarding potential safety issues associated with multiple MER trajectories.

While some debate exists regarding the necessity of MER in the context of modern imaging techniques, its role in providing real-time physiological feedback remains unparalleled.14 MER ensures that the electrodes are positioned in areas that maximize therapeutic effects while minimizing adverse side effects, particularly in complex cases where imaging alone may not sufficiently delineate the functional anatomy.

While there is no significant clinical difference in outcomes between “asleep” and “awake” DBS,11 general anesthesia enhances patient comfort, reduces movement, and alleviates the psychological and physical stress associated with awake procedures. Our study, along with others, shows that asleep DBS provides high-quality microelectrode recordings without compromising clinical outcomes.15,16

Asleep DBS is particularly beneficial for patients who cannot tolerate awake procedures due to anxiety, tremors, or other medical conditions. Although propofol and dexmedetomidine are the most widely used anesthetics, our group is the first to report the use of remimazolam for microelectrode recording during asleep DBS (Fig. 3). The use of remimazolam in our cohort allowed for stable anesthesia management, minimizing interference with MER quality and ensuring patient safety. The BIS monitoring ensured appropriate anesthesia levels, further supporting the feasibility of performing DBS under general anesthesia.

Although asleep DBS enhances patient comfort, it does not significantly impact clinical outcomes. Studies demonstrate that both awake and asleep DBS achieve similar motor function improvements and UPDRS scores.17,18 Therefore, “asleep” DBS provides a safe, effective option for patients while reducing invasiveness and discomfort.

Although we did not observe significant differences in accuracy or outcomes across the early, mid, and late phases of robotic DBS cases, procedural efficiency—particularly preparation time—improved noticeably with experience. This learning curve extends beyond the neurosurgeon and includes the entire surgical team: anesthesiologists, nurses, neuromonitoring and radiology imaging technicians. Efficient robot handling, coordination with imaging devices (e.g., mobile CT, C-arm), and system setup contribute to overall workflow improvement.

Although robotic-assisted DBS may involve higher upfront costs, its broader utility in stereotactic procedures such as biopsy and stereo-electroencephalography can improve institutional cost-efficiency. Furthermore, robotic systems include integrated planning software, whereas frame-based surgeries typically require additional commercial planning tools (e.g., Brainlab, StealthStation), potentially balancing overall cost. In anatomically challenging regions such as the temporal or occipital lobes or when targeting multiple trajectories, robotic platforms provide a practical advantage by reducing targeting complexity and minimizing the risk of X, Y and Z coordinate-related errors.

As a retrospective study, our analysis lacked standardized long-term postoperative imaging to assess potential electrode displacement after surgery. While robotic assistance enhances initial targeting accuracy, electrode tip shifts due to cerebrospinal fluid re-accumulation or brain re-expansion remain a known potential cause of electrode displacement and failure of stimulation effects after DBS.

Given the improved targeting accuracy and the safety profile demonstrated in our study, we advocate for the adoption of robotic-assisted DBS where feasible. The potential for enhancing surgical precision makes robotic DBS an attractive option for procedural standardization and optimize DBS outcomes. For institutions with access to these technologies, robotic assistance and intraoperative imaging offer a clear advantage in ensuring precise electrode placement and reducing the risk of targeting errors therefore we recommend leveraging their capabilities to enhance surgical precision and streamline workflows. Furthermore, as technology continues to advance, it is likely that future studies will further elucidate the long-term results of robotic assistance in improving both the technical and clinical outcomes of DBS.

In conclusion, robot-assisted DBS offers a significant improvement in targeting accuracy compared to manual frame-based DBS, though it does not appear to lead to a greater clinical improvement in PD symptoms. MER remains a vital tool in achieving optimal electrode placement, providing real-time feedback that complements imaging techniques and ensures optimal electrode placement. Also, use of MER in “asleep” procedures under general anesthesia is both safe and effective. For centers equipped with the necessary resources, we recommend utilizing robotic-assisted DBS as it offers advantages in terms of surgical precision, patient comfort, and overall workflow. As we continue to refine these technologies, the integration of intraoperative imaging and robotic surgery will likely play an increasingly important role in optimizing DBS outcomes for patients with PD.
