Compliance with ESAIC guidelines for fibrinogen concentrate prescription in cardiac surgery: a multicentre French cohort study
Authors: Dan Longrois, Pierre Albaladejo, Damien Bedague, Alexandre Ouattara, Adrien Bouglé, Pauline Dureau, Bernard Cholley, Paul-Michel Mertes, Sophie Provenchère, Emmanuel Robin, Guillaume Besch, Tommy Grelet, Sandrine Grosjean, Pierre-Grégoire Guinot, Bertrand Rozec, Jean-Christophe Rigal, Jean-Luc Fellahi, Pascal Colson, SFAR Research Committee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibrinogen concentrate may reduce allogeneic blood product transfusion in cardiac surgery patients with bleeding associated with acquired hypofibrinogenemia. The European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (ESAIC) has issued guidelines, but compliance to these guidelines has not been studied yet.MethodsThis multicentre observational cohort study was aimed at evaluating the compliance of fibrinogen prescription with ESAIC. Adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass in 13 French cardiac surgery centres were recruited from March 2017 to April 2018. Compliance with ESAIC guidelines was considered whenever patients received fibrinogen in case of hypofibrinogenemia and clinically relevant bleeding, or when patients did not receive fibrinogen concentrate if there was no hypofibrinogenemia and/or no clinically relevant bleeding. The primary endpoint was the percentage of patients who complied those guidelines. Secondary endpoints were to assess the consequences of non-compliance on in-hospital deaths and hospital length-of-stay.ResultsAmong 2,649 adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, 374 (14.1%) received fibrinogen concentrate. Rates of prescription among centres varied from < 1.0% to 31.2% (p < 0.001). Compliance with guidelines was observed in 2,291 (86.5%) patients, driven by a high number of patients without prescription (N = 2,158; 94.5%). In non-compliance patients (N = 358; 13.5%), fibrinogen over-prescription (N = 241) exceeded under-prescription (N = 117). In multivariate analyses, non-compliance with the guidelines was not significantly associated with in-hospital deaths or hospital stay.ConclusionsFibrinogen concentrate prescription varied significantly among centres but compliance with the ESAIC guidelines was high (86.5%). Non-compliance (13.5%)—mostly due to over-prescription—was not associated with adverse outcomes.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov identifier: (NCT03075774).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03347-w.

Background

Cardiac surgery is characterized by altered haemostasis and increased risk of bleeding [1]. Moderate and excessive bleeding concern 40% and 10% of the patients respectively [2, 3]. Both moderate/excessive bleeding and transfusion and/or prescription of blood-derived products are independently associated with an increased risk of death [1, 2, 4–6].

Because the effectiveness and the benefit-to-risk ratio of blood products are debated [4–8], blood-derived products, e.g. prothrombin complex concentrate or fibrinogen concentrate have been proposed as first-line therapies to correct haemostasis abnormalities following cardiac surgery and to decrease the use of blood products and their side effects [9, 10]. Among coagulation factors, fibrinogen concentration is the first to decrease below a critical threshold during cardiac surgery and thus contributes to increase the risk of bleeding [11]. Several studies have suggested that low pre-operative and/or low post-operative fibrinogen concentration is strongly associated with an increased risk of bleeding [12]. These observational studies have been the incentive to perform interventional studies to test the hypothesis that correction of acquired hypofibrinogenemia with fibrinogen concentrate could decrease bleeding and transfusion of blood products following cardiac surgery [13–16]. These studies and meta-analyses [17, 18] have been the basis of recommendations by scientific societies for using fibrinogen concentrate in cardiac surgery [13–18]. In 2013, then in subsequent 2017/2022 updates, the European Society of Anaesthesiology (ESA; ESAIC since 2020) recommended treatment with fibrinogen concentrate in bleeding patients [19–21]. Other narrative/systematic reviews have also adopted similar recommendations [22, 23].

Guidelines are aimed at stating on actual scientific knowledge and to incentive practitioners to homogenize their clinical practice, and, in this case of cardiac surgery, at reducing the high variability on blood and blood-derived products prescriptions among institutions (or clinicians from the same institution) [24, 25]. To the best of our knowledge, there are no prospective studies that investigated compliance with the ESAIC guidelines concerning the prescription of fibrinogen concentrate in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Evaluating compliance is important to assess their practical performance in clinical settings, which may result from two main features, comprehensibility and applicability. Furthermore, analysis of compliance with published guidelines could have an impact on subsequent guidelines updates/implementation tools [26, 27].

This study evaluates compliance with ESAIC guidelines on fibrinogen concentrate prescription in cardiac surgery and explores its impact on outcomes.

Patients and methods

Patient population

Adult patients (older than 18 years), who underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) consecutively between March 09, 2017, and April 10, 2018, were recruited.

As no ethical issues were raised by establishing a non-interventional registry, the study was not submitted to relevant Independent Ethics Committees/Institutional Review Boards. All included patients gave informed consent for the use of their medical records related to the study.

Study design and setting

The FibCard study was a multicentre, cohort, observational study conducted in 13 French cardiac surgery centres (list of centres at the end of the manuscript).

According to the ESAIC guidelines, a fibrinogen concentration of less than 1.5 to 2 g.l-1 was considered as acquired hypofibrinogenemia. However, the treatment of hypofibrinogenemia with fibrinogen concentrate was recommended only in bleeding patients [19, 20]. For the current study, hypofibrinogenemia was defined whenever plasma fibrinogen was < 2 g.l-1.

Since there is no consensus on the definition of bleeding in real-time, the study steering committee agreed to consider only postoperative blood loss in the drains to define bleeding patients. When the total volume of blood collected from chest tubes at H-24 of chest closure exceeded the 75th percentile of the cohort, patients were considered as having “clinically relevant postoperative bleeding”.

Therefore, adherence to guidelines meant either: 1) fibrinogen concentrate prescription for patients with plasma fibrinogen < 2 g.l-1 AND having postoperative clinically relevant bleeding, or 2) no fibrinogen concentrate prescription for patients with fibrinogen ≥ 2 g.l-1 AND/OR without postoperative clinically relevant bleeding.

Fibrinogen plasma concentration was collected during surgery and within the first 48 postoperative hours according to the routine clinical practice of each centre. The chronology and conditions of FC prescriptions (i.e. time of prescription, the delay between the first and the second prescription, and doses) were also recorded. The compliance to ESAIC guidelines was then checked, allowing to classify patients into 4 groups according to adherence to recommendations (R) (R ±) and fibrinogen concentrate (FC) administration (FC ±):Group 1: FC recommended, and FC administered (R +/FC +).Group 2: FC recommended, and FC not administered (R +/FC-).Group 3: FC not recommended, and FC administered (R-/FC +).Group 4: FC not recommended, and FC not administered (R-/FC-).

The allocation of the patients to one of the four groups was performed with automated coding using Excel software and checked by an adjudication committee on anonymized centre data.

Over-prescription was defined as patient receiving FC prescription while not recommended (expressed as percentage: [number of FC +/number of R-] × 100). Under-prescription was defined as patient not receiving FC while recommended (expressed as percentage [number of FC-/number of R +] × 100).

All medical and surgical procedures as well as drug prescriptions remained at the physician's entire discretion.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was to evaluate compliance with the ESAIC guidelines for the prescription of FC in adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery. The primary endpoint measure was the percentage of patients in groups 1 and 4.

Secondary endpoints included evaluation of the consequences of compliance with the ESAIC guidelines on clinical outcomes such as death during the follow-up period, and hospital lengths of stay.

Data collection

All data and variables of interest have been retrieved from the patients'medical records by research technicians, and the quality of the data was checked by specially trained members of a contract research organization.

Collected information concerning patients'characteristics included: preoperative treatments, haemostasis tests [haemoglobin, platelets, fibrinogen, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT)], international normalized ratio (INR), serum creatinine concentration, characteristics of the surgery including the type of surgery, CPB protocol (time, hypothermia, cardiac arrest), Extra Corporeal Life Support, blood products [packed red blood cells (PRBC), fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and platelets] as well as blood-derived products (prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC), recombinant activated factor VII). Cumulative blood loss at 48 h and surgical re-intervention for bleeding/tamponade were also recorded.

Follow-up data included: duration of mechanical ventilation, durations of intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital stay, and hospital mortality. Data verification was performed remotely by trained clinical research assistants to ensure data quality.

Study size

Sample size estimation was based on an anticipated 10% prevalence of patients with severe bleeding after cardiac surgery who would require the prescription of FC.2,3 In a population of 3000 patients, 300 patients may receive fibrinogen concentrate with a good compliance to guidelines. We anticipated that further 150 to 250 patients may be exposed to fibrinogen concentrate prescription, though not in the massive bleeding group, possibly outside the guidelines. Therefore, up to 500 ± 50 patients can be exposed to FC prescription.

No power analysis was conducted for secondary outcomes.

Statistical analyses

Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or for non-normal distribution, median [25;75 percentiles]. Categorical variables are expressed as the number and percentage of documented categories (n, %). No replacement of missing data was performed.

The primary endpoint was estimated with its 2-sided 95% confidence interval (95%CI). A negative binomial mixed model using a pseudo-likelihood method of estimation was used to estimate the association of compliance with the Guidelines and FC prescription with death, after adjustment (fixed factors) on the type of surgery, CPB time, and prescription of blood products (as Incidence Risk Ratio, IRR).

Hospital length of stay was considered as a surrogate of postoperative complications. A generalized mixed model (multinomial) was used to estimate the association of FC prescription with hospital duration (terciles, using a generalized mixed model, with ordinal distribution and cumulative logit) in non-deceased patients, after adjustment (fixed factors) on type of surgery, CPB time and prescription of blood products. All estimates are IRR.

The study centre was entered as a random effect in mixed models.

All statistical tests were 2-sided and at the 0.05 significant threshold. However, level 2 interactions were tested at the 0.10 statistically significant level. Statistically significant interactions were graphically explored.

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS® Version 9.4, and SAS/STAT 15.1 software (SAS Institute, North Carolina USA).

Among the 374 patients who received FC, documented hypofibrinogenemia was the reason for FC prescription in approximately 40% of the cases. Estimated important bleeding was declared as an indication for FC prescription in less than 20% of the cases (Figure A1). The first prescription occurred during CPB in 42 (11.4%) patients, between the end of CPB and the departure from the operating room in 165 (45.0%) patients, and after departure from the operating room in 160 (43.6%) patients (timing of first administration was not documented for seven patients). FC was administered at least twice in 80 (21.4%) patients. Median time between the first and second prescription was 185 [60; 323] min. The median dose of FC was 3 [1.5; 3] g for the first prescription and 1.5 [1.5; 3] g for the second.

Among the 358 (13.5%) patients for whom guidelines were not followed, 241 (67.3%) received FC outside actual recommendations, which resulted in 10.0% over-prescription (241/2399 patients for whom FC was not recommended) and 117 (32.7%) did not receive FC while recommended, with 46.8% under-prescription (117/250 patients for whom FC was recommended).

Secondary endpoints: association between compliance with ESAIC guidelines and clinical outcomes

Outcomes were worse in the Group FC + with longer mechanical ventilation (Group FC + : 7 [4; 17] hours vs Group FC-: 4 [2; 5] hours; p < 0.01), longer ICU stay (Group FC + : 3 [2; 7] days vs Group FC-: 2 [1; 4] days; p < 0.01), longer hospitalization (Group FC + : 14 [11; 22] days vs Group FC-: 12 [10; 17] days; p < 0.01) and more deaths (Group FC + : 30 (8.0%) vs Group FC-: 40 (1.8%); p < 0.01). Re-exploration for bleeding was more frequent in the Group FC + : 116 (31.0%) as compared to 167 (7.3%) in the Group FC- (p < 0.01).

According to univariate analysis, non-compliance with the guidelines was associated with an increased risk of death: 17 (4.75%) deaths and 53 (2.31%) deaths in the non-compliant and compliant groups, respectively (p < 0.01).

According to multivariable analyses (adjustment on type of surgery, CPB TIME, and perioperative transfusion of blood products), there was no association between non-compliance with guidelines and increased risk of death (IRR [95%CI]: 1.01 [0.57; 1.80], p = 0.97) (Table A3).

Similarly, there was no association between compliance with the guidelines and hospital length of stay in surviving patients after adjustment on type of surgery, prescription of blood products, and CPB time (p = 0.84) (Table A.4).

Discussion

This is the first prospective study evaluating compliance with ESAIC guidelines for FC prescription in cardiac surgery. Compliance was high, but a high inter-centre variability for FC prescription was observed. Non-compliance (mainly over-prescription) was not associated with worse outcomes.

ESAIC guidelines recommend that FC should only be prescribed when hypofibrinogenemia is documented (fibrinogen concentration < 1.5–2 g l-1), and when there is excessive bleeding [20]. First, documented hypofibrinogenemia implies measurement and there might be analytical variability when measuring fibrinogen concentration [28]. Besides, we observed a contextual interpretation of acquired hypofibrinogenemia, since the prescription of FC was mostly motivated by other reason than plasma fibrinogen concentration itself (> 60%). Second, the recommendation infers a"clinically relevant bleeding". This term is even more"uncertain"mostly because clinicians need to identify clinically relevant bleeding in real-time, especially in the operating room with the thorax open. Except for clinically obvious situations, practitioners often make"bets"(probabilistic reasoning) on excessive bleeding, possibly based on predictive scores [29]. However, their predictive positive values are low, even when adding viscoelastic tests [29, 30]. Incorporating real time bleeding may be more approachable in postoperative care. In a prospective observational study, early bleeding within the first 2 postoperative hours in the ICU was shown to predict high blood loss at 12 h. Thus, blood products or FC administration could be objectively prescribed early, before further bleeding occurs [31]. Furthermore, clinically relevant bleeding is not only a matter of definition but also of the physician's estimate of"clinical relevance", which may vary according to centres, physicians, surgical procedures, or patients [32]. Our definition of excessive bleeding aimed at overcoming these difficulties. However, the 75th percentile volume observed in our cohort was only 720 ml. According to the Universal Definition of Perioperative Bleeding classification, a volume of 601- 800 ml at 12 h is considered as mild bleeding [2]. Thus, our estimation of the incidence estimate of clinically relevant postoperative bleeding may appear high as our threshold was very low. Conversely, an absence of postoperative bleeding following a timely, pertinent FC administration in the operating room (representing most prescriptions in our cohort: 56.4%), may have resulted in overestimating undue FC prescription.

Compliance with the ESAIC guidelines appeared rather high (86.5%) despite a large variation in FC prescription between centres [33]. The high level of compliance was mainly driven by the adherence to non-indication of FC (94.9%), meaning physicians did not prescribe FC when it was not recommended. The low number of FC prescription while recommended (5%) further suggests that physicians were reluctant to use fibrinogen concentrate. Consequently, non-compliance appears rather limited (13.5%), even if “over-prescription” (9.1%) exceeded “under-prescription” (4.4%). Anyway, these numbers should be interpreted with caution due to the weakness in the ESAIC guidelines in interpreting bleeding in real-time and excluding possible adequate anticipation of a bleeding risk.

Though non-compliance with the ESAIC Guidelines was rather limited, the question arose of whether non-compliance, as well as FC prescription, might be associated with the risk of poor outcomes, i.e., in-hospital death or postoperative complications (integrated into intensive care unit/hospital length of stay for surviving patients). Our results did not show statistically significant association but demonstrated that factors independently associated with an increased risk of death or complications were the type of surgery, the CPB time, and the prescription of blood products. Of note, patients who received FC had more complex surgeries and lower platelet counts that could have impacted the outcomes. Although the statistical association is not causality, given the widely documented evidence on the association between blood products transfusion and patient outcome, one could be tempted to assert that unnecessary prescription of blood products is deleterious and should be avoided especially since this is a modifiable factor as opposed to the type of surgery or CPB time [1, 2].

This study has some limitations. It reflects French cardiac surgery practices in academic hospitals. Although all clinicians were aware of the 2017 ESAIC guidelines and the recommendations for the prescription of FC, there was no effort to develop a common definition of excessive bleeding in real time. The criterion based on total blood loss from chest tubes within 24 h after surgery was defined posteriori. The absence of bleeding definition contemporary to hypofibrinogenemia, may have induced some bias in patients’ classification especially during the operative period. Furthermore, prescription of FC and blood products was only very rarely based on viscoelastic tests, which were used in few centres only at this time. A more frequent use of viscoelastic tests would possibly have changed the way clinicians diagnosed haemostasis abnormalities. The lack of association between compliance with the guidelines and poor outcomes should be interpreted with caution, due to the low number of events (in-hospital deaths), and no adjustment to a mortality score (Euroscore II). Furthermore, the FibCard study was conceived on the 2017 ESAIC Guidelines, and because of COVID-19, the interpretation of the results was delayed. We nevertheless consider that our results would not have been qualitatively different if the 2022 update had been taken into consideration [21].

The major strength of the study was to evaluate ESAIC guidelines implementation, and consequences on outcomes, in several cardiac surgery centres. It is acknowledged that any guidelines development should be associated with implementation strategies and follow-up on compliance/non-compliance and their impact on outcomes [34, 35]. The study design underlines the difficulties in formulating recommendations explicitly enough so that they can be applied without ambiguity and to induce homogenous clinical decision-making [36, 37]. These findings could also be an incentive for the experts who participate in guidelines development to give clearer guidance on real-time bleeding assessment and standardized decision-support tools to optimize FC use.

In conclusion, compliance with ESAIC guidelines was high, but we observed a large inter-centre variability in FC prescription. Over-prescription was more common than under-prescription, likely due to uncertainty in bleeding assessment. Non-compliance was not associated with adverse outcomes, but our findings highlight the need for clearer clinical decision tools to facilitate the management of FC in cardiac surgical patients.
