A circRNA-miRNA-mRNA network potentially regulates skeletal muscle adaptation to hypoxia in Tibetan sheep
Authors: Pengfei Zhao, Yaqin Zhang, Jing Ma, Nanjie Dangzi, Wenlu Ma, Xiong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe extreme environmental conditions of high-altitude regions, particularly hypoxia, pose significant challenges to animal survival. Tibetan sheep serve as an exemplary model for studying mammalian hypoxic adaptation. Skeletal muscle, crucial for movement and energy metabolism, undergoes adaptive restructuring, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms, particularly the role of the competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network, remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the morphological changes in skeletal muscle across different altitudes and elucidate the potential ceRNA-mediated regulatory mechanisms.ResultsOur results revealed that Tibetan sheep from Qinghai-Tibet Plateau high-altitude (QH) regions exhibited significantly greater muscle fiber diameter and area in the biceps femoris compared to those from lower altitudes. To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms, a circRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA network was constructed, identifying several potential key regulatory axes. These included novel_circ_003862/miR-2440-z/LPL, novel_circ_015124/miR-20-y/LGR4, and novel_circ_012468/miR-2440-z/E2F6. Notably, the expression of LPL and E2F6 was upregulated, while LGR4 was downregulated in the QH group, suggesting a potential coordinated shift towards glycolytic metabolism to maintain energy supply under hypoxic stress.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that hypoxic environments may promote the adaptive remodeling of skeletal muscle fibers by modulating a specific ceRNA network. The altered expression of key genes like LPL, LGR4, and E2F6 potentially enhances glycolytic metabolism to cope with hypoxic stress. This study provides new insights into the molecular genetic basis of high-altitude adaptation in mammals, though experimental validation is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13033-x.Keywords: Tibetan Sheep, Hypoxia Adaptation, Biceps Femoris, ceRNA Network

Introduction

The extreme environmental conditions prevalent in high-altitude regions, characterized primarily by hypoxia, low temperatures, and intense ultraviolet radiation, present significant challenges to animal survival. Therefore, these regions serve as ideal natural laboratories for the study of animal adaptive evolution [1–3]. The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, often termed the “Roof of the World”, is home to a diverse array of unique species that have evolved through prolonged natural selection and domestication. Notably, Tibetan sheep represent not only a vital resource for the local animal husbandry economy but also serve as exemplary models for studying mammalian adaptation to hypoxic environments [4]. To survive and thrive in such challenging conditions, Tibetan sheep have evolved a complex array of adaptive mechanisms across various physiological, biochemical, and genetic dimensions [5–8]. As skeletal muscle constitutes approximately 40% of mammalian body mass and is fundamental for locomotion and energy metabolism [9], its adaptive restructuring is crucial for the resilience of Tibetan sheep against the environmental stresses of the plateau.

The fundamental unit of skeletal muscle is the muscle fiber [10], and adaptive changes in skeletal muscle are primarily characterized by transformations in fiber types. In ruminant species, muscle fibers are commonly classified into types I (slow-twitch oxidative), IIA (fast-twitch oxidative), and IIX (fast-twitch oxidative-glycolytic) based on distinct myosin heavy chain isoforms [11, 12]. Type I fibers are the thinnest in diameter, rich in mitochondria, and entirely dependent on aerobic metabolism, exhibiting exceptional fatigue resistance. Type IIA fibers possess both oxidative and glycolytic capacities, with intermediate contraction speed and fatigue resistance between types I and IIX. Type IIX fibers are the thickest in diameter, primarily depend on glycolysis for energy, contracting rapidly but are prone to fatigue [13]. Consequently, we hypothesize that the proportion of type IIX muscle fibers-which rely heavily on glycolysis for energy-will increase with altitude to compensate for reduced oxygen availability and maintain motor function [14]. Consequently, we hypothesize that the proportion of type IIX muscle fibers, which depend on anaerobic glycolysis, will increase to adapt to reduced oxygen levels and maintain necessary motor functions. The current study chose the biceps femoris, a predominant muscle in the hind limb essential for supporting and facilitating movement in animals, as the subject of investigation. Given the significant variations in diameter among different muscle fiber types, we can deduce alterations in the composition ratio of the biceps femoris muscle fibers through the measurement of the average diameter and cross-sectional area of the fibers [13]. Though direct molecular typing using specific markers (e.g., Myosin heavy chain isoforms) is required for definitive validation.

The conversion of muscle fiber types is a sophisticated biological process, fundamentally characterized by modifications in gene expression profiles. Apart from messenger RNAs (mRNAs) that serve as the executors of cellular functions, a substantial array of non-coding RNAs are pivotal in the post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression [15–17]. Notably, circular RNAs (circRNAs) exert their influence by sequestering specific microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby indirectly enhancing the expression of other target mRNAs associated with these miRNAs, culminating in the formation of a circRNA-miRNA-mRNA competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network [18]. Mounting evidence indicates that the ceRNA network is central to the regulation of muscle development and the transformation of muscle fiber types [19–21]. Consequently, we hypothesize that the ceRNA network is a fundamental molecular mechanism mediating the conversion of muscle fiber types within the biceps femoris of Tibetan sheep.

To date, inquiries into the hypoxia adaptability of Tibetan sheep have predominantly targeted physiological systems such as blood [22, 23], cardiovascular [24], and pulmonary [6, 25]. In contrast, there is a notable lack of research dedicated to skeletal muscle, particularly investigations that synthesize the analysis of muscle fiber morphological alterations with the ceRNA regulatory network. This study bridges this gap by employing a cohesive approach that amalgamates morphological assessment with high-throughput transcriptome sequencing, thereby elucidating the adaptive changes in the biceps femoris of Tibetan sheep across varying altitude gradients and their underlying molecular mechanisms. The findings of this research contribute to the discovery of novel mechanisms of muscle regulation in Tibetan sheep’s adaptation to high-altitudes, enhance our comprehension of the molecular genetic underpinnings of environmental adaptability in mammals, and offer potential molecular markers and a theoretical framework for the genetic enhancement of sheep breeds in high-altitude regions.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and sample acquisition

This study was designed to investigate the effects of different altitudes on the muscle characteristics of Tibetan sheep. A total of 15 three-year-old castrated male Tibetan sheep were selected from three distinct altitudinal gradients on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau: high-altitude (QH, 4500 m, Maduo County, Guoluo Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Qinghai Province, China); and middle (QM, 3500 m, Maqu County) and low-altitude (QL, 2500 m, Xiahe County), both in Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Gansu Province, China. Five clinically healthy sheep of similar body weight were randomly chosen from each altitude. All selected sheep were reared under traditional year‑round free‑range grazing conditions without artificial supplementation, and the three sites shared a similar alpine steppe ecosystem with common dominant forages [26, 27]. Sampling in July, when the temperature difference between altitudes is smaller compared to other seasons, collectively reduced the potential confounding effects of diet and temperature on muscle phenotypes. To ensure animal welfare, all sheep were slaughtered in strict accordance with industry standards. The procedure involved electrical stunning followed by immediate exsanguination to minimize animal suffering. Immediately after exsanguination, samples of the biceps femoris muscle were rapidly collected from the left side of each carcass. Each muscle sample was divided into two portions: one was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological analysis of muscle fiber diameter and area, while the other was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for transcriptome sequencing.

Histological analysis of biceps femoris muscle fibers

To evaluate the impact of altitude on the morphological characteristics of muscle fibers in Tibetan sheep, a histological analysis of the biceps femoris was conducted. The muscle tissue samples, previously fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, were rinsed under running water and then dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (75%, 85%, 95%, and 100%). Following dehydration, the tissues were cleared with xylene and embedded in paraffin to form paraffin blocks. The paraffin blocks were sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a Leica 2016 rotary microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were then mounted on glass slides and subjected to routine Hematoxylin and Eosin staining.

The stained sections were examined and imaged using a BA210 digital microscope (Motic, Xiamen, China) at 400× magnification. For quantitative analysis, the diameter and cross-sectional area of 10 intact and clearly demarcated muscle fibers were randomly measured from per sample using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). A one-way analysis of variance was conducted to compare the differences in muscle fiber diameter and cross-sectional area among the three altitude groups. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Transcriptome sequencing

To analyze the gene expression profiles, total RNA was isolated from the biceps femoris of five sheep per altitude group using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The integrity of the extracted RNA was visualized on a 1% agarose gel, while its purity and concentration were quantified with a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA Integrity Number (RIN) was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Only high-quality samples (concentration > 80 ng/µL, A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.1, and RIN > 7) were used. For circRNA analysis, ribosomal RNA was depleted using the Ribo-Zero Gold rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and cDNA libraries were constructed with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). For small RNA profiling, 18–30 nt fragments were isolated via polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, ligated with 3’ and 5’ adaptors, and reverse-transcribed to generate cDNA libraries using the NEBNext® Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Library quality was validated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and all libraries were sequenced by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Identification of circRNA

To ensure the integrity of downstream analyses, the raw sequencing data were initially processed using fastp v.0.18.0 to generate high-quality clean reads. This step involved discarding reads with adapter sequences, those containing more than 10% ambiguous nucleotides, and low-quality reads where over 50% of bases had a Q-value ≤ 20 [28]. Following this, to eliminate any residual rRNA, the clean reads were aligned to an rRNA database using Bowtie2 v.2.2.8, and all mapped reads were excluded [29]. The remaining, rRNA-depleted reads were then aligned to the reference genome (ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_016772045.2/) with HISAT2 v.2.1.1 [30]. Crucially, for circRNA discovery, we collected the reads that failed to align to the genome, as these are candidates for circular transcripts. For the identification of circRNAs, 20-mer anchor sequences were extracted from both ends of these unmapped reads and aligned back to the reference genome. Pairs of anchors aligning in a reverse, head-to-tail orientation, indicative of back-splicing, were processed with the CIRIquant tool [31]. A circRNA was considered valid if it was supported by at least two unique back-spliced reads in at least one sample.

Identification of miRNA

The analysis of small RNAs began with a stringent filtering of the raw reads to obtain clean tags. This process removed reads with low-quality bases (Q-value ≤ 20) or ambiguous nucleotides, those lacking a 3’ adapter, and any sequences containing a 5’ adapter without an intervening small RNA fragment. Additional exclusions were made for reads containing poly(A) tails and those shorter than 18 nucleotides in length [28]. To isolate miRNAs, the resulting clean tags were systematically compared against the GeneBank (Release 209.0) and Rfam (Release 11.0) databases. Any tags matching known non-miRNA small RNAs, such as rRNA, scRNA, snoRNA, snRNA, and tRNA, were discarded [32]. Furthermore, tags were aligned to the reference genome (ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0) to eliminate those originating from mRNA degradation (mapping to exons or introns) or from repetitive regions [30]. The remaining unannotated tags were then used for miRNA identification. Known miRNAs for the studied species were identified by searching the miRBase database (Release 22) [33]. For species not represented in miRBase, a cross-species alignment approach was employed to find conserved miRNAs. Finally, novel miRNA candidates were predicted from the remaining tags by mapping them to the genome and assessing their potential to form characteristic hairpin structures using the mirdeep2 software [34]. All tags were then assigned a final annotation based on a hierarchical priority system, and those that could not be classified were labeled as unannotated.

Quantification and differential expression analysis

To facilitate quantitative comparisons, normalization methods were applied to correct for variations in sequencing depth. The abundance of circRNAs was assessed by expressing back-spliced junction reads as Reads Per Million mapped reads. Similarly, expression levels for the total miRNA pool, encompassing existing, known, and novel miRNAs, were calculated and normalized to Transcripts Per Million. Subsequently, differential expression analysis for both circRNAs and miRNAs was performed using the edgeR package (v3.12.1) [35]. A circRNA or miRNA was considered significantly differentially expressed if it demonstrated a fold change of at least 2 (|log2FC| ≥ 1) with a p-value less than 0.05 in a given comparison [36].

Functional enrichment and expression pattern analysis

To investigate the functions of differentially expressed circRNAs (DEcircRNAs) and miRNAs (DEmiRNAs), we first predicted the target genes for all identified miRNAs (including existing, known, and novel) using a consensus approach with Miranda v.3.3a (http://www.microrna.org) and TargetScan v.7.0 (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_70/). To ensure high-confidence, only the overlapping predictions from both tools were retained as candidate targets. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO, http://www.geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) enrichment analyses were performed on two gene sets: the source genes of DEcircRNAs and the predicted targets of DEmiRNAs, using the cluster-Profiler R package v.4.16.0 [37–39]. Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were identified by applying Fisher’s exact test, with a significance threshold of q < 0.05 [40].

To elucidate the dynamic expression patterns of DEcircRNAs and DEmiRNAs, their expression levels were first calculated as log2FC relative to the L group. These normalized values were then subjected to clustering analysis using the Short Time-series Expression Miner software [41]. Model profiles with a p-value ≤ 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Construction of the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA network

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms governing muscle fiber type composition and transformation in Tibetan sheep across different altitudes, a ceRNA network was constructed. This analysis integrated the expression profiles of DEcircRNAs, DEmiRNAs, and our existing DEmRNAs data (the detailed expression changes of the DEmRNAs, including log2FC and p-values, are listed in Table S1) from the biceps femoris tissue. The fundamental principle was to identify circRNAs that act as ‘sponges’ for miRNAs, thereby modulating mRNA expression. First, potential circRNA-miRNA and miRNA-mRNA regulatory pairs were screened based on a significant negative correlation in their expression levels, using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (SCC < − 0.5) with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Subsequently, to establish the co-regulatory relationship between the circRNA and its target mRNA, a strong positive correlation (Pearson’s r > 0.7, p < 0.05) was required. By integrating these criteria, where a circRNA and an mRNA share a negatively correlated miRNA and are themselves positively correlated, the final circRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA axes were assembled. The resulting network was visualized using a Sankey diagram, and key hub nodes within this network were identified as candidate regulators potentially involved in the high-altitude adaptation of skeletal muscle fibers.

Results

Morphological differences in the biceps femoris muscle fibers of Tibetan sheep at different altitudes

No significant differences were found in the biceps femoris muscle fiber diameter and area between the QL and QM groups. However, both muscle fiber diameter and area in the QH group were significantly larger than those in the QL and QM groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of circRNAs

Analysis of biceps femoris tissues from Tibetan sheep at three altitudes led to the identification of a total of 69,026 circRNAs (Table S2, S3). Specifically, the QL, QM, and QH groups yielded 13,587, 15,216, and 13,739 unique circRNAs, respectively, with 14,730 circRNAs being common to all three groups (Fig. 2A). Chromosomal mapping revealed that these circRNAs were broadly distributed across the Tibetan sheep genome, with a significant enrichment observed on chromosomes 1–3 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the majority of the identified circRNAs were less than 1000 bp in length (Fig. 2C). In terms of sequence classification, circRNAs derived from annot-exons were the most prevalent, constituting 76.99% of the total, followed by exon-intron (12.86%) and intronic (3.58%) types (Fig. 2D).

Characteristics of miRNAs

Sequencing of the biceps femoris samples from the QL, QM, and QH groups yielded an average of 11,581,767, 11,185,965, and 12,731,379 raw reads, respectively (Table S4, S5). Following the removal of reads containing adapters, low-quality bases, poly(A) tails, or unknown nucleotides, an average of 10,917,712, 10,606,383, and 11,793,089 clean reads were obtained. Subsequently, 81.51%, 76.93%, and 75.68% of these clean reads were successfully mapped to the sheep reference genome for the QL, QM, and QH libraries, respectively.

Analysis of the length distribution revealed that the majority of small RNAs were between 21 and 23 nucleotides, with 22 nucleotides being the most abundant class, constituting 35.81%, 35.41%, and 35.93% of the total reads in the QL, QM, and QH groups, respectively (Fig. 3A). Principal component analysis demonstrated that samples from the same altitude tended to cluster together, suggesting that altitude is a key factor influencing the overall miRNA expression landscape (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, existing and known miRNAs formed the predominant component of the small RNA libraries for all three groups (Fig. 3C). In total, 638 miRNAs were identified in the biceps femoris tissues of Tibetan sheep across the three altitudes. Among these, 553 were co-expressed across all groups, while 9, 15, and 24 miRNAs were unique to the QL, QM, and QH groups, respectively (Fig. 3D).

Differential expression of circRNA and miRNA

To investigate the impact of altitude on non-coding RNA expression, we compared the expression profiles of circRNAs and miRNAs in the biceps femoris of Tibetan sheep. The analysis of circRNAs revealed a total of 555, 525, and 501 DEcircRNAs in the QL-vs-QM, QM-vs-QH, and QL-vs-QH comparisons, respectively (Table S6). This included 315, 261, and 288 upregulated DEcircRNAs, alongside 240, 264, and 213 downregulated DEcircRNAs (Fig. 4A-C). Similarly, the comparison of miRNA expression identified 107, 107, and 46 DEmiRNAs for the QL-vs-QM, QM-vs-QH, and QL-vs-QH groups, respectively (Table S7). Of these, 38, 76, and 36 were significantly upregulated, while 69, 31, and 10 were downregulated (Fig. 4D-F).

Construction of the circRNA-miRNA-mRNA ceRNA Network

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms involving circRNAs, we constructed a ceRNA network. First, we identified the target mRNAs and circRNAs for 181 DEmiRNAs across the three altitudinal groups. This analysis yielded 2044 miRNA-mRNA pairs involving 920 DEmRNAs and 1268 circRNA-miRNA pairs involving 1,282 DEcircRNAs, all with a SCC less than − 0.5 and a p-value < 0.05. By integrating these interaction datasets, we then filtered for high-confidence ceRNA pairs, retaining those with a Pearson’s r greater than 0.70 and a p-value < 0.05. This process resulted in a final network comprising 133 significant ceRNA pairs, which are visualized as a Sankey diagram in Fig. 8.

Discussion

This study utilized transcriptome sequencing to investigate the effects of altitude on the expression profiles of circRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs in the skeletal muscle of Tibetan sheep, aiming to correlate these molecular shifts with alterations in muscle fiber phenotype. Our results revealed that Tibetan sheep from QH regions exhibited significantly greater muscle fiber diameter and area (Fig. 1), Morphologically, type IIX muscle fibers are characterized by having the largest diameter among fiber types [13]. Therefore, the observed increase in fiber diameter and area is consistent with a shift in fiber composition towards type IIX fibers. This inference aligns with prior research indicating that hypoxic exposure induces significant remodeling of skeletal muscle, often characterized by a marked increase in the proportion of Type IIx fibers [42]. Possessing the largest diameter, these fibers primarily generate energy through glycolysis [13]. This metabolic strategy, reducing fatty acid and carbohydrate oxidation while enhancing glycolysis, appears to conserve limited oxygen under hypoxic conditions [43, 44]. Consequently, when glycolytic activity is heightened, ATP production can proceed with a corresponding decrease in the organism’s oxygen demand.

circRNAs are recognized as key regulators in the processes of skeletal muscle fiber transformation and energy metabolism [45–48]. Similarly, miRNAs exert their influence by targeting and suppressing key mRNAs crucial for myogenesis and muscle development, ultimately dictating muscle fiber type composition and growth [49–51]. Our transcriptome sequencing data revealed that high-altitude environments significantly influence the expression profiles of both circRNAs and miRNAs within the biceps femoris muscle of Tibetan sheep (Fig. 4). Functional analysis, including GO term annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment, highlighted that the DEcircRNAs were mainly implicated in muscle fiber transformation and energy metabolism. Notably, their source genes were significantly enriched in the mTOR, HIF-1, MAPK, and VEGF signaling pathways (Fig. 5). In parallel, the DEmiRNAs were found to primarily regulate muscle fiber type and growth, with their target genes enriched in a network of pathways including MAPK, Notch, mTOR, VEGF, HIF-1 and Wnt signaling pathways (Fig. 6). The MAPK signaling pathway actively facilitates muscle fiber transformation [52, 53], while the Notch signaling pathway critically regulates muscle fiber regeneration [54]. Acting as a central regulator, the mTOR pathway governs essential processes including muscle growth and energy metabolism [55–57]. In response to hypoxia, an interplay between the VEGF and HIF-1 signaling pathways promotes angiogenesis, thereby sustaining the energy and oxygen supply [58, 59]. Likewise, within these hypoxic environments, the HIF-1 pathway engages in crosstalk with the Wnt pathway, where hypoxia-induced HIF-1α modulates Wnt signaling to govern myogenesis [60, 61]. These findings suggest that the circRNAs and miRNAs identified in this study act as key players in the molecular machinery governing muscle fiber transformation and energy metabolism in response to hypoxic stress, likely through modulation of the pathways discussed.

By acting as a molecular sponge, circRNA can competitively bind with miRNA, thereby mitigating the miRNA’s inhibitory impact on its target mRNA [18]. Consequently, this research delineated a circRNA-miRNA-mRNA interaction network. The data predicted that novel_circ_003862 and novel_circ_004421 might attenuate the inhibition of LPL by interacting with miR-2440-z, miR-9985-z, and novel-m0122-5p. Similarly, novel_circ_015124, novel_circ_027921, novel_circ_043788, and novel_circ_051007 may alleviated the inhibition of LGR4 through binding with miR-20-y. Additionally, novel_circ_003862 and novel_circ_012468 may lessened the inhibition of E2F6 by interacting with miR-2440-z (Fig. 8). In this study, the expression levels of the LPL and E2F6 genes were significantly elevated in the biceps femoris of Tibetan sheep from the QH group, while the expression of the LGR4 gene was significantly reduced (Fig. 7). This expression pattern is highly significant when considering the distinct functions of these genes. Research has found that mice with overexpressed LPL in skeletal muscle accumulate triglycerides and develop insulin resistance, thereby maintaining hyperglycemia [62]. Conversely, the absence of LPL in skeletal muscle reduces lipid storage and enhances insulin signaling, leading to lower blood glucose [63]. Mouse models with LGR4 overexpression exhibit enhanced gluconeogenesis, which results in reduced blood glucose [64]. In contrast, LGR4 deficiency enhances the flexibility of skeletal muscle in response to glucose depletion, enabling it to switch its metabolic substrate from glucose to fatty acids. This suggests that LGR4 may play an adaptive regulatory role in the balance between glucose and lipid metabolism in skeletal muscle [65]. Maintaining high blood glucose, as facilitated by high LPL and low LGR4, helps to provide an ample substrate for the glycolytic reaction in the skeletal muscle of QH group sheep, ensuring a sufficient energy supply. Furthermore, E2F6 functions to regulate E2F activity and protects myocytes from apoptosis, which contributes to maintaining cellular homeostasis under extreme environmental conditions [66]. In summary, LPL, LGR4, and E2F6 may collectively maintain the glycolytic level of muscle fibers in the biceps femoris of the QH group Tibetan sheep. The aforementioned circRNA-miRNA-mRNA interaction network in which they participate may therefore play a crucial role in the molecular mechanism of hypoxic adaptation in the skeletal muscle of Tibetan sheep.

Despite these insights, several limitations must be acknowledged to objectively define the scope of our findings. First, the shift toward type IIX fibers was inferred from morphological data and literature, lacking direct molecular validation using specific markers (e.g., Myosin heavy chain immunofluorescence). Second, the constructed ceRNA network and the authenticity of novel circRNAs are based on bioinformatic predictions and sequencing data without experimental validation, such as luciferase reporter assays or Sanger sequencing. Third, this study focused exclusively on the biceps femoris, which may not fully represent the systemic adaptive mechanisms across different muscle types. Future studies employing in vitro and in vivo functional assays are essential to verify these specific regulatory axes and to comprehensively elucidate the multi-tissue ceRNA networks underlying high-altitude adaptation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that high-altitude induces significant phenotypic adaptation in the skeletal muscle of Tibetan sheep, characterized by increased muscle fiber diameter and cross-sectional area in the biceps femoris. This adaptive morphological change is potentially associated with a complex ceRNA regulatory network. Key circRNAs, such as novel_circ_003862, novel_circ_015124, and novel_circ_012468, are predicted to function as molecular sponges for specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-2440-z, miR-20-y), thereby modulating the expression of critical target genes LPL, LGR4, and E2F6. These genes may collaboratively sustain a glycolytic metabolic profile and cellular homeostasis in muscle fibers under hypoxic stress. While this research provides novel insights into the molecular underpinnings of hypoxic adaptation, subsequent functional validation is imperative to confirm these regulatory axes and to assess their utility as molecular markers for breeding livestock resilient to high-altitude stress.
