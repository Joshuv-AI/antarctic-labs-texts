Risk factors, incidence, and outcomes of bladder and ureteral cancer in lung transplant recipients: A single-center cohort study
Authors: Shoaib Ahmad, Eric C Hedberg, Vamsee Torri, George Kallingal, Sofya Tokman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung transplant recipients (LTRs) are at increased risk of genitourinary cancers (GUCs) but do not undergo routine surveillance. This study describes the cumulative incidence and clinical outcomes of GUC among LTRs.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective cohort study included all adult LTRs transplanted between 2010 and 2020 with histologically confirmed posttransplant bladder or ureteral cancer. The primary outcome was the cumulative incidence (CI) of GUC. We estimated the subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) for GUC using a competing risks proportional hazards model. Secondary outcomes, including survival after GUC diagnosis, were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test for comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. We also described patient and disease characteristics and treatments.ResultsAmong 835 LTRs, 15 developed posttransplant GUC (13 bladder and 2 ureteral); 11 were diagnosed >5 years post-LT. The CI was 1.8%. The cohort was predominantly male (73.3%) with a median age of 72.5 years (IQR 68–77.8) and substantial tobacco exposure (≥20 pack-years: 93.3%). GUC was diagnosed at a median of 75 months (IQR 52.1–103.6) posttransplant. Mortality was high (73.3% deceased at follow-up) with a median survival of 196 days after GUC diagnosis. Obstructive lung disease (OLD) was associated with a markedly higher subdistribution hazard of posttransplant GUC (sHR: 4.99 [p = 0.0058]), which persisted after multivariable adjustment for age at transplant, sex, and smoking history (adjusted sHR: 5.05 [p = 0.007]).ConclusionsGUCs were rare but clinically important late complications after LT, frequently presented with aggressive features, and were associated with poor survival. The association with OLD should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of events and potential confounding by cumulative tobacco exposure. These findings support further study of risk-adapted surveillance strategies in higher-risk LTRs.Keywords: lung transplantation, genitourinary cancer, bladder cancer, ureteral cancer, screening

Background

Lung transplantation (LT) is lifesaving for some patients with end-stage lung disease, but long-term survival among LT recipients (LTRs) is shorter than that of other solid organ recipients. Chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) remains the most common cause of death among long-term LT survivors; however, malignancy is also a major cause of late morbidity and mortality.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Genitourinary cancers (GUCs), including bladder and ureteral cancer, represent a clinically important but comparatively understudied category of post-LT cancers. Several reports on renal transplant recipients suggest that posttransplant bladder cancer may present at an advanced stage and with aggressive pathological features like higher tumor grade and muscle-invasive disease.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 In contrast to GUCs in renal transplant recipients, GUCs in LTRs, despite their higher prevalence of tobacco use, have been rarely characterized, and studies are mostly limited to case reports11 or not focused specifically on GUCs (ie, GUCs are grouped with other solid tumors) or LTRs (ie, solid organ recipients grouped together).12

There are currently no standardized screening or surveillance recommendations for GUCs in LTRs, and LT candidates are not thoroughly evaluated for underlying GUCs during the transplant workup even in cases of heavy tobacco exposure. Screening guidelines from the US Preventive Services Task Force conclude insufficient evidence to support routine screening for GUCs in asymptomatic adults, even among high-risk groups, due to low disease prevalence, limited test specificity, and an uncertain impact on meaningful clinical outcomes.13 Consequently, GUCs in LTRs are typically diagnosed after symptom-driven evaluation, which can lead to diagnosis at advanced stages. There is a need to define the burden and characteristics of GUCs in LTRs. With this study, we aimed to characterize the incidence, risk factors, clinicopathologic features, management strategies, and outcomes of GUCs in LTRs at a high-volume LT center. We also sought to identify the clinical and demographic characteristics of LTRs at the highest risk of developing GUC.

Methods

After obtaining Institutional Review Board approval (IRB#: Legacy-21-500-137-73-18; May 29, 2025), we conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study that included all adult LTRs transplanted at our institution between June 1, 2010 and August 1, 2020. Genitourinary cancer was defined as histologically confirmed bladder cancer or ureteral cancer diagnosed after LT. Demographic and clinical variables were abstracted for LTRs diagnosed with GUC and included age at diagnosis, sex, smoking history, underlying lung disease in accordance with the United Network of Organ Sharing classification (A, B, C, D), and time from LT to GUC diagnosis. Tumor characteristics and management-related data were obtained from oncology, pathology, and urology documentation and included GUC type, histologic grade, depth of invasion, presence of lymphovascular invasion, pathological stage, evidence of metastatic disease, and treatment modalities. Bladder cancers were further categorized as muscle-invasive bladder cancer (tumor stage T ≥2) and non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (tumor stage T <2).

The overall study cohort included all 835 adult LTRs and was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of posttransplant bladder and ureteral cancer. Of these, 778 had either obstructive lung disease (OLD) or restrictive lung disease (RLD) as the indication for transplantation. Because all 15 GUCs occurred among recipients with OLD or RLD, the exploratory competing-risks regression was restricted to these 778 recipients to permit comparison between the two underlying lung disease categories. Recipients transplanted for other indications were excluded from this regression analysis because no GUC events occurred in those diagnostic groups. A separate analysis was conducted among the 383 recipients from the OLD/RLD cohort who remained alive and free of urothelial cancer for more than 5 years after transplantation.

The primary outcome was the cumulative incidence (CI) of posttransplant GUC. The CI was compared among three groups based on post-LT survival time: <3 years (<1,095 days), 3–5 years (1,095–1,825 days), and >5 years (>1,825 days). A test for trend (linear-by-linear association) was also performed to evaluate for a stepwise increase in GUC incidence with increasing post-LT survival duration. The CI was further stratified by the underlying lung disease category, and proportions were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Additionally, to better understand the differences in GUC risk between RLD and OLD patients, we estimated the subdistribution hazard for GUC using a competing risks proportional hazards model for LTRs with either underlying RLD or OLD.14 For this model, the time from transplant to GUC diagnosis was the primary outcome, with death treated as a competing event and censoring at last follow-up. Analyses were performed in R using the cmprsk package.15 A competing risks approach was chosen to avoid overestimating the association between OLD and GUC risk in the presence of a substantial competing risk of death.16 The primary exposure was underlying OLD; models were adjusted for age at transplant, sex, and smoking status. We then estimated the subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) associated with OLD and other covariates.

Secondary outcomes included GUC-free survival and survival after GUC diagnosis. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate survival probabilities, and differences in survival stratified by underlying lung disease were compared using the log-rank test. Survival after bladder cancer diagnosis (n = 13) was also estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and median survival was compared between LTRs with muscle-invasive bladder cancer and those with non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer using the log-rank test. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, tumor features, treatments, and clinical outcomes after GUC diagnosis. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test.

Results

Of 835 LTRs, 15 developed posttransplant GUC, representing a CI of 1.8%. The incidence of post-LT GUC increased with longer survival after LT. The rates were 0.4% (1/263) for those surviving <3 years post-LT, 1.8% (3/165) for those surviving between 3 and 5 years, and 2.7% (11/407) for those surviving >5 years. Although the difference in proportions was not statistically significant (p = 0.087), a statistically significant linear trend was observed, showing an increasing incidence with increasing post-LT survival duration (p = 0.028). Furthermore, the difference between the shortest (<3 years) and longest (>5 years) survival strata was statistically significant (p = 0.022).

Baseline characteristics of LTRs with posttransplant GUCs are presented in Table 1. All GUC cases occurred among LTRs with underlying RLD or OLD (n = 778), with a significantly higher incidence in those with OLD: 0.8% (4/497) vs 3.9 % (11/281); p = 0.002. About half (8 [53.3%]) were receiving standard 3-drug immunosuppression at the time of GUC diagnosis, and mycophenolate was stopped in all cases to reduce the net state of immunosuppression. Among the 778 LTRs with either RLD or OLD, 72% were former smokers with a median age at transplant of 64 years (interquartile range [IQR] 58–69); 557 (72%) died without a GUC diagnosis, and 206 (26%) were censored at the end of follow-up or were lost to follow-up. The median observational time for censored/loss to follow-up patients, death (competing risk), and GUC (event of interest) was 2,500, 1,448, and 2,237 days, respectively. In the bivariate model, OLD was associated with an approximately 5-fold higher subdistribution hazard of GUC (sHR 4.99; 95% CI 1.59–15.60; p = 0.006). Female sex was associated with a lower but non-statistically significant subdistribution hazard of GUC (sHR 0.55; 95% CI 0.17–1.72; p = 0.300), whereas age at transplant was associated with a statistically significant higher hazard per year (sHR 1.06; 95% CI 1.00–1.13; p = 0.038). Former smoking status was associated with a higher but non-statistically significant hazard (sHR 5.48; 95% CI 0.73–41.29; p = 0.099; Table 2). After adjustment for age, sex, and smoking status, OLD remained associated with a more than five-fold higher subdistribution hazard of GUC (sHR 5.05; 95% CI 1.56–16.36; p = 0.007). The other covariates did not demonstrate statistically significant differences (female vs male: p = 0.230; age at transplant: p = 0.110; smoking history: p = 0.640; Table 3). Among the 383 recipients with OLD or RLD who were alive and free of GUC at the 5-year landmark, subsequent GUC occurred more frequently in recipients with OLD than in those with RLD (5.75% [8/139] vs 1.23% [3/244]; p = 0.021). Also within this group of 383 LTRs, those with OLD had shorter cancer-free survival than those with RLD, and the median time to GUC diagnosis was shorter for patients with OLD than those with RLD: 4,978 days (4,718–5,238) vs 5,365 days (5,254–5,476); p = 0.003. Log-rank testing confirmed a statistically significant difference in survival distributions between groups (p = 0.003).

Bladder cancer accounted for the majority of GUC cases (13/15, 86.7%), and ureteral cancer the minority (2/15, 13.3%). The median age at GUC diagnosis was 72.5 years (IQR 68–77.75), and most patients were men (11/15, 73.3%). Hematuria was identified in most patients (9/11 [81.8%] with a urinalysis within 6 months of GUC diagnosis). A history of heavy tobacco use was common, with 14 of 15 patients (93.3%) reporting ≥20 pack-years of smoking history before LT. Malignancies occurred late after LT, with a median interval of 75 months (IQR 52.1–103.6) from LT to GUC diagnosis, which suggests that these cancers developed after, rather than before LT. None of the LTRs with GUCs had chronic urinary catheters. BK virus, which may promote urothelial carcinogenesis through inactivation of tumor suppressor pathways,17 was evaluated in 6 LTRs, 2 of whom tested positive.

All 11 patients with OLD had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); of the 4 patients with RLD, 3 had idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and 1 had combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema. Pathologic evaluation frequently showed aggressive tumor features at presentation, with muscle invasion identified in 9 of 15 patients (60%) and high-grade histology in 13 of 15 (86.7%). Among the 12 patients with available data, lymphovascular invasion was observed in 5 (41.7%). Tumor staging was available in 14 patients: 1 patient (7.1%) had pTa disease, 5 (35.7%) had pT1 disease, and 8 (57.1%) had pT2-pT4 disease. Three patients (20%) had metastatic disease at the time of presentation. Both of the patients with ureteral cancer had advanced stage disease with muscle invasion (pT3 and pT4).

All 13 patients with bladder cancer underwent transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) for diagnostic and/or therapeutic purposes, and 4 patients (30.7%) subsequently underwent a cystoprostatectomy. Three of 13 patients (23.1%) received radiation therapy, while systemic chemotherapy was administered to 2 (15.4%) and intravesical chemotherapy to another 2 (15.4%). Of the 2 patients with ureteral cancer, 1 underwent nephroureterectomy with tumor debulking, and the other underwent ureterectomy followed by pelvic nodal radiotherapy.

At last follow-up, 11 of 15 patients (73.3%) had died. The median time from GUC diagnosis to death was 196 days (IQR 90–279). Among patients with bladder cancer (n = 13), those with muscle-invasive disease (n = 7) showed a trend toward shorter median survival compared with those with non-muscle-invasive disease (n = 6) (171 vs. 358 days, log-rank p = 0.058). Of the 11 deceased patients, 8 (72.3%) died of complications from GUC, 1 died of pancreatitis shortly after diagnosis, 1 died of pancytopenia from concurrent high-risk myelodysplastic syndrome, and the cause of death was unknown in 1 patient.

Discussion

Malignancy is a well-recognized long-term complication after LT and an important contributor to late morbidity and mortality. Prior studies have shown that LTRs carry a substantial burden of de novo malignancy due to the combined effects of chronic immunosuppression, recipient age, and cumulative carcinogenic exposures.4, 5 Furthermore, although early mortality is largely driven by infection and graft-related complications, malignancy becomes increasingly common among long-term survivors. Registry analyses from the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation demonstrate that cancer accounts for a growing proportion of deaths beyond 5 years after LT and represents a major contributor to late mortality in this population.18 In our cohort, GUCs were relatively rare, with an incidence of 1.8%. However, despite this low cumulative incidence, these cancers appeared to emerge preferentially among LTRs with underlying COPD. Furthermore, the incidence of GUCs increased as the time after LT increased, with long-term survivors of LT having the highest risk. Lastly, GUCs commonly showed aggressive tumor features, were frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, and were therefore associated with a grim prognosis with a median survival of 196 days (IQR 90–279) after diagnosis. Thus, the clinical burden of GUCs after LT should not be judged by incidence alone, but also by underlying pulmonary diagnosis, time after LT, and associated mortality.

In this cohort, the observed incidence of GUCs appears higher than that reported in prior mixed thoracic transplant populations. For example, in a study of 700 heart transplant recipients (HTRs) and LTRs, 28 GUCs were identified, representing an incidence of 4.1%; however, this estimate included a broad spectrum of GUCs—prostate, renal cell, penile, bladder, and upper tract urothelial cancers—with bladder and upper tract urothelial cancers accounting for only 3 of 28 cases.12 This reduced incidence may be related to the inclusion of both HTRs and LTRs in the analysis, rather than focusing on LTRs alone. A large meta-analysis of HTRs and LTRs showed that bladder cancer risk was significantly elevated among LTRs (standardized incidence ratio [SIR] 2.84, 95% CI 1.81–4.44), whereas an increased but non-statistically significant risk was observed in HTRs (SIR 1.86, 95% CI 0.83–4.16).1 These findings may be due to the high prevalence of cumulative tobacco exposure among LTRs, as tobacco smoking remains the strongest established risk factor for bladder cancer19 and is disproportionately common in patients undergoing LT for COPD. Moreover, bladder cancer risk demonstrates a strong dose-response relationship with tobacco exposure, with a greater pack-year history associated with progressively increased incidence.19, 20 Although we did not identify a history of smoking as a risk factor for GUC development, we suspect that cumulative tobacco exposure plays a significant role, as patients with underlying COPD typically have a history of heavy tobacco use.

There are currently no standardized screening or surveillance recommendations for GUC in LTRs. In the general population, the US Preventive Services Task Force concludes that there is insufficient evidence to support routine screening for bladder cancer in asymptomatic adults, even among individuals at increased risk, citing low disease prevalence, suboptimal test specificity, and uncertain impact on clinically meaningful outcomes.13 However, these recommendations are derived from non-immunosuppressed populations and therefore may not fully apply to LTRs, who represent a biologically distinct, high-risk group. Prior meta-analyses have demonstrated that bladder cancer risk is significantly higher among LTRs compared with the general population (standardized incidence ratio 2.84, 95% CI 1.81–4.44).1 Our findings extend these observations by describing the clinicopathologic characteristics, timing, and outcomes of bladder and ureteral cancers in a contemporary lung transplant cohort.

Notably, in a prospective hematuria screening study of older men at elevated risk for bladder cancer, Messing and colleagues21 demonstrated that screening enabled detection at earlier, non–muscle-invasive stages and was associated with a significant reduction in bladder cancer-specific mortality compared with symptom-based diagnosis. Long-term follow-up of this cohort confirmed a sustained stage shift and durable mortality benefit. Together, these data raise the possibility that earlier detection strategies may be particularly relevant in selected high-risk populations, such as LTRs.

In response to the reported findings, our LT program has incorporated a targeted GUC surveillance program with an annual urinalysis and urine cytology of long-term survivors of LT with underlying COPD or RLD with heavy tobacco use. Abnormal surveillance findings then prompt formal urologic evaluation (Figure 2). The proposed approach aligns with the microhematuria guidelines of the American Urological Association, which recommend confirming microscopic hematuria and referring high-risk patients for cystoscopy and further evaluation;22 however, we acknowledge that urine cytology has limited sensitivity for low-grade tumors and that hematuria is nonspecific. These screening recommendations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, as our findings do not establish the diagnostic yield, cost-effectiveness, or survival benefit of annual urinalysis and urine cytology-based surveillance for GUC after LT. Nevertheless, they identify a clinically meaningful at-risk phenotype and suggest that the absence of a standardized GUC surveillance strategy may represent an unmet need in long-term LT follow-up.

Limitations

This is a retrospective, single-center study with a small number of genitourinary malignancies. As such, there are several limitations that must be noted. First, the generalizability of the findings to other centers and transplant populations is limited. Second, we lack the ability to perform risk modeling with additional covariates and have limited precision of survival model estimates. Third, in addition to the negative impact of late diagnosis, treatment may have been limited by comorbidities, which further contributed to poor outcomes. Fourth, smoking and exposure histories were obtained by patient report within the medical record and may be subject to recall bias. For the regression model, smoking was primarily modeled as a binary variable, which does not capture cumulative tobacco exposure or time since cessation. Fifth, the association between underlying COPD and GUCs may be a reflection of cumulative tobacco exposure; thus, LTRs with RLD and extensive smoking history may also benefit from screening. Finally, the proposed screening/surveillance pathway has not yet been prospectively evaluated, and our data do not support discontinuing surveillance after a predefined interval, both of which need to be validated in larger, multicenter cohorts. Taken together, our results are suggestive and not a final word on these associations. Despite these limitations, we provide the information in this study as it is not widely reported in other studies. We hope future centers will publish additional similar studies and comparisons that can be combined with our estimates in a meta-analysis to provide a robust set of results and guidance.

Conclusion

In this single-center cohort, posttransplant bladder and ureteral cancers were rare but clinically important late complications after LT. These malignancies frequently presented with aggressive pathologic features and were associated with poor post-diagnosis survival, particularly among patients with muscle-invasive disease. Symptom-driven detection identified advanced rather than early-stage disease, suggesting the need for risk-adapted screening and surveillance strategies that may facilitate earlier diagnosis during potentially curable stages among higher-risk recipients. Although GUC was more frequently observed among recipients with underlying OLD/COPD, this association should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of events and potential residual confounding by cumulative tobacco exposure. These findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish an optimal screening strategy. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate risk-adapted surveillance approaches and determine whether earlier detection improves stage at diagnosis or survival in LTRs.
