Knowledge, attitudes, practice and factors associated with the prevalence of modern contraceptives use among women from historically marginalized community in Rwanda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jean Bosco Henri Hitayezu, Ronah Uwamahoro, Marie Laetitia Ishimwe Bazakare, Zephanie Nzeyimana, Gerard Kaberuka, Vital Mutijima, Jean Damascene Twagirumukiza, Justine Bagirisano, Gallican Rwibasira, Tafadzwa Dzinamarira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of modern contraceptive methods can reduce up to 68% of unintended pregnancies and significantly lower maternal deaths. In Rwanda, modern contraceptive use has increased from 10% to 58%. Historically marginalized people (HMPs), who make up 0.3% of the population, have faced persistent social exclusion, limited economic opportunities, and inadequate access to education and healthcare. Despite national efforts to expand family planning services, HMPs continue to experience high unmet needs, lower educational attainment, and restricted access to reproductive health services. However, data on modern contraceptive use among this group remains limited. This study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and factors influencing the use of modern contraceptives among marginalized women in Rwanda.MethodsThis was a quantitative cross-sectional study conducted in 5 districts of Rwanda. Multistage sampling was used to select 385 respondents- women of reproductive age (15–49) living in marginalized communities. Data was collected using a researcher administered structured questionnaire that was developed and pretested. Date was analyzed using STATA version 18. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square test and logistic regression were used during data analysis.ResultsAmong the 385 participants, 89.1% had poor knowledge of modern contraception, though 80% demonstrated positive attitudes toward its use. The overall usage rate was 67.9%, with implants being the most commonly used method (61.6%). Multivariable logistic regression revealed that Women aged 15–24 were 4.4 times more likely to use modern contraception compared to those aged 35 and above (AOR = 4.38; 95% CI: 1.67–11.53; p = 0.003). Similarly, women aged 25–34 were nearly 3 times more likely to use modern contraception than those aged 35 and above (AOR = 2.96; 95% CI: 1.57–6.57; p = 0.008). Married women had a 3.2 times higher likelihood of using modern contraception compared to cohabiting women (AOR = 3.20; 95% CI: 1.45–7.06; p = 0.004). Divorced or separated women were also significantly more likely to use modern contraception (AOR = 5.46; 95% CI: 2.00–14.88; p = 0.001), and those with prior modern contraceptive use (AOR = 4.88; 95% CI: 2.42–9.81; p < 0.001). Additionally, women who had discussed family planning with their partners were nearly three times more likely to use it (AOR = 2.98; 95% CI: 1.42–6.25; p = 0.004).ConclusionThe community of the historically marginalized people has limited knowledge of modern contraception surprisingly with high prevalence of modern contraceptive methods compared to indigenous people from other regions. Therefore, there is a need of consistently promoting Sexual and reproductive health education in this community more especially among adolescents and young adult women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25179-6.Keywords: Modern contraception methods, Historically marginalized people

Background

Globally, around 40% of women experience unintended pregnancies—a burden that modern contraceptives can significantly reduce [1]. Contraceptive use is crucial in lowering fertility rates and improving the physical and economic well-being of women and their families. Of the 1.9 billion women of reproductive age in 2021, 1.1 billion required family planning; 874 million used modern methods, while 164 million had unmet needs. In 2022, global contraceptive prevalence among married women was 65% for any method and 58.7% for modern methods [2]. Regions such as South Asia, Southeast Asia, and parts of Africa report high unmet needs and lower modern contraceptive use. For example, usage rates were 29.6% in Sub-Saharan Africa [3] and 45.7% in East Africa [3] and 45.7% in East Africa [4]. In Rwanda, usage increased from 10% in 2005 to 58% in 2020 [5]. Expanding access to contraception could prevent up to 72% of unsafe abortions, reduce 68% of unintended pregnancies, and significantly lower maternal deaths [6].

The use of scientifically proven methods to prevent pregnancy—such as hormonal methods (pills, injectables, implants), intrauterine copper devices (IUCDs), levonorgestrel intrauterine devices (IUDs), barrier methods (male and female condoms), sterilization, and emergency contraception—is referred to as modern contraceptive use [7, 8]. Modern contraceptive methods are more effective and reliable than traditional methods such as withdrawal or calendar-based approaches, which have higher failure rates. In addition, modern methods allow couples to engage in sexual intercourse at any mutually desired time [7, 9]. Modern contraceptive use plays a key role in ensuring universal access to sexual and reproductive health and rights, which is essential for achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 and 5—promoting health and well-being for all and achieving gender equality [10]. The World Health Organization states that protecting reproductive rights and reducing unmet family planning needs depend on access to a wide variety of modern contraceptive options [8].

The use of modern contraceptives is influenced by a range of factors. A study conducted in Senegal [11] identified key determinants including a woman’s age, educational level, her husband’s education, number of living children, ideal family size, fertility preferences, ethnicity, media exposure, wealth status, and decision-making power. Similarly, a study covering 23 countries in Latin America and the Caribbean found that modern contraceptive use was below 50% in three countries, with the lowest rates among young women aged 15–17, indigenous groups, women from poorer households, those living in rural areas, and individuals without formal education [12]. In Ethiopia, modern contraceptive prevalence was significantly lower among indigenous women (11.4%) compared to non-indigenous women (26.5%), with contributing factors including low education and Somali ethnicity [13, 14]. In Rwanda, contraceptive use is most common among women with three to four children (66%), followed by those with one to two children (62%), and lowest among women with five or more children (52%) [5]. Key influencing factors include level of education, number of children, and awareness of family planning. This study focuses on Rwanda’s Historically Marginalized Populations (HMPs), who continue to experience social exclusion, poverty, and limited access to reproductive health services [5, 15, 16]. Despite government initiatives such as free health insurance, challenges persist due to low awareness and a lack of reliable data on the health status of HMPs, making it difficult to effectively evaluate and meet their contraceptive needs [17].

In Rwanda, modern contraceptive use increased significantly from 10% in 2005 to 58% in 2020 [5, 15, 16]. Interestingly, usage is slightly higher in rural areas (65%) than in urban areas (61%), with regional variation among married women—from 54% in the Western Province to 65% in the Northern Province [5]. The Family Planning and Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health (FP-ASRH) Strategic Plan 2018–2024 aims to reach a 60% modern contraceptive prevalence rate by 2024, focusing on improving population health through expanded service access and increased uptake [18]. This plan addresses Rwanda’s demographic and health needs through expanding service availability, enhancing access, and increasing the uptake of family planning. Key strategies include scaling up health facilities, training more providers, and diversifying contraceptive options, including emergency contraception, to reduce unmet needs [16].

Prior to the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi, Rwandans were classified as Hutu, Tutsi, or Twa. After the genocide, the government implemented a de-ethnicization policy, replacing terms like “Batwa” or “indigenous people” with “Historically Marginalized People” (HMP) [19, 20]. HMPs make up about 0.3% of Rwanda’s population (approximately 30,000 to 35,000) and are mainly found in remote rural areas, living in isolated communities with poor infrastructure and housing. Historically engaged in pottery and endogamous marriages, their marginalization is rooted in longstanding poverty, cultural discrimination, and exclusion from national development. As a result, HMPs continue to face social exclusion, limited economic opportunities, low educational attainment, and inadequate access to healthcare including high unmet family planning needs and restricted access to reproductive health services [20].

Although the importance of modern contraceptives is well established, little research has focused on the knowledge, attitudes, and usage of these methods among women from Rwanda’s Historically Marginalized Communities (HMP). This study seeks to assess their Knowledge, Attitude, practice and factors association of use of modern contraceptives. The findings aim to inform strategies for reducing reproductive health disparities among marginalized and indigenous populations in Rwanda, sub-Saharan Africa, and beyond—supporting efforts toward social inclusion and universal health coverage.

Methods

Study design

This research employed quantitative methods and utilized a cross-sectional design from August to September, 2022.

Study setting

The research was carried out across five districts in Rwanda: Rwamagana (Eastern Province), Kamonyi (Southern Province), Musanze (Northern Province), Rubavu (Western Province), and Gasabo (Kigali City). These districts were purposively selected to ensure national representation by including one district from each of Rwanda’s five provinces. Gasabo represents an urban setting within Kigali City, while the other four districts represent rural areas. The selection was informed by consultations with local leaders and stakeholders, who identified these districts as having sizeable populations of historically marginalized communities the focus group of this study.

Study population

The study population consisted of women of reproductive age (15–49 years) from historically marginalized communities residing in the selected districts. Women with mental disabilities or those unwilling to participate were excluded. This was a nationwide study focusing on this specific population group, rather than a general population survey, as the aim was to assess reproductive health indicators among a population with known disparities in access to family planning services.

Sampling technique

A multi-stage cluster sampling technique was employed to select participants for the study. In the first step, five districts were selected from five provinces using purposive sampling. In the second step, one sector was purposively selected from each district. In the third stage, one cell was selected from each chosen sector using the same method. At the cell level, the researchers met with the Cell Leader and were connected to a representative of the Historically Marginalized Population (HMP), who helped mobilize participants. The sample size from each cell was determined using convenience sampling, and Historically Marginalized women aged 15–49 were selected through consecutive sampling (Table 1).

Instrument and data collection

A structured interviewer administered tool was designed both in English and mother tongue and administered by research team who equally understand the dominant language in the study districts to enable them explain the questionnaire to illiterates and semi-literate participants. The tool was developed by a panel of 8 researcher team with subject matter experts in reproductive health, midwives, and public health from university of Rwanda, Rwanda Biomedical Center, UN agencies and health facilities. Ratings from experts were statistically analyzed to determine content validity. We conducted a pilot test of the study tool on 30 women historically marginalized in reproductive age recruited from another districted to correct items (knowledge, use, and attitude related to modern contraceptives), the reliability coefficient was 0.85, and content validity index was 0.94 where each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1: not relevant to 4 equal highly relevant). The study tool gathered data on the socio-demographic characteristics of study participants, knowledge, attitude, and factors associated with use of modern contraceptive methods, Data quality was endured through onsite supervision, spot-checks, and back -checks of collected data.

Statistical data analysis

Data collected using Kobo Toolbox were exported to Excel for cleaning to ensure accuracy, completeness, and consistency. Missing data were identified and either removed or assigned reasonable values, while duplicates were eliminated based on study criteria. Inconsistent formats were standardized, and outliers were filtered out to maintain data integrity. The cleaned dataset was analyzed using STATA version 18. Descriptive statistics were presented in tables using frequencies and percentages. Crosstabulations and Chi-square tests, including the Chi-square test for trend, were conducted to examine relationships between independent variables and modern contraceptive use. Variables with p-values < 0.05 in the bivariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and results were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Units of measurement are detailed in the corresponding tables.

Ethical considerations

Prior to the study, ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at the University of Rwanda (Reference number: 332/CHMS IRB/2022). while the permissions to carry the study were secured from selected sites’ district offices. Participants voluntarily signed informed consent forms. To ensure participants’ privacy and security, all data were collected anonymously. No names were recorded on mobile data collection forms or devices, and the results were presented in aggregate, ensuring that individual identities could not be traced.

Awareness of specific contraceptive methods varied among respondents. The majority, 228 (59.2%), were aware that condoms can prevent unwanted pregnancies, while 265 (69%) understood that pills serve this purpose. However, knowledge of where to obtain modern contraception showed some disparities. A large proportion, 335 (87%), knew they could access it from health facilities (HFs), whereas fewer, 20 (5%), were aware they could obtain it from pharmacies, and only 3 (1%) knew that youth centers provided contraception.

In terms of the benefits of modern contraception, 283 (73.5%) recognized its positive impact on maternal health, while 100 (26%) were aware of its role in national development. Awareness of side effects such as vaginal spotting (205, 53.3%) and headache (115, 29.9%) was common, but the majority, 290 (75.3%) and 270 (70.1%), did not recognize backache and nausea as side effects.

The sources of information about modern contraception were predominantly health facilities (294, 76.4%) and community health workers (210, 54.6%). In contrast, television (7, 1.8%) and social media (14, 3.6%) had minimal influence. Regarding discussions about family planning with a sexual partner, 247 (65.9%) reported having such conversations, while 138 (34.1%) did not.

When it comes to decision-making responsibility regarding contraception, 204 (52.9%) believed it should be an individual responsibility, while 141 (36.6%) felt it should be a mutual decision. Regarding the timing of starting modern contraception, 242 (62.9%) thought it should begin once married and starting a family, compared to 123 (32.0%) who believed it should start once sexually active. Only 19 (4.9%) considered it essential to start contraception when having sex with an HIV-positive partner. The majority 343(89.1%) of respondents had poor knowledge towards modern contraception while 42(10.9%) of respondents had good knowledge.

Distribution of attitude towards modern contraception among respondents

When asked about the impact of family planning methods on sexual pleasure, 51 (13.3%) of respondents strongly agreed that family planning reduces sexual pleasure, while a higher proportion, 102 (26.5%), strongly disagreed. Regarding the statement that contraceptives limit family growth, 129 (33.5%) strongly disagreed, whereas 39 (10.1%) strongly agreed.

On the topic of decision-making power in family planning, the majority of respondents (164, 42.8%) strongly disagreed that men should own the decision-making capacity in family planning, while only 19 (4.9%) strongly agreed with this statement. Similarly, when asked whether men cheat on their wives when using contraceptives, the majority (122, 31.7%) strongly disagreed, while 38 (9.9%) strongly agreed.

In terms of perceptions about the negative side effects and cost of modern contraceptives, 156 (40.5%) strongly disagreed that contraceptives have negative side effects and are costly, while 20 (5.2%) strongly agreed with the statement. Regarding the belief that family planning is for women only, the majority (159, 41.3%) strongly disagreed, while 22 (5.7%) strongly agreed.

On the involvement of men in family planning, 168 (43.6%) agreed that men should be mobilized for family planning, whereas 53 (13.8%) strongly disagreed. Finally, when asked about the impact of modern contraceptive methods on infertility, 127 (32.9%) strongly disagreed, but an equal number, 127 (32.9%), strongly agreed that modern contraceptive methods cause infertility. the majority 308(80.0%) of respondents had good attitudes towards modern contraception while 77(20.0%) of respondents had poor attitudes.

Factors associated with use of modern contraception among n(385) respondents

Discussion

This study aimed at assessing the knowledge, attitudes and factors associated with the use of modern contraceptive methods use among women of the reproductive age from the historically marginalized communities of Rwanda. The study found that 67.9% of historically marginalized women use modern contraceptives, with implants being the most common method (61.6%), while indigenous people from other countries like Ecuador have a low prevalence of 19% [21, 22] and 33% from Guatemala [23]. These findings indicate that a lot has been done in the government of Rwanda to increase community engagement and accessibility to family planning services; through removing economic barriers in accessing services, health education at the health facility level and decentralization of family planning services to the community level through community health worker’s model.

The high prevalence of modern contraceptive use may be attributed to women’s exposure to family planning information, with 75% citing health facilities and 72.3% citing community health workers as their primary sources.

This comes in response to Rwanda’s commitment to reducing maternal and child mortality and move forward the ICPD25 agenda to achieve SDGs and 2063 Agenda [24]. The finding that 89.1% of respondents had poor knowledge of modern contraceptive methods may be attributed to limited access to reliable information on family planning. Inconsistent dissemination of health information at the community level and inadequate media engagement likely contribute to these knowledge gaps. Similar findings were reported in a study among indigenous women in Bolivia and Guatemala, where low levels of contraceptive knowledge were observed [25]. In contrast, a study in Thailand found that over 90% of women demonstrated good knowledge of contraceptives, possibly reflecting more consistent and widespread health education efforts in that context [21, 26]. The poor knowledge could be explained by the inconsistency of health information dissemination at the community level and media engagement. Rwanda has incorporated community health workers into providing family planning (FP) services, expecting them to be well-trained and knowledgeable about modern contraceptives. Their role involves guiding community members towards informed reproductive choices, fostering understanding of modern contraceptive methods.

Despite the community’s generally limited knowledge about modern contraception, the study revealed that the majority (80%) expressed positive attitudes toward it. This suggests that increased investment in health education for this community regarding the significance of family planning and dispelling misconceptions about modern contraceptives could significantly aid Rwanda’s goal of reducing unmet FP needs and enhancing the uptake of modern contraception among both women and men of reproductive age.

This study found a statistically significant association between respondents’ age and current use of modern contraceptives, with women aged 15–24 being 4.4 times more likely to use modern contraception compared to those aged 35 and older. This contradicts findings from a study among indigenous Māori mothers in New Zealand, where younger women faced barriers such as financial constraints, lack of information, and limited suitable contraceptive options [25]. However, in the sub-Saharan African context, variations in age-related contraceptive use have also been observed. For instance, an Ethiopian study using the 2019 mini-EDHS data reported that women aged 25–34 and 35–44 years were significantly more likely to use modern contraceptives compared to younger women (aRRR = 1.5 and aRRR = 2.4, respectively) [27]. In the context of Rwanda, the higher uptake among younger women may reflect successful national efforts to engage adolescents and young adults in sexual and reproductive health programs, including youth-friendly services and peer education initiatives targeting this age group. However, challenges such as confidentiality concerns and social stigma remain prevalent for adolescent girls and young women [28].

Marital status was also significantly associated with modern contraceptive use. Married women were more likely to use contraceptives than their unmarried counterparts, possibly due to higher perceived need for pregnancy spacing and access to family planning services within marriage. This aligns with findings from Botswana [29]; and with the study among indigenous and non-indigenous women in Jigjiga Town, Ethiopia, which showed that indigenous women with lower educational attainment and cultural constraints were less likely to use contraceptives [13]. Prior use of modern contraceptive methods emerged as another key factor. Women who had previously used contraceptives were more likely to be current users, suggesting that initial exposure and positive experiences with family planning services promote continued use. This underscores the value of strengthening follow-up counseling and ensuring quality of care during first-time contraceptive provision to foster long-term adherence.

Additionally, women who had ever discussed family planning with their sexual partners demonstrated higher likelihood of contraceptive use. Partner communication plays a critical role in joint decision-making and overcoming cultural and relational barriers to contraceptive adoption. Programs that encourage male partner involvement and couple-based counseling may therefore enhance contraceptive uptake and sustained use.

Limitation to the study

Cross-sectional design: The study employed a cross-sectional design, which captures data at a single point in time. This limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables, as it only provides a snapshot of contraceptive knowledge and practices without tracking changes over time.

Geographic limitation: Although the study was conducted in five districts across Rwanda, including both urban and rural areas, it may not fully represent the entire population of marginalized women. Other districts not included in the study could have different levels of knowledge, attitudes, and access to contraception, which could affect the generalizability of the findings.

Sampling limitation: The adjusted sample size accounting for design effect and non-response should have been 633, but due to resource constraints, the study proceeded with 385 participants. This may have reduced the precision of findings, underscoring the need for future studies to account for clustering in sample size calculations.

Limited qualitative insights: The study used quantitative methods and lacked a qualitative component. Including qualitative interviews or focus group discussions could have provided deeper insights into the attitudes, beliefs, and barriers that influence contraceptive use among marginalized women.

Conclusions

Surprisingly, compared to indigenous people from other locations, women from the historically marginalized population had a high use of modern contraceptive methods but little understanding about them. Additionally, the study’s findings showed that young women between the ages of 15 and 24 were more likely to use modern contraceptives as they have a higher risk of becoming pregnant, indicating a good progress in family planning program implementation. As a result, the authors recommend advise promoting family planning programs and comprehensive sexual and reproductive health education in this community, particularly among older women from the age of 25.
