Introduction

Without timely and appropriate treatment, hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection can progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), contributing significantly to global liver-related morbidity and mortality.1 Africa carries the highest global HBV burden, with an estimated 65 million people infected in 2022, and 25% are under 20 years of age.2 While the regional average prevalence is 5.8%,1 18 African countries have exceeded 8% HBV prevalence. 3 Vertical and early childhood HBV transmission are common, carrying chronicity risks of up to 90% and 30%–60%, respectively, and can lead to severe complications such as cirrhosis and HCC. Survival outcomes for HCC are particularly poor in Africa, averaging only 1.5 months post-diagnosis.2

Despite the availability of neonatal prophylaxis, including antivirals in pregnancy and birth-dose vaccination, significant gaps persist along the HBV care cascade, particularly in Africa. Screening uptake in pregnancy is low, linkage to care and treatment initiation is inadequate and follow-up remains poor.4 5 Despite recommendations from the WHO for all countries to provide birth-dose vaccination,6 coverage continues to be very low at 18% in WHO African countries. 1 4 Strengthening the perinatal HBV care cascade is important for achieving the WHO triple elimination initiative, which aims to eliminate the vertical transmission of HIV, syphilis and HBV.7

The HBV perinatal cascade of care

To accelerate progress towards elimination targets, the WHO recommends routine screening of every pregnancy for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), with subsequent HBV DNA. Those with HBV DNA levels above 200 000 IU/mL should initiate antiviral therapy.8 However, as HBV DNA testing is often unavailable in Africa, there was a recent shift, and the WHO now recommends antiviral prophylaxis with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) for all HBsAg-positive pregnant individuals from the second trimester until delivery or completion of the infant’s vaccination series.4

In many African countries, perinatal HBV care is provided primarily by physician specialists.4 However, strengthening prevention efforts requires empowering non-specialist providers, especially other perinatal providers, such as midwives, to take on expanded roles. These include ordering and interpreting laboratory tests, initiating antiviral therapy, providing newborn vaccination and ensuring postpartum follow-up.4 9 Clarifying midwives’ clinical authority within national health systems will be essential to ensure they can initiate these expanded roles without undue physician gatekeeping.

Midwife-led HBV care

Midwives are key skilled birth attendants in sub-Saharan Africa, assisting around 43% of all births, which accounts for most of the 64% attended by skilled personnel. 10For many years, midwives and nurses have been integrated into HIV-related perinatal care in the region.9 11 Not extending a similar empowerment model from HIV care to HBV care is a missed opportunity, especially in the context of the simplified WHO HBV guidelines, which are intended to be used by non-specialists to drive the expansion of care. Although such models remain uncommon, evidence from settings, such as Mozambique, demonstrates their feasibility and effectiveness. In this context, trained nurses and midwives are able to lead HBV care, delivering screening, counselling, antiviral initiation, vaccination and follow-up within routine antenatal and maternity services, without reliance on physician specialists, by integrating it into existing HIV and syphilis vertical transmission prevention structures.9 Building on their established role, midwives could extend these protocols to include HBV screening, maternal counselling and timely birth-dose vaccination.

A multi-country assessment of HBV birth dose implementation across five African countries found that midwives did not require physician authorisation to administer the vaccine, highlighting the feasibility of empowering trained and mandated midwives to deliver services across the entire perinatal HBV care cascade.12

While the administration of hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG) is still recommended as part of comprehensive neonatal prophylaxis, its cost, procurement and storage are difficult in many settings.6 In response to these constraints, the WHO’s recommendation on antiviral prophylaxis in pregnancy emphasises the effectiveness of TDF, with emerging evidence from selected settings, including the Democratic Republic of the Congo, demonstrating high protective efficacy when TDF is combined with the infant HBV birth-dose vaccine.4 Further strengthening this approach, a recent randomised controlled trial showed that initiating TDF at 16 weeks’ gestation alongside infant HBV vaccination is non-inferior to initiating at 28 weeks with HBIG and infant vaccination in preventing vertical transmission.13 Collectively, this evidence supports a simplified and feasible prevention pathway in which empowering midwives to deliver HBV care through existing HIV and syphilis vertical transmission prevention services9 could substantially reduce vertical transmission and advance the triple elimination strategy in Africa.

Barriers and opportunities

Despite the clear potential for midwives to play a central role in the prevention and management of HBV infection during pregnancy in Africa, multiple barriers hinder midwives’ engagement. Poor coordination across different levels of care continues to undermine continuity and consistency in HBV service delivery, limiting the ability of pregnant women to receive timely care.12 14 Limited integration of HBV care within routine antenatal care (ANC) services contributes to delays in diagnosis and intervention.12 14 These challenges are further compounded by persistent shortages of essential resources, including diagnostic test kits, antiviral medications and HBIG for newborns, which collectively restrict midwives’ ability to provide comprehensive and reliable HBV care in many settings.14 Midwives follow established HIV vertical transmission prevention protocols, but similar standardised HBV guidelines are often absent in ANC settings.14

In addition to health system constraints, limited access to HBV-specific education and in-service training represents a critical barrier affecting midwives’ engagement in HBV care. Many midwives receive minimal formal training on HBV during their pre-service education, and opportunities for continuing professional development focused on HBV screening, interpretation of test results, clinical decision-making and counselling are often limited or absent. As a result, gaps in knowledge and low confidence in managing HBV cases may lead to delays in screening and inconsistent counselling practices, which in turn reduce the timeliness and quality of care provided to pregnant women.12 14 15

Furthermore, standardised and operationalised guidelines for the prevention and management of HBV in pregnancy are frequently absent, outdated or inconsistently implemented within ANC settings across many African contexts. Where national or international guidelines exist, they are often not effectively disseminated to frontline maternity care providers, or they lack clear operational guidance tailored to midwifery practice. This absence of practical, context-specific protocols constrains midwives’ ability to make informed clinical decisions and undermines consistency in care delivery across facilities, further limiting their capacity to deliver timely and effective HBV interventions.12 14 Evidence from practice suggests that simple operational tools can partially mitigate these challenges. For example, a private hospital in Nigeria that achieved high HBV birth dose coverage introduced a checklist to be completed by maternity ward staff prior to discharge from the labour ward. This structured reminder supported staff to consistently administer the birth-dose vaccine and may contribute to improved HBV birth dose coverage.12

Strengthening midwives’ knowledge and skills in HBV management may also play a crucial role in addressing sociocultural barriers that impede the uptake of preventive services. Factors such as stigma surrounding HBV infection, prevailing traditional beliefs and a preference for home births often discourage pregnant women from accessing facility-based care or adhering to recommended interventions.12 14

In addition to addressing sociocultural challenges, midwives can actively mitigate system-level barriers that compromise timely HBV prevention and management. Issues such as vaccine stock-outs, inadequate staffing and limited access to essential supplies often disrupt routine antenatal services and birth interventions.12 14 15 Despite these challenges, midwives play a critical role in maintaining continuity of care through activities such as monitoring supply chains, mentoring auxiliary staff and coordinating community or facility-based outreach. Such practices have been shown to support timely access to HBV screening, prophylaxis and vaccination, particularly in rural settings, by ensuring that infants born outside health facilities or during off-hours receive the HBV birth dose within the recommended 24-hour window.12 14 Expanding midwives’ roles may increase workload in human resource-constrained settings.12 However, this can be mitigated through targeted training, integration of HBV services into existing ANC programmes, supported by strengthened health system infrastructure and workforce planning9 12 and adequate remuneration to compensate for the additional responsibilities.

Conclusions

Prevention of vertical transmission of HBV in Africa requires a strengthened, integrated care cascade. Midwives are uniquely positioned to lead this effort. Empowering midwives with knowledge, resources and autonomy to apply evidence-based WHO HBV guidelines can bridge key service gaps and represent a practical and essential pathway towards preventing paediatric HBV infection in Africa. To ensure consistent delivery of perinatal HBV services, policymakers will need to provide stable and sustainable funding through a combination of government budgets, national insurance schemes and coordinated donor support. Reliable funding will enable midwives to provide antiviral treatment, birth-dose vaccination and follow-up care without interruption, reducing service gaps and improving maternal and newborn outcomes. Future research should consider the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of midwife-led HBV prevention models integrated within existing perinatal programmes across diverse African settings.
