Complexity theory of orbit closure intersection for tensors: reductions, completeness, and graph isomorphism hardness
Vladimir Lysikov, Michael Walter

Abstract
A wide range of natural computational problems in computer science, mathematics, physics, and other sciences amounts to deciding if two objects are equivalent. Very often this equivalence is defined in terms of group actions. A natural question is to ask when two objects can be distinguished by polynomial functions that are invariant under the group action . For finite groups, this is just the usual notion of equivalence, but for continuous groups such as the general linear groups it gives rise to a new notion, called orbit closure intersection . This new notion has recently seen substantial interest in the community, as it captures, among others, the graph isomorphism problem, noncommutative polynomial identity testing, null cone problems in invariant theory, equivalence problems for tensor networks, and the classification of multiparty quantum states. Despite remarkable recent algorithmic progress in celebrated special cases, the computational complexity of general orbit closure intersection problems is currently quite unclear. In particular, tensors seem to give rise to the most difficult problems. In this work we start a systematic study of orbit closure intersection problems from the complexity-theoretic viewpoint. Our key contributions include: • We define a complexity class TOCI that captures the power of orbit closure intersection problems for general tensor actions. • We give an appropriate notion of algebraic reductions that imply polynomial-time reductions in the usual sense, but are amenable to invariant-theoretic techniques. • We identify several natural tensor problems that are complete for TOCI , one of which is the equivalence of PEPS tensor networks considered by Acuaviva et al (FOCS’23). • We show that the graph isomorphism problem can be reduced to these complete problems and hence GI ⊆ TOCI \textnormal{{GI}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}} . As such, our work establishes the first lower bound on the computational complexity of orbit closure intersection problems, and it explains the difficulty of finding unconditional polynomial-time algorithms beyond special cases, as has been observed in the recent literature.

Introduction

A common theme in computer science, mathematics, and many other sciences is to consider objects or structures up to some notion of “isomorphism” or “equivalence”.
Very often the equivalence is defined by describing possible transformations of an object into an equivalent one.
Examples are ubiquitous:
graphs can be considered up to graph isomorphism;
curves, surfaces, and manifolds in general are considered up to homeomorphisms or diffeomorphism;
algebraic structures such as groups or rings always come with the corresponding notion of isomorphism;
subgroups in a given group can be described up to isomorphism or up to conjugacy;
for matrices and matrix spaces we have notions of similarity and conjugacy;
quantum states shared by multiple parties are considered up to operations performed locally by these parties;
etc.

Each such notion motivates a corresponding computational problem: decide if two given objects are indeed equivalent.
The most famous problem of this kind is the graph isomorphism problem.
This problem is known to lie in
\textnormal{{NP}}\cap\mathrm{co}\textnormal{{AM}}
, but for many years resisted attempts to find a polynomial-time solution (currently asymptotically best algorithm for graph isomorphism is the recent quasipolynomial algorithm of Babai
[
1
]
, see also
[
2
]
).
It is conjectured to be an
NP
-intermediate problem (although Babai’s algorithm casts some doubt on this conjecture).
Many isomorphism problems for various discrete structures and finite algebraic structures (for example, finite groups represented by their Cayley tables) reduce to graph isomorphism.
In fact, the isomorphism problem for general finite algebraic structures with operations and relations given by listing all values is equivalent to graph isomorphism
[
3
, §15]
.
This leads to the introduction of the complexity class
GI
, which contains all problems polynomial-time reducible to graph isomorphism.

Group actions and notions of equivalence

Isomorphism problems can often be stated in terms of group actions which describe allowed equivalence transformations, and indeed this is the case for all examples mentioned above.
For example, suppose we are given a pair of graphs with the vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
(it is trivial to check if the graphs have the same number of vertices).
The symmetric group
\mathsf{S}_{n}
, which consists of the bijections of
[n]
, acts on the set of graphs with vertex set
[n]
.
Then two graphs are isomorphic precisely when they lie in the same orbit of
\mathsf{S}_{n}
, that is, when there exists a permutation
\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{n}
which transforms one graph into another.

For finite groups, the only meaningful way to formulate an equivalence relation in terms of a group action is to deem two objects equivalent if they can be related by the group action or, equivalently, if they have the same orbits.
But if we consider continuous groups acting on various spaces, such as general linear groups, the orthogonal, or the symplectic groups over
\mathbb{C}
, which represent different notions of symmetries of a complex vector space, then there is a second way to define a meaningful equivalence relation.
It is possible that two points do not lie in the same orbit, but there exist two sequences, one in each orbit, that converge to the same point.
So in addition to the standard notion of equivalence we can consider a relaxed notion of equivalence under which two points are equivalent if their orbit closures contain a common point.
Remarkably, for a wide class of groups known as
reductive algebraic groups
, which in particular contains the classical groups such as
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
,
\mathsf{SL}_{n}
,
\mathsf{O}_{n}
,
\mathsf{Sp}_{2n}
and products thereof, this second equivalence captures precisely the power of invariant functions to separate the orbits:

two points have intersecting orbit closures if and only if values of all continuous (or even polynomial) invariants on these points coincide
!

More formally, we consider a linear action of an algebraic group
\mathsf{G}
(such as the general linear group
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
) on a finite-dimensional complex vector space
X
.
The orbit of a point
x\in X
is the set
\mathsf{G}x=\{\mathsf{g}\cdot x\mid\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}\}
.
Two points
x,y\in X
are
equivalent
under the action of
\mathsf{G}
if
y=\mathsf{g}x
for some
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
(equivalently, the orbits
\mathsf{G}x
and
\mathsf{G}y
coincide).
The second, more relaxed, notion of equivalence is defined as follows.
Two points
x,y\in X
are
closure equivalent
if
\overline{\mathsf{G}x}\cap\overline{\mathsf{G}y}\neq\varnothing
(in our setting the orbit closure
\overline{\mathsf{G}x}
is the same in Zariski and Euclidean topology).
We denote equivalence by
x\sim_{\mathsf{G}}y
and closure equivalence by
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
.
A result of Mumford states that for
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
and other reductive groups,
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
is equivalent to the fact that
F(x)=F(y)
for every invariant polynomial
F
on
X
(or equivalently for any invariant continuous function).

A classical example shows the difference and relevance of both notions.
Consider the action
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
given by conjugation:
\mathsf{g}\cdot x=\mathsf{g}x\mathsf{g}^{-1}
.
The orbits of this action can be described using the classical theory of Jordan normal forms.
That is, two matrices are equivalent if they have a common Jordan normal form.
However, note that every Jordan normal form contains in its orbit closure the diagonal matrix with the same eigenvalues.
Thus the description of the closure equivalence is much simpler:
two matrices are closure equivalent if and only if they have the same eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) or, equivalently, the same characteristic polynomials.
This example highlights that closure equivalence is a meaningful relaxation of the traditional notion of equivalence that often has an easier description.
This is one of the insights behind Mumford’s geometric invariant theory (GIT)
[
4
]
, which is a powerful approach in mathematics to construct robust and well-behaved moduli spaces – geometrical spaces that parameterize objects such as curves up to closure equivalence.

Since we have two notions of equivalence, there are two computational problems associated with the action of
\mathsf{G}
on
X
:

•

Orbit equality:
given
x,y\in X
, decide if
x\sim_{\mathsf{G}}y
.

•

Orbit closure intersection:
given
x,y\in X
, decide if
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
.

To study the complexity theory of these problems, we consider a sequence of groups
\mathsf{G}_{n}
acting on vector spaces
X_{n}
depending on the problem size
n
.
For example, for the conjugation action, we have
\mathsf{G}_{n}=\mathsf{GL}_{n}
and
X_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, where the problem size
n
determines the size of the matrices.
And for the graph isomorphism problem, we take
n
to be the number of vertices, the groups
\mathsf{G}_{n}
are symmetric groups
\mathsf{S}_{n}
identified with the subgroups of permutation matrices in
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
, and we represent graphs by their adjacency matrices, which can be thought of as elements in
X_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
In the latter case the group is finite and hence both computational problems coincide.

Orbit closure intersection, algorithms and complexity

Recent work has made important algorithmic progress in special cases of the orbit closure intersection problem.

Main results

In this work we start a systematic study of orbit closure intersection (OCI) problems from the complexity-theoretic viewpoint.
We work over
\mathbb{C}
as our techniques are invariant-theoretic and work best in characteristic 0 (this setting also captures all applications mentioned above).

Orbit closure intersection problems

To formally define orbit closure intersection as a decision problem, we specify a sequence
X_{n}
of vector spaces and a sequence of groups
\mathsf{G}_{n}
such that
X_{n}
has a structure of
\mathsf{G}_{n}
-representation.
The parameter
n
is a positive integer which measures the size of the problem; we require that the dimension of
X_{n}
is polynomially bounded in terms of
n
.
More generally, we allow the parameter to be a tuple
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
.
An instance of an OCI problem
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
is then given by
n
and a pair of elements
x,y\in X_{n}
with rational coordinates.

1

More generally, we can allow coordinates from a field
\mathbb{F}
such that its elements can be represented by bitstrings and arithmetic operations on them can be performed efficiently.

Tensors and the complexity class
TOCI

We focus on the OCI problems for tensors and tensor tuples.
We can specify a sequence of representations by specifying type of each tensor factor for each tensor in a tuple and varying the dimensions of the vector spaces involved.
This captures several important group actions, such as:

•

Simultaneous conjugation:

\left({\left(V\otimes V^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)
;

•

Left-right action on matrix tuples:

\left({\left(V\otimes W\right)^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{SL}(V)\times\mathsf{SL}(W)}\right);

•

Gauge equivalence for 2D PEPS tensor networks:

\left({\left(V\otimes V^{*}\otimes W\otimes W^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)\times\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right);

•

SLOCC classification of tripartite quantum states:

\left({V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\otimes V_{3}};{\mathsf{SL}(V_{1})\times\mathsf{SL}(V_{2})\times\mathsf{SL}(V_{3})}\right).

More formally, we specify a sequence of representations by giving a formula
\mathcal{F}(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
, where the variables
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
correspond to vector spaces, and allowed operations are taking duals, direct sums, and tensor products.
Such a formula defines a sequence of
tensor tuple representations
by setting
\mathsf{G}_{\mathbf{n}}=\mathsf{GL}_{n_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathsf{GL}_{n_{m}}
and
X_{\mathbf{n}}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}},\dots,\mathbb{C}^{n_{m}})
.
All examples above can be interpreted in this way.
For convenience, we denote the sequence by
\left({\mathcal{F}(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})};{\mathsf{GL}(V_{1})\times\dots\times\mathsf{GL}(V_{n})}\right)
;
We call this a
tensor tuple sequence
.
The corresponding orbit closure intersection problem is denoted by

\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({\mathcal{F}(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})};{\mathsf{GL}(V_{1})\times\dots\times\mathsf{GL}(V_{n})}\right).

By analogy with
GI
and
TI
, we define the
complexity class
TOCI
, which contains all decision problems that are polynomial-time Karp-reducible to some OCI problem for actions of general linear groups.

Completeness of tensor OCI problems

We prove that there are orbit closure intersection problems that are complete for
TOCI
.
Note that any such problem must involve tensors with both covariant and contravariant factors.
For example, while the action of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
on

is complete for

), it is trivial from the perspective of OCI (every orbit contains

We show that the natural problem of orbit closure intersection for the gauge group of 2D PEPS tensor networks for qutrits is complete for

Theorem

V.11

Theorem I.1
.

Gauge equivalence of 2D PEPS tensor networks with physical dimension

Written in our notation, this problem is
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({\left(V\otimes V^{*}\otimes W\otimes W^{*}\right)^{\oplus 3}};{\mathsf{GL}(V){\times}\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right)

We note that the corresponding problem for 1D MPS tensor networks (simultaneous conjugaton) can be solved in polynomial-time.
This is rather suggestive as many problems that are computationally tractable for 1D tensor networks become computationally hard for 2D and higher dimensions, as discussed earlier.

We also reduce any problem in

When

In almost all remaining cases, we establish the following hardness result (

I-B

Ideas and techniques

Following
[
8
]
, we only consider a special class of reductions between OCI problems, which we call
efficient algebraic reductions
.
These are kernel reductions in the sense of
[
51
]
implemented by polynomial maps.
Most of the reductions between orbit equality problems in
[
8
]
are constructed in such a way that the output always lies in the nullcone, that is, is closure equivalent to
0
.
As such, these cannot be used as reductions between the corresponding OCI problems.
This also highlights the fact that even representations with the trivial closure equivalence problem (such as the one for the action of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
on
V^{\otimes k}
) can still have a very intricate orbit structure.
On the other hand, while some of our reductions can be adapted to work on associated orbit equality problems, other do not work on the level of orbits, but only orbit closures.
It seems that very different techniques are required for the analysis of OCI problems compared to orbit equality problems.

Consider groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
acting on vector spaces
X
and
Y
respectively.
We say that a polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y

preserves closure equivalence
if the implication

x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y\Rightarrow\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)

holds for every
x,y\in X
, and that
\alpha

reflects closure equivalence
if the opposite implication

\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)\Rightarrow x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y

holds for every
x,y\in X
.

An efficient algebraic reduction from
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
to
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({Y_{n}};{\mathsf{H}_{n}}\right)
is an efficiently computable sequence of polynomial maps
\alpha_{n}\colon X_{n}\to Y_{q(n)}
such that
q(n)
is polynomially bounded in terms of
n
, and each
\alpha_{n}
preserves and reflects closure equivalence, that is,
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}_{n}}y
if and only if
\alpha_{n}(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}_{q(n)}}\alpha_{n}(y)
.
We require that
\alpha_{n}
are polynomials with rational coefficients, so that the reduction always transforms input vectors with rational coordinates into output vectors with rational coordinates.
Our results do not depend much on the notion of efficient computability used in this definition.
In fact, in all reductions the maps
\alpha_{n}
always can be implemented by tuples of monomials.

We use invariant-theoretic methods to construct our reductions.
If a group
\mathsf{G}
acts on
X
, then a polynomial
F\in\mathbb{C}[X]
is
invariant
if
F(x)=F(\mathsf{g}\cdot x)
for all
x\in X
and
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
, that is,
F
is constant on orbits.
Polynomial invariants are in addition constant on orbit closures, because polynomials are continuous (with respect to Zariski topology).
The set of all invariant polynomials is closed under algebraic operations, so it is a subalgebra of
\mathbb{C}[X]
, denoted by
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.
The groups we consider (
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
,
\mathsf{O}_{n}
,
\mathsf{Sp}_{2n}
,
\mathsf{S}_{n}
and their products) belong to the class of algebraic groups called
reductive groups
.
For reductive groups the relation between polynomial invariants and closure equivalence is especially close, as shown by the following theorem.

Theorem I.6

(
[
4
,
52
]
)
.

Let
X
be a representation of a reductive group
\mathsf{G}
and let
x,y\in X
.
Then
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
if, and only if,
F(x)=F(y)
for all polynomial invariants
F\in\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

In other words, invariants separate orbit closures.
More generally, we can define a separating set of invariants as follows (for reductive groups this definition is equivalent to the definition of a separating set given in
[
52
]
).

Definition I.7
.

Let
X
be a representation of
\mathsf{G}
.
A set of invariants
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is a
separating set
if
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
if, and only if,
F(x)=F(y)
for all
F\in\Gamma
.

As an example, the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of a square matrix form a separating set of invariants for the conjugation action on the space of square matrices.

The idea that invariants can be used to construct algorithms for equivalence problems is well-known
[
53
]
(in this setting, a more general notion of a separating set is called a
complete invariant
).
Because of the special position of invariant polynomials in our setting, we can also use them to guide the construction of reductions between orbit closure intersection problems.
Instead of using gadgets to relate closure equivalence for different spaces directly, the reductions will provide tools to construct gadgets relating invariants.
More specifically, to establish reductions, we need to
prove that certain polynomial maps preserve and reflect closure equivalence.
To do this, we use the following key technical lemmata which characterize these properties in terms of invariants.
They can be found as
Lemmas

III.9
and
III.10
in the body of the paper.

Lemma I.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be representations of groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
respectively, with
\mathsf{H}
reductive.
Then a polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y
preserves closure equivalence if, and only if, for every invariant
F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
the pullback
F\circ\alpha
is an invariant in
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

Lemma I.9
.

Let
X
and
Y
be representations of groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
respectively, with
\mathsf{H}
reductive.
Suppose a polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y
preserves closure equivalence.
The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\alpha
reflects closure equivalence;

(b)

the set
\{F\circ\alpha\mid F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is separating;

(c)

for some separating set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
, the set

\Gamma\circ\alpha=\{F\circ\alpha\mid F\in\Gamma\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}

is separating;

(d)

for every separating set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
, the set
\Gamma\circ\alpha
is separating.

It is usually not hard to construct maps so that they preserve closure equivalence.
The main part of our proofs is usually concerned with establishing the second part, for which we use the description of invariants for
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
and reductive groups on tensor tuple representations in terms of tensor contractions.
Note that for mixed tensors over
V
(elements of tensor products with factors
V
and
V^{*}
) operations of tensor product and tensor contraction (that is, application of the trace map
\operatorname{Tr}\colon V\otimes V^{*}\to\mathbb{C}
to two factors of a tensor) are
\mathsf{GL}(V)
-equivariant.
As a consequence, if from a tuple of tensors we form a tensor product with equal numbers of factors of type
V
and
V^{*}
and then contract them in some order, the resulting expression is invariant under the action of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
.
We call these
contraction invariants
.
The following result is a main ingredient in the construction of many of our reductions (
Theorem

IV.23
).

Theorem I.10
.

The invariant algebra of a tensor tuple representation is spanned by contraction invariants.

For
\mathsf{GL}(V)
this description is classical and can be viewed as a reformulation of the classical first fundamental theorem of invariants
[
54
]
.
Similar description of invariants also hold for reductive subgroups of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
.
In this case the contraction invariants can contain a finite number of fundamental invariant tensors alongside the argument tensors.
For
\mathsf{O}(V)
and
\mathsf{Sp}(V)
these are the tensors in
V\otimes V
and
V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
representing invariant bilinear forms, for
\mathsf{SL}(V)
these are tensors representing determinants as multilinear functions of several vectors in
V
and
V^{*}
, and for the symmetric group
\mathsf{S}_{n}
the fundamental invariants are the bilinear form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{*}\otimes e_{i}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n*}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n*}
and the diagonal tensor
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{\otimes 3}\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 3}
.
The descriptions for other classical groups also follow from the corresponding first fundamental theorems, and the case of general reductive groups is considered in
[
55
,
56
]
.

To prove that a polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y
between tensor tuple representations of the groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
reflects closure equivalence we most often use
Lemma

I.9
and
Theorem

I.10
as follows.
We consider an arbitrary contraction invariant
G
on
X
and represent it as a multiple of
F\circ\alpha
for some contraction invariant
F
on
Y
.
The main part of the proof is the construction of the invariant
F
as a tensor contraction given
G
as a tensor contraction.
This construction in fact gives a condition much stronger than required by
Lemma

I.9
, namely, for our reductions the pullback map
F\mapsto F\circ\alpha
is a surjection from
\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
to
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

We note that similar surjective maps relating invariant algebras for representations of various groups are used in
[
40
]
to prove exponential lower bounds on the degree of separating invariants, but for this the surjections between invariant algebras must be degree-preserving, which is a much stronger condition than needed for our goals.

The description of invariants given in
Theorem

I.10
is only valid in characteristic
0
, which is why we restrict our attention to group actions over
\mathbb{C}
.
In prime characteristic some of the simpler reductions can be proven using different methods, but the general picture remains unclear.

I-C

Sketch of reduction from graph isomorphism to 2D PEPS

In this section we will illustrate our techniques and sketch how the graph isomorphism problem can be reduced to the orbit closure intersection problem for the simultaneous “double conjugation” corresponding to the gauge group of 2D PEPS tensor networks.

As explained earlier, the graph isomorphism problem can be modeled in terms of the action of the symmetric group
\mathsf{S}_{n}
on matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
or, equivalently, on tensors in
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}

Since
\Sym_{n}
is a finite group, its orbits are finite and, therefore, closed.
Thus,
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{S}_{n}}\right)

Our goal is to reduce this problem to
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({(V\otimes V^{*}\otimes W\otimes W^{*})^{\oplus 8}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)\times\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right)

As discussed, our approach is based on constructing algebraic reductions that relate the invariants for these groups and actions.
In particular, we will be able to obtain any arbitrary polynomial invariant of graphs from a corresponding invariant for the PEPS tensor action.

I-D

Outlook and open problems

It remains an open question to determine the exact complexity of OCI problems.
An interesting question related to this is whether orbit closure intersection problems are in fact easier than orbit equality problems.
On one hand, simple cases suggest that orbit closure intersection problems may have a simpler description.
On the other hand, in many situations a generic tensor tuple in large enough dimension has a closed orbit (that is, is
polystable
in the sense of geometric invariant theory
[
4
]
), which means that the two problems have the same answer for most inputs.
We can rephrase this question as the question about complexity classes
TOCI
and
TI
.
We note that
TI
is known to contain not only the graph isomorphism problem, but also the code equivalence problem
[
17
]
, for which no quasipolynomial-time algorithm is known.

Question I.11
.

What is the computational complexity of OCI problems for reductive groups? Can they be put in
\textnormal{{NP}}\cap\mathrm{co}\textnormal{{AM}}
like graph isomorphism?
Does
TOCI
contain the code equivalence problem?
What is the relationship between our class
TOCI
and the class
TI
from
[
8
]
?

It seems that a tensor tuple sequence does not need to be too complicated to be
TOCI
-complete.
We conjecture that as long as an OCI problem is not trivial or efficiently solvable using quiver representation techniques, it is
TOCI
-complete, similarly to the classical dichotomy of tame and wild quivers
[
57
]
; see also
[
58
,
59
]
.
A first step in the investigation of this question may be the analysis of the three remaining unclassified balanced tensor problems discussed above in
Theorem

I.2
.

Question I.12
.

Are the OCI problems for the actions of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
on
V^{\otimes 2}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes 2}
,
on
V^{\otimes 2}{\otimes}(V^{*})^{\otimes 2}\oplus V{\otimes}V^{*}
,
and on
V^{\otimes 3}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes 3}
complete for
TOCI
?
More generally, is any OCI problems for tensor tuple sequences either
TOCI
-complete, or efficiently solvable?

While we can use our techniques to work with some subgroups of general linear groups, it appears not straightforward to work with special linear groups or their products, in particular, the action of special linear groups and their products on tensors.
The main reason for this is that while the invariants of the orthogonal, symplectic, and symmetric groups can be described using tensors with bounded number of factors (
2
in the case of orthogonal and symplectic groups,
2
and
3
in the case of symmetric groups), the invariants for
\mathsf{SL}_{n}
require tensors with
n
factors (the determinant).
We note that the relation between representations of general linear and special linear groups is similarly unclear for orbit equality
[
12
, Question 9]
.

Question I.13
.

Are orbit closure intersection problems for special linear groups contained in
TOCI
?
In particular, are the problems
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({V_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes V_{m}};{\mathsf{SL}(V_{1})\times\dots\times\mathsf{SL}(V_{m})}\right)
and
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({V^{\otimes p}};{\mathsf{SL}(V)}\right)
in
TOCI
?

Our definition of a tensor tuple sequence can be extended to include symmetric/antisymmetric tensors or more general Schur functors, which specify symmetry of tensors with respect to the permutation of factors.
OCI problems for these more general sequences still lie in the class
TOCI
, as every Schur module can be included in an appropriate tensor product, and the orbit closures stay the same.
It is likely that many of these problems are also complete for
TOCI
.

Question I.14
.

Are there
TOCI
-complete problems involving symmetric tensors?

It is interesting to see if the analytic techniques developed recently for null cone and OCI problems
[
33
,
30
]
can be applied to solve OCI instances derived from difficult instances of graph isomorphism.
Purely invariant-theoretic techniques are likely to fail here, because polynomial invariants for graph isomorphism include hard to compute invariants such as permanent and “permanental characteristic polynomial”
\operatorname{per}(A-\lambda I)
of the adjacency matrix
[
60
]
while the power of analytic techniques is currently less clear.

Question I.15
.

Can reductions to OCI problems be used to construct interesting algorithms for graph isomorphism?

II-A

Notation and conventions

Throughout, we work with computational problems over the field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
.
To specify the input to such a problem, it needs to be encoded in a way that is amenable for computation.
To this end, we fix some subfield
\mathbb{F}\subset\mathbb{C}
whose elements can be represented by bitstrings and such that all operations can be computed in polynomial time.
Typical choices are
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{Q}(i)
.

We use bold font for tuples of data associated with product groups, for example, we write
\mathsf{G}_{\mathbf{n}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{GL}_{n_{i}}
where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of nonnegative integers and by
[n]
the
n
-element set
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

All vector spaces are complex vector spaces.
By a
group
we mean a linear algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
, unless stated otherwise.

Unless stated otherwise,
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
denotes Zariski closure of a set
\mathcal{X}
.
As mentioned earlier, when
\mathcal{X}
is an orbit of a complex reductive group, the Zariski closure coincides with the Euclidean one.

II-B

Algebraic groups, representations, invariants

We work with representations of linear algebraic groups over
\mathbb{C}
, except in §
V-D
where we consider the unitary groups
\mathsf{U}_{n}

For readers not familiar with algebraic groups, we introduce the relevant concepts below.
For our purposes, an affine variety is a subset of a vector space which is the common zero set of a set of polynomials with complex coefficients,
and a morphism of algebraic varieties is a map between varieties obtained as a restriction of a polynomial map between vector spaces.

Definition II.1

(General linear group)
.

For a vector space
V
, the
general linear group

\mathsf{GL}(V)
is the group of invertible linear operators on
V
.
We define
\mathsf{GL}_{n}=\mathsf{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

The general linear group
\mathsf{GL}(V)
is an
affine algebraic group
: it can be identified with the affine variety

\mathsf{GL}(V)=\{(A,c)\mid c\cdot\det A=1\}\subset\mathrm{Hom}(V,V)\oplus\mathbb{C}.

In this form the multiplication and inversion in
\mathsf{GL}(V)
are given by polynomial maps:

(A_{1},c_{1})\cdot(A_{2},c_{2})=(A_{1}A_{2},c_{1}c_{2})
and
(A,c)^{-1}=(c\cdot\operatorname{adj}A,\det A)
.

Definition II.2

(Linear algebraic group)
.

A
linear algebraic group
is a subgroup of some general linear group which is also an closed subvariety.
A
subgroup
of a linear algebraic group
\mathsf{G}
is a subgroup which is also a closed subvariety.
A
homomorphism
of algebraic groups is a group homomorphism which is also a morphism of affine varieties.

Definition II.3

(Representation)
.

A
representation
of a group
\mathsf{G}
is a vector space
X
equipped with a homomorphism
\rho\colon\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{GL}(X)
.
We write
\mathsf{g}x
for
\rho(\mathsf{g})x
if
\rho
is clear from the context.
We will often use the notation
\left({X};{\mathsf{G}}\right)
to refer to a representation of
\mathsf{G}
on
X
(the map
\rho
is left implicit in the notation).
The motivation is that we will consider reductions between representations of different groups, hence it will be useful to make the group explicit in the notation.

A
subrepresentation
of
X
is a vector subspace
Y\subset X
such that
\rho(\mathsf{g})Y\subseteq Y
for every
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
.
A representation is
irreducible
if it has no nontrivial subrepresentations.

If
X
and
Y
are representaions of
\mathsf{G}
, then a linear
\alpha\colon X\to Y
is
equivariant
if
\alpha(\mathsf{g}x)=\mathsf{g}\alpha(x)
for all
x\in X
and
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
.
An equivariant map is also called a
morphism of representations
.

As usual, if
X
is a representation of
\mathsf{G}
via
\rho\colon\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{GL}(X)
, then the structure of a representation on the dual space
X^{*}
is given by
\mathsf{g}y=\rho(\mathsf{g}^{-1})^{*}y
for
y\in X^{*}
, so that
y(x)=\mathsf{g}y(\mathsf{g}x)
.
Additionally, if
X
and
Y
are representations of
\mathsf{G}
, then
X\oplus Y
and
X\otimes Y
can also be given the structure of a representation by acting on both summands or tensor factors respectively.

Definition II.4

(Orbit, equivalence, closure equivalence)
.

Let
X
be a representation of
\mathsf{G}
and
x\in X
.
The
orbit
of
x
is

\mathsf{G}x=\{\mathsf{g}x\mid\mathsf{g}\in G\}.

We say that two elements
x,y\in X
are
equivalent (under the action of
\mathsf{G}
)

if there exists
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
such that
y=\mathsf{g}x
or, equivalently, if the orbits
\mathsf{G}x
and
\mathsf{G}y
coincide.
We say that
x
and
y
are
closure equivalent
if they have intersecting orbit closures, that is, if
\overline{\mathsf{G}x}\cap\overline{\mathsf{G}y}\neq\varnothing
.
We denote equivalence by
\sim_{\mathsf{G}}
and closure equivalence by
\approx_{\mathsf{G}}
, omitting the subscript if the group is clear from the context.

Definition II.5

(Invariant)
.

Let
X
be a representation of
\mathsf{G}
.
A polynomial
F\in\mathbb{C}[X]
is
invariant
under the action of
\mathsf{G}
if
F(\mathsf{g}\cdot x)=F(x)
for all
x\in X
and
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
.
Invariant polynomials form a subalgebra
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
of
\mathbb{C}[X]
called the
invariant algebra
.

The invariants in
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
are by definition constant on each orbit, and since they are also continuous with respect to Zariski topology, on each orbit closure.
Therefore, if
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
, then the values of all invariants coincide on
x
and
y
.
The converse holds for so-called reductive groups, which are an important class of groups with a particular well-behaved representation and invariant theory.
In characteristic zero, they can be defined as follows:

Definition II.6
.

An algebraic group
\mathsf{G}
is
(linearly) reductive
if every representation of
\mathsf{G}
can be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible representations.

Examples of reductive groups include the general linear groups
\mathsf{GL}(V)
and products thereof.
If the group
\mathsf{G}
is reductive, then invariants completely characterize closure equivalence, as follows from results of Mumford.

Theorem II.7

(
[
52
, Cor. 2.3.8]
)
.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be a reductive group acting on a vector space
X
, and let
x,y
be two elements of
X
.
Then,
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
if and only if
F(x)=F(y)
for all
F\in\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

This fact is the foundation of Mumford’s geometric invariant theory, which uses the invariant algebra
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
to construct spaces parameterizing orbits or orbit closures.

A famous result of Hilbert (see, e.g.,
[
61
, Thm. 3.5]
) shows that if
\mathsf{G}
is reductive, then the invariant algebra
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is finitely generated.
This means that if
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
is a generating set of
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
, then it is enough to check if
F_{i}(x)=F_{i}(y)
to conclude that
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
.
Sometimes one can find smaller sets of invariants which, while not generating, still have this property.
To this end we make the following definition of a separating set of invariants.

Definition II.8
.

Let
X
be a representation of reductive group
\mathsf{G}
.
A set of invariants
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is a
separating set
if for every
x,y\in X
we have
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
if and only if
F(x)=F(y)
for all
F\in\Gamma
.

For reductive groups this definition is equivalent to
[
52
, Def. 2.4.1]
.
For non-reductive groups the definition in
[
52
]
use non-distinguishability by polynomial invariants instead of closure equivalence
\approx_{\mathsf{G}}
.
The relevance of
Definition

II.8
has also been observed in the context of algebraic and geometric complexity theory, see e.g.,
[
41
]
and references therein.

In some cases we require that linear algebraic groups and homomorphisms between them are defined over the subfield
\mathbb{F}\subset\mathbb{C}
in which we perform computations, in the following sense.

Definition II.9
.

Suppose
V
is a vector space with a fixed basis.
We say that a closed subvariety of
V
is
defined over
\mathbb{F}
if it is a common zero set of a set of polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}
.
A morphism of varieties is
defined over
\mathbb{F}
if it is given by a polynomial map with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}
.
In particular, we talk about linear algebraic groups and their homomorhisms defined over
\mathbb{F}
.
If
X
is a representation of
\mathsf{G}
with a fixed basis is
defined over
\mathbb{F}
if its defining homomorphism
\rho\colon\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{GL}(X)
is defined over
\mathbb{F}
.

We refer to
[
62
]
for a comprehensive treatment of groups defined over a subfield of an algebraically closed field.
An important fact about reductive linear algebraic groups defined over
\mathbb{F}
is that the invariant algebra
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is generated by invariants with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}

[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
.

II-C

Integer linear programming

We recall a simple fact from the theory of integer linear programming which can be found, for example, in
[
63
, §16]
. It states that the set of nonnegative integral points in a rational polyhedral cone is a finitely generated monoid.

Definition II.10
.

A
rational polyhedral cone
is a set of the form

C=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid Ax\geq 0\}

for some rational matrix
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times n}
.
An
integral Hilbert basis
is a set of vectors
a_{1},\dots,a_{q}\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that every
v\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
can be written as a nonnegative integer linear combination:
v=\sum_{k=1}^{q}m_{k}a_{k}
with
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem II.11

(
[
63
, Thm. 16.4]
)
.

Every rational polyhedral cone has an integral Hilbert basis.

III
Orbit closure intersection problems and reductions

In this section we define the orbit closure intersection problem formally as a computational decision problem (
Section

III-A
).
We then discuss an appropriate notion of reductions (
Section

III-B
) and give criteria for verifying when maps gives rise to a reduction (
Section

III-C
).

III-A

Sequences of representations and orbit closure intersection problems

We now formalize the orbit closure intersection as a computational problem.
More specifically, we will describe a template orbit closure intersection problem which can be adapted to any situation where we have a family of groups depending on a “size parameter” such as
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
and a representation for each of these groups.

Definition III.1

(
p
-sequence)
.

A
polynomially bounded sequence of representations
, or a
p
-sequence of representations
, is given by a sequence of groups
\mathsf{G}_{n}
, a sequence of representations
\rho_{n}\colon\mathsf{G}_{n}\to\mathsf{GL}(X_{n})
on vector spaces
X_{n}
such that the dimension
\dim X_{n}
is polynomially bounded as a function of
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and a choice of basis in every vector space
X_{n}
.
As with representations, we often write
\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
for such a
p
-sequence of representations, leaving the homomorphisms
\rho_{n}
and bases implicit.

A
multivariate
p
-sequence of representations
is defined analogously but with
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
a tuple of parameters.

This is a very general definition.
Tensor actions provide a rich source of examples of
p
-sequences, as we will see in
Section

IV-B
below.
For now, the reader can imagine any of the concrete examples discussed in the introduction, such as the simultaneous conjugation action of
\mathsf{G}_{n}=\mathsf{GL}_{n}
on the space
X_{n}=(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n})^{\oplus p}
of
p
-tuples of
n\times n
matrices.

We are now ready to formally define the notion of an orbit closure intersection problem.

Definition III.2

(Orbit closure intersection problem)
.

Let
\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
be a
p
-sequence of representations.
Denote by
X_{n}(\mathbb{F})
the set of elements in
X_{n}
coordinates of which (in the chosen basis) lie in the subfield
\mathbb{F}
.
The
orbit closure intersection problem

\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
is the following decision problem:

Given
n
(encoded in unary) and two elements
x,y\in X_{n}(\mathbb{F})
(encoded as tuples of elements of
\mathbb{F}
, which are the coordinates in the chosen basis), decide if
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}_{n}}y
.

The requirement that
n
is encoded in unary is harmless when
\dim X_{n}
grows at least linearly with
n
, as the encodings of
x
and
y
in this case will have length at least
n
.
It is only needed to ensure that the input size is at least linear in
n
even when the dimension
\dim X_{n}
is sublinear (in particular, when it is constant).

III-B

Reductions between representations and orbit closure intersection problems

Since we consider algebraic groups, it is natural to consider reductions that are given by polynomial functions.
This leads to the following definition.

Definition III.3

(Efficient algebraic reduction)
.

Let
\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
and
\left({Y_{n}};{\mathsf{H}_{n}}\right)
be two
p
-sequences of representations.
An
algebraic reduction
from
\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
to
\left({Y_{n}};{\mathsf{H}_{n}}\right)
is a sequence of polynomial maps
\alpha_{n}\colon X_{n}\to Y_{q(n)}
for some function
q\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x,y\in X_{n}
:

\displaystyle x\approx_{\mathsf{G}_{n}}y\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\alpha_{n}(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}_{q(n)}}\alpha_{n}(y).

Such a reduction is called
efficient
if
q
is polynomially bounded, the maps
\alpha_{n}
are defined over
\mathbb{F}
, and the functions
q
and
(n,x)\mapsto\alpha_{n}(x)
for
x\in X_{n}(\mathbb{F})
are efficiently computable (with
n
given in unary and elements of
X_{n}
and
Y_{q(n)}
encoded as tuples of coordinates in the chosen bases).
Analogous definitions are made for multivariate
p
-sequences.

We use the standard notion of efficiently computable function, which can be defined for example as a function implementable by a polynomial-time Turing machine.
Our results do not depend much on the notion of efficient computability used in this definition, and can be made to work in a more algebraic setting where
\alpha_{n}
are implemented by polynomial-size circuits over a field
\mathbb{F}
, which in this case may even be uncountable.
In fact, in all reductions we construct in this paper the maps
\alpha_{n}
always can be implemented by tuples of monomials.

Efficient algebraic reductions between
p
-sequences of representations give rise to reductions between corresponding orbit closure intersection problems in the usual sense (polynomial-time Karp reductions).
These are special cases of
kernel reductions
introduced by Fortnow and Grochow
[
51
]
for general equivalence problems.

Proposition III.4
.

If there exist an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
to
\left({Y_{n}};{\mathsf{H}_{n}}\right)
,
then there exists a polynomial-time Karp reduction from
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
to
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({Y_{n}};{\mathsf{H}_{n}}\right)
.

Proof.

The required Karp reduction maps an instance
(n,x,y)
of
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
to the instance
(q(n),\alpha_{n}(x),\alpha_{n}(y))
of
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({Y_{n}};{\mathsf{H}_{n}}\right)
.
∎

Corollary III.5
.

If there exist an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)
to the trivial
p
-sequence
\left({\mathbb{C}^{n}};{\mathsf{E}}\right)
, where
\mathsf{E}
is the trivial group with one element, then
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X_{n}};{\mathsf{G}_{n}}\right)\in\textnormal{{P}}
.

Proof.

The orbits and orbit closures for the action of the trivial group are singleton sets.
Thus, the orbit closure intersection
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({\mathbb{C}^{n}};{\mathsf{E}}\right)
is just equality testing, which is in
P
.
∎

Note that an algebraic reduction as in
Corollary

III.5
consists of polynomial maps
\alpha_{n}\colon X_{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{q(n)}
such that
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
if and only if
\alpha_{n}(x)=\alpha_{n}(y)
, which means that that the coordinates of
\alpha_{n}
form a separating set of invariants.
Thus the corollary expresses in the context of orbit closure intersection problems a well-known principle that in order to efficiently solve an equivalence problem, it is enough to find an efficiently computable system of separating invariants.

III-C

Criteria for reductions

While the notion of an algebraic reduction is natural, it is not obvious how to verify this property.
In this section we give a number of useful characterizations.
We first introduce the following notions:

Definition III.6
.

Consider groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
acting on vector spaces
X
and
Y
respectively.
We say that a polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y

preserves closure equivalence
if

x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y\Rightarrow\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)

holds for every
x,y\in X
.
Conversely, we say that
\alpha

reflects closure equivalence
(or
preserves closure inequivalence
) if the opposite implication

\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)\Rightarrow x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y

holds for every
x,y\in X
.

Thus, an algebraic reduction consists of maps
\alpha_{n}
that preserve
and
reflect closure equivalence.
It is often not hard to construct maps that preserve closure equivalence.
The following lemma is applicable in many situations:

Lemma III.7
.

Let
X
and
Y
be representations of groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
respectively, and let
\alpha\colon X\to Y
be a polynomial map.
Suppose that there exists a group homomorphism
\beta\colon\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{H}
such that
\alpha(\mathsf{g}x)=\beta(\mathsf{g})\alpha(x)
for all
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
,
x\in X
.
Then
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence.

Proof.

The assumption implies that if
x,y\in X
are in the same
\mathsf{G}
-orbit then
\alpha(x),\alpha(y)
are in the same
\mathsf{H}
-orbit.
Since
\alpha
is a continuous map, it is continuous, so if
z\in\overline{\mathsf{G}x}
then

\displaystyle\alpha(z)\subseteq\alpha(\overline{\mathsf{G}x})\subseteq\overline{\alpha(\mathsf{G}x)}=\overline{\beta(G)\alpha(x)}\subseteq\overline{\mathsf{H}\alpha(x)}.

Therefore, if
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
then there exists
z\in\overline{\mathsf{G}x}\cap\overline{\mathsf{G}y}
, so

\displaystyle\alpha(z)\in\alpha(\overline{\mathsf{G}x}\cap\overline{\mathsf{G}y})\subseteq\overline{\mathsf{H}\alpha(x)}\cap\overline{\mathsf{H}\alpha(y)},

and hence
\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)
.
Thus,
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence.
∎

Corollary III.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be representations of the same group
\mathsf{G}
.
If
\alpha\colon X\to Y
is an injective
G
-equivariant linear map, then
\alpha
preserves and reflects closure equivalence.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\alpha
is an isomorphism (otherwise replace
Y
by the image
\alpha(X)
).
Then we can apply
Lemma

III.7
with
\beta=\operatorname{id}_{\mathsf{G}}
to see that
\alpha
and
\alpha^{-1}
preserve closure equivalence.
But
\alpha^{-1}
preserves closure equivalence if and only if
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence.
∎

We also have the following useful criterion:

Lemma III.9
.

Let
X
and
Y
be representations of groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
respectively, with
\mathsf{H}
reductive.
A polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y
preserves closure equivalence if, and only if, for every invariant
F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
the pullback
F\circ\alpha
is an invariant in
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence and let
F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
.
For every
x\in X
, and
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}
, it holds that
\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(\mathsf{g}x)
, and hence
F(\alpha(x))=F(\alpha(\mathsf{g}x))
because invariant polynomials are constant on orbit closures.
This means that
F\circ\alpha\in\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

Conversely, suppose that for every invariant
F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
the pullback
F\circ\alpha
is in
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.
If
x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y
, then we have
F(\alpha(x))=F(\alpha(y))
for every
F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
.
By
Theorem

II.7
, since
\mathsf{H}
is reductive, it follows that
\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)
, and hence
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence.
∎

It is often substantially more difficult to show that a map also
reflects
closure equivalence.
To this end we provide the following criterion:

Lemma III.10
.

Let
X
and
Y
be representations of groups
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{H}
respectively, with
\mathsf{H}
reductive.
Suppose a polynomial map
\alpha\colon X\to Y
preserves closure equivalence.
The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\alpha
reflects closure equivalence;

(b)

for every separating set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
, the set

\Gamma\circ\alpha=\{F\circ\alpha\mid F\in\Gamma\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}

is separating;

(c)

the set
\{F\circ\alpha\mid F\in\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is separating;

(d)

for some separating set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
, the set
\Gamma\circ\alpha
is separating.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b):

Suppose
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence and let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
be a separating set.
Then the following holds for all
x,y\in X
:

\displaystyle x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall F\in\Gamma\colon F(\alpha(x))=F(\alpha(y))

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall G\in\Gamma\circ\alpha\colon G(x)=G(y),

where we first used that
\alpha
preserves and reflects closure equivalence, then that
\Gamma
is separating, and finally the definition of
\Gamma\circ\alpha
.
Thus we have proved that
\Gamma\circ\alpha
is separating.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c)
\Rightarrow
(d):
This is clear since
\Gamma=\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
is separating set as
\mathsf{H}
is reductive (
Theorem

II.7
).

(d)
\Rightarrow
(a):

Suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}[Y]^{\mathsf{H}}
is such that
\Gamma\circ\alpha
is separating.
Then the following holds for all
x,y\in X
:

\displaystyle\alpha(x)\approx_{\mathsf{H}}\alpha(y)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall F\in\Gamma\colon F(\alpha(x))=F(\alpha(y))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall G\in\Gamma\circ\alpha\colon G(x)=G(y)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow x\approx_{\mathsf{G}}y,

where we first used that
\Gamma
consists of invariants, then the definition of
\Gamma\circ\alpha
, and finally that
\Gamma\circ\alpha
is separating.
Thus we have proved that
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence.
∎

We note that in all our reductions below the sets
\Gamma
and
\Gamma\circ\alpha
will in fact generate the respective invariant algebras, but to state the general criterion, we need to use separating sets.

IV-A

Tensor algebras and tensor contractions

We recall basic definitions related to mixed tensor spaces, mixed tensor algebras and tensor contractions following
[
64

[
55
]
.
Since these are the main objects that we work with, we usually omit the “mixed” qualification.

Definition IV.1
.

Let
V
be a vector space. The space

{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}=V^{\otimes a}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes b}

is called the
(mixed) tensor space of type
({a};\,{b})
over
V
.

Every tensor product with
V
and
V^{*}
as factors is isomorphic to
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
for some
({a};\,{b})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
by rearranging the tensor factors, so we can restrict our attention to these spaces when studying tensors formed from elements
V
and
V^{*}
.
In particular, the tensor product
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{1}}_{b_{1}}}{V}\otimes{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{2}}_{b_{2}}}{V}
is isomorphic to
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{1}+a_{2}}_{b_{1}+b_{2}}}{V}
.
This allows us to view the tensor product
t_{1}\otimes t_{2}
with
t_{1}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{1}}_{b_{1}}}{V}
and
t_{2}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{2}}_{b_{2}}}{V}
as an element of
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{1}+a_{2}}_{b_{1}+b_{2}}}{V}
.
More specifically, we define the tensor product on elementary tensors as

(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a_{1}}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b_{1}}w_{j})\otimes(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a_{2}}x_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b_{2}}y_{j})\\
=(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a_{1}}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a_{2}}x_{i})\otimes(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b_{1}}w_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b_{2}}y_{j})

where
v_{i},x_{i}\in V
and
w_{j},y_{j}\in V^{*}
, and extend it bilinearly to arbitrary tensors.
In what follows, the symbol
\otimes
applied to two tensors always denotes this tensor product (or its extension to tensors over multiple vector spaces introduced in
Section

IV-B
).

The tensor product is associative, and we can combine the tensor spaces
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
into a graded associative algebra.

Definition IV.2
.

Let
V
be a vector space.
The
(mixed) tensor algebra
over
V
is the
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
-graded algebra

\mathcal{T}(V)=\bigoplus\limits_{({a};\,{b})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}

with the tensor product operation
\otimes
defined above.

The standard action of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
on
V
induces the structure of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
-representation on each tensor space
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
and therefore on the mixed tensor algebra
\mathcal{T}(V)
.
Explicitly, the action of
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{GL}(V)
is given on elementary tensors by

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}\cdot(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b}w_{j})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}(\mathsf{g}v_{i})\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b}(\mathsf{g}^{*})^{-1}w_{j}.

(IV.3)

In addition, each tensor space
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
is also a representation of
\mathsf{S}_{a}\times\mathsf{S}_{b}
, acting by separately permuting the contravariant and covariant indices.
The action of
(\pi,\sigma)\in\mathsf{S}_{a}\times\mathsf{S}_{b}
is given by

\displaystyle(\pi,\sigma)\cdot(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b}w_{j})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{\pi^{-1}i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b}w_{\sigma^{-1}j}.

(IV.4)

The tensor algebra
\mathcal{T}(V)
carries additional structure which comes from identity tensors and tensor contractions.

Definition IV.5
.

Let
V
be a vector space.
The
identity tensor

\operatorname{Id}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{1}_{1}}{V}=V\otimes V^{*}
is the tensor corresponding to the identity map
V\to V
.

Definition IV.6
.

Let
V
be a vector space.
For every
({a};\,{b})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[a]
,
q\in[b]
the
contraction map

\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a-1}_{b-1}}{V}

contracting the
p
-th contravariant factor with the
q
-th covariant factor
is the linear map defined on elementary tensors as

\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b}w_{j}\right)=w_{q}(v_{p})\cdot\bigotimes_{i=1,i\neq p}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1,j\neq q}^{b}w_{j}.

We write
\operatorname{Tr}^{p}_{q}
if the tensor type
({a};\,{b})
is clear from the context.

Clearly, the identity tensor is
\mathsf{GL}(V)
-invariant and tensor products and contractions are
\mathsf{GL}(V)
-equivariant.
We list some simple properties of tensor products and contractions which follow directly from the definitions.

Proposition IV.7
.

Let
t\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
,
s\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{c}_{d}}{V}
, and let
p\in[a]
,
q\in[b]
.
The following properties hold:

1.

s\otimes t=(\pi,\sigma)(t\otimes s)
where
\pi
swaps the first
a
and the last
c
elements of
[a+c]
, and similarly
\sigma
swaps the first
b
and the last
d
elements of
[b+d]
.

2.

Commutation relations for tensor products and a contraction:

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}t)\otimes s

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a+c}_{q:b+d}(t\otimes s)

\displaystyle s\otimes(\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}t)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}^{c+p:a+c}_{d+q:a+d}(s\otimes t).

3.

Properties of contraction with identity tensor:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}^{p+1:a+1}_{1:b+1}(\operatorname{Id}\otimes t)

\displaystyle=((12\dots p),\operatorname{id})\cdot t

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}^{1:a+1}_{q+1:b+1}(\operatorname{Id}\otimes t)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{id},(12\dots q))\cdot t.

Interestingly, the last property means that the permutation action
Eq.

IV.4
can be recovered by using contractions and tensoring with identity tensors, because the cyclic permutations
(12\dots p)
generate the whole symmetric group.

Contractions can be composed to create more complicated tensor contractions.
After applying one contraction map
\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}
, all contravariant indices higher than
p
and all covariant indices higher than
q
decrease by one.
This leads to the following commutation relation for contraction maps:

\operatorname{Tr}^{r:a-1}_{s:b-1}\circ\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Tr}^{p-1:a-1}_{q-1:b-1}\circ\operatorname{Tr}^{r:a}_{s:b},&\text{if $r<p,s<q$}\\
\operatorname{Tr}^{p-1:a-1}_{q\phantom{-1}:b-1}\circ\operatorname{Tr}^{r\phantom{+1}:a}_{s+1:b},&\text{if $r<p,s\geq q$}\\
\operatorname{Tr}^{p\phantom{-1}:a-1}_{q-1:b-1}\circ\operatorname{Tr}^{r+1:a}_{s\phantom{+1}:b},&\text{if $r\geq p,s<q$}\\
\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a-1}_{q:b-1}\circ\operatorname{Tr}^{r+1:a}_{s+1:b},&\text{if $r\geq p,s\geq q$}\end{cases}

To avoid the necessity of iteratively tracking these changes of indices, we introduce a convenient notation for contracting multiple pairs of indices at once.

Definition IV.8
.

A
partial bijection
is a bijection
\gamma\colon P\to Q
between subsets
P\subseteq[a]
and
Q\subseteq[b]
.
Given such a partial bijection, we define

\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a-k}_{b-k}}{V}
, where
k=\lvert P\rvert=\lvert Q\rvert
, as the contraction of all contravariant factors with indices in
P
with the covariant factors with indices in
Q
according to the bijection
\gamma
, that is,

\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma}(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{b}w_{j})=\prod_{p\in P}w_{\gamma(p)}(v_{p})\cdot\left(\bigotimes_{i\notin P}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j\notin Q}w_{j}\right)

We also write
\operatorname{Tr}^{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}:a}_{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}:b}:=\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma}
when
P=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
,
Q=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\}
,
\gamma(p_{j})=q_{j}
for all
j\in[k]
.

Compositions of contraction maps are exactly
\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma}
for some partial bijection
\gamma
:

•

The identity function on
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
is
\operatorname{Tr}_{\varnothing}
where
\varnothing
is the nowhere-defined partial function.

•

If
\gamma
is a partial bijection with domain
P
and image
Q
of cardinality
k
,
then
\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a-k}_{q:a-k}\circ T_{\gamma}=T_{\bar{\gamma}}
for the partial bijection
\bar{\gamma}
extending
\gamma
with
\bar{\gamma}(\bar{p})=\bar{q}
, where
\bar{p}
is the
p
-th element of
[a]\setminus P
and
\bar{q}
is the
q
-th element of
[b]\setminus Q
.

In particular, if
a=b
and
\pi\in\mathsf{S}_{a}
is a permutation of
[a]
, then
\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{a}}{V}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{0}_{0}}{V}=\mathbb{C}
maps tensors to scalars as follows.

\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{a}w_{j})=\prod_{p=1}^{a}w_{\pi(p)}(v_{p}).

In this case,
\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}
can also be expressed using the action (
IV.4
) of the permutation group as

\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}(x)=\operatorname{Tr}\left((\pi,\operatorname{id})\cdot x\right)=\operatorname{Tr}\left((\operatorname{id},\pi^{-1})\cdot x\right)

in terms of the canonical
\operatorname{Tr}=\operatorname{Tr}_{\operatorname{id}}
that contracts the covariant and contravariant in the same order:

\operatorname{Tr}(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{a}w_{j})=\prod_{p=1}^{a}w_{p}(v_{p})

Several other constructions related to tensors can be expressed using contractions.
For example, the tensor space
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
can be identified with the space of linear maps from
V^{\otimes b}
to
V^{\otimes a}
.
The composition of linear maps induces a bilinear map
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}\times{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{b}_{c}}{V}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{c}}{V}
, which we denote by

\displaystyle t\cdot s=\operatorname{Tr}^{a+1,\dots,a+b:a+b}_{\phantom{a+}1,\dots,\phantom{a+}b:b+c}(t\otimes s)

(IV.9)

for
t\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
,
s\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{b}_{c}}{V}
.
When
c=a
we can take the trace of the above to obtain the canonical bilinear pairing between the spaces
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
and
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{b}_{a}}{V}
:

\left<t,s\right>=\operatorname{Tr}(t\cdot s)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\sigma}(t\otimes s)

where
\sigma
is the permutation swapping the first
a
and the last
b
indices, that is,
\sigma(i)=b+i
for
i\leq a
, and
\sigma(i)=i-a
for
i\geq a
.
Finally, we can also express the permutation action using tensor contractions, as follows.
For every permutation
\pi\in\mathsf{S}_{a}
, consider the linear map
P_{\pi}\colon V^{\otimes a}\to V^{\otimes a}
defined on elementary tensors as

P_{\pi}(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{i})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{a}v_{\pi^{-1}(i)}.

Identifying
P_{\pi}
with a tensor in
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{a}}{V}
, it can be written as

P_{\pi}=\operatorname{Tr}^{a+1,a+2,\dots,\phantom{\pi(}2a:2a}_{\pi(1),\pi(2),\dots,\pi(a):2a}\,(\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes(2a)}),

Then the action (
IV.4
) of
\mathsf{S}_{a}\times\mathsf{S}_{b}
-action on
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a}_{b}}{V}
can be expressed in terms of two compositions (
IV.9
):

(\pi,\sigma)\cdot x=P_{\pi}\cdot x\cdot P_{\sigma^{-1}}\quad\text{for $\pi\in\mathsf{S}_{a}$, $\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{b}$.}

The algebraic structure of
\mathcal{T}(V)
, with tensor products, contractions, and identity tensors, is called a
wheeled PROP

[
56
]
(
PROP
is short for
pro
duct and
p

We will not require the general definition of a wheeled PROP, but only define what it means for a subalgebra of
\mathcal{T}(V)
:

Definition IV.10
.

A graded subalgebra

A=\bigoplus\limits_{({a};\,{b})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}A^{a}_{b}

of
\mathcal{T}(V)
is called a
sub-wheeled PROP
if
\operatorname{Id}\in A^{1}_{1}
and
\operatorname{Tr}^{p:a}_{q:b}(A^{a}_{b})\subseteq A^{a-1}_{b-1}
for every contraction.

Given a set of tensors
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{T}(V)
, the
sub-wheeled PROP generated by
\mathcal{H}
is the minimal (with respect to inclusion) sub-wheeled PROP containing
\mathcal{H}
.
We denote it by
\left<\mathcal{H}\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
.
A sub-wheeled PROP is called
finitely generated
if it is generated by some finite set
\mathcal{H}
.

As mentioned, if
X
is a representation of a reductive group
\mathsf{G}
then the invariant algebra
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is finitely generated.
There is a variant of finite generation theorem for mixed tensor algebras, as was proved by Schrijver
[
55
]
over
\mathbb{C}
and by Derksen and Makam
[
56
]
over an arbitrary field of characteristic zero.
For the following result, recall that we have an action (
IV.3
) of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
on the tensor algebra
\mathcal{T}(V)
.

Theorem IV.11

(
[
56
, Thm. 6.2, Cor. 6.6]
)
.

Let
V
be a vector space and
\mathsf{G}
be a reductive subgroup of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
.
Then the invariant tensors

\mathcal{T}(V)^{\mathsf{G}}=\{t\in\mathcal{T}(V)\mid\mathsf{g}\cdot t=t\ \forall\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{G}\}

form a finitely generated sub-wheeled PROP.

The following lemma shows that any finitely generated sub-wheeled PROP has a concrete description in terms of contracting tensor products of tensors of the generators and identity tensors.
It applies in particular in the situation of
Theorem

IV.11
.

Lemma IV.12
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\}
with
h_{k}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{k}}_{b_{k}}}{V}
.
Then the sub-wheeled PROP
\left<\mathcal{H}\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
is linearly spanned by tensors of the form

\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma}\left(\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes d_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}h_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right),

(IV.13)

where each
d_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma
is a partial bijection.

Proof.

Note that the set of all tensors of the form (
IV.13
) is closed under contraction maps.
To prove that it is also closed under tensor product, one needs to use the simple properties listed in
Proposition

IV.7
to rearrange the order of operations in the expression

\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma}\left(\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes d_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}h_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right)\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\left(\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes d^{\prime}_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\otimes h_{k}^{\otimes d^{\prime}_{k}}\right).

Taking into account the bilinearity of tensor product and linearity of contractions we see that that the linear span of the tensors of the form (
IV.13
) is also closed under tensor products and contractions.
Since it also contains
\operatorname{Id}
, it is a sub-wheeled PROP.

It is also clear that all tensors of the form (
IV.13
) are contained in all sub-wheeled PROPs containing
\mathcal{H}
.
We conclude that their linear span coincides with
\left<h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
.
∎

We will also see expressions similar to (
IV.13
) where instead of fixed tensors
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}
we have variable tensors
x_{1},\dots,x_{p}
.
If the value of such an expression is scalar, we can get rid of identity tensors in the expression.

Lemma IV.14
.

Every function of the form

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\left(\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes d_{0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right),

(IV.15)

where
\pi
is a permutation, can be expressed as

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=(\dim V)^{c}\cdot\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi^{\prime}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}

(IV.16)

for some permutation
\pi^{\prime}
and some
c\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Use
Proposition

IV.7
(
3
) to remove the identity tensors contracted with other tensors (including other identity tensors).
The remaining identity tensors contribute some power of
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\operatorname{Id}\right)=\dim V
.
∎

So far we considered tensor spaces built from a single vector space and its dual, but the definitions generalize readily to multiple vector spaces.

Definition IV.17

(Tensor spaces and algebra)
.

Let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})

The

{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{i}}_{b_{i}}}{V_{i}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left(V_{i}^{\otimes a_{i}}\otimes(V_{i}^{*})^{\otimes b_{i}}\right).

\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\mathbf{a}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}^{\otimes a_{i}}
.
The
(mixed) tensor algebra
over
\mathbf{V}
is the
\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}
-graded algebra

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{V})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{T}(V_{i})=\bigoplus_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}

with the tensor product operation
\otimes
inherited from the
\mathcal{T}(V_{i})
.

Each
\mathsf{GL}(V_{i})
acts on the vector spaces
V_{i}
and
V_{i}^{*}
.
This induces a structure of a representation of

\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{GL}(V_{i}).

on the tensor spaces
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}
and therefore on
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{V})
.
The tensor algebras
\mathcal{T}(V_{i})
also come with the structure of a wheeled PROP.
This additional structure transfers to
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{V})
as follows.

•

For every

{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{e_{i}}}_{\mathbf{e_{i}}}}{\mathbf{V}}=V_{i}\otimes V_{i}^{*}
contains a distinguished element corresponding to the identity map, which we call the

\operatorname{Id}[V_{i}]

•

The contraction maps
\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma_{i}}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{i}}_{b_{i}}}{V_{i}}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{a_{i}-k_{i}}_{b_{i}-k_{i}}}{V_{i}}
compose to form contraction maps

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma_{i}}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}

.
In particular, we denote by
\operatorname{Tr}[V_{i}]_{\gamma_{i}}

We will also allow tuples of such tensor spaces:

IV-B

Tensor tuple sequences and the complexity class
TOCI

Definition IV.19

(Tensor tuple sequences)
.

Let

.

.

are used).

Example IV.20
.

Using the symbolic notation, the sequence of simultaneous conjugation actions on
p
-tuples of
n\times n
-matrices is given by
(\oplus_{k=1}^{p}V\otimes V^{*},\mathsf{GL}(V))
rather than by the tuples
(1,1),\dots,(1,1)
.

Definition IV.21

(
TOCI
)
.

We define the complexity class
\textnormal{{TOCI}}=\textnormal{{TOCI}}_{\mathsf{GL}}
as the class of computational problems that are reducible (by a polynomial-time Karp reduction) to any orbit closure intersection problem for a tensor tuple sequence
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
, where
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
({\mathbf{a}_{1}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{1}}),\dots,({\mathbf{a}_{p}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{p}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}{\times}\mathbb{N}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

The classes
\textnormal{{TOCI}}_{\mathsf{O}}
and
\textnormal{{TOCI}}_{\mathsf{Sp}}
are defined analogously using tensor tuple sequences of the form

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\mathbf{a}_{k}}};{\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
and

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\mathbf{a}_{k}}};{\mathsf{Sp}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
respectively.

The above classes depend on the choice of the field
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
over which we perform computations.
All our results hold for an arbitary field of characteristic zero with efficient arithmetic, so we omit the dependence on
\mathbb{F}
in the notation.

IV-C

Invariant theory of tensors tuple representations

We now describe the relevant invariant theory.
We consider individual tensor tuple representations (not sequences) and hence
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
refers to a concrete tuple of vector spaces.

Definition IV.22

(Balancedness)
.

A tensor space over
\mathbf{V}
is
balanced
if it has the form
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{c}}}{\mathbf{V}}
.
Elements of a balanced tensor space are called
balanced tensors
.
A tensor tuple representation is called
balanced
if all its summands are balanced, that is, if it has the form
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}_{k}}_{\mathbf{c}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}
.

Recall from
Theorem

IV.11
that the invariant tensor algebras are sub-wheeled PROPs.
Using
Lemma

IV.12
, this implies a fairly explicit description of the invariants for the tensor tuple representations:

Theorem IV.23
.

Let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
be vector spaces, let
\mathsf{G}_{i}
be reductive subgroups of
\mathsf{GL}(V_{i})
, and let
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.
Consider the tensor tuple representation of
\mathsf{G}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{G}_{i}
on
X=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}
.
Let
h_{i1},\dots,h_{ir_{i}}
denote homogeneous generators of
\mathcal{T}(V_{i})^{\mathsf{G_{i}}}
.
Then the invariant algebra
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
is linearly spanned by invariants of the form

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\\
=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{r_{i}}h_{i\ell}^{\otimes f_{i\ell}}\right)

(IV.24)

where the tensor in brackets is balanced and
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m}
are permutations of appropriate sizes.

Proof.

Since tensor products and contractions are
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
-equivariant, and
h_{i1},\dots,h_{ir_{i}}
are invariant under
\mathsf{G}_{i}
, all polynomials of the form (
IV.24
) are invariants.
We need to argue that they span
\mathbb{C}[X]^{\mathsf{G}}
.

Let
X_{k}={\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}
, so that
X=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}X_{k}
.
The space of multihomogeneous polynomials
\mathbb{C}[X]_{(d_{1},\dots,d_{p})}
, where
d_{k}
is the degree in
X_{k}
, is isomorphic as a representation of
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
(and therefore of
\mathsf{G}
) to
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}S^{d_{k}}X_{k}^{*}
.
The latter can be equivariantly embedded into a tensor space as follows:

\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}S^{d_{k}}X_{k}^{*}\subset\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}(X_{k}^{*})^{\otimes d_{k}}\cong\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{b}_{k}}_{\mathbf{a}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}^{\otimes d_{k}}\cong{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{b}}_{\mathbf{a}}}{\mathbf{V}}

where
\mathbf{a}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}d_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}
and
\mathbf{b}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}d_{k}\mathbf{b}_{k}
.
Thus, any invariant polynomial
F
can be recovered from a corresponding invariant tensor
t\in\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{V})^{\mathsf{G}}
as

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\left<t,\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right>.

Applying
Lemma

IV.12
to each factor of the tensor product
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{V})^{\mathsf{G}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{T}(V_{i})^{\mathsf{G}_{i}}
we see that the invariant tensors in
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{V})^{\mathsf{G}}
are spanned by contraction expressions

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma_{i}}\left(\operatorname{Id}[V_{i}]^{\otimes{f_{i0}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{r}h_{i\ell}^{\otimes f_{i\ell}}\right)\right)\\
=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\gamma_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Id}[V_{i}]^{\otimes{f_{i0}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{r}h_{i\ell}^{\otimes f_{i\ell}}\right).

Therefore, the invariants are spanned by scalar contraction expressions of the form

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\left(\operatorname{Id}[V_{i}]^{\otimes f_{i0}}\otimes\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{r}h_{i\ell}^{\otimes f_{i\ell}}\right)\right).

The identity tensors can be removed using
Lemma

IV.14
.
∎

The following definition formalizes the objects that appear in
Theorem

IV.23
, which can then be summarized as follows:
if we know tensor invariant generators for each
\mathsf{G}_{i}
, the invariants of any tensor tuple representation of
\mathsf{G}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{G}_{i}
are spanned by the corresponding contraction invariants.

Definition IV.25
.

Let
V
be a vector space and let
\mathsf{G}
be a reductive subgroup of
\mathsf{GL}(V)
.
If
\mathcal{T}(V)^{\mathsf{G}}=\left<h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
, with homogeneous
h_{k}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{u_{k}}_{v_{k}}}{V}
, then we call
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}

tensor invariant generators
for
\mathsf{G}
.

The invariants of the form (
IV.24
) are called
contraction invariants
for the tensor tuple representation
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{G}}\right)
.

We list here tensor invariant generators for some classical groups, determined in
[
55
]
over
\mathbb{C}
, but having coordinates in
\mathbb{Q}
, so valid over every field of characteristic zero, and the corresponding contraction invariants.
We first discuss the case that
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
.
In this case, there are only trace invariants.

Lemma IV.26
.

It holds that

\mathcal{T}(V)^{\mathsf{GL}(V)}=\left<\varnothing\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
.
Therefore, the invariant algebra for the action of
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
on any
X\penalty=\penalty\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}
is linearly generated by invariants of the form

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right)

Next we consider products of special linear groups.

Lemma IV.27
.

It holds that
\mathcal{T}(V)^{\mathsf{SL}(V)}=\left<\omega,\omega^{*}\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
, where
\omega\in V^{\otimes(\dim V)}
and
\omega^{*}\in(V^{*})^{\otimes(\dim V)}
are nonzero antisymmetric tensors.
Therefore, the invariant algebra for the action of
\mathsf{SL}(\mathbf{V})
on

is linearly generated by invariants of the form

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}(\omega_{i}^{\otimes f_{i}}\otimes(\omega^{*}_{i})^{\otimes f^{\prime}_{i}})\right).

where
\omega_{i}\in V_{i}^{\otimes(\dim V_{i})}
and
\omega^{*}_{i}\in(V_{i}^{*})^{\otimes(\dim V_{i})}
are antisymmetric tensors.

For the orthogonal and symplectic groups, one analogously uses the corresponding bilinear forms.

Lemma IV.28
.

Let
g\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form.
Then,
\mathcal{T}(V)^{\mathsf{O}(V,g)}=\left<g,\bar{g}\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
where
\bar{g}\in V\otimes V
is a nonzero bilinear form on
V^{*}
invariant under
\mathsf{O}(V,g)
.
The same is true for
\mathsf{Sp}(V,g)
with
g,\bar{g}
being tensors corresponding to the symplectic forms.

The nondegenerate form allows identifying
V\cong V^{*}
and accordingly one can always reduce to tensor tuple representations without covariant indices.
In this case we do not need the dual form to span the invariants:

Corollary IV.29
.

Let
g_{i}\in V^{*}_{i}\otimes V^{*}_{i}
be nondegenerate symmetric bilinear forms.
Then, the invariant algebra for the action of
\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{g})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{O}(V_{i},g_{i})
on
X\penalty=\penalty\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{V}^{\otimes a_{k}}
is linearly generated by invariants of the form

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}^{\otimes f_{i}}\right)

The same is true for
\mathsf{Sp}(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{g})
if we take the
g_{i}
to be the symplectic forms of the
V_{i}
.

Proof.

Theorem

IV.23
gives a spanning set consisting of invariants

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}^{\otimes f_{i1}}\otimes\bar{g}_{i}^{\otimes f_{i2}}\right)

Since among the tensors
x_{1},\dots,x_{p},g_{1},\dots,\bar{g}_{m}
only the
g_{i}
contains
V_{i}^{*}
-factors, in this expression every factor of every
\bar{g_{i}}
tensor must be contracted with some factor of some
g_{i}
tensor.
Using the identity
\operatorname{Tr}^{1}_{1}(g_{i}\otimes\bar{g}_{i})=\operatorname{Id}
and
Lemma

IV.14
, we can transform the expression into the required form without
\bar{g_{i}}
in the product.
∎

Finally we consider the symmetric group
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{S}_{n}
, which we can think of the subgroup of permutation matrices in
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
.

Lemma IV.30
.

Denote by
\mathsf{S}_{n}
the symmetric group on
n
embedded in
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
as permutation matrices.
It holds that

\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\mathsf{S}_{n}}=\left<g,h\right>_{\operatorname{Tr}}
,
where
g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{*}\otimes e_{i}^{*}\in(\mathbb{C}^{n*})^{\otimes 2}
and
h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 3}
.
Therefore, the invariant algebra for the action of
\mathsf{S}_{n}
on
X=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes a_{k}}
is linearly generated by invariants of the form

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\otimes g^{\otimes e_{1}}\otimes h^{\otimes e_{2}}\right).

Proof.

Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Schrijvers
[
55
, Eq. 43]
gives two systems of generators for the contraction-closed algebra
\mathcal{T}(V)^{\mathsf{\mathsf{S}_{n}}}
.
One of them consists of tensors
h_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{\otimes k}\in V^{\otimes k}
for all
k\geq 1
and the tensors
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
and

, which are generators for the orthogonal group containing

can be removed in this case.
This system of generators is redundant as

and

V
Reductions between tensor tuple sequences

In this section, we describe our main results, which are efficient reductions that clarify the computational complexity of tensor tuple sequences.
In
Section

V-A
, we establish the study the action of the general linear group and identify several problems that are complete for
TOCI
.
In
Section

V-B
, we show that actions of the orthogonal and symplectic groups can always be reduced to the preceding, so
\textnormal{{TOCI}}_{\mathsf{O}}\cup\textnormal{{TOCI}}_{\mathsf{Sp}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}}
.
In
Section

V-C
, we relate the graph isomorphism problem to a tensor tuple sequence; this proves that
\textnormal{{GI}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}}
.
In
Section

V-D
, we use related methods to show that the tensor isomorphism problem for the unitary group can be reduced to an orbit closure intersection problem for closed orbits; in particular, this shows that
\textnormal{{TI}}_{\mathsf{U}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}}\cap\textnormal{{TI}}
.

V-A

Reductions and completeness for products of general linear groups

In this section we prove the completeness of several specific orbit closure intersection problems for
TOCI
.
To achieve this, we first establish several efficient algebraic reductions between tensor tuple sequences of certain formats.
These reductions are then composed to establish completeness.

Balanced tensors play an important role in the construction of
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
-invariants.
The following lemma shows that every tensor tuple sequence can be reduced to a balanced one.

Lemma V.1
.

For every
({\mathbf{a}_{1}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{1}}),\dots,({\mathbf{a}_{p}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{p}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}
, there exist
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{q}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
and an efficient algebraic reduction from

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a\mathbf{}}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b\mathbf{}}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)

to the balanced sequence

\left({\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{q}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
.

Proof.

Consider the set of all tuples
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
such that the tensor product
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}
is balanced for
x_{k}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}
.
These tuples are exactly the integer points in the rational polyhedral cone
C

given by inequalities
d_{k}\geq 0
and linear equations
\sum_{k=1}^{p}a_{k}d_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}b_{k}d_{k}
enforcing the balance.
By
Theorem

II.11
, there is an integral Hilbert basis
\mathbf{h}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{h}_{q}\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{p}
.

Set
\mathbf{c}_{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{a}_{k}h_{\ell k}
and define a map

\alpha\colon\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}\to\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{q}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}_{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}}{\mathbf{V}}

given by

\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})\quad\text{where }y_{\ell}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes h_{\ell k}}.

Because the map is
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
-equivariant it preserves closure equivalence by
Lemma

III.7
.

We will now prove that it also reflects closure equivalence.
To see this, recall from
Lemma

IV.26
that the invariant algebra for
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
is linearly spanned by contraction invariants of the form

G(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right),

for some permutations
\pi_{i}
and where the expression in parentheses is balanced.
The latter means that
\sum_{k=1}a_{k}d_{k}=\sum_{k=1}b_{k}d_{k}
, so
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{p})\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, hence it can be represented as
\mathbf{d}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}e_{\ell}\mathbf{h}_{\ell}
for certain
e_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}=\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{q}y_{\ell}^{\otimes e_{\ell}}
, where
(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})=\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
.
Thus the invariant
G
can be represented as
F\circ\alpha
, where

F(y_{1},\dots,y_{q})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{q}y_{\ell}^{\otimes e_{\ell}}\right)

is a contraction invariant for
\left({\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{q}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}_{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
.
Applying
Lemma

III.10
, we see that the map
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence.

By applying this construction to
\mathbf{V}=(\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}},\dots,\mathbb{C}^{n_{m}})
, we get an algebraic reduction
\alpha_{\mathbf{n}}
from
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\left({\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{q}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}_{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
, where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})
.
It is clear that these maps are efficiently computable.
Thus we have constructed an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

Remark V.2
.

It is possible that no balanced tensor can be constructed from the original tensor tuple.
In this case we get a reduction to the trivial sequence
\left({0};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
, which means that every two vectors are closure equivalent because
0
is contained in every orbit closure (every tensor tuple is in the null cone).

The following lemma allows to further simplify balanced tensor tuple sequences to direct sums of the
same
tensor space.

Lemma V.3
.

Let
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{q}
and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
with
\mathbf{c}\geq\mathbf{c_{\ell}}
for all
\ell
(where
\geq
is coordinate-wise inequality).
Then there is an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{q}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}_{\mathbf{c_{\ell}}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\left({\left({\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{c}}}{\mathbf{V}}\right)^{\oplus q}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
.

Proof.

Define
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\ell}=\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{c}_{\ell}
for
\ell\in[q]
.
Note that every balanced tensor space

{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}_{\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}}{\mathbf{V}}

contains a
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
-invariant tensor
\operatorname{Id}_{\bar{c}_{\ell}}
corresponding to the identity map on
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{\ell}}

Define
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
-equivariant injective linear maps
\iota_{\ell}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}_{\mathbf{c}_{\ell}}}{\mathbf{V}}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{c}}}{\mathbf{V}}

.
By
Corollary

III.8
, it follows that
\iota
preserves and reflects closure equivalence.
By applying this construction to
\mathbf{V}=(\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}},\dots,\mathbb{C}^{n_{m}})
, we obtain an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

We can also reduce any tensor tuple sequences to one for a single
\mathsf{GL}(W)
.

Lemma V.4
.

Let
({\mathbf{a}_{1}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{1}}),\dots,({\mathbf{a}_{p}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{p}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}
and define
A_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{k,i}
and
B_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{k,i}
.
Then there is an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{A_{k}}_{B_{k}}}{W}};{\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
be a tuple of vector spaces, and let
W=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}
.
Let
\iota_{i}
be the injection of
V_{i}
into
W
as a direct summand, and let
\bar{\iota}_{i}
be the injection of
V_{i}^{*}
into
W^{*}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}^{*}

For every
({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})
we have an injective linear map
\iota_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{A}_{B}}{W}
where
A=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}
and
B=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}
, defined by
\iota_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\iota_{i}^{\otimes a_{i}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\bar{\iota}_{i}^{\otimes b_{i}}
.
This gives us an injective linear map
\iota=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\iota_{({\mathbf{a}_{k}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{k}})}
from
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}
to
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{A_{k}}_{B_{k}}}{W}
.
Let
\beta
be the standard embedding of
\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
into
\mathsf{GL}(W)
given by
\beta(\mathsf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{g}_{m})=\mathsf{g}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathsf{g}_{m}
.
Then the maps
\iota_{i},\bar{\iota}_{i}
are equivariant with respect to
\beta
, so the same is true for
\iota
, and
Lemma

III.7
shows that
\iota
preserves closure equivalence.

To prove that it also reflects closure equivalence, recall from
Lemma

IV.26
that the invariants for
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
are linearly spanned by contraction invariants of the form

\displaystyle G(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right).

(V.5)

Note that if
x\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}
and
y\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{d}}}{\mathbf{V}}
, then the tensors
\iota_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}(x)\otimes\iota_{({\mathbf{c}};\,{\mathbf{d}})}(y)
and
\iota_{({\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{c}};\,{\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{d}})}(x\otimes y)
are obtained from each other by a permutation of factors.
In addition, since for
u\in V_{i}^{*}
and
v\in V_{i}
we have
(\bar{\iota}_{i}u)\cdot(\iota_{i}v)=u\cdot v
, the injections
\iota_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}
respect contractions in the following way:

\iota_{({\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{e}_{j}};\,{\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{e}_{j}})}(\operatorname{Tr}[V_{j}]^{p}_{q}x)=\operatorname{Tr}^{P}_{Q}(\iota_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}x)

where
P=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}a_{i}+p
and
Q=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}b_{i}+q
are the indices of factors of
\iota_{({a};\,{b})}x
corresponding to the
p
-th
V_{i}
and the
q
-th
V_{i}^{*}
factors of
x
.
Using these two properties, the contraction invariant in
Eq.

V.5
can be rewritten as
G=F\circ\iota
, where
F(y_{1},\dots,y_{p})=\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}y_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}
for some permutation
\pi
.
The latter is an invariant for
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}(W^{\otimes A_{k}}\otimes(W^{*})^{\otimes B_{k}})};{\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right)
.
By
Lemma

III.10
, it follows that
\iota
reflects closure equivalence.
As in the preceding proofs, this map can be efficiently implemented (simply copy each input coordinates to the appropriate output coordinate and set all other to
0
) and hence we obtain an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

The following lemma establishes a partial converse to the preceding one:
it shows that tensor tuple sequences over one vector space can be reduced to smaller tensor tuple sequences over several vector spaces, at the expense of some overhead.

Lemma V.6
.

Let
({\mathbf{a}_{1}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{1}}),\dots,({\mathbf{a}_{p}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{p}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}
and define
A_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{k,i}
and
B_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{k,i}
.
Then there is an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{A_{k}}_{B_{k}}}{W}};{\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right)

to

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}\;\oplus\;\bigoplus_{i=2}^{m}(V_{i}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\oplus V_{1}\otimes V_{i}^{*})};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right).

Proof.

Let
W
be a vector space, and let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
be a tuple consisting of
m
spaces isomorphic to
W
.
Fix isomorphisms
\alpha_{i}\colon W\to V_{i}
and let
\bar{\alpha}_{i}=(\alpha_{i}^{-1})^{*}
be the isomorphisms
W^{*}\to V_{i}^{*}
, so that
(\bar{\alpha}_{i}u)\cdot(\alpha_{i}v)=u\cdot v
.
For every
({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}=A
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}=B
, define the map
\alpha_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}\colon W^{\otimes A}\otimes(W^{*})^{\otimes B}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}
as
\alpha_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}^{\otimes a_{i}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\bar{\alpha}_{i}^{\otimes b_{i}}
.
Let
f_{i}=\alpha_{i}\alpha_{1}^{-1}
and
g_{i}=f_{i}^{-1}=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{i}^{-1}
.
Note that
f_{1}=g_{1}=\operatorname{id}
.
We can identify
f_{i},g_{i}
with tensors in
V_{i}\otimes V_{1}^{*}
and
V_{1}\otimes V_{i}^{*}
, respectively.
Now define a map
\alpha
from
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}(W^{\otimes A_{k}}\otimes(W^{*})^{\otimes B_{k}})
to
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=2}^{m}(V_{i}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\oplus V_{1}\otimes V_{i}^{*})

given by

\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\\
=(\alpha_{({a_{1}};\,{b_{1}})}(x_{1}),\dots,\alpha_{({a_{p}};\,{b_{p}})}(x_{p}),f_{2},g_{2},\dots,f_{m},g_{m}).

The linear space isomorphisms
\alpha_{i}
induce group isomorphisms
\beta_{i}\colon\mathsf{GL}(W)\to\mathsf{GL}(V_{i})
defined as
\beta_{i}(\mathsf{g})=\alpha_{i}\mathsf{g}\alpha_{i}^{-1}
.
They can be combine into an embedding
\beta\colon\mathsf{GL}(W)\to\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
given by
\beta(\mathsf{g})=(\beta_{1}(\mathsf{g}),\dots,\beta_{m}(\mathsf{g}))
.
The maps
\alpha_{i}
are
\mathsf{GL}(W)
-equivariant in the sense that
\alpha_{i}(\mathsf{g}v)=\beta_{i}(\mathsf{g})\alpha_{i}(v)
for
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{GL}(W)
and
v\in W
, and likewise for the maps
\bar{\alpha}_{i}
.
In addition,
f_{i}
and
g_{i}
are invariant under the action of
\mathsf{GL}(W)
via
\beta_{1}
on
V_{1}
and
\beta_{i}
on
V_{i}
.
Using
Lemma

III.7
it follows that
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence.

To see that it also reflects closure equivalence, note that for every tensor
x
we have

\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\otimes a_{i}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}(g_{i}^{*})^{\otimes b_{i}}\right)\alpha_{({a};\,{b})}(x)\\
=\left(\bigotimes\alpha_{1}^{\otimes A}\otimes\bigotimes\bar{\alpha}_{1}^{\otimes B}\right)(x).

Now, every contraction invariant

\displaystyle G(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right)

for
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}(W^{\otimes A_{k}}\otimes(W^{*})^{\otimes B_{k}})};{\mathsf{GL}(W)}\right)
can be written as

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}z_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right),z_{k}=\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\otimes a_{ki}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}(g_{i}^{*})^{\otimes b_{ki}}\right)y_{k},

where
f_{1}=g_{1}=\operatorname{Id}
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{p},f_{2},g_{2},\dots,f_{m},g_{m})=\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
.
Note that the tensors
z_{k}
can be constructed from
y_{1},\dots,y_{p}
,
f_{2},g_{2}
, …,
f_{m},g_{m}
using tensor contractions (
f_{1}=g_{1}=\operatorname{Id}
can be omitted by
Lemma

IV.14
), so
this expression gives a contraction invariant
F
for
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}(V_{i}\otimes V_{1}^{*}\oplus V_{1}\otimes V_{i}^{*})};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
such that
G=F\circ\alpha
.
By
Lemmas

IV.26
and
III.10
, we conclude that
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence.

The maps
\alpha
can be efficiently computed if
W=V_{1}=\dots=V_{m}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and we take
\alpha_{i}
to be the identity maps, since then the
\alpha_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}
are also identity maps, and the
f_{i}
and
g_{i}
are the identity tensors
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}^{*}
seen as elements of
V_{i}\otimes V_{1}^{*}
and
V_{1}\otimes V_{i}^{*}
respectively.
Thus, we obtain an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

Together with the previous statements, the following lemma allows reducing any tensor tuple sequence to tuples where each tensor space is a tensor product of the different vector spaces and their duals (that is, all tensor powers are equal to one).

Lemma V.7
.

Let
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
. Suppose
M=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}>0
.
There is an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({\left({\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{\mathbf{c}}}_{\mathbf{\mathbf{c}}}}{\mathbf{V}}\right)^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\left({\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}W_{i}\otimes W_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus(p+M-1)}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{W})}\right)
.

Proof.

This is a combination of the preceding reductions.
First, use
Lemma

V.4
to reduce from
\left({({\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{c}}}{\mathbf{V}})^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\left({({\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{M}_{M}}{X})^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)
.
Then use
Lemma

V.6
with
\mathbf{a}_{k}=\mathbf{b}_{k}=(1,\dots,1)
for all
k\in[p]
to get a reduction to

\left({\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}W_{i}{\otimes}W_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}\hskip-6.0pt\oplus\bigoplus_{i=2}^{M}(W_{i}{\otimes}W_{1}^{*}\oplus W_{1}{\otimes}W_{i}^{*})};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{W})}\right).

Next, follow the proof of
Lemma

V.1
to reduce to (the right-hand side sums become tensor products)

\left({\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}W_{i}{\otimes}W_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}\hskip-6.0pt\oplus\bigoplus_{i=2}^{M}(W_{1}{\otimes}W_{1}^{*}{\otimes}W_{i}{\otimes}W_{i}^{*})};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{W})}\right).

And finally, apply
Lemma

V.3
to construct a reduction to

\left({(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}W_{i}\otimes W_{i}^{*})^{\oplus(p+M-1)}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{W})}\right)
.
∎

Now we establish a reduction of the tensor tuple sequences obtained in the preceding lemma ones that have a
constant
number of tensor factors in each summand, for a single
\mathsf{GL}(X)
.

Lemma V.8
.

For every
p,m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}\otimes V_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\left({(X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus p}\;\oplus\;(X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2})^{\oplus m}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
be an arbitrary tuple of vector spaces, and let
X=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}
.
Note that the tensor space
X^{\otimes a}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes b}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{V}^{\otimes({m\times a};\,{m\times b})}
by rearranging of factors.
Here and in the following we use the notation
m\times a=(a,\dots,a)\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.
For a permutation
\pi\in\mathsf{S}_{d}
denote by
r_{i,\pi}\colon V_{i}^{\otimes d}\to V_{i}^{\otimes d}
the map permuting the tensor factors according to the permutation
\pi
, and by
R_{i,\tau}\colon X^{\otimes d}\to X^{\otimes d}
the map applying
r_{i,\pi}
to the factor
V_{i}^{\otimes d}
.
Let
s_{i}\in V_{i}^{\otimes 2}\otimes(V_{i}^{*})^{\otimes 2}
be the tensor corresponding to the linear map
r_{i,(12)}\colon V_{i}^{\otimes 2}\to V_{i}^{\otimes 2}
swapping the two factors.
Then
S_{i}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{i-1}\operatorname{Id}_{V_{j}}^{\otimes 2}\otimes s_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=i+1}^{m}\operatorname{Id}_{V_{j}}^{\otimes 2}\in\mathbf{V}^{\otimes({m\times 2};\,{m\times 2})}\cong X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2}
corresponds to
R_{i,(12)}
.
Note that if
\tau=(pq)
is a transposition in
\mathsf{S}_{d}
, then the map
R_{i,\tau}
permuting two
V_{i}
factors can be obtained by applying
R_{i,(12)}
to the
p
-th and
q
-th factors of the product, or via contraction with
S_{i}
.
Now define the map

\displaystyle\alpha\colon\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}{\otimes}V_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}\!

\displaystyle\to\left(X{\otimes}X^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}\oplus\left(X^{\otimes 2}{\otimes}(X^{*})^{\otimes 2}\right)^{\oplus m},

\displaystyle\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p},S_{1},\dots,S_{m}).

Because
\alpha
is equivariant with respect to the embedding
\beta\colon\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})\to\mathsf{GL}(X)
given by
\beta(\mathsf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{g}_{m})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{g}_{i}
, by
Lemma

III.7
it preserves closure equivalence.

To show that it also reflects closure equivalence, we proceed as in the preceding proofs.
Consider an arbitrary contraction invariant for
\left({\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}\otimes V_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
,

\displaystyle G(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}^{\otimes m}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}R_{i,\pi}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right).

Decomposing the product of permutations into a product of transpositions, we can rewrite this as

G(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\operatorname{Tr}^{\otimes m}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}R_{i_{j},\tau_{j}}\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right).

for suitable
i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}
and transpositions
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{\ell}
.
Since the transpositions
R_{i_{j},\tau_{j}}
can be implemented by contractions with
S_{i_{j}}
, we see that
G=G\circ\alpha
for some contraction invariant
F
for
\left({(X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus p}\oplus(X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2})^{\oplus m}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)
.
As in the previous proofs,
Lemma

III.10
now shows that
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence, and we obtain an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

We can combine the preceding results to reduce from certain tensor tuple sequences for a single
\mathsf{GL}(X)
to ones for a product group
\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)
.

Lemma V.9
.

For every
p,m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an efficient algebraic reduction from

\left({(X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus p}\;\oplus\;(X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2})^{\oplus m}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)

to

\left({(X{\otimes}X^{*})^{\oplus p}\oplus(X{\otimes}X^{*}{\otimes}Y{\otimes}Y^{*})^{\oplus(m+1)}};{\mathsf{GL}(X){\times}\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right).

Proof.

First apply
Lemma

V.6
(with
m=2
and
\mathbf{a}_{k}=\mathbf{b}_{k}
equal to
(1,0)
for
p\leq k
and
(1,1)
for
p>k
) to reduce from
\left({(X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus p}\;\oplus\;(X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2})^{\oplus m}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)
to

\left((X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus p}\oplus(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*})^{\oplus m}\right.\\
\left.{}\oplus(Y\otimes X^{*}\oplus X\otimes Y^{*});\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)\right).

To balance the summand
Y\otimes X^{*}\oplus X\otimes Y^{*}
, we can use
Lemma

V.1
.
Following its proof, a Hilbert basis of the relevant cone is given by
(1,1)
, corresponding to
X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}
.
Overall, we obtain reduction to

\left({(X{\otimes}X^{*})^{\oplus p}\oplus(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*})^{\oplus(m+1)}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right).

∎

Our final technical lemma allows reducing the number of summands to a constant:

Lemma V.10
.

For every
p\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an efficient algebraic reduction from the tensor tuple sequence
\left({(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*})^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right)
to
\left({Z\otimes Z^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}\;\oplus\;(Z\otimes Z^{*})^{\oplus 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(Z)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right)
, and to
\left({Z\otimes Z^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}\oplus Z^{\otimes 2}\otimes(Z^{*})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(Z)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
X
and
Y
be arbitrary vector spaces, set
Z=X^{\oplus p}
and define
\iota_{i}\colon X\to Z
and
\bar{\iota}_{i}
as the injections into
i
-th direct summand.
Let
r,s
be the tensors in
Z\otimes Z^{*}
that correspond to the linear maps
r(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=(x_{2},\dots,x_{p},x_{1})
and
s(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=(x_{1},0,\dots,0)
, that is,
r
cyclically permutes the
p
direct summands and
s
is the projection onto the first summand.
Note that
sr^{k-1}
maps
(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
to
\iota_{1}(x_{k})=(x_{k},0,\dots,0)
.
Define a map

\displaystyle\alpha\colon(X{\otimes}X^{*}{\otimes}Y{\otimes}Y^{*})^{\oplus p}

\displaystyle\to(Z{\otimes}Z^{*}{\otimes}Y{\otimes}Y^{*})\oplus(Z{\otimes}Z^{*})^{\oplus 2},

\displaystyle\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})

\displaystyle=(y,r,s)

\displaystyle\text{where }y

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p}(\iota_{k}\otimes\bar{\iota}_{k})x_{k}.

By
Lemma

III.7
,
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence because it is equivariant with respect to the embedding
\beta\colon\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)\to\mathsf{GL}(Z)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)
given by
(\mathsf{g},\mathsf{h}\mapsto(\mathsf{g}^{\oplus p},\mathsf{h})
.

We follow the familiar recipe to prove that it also reflects closure equivalence.
Consider a contraction invariant for
\left({(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*})^{\oplus p}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right)
,

G(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=(\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{2}})\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right).

Because
y_{k}=(r^{k-1}s)\otimes(s^{*}(r^{*})^{k-1}))y=(\iota_{1}\otimes\bar{\iota}_{1})x_{k}
, we can write this as

G(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\left(\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{1}}\otimes\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{2}}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}y_{k}^{\otimes d_{k}}\right),

and since each
y_{k}
can be expressed as tensor contractions of
y
,
r
and
s
, we have
G=F\circ\alpha
for some contraction invariant
F(y,r,s)
on
Z\otimes Z^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}\oplus(Z\otimes Z^{*})^{\oplus 2}
.
Thus
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence by
Lemma

III.10
and we obtain the first of the desired efficient algebraic reduction.

For the second, observe
r=\operatorname{Tr}[Z]^{2}_{2}(r\otimes s)
and

s=\frac{1}{\dim Z}\operatorname{Tr}[Z]_{1}^{1}(\operatorname{Id}[Z]\otimes s)=\frac{1}{\dim Z}\operatorname{Tr}[Z]_{1}^{1}((r\otimes s)^{p}),

every tensor contraction of
y
,
r
and
s
can be expressed as a tensor contraction of
y
and
r\otimes s
.
Thus, if we define

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}\colon\!(X{\otimes}X^{*}{\otimes}Y{\otimes}Y^{*})^{\oplus p}

\displaystyle\to Z{\otimes}Z^{*}{\otimes}Y{\otimes}Y^{*}\oplus Y^{\otimes 2}{\otimes}(Y^{*})^{\otimes 2},

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})

\displaystyle=(y,r\otimes s),

with
y
as above, then one can proceed as above to see that
\alpha^{\prime}
preserves and reflects closure equivalence.
In this way we get the second of the desired efficient algebraic reductions.
∎

We now have all ingredients to establish the completeness of the orcit closure equivalence problems for the following simultaneous “double conjugation” action (that is, for the action of the gauge group for PEPS tensor networks with physical dimension 3).

Theorem V.11
.

The OCI problem for the tensor tuple sequence

\left({\left(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}\right)^{\oplus 3}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right)

is complete for
TOCI
.

Proof.

We need to argue that any tensor tuple sequence
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}}\right)
can be reduced to the sequence above.
This can be seen as follows.

First, use
Lemma

V.1
we reduce to a balanced tensor tuple sequence

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{q}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{c}_{k}}_{\mathbf{c}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}}\right)
.
Next, apply
Lemma

V.3
and
Lemma

V.7
to reduce to the tensor tuple sequence of the form

\left({\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}W_{i}\otimes W_{i}^{*}\right)^{\oplus r}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{W})}\right),

with
\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{M})
for some
M
.
Then, use
Lemma

V.8
to reduce from this sequence to

\left({(X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus r}\oplus(X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2})^{\oplus M}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right),

apply
Lemma

V.3
to reduce to

\left({(X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2})^{\oplus(r+M)}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right),

and
Lemma

V.9
to further reduce to

\left({(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*})^{\oplus(r+M+1)}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right).

Finally, we can use
Lemma

V.10
to reduce to

\left({X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}\oplus(X\otimes X^{*})^{\oplus 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right)

and
Lemma

V.3
to reduce to the required

\left({\left(X\otimes X^{*}\otimes Y\otimes Y^{*}\right)^{\oplus 3}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)\times\mathsf{GL}(Y)}\right).\qed

Lemma V.13

III.8
.

Using a similar idea, we can also prove completeness for another simple balanced sequence.

Theorem V.15
.

The orbit closure intersection problem for
\left({X^{\otimes 3}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 3}\oplus X\otimes X^{*}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)
is complete for
TOCI
.

Proof.

We will construct an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2}\oplus\left(X\otimes(X^{*})\right)^{\oplus 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(X)}\right)
, which has
TOCI
-complete orbit closure intersection problem by
Theorem

V.12
.
Let
P_{(12)}\in{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{2}_{2}}{X}
be the invariant tensor corresponding to the linear map
X^{\otimes 2}\to X^{\otimes 2}
swapping two tensor factors.
Consider the linear map

\alpha\colon X^{\otimes 2}{\otimes}(X^{*})^{\otimes 2}\oplus\left(X{\otimes}(X^{*})\right)^{\oplus 2}\to X^{\otimes 3}{\otimes}(X^{*})^{\otimes 3}\oplus X{\otimes}X^{*}

given by
\alpha(x,y,z)=(x\otimes\operatorname{Id}+y\otimes P_{(12)},z)
.
Note that if
\dim X\geq 2
, then the subspaces
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{2}_{2}}{X}\otimes\operatorname{Id}
and
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{1}_{1}}{X}\otimes P_{(12)}
of
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{3}_{3}}{X}
intersect only in
0
.
Indeed, consider
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{3}_{3}}{X}
as a tensor product
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{2}_{2}}{X}\otimes{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{1}_{1}}{X}
.
The matrix rank of a nonzero tensor of the form
x\otimes\operatorname{Id}
with respect to this decomposition is
1
, but the rank of a nonzero tensor of the form
y\otimes P_{(12)}
is equal to
\dim X
.
Therefore, the sum
{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{2}_{2}}{X}\otimes\operatorname{Id}+{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{1}_{1}}{X}\otimes P_{(12)}
is a direct sum and the map
\alpha
is an injective equivariant map.
By
Corollary

III.8

\alpha
preserves and reflects closure equivalence.

We almost have an efficient algebraic reduction: the case
\dim X=1
needs to be considered separately.
For
\dim X=1
the action of
\mathsf{GL}(X)
on
X^{\otimes 2}\otimes(X^{*})^{\otimes 2}\oplus\left(X\otimes(X^{*})\right)^{\oplus 2}
is trivial,
so we can take in the definition of the algebaric reduction
q(1)=2
and the first map in the sequence

\alpha_{1}(x\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes 2},y\operatorname{Id},z\operatorname{Id})=(x\operatorname{Id}^{\otimes 3}+y(\operatorname{Id}\otimes P_{(12)}),z\operatorname{Id})
, which is again an injective equivariant map (mapping invariants to invariants).
∎

Theorems

V.12
,
V.14
and
V.15
prove

-completeness of four OCI problems for four simple balanced tensor tuple sequences.
We can extend this completeness to all balanced sequences which are more complicated than these using the following lemma.

Lemma V.16
.

If
p\leq q
and
a_{k}\leq b_{k}
for all

, then there is an efficient algebraic reduction from

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}V^{\otimes a_{k}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes a_{k}}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)

to

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{q}V^{\otimes b_{k}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes b_{k}}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right).

Proof.

The proof is similar to
Lemma

V.3
.
The map

\alpha\colon\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}V^{\otimes a_{k}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes a_{k}}\to\bigoplus_{k=1}^{q}V^{\otimes b_{k}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes b_{k}}

sending

is an injective equivariant map, and therefore preserves and reflects closure equivalence.
Collecting these maps into a sequence, we obtain an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

Corollary V.17
.

The OCI problem for a tensor tuple sequence
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{q}V^{\otimes a_{k}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes a_{k}}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)

if

Proof.

The four minimal cases with

V.14
and

, the rest follows by

V.16
.
∎

V-B

Reductions and completeness for orthogonal and symplectic groups

Let
g\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
be a symmetric or symplectic form on the vector space
V
.
Interpreting

as a map from
V
to

, we get an invertible linear map equivariant with respect to the group
\mathsf{O}(V)=\mathsf{O}(V,g)
or
\mathsf{Sp}(V)=\mathsf{Sp}(V,g)

Theorem V.18
.

For every
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, there exists an efficient algebraic reduction from

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}}};{\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{V})}\right)

to
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}(V_{i}^{*})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
g_{i}\in V_{i}^{*}\otimes V_{i}^{*}
denote the tensor corresponding to the symmetric bilinear form invariant under
\mathsf{O}(V_{i})
.
It is clear that
\alpha(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p},g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
defines an equivariant map with respect to the inclusion
\beta\colon\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{V})\to\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})
, hence
\alpha
preserves closure equivalence by
Lemma

III.7
.

By
Lemma

IV.28
, the invariants for
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{V}^{\otimes\mathbf{a}_{p}}};{\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
are linearly spanned by the contraction invariants

G(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi_{i}}\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{p}x_{k}^{d_{k}}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}^{\otimes f_{i}}\right).

Note that

Thus
\alpha
reflects closure equivalence by
Lemma

III.10
.
The map
\alpha
is clearly efficiently computable and hence we obtain an efficient algebraic reduction.
∎

We also have an opposite reduction from orbit closure intersection problems for general linear groups to similar problems for orthogonal groups.

Theorem V.19
.

Let
({\mathbf{a}_{1}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{1}}),\dots,({\mathbf{a}_{p}};\,{\mathbf{b}_{p}})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{W}^{\otimes\mathbf{c}_{k}}};{\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{W})}\right)
.

Proof.

As mentioned at the beginning of the section,
W_{i}\cong W_{i}^{*}
as representations of
\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{W})
.
Therefore is suffices to construct a reduction from
\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{W}}};{\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{W})}\right)

\left({\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{W}^{\otimes c_{k}}};{\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{W})}\right)

Let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
be a tuple of vector spaces, and let
W_{i}=V_{i}\oplus V_{i}^{*}
.
Equip each space

For

\beta_{i}\colon\mathsf{GL}(V_{i})\to\mathsf{O}(W_{i})

Combining

\beta\colon\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})\to\mathsf{O}(\mathbf{W})
.

Let
\alpha_{i}\colon V_{i}\to W_{i}

V_{i}
into
W_{i}

They induce linear maps
\alpha_{({\mathbf{a}};\,{\mathbf{b}})}\colon{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{V}}\to{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{b}}}{\mathbf{W}}

III.7
.
To see that it also reflects closure equivalence, consider any contraction invariant for

Corollary V.20

We have

V-C

Reductions for symmetric groups and
GI
-hardness

In this section we consider the action of the symmetric group
\mathsf{S}_{n}
on an
n
-dimensional space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and tensor spaces constructed from it.
Since the symmetric group is finite, the orbits are always closed, so closure equivalence is just equivalence under the action of the group.
The graph isomorphism problem can be easily presented as an equivalence problem for an action of the symmetric group.
We prove that the latter can be reduced to an orbit closure intersection problem for a tensor tuple sequence for the general linear group.
As a consequence, we find that
\textnormal{{GI}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}}
.

Lemma V.26
.

The graph isomorphism problem
GI
reduces to
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{S}_{n}}\right)
.

Proof.

The reduction is given by mapping a pair of graphs to the corresponding pair of adjacency matrices.
In more detail, let
(G_{1},G_{2})
be an instance of the graph isomorphism problem.
We can assume that the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have the same number of vertices
n
and that the vertex sets of both graphs are
[n]
, and we denote the edge sets by
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.
The reduction maps this data to the instance
(n,x_{1},x_{2})
for
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{S}_{n}}\right)
, where
x_{1}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{1}}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
and
x_{2}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{2}}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
.

It is clear that the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic if and only if
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are equivalent under the action of
\mathsf{S}_{n}
.
Indeed, the graph isomorphisms from
G_{1}
to
G_{2}
are exactly the permutations
\sigma\in\mathsf{S}_{n}
such that
(i,j)\in E_{1}\Leftrightarrow(\sigma(i),\sigma(j))\in E_{2}
, or, equivalently,
\sigma\cdot x_{1}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{1}}e_{\sigma(i)}\otimes e_{\sigma(j)}=x_{2}
in terms of the group action
\mathsf{S}_{n}
on
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}
.
Since
\mathsf{S}_{n}
is a finite group,
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are equivalent if and only if they are closure equivalent.
Thus we have the desired reduction.
∎

Remark V.27
.

The same argument shows that
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{S}_{n}}\right)
is equivalent to the graph isomorphism problem for weighted graphs with edge weights in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proposition V.28
.

There is an efficient algebraic reduction from
\left({(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{S}_{n}}\right)
to
\left({V^{\otimes 2}\oplus V^{\otimes 3}\oplus(V^{*})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)
.

Proof.

For each
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, define an injective linear map
\alpha_{n}\colon V^{\otimes 2}\to V^{\otimes 3}\oplus V^{\otimes 2}\oplus(V^{*})^{\otimes 2}
by
\alpha_{n}(x)=(h,x,g)
, where
g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{*}\otimes e_{i}^{*}
and
h=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
.
Let
\beta\colon\mathsf{S}_{n}\to\mathsf{GL}(V)
be the inclusion of
\mathsf{S}_{n}
into
\mathsf{GL}(V)
as permutation matrices.
Because the tensors
g
and
h
are
\mathsf{S}_{n}
-invariant, we have
\alpha_{n}(\mathsf{g}x)=\beta(\mathsf{g})\alpha_{n}(x)
for
\mathsf{g}\in\mathsf{S}_{n}
.
Thus
\alpha_{n}
preserves closure equivalence by
Lemma

III.7
.

To see that it also reflects closure equivalence, recall from
Lemmas

IV.30
and
IV.26
that the invariant algebra
\mathbb{C}[(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}]^{\mathsf{S}_{n}}
is linearly spanned by the contraction invariants of the form

G(x)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\left(x^{\otimes d}\otimes h^{\otimes f_{1}}\otimes g^{\otimes f_{2}}\right).

For every such
G\in\mathbb{C}[(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes 2}]^{\mathsf{S}_{n}}
we can find an invariant
F\in\mathbb{C}[V^{\otimes 2}\oplus V^{\otimes 3}\oplus(V^{*})^{\otimes 2}]^{\mathsf{GL}(V)}
, namely,

F(x,y,z)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\pi}\left(x^{\otimes d}\otimes y^{\otimes f_{1}}\otimes z^{\otimes f_{2}}\right)

such that
G=F\circ\alpha_{n}
.
We conclude from
Lemma

III.10
that the map
\alpha_{n}
reflects closure equivalence.
Thus the maps
\alpha_{n}
define an algebraic reduction.
It is efficient because the maps are also efficiently computable (including as a function of
n
).
∎

Theorem V.29
.

The orbit closure intersection problem
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({V^{\otimes 2}\oplus V^{\otimes 3}\oplus(V^{*})^{\otimes 2}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)
is
GI
-hard.
Therefore,
\textnormal{{GI}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}}
.
In particular, the complete orbit closure intersection problems listed in §
I-A
are all
GI
-hard.

V-D

Reductions for unitary groups

In this section we observe that the tensor isomorphism problem for the unitary group can be reduced to an orbit closure intersection problem for the general linear group with closed orbits.
The main ingredient is the following result, which we state in the language of this article.
In its statement the complex reductive group
\mathsf{G}
is considered as a Lie group; as such it has a maximal compact subgroup
\mathsf{K}
that is unique up to conjugation.
For example, if
\mathsf{G}
is the general linear group
\mathsf{GL}_{n}
then
\mathsf{K}
can be taken to be the unitary group
\mathsf{U}_{n}
.

Theorem V.30

(
[
39
, Theorem 8.2]
)
.

Let
X
be a representation of a connected reductive group
\mathsf{G}
.
Let
\mathsf{K}
be a maximal compact subgroup of
\mathsf{G}
, and choose a
\mathsf{K}
-invariant Hermitian inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
X
.
For
x\in X
, let
\widehat{x}\in X^{*}
denote the linear functional defined by
\widehat{x}(x^{\prime}):=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
.
Then, the following are equivalent for any two
x,y\in X
:

1.

x\sim_{\mathsf{K}}y
;

2.

(x,\widehat{x})\sim_{\mathsf{G}}(y,\widehat{y})
in
X\oplus X^{*}
;

3.

(x,\widehat{x})\approx_{\mathsf{G}}(y,\widehat{y})
in
X\oplus X^{*}
.

It is proved by using the Kempf–Ness theorem to observe that
(x,\widehat{x})\in X\oplus X^{*}
is a minimum norm point in its
\mathsf{G}
-orbit, which therefore in particular is closed, and using the fact that the minimum norm points form a
\mathsf{K}
-orbit.

We remark that
x\mapsto(x,\widehat{x})
is not a polynomial map due to the antilinearity of
x\mapsto\widehat{x}
.
Indeed, since the unitary group is
not
an algebraic group (it is compact), this map is not an algebraic reduction between
p
-sequences of representations as defined in
Definition

III.3
.
Nevertheless,
Theorem

V.30
states it has exactly the right properties to relate orbit problems for
\left({X};{\mathsf{K}}\right)
and
\left({X\oplus X^{*}};{\mathsf{G}}\right)
.
Therefore, if the map is efficiently computable and we obtain a Karp reduction between the corresponding computational problems.

In particular, this is case for tensor tuple sequences (but it holds more generally).
We summarize this in the following theorem, where
OE
denotes the
orbit equivalence problem
, which is defined like
OCI
but for equivalence
\sim
in place of closure equivalence
\approx
(
Definition

II.4
); this is also called the
isomorphism problem

[
12
]
.

Theorem V.31
.

For every tensor tuple sequence

X=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{p}{\textstyle\bigotimes\nolimits^{\mathbf{a}_{k}}_{\mathbf{b}_{k}}}{\mathbf{V}}

there is a Karp reduction from the orbit equality problem
\textnormal{{OE}}\left({X};{\mathsf{U}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
to
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({X\oplus X^{*}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
, as well as to
\textnormal{{OE}}\left({X\oplus X^{*}};{\mathsf{GL}(\mathbf{V})}\right)
.
As a consequence, we have
\textnormal{{TI}}_{\mathsf{U}}\subseteq\textnormal{{TOCI}}\cap\textnormal{{TI}}
.

Proof.

The existence of the Karp reduction follows from
Theorem

V.30
and the preceding discussion.

To see the inclusion of complexity classes, recall that the unitary group orbit equality problem
\textnormal{{OE}}\left({V^{\otimes 3}};{\mathsf{U}(V)}\right)
is complete for
\textnormal{{TI}}_{\mathsf{U}}
by
[
12
, Theorem 1.7]
.
Thus, it suffices to show that this problem is in
TOCI
as well as in
TI
.
The former follows directly from the first part of the proof, because
\textnormal{{OCI}}\!\left({V^{\otimes 3}\oplus(V^{*})^{\otimes 3}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)
is in
TOCI
.
For the latter, we use that
\textnormal{{OE}}\left({V^{\otimes 3}\oplus(V^{*})^{\otimes 3}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)
reduces to
\textnormal{{OE}}\left({V^{\otimes 3}};{\mathsf{GL}(V)}\right)
by
[
59
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
8
, Theorem 7.3]
, and the latter is complete for
TI
.
∎
