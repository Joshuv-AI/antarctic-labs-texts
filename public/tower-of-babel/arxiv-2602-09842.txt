Step-Size Stability in Stochastic Optimization: A Theoretical Perspective
Fabian Schaipp, Robert M. Gower, Adrien Taylor

Abstract
We present a theoretical analysis of stochastic optimization methods in terms of their sensitivity with respect to the step size. We identify a key quantity that, for each method, describes how the performance degrades as the step size becomes too large. For convex problems, we show that this quantity directly impacts the suboptimality bound of the method. Most importantly, our analysis provides direct theoretical evidence that adaptive step-size methods, such as SPS or NGN , are more robust than SGD . This allows us to quantify the advantage of these adaptive methods beyond empirical evaluation. Finally, we show through experiments that our theoretical bound qualitatively mirrors the actual performance as a function of the step size, even for nonconvex problems.

Introduction

Training machine learning models requires algorithms that are at the same time efficient and robust. Several studies have shed light on the effect of subtle choices in architecture, loss function or optimizer on training efficiency and stability
(
42
;
16
;
28
)
. Specifically
42
focus on the effect on the range of learning rates, for which training proceeds smoothly. This is not surprising, as it is widely accepted in the optimization and machine learning community that the learning rate is arguably the most important hyperparameter to tune
(
4
)
.

For this paper, we will focus on the effect of the
optimization algorithm
on training stability. In the past, several methods have been designed to facilitate or even fully avoid the issue of tuning the learning rate (more in
Section

2
below). While empirical evaluation in machine learning is closely paying attention to the issue of learning-rate sensitivity, optimization theory on the other hand puts more focus on convergence rates (where it is sufficient that the optimal rate is attained for
some
step size).

To fill in this gap, here we compare – with a theoretical framework – how robust several
stochastic
optimization methods are with respect to their step size. In particular, we focus on stochastic gradient descent (
SGD
), the stochastic Polyak step size (
SPS
), non-negative Gauss-Newton steps (
NGN
), and stochastic proximal point (
SPP
). Throughout the paper we will use the term
sensitivity
to refer to the final loss/suboptimality as a function of (the scale of) the step size: for small step sizes, all methods will only progress slowly; for large step sizes however, some methods behave more stably than others.

We summarize our contributions below.

Summary and contributions.

•

We identify a key quantity within our theoretical framework, which we call the
stability index
, for determining how the method’s suboptimality behaves as a function of the step size. Our theory covers a general family of model-based methods and applies to
SGD
,
SPS

SPP
, and partially
NGN
in the convex setting.

•

Previous
empirical
results suggest that
SPS
,
NGN
, and
SPP
are always less sensitive to large step sizes compared to
SGD
. We will show that this is provably true.
In particular, our results show that
NGN
and
SPS
are always at least as stable as
SGD
. For
SPS
, this holds for any guess of the optimal value/lower bound; interpolation is not necessary to obtain an advantage, but it helps.
Our analysis also reveals the key quantities to determine the gain in stability, which are related to interpolation and the under-estimation of the optimal (batch) loss.

•

In several convex and nonconvex toy experiments (linear/logistic regression, training a
ResNet
on
CIFAR10
), we track the theoretical quantities previously obtained to explain the improved stability of
SPS
,
NGN
, and
SPP
. We show that the actual performance and the theoretical suboptimality bound match closely in predicting the range of learning rates that give good performance. Surprisingly, this is also true for nonconvex problems, where our theory is not applicable.

Limitations.

The goal of this paper is predominantly to explain empirically observed phenomena with a general theoretical framework. Even though our experiments suggest a close match between theory and practice, we leave it open how to exploit this for practical applications. Further, parts of our theory are restricted to convex problems, and only to a specific family of methods; in particular, it does not cover momentum, or preconditioning methods such as
Adam
, which are prevalent in practice.

6
Conclusion

We have presented a theoretical analysis for step-size stability of several stochastic optimization methods. Within our framework, we prove that adaptive methods such as
SPS
or
NGN
are always more stable than
SGD
.

An open question is how tracking the stability index
\delta_{t}
could be leveraged in practice, for example to terminate unstable runs early. Another direction for future work would be to extend our analysis to other methods, for example
Adam
, or to a broader class of problems.

\displaystyle f_{x_{t}}(x_{t+1},s_{t})

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\gamma_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4f_{t}}\gamma_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{4},

and
\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|x_{t+1}-x_{t}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\gamma_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\hskip-12.91663pt\Psi_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\gamma_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\underbracket{\big[\frac{1}{4f_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{4}+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big]}_{=\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}}(1+\frac{\alpha_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{2f_{t}})=\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{2\gamma_{t}}}

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\frac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

Altogether, we obtain
\delta_{t}^{{\texttt{NGN}}{}}=f_{t}-\Psi_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.
From
\gamma_{t}\leq\alpha_{t}
it follows that
\delta_{t}^{{\texttt{NGN}}{}}\leq\delta_{t}^{{\texttt{SGD}}{}}
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

7
.

We have

\Psi_{t}-f(x_{t},s_{t})=f(x_{t+1},s_{t})-f(x_{t},s_{t})+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|x_{t+1}-x_{t}\|^{2}.

For any
\alpha_{t}>0
, it holds

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y,s_{t})+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|y-x_{t}\|^{2}\geq\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y,s_{t}).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi_{t}-f(x_{t},s_{t})&\geq\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y,s_{t})-f(x_{t},s_{t}).\end{split}

(14)

On the other hand, due to convexity of
f(\cdot,s)
we have

\displaystyle\hskip-12.91663ptf(x_{t+1},s_{t})\geq f(x_{t},s_{t})+\langle g_{t},x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq f(x_{t},s_{t})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\rho}\|x_{t+1}-x_{t}\|^{2},

where we use Young’s inequality with
\rho>0
in the second step. Setting
\rho=\alpha_{t}
, we get

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}-f(x_{t},s_{t})\geq-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

(15)

Combining (
14
) and (
15
) , we have

\displaystyle\hskip-12.91663pt\Psi_{t}-f(x_{t},s_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\max\{-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2},\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y,s_{t})-f(x_{t},s_{t})\}

\displaystyle=-\min\{\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2},f(x_{t},s_{t})-\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y,s_{t})\}.

From (
5
), this implies
\delta_{t}^{{\texttt{SPP}}{}}\leq\min\{\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2},f(x_{t},s_{t})-\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y,s_{t})\}
.
∎

Appendix B
Discussion of Related Work

B.1
Comparison of Convergence Theorems to the Literature

Here we consider our resulting convergence results
Theorems

3
and
4
, when instantiated with each model, and compare to what is known in the literature. For the linear model and the resulting
SGD
method, we recover the standard rates as pointed out in
Section

4.1
.

For the remaining methods (
SPS
), (
NGN
), and (
SPP
) we have shown in
Lemma

5
,
Lemma

6
and
Lemma

7
, respectively, that these methods have a stability constant
\delta_{t}
that is less or equal than that of of
SGD
, that is

\delta_{t}\;\leq\;\delta_{t}^{{\texttt{SGD}}{}}=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

(16)

Using this bound in
Theorems

3
and
4
we immediately arrive at a convergence result for
SPS
and
NGN
. However, this convergence result will be pessimistic, since we bound away the possible stability benefits of
SPS
,
NGN
and
SPP
when using (
16
).

Using the pessimistic upper bound (
16
)
in
Theorem

3
with a constant step size
\alpha_{t}\equiv\alpha
we arrive at the convergence rate

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{T}-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{D^{2}}{2T\alpha}+\alpha\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}]}{2T}.

(17)

By further assuming the loss is Lipschitz continuous with
\|g_{t}\|\leq G
and a step size of
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}
this gives a
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
complexity.
This is exactly the rate of
SGD
in the convex and Lipschitz setting,
see for the an early such proof
39
and Theorem 9.7 in
17
for a didactic reference.

For
SPS
(introduced by
24
and called
SPS
max
therein), as far as we are aware, the only result for convex and Lipschitz losses also requires interpolation
(
24
, Thm. C.1)
, which gives a
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
complexity.

As for the
NGN
method, there currently exists only an analysis of the method for the Lipschitz-smooth setting. Thus (
17
) is the first complexity for
NGN
in the convex non-smooth setting.

Finally for the
SPP
method, our result (
17
) matches that of
12
under the same assumptions.

B.2
Additional Remarks on Related Work

The theory by
24
suggests that
SPS
can achieve the standard convergence rates without knowledge of the Lipschitz(-smoothness) constant.
More concretely,
24
shows that if
\alpha_{t}=\alpha
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and if each
f(\cdot,s)
is convex and
L_{s}
-smooth, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t})-f(x_{\star})]\leq\frac{\|x_{1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}}{\rho T}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}\alpha}{\rho}.

Here
\rho:=\min\{\frac{1}{2L_{\max}},\alpha\}
and
L_{\max}=\max_{s}L_{s}
.
However, this theory can not explain why
SPS
is less sensitive to the choice of
\alpha
: for
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2L_{\max}}
, the bound is increasing in
\alpha
linearly (as
\rho=\frac{1}{2L_{\max}}
). Moreover, in the smooth case, it holds

\frac{f(x_{t},s_{t})-\inf_{z}f(z,s_{t})}{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2L_{s_{t}}}\geq\frac{1}{2L_{\max}}.

For
ResNet20
on
CIFAR10
, the term on the left is around
10^{-1}
in early training, suggesting that
2L_{\max}\geq 10^{1}
and hence the bound will be increasing for
\alpha\geq 10^{-1}
. But in practice,
SPS
achieves the best performance for much larger values of
\alpha
(in between
1
and
10
, see
Fig.

4
).

For stochastic proximal point (
SPP
), several analyses show that convergence can be proven for any step size, for example
41
; however, these analyses derive rates for
SPP
under various set of (additional) assumptions, such as smoothness, strong convexity, or interpolation; our analysis on the other hand focuses on the one-step stability index
\delta_{t}
, which allows to compare stability across methods.

Appendix C
Experiments: Supplementary Material

Dataset generation for linear regression.

The data generation procedure was adapted from
24
and the reference therein: we first generate a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with entries from a standard normal. We add one to all entries to introduce a bias. We then scale each column with a factor drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
multiplied by ten. We now sparsify
\mathbf{A}
by setting each entry to zero with probability
1-30\tfrac{\ln(n)}{n}
. We finally scale each column to have norm
10
(this step affects mainly the scaling of the step size).

We then generate a vector
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with entries from a standard normal, re-scaled such that
\|\hat{x}\|=1
. We generate
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}\hat{x}
; in the noisy setting, we add a vector with entries from a standard normal to
\mathbf{A}
.

Appendix D
Generalizations of the
NGN
Step Size

The
NGN
model (
10
) is constructed as square-root
\to
linearize
\to
square. This idea can be generalized to other functions on the non-negative halfspace, and their respective inverse function. Below, we derive the model-based update when using instead
\log

\to
linearize
\to

\exp
. We will see that the resulting update is related to the Lambert–
W
function.

General maps.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\phi:U\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the following:

(B1)

For any
x\in U
we have
\psi(\phi(x))=x
.

(B2)

\psi
is differentiable and convex on
\mathbb{R}
.

(B3)

\phi
is differentiable on
U
with
\phi^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in U
.

Second,
(B1)
implies that
\phi
is injective. Therefore,
\phi
must be either monotonically increasing or decreasing. If
\phi
is concave, then its first derivative is non-increasing, and hence
\phi^{\prime}(0)>0
is sufficient for
(B3)
to hold with
U=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

We will usually choose
U=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
or
U=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
depending on the loss function being strictly positive or non-negative. (In the latter case, assume that there exists a continuous extension of
\phi^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\phi^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
).

Then, for
g\in\partial f(x,s)
, define the model

\displaystyle f_{x}(y,s)=\psi\big(\phi(f(x,s))+\phi^{\prime}(f(x,s))\langle g,y-x\rangle\big).

(18)

First, note that Assumption
(A1)
holds due to convexity of
\psi
. Denote
f_{t}:=f(x_{t},s_{t})
, and
let
u_{t}=\phi^{\prime}(f_{t})
. It holds

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{x_{t}}(y,s_{t})+\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|y-x_{t}\|^{2}

if and only if

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\alpha_{t}u_{t}v_{t}g_{t},\quad v_{t}:=\psi^{\prime}\big(\phi(f_{t})+\phi^{\prime}(f_{t})\langle g_{t},x_{t+1}-x_{t}\rangle\big).

Therefore, we get the condition

\displaystyle v_{t}=\psi^{\prime}\big(\phi(f_{t})-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big).

We can compute

\displaystyle f_{x_{t}}(x_{t+1},s_{t})=\psi\big(\phi(f_{t})-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big),

and hence

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta_{t}&=f_{t}-\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}}\|x_{t+1}-x_{t}\|^{2}-f_{x_{t}}(x_{t+1},s_{t})\\
&=f_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{t}u_{t}^{2}v_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}-\psi\big(\phi(f_{t})-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big).\end{split}

(19)

Let us focus on the last term: using convexity, we have
\psi(a+b)\geq\psi(a)+\psi^{\prime}(a)b
, and hence

\displaystyle\psi\big(\phi(f_{t})-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big)\geq\psi(\phi(f_{t}))-\psi^{\prime}(\phi(f_{t}))\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

Now, from
(B1)
and the chain rule it follows that
1=\psi^{\prime}(\phi(x))\phi^{\prime}(x)
holds for all
x\in U
. Due to
(B3)
, it follows
\psi^{\prime}(\phi(f_{t}))=1/\phi^{\prime}(f_{t})=1/u_{t}
.
Together with
(B1)
, we get

\displaystyle\psi\big(\phi(f_{t})-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big)\geq f_{t}-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}.

Plugging back into (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq f_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{t}u_{t}^{2}v_{t}^{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}-\big(f_{t}-\alpha_{t}v_{t}u_{t}\|g_{t}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}[2v_{t}u_{t}-u_{t}^{2}v_{t}^{2}]\leq\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2},

where the last step uses
1-2u_{t}v_{t}+u_{t}^{2}v_{t}^{2}=(1-u_{t}v_{t})^{2}\geq 0
.

Using log and exponential.

Consider (
18
) with
\psi=\exp
and
\phi=\log
, that is

\displaystyle f_{x}(y,s)=\exp\big(\log{f(x,s)}+\frac{1}{f(x,s)}\langle g,y-x\rangle\big).

(20)

The model-based update (
2
) has the necessary and sufficient optimality condition

\displaystyle 0=\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{f(x_{t},s_{t})}\langle g_{t},y-x_{t}\rangle\big)g_{t}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}(y-x_{t}).

Using the Ansatz
y=x_{t}-\gamma_{t}g_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}\exp\big(-\gamma_{t}\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{f(x_{t},s_{t})}\big).

(21)

Let
W_{0}
be the principal branch of the Lambert–
W
function, which is defined as the inverse function of
z\mapsto ze^{z}
. Multiplying (
21
) by
\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{f(x_{t},s_{t})}\exp\big(\gamma_{t}\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{f(x_{t},s_{t})}\big)
, we can see that (
21
) has the solution

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}=\frac{f(x_{t},s_{t})}{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}W_{0}(\alpha_{t}\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{f(x_{t},s_{t})})\quad\text{if }g_{t}\neq 0\text{ and else }\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}.

(22)

The solution lies on the principal branch as
\alpha_{t}\frac{\|g_{t}\|^{2}}{f(x_{t},s_{t})}>0
.

Denote again
f_{t}=f(x_{t},s_{t})
. Further, we have
u_{t}=\frac{1}{f_{t}}
and
\alpha_{t}u_{t}v_{t}=\gamma_{t}
. Hence,
v_{t}=\exp(\log f_{t}-\frac{\gamma_{t}}{f_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2})=f_{t}\exp(-\frac{\gamma_{t}}{f_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2})=\frac{f_{t}\gamma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}
. We can plug this into (
19
), we get

\displaystyle\delta_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2}-\exp(\log f_{t}-\frac{\gamma_{t}}{f_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2})=f_{t}-\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2}-f_{t}\exp(-\frac{\gamma_{t}}{f_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\frac{\gamma_{t}^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}}\|g_{t}\|^{2}-\frac{f_{t}\gamma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}.

To the best of our knowledge, the adaptive step size (
22
) is novel in the literature. In preliminary experiments, we observe that (
22
) is also very stable with respect to the choice of
\alpha_{t}
. However, we do not further investigate the behavior of this new step size here, as it would distract from the focus of this paper. Analyzing (
22
) might be interesting future work.
