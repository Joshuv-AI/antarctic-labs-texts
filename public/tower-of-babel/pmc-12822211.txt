Longitudinal profiling of gut microbiota dynamics in kidney transplant recipients

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota undergoes substantial alterations in kidney transplant recipients, which are linked to postoperative complications and renal function recovery. However, the underlying mechanisms of these associations remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, longitudinal cohort study at our center. Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the most effective treatment for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Although advancements in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive therapies, and postoperative monitoring have significantly improved long-term graft survival, various complications continue to pose substantial challenges [2]. These complications include T cell- or antibody-mediated rejection, metabolic disorders induced by immunosuppressive drugs, and infections of the respiratory and urinary systems [3–5]. Therefore, it is imperative to identify the underlying factors contributing to these complications and to develop targeted interventions that enhance patients’ quality of life and improve graft survival rates.

In recent years, substantial evidence from population-based and animal studies has demonstrated a close relationship between gut microbiota and kidney transplantation. For example, the immunosuppressive agent mycophenolate mofetil can induce intestinal mucosal damage through interactions with gut microbiota, while tacrolimus blood concentrations are closely associated with gut microbial composition [6, 7]. Additionally, kidney transplant recipients who experience diarrhea exhibit significant alterations in their gut microbiota [8]. However, there is still a lack of prospective longitudinal cohort studies that systematically investigate postoperative gut microbiota dynamics, complications, host immune status, and renal function recovery in transplant recipients.

In this study, we collected fecal samples from 88 patients at multiple time points (preoperative and 7, 14, 30, 90, 180 days post-surgery, referred to as baseline, T7, T14, T30, T90, T180) and conducted 16S rRNA sequencing. Comprehensive data, including postoperative complications, peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets, renal function, and other clinical indicators were recorded. Fecal sequencing results revealed significant shifts in gut microbiota species diversity and composition following transplantation. Notably, at 30 days post-surgery, Enterobacteriaceae enrichment was associated with an increased risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) and reduced lymphocyte subset counts. Conversely, patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) greater than 30 mL/min at 30 days demonstrated significant enrichment of Bifidobacterium in the gut microbiota.

Method

Study design and sample collection

This study aimed to enroll renal transplant recipients who received treatment at the Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University in June 2023. Data will be collected at multiple time points: preoperatively and postoperatively at 7, 14, 30, 90, and 180 days (referred to as baseline, T7, T14, T30, T90, T180), and subsequently every six months. The collected data will include routine diagnostic and treatment information, as well as biological samples such as blood, feces, and urine. This study is a prospective observational cohort study without interventional treatments. It has been reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Center and the Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University (Approval No. 2023-KY-083-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study involved 88 kidney transplant recipients and collected a total of 309 fecal samples.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Recipients of kidney transplantation performed at Zhujiang Hospital on or after June 1, 2023. (2) Expressed willingness to participate by providing written informed consent. (3) Demonstrated ability and commitment to comply with follow-up assessments.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Recipients of multi-organ transplants. (2) Seropositivity for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). (3) Presence of any severe cardiovascular disease, malignant tumor, metabolic disorder, or any other condition that, in the opinion of the investigators, constitutes a contraindication to kidney transplantation.

Fecal sample collection process

Patients were instructed to clean the perianal area and utilize a fecal collector following defecation. Each sample weighed approximately 3 to 10 g and required no further processing. Samples were labeled with patient information and collection data, and subsequently stored at -80 °C in a dedicated fecal freezer.

Definition of urinary tract infection (UTI)

A UTI is diagnosed based on a positive midstream urine culture or an abnormal white blood cell count in routine urinalysis, in conjunction with lower urinary tract symptoms.

Enterobacteriaceae enrichment was defined as a ≥ 1.5-fold increase in operational taxonomic unit (OTU) counts between consecutive fecal samples from patients who provided at least three samples.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing

The fecal samples were extracted from the total genomic DNA of the microbial community following the instructions provided by the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, Shanghai, China) [9]. The integrity of the extracted genomic DNA was assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, while the DNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Using the extracted DNA as a template, PCR amplification of the V3-V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene was performed with the upstream primer 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and the downstream primer 806R (5’-GACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) [10], which included the barcode sequence. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 4 µL of 5× TransStart FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of upstream primer (5 µM), 0.8 µL of downstream primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA, with the total volume adjusted to 20 µL. The amplification procedure was as follows: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72℃ for 30 s. This was followed by a final extension at 72℃ for 10 min, and the samples were then stored at 4℃ (PCR instrument: ABI GeneAmp® 9700 model). A 2% agarose gel was used to recover the PCR products, which were purified using a DNA gel recovery and purification kit (PCR Clean Up Kit, China Yuhua). The concentration and quality of the recovered products were assessed using a Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA Sequencing Kit to construct a library of purified PCR products involves the following steps: (1) linker ligation; (2) employing magnetic beads to screen and eliminate self-ligated adapter fragments; (3) enriching library templates through PCR amplification; and (4) utilizing magnetic beads to recover PCR products and obtain the final library. Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.). The raw sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1208417.

Using Fastp [11] (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, Version 0.19.6), we performed quality control on the double-ended raw sequencing data using FLASH [12] (http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash, Version 1.2.11). The stitching process involved the following steps: (1) Filter reads with tail quality values below 20, applying a 50 bp sliding window. If the average quality value within the window is below 20, trim the bases from the end of the read. Additionally, filter out reads shorter than 50 bp and remove any reads containing N bases; (2) Merge paired-end reads into a single sequence based on their overlap, requiring a minimum overlap length of 10 bp; (3) Set the maximum allowed mismatch ratio in the overlap region of the concatenated sequence to 0.2, screening out non-matching sequences; (4) Distinguish samples based on the barcodes and primers at the beginning and end of the sequences, adjusting the sequence direction accordingly. The barcode allows for 0 mismatches, while the maximum allowed primer mismatch is 2. Using UPARSE v7.1 [13, 14] software (http://drive5.com/uparse/). Based on a similarity of 97%, the quality control concatenated sequence was subjected to OTU clustering and chimeras were removed. In order to minimize the impact of sequencing depth on subsequent Alpha and Beta diversity data analysis, all sample sequences were flattened to 20,000. After flattening, the average sequence coverage (Good’s coverage) of each sample still reached 99.09%. Using RDP classifier [15] (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/, Version 2.11). Compare the Silva 16S rRNA gene database (v138) for OTU species taxonomy annotation, with a confidence threshold of 70%, and calculate the community composition of each sample at different species classification levels.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis

Peripheral whole blood samples were collected from fasting subjects using EDTA-anticoagulated tubes. The experimental procedure included two distinct panels for analyzing T-cell functional subsets and B/NK cell populations. For T-cell subset characterization, 50 µL of whole blood was mixed with 25 µL of a pre-optimized antibody cocktail containing CD3-APC-Cy7 (4AUCHT1), CD4-APC (SK3), CD45RA-FITC (4AH1100), CD197-PE (4AG043H7), CD28-PerCP/Cy5.5 (4AC028.2), HLA-DR-Pacific Blue (4ALN3), CD8-APC-A700 (B9.11), CD45-KRO (J33), and CD27-PC7 (1A4CD27). The mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min, followed by erythrocyte lysis using hemolytic reagent for 30 min. After washing with PBS and centrifugation, the cells were resuspended and analyzed. For B/NK cell typing, a no-wash protocol was employed: 50 µL of whole blood was combined with 25 µL of antibodies targeting CD3-FITC (UCHT1), CD16-PE (CB16), CD56-PE (MEM-188), CD45-PerCP (2D1), and CD19-APC (HiB19). Following a 30-minute incubation and lysis step, the samples were directly analyzed without further washing.

Data acquisition was performed on DxFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Lymphocytes were gated based on high CD45 expression (CD45^(high)) and low side scatter (SSC^(low)). Subpopulations were further defined as follows: CD4+/CD8+ T-cell subsets were classified using CD45RA, CD197, CD28, and HLA-DR markers to distinguish naive, active, memory, and effector T cells, while B cells were identified as CD3−CD19+ and NK cells as CD3−CD16+CD56+. Absolute cell counts were calculated via the instrument’s volumetric method (cells/µL = target cell count / sample volume). Data analysis utilized Kaluza software (v2.1), with fluorescence compensation matrices optimized using single-stained controls. Antibody batch consistency and cell viability (via DAPI exclusion) were rigorously monitored to ensure experimental reliability. The detailed gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Bioinformatics analysis

All data analysis is conducted on the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) as follows: Using the mothur software (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators), we calculate alpha diversity metrics such as ACE and Shannon index. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test is employed to analyze intergroup differences in alpha diversity. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA), based on the Bray-Curtis distance algorithm, is utilized to assess the similarity of microbial community structures between samples. Additionally, we combine this with the Adonis test to determine whether the differences in microbial community structure between sample groups are statistically significant. Using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA > 2, p < 0.05) (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/LEfSe), we identify bacterial groups with significant differences in abundance from the phylum to genus level between different groups. Finally, species are selected for correlation network analysis based on Spearman correlation coefficients.

Statistical methods

The results of the measurement data in the table are presented as medians with quartile ranges, while the remaining measurement data are expressed as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). GraphPad Prism 8 and IBM SPSS V22 were utilized for plotting and data analysis. Two sets of quantitative data were analyzed using either an unpaired t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while three or more sets of data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test. The Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curve was statistically evaluated using the log-rank test. All p-values are two-tailed.

Results

Clinical data and fecal samples were collected both prior to and within six months following the surgery for a comprehensive longitudinal analysis. A total of 309 fecal samples were collected, with the number of samples at each time point presented in Table 2.

Fecal sample information

We first compared the gut microbiota species diversity of 88 patients at six time points before and after surgery. The analysis results indicate that, compared to preoperative levels, the alpha diversity (measured by the ACE and Shannon index) of the intestinal microbiota in patients after kidney transplantation significantly decreased but exhibited a gradual recovery trend over time (Fig. 1A and B). The PCoA (beta diversity) results revealed significant differences in the gut microbiota structure of patients on days 7, 14, and 30 after kidney transplantation compared to preoperative levels. However, there were no significant differences on days 90 and 180 after surgery when compared to preoperative levels (Fig. 1C).

At the phylum level, we observed a significant expansion trend in the phylum Proteobacteria within 30 days post-surgery, followed by a gradual decline. Conversely, the phylum Bacteroidetes exhibited a significant decrease immediately after surgery, with a gradual rebound occurring 90 days post-surgery (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Figs. 1 A-D). At the family level, the Enterobacteriaceae displayed a trend similar to that of Proteobacteria, while the Lachnospiraceae and Bacteroidetes experienced a significant decrease after surgery, followed by a gradual increase after 30 days (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Figs. 1E-H).

We conducted a gut typing analysis on 309 fecal samples, and the results indicated that the gut microbiota formed two distinct clusters. Type 1 (p-Firmicutes) primarily includes preoperative samples, as well as postoperative samples collected at 90 and 180 days, along with a smaller number of postoperative samples collected at 7, 14, and 30 days. In contrast, Type 2 (p-Proteobacteria) predominantly consists of the majority of postoperative samples collected at 7, 14, and 30 days (Fig. 1F). Further analysis of the Microbial Dysbiosis Index (MDI) at various time points revealed that, compared to preoperative levels, the dysbiosis index significantly increased at all postoperative time points, with the highest index observed at 7 days postoperatively (Fig. 1G).

The results above indicate significant changes in the species diversity and composition of gut microbiota in patients following kidney transplantation. Within 30 days post-surgery, a clear state of microbiota dysbiosis was observed, characterized by decreased diversity and the amplification of conditional pathogens.

Enterobacteriaceae was enriched within 30 days post-surgery

The previous results indicated significant changes in the gut microbiota of kidney transplant patients 30 days post-surgery. Consequently, we conducted a further analysis of the gut microbiota characteristics during this specific time frame. Through LEfSe analysis, we found that, compared to preoperative levels, the characteristic gut microbiota of patients 30 days after surgery included Enterobacteriales, Enterobacteriaceae, Gamma Proteobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Escherichia-Shigella. This finding aligns with the results presented in the first part (Fig. 2A and B). Additionally, we analyzed the characteristic microbiota at 7 and 14 days post-surgery and observed similar findings (Supplementary Figs. 2A and B).

The phylum Proteobacteria, particularly the family Enterobacteriaceae, is closely associated with the rise of inflammatory outbreaks and severe infections [16–18]. We conducted a longitudinal analysis of Enterobacteriaceae and examined the trend among patients who provided at least three samples.

Patients exhibiting a 1.5-fold increase in Enterobacteriaceae at two fecal sampling points were classified as the enrichment group (Supplementary Table). In terms of species composition, the enriched group demonstrated a significant increase in Proteobacteria and Enterobacteriaceae, alongside a notable decrease in Bacteroidetes and Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Figs. 2C-E). Additionally, there was a significant increase in the dysbiosis index (Fig. 2D).

We further analyzed peripheral blood inflammatory factors in the enriched group of patients, specifically C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). The results indicated that while there were no significant differences in CRP or IL-6 levels between the groups, IL-6 showed a tendency toward increase in the enriched group (Fig. 2E). Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between the Enterobacteriaceae and the risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) in kidney transplant patients [19]. We documented urinary tract infection events in patients within 30 days post-surgery (Supplementary Table) and found that patients with enrichment in Enterobacteriaceae had a markedly increased risk of UTIs (Fig. 2F).

This section of the results indicates that Enterobacteriaceae is prevalent in the gut of kidney transplant patients 30 days post-surgery. The enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae heightens the risk of UTIs in these patients.

Gut dysbiosis was associated with impaired immune status

This cohort study collected peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets at multiple time points to observe longitudinal changes in the immune status of kidney transplant patients. At three time points—before surgery, 30 days and 90 days after surgery—CD4 + T cells exhibited a trend of initially decreasing and then rebounding, while B cells first expanded and subsequently declined. In contrast, NK cells gradually decreased, and CD8 + T cells gradually increased (Fig. 3A).

The increasing in Enterobacteriaceae is considered as gut dysbiosis [16]. To investigate the potential relationship between gut dysbiosis and immune status in patients following kidney transplantation, we analyzed the characteristic changes in lymphocyte subsets among patients with enriched Enterobacteriaceae at 30 days post-surgery. In the Enterobacteriaceae enrichment group, although no significant differences were observed, most lymphocyte subpopulations—including CD45⁺ lymphocytes, T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, and NKT cells—exhibited a decreasing trend (Fig. 3B-H and Supplementary Fig. 3A-C). The heat map results illustrating the correlation between gut microbiota (at the family level) and peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets before surgery, as well as 30 and 90 days after surgery, revealed no significant association between Enterobacteriaceae and specific lymphocyte subgroups. However, a significant negative correlation was identified between Erysipelatoclostridiaceae and T cells, CD45 + lymphocytes, CD4 + T cells, and CD4 + CD8 + T cells at 90 days post-surgery (Fig. 3I). These findings suggest that patients after kidney transplantation experienced gut dysbiosis, which is associated with impaired immune status.

Bifidobacterium may benefit the recovery of renal function

The gut microbiota is closely associated with the recovery of renal function following kidney transplantation [20]. We analyzed whether Enterobacteriaceae impacts renal function recovery in patients 30 days post-surgery. The results indicated that, compared to the non-enriched group, there were no significant differences in eGFR, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (Scr) levels in the enriched group (Fig. 4A-C).

A substantial body of clinical data and registry reports indicate that renal function recovery after kidney transplantation follows a predictable trajectory, with Scr levels typically reaching a relative plateau within 30 days post-surgery [21, 22]. Based on this pattern, we categorized the patients into two groups according to their eGFR: eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m² (n = 46) and eGFR ≤ 30 mL/min/1.73 m² (n = 20) (Table 3). We then assessed the potential association between post-operative renal function recovery and gut microbiota composition at this 30-day time point. We first compared the gut microbiota species diversity between two groups of patients. The analysis results indicate that there was no significant difference in alpha diversity (ACE, Shannon index) or beta diversity (PCoA) of the gut microbiota (Fig. 4D-F). Through composition analysis, we found that at the phylum level, the abundance of Proteobacteria decreased in the eGFR >30 mL/min group, while the abundance of Firmicutes increased. At the family level, the Enterobacteriaceae family exhibited a relative decrease in the eGFR >30 mL/min group, whereas the Lachnospiraceae family increased (Fig. 4G-H). Compared to the eGFR ≤ 30 mL/min group, the characteristic gut microbiota of patients with eGFR >30 mL/min included the genus Bifidobacterium and specifically Bifidobacterium longum (Fig. 4I and Supplementary Fig. 4). Correlation heatmap analysis between gut microbiota and renal function indicators revealed a significant positive correlation between Bifidobacterium and eGFR, as well as a significant negative correlation with Scr (Fig. 4J). These results indicate that patients with eGFR >30 mL/min, 30 days post-surgery, had significantly enriched Bifidobacterium, suggesting that Bifidobacterium may play a role in the recovery of renal function after surgery.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a prospective cohort analysis of kidney transplant patients at our center. We first examined the longitudinal changes in gut microbiota following surgery and identified significant alterations in both species diversity and composition. Further analysis indicated a notable enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae in the gut of kidney transplant patients 30 days post-surgery, which is closely associated with an increased risk of UTIs and immune system dysfunction. Importantly, patients who exhibited better recovery of transplanted kidney function 30 days after surgery showed an enrichment of intestinal Bifidobacterium, suggesting that these bacteria may play a role in the recovery of postoperative kidney function.

There have been numerous reports on the gut microbiota of kidney transplant patients; however, relatively few studies have focused on longitudinal sample analysis. The samples collected in this study were derived from a prospective cohort study conducted by our center, providing, for the first time, preoperative and postoperative samples at 7, 14, 30, 90, and 180 days, which hold significant reference value. Furthermore, this cohort study retains a substantial number of biological samples for the mid to long-term following surgery, facilitating research on long-term events such as rejection and graft loss.

In this study, we found significant changes in the species diversity and composition of gut microbiota in patients within one month after surgery, consistent with other research reports, which may be related to surgery, antibiotics, immunosuppressants, and other factors [23, 24]. In addition, we found that the species diversity and composition structure of the gut microbiota at 90 and 180 days after surgery were similar to those before surgery, suggesting that the patient’s gut microbiota gradually recovered to the preoperative structure over time. This conclusion still requires more mid to long term fecal samples for verification.

It is worth noting that longitudinal sample analysis revealed a significant increase in conditional pathogenic bacteria, such as Proteobacteria and Enterobacteriaceae, in the 30 days post-surgery, while Bacteroidetes and Lachnospiraceae exhibited a significant decrease. This finding aligns with previous reports [25]. Notably, these changes gradually returned to preoperative levels by 90 days after surgery. However, some studies indicate that the increase in Proteobacteria can persist for up to 22 years following surgery [26]. The composition of the long-term gut microbiota in the patients of this study remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Proteobacteria is facultative anaerobic bacteria closely associated with the host inflammatory response and opportunistic infections [27, 28], with Enterobacteriaceae being the most common cause of UTIs. In this study, LEfSe analysis identified Enterobacteriaceae as the key differential bacteria in the gut before and 30 days after surgery. Current evidence indicates that this expansion arises from a multifactorial interplay of immunosuppression, antibiotic exposure (for infection prophylaxis or treatment), dietary influences, and transplant-related physiological changes. These factors collectively contribute to a reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria, disruption of the butyrate–PPARγ signaling axis in intestinal epithelial cells, and an elevation in colonic nitrate and oxygen availability. This altered luminal environment facilitates the dysregulated proliferation of Enterobacteriaceae [18]. Further analysis indicated that the enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae was associated with an increased risk of UTIs 30 days post-surgery. However, we did not conduct 16S rRNA sequencing on urine samples, and the relationship between Enterobacteriaceae and UTIs requires further validation.

Peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets are an important means of monitoring the immune status after kidney transplantation, closely related to postoperative rejection and infection. We found that certain lymphocyte subsets were reduced in the Enterobacteriaceae enrichment group. The increase in Enterobacteriaceae was accompanied by a decrease in Bacteroidetes and Lachnospiraceae, the latter of which may be associated with a reduction in lymphocyte subpopulations. Research has indicated that a decline in the abundance of Bacteroidetes can negatively affect immune regulation [29]. Furthermore, short-chain fatty acids produced by the Lachnospiraceae in the intestine play a crucial role in the differentiation and function of intestinal barrier and immune cell [30]. Therefore, the reduction of intestinal commensal bacteria may be a significant factor contributing to the impairment of host immune status. Further experiments are needed to confirm the causal relationship among them.

Finally, we examine the relationship between postoperative 30-day renal function recovery and gut microbiota. The results indicated that there was no significant correlation between Enterobacteriaceae and renal function. Additionally, there was no notable difference in the species diversity and composition of gut microbiota between the eGFR >30 and eGFR ≤ 30 mL/min groups. However, there was an enrichment of Bifidobacterium in the eGFR >30 mL/min group. The application of microbiota-based therapies in kidney transplantation has not been extensively investigated. Currently, there are reports of its use in treating postoperative infectious diseases, such as Clostridium difficile infection, UTIs, and incision infection [31–33]. Although the causal relationship between Bifidobacterium and transplanted kidney function remains unclear in this study, it suggests the potential for developing related gut microbiota therapies, such as probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation.

Despite our findings, this study has several limitations. First, the samples we collected were obtained from a single center, necessitating validation in multiple centers. Second, the 16S rRNA sequencing with 97% OTU clustering cannot detect strain-level differences that may affect transplant outcomes. Additionally, the V3-V4 primers used may underrepresent certain clinically relevant taxa. Our analysis also did not include intestinal metabolites. Future studies using shotgun metagenomics and metabolomics could offer deeper functional insights. Third, this study primarily focuses on postoperative events occurring within three months following kidney transplantation, such as rejection and graft loss, which require ongoing follow-up, monitoring, and collection of biological samples. Fourth, our study cohort included both adult and pediatric kidney transplant recipients. Owing to the comparatively lower number of pediatric kidney transplant cases, it was not feasible to conduct stratified analyses by age group at this stage. We are actively expanding the sample size through continued enrollment and suggest that future studies focus on more homogeneous populations to facilitate age-specific investigations.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of this study indicated significant changes in the species diversity and composition of gut microbiota in kidney transplant patients following surgery. The enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae in the gut was associated with an increased risk of UTIs and dysfunction of the host immune status. Additionally, Bifidobacterium may play a role in the recovery of postoperative renal function.
