Binno: A 1st-order method for Bi-level Nonconvex Nonsmooth Optimization for Matrix Factorizations Journal: EURO Journal on Computational Optimization
Laura Selicato, Flavia Esposito, Andersen Ang

Abstract
In this work, we develop a method for nonconvex, nonsmooth bi-level optimization and we introduce Binno, a first order method that leverages proximal constructions together with carefully designed descent conditions and variational analysis. Within this framework, Binno provably enforces a descent property for the overall objective surrogate associated with the bi-level problem. Each iteration performs blockwise proximal-gradient updates for the upper and the lower problems separately and then forms a calibrated, block-diagonal convex combination of the two tentative iterates. A linesearch selects the combination weights to enforce simultaneous descent of both level-wise objectives, and we establish conditions guaranteeing the existence of such weights together with descent directions induced by the associated proximal-gradient maps. We also apply Binno in the context of sparse low-rank factorization, where the upper level uses elementwise ℓ 1 \ell_{1} penalties and the lower level uses nuclear norms, coupled via a Frobenius data term. We test Binno on synthetic matrix and a real traffic-video dataset, attaining lower relative reconstruction error and higher peak signal-to-noise ratio than some standard methods.

Introduction

Bi-level optimization problems consist of two nested optimization tasks organized in a hierarchical structure.
The upper-level problem determines variables that influence the lower-level problem.
A solution of a bi-level problem is optimal with respect to (wrt) both levels, but under a hierarchy: the upper-level decision anticipates and guides the optimal response of the lower level.
Such optimization settings naturally arise in decision science and learning problems, where dependencies exist between two coupled optimization processes
dempe2020bilevel
,
del2023bi
,
del2025penalty
.

In this paper, we are interested in the following bi-level problem

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big\{\psi_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\coloneqq f_{1}(\bm{x})+g_{1}(\bm{y})+H(\bm{x},\bm{y})\Big\}\\
\displaystyle\quad\,\textrm{s.t.}\,\penalty\ (\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big\{\psi_{2}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\coloneqq f_{2}(\bm{x})+g_{2}(\bm{y})+H(\bm{x},\bm{y})\Big\},\end{array}

(1)

where the upper level problem concerns the minimization of a non-convex nonsmooth function
\psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to(-\infty,+\infty]

with

•

f_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
and
g_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
are convex, proper and lower semicontinuous functions;

•

H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
a
C^{1}
function block-wise (with respect to one variable at a time).

Referring to (
1
), we note that the lower level problem has the same structure as the upper level one, with
f_{2}\neq f_{1}
and
g_{2}\neq g_{1}
.
Problem (
1
) with
\psi_{2}=0
frequently arises in machine learning scenario, where two variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are governed by regularizers
f_{1}
and
g_{1}
that encode constraints or feasible regions.
A classic example arises when these functions represent indicator functions of half-space constraints
combettes2011proximal
,
parikh2014proximal
.

For convex inner problems and strongly convex (often smooth) outer objectives, first-order bi-level methods with provable rates are available.
A prominent line is the Sequential Averaging Method (SAM) and its bi-level specialization BiG-SAM, which view bi-level optimization as a fixed-point selection problem and average a proximal-gradient step for the inner composite with a (contractive) gradient step for the outer objective; sublinear
O(1/k)
rates are known under standard Lipschitz/strong-convexity assumptions
sabach2017first
.
Recent variants relax projections, incorporate inertial or conditional-gradient updates, or target “simple" convex bi-level problems
cao2024accelerated
,
giang2024projection
.
However, two important gaps remain: (i) most analyses assume convexity (often strong convexity) and smoothness at the outer level; (ii) existing SAM-type schemes average one upper-level step with one inner fixed-point map, while block-structured, composite, possibly nonconvex models with explicit proximal treatment at both levels have received less attention, as far as we known.

Motivated by Proximal Alternating
Linearized Minimization (PALM) for nonconvex, nonsmooth single-level composites
bolte2014proximal
, we propose a bi-level generalization that executes blockwise proximal-gradient updates for both levels and then forms a calibrated convex combination of the upper- and lower-driven iterates to steer the sequence toward an upper-level preferred solution within the lower-level solution set.
This design preserves the modularity and per-block simplicity of proximal methods while embedding bi-level guidance directly into the iteration.

Proximal gradient update

In optimization problems, in the form of (
1
) with
\psi_{2}=0
, the proximal gradient method
martinet1970breve
,
combettes2011proximal
,
beck2017first
is a widely used solution approach.
It aims to solve optimization problems in this form:

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(\bm{x})+q(\bm{x})

(2)

where
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, differentiable,
L
-smooth (gradient is
L
-Lipschitz) and
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex, proper and lower semicontinuous.
The proximal gradient update run iterations rules as:

\bm{x}_{k+1}=\textrm{prox}_{q}^{\nu}\big(\bm{x}_{k}-\nu\nabla p(\bm{x}_{k})\big),

where
k\in\mathbb{N}
is iteration,
\nu>0
is stepsize, and
\textrm{prox}_{q}^{\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the proximal operator defined as

\textrm{prox}_{q}^{\nu}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg\{q(\bm{\xi})+\dfrac{1}{2\nu}\|\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\bigg\},M_{\nu f}\coloneqq\min_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg\{q(\bm{\xi})+\dfrac{1}{2\nu}\|\bm{\xi}-\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\bigg\},

with
M_{\nu f}
is the Moreau envelope associated to
f
.
Under certain assumptions on
p,q,\nu
(see
beck2017first
), the sequence
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
produced by proximal gradient update converges to a global minimizer of the Problem (
2
).

The following lemma is useful later.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 12.30,
Bauschke2017
)
.

If
f
is convex, proper and lower semicontinuous, then
M_{\nu f}\in C^{1}
and for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\nabla M_{\nu f}=\frac{1}{\nu}(\bm{x}-\textrm{prox}_{\nu f}(\bm{x}))
.
Consequently, its gradient is
1/\nu
Lipschitz continuous.

We use
\bm{G}
to denote the proximal gradient map of the proximal gradient operator.
For problem (
2
), let
\partial q
denotes a subgradient of function
q
, then

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}\bm{x}_{+}&=&\textrm{prox}_{q}^{\nu}(\bm{x}-\nu\nabla p(\bm{x}))&=&\bm{x}-\nu\bm{G}(\bm{x})\\
\bm{G}(\bm{x})&=&\frac{1}{\nu}\big(\bm{x}-\textrm{prox}_{q}^{\nu}(\bm{x}-\nu\nabla p(\bm{x}))\big)&\in&\nabla p(\bm{x})+\partial q(\bm{x}-\nu\bm{G}(\bm{x})).\end{array}

Proximal Alternating Linearized Minimization (PALM)

Since the structure of each level of our Problem

It considers

\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}f(\bm{x})+g(\bm{y})+H(\bm{x},\bm{y}),

that is Problem (

The PALM algorithm performs the proximal gradient update alternatively on each subproblems of (

\bm{x}_{k+1}=\textrm{prox}_{f}^{\nu}\big(\bm{x}_{k}-\nu\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}_{k})\big),\penalty\ \bm{y}_{k+1}=\textrm{prox}_{g}^{\nu}\big(\bm{y}_{k}-\nu\nabla_{\bm{y}}H(\bm{x}_{k+1},\bm{y}_{k})\big).

Sequential Averaging Method (SAM)

Consider the problem

\min_{\bm{x}}\big\{\varphi(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})+g(\bm{x})\big\},

(3)

with
f
smooth function (Lipschitz gradient
L_{f}
) and
g
proper, lower semicontinuous, convex function.
Consider a nonexpansive map
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a contraction
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, SAM generates the sequence

\bm{x}_{k}=\alpha_{k}\,S(\bm{x}_{k-1})+(1-\alpha_{k})\,T(\bm{x}_{k-1}),\quad\text{with}\penalty\ \alpha_{k}\in(0,1],\alpha_{k}\downarrow 0,\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}=\infty,

and converges to a point
\bm{x}^{\star}\in\operatorname{Fix}(T)\coloneqq\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:T(\bm{x})=\bm{x}\}
.
Also, it satisfies the variational inequality

\langle\bm{x}^{\star}-S(\bm{x}^{\star}),\,\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\rangle\geq 0
for all
\bm{x}\in\operatorname{Fix}(T)
; thus
\bm{x}^{\star}
is the fixed point according to
S
.
In a bi-level context, for the inner problem as (
3
) and outer problem
\min_{x\in X^{\star}}\omega(x)
, with
\omega
strongly convex, smooth, and
\nabla\omega
is
L_{\omega}
-Lipschitz, define the ProxGrad map

T(\bm{x})=\textrm{prox}^{t}_{g}\bigl(\bm{x}-t\nabla f(\bm{x})\bigr),\qquad t\in\bigl(0,\,1/L_{f}\bigr],

which is nonexpansive and satisfies
\operatorname{Fix}(T)=X^{\star}
, the solution set of the inner problem.
For the outer problem, set the contraction

S(\bm{x})=\bm{x}-s\nabla\omega(\bm{x}),\qquad s\in\Bigl(0,\,\frac{2}{L_{\omega}+\sigma}\Bigr].

With these choices, SAM is to:

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\textrm{prox}^{t}_{g}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k-1}-t\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k-1})\bigr),

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{k-1}-s\nabla\omega(\bm{x}_{k-1}),

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\bm{z}_{k}+(1-\alpha_{k})\bm{y}_{k},

and the iterates converge to
\bm{x}^{\star}\in X^{\star}
solving the bi-level task via the first-order optimality condition

\langle\nabla\omega(\bm{x}^{\star}),\,\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}\rangle\geq 0
, for all
\bm{x}\in X^{\star}
.

Proposition 1
.

For
f_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}\to\mathbb{R}
being the element-wise
\ell_{1}
norm, then
\|\bm{S}\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{1}\sqrt{mr}
for any
\bm{S}\in\partial f_{1}
.

Proof.

The function
f_{1}(\bm{X})=\lambda_{1}\|\bm{X}\|_{1}=\lambda_{1}\sum_{ij}|x_{ij}|
is not differentiable at
x_{ij}=0
but subdifferentiable:

\partial\|\bm{X}\|_{1}=\left\{\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}:p_{ij}\in\begin{cases}\text{sign }x_{ij}\quad\text{if }x_{ij}\neq 0;\\
p\in[-1,1]\quad\text{if }x_{ij}=0.\end{cases}\quad\right\}.

Let
\bm{S}\in\lambda_{1}\partial\|\bm{X}\|_{1}
be any element of the subdifferential.
Then

\|\bm{S}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{S}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{ij}s_{ij}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\sum\limits_{ij}|s_{ij}|^{2}}=\lambda_{1}\sqrt{mr}
.
∎

Proposition 2
.

For
f_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}\to\mathbb{R}
as the nuclear norm, then
\|\bm{S}\|_{2}\leq 2\gamma_{1}
for any
\bm{S}\in\partial f_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
, the nuclear norm
\|\bm{X}\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\sigma_{i}(\bm{X})}
is not differentiable but subdifferentiable.
Consider the SVD
\bm{X}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
with
k=\textrm{rank}(\bm{X})
,
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
,
\bm{\Sigma}=\textrm{Diag}(\sigma_{i}(\bm{X}))\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
, the subdifferential of
\|\bm{X}\|_{*}
is
watson1992characterization

\partial\|\bm{X}\|_{*}=\big\{\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}+\bm{W}\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ \bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r},\penalty\ \bm{U}^{\top}\bm{W}=\bm{0},\penalty\ \bm{W}\bm{V}=\bm{0},\penalty\ \|\bm{W}\|_{2}\leq 1\big\}.

We show
\|\partial f_{2}(\bm{X})\|_{2}\leq c_{2}
in
lemma

3
.
Let
\bm{S}\in\gamma_{1}\partial\|\bm{X}\|_{*}

\|\bm{S}\|_{2}=\gamma_{1}\|\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}+\bm{W}\|_{2}\leq\gamma_{1}\left(\|\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}\|_{2}+\|\bm{W}\|_{2}\right)\leq\gamma_{1}(1+1)=2\gamma_{1}
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

For the elementwise matrix
\ell_{1}
-norm and a matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}

\big\|\bm{X}-\textrm{prox}^{\nu}_{\lambda_{1}\|\cdot\|_{1}}(\bm{X})\big\|_{2}\leq\nu\lambda_{1}\sqrt{mr}

The operator
\textrm{prox}^{\nu}_{\lambda_{1}\|\cdot\|_{1}}

\Big[\bm{X}-\textrm{prox}^{\nu}_{\lambda_{1}\|\cdot\|_{1}}(\bm{X})\Big]_{ij}=\begin{cases}\nu\lambda_{1}&\hskip-8.53581pt\text{if }\bm{X}_{ij}<\nu\lambda_{1},\\

Lemma 7
.

For the nuclear norm, then

\big\|\bm{X}-\textrm{prox}^{\nu}_{\gamma_{1}\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\bm{X})\big\|_{2}\leq\gamma_{1}\nu

Proof.

Consider the nuclear norm, then
\textrm{prox}^{\nu}_{\gamma_{1}\|\cdot\|_{*}}
as

Then,

\hskip-2.84526pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big\|\bm{X}-\text{SVT}_{\gamma_{1}\nu}(\bm{X})\big\|_{2}=\big\|\bm{U}\big(\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{D}_{\gamma_{1}\nu}(\bm{\Sigma})\big)\bm{V}^{\top}\big\|_{2}&=&\hskip-2.84526pt\|\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{D}_{\gamma_{1}\nu}(\bm{\Sigma})\|_{2}\\
&=&\hskip-2.84526pt\displaystyle\max_{i}\big|\sigma_{i}-[\sigma_{i}-\gamma_{1}\nu]_{+}\big|.\end{array}

We have two cases

•

Case 1

\sigma_{i}>\gamma_{1}\nu
: then

\big|\sigma_{i}-[\sigma_{i}-\gamma_{1}\nu]_{+}\big|=\big|\sigma_{i}-(\sigma_{i}-\gamma_{1}\nu)\big|=|\gamma_{1}\nu|=\gamma_{1}\nu
.

•

Case 2

\sigma_{i}\leq\gamma_{1}\nu

\big|\sigma_{i}-[\sigma_{i}-\gamma_{1}\nu]_{+}\big|=\big|\sigma_{i}-0\big|=|\sigma_{i}|=\sigma_{i}\leq\gamma_{1}\nu

.

Thus
\|\bm{X}-\text{SVT}_{\gamma_{1}\nu}(\bm{X})\|_{2}\leq\gamma_{1}\nu
.
∎

5.1
Dataset

The synthetic dataset is generated as a rectangular data matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{100\times 80}
designed to exhibit a low-rank, sparse structure perturbed by mild noise.
Specifically, two latent factors
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{100\times 5}
and
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times 80}
are sampled so that approximately
30\%
of their entries are nonzero, with nonzero values drawn from a standard normal distribution.
Their product
\bm{M}_{\star}=\bm{X}\bm{Y}
serves as the clean signal and has nominal rank
r=5
.
To model measurement imperfections, we add small, entrywise independent Gaussian perturbations with standard deviation
0.01

\bm{M}=\bm{M}_{\star}+\bm{N}
.
This construction provides a controlled testbed in which the ground-truth low-rank structure and sparsity pattern are known while observations remain realistically noisy.

The real dataset is a traffic video database, consisting of 254 video sequences of highway traffic in Seattle, collected from a single stationary traffic camera over two days
1467355

chan2005classification
.
The database contains a variety of traffic patterns and weather conditions.
Each video was recorded in color with a resolution of 320 x 240 pixels with between 42 to 52 frames at 10 fps.
Each sequence (clip) was converted to grayscale, resized to 80 x 60 pixels, and then clipped to a 48 x 48 window over the area with the most total motion.
The fixed viewpoint makes the sequences well suited to low-rank/sparse modeling of background-foreground dynamics and related video decomposition tasks.

5.2
Metrics

We compare
Binno
with respect to NMFLS, NSA-v1, and NSA-v2 in terms of Peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), reconstruction error, and computational time.
These metrics jointly assess fidelity, structure-preserving accuracy, and efficiency.
Reconstruction error as Frobenius-norm relative error, computed with

Err=\lVert\bm{M}-\bm{L}\rVert_{F}/\lVert\bm{M}\rVert_{F},

where
\bm{M}
is the observed matrix and
\bm{L}
its estimate low-rank.
This quantity, standard in low-rank modeling and RPCA, serves as a concise proxy for overall reconstruction quality
[
HalkoMartinssonTropp2011
]
.
PSNR quantifies the fidelity of a reconstruction by comparing the maximum representable signal level to the average power of the reconstruction error, and is reported on a logarithmic (decibel) scale to accommodate wide dynamic ranges.
It is widely used for quantitative comparisons in image/video reconstruction and compression
[
WangBovik2006
]
.
Operationally, PSNR is computed from the mean squared error (MSE) between the reference and the reconstructed data, smaller MSE yields larger PSNR, according to

PSNR=10\log_{10}\left({\frac{{MAX}^{2}}{\mathit{MSE}}}\right).

where
MAX
denotes the peak representable value.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new approach to solving non-convex and non-smooth bi-level optimization problems.
We introduce a novel algorithm, called Binno, which is grounded in solid theoretical considerations based on the use of proximal point methods, descent conditions, and variational properties of the involved functions.
This framework allows
Binno
to preserve the descent property of the overall solution of the problem.

We also present a practical application of our theoretical method to the sparse low-rank approximation problem, which frequently arises in real-world scenarios where one seeks to extract meaningful information from large data matrices while maintaining a sparse representation.

Experiments on both synthetic and real datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of
Binno
compared to several state-of-the-art algorithms in this field, showing the power of
Binno
outperforms traditional methods.
