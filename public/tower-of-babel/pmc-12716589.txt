Developing emergency care systems in low-income and middle-income countries: a scoping review to examine the role of leadership and governance
Authors: Georgina Phillips, Deepak Sharma, Gerard O’Reilly, Lorena Romero, Peter Cameron
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMore knowledge and resources are required to strengthen ‘leadership and governance’ (L+G) as a central building block to further develop emergency care (EC) systems in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).ObjectivesThis scoping review aimed to examine and map the impact of individual, collective or institutional L+G on the development of EC systems (prehospital and facility-based) in LMICs.Eligibility criteriaEnglish language publications from January 2005 to April 2024 that linked any L+G action with the development and capacity of everyday EC in LMICs, specifically excluding disaster responses.Sources of evidenceMedline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), CINAHL, Web of Science (Clarivate), Central (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), Global Health (Ovid) and select grey literature.Charting methodsData from all eligible papers were jointly extracted using a piloted tool developed from the literature and WHO’s EC Systems Framework. L+G descriptors included level (from clinical to national) and components (informed by Siddiqi et al’s LMIC health system ‘good governance’ framework and a synthesis of EC policy documents). Impact of L+G on EC systems and key lessons were extracted from each publication.ResultsFrom an initial 9713 items, 129 papers were included for final analysis and divided by EC component: prehospital (n=35), facility-based (n=53) and ‘whole of EC system’ (n=41). Qualitative and descriptive papers were most common, and 72 out of a possible 131 LMICs were represented. Findings were heterogeneous across all building blocks of EC systems and for different components of leadership and/or governance. Cross-cutting L+G themes were identified that demonstrated consistent impact across all EC systems development: government recognition, vision and human rights framing; coalition-building for effective partnerships and trained, empowered EC clinicians demonstrating emotionally intelligent, transformational leadership.ConclusionsApplying new models such as Theories of Change and Social Network Analysis concepts may assist to illuminate how effective L+G is attained, what are the essential components and how these influence EC systems for better patient-centred outcomes. Further understanding the role of L+G for EC systems has utility for future EC clinician leadership training and policy-maker awareness, to strengthen resilience of overall health systems against likely future shocks.

Introduction

Health systems require effective leadership and governance (L+G) to ensure equitable, efficient and transparent distribution of healthcare that meets the needs of populations. In 2007, the WHO identified L+G as the most complex, yet critical component of six building blocks underpinning any health system, encompassing the stewardship of resources, intelligence, policies, coalitions and service design to ensure accountable, safe and effective health outcomes.1 2 Although often associated with governments, L+G is also the responsibility of individuals within health systems who influence service delivery and outcomes in hospitals, clinics and the broader health system.3 In low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), poor L+G compromises health workforce motivation, skill mix and retention,4 5 in contrast to collaborative and accountable L+G styles which aid health system team function and resilience.6 Clinician leaders can drive quality improvements and ensure equity and people-centred health policies and practice.7 8

Emergency care (EC) systems integrate with primary, critical and operative care to address all urgent illness and injury across the life course,9 although in many LMICs, they remain absent or underdeveloped, despite predicted life-saving efficacy.1012 Conceptualised as comprising prehospital and facility-based components in the WHO framework, EC systems are underpinned by core building blocks.13 In the Pacific regional EC context, these building blocks have been modified as: Human Resources (and Training), Infrastructure and Equipment, Data, Processes and L+G, and this contextual adaptation may have utility for other low-resource settings.14 In order to rapidly develop capacity for EC in LMICs, the WHO produced a range of resources for policy-makers and clinicians that facilitate EC action plans, training and standards to meet Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and World Health Assembly (WHA) expectations.9 15 Current WHO tools address workforce training, infrastructure guidelines, clinical registries and triage as examples of building block resources, but lack sufficient attention to L+G.16 All EC building blocks intersect to enhance and amplify each other in a complex system, with L+G arguably the most crucial component to ensure function of all other building blocks.17

Knowledge about EC leadership experience and impact is growing but largely arises from high-income country (HIC) contexts.18 Although L+G is central in building and strengthening EC systems in LMICs where it is most needed, it remains latent without better understanding the nature, role and enablers for long-term impact. Therefore, this scoping review aims to examine and map emerging evidence linking individual, collective or institutional L+G to the establishment, building or strengthening of any component of routine EC systems in LMICs. Through this approach, we seek to illuminate what types of L+G activities occur pertaining to EC systems in LMICs, their impact and the evidence gaps. This will provide directions for future action-based research into the critical link between L+G and EC systems development in LMICs.

Methods

Design and inclusion criteria

We followed the Joanna Briggs Institute recommendations for conducting a scoping review19 and report each component according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA) Checklist.20 Our protocol was publicly registered in March 2024 in Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8WFPR)21

Our research question ‘What is the impact of L+G on EC systems development in LMICs?’ contained three core elements:

L+G—as an integrated ‘building block’ concept, previously defined by the WHO.1 In recognition that ‘leadership’ and ‘governance’ are commonly identified and researched as distinct constructs, our paper also includes each of these within this first element, with the ultimate intention to weave them together during our interpretation. We use the broad and inclusive definition from Onyura et al as “any activity that involves the stewarding or exercise of influence over the change and transformation of health care service delivery, culture, resources or policy in the public interest. It can be undertaken formally or informally by clinical and non-clinical actors who have other designated responsibilities within health systems organizations.’’7 and incorporate all actors from individual clinician leaders up to governing bodies. Search strategy

Following development and piloting of the search strategy, assisted by an experienced research librarian, the following six databases were interrogated: Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), CINAHL, Web of Science (Clarivate), Central (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) and Global Health (Ovid). The search strategy used a combination of database specific subject headings, free text terms and synonyms that covered the three core elements articulated above. Our search retrieved English language publications from January 2005 to April 2024, including primary research studies, reviews, letters, policy, commentary and perspective papers. This time period was chosen to capture all relevant publications before and after two key documents published in 2007: the first globally recognised endorsement of EC as integral to universal healthcare systems in the WHA Resolution 60.22,23 and the dissemination of the WHO health system building block framework incorporating L+G.1 The complete search strategy is available in online supplemental Appendix 1. In addition, a grey literature search of the first 100 items or 10 pages was performed using Google, Google Scholar, Open Grey, WHO and World Bank libraries. Deviating from our original protocol, a manual search of reference lists was not performed.

Study selection and review process

Search results were uploaded into the Covidence software platform (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates removed automatically and manually. Two reviewers (GP and DS) independently screened titles and abstracts, with a third (GO’R) available for conflict resolution. Publications were excluded if they focused on general health systems or specific disease subsets (eg, trauma or maternal health) rather than EC systems (facility and/or prehospital), or if L+G was not a substantial focus of papers. Given the expansive definition of L+G and the potential inclusion of EC components in various health topics, we retrieved the full text of any ambiguous paper during screening. The same two reviewers independently assessed full texts, with conflicts resolved through consensus or the third reviewer (GO’R), and reasons for exclusion recorded.

Data extraction, classification and analysis

A data extraction table was developed, piloted and finalised, based on preidentified domains of L+G from existing resources. For governance, we were informed by the ten principles of the LMIC health system ‘good governance’ framework from Siddiqi et al24 which has been used to assess injury care systems in Africa,25 and includes key concepts such as vision, inclusive participation, transparency, effectiveness and ethics. The EC leadership components were derived from careful synthesis of global policy and research documents from differing resource contexts, including the International Federation for Emergency Medicine (IFEM).18 26 27 Information extracted from eligible manuscripts included: first author, publication date, LMICs, evidence type, paper summary, governance and leadership descriptors, EC system focus, EC building block component, L+G and EC system links, and overarching lessons (table 1). For ease of analysis and presentation, we divided our data according to the EC system component focus of each included paper: prehospital EC, facility-based EC and ‘whole of EC system’. Within each EC subsection, we performed a descriptive mapping and narrative summary, with numerical comparisons of governance and/or leadership focus and populations included. This was followed by a narrative description based on leadership and/or governance components and qualitative thematic synthesis of key lessons in EC development, capacity and function. Data are presented in descriptive and tabulated forms. Aligning with scoping review recommendations, and recognising the heterogeneity of inclusion criteria, we did not perform a quality appraisal of included studies.28

Types of populations included

35 papers focused on prehospital EC, 53 on facility-based EC and 41 incorporated an overarching ‘whole of EC system’ viewpoint. Included manuscripts showed heterogeneity of L+G components, with few reporting on a single discrete component of governance or leadership. Multiple EC system building blocks were conflated to describe prehospital, facility or whole system EC function, and no EC system building block was examined in isolation across the included papers. Therefore, findings are mapped and summarised to portray common patterns and synthesised thematically to illuminate key lessons arising from each of prehospital, facility-based and whole of EC system.

Prehospital EC (n=35)

Six papers included L+G together, while the majority (n=24) focused specifically on governance in prehospital systems, leaving five papers that covered leadership. A wide variety of populations were included in these papers: doctors (n=3), nurses (n=2), multidisciplinary (n=14), prehospital/paramedic providers (n=3), non-clinical actors (n=2) and 11 focused on systems or services. Details of data extracted from prehospital manuscripts are presented in online supplemental Appendix 2. Of the papers that combined L+G, two described origins and current status of a prehospital system in Indonesia29 and Pakistan.30 These were initially driven by the advocacy and strategic vision of medical leaders and subsequently adopted at national or regional levels through multiple governance domains, including strategy, policies, legislation, accountability, financing and coalition-building. Other models describe leadership in Fiji,31 Ghana32 and South Africa,33 combining the visionary, clinical, team-building and quality/safety leadership of multidisciplinary clinicians with policy, accountability and stewardship governance from community, government, professional bodies and other stakeholders. One paper identified ineffective leadership at both clinical and governance (organisational) levels as a critical barrier to effectively implementing evidence-based best practice in prehospital treatment of acute myocardial infarction.34

All but 135 of the 24 governance papers involved national and/or regional governments, often taking the lead role in a coalition or partnership with local bodies such as police, fire, community, hospitals or professional groups, plus international experts, global organisations, governments and philanthropists. All 24 papers linked governance with the Human Resources and Training building block, and almost all (n=21) linked it with Processes (eg, dispatch, guidelines, medicolegal). Infrastructure and Equipment (mainly ambulances) were commonly reported on, with Data the least noted EC component. Eleven papers highlighted a negative link between governance deficits and prehospital systems development: documenting limited, fragmented, underdeveloped and inadequate prehospital care resulting from lack of government recognition, financing, strategy and commitment.3638 The lack of a central coordinating agency, protective legislation, fear of taking responsibility and poor communication resulted in absent or inconsistent standards across all building blocks, thereby affecting quality and quantity of prehospital EC services.3944 Precarity arising from unpredictable and inadequate funding also impacted equity of care access.45 46

Nine papers described a positive influence of governance on prehospital systems, whereby vision, strategic commitment and secure funding from national or state bodies, coupled with effective stakeholder partnerships, ensured successful implementation and expansion of contextually appropriate prehospital EC services.354754 Examples from Africa,55 56 Brazil57 and globally58 highlight how barriers such as fragmented national policies, low prioritisation, insufficient financing, cultural unacceptability and disagreement between provider groups can hinder prehospital system progression even where vision and advocacy for EC exists.

Of the five prehospital leadership papers, all focused on the Human Resources and Training building block and explored aspects of provider job satisfaction, team building, confidence and workplace culture. Using quantitative methods, transformational and coaching leadership styles by managers and staff correlated with higher job satisfaction and team engagement in Brazil59 60 and Iran.61 ‘Transformational leadership’ was defined by Grivol et al59 as “modelling the style, inspiring a shared vision, challenging the process, allowing others to act, and ultimately animating the heart”. A ‘system of caring’ was an effective leadership strategy in South Africa to develop and maintain a positive workplace culture with high ethical and professional standards,62 while training specifically for paramedic leadership skills in India had the potential to improve communication and collaboration which may impact service delivery.63

Facility-based EC (n=53)

In contrast to the prehospital papers, most (n=25) facility-based EC papers focused solely on leadership, with only seven specifically highlighting components of governance. 21 papers combined L+G when linking with facility-based EC systems development and function. Nurses were the focus of many papers (n=20), with five focused solely on doctors, 24 on multidisciplinary groups and four on systems not specifically engaging with any EC populations. Details are presented in online supplemental Appendix 3.

All leadership papers linked leadership with the Human Resources and Training building block, and some included Processes, mostly in relation to quality and safety practices in the ED. Leadership and management skills were recognised as essential for advocacy, change management, quality improvement and teamwork in the ED64 65 and were highly desired by new emergency medicine (EM) specialists in Africa.66

Components and types of leadership were explored in several papers. Transformational leadership assisted in post-traumatic growth (positive psychological changes) and male ED nurse retention during COVID-19 in China,67 68 improved ED nurse work satisfaction and quality of care in the Philippines,69 and enabled the effective introduction of a quality initiative in Nepal.70 Resilient and emotionally intelligent clinical leaders were more engaged in the workplace and during emergency situations in Ghana71 and increased resilience among ED nurses during COVID-19 in China.72 In the Eastern Mediterranean, ED nurses valued patient-focused, emotionally intelligent and team-based leadership from emergency physicians,73 who overcame traditional hierarchies to improve patient care.74 Medical hierarchies and power differentials also hindered ED teamwork in China, but were overcome through collectivism, in-person communication and ‘feminised’ traits (‘patience, enthusiasm, friendship and thoughtfulness’).75 Team leadership training for doctors and nurses using telesimulation for clinical resuscitation improved leadership practice and clinical outcomes.76 Strong ED clinical leadership endorsed through hospital management policies facilitated the implementation of evidence-based clinical algorithms.77

Several papers explored how workplace culture was determined by hospital and ED management, impacting on the well-being and safety practices of ED staff, particularly nurses. Using quantitative surveys with correlational analysis, studies from China, Iran, Brazil and the Philippines found toxic leadership reduced work satisfaction,78 but a positive, ‘safety culture’ increased ED nurses’ work engagement and their likelihood of addressing errors.7983 Creating workplace conditions to support, inform and empower nurses resulted in more harmonious work cultures, successful quality initiatives and powerful, patient-focused advocacy for better EC outcomes.8488

Of the seven papers specifically about governance in facility-based EC systems, all focused on the critical role of national governments in recognising and steering progress for workforce development, facility design and standards, critical EC processes and ensuring equity of access for all peoples. Strong government policies and legislation in Russia89 and Brazil,90 and an international partnership in Haiti,91 ensured establishment and sustainability of EC units, and in Russia also clarified EC provider scope of practice and certification. By contrast, the fragmentation of training programmes and lack of government recognition depleted the EM workforce in India, which was most marked during the COVID-19 pandemic.92 In Sudan,93 the lack of government administrative and legislative frameworks has been a barrier to consistent and reliable triage development and use. From a stewardship perspective, a mix of private and publicly (government) funded EC facilities in Thailand94 and Egypt95 created access inequities due to financial barriers to care, potentially leading to under-resourcing of public EDs and underserving of poorer populations.

Many papers connected L+G together when exploring issues of quality improvement (often through PAR methods), best practice and team function within EC facilities. Clinical governance by ED leaders was critical to cardiac care improvements in Tanzania96 and Ethiopia97 and necessary to ensure timely antibiotics for pneumonia patients in Malawi.98 While ED clinical governance for quality improvement was commonly identified as a medical leadership task,99 100 the importance of nurses alone, or alongside doctors as role models and local champions for ED quality and safety was highlighted in Pakistan,101 Iran102 and Malawi.103 These L+G components, along with international partnerships and contextualised tools, were critical in overcoming barriers to acute myocardial infarct care in Tanzania,104 introducing lean principles in Ghana,105 improving paediatric sepsis care in Bangladesh106 and rolling out EC skills, knowledge and process improvements across Ugandan hospitals.107 The Ugandan example was enabled by hospital and government endorsement, which was also essential for consistent triage implementation throughout Jamaica108 and sustainable trauma data collection in Ethiopia.109 Workplace culture, determined by hospital leadership support, safety commitment and resourcing, was a key determinant of ED staff motivation and effectiveness.110112

Some papers linked L+G with overall facility-based EC systems improvements; a holistic approach using relationship-based partnerships and PAR methods to transform an ED in Zanzibar, Tanzania.113 Specific leadership and clinical governance training of EM doctors from Nepal114 empowered and equipped them for EC systems change (evidenced by the quality trauma care initiative through transformational leadership previously described).70 A twinning relationship for EM education that aligned with Ministry of Health vision ensured local medical leaders drove EC staff training and process improvements in Ethiopia.115 In Indonesia, sociopolitical, cultural and gendered norms have resulted in a lack of political power and autonomy for ED nurses, which has been speculated to influence quality and safety of clinical EC practice.116

Whole of system EC (n=41)

Papers incorporating whole of EC system focused specifically on governance (n=20), or combined L+G together (n=19). Only two papers focused on leadership. Twenty papers specifically included doctors or medical groups, while none focused on nurses in this domain. Seven papers had a multidisciplinary focus, and 14 covered systems or services without identifying a specific population group. Online supplemental Appendix 4 provides details of all data extracted.

The two leadership papers from Myanmar117 and several Pacific Island Countries118 highlighted how specialty trained EM doctors become empowered to lead EC systems change in their local contexts through role modelling, team building, advocacy and education.

All governance papers incorporate or critique the role of national governments in EC systems development, with many using WHO frameworks and tools.119123 In Africa, national governments are urged to prioritise the human right to EC with principles of good governance to ensure equity119 and to focus on quality,120 while Macedonia exemplifies this through government-led consensus policies and actions.121 Lack of national cohesion, accountability and stewardship for emergency and trauma care systems has resulted in fragmented, limited care unable to address the large burden of injury and illness in conflict-ridden Afghanistan.123 Similarly, harmful international politics and active warfare breach the human right to health and hinder any effective EC system function in Palestine.124 Several papers make the case for collective advocacy to governments for EC prioritisation through supportive partnerships and strategic leverage off global health priorities122 125 126—an imperative noted more than 20 years ago.127 128 Partnerships must involve mutual learning129 and work towards decolonisation in contexts of unequal HIC/LMIC power distribution.130

In India, three papers by the same author examined how effective advocacy from EM medical bodies ensured specialty recognition of EM, but progress on training, standards and EC systems development was delayed by poor collaboration, failure to coordinate with the legislative, social and political landscape, and opaque use of bureaucratic power.131133 Strong, cohesive and integrated national governance delivered through committed government actors was critical to driving effective EC systems development that achieved UHC and built trust within communities through different examples in Africa.134136 In Haiti, national government endorsement was necessary for international and local stakeholders to fully engage with communities to advance front-line EC services.137 In populous LMICs such as Indonesia, underdeveloped EC systems largely result from lack of government recognition and prioritisation.138

The central advocacy role of individual doctors and EM professional groups was a common narrative among papers exploring both L+G in whole of EC system development. They formed strategic alliances, often with government, to instigate training pathways and demonstrate EC value within health systems. These activities were influential for EM and EC development in China,139 140 Brazil,141 142 India,143 144 Niger,143 Ecuador,145 Argentina,146 Nigeria,147 Ghana,148 South Africa149 and the Caribbean.150 Trauma care systems in South East Asian and Western Pacific countries required advocacy and leadership from local champions to drive evidence and quality standards.151

The COVID-19 pandemic allowed EC clinicians to step up into leadership roles in Ethiopia152 and across multiple Pacific Island Countries,153 154 enhancing pandemic responses through collaboration with government stakeholders and by focusing on teamwork, safety and quality. International partnerships between HIC and LMIC government and health institutions investing in leadership training and mentoring were likely to support EC improvements across all building blocks steered by empowered local clinicians.155 156 However, an example of a successful obstetric emergency evacuation from the small island country of Kiribati illustrates how political will and grassroots support can deliver effective and comprehensive EC, even in remote and challenging contexts and where no EM specialists exist.157

Discussion

We present a comprehensive mapping of L+G activities and impact for EC system development encompassing LMICs globally and all cadres of health stakeholders who play a role in EC systems or service delivery. Although commonly described as the most neglected aspect of health systems,158 and despite our initial assumptions expecting limited evidence,21 we found research, reports, commentary and policy papers illuminating different components of L+G in EC systems. Our broad inclusion criteria retrieved change-implementation stories, project models and partnership collaborations contributing to evidence in this area, that might have been invisible with narrower framing.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine L+G together as a building block linked to EC systems development, capacity and function. Typically, these two constructs are considered separately in the literature, with papers categorised as focusing on either governance or leadership—though this is a false dichotomy. As identified in the ‘Human Resources for Health’ literature,4 ‘good governance’ at any level requires and assumes effective leadership behind it; from national governments committing to policy, legislative, accountability and financial reforms to clinical governance at the ED or prehospital coalface. All activities are initiated and led by actors who require attributes aligned with strong leadership; strategic vision, advocacy, team/coalition-building, effective communication, emotional intelligence, ethical frameworks and a commitment to accountability. To this end, we found most included papers did focus on either governance (n=51) or leadership (n=32), but over a third combined the two constructs (n=46), thereby revealing a potential for future research to articulate more clearly how L+G are interdependent for EC system function.

Cross-cutting lessons for L+G impact

By synthesising thematic lessons from each EC component (boxes1, 2 3), the cross-cutting impacts of L+G on EC systems become apparent. The primacy of national government recognition of EC, willpower, and commitment to prioritise development and sustainability of EC systems are the critical influences on the effectiveness and reach of such systems. Further, governments must act within a human rights framework to ensure equity of EC service provision and universal access to high quality EC for all populations.159 From these two key lessons, all other government level L+G acts can follow: policy, legislation, accountability mechanisms and financing for prehospital, facility-based and ‘whole of system’ EC development and function. This aligns with growing literature on the political determinants of health and confirms the reframing of L+G as a binding connector of health systems rather than a stand-alone building block alongside all others.160 161 As articulated neatly by Creaton et al31 when asserting the primacy of L+G for prehospital system development in Fiji: “In the emergency care system, human resources, training, infrastructure, equipment, and processes are like planets orbiting the powerful sun of leadership and governance and its companion moons: financing and data. Prehospital systems depend on political will, leadership and accountability, sustainable financing, and performance data.”

National and local governments also have a key role in brokering, shaping, coordinating and monitoring partnerships with multiple stakeholders. This coalition-building responsibility spans all EC system components, requiring consultation and engagement with local communities. Governments need to provide a clear vision for EC with which partners ought to align, and ensure shared resources are fit for purpose and contextualised for their country’s needs. Stakeholders, particularly external partners, can bring much needed funds and expertise to support locally driven EC capacity building projects. But they should retain humility, transparency and reciprocity which can nourish long-term relationships and avoid perpetuating structural and historical inequalities. Many papers in this review described fruitful, relationship-based partnerships between HIC and LMIC EC stakeholders for all components of EC systems development. The collective evidence clarifies that the responsibility for guiding and monitoring these collaborations to ensure integrity and sustainability lies with host governing bodies. Within Siddiqi’s commonly used principles of good health system governance,24 this role is not well defined and therefore may be particularly pertinent for EC systems which commonly involve multiple stakeholders.

Although government recognition and ownership are required at the top, it is trained and empowered EC clinicians who also occupy a cross-cutting L+G role for all components of EC systems development. Skilled EM doctors and enfranchised nurses act as advocates, quality improvement champions, educators and team-builders at the service delivery level for prehospital and facility-based care. They also have a role in shaping the appetite for governments to prioritise and invest in EC systems, which therefore requires EC clinicians to engage with and master advocacy skills for government and decision-maker influence. Training for L+G was identified as a need as well as demonstrated to be effective in several papers across all domains of EC systems, particularly focusing on leadership attributes of emotional intelligence and transformational leadership styles. ‘Transformational leadership’ was found to be the preferred method of clinical leadership for EC systems. Impacts included a more positive workplace culture, higher staff retention and better work engagement, which was extrapolated to result in more caring, patient-centred and safer EC delivery. However, evidence justifying these conclusions relied mostly on statistical correlations found in surveys, rather than robust staff or patient-focused outcome measures. This remains a substantial gap in current research, given the profile and postulated importance of leadership styles to EC prehospital and facility-based function.

These cross-cutting L+G elements: government recognition, vision and human rights framing; coalition-building for effective partnerships; and trained, empowered EC clinicians demonstrating emotionally intelligent, transformational leadership align with known EM leadership domains and health governance frameworks. The IFEM proposed a leadership administration curriculum incorporating elements of emotional intelligence, developing strategic vision, understanding of health policy and financing, and communication/negotiation skills.26 Similarly, other EC leadership programmes generally focus on domains of personal, team, clinical and process change.27 162 Our findings endorse these focus areas and highlight the need to elevate strategic leadership training to better understand the political economy of EC systems for enhanced coalition-building and advocacy power of EM doctors and nurses. Similarly, government leaders must fully understand the value of EC clinicians’ skills and attributes and provide mechanisms to elevate their leadership, networks and influence over EC policy and planning. The COVID-19 pandemic provided a window for this collaboration to occur, but for many global contexts, health (and EC) systems risk returning to past ‘business as usual’ models with even less available resources.163 164 A future challenge is to develop a combined L+G framework and guiding principles for EC systems that integrates all stakeholder levels, from clinician leaders to government decision-makers.

A new narrative: theories of change and social networks

Drawing these findings together into a cohesive narrative requires new conceptual models. A useful reframing tool for capturing the essential interrelatedness of the L+G constructs is to apply a ‘theories of change’ (ToC) framework. Proposed by Njah et al165 as a mechanism for monitoring and evaluating global health leadership training programmes, ToC enables clarity and embraces complexity by articulating all the pathways in which leadership training leads to more effective leadership and subsequent positive impacts at the individual, organisational, community and political levels. Commonly applied in capacity-development programmes, ToC has been used to design and evaluate mental health services in LMICs,166 and more recently to conceptualise a holistic and long-term acute care strengthening initiative in the Pacific region.167 Both programmes aspire to improve components of health systems through articulating and mobilising essential L+G activities, thereby suggesting the utility of ToC frameworks for deeper understanding of health system L+G more broadly. Applied to EC, and considering the evidence presented in this review, ToC can capture how targeted leadership training for EM doctors enhances management and advocacy skills leading to greater government commitment and investment towards EC. Similarly, how coaching in transformational leadership for EC nurses can empower nurse leaders to improve quality and safety of EC provision, thereby increasing community trust and government accountability for EC outcomes. Because EC system developments take time and can be impacted by sociopolitical determinants, a ToC approach has utility by allowing for the possibility of long-term outputs, incorporating unique LMIC contexts and embracing multiple methods of measuring impact and effect.166 It also makes explicit the assumed effective leadership qualities and skills underpinning good governance behaviours and actions.

Going further, applying concepts arising from social network analysis (SNA) can enable a deeper understanding of how empowered EC clinicians use their L+G skills to influence team members for improved EC service delivery at a prehospital or facility-based frontline, or for better EC planning and policy decisions at a government level. SNA methods map the patterns and strengths of relationships to illuminate how ideas are exchanged and actions catalysed.168 Individuals within social networks who build bridges between different groups and coordinate different health system actors during times of crisis are known as ‘social brokers’.169 In Ghana, SNA was used to understand the relationship between an eye care system’s function and its governance,170 and in multiple settings, SNA has been used to understand and evaluate outcomes of different leadership networks.171 Using concepts from SNA within ToC may present a unique and useful overall narrative for understanding the ‘what’ and ‘how’ of L+G influence on EC systems development and function. As an example from the COVID-19 pandemic, our findings suggest that EC clinician leaders may have acted as ‘social brokers’ who bridged a traditional ‘public health’ and ‘clinical service’ divide and contributed to a more resilient form of health governance.

Limitations

The breadth and depth of L+G influence on EC systems cannot be fully appreciated in this review owing to several limitations and biases inherent in our study. We included a likely overrepresentation of prehospital studies, due to easy recognition of this component of EC within larger clinical system papers. We also exposed a clear pattern of research that profiled nurses at the EC frontline with a focus on teamwork, styles of leadership, workplace culture and well-being. Doctors, by contrast, were documented as much more engaged in higher level systems change, with advocacy, catalysing and specialty-building roles. Specialised prehospital providers were barely included. This may reflect reality within LMICs but may also be a bias inherent in research priorities, portfolios and resourcing within medical, nursing and prehospital disciplines.

We confined our study to English language papers only and limited our grey literature search. Because of a lack of consensus on definitions of L+G in EC systems, we developed our own taxonomy based on existing literature, which introduced subjectivity and likely bias into our final paper selection. We attempted to mitigate these limitations with rigour in search strategy and screening methods.

Conclusions

This scoping review has illuminated the many influences of L+G on EC systems in LMICs, highlighting the cross-cutting impact of national government altruistic vision and EC clinician transformative leadership on all EC system building blocks for prehospital and/or facility-based care. Future research using ToC and SNA methods may address gaps linking leadership styles with EC patient and system outcomes, and better understanding of how L+G integrates and influences EC systems function across the spectrum of individual/clinical level to national government level. Developing a common L+G framework for EC systems development can have utility for future EC clinician leadership training and policy-maker awareness, particularly considering the potential role of EC clinicians as social brokers to strengthen resilience of overall health systems against likely future shocks.
