Assessment of knowledge, attitudes, and practices of CML patients and their families toward TKI therapy in China
Authors: Lixiao Song, Jun Guo, Dan Zhou, Shandong Tao, Banghe Ding, Liang Yu, Chunling Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has revolutionized chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) treatment. The knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of patients and their families play a significant role in treatment adherence and effectiveness. This study aimed to investigate the KAP of CML patients and their families regarding TKI therapy in China. From November 1 to December 31, 2022, a cross-sectional study was conducted at the Affiliated Huai’an No. 1 People’s Hospital in China. A total of 313 CML patients and 268 family caregivers were selected using convenience sampling. Participants answered a self-designed questionnaire. The questionnaire contained demographic/clinical data and assessed KAP toward CML and TKI therapy. Participants exhibited mean KAP scores of 8.91 (55.7%), 33.10 (73.6%), and 2.20 (73.3%), respectively. Family members had higher knowledge and practice scores than patients (both P < .05), with factors such as younger age, urban residency, higher education, employment, higher income, and interaction with peers correlating with better knowledge scores (P < .001). Although participants were well-informed about their diagnosis and medication (>80%), understanding of disease causes (<30%) and treatment prognosis and side effects (<50%) was limited, and cost concerns affected 80.55%. Anxiety and depression were reported more among caregivers (46.64% and 13.8%) than patients (29.71% and 11.51%). While 84.85% adhered to the doctor’s instructions, only 68.50% actively sought more CML information. Positive correlations were observed among KAP scores, indicating their interdependence (knowledge-attitude: R = 0.397; knowledge-practice: R = 0.598; attitude-practice: R = 0.353; all P < .001). The findings underscore the importance of tailored education to fill knowledge gaps about CML and the need to address financial concerns and provide psychological support. The positive correlations among knowledge, attitudes, and practices emphasize the need for comprehensive interventions. In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of tailored education, addresses financial concerns, and provides emotional support for CML patients and caregivers in China, despite limitations such as convenience sampling and questionnaire design. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of educational interventions and long-term outcomes to further enhance the overall well-being of this population.

1. Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a type of cancer that originates in the blood-forming cells of the bone marrow. It results from a specific genetic mutation known as the Philadelphia chromosome, which arises from a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, leading to the formation of the BCR-ABL1 gene.[1,2] This mutation drives the excessive growth of white blood cells. The prevalence of CML varies widely across demographics and regions.[3–6] For instance, in China, between 0.39 and 0.55 adults per 100,000 are affected by CML.[7] Such statistics highlight the significant impact of this disease, making its management crucial for a large number of people. The risk factors contributing to CML range from lifestyle choices to environmental exposures, including smoking, being overweight, exposure to certain chemicals, and chemotherapy.[8–11] Understanding these risks can help in early detection and better management.

When it comes to treating CML, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as imatinib, dasatinib, and bosutinib, play a pivotal role. They specifically target and inhibit the function of the BCR-ABL1 oncoprotein, which is responsible for the disease progression.[1] TKIs have transformed the prognosis for numerous CML patients. With these treatments, many CML patients now have a life expectancy similar to the general population.[12] However, the effectiveness of TKIs relies on patient adherence. Medication adherence and close clinical monitoring are crucial for the best outcomes in CML.[13] Yet, nonadherence is a significant issue, with many patients not consistently taking their TKIs,[14] resulting in reduced survival rates.[15,16] Reasons for this vary from medication side effects to cultural beliefs.[17–20] In some cultures, trust in traditional medicine or reliance on family members for medication reminders can influence adherence.[21] Notably, caregivers, often close family members, also experience stress due to long-term treatment regimes.[22]

Given these complexities, there is a clear need for a deeper understanding of the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) surrounding CML and its treatment. While KAP surveys are a common approach in health sciences to understand a population’s perception and actions about a topic,[23] no such studies have specifically addressed CML and TKI treatment in both patients and their families. This study aims to fill this gap. By examining the KAP in both patients and their family caregivers, we hope to provide valuable insights that can further refine strategies to improve CML management in China.

2. Methods

2.1. Subject recruitment

The cross-sectional study was conducted from November 1, 2022 to December 31, 2022 at the Affiliated Huai’an No. 1 People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Huai’an, Jiangsu, China). A total of 313 CML patients and 268 family caregivers were recruited using convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients with CML; family members caring for CML patients, such as a spouse, sibling, and other relatives; ≥18 years old; those who were able to answer questions independently and consciously; and those who were willing to take part in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: those who were going to file medical claims, those who were unable to complete the survey, and those who completed the survey in <120 seconds. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huai’an No. 1 People’s Hospital (approval No. KY-2022-135-01). All participants provided informed consent. The informed consent process involved providing participants with detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, data confidentiality, and the right to withdraw. After reviewing and asking questions, participants signed a consent form, indicating their voluntary participation.

2.2. Questionnaire development

A self-designed questionnaire was created based on National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines for CML (Version 2.2021)[24] and previous studies[21,25] and revised according to the opinions of 2 senior experts on CML. The final questionnaire was in Chinese and contained 4 categories. In addition to demographic/clinical information, the questionnaire consisted of 3 other sections: 1 assessed knowledge about CML and TKI treatment, covering topics like disease type, medications, and risk factors, while the other 2 explored attitudes and behaviors related to CML and TKI treatment, including feelings, adherence to treatment, and cost concerns. It aimed to understand the perspectives of both patients and family members regarding CML and TKI treatment (KAP Questionnaire, Supplement Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K978).

The questionnaire was previously released to 22 CML patients and 18 family caregivers before the official launch, resulting in a Cronbach coefficient of 0.803%, indicating its reliability. The knowledge category consisted of 9 questions with scores ranging from 0 to 16. For questions 1 and 2, the answer “yes” received 1 point, and the answer “no” received 0 points. For other questions, “yes” received 2 points, “ever heard about it” 1 point, and “never heard about it” 0 points. The attitude dimension consisted of 9 questions, which were scored using a 5-point Likert scale (“strongly agree” = 5 points, “strongly disagree” = 1 point). “Strongly agree” and “agree” were considered positive responses, while “neutral,” “disagree,” and “strongly disagree” were considered negative responses. The total scores ranged from 9 to 45. The practice dimension consisted of 4 questions. With the exception of the unscored second question, all other questions received 1 point for a “yes” answer and 0 points for a “no” answer. The total scores ranged from 0 to 3. KAP scores were categorized as optimal (≥75% of total score), medium (50%–74% of total score), and suboptimal (<50% of total score), as previously described (Table 1, Supplement Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K977).[26]

An electronic questionnaire link was generated by Wenjuanxing (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology, Changsha City, Hunan Province, China), a popular online survey tool in China.[27] Participants completed the questionnaire through the link distributed via WeChat (Tencent, Shenzhen City, Guangdong Province, China) or provided their responses over phone calls with specialized hematologists. Any questions related to the study were promptly addressed by our trained team to ensure data accuracy.[28]

2.3. Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX). The data does not have any missing values because data integrity was monitored and ensured during the survey. Continuous variables were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared between groups using Student t test (2 groups) or analysis of variance (3 or more groups). Nonnormally distributed continuous variables were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test for 2 groups due to its robustness against deviations from normality. For 3 or more groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed, as it extends the nonparametric approach to analyze differences among multiple groups with nonnormally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as n (%). The correlation among KAP scores was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Additionally, we used the Bonferroni method to adjust for multiple comparisons when applicable. A 2-sided P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

The study involved 313 CML patients and 268 family members. It found that participants had limited knowledge about CML, with family members having higher scores than patients. Factors such as age, education, employment, and interaction with other patients influenced knowledge and attitude. Knowledge gaps existed in understanding CML causes, prognosis, and side effects. Participants generally followed doctors’ instructions but were less proactive in learning more about CML.

3.1. Participants’ characteristics and KAP scores

Table 1 shows the sociodemographics of the participants. The mean KAP scores were 8.91 (55.7%), 33.10 (73.6%), and 2.20 (73.3%), respectively (Table 2), suggesting that there was a lack of knowledge and moderate levels of attitude and practice about CML among participants. The knowledge and practice scores of family members were significantly higher than those of patients (both P = .025 and P < .001, respectively; Table 2). Further, participants of younger ages, living in urban areas, with higher education levels, jobs, and higher incomes, participating in patient education, interacting with other patients, and visiting just one hospital scored significantly better on knowledge than their counterparts (all P < .001; Table 2). There were significant differences in attitude scores based on residency, education level, employment status, income, participation in patient education, and interaction with other patients (all P < .001; Table 2).

3.2. Knowledge, attitude, and practice assessment

The participants were well-informed about the type of leukemia and the medication they were taking (correct rates = 98.80% and 89.33%, respectively; Table 3). However, <30% knew the causes of the disease (Table 3) and <50% knew the prognosis and side effects of TKI treatment (Table 3). Although over 80% of participants responded positively to life expectancy (A2, Table 4), willingness to learn more about CML (A5, Table 4), and following doctor’s instructions (A6, Table 4), 80.55% (38.38% + 42.17%) were concerned about the cost of treatment and testing (A7, Table 4). Of note, 46.64% (18.28% + 28.36%) and 13.8% (3.73% + 10.07%) of family caregivers reported anxiety and depression versus 29.71% (4.79% + 24.92%) and 11.51% (2.24% + 9.27%) of patients (A3 and A4, Table 4).

4. Discussion

Patients and family caregivers showed poor knowledge and moderate attitudes and practices regarding CML, TKI treatment, and follow-up doctor’s appointments. In comparison with patients, family members performed better in knowledge and practice, but their attitudes were comparable. The KAP scores were significantly higher in participants who lived in an urban area, had higher educational levels, employment, and higher incomes, participated in patient education, and communicated with other patients, suggesting that these factors may be associated with KAP regarding CML. To better manage CML, these factors may need to be improved. The practical implications of significant differences in KAP scores based on demographic factors highlight the pivotal role of family support in medication adherence and the importance of patient education programs and communication for improving the overall well-being of CML patients.

The knowledge and practice scores of family members were significantly higher than those of patients. Specifically, family members performed better in knowledge assessment than patients regarding the knowledge of the unknown cause of CML and the possibility that TKI treatment may have a good or poor outcome. In the practice assessment, family members followed the doctor’s instructions for medication and follow-ups more strictly. As far as we know, no studies have identified this trend regarding CML and TKI. One of the main strategies for maintaining oral anticancer medication adherence is verbal reminders from family members.[29] A study in India showed that nearly one-third of patients needed reminders from family members to take TKI as prescribed.[21] These results indicate that family support is critical for the home management of CML. In addition, support from family members, friends, and significant others is positively associated with psychological well-being in CML patients receiving lifelong-targeted therapies.[28] We found that the KAP scores of respondents who participated in patient education programs and patient-patient communication were remarkably higher than those of their peers. Thus, improving social support can help CML patients to better cope with the disease.

In a survey of 1518 CML patients receiving TKI treatment ≥ 3 months across 27 provinces and 4 municipalities in China, patients were more concerned about TKI-related side effects than their doctors.[30] However, only 39.94% of patients and 31.34% of family members were very informed about TKI side effects in our study, suggesting that patients and their caregivers are not provided with enough information about TKI side effects. To address this knowledge gap, it is imperative to implement targeted educational programs and interventions. These initiatives may include the development of comprehensive patient-friendly materials, such as pamphlets, brochures, and online resources, offering detailed information about TKI therapy, its potential side effects, and strategies for managing them. Furthermore, enhancing patient-physician communication by encouraging open dialogs and providing patients with the opportunity to ask questions and seek clarifications would foster a deeper understanding of their treatment and potential concerns. Many factors contribute to the development of CML, including exposure to radiation and chemicals.[31] It is surprising to find that <20% of our respondents were very knowledgeable about it. In a cross-sectional study conducted among Tunisian youths, exposure to low-frequency electromagnetic radiation and pesticide use were not considered risk factors for leukemia.[32] To bridge this knowledge gap, educational interventions should prioritize educating patients and their families about the fundamental mechanisms and causes of CML, thereby enhancing their awareness and comprehension of the disease’s origins and risk factors.

As CML patients require long-term TKI therapy, their mental health is of concern. Studies indicate that CML patients experience more depression and anxiety in response to daily life challenges than healthy controls.[28,33–35] Age, number of side effects, and gender significantly affect mental health in CML patients.[36] The severity of anxiety and depression is significantly associated with the female sex, lower education level, comorbidities, advanced-line TKI therapy, and a longer TKI therapy duration.[35] CML mortality continues to decline, resulting in more people living with the disease.[37,38] However, previous studies have focused on the psychological status of CML patients and sociodemographic variables that make them more susceptible to anxiety and depression, with rare reports about the mental health of the family members. The results of our study showed that 46.64% and 13.8% of family caregivers experienced anxiety and depression, compared with 29.71% and 11.51% of patients, raising concerns about family caregivers’ mental health. It is imperative to know how to support them.

This single-center, cross-sectional study aimed to provide a comprehensive assessment of KAP regarding CML in Chinese patients and their families. However, its design limits the establishment of causal relationships or the observation of temporal changes. The majority of participants resided in Jiangsu, China, raising concerns about the generalizability of our findings to the broader Chinese population. These limitations underscore the need for caution when extending our results to other regions and call for further research to investigate factors affecting KAP independently and potential sources of bias.

5. Conclusions

The study examined the KAP of patients and their families regarding CML. While participants lacked knowledge about CML risk factors and TKI side effects, their attitudes and practices were moderate. Additionally, these findings raised concerns about caregivers’ mental health. Health care providers and policymakers should promptly develop and implement educational programs on CML and TKI therapy, while also integrating mental health support services for both patients and their families to improve overall outcomes.
