The Hands-Up Problem and How to Deal With It: Secondary School Teachers’ Experiences of Debugging in the Classroom Publication type: research-article
L.Laurie Galelabel=e1]lpg28@cst.cam.ac.uk \orcid 0009-0004-4299-6704, S.Sue Sentancelabel=e1]ss2600@cst.cam.ac.uk \orcid 0000-0002-0259-7408

Abstract
Debugging is a vital but challenging skill for beginner programmers to learn. It is also a difficult skill to teach. For secondary school teachers, who may lack time or relevant knowledge, honing students’ understanding of debugging can be a daunting task. Despite this, little research has explored their perspectives of debugging. To this end, we investigated secondary teachers’ experiences of debugging in the classroom, with a focus on text-based programming. Through thematic analysis of nine semi-structured interviews, we identified a common reliance on the teacher for debugging support, often embodied by many raised hands. We call this phenomenon the ‘hands-up problem’. While more experienced and confident teachers discussed strategies they use for dealing with this, less confident teachers discussed the generally negative consequences of this problem. We recommend further research into debugging-specific pedagogical content knowledge and professional development to help less confident teachers develop counters to the hands-up problem.

1
Introduction

The process of finding and fixing errors in a computer program is one of the most fundamental but challenging skills to learn for the beginner programmer. Learners must have sufficient understanding of several components of the programming language they are using to effectively and reliably resolve errors, which is rarely present in practice. Instead, beginners are commonly observed utilising ineffective debugging strategies (e.g.,
(
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2017
;
Whalley et al. 2023
)
) which can easily stall progress and evoke emotional distress
(
Kinnunen and Simon 2010
;
Gorson et al. 2022
)
.

This makes debugging difficult to teach, with challenges unique to the secondary school context. Teachers may not be confident in their own programming ability
(
Yadav et al. 2016a
;
Royal Society 2017
;
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
)
or have to support a large number of students with debugging
(
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. External factors may also reduce the effectiveness of debugging instruction, such as a lack of lesson time
(
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. This is detrimental to both teachers’ and students’ experiences of teaching and learning debugging in the classroom.

While most debugging education research focuses on the student perspective, such as their debugging behaviours, less research has focused on the teacher. This is particularly true for the K-12 context, where programming is becoming more widely taught
(
Falkner et al. 2019
)
. In fact, many national curricula stipulate the teaching of text-based programming languages (e.g.,
(
Department for Education 2013
;
British Columbia Ministry of Education 2016
;
Sysło 2020
;
Ripani and Vazquez-Brust 2023
;
Skolverket 2024
)
), bringing additional debugging-related challenges to teachers. Research must therefore remain close to teachers’ current challenges and needs to be of benefit. This can and should help govern future developments in K-12 debugging education.

This paper reports on an interview study capturing the experiences, perspectives, and observations of secondary computing teachers with respect to debugging in their classrooms, specifically in a text-based programming language. Semi-structured interviews with nine teachers were thematically analysed to explore the role debugging plays in programming lessons. We report on teachers’ current approaches and experiences of teaching students about debugging, their observations of students engaging in the debugging process, and their thoughts on the challenges of teaching it. The research questions for this study are:

•

RQ1: What are secondary school computing teachers’ experiences and perspectives relating to debugging in the classroom?

•

RQ2: What are secondary school computing teachers’ observations relating to their students’ debugging experiences?

The findings from this study can be used to help secondary computing teachers and researchers seeking to improve the teaching and learning of debugging. Our results also give some indication of teachers’ pedagogical content knowledge, which can be supported with further research. We argue that such research into teachers’ experiences, be it debugging or more generally, should be conducted regularly. This will help further developments in computing education research to take place with teachers’ experiences at the forefront.

2
Related Work

An increasing amount of computing education research is being directed towards the teaching and learning of debugging for beginner programmers. This is for good reason; debugging is a core aspect of programming, difficult to master, and has well-documented challenges. Even with the use of generative AI becoming more prevalent in programming education, debugging is still a vital part of the programming process. Indeed, debugging may become a more emphasised part of the process when using generative AI
(
Denny et al. 2024
)
. Research into beginner debugging education is therefore important to continue.

2.1
Debugging Education Research

Much debugging education research focuses on the student perspective. Students learning to program naturally have fragile knowledge
(
Perkins and Martin 1986
)
and misconceptions
(
Sorva 2021
)
which often manifest as errors in their programs (e.g.,
(
Du Boulay 1986
;
Pea 1986
)
). Additionally, students from primary school to university level have been observed employing debugging strategies that do not aid successful debugging, instead embodying a trial and error approach (e.g.,
(
Fitzgerald et al. 2008
;
Liu et al. 2017
;
Yan et al. 2021
;
Whalley et al. 2023
)
). This makes struggle with debugging likely, which has affective consequences. Students may experience strong negative emotions during periods of difficulty when debugging
(
Kinnunen and Simon 2010
;
Gorson et al. 2022
)
, which dampens their initial experiences of learning to program. Alternatively, students may not be motivated to program at all, meaning any opportunity to debug is ignored.

This research has borne several debugging-specific teaching aids over the last few decades. These include systematic debugging teaching processes (e.g.,
(
Carver and Risinger 1987
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019b
)
), debugging-specific tooling (e.g.,
(
Guo 2013
;
Luxton-Reilly et al. 2018
;
Ziemann and Reuß 2024
)
), beginner-friendly programming error messages (e.g.,
(
Becker 2016
;
Denny et al. 2020
;
Phung et al. 2023
)
), and debugging exercises
(
Kerslake 2024
)
. The impact of many of these approaches has been summarised in
Sun et al. 2024
and
Yang et al. 2024
, who find these ‘interventions’ have generally positive effects on measures of debugging ability. However, research on many of them is confined to one study, and evidence of wider adoption of what is taught in these interventions is lacking.

2.2
Teacher Perspectives of Debugging in the Classroom

In comparison, relatively little work has focused on school teachers’ perspectives of debugging, despite them being the main observers of students’ debugging experiences. There are particular difficulties with debugging in school classrooms, where teachers may lack confidence in their knowledge of computing
(
Yadav et al. 2016a
;
Royal Society 2017
;
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
)
, have a lack of time to teach debugging
(
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
, or be inundated with students who need support
(
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. Overall, this lack of research motivates the need for better understanding and improving teachers’ experiences of debugging in the classroom.

Teacher-centric research in debugging education can be split into two areas: teacher-student interactions and studies relating to teachers’ pedagogical content knowledge.

2.2.1
Teacher-Student Interactions

In a classroom setting, teachers frequently assist students with debugging
(
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. These interactions vary widely, but can generally be viewed through the lens of cognitive apprenticeship
(
Collins et al. 1991
)
. This is an instructional paradigm for teaching problem-solving processes using the principles of traditional apprenticeship.

Teachers’ use of
scaffolding
is a major theme in many studies of teacher-student interactions. A popular and general teaching tactic for scaffolding support is to ask students questions, with the content of the questions determining the level of scaffolding provided. Some teachers ask open-ended and generic questions
(
Nixon et al. 2023
;
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
;
Elliott et al. 2024
)
, providing relatively little scaffolding, while other questions may be far more specific
(
Yadav et al. 2016b
;
Flood et al. 2018
;
Nixon et al. 2023
;
Elliott et al. 2024
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
)
. Teachers’ initial questions to students may help the teacher to understand the error(s) in students’ program
(
Elliott et al. 2024
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
)
. The more open-ended questions also encourage the
articulation
of students’ thoughts, another aspect of cognitive apprenticeship important for debugging.

More debugging-specific techniques include
modelling
the debugging process
(
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
)
. Unsurprisingly, students’ debugging strategies can differ from their teacher’s, at which point teachers may decide to tailor their approach to the students’ strategies
(
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
)
.

While general techniques used by teachers in their interactions with students are useful to know, the support provided is incredibly contextual.
Nixon et al. 2023
frame teacher-student interactions as improvisational
(
Sawyer 2004
)
, meaning teachers’ general approaches must be adapted to a specific interaction. Other in-class factors, such as the time remaining in the lesson, number of students in the classroom, and the type of error in question, also influence the support the teacher will provide
Elliott et al. 2024
. In the same vein, these studies each have their own context, some of which are not representative of a typical classroom environment. Some were conducted in summer schools
(
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
)
or simulations of classrooms
(
Nixon et al. 2023
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
)
, so not all findings necessarily reflect teachers’ actual in-class interactions with students.

2.2.2
Pedagogical Content Knowledge

The approaches that teachers adopt when providing debugging support, and the knowledge required to employ them, can be considered part of one’s ‘debugging pedagogical content knowledge’ (PCK)
(
Shulman 1986
;
Carlson et al. 2019
;
Tsan et al. 2022
)
.
Shulman 1986
defines this as ‘the ways of
representing and formulating the
subject that make it comprehensible
to others’ (p. 9). PCK can be developed in several ways: teaching experience, research-informed teaching resources, or professional development (PD)
(
Shulman 1986
)
. Indeed, some work has reported the benefits of debugging-specific PD on teachers’ confidence with teaching debugging
(
Elliott et al. 2022
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2022
;
Nixon et al. 2023
)
.

Despite the volume of pedagogical and content knowledge required to teach debugging effectively, little work has gone into conceptualising a PCK specifically for debugging. One study has analysed school teachers’ level of debugging PCK in Scratch
(
Tsan et al. 2022
)
, but did not use or develop a formal model. Related work has instead focused on developing computing or programming PCK more generally
(
Saeli et al. 2011
;
Hubwieser et al. 2013
)
. However, debugging learning trajectories
(
Rich et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2021
)
are useful organisations of knowledge that could inform the development of a debugging PCK. Although designed for curriculum development, they could, for example, be used to visualise content knowledge to teachers in an intuitive manner. This would be particularly helpful for non-specialist teachers who need to know the fundamental debugging concepts they need to understand.

Overall, relatively little research has been conducted into teachers’ experiences of debugging in the classroom when compared to student-focused research. Studies on teacher-student debugging interactions indicate that teachers are generally aware of a variety of teaching approaches that can be applied to debugging. However, some of these studies do not take place in the classroom, where teachers’ time for interactions with students may be severely limited
(
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. For less experienced or confident teachers, debugging-specific PD is a helpful way of developing teachers’ debugging PCK, which itself is a concept that could be more formally developed.

Method

To explore both teachers’ experiences and perspectives with teaching and supporting students with debugging in the classroom, we conducted semi-structured interviews with nine teachers.

3.2
Procedure and Data Collection

We conducted semi-structured interviews with the teachers to allow for ample time and flexibility for teachers to discuss matters relevant to teaching or observations of debugging in their classrooms. Moreover, as per our research questions, we were particularly interested in teachers’
reported
feelings and experiences, which suited interviews well.

Interviews took place online from October to December 2023, using either Google Meet or Microsoft Teams, ranging in length from 35-65 minutes. Each teacher was interviewed individually, apart from Chloe and Deborah, who were interviewed together due to time constraints. Before being interviewed, each teacher completed a consent form and demographic questionnaire, in accordance with the study procedure approved by our department’s ethics committee.

The interview questions facilitated two main points of discussion. Firstly, teachers’
own experiences and perspectives
with debugging in the classroom. That is, their personal thoughts and recollections related to debugging in programming lessons. This could include teaching approaches they used, general feelings they have experienced about debugging, or beliefs around the role of debugging when teaching programming. Second, we asked teachers about their
observations of students’ experiences
with debugging, including any common errors, behaviours, or reactions that they commonly see among students. The interview schedule, along with the codebook definitions and agreement procedure, is available to view online
3
3

3

https://github.com/LaurieGale10/secondary-teachers-experiences-debugging-study-materials
. We did not have consent from the teachers to publish their interview data.

Each interview was recorded and automatically transcribed. These transcripts were then manually read through in conjunction with the recording to correct any mistakes and remove any mentions of personal or school-specific information, while also being used for initial memoing. These transcripts were sent to teachers within two weeks of the interview, who were then given two weeks to redact or clarify anything they wished to be omitted or corrected. For the teachers who took this opportunity, a final version of the transcript was sent to them for their records. After this period, transcripts were disassociated with the teachers’ contact details.

3.3
Analysis

We analysed teachers’ interviews using thematic analysis (TA)
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
.
Braun and Clarke 2021
describe TA as a ‘cluster’ of methods rather than a single analytic procedure. Out of the three ‘schools’ they propose, we describe our analysis as ‘codebook TA’. This school maintains the core values of reflexive TA, in which the subjectivity and experiences of the researcher are key aspects of the analysis, while incorporating some pragmatic approaches that seek reproducible coding
(
Braun and Clarke 2021
)
. In other words, we wished to generate themes that identify meaning beyond teachers’ actual responses while ensuring the codebook we developed was consistently applied across the interview data.

In practice, the procedure followed was still guided by Braun and Clark’s original article on TA
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
. This began with multiple passes of the interview transcripts. The first of these, as previously mentioned, was used both for correcting the automatically generated transcripts and generating initial memos. This was performed by the first author. The second author then read through the corrected transcripts and wrote initial memos.

Teachers’ interview transcripts were then migrated to NVivo 12, which was used for the rest of the analysis. Each transcript was then read through again by the first author to inductively generate open descriptive codes and note further memos. Through further inspection of the interview data, open codes were inductively abstracted into higher-level latent themes and sub-themes that aimed to provide an interpretive, and hence subjective, account of the data. This process of theme generation involved repeatedly restructuring the codebook, redefining the themes and sub-themes, and rewriting the report of the codebook, encompassing steps 4-6 in
Braun and Clarke 2006
. This process was conducted by the first author with feedback from the second author after some of the iterations. Our approach was hence more iterative than linear, which is considered a normal part of TA
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
.

The analysis culminated in a codebook containing six themes, each containing sub-themes, some of which contained their own sub-themes. Only the lowest-level sub-themes were used to code the data. The final commentary of the results aims to provide a rich description of all portions of the interviews that are relevant to our research questions.

Regarding positionality, the first author is a doctoral student with limited previous experience in conducting interviews and qualitative analysis. The regular feedback of the second author was hence important, who is an experienced computing education researcher with expertise in qualitative analysis.

3.4
Reliability and Validity

To strengthen the content validity of the interview structure, the interview questions were iteratively refined by both authors to ensure they appropriately discussed teachers’ experiences, perspectives, and observations specifically related to debugging. This involved a pilot interview with a single teacher to assess the wording of the questions and the timings of the interview. This teacher was personally contacted by the first author and received the same bookshop voucher as the other participating teachers.

The reliability with which the codebook was applied was improved by seeking agreement between the authors without calculating an interrater reliability (IRR) coefficient, which
Campbell et al. 2013
call ‘negotiated agreement’. We wanted to improve the consistency with which the first author coded the interview data according to their codebook definitions (also known as
intracoder reliability

(
O’Connor and Joffe 2020
)
). Seeking agreement, by identifying points of disagreement, is appropriate for this case
(
Campbell et al. 2013
;
McDonald et al. 2019
)
. This was particularly important given the first author was inexperienced at qualitative analysis. Negotiated agreement hence improved the clarity of definitions and the application of the codebook.

Calculating an IRR coefficient was not required for the above process and was considered detrimental for our study. Our interview transcripts were long and conversational, including frequent tangents and interruptions. Naturally, this makes it difficult for coders to agree on units of meaning, which is required for calculating agreement with IRR coefficients. Combined with the complexity of the codebook, any IRR coefficient is likely to be positively skewed and less representative of the reliability of the coding system
(
Campbell et al. 2013
)
. Furthermore, there is little consensus on acceptable IRR coefficient values for semi-structured interview data, which undoubtedly differs from research using other qualitative data or with different objectives
(
Krippendorff 2004
;
Campbell et al. 2013
)
.

Agreement was hence sought as follows. As described in the previous section, the first author developed the coding frame with regular input from the second author. Two interview transcripts (Gideon and Hannah) were then randomly selected to be coded by the second author, in line with the guidelines suggested by
O’Connor and Joffe 2020
. They were provided with the codebook, complete with definitions, and an uncoded version of the interview transcripts. Once coded by the second author, disagreements in the coding of sub-themes were identified using the coding stripes feature on NVivo. Disagreements and ambiguous sub-themes were then discussed and where necessary, revisions were made to the structure and definitions of the codebook.

3.5
Limitations

A limitation of our methodology was that we kept the final themes close to research questions (i.e., closely related to debugging). This was done to avoid generating an overly large codebook, but in the process likely missed some information that contributes to teachers’ experiences of debugging in the classroom. Additionally, our sample was, within certain constraints, self-selecting. Although we would not be seeking a generalisable population for an in-depth qualitative study in the first place, the participating teachers are likely engaged with computing education research to some degree. As a result, they may be more reflective or intentional about their teaching practice than most teachers, potentially affecting the themes we identified.

4.1
Barriers to successful debugging

A particularly widespread theme identified in many parts of the interviews was the obstacles that impede students from successfully debugging their programs. These challenges related to schools’ allotted time for teaching programming, students’ attitudes, and the nature of the Python language and environment.

A barrier outside of the teachers’ control was the
lack of time for sufficient debugging practice
. Teachers were generally dissatisfied with the lesson time they had to teach programming, sometimes constrained to as little as six hours per year. As well as making any progress beyond basic programming constructs difficult, this has effects for debugging. Teachers simply do not have the lesson time for their students to frequently encounter common errors, nor teach effective debugging strategies. As succinctly put by Adam:

“Do I have frustrations teaching students how to debug? Yeah, we don’t have enough time to do it!”
- Adam

Barriers related to the difficulties of learning to program (
programming-related
), some of which were specific to Python, also make debugging difficult for students. The
difficulty of getting syntax exactly right
and
conceptual difficulties
, sometimes manifesting as logical errors, were both mentioned as struggles for students. Many teachers described the common errors students encounter, mostly related to minor incorrect formatting, illustrating the ‘syntax barrier’
(
Denny et al. 2011
)
present for many beginner programmers. Esther makes the point that such syntax errors may not be due to a lack of correct recall, but a
“a lack of the deeper understanding of what the language is actually trying to do”
.

Some teachers also commented on the
hard to interpret programming error messages
in the Python environment students were using
6
6

6

At the time of the interviews, the most recent stable version of Python was 3.12.
. While they can be positively utilised (see Section
4.5
), teachers expressed the unclear and intimidating nature of them for students, not helped by the red text they usually appear in. Hannah explains that the content of the error message is not pitched at a level that most students understand, in turn increasing the difficulty of resolving syntax or runtime errors.

“The error message that comes back is really quite cluttered … It could just be, ‘here’s the line number’. And it’s not very in student speak, so it will say like, ‘identification error’.”
- Hannah

Not all the barriers to successful debugging identified by teachers were external to the student. Students may also have
unhelpful attitudes towards computing or programming
which impede their ability or motivation to debug.

“Why don’t they like to debug? It’s because … they don’t like coding, they just don’t like the lesson, they don’t like the subject … they’ve never known the success because they’ve given up too early or they’ve been put off it.”
- Gideon

Gideon emphasises that students’ negative attitudes span from debugging as a skill to computing as a subject. He also alludes to a lack of success and motivation as drivers for these negative attitudes. The former relates to the ‘performance accomplishment’ determinant of self-efficacy
(
Bandura 1978
)
, which is derived from a learner’s initial experiences with a subject. For most students in England, initial experiences of programming come at primary school. However, unhelpful attitudes could also arise from students’ experiences of learning more generally, which Isabel believes to be the case.

“Maybe some of them do not expect themselves to finish successfully because … of their previous experiences in, not just in computing, but across the board of other subjects.”
- Isabel

The effect of these attitudes often manifests as a lack of motivation to debug their programs. This has consequences for the teacher, who has the challenge of motivating these students.

4.2
The emotional nature of debugging

As well as attitudes towards debugging, teachers discussed a range of emotional reactions they observe when their students are debugging. These reactions are generally physical or verbal, observed at the point of encountering an error, or after successful or unsuccessful attempts to resolve an error. Visible reactions are not universal, however. Some teachers specifically pointed out the
reserved reactions of quieter students
.

Most reactions represented
negative emotion during debugging struggle
, conveying feelings such as frustration and disappointment. An interesting observation mentioned by several teachers is the emotional attachment students develop towards their programs and the distress this can cause when errors occur. Getting an error in students’ own programs can be far more costly than an erroneous program written by the teacher, leading to more dire emotional reactions. Felix describes this as potentially depressing for students.

“When they’re starting to write upwards of ten lines of code, you’re invested in getting it right and then if it fails that can flip and that can be quite depressing.”
- Felix

Repeatedly experiencing such negative emotions when debugging is difficult for students to deal with. Some students may therefore disengage from the debugging process if they become stuck too often.

“Once they’ve hit the brick wall, that’s kind of the green light to go, ‘I’m just gonna do anything.’ Sit there in silence, they’re not, they’re not causing any commotion.”
- Gideon

Despite the lack of visible frustration, disengagement may indicate more severe emotional distress, as students may not be able to bear any more errors in their programs. Alternatively, students may disengage due to the difficulty of the error. Either way, some students’ motivation to debug is reduced amid repeatedly encountering errors. It can be difficult for busy teachers to notice these students who, as Gideon mentions, are ‘not causing any commotion’.

Not all the emotional reactions reported were negative. The
elation when solving an error
was mentioned by some teachers. These reactions were also acute, indicating feelings of intense joy and pride.

“That dopamine hit when you get it right …I’ve seen kids struggling and struggling and struggling and all of a sudden, the light goes on or something happens …they even shake with excitement.”
- Felix

Felix’s observations allude to the physical enactment of students’ emotions, demonstrating the sheer joy that can be experienced from debugging. However, such positive reactions were rarely mentioned, perhaps due to the perseverance required to experience them.

We also found some teachers alluding to the
resilience of motivated students
, who do not experience such polarising emotions when debugging. Instead, these students remain composed and try different solutions, not afraid to add further errors to their programs. Hannah explains that most of her students have such resilience.

“They are all interested in finding a solution to a problem. And I think that that’s really important in having the resilience to bother to debug the program.”
- Hannah

‘The resilience to bother to debug’, to a large extent, comes from the student. From Hannah’s perspective, this derives from students’ interest in programming. Other factors are undoubtedly at play, such as the debugging-specific teaching strategies teachers use (see Section
4.5
), their work in normalising errors in the classroom (see Section
4.6
), and their access to computers at home. Hannah teaches in a selective school, where high levels of interest are perhaps not as surprising. Generally, however, negative emotions and disengagement were the most widely discussed ideas we identified in this theme.

4.3
Reliance on the teacher

A combination of the barriers to successful debugging and its emotional nature contribute to many students relying on the teacher for debugging support. This is typically embodied by students raising their hands when encountering errors. Often, there are enough hands up to make it unrealistic for the teacher to effectively help every student requesting support.

Several teachers, such as Chloe and Esther, discussed
the commonness of teacher reliance
. When asked about the atmosphere of her programming lessons, Esther simply stated
“Chaotic …lots of hands up and down all the time.”
and that these students’ problems are
“almost always about errors in code.”
. Chloe similarly reports
“I think at least half the class will ask all the time.”
; far beyond any teacher’s capacity to meaningfully assist every student.

Several teachers confidently posited that the cause of such reliance is a lack of resilience or motivation, rather than the difficulty of the error they are debugging. Some students want their errors to be instantly resolved, which they cannot always do themselves. This creates a preference for waiting for teacher support over sustained independent effort.

“They want you to give them the answer (laughs). They don’t want to think too much. Only very few of them would want to think and figure out the errors.”
- Deborah

Once a student raises their hand, teachers generally state that students will continue debugging until their teacher approaches them. This is of course negative for the student, with emotional reactions likely to ensue. However, this reliance also has a
toll on the teacher
. Some teachers reported that
‘programming lessons are really, really busy lessons’
(Chloe), which can
“erode their [teachers’] professional confidence”
(Adam). Esther talks about the obligation they feel to help every student, while Gideon feels the challenge of not being able to help every student.

“You’re always thinking about, ‘while their hands are up, they’re not doing anything or making progress’. So you sort of feel this pressure to go and help everybody.”
- Esther

“This is the problem with debugging I suppose in general …I’ll have my program and then I got 20 other programs, so I’m potentially debugging 25 programs in a lesson …This is beyond the capability of the human.”
- Gideon

The impact of this pressure on the teacher is multi-faceted. As well as the ‘pressure to go and help everybody’, many teachers will feel a pressure to prove that their students have learnt something every lesson. This is very difficult when so many students’ progress depends on the individual support of the teacher.

Some teachers accept that it is not possible to personally help every student in need of debugging support. Instead, teacher reliance must be carefully managed. As Adam states,
“there has to be a more scalable model than going around and solving individual people’s code problems”
.

Consequently, strategies that
motivate teacher-independent debugging
have been utilised by some teachers. These aim to provide students with the confidence to debug without the teacher by their side. For example, some teachers tell their students to consult other sources of information before approaching them. These may be resources that teachers have curated (
signposting to resources for assistance
) or their peers (
encouraging helpful collaboration
). The latter does not just reduce the in-class burden on the teacher; explanations from peers
“just seem to go in better”
(Hannah). Alternatively, some teachers will simply
refuse to help students
until they have taken some effort to solve it themselves, such as Hannah.

“If they ask me any questions and they haven’t tried to solve it themselves, I will just tell them, ‘you need to try it yourself first’. So there’s not like an easy solution of, ‘I’m lazy. I’m just gonna ask for the answer’.”
- Hannah

As a result, the teachers’ role regarding debugging support becomes less diagnostic and more flexible. In Isabel’s words,
“they do ask [for debugging support], we just redirect them”
. Students exposed to these strategies consequently become accustomed to
teacher-independent
debugging instead of
teacher-dependent
debugging. Teachers who employ such approaches explain how this helps to develop a resilience among students that is often lacking at the beginning of their programming journey.

‘They get better at these things, but there’s just as many older students who make the same mistakes and they’re like ‘oh, that was a silly error rather than ‘that was a killer.’ - Benjamin

4.4
Varying levels of scaffolding for helping students to debug

Although students can be redirected, there are of course times when teachers do need to provide debugging support to an individual student. This initiates a teacher-student interaction, where a teacher may provide a range of scaffolding techniques. The amount of scaffolding a teacher applies partially depends on teachers’ knowledge of the students, as well as practical factors such as lesson time remaining
(
Elliott et al. 2024
)
.

Some scaffolding methods are generic teaching tactics applied to the context of debugging, while others are programming- or debugging-specific. The level of scaffolding varies across a continuum, from very little, placing the onus on the student, to near-complete scaffolding of the problem, placing the onus on the teacher.

When putting the onus on the student, some teachers simply ask students to share their thoughts about the problem at hand (
encouraging externalisation of ideas
) or
ask students questions
. Both of these approaches encourage students to outwardly reflect on their understanding of the problem, which might be what is stopping them from successfully resolving errors.

“It’s that rubber ducking process, sometimes students don’t explain it to themselves, and when they go back and explain their problem they spot it themselves.”
- Benjamin

The questions teachers mentioned were naturally of varying specificity, which again shifts the level of scaffolding. Adam deliberately uses generic questions when beginning interactions with his students.

“You’re gonna have to tell me what do you want it to do, what does it do at the moment and what have you tried already?”
- Adam

These questions are useful for specialist and non-specialist teachers, as they do not require expert knowledge nor time comprehending students’ code. Furthermore, once students understand they will be asked generic questions, they may be more inclined to debug more independently of the teacher.

Heavier forms of scaffolding may be used if a student has been trying to debug an error for a sustained period of time, or the error is difficult to localise. Some teachers may
give students the solution
or
give students hints
specific to the error(s) the student is struggling with. The latter is particularly useful when localising an error.

“If they’re struggling to find it, I can see they’re getting frustrated, first thing I would is I’d say, ‘Have a look at that particular line’. This is assuming I know where the error is.”
- Benjamin

4.5
Improving students’ debugging ability

As well as general scaffolding, the majority of teachers reported using teaching approaches that are designed to help students become more successful debuggers. A more general sub-theme of this theme is teaching
programming strategies to make debugging easier
. These are patterns of programming that prevent many errors from accumulating, or help keep a student aware of the purpose of their program. Adam and Hannah, for example, encouraged an iterative process of small changes and testing.

“Every five minutes, they need a reminder, ‘you need to save and you need to debug’. And so that process of make one change, test it, make another change, test that, is really, really important.”
- Hannah

Additionally, some teachers
guide students through debugging strategies
. This typically involves explicitly modelling the debugging process with an example or student program. Live coding was a popular medium for this. Some teachers, such as Gideon, model to groups of students who are struggling with a particular error, in turn helping several students at once.

“Sometimes if I’m getting two or three students with the same error, then I’ll be like, ‘right come round the board. I need to demonstrate, I need to model’.”
- Gideon

A few teachers were strong advocates of
utilising programming error messages
(PEMs). Although they are not always intuitive to understand, Adam and Felix viewed PEMs as valuable sources of immediate feedback.

“In Python, although it might look intimidating, that red line of text, actually that’s really helpful because it’s instant and it tells you where to start looking.”
- Adam

This positive outlook on PEMs drives these teachers to frequently discuss them. The act of ‘decoding’ PEMs was subsequently mentioned by some of these teachers.

Through repeated exposure and practice, guided by the strategies they teach, teachers do observe positive change in their students’ debugging behaviours. Benjamin’s students, who are
‘pre-cued to look for common errors’
, become drilled in doing this themselves.

“They get better at looking for the obvious errors …the top five syntax errors, they will look for those straight away as they have the practice.”
- Benjamin

This positive change appears to be more frequently reported by the more confident teachers we interviewed. Regardless, such improvement is to be celebrated. These teachers show how their teaching strategies help students to go about it with independence and confidence. With this comes more of the aforementioned
elation of solving an error
, as well as positive initial experiences for students learning program.

4.6
Promoting a positive error culture

Students’ improvement in debugging is not solely due to instructional strategies. Cultivating a positive error culture, an environment where students feel comfortable making errors in their programs, also helps students become more resilient to errors. One useful method for promoting a positive error culture is
getting used to erroneous code
. This may involve sabotaging activities (Benjamin and Hannah), where students actively add errors to their peers’ programs, or giving students erroneous code (Chloe and Gideon). Hannah points out that sabotaging is useful because it exposes students to errors in a depersonalised format, reducing the potential emotional angst around debugging.

“It doesn’t matter if it doesn’t work. It’s not yours. And so that was a really nice way to kind of break down the anxiety about that there will be errors.”
- Hannah

Another method of normalising errors in the classroom is by highlighting that errors are to be expected rather than dreaded. Felix explains how ‘failures’ should not just be reported, but also celebrated to remove the stigma around them.

“Spectacular failures. I want those reported and celebrated as well. If something should have gone right and [went] badly wrong but somebody found something interesting on the way … celebrate it. Take the fear out of it.”
- Felix

Felix’s response also highlights the practice of
celebrating the effort as well as the outcome
. If students are actively rewarded for sustained debugging effort, as also in Adam’s case, this can act as a further motivator for teacher-independent debugging.

“If we have a culture in a classroom where students know, ‘ok, sir’s not just going to give me a reward if I get things right … he’s gonna give me a reward if I try and get it right’ and that’s the big breakthrough for debugging.”
- Adam

These approaches, combined with
verbal encouragement
throughout the debugging process, help create an environment where both encountering and struggling with errors are natural experiences. Such measures help make debugging a healthy, normal activity that can be a source of joy.

5
Discussion

Teachers’ experiences of debugging in the classroom spanned a range of insights and issues. Many of these align with the related work discussed in Section
2
, particularly that related to the teacher-student interaction (Section
2.2.1
). While these studies focus on how students assist a particular student(s), the teachers we interviewed shared more general experiences and feelings with debugging in the classroom.

A major motif that we found in teachers’ experiences is the common reliance students have on their teacher for debugging support. This tended to be the case for less motivated students, who would raise their hands at the point of or soon after encountering an error. We think much of our results, and some other reported teachers’ experiences, can be conceptualised through a phenomenon we call the “
the hands-up problem
”.

5.1
The Hands-Up Problem

The hands-up problem is the in-class difficulty deriving from the common student tendency to raise their hand when struggling to complete a task. Since the need for teacher support is reportedly common (see
the commonness of teacher reliance
and
Bagge 2015
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
;
Uppal 2020
;
Kerslake 2024
), the problem derives from many students raising their hands at once. Therefore, the severity of the problem scales with the size of teachers’ classrooms, making it more difficult for typical school teachers whose classes contain up to 30 students
(
Yadav et al. 2016a
)
.

This phenomenon may indeed happen in any subject; hand-raising is a common classroom behaviour. However, many studies outside of computing education investigate hand-raising and student engagement in the context of whole class discussions (e.g.,
(
Turner and Patrick 2004
;
Böheim et al. 2020a
;
Böheim et al. 2020b
)
). The dynamics of motivation, emotion, and classroom atmosphere in this setting are different to when students are programming themselves. We therefore focus on the context of debugging.

According to the teachers we interviewed, students generally raised their hands at the point of encountering an error. However, hand-raising may occur after students have put some initial effort into debugging
(
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
)
. Either way, the consequences for teachers and students are generally negative. We now describe some drivers, consequences, and counters to the hands-up problem, visualised in Figure
1
.

5.1.1
The Drivers

There are a range of factors that prompt a student to raise their hands during debugging struggle, which we call drivers. These drivers may be
domain-agnostic
or
domain-specific
. This list is not meant to be exhaustive, but aims to give a general categorisation of some factors at play.

Domain-agnostic
drivers for hand-raising are factors that can contribute to the hands-up problem in any subject. We believe that these are generally related to students’ affect. Teachers in our study often discussed a general lack of resilience to work through a problem, leading to reliance on the teacher. This problem is also highlighted by teachers in
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
and likely relates to students’ motivation levels. However, the very act of hand-raising must indicate some motivation to progress, otherwise students would disengage (see
negative emotion during debugging struggle
). Therefore, drivers at play here may be the motivation to problem-solve independently of the teacher, or attitudes of low self-efficacy or self-reliance.

Several
domain-specific
drivers for debugging may also contribute to the hands-up problem. These include difficulties specific to Python and learning to program more widely, such as the
difficulty of getting syntax exactly right
and
conceptual difficulties
. Lots of research has already reported on common beginner programmers’ errors and misconceptions (e.g.,
(
Du Boulay 1986
;
Pea 1986
;
Sorva 2021
)
), including the ‘syntax barrier’ beginners face
(
Denny et al. 2011
;
Denny et al. 2012
)
. The size of these research areas indicates the number of potential stumbling blocks that may contribute to a student seeking teacher support.
Difficult to interpret PEMs
are also certainly a driver, which relates to the active field of PEM readability with computing education research (e.g.,
(
Becker et al. 2019
;
Denny et al. 2021
;
Phung et al. 2023
)
).

Generally, domain-specific drivers can be viewed through cognitive load theory
(
Sweller 1988
)
. If a student’s working memory is near-saturated when programming, errors and difficulty with problem-solving follow. If this is the case, hand-raising is arguably a logical behaviour, because they lack the cognitive capacity to debug themselves. However, this has the side-effect of contributing towards the hands-up problem if the intrinsic cognitive load of the task in question is too high.

5.1.2
The Consequences

Most teachers in our study alluded to many students raising their hands for debugging support, particularly when starting to learn to program. This is why we call this hands-up phenomenon a problem; in typical classroom settings, teachers simply cannot go around and effectively support every student
(
Yadav et al. 2016a
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. This has both
student-side
and
teacher-side
consequences.

The
student-side consequences
are multi-faceted. Once students raise their hands, teachers in our study described how unlikely it was for students to progress until they come to help. Given the drivers discussed, this is not surprising, as students may not have the working memory to continue their debugging efforts. As a result, some students will linger in a state of being stuck, or emotional angst, for an extended period. Even when a teacher does come to provide support, the nature of the interaction will be altered if there is a time pressure
(
Elliott et al. 2024
)
. Such a classroom atmosphere makes it very difficult to help students learn from an error they are struggling with.

The emotional consequences of debugging are also important to consider. Feelings of frustration, anxiety, and disappointment are documented in this study (
the emotional nature of learning to debug
) and previous work
(
Kinnunen and Simon 2010
;
Gorson et al. 2022
;
Gale and Sentance 2023
;
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
)
. If these feelings occur repeatedly, students may simply disengage in future encounters with errors (see
negative emotion during debugging struggle
). If these experiences are students’ first with learning to program, such emotional arousal and lack of success can negatively form students’ programming self-efficacy
(
Bandura 1978
;
Gorson and O’Rourke 2020
)
. Such experiences are therefore important to limit, but hard to do so if students are reliant on teacher support.

There are also
teacher-side consequences
of the hands-up problem. Some teachers described their classroom atmosphere as ‘chaotic’ and ‘busy’ when lots of hands are raised. Some also reported feeling a duty to help every student requesting assistance. Failing to do so can evoke feelings of inadequacy or disappointment (see
the toll on the teacher of this reliance
). Furthermore, if teachers are regularly assessed or inspected, a sea of raised hands may give the impression of an unproductive lesson. This again can evoke negative feelings in teachers, who may over-compensate by providing too much assistance to students. In
Uppal 2020
’s reflections on their in-class debugging practice, “in the midst of a busy classroom where as a teacher I was multitasking on many levels, it was all too easy to over-scaffold and provide too much direction, to give the appearance of a seemingly successful lesson” (p.15).

5.1.3
The Counters

So far, we have characterised the hands-up problem in fairly negative terms. However, it can be counteracted. Several teachers in our study (see
motivating teacher-independent debugging
) and related work
(
Bagge 2015
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
;
Wachter and Michaeli 2025
)
report using teaching approaches that encourage teacher-independent debugging. Like the drivers, these counters can be divided into
domain-agnostic
techniques and
domain-specific
counters.

Domain-agnostic
counters are strategies that teachers of any subject can apply to alleviate the hands-up problem. For instance, teachers may refuse to help students until they evidence some attempt at teacher-independent problem-solving (see
refusing to help students
and
Bagge 2015
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
). Some may also encourage collaboration with peers before providing support (see
encouraging helpful collaboration)
.

Debugging-specific counters include referring students to resources and tools that aid successful debugging (see
signposting to resources for assistance
and
Nixon et al. 2023
;
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
;
Elliott et al. 2024
) and modelling the debugging process to the whole class (see
Guiding students through debugging strategies
and
Ryan and DeLiema 2023
). An obvious benefit of these approaches is that a whole class can benefit from them simultaneously. Teachers may also teach heuristics for ‘decoding’ PEMs due to their oft-ambiguity (
utilising programming error messages
). An attitudinal counter also mentioned by some teachers is the enforcement of a positive error culture
(
Hennessy Elliott et al. 2023
)
. When students learn to program in an environment where errors are considered normal, they will likely feel more confident to attempt independent debugging.

These strategies may take time for students to become accustomed to
(
Bagge 2015
)
. However, the teachers who report consistently using these counters also report their students becoming more perseverant and less reliant on the teacher when debugging
(
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
)
. Ultimately, this gives the teacher more time to provide more meaningful support to a smaller number of struggling students.

From our sample of teachers, teachers who reported these counters tended to be more confident and have decades of experience. This is less the case for the less confident teachers, leaving them more susceptible to the consequences of the hands-up problem. More generally, how widespread such counters are in computing classrooms is unclear. Results from
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
suggest only a fraction of teachers have strategies for teaching debugging and resilience to errors, with lack of time to teach programming probably a limiting factor.

5.2
The Implications

Given the increasing volume of computing education research focused on debugging, it is surprising that some teachers report struggling with the hands-up problem. Although we only interviewed nine teachers, we expect our findings align with more teachers’ experiences due to the commonality of non-specialist computing teachers and reported lack of content knowledge by some
(
Yadav et al. 2016a
;
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
)
. For teachers who report using counters, experience or professional development seemed to be the reason. This begs the question: how can less experienced teachers
7
7

7

This includes both newly qualified and non-specialist computing teachers.
more confidently teach debugging? Or in terms of Figure
1
, how can counters to the hands-up problem be developed? We did not identify any themes related to these questions in our analysis and therefore only provide an initial recommendation for future research.

An obvious suggestion is to improve less experienced teachers’ debugging PCK. Despite its integrity to programming and influence on classroom practice, we are not aware of a conceptualisation of debugging PCK in the research literature. Admittedly, a debugging PCK could already exist in debugging teaching resources or PD, but investigating this is beyond the scope of this study.

A debugging PCK would inevitably have some commonalities with conceptualisations of programming PCK
(
Saeli et al. 2011
)
, such as the motivation for teaching it and common student difficulties. But it would additionally include debugging-specific pedagogy, techniques for effectively managing teacher-student interactions, and other aforementioned counters to the hands-up problem. Therefore, even if a teacher does not have time to explicitly teach the debugging process, a more developed debugging PCK would still equip them to deal with the hands-up problem.

Once a concept of debugging PCK is more well-developed, it can be spread through professional development. Some studies have already reported successful debugging-specific PD
(
Elliott et al. 2022
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2022
;
Nixon et al. 2023
)
, which provides a good starting point for further development. However, given some teachers still report findings aligned with the hands-up problem
(
Bagge 2015
;
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
;
Uppal 2020
;
Kerslake 2024
)
, debugging-specific PD is either not widespread enough or not being effectively translated into practice. If the former is the case, more debugging-related PD needs to be developed or expanded. This does not need to solely focus on debugging; more emphasis on debugging could be included in programming PD workshops, given the intertwined nature of these skills.

6
Conclusions and Further Work

We have reported secondary school teachers’ experiences of debugging in the classroom, with a specific focus on text-based programming. General experiences and perspectives of debugging, as well as teachers’ observations of students debugging, were all discussed and analysed through thematic analysis.

The themes we identified notably related to the common reliance of students on the teacher for debugging support, corroborating with some previous work
(
Bagge 2015
;
Sentance and Csizmadia 2017
;
Michaeli and Romeike 2019a
;
Uppal 2020
;
Kerslake 2024
)
. Due to this reliance being reflected through lots of raised hands in the classroom, we conceptualised our themes through the phenomenon of the ‘hands-up problem’. We define this as the in-class difficulty deriving from the common student tendency to raise their hand when struggling to complete a task. There are a number of
drivers
for the frequent hand-raising that contribute to a busy classroom atmosphere. This has generally negative
consequences
; students are often left stuck and frustrated, and teachers flustered. Some teachers, however, had developed
counters
to the hands-up problem, usually through experience. These consisted of general classroom management techniques as well as debugging-specific teaching approaches. Over time, these help students to become less reliant on the teacher and more resilient to errors in their programs.

Based on these results, we suggest three areas of further work. First, developing a conceptualisation of debugging PCK would be a useful way of synthesising research on teacher-centric perspectives of debugging in the classroom. This should include resources and approaches developed by both researchers and teachers, which would allow for iterative testing and refinement in a classroom context. Teachers’ debugging PCK could then be improved through professional development. Further development and expansion of debugging-related PCK is a second area of further work we recommend.

Finally, understanding what has helped confident computing teachers to foster teacher-independent debugging would be useful. Whether teachers attribute this to experience, professional development, or intentional reflection on their teaching practice would play an important role in the way that debugging PD is structured and engaged with. This development of counters to the hands-up problem, illustrated by the far-left box in Figure
1
, is not something our thematic analysis identified. Further capturing the experiences of teachers will help to improve their, and their students’, experiences with debugging.
