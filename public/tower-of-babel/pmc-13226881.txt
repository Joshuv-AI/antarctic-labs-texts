Integrative omics identify NID2 as a therapeutic target linking depression and colorectal cancer in humans
Authors: Li Huang, Ji Wang, Haodong Tian, Wen Wang, Zhaowen Nie, Xuehao Cui, Lilong Zhang, Yu Jin, Yang Ding, Zhongchun Liu, Honggang Yu, Lianlian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDepression is frequently comorbid with gastrointestinal malignancies, yet the biological links governing this relationship require precise characterization. Analysis of a prospective colonoscopy cohort (n = 2,857) and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data (n = 37,597) shows that major depression is associated with an increased prevalence of colorectal precancerous lesions and cancer risk. Two-sample Mendelian randomization supports a causal effect of depression on colorectal tumorigenesis. By integrating plasma protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) with colorectal neoplasia genome-wide association data through Bayesian colocalization, we identify nidogen-2 (NID2) as a shared causal mediator. This finding is supported by observed reductions of NID2 levels in plasma and colorectal tissues, alongside consistent transcriptomic and proteomic evidence from public datasets. These results establish NID2 as a biomarker and therapeutic target linking depression to colorectal cancer, providing a molecular framework for understanding how psychological stressors influence oncological progression.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, oncology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 Beyond established biological and clinical determinants, psychosocial factors such as depression are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to cancer outcomes.2,3 As one of the most prevalent comorbidities in oncological settings, depression has been associated with a 23%–83% increase in cancer-specific mortality.4,5 Furthermore, evidence suggests that the association between depression and CRC may be bidirectional.6 While CRC can precipitate depression, depression itself may also increase both the risk and mortality of CRC through multiple biological and behavioral pathways.7,8,9 However, since a cancer diagnosis itself often induces psychological distress, investigating the direct impact of depression on CRC risk is frequently confounded by reverse causality.

Different from CRC, colorectal precancer (CRPC) lesions, which are crucial steps in CRC progression following a genetic cascade that transforms normal mucosa into polyps, benign adenomas, and ultimately invasive adenocarcinoma, can be treated by endoscopic removal to halt progression.10 Notably, the diagnosis of CRPC seldom triggers significant psychological distress, making patients less susceptible to reactive depression. This characteristic positions CRPC as an ideal model for investigating the influence of depression on the early stages of colorectal tumorigenesis. Nevertheless, research regarding the prevalence of depression and its specific associations with CRPC remains limited.

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward human plasma proteins, which play key roles in disease pathogenesis and constitute primary targets of approved therapies. Advances in proteomic technologies now enable the comprehensive identification of aberrant protein expression levels, facilitating the discovery of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer.11,12 Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of plasma proteins have further uncovered genetic variants associated with protein expression, termed protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL).13 Dysregulated plasma proteins may serve as mediators linking depression with CRPC and CRC, although these mechanisms remain poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the role of depression in the development of CRPC and CRC and identified potential plasma protein mediators linking depression to cancer progression. We integrated evidence from a prospective clinical cohort and population-scale data (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, NHANES dataset), applying Mendelian randomization (MR)14,15 to assess the causal effect of depression on CRPC and CRC. We further examined the mediating role of plasma proteins through proteome-wide colocalization, clinical validation, and bioinformatic analyses.

Result

Depression severity is associated with an increased prevalence of CRPC and CRC

To evaluate the associations between depression and colorectal neoplasia, we analyzed two distinct cohorts: a prospective clinical cohort for CRPC and the population-based NHANES database for CRC. Initially, for the CRPC analysis, we conducted a prospective study at the Endoscopy Center of Renmin Hospital, Wuhan University (RMWU), enrolling a total of 2,913 patients scheduled for colonoscopy. We excluded individuals with known high-grade neoplasia or cancer (n = 33), other severe physical comorbidities (n = 5), recent major life stressors within the past 2 weeks (n = 2), alcohol or drug dependence, psychiatric disorders, or the use of psychotropic medications (n = 16). Consequently, 2,857 patients were eligible for the final analysis, categorized by depression severity: non-depression (n = 2,524), minimal depression (n = 218), minor depression (n = 86), and major depression (n = 29) (Figure 1A). We observed that higher depressive symptom severity was significantly associated with an increased detection rate of CRPC (odds ratio, OR, [95% confidence of interval, CI]) = 2.39 (1.02–5.62); p = 0.039 (Figure 2A). Given the limited sample size, the potential influence of participants’ age and gender on the results was not reported and warrants further investigation.

For the CRC analysis, we utilized data from the NHANES 2005–2023 cycles. Among the 88,429 initial participants, 50,832 were excluded due to age <18 years (n = 34,296) or missing data regarding body mass index (BMI, n = 461), education level (n = 3,001), poverty-income ratio (PIR, n = 4,540), alcohol consumption (n = 8,034), smoking status (n = 28), diabetes (n = 25), PHQ-9 depression scores (n = 419), or CRC diagnosis (n = 28). The final analytical sample comprised 37,597 participants, classified into four groups based on depression severity (Figure 1B). Multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for sex, BMI, ethnicity, education, PIR, alcohol use, smoking status, and diabetes, demonstrated that higher depression severity was independently associated with increased odds of CRC (Figure 2B; Table S1).Specifically, individuals with major depression exhibited 2.3-fold increased odds of CRC compared to those without depression [OR (95% CI) = 2.3 (1.6–3.4)], while minor and minimal depression showed progressively attenuated but still significant associations. These associations persisted after further adjustment for physical activity and dietary patterns, suggesting that the findings are robust against lifestyle confounding. Notably, the dose-response relationship across depression severity levels reinforces a potential biological gradient, potentially implicating chronic stress pathways, inflammation, or health-behavioral mediators in CRC pathogenesis.

MR supports a causal relationship between depression and colorectal neoplasia

To explore the potential causal effect of depression on CRPC and CRC, MR analyses were conducted.

For depression and CRPC, we first performed two-sample MR with depression as the exposure and CRPC as the outcome. In the discovery dataset, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method indicated a significant positive association between genetically predicted depression and CRPC risk (β = 0.24, SE = 0.08, p = 0.004) (Figure 2C; Table S1). Other MR methods, including MR Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode showed a similar positive association but were not statistically significant. In the replication dataset, the IVW method again suggested a significant association between depression and CRPC [β = 0.007, SE = 0.003, and p = 0.007]. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses further suggested that the results were not driven by any single SNP, supporting the robustness of the causal inference (Figure 2E).

For depression and CRC, the discovery MR showed a significant association between genetically predicted depression and CRC using the Wald ratio method [β = 18.21, SE = 8.94, p = 0.042] (Figure 2D; Table S1). In the replication dataset, the IVW method still supported a significant association between depression and CRC [β = 0.645, SE = 0.262, p = 0.014]. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses suggested that the association was not driven by any single SNP, supporting the robustness of the causal inference (Figure 2F).

Proteome-wide colocalization identifies shared genetic signals between plasma proteins and colorectal neoplasia

To evaluate whether genetically predicted plasma protein levels and colorectal neoplasia share common genetic architectures, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis integrating pQTLs with Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) summary statistics for CRPC and CRC. Utilizing the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) dataset, we specifically examined cis-pQTL signals, defining the colocalization window as the genomic region within ±500 kb of each protein-coding gene.

Colocalization analyses provided strong evidence for a shared causal signal between plasma nidogen-2 (NID2) and CHRDL2 cis-pQTLs and CRPC/CRC risk loci (PP.H4 = 0.992 and 0.985, respectively). UBAC1, MAP4K5, and PDE5A also showed moderate-to-strong evidence of colocalization (PP.H4 > 0.7) (Figures 3A and 3B; Table S3). Notably, several of these proteins have been implicated in colorectal tumor biology. For example, elevated NID2 has been associated with poorer prognosis in CRC. CHRDL2 has been reported to modulate CRC progression as an antagonist of BMP signaling, and PDE5A has been linked to current good manufacturing practice (cGMP) signaling and CRC-related clinical outcomes.16,17,18,19,20,21

To complement the genetic prioritization with disease-tissue evidence, we further evaluated these candidates in the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) CRC proteomics dataset. Notably, NID2, CHRDL2, UBAC1, and PDE5A showed significantly reduced abundance in CRC at the protein level (NID2, p = 5.83 × 10−19; CHRDL2, p = 2.77 × 10−2; UBAC1, p = 2.52 × 10−24; PDE5A, p = 6.49 × 10−47), whereas MAP4K5 (p = 6.97 × 10−2) showed less consistent evidence across datasets. The expression patterns of these candidates in CPTAC are summarized in Table S3 and Figure 3C.

Plasma NID2 levels are significantly reduced in individuals with major depression and in patients with CRPC/CRC

Of the five prioritized proteins, NID2 and CHRDL2 pQTLs exhibited the strongest evidence of colocalization with CRPC/CRC risk loci. While CHRDL2 is a well-characterized BMP pathway antagonist in colorectal tumor progression18; we focused on NID2 as the primary candidate for downstream analysis due to its high colocalization probability.

We first quantified plasma NID2 levels across four groups: individuals with major depression (n = 10), age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 10), treatment-naive CRPC patients (n = 10), and treatment-naive CRC patients (n = 10). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results demonstrated that plasma NID2 levels were significantly lower in the major depression group compared to healthy controls (0.49 vs. 3.33 ng/mL, p = 0.0004; Figure 4A). Similarly, plasma NID2 levels were markedly decreased in both CRPC and CRC patients relative to healthy controls (normal vs. CRPC: 3.33 vs. 1.47 ng/mL, p = 0.032; normal vs. CRC: 3.33 vs. 0.62 ng/mL, p = 0.0007; Figure 4A).

To assess NID2 expression in colorectal tissues, we retrospectively collected formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded specimens from 94 individuals, including 20 CRC tumors with 20 matched adjacent non-tumorous tissues, 44 CRPC lesions, and 30 normal colorectal mucosal tissues, and performed NID2 immunohistochemistry (IHC). NID2 protein expression was markedly reduced in CRC tissues compared with normal mucosa (integrated optical density [IOD]: CRC, 0.83 × 107 vs. normal, 3.76 × 107; p < 0.00001). NID2 expression in CRPC was slightly higher than in CRC but remained significantly decreased relative to normal mucosa (IOD: CRPC, 0.92 × 107 vs. normal, 3.76 × 107; p < 0.0001). Adjacent non-tumorous tissues showed intermediate NID2 levels, lower than normal mucosa yet higher than CRC tumors (IOD: adjacent, 1.79 × 107 vs. normal, 3.76 × 107; p < 0.0001) (Figures 4B and 4C). The age and gender distributions of the patients from whom the samples were derived for ELISA and IHC were comparable, suggesting that these demographic factors are unlikely to have a significant impact on the results.

Consistent with these findings, analysis of the CPTAC dataset confirmed significantly lower NID2 expression in CRC tissues compared to normal colorectal tissues at the proteomic level (Figure 4D). To evaluate the diagnostic potential of NID2, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis within the CPTAC cohort, which demonstrated robust discrimination for CRC with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.840. Using the optimal cutoff value of 0.67 (determined by the Youden index), NID2 achieved a sensitivity of 79.4%, a specificity of 83.0%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 81.9%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 80.6% (Figure 4E).

To explore the molecular landscape associated with NID2, we stratified The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-colon adenocarcinoma (COAD)/ rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) samples into NID2-high and NID2-low groups based on the median expression level of NID2. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis identified a signature of significantly dysregulated genes (|log2 FC| ≥ 1, adjusted p < 0.05), which are visualized in the volcano plot (Figure 4F).

For enrichment analysis of NID2-related DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed and visualized as bubble plots (Figure 4G and 4H). GO analysis covered terms including oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly, and synapse organization. KEGG analysis highlighted pathways such as ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, and focal adhesion, elucidating key functional categories and pathways of NID2-related DEGs.

Finally, using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) to integrate experimentally supported and curated protein-protein interaction evidence, we identified NID2-interacting partners, including laminins (e.g., LAMC1, LAMB1, LAMA4, and LAMA5) and HSPG2, which are enriched in processes related to extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and cell adhesion (Figure 4I).

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that major depression is associated with an increased prevalence of CRPC in a prospective colonoscopy cohort and elevated CRC risk in the NHANES database. Two-sample MR analyses provided further evidence for a potential causal effect of depression on both CRPC and CRC. Proteome-wide colocalization analysis identified NID2 as the plasma protein exhibiting the strongest shared genetic signal with CRPC/CRC risk. Furthermore, subsequent ELISA and IHC assays in clinical samples confirmed that NID2 levels are consistently reduced in individuals with major depression and in patients with CRPC or CRC. Collectively, these findings suggested that downregulated NID2 may serve as a critical mediator linking psychological distress to colorectal tumorigenesis.

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract is increasingly recognized as an organ that is tightly coupled to emotional and neuropsychological states. Accordingly, a range of functional and inflammatory GI disorders, including irritable bowel syndrome, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and inflammatory bowel disease, have been repeatedly associated with depression.22,23,24,25 In contrast, evidence regarding the impact of depression on GI malignancies remains limited.2,3 Current perspectives increasingly support a bidirectional relationship between mood disorders and GI disease. Although many studies have documented a high prevalence of depression among patients with CRC,9 far fewer have investigated whether depression itself confers an elevated risk of CRC.7,26 In this study, analyses of a prospective clinical cohort and the NHANES dataset consistently showed positive associations of depression with both CRPC and CRC, and two-sample MR further supported a potentially causal relationship. These findings extend prior epidemiologic observations and strengthen the evidence linking depression to colorectal tumorigenesis.

Diagnosis and treatment of CRC can impose substantial psychological stress and may precipitate depressive symptoms, raising the possibility that observed depression-CRC associations are, at least in part, attributable to reverse causation.6 In contrast, CRPC is a precancerous condition that is typically detected by colonoscopy, is often amenable to endoscopic removal, and generally does not entail the same diagnostic or treatment-related psychological burden. Regarding the concern that colonoscopy itself may induce depression-like symptoms due to stress, the PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptom burden over the preceding 2 weeks and was administered at the time of scheduling or prior to the procedure, rather than after colonoscopy. While colonoscopy preparation and anticipation may cause transient anxiety, this would be less likely to produce sustained depressive symptoms captured by the PHQ-9. Moreover, the proportion of major depression observed in our cohort was not higher than the prevalence reported in the general Chinese population, suggesting that the potential impact of colonoscopy-related stress on our depression assessment is unlikely to substantially bias the main findings.27 While residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, these results strengthen the inference that depression may contribute to the initiation of colorectal neoplasia, warranting further investigation into the underlying biological mechanisms.

The interplay between depression and cancer is complex, and accumulating evidence highlights the role of the brain-gut axis in tumorigenesis.28,29 Circulating plasma proteins provide a convenient, minimally invasive means to detect disease-specific signals, and plasma proteins associated with depression and CRC may therefore serve as useful biomarkers.30,31 Our colocalization analysis highlighted five plasma proteins (NID2, CHRDL2, UBAC1, MAP4K5, and PDE5A) as genetically prioritized candidates potentially linking depression to CRPC/CRC. Among them, NID2 has been reported as a promising biomarker for diagnosis of CRC.16,17 CHRDL2 has been reported as a BMP pathway antagonist involved in colorectal tumor progression, and PDE5A participates in cGMP signaling with potential relevance to CRC biology and clinical outcomes.18,19 UBAC1 and MAP4K5 are less well characterized in CRC but are functionally connected to ubiquitin-related processes and MAPK signaling, pathways broadly implicated in tumor development.20,21 These candidates therefore provide a focused set of testable targets for downstream validation.

By integrating genetic prioritization with proteomic evidence, we observed a concordant downregulation of NID2 across multiple molecular layers, supporting its candidacy as a key effector at the shared genetic locus. Given its exceptionally high colocalization probability (PP.H4 = 0.992), reduced protein abundance in the CPTAC dataset, and biological relevance to ECM and basement membrane remodeling, we prioritized NID2 for further investigation. NID2 is a vital basement membrane ECM protein that maintains matrix organization and regulates cell-ECM adhesion, migration, and survival.16,30,31 While previous reports have noted reduced NID2 in CRC,32 our IHC assays revealed a clear stepwise decrease in NID2 expression along the colorectal carcinogenesis continuum: from normal mucosa to tumor-adjacent mucosa, then to CRPC, and finally to invasive CRC. Notably, the precancerous stage (CRPC), which is typically amenable to endoscopic resection, already exhibited significant NID2 depletion. Furthermore, tumor-adjacent mucosa displayed lower expression than normal mucosa from healthy individuals. This finding is consistent with the perspective that tumor-adjacent mucosa, even when sampled approximately 2 cm from the lesion, does not represent a truly “normal” state but rather reflects a molecularly altered field.33 Collectively, these patterns suggest that NID2 dysregulation is an early and persistent event in colorectal tumorigenesis, supporting its potential utility as both a circulating biomarker for early detection and a mechanistic link between depression and CRC development.

In summary, our study supported a relationship between depression and colorectal neoplasia and highlights NID2 as a genetically prioritized plasma protein associated with this axis. These findings offer a translational opportunity to improve risk stratification and management in patients with comorbid depression and colorectal neoplasia.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the clinical associations were evaluated in two complementary cohorts rather than within a single unified population, because colonoscopy- and pathology-adjudicated CRPC endpoints and sufficiently powered CRC outcomes are rarely available in the same cohort. Although this design enables assessment across both the premalignant and malignant stages of colorectal carcinogenesis, heterogeneity in participant characteristics and outcome ascertainment cannot be fully excluded.

Second, although the PHQ-9 was selected for its clinical feasibility, it only captures depressive symptoms during the 2 weeks preceding colonoscopy. Because most participants lacked prior psychiatric diagnoses, we could not ascertain the exact onset or long-term duration of depression. Furthermore, the stress of the upcoming colonoscopy procedure itself may have influenced PHQ-9 scores, potentially introducing exposure misclassification. Consequently, the impact of depression duration on colorectal neoplasia risk remains to be elucidated in our current analysis.

Third, follow-up in the RMWU cohort ended after colonoscopy and final pathological diagnosis, and post-procedure assessments of depressive symptoms were unavailable; therefore, we could not assess whether depressive symptoms changed after treatment. Future large-scale longitudinal cohorts capturing both CRPC and CRC outcomes, with long-term follow-up and repeated assessments of depressive status (ideally complemented by multi-omics profiling), are warranted to address these issues.

Fourth, plasma NID2 was measured by ELISA in individuals with CRPC or CRC who did not have depression, rather than in a single cohort jointly stratified by both depression status and lesion status (e.g., healthy controls, depressed individuals without lesions, and depressed individuals with CRPC or CRC). As a result, the depression-NID2-CRC relationship could not be directly evaluated within the same individuals, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies with systematic biospecimen collection and follow-up.

Fifth, because public resources such as TCGA do not include CRPC data, the bioinformatics analyses were restricted to CRC. Sixth, while our results nominate NID2 as a potential mediator linking depression to CRC, mechanistic experiments were not performed. Finally, the differential expression and enrichment analyses were conducted in a single dataset without external validation. Independent cohorts will be required to confirm these findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Prospective cohort study

From September 22, 2022, to June 15, 2023, a cohort study was carried out at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (RHMU) to explore the relationship between depressive symptoms and the detection of CRPC. Individuals scheduled for screening colonoscopy were invited to participate and were asked to complete the PHQ-9 questionnaire together with baseline surveys. Based on PHQ-9 scores, participants were classified into four groups: non-depression (≤4), minimal (5–9), mild (10–14), and major (≥15). Eligibility criteria required participants to: (1) be able to read, understand, and provide written informed consent; (2) undergo colorectal endoscopic evaluation; and (3) demonstrate willingness and capacity to comply with study procedures and follow-up. Exclusion criteria included: (1) previous diagnosis of colorectal advanced neoplasia or carcinoma; (2) serious comorbidities such as severe cardiovascular disease or end-stage organ failure; (3) major life stressors within two weeks before enrollment; and (4) alcohol/drug dependence, diagnosed psychiatric disorders, or current use of psychotropic medications/antidepressants. Colonoscopy findings were recorded, and patients identified with colorectal polyps or polypoid lesions were considered CRPC cases. Detection rates across the different depression categories were compared using chi-square analysis. Detailed baseline information of participants were provided in Table S4.

NHANES study

NHANES is an annual survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm), which collects nationally representative data with ethical approval and informed consent. For this study, we included data from 2005 to 2023. Following previous reports, potential risk factors included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, education, poverty income ratio (PIR), alcohol use, smoking, diabetes, and depression (assessed by PHQ-9). After excluding individuals with missing data, CRC was defined as the study endpoint.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between depression severity and CRC. The multivariable model adjusted for age (continuous), sex (male/female), BMI (continuous), ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, Other Hispanic, and Other/Multi-Racial), education (<high school, high school, ≥college), PIR (continuous), alcohol use (yes/no), smoking (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), and depression severity (non-depression, minimal, minor, and major; PHQ-9 scores 0–4, 5–9, 10–14, and ≥15). Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to compare CRC risk across depression categories.

ELISA

To experimentally evaluate NID2 as a candidate mediator linking depression to CRPC/CRC, we measured plasma NID2 levels in a prospective cohort including 10 patients with major depression, 10 healthy controls, 10 CRPC and 10 CRC individuals. Detailed baseline information of above participants were provided in Table S5.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens were retrospectively collected from 94 individuals, including 20 CRC tumors with matched adjacent non-tumorous tissues, 44 CRPC lesions, and 30 normal colorectal mucosal tissues from healthy controls. Detailed baseline information of above participants were provided in Table S6.

Method details

Exploring the relationship between depression and CRPC, CRC

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis: Depression to CRPC and CRC

To further explore the causal relationship between depression and CRPC/CRC, two-sample MR analyses were conducted.

In the discovery stage, for depression and CRPC, summary statistics for depression were obtained from a meta-analyzed dataset (246,363 depression and 561,190 controls, European ancestry), and summary statistics for CRPC were obtained from the FinnGen consortium (R9 release) (BENIGN_COLORECANI) (FinnGen Consortium. https://www.finngen.fi/en) (Table S3). For depression and CRC, summary statistics for depression were obtained from a GWAS in 8,276 depression cases and 209,308 controls (British ancestry). Summary statistics for CRC were obtained from the metadata (4,419 CRC cases, 4,335 controls, European ancestry) (Table S2).

For replication of depression and CRPC, summary statistics for depression were obtained from the UK Biobank (11,705 depression cases, 135,691 controls, European ancestry),33 and summary statistics for CRPC were downloaded from the Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit website (MRC-IEU, http://www.bristol.ac.uk/integrative-epidemiology/, ukb-d-D12), containing 8,877 CRPC cases and 352,317 controls of European ancestry. For depression and CRC, summary statistics for depression were obtained from https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/studies/GCST005323, European ancestry, and summary statistics for CRC were obtained from https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/studies/GCST90013862, European ancestry (Table S3).

Genome-wide significant SNPs (p < 5 × 10−8) for depression, CRPC, and CRC were selected and clumped to obtain independent instruments (r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000-kb window). Clumping was performed to minimize linkage disequilibrium (LD) among selected SNPs. For single-instrument analyses, causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio (the SNP–outcome association divided by the SNP–exposure association). For multi-instrument analyses, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary approach under the assumption that all instruments are valid and there is no directional horizontal pleiotropy. As sensitivity analyses, we additionally applied MR-Egger regression, the weighted median method (which provides consistent estimates if up to 50% of instruments are invalid), and the simple mode and weighted mode estimators (which cluster SNPs with similar causal estimates and are valid if the largest cluster reflects the true causal effect). Finally, we conducted leave-one-out analyses to assess whether any single SNP disproportionately influenced the results.

Exploring the role of plasma protein between depression and CRPC, CRC

Colocalization analysis

To investigate whether plasma protein levels share causal variants with CRPC and CRC risk loci, we performed colocalization analyses between 2,940 protein cis-pQTLs from UKB-PPP and GWAS signals for CRPC/CRC. GWAS summary statistics for CRPC and CRC were obtained from the FinnGen R9 release. Colocalization analysis assumes a single causal variant within each tested region. For each locus, posterior probabilities (PPs) were computed for five hypotheses: (H0) neither trait is associated in the region; (H1) only trait 1 is associated; (H2) only trait 2 is associated; (H3) both traits are associated but driven by distinct causal variants; and (H4) both traits are associated and share a single causal variant. Prior probabilities were set to p1 = 1 × 10−4, p2 = 1 × 10−4, and p12 = 1 × 10−4.

For each protein, the colocalization region was defined as ±500 kb around the lead cis-pQTL variant. Evidence for colocalization was classified as very strong (PP.H4 > 0.9), strong (0.7 < PP.H4 ≤ 0.9), or moderate (0.5 < PP.H4 ≤ 0.7). Colocalization analyses were conducted using the R package coloc with default settings.

Clinical validation and bioinformatic analysis of NID2 linking depression with CRPC and CRC

ELISA

To experimentally evaluate NID2 as a candidate mediator linking depression to CRPC/CRC, we measured plasma NID2 levels in a prospective cohort including 10 patients with major depression, 10 healthy controls, 10 CRPC and 10 CRC individuals. Plasma NID2 concentrations were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (ELK3649; ELK Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 96-well plates pre-coated with an NID2 capture antibody were incubated overnight at 4°C. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, diluted plasma samples and standards were added and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Plates were washed and sequentially incubated with a biotinylated anti-NID2 antibody for 1 h, streptavidin–HRP for 30 min, and TMB substrate for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped with 2 M sulfuric acid, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an EnSight microplate reader (PerkinElmer). Comparisons between groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens were retrospectively collected from 94 individuals, including 20 CRC tumors with matched adjacent non-tumorous tissues, 44 CRPC lesions, and 30 normal colorectal mucosal tissues from healthy controls. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Adjacent tissues were defined as non-tumorous colorectal mucosa from the surgical margins (typically >2 cm from the primary tumor) of CRC resections, whereas normal tissues were obtained from healthy controls.

All sections were reviewed on corresponding H&E slides by experienced pathologists prior to immunohistochemistry (IHC); areas containing residual tumor glands, dysplasia, or severe necrosis were excluded. For CRC specimens, tumor regions with high tumor cellularity were selected, whereas only histologically normal epithelial glands were evaluated in adjacent and healthy-control tissues.

IHC was performed using a Nidogen 2 polyclonal antibody (PA5-145301; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) incubated overnight at 4 °C, following standard protocols. Staining intensity was quantified as integrated optical density (IOD). Comparisons between groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.

Comparison of NID2 mRNA expression and protein abundance in tumor and adjacent normal tissues.

Proteomic data for NID2 in CRC and normal tissues were obtained from the CPTAC data portal. Protein abundance values were log2-normalized within each sample. For each protein, a Z score was calculated for each sample as the number of standard deviations from the median across samples. Group differences were evaluated using a two-tailed Student’s t test. In the CPTAC cohort, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the discriminatory performance of NID2. The optimal cutoff was determined using the Youden index, and performance was summarized by sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). PPV/NPV were calculated within the CPTAC sample set.

Differential expression analysis and enrichment analysis

RNA-seq raw counts (GDC harmonized) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) cohort were used. Primary tumor samples with available NID2 expression and count data were retained. Samples were stratified into NID2-high and NID2-low groups based on the median NID2 expression (ties at the median were assigned to the low group to avoid group imbalance). Genes with very low counts were prefiltered (e.g., removed if total counts across all samples were <10).

Differential expression analysis was performed on raw counts using DESeq236(Wald test; default independent filtering). Effect sizes are reported as log2 fold changes (log2FC), and multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as FDR <0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted against KEGG and Gene Ontology biological process (GO-BP) collections using default settings, based on a genome-wide ranked list derived from the differential expression results.

Protein–protein interaction network analysis

To characterize the molecular interaction landscape of NID2, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database. Analyses were restricted to Homo sapiens. Only interactions supported by experimental evidence and curated database annotations were included, using a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 (medium confidence).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Detection rates across the different depression categories in prospective cohort study were compared using chi-square analysis, data are presented as mean ± SD. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between depression severity and CRC in NHANES study. Comparisons of ELISA, IHC assay and CPTAC used two-tailed Student’s t test. p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.
