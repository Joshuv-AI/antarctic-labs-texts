Upregulated GLUT1 expression correlates with type I interferon activity and disease severity in cutaneous lupus erythematosus
Authors: Laura Anna Bokor, Botond L. Szabolcs, Zsófia Király, Anita Mohos, Ágota Szepesi, Viktória Varga, Anna Sebestyén, Lajos V. Kemény, Péter Holló, Bernadett Hidvégi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pathogenesis of cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) is driven by the overactivation of the type I interferon (IFN-I) system. Although dysregulated glucose metabolism has been implicated in autoimmune inflammation, its role in CLE remains largely unexplored. This study aims to elucidate the role of glucose metabolism in CLE, specifically exploring its correlation with IFN-I activity and its contribution to disease severity.MethodsSkin biopsies were obtained from 51 patients with active, untreated CLE — including two of its most prevalent subtypes: 26 with subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SCLE) and 25 with discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) — and 8 healthy controls. Glucose uptake and IFN-I system activation was evaluated via GLUT1 (SLC2A1) and MxA (MX1) immunohistochemistry, respectively. Disease activity was measured using the CLASI-A score. Publicly available transcriptomic datasets were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation to evaluate the association between SLC2A1 and MX1 mRNA levels.ResultsGLUT1 expression was significantly increased in both CLE subtypes compared to healthy controls (SCLE: p = 0.0344, DLE: p = 0.0356). MxA expression in keratinocytes and lymphocytes was also markedly elevated in CLE samples (p = 0.0031, p = 0.0059). Keratinocyte GLUT1 expression positively correlated with keratinocyte and lymphocyte MxA expression (p = 0.003, r = 0.41; p < 0.001, r = 0.483) and with CLASI-A scores (p < 0.001, r = 0.47). Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between SLC2A1 and MX1 mRNA expression across multiple cell lines.ConclusionOur findings identify a link between elevated GLUT1 expression, IFN-I pathway activation, and CLE disease activity, suggesting that enhanced glucose metabolism may contribute to the immunopathogenesis and clinical severity of CLE.

Introduction

Cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) is an autoimmune skin disease characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations and multiple subtypes. The lupus erythematosus disease spectrum ranges from localized cutaneous plaques to severe multi-organ involvement (systemic lupus erythematosus, SLE), often resulting in substantial impairment of patients’ quality of life [1,2].

The classification of CLE subtypes is based on the Düsseldorf criteria [3,4]. Among these, discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) emerges as the most common subtype, presenting with erythematous, hyperkeratotic plaques predominantly affecting the face, ears, and scalp, often resolving with residual scarring. Another frequent clinical presentation of CLE is subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SCLE), which primarily presents with erythematous, scaly plaques or psoriasiform papules in areas exposed to ultraviolet (UV) radiation [5,6]. Almost one-quarter of newly diagnosed CLE patients (24%) present with pre-existing SLE, while an additional 18.7% develop systemic disease during the following year, thereby increasing their mortality and morbidity risk [7].

For the quantification of disease activity (CLASI-A) and damage (CLASI-D) in patients with CLE, the Cutaneous Lupus Erythematosus Disease Area and Severity Index (CLASI) is used, which is a validated and reliable clinical scoring system [8].

The pathomechanism of CLE is driven by the self-amplifying overactivation of the type I interferon (IFN-I) system, leading to an anti-epithelial cytotoxic immune response and the subsequent release of cellular debris, further stimulating innate immune pathways [9]. This pro-inflammatory, self-amplifying loop can be triggered by various factors, with ultraviolet (UV) radiation and certain drugs being of particular relevance [10]. Myxovirus resistance protein A1 (MxA, MX1) is widely used as a surrogate marker of IFN-I activity in inflammatory diseases, as its expression is selectively regulated by type I interferons and directly reflects the extent of IFN-I pathway activation [11,12].

A rapid metabolic shift to glycolysis and increased glucose uptake have been recognized as major drivers of immune cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation [13]. This process relies on the glucose transporter (GLUT) protein family of monosaccharide transporters, encoded by the solute carrier family 2 (SLC2) genes, which mediate the facilitated diffusion of hexose and pentose sugars through the plasma membrane, enabling intracellular glucose abundance and increased ATP production [14]. Among the 14 members of this protein family, GLUT1 (SLC2A1) is the ubiquitous transporter that facilitates basal glucose uptake in most mammalian cells [15].

Effector T cells (Teff), including Th1, Th2, and Th17 subsets, exhibit markedly elevated GLUT1 expression and rely predominantly on glycolysis as their primary catabolic pathway. Glycolytic metabolism not only ensures the survival of these cells but also plays a crucial role in their activation and proliferation [16]. Similarly, GLUT1 is highly expressed in basal epidermal cells to sustain steady-state keratinocyte turnover. Furthermore, its rapid upregulation during wound healing and UVB-induced hyperplasia underscores its critical role in driving epidermal proliferation [17].

Additionally, GLUT1 has been reported to be upregulated in several autoimmune and inflammatory skin diseases, including vitiligo, atopic dermatitis, chronic spongiotic dermatitis, and oral lichen planus [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. GLUT1 expression was also elevated in the skin of patients with psoriasis and positively correlated with clinical disease severity, as measured by the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) [22].

While changes and imbalances in glucose metabolism have been demonstrated to contribute to the overactivation of innate and adaptive immune responses in autoimmunity, the role of immunometabolism, particularly glucose metabolism, in CLE remains largely unexplored. The precise pathophysiology of CLE is unclear, posing challenges for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate the role of glucose metabolism in CLE, specifically exploring its correlation with IFN-I activity and its contribution to disease severity. Furthermore, we compared the alterations in glucose metabolism between the two most prevalent CLE subtypes, DLE and SCLE.

Methods

Patients and sample collection

Skin biopsy specimens (N = 51) were collected from patients with active, untreated CLE lesions at the Department of Dermatology, Venereology, and Dermatooncology, Semmelweis University. Control samples (N = 8) were obtained from perilesional skin adjacent to excised nevi in healthy individuals. All samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde overnight and subsequently embedded in paraffin.

The severity of cutaneous lesions was quantitatively assessed at the time of the skin biopsy using the activity domain of the CLASI (CLASI-A).

The study adhered to the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, as it was conducted on human samples. Ethical approval was granted by the Regional and Institutional Committee of Science and Research Ethics at Semmelweis University (approval number: 75/2024, approval date: 05/14/2024).

Immunohistochemistry

All immunohistochemistry stainings were performed on sections prepared from the paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. Following deparaffinization, heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed, after which the sections were incubated overnight with the primary antibodies (monoclonal rabbit GLUT1 antibody [Abcam, EPR3915, ab115730] 1:400 dilution, monoclonal rabbit MX1 antibody [Cell Signaling Technology, D3W7I, 37849S] 1:200 dilution). This was followed by sequential 30-min incubations with the NovoLink post-primary reagent and the NovoLink polymer detection system (Leica Biosystems). The reaction was visualized using DAB-peroxidase chromogen substrate (Dako), followed by hematoxylin staining for nucleus visualization. The sections were scanned using a Pannoramic Scan system (3D Histech, Budapest, Hungary) equipped with a Carl Zeiss objective (NA = 0.83; Carl Zeiss MicroImaging Inc., Jena, Germany).

Staining intensity was evaluated by two independent dermatopathologists. (A.M. and Á.SZ.) Positivity was defined as a membranous staining pattern for GLUT1 and a cytoplasmic staining pattern for MxA. Expression levels were assessed using a semi-quantitative scoring system, ranging from 0 to 3+: 0 (no expression), + (mild expression, <25% of cells are stained), ++ (moderate expression, 25-75% of cells are stained), +++ (strong expression, >75% of cells are stained). GLUT1 expression was assessed exclusively in the epidermis, whereas MxA expression was evaluated in keratinocytes and dermal infiltrating lymphocytes. The final expression score for each biopsy was determined by averaging the individual scores assigned by the two dermatopathologists.

Correlation analysis using publicly available datasets

The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE, 2012) dataset was retrieved from cBioPortal on 21.08.2025, selecting only complete cases (n = 877) [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. The GSE109248 dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) on 22.07.2025 [27,28]. The ARCHS4 keratinocyte dataset was downloaded from the ARCHS4 website on 14.11.2025 [29]. Data processing and normalization were performed in Python using the pandas, json, and ARCHS4py modules. Samples were manually filtered to primary untreated keratinocytes.

Statistics

Spearman rank correlation, Pearson correlation analysis, and ANOVA were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, www.graphpad.com). Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Skin biopsy samples were obtained from the lesional skin of 51 patients diagnosed with CLE (SCLE: 26; DLE: 25). The cohort had a mean age of 66 ± 18 years, with 98% of participants being female. Concomitant SLE was present in 19,6% (10/51) of the patients. The mean CLASI-A score was 9.8 ± 6.8 for the SCLE and 6 ± 3.2 for the DLE group. GLUT1 expression is upregulated in SCLE and DLE skin samples

Significantly increased GLUT1 expression was observed in keratinocytes in both subtypes of CLE compared to healthy controls (SCLE: p = 0.0344, DLE: p = 0.0356, Fig. 1D). MxA expression is elevated in CLE skin

To investigate IFN-I signaling in CLE lesional skin, we analyzed MxA expression as an indicator of a high IFN signature. MxA expression in both keratinocytes and lymphocytes was significantly higher in all CLE skin samples compared to healthy controls (p = 0.0031, p = 0.0059, Fig. 1E–L). Within each sample, keratinocyte MxA expression showed a significant positive correlation with lymphocyte MxA expression (p < 0.001, r = 0.602). No significant difference in either keratinocyte or lymphocyte MxA expression levels was detected between SCLE and DLE lesional skin (p = 0.6844 and p = 0.9493).

Keratinocyte GLUT1 expression correlates with a high IFN signature

To explore the link between GLUT1 expression and IFN-I pathway activation in CLE, we analyzed the correlations between keratinocyte GLUT1 expression and MxA expression in both keratinocytes and lymphocytes in CLE lesional skin. A significant positive correlation was observed between keratinocyte GLUT1 expression and both keratinocyte and lymphocyte MxA expression (p = 0.003, r = 0.41; p < 0.001, r = 0.483; Fig. 2B). When analyzed separately by disease subtype, significant positive correlations between keratinocyte GLUT1 and MxA expression were observed in both SCLE (p = 0.0236, r = 0.4426) and DLE (p = 0.0433, r = 0.4073) groups. Consistently, a similar positive correlation was observed between keratinocyte GLUT1 and lymphocyte MxA expression in both subtypes (SCLE: p < 0.001, r = 0.6529; DLE: p = 0.0306, r = 0.433).

Keratinocyte GLUT1 expression correlates with the CLASI score

To characterize the association between elevated GLUT1 expression and CLE disease activity, we correlated keratinocyte GLUT1 expression in lesional skin with the CLASI-A scores, revealing a significant positive correlation (p < 0.001, r = 0.47). Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was detected between keratinocyte MxA expression and the CLASI score (p = 0.048, r = 0.278). No significant correlation was found between lymphocyte MxA expression and CLE disease activity (p = 0.087, r = 0.242). Correlations between all variables are presented in Fig. 2B.

SLC2A1 mRNA expression correlates with MX1 across multiple datasets

To analyze whether GLUT1 and MxA may be co-regulated within keratinocytes, we performed Spearman correlation analyses on a panel of cell lines (Fig. 2C) and on samples from acute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (ACLE), SCLE, DLE, and healthy skin controls (GSE109248, n = 23, Fig. 2D). We observed a significant positive correlation between SLC2A1 and MX1 expression in cell lines (r = 0.3098, p < 0.001), and a non-significant correlation across the skin samples.

Next, we analyzed the ARCHS4 keratinocyte dataset, which integrates keratinocyte RNA-seq samples from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Across all available keratinocyte samples, we observed a significant positive correlation between SLC2A1 and MX1 expression (r = 0.5368, p < 0.0001, Fig. 2E). When the dataset was restricted to primary, untreated human keratinocytes, the positive correlation remained significant (r = 0.2251, p = 0.0003, Fig. 2F). These results suggest that SLC2A1 and MX1 expression may be co-regulated in a cell intrinsic manner.

Discussion

The rapidly growing field of immunometabolism has substantially advanced our understanding of the intricate interplay between metabolic regulation and the immune system. Metabolic reprogramming during immune cell activation is now recognized as a key driver of various immune-mediated pathologies. Over the past few decades, its role has been extensively studied in cancer progression, obesity, T2DM, and several autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]].

Metabolic dysregulation of immune cells has also been implicated in SLE pathogenesis [40]. In lupus-prone mice, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are markedly upregulated in CD4+ T cells, and inhibiting glycolysis reduced their activation and proliferation while improving disease phenotype [41]. Similarly, selective inhibition of GLUT1 transporters has been shown to attenuate autoimmune activation in CD4+ T-cells from both lupus-prone mice and SLE patients [42].

While the role of altered immunometabolism has been studied in various autoimmune diseases, whether keratinocytes and immune cells undergo similar metabolic rewiring in CLE remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the role of glucose metabolism in CLE pathogenesis by examining keratinocyte GLUT1 transporter expression in lesional skin. We further analyzed correlations between GLUT1 expression and type I IFN system activity, as assessed by MxA expression, and clinical disease activity, measured by the CLASI-A score.

In this study, we demonstrated that GLUT1 expression is significantly elevated in keratinocytes of both DLE and SCLE lesions compared to healthy controls. These findings align with a previous report of increased GLUT1 transporter expression in CLE lesions, although that study primarily examined chilblain lupus erythematosus, a rare CLE subtype [43]. In addition, we evaluated the expression of MxA, a well-established surrogate marker of IFN-I pathway activation [11]. MxA was markedly upregulated in both keratinocytes and dermal infiltrating lymphocytes of CLE lesional skin compared to healthy controls, which is consistent with a prior report [44]. No differences in GLUT1 or MxA expression were detected between SCLE and DLE samples. Furthermore, we found a significant positive correlation between GLUT1 expression and both lymphocyte and keratinocyte MxA expression, suggesting a potential link between GLUT1 upregulation, increased glucose metabolism, and IFN-I pathway activation in CLE. These results were further supported by analysis of publicly available cell line datasets, which demonstrated an association between MX1 and SLC2A1 in a cell intrinsic manner. A similar association was also identified in primary human keratinocytes and in keratinocyte samples, suggesting a possible coregulation of the two genes. However, no significant positive association was detected when analyzing healthy human ex vivo tissues and ACLE, SCLE, and DLE samples, likely due to the limited sample size and the inherent limitations of bulk gene expression data derived from heterogeneous skin samples. Such data represent an averaged expression signal across multiple cell types, lacking the cell type-specific resolution afforded by cell line datasets and our immunohistochemical analyses.

Our observations support the emerging concept that enhanced glucose metabolism and increased GLUT1 expression are associated with immune cell activation. GLUT1 was found to be markedly upregulated in activated T cells, with the highest levels observed in Th17, Th2, and follicular helper T cells (Tfh) in both mouse models and blood samples from SLE patients [16,45]. The critical role of glucose metabolism in adaptive immune responses is demonstrated by the impaired activation, proliferation, and differentiation of CD4+ T cells, as well as a reduced Teff lifespan in GLUT1-deficient mouse models [46]. In addition, GLUT1 upregulation plays a pivotal role in innate immunity by promoting pro-inflammatory macrophage functions, including enhanced cytokine production and phagocytic activity [47]. Likewise, GLUT1 inhibition impairs dendritic cell (DC) cytokine secretion and diminishes their capacity to stimulate T cells [48].

In alignment with its function in immune cells, GLUT1 is highly expressed in basal epidermal keratinocytes. GLUT1 is essential for enhanced keratinocyte proliferation, and its expression is markedly upregulated during wound healing and UVB-induced epidermal hyperplasia [17]. The central role of GLUT1 in keratinocyte glucose metabolism is further supported by findings showing that keratinocyte glucose transport was reduced by 95% in GLUT1-deficient mice. Furthermore, neither UV-induced hyperplasia nor physiological hyperplasia during wound healing was observed in GLUT1 knockout mice, and they did not develop psoriasiform hyperplasia following imiquimod or IL-23 treatment [49]. Additionally, chemical inhibition of GLUT1 reduced keratinocyte proliferation and significantly decreased inflammatory cell infiltration in skin samples from imiquimod-pretreated mice. From a clinical perspective, elevated GLUT1 expression may represent an initial step in psoriasis development by promoting keratinocyte hyperproliferation and triggering inflammatory and angiogenic responses [22].

A parallel dynamic exists in CLE, where both CD4+ T cells and keratinocytes play a central role in disease pathogenesis [50]. When pattern recognition receptors in keratinocytes detect endogenous nucleic acids from cellular debris, the production of high levels of type I IFNs is induced, along with other pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [51]. This cytokine milieu drives the recruitment, activation, and differentiation of CD4+ T cells, ultimately leading to keratinocyte cell death and the activation of innate immune responses [52]. Additionally, CD4+ T cells play a central role in the pathogenesis of CLE by promoting cellular immune responses and supporting the function of CD8+ T cells and macrophages [53]. Keratinocytes also express various chemokine ligands and receptors, and some are suggested to function as immune cells themselves [54]. Increased GLUT1 expression and glucose metabolism may enhance this immunological crosstalk between keratinocytes and immune cells, sustaining the autocrine inflammatory loop that drives disease severity.

Having observed elevated GLUT1 expression in keratinocytes and lymphocytes in lesional skin, we further observed the clinical implications of upregulated glucose metabolism in CLE. We identified a significant positive correlation between GLUT1 expression and the CLASI-A score, indicating a possible contribution of enhanced glucose uptake to CLE disease activity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to highlight the potential link between altered glucose metabolism and CLE clinical severity. Our results may therefore advance the understanding of glucose metabolism in CLE and contribute to the development of novel anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategies targeting immunometabolic pathways. Inclusion of a large patient cohort with comprehensive clinical data strengthens the robustness of our findings, which is further supported by transcriptomic data analysis.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of certain limitations. First, while we focused on the most prevalent CLE subtypes, the inclusion of rarer clinical presentations would provide a more comprehensive understanding of subtype-specific metabolic changes. Additionally, further studies exploring amino acid and fatty acid metabolism in CLE are warranted to clarify their contributions to disease activity.

Conclusion

Our findings identify a link between enhanced glucose metabolism, the overactivation of the IFN-I pathway, and disease severity in CLE. A deeper understanding of the dynamic interplay between metabolic regulation and immune cell activation may reveal novel therapeutic targets for CLE.
