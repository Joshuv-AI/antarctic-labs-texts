Tumor-Free Circulating Tumor DNA in Node-Negative Rectal Cancers Undergoing Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy, Excision, and Observation in the Phase II NEO Trial (CO.28)
Authors: Jonathan M. Loree, Emma Titmuss, Carl J. Brown, Thereasa Rich, Kimberly Banks, Vallerie Gordon, Sunil Patel, Marianne Krahn, Antonio Caycedo-Marulanda, Manoj Raval, Dongsheng Tu, Derek J. Jonker, Christopher J. O'Callaghan, Hagen F. Kennecke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEOrgan preservation is a priority in early-stage rectal cancer as total mesorectal excision (TME) can reduce quality of life. The Canadian Cancer Trials Group CO.28 trial demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by transanal excision was effective in patients with cT1-3, node-negative (N0) rectal cancer. Here, we explored circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) from the trial as a potential future decision aid to guide TME.MATERIALS AND METHODSFifty-three of 58 patients (91.4%) from CO.28 had at least one sample available for ctDNA analysis using the Guardant Reveal epigenomic assay from the following time points: prechemotherapy, postchemotherapy, pretransanal endoscopic surgery (TES), and on follow-up at months 12, 24, and 36.RESULTSctDNA was detected in 45.8% of patients before neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Detection was similar by T-stage (P = .18). The rate of detectable ctDNA significantly decreased after chemotherapy (and pre-TES) to 8.7% (P < .0001), with 41.4% of evaluable patients moving from detectable ctDNA to completely undetectable following chemotherapy. ctDNA detection postchemotherapy and pre-TES was associated with poor response and persistent cancer (P = .03), with recommended TME for 100% (n = 4 of 4) of patients with ctDNA detected versus 38.1% (n = 16 of 42) of patients with undetectable ctDNA. Five patients had recurrence during follow-up (two local, three distant), 50% of whom (1 of 2, both local) had longitudinal plasma collected had detectable ctDNA at progression.CONCLUSIONctDNA testing identified cancers with inadequate response to chemotherapy in patients for whom TME was recommended. A tissue-free assay may enable faster turnaround times compared with tumor-informed assays and provide a tool to support organ preservation. Further exploration of these assays in early-stage rectal cancers is warranted.

Introduction

Organ preservation has become an important consideration when designing a multidisciplinary plan for patients with early-stage rectal cancer. Surgical resection for rectal cancer with total mesorectal excision (TME) can have a significant impact on patient quality of life with temporary or permanent stomas and reduced bowel and sexual function. The addition of perioperative radiation to surgery can result in low anterior resection syndrome in up to 60% of patients.1-3 In a patient preference survey, patients were willing to accept a 20% absolute decrease in overall survival to avoid an abdominal perineal resection.4 Transanal excision surgery (TES) is used for treatment of select early-stage, node-negative rectal cancers with favorable pathologic features and requires rigorous preoperative staging and postoperative surveillance to identify appropriate candidates and recurrences. Multiple single-arm randomized studies have assessed its use after chemoradiation for cT1-3 rectal cancer as a means of extending this organ-preserving option to a larger proportion of node-negative rectal cancers. Unfortunately, this approach can increase wound healing complications, as well as bowel and sexual dysfunction, thought to be largely related to tissue damage from radiotherapy.5-10

The phase II Canadian Cancer Trials Group (CCTG) CO.28 NEO trial successfully showed that 3 months of neoadjuvant capecitabine and oxaliplatin (CAPOX) or infusional fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin without radiotherapy followed by TES can be an organ-preserving treatment option for patients with node-negative (N0) T1-T3 rectal cancer. It demonstrated favorable quality-of-life metrics compared with historic metrics with chemoradiotherapy followed by local excision, and the approaches are now being directly compared in a randomized phase III clinical trial across the National Clinical Trials Network (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06205485).11 A challenge for all approaches involving nonoperative management is surveillance and response assessment. This is typically performed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), endoscopy, physical examination, and computed tomography imaging at set intervals. However, there are situations where it can be difficult to determine if a response is a clinical complete response and the importance of detecting recurrences early to allow salvage resection is key.12 Incorrect assessment of clinical response risks patients with residual cancer having progression of their disease instead of definitive treatment or, by contrast, undergoing a more extensive resection than needed, potentially affecting quality of life. An additional decision aid may help improve response assessment and derisk strategies that avoid surgical resection.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assays for detecting molecular residual disease (MRD) have significant potential in colorectal cancer care13 and are being explored in many different clinical settings,14 but there is limited knowledge on its utility in earlier stage cancers, particularly in the setting of organ preservation. We previously explored the use of a tumor-informed assay in this setting15; however, given the lower tumor burden of these tumors, we also sought to explore the use of a tissue-free epigenomic assay. In this study, we used the Guardant Reveal assay14 in retrospective analyses of prospectively collected samples from the CO.28 trial to determine whether ctDNA in node-negative rectal cancers undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy may aid in clinical decision making.

Materials and Methods

Study Enrollment

Fifty-eight patients were enrolled in the CO.28 trial, of which 53 had at least one ctDNA sample during their follow-up available for analysis in this study. Patients enrolled in the trial had node-negative (N0), stage cT1-T3ab (determined by pelvic MRI), invasive, and well- or moderately differentiated rectal adenocarcinoma. The study was approved by the institutional review board and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided written informed consent. Full enrollment criteria can be found in the trial publication, the study by Kennecke et al.11

Sample Collection and Analysis

Whole blood was collected in EDTA tubes at the following time points: prechemotherapy, postchemotherapy (before TES), at progression, and yearly follow-up for 3 years (12, 24, and 36 months). Sample numbers collected and analyzed at each time point are included in Figure 1A. Samples were sequenced using the Guardant Reveal assay, a tissue-free epigenomic assay assessing >20,000 regions for ctDNA detection. The median cell-free DNA (cfDNA) yield per sample was 14.1 ng. The mean input into the assay was 16.4 ng, and the maximum was capped at 30 ng. A total of 190 samples (97.4%) were sequenced successfully. Samples with detectable ctDNA by the Reveal assay were subjected to comprehensive genomic profiling with the Guardant 360 panel (739 genes).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v3.6.3), and data were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.5.1). Associations between clinical features and detection rates or patient outcomes were assessed using Fisher's exact tests and univariable Cox proportional hazards models, respectively. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Time to recurrence and overall survival were measured from the date of study enrollment to recurrence or death, respectively, with patients alive at the time of last follow-up and who were without an event being censored. Patients with post-TES yp staging of ypT1 tumors with poor prognostic features, ypT2/3, or any node-positive were recommended TME, with full details in the original study publication.11

Results

Of the total trial population (N = 58), 53 (91.4%) patients had at least one successfully sequenced ctDNA sample available for analysis. The ctDNA analyzed population was similar demographically to the overall trial population (Table 1, Fig 1A). Most patients had a prechemotherapy (n = 48, 91%, Fig 1) or postchemotherapy and pretransanal excision surgery (TES; n = 46, 87%) sample, and 41 patients had both (77%). Yearly follow-up samples were available for 34 (64%), 32 (60%), and 28 (53%) patients, respectively, and 18 (34%) patients had all five time points available. Two samples were taken from two patients on progression (OP, both between months 12 and 24).

The median cfDNA yield prechemotherapy was 10.3 ng, which increased after chemotherapy to 37.4 ng (P < .0001, Fig 1B). ctDNA was detected prechemotherapy in 45.8% of patients with available samples at that time point (n = 22 of 48, Fig 1C). ctDNA detection prechemotherapy was similar across clinical T stages, though numerically higher in patients with T2 or T3 disease (P = .18, Fig 1C, 14% T1, 53% T2, 44% T3). The estimated tumor fraction in those with ctDNA detected was highest in stage cT3 samples (Fig 1D, P = .042, median T3 ctDNA detected, 0.32%; T2, 0.025%; T1, 0.033%). ctDNA detection prechemotherapy was higher in patients with higher Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) status (ECOG 1 [n = 8 of 8] 100.0% v ECOG 0 [n = 14 of 40] 35.0%, P = .0009, Fig 1E) and was similar by sex (P = .76, 40.0% [n = 6 of 15] female v 48.5% [n = 16 of 33] male) and race (P = .31, 33.0% [n = 1 of 3] Asian, 80.0% [n = 4 of 5] indigenous, 42.5% [n = 17 of 40] White).

In patients with ctDNA detected prechemotherapy, somatic variants were detected in 91% (n = 20 of 22; no variants in patients 20 and 24).

After neoadjuvant chemotherapy (median 14.5 days postchemotherapy, range, 0-56 days), ctDNA detection was lower overall, with only four patients having ctDNA detected (n = 4 of 46, 8.7%, P < .0001 v prechemotherapy, Fig 1C). The largest difference was observed in stage clinical T2 tumors (53.1% ctDNA detected prechemotherapy v 3.6% postchemotherapy, P < .0001, Fig 1C). The estimated tumor percentage in samples with ctDNA detected remained similar at both time points (0.028% prechemotherapy v 0.037% postchemotherapy and pre-TES, P = .75, Fig 1F). Of the 41 patients with both pre- and postchemotherapy samples available, 48.8% (n = 20, Fig 1F) did not have ctDNA detected at either time point, 43.9% (18) had reduced tumor fraction after chemotherapy (the majority completely cleared ctDNA [94.4%; 17 of 18]), and 7.3% had increased tumor fraction (n = 3).

A total of 23 patients were recommended TME after a poor response to chemotherapy or high-risk features at the time of TES (Fig 2A, Methods). All four patients with ctDNA detected postchemotherapy (patients 22, 24, 30 and 37) were recommended TME (100.0% v 38.1% without ctDNA detected, P = .03, Figs 2A and 2B) although only two underwent the procedure (patient 24 and 37). The others declined TME (patients 22 and 30, post-TES staging T2 and T1, respectively). None of the patients, 22, 30, or 37, have had ctDNA detected on longitudinal sampling and are all still alive with a follow-up of over 3 years, whereas patient 24 did recur. Of the 17 patients who cleared ctDNA after chemotherapy (Figs 1F and 2A), 35.0% (n = 6) did not have sufficient downstaging after chemotherapy and were recommended TME. There was no difference in recommendation of TME between patients who had detectable or undetectable ctDNA prechemotherapy (50.0% detected v 38.5% not detected, P = .38, Fig 2B). For patients who were recommended TME, we did not observe any difference in clinical characteristics between those who had ctDNA detected postchemotherapy and those that did not (clinical T stage, P = .19; ECOG status, P = .25; race, P = .37; sex, P = 1; age, P = .80).

In addition to recommendation of TME, we also explored the impact of ctDNA detection both pre- and postchemotherapy on patient outcomes. We did not note differences in recurrence rate based on detection of ctDNA before or after chemotherapy (Fig 2C) although sample size affected this comparison. We observed similar recurrence-free survival (RFS) between patients who had detectable ctDNA versus not detectable ctDNA at both the prechemotherapy (hazard ratio [HR], 0.79 [95% CI, 0.13 to 4.75], P = .80; 3-year RFS 90.9% v 88.5%; Fig 2D) and postchemotherapy/pre-TES time point (HR, 2.82 [95% CI, 0.31 to 25.29], P = .36; 3-year RFS 75.0% v 90.5%; Fig 2D).

All five patients with a clinical recurrence had both pre- and postchemotherapy samples available. Three of these (patient 8, 17 and 57) had no ctDNA detected at both time points. Both patients 8 and 17 had distant recurrence to the lungs and lymph nodes approximately 2.5 years after TES and patient 57 had a local recurrence at approximately 1.5 years. Patient 57 had longitudinal sampling available at 12, 24, and 36 months post-TES, as well as a ctDNA sample at the time of progression, all of which did not have ctDNA detected. All three patients are still alive with a follow-up of more than 4 years. Another patient (Patient 24) had ctDNA detected at both pre-TES time points, with the estimated tumor percentage decreasing 8-fold from 0.25% to 0.030% after chemotherapy. The patient did not have sufficient downstaging after chemotherapy (cT2 to pT2) and so underwent the recommended TME surgery and later had distant clinical recurrence in lymph nodes approximately 2 years after TES, which led to death approximately 3.5 years after TES. This patient did not have any ctDNA samples taken after the postchemotherapy time point. The remaining patient with a clinical recurrence (patient 19) had ctDNA detected prechemotherapy but not postchemotherapy and pre-TES. They were recommended TME after a poor response to chemotherapy; however, the patient declined. Longitudinal sampling at 12 months post-TES did not have ctDNA detected. The patient had local recurrence to the rectum 18 months post-TES, at which point another ctDNA sample was taken (at progression), which had ctDNA detected. No OP ctDNA was available for those with distant recurrences (n = 3 of 5), and no ctDNA was detected in all 90 longitudinal samples from patients without recurrence.

Discussion

In this trial of node-negative, early-stage rectal cancers, we observed detectable ctDNA using a tissue-free assay before neoadjuvant chemotherapy in 45.8% of patients. This compared favorably with prior work our group has presented, with a tumor-informed assay demonstrating a pretreatment detection rate of 12 of 49 (24.5%) in the same clinical trial population.15 Our observation of no difference in detection rates by clinical T stage also differed from our prior work that showed higher detection rates with higher clinical stage.15 This supports the idea that results from ctDNA studies must be continually reassessed as assay performance changes. This is particularly true with the use case of node-negative rectal cancer where tumor burden is low.

Despite the small cohort size and low event rate, we did observe that detectable ctDNA postchemotherapy was associated with persistent cancer and recommendation of TME, with one of the four positive patients experiencing death from relapsed rectal cancer despite otherwise having low clinical risk disease. The potential for ctDNA to identify patients at risk of poor response and outcome is particularly important when considering ctDNA as an adjunct to current clinical decision making as such patients may benefit from closer follow-up.

Our study presents some of the earliest stage rectal cancers to be profiled for MRD with ctDNA (including 15% of patients with T1N0 disease). Node-negative tumors are a particularly important subgroup to interrogate with ctDNA as node status is challenging to assess using MRI compared with other prognostic markers.16 The results presented here indicate that tissue-free assays, such as Guardant Reveal, could be clinically useful to aid in decision making in this early-stage setting. The best possible test for each patient and clinical indication may differ. In this study, the use of a tumor-naïve assay was important as approximately 10% of patients were previously shown in this cohort to have insufficient diagnostic tissue to create a tumor-informed panel.15

Other studies exploring ctDNA in locally advanced rectal cancers have included patients with either higher-stage disease and positive nodes.17 Most of these previous studies used mutation-based panels (tumor-naïve and/or tumor-agnostic) or explored cfDNA concentrations alone as opposed to a methylation-based approach. In the NO-CUT trial, the same Guardant Reveal assay was used as this study; however, it included patients with cT3-4 and/or cN1-2 pMMR rectal adenocarcinomas who were receiving total neoadjuvant therapy with four cycles of CAPOX and concurrent chemoradiotherapy.18 In that study, they also saw a higher rate of ctDNA detection (8 v 31%, P = .008) for patients with an incomplete response, compared with those with a complete response. In their larger trial with more events, they also saw that ctDNA detection after surgery had an increased risk of recurrence (HR, 6.99 [95% CI, 2.19 to 22.39], P = .001). We did not see the same association with prognosis based on ctDNA detection after surgery; however, the event rate in our study was very low because of the low-risk nature of the studied population, making our study underpowered for such comparisons.

Another recent study using the Guardant Reveal assay was the PEGASUS trial, exploring stage III and high-risk T4N0 stage II patients with colon cancer in the postsurgical and postadjuvant setting. The PEGASUS study displayed a similar proportion of recurrences in patients without ctDNA detected (ie, false negatives; 12% compared with 9.5% from this study). Both PEGASUS and this study had collected samples for ctDNA at a similar time postsurgery/chemotherapy (approximately 2 weeks [2-4 PEGASUS, median 14 days this study]), which may affect ctDNA detection because of increased shedding of total cfDNA.19-21

The limitations of this study, namely, small cohort size, low recurrence rate, and missing longitudinal sampling in some patients, may result in an underestimation of sensitivity and lead time for recurrence prediction. This is of particular importance in this setting of organ preservation as patients who declined TME have the highest risk of recurrence and thus the most to benefit from additional decision tools. Local recurrences are of particular importance in this early-stage population, and we did detect ctDNA in one of the two local recurrences seen in our trial. Our findings suggest that further studies are warranted to understand the clinical utility of ctDNA assays as supplemental tools to endoscopic and MRI assessments in selecting patients for organ preservation. Larger, ideally prospective interventional studies, with longer follow-up, may be particularly beneficial to evaluate ctDNA in this low-risk population and allow for exploration of ctDNA in combination with other clinical assessments.
