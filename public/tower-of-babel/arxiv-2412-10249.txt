Stochastic Multiresolution Image Sketching for Inverse Imaging Problems Thanks: Submitted to the editors of SIIMS.
Alessandro Perelli, Carola-Bibiane Schönlieb, Matthias J. Ehrhardt

Abstract
A challenge in high-dimensional inverse problems is developing iterative solvers to find the accurate solution of regularized optimization problems with low computational cost. An important example is computed tomography (CT) where both image and data sizes are large and therefore the forward model is costly to evaluate. Since several years algorithms from stochastic optimization are used for tomographic image reconstruction with great success by subsampling the data. Here we propose a novel way how stochastic optimization can be used to speed up image reconstruction by means of image domain sketching such that at each iteration an image of different resolution is being used. Hence, we coin this algorithm ImaSk . By considering an associated saddle-point problem, we can formulate ImaSk as a gradient-based algorithm where the gradient is approximated in the same spirit as the stochastic average gradient amélioré (SAGA) and uses at each iteration one of these multiresolution operators at random. We prove that ImaSk is linearly converging for linear forward models with strongly convex regularization functions. Numerical simulations on CT show that ImaSk is effective and increasing the number of multiresolution operators reduces the computational time to reach the modeled solution.

Introduction

Inverse problems involving estimating unknown images from noisy measurements arises in many medical and non-medical imaging applications such as X-ray Computed Tomography (CT), Positron Emission Tomography (PET) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI).
If the noise is modeled as Gaussian, then the problem can be mathematically described as
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n}
where
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
represents the physical forward model and
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the mean-zero Gaussian noise. Nowadays with the deluge of acquired data and the aim to reconstruct images at fine resolution, an emerging challenge is constituted by reducing the computational cost while preserving the accuracy of the solution
[
2
]
.

An established framework for estimating the unknown high-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}
from the measurements
\mathbf{b}
is to solve a model-based optimization problem of the form

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+R(\mathbf{x})

(1)

where
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
describes the regularization term which enforce a particular structure on the possible solutions for
\mathbf{x}

[
15
]
.
Many iterative solvers have been developed to minimize the original model-based cost function
[
18
]
.
First-order methods and its accelerated versions generally involve operations like matrix-vector multiplications and evaluations of proximal operators
[
10
]
, which are for many problems in closed form or easy to compute iteratively
[
6
]
.
From a computational point of view, a main bottleneck of any iterative solvers used to minimize
eq.

1
is the application of the forward operator
\mathbf{K}
and its adjoint
[
1
,
26
]
.

A popular approach to overcome this computational bottleneck is to change the discretisation of the image. E.g. in
[
21
]
chooses a nonuniform triangular grid for PET reconstruction where the resolution is selected a-priori from a second modality. An inexact multi-level version of FISTA is proposed in
[
20
]
.

A similar concept was previously exploited by multi-grid reconstruction methods which are based on partitioning the image of interest in order to reduce the computation time and memory required
[
22
]
, since in practical settings often only a specific region of interest needs to be analyzed at full resolution. Alternative methods are based on the multiresolution wavelet structure
[
33
]
which allows to iteratively impose regularization constraints on an hierarchy of fine to coarse resolution images.

Recently a wide area of research is constituted by the development of scalable gradient-based algorithms which use only a random subset of the measurements at each iteration; notable methods are stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
19
]
and other Stochastic Variance Reduced methods
[
35
,
17
]
such as the improved stochastic average gradient (SAGA)
[
11
]
which can reduce the variance of the stochastic gradient estimator progressively and improve the convergence of SGD methods. Stochastic algorithms have also been applied to saddle-point optimization problems
[
3
,
9
,
14
,
12
]
.

Furthermore, different types of dimensionality reduction operators, called sketches, in the measurement domain have been developed within iterative convergent algorithms
[
34
]
. Random sketches are used in second-order method for optimization to perform approximate Newton step using a randomly projected or sub-sampled Hessian
[
27
]
.

Randomized algorithms using sketched gradients are leveraged to solve least squares problems or to reduce the computation of proximal algorithms
[
30
]
or optimization using regularization by denoising
[
25
]
. Over the last years, several of the aforementioned algorithms have been applied to imaging problems, most notably to CT and PET, see e.g.
[
14
,
31
,
29
]
but also other imaging modalities like magnetic particle imaging
[
36
]
. For an overview on the field and further references, we refer to
[
13
]
. As in machine learning, all of these approaches randomly sample the data. In contrast, we use a novel random sampling in image space.

1.1
Contribution

Inspired by the notion of sketching, we propose a novel algorithm that uses cheaper matrix-vector products essentially by sketching in the image domain. To this end we decompose the forward operator using a family of multiresolution operators and consider the corresponding saddle point formulation. The latter reveals a structure commonly encountered in stochastic optimization which can be approached by various gradient estimators such as SAGA. Note that the original formulation does not the reveal the averaging property that is used for efficient stochastic gradient based solvers. From a computational perspective, this allows to reduce the per-iteration cost of the iterative algorithm and thereby potentially an overall more efficient algorithm. We analyze its linear convergence rate and give sufficient conditions on its parameters for convergence. While this approach is similar to
[
3
]
, our analysis reveals more general conditions and better convergence rates. We also study the behavior of the algorithm for CT image reconstruction in terms of the number of resolutions, step-size, convergence rate and computational time.

3
Randomized Algorithm for Saddle-Point Problems

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be Hilbert spaces of any dimension and consider

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x})\right\},

(5)

where
f:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty},g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
are convex functions and
\mathbf{A}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a bounded linear operator. We consider solving the general optimization problem
eq.

5
via a family of operators
\mathbf{A}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
such that

\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}.

By construction
\mathbf{A}_{i}/p_{i}
is an unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{A}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathbf{A}_{i}/p_{i}\right]=\mathbf{A}
. Then
eq.

5
can be reformulated as

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\Psi(\mathbf{x}):=f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{x}\right)+g(\mathbf{x})\right\}.

(6)

A close look at
eq.

6
shows that this objective function
\Psi
is not affine in the sampling. This prevents the use of traditional stochastic gradient descent (SGD) solvers which usually use ”unbiased” samples of gradients.

In order to operate in a regime amendable to current stochastic optimization algorithms, instead of solving
eq.

6
, we reformulate the original minimization as a saddle-point problem by using the convex conjugate of
f

[
4
]
,

f^{*}(\mathbf{y}):=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{dom}f}\Big\{\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{z})\Big\}
. Using the convex conjugate, it results that solving
eq.

6
is equivalent to finding the primal vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
of a solution to the saddle point problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{x}\rangle-f^{*}(\mathbf{y})+g(\mathbf{x})\right\}.

(7)

It is important to remark that the problem
eq.

7
cannot be solved by other stochastic primal-dual algorithms, such as the Stochastic Primal-Dual Hybrid Gradient algorithm (SPDHG)
[
7
]
since they require the objective to be separable in the dual variable, i.e.,
f
and
\mathbf{y}
are indexed by
i
.

Since the proposed strategy for
eq.

7
is built upon SAGA-type gradient estimators, we review the SAGA method for optimization.

Convergence Analysis

It is convenient to analyze the convergence via a single-variable formulation, see
algorithm

5
. We define the joint variable
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{\widetilde{x}},\mathbf{\widetilde{y}})\in\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
where the
\mathcal{Z}
is equipped with the inner product
\langle(\mathbf{\widetilde{x}},\mathbf{\widetilde{y}}),(\mathbf{\widetilde{x}}^{\prime},\mathbf{\widetilde{y}}^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{\widetilde{x}},\mathbf{\widetilde{x}}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{\widetilde{y}},\mathbf{\widetilde{y}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{z}):=\widetilde{g}(\mathbf{\widetilde{x}})+\widetilde{f}^{*}(\mathbf{\widetilde{y}})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{B}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}^{*}\\
-\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right].

We denote its norm by
L:=\|\mathbf{B}\|
. Note that
\mathbf{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{B}_{i}
with

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}^{*}_{i}\\
-\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}_{i}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right].

\mathbf{z}^{K}

\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}&\text{else}\end{cases}

Associated to
\mathbf{B}
is two further operators related to the correlations between the
\mathbf{B}_{i}

Let
\bar{\mathbf{B}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{n},(\bar{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{z})_{i}:=\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}
and its size
\bar{L}:=\|\bar{\mathbf{B}}\|
will be an important constant in the convergence analysis. Note that
\bar{L}
can alternatively be defined as

\bar{L}^{2}=\sup_{\|\mathbf{z}\|\leq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}\|^{2}.

(13)

Furthermore, another important constant will be
\bar{L}_{p}
defined as the norm of
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{p}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}^{n},(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{p}\mathbf{z})_{i}:=p_{i}^{-1/2}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}

The operator
\mathbf{B}
has some properties that will be exploited in the convergence analysis.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be defined as in
section

3.4
. Then
L=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|
and
\mathbf{B}
is skew-symmetric, in particular
\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}\rangle=0
.

Proof.

Regarding the first assertion, given
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}^{*}\mathbf{y},-\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\mathbf{x})\right\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}^{*}\mathbf{y}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|^{2}(\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2})=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2},

thus,
\|\mathbf{B}\|\leq\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|
. Now, for
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
with
\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|
and
\mathbf{y}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}\|=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|\|\mathbf{z}\|.

Therefore, the upped bound is obtained, leading to
L=\|\mathbf{B}\|=\|\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\|
.

Regarding the second assertion,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}\rangle=\langle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),(\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}^{*}\mathbf{y},-\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\mathbf{x})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}^{*}\mathbf{y}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}\mathbf{x}\rangle=0.

Remark 4
.

In order to understand these constants better, consider the case of uniform sampling
p_{i}=1/n
and the same operator
\mathbf{\widetilde{A}}_{i}=\mathbf{C},i=1,\dots,n
. Then
L=\bar{L}_{p}=n\|\mathbf{C}\|
and
\bar{L}=\sqrt{n}\|\mathbf{C}\|
.

We are now in a position to state the main convergence theorem for
algorithm

5
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathbf{z}^{k}
be the iterations defined by
algorithm

5
and
\mathbf{z}^{*}
the saddle point of
eq.

10
. Then after
K
iterations, for any
a,b>0
the iterates satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}^{K}-\mathbf{z}^{*}\|^{2}\leq\theta^{K}C

with the convergence factor

\displaystyle\theta:=\max\left(\frac{1+\sigma^{2}(L^{2}+(1+a)\bar{L}_{p}^{2})}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}+b\bar{L}^{2},\frac{\left(1+a^{-1}\right)\sigma^{2}}{b(1+\sigma)^{2}}+1-\min_{i}p_{i}\right)

and constant
C:=\|\mathbf{z}^{0}-\mathbf{z}^{*}\|^{2}+b\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{0}_{i}\|^{2}
.

We prove
theorem

5
by combining
lemma

7
and
lemma

8
. In order to simplify the analysis in

7
we use the following classical lemma which estimates the variance by its second-order moment.

Lemma 6
.

Let be
X
a random variable, then
\mathbb{E}\|X-\mathbb{E}X\|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{2}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}
denote the expectation with respect to
i_{k}
and define the error in the iterates by
\Delta_{k}:=\|\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{z}^{*}\|^{2}
and the error in the gradient by
\Gamma_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}
where
\mathbf{z}^{k}
are the iterates defined via
algorithm

5
.

Then for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
a>0
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\Delta_{k+1}\leq\frac{1+\sigma^{2}(L^{2}+(1+a)\bar{L}_{p}^{2})}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\Delta_{k}+\frac{(1+a^{-1})\sigma^{2}}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\Gamma_{k}.

Proof.

Note that the saddle point
\mathbf{z}^{*}
of
eq.

10
satisfies
\mathbf{z}^{*}=\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma h}\big(\mathbf{z}^{*}-\sigma\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}^{*}\big)
.
Let
\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}:=\frac{1}{p_{i_{k}}}\left(\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{g}_{i_{k}}^{k}\right)
with expected value
\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}^{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}
. Then we can write
\mathbf{z}^{k+1}=\operatorname{prox}_{\sigma h}(\mathbf{z}^{k}-\sigma(\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}))
. Using the fact that the proximal mapping of strongly convex functions with constant 1 is contractive
[
24
]
in the sense that

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma h}(\mathbf{z})-\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma h}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\|\leq(1+\sigma)^{-1}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\|,

we derive the first estimate on the error
\mathbf{e}^{k}:=\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{z}^{*}
as

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{e}^{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\sigma}\left\|\mathbf{z}^{k}-\sigma\left(\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\right)-\left(\mathbf{z}^{*}-\sigma\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}^{*}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\sigma}\left\|\mathbf{e}^{k}-\sigma\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}^{k}-\sigma\left(\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}-\mathbb{E}_{j_{k}}\mathbf{f}_{j_{k}}^{k}\right)\right\|.

Using elementary properties of the expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\Delta_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\left\|\mathbf{e}^{k}-\sigma\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}^{k}+\sigma\left(\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}-\mathbb{E}_{j_{k}}\mathbf{f}_{j_{k}}^{k}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\|\mathbf{e}^{k}-\sigma\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\|\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}-\mathbb{E}_{j_{k}}\mathbf{f}_{j_{k}}^{k}\|^{2}.

(14)

We analyze the deterministic and random terms in
eq.

14
separately. Using
lemma

3
we can bound the deterministic term as

\|\mathbf{e}^{k}-\sigma\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}^{k}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{e}^{k}\|^{2}-2\sigma\langle\mathbf{e}^{k},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}^{k}\rangle+\sigma^{2}\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}^{k}\|^{2}\leq(1+\sigma^{2}L^{2})\Delta_{k}.

(15)

Using
lemma

6
and the definition of the expectation we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\|\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}-\mathbb{E}_{j_{k}}\mathbf{f}_{j_{k}}^{k}\|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\|\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}.

With
\|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\|^{2}\leq(1+a)\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}+(1+a^{-1})\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
for any
a>0
we can further estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{j_{k}}\|\mathbf{f}_{j_{k}}^{k}-\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{f}_{i_{k}}^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{k}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{e}^{k}+\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+a}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{e}^{k}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+a^{-1}}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+a)\bar{L}_{p}^{2}\Delta_{k}+(1+a^{-1})\Gamma_{k}.

(16)

Inserting
eq.

15
and
16
into
eq.

14
yields the assertion.

Lemma 8
.

Using the same notation as in
lemma

7
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\Gamma_{k+1}\leq\bar{L}^{2}\Delta_{k}+(1-\min_{i}p_{i})\Gamma_{k}.

Proof.

To analyze
\Gamma_{k}
, we note that
\mathbf{g}^{k+1}_{i}=\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}
if
i\neq i_{k}
, while
\mathbf{g}^{k+1}_{i_{k}}=\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{k}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\Gamma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\left(\sum_{i\neq i_{k}}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{p_{i_{k}}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{k}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}\left(\frac{1}{p_{i_{k}}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{p_{i_{k}}}\|\mathbf{B}_{i_{k}}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i_{k}}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{e}^{k}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{*}-\mathbf{g}^{k}_{i}\|^{2}

and the assertion follows.

Proof of
theorem

5
.

Taking a linear combination of the estimates in
lemma

7
and
lemma

8
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}(\Delta_{k+1}+b\Gamma_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1+\sigma^{2}(L^{2}+(1+a)\bar{L}_{p}^{2})}{(1+\sigma)^{2}}+b\bar{L}^{2}\right)\Delta_{k}+\left(\frac{\left(1+a^{-1}\right)\sigma^{2}}{b(1+\sigma)^{2}}+1-\min_{i}p_{i}\right)b\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\theta(\Delta_{k}+b\Gamma_{k}).

Taking expectations, we recursively derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{K}+b\Gamma_{K})\leq\theta^{K}(\Delta_{0}+b\Gamma_{0}).

The assertion follows since
\Gamma_{K}
is nonnegative.

3.5
Range of Admissible Step-Sizes

In the following we will discuss the consequences of
theorem

5
on the step-size parameter
\sigma
.
It is convenient to reparameterize the step-size as
\eta=\sigma/(1+\sigma)\in(0,1)
such that
1-\eta=1/(1+\sigma)

and the convergence factor becomes

\displaystyle\theta_{a,b}(\eta)=\max(\phi_{a,b}(\eta),\psi_{a,b}(\eta))

(17)

with

\displaystyle\phi_{a,b}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=c^{-1}(\eta-c)^{2}+1+b\bar{L}^{2}-c,\quad\psi_{a,b}(\eta):=\frac{1+a^{-1}}{b}\eta^{2}+1-\min_{i}p_{i}

(18)

and
c=(1+L^{2}+(1+a)\bar{L}_{p}^{2})^{-1}
. Note that since
a>0
we have
c\in(0,\bar{c})
with
\bar{c}:=(1+L^{2}+\bar{L}_{p}^{2})^{-1}\leq 1
.
Since
\phi_{a,b}
is minimized at
c
with
\phi_{a,b}(c)=1+b\bar{L}^{2}-c
, we have immediately a necessary condition on
b
.

Lemma 9
.

If
b\bar{L}^{2}\geq c
, then for all
\eta\geq 0
it holds that
\phi_{a,b}(\eta)\geq 1
. Similarly, if
b\bar{L}^{2}<c
, there exists
\eta\geq 0
such that
\phi_{a,b}(\eta)<1
.

Due to
lemma

9
and the aforementioned discussion, it makes sense to reparametrize
\phi_{a,b}
and
\psi_{a,b}
. To this end, let
\rho\in(0,1)
and
b=\rho c/\bar{L}^{2}
. Then we have
b\bar{L}^{2}=\rho c<c
and the assertion of
lemma

9
is naturally satisfied. Using
b=\rho c/\bar{L}^{2}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\phi_{\rho,c}(\eta)

\displaystyle=c^{-1}(\eta-c)^{2}+1-(1-\rho)c.

Of course, here we abused the notation by reusing the symbol
\phi
but it should be clear from the context which parametrization is being used.

Similarly, we can express
a^{-1}
in terms of
c
as

\displaystyle a^{-1}=\frac{\bar{L}_{p}^{2}c}{1-(1+L^{2}+\bar{L}_{p}^{2})c}

and thus, with

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}:=\frac{1+a^{-1}}{b}=\frac{1-(1+L^{2})c}{(1-(1+L^{2}+\bar{L}_{p}^{2})c)b}=\frac{\bar{L}^{2}}{\rho c}\frac{1-(1+L^{2})c}{1-(1+L^{2}+\bar{L}_{p}^{2})c}

(19)

we have

\displaystyle\psi_{c,\rho}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1}\eta^{2}+1-\min_{i}p_{i}.

With these reparametrizations, one can readily see the admissible ranges.

Lemma 10
.

Let
c\in(0,\bar{c}),\rho\in(0,1)
. Then
\phi_{c,\rho}(\eta)<1
if and only if

\eta\in(c-c\sqrt{1-\rho},c+c\sqrt{1-\rho}).

Proof.

This result follows immediately from solving the quadratic equation
\phi_{c,\rho}(\eta)=1
in terms of
\eta
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
c\in(0,\bar{c}),\rho\in(0,1)
. Then
\psi_{c,\rho}(\eta)<1
if and only if
\eta<\sqrt{\alpha\min_{i}p_{i}}
.

Proof.

This result is immediately obtained by solving the quadratic equation
\psi_{c,\rho}(\eta)=1
in terms of
\eta
.

Proposition 12
.

Let
c\in(0,\bar{c}),\rho\in(0,1)
. Then
\theta_{c,\rho}(\eta)<1
if and only if

\eta\in(c-c\sqrt{1-\rho},c+c\sqrt{1-\rho})\cap(0,\sqrt{\alpha}\min_{i}\sqrt{p_{i}}).

Proof.

\theta_{c,\rho}(\eta)<1
is the case if and only if
\phi_{c,\rho}(\eta)<1
and
\psi_{c,\rho}(\eta)<1
. Then the result follows directly from
lemma

10
and
lemma

11
.

3.6
Optimal Step-Sizes

Notice that
\phi_{c,\rho}
and
\psi_{c,\rho}

\phi_{c,\rho}
is minimized at

\phi_{c,\rho}(c)=1-(1-\rho)c

\psi_{c,\rho}

\psi_{c,\rho}(0)=1-\min_{i}p_{i}

Lemma 13
.

There is an unique optimal
\eta^{*}\in[0,c]
that minimizes the convergence factor
\theta_{c,\rho}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\theta_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})\geq 1-\min\{(1-\rho)c,p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}.

Theorem 14
.

There is an unique optimal
\eta^{*}\in(0,c]
that minimizes the convergence factor
\theta_{c,\rho}
. Furthermore, if

\displaystyle\min_{i}p_{i}>\left(\frac{\bar{L}^{2}}{\rho}\frac{1-(1+L^{2})c}{1-(1+L^{2}+\bar{L}_{p}^{2})c}+1-\rho\right)c,

(20)

\eta^{*}=c
and
\theta_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})=1-(1-\rho)c

Otherwise,

\displaystyle\eta^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{1+(\alpha^{-1}-c^{-1})(\min_{i}p_{i}+\rho c)}-1}{\alpha^{-1}-c^{-1}}

\theta_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})=\phi_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})=\psi_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})

Proof.

With

for all
\eta\in[0,c]
iff
\phi_{c,\rho}(0)<\psi_{c,\rho}(0)
iff
\eta^{*}=0
and
\theta^{*}=\psi_{c,\rho}(0)

3.

\eta^{*}\in(0,c)
and
\theta^{*}=\phi_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})=\psi_{c,\rho}(\eta^{*})

The first case is equivalent to
eq.

20

Note that this is true for sufficiently small

The second case is equivalent to

In the third case, the optimal step size can be computed as in (

Numerical Results

4.1
Efficient Image Sketching

For simplicity, we outline the algorithm in 2D for square images but it can be readily generalized to other dimensions and geometries. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d=2^{n},\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, be the vectorized version of an
\sqrt{d}\times\sqrt{d}
image at
“full”
resolution which is mapped to measurements
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by a linear forward operator
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. For instance, in CT the operator
\mathbf{K}
models the X-ray line tracing geometry from source to detectors, crossing through the discretized image pixels at different angles and maps into projection measurements (sinogram). The measurements dimension
m
is dictated by the geometry and can be calculated as the product of the size of the detector array and the number of angles. In a naive sequential implementation, this operator scales linearly in the image dimension
\sqrt{d}
.
Figure

4
shows a numerical demonstration, with an implementation of the operator
\mathbf{K}
for the X-ray CT problem, that in practice the relationship can be fit as a linear scaling. Thus, in order to reduce the computation, we aim to construct operators
\mathbf{K}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
which map an image in a low-resolution discretized grid of dimension
\sqrt{d_{i}}\times\sqrt{d_{i}}
to measurements of the original dimension
m
which are similar to
\mathbf{K}
. As long as reducing the dimension is cheap, the computational cost of
\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{x}
compared to
\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}
is then about
\sqrt{d}/\sqrt{d_{i}}
cheaper.
We define the operator
\mathbf{M}
to convert a
d
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into a
\sqrt{d}\times\sqrt{d}
matrix
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}
.

The linear mappings from full to low resolution grids in the image domain can be modeled with the decimator operators
\mathbf{T}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}},\,i=1,\ldots,r-1
from the full-resolution
r
, images
\sqrt{d}\times\sqrt{d}
, to low-resolution
\mathbf{M}\left[\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{x}\right]
images by performing downsampling of the factor
2^{i}\times 2^{i}
. The simplest of such operators are
2^{i}\times 2^{i}
-block averaging defined as following:

\mathbf{M}\left[\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{x}\right]\left(j,k\right)=\frac{1}{2^{2i}}\sum_{s=1}^{2^{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{2^{i}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\left(2^{i}(j-1)+s,2^{i}(k-1)+t\right),\quad j,k\in\left\{1,\ldots,\sqrt{d}/2^{i}\right\}

(23)

with adjoint

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\left[\mathbf{T}^{*}_{i}\mathbf{x}\right]\left(j,k\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\left(\left\lceil j/2^{i}\right\rceil,\left\lceil k/2^{i}\right\rceil\right),\quad j,k\in\{1,\ldots,\sqrt{d}\}.

It is important to note that
\mathbf{S}_{i}=\mathbf{T}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{T}_{i}
is proportional to the projection onto the subspace of piece-wise constant images, see also

.

Given a full resolution image
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the bounded linear operator
\mathbf{K}
, it is possible to define a family of low resolution linear sketches
\mathbf{K}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m},i=1,\ldots,r-1
as following

\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{x}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{T}^{T}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{R}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{x}&,&i=1,\ldots,r-1\\
\mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}_{r}\mathbf{x}&,&i=r\end{array}\right.

(24)

where
p_{i}
are the probability of selecting low resolution sketches while
p_{r}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}p_{i}
is the probability of selecting the full resolution operator.
It is important to highlight that in practice in the low resolution case,
i=1,\ldots,r-1
, the operation
\mathbf{K}\mathbf{T}^{*}_{i}
can be approximated with
\mathbf{R}_{i}
which represents the application of the forward model on the
i
th low resolution grid. This means that it is never required to compute matrix-vector multiplications at full resolution. Moreover, the up-sampling
\mathbf{T}^{*}_{i}
is never explicitly computed. Recalling the CT case,
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times m}
represents the X-ray tracing model from the low dimensional
\sqrt{d_{i}}\times\sqrt{d_{i}}
input grid to the
m
measurements.
In
eq.

24
for the full resolution case,
i=r
, the linear mapping
\mathbf{T}^{*}_{r}\mathbf{T}_{r}
is derived as a result of the constraint
eq.

2
as

\mathbf{S}_{r}=p_{r}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}p_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{T}_{i}\right).

6
Discussion

The numerical simulations clearly show the benefit of using low resolution sketches to reduce the computational time to reach the convergence; although the theoretical bound are not tight with the numerical results it is worth noting how the slope of the numerical results for different number of resolutions are consistent with the bounds. In the analysis of the computation of the CT operator with ASTRA implementation, we have considered a fixed linear factor of 2 for the computation gain at lower resolutions but it emerges that at higher full resolution the linear factor (respect to lower iterations) is higher than 2, meaning that the computational gain using
ImaSk
can further improve. Moreover,
ImaSk
performance can noticeably improve for imaging system whose forward operators can enjoy a sub-linear time cost for lower resolutions. It is worth noting that although the theory requires the regularization function to be a
\mu_{g}
strongly convex function, the numerical results show that also with the TV regularization with nonnegativity constraint the
ImaSk
algorithm is converging.

The following aspects of the
ImaSk
algorithm can be further analyzed in the future:

•

The current
ImaSk
algorithm is derived and analyzed in the case where the operators both for the primal and dual update are selected using a common random variable from a single probability distribution. This framework can be further extended to the case where the primal and dual operators are selected using two independent random variables from the discrete probability distributions; this scenario models the case where primal and dual operators can have different low-resolution at the same iteration of the
ImaSk
algorithm. This study can leas to understand what is the optimal sketch selection configuration to minimize the overall computational complexity of the algorithm.

•

The current method can be extended to also incorporate random sketching operators in the measurement domain such as random row sub-selection which could in theory further reduce the overall computation.

•

Furthermore, the proposed multiresolution
ImaSk
can be extended to non-linear measurement models which arise in many imaging applications such as spectral CT.

7
Conclusion

In this work we presented a multi-resolution sketch algorithm, called
ImaSk
to reduce the computational time fro solving inverse imaging problems. By using the saddle-point formulation of the original regularized optimization problem, we developed a convergent stochastic primal dual algorithm that is able to use at each iteration a low resolution deterministic sketch of the full resolution image and we theoretically and numerically analysed the case where the low resolutions are selected using a uniform discrete probability distribution. We prove that the
ImaSk
algorithm converges to the same solution while reducing the overall time compared to using the full resolution operators. We presented the numerical results for the CT reconstruction problem using
L_{2}
and non-smooth TV regularization. Finally, we introduced the
ImaSk-Seq
variant which uses the primal-dual sequential updates and we numerically show that it significantly improves computationally compared to
ImaSk
