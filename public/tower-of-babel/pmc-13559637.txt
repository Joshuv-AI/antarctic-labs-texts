The risk factors and patterns for distant metastasis in early-stage cervical adenocarcinoma patients receiving radical surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy: the ATTRACT study
Authors: Jia-Wei Zhu, Xi-Lin Yang, Yun-Can Zhou, Yan You, Peng Peng, Hong-Nan Zhen, Xi Wang, Xin Lian, Jun-Fang Yan, Fu-Quan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical adenocarcinoma (ADC) accounts for 15–25% of cervical cancer cases and carries a higher risk of distant metastasis (DM) and a poorer prognosis than squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). However, the DM patterns and risk factors in ADC patients receiving radical surgery and postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to explore the DM pattern and risk factors associated with DM in ADC patients receiving radical surgery and postoperative radiotherapy.MethodsPatients with pathologically confirmed ADC after radical surgery were selected. The DM pattern was described using a metastatic organ distribution map. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and intergroup differences were compared using the log-rank test. Risk factors for DM were identified through Cox regression analysis. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve was used to evaluate the association between the odds ratio (OR) for DM and cumulative platinum dose throughout treatment.ResultsThe most prevalent DM organ was the lung (51.1%), followed by liver (26.7%) and bone (24.4%). No significant difference regarding 5-year overall survival (OS) was observed between patients with single-site DM and those with multiple-site DM (44.2% vs. 44.9%; P>0.99). Similarly, patients without synchronous loco-regional failure (LRR) did not outperform those with synchronous LRR (49.6% vs. 44.4%; P=0.21). Lymph node metastasis (LNM) and depth of myometrial invasion (DOI) ≥1/2 were independent risk factors for DM. Three risk subgroups with distinct 5-year distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) were constructed, including high-risk (LNM+ and DOI ≥1/2), middle-risk (LNM+ and DOI <1/2; LNM− and DOI ≥1/2), and low-risk subgroup (LNM− and DOI <1/2). Furthermore, cumulative platinum doses of 50 to 150 mg/m2 were associated with a lower OR for DM, though this association did not reach statistical significance (P=0.02).ConclusionsThe most common DM site for ADC patients after surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy was the lung. The risk factors for DM in those patients were LNM and DOI ≥1/2. Additionally, the prevention of DM could not be simply achieved by increasing the platinum dose during the primary treatment period.Keywords: Cervical adenocarcinoma (ADC), adjuvant radiotherapy, distant metastasis (DM), cumulative platinum dose

Highlight box

Key findings

• In cervical adenocarcinoma (ADC) patients undergoing radical surgery plus postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy (PORT), the distant metastasis (DM) rate was 18.9%, with the lung as the dominant metastatic organ. Depth of myometrial invasion ≥1/2 and lymph node metastasis were independent DM risk factors. A three-layer risk model built on these two markers yielded distinct 5-year distant metastasis-free survival.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior data indicated ADC carries a higher metastatic risk than cervical squamous carcinoma, yet unified predictive stratification for DM after PORT was lacking.

• This study constructs a simple dual-factor risk model to precisely stratify DM risk for early-stage ADC patients receiving PORT.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This bedside-friendly risk grading guides individualized follow-up and systemic treatment planning.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide (1) and the second most common cancer in women in developing countries (2). The pathological types of cervical cancer mainly include squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and cervical adenocarcinoma (ADC), accounting for 70–80% and 15–25% of all cases, respectively (3,4). With the application of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines, the incidence of ADC is gradually increasing, and the age of onset tends to be younger (5,6). ADC is relatively insensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and is more prone to metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance, resulting in a poorer prognosis than SCC (7). Studies have shown that the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of non-squamous cell carcinoma (non-SCC) is 10 to 20 percentage points lower than that of SCC (8,9). In addition, ADC has a higher risk of metastasis. Among 229 patients with stage Ib ADC, the distant metastasis (DM) rate (37%) was significantly higher than that of 1,538 SCC patients (21%) (P<0.01) (10). Li et al. included 7,201 patients with ADC based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and found that lung metastasis was the most common (37.3%), followed by liver (17.5%) and bone (15.1%) (11).

According to the definition of the “Sedlis criteria”, cervical cancer cases with high-risk factors have a higher DM rate, including lymph node metastasis (LNM), surgical margin status, and parametrial involvement (12,13). Emerging evidence highlights the biological heterogeneity of ADC, with distinct recurrence patterns and therapeutic responses across histologic subtypes. The 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) classification categorizes endometrioid adenocarcinoma into HPV-associated (HPVA) and HPV-independent (HPVI) adenocarcinomas. A large-scale validation study conducted by Shi et al. (2022) confirmed that HPVI is associated with adverse clinicopathological factors, including deep stromal invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), and advanced International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage (14). Although surgery combined with postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy (PORT) can improve the local control rate in ADC, treatment options for locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic cervical cancer patients are limited. A comprehensive analysis of these factors is crucial for optimizing adjuvant treatment strategies.

In this study, we included patients with ADC who received PORT in our center, focusing on analyzing their DM patterns and related risk factors to better understand the metastatic behavior of ADC and help develop personalized treatment strategies for patients with metastatic adenocarcinoma. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1079/rc).

Methods

The ATTRACT study

The ATTRACT study (Assessment of Treatment Response and Therapeutic Outcomes in Cervical Adenocarcinoma following Radiotherapy) was a large-scale, single-center, retrospective cohort study (NCT06741046) aimed at assessing treatment efficacy and delineating prognostic factors in ADC patients receiving curative or adjuvant radiotherapy. Leveraging our institution’s role as the tertiary referral center for rare gynecologic cancers, the study enrolled a demographically and geographically heterogeneous cohort of ADC patients. The trial was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov prior to data collection. This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) (No. K6420). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Study participants

This retrospective analysis included consecutively treated cervical cancer patients at our institution between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2023. All clinical and pathological data were retrospectively collected through a systematic review of electronic medical records and institutional databases at PUMCH. The final cohort comprised ADC cases involving 238 patients managed with radical surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy. All patients were staged I–II or IIIC according to the FIGO 2018 staging system. Exclusion criteria applied to patients meeting any of the following conditions: (I) patients received palliative surgical intervention; (II) previous radiotherapy for another pelvic carcinoma; (III) patients did not finish the entire course of adjuvant radiotherapy; (IV) patients were less than 18 years old or without complete follow-up information. Treatment

All patients underwent radical hysterectomy with comprehensive pelvic lymphadenectomy; sentinel lymph node biopsy was not routinely performed during the study period, as its adoption at our institution occurred after the completion of this cohort. Surgical approach (open abdominal or minimally invasive surgery) was determined by surgeon preference and patient characteristics. Adjuvant therapy consisted of image-guided pelvic radiotherapy utilizing advanced radiation systems such as intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), with treatment planning strictly adhering to institutional protocols that conformed to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendations (15).

Radiation region routinely incorporated the proximal vaginal cuff (superior 3–4 cm segment), parametrial regions, and all relevant lymphatic drainage basins encompassing the common iliac, internal/external iliac, obturator, and presacral nodal chains. Two external beam radiation (EBRT) fractionation regimens were adopted: 45 Gy in 25 fractions or 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions. The selection of the EBRT dose was based on the presence of high-risk pathological features. Specifically, the standard dose of 45 Gy was prescribed for patients without residual disease or with only intermediate-risk factors (e.g., tumor size ≥4 cm, LVSI, or deep stromal invasion). The higher dose of 50.4 Gy was reserved for patients with high-risk features, including positive surgical margins, parametrial invasion (PI), or LNM identified on final pathology, to optimize local control in regions at increased risk of residual disease. Patients underwent brachytherapy (BRT) (10 Gy/2 fractions) with the choice based on tumor characteristics and shared decision-making with patients.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) was defined as chemotherapy administered prior to radiation therapy rather than surgery. The decision to administer NACT was made by the multidisciplinary tumor board, primarily for patients with bulky tumors (maximum diameter ≥4 cm), PI, or pathologically confirmed lymph node involvement. For concurrent and adjuvant chemotherapy, weekly cisplatin (40 mg/m2) served as the preferred radiosensitizer during pelvic radiotherapy. Carboplatin [area under the curve (AUC) 5–6 administered every 3 weeks] was used as an alternative regimen for patients with cisplatin contraindications, including preexisting renal dysfunction, severe neurotoxicity, or intolerance developed during prior cisplatin cycles.

Data collection

All included patients were divided into the DM group and the non-DM group according to whether DM occurred during follow-up. DM was defined as histologically confirmed or radiographically evident metastases occurring outside the pelvic cavity. Imaging diagnoses were based on computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) findings consistent with metastatic lesions, as interpreted by board-certified radiologists or nuclear medicine physicians. For cases with equivocal imaging results, histological confirmation via biopsy was performed whenever clinically feasible. Notably, retroperitoneal LNM was classified as loco-regional failure (LRR) rather than DM in this study. This classification complies with the standardized regional lymphatic drainage definition for cervical cancer, as recommended by contemporary radiotherapy contouring guidelines (e.g., RTOG and EMBRACE consensus) and the 2018 FIGO staging system. Specifically, para-aortic nodal involvement is regarded as regional spread (stage IIIC2) instead of distant dissemination. The distinction is essential for accurately identifying failure patterns and optimizing adjuvant treatment strategies, given that LRR and DM differ substantially in prognosis and clinical management. The date of DM diagnosis was defined as the first day of imaging or pathological confirmation of metastatic disease. All patients developing DM at any time after primary treatment were allocated to the DM group, while those without evidence of DM throughout follow-up were assigned to the non-DM group.

In addition, the information on patients’ age, pretreatment cancer antigen 125 (CA125) concentration, FIGO 2018 stage (I or II/IIIC), HPV genotyping (HPVA, HPVI or unknown), tumor size, surgical approach (open or laparoscopic), lympho-vascular space invasion (LVSI) (no or yes), depth of myometrial invasion (DOI) (<1/2 or ≥1/2), uterus corpus involvement (UI) (no or yes), vaginal invasion (VI) (no or yes), PI (no or yes), positive margin (no or yes), LNM (no or yes), EBRT dose (45 Gy/25 fractions, 50.4 Gy/28 fractions or unknown), BRT (no or yes), NACT (no or yes), concurrent chemotherapy (no or yes), adjuvant chemotherapy (no or yes) and cumulative cisplatin dose during whole treatment period were gathered. Notably, NACT in the current study referred to chemotherapy before radiation instead of before surgery.

The surveillance protocol required every three months clinical visits for the first 2 years post-treatment, with the frequency decreasing to every 6 months between years 2–5, and ultimately transitioning to yearly check-ups beyond the fifth year (15).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were assessed with Student’s t-tests, while categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square tests. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences among groups were statistically evaluated by log-rank tests. A descriptive summary of the DM pattern in those patients was presented with a metastatic organ distribution map. We further compared survival rates between two distinct patient cohorts: those with single-site DM versus those with multiple-site DM, as well as between patients developing synchronous LRR versus those without.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed in the entire cohort to identify the risk factors associated with DM. In specific, variable selection for multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using a two-step approach. First, univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted for all candidate clinicopathological and treatment-related variables. Variables with a P value <0.05 in univariate analysis were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable model. This threshold was chosen to balance the risk of omitting potentially important predictors while maintaining model parsimony. A risk stratification system incorporating variables identified from multivariate regression analysis was constructed accordingly. Patients were stratified into subgroups with distinct DMFS. This stratification was prespecified to reflect the combined prognostic impact of both factors. For restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, four knots were placed to flexibly model the dose-response relationship between cumulative platinum dose and the odds ratio (OR) for DM.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software (version 3.6.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The missing data belonged to a small sample, so the simple deletion method was used to deal with the missing data.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 238 ADC patients treated with surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy were enrolled, including 193 (81.1%) in the non-DM group and 45 (18.9%) in the DM group. Follow-up data were collected through regular clinical follow-up visits in accordance with the institutional surveillance protocol. The follow-up duration was calculated from the date of radical surgery to either the last contact date or death. The median follow-up for the entire cohort was 59.8 months (interquartile range, 28.5–91.5 months). As shown in Table 1, the proportion of FIGO stage II/IIIC was 48.89% in the DM group, which was significantly higher than the 24.87% in the non-DM group. Among them, 3 patients were found to have para-aortic LNM and were staged as FIGO IIIC2. Patients in the DM group had a higher proportion of HPVI (35.56% vs. 27.98%, P<0.05) and larger tumor sizes (3.11 vs. 2.51 cm, P<0.05). Patients in the DM group tended to have a higher level of CA 125 than the non-DM group (50.83 vs. 32.73 U/mL). In the DM group, 3 (16.7%) developed metastasis after local recurrence, and 15 developed metastasis and local recurrence simultaneously. In terms of the pathologic characteristics, there were no differences in the LVSI and surgical margin status between the two groups (all P>0.05). The DM group had a deeper DOI (≥1/2 depth: 86.67% vs. 56.99%). The DM group had higher proportions of UI (46.67% vs. 26.42%), VI (20.0% vs. 8.29%), PI and LNM (44.44% vs. 19.17%), which were consistent with their later stage.

Treatment characteristics

All the patients received adjuvant radiotherapy after surgery. One hundred and thirty-four (56.3%) of them received EBRT with an irradiation dose of 50.4 Gy/28 fractions, and 84 (35.3%) received EBRT with an irradiation dose of 45 Gy/25 fractions. One hundred and ninety-eight patients (83.2%) received BRT with a prescribed dose of 10 Gy/2 fractions. One hundred and ninety-two (80.7%) patients received cisplatin-based chemotherapy, and the remaining 46 (19.3%) received carboplatin-based chemotherapy. There were similar proportions in receiving concurrent chemotherapy (62.2% vs. 59.6%, P>0.05) and adjuvant chemotherapy (11.1% vs. 7.8%, P>0.05) between the DM group and non-DM group. In the DM group, 21 (46.7%) patients received NACT, which was significantly higher than the 22.8% of the non-DM group. In addition, the cumulative dose of cisplatin in the DM group was 213.1±21.7 mg, which was significantly higher than the 135.3±7.3 mg in the non-DM group (P<0.001, Table 1).

The patterns of DM

The median interval between diagnosis and metastasis was 24.6 months. The most common sites of metastasis were the lung (51.1%), liver (26.7%), bone (24.4%), omentum/colon (17.8%), mediastinal lymph node (6.7%), and cervical lymph node (4.4%), as shown in Figure 2A. Among the 45 patients, 32 (71.1%) were diagnosed with single-site metastasis, and the remaining 13 (28.9%) were diagnosed with multiple-site metastases. There was no significant difference in the 5-year OS (44.2% vs. 44.9%; P>0.99) between the patients with single-site metastasis and patients with multiple-site metastases, as shown in Figure 2B. Similarly, no statistically significant difference of 5-year OS was observed between patients with and without synchronous LRR (44.4% vs. 49.6%; P=0.21) (Figure 2C).

Risk factors associated with DM

In the univariate analysis, FIGO stage, tumor size, DOI, UI, VI, PI, LNM, 50.4 Gy, neoadjuvant, and cumulative dose of cisplatin were found to be associated with a higher rate of DM, as shown in Table 2. In the multivariate analysis, DOI and LNM were found to be associated with a higher rate of DM (all P<0.05). Accordingly, patients were divided into three risk subgroups including high-risk subgroup (LNM+ and DOI ≥1/2), middle-risk subgroup (LNM+ and DOI <1/2; LNM− and DOI ≥1/2) and low-risk subgroup (LNM− and DOI <1/2) (Table 3). Moreover, a significant difference regarding 5-year DMFS was observed among different risk subgroups, with a 5-year DMFS of 48.8% (95% CI: 40.3–57.3%), 82.7% (95% CI: 78.9–86.5%) and 92.6% (95% CI: 89.0–96.2%), respectively (P<0.001, Figure 3A).

LNM and DOI ≥1/2 were identified as high-risk factors for developing DM after PORT, and a two-factor model was established. Three risk subgroups, including high-risk (LNM+ and DOI ≥1/2), middle-risk (LNM+ and DOI <1/2; LNM− and DOI ≥1/2), and low-risk subgroup (LNM− and DOI <1/2), were found to have distinct 5-year distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). The 5-year DMFS of the low-, middle-, and high-risk was 92.6% (95% CI: 89.0–96.2%), 82.7% (95% CI: 78.9–86.5%), and 48.8% (95% CI: 40.3–57.3%), respectively.

Cumulative cisplatin dose and DM

The OR for DM increased as the increasing of cumulative cisplatin dose before the adjustment for covariates (P=0.02, Figure 3B). Apparently, cumulative cisplatin doses ranging from 50 to 150 mg/m2 during treatment were associated with a markedly decreased risk of DM after adjustment for covariates screened via Cox regression analysis (LNM and DOI). However, this association failed to reach statistical significance (P=0.11, Figure 3C), indicating that elevating cisplatin dosage in primary treatment alone cannot effectively achieve DM prevention.

Discussion

This study systematically explored the DM patterns and risk factors in ADC patients treated with PORT, providing new insights into the clinical management of this aggressive subtype. The overall DM rate in our cohort was 18.9%, which aligns with the aggressive biological behavior of ADC reported in previous studies (12,16). The median interval between diagnosis and DM (24.6 months) emphasized the need for long-term follow-up, even after seemingly successful primary treatment. Notably, lung metastasis (51.1%) was the most common, followed by liver (26.7%) and bone (24.4%), consistent with previous studies, which highlighted the lung as the predominant metastatic site for ADC (11,17,18). Additionally, the finding that 28.9% of metastatic cases involved multiple sites underscores the systemic nature of ADC progression, emphasizing the limitations of local therapies alone in preventing distant spread.

Radical hysterectomy with lymph node dissection was the standard treatment for early-stage CC (FIGO 2018 IA1–IIA1), while adjuvant radiotherapy was usually recommended if pathological findings met intermediate-risk factors following Sedlis criteria (19). In a 2014 retrospective study by the Korean Gynecologic Oncology Group, A 4-factor model was developed defining the intermediate-risk group as having any two of the following: tumor diameter ≥3 cm, LVSI, stromal invasion ≥2/3, or adenocarcinoma/adenosquamous carcinoma (20). At present, LNM, positive surgical margin, and parametrial involvement were identified as high-risk factors for both ADC and SCC according to NCCN guidelines (21). Our multivariate analysis identified DOI ≥1/2 and LNM as independent predictors of DM after PORT, highlighting the critical role of aggressive pathological features in ADC prognosis (22,23). DOI indicates a higher likelihood of tumor cells infiltrating lymphovascular spaces and entering systemic circulation, while LNM directly reflects the tumor’s ability to spread via lymphatic pathways. These findings reinforce the importance of meticulous pathological assessment after surgery that accurate measurement of invasion depth and thorough lymph node dissection are not only prognostic but also guide adjuvant treatment decisions.

Notably, the two-factor model we constructed (high-risk: LNM+ and DOI ≥1/2; middle-risk: LNM+ and DOI<1/2 or LNM− and DOI ≥1/2; low-risk: LNM− and DOI <1/2) yielded significantly different 5-year DMFS rates (48.8%, 82.7%, and 92.6%, respectively). This three-tiered risk stratification shares consistent prognostic biological rationale with the Silva pattern-based recurrence prediction model for intermediate-risk ADC (24), rather than merely adopting an identical three-group classification framework. The Silva model categorizes ADC according to glandular morphology and stromal invasive patterns (Silva A, B, and C), among which infiltrative Silva C lesions correlate strongly with deep stromal invasion and elevated LNM risk. Such mechanistic convergence indicates that pathological markers reflecting tumor invasive potential—either direct pathological metrics (DOI and LNM status) or histomorphologic invasive patterns (Silva subtypes)—are equally vital for risk stratification among patients with ADC.

The association between advanced FIGO stage, larger tumor size, and higher DM risk in univariate analysis is consistent with the natural history of cervical cancer, where more advanced disease at presentation correlates with increased metastatic potential (11,14). All patients received PORT, with similar proportions undergoing EBRT, BRT, and concurrent chemotherapy. Intriguingly, despite the DM group receiving a significantly higher cumulative cisplatin dose (213.1±21.7 vs. 135.3±7.3 mg in the non-DM group), our analysis showed no statistically significant association between higher cisplatin doses and reduced DM risk. A cumulative dose of 50–150 mg/m2 appeared to trend toward lower DM odds, but this was not significant (P=0.11). This finding aligns with the known relative resistance of ADC to cisplatin-based chemotherapy compared to SCC (7). The paradox, higher chemotherapy intensity in high-risk patients yet developing DM, underscores the need for alternative systemic strategies. These high-risk subgroups may benefit from novel agents such as immunotherapy, antibody-drug conjugates, or targeted therapy, particularly for biologically aggressive subtypes like gastric-type adenocarcinoma, which have been shown to have poor responses to standard treatments (25-27).

When exploring the association between cumulative platinum dose and the risk of DM, several methodological considerations should be addressed. Patients with more aggressive disease presenting high‑risk pathological features—such as LNM and deep stromal invasion—tended to receive higher cumulative platinum doses via extended adjuvant chemotherapy cycles or additional neoadjuvant regimens. This practice introduces confounding by indication, wherein patients at inherently higher risk of DM received more intensive chemotherapy, complicating causal inference. Furthermore, heterogeneity across chemotherapy regimens further limited the isolation of the independent effect of cumulative platinum exposure. Specifically, 19.3% of patients received carboplatin instead of cisplatin due to clinical contraindications, and chemotherapy timing varied across neoadjuvant, concurrent, and adjuvant settings. After adjusting for LNM and DOI, the RCS analysis showed no statistically significant association between higher cumulative platinum doses and reduced DM risk (P=0.11). Taken together, these findings suggest that escalating the platinum dose during primary treatment is unlikely to simply prevent DM, highlighting the need for alternative systemic strategies, particularly for high-risk patients, such as novel agents targeting biologically aggressive adenocarcinoma subtypes.

Although a higher proportion of HPVI adenocarcinoma was observed in the DM group compared with the non-DM group (35.56% vs. 27.98%, P<0.05), HPV status was not included in the multivariable model. Nonetheless, the prognostic relevance of HPV status in ADC is well recognized, with HPVI tumors associated with more aggressive clinicopathological features. The higher proportion of HPVI cases in the DM group observed in our study aligns with this established biological behavior. Future studies with larger sample sizes and comprehensive HPV genotyping are warranted to further elucidate the independent contribution of HPV status to distant metastatic risk in patients receiving adjuvant radiotherapy.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as treatment decisions (e.g., NACT use) were not randomized. Second, the sample size of the DM group (n=45) limits subgroup analyses, such as comparing metastasis patterns between HPVA and HPVI subtypes. Third, heterogeneous radiotherapy doses (50.4 vs. 45 Gy) and chemotherapy protocols may have influenced outcomes, though no significant association between radiation dose and DM was found. Future prospective trials with standardized treatment regimens and molecular profiling are needed to validate our findings and explore targeted therapies. Additionally, long-term surveillance protocols tailored to high-risk subgroups (e.g., more frequent imaging for lung/liver metastases) could improve early detection of DM.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study confirms that LNM and DOI ≥1/2 are critical predictors of DM in ADC patients treated with PORT, with the lung being the most common metastatic site. The limited efficacy of higher cisplatin doses underscores the need for novel systemic strategies, particularly for aggressive subtypes. These findings contribute to a better understanding of ADC’s metastatic behavior and may inform personalized treatment approaches to improve patient outcomes.
