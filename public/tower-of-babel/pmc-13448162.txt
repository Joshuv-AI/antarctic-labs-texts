Continuous Subcutaneous Foslevodopa/Foscarbidopa in Advanced Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Mario Hero, Nadja Grozdanić, Romana Brnić Andrić, Eliša Papić, Olivio Perković, Vladimira Vuletić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContinuous subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp) provides 24‐h delivery of levodopa‐carbidopa prodrugs for advanced Parkinson's disease. Real‐world 1‐year European data remain limited.ObjectivesTo characterize 1‐year changes in motor, non‐motor, autonomic, quality‐of‐life and sleep outcomes, and in concomitant therapy.MethodsRetrospective single‐center observational study of 32 patients initiated on LDp/CDp between April 2024 and January 2026.ResultsTwenty of 32 patients (62.5%) completed 1‐year follow‐up. MDS‐UPDRS Part III improved by 30.3%, Part IV by 36.0% and Part II by 13.2% (all significant); non‐motor and autonomic totals were unchanged. Quality of life improved (p = 0.040), driven by bodily discomfort. Levodopa‐equivalent dose rose by 31.7% while oral dopamine agonist use fell from 70% to 25% (p = 0.004).ConclusionsOne year of LDp/CDp was associated with substantial motor benefit and improved quality of life, achieved with higher levodopa equivalent and reduced dopamine agonist use.

In advanced Parkinson's disease (PD), the therapeutic window of oral levodopa narrows progressively. Motor fluctuations and dyskinesias emerge, and non‐motor symptoms add to the burden. 1 , 2 When oral therapy is no longer enough, device‐aided options include deep brain stimulation, 3 intrajejunal levodopa‐carbidopa(−entacapone) intestinal gel, 4 apomorphine subcutaneous infusion, 5 and, more recently, subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa (LDp/CDp), a non‐surgical levodopa‐based therapy delivered through a portable 24‐h pump without jejunostomy. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 The pivotal phase 3 trial showed gains in normalized “ON” time without troublesome dyskinesia compared with optimized oral levodopa‐carbidopa, 6 and the 12‐month open‐label extension supported sustained benefit. 7 Post‐marketing experience has been dominated by infusion‐site reactions10, 11, 12, 13 recent histopathology suggests that some clinically diagnosed cases may be aseptic inflammatory reactions rather than infections. 14 Real‐world data from European centers outside the registration network, with broad assessment beyond motor scores, are still scarce. The Clinic of Neurology of Clinical Hospital Centre Rijeka is the Croatian national referral center for invasive treatment of movement disorders and was among the first to offer LDp/CDp; we followed patients with a comprehensive battery of motor, non‐motor, autonomic, sleep and quality‐of‐life instruments. We aimed to describe the 1‐year changes in these domains, the parallel changes in concomitant antiparkinsonian therapy, and the tolerability profile in routine practice.

Methods

This was a retrospective single‐center observational study at the Clinic of Neurology, Clinical Hospital Centre Rijeka, Croatia. We followed patients with advanced PD who started LDp/CDp pump therapy between April 2024 and January 2026 within the center's movement disorders program. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Clinical Hospital Centre Rijeka (Class 003‐05/26‐01/06; Reg. No. 2170‐29‐02/15‐26‐2; 22 January 2026) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion followed the center's clinical pathway: clinically established or probable PD by MDS criteria;15 motor fluctuations or troublesome dyskinesias not adequately controlled despite optimized oral therapy, broadly in line with the 5‐2‐1 framework;2 preserved or only mildly impaired cognition, judged adequate for safe device use by joint neurologist and clinical‐psychologist assessment rather than a fixed MoCA cut‐off; and a clinical decision in favor of subcutaneous infusion after multidisciplinary review.

Patients were assessed before pump initiation and after 1 year using MDS‐UPDRS parts I–IV, 16 the Non‐Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQ), 17 the Scale for Outcomes in Parkinson's Disease–Autonomic dysfunction (SCOPA‐AUT), 18 the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score‐31 (COMPASS‐31), 19 the 39‐item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ‐39) 20 and the Parkinson's Disease Sleep Scale‐2 (PDSS‐2). 21 Modified Hoehn and Yahr stage and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were recorded at baseline. MDS‐UPDRS parts I–IV were rated by two ward physicians, with the final score agreed by team consensus. Part III was rated in the OFF state at baseline and in the ON state on continuous infusion at 1 year. Total daily levodopa‐equivalent dose (LEDD) was calculated using the conversion factors of Tomlinson et al, 22 including the LDp/CDp contribution from the daily prescribed levodopa equivalent. Oral dopamine agonist use was recorded at both timepoints. LDp/CDp was started according to the manufacturer's titration scheme, 11 with 24‐h continuous subcutaneous infusion and patient‐ or caregiver‐controlled extra‐doses, with the nocturnal infusion rate set approximately 30% below the daytime rate in all patients. 23 Scheduled visits at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months included infusion‐site inspection and recording of any complications. Paired comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test; the change in the proportion of patients on a dopamine agonist was tested with the McNemar exact test. For interpretation we used the minimum clinically important differences reported by Horváth and colleagues: −4.7 points for the PDQ‐39 SI 24 and −3.4 points for the PDSS‐2. 25 Analyses were done in Statistica 13.3 (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA).

Results

Of the 32 patients started on LDp/CDp, 20 (62.5%) completed 1‐year follow‐up and formed the paired analysis cohort. The other 12 patients did not contribute to the paired analysis: two withdrew because of loss of caregiver support, one died of non‐neurologic causes, one was switched to intrajejunal levodopa‐carbidopa intestinal gel at the family's request and eight had not yet reached the 1‐year visit at database lock; cohort flow is shown in Table 1. The paired cohort included 10 men and 10 women, with a mean age of 68.8 ± 5.5 years, a mean disease duration of 10.6 ± 4.9 years, a median modified Hoehn and Yahr stage of 3 and a mean baseline MoCA of 23.6 ± 3.2. Total daily LEDD rose from 1141 ± 339 to 1503 ± 424 mg/day (+362 mg/day, +31.7%; p < 0.001). Oral dopamine agonist use fell from 14 of 20 (70.0%) at baseline to 5 of 20 (25.0%) at 1 year: 9 patients discontinued the agonist and none started one de novo (McNemar p = 0.004). MAO‐B inhibitor use (9 of 20, 45%) and COMT inhibitor use (2 of 20, 10%) were unchanged over follow‐up, and no patient remained on a scheduled oral levodopa formulation once established on the 24‐h pump (Table 1).

The MDS‐UPDRS IIImotor score fell from 35.45 ± 8.83 in the OFF state at baseline to 24.70 ± 7.96 in the ON state at 1 year (−30.3%; p < 0.001), with all but one patient either improving or remaining stable. NMSQ, SCOPA‐AUT and COMPASS‐31 totals showed small numerical changes that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.634, 0.110 and 0.198, respectively). At the SCOPA‐AUT subdomain level, the gastrointestinal subscale improved (p = 0.012), while urinary, cardiovascular, thermoregulatory, pupillomotor and sexual subscales were essentially unchanged. The PDQ‐39 Summary Index improved from 35.27 ± 12.84 to 30.56 ± 12.42 (−4.71 points, −13.4%; p = 0.040), at the threshold of the previously reported MCID of −4.7 points. 24 The change was driven by the bodily discomfort domain (48.33 ± 18.85 → 32.92 ± 18.03, −31.9%; p = 0.002); other domains improved numerically without crossing significance. Per‐patient changes are shown in Figure S2. The PDSS‐2 total score decreased from 22.10 ± 10.56 to 17.05 ± 8.88 (−5.05 points, −22.9%); the change reached the prespecified trend threshold (p = 0.070) and exceeded the MCID of −3.4 points. 25 The distribution shifted across severity bands in a clinically meaningful direction (Fig. S3).

Skin and infusion‐related adverse events were recorded in 12 of 20 completers. Cellulitis at the infusion site was the most frequent event (n = 9) and usually occurred within the first month; three patients required short hospitalization and one underwent surgical drainage, with topical mupirocin combined with oral cephalexin or clindamycin as the antibiotic regimens. Two patients had infusion‐site erythema without infection and one developed a small subcutaneous hematoma that needed no specific treatment. No hallucinations, psychotic symptoms, or new or worsening impulse‐control disorders were observed during follow‐up. No patient permanently discontinued therapy because of a serious adverse event.

Discussion

In this real‐world cohort at the Croatian national referral center for invasive treatment of movement disorders, 1 year of subcutaneous LDp/CDp was associated with substantial motor benefit and improved quality of life. The main motor symptoms, motor complications and motor experiences of daily living all improved, and sleep showed a favorable trend. The magnitude of the MDS‐UPDRS III change (−10.75 points, −30.3%) and the MDS‐UPDRS IV change (−2.0 points, −36.0%) matches the pivotal phase 3 trial and its 12‐month open‐label extension, 6 , 7 and is comparable to values reported for intrajejunal levodopa‐carbidopa intestinal gel and apomorphine subcutaneous infusion in similar populations. 4 , 5 Because baseline Part III was rated in the OFF state and the 1‐year score in the ON state, part of this difference is the expected OFF‐to‐ON dopaminergic response rather than an improvement in best‐ON performance, which continuous dopaminergic stimulation would not be expected to change. The 1‐year gain therefore corresponds to the motor benefit of moving from fluctuating oral therapy to continuous infusion, in line with recent 24‐h real‐world cohorts. 26

The 31.7% rise in total daily LEDD is in line with what is seen when patients move from oral to continuous dopaminergic delivery. 4 , 9 , 11 The 24‐h infusion rate is titrated upward over the first weeks to give a stable plasma levodopa profile, and the total levodopa equivalent typically exceeds the prior oral dose. Part of the motor benefit is therefore due to dose optimization enabled by the continuous‐delivery format, not to the route of administration alone. In parallel, oral dopamine agonist use fell from 70% to 25% (McNemar p = 0.004). Continuous levodopa‐based infusion simplifies oral adjunct therapy, and dopamine agonists are often the first class to be reduced in this older, advanced‐disease population because of their links to impulse‐control disorders, daytime sleepiness, oedema and neuropsychiatric side effects. 27 , 28 The combined rise in LEDD and fall in dopamine‐agonist use therefore reflects a shift towards continuous levodopa delivery, not a blanket intensification of all dopaminergic drugs.

The PDQ‐39 Summary Index improved by 4.7 points, above the threshold of the reported MCID for this instrument. 24 The bodily discomfort change (−15.4 points, −31.9%) is well above any conventional threshold for clinical relevance and is the main driver of the Summary Index change. Bodily discomfort in PD has been linked to fluctuation‐related pain and to dystonic OFF‐state pain, both of which respond to a more continuous plasma levodopa profile. 8 , 16 The parallel 22.9% reduction in PDSS‐2 score, exceeding the MCID of −3.4 points, 25 fits with the known effect of continuous dopaminergic delivery on nocturnal motor symptoms, RBD‐related awakenings and OFF‐state pain. 8 , 21 The contrast between significant improvement in MDS‐UPDRS II and stability of MDS‐UPDRS I is biologically reasonable. Part II captures motor experiences of daily living, which are sensitive to OFF time and dyskinesias; Part I aggregates non‐motor experiences whose response to dopaminergic therapy is heterogeneous and time‐dependent. 16 Nocturia is captured within the SCOPA‐AUT urinary subscale, which was essentially unchanged in our cohort, but we did not analyze it as a separate endpoint; a recent real‐world study using a dedicated assessment reported improvement in nocturia with overnight foslevodopa/foscarbidopa administration. 29

As in the registration trials and post‐marketing experience, the dominant adverse‐event signal was infusion‐site reactions. 6 , 7 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The fact that most events were early suggests that structured patient and caregiver education on infusion‐site rotation, asepsis and early recognition of erythema is the single most impactful local quality‐improvement step for centers starting this therapy. Recent histopathology showing neutrophil‐rich aseptic inflammatory infiltrates in some clinically diagnosed cellulitis cases has a practical implication: in selected patients with non‐systemic inflammatory plaques, potent topical corticosteroids may be more appropriate than empirical antibiotics. 14 We recorded no hallucinations or psychotic symptoms over the year. As nocturnal levodopa over delivery has been associated with cognitive and psychiatric adverse effects, 30 the nocturnal infusion rate was reduced by approximately 30% in all patients; whether this contributed to the neuropsychiatric tolerability or simply reflects the small sample and short follow‐up cannot be determined here. Where the reduced nocturnal rate produced morning akinesia or a delayed morning ON, an extra dose delivered through the pump on waking, administered by the patient or caregiver, was sufficient in most cases.

The principal limitations are the small completer sample (N = 20), the single‐center design and the absence of a control arm. Some of the motor benefit may also reflect optimization of concomitant oral medication around pump initiation, which is part of routine care: starting the pump involves deliberately adjusting the oral regimen, mainly up‐titrating the continuous levodopa equivalent and withdrawing oral dopamine agonists, so the observed change reflects this combined package rather than the infusion route alone. Patient‐reported scales may be sensitive to expectation effects in an open‐label cohort. In the per‐patient supplementary figures, patients are ordered by the magnitude of change rather than chronologically and therefore show no temporal learning curve; baseline age and disease duration were comparable across the enrolment period, and the few patients who worsened on the PDQ‐39 or MDS‐UPDRS IV were generally those with persistent dyskinesia or intercurrent psychosocial stress. The 1‐year horizon is limited; longer follow‐up and multicenter registries are needed to clarify the late tolerability profile of LDp/CDp. 8 , 13 In summary, 1 year of subcutaneous LDp/CDp in this Croatian cohort was associated with substantial motor benefit, an improvement in quality of life driven by bodily discomfort and a favorable sleep trend, achieved alongside a one‐third rise in total daily LEDD and a halving of oral dopamine agonist use. The findings support the effectiveness of LDp/CDp in routine European practice and reinforce the importance of dedicated infusion‐site monitoring and transparent LEDD tracking.
