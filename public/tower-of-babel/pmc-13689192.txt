Dental anxiety in cooperative preschool children during their first dental visit to the pediatric dentistry department: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Alaa Snobar, Chaza Kouchaji, Jamila Bchara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental fear and anxiety (DFA) is a psychological response ranging from mild apprehension to severe phobia of dental procedures, affecting children and adolescents. Its prevalence is highly variable globally, influenced by culture, family conditions, personality traits, and assessment tools.This study aimed to assess dental anxiety levels in cooperative children aged 4–6 during their first dental visit using both self-reported and physiological measures.MethodsA cross-sectional study included 100 children attending a pediatric dental clinic for the first time. The sample was collected from patients visiting the pediatric dentistry department in the faculty of dentistry. Self-reported anxiety was assessed using the Facial Image Scale (FIS), and pulse rate was recorded as a physiological indicator.ResultsThe sample comprised 44% males and 56% females. Background

Dental anxiety is a psychological reaction ranging from mild fear to severe phobia of the dental environment or treatment [1–3]. In children and adolescents, dental fear and anxiety (DFA) is a multifactorial condition that directly affects behavior during dental procedures [3]. Its global prevalence varies widely, from 4% to 98%, due to differences in culture, family environment, personality traits, and measurement tool [1].

The development of DFA is influenced by personal, social, and environmental factors. Personal associated factors include age, gender, child personality, shyness, intelligence, and general fear [2]. Social associated factors include parental anxiety, socio-economic status, child preparation before the visit, and parental expectations. Environmental associated factors include the dental clinic, instruments, clinical staff, and the nature of the dental experience. The behavior of dentists and staff during the first visit significantly affects anxiety levels, while parental presence has variable effects, with most studies reporting no significant difference [1, 3].

The first dental visit is critical in shaping future dental behavior and anxiety. Age and cognitive-emotional development play key roles; some studies indicate DFA decreases with age, while others suggest anxiety may increase during early school years [4]. Children with regular dental visits typically show lower anxiety than those with irregular or emergency visits [3]. Previous medical experiences, such as hospital visits or painful procedures, can increase the likelihood of DFA [3].

DFA negatively affects oral health, increases the need for complex treatments [5, 6], and impacts the child’s quality of life, self-confidence, and family wellbeing [6, 7], highlighting the necessity of early intervention [8],. Therefore, early and regular dental exposure serves as a protective factor, as positive experiences reduce the risk of future anxiety and fear [9]. As differences in anxiety responses have been noted between typically developing children and those with developmental disabilities during their first visit [10]. Identifying the factors that contribute to an increased predisposition to dental anxiety in preschool children is of paramount clinical relevance, as anxiety serves as a significant barrier to effective oral healthcare [1].Approximately one-third of children aged two to six exhibit some form of dental fear, which complicates clinical procedures and often necessitates more invasive behavior management techniques [12]. There is a critical need to identify specific predisposing factors including patient age, child temperament, socioeconomic status, and prior negative treatment experiences, to enable practitioners to implement tailored preventive strategies and anticipatory behavior management [11–13]. By recognizing these risks early, clinicians can facilitate more positive initial dental encounters, engage in targeted parental counseling, and employ appropriate treatment sequencing [1, 14].

Moreover, assessing anxiety in preschool children (typically aged 3 to 6 years) presents unique challenges due to their limited cognitive and verbal abilities [15]. Despite the growing body of research on dental anxiety in children, limited data are available specifically for preschool-aged children during their first dental visit, particularly using combined subjective and physiological assessment methods.

This study aimed to assess cooperative preschool child’s dental anxiety in the first dental visit to the pediatric dentistry department and different associated factors.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This observational study was designed as a single center Cross-sectional study to investigate the level of dental anxiety in Children Aged 4–6 Years During Their First Dental Visit, this occurred from July 2025 until December 2025.The children who were recruited for the study were recruited using convenience sampling from patients attending the pediatric dentistry department at Damascus University.

Ethical approval

All methods were carried out in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations and declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional Review Board of faculty of Dentistry, Damascus University Approval N0.2024–3176. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children prior to inclusion in the study.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 based on detecting differences in pulse rate between groups using analysis of variance (ANOVA). An effect size of 0.7 was derived from a previous similar study (16). With a significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 85%, the required sample size was estimated accordingly.

Sample collection

A total of 100 children aged 4–6 years who visited the dentist for the first time, and needed dental treatment were included in this study. Patients with communication disabilities, or medical or psychological disorders, and patients with severe dental pain or acute abscess, and patients with absolutely negative or negative ranking according to the Frankl Behavior Rating Scale were also excluded to avoid any potential discrepancies in the recorded data between healthy children and children with any medical condition. Moreover, the used measurement requires cooperation from the patients. These criteria resulted in a sample consisting primarily of cooperative and medically healthy children, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Study protocol

A single trained examiner performed all study procedures, including obtaining the medical and dental history from parents, assessing eligibility criteria, administering the Facial Image Scale (FIS), and recording pulse rate measurements using a finger pulse oximeter.

All assessments were conducted using a standardized protocol for all participants. Examiner blinding was not feasible due to the observational nature of the study. Medical and dental history information was obtained from the parents in the waiting area before the child entered the dental operatory. Before anxiety assessment, the child entered the dental operatory, sat in the dental chair, and was exposed to the general dental environment and visible equipment. Children were initially observed during their first interaction with the dental environment. Cooperation was then assessed using the Frankl Behavior Rating Scale by the study examiner before enrollment. Children classified as negative or definitely negative were excluded from participation. After enrolling the children, they sat on the dental chair after waiting for 10 min in waiting room before any treatment with the parents present at the dental settings alongside the child. The parent remained present in the operatory throughout this period. After explaining the measuring procedure to the child and obtaining basic demographic characteristics. Dental anxiety was assessed using the Facial Image Scale (FIS), consisting of five faces representing increasing levels of anxiety (Fig. 1). The Facial Image Scale (FIS) was administered by the same trained examiner who explained the scale to each child in a standardized manner.

facial image scale

Pulse rate was recorded as a physiological measure using a finger pulse oximeter. The measurement was taken after the child had been seated in the dental chair for approximately 10 min in the presence of a parent, prior to any dental procedure. This waiting period was intended to allow the child to acclimatize to the clinical environment. A single pulse rate reading was recorded once the child appeared calm and cooperative with no visible distress or discomfort. The measurement was obtained immediately after the assessment of dental anxiety using the Facial Image Scale (FIS). Age, gender, socioeconomic status and parental education level were recorded as a potential associated factors. Age was recorded in years and categorized into three groups (4, 5, and 6 years) for analysis. This categorization was chosen to reflect clinically relevant developmental stages in preschool children, as cognitive and emotional responses to dental settings may vary across these age groups in a non-linear manner. Socioeconomic status was classified based on parental self-report into three categories (low, middle, and high) according to overall household income and living conditions. Parental education level was categorized into three groups: primary school (completion of basic education), high school (secondary education), and university (completion of higher education).

Statistical Analysis: the collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS software version 23. (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Normality was tested with Kolmogorov–Smirnov (age p-value = 0.05; pulse p-value = 0.049). Although pulse rate showed borderline deviation from normality, general linear models were applied as they are robust to minor violations of normality, particularly in moderately sized samples. Two separate univariate general linear models were constructed for each outcome variable (FIS score and pulse rate). Each model included age group, gender, and their interaction term as independent variables. Moreover, the Facial Image Scale (FIS) is an ordinal measure, it was analyzed using a general linear model to allow comparison with the continuous physiological outcome and to assess interaction effects. Given that the Facial Image Scale (FIS) is an ordinal variable and showed a skewed distribution, nonparametric tests were also performed as sensitivity analyses. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare FIS scores between genders, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons across age groups. Additional models were constructed to evaluate the effects of socioeconomic status and parental education. Given the limited number of planned comparisons and the exploratory nature of the study, no formal multiple-comparison correction was applied. This approach is consistent with similar observational studies; however, results were interpreted with caution. Also, no missing data was reported.

Discussion

Dental anxiety is a common issue among children and often becomes evident during their first dental visit. This early anxiety directly influences the child’s behavior and level of cooperation during treatment, as previous literature indicates a strong link between initial anxiety and behavioral responses in subsequent visits [16, 17]. Children who undergo painful dental experiences or exhibit high anxiety during their first appointment are more likely to show uncooperative behavior in future sessions, and some may even avoid dental care altogether [17, 18]. However, this study consisted mainly of healthy cooperative preschool children only.

The age group of 4 to 6 years was selected because it represents a sensitive developmental stage in which behavioral responses to clinical procedures are being shaped. During this period, children are particularly susceptible to sensory and emotional stimuli in the dental environment, making early experiences highly influential [1, 19]. Dental anxiety is recognized as a multidimensional phenomenon, requiring more than one measure to capture its complexity [3]. It involves both a cognitive component, related to emotions and expectations, and a physiological component, reflected in bodily responses to stress [20]. Moreover, children may experience varying levels of anxiety even when exposed to the same stimuli, which reinforces the need for multiple complementary assessment tools [21].

In this study, the Facial Image Scale (FIS) was used as a subjective, image-based measure suitable for young children because it does not require advanced cognitive skills [21]. Additionally, pulse rate was employed as a reliable physiological indicator of dental anxiety, as its effectiveness has been demonstrated in several studies evaluating children’s anxiety during dental treatments [22]. But it should also be noted that pulse rate is a nonspecific physiological marker that may reflect general arousal, including novelty, movement, or anticipatory stress, rather than dental anxiety alone.

Many literatures indicate that children in this age range, regardless of the exact age group, normally experience moderate to high levels of anxiety when facing the dental environment for the first time [23–25] (Fuux- noy et al., 2023; Bakardjiev et al., 2025; Nikolskaya et al., 2025). This is attributed to the limited prior experience in the clinic and the natural emotional responses associated with early childhood [1, 3, 25]. As children interpret dental sounds and instruments as threatening or unfamiliar [9, 11]. This study findings suggest that cooperative children at this age shows lower anxiety levels than those examined by the literatures.

Although no significant differences were observed between age groups in FIS scores, physiological indicators (pulse) showed clear differences between genders, with girls recording higher average pulse than boys. Even though this result may be attributed to the new dental setting, as females demonstrated higher pulse rate values, no significant differences were observed in self-reported anxiety (FIS). This suggests that physiological responses may differ by gender in this setting, while subjective expressions of anxiety remain similar, which aligns with previous studies showing higher physiological responses in girls compared to boys in dental anxiety situations. These differences are often attributed to psychological and social factors, as girls are believed to be more inclined to express their emotions, including pain and anxiety, due to educational and cultural influences that encourage them to express feelings more than boys [26].

This result is consistent with Rajwar et al., who indicated that dental anxiety levels are higher in girls than boys during dental procedures [27], and differs from the study by Jervøe-Storm et al. [28], which found no differences in anxiety according to child gender, possibly due to differences in age group (5–12 years) and the method of anxiety assessment through parents using the CFSS-DS scale.

This difference can be explained by the fact that physiological measurement reflects latent anxiety, while the FIS reflects overt anxiety or the ability to express feelings, demonstrating similarity in outward expression of anxiety between males and females. As a self-report expressive tool, the FIS may be less sensitive to anxiety differences that appear between genders compared to other measures such as pulse [29]. In addition, the distribution of the FIS scores was highly skewed toward the lowest anxiety categories, with the majority of children selecting “very happy” or “happy.” This indicates a potential floor effect, which may have reduced the sensitivity of the scale to detect differences between groups, but the consistency between parametric and nonparametric analyses supports the robustness of the findings despite the ordinal and skewed nature of the FIS data.

These results are in agreement with the study by Kılınc et al. [30], which found no significant differences in expressed dental anxiety between genders in early childhood using facial expression scales, despite differences in physiological or behavioral responses.

No statistically significant relationship was found between parental education and children’s dental anxiety, overall, previous studies have shown an inconsistent relationship between parental education level and their child’s dental anxiety, and this could be attributed to the different cognitive abilities of the children which develop over time [31–33].

In terms of the relationship of dental anxiety and socioeconomic status, in this sample, children in families with lower income usually present higher anxiety levels, this can be due to the amount of untreated teeth and fewer dental visits. The results are inconsistent the ones of Irving et al. [34], and Singh et al. [35] as socioeconomic status did not affect the anxiety levels.

This study has several limitations. First, it relied on only two measures of dental anxiety—FIS and pulse rate—which may not capture all psychological or behavioral dimensions of anxiety. Second, it was conducted at a single center and used a cross-sectional design, limiting generalizability and precluding causal inferences. Future research could include multi-center recruitment, longitudinal follow-up, and additional validated measures of dental anxiety, such as behavioral observation or biochemical stress markers, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of pediatric dental anxiety. Finally, the present study included only cooperative children, as those with negative or definitely negative behavior according to the Frankl scale were excluded. Additionally, children with medical, psychological, or communication disorders were not included. This selection may introduce bias and limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of preschool children, particularly those who are uncooperative or have special healthcare needs.

Conclusion

The first dental visit represents a critical period that can influence the development and progression of dental anxiety in children. Within the limitations of this cross-sectional study, cooperative preschool children attending their first dental visit demonstrated generally low levels of dental anxiety, regardless of parental education and socioeconomic status. However, these findings highlight the need for further research to identify factors associated with an increased predisposition to dental anxiety, as well as to evaluate the effectiveness of different behavioral management strategies.
