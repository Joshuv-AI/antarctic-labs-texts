Association between the cumulative average triglyceride-glucose-waist circumference index and new-onset hypertension among Chinese middle-aged and older adults-a national prospective study
Authors: Zihao Kuang, Beien Zhang, Siyin Lin, Chao Wang, Yanming Gao, Jiaxing Zheng, Guanhua Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertension is one of the most important chronic diseases among middle-aged and older adults in China, characterized by high prevalence, low control rates, and a substantial burden of complications. Insulin resistance (IR) is considered a key metabolic pathway in the development and progression of hypertension; however, traditional IR measurements (e.g., the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp and HOMA-IR) have limited accessibility in large community-based samples. The triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) and its combinations with adiposity/central obesity indicators (e.g., TyG-WC) have been proposed as simple surrogate markers of IR, with advantages of low cost and easy availability. Nevertheless, prospective evidence remains relatively limited regarding the relationship between the cumulative average TyG-WC—an indicator of long-term exposure—and the risk of new-onset hypertension.MethodsThis prospective cohort study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We first excluded participants with hypertension at baseline (2011) and then excluded those who met hypertension criteria at the second exposure assessment in 2015 or lacked hypertension information in 2020; therefore, the final analytic cohort included 2571participants aged ≥ 45 years who were hypertension-free before the 2015 TyG-WC measurement. The exposure was the cumulative average TyG-WC, calculated as the mean of TyG-WC measured in 2011 and 2015. New-onset hypertension was ascertained at the 2020 follow-up after the second exposure measurement and was defined by self-reported physician diagnosis, antihypertensive treatment status, or measured blood pressure thresholds. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used as the primary association analysis, and logistic regression models were used as complementary analyses of cumulative incidence because exact calendar dates of hypertension onset were unavailable. Restricted cubic spline (RCS), subgroup and interaction, and exploratory receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed.ResultsBy the 2020 follow-up, 226 participants (8.8%) developed new-onset hypertension after the exposure window. Compared with those who remained normotensive, participants with incident hypertension were older, more likely to reside in rural areas, and had significantly higher baseline BMI, waist circumference, fasting blood glucose, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, and cumulative average TyG-WC (all P < 0.05). In the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, each 1-IQR increase in cumulative average TyG-WC was associated with a higher risk of hypertension (HR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.13–1.73, P = 0.002); compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the highest quartile (Q4) had the highest risk (HR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.27–3.14, P = 0.003). Logistic regression produced directionally consistent odds estimates (per 1-IQR increase: OR = 1.44, 95% CI 1.15–1.79, P = 0.001; Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 2.12, 95% CI 1.32–3.42, P = 0.002), but these ORs were interpreted cautiously as odds ratios rather than risk ratios. RCS analysis indicated a significant positive and approximately linear dose–response relationship (P for overall association < 0.01; P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Subgroup analyses demonstrated a generally consistent association across most strata, with a significant interaction observed only in the heart disease subgroup (P for interaction < 0.05). Adding cumulative average TyG-WC to the base model modestly increased the AUC from 0.628 to 0.642.ConclusionsAmong Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years who remained free of hypertension through the 2015 exposure assessment, the cumulative average TyG-WC was positively associated with the risk of new-onset hypertension by 2020 and showed an approximately linear dose–response pattern(Fig. 1). As a composite metabolic risk indicator derived from routine clinical measurements, cumulative average TyG-WC may help inform community-level risk stratification and primary prevention; however, the causal and clinical predictive interpretation of this study requires further validation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Cumulative average TyG-WC, New-onset hypertension, CHARLS

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is a chronic non-communicable disease characterized by elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP) and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) [1]. It can cause structural or functional changes in the arterial vascular system and/or its supplied organs (including the brain, kidneys, central and peripheral arteries, and eyes), and is a key risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [2]. Hypertension has become one of the leading causes of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 9.4 million deaths each year [3, 4]. In community medicine and public health practice, relying solely on blood pressure values for risk identification is often too late; that is, when an individual reaches the diagnostic threshold for hypertension, metabolic abnormalities and vascular injury processes have often accumulated for years. Therefore, developing low-cost, repeatable, and primary-care-compatible metabolic risk indicators to shift risk management earlier—toward stages before blood pressure rises markedly—is an important strategy for primary prevention of hypertension (See Fig. 1).

A diagram illustrating the mechanism by which cumulative average TyG-WC identifies the risk of hypertension in Chinese individuals aged 45 and older

Insulin resistance (IR) is a core pathological feature of diabetes and metabolic syndrome and plays a critical role in the development of cardiometabolic risks such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, arterial stiffness, and coronary artery calcification [5, 6]. IR and the resulting hyperinsulinemia may elevate blood pressure by activating the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [7]. Nevertheless, traditional IR measurements are constrained by cost and accessibility in community cohorts and are difficult to implement as routine tools in primary care assessments.

Against this background, the triglyceride–glucose index (TyG), constructed from fasting triglycerides (TG) and fasting blood glucose (FBG), has been proposed as a simple surrogate marker of IR and has demonstrated good applicability in studies across different populations [8, 9]. However, blood pressure risk arises not only from abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism, but is also closely related to obesity, especially central obesity. Substantial evidence indicates stable associations of weight gain, obesity, and abdominal fat accumulation with the occurrence of hypertension [10–12]. Although BMI is a commonly used obesity indicator, it cannot accurately reflect fat distribution; waist circumference (WC) better reflects abdominal obesity burden and is particularly related to visceral fat [13, 14]. The TyG-WC indicator, constructed by combining TyG with WC, essentially couples the metabolic base (glucose–lipid metabolism) with the visceral fat burden (central obesity), potentially allowing more comprehensive characterization of metabolic risk profiles related to hypertension without increasing testing costs. Previous studies suggest that TyG-WC may have potential value in predicting or associating with hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, acute ischemic stroke, fatty liver disease, myocardial infarction, and related outcomes [15–20]. Other TyG-derived composite indices, such as TyG-BMI, have also been validated as reliable predictors for adverse clinical outcomes in cardiovascular disease populations [21].

It should be emphasized that etiological research on chronic diseases focuses more on long-term exposure burden, whereas single measurements are susceptible to short-term behavioral and physiological fluctuations, measurement error, and regression dilution bias, which may underestimate true association strength. Therefore, using a cumulative average exposure is conceptually closer to long-term metabolic status. Averaging two measurements can reduce the influence of short-term fluctuations and random measurement error in principle, but this assumption was not directly validated within the present dataset. Most previous studies used single exposure measurements and were limited in reflecting long-term average metabolic burden; thus, the cumulative average TyG-WC may fill a key gap regarding long-term metabolic load in chronic disease etiological research in community populations.

Therefore, based on the nationwide CHARLS cohort and focusing on Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, this study aimed to address the following key questions: Is the cumulative average TyG-WC associated with the risk of new-onset hypertension? Does this association exhibit a linear dose–response relationship? Do different population characteristics modify this association? These pieces of evidence will provide more actionable indicators and strategic bases for hypertension risk stratification and precision prevention at the community level. In summary, this study constructed the cumulative average TyG-WC and systematically evaluated its association with new-onset hypertension risk, its dose–response form, and potential effect modification, with the goal of providing translatable evidence for community hypertension risk stratification and primary prevention strategies.

Methods

Data source and study design

This study was a prospective cohort study based on CHARLS (a secondary data analysis). CHARLS was initiated in 2011 and adopted a multistage probability sampling framework with probability proportional to size (PPS), covering 28 provinces, 150 counties (districts), and 450 villages/neighborhood committees across China. It included approximately 10,000 households and more than 17,000 individuals, providing good national representativeness. CHARLS uses a longitudinal follow-up survey design. Interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews in participants’ homes using computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) to collect information on demographics, health status, medical care and insurance, employment and economic status, and related domains, and also performed physical measurements and blood sample collection. The survey covered multiple dimensions including basic demographic information of respondents and their families, economic transfers among family members, respondents’ health status, medical and insurance coverage, employment status, income level, consumption expenditures, and assets. In addition, CHARLS included measurements of 13 physical indicators and blood sample collection.

Throughout data collection, the CHARLS project strictly adhered to ethical review requirements; ethical review was conducted by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University. All participants signed formal written informed consent after fully understanding the study purpose and content to ensure their rights were protected. In addition, the design and implementation of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. According to the above criteria, 2571 participants were ultimately included in the analytical cohort (Fig. 2).

Participant filter flowchart

To clarify temporality, after exclusion of participants with hypertension at baseline (2011), participants who met hypertension criteria at the 2015 visit or lacked hypertension status in 2020 were also excluded (n = 1,251 as shown in Fig. 2). Consequently, within the final analytic cohort, no participant had hypertension before or at the second TyG-WC measurement in 2015; all 226 incident cases were ascertained after the exposure window at the 2020 follow-up.

Exposure assessment: cumulative average TyG-WC

The exposure in this study was the cumulative average TyG-WC, intended to characterize the long-term average level of the combined burden of IR and central obesity.

(1) First, TyG-WC in 2011 and 2015 was calculated separately:

TyG-WC = ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL) / 2] × WC (cm).

(2) Then, the cumulative average was calculated:

Cumulative average TyG-WC = (TyG-WC_2011 + TyG-WC_2015) / 2.

To enhance interpretability at the population level, we categorized the cumulative average TyG-WC into quartiles (Q1–Q4) and also included it as a continuous variable in regression analyses, reporting effect sizes per IQR (interquartile range).

Participants who had hypertension by the 2015 survey were excluded before constructing the final analytic cohort. Thus, cumulative average TyG-WC was defined only among participants who were free of hypertension at both the 2011 baseline and the 2015 s exposure assessment, ensuring that exposure preceded outcome ascertainment in 2020.

Outcome assessment: new-onset hypertension

New-onset hypertension was the primary outcome of this study. In the CHARLS survey, participants were asked whether a doctor had ever told them that they had hypertension and whether they were currently receiving treatment (Chinese medicine, Western medicine, other treatment, or none) to treat or control hypertension. Participants with self-reported physician diagnosis, antihypertensive treatment, mean SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, or mean DBP ≥ 90 mmHg were defined as having hypertension. Because the present exposure used TyG-WC measurements from both 2011 and 2015, participants who met any hypertension criterion at baseline or at the 2015 visit were excluded from the analytic cohort. Incident hypertension therefore referred to hypertension newly identified at the 2020 follow-up. Exact calendar dates of hypertension onset between survey waves were not available, so the timing of onset was treated as interval-censored at the survey-wave level.

Participants who self-reported a history of diabetes and/or received any glucose-lowering treatment and/or had baseline FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L were considered to have diabetes. Other diseases were identified based on self-reported history or receipt of any specific treatment.

Blood pressure, waist circumference, and blood biomarkers were collected according to standardized CHARLS protocols [22, 23]. Blood pressure was measured three times using an automatic electronic monitor after the participant was seated, with the left arm supported and the cuff placed directly on the skin; the mean SBP and DBP were used in analyses. Waist circumference was measured at the level of the navel with a tape measure held horizontally and snugly but not tightly, after removal of bulky clothing and at the end of expiration. Venous blood samples were collected by trained medical personnel/local CDC staff. Complete blood count testing was conducted locally shortly after blood collection, whereas plasma, white blood cell layer samples, and HbA1c samples were transported and stored under cold-chain conditions and assayed centrally according to the CHARLS biomarker protocols. These procedures support cross-center standardization and quality control of BP, WC, and laboratory measures in the public CHARLS data.

Handling of missing values

The main analysis used a complete-case strategy. Records with missing exposure, outcome, or covariate data were excluded rather than imputed; therefore, random forest multiple imputation was not performed and MAR diagnostics for such imputation were not applicable. The stepwise exclusion process is shown in Fig. 2, including missing baseline TyG-WC information (n = 8,835), age < 45 years (n = 251), baseline hypertension/status-related exclusions (n = 2,385), missing baseline covariates (n = 417), missing 2015 TyG-WC information (n = 1,980), and hypertension at 2015 or missing 2020 hypertension status (n = 1,251). All major models and extended analyses were conducted among the 2571participants with complete data required for the corresponding analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analysis

To describe baseline characteristics, participants were divided into two groups according to hypertension status at follow-up (non-hypertension vs. hypertension). Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (P25, P75) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test.

(2)Primary association analysis

Primary association analysis

To evaluate the relationship between cumulative average TyG-WC and hypertension incidence, Cox proportional hazards models were used as the primary association models and logistic regression models were used as complementary analyses. CHARLS provides hypertension status at survey waves rather than exact calendar dates of onset; therefore, the Cox analysis used survey interval follow-up, whereas logistic regression summarized the cumulative occurrence of hypertension by 2020. Cox models reported hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and logistic models reported odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs; because ORs may overestimate risk ratios when outcomes are not rare, the logistic estimates were interpreted cautiously and mainly for consistency with the Cox results. The cumulative average TyG-WC was analyzed both continuously (per 1-IQR increase) and categorically (quartiles Q1–Q4). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, marital status, education level, residence, smoking status, drinking status, and night sleep duration. Model 3 additionally adjusted for diabetes, stroke, heart disease, dyslipidemia, BMI, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, CRP, WBC, PLT, creatinine, and HbA1c. Baseline SBP and DBP were not included in the primary fully adjusted model because high-normal BP may be a preclinical intermediate state between metabolic exposure and hypertension; however, residual confounding by baseline BP severity cannot be completely ruled out and is acknowledged in the Discussion. Because WC, TG, and FBG are mathematical components of TyG-WC, they were not co-adjusted with TyG-WC in the primary model to avoid structural collinearity and overadjustment of exposure components. Multicollinearity among the variables actually included in the primary models was assessed using VIF/GVIF diagnostics, with GVIF^(1/(2×Df)) < 2 and VIF < 5 considered acceptable.

(3)Dose–response and nonlinearity analysis

Dose–response and nonlinearity analysis

To examine the dose–response relationship and potential nonlinearity between the cumulative average TyG-WC and the risk of incident hypertension, fully adjusted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed based on Model 3. Both Cox and logistic RCS regression analyses were conducted. P values for overall association and nonlinearity were reported.

(4)Subgroup and interaction analysis

Subgroup and interaction analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the relationship between the cumulative average TyG-WC and hypertension incidence was consistent across different population strata. Participants were stratified by age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), sex (male vs. female), marital status, education level, place of residence (urban vs. rural), smoking status, drinking status, sleep time, diabetes status, heart disease status, dyslipidemia status, and BMI (using the median as the cutoff). Fully adjusted analyses based on Model 3 were performed within each subgroup. Interaction terms between the cumulative average TyG-WC and each stratification variable were added to test effect modification, and P values for interaction were reported.

(5)Predictive performance analysis

Predictive performance analysis

To explore the incremental discriminative value of cumulative average TyG-WC, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was compared between a base model including conventional risk factors and an enhanced model adding cumulative average TyG-WC.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.1. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics and follow-up outcomes

There were 2571participants in the final analytic cohort, including 1,177 men (45.8%) and 1,394 women (54.2%). By design, these participants were free of hypertension at baseline and at the 2015 exposure assessment. During the post-2015 follow-up to 2020, 2,345 participants (91.2%) remained free of hypertension and 226 participants (8.8%) developed new-onset hypertension.

Association between the cumulative average TyG-WC and new-onset hypertension

The log-rank test comparing cumulative incidence curves across TyG-WC quartiles was statistically significant (P = 0.0017). The curves separated mainly during the early survey intervals, and the Q4 group showed the greatest decline in the number at risk, consistent with the highest observed incidence risk. Because all participants included in these curves were free of hypertension at the 2015 exposure assessment, these results support a temporal sequence in which higher cumulative average TyG-WC preceded incident hypertension identified by 2020 (Fig. 3).

Difference in cumulative incidence rate of hypertension over follow-up time in different groups grouped according to the quartile of the cumulative average TyG-WC

Based on the regression results shown in Table 2, hypertension risk increased across quartiles of the cumulative average TyG-WC in both Cox and logistic regression models, with significant trend tests (all P for trend < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model (Model 3), each 1-IQR increase in cumulative average TyG-WC was associated with a higher risk of new-onset hypertension (HR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.13–1.73). Compared with Q1, Q4 had the highest risk (HR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.27–3.14). In the complementary logistic regression model, each 1-IQR increase was associated with higher odds of hypertension (OR = 1.44, 95% CI 1.15–1.79), and Q4 showed the highest odds relative to Q1 (OR = 2.12, 95% CI 1.32–3.42). Given the cumulative incidence of 8.8%, ORs were not interpreted as risk ratios; instead, they were used to verify directional consistency with the Cox estimates.

From a public health perspective, although the increment in HR/OR is not extremely large, given the relatively high baseline incidence risk of hypertension in middle-aged and older populations, even a moderate increase in relative risk may translate into a considerable increase in absolute event burden at the population level, and thus has practical significance.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analysis

Based on the fully adjusted Model 3, RCS analysis showed a significant overall association between cumulative average TyG-WC and the risk of new-onset hypertension (both Cox and logistic P for overall < 0.01), while evidence for nonlinearity was not statistically significant (Cox P for nonlinear = 0.119; logistic P for nonlinear = 0.122), indicating an approximately linear positive dose-response relationship (Fig. 4). This pattern is consistent with the etiological hypothesis.

Subgroup analysis and interaction

To further investigate whether the relationship between cumulative average TyG-WC and hypertension incidence is stable across different subgroups, subgroup analyses were performed.

Subgroup analyses showed that the positive direction of association between cumulative average TyG-WC and new-onset hypertension was generally consistent across most strata. In sex-stratified analyses, the association was positive in women (OR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.20–2.24) and directionally positive in men (OR = 1.17, 95% CI 0.86–1.59), with no significant sex interaction (P for interaction = 0.893). A statistically significant interaction was observed only for heart disease status (P for interaction = 0.006); this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of participants with heart disease (Fig. 5).

ROC curve analysis

The exploratory ROC analysis compared a base model with an enhanced model that additionally included cumulative average TyG-WC. The enhanced model showed a modest increase in AUC from 0.628 to 0.642 (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Main findings and interpretation

This study was conducted based on the following evidence background (Evidence before this study): (1) There is a clear biological and epidemiological link between IR and hypertension, but traditional IR measurements are not accessible in large community-based studies. (2) TyG and combined indices of TyG with obesity indicators have been widely used in cardiometabolic risk research, and some studies suggest that TyG-WC may have certain advantages in identifying hypertension risk. (3) Existing evidence is mostly based on one-time exposure measurements, which are insufficient for characterizing long-term metabolic load and are easily affected by short-term fluctuations.

Using the nationwide CHARLS cohort of middle-aged and older adults, this study used the cumulative average TyG-WC to characterize long-term metabolic load and found a stable positive association with the risk of new-onset hypertension, showing an approximately linear dose–response relationship. From a community epidemiology perspective, these findings provide evidence that is closer to the natural history of chronic diseases: the combined burden of long-term dysglycemia/dyslipidemia and central obesity can be captured by the cumulative average TyG-WC—a composite indicator derived from routine clinical measurements that is stably linked to hypertension risk at the population level.

Compared with previous studies largely based on single measurements, this study highlights the methodological value of long-term average exposure. A single TyG-WC measurement is susceptible to diet composition, seasonal variation, short-term weight fluctuations, stress state, and laboratory error, leading to regression dilution bias and underestimation of associations. The cumulative average can, to some extent, reduce the influence of random fluctuations and is conceptually closer to the long-term metabolic exposure background that chronic disease development requires. This is particularly critical for hypertension, whose core mechanism is long-term cumulative damage.

This study demonstrated a significant increasing trend in hypertension risk across quartiles of the cumulative average TyG-WC (P for trend < 0.001). Compared with Q1, individuals in Q4 had the highest risk of hypertension in the fully adjusted Cox model (HR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.27–3.14, P = 0.003). The corresponding logistic model yielded an OR of 2.12 (95% CI 1.32–3.42, P = 0.002), which should be interpreted as an odds ratio and not as a risk ratio. Fully adjusted RCS analyses further suggested an approximately linear association (P for overall < 0.01; Cox P for nonlinearity = 0.119; logistic P for nonlinearity = 0.122). These findings support an independent association after adjustment for measured covariates, but they do not prove causality or establish clinical prediction validity.

Several issues require cautious interpretation. First, baseline SBP and DBP differed between participants with and without subsequent hypertension. We did not adjust for baseline SBP/DBP in the primary model because high-normal blood pressure may be a preclinical intermediate on the pathway from metabolic burden to hypertension; nevertheless, residual confounding by baseline BP severity may persist. Second, although medication information was collected in CHARLS, time-varying initiation, dose, adherence, and duration of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering treatments were not incorporated into the current main models, and these treatments may influence both TyG-WC values and hypertension ascertainment. Third, the complete-case design may introduce selection bias if missingness was related to exposure or outcome. Fourth, the current ROC analysis was exploratory and did not include calibration or reclassification metrics. Finally, this study focused on Chinese middle-aged and older adults, so external generalizability requires validation in other populations.

Possible mechanisms linking TyG-WC to hypertension

From the mechanistic chain, TyG-WC integrates two key risk pathways. First, the IR pathway: IR and hyperinsulinemia can promote blood pressure elevation through sympathetic activation, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, renal sodium retention, vascular smooth muscle proliferation, and endothelial dysfunction, and can form a mutually reinforcing vicious cycle with chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress [24–27]. TyG is considered a reliable biochemical surrogate marker of IR; thus, the TyG component can reflect this metabolic pathway. Second, the central obesity pathway: central obesity, characterized by increased waist circumference and visceral fat accumulation, is closely associated with an adverse lipid profile, insulin resistance, elevated diastolic blood pressure, arterial stiffness, and impaired overall cardiovascular health [28–30]. The stable association between obesity and hypertension has been confirmed by many studies [10–12]. Therefore, TyG-WC, which combines TyG with waist circumference, may more comprehensively represent the joint burden of metabolic abnormality × fat distribution relevant to hypertension.

The approximately linear dose–response relationship shown by the RCS analysis further supports, from an epidemiological perspective, the gradient effect hypothesis that the higher the long-term metabolic burden, the higher the blood pressure risk.

Comparison with previous studies

Previous studies have reported associations between TyG-related indicators and hypertension or cardiometabolic risks in different populations. TyG and its derived indicators are related to IR levels and have certain predictive and stratification values [31, 32]. Multiple studies further indicate that TyG-WC is associated with hypertension, cardiovascular risk, or other metabolic outcomes, including cross-sectional evidence on hypertension and cardiovascular risk [20], comparative studies of different IR indices and incident hypertension [33–35], and association studies of TyG-WC with coronary heart disease and metabolic syndrome [36, 37].

Overall, the results of this study are consistent with the above evidence, but methodologically extend exposure assessment from a single measurement to a cumulative average and provide evidence of an approximately linear dose–response shape, offering clearer structural information for subsequent threshold exploration and development of community risk assessment tools.

Implications for public health and primary care chronic disease management

The public health implication of this study is that cumulative average TyG-WC is low-cost, easy to calculate, and repeatedly measurable from routine TG, FBG, and waist circumference data. In community chronic disease management, it may serve as an accessible marker for identifying individuals who may benefit from earlier lifestyle counseling, waist management, and closer blood pressure surveillance. However, because the present study was observational and showed only modest exploratory AUC gain, its clinical use should be considered risk-stratification support rather than a validated prediction model.

In primary care services and community health examination settings, TyG-WC has three prominent advantages: first, data accessibility—TG, FBG, and waist circumference are routine examination items with low additional cost; second, standardized calculation—the TyG-WC formula is explicit and can be embedded into information systems for automatic generation; third, utility for stratified intervention—for individuals with persistently high cumulative average TyG-WC, priority can be given to weight and waist management, diet and exercise interventions, education on glucose–lipid metabolic risk, and more intensive blood pressure follow-up, thereby achieving earlier and stratified primary prevention of hypertension.

Although this study provides associative evidence and is insufficient to directly infer causality, in the formulation of chronic disease prevention strategies, accessible indicators with biological plausibility and stable associations can still serve as reference tools for risk stratification and resource allocation.

Limitations

This study also has limitations that require cautious interpretation: (1) although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual and unmeasured confounding may still exist, especially by baseline high-normal BP severity; (2) several metabolic variables may lie on the causal pathway, and adjustment choices may therefore under- or over-estimate the total association; (3) complete-case analysis was used, so selection bias due to missing data cannot be excluded; (4) antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering treatments initiated during follow-up were not modeled as time-varying exposures; (5) the incremental value of cumulative average TyG-WC was compared only with a base model and not directly against cumulative TyG alone or cumulative WC alone; and (6) ROC analysis was exploratory and did not include calibration, reclassification, or external validation. Because this study was based on Chinese middle-aged and older adults, extrapolation to populations of different races and socioeconomic environments still requires further validation.

Conclusions

Based on CHARLS data, this study demonstrated a statistically significant association between cumulative average TyG-WC measured before hypertension onset and subsequent new-onset hypertension. The cumulative average TyG-WC showed a stable positive association and an approximately linear dose–response relationship. As a long-term metabolic load indicator available from routine community health examination data, cumulative average TyG-WC may support risk stratification for primary prevention, but its causal role and clinical prediction utility require further validation with more detailed longitudinal timing, medication information, and external prediction-performance assessment.
