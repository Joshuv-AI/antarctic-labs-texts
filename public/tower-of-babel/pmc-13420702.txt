History of Presentation

An 8-month-old male infant presented after achieving return of spontaneous circulation from prolonged cardiorespiratory arrest lasting 53 minutes. Ventricular tachycardia occurred during resuscitation and was terminated with 2 defibrillation attempts.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit without vasoactive support and on mechanical ventilation, with a planned diagnostic workup to investigate the etiology of the arrhythmic episodes.

On admission, the patient presented with a heart rate of 194 beats/min, respiratory rate 26 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 98%. The patient was intubated and mechanically ventilated in controlled mode, sedated with midazolam and fentanyl. Extremities were cold. Pupils were isocoric and reactive to light. Lung auscultation revealed clear breath sounds without adventitious sounds, and cardiac auscultation demonstrated a regular rhythm without cardiac murmurs.

During hospitalization, the patient experienced intermittent episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, both documented on telemetry and confirmed with serial Holter monitoring (Table 1).

Past Medical History

The patient had no prior medical conditions, as well as no known family history for inherited cardiomyopathies, sudden cardiac death (SCD), or prior diagnosed genetic syndromes.

Differential Diagnosis

In pediatric patients, ventricular tachycardia in the setting of a structurally normal heart suggests channelopathies or idiopathic ventricular tachycardia, whereas the presence of structural heart disease favors cardiomyopathies, myocarditis, or cardiac tumors, with polymorphic or stress-induced arrhythmias raising suspicion for catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia or long QT syndrome.1

The differential diagnosis of an intracardiac mass in infancy includes cardiac fibroma, rhabdomyoma, hemangioma, and less likely malignant primary cardiac tumors.1

Investigations

An initial electrocardiogram, after return of spontaneous circulation, showed normal sinus rhythm with a PR interval of 126 ms, narrow QRS complexes, left axis deviation, and a heart rate of 81 beats/min. Pathologic Q waves were present in leads II, III, aVF, and V4 to V6, consistent with electrically inactive areas in the inferior and lateral walls. Signs of left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and nonspecific secondary repolarization abnormalities were also observed (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large intracardiac mass causing restriction of left ventricular filling and involving the mitral subvalvular apparatus (Video 1). Further examination defined an extensive, well-defined, heterogeneous, and infiltrative tumor involving the entire lateral wall and apex of the LV, associated with moderate systolic dysfunction due to posterior wall hypokinesia related to tumor infiltration (Figures 2 and 3, Video 2). Given this finding, a follow-up cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was ordered.

This revealed a well-circumscribed lesion attached to the lateral and inferior walls of the LV, measuring 5.3 × 3.2 × 3.8 cm, with homogeneous signal intensity and progressive contrast enhancement. The mass caused near obliteration of the LV cavity during diastole, without LV outflow tract obstruction (Figure 4).

The findings were consistent with a cardiac fibroma, a tumor known for progressive growth and a high arrhythmogenic potential. Differentiating from other common pediatric benign primary cardiac masses, such as rhabdomyomas, is essential for accurate diagnosis and management.2

Management

The patient developed recurrent episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia documented on continuous telemetry monitoring. Antiarrhythmic therapy with amiodarone 4 mL (1 mg/kg/d) and propranolol 7 mL every 8 hours (2.6 mg/kg/d) was initiated, resulting in a reduction in ventricular ectopic burden.

Initial surgical treatment was not pursued due to catastrophic and permanent neurological injury following prolonged hypoxic-ischemic insult.

Surgical resection is the treatment of choice for cardiac fibromas when the tumor is well circumscribed and resectable without compromising ventricular function. In this case, extensive infiltration of the LV wall and near cavity obliteration made resection technically unfeasible and high risk.

Heart transplantation may be considered in unresectable tumors; however, severe neurological impairment represented a major contraindication.

In contrast, in patients without neurological injury and with less extensive disease, surgical options may include complete or partial resection or, in selected cases, transplantation.

Given the overall clinical condition, the case was discussed by a multidisciplinary team including cardiology, electrophysiology, cardiac surgery, neurology, and heart transplantation specialists. Therefore, a conservative management strategy with antiarrhythmic therapy and multidisciplinary outpatient follow-up was adopted.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At hospital discharge, the patient was hemodynamically stable, breathing room air (SpO2 96%) with a heart rate of 54 beats/min. Physical examination showed a hypoactive infant with limited responsiveness due to chronic hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Cardiac auscultation revealed a regular rhythm without murmurs, lung examination showed preserved breath sounds without respiratory distress, and the abdomen was soft with a gastrostomy tube in place and no signs of infection.

At outpatient follow-up 2 months later, the patient remained clinically stable but with significant neurological impairment, including reduced responsiveness.

Holter monitoring showed a reduction in the burden of ventricular ectopy from 23% to 3%, allowing a decrease in the propranolol dose to 3.5 mL every 8 hours. After multidisciplinary reassessment, the patient was considered not eligible for heart transplantation. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapy was also considered but deferred due to severe neurological sequelae and limited life expectancy.

After shared decision making with the family, a palliative care strategy focusing on symptom control and quality of life was adopted. The patient currently remains under multidisciplinary follow-up with pediatric cardiology, neurology, and palliative care teams. Persistent neurological sequelae include dystonia, epileptic syndrome, and autonomic dysfunction, while cardiac symptoms remain controlled with medical therapy.

Discussion

Cardiac fibromas are nonencapsulated tumors composed of fibroblasts and connective tissue and are mostly diagnosed by echocardiography. Once a cardiac mass is identified, the differential diagnosis includes other benign tumors, such as myxomas, rhabdomyomas, lipomas, and hemangiomas.3

CMR plays a fundamental role in defining lesion extent and tissue characteristics, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic planning. On CMR imaging, rhabdomyomas typically demonstrate T2 hyperintensity with absent or minimal late gadolinium enhancement, whereas fibromas exhibit T2 hypointensity associated with intense late gadolinium enhancement, allowing differentiation between these entities.4

In pediatric patients, clinical presentation may include syncope, dyspnea, chest pain, ventricular arrhythmias, and SCD. Complete surgical resection or heart transplantation is considered the treatment of choice when feasible.5

Clinical manifestations may vary and can be detected late in the disease course. Because of the potential complications associated with surgical biopsy, diagnosis of cardiac fibroma relies primarily on multimodal imaging using echocardiography, computed tomography, and CMR.5

Conduction disturbances such as ventricular ectopy are common that may progress to complex ventricular arrhythmias and SCD. However, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement is dependent on an individualized analysis. Although rare, the presence of electrically inactive myocardial areas in a pediatric patient has been described in association with cardiac fibromas.1

Untreated cardiac fibromas are associated with a high risk of mortality, primarily due to life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias. Early detection and appropriate clinical management are critical to reduce the risk of sudden death.4

Conclusions

Cardiac fibromas are the second most common primary cardiac tumors in childhood. The apparent increase in incidence in recent years is likely related to advances in cardiac imaging and diagnostic techniques. Although histologically benign, cardiac fibromas exhibit a broad spectrum of clinical presentations, ranging from asymptomatic cases to severe outcomes such as SCD. In children, cardiac fibromas are often not amenable to surgical resection because of their size and extensive myocardial infiltration, resulting in a worse prognosis and potentially requiring implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapy or heart transplantation.
