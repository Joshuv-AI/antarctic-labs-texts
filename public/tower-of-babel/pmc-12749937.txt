A lightweight and generalizable deep learning framework for early detection of rice leaf diseases in complex field environments
Authors: Chong Zhang, Xiaoxi Hao, Jianan Liang, Lili Li, Wenwei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rice leaf diseases pose a significant and escalating threat to global food security. Timely and accurate detection, particularly in the critical early stages characterized by subtle lesions, is paramount for effective disease management. However, existing solutions often struggle with the complexities of real-world field environments (e.g., variable lighting, occlusions, complex backgrounds), computational constraints on edge devices, and limited generalizability across diverse disease types and plant species. To address these challenges, this study proposes a novel lightweight deep learning framework specifically designed for robust rice leaf disease detection. Our key innovations include: (1) A Multi-branch Large-kernel Fusion Depthwise (MLFD) module enhancing multi-scale contextual feature extraction critical for identifying subtle early lesions; (2) A Multi-scale Dilated Transformer Attention (MDTA) module integrating spatial and channel attention mechanisms to improve feature representation under complex conditions; (3) A Lightweight Detection Head (Lo-Head) optimized with grouped and depthwise convolutions, drastically reducing model complexity without sacrificing accuracy. Crucially, extensive experiments demonstrate the framework’s superior performance. On a dedicated rice leaf disease dataset, it achieves a mean Average Precision mAP@0.5:0.95 of 62.62%, outperforming state-of-the-art lightweight detectors including YOLOv5n (56.73%), YOLOv8n (57.41%), YOLOv10n (56.14%), and the baseline YOLOv11n (60.85%), while maintaining low computational demands (6.3 GFLOPs, 2.66M parameters). Significantly, rigorous generalization experiments validate the model’s exceptional transferability. Evaluated on independent datasets encompassing potato and tomato leaf diseases, the proposed framework consistently surpasses comparable models in mAP@0.5:0.95, demonstrating its robust capability to detect diseases across different plant species. This combination of high accuracy, computational efficiency, and remarkable cross-crop generalizability positions our framework as a highly promising tool for practical deployment on resource-limited edge devices (e.g., drones, field sensors) in smart agriculture systems, enabling proactive disease surveillance and precision control strategies across diverse crops.

Introduction

Rice is an essential staple food globally, supporting vast populations and remaining central to agricultural systems across many Asian nations. Its high yield per unit area allows for efficient land resource utilization. Consequently, safeguarding rice health is synonymous with preserving food security, enhancing land-use economics, and contributing to ecological stability1,2. For many decades, China has been a primary contributor to global rice output and domestic consumption. Despite this, rice production is increasingly jeopardized by shifting climates, changes in cultivation practices, and the proliferation of pests and diseases3. Specifically, leaf diseases–including rice blast, brown spot, bacterial streak, and blight–are highly destructive and represent a critical menace to food supplies4.

Rice blast, a fungal disease, is considered the most intricate and destructive rice pathology due to its multi-site infestation and ability to persist across the entire growth cycle5. Its symptoms include diamond, oval, or spindle-shaped leaf spots featuring gray/white centers and brown borders. Severe outbreaks can cause total leaf desiccation, compromising photosynthesis and growth, which precipitates significant yield reduction. Similarly, brown spot manifests as small, oval lesions, initially light brown but darkening centrally, often surrounded by a yellow halo, resulting in localized leaf tissue necrosis6. Bacterial leaf streak, a bacterial infection, thrives in warm, humid conditions with ample rainfall. The pathogen invades leaves to form small, dark green, translucent spots that elongate into yellowish-brown streaks confined by the leaf veins7. Finally, bacterial leaf blight, caused by the bacterium Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae, typically enters via stomata or marginal wounds. This disease is characterized by initial small, water-soaked, yellow-green lesions along leaf margins that rapidly lengthen along the veins, forming long, wavy, yellow-to-white stripes. Coalescence of these lesions causes the leaf to dry out completely. Advanced infection stages result in premature senescence, diminished photosynthetic capacity, and severe crop loss8. The reliance of traditional disease detection techniques on manual expertise renders them time-consuming, inherently subjective, and inadequate for the rapid scaling requirements of modern agriculture. Consequently, developing highly efficient and accurate detection technologies is paramount for enhancing disease control capabilities and securing food supplies9.

Swift diagnosis and accurate localization of rice diseases are fundamental requirements for effective preventative management. In this context, image-based object detection technology has gained substantial traction as a research focus, offering solutions that are efficient, objective, and scalable10. By integrating high-resolution imaging (often from cameras or drones) with advanced deep learning algorithms, accurate disease identification and spatial localization can be achieved11. Agricultural disease detection research generally employs two categories of deep learning models. The first consists of two-stage detection frameworks, such as Faster R-CNN12 and Mask-RCNN13. These models prioritize detection precision by utilizing a region proposal stage before final classification. The second category comprises one-stage, regression-based networks like YOLO14 and SSD15, which are highly valued for their exceptional real-time inference speed. Over recent iterations, the YOLO network specifically has seen improvements that boost both detection accuracy and processing speed, leading to its widespread application in industrial defect analysis and large-scale agricultural monitoring of crops and fruit16.

Related work

Current research has demonstrated considerable advancements in automated plant leaf disease detection. For instance, Ramadan et al.17 applied Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to augment rice leaf datasets, successfully increasing data volume and diversity. This strategy mitigated overfitting risks and consequently boosted the overall detection performance. Tao et al.18 introduced CEFW-YOLO, a lightweight network built upon YOLOv11n, which integrated channel compression convolution and a cross-channel attention mechanism. This architectural change significantly strengthened the network’s capacity for global feature capture, leading to improvements in both accuracy and operational robustness. Wang et al.19 focused on enhancing the YOLOv8 framework, achieving a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 94.8%, which enabled the detection and subsequent grading of fresh tea tree leaves. Xu et al.20 designed the novel SPLDPvB network, which proved capable of precisely identifying leaf spot disease and common pests in sweet potatoes. In a different crop context, Zhou et al.21 developed a high-precision methodology for detecting gray spot disease in apples. Furthermore, Shi et al.22 improved field-level cabbage seedling detection through the incorporation of the CBAM23 and BiFPN modules24. Abulizi et al.25 enhanced the YOLOv9 structure by integrating DySample26 and MPDIoU27, aiming to improve the feature extraction for small lesions, refine boundary accuracy, and reduce background interference. Focusing on feature granularity, Zhou et al.28 introduced cross-modal retrieval for rice leaf lesion detection by employing ViT29 and BERT30 for fine-grained feature extraction. Their model’s learning efficiency was further optimized via a bidirectional hybrid self-attention mechanism and a warm-up bat algorithm for dynamic learning rate scheduling31. Finally, Deari et al.32 demonstrated robust rice leaf disease classification by fusing classification and detection networks, an approach that effectively minimized the occurrence of false positives.

In parallel with advancing model accuracy, the topic of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) in deep learning for agriculture has gained increasing prominence. Understanding why a model makes a specific prediction is crucial for building trust and reliability, particularly in real-world applications where decision errors can be costly. Research efforts have explored various techniques to interpret complex deep neural networks. For instance, studies have employed attention mechanisms and feature analysis to enhance model interpretability in crop classification tasks33,34. Other works have integrated hybrid feature aggregation networks with specialized encoding attention mechanisms to improve both classification performance and the interpretability of disease identification in crops like cassava35. Furthermore, the application of ensemble learning alongside XAI techniques has been investigated to enhance the reliability and transparency of plant leaf disease detection systems36. These approaches highlight the growing consensus that interpretability is as essential as raw performance in modern agricultural AI systems.

Research contributions

To address the aforementioned limitations, we propose a novel lightweight and generalizable deep learning framework specifically engineered for early rice disease detection in complex field environments. Our core contributions are explicitly outlined below:Multi-branch Large-kernel Fusion Depthwise (MLFD) module: We propose a novel MLFD module that replaces standard convolutions. It utilizes parallel large-kernel depthwise convolutions to significantly enhance multi-scale feature extraction for subtle early lesions, improving the model’s ability to detect small and faint spots while maintaining computational efficiency.Multi-scale Dilated Transformer Attention (MDTA) module: We introduce the MDTA module, which integrates spatial-channel attention mechanisms with dilated convolutions. This module effectively expands the receptive field without increasing parameters, improving feature representation and enhancing the model’s robustness against environmental noise (e.g., shadows, light variation) and background clutter.Lightweight Detection Head (Lo-Head): We design an optimized Lightweight Detection Head (Lo-Head) that utilizes grouped convolutions. This optimization drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, enabling high-speed, real-time inference on resource-limited edge devices for practical agricultural deployment.Demonstrated Cross-Species Generalizability and Superior Performance: Beyond validating our model’s effectiveness on rice, we conducted a systematic evaluation of its cross-species generalizability. When training our proposed method on both potato and tomato leaf disease datasets, our approach achieved a significant improvement in detection accuracy compared to the baseline model on both new crops. This result confirms the framework’s robust cross-crop versatility and superior performance, offering an efficient solution for unified multi-crop disease monitoring.

General technical route

Fig. 3Overall Framework for the Development and Iterative Optimization of the Proposed Rice Leaf Disease Detection Network.

Overall Framework for the Development and Iterative Optimization of the Proposed Rice Leaf Disease Detection Network.

This study systematically details the development of a rice leaf disease detection network built upon the enhanced YOLOv11 architecture, adhering to established protocols for deep learning model engineering. Our research commenced with the Data Preparation phase, aiming to provide stable and high-quality input for the subsequent model training process through strict and comprehensive processing. In this stage, we performed key operations such as Data Cleaning, Image Preprocessing, Data Partitioning, and Data Augmentation. Subsequently, the process enters the Model Optimization/Performance Evaluation phase, where the improved network architecture is defined through Model Configuration, and efficient training algorithms are utilized to accurately learn disease characteristics. The key in this phase lies in Model Training and Hyperparameter Tuning, which, combined with Testing and Validation and Performance Evaluation, form a tight feedback loop to ensure optimal model performance. Finally, the rigorously evaluated model is prepared for Final Model Deployment. The stages of the entire research process are closely connected and iteratively optimized. The trained model is ultimately applied to the test data for inference and validation to rigorously examine its effectiveness in real-world scenarios. The detailed flow is shown in Fig. 3.

The complexity of early-stage rice leaf disease detection, characterized by subtle, fine-grained lesions in dynamic field environments (variable light, shadows), necessitates a highly efficient yet robust architecture. Our proposed framework introduces three specialized modules integrated into the YOLOv11n backbone: MLFD, MDTA, and Lo-Head. First, we replace the original C3k2 component with the novel Multi-branch Large-kernel Fusion Depthwise (MLFD) module. This module is designed as the primary, computationally-light backbone feature extractor. Its architecture employs a parallel multi-branch topology and integrates large-kernel depthwise separable convolutions, which is motivated by the need to efficiently capture the subtle texture and extended context of early lesions while mitigating the trade-off between receptive field size and computational cost.

To refine contextual feature representation and address the known limitations of standard channel-wise attention within multi-scale feature hierarchies, we introduce the Multi-scale Dilated Transformer Attention (MDTA) module. MDTA is strategically incorporated into the network’s neck to bolster feature resilience against complicated backgrounds and varying target scales. It achieves this by combining a Transformer-inspired attention mechanism with spatial dilation. This structural choice grants the model an exponentially expanded receptive field without introducing additional parameters, enabling the capture of vital long-range dependencies and non-local context. This capability is key to effectively discriminating small lesions from surrounding healthy tissue or environmental noise. Within the MDTA structure, CA signifies Channel Attention, while SA denotes Spatial Attention, highlighting its dual-path architecture for comprehensive feature refinement.

Subsequently, the conventional detection head is substituted with a lightweight architectural variant termed Lo-Head. This modification is motivated by the critical need for real-time inference capability on edge devices with restricted resources. The Lo-Head drastically minimizes both the parameter count and floating-point operations (FLOPs), primarily by utilizing group convolutions and an optimized decoupled structure. This ensures the resultant final architecture is fully suitable for deployment on agricultural platforms demanding high Frames Per Second (FPS) performance. The complete structural design of the proposed framework, illustrating the seamless integration of the MLFD, MDTA, and Lo-Head modules into the overall detection pipeline, is presented in Fig. 4.

The C3k2 module in the original network, while capable of combining different convolutional kernels for multi-scale features, inherently increases computational complexity, affecting real-time performance. Furthermore, its capacity to manage variations from shading, complex backgrounds, and capture fine details of very small targets is often insufficient for the diverse and complex data found in natural field environments. This makes the original C3k2 module weak in early disease detection of rice leaves.

The MLFD module is engineered to amplify feature diversity and enrich semantic representation while maintaining minimal computational overhead. As depicted in Fig. 5, its architectural foundation is a parallel, multi-branch topology (refer to subfigure (a)), which processes the incoming feature map simultaneously across several independent pathways. To foster inter-branch synergy, the MLFD module implements a cascaded fusion strategy, utilizing ELAN-style feature concatenation to merge the diverse representations (as illustrated in subfigure (b)). This mechanism promotes optimal information flow and ensures the network learns more discriminative and potent features. The internal design of the DepthBottleneckUniv2 unit–detailed in subfigure (c)–is constructed from depthwise and pointwise convolutions, specifically tailored to strike an optimal balance between processing efficiency and representational capacity.

Accurate rice leaf disease detection requires robust feature representation that can withstand severe environmental variations and effectively link localized lesions to their global context. The baseline C2PSA attention module, relying primarily on local convolutional operations, is deficient in capturing long-range dependencies and global spatial context. To overcome this limitation, we propose the MDTA module. MDTA is designed to enhance feature robustness by integrating a Transformer-inspired attention mechanism with multi-scale dilated convolutions. The core architectural choice is the strategic use of dilation to achieve an exponentially expanded receptive field without introducing extra parameters, enabling the capture of non-local relationships essential for complex scene understanding and improved contextual awareness across feature scales. The main network structure of MDTA is illustrated in Fig. 6.

The MDTA module integrates a distinct two-phase attention mechanism for comprehensive feature map refinement, consisting of the Channel Group Attention (CGA) and Window Multi-head Attention (WMA) components. These stages operate in sequence to augment mutually supportive information. The CGA mechanism, taking cues from efficient group-based interaction methodologies, prioritizes strengthening inter-channel relationships through the factorization of the input tensor into Query (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Q}$$\end{document}), Key (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{K}$$\end{document}), and Value (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{V}$$\end{document}) representations. This channel-based weighting is applied post-LayerNormalization–a step vital for training stability–to enable the network to prioritize the most diagnostically significant feature channels. Following this, the WMA module executes localized attention calculation within designated, non-overlapping spatial windows. This structural parallel, resembling the window attention utilized in the Swin-Transformer, is essential for efficiently extracting fine-grained spatial relationships and localized structure patterns while preserving computational parsimony.

The foundational concept for the CGA design originates from the efficient group interaction methodology presented in Restormer40. The process initiates by using a convolutional layer to partition the input tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}$$\end{document} into its query (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Q}$$\end{document}), key (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{K}$$\end{document}), and value (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{V}$$\end{document}) components. The resulting attention map is derived by first conducting the scaled dot-product attention operation between the queries and keys, and then performing a weighted summation over the values. Crucially, the full input tensor undergoes normalization using LayerNorm prior to this, a step that stabilizes the feature distribution’s mean and variance, thereby accelerating the model’s convergence during training.

The input feature map is systematically divided into distinct, non-overlapping spatial windows. Following the sequential application of CGA and WMA, the MDTA module employs a Feed-Forward Network (FFN) to further process the attended features. This FFN is a critical architectural choice, as it is constructed using dilated convolutions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Conv}_{r}(\cdot )$$\end{document} (also known as atrous convolutions) rather than standard convolutions. This design deepens the feature abstraction while actively maintaining and leveraging the large, non-local receptive field established by the Transformer-style attention. The inclusion of the dilation rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r> 1$$\end{document} ensures that the FFN actively maintains and leverages the large, non-local receptive field established by the preceding attention mechanisms. This structural coupling of attention and dilated FFN enhances the overall robustness and feature discrimination capability of the MDTA module.

The MDTA module’s architecture provides a significant advantage in capturing the fine-grained and heterogeneous characteristics of rice leaf lesions. The combination of CGA and WMA creates a powerful dual-attention mechanism, allowing the network to selectively focus on crucial diagnostic features across both the channel and spatial dimensions. Crucially, the integration of dilated convolutions ensures the model possesses the requisite global contextual awareness, improving its capability to handle the spatial heterogeneity and occlusions prevalent in field environments. This ability to integrate long-range context and local fine-grained details is the key technical contributor to the model’s superior robustness and accuracy compared to the baseline C2PSA.

The Stem unit within the Lo-Head detection structure incorporates two sequential \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 3$$\end{document} group convolutional layers, which effectively curb both the parameter count and the overall computational load. Group convolution operates by segmenting the input channels into several distinct groups, with each group undergoing independent convolution. The resulting feature maps are then aggregated via concatenation along the channel dimension to construct the complete feature representation. This architecture not only reduces computational intensity but also retains essential spatial and semantic fidelity within the features. Consequently, this module is ideally suited for deployment on resource-constrained embedded platforms, facilitating real-time operational capacity in agricultural settings.

Lo-Head ensures effective feature integration through the deployment of two specialized branches: the cv2 module, which is dedicated to extracting bounding box regression features, and the cv3 module, responsible for generating class probability features. During the feature combination stage, the outputs from these two branches are concatenated channel-wise, allowing the network to synthesize spatial and semantic information into a unified representation. This holistic fusion process substantially improves the model’s decision-making capacity when identifying lesion regions, especially under challenging field conditions. By capitalizing on the complementary nature of localization and classification features, the model achieves superior detection performance for rice leaf diseases.

Cross-validation strategy

To ensure the statistical robustness and generalization capability of our final reported results, we employed a 5-Fold Cross-Validation strategy. The entire Rice Leaf Disease Detection Dataset was initially and comprehensively partitioned into five non-overlapping subsets (folds) of equal size. This strict partitioning guarantees that every data point is used exactly once for validation. The model was subsequently trained and evaluated five times, with each of the five folds serving exactly once as the validation set while the remaining four folds were collectively used for training (Fig. 8).

The final performance metrics for the proposed model (as reported in Table 5, Figs.9 and 10) are presented as the mean and standard deviation across the results obtained from the five independent validation runs. This rigorous methodology confirms that the reported performance is not merely an artifact of a single, fortuitous train-test split, significantly boosting the credibility of our findings.

To provide rigorous statistical validation for the high performance achieved by the proposed framework and to address the necessity for a scientific assessment of model stability, a 5-fold cross-validation (5-CV) experiment was conducted. This approach responds directly to the need for reporting variance and robust performance metrics, as is standard practice for high-accuracy models in complex detection tasks.

The 5-CV procedure involved partitioning the entire dataset into five distinct folds, ensuring that each fold was used exactly once as the validation set while the remaining four folds were used for training. Crucially, this experiment was performed on the full dataset without the application of data augmentation, thus isolating the model’s intrinsic ability to generalize across different subsets of the original data. The primary objective was to report the mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}) and standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}) of key performance indicators, providing a comprehensive measure of the model’s reliability and its sensitivity to the specific training/validation split. To stringently validate the cross-species transferability and superior generalization capability of the proposed framework, an extensive transferability assessment was executed. Leveraging the foundational knowledge acquired from the primary rice leaf dataset, the model’s effectiveness was rigorously tested against two distinct and publicly accessible external datasets sourced from the Roboflow platform: Potato Leaf Diseases and Tomato Leaf Diseases. The Potato set, comprising \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {7,172}$$\end{document} images, features two major diseases (Early-Blight and Late-Blight). Discussion

This study successfully developed a novel methodology for rice leaf disease detection, building upon an enhanced iteration of the YOLOv11 architecture. The ultimate framework incorporates three principal architectural innovations: the Multi-Branch Large-Kernel Fusion Depthwise (MLFD) module, the Multi-Scale Dilated Transformer Attention (MDTA) module, and an efficient, lightweight Lo-Head detection head. These integrated components collectively overcome several critical challenges intrinsic to conventional detection techniques, including the complexities of field backgrounds, the difficulty of identifying minute lesions, and the need for computational efficiency in resource-constrained deployment environments.

Analysis of core technical contributions and performance

The successful integration of the proposed modules is key to the framework’s superior performance and balanced efficiency, as evidenced by the ablation study (Table 4).Feature Augmentation (MLFD and MDTA): The MLFD component utilizes its multi-branch topology and large convolutional kernels to enrich diverse feature representation and contextual information aggregation, a process critical for identifying disease characteristics under varying environmental conditions. Complementing this, the MDTA module further enhances the model’s capacity to adapt to complex, multi-scale disease manifestations by integrating attention mechanisms for effective fine-grained feature detection. This potent synergy is evidenced by the enhanced feature extraction capabilities observed in the module variants.Efficiency Optimization (Lo-Head): The Lo-Head component substantially reduces the computational burden of the model, achieving a highly optimized model size (5.6 MB) and maintaining a competitive inference speed (106.48 FPS). This lightweight design ensures the scheme is suitable for real-time applications where computational resources are limited.Overall Performance Gain: Experimental results show that the proposed framework network exhibits superior performance in terms of precision, recall, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {mAP}$$\end{document} compared to baseline models and other state-of-the-art detectors (Table 6). Especially in the critical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {mAP}@0.5:0.95$$\end{document} metric, the model improves from the baseline’s 0.6085 to 0.6262. The modest increase in model size (0.4 MB) for a significant performance gain validates the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed component fusion.

Our current work utilized Grad-CAM to generate heatmaps (Fig. 14), visually confirming that the proposed framework focuses on relevant lesion regions, thereby providing a foundational level of model explanation.

However, the insights provided by gradient-based methods like Grad-CAM are localized and may not fully explain the model’s global decision-making process.
