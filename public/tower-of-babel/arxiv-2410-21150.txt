Edge multiscale finite element methods for semilinear parabolic problems with heterogeneous coefficients
Leonardo A. Poveda, Shubin Fu, Guanglian Li, Eric T. Chung

Abstract
We develop a new spatial semidiscrete multiscale method based upon the edge multiscale methods to solve semilinear parabolic problems with heterogeneous coefficients and smooth initial data. This method allows for a cheap spatial discretization, which fails to resolve the spatial heterogeneity but maintains satisfactory accuracy independent of the heterogeneity. This is achieved by simultaneously constructing a steady-state multiscale ansatz space with certain approximation properties for the evolving solution and the initial data. The approximation properties of the multiscale ansatz space are derived using local-global splitting. A fully discrete scheme is analyzed using a first-order explicit exponential Euler scheme. We derive the error estimates in the L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} -norm and energy norm under the regularity assumptions for the semilinear term. The convergence rates depend on the coarse grid size and the level parameter. Finally, extensive numerical experiments are carried out to validate the efficiency of the proposed method.



1
Introduction

We consider in this paper the efficient multiscale method for a semilinear parabolic equation with heterogeneous coefficient
\kappa
in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\mathrm{\nabla\cdot}(\kappa\nabla u)+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla u=R(\mathrm{x},u)

with certain boundary conditions and initial conditions. Here,
R(\mathrm{x},u)
denotes the reaction term that can be nonlinear. This model type occurs in many applications, such as the phase field problems in the Allen-Cahn equation, which describes phase transition and separation
(
Allen and Cahn, 1979
)
. Other examples include the Ginszburg-Landau equation that studies the behavior of superconductivity
(
Caliari and Cassini, 2024
)
, the Navier-Stokes equations in highly heterogeneous media, which are used for the study of fluid dynamics and multiphase problems
(
Muljadi et al., 2015
)
.

Classical numerical methods, such as finite element methods, resolve the coefficient’s heterogeneity to obtain a solution with sufficient accuracy, resulting in huge computational complexity.
During the last few decades, many multiscale model reduction techniques have been developed and analyzed to make computational complexity independent of the multiple scales, such as multiscale finite element methods
(
Hou and Wu, 1997
)
, multiscale variational methods
(
Hughes et al., 1998
)
, heterogeneous multiscale methods
(
E and Engquist, 2003
)
, multiscale mortar methods
(
Arbogast et al., 2007
)
, localized orthogonal decomposition methods
(
Mlqvist and Peterseim, 2014
)
, generalized multiscale finite element methods
(
Efendiev et al., 2013
;
Chung et al., 2023
)
. These methods have demonstrated extremely satisfactory numerical results for various problems and have become increasingly popular. In this work, we will consider Edge Multiscale Methods (EMsFEM), which was initially proposed in
Li (2019)
;
Fu et al. (2019)
and has been applied to several linear problems with heterogeneous coefficients
Fu et al. (2021)
;
Li and Hu (2021)
;
Fu et al. (2025)
;
Li (2024)
. Its main idea is to first construct a local splitting of the solution in a sequence of overlapping subdomains, and then establish the global splitting by means of the partition of unity functions
Melenk and Babuška (1996)
. In this manner, the global error estimate is determined by the local error estimate in each subdomain. Consequently, determining the local error estimate is a crucial task in the analysis. Its proof is inspired by the transposition method that provides
a priori
estimate in weighted
L^{2}
-norm of the homogeneous elliptic problem with nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary data in weighted
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm
Lions and Magenes (1972)
. The semilinearity makes the local splitting nontrivial, and the multiscale basis functions potentially rely on the nonlinear term. However, upon assuming the boundedness of the first-order partial derivative of the nonlinear term with respect to the solution, we prove that the multiscale space is independent of the semilinear term, and thus, one multiscale space is sufficient for all time steps. The local error estimate is presented in Lemma
3.3
. Based upon this, we can derive an error estimate for the elliptic projection in Lemma
3.4
, then a standard error estimate yields the semidiscrete error estimate in Theorem
3.5
.

Moreover, standard numerical approaches such as implicit methods have been proposed and analyzed for temporal discretization, which are attractive due to their unconditional stability. However, they involve solving nonlinear equations at each time step and thus are computationally expensive. In contrast, Exponential integrators are robust, with the linear part handled exactly and the non-linear part explicitly
(
Hochbruck and Ostermann, 2010
;
Caliari et al., 2024
;
Contreras et al., 2023
;
Poveda et al., 2024
)
, and we explore their performance for this semilinear parabolic problem. We present a rigorous convergence analysis of the proposed method under smooth initial data and certain regularity of the semilinear term in Theorem
4.2
, validated by extensive numerical experiments.

The outline of the paper is as follows. Section
2
is dedicated to the problem of the model and its spatial discretization. The multiscale edge space is constructed in Section
3
. Section
3.3
presents the construction of the hierarchical bases. In Section
4
, we present the explicit exponential method. Under appropriate assumptions of the exact solution and the nonlinear reaction term, we show the fully discrete error analysis of the proposed approach. Numerical experiments are provided in Section
5
. Finally, conclusions and final comments are drawn in Section
6
.

3
The edge multiscale method

We present in this section the edge multiscale method to solve (
2.1
) for
d=2
only for simplicity. The generalization of this method to
d=3
can be followed similarly. The main idea of this approach is that first, we generate a coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}_{H}
on the spatial domain
D
, which cannot resolve the microscale feature in the coefficient
\kappa
and then introduce a sequence of overlapping subdomains using
\mathcal{T}_{H}
. Note that standard numerical methods cannot produce a reasonable solution under this coarse mesh
\mathcal{T}_{H}
due to the pre-asymptotic effect. Second, we derive a local splitting of the solution on each subdomain by a summation of a local bubble function and a local Harmonic extension function. In contrast, the former can be solved locally, and the latter can be defined over the internal edges of the coarse mesh
\mathcal{F}_{H}
. This local splitting induces naturally a global splitting using the partition of unity functions. Third, we propose to use hierarchical bases up to level
\ell
as the ansatz space for the solution on
\mathcal{F}_{H}
. The most crucial component in the theoretical analysis is to transfer the approximation properties over the coarse skeleton
\mathcal{F}_{H}
to the internal of each subdomain without extra regularity assumptions on the solution. This was first proved in

(
Li, 2019
, Appendix A)
by defining a very weak solution in each subdomain and establishing an
a priori
estimate inspired by the transposition method in
Lions and Magenes (1972)
.

3.2
Local-global splitting

Note that the solution
u
of problem (
2.1
) satisfies

{\cal L}_{i}u:=\mathrm{\nabla\cdot}(\kappa\nabla u)+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla u=R(\cdot,u)-\partial_{t}u,\quad\mbox{in }\omega_{i},

which can be split into the summation of two parts,

u|_{\omega_{i}}=u^{i,\mathrm{I}}+u^{i,\mathrm{II}}.

(3.3)

Here, each component
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}
and
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}
are given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\cal L}_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{I}}&=R(\cdot,u)-\partial_{t}u&&\mbox{in }\omega_{i},\\
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}&=0&&\mbox{on }\partial\omega_{i},\end{aligned}\right.

(3.4)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\cal L}_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}&=0&&\mbox{in }\omega_{i},\\
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}&=u&&\mbox{on }\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D\\
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}&=0&&\mbox{on }\partial\omega_{i}\cap\partial D.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.5)

Observe that
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}
contains the local information that can be solved locally, and
u^{i,\mathrm{II}}
encodes the global information over the inner coarse skeleton
{\cal F}_{H}
. This local splitting induces a global splitting of
u
by means of the partition of unity functions,

u=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\right)u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u|_{\omega_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}(u^{i,\mathrm{I}}+u^{i,\mathrm{II}}):=u^{\mathrm{I}}+u^{\mathrm{II}},

(3.6)

where

u^{\mathrm{I}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\text{ and }u^{\mathrm{II}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}.

(3.7)

3.3
Hierarchical bases

Next, we introduce the hierarchical bases on the unit interval
I:=[0,1]
, which facilitates hierarchically splitting the space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)
.

Let the level parameter and the mesh size be
\ell
and
h_{\ell}=:2^{-\ell}
with
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the grid points on level
\ell
are

x_{\ell,j}=j\times h_{\ell},\quad 0\leq j\leq 2^{\ell}.

We can define the basis functions on level
\ell
by

\psi_{\ell,j}(x):\begin{cases}1-\left|\frac{x}{h_{\ell}-j}\right|,&\quad\mbox{if }x\in[(j-1)h_{\ell},(j+1)h_{\ell}]\cap[0,1],\\
0,&\quad\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Define the set on each level
\ell
by

B_{\ell}:=\left.\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\begin{cases}j=1,\dots,2^{\ell}-1,j\mbox{ is odd},&\quad\mbox{if }\ell>0\\
j=0,1,&\quad\mbox{if }\ell=0\end{cases}\right\}\right\}.

The subspace of level
\ell
is

\mathrm{W}_{\ell}:=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{\ell,j}:j\in B_{\ell}\}.

We denote
V_{\ell}
as the subspace in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)
up to level
\ell
, which is defined by the direct sum of subspaces

\mathrm{V}_{\ell}:=\bigoplus_{m\leq\ell}\mathrm{W}_{m}.

Consequently, this yields the hierarchical structure of the subspace
\mathrm{V}_{\ell}
, namely

\mathrm{V}_{0}\subset\mathrm{V}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathrm{V}_{\ell}\subset\mathrm{V}_{\ell+1}\cdots.

Furthermore, the following hierarchical decomposition of the space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)
holds

\mathrm{L}^{2}(I)=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\bigoplus_{m\leq\ell}\mathrm{W}_{m}.

Note that one can derive the hierarchical decomposition of the space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(I^{d-1})
for
d>1
through the tensor product, which is denoted as
\mathrm{V}_{\ell}^{\otimes^{d-1}}
. We will use the subspace
\mathrm{V}_{\ell}
to approximate the exact solution

{\cal F}_{H}
.

Next, we present the approximation properties of the hierarchical space
\mathrm{V}_{\ell}
, which can be derived from standard

-projection error

Proposition 3.1

(Approximation properties of the hierarchical space
\mathrm{V}_{\ell}
)
.

Let
d=2,3
,
s\in(0,1]
and let
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(I^{d-1})\to\mathrm{V}_{\ell}^{\otimes^{d-1}}
be

-projection for each level
\ell\geq 0
, then there holds,

\displaystyle\|v-\mathcal{I}_{\ell}v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(I^{d-1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-s\ell}|v|_{\mathrm{H}^{s}(I^{d-1})}\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(I^{d-1}).

(3.8)

Here,
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{H}^{s}(I^{d-1})}
denotes the Gagliardo seminorm in the fractional Sobolev space (or Slobodeskii space)

\displaystyle|v|^{2}_{\mathrm{H}^{s}(I^{d-1})}:=\int_{I^{d-1}}\int_{I^{d-1}}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d-1+2s}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

The corresponding full norm is denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s}(I^{d-1})}
.

3.4
Edge multiscale ansatz space

Next, we introduce the construction of the edge multiscale ansatz space.

First, we define the linear space over the inner boundary of each coarse neighborhood
\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D
. Let the level parameter
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, and let
\Gamma_{i}^{j}
with
j=1,\cdots,m
be a partition of
\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D
with no mutual intersection,
i.e.
,
\cup_{j=1}^{m}\overline{\Gamma_{i}^{j}}=\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D
and
\Gamma_{i}^{j}\cap\Gamma_{i}^{j^{\prime}}=\emptyset
if
j\neq j^{\prime}
. Here,
m\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of internal edges for
\omega_{i}
. Furthermore, we denote
\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell}^{j}\subset C(\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D)
as the linear space spanned by hierarchical bases up to level
\ell
on each coarse edge
\Gamma_{i}^{j}
, which is continuous over
\partial\omega_{i}
and vanishes on
\partial D
. The local edge space
\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell}
defined over
\partial\omega_{i}
is the smallest linear space having
\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell}^{j}
as a subspace. Let
\{\psi_{\ell,i}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}
and

\{\mathrm{x}_{\ell,i}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}
be the nodal basis functions and the associated nodal points for
\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell}
. We can represent the local edge space
V_{i,\ell}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell}:=\text{span}\left\{\psi_{\ell,i}^{j}:1\leq j\leq n_{i}\right\}.

(3.9)

Next, we introduce the local multiscale space over each coarse neighborhood
\omega_{i}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell}):=\text{span}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\psi_{\ell,i}^{j}):1\leq j\leq n_{i}\right\}.

(3.10)

Here,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\psi_{\ell,i}^{j}):=v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\omega_{i})
is the solution to the following local problem,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{i}v&:=-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla v)+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla v=0&&\mbox{in }\omega_{i},\\
v&=\psi_{\ell,i}^{j}&&\mbox{on }\partial\omega_{i}\backslash\partial D\\
v&=0&&\mbox{on }\partial\omega_{i}\cap\partial D.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.11)

By this construction, the dimension of
\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\mathrm{V}_{i,\ell})
equals

. In practice, a number of

Finally, the edge multiscale ansatz space is defined by the Partition of Unity

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}:=\text{span}\left\{\chi_{i}\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{i}(\psi_{\ell,i}^{j}):\,\,1\leq i\leq N\text{ and }1\leq j\leq n_{i}\right\}\subset\mathrm{V}.

{\cal P}_{i,\ell}v:={\cal L}_{i}^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{i,\ell}v|_{\partial\omega_{i}}).

Observe that any

can be expressed by

v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}v|_{\omega_{i}}.

Then the global interpolation
{\cal P}_{\ell}
of level
\ell
is defined by means of the local projection,

{\cal P}_{\ell}v:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}({\cal P}_{i,\ell}v),\quad\quad\mbox{for each }v\in\mathrm{V}.

(3.14)

In this section, we are concerned with deriving the convergence rates of (

;
Fu et al. (2025)

The following result is a slight modification of Lemma 5.1 from

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
u^{\mathrm{I}}
be defined in (
3.7
). Then we have

\displaystyle\|u^{\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}+H\|\nabla u^{\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}\leq H^{2}\left(\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(D)}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\right).

(3.15a)

Proof.

First, we multiply (
3.4
) by
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}
and integrate over
\omega_{i}
to obtain

\int_{\omega_{i}}\kappa|\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\omega_{i}}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\right)u^{i,\mathrm{I}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{x}}=\int_{\omega_{i}}(R(\mathrm{x},u)-\partial_{t}u)u^{i,\mathrm{I}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{x}},

using the fact
u^{i,\mathrm{I}}|_{\partial\omega_{i}}=0
, we reduce the expression above to

\int_{\omega_{i}}\kappa|\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{x}}=\int_{\omega_{i}}(R(\mathrm{x},u)-\partial_{t}u)u^{i,\mathrm{I}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{x}}.

Combining the Schwarz inequality and the Friedrich’s inequality, we arrive at

\|\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(\omega_{i})}^{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}H}{\pi}\|R(\cdot,u)-\partial_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega_{i})}\|\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(\omega_{i})}.

Since
R(\mathrm{x},0)=0
, together with Assumption
2.1
and Lemma
2.3
, we obtain the following

\|\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(\omega_{i})}\leq CH\left(\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\omega_{i})}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega_{i})}\right).

For the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
estimate for the local function, we have again used the Friedrichs-Poincaré inequality.

\|u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega_{i})}\preceq H\|\nabla u^{i,\mathrm{I}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(\omega_{i})}\preceq H^{2}\left(\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\omega_{i})}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega_{i})}\right).

Finally, combining equation (
3.7
) and the overlap condition (
3.1
) gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d=2,3

(3.16b)

Here,

\displaystyle\eta(H,\ell,s):=\|\kappa\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\cal F}_{H})}^{1/2}2^{-s\ell}H^{s-1/2}.

(3.17)

Proof.

First, the property of the partition of unity of

By definition,

\displaystyle\|u^{i,\mathrm{II}}-{\cal P}_{i,\ell}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\omega_{i})}\leq C2^{-s\ell}H^{s}\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s+1/2}(\omega_{i})}.

Following the rescaling argument from

\displaystyle\|u^{i,\mathrm{II}}-{\cal P}_{i,\ell}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\widetilde{\kappa}}(\omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\|\chi_{i}\nabla(u^{i,\mathrm{II}}-{\cal P}_{i,\ell}u^{i,\mathrm{II}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(\omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C2^{-s\ell}H^{s-1/2}\|\kappa\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\partial\omega_{i})}^{1/2}\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{s+1/2}(\omega_{i})}.

\begin{split}\|\nabla(u^{i,\mathrm{II}}-{\cal P}_{i,\ell}u^{i,\mathrm{II}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}&\leq\sqrt{2C_{\mathrm{ov}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{\mathrm{G}}^{2}H^{-2}\|u^{i,\mathrm{II}}-{\cal P}_{i,\ell}u^{i,\mathrm{II}}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\widetilde{\kappa}}(\omega_{i})}\right.\\

4
Full discretization

In this section, we introduce the fully discrete scheme of (
2.5
) based on exponential integration techniques
Hochbruck et al. (1998)
;
Hochbruck and Ostermann (2005)
;
Hochbruck and Ostermann (2010)
. The effectiveness of these approaches is attributed to the computation of the matrix-valued exponential integrator. Their prominence has increased in the past decade due to advancements in numerical linear algebra and efficient algorithms for computing these functions
(
Caliari et al., 2024
;
Caliari and Cassini, 2024
)
. Consequently, these methods have become significantly more competitive, offering an alternative to traditional temporal discretizations.

Let
0=t^{0}<t^{1}<\cdots<t^{N_{t}-1}<t^{N_{t}}=T
be a uniform partition of the interval
[0,T]
, with the time-step size given by
\Delta t=\max_{n=1,\dots,N_{t}}\{t^{n}-t^{n-1}\}
, where
N_{t}
is a positive integer. We recall exponential integration techniques relevant to the proposed multiscale finite element method.

First, we define the discrete solution
u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t)
evolving in time. Since that
A(\cdot,\cdot)
is bounded and coercive over
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\times\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}
, then Riesz’s representation Theorem implies the existence of a bounded linear operator
L_{\mathrm{ms}}:\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\to\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{\prime}
, satisfying

\displaystyle A(u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell},v_{\mathrm{ms},\ell})=\langle L_{\mathrm{ms}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell},v_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\rangle\quad\quad\mbox{for each }v_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\in\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}.

(4.1)

Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the duality paring.
Then we can rewrite (
2.5
) in a semidiscrete formulation in
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}+L_{\mathrm{ms}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{ms}}R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell})

\displaystyle\quad t\in(0,T],

(4.2)

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(0)

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{ms}}u_{0}

where
P_{\mathrm{ms}}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)\to\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}
denotes
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-projection.

Following
Henry (1981)
, the
\mathrm{V}
-elliptic property of
L_{\mathrm{ms}}
implies that
-L_{\mathrm{ms}}
is a sectorial on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)
(uniformly in
h
),
i.e.
, there exist
C>0
and
\theta\in(\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,\pi)
such that

\|(\lambda I+L_{\mathrm{ms}})^{-1}\|_{L(\mathrm{L}^{2}(D),\mathrm{L}^{2}(D))}\leq\frac{C}{|\lambda|},\quad\lambda\in S_{\theta},

where
S_{\theta}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\lambda=\rho e^{i\phi},\rho>0,0\leq\phi\leq\theta\}
. The discrete operator
-L_{\mathrm{ms}}
is the infinitesimal of the exponential operator (or semigroup)
e^{-tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}
on
\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}
such that

e^{-tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma}e^{t\lambda}(\lambda I+L_{\mathrm{ms}})^{-1}d\lambda,\quad t>0,

where
\Gamma
represents a path surrounding the spectrum of
-L_{\mathrm{ms}}
. According to Duhamel’s principle, we ensure the existence and uniqueness of the solution, which is given by the integral form

u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n+1})=e^{-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n})+\int_{0}^{\Delta t}e^{-(\Delta t-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}P_{\mathrm{ms}}R\left(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n}+s)\right)ds.

(4.3)

Approximating
u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n}+s)
by
u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n})
for
s\in[0,\Delta t]

(
Hochbruck and Ostermann, 2010
)
, we can obtain a fully discrete multiscale method for Problem (
3.13
) as follows: for
n=0,\dots,N_{t}-1
,

u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n+1}=e^{-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}+L_{\mathrm{ms}}^{-1}(I-e^{-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}})P_{\mathrm{ms}}R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}).

Together with the fact
e^{-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}=-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}\phi_{1}(-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}})+I
with
\phi_{1}(z)=\frac{e^{z}-I}{z}
,
this implies

u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n+1}=u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}+\Delta t\phi_{1}(-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}})\left(P_{\mathrm{ms}}R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n})-L_{\mathrm{ms}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}\right),

(4.4)

which is the so-called exponential Euler scheme. Note that this scheme involves huge computational complexity when applied to (
2.1
) directly using standard numerical methods such as the finite element method, due to the existence of heterogeneous coefficient
\kappa
and the evaluation of the exponential-like matrix function
\phi_{1}(\cdot)
. In sharp contrast, our proposed edge multiscale method (
3.13
) offers a significant advantage. It reduces the size of the exponential-like matrix function
\phi_{1}(\cdot)
, thereby making exponential integration feasible even for such challenging multiscale problems.

The following result will be mainly used in this work.

Proposition 4.1

(Properties of the semigroup
Henry, 1981
)
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
and any given parameter
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
, then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|L_{\mathrm{ms}}^{\alpha}e^{-tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\leq Ct^{-\alpha},\quad\mbox{for }t>0,

\displaystyle\|L_{\mathrm{ms}}^{-\gamma}(I-e^{-tL_{\mathrm{ms}}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\leq Ct^{\gamma},\quad\mbox{for }t\geq 0.

4.1
Error estimates

This subsection concerns the convergence study of fully discrete scheme (
4.4
).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\ell
be a non-negative integer and
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{H}}^{2}(D)
. Let
u\in\mathrm{V}
and
u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}
be the solution to Problems (
2.1
) and (
4.4
), respectively. When the stepping size
\Delta t
is sufficiently small, there holds

\displaystyle\|u(t^{n})-u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\preceq\eta(H,\ell,1)+H+\Delta t.

\displaystyle\|\nabla({u(t^{n})-u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}

\displaystyle\preceq\eta(H,\ell,1)+H+\Delta t\left(t^{n}\right)^{-1/2}.

Here, the hidden constant may depend on
R,\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and
T
.

Proof.

The proof to the first estimate is similar to
(
Tambue, 2016
, proof to Theorem 4.1)
. To obtain the second estimate,
an application of the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\|\nabla({u(t^{n})-u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla(u(t^{n})-u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n}))\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}+\|\nabla(u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n})-u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}

\displaystyle:=I_{1}+I_{2}.

From Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
2.2
, we get an upper bound for the first term
I_{1}
. Thus, our focus is on the second term
I_{2}
. Note that the integral form of the semidiscrete solution
u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}
(
4.3
) and its approximation
u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}
(
4.4
) indicate,

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n})

\displaystyle=e^{-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t^{k}}^{t^{k+1}}e^{-(t^{n}-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}P_{\mathrm{ms}}R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(s))\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}

\displaystyle=e^{-\Delta tL_{\mathrm{ms}}}u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(0)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t^{k}}^{t^{k+1}}e^{-(t^{n}-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}P_{\mathrm{ms}}R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{k})\mathrm{d}s.

Hence, subtracting the second equation from the first one, we obtain the temporal discretization error,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}:=u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(t^{n})-u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t^{k}}^{t^{k+1}}e^{-(t^{n}-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}P_{\mathrm{ms}}\left(R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(s))-R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{k})\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Then, together with the triangle inequality and the equivalence of norms by (
2.6
) and (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla v_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}=\|L_{\mathrm{ms}}^{1/2}v_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)},\quad\quad\mbox{for each }v_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}\in\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{ms},\ell},

this leads to

\displaystyle\|\nabla\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t^{k}}^{t^{k+1}}\left\|\nabla e^{-(t^{n}-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}P_{\mathrm{ms}}\left(R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(s))-R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{k})\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t^{k}}^{t^{k+1}}\left\|e^{-(t^{n}-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}L_{\mathrm{ms}}^{1/2}P_{\mathrm{ms}}\left(R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(s))-R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{k})\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\mathrm{d}s.

Since
P_{\mathrm{ms}}
is the
L^{2}
-projection, we get therefore,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\kappa}(D)}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t^{k}}^{t^{k+1}}\left\|e^{-(t^{n}-s)L_{\mathrm{ms}}}L_{\mathrm{ms}}^{1/2}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\left\|R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}(s))-R(\cdot,u_{\mathrm{ms},\ell}^{k})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(D)}\mathrm{d}s.

The remaining proof is similar to
(
Tambue, 2016
, proof to Theorem 4.1)
using Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
2.2
.
∎

6
Conclusions

We have developed an efficient multiscale method to treat the semilinear parabolic problems with a heterogeneous coefficient. The approximation properties of the corresponding steady-state multiscale ansatz space are derived. Since the nonlinear term lies in the reaction term, we can develop a time-independent multiscale ansatz space in this work. The case with the quasilinear term has many important applications,
e.g.
, in the metamaterial, and is more computationally involved and probably requires time-dependent, multiscale ansatz space. We plan to investigate it in the future.
