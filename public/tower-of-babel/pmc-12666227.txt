Cardiopulmonary exercise test variations in patients with a Fontan circulation: impact of fenestration patency and associations with clinical outcomes
Authors: Laura M Ortega-Aviles, Sebastian D Santos-Patarroyo, Derek N Opp, Frank Cetta, Alexander C Egbe, William R Miranda, Thomas G Allison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with single-ventricle physiology are often palliated with the Fontan operation, which may involve the creation of a fenestration. We aimed to evaluate differences in cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) performance between patients with fenestrated and non-fenestrated Fontan circulation.MethodsPatients with a Fontan circulation referred to CPET between 2006 and 2024 were included and were categorised based on their fenestration status at the time of CPET. Logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the impact of fenestration on peak oxygen consumption (VO2), and Cox proportional hazard to evaluate the impact of fenestration on cardiovascular outcomes (death, heart transplant and Fontan-related hospitalisation).ResultsOf the 184 patients, 141 were classified as non-fenestrated and 43 as fenestrated. The minute ventilation-carbon dioxide production (VE/VCO2 slope) was higher in the fenestrated (36.9±7.9) versus (33.2±6.2; p=0.009) in the non-fenestrated group. There was no significant difference in predicted VO2 between groups (non-fenestrated 51.9%±14.4 vs 51.3%±15.6; p=0.7). Resting oxygen saturation was higher in the non-fenestrated group (93%±4.7 compared with fenestrated group 90.1%±5; p<0.001). Fontan fenestration was not significantly associated with the composite outcome; older patient age at the time of the CPET, cirrhosis and ventricular ejection fraction <40% were significantly associated with higher risk, while higher resting systolic blood pressure, left ventricular morphology and higher predicted peak VO2 were protective.ConclusionsThe increased hypoxia and reduced ventilatory efficiency associated with Fontan fenestration offset any potential benefits, resulting in similar exercise performance between the groups. Fontan fenestration was not significantly associated with cardiovascular outcomes.Keywords: Fontan Procedure, HEART FAILURE, Congenital Abnormalities

Introduction

The Fontan operation is the standard surgical palliation for patients born with single ventricle physiology.1 2 In specific scenarios, creation of a fenestration between the inferior vena cava and the common atrium is implemented to improve cardiac output and limit the postoperative increase in systemic venous pressure.1 3 4 However, subsequent abnormalities in systemic arterial oxygenation due to the persistent right-to-left shunt and the need for late fenestration closure as a strategy to decrease cyanosis and minimise complications have been described.5 6

Despite novel advancements in the management of patients with single ventricle physiology, most patients with Fontan circulation still exhibit limited exercise capacity.7,9 This is often evaluated through the peak oxygen consumption (VO2) during the cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET).7,9 This tool functions as an objective measure of aerobic capacity and serves in the prognostic assessment of patients with cardiovascular diseases.7,9 Regardless of the physiological advantages conferred by fenestration, its effect on exercise capacity and its long-term impact remain controversial.6 To address this knowledge gap, our study aimed to: (1) evaluate differences in cardiopulmonary exercise performance between fenestrated and non-fenestrated patients with Fontan circulation, including peak VO2, minute ventilation required to eliminate carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2) slope and oxygen saturation (SpO2); (2) assess the impact of a patent fenestration on long-term clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study population

In this retrospective single-centre study, we identified paediatric (<18 years of age) and adult patients with a Fontan circulation who performed a clinically indicated CPET at Mayo Clinic Rochester, Minnesota, from October 2006 to March 2024. We included patients with complete CPET data and transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) data for evaluating the fenestration patency at the time of the CPET. Time difference between echocardiography reports and CPET studies was 1 day (IQR 0–2 days). Individuals who underwent CPET studies conducted after heart transplant, young children (<6 years of age) or adults with severe physical limitations unable to perform a symptom-limited test were excluded from our analysis.

Clinical data including baseline CHD diagnosis and age of the patient, age of the current Fontan circulation, sex, body mass index (BMI), use of heart rate lowering drugs (eg, beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, digitalis) and presence of pacemaker were abstracted from the electronic medical record. Fenestration status at the time of initial CPET was included, along with type of Fontan circulation (classic atrial pulmonary, lateral tunnel and extracardiac) and morphology of systemic ventricle (left, right or common). Ventricular ejection fraction (EF) was included in the data set and significant compromise of the A-V valve. The clinical record was also reviewed to record whether there was evidence of diabetes, systemic hypertension, protein-losing enteropathy (PLE), Fontan-associated liver disease (FALD) or cirrhosis at the time of the CPET.

The first CPET conducted within the study period was considered the baseline encounter, and clinical data collected within 1 month of the CPET date were used to define the baseline clinical characteristics of the cohort. We defined a primary composite outcome of all-cause mortality, heart transplant and any clinical Fontan-related complication requiring hospitalisation, defined as any event related to the Fontan circulation (eg, PLE, plastic bronchitis, FALD, thromboembolism, significant arrhythmia or acute heart failure). Only patients who provided research authorisation for the use of medical records for research purposes were included.

Cardiopulmonary exercise test

Patients underwent symptom-limited CPET on a motor-driven treadmill (Quintin, Seattle, Washington, USA) using an incremental protocol (accelerated Naughton protocol), which consisted of 2 min stages, with 2 metabolic equivalents increase per stage or until maximal perceived exhaustion.10 11 A limited number of patients had orthopaedic limitations requiring use of a semirecumbent bicycle protocol with 25 W increments every 2 min.10 11 A PhD exercise physiologist and a cardiologist reviewed every test.10 11 Variables were calculated with BreezeSuite Software provided by MGC Diagnostics.10 11 The methodology for performing CPET in patients with high-risk cardiovascular diseases at Mayo Clinic is described elsewhere.10 11

Peak VO2 was expressed in mL/kg/min and was determined as the highest 30-second average of VO2 during exercise. Due to sex and age-related differences, it was also expressed as a percentage of predicted, calculated using the reference standards from the Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database reports for adult and Cooper for paediatric patients.12 13 ‘Minimal ectopy’ was defined as premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) or premature atrial contractions (PACs) not greater than 5 per minute during the CPET. ‘Normal rhythm’ was defined as sinus rhythm with normally sensed ventricular conduction.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as means±SD; categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Clinical characteristics and CPET parameters were compared based on fenestration status at the time of the CPET. Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables; t-test was used to compare continuous variables. Linear regression was used to evaluate the correlation between clinical variables including predicted peak VO2, VE/VCO2 slope and peak systemic SpO2. Covariates were selected based on clinical importance and potential impact on Fontan exercise physiology. The variables with p<0.10 on univariable analysis were used to create the multivariable model. Results were presented as parameter values with SEs. Survival curves were created using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the cumulative incidence of composite outcome across groups was compared using a log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the effect of clinical variables on composite outcome. Findings were reported using HRs with 95% CIs and p values, adjusted for previously mentioned methodology. Note there were too few deaths and heart transplants in our cohort to make mortality, time-dependent transplant-adjusted mortality, or death or transplant the primary outcome. All statistical analyses were performed with SAS Studio, V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and BlueSky Statistics V.10 (BlueSky Statistics, Chicago, Illinois, USA), and GraphPad Prism V.9 was used to generate figures.

Additional analyses of other CPET variables revealed that patent fenestration was associated with poorer ventilatory efficiency, reflected by a higher VE/VCO2 slope. An extracardiac conduit, presence of a pacemaker and older age of the Fontan graft were also associated with higher VE/VCO2 slope. Patent fenestration was also associated with lower peak SpO2 during exercise, as was a higher VE/VCO2 slope, while higher rest SpO2 and minimal ectopy (likely reflecting a more stable signal to the forehead pulse oximeter) were associated with a higher peak SpO2, as also presented in table 3.

Prognostic implication of Fontan fenestration

During a median follow-up of 3.6 years (IQR 1.8–5.6), the composite outcome demonstrated no difference between fenestrated versus non-fenestrated patients (log-rank test; p=0.094), as illustrated in figure 2. Results of the multivariable Cox regression analyses evaluating the composite outcome are presented in table 4. Factors predicting a higher risk of the primary outcome included older patient age at the time of the CPET, cirrhosis and EF ≤40%, while higher resting systolic blood pressure (SBP), left versus right or common ventricle and percent predicted peak VO2 were protective factors. We might comment quickly here that the six clinical factors predicting outcomes make good physiological sense. Older age is universally associated with higher mortality, as is reduced function of the systemic ventricle. Cirrhosis represents a serious consequence of restricted Fontan flow. Having a morphologic left ventricle, exhibiting a higher resting blood pressure and achieving a higher peak VO2 on the CPET similarly to all points to better cardiac performance.

In addition to the composite outcome, 48 patients (26%) underwent an elective procedure related to their Fontan circulation (not including heart transplant). Most common was repair of the graft (n=26, 14%), mostly by placement of a stent during right heart catheterisation. Another 10 patients (5%) underwent catheter-based therapy of other parts of the cardiopulmonary circulation, and 7 (4%) required a surgical approach. Finally, five patients (3%) underwent treatment for a rhythm issue, including pacemaker implantation or revision and ablation for tachyarrhythmias. Though elective, these procedures further emphasise the medical burden of Fontan circulation.

Discussion

In this study, we compared exercise performance and clinical outcomes between patients with a Fontan circulation with and without fenestration. The main findings are: (1) ventilatory variables showed significant differences when a fenestration was presented, likely secondary to V-Q mismatch from increased right-to-left shunt; (2) percent predicted peak VO2 was similar between groups; (3) Fontan fenestration was not significantly associated with poorer clinical outcomes, though results must be interpreted with caution due to the large number of potential predictive variables in relation to the number of clinical outcomes and potential referral bias.

Exercise intolerance and reduced functional aerobic capacity are nearly universal in patients with a Fontan circulation compared with individuals with a biventricular circulation.7,914 This is confirmed in the present cohort. Peak VO2 averaged only 53±14% predicted, and no patient achieved 100% of predicted VO2, the best performance being 93%. Test effort was good with peak respiratory exchange ratio=1.13 ± 0.14, and 172 patients (93%) gave a peak rating of perceived exertion of 17 (‘very hard’) or higher on the standard 6–20 Borg scale.

Peak heart rate and heart rate reserve were impaired, not surprising as 120 (65%) patients were either taking a heart rate lowering drug or had a pacemaker. Only 48 patients (26%) achieved at least 85% of predicted peak heart rate. Rest and exercise SBPs were also low. 35 patients (19%) had a resting SBP less than 100 mm Hg, and 41 patients (22%) had peak SBP less than 120 mm Hg. Medications and the haemodynamics of Fontan circulation ± having a right or common systemic ventricle likely contributed to the low blood pressures.

Both atrial and ventricular ectopy were common during exercise, though 165 (90%) of patients had what was defined as minimal ectopy during the CPET. Though intermittent atrial or ventricular pacing was common (44 patients: 24%—had a pacemaker), and 3 patients were in junctional rhythm and 4 in atrial fibrillation, 142 (77%) of patients had normal rhythm on the CPET.

The current study demonstrates no differences in % predicted peak VO2 between fenestrated and non-fenestrated groups. A theoretical increase in cardiac output in fenestration from an increase in ventricular preload from right-to-left shunting occurs at the expense of a decrease in both resting and exercise oxygen saturation.14,16 Although cardiac output is not directly measured in routine CPET, peak VO2 is a reasonable surrogate, though one must exercise caution since fenestrated patients have lower arterial PO2 levels.14,16 Differences in ventilatory variables suggest that Fontan fenestration or other shunts (eg, veno-venous collateral, pulmonary arterio-venous malformation) may be an important determinant by restricting the augmentation of pulmonary blood flow, reflected by an increase in dead space ventilation.16 17

Our analysis revealed that approximately 25% of patients with a fenestrated Fontan circulation exhibited resting SpO2 greater than 94%, while approximately 25% of the non-fenestrated group had resting SpO2 values less than 94%; suggesting the presence of other sources of right-to-left shunting, which can act as fenestration. Therefore, the presence of fenestration may not completely capture the clinical impact of desaturation in this group of patients.16 17

Our findings are in agreement with the observations of Heal et al18 who reported no differences in VO2 but reported greater systemic desaturation at peak exercise in patients with patent fenestration.18 However, their data reported no differences on VE/VCO2 slope. The differences between Heal et al18 and the current study may be due to their participants’ younger age.18 Conversely, Jain et al9 found that VE/VCO2 values were elevated in adult patients with a Fontan circulation but demonstrated no significant association with pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP). Their findings highlighted that lower peak VO2 and heart rate were associated with worse Fontan haemodynamics.9 Moreover, Meadows et al19 demonstrated an increased SpO2 level and improvement in ventilatory variables after elective fenestration closure in patients with Fontan palliation, but no improvements in exercise capacity were observed.19

To the contrary, observations from Mays et al20 showed that children with fenestrated Fontan who underwent transcatheter closure exhibited significant improvement in aerobic capacity and SpO2 following fenestration closure.20 Discrepancies observed by Meadows et al19 and Mays et al20 from our findings may be attributed to differences in study design, as they both were prospective studies limited to patients with Fontan circulation referred for elective fenestration closure.19 20 Therefore, they were evaluating short-term physiological changes following fenestration closure.19 20

The current analysis revealed that higher BMI, cirrhosis and ventricular EF ≤40% were associated with lower predicted peak VO2; in contrast, left ventricular morphology and higher rest SpO2% were associated with higher predicted peak VO2. This aligns with the previous Mayo report, where exercise test data on 323 patients with Fontan circulation showed that lower BMI, higher SpO2 and lower Fontan pressures were correlated for higher predicted peak VO2.7 Several studies have highlighted the prognostic value of peak VO2 in patients with univentricular physiology.7,914 A prior study from Mayo demonstrated that lower predicted peak VO2 is associated with an increased risk of death or heart transplant.7 Conversely, Diller et al21 reported that CPET variables were associated with heart failure hospitalisation but not with other outcomes (eg, death or heart transplant).21 Importantly, these results remained unchanged, even after excluding patients with fenestration and other shunts.21 Differences between the findings of Diller et al21 and the current study may be attributed to the concomitant prevalence of clinical comorbidities among our study’s participants.21 One important difference is the higher proportion of patients having a history of atriopulmonary anastomosis, which has been demonstrated to have increased long-term morbidity and mortality compared with other strategies of Fontan completion that are nearly universally done in the current era.22 Our study demonstrated that patients with a Fontan circulation with left ventricular morphology were associated with lower risk of developing the composite outcome. These findings are in contrast with Atz et al.23 and Schwartz et al24 where they reported no significant association between type of ventricular morphology and outcomes such as death or transplant.23 24 Likewise, fenestration patency, older age, cirrhosis and a low ventricular EF were predictors of adverse outcomes. The association of these variables may be reflecting chronic haemodynamic compromise in addition to systemic venous congestion related to the Fontan circulation.714,16 A 10 year follow-up study demonstrated no difference in survival between fenestrated and non-fenestrated patients, although those with fenestration had higher incidence of thromboembolic events.25 A retrospective study on paediatric patients with Fontan circulation found that fenestrated patients exhibited a higher incidence of Fontan-related comorbidities, such as PLE, plastic bronchitis, heart transplant or death.26 Our study would suggest that exercise performance is not significantly impacted by the presence of a fenestration or not.

Future directions

Our study supports the need for a comprehensive assessment in patients with Fontan circulation.16 In specific patient populations, fenestration closure may be a consideration in those with limited exercise capacity attributed to chronic desaturation or if the patient is deemed high risk for thromboembolic events.5 6 27 Emerging interventional evaluations, such as Fontan stenting, have demonstrated improvements in functional class in symptomatic patients; nevertheless, no change was found on exercise capacity.27 Other strategies, like pulmonary vasodilators, have shown some promise in improvement on some metrics of haemodynamic responses to exercise.16 28 Likewise, diuretics can relieve symptoms in those with established diastolic dysfunction.16 Other non-invasive interventions, such as cardiac rehabilitation programmes, offer an improvement in exercise capacity and quality of life, suggesting a cost-effective approach.29

Study strengths and limitations

In terms of strengths, all CPETs were conducted in a single centre by highly experienced personnel. CPET studies are part of the standard procedure for patient evaluation in the Mayo Congenital Heart Center and not ordered selectively by individual providers. Most tests (159, 86%) were performed on a treadmill according to one standard protocol. In the few cases where a different exercise modality or protocol was used, standard equations were used to calculate predicted values for peak exercise to account for differences in age, sex and type of exercise modality.12 13

We accept that there are certain limitations. This retrospective cohort referred to CPET prone to referral bias. However, a full cohort of CPET results for all patients who underwent Fontan circulation at a particular institution over a given time frame would be exceedingly hard to accomplish. The relatively small sample size of our study, combined with the heterogenous clinical characteristics of our participants, may contribute to type II error. Another methodological weakness was the use of a composite outcome; however, due to the limited number of deaths in the current cohort, we were unable to perform a time-dependent, transplant-adjusted analysis of mortality. (Simply combining transplant—to some degree an elective procedure—with death, obviously non-elective, as has been done in other studies, has its own theoretical limitations.) Despite these limitations, the study allowed a comprehensive comparison of the exercise performance and outcomes related to the fenestration status in patients with a Fontan circulation.

Conclusion

Exercise limitation is universal among patients with Fontan circulation. Although Fontan fenestration may offer some haemodynamic advantages, the percentage predicted peak VO2 was similar between fenestrated and non-fenestrated patients. In multivariable analysis, Fontan fenestration patency was not significantly associated with clinical outcomes.
