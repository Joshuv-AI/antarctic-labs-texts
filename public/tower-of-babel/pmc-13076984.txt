Open micro-valley chip reveals long-term viscosity-induced glioblastoma cellular invasion states
Authors: Haotian Jiang, Chao Xu, Cheng Zeng, Xun Liu, Lan Deng, Yi Jian, Chuan Shao, Gang Zhang, Yigang Shen, Yaxiaer Yalikun, Ming Li, Tao Tang, Nan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The glioblastoma (GBM) microenvironment exhibits elevated viscosity and spatial confinement that strongly influence tumor invasion, yet these mechanical features are difficult to reproduce in open experimental systems. We developed an open two-layer microfluidic membrane that enables precise control of migration onset and real-time visualization of cellular mechano-adaptation. The detachable cap confines a defined droplet, while the ring-shaped micro-valley topography provides localized confinement that deforms nuclei and activates YAP signaling, recapitulating the mechanical stress experienced by invading tumor cells at the GBM invasive front. Using this platform, we found that long-term culture in a 7.1 cP viscous medium produced smaller, more deformable cells with enhanced migration through confined regions, revealing clear cell-type-dependent differences in motility and adaptive capacity. Transcriptomic analysis further showed that U-251 cells underwent mesenchymal-like reprogramming and gained greater invasive potential, whereas LN-229 cells exhibited limited transcriptional change despite similar structural remodeling. These findings demonstrate that this open microfluidic platform bridges biophysical modeling and cellular mechanobiology, enabling direct investigation of viscosity-driven adaptation in GBM.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in regulating tumor cell invasion and phenotypic plasticity1,2. Among its biophysical factors, extracellular viscosity3,4, a key determinant of fluid resistance and cellular motility, remains poorly characterized. There is evidence that suggests that elevated viscosity can remodel the cytoskeleton and activate YAP-mediated mechanotransduction pathways, thereby potentially promoting invasive state transitions5,6. This phenomenon might be particularly relevant in glioblastoma (GBM)7, an aggressive and lethal brain tumor exhibiting a unique viscosity gradient: a high-viscosity invasive periphery (~6.6 ± 0.3 cP)8 surrounding a fluid-like necrotic core (~1.6 ± 0.2 cP)9,10. This inverted profile, which is distinct from that of epithelial carcinomas, which typically display a more viscous core11, requires GBM cells to invade through regions of increasing fluidic resistance. However, it remains unclear how such a rising viscous environment influences GBM cell locomotion and invasive dynamics.

To visualize GBM cell locomotion in viscous environments, we developed an open-top microfluidic membrane platform that enables systematic investigation of tumor cell motility under precisely controlled extracellular conditions. In conventional closed microfluidic systems12–14, cells migrate through narrow microchannels with dimensions comparable to the nuclear size, which often restricts long-term observation due to limited nutrient and oxygen exchange and can substantially alter cell behavior15–17. Moreover, migration within enclosed channels is dominated by geometric confinement18,19 and wall-induced shear forces20, complicating efforts to isolate the specific contribution of extracellular viscosity.

To address these limitations, open microfluidic configurations have been introduced to reduce confounding physical constraints and allow interrogation of individual biophysical cues21,22. Building on this concept, the present open membrane design minimizes flow-induced artifacts23,24, avoids channel clogging, and permits direct access for long-term culture, high-resolution imaging, and post hoc analyses such as immunostaining without the need for pre-labeling or genetic modification. This platform, therefore, provides a suitable framework to examine how extracellular fluid resistance regulates nuclear deformation, cytoskeletal remodeling, and YAP activation, advancing our understanding of viscosity-mediated invasion dynamics in the glioblastoma microenvironment.

To investigate how elevated extracellular viscosity influences GBM invasion, we modeled the high-viscosity microenvironment characteristic of the invasive periphery. Human GBM cell lines (U-251 and LN-229) were long-term adapted to viscous culture conditions for approximately one month, allowing the establishment of stable, viscosity-dependent phenotypes. This preconditioning period facilitated the examination of sustained changes in cellular motility, cytoskeletal organization, and mechanotransduction signaling under controlled biophysical conditions. Utilizing our open-top microfluidic platform, we directly visualized nuclear deformation and migration through micropillar arrays and systematically compared cell behaviors between low- and high-viscosity environments. By integrating long-term culture, live-cell imaging, post-migration immunofluorescence, and RNA-sequencing analyses, we aimed to determine whether increased extracellular viscosity can drive an invasive phenotypic transition in GBM cells.

Material and Methods

Cell culture and reagents

The human glioblastoma cell lines U-251 and LN-229 (Cell Resource Center, Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, high-glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Methylcellulose (MC; MedChem Express, CAS No. 9004-67-5) was dissolved in DMEM to prepare a 0.3% (w/v) stock solution, as previously reported3,5,6. Briefly, DMEM was equilibrated to 37 °C prior to use, after which MC powder was gradually introduced under continuous stirring to minimize aggregation. The mixture was maintained under constant agitation at 37 °C for 6–8 h until a homogeneous viscous solution was obtained. The solution was then cooled to room temperature and sterilized by filtration through a 0.45 µm membrane under aseptic conditions. The sterile MC solution was stored at 4 °C in the dark for up to one month and gently mixed and equilibrated to 37 °C before use. MC was selected because it is a non-ionic, biocompatible, and optically transparent polysaccharide that allows precise modulation of medium viscosity with minimal impact on ionic strength or osmolarity, thereby preserving cellular physiology under open microfluidic conditions. The suitability of MC for generating controlled viscous microenvironments has been well established in previous studies5,6.

To mimic the viscosity of the glioblastoma (GBM) peripheral microenvironment, viscous culture media were prepared by diluting the MC stock solution to the desired concentration. Medium without MC (0% MC) served as the control with a viscosity of 0.9 cP, whereas 0.3% MC produced a viscosity of 7.1 cP, comparable to that reported for the GBM invasive periphery. Culture media were refreshed every three days. For viscosity pre-conditioning, cells were cultured continuously in viscous medium for one month prior to subsequent experiments.

Microfluidic membrane fabrication

The microfluidic membrane consisting of two PDMS layers was designed and fabricated using standard soft lithography protocols, where the bottom PDMS layer has a microcolumn ring array with a minimum column gap of 2 or 4 μm. The PDMS base was mixed thoroughly with curing agent at a ratio of 10:1 (w/w), and then poured over a SU-8 master containing microchannel features fabricated by standard photolithographic methods. The PDMS solution was degassed in a vacuum chamber for 1 hour, cured at 70 ° C for 2 hours, and then peeled off from the mold. Using a biopsy punch with a diameter of 1.5 mm to punch holes in the center of the top PDMS membrane. Cleaning the two PDMS layers with tape and sterilizing them using a UV irradiation device for 30 minutes. Then, treat them with oxygen plasma and add fibronectin (50 μg/mL, 20 μL per sheet) to promote cell adhesion.

The two-layer device maintained mechanical integrity throughout culture. The top cap and bottom micro-structured membrane were held together and remained aligned by conformal contact, providing sufficient stability to confine the medium droplet without observable layer shift or deformation. In typical experiments, the cap was maintained for approximately 8 h before removal, and no mechanical instability was detected during this period or during extended culture of up to 48 h, as shown in Fig. S1 (Supplementary Materials).

Immunofluorescence staining

After completion of cell culture, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were permeabilized with Triton X-100 (Beyotime, P0096) and blocked with immunostaining blocking solution (Beyotime, P0102) to minimize non-specific binding. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies against target proteins (e.g., YAP1, Paxillin) diluted according to the manufacturers’ recommendations and left at 4 °C overnight. Following PBS washes, specimens were incubated with appropriate fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted microscope equipped with confocal capabilities.

RNA sequencing

U-251 and LN-229 cells were cultured for one month in media with viscosities of 0.9 cP or 7.1 cP. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Life Technologies, USA), quantified, and quality-assessed. After rRNA depletion, sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were processed with in-house Perl scripts, and Q20/Q30 scores, GC content, and duplication levels were evaluated. High-quality clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and transcript abundance was quantified as FPKM values. Differential gene expression between viscosity conditions was analyzed with DESeq2, applying Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Genes with adjusted P < 0.01 and fold change ≥2 were defined as differentially expressed. The processed gene expression matrix used for downstream analyses is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Western blot assays

Prechilled RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to prepare total protein. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) for 1 h and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane via ice-cold electroblotting for 2 h. The membrane was blocked with 10% non-fat milk in TBST at room temperature for 2 h, followed by washing and incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Enhanced chemiluminescence was used for visualization.

Transwell assay

Transwell assays were used to estimate GBM cell invasive abilities with chambers that have 0.8 μm micropores (Corning Life Sciences, NY, USA). The cells were seeded in the upper chambers of each insert, which contained a Matrigel-coated membrane (Corning Life Sciences), and cultured with FBS-free medium. Medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers of the 24-well plates. After incubation for 18 h at 37 °C, cells that passed through the filter were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. These cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution and counted under a microscope in 5 randomly selected fields.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Image analysis was performed using custom Python scripts developed to enhance salient features and enable quantitative evaluation. For images acquired from the microfluidic chips, single-cell segmentation was first carried out with the open-source deep-learning model Cellpose25,26, and subsequent feature extraction and quantification were performed using custom scripts. The used code is available in Supplementary Materials. Unless otherwise stated, each experiment was repeated at least three times using biologically independent samples. Statistical tests and criteria for significance are described in the figure legends or in the Statistical Analysis section.

Results

Two-layer microfluidic membrane enabling spatially restricted initiation

The open microfluidic design is illustrated in Fig. 1a. The system consists of a detachable top cap and a bottom microstructured membrane that together confine a defined droplet of cell suspension. The bottom layer incorporates a ring-shaped micropillar array (height = 5 μm; minimum spacing = 2 or 4 μm), providing mechanical support and a quasi–three-dimensional microenvironment for adherent cells. The top cap contains a 1.5 mm seeding aperture that aligns with the inner circle of the micropillar ring on the bottom membrane, enabling accurate droplet placement and controlled initiation of migration.Fig. 1Two-layer microfluidic membrane enabling cap-controlled migration onset.a Schematic of the two-layer membrane and operation procedure. In experiments, a 10 μL cell droplet was confined within the central 1.5 mm region for 8 h, maintaining cells in a compact, non-migratory circular configuration and defining a reproducible starting point for migration. To minimize evaporation during this period, the device was placed in a Petri dish containing 1 mL sterile deionized water without submerging the chip, and no medium exchange was required. After 8 h, the confined droplet was gently removed by micropipette, the top cap was detached, and the surrounding reservoir was replenished with 3 mL complete culture medium. Upon cap removal, the microenvironment transitioned to an open state, allowing cells to spread radially into the surrounding micropillar valleys and initiate migration from a well-defined onset position.

As shown in Fig. 1b, fluorescence imaging demonstrates that capped cultures maintain a well-organized circular distribution within the inner ring region, whereas uncapped cultures exhibit a heterogeneous distribution, with many cells adhering to micropillar tops rather than migrating within the inter-pillar micro-valleys. Consistently, Fig. S1 shows that when the top cap is maintained for 48 h, cells remain densely confined within the capped region, with migration into the surrounding microstructured area effectively suppressed. Together, these results indicate that the cap-controlled transition provides a simple and reproducible means to synchronize the onset of cell migration in an open microfluidic platform, enabling precise temporal control without the need for external pumps, valves, or continuous flow.

Following cap removal, the microstructured membrane was submerged in culture medium and maintained for approximately 5 days. As shown in Fig. 2b, fluorescence imaging of F-actin (green) and nuclei (DAPI, blue) revealed distinct migration behaviors. In the 2 μm gap arrays, cells preferentially migrated over the pillar tops rather than through the narrow interspaces, avoiding excessive nuclear deformation caused by strong physical confinement. In contrast, in the 4 μm gap arrays, cells were able to migrate between the pillars, showing elongated morphologies and moderate nuclear deformation within the micro-valleys. Segmentation of nuclei and cytoplasm using Cellpose enabled quantitative evaluation of cell spreading and nuclear shape across these different topographies.

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 2c) confirmed that increasing the gap from 2 μm to 4 μm significantly enhanced inter-valley migration, as reflected by larger spreading areas and increased nuclear eccentricity (p < 0.01, two-tailed Student’s t-test). These results indicate that micro-valley width critically regulates migration strategy. Narrow gaps impose excessive confinement that promotes a mechano-protective over-pillar migration mode, likely adopted to preserve nuclear integrity and cell survival. In contrast, wider gaps provide a mechanically accessible pathway that permits between-pillar migration with tolerable nuclear deformation. Although the system is geometrically open, the microstructured topography imposes sufficient mechanical constraints to regulate both cell body deformation and nuclear mechanics.

Fluorescence imaging revealed that cells located inside the micro-valley region exhibited reduced spreading and elevated nuclear deformation, accompanied by pronounced YAP enrichment in the nucleus compared with cells positioned before (Pre-) or beyond (Post-) the confined zone. Nuclear localization of YAP has been reported to correlate with nuclear compression27,28. Quantitative segmentation using Cellpose confirmed these trends, showing smaller cell surface areas, higher nuclear eccentricity, and increased nuclear YAP intensity inside the micro-valleys relative to surrounding regions (Fig. 3b). Statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA demonstrated that these changes were significant (p < 0.05).

These results suggest that, even without a closed chamber, the open microfluidic topography imposes spatial confinement that alters nuclear shape and activates YAP signaling.

Viscous pre-conditioning remodels glioblastoma cell mechanics and migration capability

To determine whether prolonged exposure to elevated extracellular viscosity induces stable phenotypic adaptation, U-251 and LN-229 glioblastoma cells were pre-cultured for one month in 7.1 cP medium prior to re-introduction into the open microfluidic platform (Fig. 4a). Following cap removal, both cell lines initially formed confined circular monolayers, and radial migration was monitored at Day 0, Day 3, and Day 5. Compared with non-conditioned controls, viscosity-adapted cells displayed significantly greater migration distance and expansion rate, particularly under high-viscosity (7.1 cP) conditions. While elevated viscosity imposed substantial drag and reduced motility in control cells, pre-conditioned cells retained and even enhanced their migratory capacity in the same environment. Fluorescence imaging of F-actin and nuclei (Figs. 4b, S2) revealed that pre-conditioned cells actively navigated the micro-valley topographies with more polarized morphologies, extended protrusions, and efficient traversal through micropillar gaps. In contrast, control cells spread extensively but less directionally, often filling the inter-pillar spaces and exhibiting nuclear obstruction within confined regions. Notably, in pre-conditioned populations, smaller and more compact cells tended to lead migration fronts, creating space for larger trailing cells. These cells spread only as needed to advance through gaps rather than expanding randomly. Quantitative analyses (Fig. 4c) confirmed that these pre-conditioned leading cells possessed significantly smaller cell and nuclear areas compared with controls, while nuclear eccentricity remained unchanged. This size reduction likely decreases mechanical resistance, facilitating smoother entry and passage through the micro-valleys. Measurements confirmed that osmotic pressure remained consistent across different media viscosities (Fig. S3), thereby excluding osmolarity as a driver of the observed morphological changes.

To further validate that long-term viscous pre-conditioning enhances glioblastoma invasiveness, we performed a conventional Transwell invasion assay as an independent and complementary approach. LN-229 and U-251 cells pre-conditioned in viscous medium (7.1 cP) for one month exhibited a significantly higher number of invaded cells compared with control cells cultured under standard conditions (Fig. 4d). Quantitative analysis of invaded cell density confirmed a marked increase in invasive capacity for both cell lines following viscous pre-conditioning. These results are consistent with the enhanced migration dynamics observed in the open micro-valley platform and support the conclusion that prolonged exposure to elevated extracellular viscosity promotes a more invasive cellular phenotype.

Supplementary quantification (Fig. S4) comparing cells before entry and inside the micro-valley region showed no further size reduction, indicating that the enhanced migration arises from pre-established cytoskeletal and mechanical adaptations rather than acute deformation during entry. Together, these findings demonstrate that chronic exposure to viscous stress induces a smaller, more deformable, and invasive phenotype in glioblastoma cells, enabling rapid migration through topographically confined yet open microenvironments.

Fluorescence imaging of F-actin, paxillin, and YAP revealed similar cytoskeletal organization, focal adhesion distribution, and YAP localization in both cell types, suggesting broadly comparable structural adaptation to long-term viscous stress (Fig. 5a), consistent with previous reports3–6. Quantitative analyses showed only mild increases in focal adhesion length and YAP nuclear localization after pre-conditioning, with more evident changes in U-251 than in LN-229 (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, these findings indicate that both cell types exhibit mechano-sensitive adaptation, though the degree of responsiveness varies, reflecting distinct intrinsic mechano-sensitivities.

Despite these similar morphological responses, transcriptomic profiling revealed cell-type-specific molecular adaptations. t-SNE visualization showed clear segregation between control and pre-conditioned U-251 cells, whereas LN-229 cells exhibited substantial variation even among parallel groups (Fig. 5c). Subtype enrichment29,30 and gene expression heatmaps demonstrated that pre-conditioned U-251 cells shifted toward a mesenchymal-like transcriptional state characterized by elevated motility and extracellular matrix remodeling (Fig. 5d, e). In contrast, LN-229 cells largely retained a proneural or classical signature, reflecting limited transcriptional plasticity. Bulk RNA-seq further indicated that the pre-conditioning response was heterogeneous, with only subsets of cells exhibiting transcriptional activation, explaining the broader variation observed in LN-229.

Together, these results show that viscosity-induced adaptation occurs at both structural and molecular levels but is strongly dependent on the intrinsic cell state. U-251 cells exhibit greater transcriptional plasticity and acquire a mesenchymal-like, invasion-prone phenotype, whereas LN-229 cells remain transcriptionally stable despite similar structural adaptation. This divergence likely underlies the stronger migration enhancement observed in U-251 cells, even though both exhibit reinforced cytoskeletal and focal adhesion organization indicative of increased migratory potential.

To corroborate the transcriptomic trends at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed for representative metabolic and mesenchymal-associated markers (Fig. 5f). Consistent with the RNA-seq results, viscosity pre-conditioned U-251 cells exhibited increased expression of N-cadherin, vimentin, CD44, and HK2, whereas LN-229 cells showed comparatively modest changes. Notably, returning pre-conditioned cells to normal-viscosity medium (0.9 cP) did not substantially alter protein expression levels, indicating that long-term viscous pre-conditioning induces a stable, non-transient phenotypic adaptation rather than an acute reversible response.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that localized mechanical confinement generated by open micro-valley topographies is sufficient to induce nuclear deformation and activate YAP signaling, even in the absence of fully enclosed microchannels. These results indicate that mechanotransduction during glioblastoma invasion can arise from spatially patterned surface interactions rather than global geometric confinement. Moreover, the enhanced migration observed after long-term viscous pre-conditioning suggests that extracellular viscosity functions as a sustained biophysical cue capable of stabilizing mechanically adaptive invasion states in a cell-type-dependent manner.

Compared with previously reported open microfluidic invasion platforms22, which typically rely on static architectures where migration initiates immediately upon seeding or occurs under permanent confinement, the present cap-controlled, two-layer PDMS membrane enables reversible confinement and precise temporal synchronization of migration onset without enclosed channels or continuous flow. This design allows invasion to initiate from a well-defined spatial and temporal origin while preserving an open culture environment. When combined with micro-valley topographies that permit gap-dependent migration mode switching and long-term viscous pre-conditioning, the platform provides experimental capabilities that are not readily accessible with existing open or pillar-based systems.

The platform also demonstrates practical robustness and adaptability. Individual chips can be placed in standard 6-well plates (Fig. S5, Supplementary Materials), enabling parallelized operation and moderate- to high-throughput assays using conventional cell culture and imaging workflows. Each chip accommodates a single cell droplet, ensuring experimental consistency across wells, and the device geometry is compatible with standard well plates and Petri dishes.

Importantly, validation using primary GBM-3 cells (Fig. S6, Supplementary Materials) confirms that the platform supports invasion of patient-derived cells, which exhibit distinct migration morphologies compared with established GBM cell lines, underscoring the adaptability of the system to different cell types.

Several limitations of the current design should be noted. The detachable top cap relies on manual alignment with the bottom microstructured membrane, which may introduce slight positional offsets and minor circumferential differences in the timing of initial cell–topography contact after cap removal. Although migration remains radially organized from a common onset region and quantitative analyses are not affected, such variability may influence early local cell–structure interactions. In addition, while nuclear confinement predominantly occurs within the micro-valley regions, cytoskeletal structures can partially engage with the tops of adjacent micropillars, indicating that nuclear and cytoskeletal confinement are not always fully co-localized. Future refinements incorporating guided alignment features or optimized pillar geometries could further improve spatial uniformity and mechanical specificity.

Finally, the generality of the observed migration behaviors should be interpreted in the context of confinement geometry. Continuous microchannels provide strong contact guidance and impose persistent directional cues21,31, whereas the separated micropillar structures in the present open micro-valley design do not function as leading tracks. Consequently, cells are free to explore multiple migration routes, resulting in more heterogeneous and adaptive migration states. The mechanoprotective over-the-pillar migration observed at narrow gaps is therefore a geometry-specific behavior enabled by the open, low-ceiling environment and would not occur in fully enclosed 3D channels or matrix pores. Nevertheless, the viscosity-induced nuclear deformation, YAP activation, and invasion enhancement observed here reflect fundamental mechanosensitive responses to localized confinement and extracellular fluid resistance. Extending these findings to alternative confinement architecture represents an important direction for future studies.

Conclusion

In this work, we developed an open microfluidic membrane enabling controlled migration onset and direct observation of mechanosensitive behavior. The micro-valley topography imposed confinement sufficient to deform nuclei and activate YAP signaling. On the chip, we noticed that long-term viscous pre-conditioning produced smaller, more motile cells, with U-251 showing stronger mesenchymal-like adaptation than LN-229. These results reveal cell-type-dependent mechano-adaptation driven by viscosity-induced stress. Beyond mechanistic studies, the open microfluidic membrane design, together with its compatibility with long-term culture and real-time observation, suggests potential applicability for drug screening. In particular, the platform could be adapted to evaluate anti-invasive or mechanotransduction-targeting therapies under viscosity-modulated microenvironments that more closely reflect in vivo conditions.
