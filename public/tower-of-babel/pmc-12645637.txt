Weighing the impact of evidence in orthopaedic trauma registries: a systematic review of national and international registry data
Authors: Akhshay John George, Neil Ashwood, Andrew P Dekker, Paul Wilson, Adrain Crawford, Arnov Mukherjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWorldwide, there are 15 established trauma databases collecting data to better understand the patterns of injury and effectiveness of interventions, but interpreting the information is hampered by the varied approaches. The aim of this study was to determine the impact, practices, evolution in design and methods of analysis that are standardised and comparable within registries.DesignA thematic analysis using a narrative synthesis was used to develop threads for future study and identify the limitations in current practice.Data sourcesPubMed, Ovid, Scopus and EMBASE were searched on the 2 October 2025. At the same time, ChatGPT (Open artificial intelligence) identified the most cited articles in orthopaedic trauma registries, cross-referencing lists as a form of triangulation to aid in snowballing references.Eligibility criteriaThe review included 174 papers from trials and observational studies that analyse data from established trauma orthopaedic registries published in English.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers used standardised methods to search, screen and code included studies assessing the papers using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist to assess the observational and cohort studies and the Downs and Black Quality Criteria for the remaining papers.ResultsOutcome measures other than mortality are poorly collected, undermining the value of registries. Trauma patients reported considerable impairment 6 and 12 months after injury. Association between level of trauma care and mortality is evident for major trauma populations, but does not hold for general trauma populations. Level I trauma centres produce improved survival in severely injured, but this association could not be proven for non-fatal outcomes in general populations. There is a disparity between resources allocated to save and salvage cases within major trauma units, and hence, routine cases often have lower priority and delayed care.ConclusionsThere is a need to develop a standardised and reproducible method to evaluate data quality in trauma registries. National performance guidelines and trauma centre audits are integral steps towards optimum results. Routine collection of postinjury outcome measures beyond mortality will enable the development of quality improvement metrics that better reflect patient outcomes.Keywords: REGISTRIES, TRAUMA MANAGEMENT, Health Services

Introduction

Clinical databases are either administrative in nature, such as hospital episode statistics (HES) database (England) or insurance claims data, such as Medicare (USA) managing reimbursement following intervention, rather than clinical registries.1 Orthopaedic registers began in the 1970s, notably in Sweden, with the purpose of collecting information on patients, implants and procedures that would help monitor and enable changes to improve the outcomes of arthroplasties.2,4 Trauma registries began in the USA in 1989 with a similar aim to capture the outcomes and monitor care of the injured. Those patients affected by trauma have pain, disability and loss of income due to an inability to return to work.5 In 2010, the UK National Audit Office estimated economic losses associated with major trauma in England between 3.3 and 5.3 billion Great British Pounds (GBP).6 7 This led to the introduction of major trauma centres within the UK, that is estimated to have saved an additional 1600 lives by 2018.7 Implementing trauma registry systems improves survival after injury.7 8

Registries not only provide information on patient-reported outcomes (PROs) but also serve to improve surgeon’s individual performance through continuous feedback and creation of best practice guidelines. While national performance guidelines and trauma centre audits are integral steps in driving improved outcomes following injury, it is less clear which specific processes contribute to improvement in trauma care.8 Trauma registries help identify patient subgroups, specific injuries and procedures that result in high morbidity or mortality.5 This has led to the development of specific pathways to facilitate care, quality improvement initiatives and comparative research.4 5 The aim of this study is to understand the value of trauma registry data and the advantages of prospectively monitoring trauma patients and pathways of care. Organisation of these registries, methods used, approach and dissemination of results are presented.

Methods

Eligible studies were searched on Medline, EMBASE, OVID and SCOPUS databases using the algorithm (exp “Outcome Registries” OR (Trauma Registry* OR Trauma Outcomes Registry*) AND orthopaedics decision making AND indications AND/OR procedures AND/OR outcomes) with no specific date limit. This search was undertaken in 2024 and rerun on the 2 October 2025 to maximise sensitivity in identifying relevant articles using criteria mentioned in table 1. A similar search was undertaken by asking ChatGPT (Open artificial intelligence (AI)) to identify most cited articles in orthopaedic trauma registries and cross-referencing lists as a form of triangulation as well as snowballing references. The list was then assessed to look for papers where results from the registries were reported and analysed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Criteria

The first search identified 763 papers for the assessment of the full text after abstract and title screening for orthopaedic trauma registries; from these, 387 papers were excluded in view of the study design not providing robust clinical data with final screening. 174 studies were available for analysis. The number of studies excluded at each stage, including the title, abstract and full-text screening, was recorded systematically with reasons (eg, no outcome data). Two researchers participated in both title, abstract and full-text screening stages (APD and NA), and where there was uncertainty in study eligibility, a consensus decision was made by at least two screeners. Endnote captured and managed the references at each stage of screening. The researchers extracted the study design, intervention characteristics and details of outcomes for the most influential papers. An internet-based search of international trauma registry websites was also included looking at the data dictionaries used and annual reports to analyse the learning and recommendations for future developments to ensure relevance and effectiveness. A file, with the precise and full search strategy for all databases, registers and websites have been included as a supplementary document (online supplemental file 1). Assessing the quality of the papers used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist9 to assess the observational and cohort studies and the Downs and Black Quality Criteria for the remaining papers.10

Results

Data linkage and quality

The purpose of trauma registries in comparison with administrative databases is in the assessment of the quality of care and diagnostic/therapeutic interventions,11 for trauma patients requiring meticulous data quality provided in part through triangulation with different data sources.12 13 Figure 1 displays the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart.12

The strength of nationalised registries comes in part from the inclusivity of the population studied where all are entered onto the database enabling data completeness.13 Registries in the UK must abide by the Data Protection Act 1998, which creates rights for patients whose data are stored, and responsibilities for persons who store, process or transmit it.14

A drawback of registries is that data collected must contain relevant detail to ensure appropriate decision-making for now and future. Regular changes to the dataset make analysis difficult and time-consuming. One solution for variables not collected in the registry is to use other centralised sources to obtain missing data.15

Basic demographic patterns and comparisons

Registries show the demographic patterns of injury that help clinicians and institutions plan services. There was male predominance (60:40) and age<65 years, with 50% of cases being road traffic accidents.5 Elderly individuals were found to be at higher risk for major trauma, and an injury severity score (ISS)>30 at admission was strongly predictive of higher mortality.16 Age, ISS, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), anticoagulant and ventilator use are significant survival predictors. In comparison, type of injury and surgery performed were not found to be of equal significance.17

Treatment is site specific with femur fractures and spinal injuries (25% of cases), managed surgically 90% and 80% of times, respectively.5 As registries have become more established, it has allowed comparison in outcomes between different implants, for instance, intramedullary nails (IMNs) and anatomical locking plates or a combination of both for distal femur fractures. Triangulation with randomised studies favours IMN.18

Common outcomes following trauma include eventual return to work in 50%, but in the other half, there is significant time away from work, loss of productivity/income with unemployment occurring in 1/25 cases.19

Loss to follow-up is a persistent obstacle to orthopaedic trauma care,20 especially when trying to identify surgical complications, such as wound dehiscence, infection, venous thromboembolism and fracture non-union/malunion.21 Steps to ensure the rigorous collection of postinjury data are required to ensure the understanding of outcomes, with Zelle et al demonstrating that a 20% loss to follow-up alters statistical significance of functional outcomes in nearly one-thirds of reports.22 In the UK, for instance, this is through the Data Access Request Service in England and Wales, the Northern Ireland Statistics and Research Agency in Northern Ireland and the Electronic Data Research and Innovation Service in Scotland. Similarly, in Sweden, the Swedish Fracture Registry (SFR) trauma registry collects data since 2021 from all 54 of its trauma and orthopaedic departments achieving full coverage of its citizens who are easy to track as they have a personal identity number to ensure data linkage.23

Data accuracy is essential to results and conclusions in outcomes research.24 When reviewing data quality reports from provincial and international trauma registries, completeness was found in 47% for mechanism of injury and 100% for demographics.25 A high proportion of fractures is also not classified and, when classified, there is not always agreement between coders. Sometimes, code changes as further imaging is obtained.26 Researchers will then not be able to risk adjust accurately,24 which is pivotal to credible risk-adjusted modelling and benchmarking.27

A similar issue occurred when looking at outcome measures being accurate for ISS and Glasgow coma scale (GCS) score in 48% and 83% of occasions, respectively. Defining the injury was accurate in 87.5% of cases using the International Classification of Disease (ICD-9-CM), but better with the abbreviated injury scale. Current evaluation of data quality in trauma registries concentrates on completeness rather than standardising data fields and ensuring reproducibility. While level I trauma centres produce improved survival rates, this association could not be proven for non-fatal outcomes in general populations, partly because of inconsistencies in the body of evidence.25

Outcome measures

Improving disability seems to be more difficult to achieve.25 Looking at registries around the world, only 15% used a disability score to measure outcome.2 Complications and mortality have traditionally been recorded more robustly for trauma registry patients.28 Only a third of the ≥ 80 age group can be discharged home following trauma due to a dip in functional status having a massive impact on the quality of life. Understanding what interventions reduce this risk by understanding outcomes is key to improved functional care.29 A comparative study looked at the value of the Glasgow outcome scale (GOS), GOS-extended (GOSE), functional independence measure (FIM) and modified FIM in 243 major trauma patients with/without significant head injuries. The GOSE and GOS appeared the most responsive with good effect sizes of 5.3 and 4.4, respectively, and the lowest ceiling effect being seen in GOSE.30

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine in the USA recommended the inclusion of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and PROs metrics to benchmark the quality of trauma care after discharge for at least 1 year.31 The collection of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) necessitates significant time and financial investment, but facilitates patient-centred and value-based care.32 33 More importantly, PROMs assess the overall experience and satisfaction associated with received care.34 A wide variety of PROMs are available in five main health domains: physical, mental, social, cognitive and quality of life. So far, efforts to fully understand the health outcomes of trauma patients remain inconsistent and insufficient.35

The advantages of centralised PROMs collection include a uniform method of communication with patients, who are also more likely to respond to hospital-based collection, as there is a clearer invested relationship. Often, legislation and funding influence how this is undertaken.32

Certification of trauma centre

National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) in the USA, which was set up in 1989, by 2010 had 682 trauma centres submitting data. The American College of Surgery (ACS) requires level I and II trauma centres to submit data and produce reports, which help implement quality improvement.36 By 2021, the NTDB had become the largest repository of trauma data in the USA, Puerto Rico and Canada, with 7.5 million records from >900 trauma centres.37 Similarly, the Trauma Audit and Research Network (TARN) was established to audit trauma care across England, Wales and Ireland, collecting data from the major trauma units established in 2012, but widening quickly to include all units that accept trauma within the UK. The best practice tariff for major trauma centres promotes timely entry of clinical data to TARN to help improve standards. While this approach has improved mortality rates within trauma centres, other types of trauma, such as femoral fractures in the frail elderly, require a similar focus.38 The Dutch trauma registry has included the increasing elderly population where there is a high risk of mortality due to frailty.39 Application of the more stringent inclusion criteria, such as those used by TARN (UK) and the German trauma registry, results in an underestimation of trauma impact.40

Research

Retrospective observational studies are easy to undertake using data captured within registries enabling researchers to explore observations, such as different outcomes for pelvic-acetabular fractures in morbidly obese patients (body mass index (BMI) 40) and with age, as adolescents differ from children and adults in the NTDB.41 The Functional Outcomes and Recovery after Trauma Emergencies project in the USA retrospectively uses hospital trauma registry to look at patients with an ISS of 9 or more at 6 or 12 months after injury to evaluate continuing care needs and the mental impact of trauma on a patient. Approximately, 20% patients screened positive for post-traumatic stress disorder, and half had not yet returned to work. Because of this, a programme to tackle mental wellness has been launched to help patients.31

TARN has patient information advisory group approval under the Health and Social Care Act (2001) to use data that it holds for change and has helped identify changing demographics within trauma population.38 42 The robustness of registries has enabled a plethora of research opportunities to develop, including the World Hip Trauma Evaluation clinical trials, now embedded into national registry looking at different aspects of hip fracture care.43 The SFR was one of the first platforms to facilitate register-based, randomised controlled trials (r-RCTs) in fracture care.23

Analysis of outcomes

Looking at any paper or research requires a clear research question that targets a meaningful population/question and that remains important when considering outcomes in trauma cases.44 The James Lind Alliance (2024) has helped clinicians understand questions important to patients, such as pain control, rather than the details of implant most effective in trauma care.45 There are considerable differences in the covariates used for case adjustment in studies looking at mortality, with the three most frequently used being age (95%), ISS (85%) and sex (78%) in 43% of studies. Best practices are needed to further improve the quality of research from trauma registries with international consensus.46

Measuring treatment benefits can be assessed in terms of the relative risk or risk ratio (RR), which in the case of trauma is the injury event and subsequent outcome, respectively. In patients following hip fracture, multidisciplinary rehabilitation may result in fewer deaths in hospital, but the CI does not exclude a small increase in number of deaths (RR 0.77).47 Values <1 mean that the exposure to the rehabilitation correlates with less deaths, but this does not ensure a causative link; rather, it may be a protective factor rather than a risk factor, reducing the risk of poor outcome following trauma. Relative risk measurements are only significant when the absolute risk of an event is frequent enough. For hip fractures, these are very common, but the same method of reporting might not be as relevant for injuries with a lower incidence.48

Data from registries have identified several patient-related determinants of pain relief, functional outcome and satisfaction, such as emotional health, chronic pain, medical conditions impairing ambulation, socioeconomic status7 and obesity.49 Information about these covariables has helped in the shared decision-making process with patients, giving realistic expectations for treatment and recovery.32

Previous studies looking at registry data have shown only small differences observed between patients with and without comorbidities.50 There is a risk of a healthy-surgical patient effect, for any patient selected for surgery, as they pass a preoperative assessment and surgical episode, given that they have a different demographics to those confined to conservative for health reasons. In fact, patients with major orthopaedic injuries have in some studies reported better preinjury health status compared with population returning to this level by 6–24 months. People with orthopaedic trauma are either healthier than the general population before injury or have an inflated impression of their preinjury health.51

Predicting fracture outcomes from clinical Registry data using AI-Supplemented models for Evidence-informed treatment (PRAISE) is a study that has used AI methods on unstructured data in wrist fractures to better understand clinical management for each anatomical pattern. Geographical distribution of trauma registries: a global overview

The geographical distribution of established trauma registries is highly uneven, and a clear overview of this distribution is essential to underscore the limitations of current evidence and identify critical gaps for future data collection initiatives. Trauma registries are primarily concentrated in high-income countries (HICs), often integrated into formal, government-mandated trauma care systems. This concentration has led to a wealth of high-quality data in these regions.

North America: the NTDB in the USA, established in 1989, is a major repository of data, with the ACS requiring levels I and II trauma centres to submit data.34 35

Europe: multiple countries have established national or regional registries.

The TARN audits care across England, Wales and Ireland, promoting timely data entry to improve standards.36

The SFR, active since 2021, achieves 100% national coverage for all ages and all fracture types across its 54 trauma and orthopaedic departments, facilitating r-RCTs.21

The Dutch trauma registry actively includes the increasing elderly population at high risk of mortality due to frailty.37

It is important to recognise the major gaps in global trauma registry coverage (table 3) with the most significant gap being the vast under-representation of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for approximately 90% of global trauma-related deaths.53

Discussion

Synthesis of the evidence: the value proposition of trauma registries

This systematic review underscores the profound value of orthopaedic trauma registries, distinguishing their utility from administrative databases primarily focused on reimbursement rather than clinical detail.1 11 52 The evidence shows that successful registries, bolstered by funding, trained staff and engaged leadership, meticulously collect data to enable continuous quality assessment and drive improvements in care.12 They are instrumental in understanding injury patterns, monitoring care effectiveness and facilitating changes that enhance patient outcomes.2,47 8 11

Registries provide essential information for clinicians and institutions, revealing demographic patterns of injury, such as male predominance, age distribution and common mechanisms like road traffic accidents.5 They highlight patient subgroups at higher risk (eg, the elderly for major trauma or those with ISS>30 for higher mortality).14 Furthermore, registries allow for crucial comparisons between different implants and procedures, influencing best practice guidelines and validating findings from randomised studies, such as the preference for IMNs in distal femur fractures.4 16 They also inform the development of specific care pathways and quality improvement initiatives, ultimately contributing to improved survival after injury, as demonstrated by the UK’s major trauma centre introduction saving an estimated 1600 lives by 2018.5 7 8 The integration of detailed clinical information (eg, from emergency department (ED) presentation to discharge, including transport and treatment times) further enhances their utility for effective pathway planning.7

The global imperative: geographical disparity and coverage gaps

While the systematic review identified 15 established trauma databases worldwide, their geographical distribution is markedly uneven, creating a significant bias in the available evidence. The vast majority of comprehensive, national-level registries are concentrated in HICs. Examples include the NTDB in the USA, which by 2021 was the largest repository with 7.5 million records from over 900 trauma centres across the USA, Puerto Rico and Canada.35 Similarly, the TARN in the UK, established in 2012, audits care across England, Wales and Ireland, with its best practice tariff promoting timely data entry to improve standards.36 The SFR provides 100% national coverage for all ages and fracture types since 2021, showcasing the potential for comprehensive data collection.21 The Dutch trauma registry also actively includes the increasing elderly population at high risk of mortality due to frailty.37

This concentration in HICs means that current insights into injury patterns and optimal management largely reflect resource-rich settings. This creates a critical global disparity, as approximately 90% of all trauma-related deaths occur in LMICs. Yet, only a small number of trauma registries exist in developing countries, often exhibiting large variations in processes, which limits their resourcefulness in areas where the need for robust data is paramount.2

Furthermore, the application of stringent inclusion criteria by some HIC registries, such as TARN (UK), can result in an underestimation of the broader trauma impact and resource utilisation.38 These systems tend to emphasise high-energy trauma in younger populations, potentially overlooking the significant burden of trauma in other groups, such as femoral fractures in the frail elderly who may present to lower level units and be coded as ‘trivial’ injuries.36 54 This focus can lead to a disparity where routine cases in major trauma units receive lower priority and delayed care, as most resources are allocated to critically injured patients.55 This geographical and methodological bias in registry data collection significantly impacts the generalisability of findings and underscores the urgent need for expanded and standardised registry development in LMICs and for more inclusive data collection strategies globally.

The inherent limitations of registry data: quality, coding and confounding

While registries offer significant advantages, this review highlights several inherent methodological challenges that limit their impact and utility. A central issue is the variable quality and completeness of data. Although nationalised registries benefit from studying entire populations, ensuring data completeness (eg, Sweden’s personal identity number system),13 21 data accuracy for critical outcome measures like ISS and GCS can be as low as 48% and 83%, respectively.23 Furthermore, a high proportion of fractures are often not classified, and the classification can change with further imaging, impacting the ability to accurately risk adjust for treatments and outcomes.22 24

The current evaluation of data quality in trauma registries predominantly focuses on completeness rather than standardisation of data fields to ensure reproducibility and improve impact.23 This inconsistency in the body of evidence contributes to situations where, despite level I trauma centres improving survival rates in major trauma populations, this association cannot always be proven for non-fatal outcomes in general populations, partly due to coding errors.23 24 These issues underscore the pervasive challenge of residual confounding variables, responder bias and missing data inherent in observational studies using registry data, which is a major constraint when drawing conclusions.46 While randomised controlled trials excel in internal validity, registry studies are often lauded for their external validity; however, this advantage is undermined by quality variations in risk-adjusted analyses, necessitating an international consensus on best practices for methodology and quality assurance.34

Observational studies are limited by residual confounding variables, responder bias and missing data. Data collection immediately before and after 1 year of surgery/trauma is recommended with a threshold of 60%, for acceptable frequency of response. Documentation of non-responders and incomplete/missing data helps researchers understand the demographic of this subgroup.49

Moreover, registries do not always collect all relevant information for reliable clinical decision-making. For instance, TARN, while excellent for major trauma, has limitations in reporting specifics of trauma care across the entire UK, particularly lacking data from Scotland and potentially underemphasising the outcomes for the frail elderly in lower level units.56 Persistent obstacles, such as loss to follow-up (affecting up to 20% of patients), can alter the statistical significance of functional outcomes, hindering comprehensive understanding of postoperative recovery and complications.18,20

Shifting the focus: from mortality to patient-centred outcomes

Traditionally, complications and mortality have been the most robustly recorded outcomes in trauma registries.26 However, a critical finding of this review is the poor collection of PROMs and disability scores, undermining the true value of registries in assessing long-term impact on patients.2 Only a third of patients aged 80 or above can be discharged home following trauma, indicating a significant dip in functional status and quality of life.27 This highlights a major gap, as only 15% of registries worldwide use a disability score to measure outcomes, despite the responsiveness of instruments like the GOSE.2 28

The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine in the USA have recommended the inclusion of HRQoL and PROMs metrics to benchmark trauma care quality for at least 1-year postdischarge.29 PROMs, which assess the overall experience, satisfaction and outcomes across physical, mental, social, cognitive and quality of life domains, are essential for patient-centred and value-based care.30,33 While their collection requires significant time and financial investment, centralised PROM collection offers advantages in uniformity and patient engagement.30 However, efforts to fully understand the holistic health outcomes of trauma patients remain inconsistent and insufficient, with studies showing patients experiencing considerable impairment at 6 and 12 months, including significant time away from work, loss of productivity and high rates of post-traumatic stress disorder.17 29 This necessitates developing quality improvement metrics that better reflect patient outcomes beyond merely survival.57

Promoting data uniformity and the role of future technology

The review highlights that interpreting information from trauma registries is hampered by varied approaches, even among the established 15 databases worldwide. While full harmonisation of datasets across countries is unlikely due to diverse healthcare systems, promoting greater uniformity is essential. This requires developing a standardised and reproducible method to evaluate data quality, focusing on common data fields rather than just completeness.23 An international consensus on quality indicators and core covariates, for example, age, ISS and sex, for risk-adjusted analyses, is critical to improve research quality and enable more robust benchmarking.23 34 44

AI and deep learning algorithms are already proving capable of analysing complex metadata in large datasets, going beyond routine analytical methods.4 58 For instance, AI-supplemented models, such as the PRAISE study on wrist fractures, have been used to analyse unstructured data to better understand clinical management for specific anatomical patterns, demonstrating the potential for robust predictive modelling.50 This could create a ‘virtual’ harmonisation, enabling more robust international comparative effectiveness research currently limited by methodological inconsistencies.23 54 59

However, these AI-driven approaches are still exploratory and require substantial advances in data processing infrastructure and personnel.58 Crucially, any such translation tools necessitate rigorous validation to ensure that automated data processing does not introduce new systematic errors, confounders or biases when applied to credible risk-adjusted modelling and international benchmarking.25 60 Therefore, while these technologies hold significant promise for overcoming current data heterogeneity, their development and application must be approached with careful scrutiny.

Limitations of this study

The systematic review itself encountered inherent methodological challenges related to assessing the quality of studies based on the registry data because of limitations in the quality assessment tools,9 44 design trade-offs46 55 56 and the search strategy employed. The quality assessment relied on general checklists, such as the STROBE statement44 and the Downs and Black Quality Criteria.9 These tools, while robust for general methodology, may not fully capture the unique methodological flaws inherent to studies using secondary registry data, such as data entry errors, variable definitions and inconsistent risk-adjustment methods.44

Although registry studies possess greater external validity than traditional trials,61 they are inherently limited by residual confounding variables, responder bias and missing data,46 which is a major constraint when drawing causative conclusions.

While the search maximised sensitivity using four major databases (PubMed, Ovid, Scopus and EMBASE), the use of ChatGPT (Open AI) for identifying highly cited articles introduced a method for ‘triangulation to aid in snowballing references’, but this technique may inadvertently bias the review towards older, established research, potentially overlooking newer research with less citation density.

Conclusion

In conclusion, trauma registries hold significant promise for enhancing the quality of care and evaluating interventions in trauma management, often surpassing the utility of administrative databases.11 Their success hinges on dedicated funding, well-trained staff, strong local leadership and the active engagement of all relevant stakeholders. A clear purpose, meticulous data quality and effective data utilisation are paramount.12 While they have driven significant improvements in HICs by enabling national performance guidelines and trauma centre audits, particularly in reducing mortality rates,36 the geographical disparity in their implementation, especially the vast under-representation in LMICs, remains a critical challenge. Furthermore, a concerted effort is needed to standardise data collection, particularly for PROs beyond mortality, to truly reflect the long-term impact on the patient quality of life and disability.2 29 Embracing technological solutions like AI for data harmonisation, alongside international consensus on quality metrics, will be integral steps towards optimising the impact of trauma registries globally.
