Arithmetic Circuits with Division
Silas Cato Sacher

Abstract
We study the computational complexity of the membership problem for arithmetic circuits over natural numbers with division. We consider different subsets of the operations { ∪ , ∩ , ¯ , + , × , / } \{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/\} , where / / is the element-wise integer division (without remainder and without rounding). Results for the subsets without division have been studied before, in particular by McKenzie and Wagner [ 12 ] and Yang [ 20 ] . The division is expressive because it makes it possible to describe the set of factors of a given number as a circuit. Surprisingly, the cases { ∪ , ∩ , ¯ , + , / } \{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,/\} and { ∪ , ∩ , ¯ , × , / } \{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},\times,/\} are PSPACE \mathrm{PSPACE} -complete and therefore equivalent to the corresponding cases without division. The case { ∪ , / } \{\cup,/\} is NP \mathrm{NP} -hard in contrast to the case { ∪ } \{\cup\} which is NL \mathrm{NL} -complete. Further upper bounds, lower bounds and completeness results are given.

Introduction

Arithmetic circuits are a generalization of formulas over sets of natural numbers (including
0
). In such formulas, the numbers are interpreted as singleton sets that are combined through some operations (initially only
\{\cap,\cup,\overline{\phantom{c}},+\}
) similar to the way regular languages can be expressed by regular expressions. For example
2\times\overline{0\cap 1}
describes the set of even numbers, because
\overline{0\cap 1}
describes the set of natural numbers. In fact, such formulas were introduced by Stockmeyer and Meyer in 1973 in a work that otherwise considered regular expressions
[
16
]
. The arithmetic circuits was first considered by Wagner in 1984
[
19
]
.

An arithmetic circuit
C=(V,E,g_{c},\alpha)
is an acyclic directed graph, with a map
\alpha:V\rightarrow\mathcal{O}\cup\mathbb{N}
labeling the nodes (called gates) and a fixed output gate
g_{c}
. To the gates, the map assigns either natural numbers (interpreted as singleton sets) or an operation that describes how the sets from the predecessor nodes are combined into a new set. Arithmetic operations are applied element-wise, for example,
A+B=\{c\in\mathbb{N}\mid\exists a\in A\;\exists b\in B:c=a+b\}
for sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. Hence circuits are essentially formulas in which parts of the formula can be reused. Therefore, circuits can provide a more compact representation in certain cases. Wagner proposed the question of computational complexity of the membership problem for circuits with the operations
\{\cup,+,\times\}
. The membership problem on the instance
(C,b)
is the question whether the natural number
b
is contained in the set described by the circuit
C
. In 2000 Yang proved that the problem described by Wagner is
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete
[
20
]
.
Based on this, McKenzie and Wagner in 2003 provided a detailed description of the complexity of the membership problem on natural numbers for the various subsets of the operations
\{\cap,\cup,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times\}

[
12
]
. However, some question–including the decidability of the general problem–remained open. They also provided insight into why determining the complexity of the general problem is difficult: It is possible to describe the set of prime numbers
\mathbb{P}
with the arithmetic circuit shown in
Figure

Consider Goldbach’s conjecture. This conjecture states that every even natural number greater than two is the sum of two prime numbers. It was first proposed in a letter exchange between Goldbach and Euler in 1742 and is a part of Hilbert’s eighth problem. Glaßer first observed that one can construct a circuit that produces the number
0
in its output gate, if and only if Goldbach’s conjecture holds
[
12
]
. Therefore an algorithm for the general membership problem could determine whether Goldbach’s conjecture holds or not.

There has been extensive research on arithmetic circuits: Arithmetic circuits with different base sets (such as integers
[
17
]
, positive numbers
[
2
]
, balanced sets (specific subsets of
\mathbb{N}
)
[
3
]
or even functions
[
13
]
) were studied. Different problems such as the equivalence problem
[
4
]
(“Do two given circuits describe the same set?”) and the emptiness problem
[
1
]
(“Does a given circuit describe the empty set?”) have been studied as well. There have also been generalizations of the membership problems: In 2007 Glaßer et al. considered satisfiability problems for circuits over sets of natural numbers
[
6
]
. Furthermore, arithmetic circuits over semi-rings in non-commutative settings have been considered as well
[
11

.

In this work, we study the computational complexity of the membership problem for circuits over natural numbers with division. We will consider the different sets of operations
\mathcal{O}
such that
\{/\}\subseteq\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\cap,\cup,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/\}
, where
/
is the element-wise division without remainder or rounding, that is, for sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,
A/B=\{c\in\mathbb{N}\mid\exists a\in A\;\exists b\in B\setminus\{0\}:a=c\cdot b\}
.
The author found this operation to be especially expressive compared to division with rounding, because one can easily express sets like the set of factors of a given number
b
(Consider the formula
b/\overline{0\cap 1}
.), the prime factors of a given number and the common divisors of two given numbers. We were already able to express the set of all multiples of a given number and the set of numbers that are the product of exactly
k
prime numbers with multiplication. These properties bring the membership problem of arithmetic circuits with division (but without addition) close to Skolem arithmetic.
Skolem arithmetic is decidable, but the best known algorithm has a triple exponential running time.
One can reduce the membership problem for the operations
\{\cap,\cup,\overline{\phantom{c}},\times,/\}
to Skolem arithmetic. This can be proven in a way similar to the results of Glaßer et al.
[
5
, Thm. 1]
. However, the proof will not be given in this work because instead we will prove that the problem is
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete. Indeed we will show that the cases
\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,/\}
and
\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},\times,/\}
are
\mathrm{PSPACE}
-complete and equivalent to the corresponding cases without division. In addition, we prove that the case
\{\cup,\cap,+,\times,/\}
is
\mathrm{NEXP}
-complete and equivalent to the case
\{\cup,\cap,+,\times\}
. The case
\{\cup,/\}
is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard in contrast to the case
\{\cup\}
which is
\mathrm{NL}
-complete. Therefore, adding division will–under common assumptions–increase the computational complexity. Further upper bounds will be given in
Section

3
and further lower bounds will be given in
Section

4
.

Preliminaries

We consider
0
to be a natural number and define the function
\log:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
as
\log(n)=\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor
for all
n>0
and
\log(0)=0
. We write the set of prime numbers as
\mathbb{P}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
[k]:=\{1,\dots,k\}
.
We assume that the reader is familiar with several complexity classes, especially the deterministic time complexity classes
\mathrm{P}
,
\mathrm{E}=\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{O(n)})
and
\mathrm{EXP}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{O(n^{k})})
, the nondeterministic time complexity classes
\mathrm{NP}
and
\mathrm{NEXP}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathrm{NTIME}(2^{O(n^{k})})
, the space complexity classes
\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{NL}
,
\mathrm{PSPACE}
and the probabilistic complexity class
\mathrm{PL}
.

\mathrm{ATIME}(t)
is the set of all languages accepted by an alternating Turing machine–a model for parallel computers–in time
t:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
. An introduction to alternating Turing machines in the context of circuits can be found in
[
18
, Section 2.5]
.

\mathrm{FL}
is the class of all functions
f:\Sigma_{1}^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}^{*}
for alphabets
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
calculated in space
O(\log(n))
by a deterministic (multi-tape) Turing machine with output. We write
A\leq^{\log}_{m}B
, if and only if the language
A
is many-one-reducible to a language
B
in logarithmic space and
A\equiv^{\log}_{m}B
, if and only if
A\leq^{\log}_{m}B
and
B\leq^{\log}_{m}A
. It is known that
\mathrm{FL}
is stable under composition and that
\leq^{\log}_{m}
is reflexive and transitive. In addition to
\leq^{\log}_{m}
, we sometimes consider
\leq^{\mathrm{P}}_{m}
for polynomial time and
\leq^{\mathrm{PSPACE}}_{m}
for polynomial space reduction. We investigate lower and upper bounds for membership problems. By a lower bound
\mathcal{C}_{1}
we mean that a problem
A
is
\leq^{log}_{m}
-hard for the complexity class
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and by an upper bound
\mathcal{C}_{2}
we mean that
A\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
. In this work, we say that a problem
A
is
\mathcal{C}
-hard (or
\mathcal{C}
-complete) if
A
is
\leq^{\log}_{m}
-hard (or
\leq^{\log}_{m}
-complete) for
\mathcal{C}
.

2.1
Arithmetic Circuits

Definition 1

Let
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/\}
be a set of operations on sets of natural numbers. The operations
\cup
,
\cap
and
\overline{\phantom{c}}
are the union, intersection and complement for sets of natural numbers. We write the multiplication as
\times
. The operations
\sigma\in\{+,\times,/\}
are extended for sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
:

A\sigma B:=\{c\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;\exists a\in A,b\in B:\;c=a\sigma b\}
.
An
(arithmetic)
\mathcal{O}
-circuit

C=(V,E,g_{C},\alpha)
is an acyclic, directed multigraph
G=(V,E)
, whose nodes have indegree 0, 1 or 2. Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The nodes of
G
are called
gates
. Gates with indegree 0 are called
input gates
and the specified gate
g_{C}\in V
is called
output gate
. The gates are labeled with the function
\alpha:V\rightarrow\mathcal{O}\cup\mathbb{N}
, such that for each input gate
g
we have
\alpha(g)\in\mathbb{N}
, for each gate
g
with indegree
1
we have
\alpha(g)=\overline{\phantom{c}}
(only if
\overline{\phantom{c}}\in\mathcal{O}
) and for all other gates we have
\alpha(g)\in\mathcal{O}\setminus\{\overline{\phantom{c}}\}
. A gate
g
is also called an
\alpha(g)
-gate
. For each gate
g\in V
we define the
result set

I(g)\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. For each input gate
g
,
I(g):=\{\alpha(g)\}
. For a gate
g
with label
\alpha(g)=\overline{\phantom{c}}
and predecessor
p
,
I(g):=\mathbb{N}\setminus I(p)
. We define the predecessor function
p(g):=p
for
\overline{\phantom{c}}
-gates. For a gate
g
with label
\alpha(g)=\sigma\in\{\cup,\cap,+,\times,/\}
and predecessors
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
with
g_{1}\leq g_{2}
,
I(g):=I(g_{1})\sigma I(g_{2})
. We call
g_{1}
the first and
g_{2}
the second predecessor of
g
and define the predecessor functions
p_{1}(g):=g_{1}
and
p_{2}(g):=g_{2}
.
I(C):=I(g_{c})
is the
result set of
C
.

Definition 2

For
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/\}
, the
membership problem for
\mathcal{O}
-circuits
is

\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{O}):=\{(C,b)\;|\;C\text{ is an }\mathcal{O}\text{-circuit and }b\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }b\in I(C)\}
.
We use the shorthand notation
\mathrm{MC}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
for
\mathrm{MC}(\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}\})
.

Remark 1

For
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{O}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{2}\subseteq\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/\}
,
\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{O}_{1})\leq^{\log}_{m}\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{O}_{2})
.

Encoding of circuits

In order to determine the complexity of the membership problem, we must understand circuits as inputs of Turing machines. To do this, we encode a circuit
C=(V,E,g_{c},\alpha)
as follows: The circuit is represented as a list of its gates in reverse topological order. Each gate
g\in V
has the representation
(g,\alpha(g),l(g))
. Where
l(g)
is the list of predecessors of
g
. If
g
has an ingoing double edge, the corresponding predecessor is enumerated twice.
We encode this list in bits. (Hence, the integer labels of the input gates are encoded in binary.) We write the length of the encoding as
|C|
. The value
|C|
depends on both the size of the underlying multigraph and the labels of the input gates. Topological ordering of the edges is possible because the graph is acyclic. It is possible to deterministically check in logarithmic space whether the order is topological. This makes it possible to deterministically check in logarithmic space whether the input is a valid encoding of a circuit.

3
Upper Bounds

We do not solve the general problem
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/)
. The open problem
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times)
is reducible to it using logarithmic space. Instead we look at problems without addition, without complement and without multiplication, respectively.

3.1
Circuits without multiplication

We will solve the membership problem for circuits without multiplication on alternating Turing machines. First, we need a technical lemma: Glaßer et al. state that for
\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+\}
-circuits there is a limit beyond which either all values are described by the circuit or none
[
4
, Prop. 1]
. This is preserved if we add
/
-gates. We prove the following by induction:

Lemma 1

Let
C
be a
\mathcal{O}
-circuit with
\emptyset\not=\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,/\}
. A number
n\leq 2^{|C|}+1
exists such that for all
z\geq n
it holds that
z\in I(C)
if and only if
n\in I(C)
.

Proof

Let
C
be a
\mathcal{O}
-circuit. Let
C_{g}
be the circuit formed from
C
using
g
as the output gate and removing all gates from which
g
cannot be reached. We show for each gate
g
from
C
that a natural number
n_{g}\leq 2^{|C_{g}|}+1
exists such that for each
z\geq n_{g}
,
z\in I(g)
if and only if
n_{g}\in I(g)
. We show this statement by induction.

Base case:
Consider an input gate
g
. The gate
g
is the only gate in
C_{g}
and
I(g)=\{\alpha(g)\}
. Define
n_{g}=2^{|C_{g}|}+1
. For each
z\geq n_{g}
,
z\geq 2^{\log(\alpha(g))+1}+1>\alpha(g)
and
z\notin I(g)
.

Induction step:
Let
g
be a gate with predecessors. We assume that the statement holds for the predecessors of
g
(induction hypothesis). We only consider the case where
g
is a
/
-gate, because Glaßer et al. already proved the statement of the lemma for
\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+\}
-circuits
[
4
, Prop. 1]
. Let
n_{g}:=n_{p_{1}(g)}
. It holds that

n_{g}=n_{p_{1}(g)}\leq 2^{|C_{p_{1}(g)}|}+1\leq 2^{|C_{g}|}+1
. First, consider the case
n_{p_{1}(g)}\notin I(p_{1}(g))
. Hence
I(p_{1}(g))\subseteq[0,n_{p_{1}(g)})
and
I(g)=I(p_{1}(g))/I(p_{2}(g))\subseteq[0,n_{g})=[0,n_{p_{1}(g)})
. Now consider the case
n_{p_{1}(g)}\in I(p_{1}(g))
. If
I(p_{2}(g))\subseteq\{0\}
,
I(g)
is the empty set and the statement holds. Now assume that
I(p_{2}(g))\not\subseteq\{0\}
. Hence, a number
a\in I(p_{2}(g))\setminus\{0\}
exists. For each
z\geq n_{g}
,
za\geq n_{g}=n_{p_{1}(g)}
. According to the induction hypotheses
za\in I(p_{1}(g))
and
z=za/a\in I(p_{1}(g))/I(p_{2}(g))=I(g)
. ∎

Corollary 1

Let
C
be a
\mathcal{O}
-circuit with
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,/\}
. Then for all
z\geq 2^{|C|}+1
,

z\in I(C)\;\Leftrightarrow\;2^{|C|}+1\in I(C)
.

-gate and each rejecting configuration is represented by a
1
-gate. In
C_{2}^{x}
we label the input gates the other way around.
Let
n
be the number of accepting paths and
m
be the number of rejecting paths of
M^{\prime}
with input
x
. Hence
I(C_{1}^{x})=2^{n}
and
I(C_{2}^{x})=2^{m}
. Furthermore for each input
x
,
prob_{M^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{n}{2^{p(|x|)}}
and
M^{\prime}
rejects
x
with the probability
\frac{m}{2^{p(|x|)}}
.
The following applies:

x\in A\;\Leftrightarrow\;prob_{M^{\prime}}(x)>\frac{1}{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;n>m\;\Leftrightarrow\;2^{n}\geq 2^{m+1}\;\Leftrightarrow\;2^{n}/2^{m+1}\in\mathbb{N}\;\Leftrightarrow\;1\in I(C^{x})

Since
C^{x}
can be constructed in logarithmic space , we get
A\leq^{\log}_{m}\mathrm{MC}(\times,/)
. ∎

4.5
Division only circuits

We can show, that
\mathrm{MC}(/)
is
\mathrm{NL}
-hard by reducing the Graph Accessibility Problem to the complement of
\mathrm{MC}(/)
.

Definition 8

The
Graph Accessibility Problem
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{GAP}=\{(G,u,v)\mid

\displaystyle\text{$G$ is a directed graph that has a path form $u$ to $v$}\}.

It is known that
\mathrm{GAP}
is
\mathrm{NL}
-complete
[
15

.

Theorem 4.4

\mathrm{MC}(/)
is
\mathrm{NL}
-hard.

Proof

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed acyclic graph with

. We can modify
G
in logarithmic space such that
s
is a source,
t
is a sink, and every node
v\in V
has indegree 0 or 2. We also assume
s\neq t
. Note that vertices with indegree 0 (except
s
) are never part of any
s
-
t
-path (that is, a path from
s
to
t
). Still, we keep these vertices to ensure that each other gate has indegree 2.
For every node
v
with
v\neq s
and indegree 0 let
\alpha(v)=1
and for all nodes
w
with indegree 2 let
\alpha(w)=/
. Also, let
\alpha(s)=0
.
Then
C=(G,E,t,\alpha)
is a
\{/\}
-circuit. The order of the predecessors of the nodes with indegree 2 can be chosen arbitrarily. Inductively one can show for all
v\in V
,
I(v)\in\{\{0\},\{1\},\emptyset\}
and that there is
s
-
v
-path in
G
, if and only if
I(v)\in\{\emptyset,\{0\}\}
. This shows that
(G,s,t)\in\mathrm{GAP}
if and only if there is an
s
-
t
-path in
G
if and only if
I(t)\in\{\emptyset,\{0\}\}
if and only if
(C,1)\in\overline{\mathrm{MC}(/)}
. The problem
\mathrm{MC}(/)
is
\mathrm{NL}
-hard, because
\mathrm{GAP}
is
\mathrm{NL}
-complete and
\mathrm{NL}=\mathrm{coNL}
. ∎

5
Conclusion and open questions

There are both cases in which
\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{O})\equiv^{\log}_{m}\mathrm{MC}(\mathcal{O}\cup\{/\})
and cases in which–if common assumptions hold–this is not the case. In this sense, the following lower bounds are interesting compared to the results by McKenzie and Wagner
[
12
]
:

•

\mathrm{MC}(\cup)
is
\mathrm{NL}
-complete.
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,/)
is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard.

•

\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap)
and
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}})
are
\mathrm{P}
-complete.
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap,/)
and
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},/)
are
\mathrm{NP}
-hard.

•

\mathrm{MC}(\times)
is
\mathrm{NL}
-complete.
\mathrm{MC}(\times,/)
is
\mathrm{PL}
-hard.

•

\mathrm{MC}(+,\times)
is
\mathrm{P}
-hard.
\mathrm{MC}(+,\times,/)
is
\mathcal{PIT}
-hard.

The question of whether
\mathrm{MC}(\cup,\cap,\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/)
and
\mathrm{MC}(\overline{\phantom{c}},+,\times,/)
are decidable remains open.
Several bounds are not tight and closing these gaps remains open. Membership problems for formulas with division have not been studied so far. It would be interesting to also consider subtraction and other forms of division and compare them to the division without remainder and without rounding. I am also looking at emptiness and equivalence problems with division.
