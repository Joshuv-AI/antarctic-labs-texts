Salmonella encodes murein lipoproteins differing in the anchoring to peptidoglycan and intermolecular association
Authors: Marcos Peñalver, Juan J Cestero, Alberto Paradela, Felipe Cava, Francisco García-del Portillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Murein lipoprotein, Salmonella, Peptidoglycan, Disulphide bridge

Introduction

A prominent feature of the cell envelope of Gram-negative bacteria is the presence of an outer membrane rich in proteins and lipopolysaccharides (Silhavy et al., 2010).The stability of this envelope depends on the physical connection of the outer membrane to the peptidoglycan (PG) layer, a role played by specialized proteins like Braun's lipoprotein (Lpp). This Lpp was discovered in the pioneering studies of Braun and colleagues and has been extensively characterized in Escherichia coli (Asmar and Collet, 2018; Braun and Rehn, 1969; Braun and Sieglin, 1970; Braun and Wolff, 1970). Like other lipoproteins, E. coli Lpp is synthesized as a pre-lipoprotein that is lipidated at cysteine 21 (C21) and subsequently cleaved at this residue by the lipoprotein-specific signal peptidase (El Rayes et al., 2021; Tokuda and Matsuyama, 2004). Transfer of mature Lpp from the inner to the outer membrane is mediated by the LolABCDE system, acting LolCDE as a dedicated ABC transporter, LolA as a periplasmic chaperone and LolB as the carrier that finally insert the lipoprotein to the outer membrane (El Rayes et al., 2021). In E. coli it has been estimated that 5–15 % of Lpp molecules are covalently anchored to the PG (Burman and Park, 1983) via a reaction catalysed by the L,D-transpeptidases (LDTs) LdtA, LdtB or LdtC (Magnet et al., 2008; Magnet et al., 2007). The proportion of PG-bound Lpp increases in some physiological conditions such as the entry in stationary phase (Glauner et al., 1988; Pisabarro et al., 1985). These LDTs bind the ε-amino group of the C-terminal lysine (K) residue of Lpp to the α-carboxyl group of meso-diaminopimelic acid (meso-DAP) in position 3 of the PG stem peptide. Thermodynamic and crystallographic studies of purified mature Lpp have shown that it associates mainly as three alpha-helical coil coiled units (Dragan et al., 2004; Shu et al., 2000). This association as an homotrimer was also reported in vivo for the Lpp free form not bound to PG (Choi et al., 1986). Data obtained in E. coli with biotin-containing labelling agents that do not permeate the outer membrane supported a model with the Lpp free population being mostly surface exposed, therefore not interacting with PG-bound Lpp (Cowles et al., 2011).

Braun's Lpp is the most abundant protein in E. coli, estimated in almost one million copies per cell (Li et al., 2014). This abundance agrees with the essential role that Lpp plays in maintenance of envelope architecture. Thus, E. coli mutants lacking Lpp exhibit phenotypes like increased membrane blebbing (Hirota et al., 1977; Yem and Wu, 1978). Lpp also plays an important role in adjusting the size of the periplasm by establishing a fixed distance between the outer membrane and the peptidoglycan based on its size of only 58 amino acids in the mature protein (Asmar et al., 2017). The control of this inter-membrane distance is fundamental to processes such as assembly of the rod part of the flagellum basal body (Cohen et al., 2017) or, the response to envelope damage mediated by the Rcs system (Asmar et al., 2017).

In line with the role played by Lpp in maintaining envelope architecture, several studies proved its contribution to virulence in pathogenic E. coli isolates and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (Diao et al., 2017; Sha et al., 2004; Uhlich et al., 2009). The study in S. Typhimurium revealed the presence of two tandemly located genes encoding LppA, with 97 % identity at the amino acid level to E. coli Lpp; and LppB, 84 % identical to E. coli Lpp and with 86 % identity to LppA (Sha et al., 2004). The work by Sha et al., based on single or double mutants lacking either LppA, LppB, or both, did not however report biochemical characterization of these two lipoproteins or the mode these two murein lipoproteins are anchored to the PG.

In this study, we accomplished the biochemical characterization of LppA and LppB of S. Typhimurium. Our data demonstrate a new mode of LppB-LppB association involving a cysteine 78 (C78) residue in the C-terminal region that forms an intermolecular disulphide bridge. We also addressed the type of muropeptides to which LppB binds covalently. Another aspect examined in the study was the diversity of murein lipoproteins found in subspecies and serovars of Salmonella, which reaches up to 31 Lpp variants differing mainly in their C-terminal region.

Results

S. enterica serovar Typhimurium expresses LppB in laboratory conditions

The genome of S. enterica serovar Typhimurium (hereafter S. Typhimurium) has two tandemly located genes, lppA-lppB, predicted to encode murein lipoproteins LppA and LppB of 78 aa and 79 aa, respectively (Fig. 1A). LppA and LppB are 84.8 % identical at the amino acid level, being LppA the orthologue of E. coli Braun's Lpp with 96.1 % identity. In contrast, LppB exhibits only 81.8 % identity to E. coli Lpp (Fig. 1A). The main difference between LppB and either S. Typhimurium LppA or E.coli Lpp maps in the C-terminus (Fig. 1 A). These data suggest that lppB could be generated from lppA following a duplication event. In E. coli Lpp, the C-terminal sequence is critical for the covalent anchoring to peptidoglycan (PG) (Braun and Sieglin, 1970). Both LppA and LppB of S. Typhimurium have the conserved cysteine required for lipidation and processing by the lipoprotein-specific signal peptidase, residues C21 and C22, respectively (Fig. 1 A).

Consistently to the proposed duplication of lppA in S. Typhimurium, we identified almost identical hairpins predicted to act as Rho-independent transcriptional terminators. These hairpins are formed by palindromic sequences located in the lppA-lppB intergenic region and downstream of lppB (Fig. 1B). Based on the features of this particular genomic region, we constructed in the genetic background of the virulent strain S. Typhimurium SV5015 (Gonzalo-Asensio et al., 2013), a series of isogenic single and double knock-out mutants lacking lppA, lppB, or both (Fig. 1C). We next performed Western blotting with a rabbit polyclonal antiserum raised against E. coli Braun's Lpp, kindly provided by S. Daefler (University of Texas Southwestern, USA). When tested against total protein extracts obtained from S. Typhimurium ΔlppA or ΔlppB mutants, this antiserum proved to recognize both LppA and LppB of S. Typhimurium (Fig. 1D). Based on this result, we next sought to determine whether S. Typhimurium produces simultaneously LppA and LppB. Although it was previously inferred from transcriptomic data that LppB is not expressed in laboratory conditions (Canals et al., 2019; Kroger et al., 2013), this has not been tested experimentally at the protein level. Since the anti-Lpp antiserum does not discriminate between LppA and LppB (Fig. 1D), we used mass spectrometry to unequivocally identify both proteins based on subtle differences in their amino acid sequences resulting in two unique tryptic peptides (Fig. 2A). To assess the amount of LppA and LppB covalently anchored to PG, we prepared from wild type S. Typhimurium cells a subcellular fraction enriched in proteins that remain associated to PG after boiling in 4 % SDS (Pucciarelli and García-del Portillo, 2003). This protein extract, named “PG fraction”, was prepared after muramidase digestion of the PG and expected to contain exclusively the covalently bound forms of LppA and/or LppB. Samples were prepared in two different growth conditions, aerobiosis and anaerobiosis. The rationale for including anaerobiosis was based on transcriptomic analyses showing that oxygen deprivation results in a slight increase in the number of lppB transcripts (Canals et al., 2019; Kroger et al., 2013).

Ultra performance liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) of these PG fraction samples was used in parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) mode to monitor the two unique peptides of LppA (IDQLSSDVQTLNAK and VDQLSNDVNAMR) and LppB (IDQLSSDVQTLSAK and VEQLSNDVNAMR) (Fig. 2B-C). The intensity ratios for the IDQLSSDVQTLNAK / IDQLSSDVQTLSAK pair were 353.3 and 243.2for aerobiosis and anaerobiosis, respectively (Fig. 2B). These ratios for the VDQLSNDVNAMR / VEQLSNDVNAMR pair reached values of 413.7 and 379.3, respectively (Fig. 2C). These data indicated that S. Typhimurium produces LppB and that this murein lipoprotein is anchored to the PG, although in a lower amount estimated in a ∼ 1:400 ratio compared to LppA. In concordance with the transcriptomic data, this ratio slightly increases in response to anaerobic growth conditions.

LppB dimerizes with a cysteine 78 (C78)-C78 disulphide bridge

A feature that differentiates LppB from LppA is the presence in the C-terminal region of a cysteine residue (C78) (Fig. 1A). Considering the scarce amounts of LppB produced by wild type S. Typhimurium in laboratory conditions, we reasoned that it could be possible to characterize biochemically LppB in the absence of LppA using an ΔlppA mutant expressing LppB from the strong promoter located upstream of lppA (see Fig. 1C). As a parallel control, we included an isogenic S. Typhimurium ΔlppB that produces only LppA.

To determine whether the C78 residue of LppB forms a disulphide bridge, we prepared membrane (Mb) and PG-associated protein extracts (PG fraction) from wild type cells and isogenic ΔlppA and ΔlppB mutants. The extracts were resolved by SDS/PAGE in the absence/presence of the reducing agent β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) and analysed by Western blotting. In the presence of β-ME, LppA and LppB profiles are similar when comparing membrane and PG-associated fractions (Fig. 3A). By contrast, in the absence of the reducing agent, new bands displaying lower mobility were observed exclusively for LppB with concomitant reduction in the signal of bands corresponding to monomeric LppB (Fig. 3A). The new LppB bands appearing in the absence of β-ME match double the size of LppB, approximately ∼14–15 kDa, thus compatible with the presence of a LppB-LppB homodimer (Fig. 3A). Similarly to LppB present in the membrane fraction, the LppB forms in the PG-fraction without β-ME showed predicted molecular weight doubling the size of LppB in samples with the reducing agent (Fig. 3A). The lack of species compatible with the LppB dimer in extracts of wild type cells (Fig. 3A) was consistent with the low LppB:LppA ratio of ∼1:400 estimated from the MS/MS data(Fig. 2B-C).

Next, we sought to confirm whether the single free cysteine of mature LppB, the C78 residue, is involved in formation of the LppB-LppB dimer. To this aim, we engineered new S. Typhimurium strains derived from the ΔlppAΔlppB double mutant expressing from plasmid either LppA, LppB or a LppB-C78R variant. As expected, the Lpp-C78R variant did not show bands of higher molecular size in the absence of β-ME (Fig. 3B), supporting the requirement of C78 to form the disulphide bridge.

We also determined the presence of LppB-LppB homodimers in vivo using isogenic ΔlppB and ΔlppA single mutants expressing exclusively LppA or LppB from their native chromosomal locations (see Fig. 1C). To this purpose, we incubated intact cells with the reducing agent 1,4-dithiothreitol (DTT) and processed the samples in the absence/presence of 4′-acetamido-4′- maleimidylstilbene-2,2′-disulfonic acid (AMS), an alkylating agent that reacts with the sulfhydryl group of free cysteines. Any free cysteine not involved in disulphide bridge formation binds AMS, contributing to increase the apparent molecular weight of the protein (Pucciarelli et al., 2017). Western blotting showed in the ΔlppB mutant incubated with AMS a minor band of higher molecular weight irrespectively of the previous addition of DTT (Fig. 3C). This band was interpreted as AMS bound to the C21 of LppA in the pre-lipoprotein (unprocessed) stage. In the case of LppB, DTT treatment abrogated most of the C78-C78 disulphide bridges, making accessible the resulting monomer for binding of AMS to C78 (Fig. 3C). Unlike LppA, the signal of AMS-bound LppB monomer of the DTT-treated samples was visualized with a stronger signal (Fig. 3C). This is compatible with binding of AMS to the C78 residue. In samples without DTT, the relative amount of LppB with AMS-bound resembled that of LppA, with minor shifts probably corresponding to the proportion of molecules not forming disulphide bridge or pre-LppB precursors.

To definitively prove that C78 contributes to the formation of LppB-LppB dimers, we performed in vivo experiments incubating cells with DTT and then with AMS using isogenic ΔlppAΔlppB strains expressing from plasmid LppA, LppB or an LppB-C78R variant (Fig. 3C). Consistent with the presence of a second “non-lipidated” cysteine residue (C78), all LppB in the +DTT + AMS sample show gel shift (Fig. 3C). Moreover, most LppB molecules seem to form the C78-C78 bridge under this condition since no monomer was observed in the minus (−) DTT samples (Fig. 3C). Intriguingly, a faint band was visible in the -DTT + AMS sample of LppB, supporting formation of the C78-C78 disulphide bridge in a stage preceding lipidation of residue C22 (Fig. 3C). Altogether, these results obtained in vitro with or without reducing agent and in vivo adding reducing agent to intact cells, support the existence in S. Typhimurium of LppB-LppB dimers stabilized by a C78-C78 intermolecular bridge.

LppB is covalently bound to the S. Typhimurium PG in its C-terminal Lys79 (K79) by the L,D-transpeptidase LdtB

LdtA, LdtB and LdtC, formerly named ErfK, YbiS and YcfS, respectively, were shown in E. coli to covalently anchor Braun's Lpp to the PG (Magnet et al., 2008, 2007). Based on this evidence, we generated a S. Typhimurium isogenic mutants defective in each of these three L,D-transpeptidases (ΔldtA, ΔldtB, and ΔldtC) and expressing exclusively either LppA or LppB. Unlike the case of LppA, the anchoring of LppB to the PG was abrogated in the mutant lacking LdtB, with concomitant loss of the characteristic ladder corresponding to protein species that are covalently bound to PG (Fig. 4A). Consistently with the data reported for E. coli, the lack of the three L,D-transpetidases LdtA, LdtB and LdtC in a strain expressing LppA from plasmid resulted in loss of anchoring of this murein lipoprotein to the PG (Fig. 4B). Altogether, these results support the involvement of known L,D-transpeptidases in the covalent binding of both LppA and LppB to the PG of S. Typhimurium. However, unlike LppA for which no apparent difference exists for any of the three L,D-transpeptidases, LppB is a preferred substrate of LdtB.

S. Typhimurium LppB shares with LppA the presence of a C-terminal lysine, K79 and K78, respectively (Fig. 1A). This C-terminal lysine is absolutely essential in E. coli for covalent binding of Braun's Lpp to the PG (Braun, 1975; Braun and Rehn, 1969). To verify whether a similar role is played by K79 of LppB, we generated an LppB-K79R variant that was expressed from plasmid in the S. Typhimurium ΔlppAΔlppB double mutant. In contrast to wild type LppB and LppB-C78R, which partitioned between membrane and PG fractions, the LppB-K79R variant was visualized mostly in the membrane fraction without forms of higher molecular weight in the PG fraction (Fig. 4B). These results confirmed the requirement of the C-terminal K79 for covalent anchoring of LppB to the PG of S. Typhimurium.

LppA and LppB of S. Typhimurium binds with distinct specificity to cross-linked muropeptides

Given the strict recognition of LppB by LdtB, we raised the possibility that LppB could bind to distinct muropeptides compared to LppA. To test this, we prepared from S. Typhimurium wild type cells highly pure PG-fractions as material insoluble after boiling in 4 % SDS, therefore enriched in proteins strongly bound to PG. These extracts were resolved by SDS/PAGE and the bands migrating around the 11 kDa region individually processed for trypsin digestion and subsequent UPLC-MS/MS analysis. As negative control sample not expected to contain any Lpp species covalently bound to PG, we included the protein band visualized in membrane (Mb) extracts that run with a predicted molecular weight of ∼7 kDa (Fig. 5A). Trypsin digestion products of three protein bands, L1 (Mb fraction), L2 and L3 (PG fraction), were fractionated by UPLC and analysed by MS/MS (Fig. 5A). We resolved in all bands two internal LppA peptides (peak 1, VDQLSNDVNAMR; peak 2, IDQLSSDVQTLNAK) and three unique masses (peaks PG1, PG2 and PG3) in bands L2 and L3, compatible with a -Y-R-K- terminal sequence bound to: i) uncross-linked muropeptide M3 [N-acetyl-glucosamine (GlcNAc)-N-acetyl-muramic acid (MurNAc)-L-Ala-D-Glu-meso-DAP-] (peak PG1); ii) cross-linked muropeptide D33 [GlcNAc-MurNAc-L-Ala-D-Glu-meso-DAP-meso-DAP-D-Glu-L-Ala-MurNAc-GlcNAc] (peak PG2); and, iii) cross-linked muropeptide D43 [GlcNAc-MurNAc-L-Ala-D-Glu-meso-DAP-D-Ala-meso-DAP-D-Glu-L-Ala-MurNAc-GlcNAc] (peak PG3). As it was reported for Braun's Lpp of E. coli (Braun, 1975; Braun and Rehn, 1969), the C-terminal lysine (K78) of S. Typhimurium LppA is in all cases linked by its ε-amino group to the α-carboxyl group present in the L-center of the meso-DAP residue. A distinctive feature for assigning the muropeptide signature to a particular compound was the presence among the fragmentation products of a mass of 204.08 Da and its derivate fragments, corresponding unequivocally to the O-GlcNAc oxonium ion moiety. An example is shown in Fig. 5B for product of peak PG1. The structure resolved for compound PG1, consisting of muropeptide M3-[K-R-Y], in also shown in Fig. 5C. Thus, UPCL-MS/MS analyses unequivocally proved that LppA of S. typhimurium, which a C-terminus ending in -Y-R-KCOOH, binds covalently to canonical muropeptides of known structure like M3, D33 and D43.

We next sought to identify the nature of muropeptides to which LppB binds. To that purpose, we used a ΔlppAΔlppB double mutant bearing a plasmid expressing LppB. Four protein bands, one in the Mb fraction (L4) and three in the PG fraction (L5, L6 and L7), were excised from the gel and processed for UPLC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 6A). A total of six different compounds unique to the PG fraction sample (PG4 to PG9), were resolved for bands L6 and L7 (Fig. 6A). Representative fragmentation spectra of two of these masses, PG5 and PG6, are shown in Fig. 6B. Unfortunately, in the analysis of band L5 and, despite identifying two internal peptides of LppB (peaks 1,2), we were unable to detect any fragmentation product at m/z 204.08 corresponding to the O-GlcNAc oxonium ion (Fig. 6A). Compound of band L5 was therefore concluded as uncharacterized LppB-muropeptide of yet unknown nature.

Besides this enigmatic L5 compound involving LppB, we noted two additional differences of LppB in comparison to LppA. First, the peaks PG4, PG6, PG7 and PG9 were resolved as compounds with the I-C-K signature bound to uncross-linked and cross-linked muropeptides but with mass increases of either 14.02 Da (peaks PG6 and PG9) or 30.02 Da (peaks PG4 and PG7) (Fig. 6A). This difference was registered in the respective fragmentation spectra, as exemplified by the comparison between spectra of PG5 and PG6 (Fig. 6B). In their resolved structures, corresponding to LppB bound to uncross-linked muropeptide M3, the increase of 14.02 Da remains visible in the fragmentation product containing the C78-K79 dipeptide (m/z = 321.16, Fig. 6B). Since the C78 residue is carbamidomethylated in its sulfhydryl group during sample processing for proteomics, our interpretation for this modification is that there is addition of a methyl group to the free carboxy group (-COOH) of terminal lysine K79, resulting in formation of a methyl ester, -COOCH3 (Fig. 6B-D). The mass of 30.02 Da, which in all cases it was mutually exclusive to that of 14.02 Da, may indicate the presence an additional atom of oxygen to the methyl ester. A second difference between LppA and LppB was the nature of the cross-linked muropeptides to which they bind. Whereas LppA binds to D33 and D43 dimers, LppB binds to the D43 dimer (Figs. 5A, 6A), thus apparently avoiding muropeptides having the 3,3-meso-DAP-meso-DAP cross-link.

Due to its proximity to the terminal K79, we hypothesized that the C78 residue of LppB could be responsible for the differences in the mode this murein lipoprotein binds to PG. To test this, we monitored the types of muropeptides to which the LppB-C78R variant binds. Surprisingly, no differences were observed in the pattern of muropeptides to LppB-C78R binds in comparison to LppA. Thus, no increase in mass of either 14.02 Da or 30.02 Da was observed in any of the resolved structures. Moreover, LppB-C78R binds to both the D43 and D33 dimer cross-linked muropeptides (Fig. S1-A-C, supplementary information). The only exception that is shared by LppB and LppB-C78R is the protein band of the PG fraction not resulting in fragmentation product of 204.08 Da, which in the case of LppB-C78R is band L9, equivalent to L5 of LppB, of yet unknown structure (Fig. 6A and Fig. S1 supplementary information).

In conclusion, apart from the distinct C-terminal regions of LppA, LppB and LppB-C78R, the muropeptide part of the different compounds analysed by UPLC-MS/MS was undistinguishable for most the uncross-linked (Fig. 5, Fig. 6 and S1 supplementary information) and cross-linked (Fig. S2 supplementary information) types. Some exceptions were however identified consisting in O-methylation of LppB in the K79 residue and the apparent lower efficiency for binding of this alternative murein lipoprotein to D33 cross-linked muropeptides.

Proliferation of murein lipoprotein variants in the genus Salmonella

We restricted in a first phase the biochemical characterization to LppA and LppB of serovar Typhimurium, which is only one of the more than 1500 serovars that form S. enterica subspecies enterica. The genus Salmonella is divided in two species, S. enterica and S. bongori, the former composed by pathogens of warm- and cold-blooded animals (Lan et al., 2009; Tindall et al., 2005). The serovars of S. enterica subspecies enterica, also referred as subspecies I, accounts for most infections in warm-blooded animals (Alikhan et al., 2018; Eng et al., 2015). These serovars cause two main type of diseases, either systemic or restricted at the gastrointestinal level, known as typhoidal and non-typhoidal, respectively (Baumler and Fang, 2013; Dougan and Baker, 2014; Gal-Mor et al., 2014). To date, genomic analyses propose up to ten S. enterica subspecies, accounting for enterica (I), salamae (II), arizonae (IIIa), diarizonae (IIIb), houtenae (IV), indica (VI), londinensis (VII), brasiliensis (VIII), hibernicus (IX) and essexiensis (X) (Alikhan et al., 2018; Pearce et al., 2021; Tindall et al., 2005). More than 2500 serovars have been reported for the entire Salmonella genus, many showing distinct level of host adaptation (Stevens and Kingsley, 2021). For example, most serovars of “non-enterica subspecies” of S. enterica colonize cold-blooded animals (Lamas et al., 2018).

Given the enormous taxonomic diversity of the genus Salmonella, we hypothesized about the possibility of additional murein Lpp variants in some species, subspecies or serovars. To this aim, we initially analysed a total of 358,030 Salmonella genomes downloaded from the Enterobase website (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/). We considered for further analysis 158,624 of those genomes filtering with parameters such as unequivocal annotation of the subspecies and accession to genome sequences required to perform BLAST searches (Table S1, supplementary information). These genomes were examined using BLASTP and LppA of S. Typhimurium (UniProt entry: A0A0H3NCM8) as query. As threshold values in the search, we considered length ≥ 70 aa residues, ≥ 80 % identity to query and p value ≤0.05. We also filtered for hits with a terminal lysine (K) residue to rule out putative partial sequences derived from ends of contigs. Using these criteria, we obtained a total of 332,426 hits as LppA homologues (Table S2, supplementary information). The diversity of hits was notorious with variable sequences mapping in most instances in the C-terminal region (Fig. 7A). Up to 454 unique Lpp sequence variants were identified among the pool of homologues, representing a total of 31 distinct C-terminal sequences when considering the last eight residues (Fig. 7A). Despite this variability, there was some consensus in defined positions of the C-terminus regarding the amino acid type. Thus, in all cases Lys (K) occupies the last position whereas Arg (R) or Cys (C) alternate in position −1 respect this Lys(K); a positively charged residue [Arg (R) / His (H) / Lys (K)] in position −3; uncharged residues [Val (V) / Ala (A)] in position −5; and, a positive charged residue [Asp (N) / Lys (K)] in position −7 (Fig. 7B).

Based on the marked variability detected in the C-terminus, we tested whether another alternative Lpp with a different C-terminus compared to LppA and LppB of serovar Typhimurium could covalently bind to PG. To that purpose, we used serovar Gallinarum wild type strain UY0169, a gift of Dr. Laura Betancor, Universidad de Monteviedo, Uruguay. The genome of this strain shows two tandemly located genes encoding an LppA orthologue terminating in -NQATKYRKCOOH and an alternative Lpp variant with a C-terminal sequence of -KVVSHVRKCOOH, which, in this case, we named Lpp2 for clarity purposes. A derivate strain lacking both lipoproteins was generated to subsequently introduce a plasmid expressing only Lpp2. Like LppB of serovar Typhimurium, Lpp2 was detected in both membrane (Mb) and PG fractions (Fig. 7C), indicating that Lpp2 of serovar Gallinarum, similarly to LppB of serovar Typhimurium, binds covalently to the PG.

We next examined the evolution within the genus Salmonella of the 454 unique murein Lpp variants identified in our BLASTP search. The phylogenetic tree depicted three major branches (Fig. 7D). The first, a group of “canonical” Lpp terminating in either -KYRKCOOH or -KFRKCOOH that exhibits the highest identity to Braun's Lpp of E.coli. Examples are LppA in serovar Typhimurium and, for -KFRKCOOH, some serovars of S. enterica subspecies II (Table S2, supplementary information). Second, a group of Lpp variants with slightly variable residues in the four last positions of the C-terminus, -HVRKCOOH/-RVRKCOOH /-RARKCOOH. Third, a branch characterized by the presence of murein Lpp variants with a Cys residue in position −1 of the C-terminus, -RICKCOOH /-HICKCOOH, exemplified by LppB of serovar Typhimurium (Fig. 7D). The second branch also included a few variants with an -HICKCOOH terminus.

Interestingly, murein Lpp variants distinct to LppA are distributed in many serovars of subspecies I (enterica) with only a very small representation (less than 5 % of genomes) in other S. enterica subspecies, II, IIIa, IIIb, IV and VI (Fig. 7E). No gene encoding alternative murein Lpp was found in genomes of the other subspecies or in the species S. bongori (Fig. 7E). A fascinating fact was that most annotated genomes within a particular serovar encode the same type of Lpp variant (Table S2, supplementary information). It was also relatively common in some serovars the presence of up to three murein Lpp encoded by tandemly located genes and belonging to each of three major linages uncovered by the phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 7D, Table S2, supplementary information). In a few rare cases, we also detected serovars of subspecies I encoding exclusively the canonical murein Lpp of the -KYRKCOOH group (Table S2, supplementary information). Taken together, these data provide evidence for an unprecedented high number of murein Lpp variants in the genus Salmonella, which evolved at a larger extent in S. enterica subspecies enterica (I).

Discussion

In this study, we have explored multiple murein Lpps encoded by the subspecies and serovars that compose the genus Salmonella. The results are in line with the capacity of these bacteria to use specialized enzymes or proteins linked to peptidoglycan (PG) metabolism. This is the case of PG synthases involved in morphogenesis, named PBP2SAL and PBP3SAL, which drive cell elongation and division in acidic environments independently of the canonical PBP2 and PBP3 (Castanheira et al., 2017; Castanheira and García-Del Portillo, 2023). The presence in serovar Typhimurium of tandemly located genes encoding murein lipoprotein homologues took our attention for investigating in depth this phenomenon of duplication and apparent divergent evolution. We also considered the fact that the previous studies with S. Typhimurium mutants lacking lppA, lppB or both, was limited to examine virulence-related phenotypes (Sha et al., 2004; Fadl et al., 2005b, Fadl et al., 2005a) but not addressing the biochemistry or the mode anchoring to the PG of these two murein lipoproteins.

To our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence supporting the simultaneous production in the cell surface of S. typhimurium of two murein lipoproteins in physiological conditions. The genetic organization of the lppA-lppB locus, with two predicted strong Rho-independent transcriptional terminators and one mapping in the intergenic region, may explain the preferred production of LppA. This mode of regulation was corroborated by the MS/MS data, which showed a LppA:LppB ratio of ∼400:1 in a subcellular fraction enriched in PG-associated proteins. With the available data, we cannot however rule out conditions in which LppB could be the preferred murein lipoprotein instead of LppA. In such scenario, lppB expression should be dependent on a promoter distinct from that upstream of lppA, probably internal in lppA and requiring opening of the hairpin located in the lppA-lppB intergenic region. A large variety of conditions tested, including limited nutrients, different pH values, high osmolarity and presence of stressors such as reagents generating oxygen-reactive species, failed to achieve preferred expression of LppB versus LppA (Fig. S3, supplementary information). These observations favour the idea of LppB as specialized lipoprotein probably responding to a unique combination of environmental cues.

The data obtained in a S. Typhimurium strain expressing exclusively LppB showed that this murein lipoprotein forms a C78-C78 disulphide bridge, resulting in homodimers in a fraction of both the free and PG-bound pools. Considering structural evidence that supports folding of E.coli Lpp into an α-helical trimeric coiled-coil (Shu et al., 2000), only two of three LppB monomers fitting to that structure could theoretically form the disulphide bridge. It should also be considered the assembly of LppB in complexes with an “even” number of monomers. Interestingly, the co-existence of homodimers besides the trimeric state was previously demonstrated for Braun's Lpp in E. coli using cross-linking reagents like disuccinimidyl suberate, DSS (Cowles et al., 2011). This study also provided evidence for a free Lpp population being surface exposed and not interacting with the PG-bound pool (Cowles et al., 2011). It would be of interest to address whether such organization is maintained in S. Typhimurium for LppA and LppB and, in addition, if both murein lipoproteins could interact in the form of heterocomplexes.

At present, we are uncertain about the advantage for LppB of forming a disulphide bridge. We could speculate that such bridge could impose steric constrains facilitating or preventing interactions with specific envelope components. Atomic simulations of the E. coli envelope support interactions of Braun's Lpp with OmpA dimers and a role of this murein lipoprotein facilitating the interaction of OmpA monomers with the PG (Samsudin et al., 2017). Whether LppB could play similar structural roles in S. Typhimurium is worth to be investigated in the future. The Lpp-C78R variant generated in this study and simulations in the presence/absence of LppA could be valuable for this purpose.

The MS/MS data revealed some differential features between LppA and LppB regarding their covalent binding to the PG. The binding of LppB to 3,3-cross-linked muropeptides was below the detection limit, inferring either no anchoring to this type of muropeptide or a much lower efficiency compared to LppA. Intriguingly, the ldtE (ynhG) gene encoding an L,D-transpeptidase catalysing the formation of 3,3 cross-links in the PG, maps in opposite orientation to lppA-lppB with only 65 nt in the ldtE-lppB intergenic region. S. Typhimurium expresses ldtE at higher levels compared to lppB (Kroger et al., 2013) and this may explain why lppB expression is low in laboratory conditions. The fact that LppB shows an apparent absence of binding to 3,3-cross-linked muropeptides argues in favour of this potential inverse regulation between LdtE and LppB. This means that conditions that upregulate lppB expression should impact the amount of 3,3-crosslinks in macromolecular PG and the levels of LdtE. The availability of the ΔlppA mutant that overexpresses LppB will be valuable to test these tempting hypotheses.

We also detected modifications of 14.02 and 30.02 Da in the terminal K79 residue of LppB, not seen either in LppA or the LppB-C78R variant. These findings suggest that the formation of the LppB-LppB dimer and the postulated O-methylation of the K79 residue could be related events. In the future, we plan to analyse by MS the band corresponding to monomeric LppB in samples non treated with reducing agent. Despite O-methylation of lysine not being previously reported as a natural modification, we were able to trace an increase in mass of 14.02 Da to a fragmentation product consisting of dipeptide C78-K79 in which cysteine is carbamidomethylated by the iodoacetamide treatment (Fig. 6B, D). Carbamidomethylation, widely used in proteomics, prevents further changes at the highly reactive nucleophilic sulfhydryl group of cysteine. Considering this, the only reactive group of the C78-K79 dipeptide is the hydroxyl present in the carboxyl group of K79 residue, for which we postulated could be methylated. A definitive confirmation for this modification can be obtained by future NMR spectroscopy analyses.

The MS/MS analyses also uncovered a compound in the LppB and LppB-C78R samples associated with binding of the lipoprotein to the PG but with no visualization of the expected mass of 204.8 Da, corresponding to the O-GlcNAc oxonium ion. Because the internal peptides of LppB were identified, this unequivocally proves that it is a unique and unconventional LppB form. Although it could be claimed that it may represent LppB not covalently bound to PG, we see it unlikely given it shows higher electrophoretic mobility than the free LppB form present in the membrane fraction (see Fig. 3 for some examples). In our opinion, this difference sustains a unique type of binding of LppB of the PG yet to be determined.

We also examined the multiplicity of murein lipoproteins in the genus Salmonella. Enigmatically, the large number of additional murein Lpp occurs mainly in S. enterica subspecies enterica (I), formed by serovars that cause disease in warm-blooded animals (Lan et al., 2009; Tindall et al., 2005). Considering that Braun's Lpp is recognized by the host innate immune system via Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) (Abhilasha and Marathe, 2021; Lakshmikanth et al., 2016), it is tempting to speculate about a link between the proliferation of murein lipoproteins and strategies to reduce immune recognition. TLR2 recognizes tri-acylated lipoproteins like Braun's Lpp when forming a heterodimer with another receptor of the same family, TLR1 (Abhilasha and Marathe, 2021). Strikingly, TLR1 homologues are absent in reptiles (Nie et al., 2018), cold-blooded animals that are preferred hosts of most “non-enterica” subspecies of S. enterica (Lamas et al., 2018). This correlation supports the idea of multiplicity of murein Lpp variants in subspecies enterica (I) evolving as strategy to evade host innate defences based on the TLR1-TLR2 receptor. It is also noteworthy that Salmonella is the enteric bacterial pathogen with the highest host range known, capable of colonizing multitude of animals, from amphibians, reptiles, fish and birds to mammals, being this latter the preferred hosts for many serovars. This unique behaviour, not seen in other phylogenetically close γ-proteobacteria of the Enterobacterales order having a single murein Lpp, clearly establishes a link between the increase in murein Lpp variants with expansion of the host range.

We also observed that the divergence in the 31 Lpp variants identified in the genus Salmonella increase notoriously in the C-terminal region. Why have these changes accumulated at this defined region of the protein? We do not yet understand the evolutionary pressures that forced such evolution, but it could be argued that it is the C-terminal region that it is recognized by the immune system. This line of reasoning agrees with data obtained by Cowles et al., which demonstrated in E.coli that a deletion of the last four residues (55–58) of mature Braun's Lpp, -KRYKCOOH, is sufficient to prevent binding of externally-added biotin (Cowles et al., 2011). This observation is consistent with a minor part of the C-terminal region of Lpp exposed to the surface, precisely the region with greater variability in the 31 Lpp variants identified in our study. This tentative model would also imply recognition of murein Lpp on the surface of intact bacterial cells as opposed to free circulating murein Lpp.

Lastly, the three groups of Lpp variants in the genus Salmonella identified in the phylogenetic analyses point to non-redundant roles in the biology of the bacterium. Thus, whereas the Lpp belonging to the group more identical to Lpp of E.coli is conserved in all members of the genus due probably to its structural role, for the case of “alternative” Lpps, either there is a representant or, when there are two, they are from separate phylogenetic groups. An attractive future challenge will be to understand the role of these multiple alternative murein Lpp in the genus Salmonella, predicted by this work to modulate the interaction of this successful pathogen with the host.

Methods and materials

Bacterial strains and plasmids

All S. Typhimurium/E. coli strains and plasmids used in this study are indicated in Table S3 of supplementary information. The S. Typhimurium strains derive from the virulent wild type SV5015 (Gonzalo-Asensio et al., 2013), a prototroph His+ strain of the virulent strain SL1344 isolated from calf (Hoiseth and Stocker, 1981). Unless otherwise indicated, bacteria were grown in Lysogeny broth (LB), composed of 1 % (w/v) casein peptone, 0.5 % (w/v) yeast extract and 0.5 % (w/v) sodium chloride. When needed, media were supplemented with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and the antibiotics ampicillin (Amp, 100 μg/mL), chloramphenicol (Cm, 10 μg/mL),or kanamycin (Km, 30 μg/mL).

Monitoring of LppA and LppB production in different growth conditions

To determine whether LppB production responds to defined growth conditions, the S. Typhimurium wild type strain SV5015 was exposed to different stressors. For hydrogen peroxide shock, overnight cultures were diluted in LB medium to a initial OD600 of 0.02 and incubated for 2.5 h (OD600 ∼ 0.3). Hydrogen peroxide shock was induced by adding 1 mM H2O2 and incubation for 12 min.

For microanaerobic conditions, overnight cultures grown in LB without shaking in tubes filled to the top were diluted to a final OD600 of 0.02 in fresh medium and further growth for additional 6 h (final OD600 ∼ 0.2). To test the effect of different salts, overnight cultures grown in LB were washed twice with PBS, diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.02 and incubated for 3 h in YP medium (5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L peptone) supplemented with either 1 mM CuSO4; 200 mM MgCl2; 200 mM Na2SO4; 200 mM KCl; or, 200 mM NaNO3. Following these treatments, bacteria were processed for preparation of total protein extract samples and Western blotting in non-reducing conditions as described below.

DNA manipulation techniques

All primers used are listed in Table S4, supplementary information. PCR were performed using Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs) according to manufacturer instructions. PCR fragments were purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel, ref. 740,609.50) and the concentration measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Plasmids were purified using the NucleoSpin Plasmid (NoLid) (Macherey-Nagel, ref. 740,499.50).

The genes lppA and lppB of S. Typhimurium strain SL1344 were amplified by high-fidelity PCR from genomic DNA using primers listed in Table S4, supplementary information. These primers also contained the restriction sites for BamHI and HindIII for cloning in the pUHE21 vector under the control of an IPTG-inducible promoter (Soncini et al., 1995). In the case of the lppB-C78R variant, the mutated codon was designed in the reverse primer. For lppB of serovar Gallinarum the lpp2 gene was amplified from S. Gallinarum UY169, isolated from egg, a gift from Laura Betancor (Universidad de Medicina, Montevideo, Uruguay).

All constructs were sequenced to confirm absence of undesired mutations. Purified DNA fragments were sequenced by Macrogen (https://dna.macrogen.com/esp/).

Construction of knock-out S. typhimurium mutant strains by one-step inactivation

Gene inactivation was carried out following the one-step inactivation protocol, as described (Datsenko and Wanner, 2000). This procedure leaves a scar of 80 extra nucleotides after the recombination, corresponding to short flippase recognition target (FRT) sequence (Cherepanov and Wackernagel, 1995).

Construction of scarless S. typhimurium mutant strains

For the construction of the ΔlppA clean scarless mutant (Fig. 1C), the method described by Hoffmann et al. was used (Hoffmann et al., 2017). S. Typhimurium SV5015 was first transformed with plasmid pWRG730, which is thermosensitive and encodes a heat-induced λ-Red recombinase that was induced at 42 °C for 12 min. This strain was electroporated with a DNA fragment amplified from plasmid pWRG717, which contains a I-SceI cleavage site and the aph gene (conferring kanamycin resistance) flanked by homologous sequences targeting the chromosomal region to delete. Transformants were selected at 30 °C on Km- and Cm-containing LB plates. Integration of the I-SceI-aph cassette at the desired chromosomal locus was confirmed by PCR.

A transformant clone with the pWRG730 plasmid (CmR) and an adequate expression of I-SceI, observed as a deficient growth when induced with anhydrotetracycline (AHT), was selected. Competent bacteria of this transformant clone expressing the λ-Red recombinase (42 °C, 12 min) were electroporated with a DNA fusion formed by 400 bp of the precise upstream region of the deleted region and 30 bp from the downstream region in its 3′ end. Transformants were selected in AHT and Cm-containing LB plates at 30 °C. These conditions allow the expression of I-SceI endonuclease that cleaves DNA at the I-SceI recognition site, allowing only growth of recombinants that have lost the I-SceI-aph cassette. Finally, pWRG730 plasmid was cured by incubation at 42 °C. Correct deletion of the target region was confirmed by PCR and sequencing.

Total protein extracts

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 1:100 in 2 mL of fresh LB medium and grown to late-exponential phase (OD600 ∼ 0.5–0.6). The bacteria were harvested (6800 x g, 4 min, 4 °C), washed in PBS pH 7.4 and resuspended in 100 μL of Laemmli lysis buffer per optical density unit (ca. ∼109 bacteria/unit). Samples were boiled at 100 °C for 5–10 min and centrifuged to remove cell debris (6800 xg, 5 min, RT).

Isolation of PG fraction enriched in peptidoglycan-associated proteins

The PG fraction was obtained as previously described (Pucciarelli and García-del Portillo, 2003). Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures were diluted in 80 mL of LB to an initial OD600 of 0.01 and incubated at 37 °C. After 2 h, 1 mM IPTG was added to induce the expression of the proteins encoded by the pUHE21 vector. After 3 h, when cells reached early stationary phase (OD600 ∼ 1.0), the bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 x g for 15 min, 4 °C and resuspended in 1.4 mL of PBS pH 7.4. This solution was gently added drop by drop to an equal volume of boiling 8 % (v/v) SDS. The PG was pelleted by ultracentrifugation at room temperature (307,600 x g, 30 min, RT) and, washed repeatedly (3–5 times) in warm distilled water followed by ultracentrifugation until it was free of SDS as denoted by absence of bubbling upon agitation. The PG pellet was resuspended in 0.4 mL of muramidase buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate pH 4.9) and digested with muramidase Cellosyl (Hoechst) (20 μg/mL, 37 °C, 18 h). Reaction was stopped by boiling the samples for 20 min. Denatured proteins were recovered in the pellet by centrifugation (10,000 xg, 10 min, RT) and resuspended in 100 μL of Laemmli buffer without reducing agents. Samples were split in two equal aliquots and 5 % (v/v) of β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) added to one of them.

Subcellular fractions enriched in membrane proteins

This fraction was obtained as described elsewhere (Pucciarelli and García-del Portillo, 2003). Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted in 5 mL of LB to a OD600 of 0.01 and incubated at 37 °C. After 2 h, 1 mM of IPTG was added to induce expression of the proteins encoded in the pUHE21 vector. After 3 h, when cells reached the early stationary phase (OD600 ∼ 1.0), bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 xg for 15 min, 4 °C and resuspended in 20 mL of PBS pH 7.4. Cells were subsequently disrupted by three passes on the French Press. Unbroken cells were removed within the pellet by two steps of gentle centrifugation (6300 xg, 5 min, 4 °C). Membrane fraction was separated from supernatant by centrifugation (150,000 xg, 40 min, 4 °C). Pellet was resuspended in 400 μL of Laemmli buffer without reducing agent. Samples were then split in two and β-ME was added to one of them to a final concentration of 5 % (v/v).

Protein alkylation using 4-acetamido-4′-maleimidylstilbene-2,2′-disulfonic acid (AMS)

Aliquots of 100 μL containing ∼5–6 × 108 bacteria grown until late-exponential phase (OD600 ∼ 0.5–0.6) were used for this assay. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to two of these aliquots to a final concentration of 100 mM and incubated for 10 min on ice followed by 10 min at 100 °C. Afterwards, the four aliquots were incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with 10 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) to preserve the in vivo oxidation status of proteins. Precipitated proteins were centrifuged at 14,000 x g, 15 min, 4 °C and pellets washed once with 1 mL of ice-cold acetone and air-dried. One of the aliquots previously treated with DTT and another non-treated were resuspended in 20 μL of alkylating buffer (150 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 2 % (w/v) SDS) containing 15 mM AMS. The two remaining aliquots were resuspended in the same alkylating buffer without AMS. After 1 h incubation for at room temperature, 6.7 μL of Laemmli buffer without β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) was added to all samples and used for Western blotting.

SDS-PAGE and western blotting

To resolve the distinct forms of Lpp (∼7.0 kDa), we used 15 % acrylamide gels and Tris/Tricine electrophoretic system (Schägger and von Jagow, 1987). Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes and incubated with the corresponding antibodies. Detection was performed by Clarity Western ECL Substrate chemiluminescence kit (Bio Rad, ref. 1705061) in a ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio Rad). Once used for Western blotting, the PVDF membranes were stained with Coomassie blue solution to verify loading of equivalent amount across samples. The primary antibody used was rabbit polyclonal anti-E. coli Lpp (1:150,000; Dr. S. Daefler, University of Texas Southwestern, USA). Goat polyclonal anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (ref. 1706515, 1:50,000, BioRad) was used as the secondary antibody.

Parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) analysis

Protein extracts corresponding to PG fractions were digested in solution with trypsin according to a standard protocol. Tryptic peptides were dried in a speed-vacuum system and resuspended at 200 μg/μL, according to QUBIT quantification (Thermofisher Scientific). 5 μL of each sample (equivalent to 1 μg) were loaded online on a C18 PepMap 300 μm I.D. 0.3 × 5 mm trapping column (5 μm, 100 Å, Thermo Scientific) and analysed by LC-ESI MSMS using a Thermo Ultimate 3000 RSLC nanoUPLC coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris OE240 mass spectrometer. Peptides were separated on a 15 cm, 75 μm ID column, with a flow rate of 250 nL/min and a 50 min long gradient. The liquid chromatographic system was coupled via a nanospray source to the mass spectrometer. The mass-spec method used worked in PRM mode (parallel reaction monitoring) monitoring two LppA (IDQLSSDVQTLNAK and VDQLSNDVNAMR) and two LppB (IDQLSSDVQTLSAK and VEQLSNDVNAMR) unique peptides. Experimental settings for PRM analysis, and selection and extraction of each of the transition areas (Table S5, Supplementary Information), was carried out with the Skyline v21.2 software (MacLean et al., 2010).

Processing of gel-excised samples for proteomic analysis

Protein extracts corresponding to membrane and PG fractions were run on a denaturing 15 % acrylamide SDS-PAGE using the Tris-Tricine system. Up to 10 protein bands were excised from the gels and the slices individually loaded in a 96-well plate. Tryptic digestion was performed using an Opentrons OT-2 robot and the protocol described by Shevchenko et al. (Shevchenko et al., 1996). Following digestion, peptides were extracted, pooled at the sample level, dried by speed-vac centrifugation and stored at −20 °C until needed. They were later analysed by LC-ESI-MS/MS. Samples were subjected to independent liquid chromatography coupled to electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC ESI-MS/MS) analysis using an Ultimate 3000 nano HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled online to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All instrument parameters were controlled by Xcalibur software, version 4.6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, California). Muropeptides were loaded onto a 50 cm × 75 μm Easy-spray PepMap C18 analytical column at 50 °C and separated at a flow rate of 250 nL/min using a 60 min gradient ranging from 2 % to 35 % mobile phase B [mobile phase A: 0.1 % formic acid (FA) in water; mobile phase B: 0.1 % FA in 80 % acetonitrile (ACN)]. All mobile phases were LCMS grade. The loading solvent was 0.1 % FA in 2 % ACN and injection volume was 5 μL.

Data acquisition was performed using a data-dependent top-20 method, in full scan positive mode, scanning 250 to 1200 m/z. Survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 at m/z 200, with normalized Automatic Gain Control (AGC) target (%) of 300 and a maximum injection time (IT) set to AUTO. The top 20 most intense (including 1–4 charge states) ions from each MS1 scan were selected and fragmented via higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). Resolution for MS2 (HCD) spectra was set to 30,000 at m/z 200, with AGC target of 50 and a maximum ion injection time set to AUTO. First mass was set to 50 m/z. Isolation of precursors was performed with a window of 1 m/z, dynamic exclusion of 1 s and the HCD collision energy was 24.

Genomic analyses

To analyse the distribution of murein Lpp-encoding genes in the genus Salmonella, the information from 664,948 genomes of S. enterica was retrieved from the Enterobase database (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/) on June 2025. A subset of 358,030 genomes was manually selected based on the availability of annotations of subspecies and serovar as well as accession to the sequences to perform searches. The NCBI biosample ID of each genome was used to download the associated assembly as protein fasta file, if available in RefSeq, or otherwise GenBank. In total, 158,624 genome assemblies representing a significant diversity of S. enterica subspecies and serovars were included in the following analysis.

Lpp distribution and phylogenetic tree

The dataset of the selected 158,624 genomes in protein fasta format was interrogated using BLASTp from BLAST+ suite (Camacho et al., 2009), using LppA from S. Typhimurium SL1344 as query (Uniprot accession: A0A0H3NCM8). Murein Lpp homologues were retrieved using an identity threshold greater than 80 % and a p-value cutoff of less than 0.05. To prevent the inclusion of partial Lpp sequences as hits, those shorter than 70 aa or not having a Lys (K) residue in its C-terminus, were discarded. Using these criteria, 332,426 sequences were identified as full-length proteins representing murein Lpp homologues.

A custom script was used to group all the identical sequences, retaining only one representative sequence per group for further analysis. The representative sequences were later aligned using MAFFT-L-INS-I(Kuraku et al., 2013), which allowed the identification of 31 different C-terminal motifs based on comparison of the last eight residues. A phylogenetic tree was them constructed from the protein alignment built using the IQ-TREE web server (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016) visualized using iTOL v.6 (Letunic and Bork, 2021). Information on the number of identical proteins per group was manually added to the tree. Additionally, all sequences were traced back to their source genomes and classified according to their corresponding C-terminal motif, allowing to study their distribution across different Salmonella subspecies and serovars (Table S2, supplementary information).

To obtain a phylogenetic tree of the Salmonella species and subspecies, a representative genome from each S. enterica subspecies plus one from the species S. bongori were selected to build a Maximum Likelihood (ML) tree using a reference-free approach with the kSNP4.1 software (Hall and Nisbet, 2023) with a k-mer size of 21, as previously described (Hudson et al., 2023).
