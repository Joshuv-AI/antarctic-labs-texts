Digitally assessed home FEV1 to identify the cause of poorly controlled asthma: a protocol paper for a prospective replicate cohort study
Authors: Cara M Gill, Patrick J Kerr, Ciara Ottewill, Vincent Brennan, Helen Doherty, Orla Smith, Elaine MacHale, Breda Cushen, Dorothy Ryan, Garrett Greene, Richard W Costello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA significant proportion of patients with asthma remain uncontrolled despite treatment with inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators. Multiple reasons exist for this. Suboptimal inhaler adherence, inadequate inhaler technique and continuous exposure to allergens are often found to contribute to poor asthma control. Additionally, symptoms from some comorbid conditions, such as obesity, GORD and deconditioning, may be perceived as arising from asthma. The first priority should always be to confirm if asthma is the cause of the patients’ current symptoms.Methods & analysisIn this protocol paper, we describe the design of a prospective cohort replicate study. We will test the hypothesis that digitally acquired data on lung function and medication use can better establish that asthma is the cause of symptoms, rather than guideline-recommended practice. Introduction

A significant proportion of patients with asthma remain uncontrolled despite treatment with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and bronchodilators (BDs). However, it is estimated that only 5%–10% of these patients actually have severe asthma, requiring advanced therapies in an attempt to achieve control of airway inflammation and variable airflow obstruction.1,3 Most difficult-to-treat asthma cases remain uncontrolled due to insufficient treatment adherence, poor inhaler technique or even initial misdiagnosis.4 Overdiagnosis and under-diagnosis are common and have been shown to be present in 30% of this cohort.5 Mischaracterisation of patients may arise due to the absence of a universally accepted gold standard diagnostic criteria and the temporal variability in the presence of asthma features.

In the evaluation of people suspected to have uncontrolled asthma, clinicians need to collect data from multiple sources to confirm the diagnosis. A comprehensive assessment should involve clinical evaluation of patient history, detection of evidence of type 2 (T2) inflammation and evidence of variable expiratory airflow limitation. There is discordance between the three major guidelines on how to best assess these variables and in what order.6,8 In the absence of universally accepted gold standard diagnostic criteria, this epistemological problem is susceptible to circular reasoning. In practice, clinicians follow the theory best supported by current evidence and arrive at the inference to the best explanation.9 10 Using this method, a clinician collects the above information and repeatedly includes an assessment of response to treatment. To be truly comprehensive, an assessment should also incorporate assessment of inhaler adherence and technique.11 Response to treatment should also consider treatment for other conditions that may be the cause of patients’ current symptoms. The obvious disadvantages of this approach are that it is time-consuming, placing a burden on already strained healthcare resources.12 A more efficient, cost-effective method of characterising poorly controlled asthma is urgently needed. Home monitoring of patients with digitally enabled inhalers and lung function monitors, in conjunction with markers of T2 inflammation, provides a potential solution to this problem.

In this protocol paper, we describe the design of a prospective cohort replicate study. We will test the hypothesis that digitally acquired data recording lung function and medication use can better establish the cause of poor control than current guideline-recommended tests. Patients with clinician-diagnosed asthma who remain symptomatic1 despite ICS treatment will be enrolled. Over a minimum of 26 weeks and five visits, symptoms, markers of T2 inflammation and lung function will be assessed using a combination of clinic visits and digital home monitoring of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and inhaler use. The ‘ground truth’ diagnosis for each patient will be established using a multimodal template comprised of lung function, T2 inflammation, response to treatment, consideration of alternative diagnoses and patient outcome over time.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a prospective, 26-week observational study. It will include two replicate cohorts of patients with clinician-diagnosed asthma who remain uncontrolled despite guideline-directed treatment. These groups will represent dual validation cohorts to test our novel lung function and T2 inflammation metric. This metric has been derived from two separate cohorts, encompassing both healthy controls and patients with severe asthma.13 After confirming eligibility criteria and undertaking informed consent, patients will attend five study visits over a minimum of 26 weeks.

Setting

The study will be conducted in the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland (RCSI) Smurfit Clinical Research Centre at Beaumont Hospital, Dublin.

Study procedures

Potential participants will be screened for inclusion/exclusion criteria by phone and will be introduced to the study design and expectations if enrolled.

At visit 1, written informed consent will be sought and their eligibility criteria confirmed. Education will involve instruction regarding the correct use of their current inhalers. Participants will be provided with a digitally enabled lung-function monitor, instructed on its proper use and advised to undertake two times per day measurements. The Vitalograph asma-1 records both peak expiratory flow (PEF) and FEV1. This device provides immediate visual feedback on the quality of participants’ blow, prompting them to provide repeat measurements if required. Full blood count, renal profile, liver profile, c-reactive protein (CRP) and total and specific IgE levels will be collected. An additional serum sample will be stored for future biomarker analysis. Spirometry with bronchodilator responsiveness (BDR), impedance oscillometry (IOS) with BDR and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) measurements will be recorded. Participants will complete symptom burden and quality of life questionnaires, including the Asthma Control Test (ACT), Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: Asthma (WPAI: Asthma) and EuroQol Five-Dimensional Three-Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L). Participants will also have a digital adherence monitor attached to their short-acting beta agonist to record usage.

Visit 2 takes place 2–4 weeks later, at which stage a respiratory clinician will perform a full structured clinical assessment. This will involve the collection of free-text and ordinal data regarding symptoms, risk factors for airway disease, medical comorbidities and current medications. Clinicians will determine the likelihood of the participants’ symptoms being due to asthma based on a multimodal template as outlined in table 1. This structured clinical evaluation will also assess if other conditions (eg, rhinitis, cardiac disease, obesity, anxiety, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) are present. Appropriate therapy for any present comorbidities will be prescribed as required. Any potential condition that requires further investigation will have the appropriate tests arranged. If asthma is felt to be present, participants will be commenced on maintenance inhaled corticosteroid/long acting beta agonist (ICS/LABA) therapy (fluticasone furoate/vilanterol) via an Ellipta device with an attached CE-marked adherence monitor, and inhaler technique education will be provided. Therapy can be started at either 92/22 mcg or 184/22 mcg in keeping with the participant’s current ICS usage and degree of objective evidence of inflammation and obstruction to date. If the clinician considers that asthma is unlikely to be present, ICS therapy will be stopped, or the dose will be reduced. Digital home monitoring of FEV1 and reliever usage will continue. Study nurses will assess participants’ technique of measuring FEV1 and inhaler use, and re-education will be provided if necessary. Those requiring more than three attempts to produce an adequate technique will also have this noted and factored into data analysis.

At weeks 8 and 12 (visits 3 and 4), participants will repeat the same process as at visit 2. Participants’ treatment for asthma and any coexisting relevant comorbidities will be escalated or de-escalated throughout these visits as required. Referral for additional investigations, including but not limited to CT imaging, bronchial challenge testing or referral to other specialties (commonly gastroenterology or ENT) to confirm or refute the diagnosis, will be scheduled during this time. Digital monitoring finishes at visit 4.

If participants become unwell from a respiratory perspective during the study duration, they are instructed to contact the research centre, and an unscheduled visit will be arranged with clinical evaluation, bloods, FeNO, IOS and spirometry performed as indicated by their clinical presentation.

At visit 5, participants will undergo a final clinical evaluation. If they remain symptomatic or there is any doubt surrounding the certainty or severity of asthma diagnosis, participants will also undergo blood tests, spirometry with BDR, IOS with BDR, FeNO measurement and symptom burden questionnaires. This visit also allows an opportunity to ascertain the benefit of any lifestyle modifications or other interventions. Primary aim

The primary aim of this study is to compare the sensitivity and specificity of a novel lung function classification metric, ASTma fEv1 Composite (ASTeC) score, with the three current guideline recommendations for the diagnosis and assessment of asthma. The novel lung function classification metric incorporates baseline lung function, variability over time and day, BD use and steroid use (both inhaled and oral), in combination with evidence of T2 inflammation.

Determining the presence of asthma

In the absence of a universally agreed on gold standard for the diagnosis of asthma, we will determine the diagnosis based on a predefined template of objectively collected data, as outlined in table 1. This template has been derived from diagnostic tools that are currently used in guidelines, as well as previous research demonstrating the role of biomarkers in predicting response to asthma treatment.6,814 We have used the midpoint of biomarker values used in prior research evaluating asthma treatment regimens in patients at low to moderate risk of exacerbations as our lower estimated value.

Statistical analysis plan

Descriptive statistics

The first stage of analysis will be to use descriptive statistics to describe recruited individuals to investigate the comparability of the trial cohorts (validation 1 and validation 2) at baseline. Continuous variables will be reported with mean and SD, which are normally distributed, and with median and IQRs for skewed distributions. Categorical variables will be reported as the number and percentage of the total in each category. All variables will be described for each study group separately and for all participants together. All results and parameter estimates will be presented along with 95% CIs. Unless otherwise stated, a two-sided significance level of 0.05 will be used in all tests of statistical significance. Descriptive statistics of baseline demographics, medication burden and presence of comorbidities will be presented in a table highlighting all patients together, and those with and without asthma as the primary cause of their symptoms separately.

Primary analysis

A novel metric incorporating data on baseline lung function, variability over time and day, BD use and corticosteroid use, combined with evidence of T2 inflammation, will be compared with current guideline-recommended methods for diagnosis. As previously described, asthma will be determined based on a structured, data-driven template (table 1) comprising multimodal components used in expert clinician diagnosis. Secondary analysis

Symptoms will be analysed by hierarchical cluster analysis in isolation and in clusters. Predefined symptoms include dyspnoea, cough, wheeze, chest tightness, chest pain, recurrent infections and asthma exacerbations. Symptoms suggestive of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, rhinosinusitis, cardiac disease, anxiety or depression will also be systematically recorded. Neonatal and early childhood risk factors will be recorded and included as risk factors in predefined feature clusters. Cluster and Venn diagrams will show the combination of symptoms present in all patients, those with asthma and those without asthma separately. Symptom response to ICS or to other treatment will also be recorded, as will adherence to LABA/ICS therapy.

Regression analysis will be used to identify unbiased clusters or specific features from clinical assessment that will predict the likelihood of asthma being the cause of current symptoms.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV of all diagnostic tools will be recorded using predefined cut-offs. Logistic regression analysis will be used to determine the relationship between the presence of asthma and each of these objective outcome measures. ORs and 95% CI will be reported. Receiver operating curve characteristics will also be used for each continuous variable, with cut-off points identified to optimise sensitivity and specificity of these tests for diagnosing asthma in this cohort already on treatment.

Economic outcomes

Cost-utility analysis will be performed based on an Irish public healthcare setting perspective. A direct cost analysis will be performed comparing serial digital home lung function monitoring combined with adherence and T2 inflammation to usual diagnostic procedures. Cost-utility analysis will compare the direct total and mean costs of both diagnostic strategies, presented as incremental cost-effectiveness ratios. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis will be plotted as a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve. Furthermore, we will perform an economic cost analysis on the advantages of dediagnosing patients without evidence of the disease.

Sample size

Participants will be recruited directly from primary care centres or newly referred to respiratory clinics. Previous studies have demonstrated that 30%–40% of participants in similar cohorts do not have evidence of asthma.22 23 We have previously shown that 70% of participants undertake home lung function measurements satisfactorily.24 Other studies have shown variability in the correlation between unsupervised home FEV1 and supervised clinic FEV1 measurements in asthma populations, depending on the device and degree of education and coaching provided.25,27 From derivation cohorts, the mean difference in ASTeC score between groups (healthy controls vs patients with uncontrolled asthma) is 1.64±2.7. Using these assumptions and a 20% dropout rate, we calculate with 80% power and 95% CI that a cohort of at least 106 participants will be required in each validation cohort. We will validate this score independently in both cohorts.

Missing data

Additional per-protocol analysis will be performed to assess the effects of differential drop-out and sensitivity to missing data. A CONSORT diagram will be produced to describe study flow, including screening, withdrawal of participants and other causes of missing data. For intention-to-treat analysis, missing primary outcome data will be imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations in Stata 18.5. Data missing due to device malfunction will be considered missing completely at random. Data missing due to subject withdrawal will be assumed to be missing at random, unless a clear reason given for withdrawal contradicts this assumption.

Data collection

Participants will be assigned a study ID number. Participant name is retained only on the consent form kept by the principal investigator and tracking logs kept at the research site. Data will be collected during study visits and entered onto both paper and electronic case report forms (CRFs) identified through the study ID. Electronic CRFs were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools hosted at RCSI. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, with access via individual username and password, providing an audit trail for tracking data manipulation and export procedures.28 29

Conclusion

Diagnosing asthma is inherently challenging due to its variable nature. Fluctuations in symptoms, inflammation and airflow limitation are core aspects of the disease. In practice, clinicians integrate variability in clinical features along with the components of different diagnostic guidelines to arrive at a diagnosis. A major strength of this study is that it formalises this approach, providing a structured framework for what is currently considered expert judgement. This aligns with current practice in secondary care where clinicians leverage all available evidence (clinical features, test results, response to treatment and counterfactual arguments for other conditions) to confirm or refute the diagnosis of asthma. Through simultaneous comprehensive evaluation of alternative diagnoses, this template also offers a framework for identifying instances when asthma may not be the primary cause of a patient’s current symptoms.

A second unique innovation of this study is the inclusion of a novel metric combining home spirometry with biomarkers of T2 inflammation and actual inhaler usage. By combining multiple asthma-related indicators rather than relying on a single positive test result, we minimise the risk of misclassification. This rate of misclassification is often exacerbated by borderline or negative results when using current guideline cut-offs, highlighting the need for a more nuanced diagnostic method.

In summary, a replicate study of a multimodal assessment of patients referred to secondary care with features suggestive of asthma is described. This study seeks to address the urgent unmet need for comprehensive diagnostic criteria for asthma, shifting from traditional, stepwise approaches to a more integrated, multimodal algorithm.
