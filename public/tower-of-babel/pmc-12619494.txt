Creating “Safer Ground”: implementation of a service-rich housing program for people experiencing homelessness during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Rania Ali, Rebecca Perry, Shannon Eng, Ariana W K Katz, Sue Napierala, Jennifer Lorvick, Megan Comfort
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOperation Safer Ground (OSG) was created in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Without safe locations to shelter in place, people experiencing homelessness (PEH) were especially vulnerable to COVID-19. In March 2020, California’s Governor signed an executive order that provided local governments with flexible emergency funding to develop “immediate solutions” to mitigate COVID-19 infection risk among PEH. Alameda County Health used those funds to create OSG, a robust and expansive program to address not only COVID-19 risk among PEH, but also the larger health and housing challenges faced by this population.MethodsIn this study, we use the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework to explore key phases of the OSG implementation process and better understand facilitators and barriers that arose. We conducted semi-structured interviews with 34 public health officials and staff who were involved in the development and implementation of OSG. We used a hybrid deductive and inductive qualitative analysis approach for data analysis.ResultsCross-sector collaboration emerged as a critical component across all phases of OSG. Alameda County Health’s preparation phase was significantly accelerated by prior work establishing cross-agency partnerships integrating medical, behavioral, and social services for PEH with complex needs, and developing a robust information-sharing infrastructure. This existing foundation positioned the County to effectively leverage emergency COVID-19 funding. Additionally, OSG’s nimble and engaged leadership contributed to the swift development and relatively smooth launch of the program. Collapsing bureaucratic hierarchies and empowering key staff and partners to drive decision-making during the preparation phase made people feel included, excited, and recognized for their expertise. During implementation, the presence of leadership on-site allowed for real-time feedback and adaptive problem-solving. However, securing permanent housing for OSG participants remained a persistent challenge. The primary barrier was structural: a critical shortage of affordable housing within the County.ConclusionsOur findings highlight that when key elements such as cross-sector collaboration, engaged leadership, and flexible funding align, significant progress can be made in providing housing and integrated medical, behavioral, and social services for PEH with complex needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24866-8.Keywords: Homelessness, Operation Safer Ground, Project Roomkey, Alameda County, COVID-19, Temporary housing, Program implementation, EPIS framework, Emergency funding, Cross-Sector collaboration

Background

As the COVID-19 pandemic erupted in early 2020, a key public health concern was the increased risk of mortality among people experiencing homelessness (PEH) living in congregate shelters or unsheltered environments [1]. To protect the health of PEH, local officials across the United States initiated a broad spectrum of responses that were shaped by factors such as local infection rates, size of the unhoused population, governmental priorities, and existing health infrastructure [2–7].

Over a quarter of Americans experiencing homelessness live in California [8]. In March 2020, California Governor Gavin Newsom declared a State of Emergency and signed an executive order that provided local governments with the flexibility to spend $150 million (separately authorized by Senate Bill 89) on “immediate solutions” for mitigating COVID-19 infection risk among PEH [9]. Referred to as “Project Roomkey” (PRK), this initiative enabled counties to lease hotels as temporary non-congregate housing for PEH. Fifty-four of California’s 58 counties implemented PRK services [2].

In Alameda County, which includes the cities of Oakland and Berkeley, approximately 8,000 people were unhoused when the COVID-19 pandemic began [10]. As PRK came into existence, public health officials at the Alameda County Health Department (AC Health) quickly established short-term isolation and quarantine housing for PEH who had confirmed cases of COVID-19 or COVID-19 exposure. Simultaneously, AC Health also spearheaded Operation Safer Ground (OSG), an inter-agency initiative that provided temporary housing, predominantly in hotels and trailers, to PEH at high risk of COVID-19 infection due to age or underlying medical conditions. The overall philosophy of OSG was based on the Housing First policy, prioritizing housing as an entryway to addressing social service and health care needs [11]. Depending on the housing site, OSG’s operations included three meals a day, in-room caregiving support, on-site medical assistance and pharmaceutical services, and transportation to off-site medical appointments, as well as an assigned housing navigator to help prepare and submit applications for permanent placements [12]. OSG enrolled its first resident on March 27, 2020, just ten days after Governor Newsom’s executive order. By the time the program wound down in September 2022, OSG had served 2,614 PEH, 66% (1,716) of whom transitioned to permanent housing [13].

Alameda County engaged in a robust and expansive program to address not only COVID-19 risk among PEH, but also the complex health and housing challenges faced by this population. In this paper, we used the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) framework to highlight key phases of the OSG implementation and better understand facilitators and barriers to implementation of the program in Alameda County, California. This includes describing the context in which AC Health and its partners developed and rapidly implemented this large-scale intervention during a global pandemic. We ultimately sought to identify key lessons learned to inform the future implementation of temporary or transitional health and housing programs for PEH.

Methods

This paper presents qualitative findings from a larger, mixed-methods evaluation of OSG. We employ qualitative data because it is well-suited to assessing implementation factors, particularly contextual factors such as leadership or organizational characteristics, which can be difficult to quantify. We employed a retrospective design, conducting semi-structured interviews with public health officials who were involved in the development of OSG, staff members who implemented OSG, and PEH who resided in OSG temporary housing. Findings from the resident interviews are presented elsewhere [14]. Interview questions for public health officials and staff were guided by the EPIS framework. We selected the EPIS framework because it allowed for a multilevel examination of the OSG implementation process, with deliberate consideration of each phase.

This research was reviewed and approved by the RTI International’s Institutional Review Board prior to initiating human subjects research.

Sampling and recruitment

We used a combination of purposive and snowball sampling methods to recruit OSG program staff and public health officials occupying different roles in the implementation of the program (see Table 1). We approached public health officials who had been closely involved in developing OSG and were referred by them to other knowledgeable public health officials and managers of key OSG functions. We then recruited managers to participate in interviews and asked them to refer us to frontline staff. Some managers and staff worked directly for AC Health, while others worked for collaborating service agencies. In total, we emailed interview requests to 40 individuals and followed up with phone calls as needed; 34 people completed interviews, three declined, and three did not respond.

Data collection

Trained qualitative interviewers (authors AK, MC, and JL) conducted in-depth, semi-structured interviews ranging from 34 to 125 min via Zoom between May 2022 and May 2023. The study team developed two interview guides—one tailored for public health officials and management staff involved in the design and launch of OSG, and another tailored for frontline staff responsible for the program’s day-to-day implementation. Questions for both groups focused on understanding each phase of OSG development and implementation and the participant’s role within OSG, including how they first became involved; experiences with or observations of OSG implementation, including challenges, successes, and adaptations over time; recommendations for improving OSG; and advice for other localities looking to implement housing interventions (Supplementary File 1). Public health officials and management staff were also asked about their experiences developing OSG and providing training for OSG staff, and frontline staff were asked about training they received and their perspectives on OSG residents’ service needs. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim, and notes taken during the interviews were integrated into the transcripts. Interview data were supplemented with document reviews to cross-verify information regarding organizational history, timelines and program details mentioned by participants.

Analysis

We used a hybrid deductive and inductive qualitative analysis approach for data analysis [16, 17]. The EPIS framework conceptualizes the implementation of a given intervention as comprised of four distinct phases: Exploration, Preparation, Implementation and Sustainment [18]. Our deductive codebook used these phases as parent codes and nested inner and outer context and bridging and innovation factors as sub-codes within the phases. A team of two analysts (authors RA, and RP) used NVivo 12 QSR to code the transcripts. To start, each analyst separately conducted a test code on one transcript and then they compared their coding. Portions of the interview or codes that had less than a 90% level of agreement between the two analysts and a Kappa coefficient of less than − 0.05 were discussed. The coding team used these discussions to revise the codebook. Next, five transcripts were double coded to ensure reliability, and discrepancies were resolved through harmonizing discussions and further revision of the codebook [19]. After completing all deductive coding, the analysts reviewed the code reports and conducted inductive coding by identifying emerging themes. Inductive codes were then compared across code reports and analysts identified common themes across EPIS phases, as well as dissimilar or conflicting findings. Appendix A illustrates the deductive codes that are nested within each EPIS phase along with the inductive codes that emerged within them.

Results

Table 2 summarizes the activities that correspond to each EPIS phase, as well as the themes that emerged during the inductive analyses as contextual, bridging and innovation factors assessed within each phase. Although aspects of each EPIS phase were iterative in nature, the overall implementation followed a general timeline. The Exploration phase began in 2016, preceding COVID-19 and the OSG program, because during this time AC Health developed a County-wide network of health and social service providers as part of a Medicaid-funded “Whole Person Care” pilot. This pilot laid the groundwork for OSG (see Exploration section of results). The Exploration phase also includes January-early March 2020, the period when COVID-19 infection was identified among Californians. During this time, PEH were prioritized as a focal population for COVID-19 related-funding. The Preparation phase began on March 17, 2020, with the Governor’s Executive Order enabling the establishment of emergency housing for PEH and continued until the first resident moved into an OSG site on March 27, 2020. As AC Health officials quickly mobilized to protect the health of PEH during this 10-day period, the exploratory work conducted under the Whole Person Care pilot served as a foundation for prevention efforts. The Implementation phase focuses on the execution of the intervention, during which OSG residents occupied housing from March 2020 until the closing of the largest OSG site in August 2022. We considered intervention sustainment as the effort to transition residents to permanent housing, ending in June 2023 when our data collection ended.

Exploration

At the time the COVID-19 outbreak occurred, AC Health was deeply engaged in a Whole Person Care pilot program that had been launched in 2016 under a Medi-Cal 2020 Section 1115 waiver, funded by a demonstration contract from the California Department of Health Care Services [20]. The goal of the pilot, called Alameda County (AC) Care Connect, was to coordinate physical health, behavioral health, and social services for Medicaid enrollees with complex needs [21]. As one operations manager explained, “[the AC] Care Connect effort was to try to bring housing and health together in a way to… recognize the whole person and all of the different needs that a person might have.”

AC Care Connect placed a strong emphasis on inter-agency relationship building, with the aim of facilitating information sharing, referrals, and coordination of care. As one component of building these relationships, representatives from Alameda County service provider agencies were invited to participate in a series of “Care Communities” meetings during which they learned more about each agency’s focal areas, its processes and procedures, and the challenges the agency or its clients faced, particularly in relation to gaps that other agencies potentially could fill. In addition to these opportunities for discussion and collaboration, AC Care Connect also led the development of the Community Health Record (CHR) data system for providers, which centralized information about Medicaid enrollees with complex needs and the medical, behavioral health, housing, and social services that they used. The CHR was a key accomplishment of AC Care Connect because it enabled providers to understand the full landscape of people’s needs and the services they were – or were not – accessing.

AC Care Connect was an ideal infrastructure upon which to build OSG. When PRK was launched, leaders at AC Health immediately realized that this emergency housing initiative could be coupled with the overarching goals of AC Care Connect to create a novel program. Prior to the pandemic, obtaining housing was one of the most pressing needs faced by the County’s vulnerable residents. With the sudden financial and logistical support from the State to lease hotels and trailers, AC Health decided to combine the AC Care Connect approach with the Housing First model [11] and provide wraparound medical, behavioral, and social services to people residing in OSG sites.

The Care Communities network and CHR, both of which involved unprecedented communication and coordination among agencies across a multitude of sectors as well as strong leadership from AC Health, were crucial innovation factors in the Exploration phase. These processes broke down siloes between and within agencies and initiated an ethos of collaboration among providers. It also fostered communication between AC Health and these agencies. The implications of this innovation were highlighted in interviews, when public health officials and managers often struggled to respond when asked why AC Health chose to launch a robust program aimed at stabilizing PEH and improving their health and wellbeing instead of focusing more narrowly on preventing the spread of COVID-19. “It’s just the way we all believed it had to be done” said one medical provider, who explained that the key decision-makers were so steeped in thinking about the full spectrum of people’s needs that they did not even consider limiting services. As described in the next section, the substantial groundwork laid during the Whole Person Care pilot also contributed extensively to AC Health’s ability to design and launch a new program within ten days during a time of uncertainty, fear, and confusion.

Preparation

Following the State of Emergency declared by Governor Newsom in March 2020, AC Health was able to strategically leverage a combination of state and federal funding sources to support the rapid launch of OSG. Initial funding came through new state resources made available by the Executive Order and Senate Bill 89, which prioritized immediate COVID-19 mitigation efforts for PEH. This was supplemented by federal CARES Act funding, including the Emergency Solutions Grant – COVID-19 (ESG-CV), which supported early setup and operations. Beginning in January 2021, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) Public Assistance Program began reimbursing eligible shelter-related costs. Together, these funding streams enabled the County to quickly scale up non-congregate shelter and essential support services.

The Preparation phase of OSG also relied heavily on the strong networks, collaborative processes, and innovative thinking established through AC Care Connect. As one housing provider observed, “We were well-prepared for something like [OSG]. You could say the County was accustomed to trying new things and had people who were nimble enough to be deployed.”

As soon as Governor Newsom’s Executive Order was announced, the Medical Director of AC Health convened the AC Care Connect management team, who reached out to their Care Community partners. They began gathering daily in the ballroom of a large hotel in downtown Oakland that was to serve as a primary OSG housing site. A program specialist described the scene: “[Representatives from] all these different agencies were sitting on the ground, drawing visuals on big white papers, covering the whole ballroom with easel paper and Post-its.” An interviewee in upper management recalled the preparation process similarly:

We were in the hotel … [laughter] you know, at the hotel working with chart paper and, literally people [were] working out the procedure manual together. It was a very communal [process and] we had a foundation of a Whole Person, person-centered approach that had been evolving in Care Connect. … Basically, the approach that we had been trying to encourage and foster in all the work that we were doing [in Care Connect] was the starting point for OSG.

Interviewees who participated in the “ballroom” meetings consistently described the intense ten-day OSG development process as exciting and energizing. The AC Care Connect management team easily identified the agencies that were best equipped to oversee each program component due to the relationships established through the Care Communities, and they were encouraged by AC Health leadership to reach out to their primary contacts and leap into action. These team members remarked upon the substantial autonomy they were granted to make decisions and move tasks forward as a refreshing departure from the customary requirement to run every decision through the time-consuming County bureaucracy. An operations manager explained that prior to the pandemic, “[If] you need help on a project, you can’t just ask somebody else in another department for help…You need to get approval from your boss and from their boss for permission to work on something [together].” The flattening of decision-making hierarchies during OSG’s development made key staff feel empowered and kept morale high during a period of global uncertainty.

The newly formed AC Health OSG leadership team and their partners created operational policies and processes to conduct outreach to identify and refer PEH to intake coordinators; screen PEH for eligibility and enroll eligible people as residents; assign residents to rooms in OSG sites; and provide a wide range of services (see Fig. 1). In addition to planning extensive wraparound services, the OSG development team deliberately focused on reducing barriers commonly experienced by PEH when trying to access short-term housing. For example, in recognition that being separated from loved ones could cause PEH to refuse shelter placements, OSG residents were permitted to live in the same room with partners, adult or minor children, and pets. Additionally, in alignment with the Housing First model [22], residents could receive substance use counseling but were not required to abstain from using alcohol or drugs. Such operational decisions required collaboration and buy-in from all the partner agencies, since OSG policies and practices needed to be created to work across a spectrum of person-centered contexts.

Provided to OSG Residents

Innovations also arose as AC Health and partner agencies adapted existing personnel roles and systems to meet OSG’s programmatic needs. In the bustle of the ballroom, plans were made to relocate nurses from clinics to deliver on-site medical care, modify homeless shelter management operations to fit the hotel environment, and retain hotel staff members to deliver in-room meals and provide housekeeping and laundry services. Creatively redeploying available resources was a key strategy for getting OSG up and running so quickly, as one operations manager explained:

One of my favorite stories to tell is about [getting a] whole referral system [set up at] the hotel site. We were struggling with how we were going to do this, and the [shelter operator] staff member said, “You know, we have the Salesforce system that we use for other referral purposes. I suspect I could call our data person and ask what it would take to modify that system so we could use it.” And I swear, within twenty-four hours, we had a system up and running that the [shelter operator] had put together.

All in all, while confusion and uncertainty swept the globe between March 17 and March 27, 2020, a team of committed leaders joined forces in a hotel ballroom to prepare the launch of a novel program designed to protect vulnerable PEH from COVID-19 infection and provide integrated services to address many other needs. As a shelter operator reflected, “It was less than two weeks. We set this up quickly! We knew it wasn’t perfect, but we maintained open communication, identified the resources we needed to add along the way, and stayed in constant contact.” On March 28, 2020, the first OSG residents were enrolled, and program implementation began.

Implementation

Leadership, staffing, and training

A consistent culture of strong cross-agency communication played a key role in coordinating care for OSG residents. Frequent one-on-one meetings with team leads took place to address challenges and develop solutions. Also, daily “huddles” were consistently used to share information across the multiple teams involved in residents’ care in each hotel. A nurse described the process: “We started every day with a huddle in the ballroom at the site and would basically go through the report of how many people we had in rooms that day, if we knew people were coming in or out, [and] major concerns with particular individuals.”

Partnerships between OSG agencies and external organizations were crucial for facilitating extensive service provision at OSG sites. For example, OSG partnered with community pharmacies to ensure medical support for residents with complex conditions, including substance use disorders and mental health issues. One OSG pharmacist described their role:

I spend a lot of my time communicating with those outside partners [pharmacies and primary care providers] to ensure that they are routing new prescriptions, answering questions about what types of treatments are allowed or not allowed at the facilities, depending on what resources we have at the time, and … clarifying what it is that we are able to do and how we’re able to support the patients in those moments.

At times the breadth of services offered by OSG contributed to challenges in cross-agency communication. One peer outreach advisor emphasized the need for a more integrated system to ensure that service and call logs, which tracked interactions and service delivery for residents, were used proactively to address residents’ needs:

It was like this was a major missed opportunity…I think that a lot of the [staff] didn’t understand just because someone could walk down and get their food one day didn’t mean they could do it the next day because of their chronic condition…So the potential of using our system in order to communicate, I think there could have been more.

Nonetheless, in the majority of interviews, internal and interagency collaboration was described as exceptional. As one medical provider remarked, “There was this really unprecedented level of sort of integration of services and efforts that required the coordination of numerous agencies that you just wouldn’t ever have seen prior to [OSG].” Partner agencies reported a seamless relationship with AC Health and commended the support, collaboration, respect, and responsiveness they received. One shelter operator described AC Health staff as their favorite among all the counties they had worked with, stating: “I really felt well respected in the sense that they didn’t micromanage us, they really trusted us that we knew what we were doing.”

Housing navigation

A major development that occurred early in the Implementation phase was the decision to provide residents with housing navigation assistance to help them identify, apply for, and transition into permanent housing. One program/operations manager observed how this evolved:

At the very beginning [of OSG] everybody was so focused on just opening places and trying to save lives… I don’t think at the very beginning we were thinking about the exits to permanent housing, but as soon as we moved from crisis to planning and started thinking about the [eventual] closing [of the OSG sites], the intent was to…offer a permanent solution to every person exiting [the program].

Being able to interact with people in a consistent residential setting facilitated the work of housing navigators and other service providers. A peer advisor described this design as “genius,” pointing out that a stable living environment maximized providers’ ability to maintain consistent contact with PEH and thereby to understand and address the full spectrum of their care needs: “[With] Whole Person Care, you are looking at the individual, top to bottom, in and out… We’re tapping into people’s heartstrings: ‘When was the last time you talked to a family member? When was the last time you seen a doctor?’ … [The OSG model] was just great.”

All residents were assigned a housing navigator once a permanent housing component was added to OSG. Housing navigators worked for partner organizations and not for AC Health. Obtaining permanent housing was challenging due to the ongoing scarcity of safe and affordable options in Alameda County [14]. It is estimated that over 17,000 new units, with varying supports, are needed to end unsheltered homelessness in the County [23]. One housing navigator described the process as time-consuming, tedious, and involving lots of paperwork and “housing problem solving.” Staff also highlighted that housing for families was particularly challenging to find because availability was limited and demand for it was high.

Sustainment

OSG was intended to end when COVID-19 no longer posed an imminent threat to public health. In reality, the program faced the threat of premature closure many times. Repeatedly, funding was set to expire and then extended, which led to inconsistent messaging to staff about their employment end dates and to residents about how long they could remain in the OSG sites. Eventually, the American Recovery Plan Act of 2021 provided funding for Alameda County that, in conjunction with County funding, stabilized the program until it ended in September 2022.

Another challenge to keeping OSG operating as planned arose due to workforce shortages, which were exacerbated by the pandemic. Although many staff expressed strong commitment to the mission of OSG, the day-to-day work was often exhausting, especially given the external circumstances of a global shutdown. A housing services director spoke at length about these difficulties and potential solutions:

People were getting burned out, including me, with the long hours we were working. We just have to build better systems, better policy regulations and funding, and have resources structured so that the workloads are reasonable. … I think that this is one of the major challenges in the public sector, healthcare, and social services workforce, as disasters become more frequent. We’re disaster responders… I just think we’ve built systems that are dependent on heroes rather than systems that are built on thoughtful plans that are realistic in what we can achieve.

Although OSG was designed to be temporary, the intention was for residents to establish stability that could be sustained after the program. Staff highlighted the need for ongoing services and expressed concern about service access among residents once they left OSG’s supportive environment, since many people had not been connected to services before entering the program. As one shelter services manager explained, “It’s not necessarily that easy to house people out of shelter if there’s no support services there. … A lot of folks hadn’t necessarily touched the homeless system support services [prior to OSG].” Certain providers were able to develop plans to continue services for residents who transitioned to permanent housing. For example, mental health providers set up appointments with OSG residents to see them in the community, and the on-site OSG pharmacist worked with residents to transfer prescriptions to a pharmacy or clinic near their new housing location. However, changes at the state and county levels led to cuts in behavioral health funding and changes in the administration of Medi-Cal for those with mental health issues that made it difficult for some residents to continue receiving care and services in the community. Over 60% of OSG residents exited to permanent housing, and the remaining residents exited either back to the streets or to various temporary housing settings [14].

Leadership, staffing, and training

The strong leadership demonstrated during the Preparation phase carried into the Implementation phase. Senior leaders, including medical directors, regularly spent time at OSG locations and interacted with frontline staff. Their routine presence enabled them to stay informed about daily operations and promptly address emerging issues. Leadership recognized that although staff might not always reach out proactively with problems, being on-site would allow them to hear about issues directly and make quick changes to resolve them, contributing to the nimbleness of the program. One medical director highlighted the importance of this approach:

Well, both of us [medical directors] were present most days at most sites so we spent a lot of time moving site to site. There’s really no substitute for the physical presence there. [For example] the nurses, they won’t always call you if there’s a problem, but if you sit down next to them, they’ll sure let you know what’s happening in their world.

Staffing processes at the beginning of OSG’s Implementation phase also shared commonalities with the relationship-centric, action-oriented practices that characterized the Preparation phase. Partner agencies drew on existing staff and other people in their networks whom they knew had relevant prior experience for OSG roles. Further, in face of the urgent need to implement, OSG staff and partners focused the scope of their work on their prior experience which minimized or even eliminated overlapping roles and turf battles. Given the program’s urgent and compressed timeline, opportunities to train staff were limited and focused primarily on the use of personal protective equipment (PPE). When asked if they received training or orientation before starting work at an OSG site, one shelter services staff member stated, “Not really. I did visit the [site] for an hour or so just to see what they were doing there, but it all just kind of came from my own expertise in running shelters.” Many OSG staff had experience working with PEH, and some had lived expertise, which facilitated staff feeling comfortable with the new program, even in the absence of formal training. For example, peer support staff emphasized that their previous experiences equipped them better than any training could have, particularly in relating to and understanding what residents were going through. In one person’s words:

When we share those lived experiences, we’re able to connect with the clients and help them elevate because we can say, ‘We’ve been there. We’ve experienced that. We know what it looks like if someone has a substance abuse or alcohol abuse situation, incarceration, or having a family member incarcerated.’ So, it’s not just the training, but those lived experiences that really match, and sometimes exceed, the level of training received.

Due to the dangers posed by the pandemic, the policy of OSG not to expel residents who engaged in challenging behaviors, but rather to work with them, focusing on de-escalation and communication strategies. As a rule, frontline staff supported this approach. Peer support staff, in particular, emphasized the importance of having staff and leadership with lived experiences to understand the nuanced cultural differences and barriers that influence help-seeking behavior. Conversely, hotel and security staff faced more difficulty with this approach, due to lack of experience working with PEH or other people who presented behavioral challenges. These staff relied heavily on more experienced staff to provide on-the-job support. In interviews, some hotel and security staff expressed a specific need for de-escalation training, noting that they were accustomed to managing tourists or customers in short-term interactions, and felt ill equipped for long-term interaction with OSG residents who were suffering from serious mental or physical health issues. For example, security staff had been previously trained to remove disruptive persons rather than work with them. In the words of one security staff member, additional training was needed for their colleagues on “how to handle aggressive people, how to make proper incident reports, handling themselves in case someone is aggressive, handling themselves [when] someone comes and insults them verbally [since] they can’t also respond in the same way.”

As the Implementation phase evolved, ongoing training was critical to ensure adherence to ever-evolving COVID-19 guidelines. As a nurse described, “We figure out things that need to be going better and we’d be keeping track of what the CDC [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention] was updating, the criteria of COVID, looking at symptomology, etcetera, and then we’d try to do updates as infrequently as possible but frequently enough that we could capture stuff without being too static.” Later in this phase, AC Health and shelter operators facilitated trainings, which covered a range of topics including PPE, temperature taking and recording, OSG referral systems, intake procedures, de-escalation skills, and Narcan administration for opioid overdose response. Throughout the program, new hires continued to gain on-the-job learning by shadowing experienced staff.

Many staff had a positive outlook about their roles in OSG and a sense of a shared mission in responding to a public health emergency. They expressed optimism, hopefulness, and confidence in the program’s potential, while acknowledging challenges and showing readiness to overcome these challenges. One housing navigator reflected: “I’m glad I was a part of [OSG]. I’m glad we were a part of helping people gain stability, gain their independence, gain their dignity back.” Notwithstanding, staff turnover was a challenge, especially in the early stages of the program. The pandemic was still evolving, the risks involved were uncertain and roles were being developed and adapted as they went. In addition, the work was demanding: a transition services specialist, for example, expressed a sense of the “endlessness” of the work required to support residents’ needs.

Discussion

This study used the EPIS framework to guide exploration of the development and implementation of OSG, a novel program designed to provide temporary housing and wraparound services to PEH at high risk of acquiring COVID-19 during the pandemic. Although developed to respond to a public health emergency, our analysis highlights key elements for future consideration when developing and implementing housing programs for PEH more broadly.

Cross-sector collaborations were critical across all phases of OSG. For example, the preparation phase was accelerated due in large part to the groundwork laid by the AC Care Connect program, which established cross-agency relationships, coordinated medical, behavioral health, and social services for PEH with complex needs, and developed a technology infrastructure for sharing information. Future housing programs responding to public health emergencies would benefit similarly from having integrated, multi-service systems, in addition to early engagement with key interest holders across healthcare, housing, and social services to allow for rapid deployment in times of crisis. Beyond emergency situations, efforts to build strong partnerships among local agencies serving PEH can yield innovative care coordination strategies that center the needs of the people receiving services and contribute to long-term stabilization.

Because AC Health had an established infrastructure, they were poised to take advantage of the infusion of funding that came to address the pandemic. Flexibility in the use of funding enabled the County to move quickly from exploration to preparation and into the implementation. The importance of adequate funding in facilitating a county’s effectiveness is echoed in the literature where funding was associated with the odds of a county overperforming in county health rankings [15].

Additionally, OSG’s nimble and engaged leadership contributed to the swift development and relatively smooth launch of the program. Collapsing bureaucratic hierarchies and empowering key staff and partners to drive decision-making during the preparation phase made people feel included, excited, and recognized for their expertise. Once the program was up and running, leaders were present on-site, enabling staff to share concerns directly and allowing for quick, adaptive responses. Future rapid housing programs led by health departments would benefit from cultivating an agile implementation climate with accessible leadership. A study analyzing self-reported responses from health departments in the Public Health Accreditation Board’s Annual Report found that leadership engagement was a key facilitator to implementing new interventions in a department [24]. At the same time, the rapid pace of OSG preparation underscored the importance of having a trained and well-equipped workforce with experience working with PEH.

Securing permanent housing for residents was a persistent challenge for OSG. The major barrier was systemic, because there is insufficient housing in the County [23]. In addition, the process of connecting residents to the permanent housing that existed was slow and bureaucratic. More streamlined and coordinated efforts between emergency and long-term housing systems are critical to prevent gaps in housing for PEH [25]. Further, staff raised concerns about housing and social service continuity post-OSG, particularly for residents who had never accessed social support services prior to OSG enrollment. Imprved policies could increase PEH access to longer-term housing stability by expanding affordable housing through zoning reforms and tax incentives, increasing rental assistance, and providing supportive services like housing navigation.

This study benefited from several strengths. The use of in-depth semi-structured interviews with staff members from a diverse range of roles enhances the richness of the data and provides a nuanced understanding of the factors, processes, and challenges throughout this rapid housing intervention. A limitation of the study is that the interviews lagged behind the actual implementation of the program, meaning that we relied on participants’ memory and recall of their experiences during the exploration and preparation phases, which could have been misremembered or subject to recall bias. We made an effort to address this potential bias by triangulating information across staff interviews and also with information gained by reviewing manuals and documents. Further, not all staff interviewed participated in the exploration or preparation phases of OSG; and instead, were engaged only in the implementation phases. These perspectives, though only focused on the implementation phase, mean that we had fewer insights on these earlier phases than the later ones. There was potential for bias in the sample of interviewees, as we primarily spoke with staff who were still employed by AC Health or its agency partners at the time of their interview. Lastly, data from interviews with residents are not included in this manuscript, which only offers public health leader and staff perspectives. One of the co-authors of this manuscript analyzed OSG residents’ interviews to ensure harmonization, and findings from the residents’ data analysis are published elsewhere (Ruwala, et. al. in-review).

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into the critical factors that contributed to the rapid and successful implementation of OSG. In light of the US Supreme Court’s 2024 decision to allow American jurisdictions to fine, ticket or arrest people living unsheltered, organizations serving PEH have a heightened commitment to providing shelter for these individuals [26]. Although shelter alone is not a solution to homelessness, it plays a crucial role in addressing a foundational need of elderly and medically vulnerable populations who are without housing. Our findings highlight that when key elements such as cross-sector collaboration, pre-existing infrastructure, and diversified funding align, significant progress can be made in addressing homelessness.

The lessons learned from OSG extend beyond this program and offer broader implications for future interventions. The pandemic presented an unprecedented opportunity to rapidly mobilize resources and implement large-scale housing interventions. This study highlights that coordinated efforts between government agencies, healthcare providers, and social services, supported by flexible state and federal investments, can succeed in providing housing and integrated social services for PEH. Moreover, OSG’s Whole Person Care approach reinforces interconnectedness of public health services such as housing and healthcare. Through this approach, OSG was able to leverage strong collaboration across the public health infrastructure to efficiently address the needs of PEHs. Future interventions may want to invest in and promote a Whole Person Care approach to unify partners especially in instances when social service funding may be limited, and public health priorities are dispersed.

Looking forward, it is essential to maintain the collaborative momentum and infrastructure generated by OSG and to continue investing in flexible, multi-source funding mechanisms that can adapt to future crises. The integration of housing, healthcare, and social services must remain a priority to address the complex needs of PEH. Future research could focus on evaluating the long-term impacts of these integrated approaches, particularly in tracking the outcomes for medically vulnerable populations, many of whom entered OSG with complex health needs and without connection to social support services. Understanding how these interventions can be scaled and replicated in different jurisdictions will also be crucial for improving housing stability and health outcomes for unhoused populations across the country.
