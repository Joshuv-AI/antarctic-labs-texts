Renal atrophy and acute contralateral obstructive uropathy secondary to 2,8-dihydroxyadenine nephropathy: A case report based discussion on diagnostic pitfalls, FTIR spectroscopy, and literature review
Authors: Vipul Agrawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAPRT deficiency leads to 2,8-DHA nephropathy. It manifests as recurrent radiolucent calculi and progressive renal failure. These stones are misidentified as uric acid stones. We report a 30-year-old male with recurrent left-sided nephrolithiasis, resulting in a poorly functioning left kidney. He subsequently presented with acute obstructive uropathy in his hypertrophied right kidney. FTIR post-ureteroscopy identified the composition as 2,8-DHA. This case underscores the necessity of precise stone analysis in recurrent urolithiasis. Increased awareness and knowledge of the limitations of some methods of laboratory analysis for renal stones should help to identify this type of stone and prevent renal damage.

Introduction

Nephrolithiasis and crystalline nephropathy secondary to 2,8-dihydroxyadenine (2,8-DHA) accumulation represent a severely underdiagnosed cause of recurrent urinary stone disease.1,2 This condition stems from an autosomal recessive functional deficiency in the purine salvage enzyme adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT).3 The absence of functional APRT leads to the accumulation of 2,8-DHA, an extremely insoluble compound that readily precipitates within the renal parenchyma and urinary tract.3,4 On computed tomography (CT), these calculi display low attenuation values (100–300 HU), making them mistaken for uric acid stones.5 Delayed diagnosis frequently allows insidious progression to obstructive uropathy, permanent cortical thinning, renal atrophy, or end-stage renal disease (ESRD).1,2

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male presented to our clinic complaining of left flank pain and dysuria. His medical history was notable for multiple ureteroscopies over the preceding four to five years for recurrent left-sided ureteric calculi; however, detailed records of these procedures were unavailable. Interestingly, he reported no prior right-sided symptoms or interventions. Initial laboratory workup revealed a normal serum creatinine. Diagnostic imaging demonstrated multiple left ureteric and renal pelvic calculi accompanied by moderate left hydroureteronephrosis. The left kidney was shrunken with profound cortical thinning, while the right kidney showed mild compensatory hypertrophy with a small, non-obstructive lower pole calculus. All visualized stones displayed low attenuation values between 100 and 300 Hounsfield Units (HU).

The patient underwent left flexible ureteroscopy (f-URS) and double-J (DJ) stent placement. A month later, a follow-up ureteroscopy was performed, and the stent was successfully removed. At his 4-month review, the patient was asymptomatic with normal serum creatinine, though renal ultrasonography (USG) noted small, bilateral, non-obstructive 3–4 mm calculi. Following this visit, the patient was lost to follow-up for 9months.

Nine months later, he presented to the emergency department with severe, acute right flank pain—his first symptomatic episode on the right side. Evaluation revealed acute kidney injury (AKI) with a serum creatinine of 3.51 mg/dL. An urgent USG demonstrated the known small left kidney with a tiny, non-obstructive stone, alongside a hypertrophied right kidney with a large, obstructing calculus lodged at the pelviureteric junction (PUJ), causing moderate to gross hydronephrosis. Emergency right retrograde DJ stenting was done, leading to a prompt recovery of renal function, which stabilized at a baseline serum creatinine of 1.18 mg/dL after one month.

He subsequently underwent a definitive right f-URS to clear the remaining stone burden, followed by stent removal two months later. The retrieved stone fragments were sent for analysis. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of the stone fragments identified the composition as 100% 2,8-DHA & non-reactivity to standard wet chemical analysis. This establishing a diagnosis of 2,8-DHA nephropathy & ruled out uric acid as the possible stone type. The patient was immediately started on targeted medical therapy consisting of allopurinol (300 mg/day) along with dietary counselling emphasizing high fluid intake and strict restriction of purine-rich foods. The patient remains on regular follow-up with sustained normal serum creatinine and no further stone recurrence or surgical interventions.

Discussion

Adenine is a vital purine base utilized in nucleic acid synthesis. All of the body adenine is synthesized endogenously and not typically found in the diet.3 The adenine generated during routine purine metabolism is recycled back into the adenosine monophosphate (AMP) pool by the salvage enzyme APRT.3,4 When APRT is deficient, the excess adenine accumulates and is instead oxidized by xanthine oxidase into 2,8-DHA.4 At typical urinary pH ranges (5.0–7.0), 2,8-DHA is highly insoluble, leading to rapid crystal precipitation, urinary stone formation, and an elevated risk of progressive chronic kidney disease.1,5

APRT deficiency is an exceptionally rare, autosomal recessive metabolic error leading to 2,8-DHA stones.1,3 While its calculated homozygous prevalence ranges between 1 in 50,000 and 1 in 100,000 based on heterozygous carrier frequencies, fewer than 400 cases have been clinically documented worldwide.2,3,5 This massive discrepancy points to severe underdiagnosis.

There are many reasons for this discrepancy. Because 2,8-DHA stones are completely radiolucent on conventional X-rays and exhibit low Hounsfield units (100–300 HU) on CT scans, they are routinely misidentified as uric acid.1,5 Compounding the issue, standard wet chemical stone analysis methods cannot differentiate between uric acid and 2,8-DHA.2,6 Definitive identification requires FTIR spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction—modalities that are rarely utilized in routine urolithiasis screening.2,6

However, identifying 2,8-DHA crystals or stone components is entirely pathognomonic for APRT deficiency.1,6 Under polarized light microscopy, these urinary crystals characteristically present as spherical, yellowish-brown structures with a distinct central “Maltese cross” pattern.2,5 Being highly underdiagnosed and misidentified as uric acid stones, the number of confirmed cases in global literature remains remarkably small.1,2

Approximately 85% to 90% of affected individuals present primarily with recurrent nephrolithiasis.1,5 The remaining patients present directly with progressive, asymptomatic chronic kidney disease (CKD) or ESRD caused by diffuse 2,8-DHA crystal deposition within the renal tubules, which induces tubular necrosis and interstitial fibrosis.3,4 Another critical presentation occurs in the post-transplant setting, where unrecognized APRT deficiency causes rapid, recurrent crystalline allograft nephropathy, often leading to early graft failure.1,7

Unlike uric acid lithiasis, 2,8-DHA stones cannot be dissolved via urinary alkalinization.1,3 Raising the urine pH provides no therapeutic benefit to 2,8-DHA solubility and can dangerously delay appropriate management.5 The mainstay of treatment involves maintaining high fluid intake, adhering to a low-purine diet, and executing lifelong therapy with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor such as allopurinol or febuxostat.1,3 These agents directly block the conversion of adenine to 2,8-DHA, allowing the highly soluble adenine precursor to be safely excreted in the urine.3,4

Learning points

•Think beyond uric acid: In patients with recurrent radiolucent or low-attenuation (100–300 HU) stones, consider 2,8-DHA nephropathy, especially if they are refractory to urinary alkalinization.

Conclusion

This case highlights several hallmark features of APRT deficiency and 2,8-DHA nephropathy. APRT deficiency is a rare but vital differential diagnosis in patients with recurrent low-density stones, with progression to chronic parenchymal damage or unexpected acute renal failure.2

This patient's clinical course clearly points out that a delayed diagnosis can silently destroy an entire renal unit. Prompt treatment with near-normal restoration of biochemical deficiency underlines the importance of early diagnosis. Because this metabolic defect responds remarkably well to inexpensive, targeted medical therapy, early identification using infrared spectroscopy is vital.3,5 This is a form of severe renal damage requiring dialysis or renal transplant which can be prevented by cheap & easily available medications, efficiently.1,2
