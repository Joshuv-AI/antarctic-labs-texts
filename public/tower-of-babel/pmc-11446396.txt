Dual-plasmid interactions stimulate the accumulation of valencene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Authors: Chaoyi Zhu, Shengliang Cai, Peiling Liu, Dongying Chen, Jingtao Zhou, Min Zhuo, Shuang Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasmids are one of the most commonly used basic tools in the construction of microbial cell factories, the use of which individually or in pairs play an important role in the expression of exogenous gene modules. However, little attention has been paid to the interactions of plasmid-plasmid and plasmid-host in the widespread use of the double plasmid system. In this study, we demonstrated that dual-plasmid interactions facilitated to cell growth and product accumulation in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The strain containing both the expression plasmid pEV (a plasmid carrying the gene encoding valencene synthase) and the assistant plasmid pI (an empty plasmid expressing no extra gene) showed a significant improvement in relative growth rate, biomass and valencene production compared to the strain containing only the pEV plasmid. The transcriptional level analysis revealed an up-regulated expression of specific gene on the expression plasmid pEV stimulated by the assistant plasmid pI in the dual-plasmid interactions. Further investigations demonstrated the essential roles of the promoters of the expression plasmid pEV and the CEN/ARS element of the assistant plasmid pI in the dual-plasmid interactions. Combined with the results of predicted nucleosome occupancy, a response model of interaction based on the key T(n)C and CEN/ARS element was established. Moreover, the transformation order of the two plasmids significantly affected the response effect, implying the dominance of plasmid pI in the dual-plasmid interactions. Our finding first demonstrated that dual plasmids regulate the gene expression through spatial interactions at DNA sequences level, which provides a new perspective for the development of microbial cell factories in future.Keywords: Dual-plasmid interactions, CEN/ARS element, Cell response, Poly(dA:dT), Saccharomyces cerevisiae

Introduction

In the development of recombinant DNA technology, plasmids have become the indispensable vectors in cell factory construction due to their flexibility and variability.1,2 In general, as the “parasite”, plasmids tend to impose a metabolic burden on host cells for their replication and maintenance, thus causing various physiological changes in the host, including disturbances in DNA replication, transcription and translation, slow growth, decreased biomass, and even triggering stress responses.3, 4, 5, 6 Several theoretical analyses and experiments have demonstrated that as the length and copy number of the plasmid increases, the more significant the growth burden it puts on the host. However, in addition to the classical views about plasmid burden, there were evidences suggesting that plasmids stimulate the uptake of glucose and oxygen.7, 8, 9, 10 The rate of glucose uptake in E. coli HB101 was elevated after transferring the pUC19 plasmid, accompanied by increased levels of intracellular ATP and metabolic by-products.7 Diaz-Ricci et al. hypothesized that the plasmid would affect host metabolism by regulating the cAMP-CRP complex, resulting in the relief of growth inhibition and alteration of cellular glucose uptake rate.7 Similarly, glucose utilization and oxygen uptake were also increased in E. coli BL21 containing plasmid ColE1.4 Meanwhile, plasmids were found to stimulate an increase in RNA and protein content in host B. subtilis BD170.8 To recapitulate, plasmid offered boundless unexplored area and unknown advantages potential to the host.11

Dual-plasmid systems were increasingly popular in cell factory construction due to their ability to carry more genes, in which the relationship between the plasmids pairing and gene expression was more concerned.12, 13, 14 For example, Gruber et al. developed a dual-plasmid technology for strictly regulating gene expression by the lac promoter in a high-copy vector to enhances the high expression of the ccdB protein.12 The CRISPR/Cas9 system was also expressed as dual plasmids to flexibly regulate the coordinated expression of Cas9 proteins and guide RNA to achieve higher editing efficiency.14 However, studies have paid little attention to plasmid-plasmid and plasmid-host interactions. The effects of the plasmids themself on the host, such as changes in ATP and metabolite content, were usually slight, which were often neglected and regarded as experimental error. Therefore, the response mechanisms of dual-plasmid interactions and plasmid-host responses were rarely clearly resolved.

In this study, based on the early phenomenon of dual plasmids affecting the growth and metabolism in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the core elements and key sequences in the dual-plasmid interactions were identified by molecular characterization, transcriptional level assay and product analysis. Finally, the response model of dual-plasmid interactions was built combining with the analysis of nucleosome occupancy prediction. Here, we provided new insights on response mechanisms of plasmid-plasmid and plasmid-host.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

The strains and plasmids used in the study are listed in Tables S1 and S2.

E. coli DH5α competent cells (Invitrogen, USA) were used for gene cloning and plasmid construction. E. coli strains were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB, containing 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 10 g/L sodium chloride) medium, and the recombinant E. coli strains were grown in LB medium containing 100 mg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C. The S. cerevisiae strains were derived from BJ5464 and kept in our laboratory.15 The BJ5464 was used for exogenous gene expression and gene editing. The original BJ5464 strain was cultured in yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD, containing 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, and 20 g/L glucose) medium at 30 °C. The BJ5464 strain transformant with the corresponding shuttle vector was cultured in auxotrophic synthetic dextrose (SD, containing 6.7 g/L YNB, 0.62 g/L with appropriate dropout supplement, and 20 g/L glucose) medium at 30 °C. For instance, LEU deficiency for plasmid YEplac181, TRP deficiency for plasmid pI, URA deficiency for plasmid YEplac352, and p426 in URA deficit media, which is a primary plasmid used in this study for various construction purposes.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Yeast at the logarithmic growth stage was collected for DNA and RNA extraction by E.Z.N.A.® Yeast DNA Kit and the E.Z.N.A.® Yeast RNA Kit (Omega, China), respectively. Preliminary evaluation of DNA and RNA integrity was examined by agarose electrophoresis, followed by HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China) for reverse transcription and impurity removal. Reverse transcription reactions were conducted in 10 μL volume with QPCR primer as listed in Table S3 and ChamQ™ SYBR® qPCR Master (Vazyme, China) using the LightCycler 96 (Roche, Switzerland). ERG9 was selected for the replication level housekeeping gene, and ACT1 was chosen as an internal control gene for the transcription level housekeeping. The relative changes in the mRNA level and the replication level expression were calculated using the −2ΔΔCT method, with CT = threshold cycle, ΔCT = CT(target gene) − CT(ACT1), and ΔΔCT = ΔCT(experiment) − ΔCT(control). The ΔCT values of the strain BJ5464 were used as controls.16

Construction of plasmid element library

Primers synthesis by Sangon used for DNA amplification are listed in Table S3. The construction of plasmids was implemented by either the Gibson assembly strategy or enzyme ligation. (1) For the pI and pEV plasmid feature modification, an amplification from basic pI or pEV by PrimerSTAR(TaKaRa, China)as backbone and amplification of other plasmids to obtain a shorter fragment (feature). (2) To construct pEV with a mutated promoter sequence, a base substitution on a designed primer, MF\MR, amplifies a promoter with point mutation and primers TF\TR to amplify the plasmid backbone. The backbone and target fragments were then homologously recombinant by ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit(Vazyme, China)to obtain the reconstructed plasmid. Similarly, (1) the recombinant of different expression cassettes carrying either YPet, GFP, mCherry, ERFP and GUS was amplified from product plasmids of our lab. (2) While various endogenous promoters were obtained by amplifying the genome of S.cerevisiae BJ5464 using KOD DNA polymerase (TOYOBO, Japan). The backbone of plasmid pEV and the corresponding target gene was ligated by employing T4 DNA Ligase to form various reporter protein recombinant plasmids. The recombinant plasmids transformed into E. coli by heat shock method, and the positive clones were verified by Sanger sequencing (Sangon, China). 500 ng of target plasmid were transformed into yeast cells using yeast EasyComp™ Transformation Kit (Invitrogen, USA) and spread plate on SD agar dish at 30 °C incubation for 3 days.

Strain culture for production

The seed was cultured in a 5 mL SD liquid media test tube at 30 °C, 220 rpm, up to the optical density 600 nm (OD600) reached 1–3. 0.05 of initial OD600 of seed culture was added into a 50 mL conical flask containing 10 mL of SD liquid medium with a corresponding nutrition deficiency. An additional 2 mL n-dodecane is supplemented into the conical flask to fabricate a bi-phase system for valencene production shake-flask fermentation. Cultures were performed at 220 rpm, 30 °C and lasted 48 h. For the expression of fluorescence intensity and β-glucuronidase, cultures were incubated at 220 rpm, 30 °C for 24 h.

Analysis methods

OD600 measurement by an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, GENESYS 10) after the shake flask incubation. For the determination of valencene content, n-dodecane on the upper phase of the fermentation broth sample was extracted with an equal volume of ethyl acetate for Gas chromatography (GC) quantification. GC analysis was performed on a Shimadzu GC-2014C device equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) using an HP-5 column (Agilent Technologies) (crosslinked 5 % Ph-Me siloxane; 30 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm). Sample aliquots of 1 μL were injected in split mode at a ratio of 15:1 with nitrogen as carrier gas. The running program for the GC device was as follows: the initial GC oven temperature was set to 100 °C for 10 min, then increased to 150 °C with a ramp rate of 8 °C per min, up to the final temperature of 200 °C with a ramp rate of 30 °C min−1 for 3 min. Valencene concentration was determined using calibration curves created from standard valencene.

The analysis of fluorescence intensity reading using a microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). To determine YPet, GFP, ERFP, and mCherry, their excitation and emission spectrum was set to 485 and 528 nm, 480 and 510 nm, 580 and 610 nm and 580 and 610 nm, respectively. All of the fluorescence measurements were normalized to the OD600 values.

For the assay for determining β-glucuronidase (GUS), pre-treatment cell culture is collected and resuspended in 10 mM, pH 7.5 Tris-HCl buffer. The resuspended bacterial solution was lysed with 10 U/mL Zymolyase (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in a 96-well plate at 30 °C for approximately several hours until the lysed solution turned translucent. Each well contained 90 μL of 50 mM PBS buffer,2 μL of lysed cell solution and 89 μL of 1 g/L p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucuronide. 20 μL of 0.1 M NaOH was added promptly into the reaction for termination after 10 min incubation at 37 °C. The absorbance value was measured at 405 nm on a microplate reader, while the enzyme activity was converted and calculated according to the literature.17

As for the lysis rate assessment, cells harvest and washing were similar to the GUS assay activity described above. Then, cells were lysed with Snailase (Sangon, Shanghai, China) in a 96-well plate according to the instructions and the turbidity was measured on 600 nm absorbance on a microplate reader. The lysis rate was calculated as the division of pre-and post-lysis of OD600 value.

The GC content and repetitive sequences of CEN/ARS was analyzed by an online sequence analysis website (http://www.detaibio.com/tools/). The transcription factors of natural promoters of brewing yeast were predicted by online toll YEASTRACT (Yeast Search for Transcriptional Regulators And Consensus Tracking) (http://www.yeastract.com/formfindregulators.php), and the selected transcription factor data were graphed. The dot matrix analysis was generated with DNAMAN software. The nucleosome occupancy of promoter was predicted using the NuPoP program (http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/NuPoP.html).

Results and discussion

Dual-plasmid interaction stimulates the growth and metabolism of S. cerevisiae

It is generally believed that plasmid could bring lots of potential impacts to the hosts, such as slow growth and metabolic burden. However, an exciting discovery in our previous work on constructing valencene-producing yeast overturned the general belief. For valencene production and the subsequent gene modification, the plasmid pEV carrying the gene encoding valencene synthase (VS) (Fig. 1C) and plasmid pICas carrying the gene encoding gene editing protein Cas9 (Fig. 1D) were constructed in our previous work, and thus the original strain BJ-V with plasmid pEV and the pre-engineered strain BJ-CV with plasmid pEV and pICas were generated. However, the valencene yield of the pre-engineered strain BJ-CV was 1.5-fold higher than those of the original strain BJ-V, which was supposed to be the same since both strains were not metabolically optimized. Moreover, the cell density and growth rate in BJ-CV were increased by 1.2-fold compared to that in BJ-V, and correspondingly the specific valencene production of BJ-CV was increased by 1.2-fold (Fig. 1A&B). The only difference between the two strains was that the strain BJ-CV contained an additional plasmid pICas compared to the strain BJ-V. That is to say, the additional plasmid pICas showed a promotive effect on growth and production in yeast.

For this result, the gene editing protein Cas9 expressed by the plasmid pICas was first suspected to be the main factor. Therefore, the empty plasmid pI was constructed by deleting the Cas9-encoding gene from the plasmid pICas. However, the growth and valencene yield of the resulting strain BJ-IV containing plasmid pEV and pI were similarly identical to those of BJ-CV (Fig. 1A&B). The results implied that the empty plasmid pI expressing no extra gene played a role in accelerating cell growth and product synthesis in S. cerevisiae.

Our results contradicted previous studies on plasmid-host, which have pointed to the selfish characteristics of plasmid and long held that dual plasmids would aggravate the metabolic burden and fitness cost on host cells and even lead to antagonism between plasmids. These interesting finding drives us to explore further the possible mechanisms of the empty plasmid pI in yeast and its potential interactions with the expression plasmid pEV.

Identification of the response level in dual-plasmid interactions

As a derivative of the secondary metabolite in yeast, the synthesis of valencene involves complex metabolic regulation. Hence, to examine the possibility that the improvement of the valencene yield was the result of the plasmid pI acting on the specific metabolic pathways, five reporter proteins including β-glucuronidase (GUS) and fluorescent proteins (GFP, ERFP, mCherry and YPet) were selected to replace the valencene synthase on the expression plasmid pEV. After introducing the resulting plasmids, the enzymatic activity and fluorescence intensity of strains containing plasmid pI were significantly higher than those without plasmid pI (Fig. 2), which suggested the universality of the improvement effect across multiple products and little link to the specific metabolic regulation.

The enzyme activity of β-glucuronidase (GUS) (A) and fluorescent intensity of respective fluorescent proteins (GFP, ERFP, mCherry and YPet) (B–E) in strains BJ-X containing the expression plasmid and strains BJ-IX containing expression plasmid and plasmid pI. Significant differences compared to the control are denoted as **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Based on the results above, we speculated that the improvement in valencene production was related to the dual plasmids themselves, possibly the interaction between them leading to an increase in the copy number of the expression plasmid pEV or the up-regulated expression of the gene on plasmid pEV. The analysis of quantitative PCR assays of the strains BJ-V and BJ-IV revealed no significant difference in the copy number of plasmid pEV, whereas the expression level of the valencene synthase encoding gene on plasmid pEV was dramatically increased by 5-fold in strain BJ-IV compared to BJ-V (Fig. 3). More interestingly, the expression level of β-isopropylmalate dehydrogenase encoding gene (LEU2), the nutrient-deficient marker on the plasmid pEV, showed no significant difference between strain BJ-IV and BJ-V (Fig. 3B). These results indicated the plasmid pI stimulated the up-regulated expression of the specific gene on the expression plasmid pEV. The increase in valencene yield was attributed to the elevation in transcriptional level rather than replication level, consistent with previous reports of the cost-effectiveness of the selfish plasmids.18 The copy number of plasmid in yeast was strictly limited by a complex communication between the isolation system and amplification system to minimize the conflict with host and sustain long-term persistence. In comparison, transcriptional control directed at target gene on plasmid may be less prone to plasmid-host conflicts.

Deconstruction of the elements of the two plasmids to probe into the essential features and its response mechanism

For the above result of the up-regulated expression of VS genes on expression plasmid pEV in the presence of plasmid pI in S. cerevisiae, the elements on both plasmids were analyzed to explore the key components of the response mechanism.

The assistant plasmid pI was first in focus by substitution of its essential elements and deletion of non-essential elements. The valencene yields of the strains containing the plasmid pI with multiple cloning sites deleted or f1 bacteriophage origin of replication deleted or TRP1 marker replaced by URA3 marker were still as high as that of strain BN-IV with the original plasmid pI. However, the replacement of centromere and autonomous replication sequence (CEN/ARS) with 2μ origin of replication (2μ ori) on plasmid pI, labeled as plasmid pI2, failed to elevate the production of valencene in yeast (Fig. 4A). The replication sequence showed the essential role in the dual-plasmids promotive effect. To further validate the impact of replication sequences, several commercial plasmids with different replication sequences were co-transformed into yeast with plasmid pEV. Only the strain BN-I6V containing plasmid pCTcon2 with CEN/ARS element exhibited an elevation in valencene yield, while the strains containing plasmid YEp352, xw06, or p426 with 2μ ori element showed comparable yields to strain BN-V (Fig. 4B). These data demonstrated the crucial importance of the CEN/ARS element in the dual-plasmid interactions. CEN/ARS elements are essential for stable plasmid maintenance and correct plasmid distribution during cell division for yeast centromeric plasmids (YCps), which persist in yeast cells like mini-chromosomes by exploiting endogenous replication and chromosome segregation mechanisms.19,20 Our results provide the first insight into the role of the replication sequence CEN/ARS as the assistant sequences in a dual-plasmid system to stimulate the expression of the gene on expression plasmid.

The expression plasmid pEV was treated with similar substitution strategies to probe its response elements. The resulting plasmids with 2μ ori replaced by CEN/ARS or LEU2 marker replaced by URA3 marker still responded to plasmid pI and ultimately led to an increase in valencene production (Fig. 4C). Inspired by the above result of no up-regulation of LEU2 gene in the presence of plasmid pI, its promoter PLEU2 was selected to replace the promoter PTDH3 to control the expression of VS gene. There was no significant difference in valencene yield of strains either with or without plasmid pI. These results reflect the decisive role of the promoter in the response mechanism, as only specific promoters could respond to plasmid pI and thus transcriptionally upregulate a target gene.

Dual-plasmid system was commonly used for the expression of two or more genes in previous studies, in which the relationship between the plasmid collocation and product synthesis was the main concern. In our cases, plasmid pI did not carry any redundant functional genes and could not provide any precursor or donor assistance for product synthesis. The increase in the valencene yields was achieved just by the response of the specific promoter on the expression plasmid pEV to the CEN/ARS sequence of the assistant plasmid pI.

Assessment of poly(dA:dT) sequences on CEN/ARS element and promoter sequences

Promoters are regarded as the fundamental regulatory elements responsible for transcription initiation.21 To identify the specificity on the responding promoter, a series of common promoters in yeast were constructed in plasmid pEV to control the expression of VS gene. To our surprise, in the presence of the plasmid pI, in addition to the predictable increase or no change in valencene yields of some strains, it also showed decreased yields in several strains in response to the dual-plasmids interaction (Fig. 5). These findings suggested the type of promoters determined the response effect, which further raised the complexity on this response mechanism.

Among them, purple, orange, and blue columns were used to distinguish no response, increased response, and decreased response for the dual-plasmid interactions, respectively. The promoters used above were derived from various metabolic pathways in yeast, comprising the glycolytic pathway, pentose phosphate pathway, ethanol metabolism, structural genes encoding cell walls, RNA transport, and amino acid metabolism (Table S4). They possessed typical characteristics of yeast promoters, such as the diversity of predicted transcription factor motifs, high AT content and containing poly(dA:dT) (Fig. S1). Coincidentally, sequence analysis on the CEN/ARS sequence of plasmid pI also exhibited an overall distribution of the high AT and poly(dA:dT) content, which contained a large number of AT base repeats and palindromic sequences (Fig. S2). Through dot-matrix alignment between the sequence of CEN/ARS and promoters, the highly similar poly(dA:dT) sequences T(n)C to CEN/ARS were found in all decreased-response promoters, such as TTTTTTCTTTCTTTTTT for PTEF2, TTTTTTTTTTTTCTTTCTTTCTCTTT for PCDC19 and TTTTTCTTTTTTTTTCTTCCTTCTTTTTC for PENO1 (Fig. S1B). The more regrettable part was that no more sequence similarities or regularities were found in these promoters to distinguish or cluster the three response groups (increase/no response/decrease).

Several studies have reported that poly(dA:dT) sequence was usually present in yeast promoters and stimulated transcription through their intrinsic particular DNA structure, which was also described as nucleosome adverse sequence for its impact on nucleosome formation.22, 23, 24 Combined with the above conclusion about the response mechanism being relating to transcription, the similar T(n)C sequence found could be considered as a breakthrough point. Therefore, we conducted a series of molecular modification strategies for the identified T(n)C sequence to verify the regulatory effect of the sequence in the response mechanism. Deletion of the T(n)C sequence from the promoters resulted in a change in the direction of the dual-plasmid response in the strains, from a decrease in valencene yield to an increase or no response (Fig. 6A(a)). Moreover, disruption of poly(dA:dT) by base mutation in the T(n)C sequence could also shift the direction of the response (Fig. S3). These results implied the vital role for the T(n)C sequence of promoters in the response mechanism.

To further investigate the dual-plasmid response effect, the T(n)C sequence from promoter PTEF2, which was shorter but showed a greater variation after being deleted in the response effect, was inserted into some promoters of the other response groups. The insertion position of the T(n)C sequence could not affect the response status and showed similar fold change for response with the insertion position shifted (Fig. S4), reflecting the position-less preference of the T(n)C sequence on promoter. Although the insertion of the T(n)C sequence in these promoters did not change the response status to decrease as we had predicted, it still affected the response effect to different degrees (Fig. 6A(b)). It suggested that the deletion or insertion of T(n)C sequences in the promoters could not simply corresponding to the response status of increase or decrease or no-response for the dual-plasmid interactions. Considering the well-known transcriptional regulation mechanism about poly(dA:dT) sequences and nucleosome,24, 25, 26 the nucleosome occupancy for the promoters were next predicted and analyzed.27 The more significant the change of the response effect with the insertion or deletion of T(n)C sequence to the promoter, the greater the alteration in its nucleosome occupancy (Fig. 6). These results suggested that the impact of insertion or deletion of the T(n)C sequence on the transcriptional environment of the promoter determined the change in response effect of the dual-plasmid interactions. Hence we speculated the dual-plasmid interaction might be related to the change of spatial three-dimensional structure associated to the poly(dA:dT) sequence and the dynamic regulation with nucleosome.

Furthermore, as shown by the prediction of nucleosome occupancy for each promoter, the promoters in the response groups (increase and decrease) presented multiple regions with extremely low nucleosome occupancy and most of the promoters contained nucleosome-depleted region (NDR) (Fig. S5). NDR often contained transcription factor binding sites, whose accessibility allows appropriate response of genes to changes in the transcription factor activity. Meanwhile, the promoters in the no-response group either presented generally higher nucleosome occupancy at the ends or possessed shorter fragments thus lacking regulatory regions. Therefore, based on the above analysis, we proposed a conjectural model for the response mechanism of dual-plasmid interactions. The three-dimensional transcriptional environment of the plasmids might be changed when the CEN/ARS element on the assistant plasmid pI interact with the promoter element on the expression plasmid pEV, hence leading to a tuning of transcriptional regulation. For the promoters with looser chromatin environment (possibly due to a lower nucleosome occupancy or due to the bending degree of the nucleosomes creating gaps at certain positions), they might be suitable for the binding of CEN/ARS element. The binding could made the environment around the promoter more conducive to binding with potential transcription factors, as the poly(dA:dT) sequence in CEN/ARS element play its role in reducing nucleosome accessibility and promoting transcription factor binding, acting similarly to an enhancer or silencer, which ultimately increased or decreased the transcription level. As for promoters with high nucleosome occupancy, tightly packed nucleosome, or limited regulatory region, they might be difficult to bind with CEN/ARS element, making the difficulty of dual-plasmid interactions and thus resulting in unchanged transcription level. Under the influence of changes in the three-dimensional spatial structure of promoters and the diversification of transcription factors and transcription complexes, the regulation patterns of the dual-plasmid interactions also exhibited the diversity and variability.

Our study revealed the distant spatial interactions between double plasmids at the DNA sequence level to regulate plasmid expression. It was the first report regarding the interaction of two DNA sequences with relatively far physical distances. Previous studies have described the interactions at the RNA level, with miRNAs capable of inducing gene silencing by binding to mRNA and ceRNAs able to regulate gene expression by competitively binding miRNAs.28 Vasudevan Lab discovered that miRNA is not always a negative regulator of gene expression; in certain conditions, it can also up-regulate gene expression.29,30 Similar to miRNAs and ceRNAs both being non-coding genes,28 our double plasmid elements CEN/ARS and promoter are also non-coding genes, demonstrating the potential of non-coding genes to regulate other genes unobtrusively.

To seek and probe into the dominant plasmid out of the dual-plasmid

In the further explorations, we found that the entry order of the two plasmids into yeast was the key contributing factor for the dual-plasmid interactions. Transformation of the plasmids pEV and pI were performed either simultaneously or sequentially, resulting in the recombinant strains BJ-IV, BJ-V-I and BJ-I-V in different transformation orders (Fig. 7A). Compared to the strain BJ-V carrying plasmid pEV only, strains BJ-IV and BJ-I-V exhibited a significant increase in valencene yield and growth, whereas the strain BJ-V-I showed a similar growth and yield to strain BJ-V (Fig. 7B). These results indicated that for dual-plasmid interactions, the assistant plasmid pI should not enter into the yeast behind the expression plasmid pEV. It was different in amount of the two plasmids in the yeast, as the plasmid pEV was the multi-copy plasmid and pI was the single-copy plasmid. The difference in copy number of the two plasmids might make them present in yeast in different manner (e.g., morphology, surround, etc.), which might affect the dual-plasmid interactions in terms of three-dimensional space. Considering the possible variability of the position of the plasmid in cells, we speculated that the plasmid pI played a recruiting effect on plasmid pEV, which facilitated the contact between the two plasmids and created the transcriptional-level interactions. For example, if the plasmid pEV was introduced first, they might have aggregated or dispersed in the cell before the plasmid pI was transformed into the yeast. The plasmid pI might not be able to pass across the plasmid pEV cluster or locate the pEV plasmid. However, suppose the plasmid pI was transformed first or simultaneously with plasmid pEV, it would orbit to the site beforehand and potentially recruit the plasmid pEV in time and space (Fig. 7C). These results reflected the decisive role of plasmid pI for the mechanism of dual-plasmid interactions.

Conclusion

In this study, we established a response model of dual-plasmid interactions centered on the T(n)C sequence on promoters of expression plasmid pEV and CEN/ARS element of assistant plasmid pI. Our researches developed a novel strategy in regulating gene expression with dual plasmids in S. cerevisiae, which opened a new avenue for construction of microbial cell factories.
