Patterns and outcomes of abdominal wound injuries in Sudanese armed conflict survivors: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ahmed Rafei, Swsan A Elsharif, Elaf M Abdelraheem, Mustafa A A Elamin, Ahmed Osman, Mohanned Salman, Mohanad Elbashier, Mohammed Ganim, Razan Rafei, Ghssan Alhajbakeet, Ameer Abaker, Mohamed Mamoun, Amna Hussein, Hossam Khalifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimOn April 15, 2023, the people of Khartoum were woken by the drums of war. The armed conflict resulted in >1200 reported fatalities and over 12 000 injuries. The abdomen, housing vital organs and lacking protective bony structures, is highly vulnerable in such settings. Understanding abdominal wound injuries (AWIs) is essential for improving management and outcomes. This study aimed to analyze the patterns and outcomes of AWIs among survivors of the Sudanese armed conflict.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at Wad Madni Teaching Hospital, Gezira State, Sudan, from April 15 to October 31, 2023. All patients with AWIs who underwent exploratory laparotomy and had complete records were included. Data were collected from medical records and analyzed using SPSS V.26. Demographic and clinical variables, mechanisms of injury, intraoperative findings, complications, and outcomes were assessed.ResultsThirty-three patients were admitted with AWIs during the study period. Most were civilians (87.5%) and male (93.9%), with a mean age of 28 years. The primary mechanisms were stabbing (54.5%) and gunshots (33.3%). The small intestine (33.3%) and large intestine (30.3%) were the most affected organs. Intraoperative findings showed visceral injuries in 66.7% of cases. The mean delay from injury to admission was 27 hours (range 1–148). Combat-related injuries were more often gunshot wounds, involved multiple organs, and were associated with longer delays to admission (47 vs 19 hours, p=0.003), longer hospital stays (7 vs 5 days, p=0.009), and higher mortality (18.2% vs 0%, p=0.039). Overall postoperative mortality was 6.1%, mainly due to sepsis.ConclusionAWIs in the Sudanese conflict were predominantly penetrating, with high intestinal involvement and significant prehospital delays. Combat-related injuries were more severe and carried higher mortality. These findings highlight the need for faster referral systems, context-specific surgical strategies, and infection-control measures in resource-limited conflict zones.Level of evidenceLevel III (retrospective cohort study).Keywords: abdominal injuries, War-Related Injuries, wounds and injuries

Combat-related abdominal injuries account for approximately 11% of battlefield trauma and are associated with high morbidity and mortality. Injury severity is influenced by factors such as wound size, number of affected organs, fecal contamination, intraoperative blood loss, and delays in definitive surgical management.

Introduction

On April 15, 2023, the people of Khartoum were woken up by the drums of war. An armed conflict trapped these innocent people into a prison of uncertainty.

Since the escalation of violence in Sudan, the conflict has resulted in significant casualties, with over 1200 fatalities and 12 000 people injured directly or indirectly. Although the actual number of casualties is expected to be much higher,1 2 escaping this ongoing catastrophe has led to the displacement of 5.3 million people within Sudan and its neighboring countries.3

In war-torn Khartoum, access to healthcare services has been critically jeopardized, with a shocking 79% of hospitals (70 out of 89) non-functional in conflict-affected areas such as Khartoum, North Kordufan, and Darfur, according to a report from the Preliminary Committee of Sudan Doctor’s Trade Union.1 2

These injuries present a unique surgical challenge because of the specific nature of the wounds and the often chaotic conditions in which they are treated. Abdominal wound injuries (AWIs), the primary focus of our study, contribute to an overall average of 7% of the anatomical distribution of major wounds based on historical approximations.4

Previous studies have indicated that the risk of bodily harm varies depending on the weapon used, the nature of the combat situation, the specific military activity being conducted, and the body part exposed. According to Carey et al5, the prevalence of abdominal exposure and harm in military combat scenarios is approximately 11%.6

The widely variable forms of firearms and shotguns used in war battlefields result in unpredictable patterns and variable kinetics of AWIs, leading to discrete immediate complications and long-term re-admissions for AWI-related disabilities.

The outcome of abdominal injuries is determined by the size of the wound and several other factors, including the number of organs injured, the degree of fecal contamination, intraoperative blood loss, and delay in operative treatment.6

The standard management approach involves performing a laparotomy, a procedure crucial because of the potentially life-threatening nature of such injuries, characterized by significant bleeding from abdominal organs.7 For instance, if the pancreas is affected, there is an added risk of injury from autodigestion. Liver injuries, known for presenting with shock, are exacerbated by the extensive blood supply to the liver tissue. In addition, the intestines are at risk of perforation, introducing further complications such as the penetration of fecal matter.

This presented article is a historical necessity and provides valuable insight into the epidemiology, mechanisms, and outcomes of AWIs in Sudanese civil war survivors.

To the best of our knowledge, no study has comprehensively described the impact and pattern of AWIs across Sudan from the Sudanese armed conflict. Barely any studies were directed toward studying the grave impact the Sudanese armed conflict had and still has on people’s lives.

In this study, we retrospectively evaluated patients operated on for AWIs, brought to Wad Madni Teaching Hospital during the armed conflict. We aimed to examine the patterns and characteristics of AWIs and associated outcomes.

Methods

This study employed a retrospective cohort design to investigate the impact and pattern of AWIs among survivors of the Sudanese armed conflict, adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies. The retrospective approach facilitated the analysis of existing medical records to assess outcomes of interest.

The research was conducted at Wad Madni Teaching Hospital in Wad Madni city, Sudan. Located in Gezira state, 180 km south of Khartoum—the main area of conflict and casualty—Wad Madni city served as a significant center for health services, becoming a key location for the treatment of conflict-related injuries. The study period included patients admitted from the beginning of the conflict (April 15, 2023) to October 31, 2023.

The target population comprised Sudanese armed conflict survivors seeking medical treatment at the emergency department of Wad Madni Teaching Hospital. Inclusion criteria encompassed individuals of all ages with AWIs from the armed conflict who underwent laparotomy, all patients with suspected abdominal injury admitted to the hospital underwent exploratory laparotomy, and patients with complete medical records. The exclusion criteria included patients treated outside the hospital and those with incomplete medical records.

The study adopted a census approach that encompassed all eligible medical records of patients meeting the criteria admitted to the emergency department during the study period. This approach ensured a representative sample of the population under investigation.

Variables of interest included independent variables such as demographic factors (age, gender) and conflict-related variables (mechanism and type of injury, situation when the patient got injured). Outcomes included the type of intraoperative findings, the interval between injury to admission, length of hospitalization, and early postoperative complications.

Regarding combat involvement, individuals were classified into two categories. The combat group included patients injured while actively engaged in fighting, such as those participating in firefights, exposed to direct enemy fire, or directly involved in armed clashes. The non-combat group referred to patients who sustained abdominal penetrating injuries in the context of the conflict but were not directly engaged in combat. This category included unarmed civilians such as bystanders, internally displaced persons, or individuals injured during assaults, shelling, or stray gunfire, who were affected by the violence of the conflict but were not themselves combatants.

Data for this study were retrospectively obtained from medical records in the emergency department and analyzed using SPSS for Windows, V.26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Qualitative variables (gender, patients’ characteristics, and injury features) are presented as frequencies and percentages, and injury timelines were depicted as a line chart. Quantitative variables (age and the interval from injury to admission) were reported as mean±SD. Cross-tabulations categorized patients’ and injury characteristics by the mechanism of injury, and χ2 tests assessed the distribution of injury characteristics by the mechanism. Additionally, comparisons between non-combat and combat situations were performed using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. A p value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From April 15, 2023 to October 31, 2023, Wad-Madni Teaching Hospital in Wad Madni, Sudan, admitted 33 patients with AWIs due to armed conflict, comprising 31 males and two females with ages ranging from 15 to 60 years (mean age: 28±11 years). The injuries were caused by stabbing (54.5%), gunshots (33.3%), blunt objects (9.1%), and shrapnel (3%). The interval from injury to admission varied from 1 to 148 hours (mean interval: 27±40 hours). Injury characteristics

Notably, 33.3% of the patients had one injured organ, 42.4% had two injured organs, and 3% had three injured organs. Patients with negative intraoperative findings accounted for 21.2%.

Concerning extra-abdominal injuries, nine patients had thoracic injuries, most of whom developed hemothorax; five had musculoskeletal injuries involving the extremities; two had neurological injuries resulting in paraplegia; and two had pelvic injuries involving the urethra (tables3 4). Significant differences were observed between non-combat and active combat injuries. Civilians were mostly injured in non-combat situations, while combatants were exclusively injured in combat (p=0.003). Stabbing predominated in non-combat injuries, whereas gunshot wounds were more frequent in combat (p<0.001). Combat-related injuries involved a higher number of injured organs (p=0.014) and were associated with postoperative mortality (18.2% vs 0%, p=0.039). The mean interval from injury to admission was significantly longer in combat injuries (47 vs 19 hours, p=0.003), and hospital stays were also longer (7 vs 5 days, p=0.009). Differences in sex (p=0.606), visceral (p=0.192), vascular (p=0.451), solid organ (p=0.731), and diaphragmatic injuries (p=0.451) were not statistically significant. Regarding early postoperative complications, two patients developed sepsis. In addition, one patient experienced acute kidney injury and another had paralytic ileus. No documented cases developed surgical site infection, anastomotic leaks, bile fistulas, urinary leaks, intra-abdominal abscesses, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or pancreatic fistulas.

All patients who underwent laparotomy were transferred from the operating room to the general ward. Hospital stays ranged from 2 to 13 days (mean period: 5.64 days). Two patients passed away before discharge; both had sepsis as postoperative complications. Both patients had sustained injuries to their large and small intestines, along with vascular compromise. The time from injury to admission differed significantly. The first patient presented after 13 hours, while the second patient waited 114 hours before seeking medical attention. Postoperative mortality occurred only in active combat injuries, with longer hospital stays on average.

Discussion

In this study, we highlighted the patterns and characteristics of abdominal war injuries among patients admitted to Wad Madani Hospital. Our population is mainly male civilians, mostly injured by stab wounds with an average delay of admission of 27 hours. The small and large bowls were the most injured organs. The results obtained from our study, along with previous related literature, affirm the strong association of the abdomen with an increased incidence of war-related injuries, management complexities, and life-threatening complications. These reported outcomes can be attributed to the unique anatomical properties of the abdomen, such as housing vital organs, the absence of an outer bony surface, and vulnerability to horizontal injuries during combat.8 9

Our study included 33 patients with ages ranging from 15 to 60 years (mean age: 28 years). Of these patients, 31 (93.9%) were males and only two (6.1%) were females. The predominance of males in this age group can be attributed to the fact that armed personnel are commonly males, and for civilian males, their inclination to take daily risks to provide for their families during these vicious and murderous days is a factual tendency, as suggested by numerous conflict-related studies.410,12

Additionally, even under conflict-free conditions, males tend to display more violent behavior, contributing to their higher rate of trauma.13,15 Civilians have been significantly affected in recent wars and face a higher risk of injuries.16 Our research reveals that 87.5% of total reported injuries are civilians, while 12.5% were combatants. These attained statistics align with reported information from the Lebanon and Gulf wars, where civilians accounted for 70% and 50% of the injured population, respectively.17 18 This can be explained by the nature of all-out wars with a wide spectrum of heavy artillery used in overcrowded residential regions, which enormously affects civilians.19

Moreover, it is worth noting that combatants usually receive medical treatment in military hospitals where access to data is prohibited, which may considerably alter these findings.10

The time between injury and admission to a health facility varied widely, ranging from 1 to 148 hours. On average, it took about 27 hours for patients to be admitted. This broad variability in time can be linked to the catastrophic collapse and limited availability of health services in war-torn cities, as well as challenges related to distance and security when attempting to reach a health facility.20

Surprisingly, the most noticeable mechanism of injury was stabbing (54.5%), followed by gunshot (33.3%), and shrapnel showed the least percentage (3.0%). This unexpected finding can be attributed to the widespread chaos and lack of security forces in the war-ridden regions. This contrasts with previous studies that have identified explosive devices as the primary cause of injury.11 21 The frequency of penetrating injury increases drastically in weapons-abundant environments, such as full-blown military conflict.7 In this manner, it is obvious from our results that the ratio of penetrating to blunt injuries is extremely high (10:1), consistent with studies conducted in Lebanon17 and in Nigeria during the Jos civil crisis.12

According to our investigations, the small and large intestine are the most commonly injured organs, with percentages of 33.3% and 30.3%, respectively. Following closely are the stomach, liver, and bladder, each accounting for 9.1% of cases. The extrahepatic biliary system and spleen had injury rates of 3.0%. Similar studies agreed that the small and large intestine were the most prevalent injured organs. The vulnerable anatomical sites of both intestines in terms of covering periphery and center of the abdominal cavity, along with the previously mentioned high rates of penetrating trauma, can explain this finding.4 10 21 22

It is worth noting that 33.3% of patients had an injury to one organ, 42.4% had injuries to two organs, and only 3.0% had injuries to three organs. A study conducted in Syria yielded similar results, with 34% of patients having injuries to one organ and 56.4% having injuries to two or more organs.10 The chief prevalence of multiple organ injuries points toward the fatal firearms and shotguns used in battlefields.

The percentage of patients with negative intra-operative findings was found to be 21.2%, which was significantly higher compared with parallel studies conducted in Syria, Lebanon, and Turkey (10.2%, 8.3%, and 7.0%, respectively).10 11 21 23 The current disastrous situation of the health system has made access to pre-operative imaging nearly impossible; thus, any patient with suspected abdominal organ injury has undergone laparotomy.20

In line with previous research,13 24 our findings confirm that the thorax is the most common extra-abdominal site of injury, accounting for 27.3% of total injuries, likely due to the close proximity of the thorax to the abdomen in terms of anatomy.

Overcrowded health facilities usually shorten hospital stay days with an average of 5.64 days. The average time from injury to admission was 27 hours, with wide variability (1–148 hours). Such delays stem from the collapse of health infrastructure in Khartoum, insecurity during travel, and the 140 km distance from frontline areas in Khartoum to Wad Madani, the main referral center.25 Distance and delays likely influenced outcomes: patients who survived long transfers were those stable enough to reach the hospital, while more critically injured patients may have died before arrival. This survivorship effect partly explains the relatively low postoperative mortality of 6.1% in our cohort compared with higher rates reported elsewhere.4 11 12 Shorter hospital stays due to overcrowding and limited follow-up may have further contributed to the appearance of lower mortality.

Amid these trying situations, war-related injuries become more complicated and different from civilian trauma injuries, as they are often contaminated with debris, the harm caused is enormous and uncertain, and hospitals are overwhelmed with similar cases. With these unfavorable conditions, infection becomes a major risk factor in these cases, and thus, septic surgery protocols must be followed to minimize the risk of infection.6

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective and based on a single referral hospital, introducing selection bias, as most patients were civilians from Khartoum and nearby regions, while victims from other conflict zones such as Darfur or Kordufan were not represented. Second, only patients who survived transfer and reached Wad Madani were included; battlefield deaths and those who died en route were not captured, leading to underestimation of true mortality. Third, poor healthcare infrastructure, lack of preoperative imaging, incomplete records, and unmeasured factors such as quality of resuscitation and blood transfusion availability limited the completeness of data. Finally, the lack of long-term follow-up means late complications and delayed mortality were not assessed. These constraints limit the generalizability of findings but reflect the real-world challenges of conducting research during an active conflict.

In conclusion, during Sudan’s armed conflict, our study highlights the devastating impact of abdominal injuries, underscoring the unique challenges posed by war-related trauma. Defining the affected population and injury patterns, especially at a national level, can identify areas of focus for further research, reflect the magnitude of the impact of this war on injury-related morbidity and mortality, and is a crucial initial step toward the development of effective injury prevention programs, guiding treatment directions, and honing surgical practices amid this humanitarian crisis.
