Recovery and regeneration: exploring positive sequelae of keratitis
Authors: Ali Kutay Kılınç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisruption of the corneal epithelial barrier and the use of contact lenses are important risk factors for keratitis. Severe cases of keratitis can cause serious problems, including corneal scar and perforation, which can cause vision loss. Although keratitis is a significant cause of blindness, the healing process can offer substantial relief to patients who develop it after bullous keratopathy.Case presentationAn 85-year-old patient, using bandage contact lenses for bullous keratopathy, presented with redness, mild pain and a sticky sensation in their left eye. His best visual acuity was 20/32 in the right eye and no light perception in the left eye. Biomicroscopic examination revealed pseudoexfoliation syndrome in the right eye, as well as circumferential and multifocal satellite lesions with hypopyon in the left eye. Fundus examination showed glaucomatous optic cupping in the right eye, while the left fundus could not be visualized. Ultrasonographic findings were normal. Following treatment for the fungal infection, the patient experienced significant relief of his symptoms due to corneal recovery and scar tissue formation.ConclusionThis case report demonstrates the beneficial impact of tissue repair and regeneration on the healing trajectory of cornea in a patient presenting with bullous keratopathy and stinging symptoms.

Introduction

Corneal dehydration can be affected by several factors, including epithelial barrier and endothelial pump dysfunction, alterations in stromal swelling pressure or intraocular pressure (IOP) and increased tear evaporation [1]. Corneal edema due to increased IOP does not occur in the presence of a healthy endothelium unless the IOP surpasses the stromal swelling pressure. Even if with the edema develops, the cornea can recover through endothelial compensation if the endothelial cell number and function are adequate. However, when endothelial decompensation occurs, fluid accumulates within the interstitial spaces of the stroma and epithelial layers, leading to a disruption in corneal transparency [2]. Patients with glaucoma and elevated IOP exhibited reduced corneal endothelial cell counts compared to their counterparts [3]. Antiglaucomatous medications have also been associated with an increased risk of this condition [4]. In cases of bullous keratopathy where the corneal endothelium is irreparably damaged, the only definitive treatment option is corneal transplantation. While corneal transplantation is the definitive treatment, alternative approaches can be used before surgery to address the patient’s condition [2]. Failures in the managing corneal decompensation can lead to serious problems, such as keratitis [5].

Microbial keratitis can arise from various sources, including bacteria, viruses, fungi and protozoa [6]. Among the various etiologies of infectious keratitis, fungal keratitis is the second most common cause of infectious keratitis after bacterial keratitis [7], yet it poses a greater threat for corneal blindness. Fungal keratitis has relatively slow progression and patients with fungal keratitis typically experiencing milder symptoms compared to bacterial keratitis. Additionally, it generally has a longer disease duration, because the relatively lengthy process required for fungal cultures, coupled with the low corneal penetration of antifungal medications and a delayed response to treatment. With all these characteristics, fungal keratitis contributes to a poorer prognosis and a higher prevalence of corneal perforation compared to bacterial keratitis [8].

Fungal keratitis is estimated to affect one million people annually worldwide, with the highest incidence rates reported in Asia and Africa [9, 10]. The most prevalent fungal species responsible for keratitis are Fusarium, Aspergillus and Candida, with their frequency often varying based on factors like temperature, climate and urbanization [11]. Typical fungal keratitis patients are outdoor workers and presents with history of microtrauma caused by organic plants. Besides vegetative trauma, equatorial geographic location and warmer climate, risk factors include chronic ocular surface diseases, epithelial defects, diabetes, topical corticosteroid use, systemic immunosuppression and the use of hydrophilic contact lenses [12, 13]. It occurs when pathogens invade the corneal stroma through defects in the corneal epithelium. Fungi proliferate within the stroma, exhibiting a pattern of circumferential expansion and the formation of satellite lesions. The progressive invasion of the fungi allows them to penetrate deeper into the stroma, breaching Descemet’s membrane and gaining access to the anterior chamber. Corneal invasion and colonization, including the production of toxins and enzymes; such as serine proteases and matrix metalloproteinases; can result in tissue destruction and necrosis [14, 15]. Without effective intervention, either from the body’s natural defenses or appropriate treatment, this ongoing progression may ultimately lead to corneal perforation and the development of endophthalmitis [16].

Case presentation

In Fabuary 2023, The 85-year-old man was being followed in the glaucoma department for his bilateral pseudoexfoliation syndrome. He had previously undergone bilateral cataract surgery and a right trabeculectomy procedure 20 years ago, but despite maximum topical treatment, his IOP remained elevated above 20 mmHg. This asymptomatic patient, who lived in a rural village, was scheduled for follow-up appointments every 6 months.

In June 2023 the patient presented with a stinging complaint in his left eye and was found to have bullous keratopathy. Dorzolamide has been stopped among antiglaucoma drops. Initially, artificial tears was prescribed, but this did not provide relief. Bandage contact lenses and topical antibiotic (netilmicin 0,3%, one drop per night) were recommended for the patient, who was unable to see light with lefte eye and had difficulty with follow-up care. The patient’s general condition disabled him from undergoing surgical intervention. He was advised to remove the contact lens at appropriate intervals. However, October 2023, the patient reported not following the recommended hygienic conditions and returned to our clinic with complaints of pain, watering and redness. Biomicroscopic examination revealed the presence of hypopyon, multifocal and satellite infiltrates, as well as the development of an endothelial plaque and hypopyon. (Fig. 1) The patient’s antibiotic use and farming history suggested a fungal keratitis diagnosis. Scraped corneal materials cultured on blood agar and Sabouraud dextrose agar were negative for fungal species. Topical fortified vancomycin (5 mg/0,1 ml), ceftazidime (5 mg/0,1 ml) and voriconazole (1 mg/0,1 ml) drops were started in the patient’s treatment, alternating every hour. Voriconazole intravenous (after loading dose, 2 × 3 mg/kg) therapy was added and was planned to be continued for 10 days. In addition to topical drops, artificial tears were administered every hour and %1 tropicamide eyedrop was added three times a day. During treatment period, the patient’s antiglaucomatous drops was discontinued and her ocular pressure was followed digitally. Clinical improvement was observed within 4–5 days of the prescribed treatment. (Fig. 2). One week later, fortified vancomycin and ceftazidime were stopped. The patient’s treatment continued with artificial tears, topical voriconazole and tropicamide eye drops. At the end of one month, the patient’s hypopyon resolved, corneal infiltrates disappeared, and the conjunctival scar on the corneal surface healed, significantly relieved the patient’s stinging symptom. (Fig. 3) This unique case highlights the potential for positive outcomes and corneal regeneration in severe cases of keratitis.

Discussion

In cases of blindness associated with neural system disorders, the functional status of the optic system is often overlooked, as it is presumed to be nonfunctional. Our patient, who experienced complete blindness due to optic atrophy, nevertheless faced the inevitability of corneal decompensation. Bullous keratopathy is a condition characterized by endothelial decompensation, leading to stromal edema and epithelial cysts due to impaired endothelial pump function. Also our patient’s history of previous glaucoma surgery, elevated intraocular pressure and resulting corneal decompensation illustrate the interplay between glaucoma management and corneal health.

The exposure of nerve endings resulting from epithelial detachment can contribute to significant limitations in daily activities, insomnia, decreased productivity and poor quality of life [17]. Additionally, this condition substantially increases the risk of infection. Various treatment options, such as hypertonic saline eye drops, bandage contact lenses, amniotic membrane transplantation, corneal tattooing, Gundersen conjunctival flap, anterior stromal puncture, phototherapeutic keratectomy and corneal cryopexy can be used to provide pain relief for patients suffering from symptomatic bullous keratopathy before they undergo Therapeutic Keratoplasty [18–20]. Although alternative approaches can alleviate pain and discomfort, replacing the abnormal cornea with healthy donor tissue is the most effective treatment. However, our patient’s lack of light perception and the possibility that his general condition would not tolerate surgery made this approach a secondary consideration. The treatment of fungal keratitis facilitated tissue repair on the corneal surface, leading to a conjunctivalized appearance similar to that achieved through a surgical conjunctival flap procedure for the patient.

The presence of bullous keratopathy in this patient was a risk factor for keratitis in itself. Although contact lens use was for therapeutic purposes, it increased this risk if hygiene was not maintained. We added prophylactic antibiotics in addition to artificial tear support for the patient. This may have given us some time for infection with typical microorganisms. However, the risk continues for agents such as acanthamoeba (parasite) and fungus (fusarium) that cause contact lens-related keratitis [21]. When the patient presented with keratitis, it was already clinically advanced. The patient received aggressive treatment in the current clinic situation. Voriconazole, a fluconazole derivative, was used instead of natamycin, which was the algorithmic first choice [8]. Considering the patient’s use of contact lenses, voriconazole would also be the first choice for the potential pathogen Fusarium [22]. However, considering that voriconazole may trigger metaplastic changes, clinical follow-up of the patient is necessary [23]. Our treatment approach, which we implemented considering the patient’s general health status and life expectancy, seemed unsuccessful, it resulted in positive outcomes for both the patient and us.
