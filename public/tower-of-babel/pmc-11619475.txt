Radioresistance-related gene signatures identified by transcriptomics characterize the prognosis and immune landscape of pancreatic cancer patients
Authors: Dandan Wang, Jun Cao, Yanhui Chen, Lisha Zhang, Chan Zhou, Litao Huang, Yanliang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiotherapy (RT) is an important means of local treatment of solid tumors, and radioresistance is the main reason for RT failure for tumors, especially pancreatic cancer (PC). It is urgent to distinguish key genes and mechanisms of radioresistance in PC.MethodsWe acquired the data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), obtained the gene modules associated with radioresistance by weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA), and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and tumor samples. Radioresistance-related genes (RRRGs) were determined with the intersection of WGCNA and DEGs. The hub RRRGs associated with prognosis were distinguished by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. We established a risk score model using multivariate Cox regression. Immune cell infiltration and drug sensitivity were evaluated through the CIBERSORT algorithm and the “OncoPredict” software package, respectively. The association of the key gene RIC3 and PC clinical features was verified in public databases, and its biological behaviors were explored in vitro.ResultsThe intersection of DEGs and WGCNA confirmed 14 RRRGs, then six hub RRRGs were identified using LASSO. A key four genes (DUSP4, ADORA2B, SCGB2A1, and RIC3) risk score model was constructed and proved to be capable of independently estimating the prognosis of PC. There is no significant difference between risk score groups in various immune cell infiltration and response to immunotherapy. Although the low-risk group seemed to exhibit greater sensitivity to antitumor drugs, the four drugs (5-fluorouracil [5-FU], leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) currently used for PC patients had no statistical difference for the low- and high- group. The overexpression of RIC3 had a synergy effect with irradiation on inhibited malignant biological properties of PC cells, which was verified by detecting the proliferation ability, apoptosis rate, cell cycle distribution, and migration ability of PANC-1 cells.ConclusionsWe herein presented signature genes correlated with radioresistance in PC and established a risk score model competent in estimating patients’ clinical outcomes and response to antitumor drugs. The above evidence could contribute to comprehending the mechanisms of radioresistance and identifying the underlying therapy targeting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13231-4.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) portends a dismal ending, a 5-year survival rate of no more than 10%, and ranks fourth among cancer-related causes of death [1, 2]. Radical surgical resection is the only method for significantly increasing the survival and prognosis of patients with PC [3]. Unfortunately, the newly diagnosed PC patients who qualified for surgical resection account for only 15–20%, and most patients are in the inoperable stage [4]. Because of the invasive nature of pancreatic tumors, a multimodal approach to treatment, including resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy (RT), targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, is necessary to prevent the recurrence of PC [5].

RT is a crucial local treatment for PC, and its fundamental mechanism involves direct or indirect interactions between ionizing radiation and tumor cells [6]. The median overall survival (OS) for local advanced pancreatic cancer (LAPC) of conventional fractionation radiotherapy (CFRT) (Dose: 50.4–60.0 Gy) is about 8.6–15.7 months, and the chemoradiotherapy did not improve the OS in comparison with chemotherapy alone [7–10]. Thus, the increase in radiation dose is urgent. The new RT technique, such as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), which delivers higher doses to tumors in a few fractions, can improve the 2-year OS modestly [11]. PC is an extremely hypoxic tumor characterized by hypovascularity as well as dense fibrosis [12]; the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) of PC restricts its benefit from radiotherapy and leads to poor prognosis [13]. This reflects the urgent need to identify biomarkers that induce radioresistance. Studies on various types of cancer have revealed that a multitude of factors can induce radioresistance, including the perception and repairment of DNA damage, the arrest of the cell cycle, the production of cancer stem cells (CSCs), the variation of the TME and metabolism [13–15]. However, the specific molecular mechanism of radioresistance in PC is not elucidated, and there are no reliable biomarkers to guide clinical practice [13].

Recently, studies of bioinformatics exploring tumor-specific biomarkers have been increasing, and these have emerged as significant contributors to cancer diagnosis and treatment. Many of these studies were based on individual genes or biomarkers, which finally limited their sensitivity and specificity in predicting tumor prognosis. Moreover, research using bioinformatics to explore the underlying mechanisms of radioresistance is very limited. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) clusters genes with semblable expression patterns and further studies the relationship between various clustering modules and clinical phenotypes [16]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis was used to investigate potential biological behaviors and molecular mechanisms of genes in different physiological or pathological states [17]. We obtained the radiation-related genes with the intersection of the genes generated from the two methods mentioned above with the use of public databases, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), etc. After the least absolute shrinkage selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis, a multi-gene risk score model was established. We then scrutinized the relevance between the key genes in this model and tumor immune infiltration and the underlying radioresistance mechanisms, hoping to obtain the prediction-specific radiosensitivity biomarkers of PC.

Materials and methods

Study design and project extraction

We acquired PC patients’ gene expression profiles and clinical data from the TCGA database [18]. A total of 183 samples were involved in the TCGA-PC cohort, of which 179 were tumor samples, and 4 were normal samples. Twenty-seven patients received RT with obtainable follow-up results, of which 14 cases with complete or partial response after RT were defined as radiation sensitive (RS), and 13 patients with disease progression or stability were called radiation resistance (RR). All TCGA expression profiles and clinical details were downloaded using R software [19, 20]. The present study’s design is displayed in Fig. 1.

Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA clusters genes with semblable expression patterns and further studies the relationship between various clustering modules and clinical phenotypes [16]. We adopted this method to calculate the correlation between clinical phenotypes and gene expression. In the present study, we selected patients who received radiotherapy from the TCGA-PC cohort. We constructed a gene expression coexpression network targeting the RS and RR groups using the R software package “WGCNA”. The optimum soft threshold was identified to guarantee that gene interactions maximally followed a scale-free distribution. We calculated the adjacency degree and similarity between genes and established a clustering tree diagram; then, we utilized the dynamic tree-cutting algorithm to further segment modules and merge similar modules. Each color represents a module, each containing genes with similar expression patterns. Pearson correlation was performed between each module and sample traits, and the downstream analyses were subsequently performed using the module genes own higher absolute values.

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

The DEGs between the RR and RS groups in the TCGA-PC cohort were determined using the “limma” package [17]. The screening thresholds were set at |log 2 (fold change, FC)| ≥1 and P value ≤ 0.05 [21]. We presented the visualization of DEGs by generating volcano plots and heatmaps, pictured by the “ggplot2” package and “pheatmap” package, respectively. The DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses by means of the package “clusterProfiler” in R software [22].

Establishment and verification of the gene risk score model

We first screened out the radioresistance-related genes (RRRGs), combined these gene data with survival data, and used the createDataPartition function in the “caret” package to partition 170 patients in TCGA into a training set randomly and a validated set at a ratio of 7:3. Subsequently, the “glmnet” package and the “randomForestSRC” package were applied for LASSO regression and random survival forest, respectively, to identify RRRGs associated with PC survival [23].

The RRRGs associated with prognosis with the minimum lambda value were selected in LASSO regression. Subsequently, the hub genes were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis for the construction of the prognostic signature. We established a risk score model on the basis of these RRRGs through multivariate Cox regression analysis in the TCGA training set (n = 119). Patients were categorized into low-risk score (LS) and high-risk score (HS) groups according to their median risk score. We evaluated the OS of patients in different groups using the Kaplan-Meier analysis, and the effectiveness of the model was assessed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which were established by the SurvivalROC package (version 1.0.3). The risk score model was externally validated using the data from the GEO database (GSE85916, n = 79), the TCGA validation set (n = 51), and the TCGA all set (n = 170). Subsequently, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox analyses to investigate the chance the risk score independently predicted the prognosis for patients with PC. The 1-, 2-, and 5-year survival times of PC patients were predicted by the nomogram, and the calibration curves were adopted to calculate the performance of the nomogram.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

According to RNA-seq data of PC patients, the relative percentage of 22 infiltrating immune cells in different groups was inferred by the calculation of the CIBERSORT algorithm, and Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between gene expression profiles and immune cell content [24]. The immune checkpoint expression levels of different risk score subgroups were compared based on TCGA-PC patient expression profiles. Furthermore, the potential for immune escape in tumors between the HS and LS groups was anticipated by the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm [25].

Assessment of drug sensitivity

The immune phenotypic score (IPS) was derived from The Cancer Immune Atlas (TCIA) database, which can soundly predict cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen-4 (CTLA4) and programmed death 1 (PD1) reactivity and thus help researchers master the response during immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in the HS and LS groups [26]. On the basis of the genomics of drug sensitivity in cancer (GDSC), a comprehensive pre-trained prediction model was generated with the use of the “oncoPredict” package. We used the TCGA-PC gene expression profiles to figure out the 50% maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each patient to predict drug reactions accurately [27].

Cell culture

The human PC cell line, PANC-1, was maintained in RPMI-1640 basal medium (Gibco, Hangzhou, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell, Suzhou, China) in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Overexpression of RIC3 in PANC-1 cells

The plasmids were constructed and compounded to overexpress RIC3 by GenePhama (Suzhou, China). And the Lipo8000™ (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to transfect the plasmids into PANC-1 cells.

RAN isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA was obtained from PANC-1 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). The qRT-PCR was performed using the Hieff ® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix kit (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). The 2-ΔΔCt method was used to evaluate the results with the β-actin gene as the housekeeping gene. For the RIC3 gene, the forward primer was 5’- CAGATCATTTGGGTTGGGAAA − 3’, and the reverse primer was 5’ - CACACGAGGTAACAGAATTATCTTCCT − 3’.

Western blotting assay

The experimental process was performed as described previously [28], the primary antibodies of RIC3 and GAPDH was purchased from proteintech Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China).

Cell irradiation and colony formation assay

A medical linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, Inc.) was used to process the cells at a dose rate of 2 Gy/min. Appropriate cell densities were inoculated into the 6-well plates, cultivated overnight, and then irradiated with anticipated doses of X-rays (0, 2, 4, or 6 Gy). About 12–14 days after the initial inoculation, the cells were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Then, we counted the number of colonies (at least 50 cells). The survival curve was plotted using the linear-quadratic equation: surviving fraction (SF) = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:e^(-(\alpha\:D+\beta\:D^2)$$\end{document}. The experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

PANC-1 cells were cultivated into 96-well plates (2000 cells/well) and irradiated X-ray at the anticipated time, and 20 µl CCK-8 reagent (Sunview, Shenzhen, China) were added to every well after 0, 24, 48, and 72 h of irradiation, after 2 h incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance at 450 nm was detected through the use of a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite 200 M; Männedorf, Switzerland).

Wound healing assay

The wound healing assay is a simple and efficient way to measure the migration ability of cells. Briefly, cells were planted into 6-well plates and scratched with a sterile 200 µl pipette tip, then the serum-free medium was added to each plate. At 0 h and 24 h, the scratch area was acquired using a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed by ImageJ-2.1.0 software.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis and cell cycle

For apoptosis, we used the Annexin V-FITC/PI cell apoptosis analysis kit (YEASEN, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction, and samples were analyzed by IDEAS Application v6.0. Software. For the cell cycle, we harvested cells at an anticipative time and fixed cells with 75% ethanol overnight at -20 °C. The fixed cells were rehydrated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), stained with 500 µl DNA staining solution for 30 min (Liankebio, Hangzhou, China), tested on IDEAS Application v6.0 software, and analyzed by FlowJo-V10.8.1 software. 10,000 and 15,000 cells for apoptosis and cell cycle for each sample were acquired, respectively.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized R software (version 4.2.2) and its associated software packages to process, statistically analyze, and visualize the data. The Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA were used for two and multiple-group comparisons for normally distributed variables, respectively. The Wilcoxon test/Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test were used for two and multiple-group comparisons for nonnormally distributed variables, respectively. The Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses were developed to analyze correlations for normally and nonnormally distributed variables, respectively. *P < 0.05 was deemed significant, while NS represented no significance. Results

Recognition of radioresistance-related gene modules by WGCNA

To investigate the key genes that most influence the survival of PC patients receiving radiotherapy, we selected the expression profiles of 27 patients receiving radiotherapy from the TCGA-PC cohort for WGCNA. We used R software to cluster the 27 patients (Fig. 2A) and then selected β = 6 as a suitable soft threshold to structure a scale-free network (scale-free R2 = 0.80) (Fig. 2B). After merging similar gene modules, a dynamic cutting tree was subsequently created (Fig. 2C). The correlation heatmap between the gene modules and clinical features on the basis of the Pearson correlation coefficient is shown in Fig. 2D. Among the 13 gene modules obtained, the module with the highest correlation with radioresistance (brown module), which contained a total of 538 coexpressed genes (Supplementary Table 1), was selected for subsequent analysis.

Screening and functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

We found 1238 DEGs between the normal and tumor samples, and the up-and down-regulated genes were 348 and 890, respectively (Fig. 3A). We presented the expression of the DEGs using the heatmap (Fig. 3B), and the full DEGs lists were enumerated in Supplementary Table 2. The GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were generated to explore the related function and signaling pathways of the DEGs. The top enriched pathways of the three aspects of GO analysis were leukocyte-mediated immunity for the biological process (BP), the outer side of the plasma membrane for the cellular component (CC), and the immune receptor activity for the molecular process (MF) (Fig. 3C). Figure 3D displayed the significantly enriched pathways of the KEGG analysis, of which the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction was the most enriched one. There were 14 intersection genes between the 1238 DEGs and the 538 key modular genes (KMGs) employed to identify the key RRRGs (Fig. 3E).

Identification of signature RRRGs and structure of a four-gene prognostic risk score model

The gene expression profiles and the survival data were combined to obtain the key RRRGs associated with the prognosis in PC patients, further screened the 14 genes using LASSO, and finally obtained six key genes: CREB3L1, DUSP4, NCAM1, ADORA2B, SCGB2A1, and RIC3 (Fig. 4A, B). These six genes were subsequently adopted for multivariate Cox regression, and a risk score model consisting of four key RRRGs (DUSP4, ADORA2B, SCGB2A1, and RIC3) was established (Fig. 4C). With reference to the median risk score, which was gained from the expression and coefficients of the above four genes, patients in the training set were distributed to the LS group (n = 60) and HS group (n = 59). Figure 4D exhibited the distribution of risk scores for each individual PC patients in the training set. The heatmap illustrated the different expression patterns of these four genes in the two groups (Fig. 4E). Additionally, patients in the LS group exerted significantly longer survival than the HS group using the Kaplan-Meier analysis (P = 0.012) (Fig. 4F). The time-varying ROC curve was adopted to verify the effectiveness of the risk score model. The area under the curve (AUC) of the TCGA training set was 0.628, 0.709, and 0.878 at 1-, 3-, and 5-years, respectively (Fig. 4G).

Subsequently, we used the TCGA validation cohort (the LS group [n = 26], the HS group [n = 25]) (Fig. S1) and the TCGA all set (the LS group [n = 85], the HS group [n = 85]) (Fig. S2) to further validate the performance of prognostic signature. Consistently, patients in the LS group had a significantly better OS than those in the HS group using Kaplan-Meier analysis (For the TCGA validation cohort: P = 0.046, Fig. S1C; For the TCGA all set: P < 0.001, Fig. S2C). Furthermore, AUC for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.749, 0.651, and 0.704 in the TCGA validation cohort, respectively (Fig. S1D), and 0.679, 0.677, and 0.784 in the TCGA all set, respectively (Fig. S2D). Based on the above results, we concluded that the risk score model had moderate accuracy.

The riskScore can independently predict prognosis in patients with PC

The ability of the risk score and other clinical characteristics to estimate the survival of PC patients was verified by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The risk score (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.346, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.515–3.633. P < 0.001) and the TNM stage (HR = 2.310, 95%CI: 1.058–5.042, P = 0.036) were significantly associated with the prognosis of the PC patients in the univariate results (Fig. 6A). The risk score (HR = 2.308, 95%CI: 1.491–3.574, P < 0.001) and TNM stage (HR = 2.186, 95%CI: 1.002–4.767, P = 0.049) were positively associated with the survival for PC patients (Fig. 6B). The risk score was combined with the TNM stage to create the nomogram, which can work out the total score of each patient, to estimate the survival of PC patients. The total score was inversely correlated to patient survival (Fig. 6C). As shown in Fig. 6D, the 1-, 3- and 5-year OS rates obtained from the calibration curve were not significantly different from the observed OS rate.

Evaluation of the immune microenvironment of different groups

The immune microenvironment is vital for the occurrence, progression, and outcome of tumors, including the PC. Understanding the immune microenvironment better can direct clinical practice [29, 30]. The infiltration level of 22 distinct immune cell types between the LS and HS groups was explored using the CIBERSORT algorithm. The LS group exhibited a greater infiltration of naive B cells, activated mast cells, and CD8+ T cells. Meanwhile, the infiltration of M0 macrophages, plasma cells, and follicular helper T cells was more serious in the HS group (Fig. 7A, B). There is no significant correlation between the risk score and various immune cells in PC patients (Fig. 7C).

Assessment of treatment response according to the risk score model

We compared the expression levels of immune checkpoints between the LS group and the HS group and found that 26 of the 44 immune checkpoints showed differences in the two groups. In addition to CD44 and CD276, the remaining 24 were highly expressed in the LS group, including LAG3 and CTLA4 (Fig. 8A). Evidence showed that the treatment effect of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy can be precisely obtained by IPS [26]. We calculated the IPS of TCGA-PC patients, and the outcomes implied that the LS and the HS group patients had no difference in response to immunotherapy (Fig. 8B-E).

Prediction of the relationship between RIC3 and the prognosis of PC using public databases

To validate the role of key genes in PC radioresistance, the RIC3 gene that had the greatest impact on clinical outcomes was selected on the basis of the hazard ratio values of multivariate Cox regression analysis, and we used the online bioinformatics tool Sangerbox to analysis the relationship between RIC3 and the prognosis of PC [31]. Briefly, this tool contains data on gene expression, prognostic information, clinicopathological features of various solid tumors in TCGA database, and the normal tissue information in Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTXs) database [32]. We found that the expression of RIC3 was markedly decreased in most solid tumors, including PAAD (Fig. 10A). Kaplan-Meier curves showed that the lower the expression of RIC was, the higher the OS, progression-free survival (PFS), disease-free survival (DFS), and disease-specific survival (DSS) were, which implied a promoting effect of RIC3 on the survival rates of PC patients (Fig. 10B-E). Lower RIC3 expression tended to be associated with worse clinicopathological features (Fig. 10F, I), there was a trend of a lower RIC3 expression with an advanced N stage (Fig. 10G), and M stage (Fig. 10H), but significance was not observed. The gender of PC patients did not influence RIC3 expression (Fig. 10J). Together, these results showed that RIC3 might play an anti-cancer role for PC patients.

Verification of the relationship between RIC3 and radioresistance in PC cell line

The biological functions of RIC3 in PC were verified in vitro. We selected the PANC-1 cells to overexpress RIC3; the overexpression efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and Western Blotting assay (Fig. 11A, B). The CCK-8 assay showed that the overexpression of RIC3 could inhibit the proliferation of PANC-1 cells (P < 0.0001), this phenomenon was more significant in combination with X-ray irradiation (P < 0.01) (Fig. 11C). As shown in Fig. 11D, E, the clone formation of the RIC3 group was decreased in comparison with the Vector group, the SF (2 Gy) was 32.83% ± 2.32% for the RIC3 group, 15.99% ± 1.53% for the Vector group, respectively (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the overexpression of RIC3 induced a suppressive effect on the malignant phenotype of PANC-1 cells, as evidenced by increased apoptosis, increased G2/M phase arrest, and decreased cell migration, as observed in the apoptosis, cell cycle, and wound healing assays (Fig. 11F-K).

Discussion

RT has represented an important local treatment for patients with PC. The most commonly used radiation types include photon therapy (X-ray and γ-ray) and particle therapy (protons and heavy ions) [33, 34]. Despite significant advances in RT techniques, radioresistance is inevitable, regardless of the type of radiation and dose fractionation method used. Compared with conventionally fractionated radiotherapy, hypofractionated radiotherapy have somewhat improved local control in PC patients, but the benefits are not enormous [35]. Trials of proton and heavy ion therapy for PC have shown that advanced radiotherapy techniques can improve local control and prolong survival. However, some patients still have no response to RT treatment [36, 37]. In brief, researchers have conducted many investigations to overcome PC’s radioresistance. However, in clinics, the methods of radiation sensitization rarely substantially improve patients’ prognosis. Therefore, it is imperative to fully understand the heterogeneity of PC, identify radiosensitive and radioresistant groups, understand the mechanism of radioresistance, and develop new strategies to overcome radioresistance, which can eventually contribute to prolonging the survival of PC patients.

The RNA-seq data from patients receiving RT in the TCGA-PC cohort was analyzed, and six key RRRGs were screened in our study. We then used four of these RRRGs to construct a risk score model to predict the survival of patients with PC. The results showed that the LS group had prolonged survival and tended to be the beneficiaries of chemotherapy treatment and targeted therapy. In addition, our outcomes concluded that patients in different risk groups owned different immune landscapes. These findings provide some guidance for clinical decision-making.

In the present study, GO and KEGG analysis showed that radioresistance-related genes of PC were enriched in the immune regulatory pathways of TME. Previous studies reported that RT could induce the death of T lymphocytes in the circulating bloodstream and thus create a hypoxic environment in the irradiated area, which facilitated the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2 macrophages, and the production of inhibitory cytokines, such as TGF-β [38, 39]. This was in accordance with our KEGG enrichment analysis, which showed that radioresistance-related genes were enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and chemokine signaling pathways. Concurrently, RT-induced fibrosis, vascular damage, chronic inflammation, and immunosuppression in the TME might lead to radioresistance and thus contribute to tumor survival [39, 40]. Radiation-induced immunosuppression may be one of the reasons why some patients do not respond to RT.

RT kills tumors locally and exerts antitumor effects by stimulating local or systemic immune responses, which can be combined with immunotherapy and cause mutual sensitization. The combination of RT with immunotherapy was implemented in numerous clinical trials and achieved exciting results in various malignancies [41]. Strom et al. reported that the higher the tumor radiosensitivity, the higher the immune activation after investigating 10,240 genomically profiled distinct solid primary tumors [42]; consistently, Dai et al. identified similar results with the use of the public database [43]. However, it is worth noting that the results of the above two studies were based on various solid cancers, which may not truly reflect the situation of PC. There are studies reported that simultaneous targeting of immune checkpoints with RT inhibits PC growth and metastasis in vivo [44], but PC is an immunologically cold tumor with a specific immunosuppressive microenvironment in that the T-cell infiltration is very low; meanwhile, the low tumor mutational burden of PC means that its neoantigens count was low and the immune surveillance was correspondingly decreased. Therefore, the clinical outcome of immunotherapy in PC treatment was unsatisfactory [45, 46]. We calculated the IPS of TCGA-PC patients, and the outcomes implied that patients with different risk scores had no significant difference in response to immunotherapy. Consistently, the clinical results of RT combined with ICI had no significant benefit among patients with metastatic PC, whose median PFS was 2.5 months receiving nivolumab plus ipilimumab and conformal RT [47]. Moreover, although there is evidence that hypofractionated radiotherapy, such as SBRT, can induce tumor vascular damage, leading to secondary delayed tumor cell death and deterioration of TME, and thus promoting the release of tumor antigens [48, 49], the clinical trials of PC did not observe the exciting results; the median PFS was 2.3 months for metastatic PC patients receiving durvalumab plus tremelimumab and SBRT [50]. Thus, the combination of ICI and RT did not show sufficient antitumor efficacy among PC patients.

The current first-line treatment for PC patients is FOLFIRONOX (a combination of 5-fluorouracil [5-FU], leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) or gemcitabine plus albuminbound (nab) paclitaxel [51]. Our drug sensitivity prediction analysis represented some antineoplastics that were commonly used in PC patients, such as Paclitaxel, Epirubicin, etc. had different drug sensitivity between different risk score groups. At the same time, some targeted drugs with positive risk score model results, such as sorafenib, were used to explore its therapeutic effect for PC in clinical trials [52]. AZD8055, an mTOR signaling pathway inhibitor, has been shown to have anti-proliferation effects on PC in laboratory [53, 54]. Meanwhile, we unraveled that the sensitivity of the four chemotherapeutic drugs of FOLFIRONOX did not differ between the two risk score groups. Because of the single mechanism, tumor drug resistance, and individual differences in treatment, a single agent may not achieve the ideal therapeutic effect, especially for PC, which is a lethal malignancy with sophisticated TME. Multidrug regimens can combine drugs with different mechanisms, metabolic pathways, and side effects to increase the killing effect on tumor cells. This opinion was supported by multiple clinical trials; FOLFIRINOX or doublet therapy (gemcitabine and albuminbound [nab] paclitaxel) both improved OS, PFS, and overall response rate (ORR) compared with gemcitabine monotherapy in patients with metastatic PC [55, 56]. This is perhaps why our risk score model had a limited effect in predicting drug sensitivity in PC.

Dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSPs) are a crucial modulator that dephosphorylates tyrosine and serine/threonine residues [57]. Twenty-five DUSPs have been identified in humans, including DUSP4, also known as mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-2 (MKP-2) [58]. DUSP4 can dephosphorylate and thus inactivate MAPKs (e.g., ERK, p38, and JNK) in cancer cells, affecting cancer development or treatment resistance [59–61]. There is no direct evidence of the effect of DUSP4 on radioresistance, but a gene expression profiling of 30 cancer cell lines revealed DUSP4 as a mediator of chemoresistance [62]. Meanwhile, DUSP1 and DUSP10, the family numbers of DUSPs, were identified to promote radioresistance by inhibiting p-ERK1/2 activation [63] and reversing the overexpression of the latent radiosensitizer miR-450 A-5p in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, respectively [64]. The adenosine A2B receptor (ADORA) is a type of G protein-coupled receptor and one of the four adenosine receptors that are crucial for inflammatory responses, organ fibrosis, and cancer progression [65, 66]. The functions of ADORA in cancers have presented conflicting results in recent studies. One study showed that the migration of ovarian cancer cells was reduced with the pharmacological stimulation of ADORA in vitro and that the level of ADORA was positively related to the prognosis of ovarian cancer patients [67]. In contrast, ADORA promotes the progression of tumors by inhibiting CD8+ T cells and enhancing cancer-associated fibroblasts in breast cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma [68, 69]. The multivariate Cox regression analysis in our study identified ADORA as an oncogene that promoted the radioresistant of PC. Consistently, previous studies reported that ADORA contributed to γ-radiation-induced acquisition of malignant phenotypes and was involved in radioresistance in lung cancer and glioblastoma [70, 71]. Becker et al. first characterized the SCGB2A1 (mammaglobin B) in 1998 [72], a member of the uteroglobin gene family. The expression of SCGB2A1 is high in breast, ovarian, and colorectal cancers and demonstrates poor prognosis, which probably acts as a potential therapeutic target [73–75]. What is noteworthy is that Munakata et al. indicated that the enforced expression of SCGB2A1 is correlated with chemoresistance, radioresistance, and cancer cell stemness in colorectal cancer [76], in line with the above results, we identified SCGB2A1 was prone to radioresistant in PC.

Resistance to inhibitors of cholinesterase 3 (RIC3) is the third cholinesterase inhibitor resistance gene identified in nematode genetic screens [77, 78]. RIC3 is widely expressed in mammals, and studies have shown that RIC3 is involved in bipolar disorder, Parkinson’s disease, and cognitive decline [79–81]. We demonstrated that higher levels of RIC3 were related to longer survival rates in PC patients, and this similar result was also confirmed by Chen et al., who proved that RIC3 played a protective role in PC [82]. Its ability to inhibit the malignant phenotype of PC cells in combination with irradiation has been demonstrated in vitro.

It should be noted that this study is subject to certain limitations that require further improvement. First, the ability of the risk score model constructed from key genes to predict and validate the clinical outcome, immune invasion status, and antitumor drug response of PC patients depended on public databases and in vitro experiments. However, these datasets cannot fully represent the heterogeneity and complexity of tumors in the human body. Second, the potential mechanisms by which the key genes cause radioresistance have yet to be elucidated fully. Further research is demanded to reveal their potential as therapeutic targets. We will explore these key genes further to address the shortcomings of this study later, for example, to understand better how gene panels guide treatment decisions through real-world study, and to understand the mechanism of key genes in radioresistance through sequencing, tumor biopsy, and other technologies.

Conclusion

In summary, we screened radioresistance-related genes in PC by differential expression analysis and WGCNA. A four-gene risk score model was established by employing LASSO and multivariate Cox regression to predict immune cell infiltration, response to antitumor therapy, and clinical outcomes in PC patients, thus providing a reference for clinical decision-making. These four key molecules could be potential targets for overcoming radioresistance in PC.
