An intrascrotal bioadhesive nanoplatform for sustained hydrogen delivery ameliorates age-associated testicular dysfunction
Authors: Shu Liang, Jiawei Lv, Zun Li, Zheng Li, Jiachun Jiang, Shaoqing Yu, Cheng Wang, Xiongjun Wang, Lingyun Xiao, Guanzhen Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTesticular aging is characterized by progressive spermatogenic decline. Accumulating oxidative injury and insufficient metabolic support from Sertoli cells may contribute to this process. Although molecular hydrogen has shown antioxidant potential, its therapeutic application in reproductive aging is limited by rapid dissipation after administration. To overcome this barrier, we developed the localized hydrogen-releasing system S-H/B. This platform integrates hydrogenated silicon nanosheets (S-H) with a bioadhesive polymer, aldehyde-functionalized polylactic acid-hyperbranched polyglycerol (PLA-HPG-al). The resulting formulation adhered to peritesticular tissue following intrascrotal injection, showed prolonged local retention, and enabled sustained hydrogen release under physiological conditions. This sustained local hydrogen delivery reduced age-associated testicular degeneration and promoted spermatogenesis in aged mice. Notably, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed enhanced HIF-1 signaling and glycolysis-related transcriptional programs in aged Sertoli cells following S-H/B treatment. Further mechanistic analyses indicated that S-H/B reduced ONOO− levels and limited PHD1-dependent HIF-1α hydroxylation, thereby slowing HIF-1α degradation and promoting its nuclear localization. Consistently, S-H/B increased glycolytic enzyme expression, glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, and lactate production in Sertoli cells, whereas HIF-1α inhibition attenuated these metabolic changes. The increased lactate production may have contributed to improved seminiferous epithelial function. Overall, this work presents a bioadhesive hydrogen delivery strategy for age-associated male reproductive dysfunction and suggests that metabolic remodeling in Sertoli cells may contribute to its beneficial effects.Keywords: Localized hydrogen therapy, Bioadhesive nanoplatform, Testicular aging, Sertoli cells, Glycolytic reprogramming

Introduction

Male reproductive aging is increasingly recognized as a driver of infertility and adverse reproductive outcomes [1,2], yet effective interventions to restore testicular function remain lacking. Testosterone replacement therapy, the primary clinical option, provides limited benefit and is associated with prostate-related and cardiovascular safety concerns, as well as suppression of spermatogenesis [3,4]. Safer and more effective strategies are warranted to mitigate age-related decline in male reproductive function.

Oxidative stress has been implicated in male reproductive aging, as excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, together with impaired antioxidant defenses, can damage both somatic and germ cells in the testis [5,6]. For example, the hydroxyl radical (·OH) has been shown to contribute to sperm DNA fragmentation during reproductive aging [6,7]. Similarly, peroxynitrite (ONOO−), a potent oxidant and nitrating species, has been identified as a key mediator of oxidative damage in aged Sertoli cells [8]. Notably, Sertoli cells represent the principal somatic cell type within the seminiferous epithelium [9]. They are indispensable for maintaining testicular architecture and providing metabolic support for germ cells, thereby positioning the Sertoli cell population as a key responder during male reproductive aging. Beyond causing direct oxidative injury, ONOO− has been proposed to act as an oxygen donor at the catalytic iron center of prolyl hydroxylase domain-containing proteins (PHDs), thereby promoting hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) prolyl hydroxylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation under hypoxic conditions [10].

Molecular hydrogen (H2), a therapeutic gas, selectively scavenges highly reactive radicals, including ·OH and ONOO− [11,12]. Indeed, hydrogen-based interventions have shown potential across a range of age-associated disorders, including neurodegenerative, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases [13]. Its benefits also extend to male reproductive health, including improved erectile function, enhanced sperm motility, and reduced testicular injury [14]. For instance, hydrogen-rich saline mitigated ionizing radiation-induced reproductive damage in mice by scavenging ·OH [15]. Daily oral intake of hydrogen-rich water improved sperm count and motility in 12-month-old mice, supporting its potential to counteract age-associated reproductive decline [16]. These findings support hydrogen therapy as a candidate strategy for age-associated male reproductive dysfunction.

Conventional delivery approaches, including hydrogen inhalation and hydrogen-rich water, provide limited control over local hydrogen exposure because of its low aqueous solubility and rapid diffusion throughout the body [17]. Advanced platforms, including molecular precursors and drug delivery carriers, have therefore been pursued to improve tissue accumulation and retention [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. However, existing hydrogen carriers face several practical limitations. For example, ammonia borane and metal borohydrides serve as chemical hydrogen-storage materials, but their hydrogen release usually requires acidic conditions or catalysts that are absent in most physiological environments [[22], [23], [24]]. Reactive metals and metal hydrides can release hydrogen under physiological conditions, but the concomitant rise in local pH constrains their use in many biological settings [25,26]. Hydrogenated silicon nanosheets (S-H) enable water-triggered hydrogen generation under physiological conditions without the formation of known harmful byproducts, providing a suitable precursor for localized in situ hydrogen production [27]. Despite these advantages, the relatively rapid hydrogen release from uncoated S-H may favor short-term local applications and be less suitable for age-associated reproductive dysfunction, for which prolonged local exposure with fewer administrations is desirable. Moreover, unanchored nanoparticles within body cavities may be cleared through lymphatic drainage, further limiting their local retention [28]. A bioadhesive coating of aldehyde-functionalized polylactic acid-hyperbranched polyglycerol (PLA-HPG-al) offers a potential means of addressing both limitations. Its hydrophobic PLA segment may restrict water access to S-H and slow hydrogen release, whereas the aldehyde groups of HPG-al may form dynamic Schiff-base linkages with amine groups on tissue proteins, potentially promoting uniform tissue adhesion and limiting lymphatic clearance.

Guided by this rationale, we coated S-H with PLA-HPG-al to construct bioadhesive-coated hydrogenated silicon nanosheets (S-H/B), an injectable bioadhesive platform designed to support sustained hydrogen release and prolonged tissue retention for localized intervention in male reproductive aging. Moreover, although hydrogen has been reported to scavenge highly reactive species and may have potential in male reproductive dysfunction, its mechanisms of action have not been comprehensively or systematically defined. This may be due, at least in part, to the lack of stable local hydrogen delivery systems and the limited ability to resolve cell-specific responses with conventional analyses. Here, we evaluated S-H/B as an in situ hydrogen-generating carrier in a model of male reproductive aging and used single-cell RNA sequencing to provide a broad, cell-resolved assessment of testicular responses across multiple cell populations. Molecular and metabolic analyses were subsequently performed to investigate the cell-type-specific effects of S-H/B (Scheme 1).

Schematic illustration of bioadhesive-coated hydrogenated silicon nanosheets (S-H/B) and the proposed mechanism for ameliorating age-associated testicular dysfunction. Hydrogenated silicon nanosheets (S-H) were prepared from CaSi2 and encapsulated within a bioadhesive coating composed of aldehyde-functionalized polylactic acid-hyperbranched polyglycerol (PLA-HPG-al) to form S-H/B with a core-shell architecture. Results

Preparation and characterization of S-H/B

S-H/B was prepared by synthesizing S-H from CaSi2, followed by ultrasonication to generate well-dispersed nanosheets and coating the surface with PLA-HPG-al (Fig. 1A). The color change from black CaSi2 to a green product indicated successful formation of S-H (Fig. 1B). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra further supported the expected chemical features: S-H displayed characteristic Si–H vibrational bands at approximately at ∼639, 876, and 2107 cm−1, corresponding to wagging, bending, and stretching modes, respectively (Fig. 1C), consistent with previous reports [27]. PLA-HPG-al exhibited a characteristic band at ∼1748 cm−1, attributed to aldehyde groups, consistent with our previous study [29]. Notably, S-H/B exhibited both the Si–H bands and the ∼1748 cm−1 feature of PLA-HPG-al in the FTIR spectra, indicating successful PLA-HPG-al coating of S-H (Fig. 1C). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) showed a larger hydrodynamic diameter for S-H/B than for S-H, supporting core-shell assembly (Fig. 1D). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that both S-H and S-H/B preserved a nanosheet morphology (Fig. 1E).

S-H generates hydrogen upon contact with water under physiological conditions, while the PLA-HPG-al coating restricts water access and thereby sustains hydrogen release. Consistent with this mechanism, both S-H and S-H/B produced hydrogen bubbles in PBS, but bubble expansion was slower for S-H/B (Fig. 1F). Quantitative profiling showed that uncoated S-H released ∼66% of its total hydrogen capacity within 4 h, whereas S-H/B released only ∼31% by 72 h (Fig. 1G). These findings indicate that the PLA-HPG-al coating prolonged hydrogen release.

We next evaluated the in vitro cytocompatibility and reactive species-scavenging activity of S-H and S-H/B. Neither material showed detectable cytotoxicity in TM4 Sertoli cells at S-H concentrations of 12.5–200 μg/mL (Fig. 1H). In cell-free assays, both S-H and S-H/B effectively scavenged ·OH and ONOO− in solution (Fig. 1I and J). Consistently, S-H and S-H/B dose-dependently improved TM4 cell viability under ·OH or ONOO− exposure (Fig. 1K and L). At 200 μg/mL, both treatments increased viability from approximately 20% to 75% in ·OH-exposed cells and to 70% in ONOO−-exposed cells. Together, these data indicate that S-H/B enables sustained hydrogen delivery and preserves antioxidant activity against ·OH and ONOO−, conferring protection against oxidative injury.

Long-term in vivo retention of S-H/B with favorable biocompatibility

Aldehyde-functionalized HPG in the PLA-HPG-al coating was expected to confer bioadhesion through dynamic Schiff-base formation with tissue proteins (Fig. S1). We next assessed the bioadhesion and in vivo retention of S-H/B using a non-adhesive PLA-HPG-coated control (S-H/C). Both formulations were labeled with PLA-Cy7.5 for fluorescence tracking, hereafter referred to as S-H/Cy7.5@C and S-H/Cy7.5@B. Ex vivo, S-H/Cy7.5@B showed greater adhesion to testicular tissue, with a fluorescence intensity more than three times that of S-H/Cy7.5@C (Fig. 2A and B). Following intrascrotal administration, fluorescence intensity in the scrotal region remained at approximately 40% of the initial level on day 30 in the S-H/Cy7.5@B group. By contrast, it fell below 30% by day 15 in the S-H/Cy7.5@C group (Fig. 2C and D). To assess potential toxicity, we conducted safety evaluations in young mice. Neither S-H nor S-H/B significantly affected body weight, serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, or serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 2E–G). No apparent histopathological abnormalities were observed in the testes or epididymides (Fig. 2H and I). These findings indicate no overt systemic or local toxicity within the measured endpoints.

S-H/B ameliorates reproductive aging in male mice

We next assessed the therapeutic efficacy of S-H/B in aged mice. No significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups of 18-month-old mice (Fig. 3A). Gross examination showed reduced testicular size in aged mice (Fig. 3B). This reduction was partially attenuated by S-H/B treatment, with a smaller change observed in the S-H group. The testicular index increased from approximately 5.0 in the Aged group to 6.3 following S-H/B treatment (p < 0.05, Fig. 3C), providing quantitative support for this improvement. Consistently, H&E staining showed partial improvements in seminiferous tubule architecture following S-H/B treatment, with less age-associated distortion and tubular shrinkage (Fig. 3D and E). No discernible histological differences were observed among groups in epididymal tissues (Fig. S2). Immunostaining for the germ cell marker DEAD-box helicase 4 (DDX4) revealed germ cell depletion in aged mice (Fig. 3F and G). S-H/B treatment approximately doubled the number of DDX4-positive cells compared with the Aged group and yielded significantly more DDX4-positive cells than S-H treatment (p < 0.05). Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and synaptonemal complex protein 3 (SCP3) immunostaining further showed fewer proliferating PCNA-positive cells and meiotic SCP3-positive cells in aged testes (Fig. 3H–K). S-H/B treatment increased the numbers of PCNA-positive and SCP3-positive cells to approximately twice those in the Aged group, and both counts were significantly higher than those in the S-H group (both p < 0.05).

Sperm-related functional outcomes were further assessed. Epididymal sperm concentration was significantly reduced in aged mice, whereas S-H/B treatment increased it to more than twice that in the Aged group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3L). Progressive motility showed a trend similar to that of sperm concentration, whereas the proportion of immotile sperm displayed an inverse pattern (Fig. 3M). H&E-based morphology analysis further revealed an increased fraction of abnormal sperm in aged mice, which was reduced from approximately 50% to 25% by S-H/B treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3N and O). Histological examination revealed no overt pathological changes in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney within the tested parameters (Fig. S3). Together, these results indicate that S-H/B alleviates age-associated testicular degeneration and promotes spermatogenesis in aged mice.

S-H/B stabilizes HIF-1α in Sertoli cells

To explore the molecular basis of S-H/B efficacy, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse testes. Marker-based clustering resolved eight major cell populations, as visualized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and confirmed by canonical marker expression (Fig. 4A and B). The eight populations comprised four somatic cell types (Sertoli cells, Leydig cells, endothelial cells, and macrophages) and four germ lineages (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, round spermatids, and elongated spermatids). S-H/B treatment did not appreciably alter the overall cell-type composition (Fig. 4C). At the transcriptional level, however, 90.4% of all differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in somatic cells (Fig. 4D).

Given the role of Sertoli cells in spermatogenesis and their susceptibility to aging [30,31], DEGs were further examined in this population. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed enrichment of glycolysis-related pathways, hypoxia responses, and HIF-1 target gene signatures in Sertoli cells following S-H/B treatment (Fig. 4E–H). Consistently, the top-ranked S-H/B-upregulated HIF-1 target genes in Sertoli cells were predominantly glycolysis-related (Fig. 4I). Notably, Hif1a mRNA levels remained unchanged both in the Sertoli cell population identified by single-cell RNA sequencing (Fig. 4J) and in isolated Sertoli cells (Fig. 4K). In contrast, HIF-1α protein abundance increased following S-H/B treatment (Fig. 4L), supporting post-translational stabilization of HIF-1α. This was supported by a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay indicating slower HIF-1α degradation (Fig. 4M). Together, these findings were consistent with post-translational stabilization of HIF-1α by S-H/B treatment. HIF-1α nuclear localization was also increased following S-H/B treatment (Fig. 4N and O).

Hydrogen released from S-H/B may contribute to the neutralization of highly reactive species, including ·OH and ONOO− [11,12]. Given that ONOO− has been implicated in promoting HIF-1α degradation [32], we quantified ONOO− in isolated aged Sertoli cells and observed a significant reduction following S-H/B treatment (Fig. 4P). In TM4 cells, S-H/B also attenuated intracellular ONOO− accumulation induced by the ONOO− donor 3-morpholinosydnonimine (SIN-1) (Fig. 4Q). Consistent with previous reports [32], SIN-1 markedly reduced HIF-1α protein abundance, whereas Hif1a mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4R and S). Co-treatment with S-H/B restored HIF-1α protein levels in SIN-1-treated cells. The CHX chase assay further showed that S-H/B slowed HIF-1α degradation in the presence of SIN-1 (Fig. 4T). In addition, S-H/B promoted HIF-1α nuclear localization in SIN-1-treated TM4 cells (Fig. 4U and V). Collectively, these data indicate that S-H/B stabilizes and activates HIF-1α in aged Sertoli cells, potentially through ONOO− scavenging.

S-H/B promotes glycolysis and lactate production in aged Sertoli cells

As a central transcriptional regulator of glycolysis, HIF-1α drives the expression of glucose transporters and multiple glycolytic enzymes [33,34]. Single-cell RNA sequencing identified upregulation of Slc2a1 and Slc2a3, which encode glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) and glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3), respectively, in Sertoli cells from S-H/B-treated aged mice (Fig. 5A). Several glycolytic enzyme genes, including Hk2, Pfkl, Aldoa, Tpi1, Gapdh, Pgk1, and Pkm, were also upregulated. Consistent changes were observed in isolated Sertoli cells from aged mice and SIN-1-treated TM4 cells, where S-H/B treatment was associated with higher mRNA levels of these glycolysis-related genes (Fig. 5B and C). Protein abundance showed a similar overall pattern (Fig. 5D and E). Immunofluorescence staining further showed increased GLUT1 and GLUT3 signals following S-H/B treatment (Fig. 5F and G). In addition, 2-NBDG uptake assays indicated increased glucose uptake following S-H/B treatment in both isolated Sertoli cells and SIN-1-treated TM4 cells (Fig. 5H and I).

We next examined whether these molecular changes were accompanied by increased glycolytic activity. Metabolic flux analysis based on extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) revealed that S-H/B increased basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve in both cell models (Fig. 5J and K). Consistently, S-H/B also increased intracellular and extracellular lactate levels (Fig. 5L and M). Pretreatment with the glycolytic inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) markedly reduced extracellular lactate levels and eliminated the S-H/B-associated increase (Fig. 5N and O). Collectively, these results indicate that S-H/B enhances glycolytic flux and lactate production in Sertoli cells, likely by upregulating key glycolytic enzymes.

S-H/B stabilizes HIF-1α by suppressing PHD1-dependent degradation

HIF-1α stability is primarily governed by PHD1–3-mediated prolyl hydroxylation at Pro402 and Pro564, which enables VHL-dependent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [35]. By contrast, FIH hydroxylation at Asn803 mainly limits HIF-1α transcriptional activity by restricting p300/CBP recruitment [35]. Following MG-132 treatment, S-H/B did not further increase HIF-1α protein levels (Fig. 6A and B), supporting a proteasome-dependent mechanism. PHD inhibition by CoCl2 increased HIF-1α protein abundance and the levels of downstream glycolytic enzymes (Fig. 6C and D). S-H/B did not further increase either response. CoCl2 likewise elevated lactate production and hypoxia response element (HRE) reporter activity in isolated Sertoli cells and TM4 cells, with no additional increase upon S-H/B treatment (Fig. 6E–H). Under MG-132 blockade, S-H/B reduced HIF-1α hydroxylation (Fig. 6I), whereas CoCl2 decreased hydroxylation to a similar extent and precluded any further reduction by S-H/B (Fig. 6J). Collectively, these results suggest that the effect of S-H/B on HIF-1α involves PHD-mediated hydroxylation.

To pinpoint the key PHD isoform, we performed isoform-specific knockdown in SIN-1-treated TM4 cells. Knockdown of PHD1, but not PHD2, PHD3, or FIH, markedly increased lactate production (Fig. 6K). Consistently, PHD1 knockdown increased HIF-1α protein abundance and HRE reporter activity, with no further increase upon S-H/B treatment (Fig. 6L and M). Under MG-132-mediated proteasome inhibition, PHD1 knockdown reduced HIF-1α hydroxylation, and S-H/B produced no further reduction, whereas total HIF-1α levels remained comparable across groups (Fig. 6N). These findings indicate PHD1-dependent hydroxylation as a key step affected by S-H/B.

We next sought to define how S-H/B influences PHD1-mediated regulation of HIF-1α stability. Co-immunoprecipitation showed that S-H/B did not alter PHD1-HIF-1α binding, suggesting that S-H/B is unlikely to regulate HIF-1α by altering this interaction (Fig. 6O). As hydrogen scavenges ONOO− and ONOO− can serve as an oxygen donor at the catalytic Fe2+ center of PHDs, we reasoned that S-H/B may regulate PHD1-mediated HIF-1α hydroxylation by limiting ONOO− availability. In the presence of MG-132, total HIF-1α and PHD1 protein levels remained relatively constant, whereas high-dose SIN-1 increased HIF-1α hydroxylation compared with low-dose SIN-1 (Fig. 6P). High-dose SIN-1 also attenuated the S-H/B-mediated reduction in HIF-1α hydroxylation. We further examined this possibility using an in vitro HIF-1α hydroxylation assay (Fig. S4). Protein preparations from control and PHD1-knockdown cells were used as the source of hydroxylase activity, with purified HIF-1α oxygen-dependent degradation domain protein (HIF-1αODD; residues 530–826) serving as the substrate, following a previously reported method [36]. In preparations from cells with normal PHD1 expression, HIF-1αODD Pro564 hydroxylation was low in the absence of SIN-1 and increased with increasing SIN-1 concentrations. By contrast, hydroxylation remained low in preparations from PHD1-knockdown cells despite increasing SIN-1 concentrations. Together with the observation that S-H/B reduced SIN-1-induced ONOO− levels in both isolated Sertoli cells and TM4 cells (Fig. 4P and Q), these findings support a model in which S-H/B limits PHD1-dependent HIF-1α hydroxylation by reducing ONOO− availability. The resulting reduction in HIF-1α hydroxylation may suppress its proteasomal degradation, thereby contributing to the observed increase in HIF-1α protein levels.

HIF-1α is required for the therapeutic efficacy of S-H/B in male reproductive aging

The requirement of HIF-1α for S-H/B efficacy was next evaluated. Pharmacological inhibition of HIF-1α using HIF-1α-IN-2 reduced HIF-1α protein abundance and the levels of downstream glycolytic enzymes (Fig. 7A and B). HIF-1α-IN-2 also attenuated the S-H/B-induced increases in these proteins in primary aged Sertoli cells and SIN-1-treated TM4 cells. Consistently, HIF-1α inhibition attenuated the S-H/B-induced increase in glycolytic flux (Fig. 7C and D; Fig. S5A and B). In TM4 cells with HIF-1α knockdown, HIF-1α and its downstream glycolytic enzymes were reduced (Fig. 7E). Under this condition, S-H/B failed to elevate HIF-1α or glycolytic enzymes, whereas re-expression of HIF-1α restored the response to S-H/B (Fig. 7F). Lactate measurements further supported these findings (Fig. 7G).

Subsequently, the role of HIF-1α in mediating the effects of S-H/B on spermatogenesis was examined in vivo. HIF-1α inhibition with HIF-1α-IN-2 aggravated testicular atrophy in aged mice, as reflected by a reduced testicular index (Fig. 7H and I). It also attenuated the increase in testicular index following S-H/B treatment. Consistently, H&E staining showed greater seminiferous tubule degeneration following HIF-1α-IN-2 administration, accompanied by germ cell loss and vacuolization (Fig. 7J and K). Under HIF-1α-IN-2 treatment, S-H/B provided minimal histological improvement. Epididymal sperm analysis further revealed that HIF-1α-IN-2 decreased epididymal sperm concentration and progressive motility, and S-H/B failed to restore either parameter (Fig. 7L and M). Moreover, S-H/B failed to reduce the elevated sperm abnormality rate induced by HIF-1α inhibition (Fig. 7N and O). Collectively, these results indicate that HIF-1α plays an important role in mediating the protective effects of S-H/B against age-associated male reproductive dysfunction.

Discussion

Accumulation of oxidative injury is implicated in the progression of male reproductive aging. Hydrogen is an attractive therapeutic gas because it selectively neutralizes highly reactive oxidants such as ·OH and ONOO− while sparing physiologically important O2·− and H2O2, thereby preserving redox signaling [11,12]. However, clinical translation remains challenging due to its low solubility and poor targeting with conventional delivery approaches. Here, we developed S-H/B, a hydrogen-delivery platform designed for sustained hydrogen release and prolonged retention within the scrotal cavity. In aged mice, S-H/B attenuated testicular degeneration and improved spermatogenesis. Mechanistically, our findings were consistent with a model in which S-H/B scavenged ONOO− and limited PHD1-dependent HIF-1α degradation in aged Sertoli cells. The resulting increases in glycolytic enzyme expression and lactate production may contribute to improved spermatogenesis.

Hydrogen therapy for male reproductive aging remains at an early stage. Studies in other settings suggest several factors to consider when improving the therapeutic efficacy of hydrogen. First, excessively rapid hydrogen generation may lead to gas loss before absorption. Mg microparticles decomposed almost completely within 5 min in the simulated gastric acid solution, whereas Zn-Fe systems with an Fe/Zn ratio of 1:100 sustained hydrogen generation for approximately 3 h, matching the gastric-emptying window in mice and potentially favoring hydrogen absorption [37]. Second, hydrogen concentrations may decline rapidly after administration, indicating the need for sustained delivery to maintain tissue exposure. After 3–4% hydrogen inhalation ceased, the arterial and venous hydrogen concentrations fell below 10% of plateau levels within approximately 6 and 18 min, respectively [38]. Third, site-specific hydrogen release may enhance local exposure and therapeutic efficacy. MgH2@EC@ES microcapsules enabled gastric passage followed by sustained intestinal hydrogen generation and attenuated colitis-associated injury in mice [39]. Together, these findings highlight the importance of sustaining hydrogen availability at the target site, guiding our approach to local hydrogen delivery for testicular aging.

Existing reproductive studies support the protective potential of hydrogen delivered through several routes. In rats with testicular ischemia/reperfusion injury, 2% hydrogen inhalation initiated immediately after injury induction and continued for 30–120 min reduced tissue injury, lipid peroxidation, and germ-cell apoptosis [40]. Another study showed that intraperitoneal administration of hydrogen-rich saline 15 min before detorsion reduced apoptosis and malondialdehyde levels and increased superoxide dismutase activity in rats with testicular ischemia/reperfusion injury [41]. Daily oral administration of hydrogen-rich water for 4 weeks also improved sperm production and motility in a study including middle-aged (12-month-old) mice, but its effects in older mice were not evaluated [16]. Orally administered solid hydrogen-generating materials have also been investigated. MgH2 administered by gavage for 3 days before irradiation and again immediately afterward attenuated radiation-induced declines in sperm motility and semen quality and preserved spermatogenesis in male mice [26]. In rats with varicocoele, dietary supplementation with a 1% silicon-based hydrogen-generating agent for 4 weeks increased sperm motility from 57.6% to 66.9% and in vitro fertilization rates from 26.3% to 51.8% [42]. More recently, supplementation with a 1% silicon-based agent for 5 weeks increased sperm count in 85-week-old mice, although total sperm motility did not improve significantly [43]. These studies suggest that hydrogen delivered by inhalation or hydrogen-rich saline can protect against acute reproductive injury when administered around the time of injury, while prolonged oral supplementation with hydrogen-rich water or hydrogen-generating materials has shown benefits in chronic conditions and aging. Based on these reported outcomes, long-term hydrogen delivery strategies may benefit from further optimization, for example, through sustained local hydrogen delivery around the aged testis.

Hydrogen delivery strategies can be broadly divided into the administration of preformed hydrogen and in situ hydrogen generation. The former includes inhalation and administration of hydrogen-rich water or saline, which have shown reproductive benefits with short-term or repeated dosing. However, as described above, the rapid decline in hydrogen concentrations after administration makes sustained local exposure difficult to maintain. In situ hydrogen generation has been explored using acid-responsive precursors, light- or ultrasound-responsive catalysts, engineered microorganisms, and reactive solid materials. Boron-based compounds require acidic conditions for hydrogen release, photocatalytic and sonocatalytic materials require light or ultrasound, and engineered microbial systems rely on microbial activity [23,[44], [45], [46], [47]]. These dependencies make them less suitable for sustained hydrogen delivery around the testis without additional intervention.

Reactive solid materials, including Mg, MgH2, Zn-Fe composites, and silicon-based agents, offer another route to sustained hydrogen generation. Mg and MgH2 generate hydrogen through reactions with water, but Mg(OH)2 formation can passivate the particle surface and cause local alkalinization. Acidic conditions promote hydrogen generation, but release may be excessively rapid, as illustrated by the near-complete Mg microparticle decomposition within 5 min in simulated gastric acid [37]. Polymer coatings can partially mitigate these limitations by regulating water access and surface chemistry. Mg-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) microparticles generated hydrogen continuously following intramuscular injection near osteoarthritic knees, with PLGA modulating water access and a Mg passivation-activation cycle occurring in body fluids [48]. In a PLGA-based MgH2 microneedle patch for diabetic wound healing, acidic PLGA degradation products alleviated surface passivation, increasing cumulative hydrogen production in water to 3.50 times that of unformulated MgH2 by day 7 [49]. Nevertheless, passivation and potential alkalinization require careful optimization of factors such as component ratios, coating properties, local pH, fluid availability, and ionic strength, potentially complicating prolonged peritesticular hydrogen delivery. Zn-Fe composites regulate hydrogen generation through galvanic coupling, although their gastric-acid-dependent kinetics cannot be directly extrapolated to the scrotal environment [37].

Silicon-based materials can likewise generate hydrogen through reactions with water, but of particular interest, this process can occur under physiological conditions, with release kinetics depending on material structure and formulation. Elemental silicon exhibits relatively low reactivity toward water, and surface oxide passivation can impede hydrogen generation. Smaller crystallites and mildly alkaline conditions can accelerate this reaction [50]. An elemental silicon-based agent generated hydrogen for more than 24 h under simulated intestinal conditions (pH 8.3, 36°C), producing approximately 400 mL of hydrogen per gram within 24 h [51]. However, its slow reaction kinetics and surface passivation under physiological conditions may limit local testicular use. S-H offer enhanced water reactivity through their hydrogenated surface structure, enabling hydrogen generation without external stimulation. Intramuscular S-H administration modulated macrophage polarization and reduced disuse muscle atrophy in mice, with 20 mg S-H in PBS generating approximately 0.60 mmol hydrogen within 1 h and 0.73 mmol by 2 h [52]. d-α-tocopheryl polyethylene glycol 1000 succinate (TPGS)-modified S-H (HyNDs) improved aqueous dispersibility and slowed hydrogen release relative to uncoated S-H through a shielding effect [53]. Nevertheless, most hydrogen from HyNDs was released within 200 min. In the glaucoma mouse model, a reduction in intraocular pressure was detectable 3 h after administration of HyNDs, but maintaining this effect over the 7-day treatment period required once-daily topical dosing. Building on these considerations, we developed S-H/B by coating S-H with PLA-HPG-al to establish a sustained local hydrogen source around the testis. The hydrophobic PLA layer restricts water access to the S-H core under physiological conditions, whereas aldehyde-functionalized HPG forms dynamic Schiff-base linkages with tissue proteins, promoting peritesticular adhesion and retention. Bioadhesion may also facilitate a more homogeneous distribution of nanoparticles within body cavities [29]. In PBS, S-H/B released ∼31% of its total hydrogen capacity by 72 h, versus ∼66% from S-H within 4 h (Fig. 1G). In aged mice, S-H/B retained >40% of its initial fluorescence signal at day 30, whereas the non-adhesive S-H/C control fell below 30% by day 15 (Fig. 2C and D). Although direct quantification of in vivo hydrogen-release kinetics and utilization remains technically challenging, the slow in vitro release, prolonged carrier retention, and limited water availability at the scrotal injection site suggest sustained local hydrogen generation in vivo. Improved sperm parameters at day 30 after a single administration are consistent with this interpretation.

During male reproductive aging, a progressive decline in endogenous antioxidant defenses exacerbates oxidative damage in the testis [54,55]. Sertoli cells are particularly susceptible to aging-associated insults, undergoing structural and functional deterioration before germ cell abnormalities emerge [56]. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling has further highlighted their heightened sensitivity to aging-associated oxidative stress [30,31]. Notably, ONOO− has been implicated in oxidative stress in aged Sertoli cells [8]. PRDX6 contributes to ONOO− clearance in these cells, and its deficiency is associated with age-dependent spermatogenic defects [[57], [58], [59]]. Hydrogen selectively scavenges ·OH and ONOO− [11,12]. Accordingly, hydrogen released from S-H/B scavenged ONOO− in TM4 cells and Sertoli cells isolated from aged mice, thereby attenuating ONOO−-associated cellular injury.

ONOO− not only induces oxidative damage but can also serve as an alternative oxygen donor for PHDs under hypoxia [10]. Among the NO donors tested, SIN-1 was reported to suppress HIF-1α, and this effect depended on ONOO− generation [32]. Consistently, we found that ONOO− reduced HIF-1α abundance in TM4 Sertoli cells (Fig. 4S). This effect was attenuated by S-H/B through ONOO− scavenging; however, the restoration was lost when ONOO− was present in excess (Fig. 6P). Under normoxia, HIF-1α stability is regulated by PHDs, whereas hypoxia inactivates these enzymes and permits HIF-1α accumulation [35]. ONOO− has been proposed to serve as an oxygen donor for PHDs, thereby sustaining HIF-1α hydroxylation and ubiquitin-dependent degradation even under hypoxic conditions [10]. In agreement with this mechanism, ONOO−-induced HIF-1α hydroxylation and degradation were observed in Sertoli cells and were largely dependent on PHD1 (Fig. 6L). CoCl2-mediated PHD inhibition or PHD1 knockdown markedly attenuated ONOO−-induced HIF-1α loss and diminished the restoration of HIF-1α by S-H/B (Fig. 6C, D, and L).

The testis is physiologically hypoxic, and oxygen tension further declines with age [60,61]. HIF-1α coordinates hypoxic adaptation and metabolic remodeling [62]. It promotes glycolysis by increasing glucose uptake (e.g., via GLUT1/3) and inducing glycolytic enzymes [33,34]. In Sertoli cells, HIF-1α stabilization supports glycolysis, lactate production, and cell survival [63]. HIF-1α has been reported to be upregulated by follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) in Sertoli cells, thereby enhancing lactate output [64]. However, the role of HIF-1α in Sertoli cell function during reproductive aging remains insufficiently defined. Here, S-H/B stabilized HIF-1α in aged Sertoli cells and ONOO−-challenged TM4 cells. Consistent with HIF-1α stabilization, S-H/B increased the expression of its downstream targets (including Slc2a1, Slc2a3, Aldoa, Tpi1, and Pkm), glycolytic flux, and lactate production (Fig. 7A–G). In vivo pharmacological inhibition further indicated that HIF-1α is required for the beneficial effects of S-H/B on age-associated male reproductive dysfunction.

HIF-1α plays highly context-dependent roles in aging across tissues [65]. In aged muscle, its stabilization is often linked to functional decline through disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis and inflammation [66,67]. By contrast, HIF-1α activation in skin has generally been linked to protective effects during aging [68]. A similar dichotomy exists in the reproductive system: HIF-1α is required for normal spermatogenesis [60,64], but can be detrimental under pathological conditions such as varicocele or toxicant exposure [[69], [70], [71]]. Our findings implicate an ONOO−-HIF-1α pathway in the metabolic dysfunction of aged Sertoli cells. Age-associated oxidative stress elevates ONOO− and compromises HIF-1α stability in Sertoli cells. S-H/B scavenges ONOO− via sustained hydrogen release, thereby restoring HIF-1α protein levels, enhancing glycolysis, and increasing lactate production to support spermatogenesis.

Sertoli cells convert glucose to lactate, which is shuttled to post-meiotic germ cells as a preferred energy source [72]. In germ cells, lactate can be converted to pyruvate by lactate dehydrogenase C4 (LDHC4), thereby supporting mitochondrial ATP production and spermatogenesis [72]. Notably, LDHC4 deficiency causes severe male infertility in mice and has been explored as a potential contraceptive target [73,74]. Lactate also exerts cytoprotective effects: intratesticular lactate infusion reduced spermatocyte apoptosis in cryptorchid rats [75], whereas lactate supplementation enhanced germ cell survival in cultured human testicular tissues [76]. In this study, S-H/B enhanced glycolytic flux and lactate production in aged Sertoli cells by scavenging ONOO− and stabilizing HIF-1α, likely supporting germ cell metabolism and survival, thereby improving spermatogenesis in aged mice.

Regarding clinical translation, the localized and sustained hydrogen delivery achieved by S-H/B may limit systemic exposure and reduce the need for frequent administration. Local administration through the scrotal route has clinical precedent in selected urological procedures, such as aspiration and sclerotherapy for hydrocele [77,78], although the optimal anatomical target, injection volume, and administration protocol for S-H/B remain to be established in humans. PLA-HPG-based nanoparticles have been reported to be biodegradable following systemic administration [79]. However, their degradation kinetics and clearance following intrascrotal administration require further investigation. The prolonged retention observed in mice provides a rationale for exploring extended dosing intervals, although it should not be directly extrapolated to humans. The appropriate dose and treatment frequency will need to be defined through dose-ranging, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic, and repeated-dose safety studies. Given the water-responsive nature of S-H, formulation stability and storage conditions will also require optimization to prevent premature hydrogen generation. Overall, the localized, injectable, and sustained-release properties of S-H/B provide a basis for further translational evaluation, although these challenges will need to be carefully addressed.

Beyond these translational considerations, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the therapeutic efficacy of S-H/B was evaluated primarily in naturally aged mice. Validation in large-animal models or human tissues will be important to determine its broader applicability. Second, although S-H/B improved testicular histology and sperm parameters, its effects on reproductive outcomes remain to be established. Future studies should assess natural mating, pregnancy, live birth, offspring health, and the durability of these benefits. Third, the current safety evaluation was restricted to the measured biochemical and histological endpoints. Longer-term and repeated-dose studies are needed to more comprehensively evaluate local and systemic safety. Finally, a number of translational issues, including the feasibility of intrascrotal administration, material degradation and clearance, the optimal dosing regimen, and formulation stability, remain unresolved and require further evaluation before clinical application.

Conclusion

In summary, this work provides proof-of-concept evidence for S-H/B as a platform for localized and sustained hydrogen delivery. S-H/B improved spermatogenesis in aged mice, with no overt toxicity observed within the measured endpoints. This effect may involve regulation of the ONOO−-PHD1-HIF-1α pathway in Sertoli cells, leading to enhanced glycolysis and lactate production. These findings provide a basis for further evaluation of S-H/B in age-associated male reproductive dysfunction.

Preparation of S-H/B

S-H was synthesized as previously described with modifications [27]. Briefly, 500 mg of CaSi2 was added to 50 mL of precooled concentrated HCl (37% w/w) at −25°C and stirred for 7 days. After the reaction, the product was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and washed three times with ethanol. To reduce the size, the product was redispersed in ethanol, sonicated for 24 h in an ice bath, and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min to isolate the precipitate.

PLA-HPG and PLA-Cy7.5 were prepared as described earlier [29]. To synthesize PLA-HPG-al, PLA-HPG (100 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL DMSO, and the solution was rapidly injected into 40 mL ultrapure water under continuous vortexing to form nanoparticles. The dispersion was filtered through a 100 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration membrane and washed three times with ultrapure water to remove residual free polymers. The nanoparticles were resuspended in ultrapure water to achieve a 25 mg/mL suspension. For oxidation, an equal volume of 0.1 M NaIO4 solution was added, and the resulting mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 min. The reaction was immediately quenched by adding three volumes of 0.2 M Na2SO3 solution. The final product was purified through four cycles of ultrafiltration (100 kDa MWCO) and lyophilized to obtain PLA-HPG-al.

For S-H/B preparation, 10 mg of S-H was combined with 13 mg PLA-HPG and 7 mg PLA-HPG-al in DMSO. The mixture was rapidly injected into 12 mL of ultrapure water under continuous vortexing. The resulting composites were filtered through a 100 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration membrane and washed three times with ultrapure water to remove unbound materials. After purification, S-H/B was collected for subsequent experiments. As a non-adhesive control (S-H/C), 10 mg of S-H was mixed with 20 mg PLA-HPG (without PLA-HPG-al) and processed similarly.

Structural and size characterization

Morphological analysis was performed using TEM. Samples were deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and air-dried at room temperature for TEM imaging. The hydrodynamic diameter was measured by DLS after dispersing the samples in ultrapure water.

In vitro hydrogen release

S-H and S-H/B (0.5 mg S-H equivalent) were resuspended in 3 mL PBS and deposited on glass slides. Hydrogen bubble formation was monitored over time using an optical microscope. For hydrogen quantification, S-H and S-H/B containing equivalent amounts of S-H (0.5 mg) were dispersed in 3 mL of PBS [27]. The suspensions were transferred to round-bottom flasks pre-purged with nitrogen, and hydrogen concentrations in the headspace were measured at fixed intervals over 72 h at 37°C using a calibrated UNISENSE hydrogen microsensor [80]. Two-point gas calibration was performed using high-purity nitrogen (0% hydrogen) and 2% hydrogen in nitrogen at 37°C, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Antioxidant activity of S-H/B in cell-free systems

For ·OH scavenging, ·OH was generated by mixing 1 mM FeSO4 and 2 mM H2O2 (Fenton reaction). S-H or S-H/B (both at 50 μg/mL S-H equivalent) was added, incubated at 37°C for 10 min, and then 10 mM TMB (in DMSO) was introduced. After a 15-min incubation at 37°C in the dark, absorbance at 650 nm was measured using a microplate reader. For ONOO− scavenging, S-H or S-H/B (both at 1 μg/mL S-H equivalent) were mixed with 10 μM SIN-1 (ONOO− donor) in PBS and incubated in the dark for 5 min. HKGreen-4I (10 μM) was then added, and fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 520/543 nm) was measured using a microplate reader.

Cytotoxicity and antioxidant effects of S-H/B in vitro

TM4 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 5% horse serum, 2.5% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 (normoxic conditions). The cytotoxicity and antioxidant assays were performed under these normoxic culture conditions.

To assess the cytotoxicity of S-H/B, TM4 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to S-H or S-H/B at S-H-equivalent concentrations of 0–200 μg/mL for 48 h. Cell viability was then assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay according to the manufacturer's instructions.

To assess the protective effects of S-H/B against ·OH-mediated injury, TM4 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Cells were pretreated with S-H or S-H/B at S-H-equivalent concentrations of 0–200 μg/mL in serum-free medium for 30 min and then challenged with Fenton's reagent (400 μM H2O2 and 40 μM FeSO4) for 2 h to induce ·OH-mediated injury. Cell viability was quantified using the CCK-8 assay. For ONOO−-induced injury, the same procedure was followed, except that cells were challenged with 500 μM SIN-1, an ONOO− donor, for 24 h.

Animal experimental design, randomization, and exclusion criteria

For all animal experiments, group sizes were selected based on previous studies using comparable animal models. Before treatment, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number table. Animals were excluded if treatment administration was incomplete or if they exhibited > 20% body-weight loss, persistent inability to eat or drink, severe lethargy or impaired mobility, unrelieved pain or distress, or severe scrotal ulceration, necrosis, or infection. Across all animal experiments in this study, no animals died, met any exclusion criterion, or were removed; thus, all randomized animals were included in the final analyses. Following collection, all animal samples were assigned identification codes, and investigators conducting histological and immunostaining quantification, seminiferous tubule diameter measurements, and sperm analyses were blinded to group allocation until all assessments were completed.

Tissue adhesion of S-H/B

For fluorescence labeling, 0.5 mg of PLA-Cy7.5 was premixed with the S-H used in the original formulation before coating to generate S-H/Cy7.5@B and S-H/Cy7.5@C. The formulations, each at an S-H concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, were applied evenly to freshly excised testes from 18-month-old mice and incubated in a humidified chamber at 34°C for 30 min. The tissues were then rinsed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent material. Tissue adhesion was assessed by fluorescence imaging using a NightOWL LB 983 in vivo imaging system (Berthold Technologies).

Intrascrotal retention of S-H/B

PLA-Cy7.5-labeled formulations (S-H/Cy7.5@B and S-H/Cy7.5@C) were administered by a single bilateral intrascrotal injection into 18-month-old mice. The injection dose was the same as described above: 25 μL per side (50 μL per mouse) at an S-H-equivalent concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. At the indicated time points, fluorescence signals from the scrotal region were imaged using a NightOWL LB 983 in vivo imaging system. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined over the scrotal area, and fluorescence intensity was quantified to assess intrascrotal retention over time.

In vivo biocompatibility assessment

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–7 weeks old) were randomly assigned to PBS, S-H, and S-H/B groups. On day 0, mice received a single bilateral intrascrotal injection (25 μL per side; 50 μL per mouse) of PBS, S-H (0.5 mg/mL), or S-H/B (at an S-H-equivalent concentration of 0.5 mg/mL). Body weight was monitored every other day. On day 30, mice were euthanized for sample collection. Blood was collected for serum isolation, and the testes and epididymides were harvested. Serum ALT and AST levels were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer. Testes and epididymides were fixed in testis tissue fixative (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 24 h at room temperature, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining for histopathological evaluation.

S-H/B efficacy in aged male mice

18-month-old naturally aged male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to four groups: Aged (PBS), S-H, Vehicle, and S-H/B. 12-week-old male C57BL/6 mice served as young controls and received PBS. On day 0, mice received a single bilateral intrascrotal injection of PBS, S-H, Vehicle, or S-H/B (25 μL per side; 50 μL per mouse). S-H was administered at 0.5 mg/mL, and S-H/B was formulated to contain 0.5 mg/mL S-H (corresponding to 1.5 mg/mL S-H/B). The Vehicle group received the same injection volume of an S-H/B-matched formulation in which S-H was replaced with silica nanosheets. 30 days after injection, mice were euthanized, and samples were collected for subsequent analyses. Body weight and the combined weight of both testes were recorded, and the testicular index was calculated as combined testis weight (mg)/body weight (g). Histological alterations in the testes and epididymides were evaluated by H&E staining. Ten seminiferous tubules per mouse were randomly selected from H&E-stained testicular sections for diameter measurements.

Immunofluorescence staining

Testicular tissues were fixed in testis tissue fixative for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. After deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by boiling for 20 min. The sections were then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. They were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies (anti-DDX4, anti-PCNA, or anti-SCP3) at 4°C overnight. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI for 10 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope. For quantitative analysis, ten testicular sections were randomly selected from each group for quantification.

Sperm count, motility, and morphology

The cauda epididymidis was collected immediately after euthanasia and placed in pre-warmed PBS (37°C). The tissue was minced and incubated at 37°C in a CO2 incubator for 1 h to allow sperm release. The sperm concentration was determined using a hemocytometer, and sperm motility was classified as progressively motile (PR), non-progressively motile (NP), or immotile (IM). Total motility was calculated as PR + NP. Sperm morphology was assessed on stained sperm smears under light microscopy, and the percentage of abnormal sperm was calculated. At least 200 sperm per mouse were evaluated separately for motility and morphological abnormalities.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Fresh testicular tissues from three mice per group were collected 30 days after the single intrascrotal injection, pooled, and dissociated using GEXSCOPE Tissue Dissociation Solution (Singleron Biotechnologies) with gentle mechanical disruption to generate a single-cell suspension. The suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. All samples showed >90% cell viability as assessed by trypan blue exclusion and were used for library construction. Single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were prepared following the manufacturer's protocol for the Singleron GEXSCOPE platform, utilizing microfluidic chip-based cell partitioning and barcoding. Library quality was assessed using a High Sensitivity DNA Chip (Agilent Technologies), and paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 system. FASTQ files were processed using SCOPE-tools to generate gene-barcode count matrices. Downstream analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1) with Seurat (v4.2.0). Data were normalized using Seurat, and PCA was performed using the top 2000 variable genes. Cell clusters were then identified using SNN-based clustering with a resolution of 0.7 and visualized using UMAP. Cell types were annotated using the SingleR package, and all visualizations were generated in R using ggplot2. The single-cell RNA sequencing data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive at the National Genomics Data Center, China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA: CRA043875).

Isolation of Sertoli cells

Primary Sertoli cells were isolated using a sequential enzymatic digestion protocol. Briefly, decapsulated testes were minced and subjected to three digestion steps at 37°C: (1) 2 mg/mL collagenase I and 1 mg/mL DNase I in PBS for 30 min; (2) 2 mg/mL collagenase I, 1 mg/mL DNase I, and 1 mg/mL hyaluronidase for 20–30 min; and (3) 2 mg/mL collagenase I, 1 mg/mL DNase I, 2 mg/mL hyaluronidase, and 1 mg/mL trypsin for 20 min. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and washed with DMEM/F12. Cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C under 5% CO2. After 24 h, residual germ cells were removed by hypotonic shock with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 min.

Treatment of isolated Sertoli cells and TM4 cells

Isolated Sertoli cells and TM4 cells were maintained in their respective culture media described above. Except for the cytotoxicity and cell-based antioxidant assays, all other experiments involving isolated Sertoli cells or TM4 cells were performed at 37°C in a humidified hypoxic atmosphere of 3% O2, 5% CO2, and 92% N2, unless otherwise specified. Isolated Sertoli cells were treated with Vehicle or S-H/B for 24 h. TM4 cells were pretreated with Vehicle or S-H/B for 2 h and then exposed to SIN-1 (50 μM) for 24 h. S-H/B was used at an S-H-equivalent concentration of 50 μg/mL, and the Vehicle dose was matched accordingly.

Targeted gene knockdown and overexpression

For targeted gene silencing, TM4 cells were transduced with lentiviral shRNAs targeting the genes encoding PHD1, PHD2, PHD3, FIH, and HIF-1α, with target sequences listed in Table S1. To restore HIF-1α expression after knockdown, cells were transfected with an HIF-1α expression vector containing silent mutations in the shHIF-1α#1 target site (5′-GT CGT TCT ATC TAC GAG TA-3′, nt 818–836). The underlined nucleotides indicate the mutations introduced to confer shRNA resistance. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells by co-transfecting pLKO.1-shRNA constructs with packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G) using Lipofectamine 2000. Viral supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection. Cells were transduced in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL). At 72 h post-transduction, puromycin (2 μg/mL) was applied for 5 days to select stable knockdown cells. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR and immunoblotting. MCF-7 cells expressing shNT or PHD1-targeting shRNAs were generated using the same lentiviral transduction and puromycin-selection procedure.

Measurement of ECAR

ECAR measurement was performed using a Seahorse XF extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent, USA). Cells were seeded in XF microplates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Prior to the assay, the growth medium was replaced with fresh assay medium. During the Glycolysis Stress Test, glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 μM), and 2-DG (50 mM) were sequentially injected at the indicated time points. The ECAR was recorded in real-time and analyzed using the Seahorse XF software.

qPCR, Co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting

Total RNA was isolated, and cDNA synthesis followed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed as previously described [81]. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. Co-immunoprecipitation was performed with 5 μg of antibody per sample. For immunoblotting, proteins were extracted and detected using the indicated primary antibodies (diluted 1:1000), with β-actin used as a loading control [81].

HRE reporter activity assay

HIF transcriptional activity was assessed using an HRE-driven luciferase reporter. Cells were transfected with the HRE-luciferase plasmid along with a Renilla luciferase control using Lipofectamine 2000. After 24 h, cells were treated as indicated, and dual-luciferase reporter activity was measured. Firefly/Renilla luciferase ratios were calculated and normalized to the Vehicle group (set to 100%).

Lactate measurement

Intracellular and extracellular lactate levels were determined using a Lactate Assay Kit (Beyotime). For extracellular lactate, culture supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation. For intracellular lactate, cells were washed with PBS and lysed; the lysates were centrifuged to remove debris. Samples were processed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and lactate concentrations were calculated from a standard curve. Lactate values were normalized to the Vehicle group (set to 100%).

In vitro hydroxylation assay

MCF-7 cells carrying a non-targeting control shRNA (shNT) or either of two PHD1-targeting shRNAs (shPHD1#1 and shPHD1#2) were treated with MG-132 (5 μM) for 4 h before harvest. Cells were lysed in reaction buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 5 mM KCl, and 1.5 mM MgCl2) by two freeze-thaw cycles. Lysates were passed through 21- and 26-gauge needles and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatants contained different levels of endogenous PHD1 and were used as cell extracts in the hydroxylation assay. Under hypoxic conditions (3% O2, 5% CO2, and 92% N2), 25 μL of cell extract was incubated with 1 μg of purified human HIF-1αODD protein (residues 530–826) and SIN-1 (0, 25, 50, or 100 μM) for 15 min at 37°C. Reactions were stopped with SDS loading buffer, and HIF-1αODD Pro564 hydroxylation was assessed by immunoblotting using an anti-hydroxy-HIF-1α antibody, as previously described with modifications [36].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Specific statistical tests, sample sizes, and data presentation formats are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
