Abstract:

Motivated by Calabi’s calculation of the second variation sign for locally strongly convex affine maximal surfaces in equiaffine geometry,
we first prove that every Calabi extremal surface is also maximal in the Calabi affine geometry. By employing suitably chosen orthonormal frame fields
and analyzing the corresponding Codazzi equations, we then obtain local classifications for certain special classes of Calabi affine maximal surfaces
and hyperbolic centroaffine extremal surfaces. These examples inspire the construction of new, complete Calabi affine maximal surfaces
and centroaffine extremal hypersurfaces. Notably, the complete centroaffine extremal hypersurfaces we establish answer
all five centroaffine Bernstein problems posed by Li- Li-Simon in 2004.

2000 AMS Classification:
Primary 53A15; Secondary 35J30, 53C24.

Key words:
affine maximal surface; centroaffine extremal surface;
centroaffine Bernstein problems; affine maximal type equation; completeness.

1.
Introduction

Considering the analogy that in the Euclidean and in the affine hypersurface theory both Euler-Lagrange
equations are given by the vanishing of the trace of the associated shape operator, Blaschke and his school
originally called hypersurfaces with vanishing affine mean curvature (
L_{1}=0
)
affine minimal hypersurfaces

without calculating the second variation of the volume functional. In contrast with the Euclidean case,
the evaluation of the second variation of the unimodular affine area functional is very complicated and difficult.
This important contribution was done by Calabi
[
1
]
in 1982, and he suggested to call locally strongly convex
hypersurfaces with
L_{1}=0

affine maximal hypersurfaces
. Calabi considered infinitesimal deformations
of pieces of strongly convex hypersurfaces which locally are graphs, and which remain strongly convex under deformation.
If the affine mean curvature vanishes on the original graph hypersurface, then the affine hypersurface associated
to the given hypersurface is truly larger than that of every hypersurface that forms through infinitesimal deformation.
For those so-called affine minimal surfaces that are not locally graphs, Calabi also proved that the second variation of
the area integral is negative definite under the infinitesimal deformations considered.

In 1994, Wang
[
27
]
studied the Euler-Lagrange equation for the area functional of a centroaffine hypersurface
and calculated its second variation formulas. The hyperbolic equiaffine hyperspheres centered at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

are stable (
V^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
), and the ellipsoid centered at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is unstable. As there are no general results
about the sign of the second variation of the centroaffine area integral, one usually use the terminology

centroaffine extremal hypersurface
in case the Euler-Lagrange equation is satisfied.
Let
x:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a locally strongly convex centroaffine graph hypersurface given by

x_{n+1}=f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}).

Then
x
is centroaffine extremal if
and only if
f
satisfies the PDE (see Proposition 3.2 in
[
15
]
)

(1.1)

\Delta\left\{\ln\left(\tfrac{\det(f_{ij})}{(f-\sum_{i}x_{i}f_{i})^{n+2}}\right)\right\}=0.

For the graph hypersurface given by a strictly convex
f
defined on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
Calabi
[
2
]
introduced a natural Riemannian metric
G:=\sum\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx_{i}dx_{j}

on
\Omega
in order to obtain Bernstein-type conclusions of Monge-Ampère equation, which originally was called
the
Schwarz-Pick metric
but now known as the
Calabi metric
. The volume variation with respect to the
Calabi metric was originally studied in
[
8
]
, see also
[
12
]
or p.111 of
[
14
]
. Gao
[
8
]
computed the first
and the second variation of the area integral for a convex graph with
Calabi affine normalization
, and proved that parabolic
affine hyperspheres are stable. The Euler-Lagrange equation of this variation can be written as the following fourth order PDE

(1.2)

\Delta\ln\det(f_{ij})=0.

A graph hypersurface defined by the solution of the PDE (
1.2
)
is called
Calabi extremal hypersurface
. As recalled above, it is difficult to determine the sign
of the second variation of volume functional of an affine extremal hypersurface
[
1
,
8
,
27
]
.
In this paper we use Calabi’s method in
[
1
]
to investigate Calabi extremal surfaces and prove

Theorem 1.1
.

The second variation of the area integral of the Calabi extremal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is negative definite.

Therefore any Calabi extremal surface is stable. What’s more, the area of Calabi extremal surface is truly
larger than that of every surface that forms through infinitesimal deformation, see Theorem 3.2 in section 3.
Hence we suggest to call Calabi surfaces satisfying the PDE (
1.2
)
Calabi affine maximal surfaces
.
Using the similar method, we also obtain that hyperbolic centroaffine extremal surfaces are stable,
which partially generalizes the Corollary 3.1 in
[
27
]
.

Bernstein problem for affine maximal surfaces is a core problem in affine differential geometry.
There were two famous conjectures on complete affine maximal surfaces, one due to Calabi
[
3
]
,
the other to Chern
[
4
]
. Both conjectures were solved with different methods about two decades ago,
see
[
13
,
25
,
26
]
. So far the higher dimensional affine Bernstein problems are still open.
The second purpose of this paper is to study the five Bernstein problems for centroaffine extremal hypersurfaces
in centroaffine geometry, which were formulated by Li-Li-Simon
[
15
]
in 2004.
They were long standing open problems due to the complication of PDE (
1.1
). In particular, known complete centroaffine
extremal hypersurfaces are extremely rare, aside from proper hyperspheres centered at the origin, they are all
canonical
.
In recent papers
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
, we have come to realize that
centroaffine geometry and Calabi affine geometry are not only complementary to each other as two special types of relative geometries,
but also intimately related. What’s more, the authors positively answer the centroaffine Bernstein problems III and V in
[
19
]
.
In this paper, we introduce a new Calabi affine maximal surface with complete flat Calabi metric. Applying it and the type II Calabi product
defined in
[
19
]
, we construct an elliptic centroaffine extremal hypersurface that is complete with respect to the centroaffine
metric and whose Tchebychev vector field has a nonconstant norm. This not only positively answers centroaffine Bernstein problem IV
but also provides a counterexample to centroaffine Bernstein problem I, while also yields a new example to centroaffine Bernstein problems III and V.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
x:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Calabi surface parameterized as

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} x_{1}&=\int_{0}^{u_{1}}e^{t^{2}}\,dt,\hskip 9.24994ptx_{2}=\int_{0}^{u_{2}}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt,\\
x_{3}&=\int_{0}^{u_{1}}\left(e^{t^{2}}\,(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-s^{2}}ds)\right)\,dt+\int_{0}^{u_{2}}\left(e^{-t^{2}}\,(\int_{0}^{t}e^{s^{2}}ds)\right)\,dt,\end{aligned}\right.

where
(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then

(i)

x
is a Calabi affine maximal surface with complete flat Calabi metric. Furthermore,
it is also Euclidean complete, and its Tchebychev vector field has a nonconstant norm.

(ii)

The type II Calabi product of a point and the Calabi surface
x
given by

(1.4)

y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{4})=(e^{t},e^{t}x_{1},e^{t}x_{2},e^{t}x_{3}+te^{t})

is a centroaffine extremal hypersurface of elliptic type in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with complete flat
centroaffine metric, and the norm of the Tchebychev vector field is nonconstant.

In order to solve centroaffine Bernstein problem II, we directly use the similar method
in the centroaffine geometry in the last part. We locally classify some special hyperbolic centroaffine extremal surfaces and
obtain two classes of new hyperbolic centroaffine extremal surfaces that are complete with respect to the centroaffine metric
and whose Tchebychev vector fields have nonconstant norms. It positively answers the centroaffine Bernstein problems II and IV,
while also yields new counterexamples to centroaffine Bernstein problem I.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a centroaffine surface given by one of the following two types of surfaces:

(i)

x_{3}=\tfrac{1}{x_{1}}+x_{1}(\ln{x_{1}}-\ln{x_{2}});

(ii)

x_{2}^{1-\alpha}x_{3}^{1+\alpha}-x_{1}^{2}=1,\quad 0<\alpha<1.

Then
x
is a hyperbolic centroaffine extermal surface of constant curvature
-1
, which is both centroaffine complete and Euclidean complete.
Up to a centroaffine transformation, it can be represented as a graph over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, we review the elementary facts of Calabi hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
In Sect. 3, we study stability of Calabi extremal surfaces. In sect. 4, by constructing a typical orthonormal frame field on Calabi surfaces and
using the Codazzi equations, we classify some special Calabi affine maximal surfaces and affine maximal type surfaces.
Finally, in Sect. 5 and 6, we complete the proof of Theorem 1.2 and 1.3, respectively.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we shall recall some basic facts for the Calabi geometry, see
[
2
,
22
]
or section 3.3.4 in
[
14
]
.

Let
x:M^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a locally strongly convex hypersurface immersion of a smooth, connected
manifold into real affine space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Assume that
Y=(0,\ldots,0,1)
is a relative normalization of the hypersurface

M^{n}
, which is called the
Calabi affine normalization
. Here and later we call such immersion equipped with the Calabi
affine normalization a
Calabi hypersurface
. For
M^{n}
, choose a local coordinate system
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
.
For partial derivation of the vector valued function
x
we use the notation

x_{u_{i}}=\frac{\partial x}{\partial u_{i}}
,
x_{u_{i}u_{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{j}}
, etc,
while covariant derivation with respect to the Levi-Civita connection of the relative metric
G
is denoted by
{x}_{,ij}
etc.
As usual we adopt Einstein’s summation convention in all sections of this paper. The
structure equations
of relative hypersurface theory read

(2.1)

D_{\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}}\tfrac{\partial x}{\partial u_{j}}=x_{*}(\nabla_{\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}})+G(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}},\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}})\,Y,

Y_{i}=-\sum B_{i}^{j}{x}_{u_{j}}=0,

where
D
is the standard flat connection of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\nabla
and
G
are called the induced connection
and the relative metric of
M^{n}
induced by the relative normalization
Y
.

Since the immersion is locally strongly convex, the
relative
metric

G
(also called
Calabi metric
) is definite, and it is positive definite by appropriate orientation of
M^{n}
.
Denote by
\hat{\nabla}
the Levi-Civita connection with the Calabi metric of
M^{n}
.
(1,2)-type difference tensor
A
, defined by

(2.2)

A(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}},\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}):=A_{\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}:=\nabla_{\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}-\hat{\nabla}_{\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}},

is called the
Fubini-Pick tensor
. It is symmetric as both connections
\nabla
and
\hat{\nabla}
are torsion free.
Thus, in covariant form, the Gauss structure equation for
{x}
reads

(2.3)

{x}_{,ij}=\sum A^{k}_{ij}\,{x}_{u_{k}}+G_{ij}\,Y.

To
A
associated there is the cubic form or
Fubini-Pick form
:

A^{\flat}:=\sum_{i,j,k}A_{ijk}du_{i}du_{j}du_{k}:=\sum_{i,j,k,l}A_{ij}^{l}G_{lk}du_{i}du_{j}du_{k},

which is totally symmetric, i.e.,

A_{ijk}=A_{ikj}=A_{kij}
.
In case the meaning is clear one also sometimes simplifies the notation for
A^{\flat}
, just writing the cubic form by
A
.
The tensors
G
and
A
are basic invariants, which determine a Calabi hypersurface, up to an affine equivalence (for details, see
[
28
]
).
The tangent vector field

(2.4)

T:=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum G^{ij}A_{ij}^{l}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{l}}

is called the
Tchebychev vector field
of the hypersurface
M^{n}
, and the operator
\mathcal{T}:=\hat{\nabla}T
is
called
Tchebychev operator
. The invariant function

J:=\tfrac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum G^{il}G^{jp}G^{kq}A_{ijk}A_{lpq}

is named as the
relative Pick invariant
of
M^{n}
, where
(G^{ij})
denotes the inverse matrix of metric matrix
(G_{ij})
.

Denote by
\hat{R}
the Riemannian curvature tensor of the Calabi metric.
For any vector fields
X,W,Z
tangent to
M^{n}
, we have the equations of Gauss and Codazzi

(2.5)

\hat{R}(X,W)Z=A_{W}A_{X}Z-A_{X}A_{W}Z,

(2.6)

(\hat{\nabla}_{Z}A)(X,W)=(\hat{\nabla}_{X}A)(Z,W).

Write
\hat{R}(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}})\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{k}}=:R_{kij}^{l}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{l}}
. Then the
Gauss
and
Codazzi equations
can be written as

(2.7)

R_{kij}^{l}=\sum(A_{ik}^{m}A_{jm}^{l}-A_{jk}^{m}A_{im}^{l}),

(2.8)

A_{ij,l}^{k}=A_{il,j}^{k}.

From (
2.7
) we get the
Ricci tensor

(2.9)

R_{kj}=\sum R^{l}_{klj}=\sum(A_{lk}^{m}A_{jm}^{l}-A_{jk}^{m}A_{lm}^{l}).

Thus, the scalar curvature
R
is given by

(2.10)

R=n(n-1)J-n^{2}|T|^{2},

where
|T|^{2}
is the square of the length of Tchebychev vector field
T
.

Using the Ricci identity, (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we have two useful formulas.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
28
]
)
.

For a Calabi hypersurface of dimension
n
, the following formulas hold

(2.11)

\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta|T|^{2}=\sum T_{i,j}^{2}+\sum T_{i}T_{j,ji}+\sum R_{ij}T_{i}T_{j},

(2.12)

\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\Delta J=\sum(A_{ijk,l})^{2}+\sum A_{ijk}A_{lli,jk}+\sum(R_{ijkl})^{2}+\sum R_{ij}A_{ipq}A_{jpq},

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian with respect to the Calabi metric
G
.

Denote by
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})
the affine coordinates of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Because
Y=(0,\ldots,0,1)
is
the relative normal of Calabi hypersurface
M^{n}
, we use the implicit function theorem to obtain that

x(M^{n})
can be locally described in terms of some strictly convex function
f
, defined on a
domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:
x_{n+1}=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. As usual, see the section 2.7.2 in
[
16
]
,
or the section 3.3.4 in
[
14
]
, we write a Calabi hypersurface as graph immersion of some strictly convex function
f

M_{f}:=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})\;|\;x_{n+1}=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\;(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\Omega\}.

In this case, the Calabi metric
G
and the Fubini-Pick form
A
have a simpler expression:

(2.13)

G=\sum\tfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}dx_{i}dx_{j},\quad\hbox{and}\quad A_{ijk}=-\tfrac{1}{2}f_{ijk}.

Let
A(n+1)
be the group of
(n+1)
-dimensional affine transformations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Then

A(n+1)=GL(n+1)\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, the semi-direct product of the general linear group
GL(n+1)

and the group
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
of all the translations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Define

(2.14)

SA(n+1)=\{(M,b)\in A(n+1)=GL(n+1)\ltimes{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1};\ M(Y)=Y\},

where
Y=(0,\ldots,0,1)^{t}
is the Calabi affine normal. Then the subgroup
SA(n+1)
consists of all
the transformation
\phi\in A(n+1)
of the following type:

\displaystyle X:

\displaystyle=(X^{1},\ldots,X^{n},X^{n+1})^{t}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mapsto\phi(X):=\left(\begin{matrix}a^{i}_{j}&0\\
a^{n+1}_{j}&1\end{matrix}\right)X+b,\quad\forall\,X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1},

for some matrix
(a^{i}_{j})\in A(n)
, constants
a^{n+1}_{j}
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) and some constant vector

b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Clearly, the Calabi metric
G
is invariant under the action of
SA(n+1)
on the Calabi hypersurfaces.

Definition 2.1

(
[
28
]
)
.

Two Calabi hypersurfaces
x:M^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
and
\tilde{x}:\bar{M}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

are called Calabi affinely equivalent if they differ only by an affine transformation
\phi\in SA(n+1)
.

3.
Stability of Calabi extremal surfaces

Consider a Calabi hypersurface
x:M^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the graph function
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
The first variational formula of the area integral with respect to the Calabi metric
G
is (see
[
8
]
)

V^{\prime}(0)=-\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{M}\varphi\Delta\ln\det(f_{ij})dV,

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian of the Calabi metric
G
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(M)
. The equation

(3.1)

\Delta\ln\det(f_{ij})=0

is the Euler-Lagrange equation for the variational problem considered.
Moreover, the fourth order PDE (
3.1
) is geometrically equivalent to the fact that the Tchebychev vector field
T

is divergence free with respect to the Calabi metric, namely,
\text{tr}\mathcal{T}=0
.
Furthermore, Gao
[
8
]
calculated the second variation of the area integral and proved

(3.2)

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{M}\left[(\Delta\varphi)^{2}-n^{2}\langle\nabla\varphi,T\rangle^{2}-2n\Delta\varphi\langle\nabla\varphi,T\rangle-2nf^{ik}f^{lj}T_{ij}\varphi_{k}\varphi_{l}\right]dV,

where
(f^{ik})
is the inverse matrix of
(f_{ik})
.

In the following, we shall use Calabi’s idea in
[
1
]
to consider the sign of the second variation (
3.2
).
Here we only prove the case
n=2
, namely Theorem 1.1 in this paper.
Just as the second variation of affine maximal hypersurfaces, a more general result in the case of locally strongly convex hypersurfaces
is not yet known. In fact, Krauter
[
11
]
proved that it is not possible to establish the corresponding result
for dimension
n\geq 3
affine maximal hypersurfaces by a suitable reduction of the quadratic form given by
the second variation to a sum of squares as was done by Calabi for
n=2
.

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Calabi extremal surface with graph function
f
and
boundary
\partial M
. Let
x_{t}:M^{2}\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an admitted
compact supported variation of
x
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x_{t}=(x_{1},x_{2},f(x_{1},x_{2},t))
is the graph of a strictly convex function
f(x_{1},x_{2},t)
.
For simplicity, denote
f_{t}=f(x_{1},x_{2},t)
.
Therefore

(3.3)

\tfrac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{1}}=(1,0,\tfrac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial x_{1}}),\quad\tfrac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{2}}=(0,1,\tfrac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial x_{2}}),\quad\tfrac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial t}=(0,0,\tfrac{\partial f_{t}}{\partial t}).

We denote the variation vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

(3.4)

\tfrac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial t}=\varphi Y+\psi^{i}\tfrac{\partial x_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}

for some smooth functions
\varphi
and
\psi^{i}
with
\varphi=\psi^{i}=0
on
\partial M
.
By (
3.4
) and the admitted variation, we have
\varphi_{i}=\tfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}=0
on
\partial M
.
Then the divergence theorem yields

\displaystyle 0=\int_{M}\mathrm{div}(|\nabla\varphi|^{2}T)dV=\int_{M}2\varphi^{i}\varphi_{,ij}T^{j}dV,

where
\varphi^{i}=f^{ik}\varphi_{k}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{M}\left[\Delta\varphi\langle\nabla\varphi,T\rangle+f^{ik}f^{lj}T_{ij}\varphi_{k}\varphi_{l}\right]dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{M}\left[-T_{i,j}\varphi^{i}\varphi^{j}+T_{ij}\varphi^{i}\varphi^{j}\right]dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{M}T_{k}A^{k}_{ij}\varphi^{i}\varphi^{j}dV,

where we have used
A^{k}_{ij}=-\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
, in which
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
denote the connection coefficients of
\hat{\nabla}
.
Then (
3.2
) can be rewritten as

(3.5)

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\int_{M}\left[\tfrac{1}{4}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}-\langle T,\nabla\varphi\rangle^{2}+\langle A(\nabla\varphi,\nabla\varphi),T\rangle\right]dV.

Denote two trace-free operators

\displaystyle(L\varphi)_{ij}:=

\displaystyle\varphi_{,ij}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta\varphi)f_{ij},

\displaystyle(\hat{A}\varphi)_{ij}:=

\displaystyle A_{ij}^{h}\varphi_{,h}-(T^{h}\varphi_{,h})f_{ij},

and denote by
(L\varphi,\hat{A}\varphi)
the Hilbert inner product

\int_{M}(L\varphi)_{ij}(\hat{A}\varphi)_{kl}f^{ik}f^{jl}dV.

Applying Stokes’s theorem and the Ricci identities, for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(M)
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{M}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}dV=\int_{M}(f^{jl}f^{ik}\varphi_{,ij}\varphi_{,kl}+R^{hl}\varphi_{,h}\varphi_{,l})dV.

On the other hand, we use the (pointwise) orthogonal decomposition of
\varphi_{,ij}

\varphi_{,ij}=(\varphi_{,ij}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta\varphi)f_{ij})+\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta\varphi)f_{ij}=(L\varphi)_{ij}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta\varphi)f_{ij},

and the Ricci formula (
2.9
), yielding

\displaystyle\int_{M}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}dV=(L\varphi,L\varphi)+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}dV+\int_{M}(A_{j}^{hi}A^{jk}_{h}\varphi_{,i}\varphi_{,k}-2A^{ij}_{l}\varphi_{,i}\varphi_{,j}T^{l})dV.

Then

\displaystyle\int_{M}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}dV=2(L\varphi,L\varphi)+2(A\varphi,A\varphi)-4\int_{M}A(\nabla\varphi,\nabla\varphi,T)dV.

Note that

(A\varphi,A\varphi)=(\hat{A}\varphi,\hat{A}\varphi)+2\int_{M}\langle T,\nabla\varphi\rangle^{2}dV,

therefore

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{M}(\Delta\varphi)^{2}dV=2(L\varphi,L\varphi)+2(\hat{A}\varphi,\hat{A}\varphi)+4\int_{M}\biggl(\langle T,\nabla\varphi\rangle^{2}-A(\nabla\varphi,\nabla\varphi,T)\biggl)dV.

Inserting (
3.7
) into (
3.5
), we obtain

V^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(L\varphi,L\varphi)+(\hat{A}\varphi,\hat{A}\varphi)\right].

It is obviously that
V^{\prime\prime}(0)\leq 0
. In fact,

L\varphi=\hat{A}\varphi=0

is an overdetermined system of PDEs, which can only be satisfied by the trivial solution
\varphi=0
.
Thus the proof of Theorem 1.1 are completed.
\Box

In
[
27
]
Wang showed the following interesting conclusion that the hyperbolic equiaffine hyperspheres centered at

0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
are stable (
V^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
), and the ellipsoid centered at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is unstable.
Using the similar method in the centroaffine geometry as above, one can prove that:

Proposition 3.1
.

Hyperbolic centroaffine extremal surfaces are stable.

It partially generalizes Wang’s Corollary 3.1 in
[
27
]
. Here we omit its proof because the calculations are similar.

In the following we use Calabi’s method in
[
1
]
(see also Theorem 5.5 in
[
16
]
) to prove that the area of
a Calabi extremal surface is truly larger than that of every surface that forms through infinitesimal deformation.
We omit the detailed proof as it follows a similar argument.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
x
,
x^{\sharp}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be two graphs defined on a compact domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
by strongly convex functions
f
,
f^{\sharp}
, namely

x_{3}=f(x_{1},x_{2})\;\;\;and\quad x^{\sharp}_{3}=f^{\sharp}(x_{1},x_{2}),

respectively. Assume that on
\partial\Omega
:
f=f^{\sharp},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial f^{\sharp}}{\partial x_{i}},\ 1\leq i\leq 2
. Denote by
dV,dV^{\sharp}
their volume elements
with respect to the Calabi metric, respectively. If the graph of
f
is a Calabi extremal surface, then

\int_{\Omega}dV^{\sharp}\leq\int_{\Omega}dV,

and equality holds if and only if
f=f^{\sharp}
on
\Omega
.

It follows from Theorem 3.2 that the area of Calabi extremal surface is maximal.
Consequently, based on Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 3.2 above, we propose to call Calabi surfaces
satisfying the PDE (
3.1
)
Calabi affine maximal surfaces
.

4.
Some special Calabi affine maximal surfaces

This section is aimed to find some special nontrivial solutions to the PDE (
3.1
). We begin by constructing typical
orthonormal frame fields on Calabi surfaces. Then, by analyzing the Codazzi equations, we derive information of
the difference tensor and the connection with respect to the typical frame. It enables us to classify Calabi affine
maximal surfaces under specific conditions.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Calabi surface. Here we only consider the non-trivial solutions to the
PDE (
3.1
). Thus we assume that the Tchebychev vector field
T\neq 0
on an open set
U_{0}\subset M^{2}
.
Since our result is local in nature, in the following we restrict to considering Calabi surface
M^{2}
such that

U_{0}=M^{2}
, i.e., we assume that
T\neq 0
on
M^{2}
.

We now fix a point
p\in M^{2}
. For subsequent purposes, we will employ a typical orthonormal basis for
T_{p}M^{2}

introduced by Ejiri, which has been widely applied and proved to be very useful for various situations,
see e.g.,
[
5
,
10
,
18
]
. Denote the set of unit vectors in
T_{p}M^{2}
by
U_{p}M^{2}:=\left\{u\in T_{p}M^{2}\mid G(u,u)=1\right\}
, which is a compact set. Define a continuous function

F(u)=G\left(A_{u}u,u\right)
on
U_{p}M^{2}
. Since the Fubini-Pick tensor
A(p)\neq 0
, the function

F
attains an absolute maximum at some direction
e_{1}\in U_{p}M^{2}
such that
\lambda:=F\left(e_{1}\right)>0
.
Then the following lemma holds, see Lemma 3.1 in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
T_{p}M^{2}
such that the following hold:

(4.1)

A_{e_{1}}e_{1}=\lambda e_{1},\quad A_{e_{1}}e_{2}=\mu e_{2},

where
\lambda>0,\lambda\geq 2\mu
. If
\lambda=2\mu
, then
G(A_{e_{2}}e_{2},e_{2})=0.

In the following, we extend the above basis of
T_{p}M^{2}
to be a local orthonormal frame field on a neighbourhood of
p
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a Calabi surface. Then, for any
p\in M^{2}
,
there exists a local orthonormal frame field
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
on a neighbourhood
U
of
p

such that the Fubini-Pick tensor
A
satisfies one of the following:

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} A_{E_{1}}E_{1}&=2\sqrt{-K}E_{1},\quad A_{E_{1}}E_{2}=\sqrt{-K}E_{2},\\
A_{E_{2}}E_{2}&=\sqrt{-K}E_{1},\end{aligned}\right.

where
K
, the Gaussian curvature of the Calabi surface
x(M^{2})
, is a negative constant.

(4.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} A_{E_{1}}E_{1}&=f_{1}E_{1},\ A_{E_{1}}E_{2}=gE_{2},\\
A_{E_{2}}E_{2}&=gE_{1}+f_{2}E_{2},\end{aligned}\right.

where the functions
f_{1},g
and the Gaussian curvature
K
satisfy

(4.4)

f_{1}>0,\quad f_{1}>2g,\quad g=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(f_{1}-\sqrt{f_{1}^{2}+4K}\right).

Proof.
For any
p\in M^{2}
, let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be the orthonormal basis of
T_{p}M^{2}
described in Lemma 4.1.
Letting
j=k=1
in (
2.9
) gives

K(p)=\mu^{2}-\lambda\mu,\quad p\in M^{2}.

By
\lambda\geq 2\mu
, we have

(4.5)

\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4K(p)}).

Recall the notion of the function
\theta
introduced by Zhao-Cheng-Hu
[
30
]

(4.6)

\theta(p):=\max_{u\in U_{p}M^{2}}G(A_{u}u,u),\quad p\in M^{2},

which is a continuous function on
M^{2}
, see Lemma 3.2 in
[
30
]
.
Since (
4.5
) implies that
\theta^{2}+4K\geq 0
on a neighbourhood
U
of
p
,
we now consider the following two cases separately.

Case (1)
:
\theta^{2}+4K\equiv 0
on
U.

This implies
K<0
and
\theta\equiv 2\sqrt{-K}
on
U
. Then we obtain
\lambda=\theta(p)=2\sqrt{-K(p)}

and it follows from (
4.5
) that
\mu=\sqrt{-K(p)}
. Hence, we have

(4.7)

A_{e_{1}}e_{1}=2\sqrt{-K(p)}e_{1},\quad A_{e_{1}}e_{2}=\sqrt{-K(p)}e_{2},\quad A_{e_{2}}e_{2}=\sqrt{-K(p)}e_{1}.

Therefore, the Tchebychev vector field satisfies

T(p)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}A(e_{i},e_{i})=\tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{-K(p)}e_{1}.

Put
E_{1}:=\tfrac{2}{3\sqrt{-K}}T
, so that
E_{1}(p)=e_{1}
. As the function
\theta\equiv 2\sqrt{-K}
on
U
,
we have
A_{E_{1}}E_{1}=2\sqrt{-K}E_{1}.
This implies that the distribution
\{E_{1}\}^{\perp}
is
A_{E_{1}}
-invariant.
Let
E_{2}\in\{E_{1}\}^{\perp}
be the unit eigenvector of
A_{E_{1}}
, then we obtain (
4.2
). Consequently,
by Proposition 3.2 in
[
24
]
,
K
is a negative constant.

Case (2)
:
\theta^{2}+4K\not\equiv
0 on
U.

Define
U^{{}^{\prime}}:=\{q\in U\,\mid\,\theta^{2}(q)+4K(q)=0\}
. If
U^{{}^{\prime}}
is an open subset, then the situation reduces to
Case (1). If
U^{{}^{\prime}}
contains no open subset of
M^{2}
, then
U^{\prime}_{0}:=\{q\in U\mid\theta^{2}(q)+4K(q)>0\}
is an
open dense subset. Since our result is local in nature, we restrict to considering
U^{\prime}_{0}=U
. We assume that

\theta^{2}+4K>0
on
U
. In particular, we have
\lambda>2\mu
at
p
.

The argument is now standard: using the implicit function theorem, we extend the orthonormal basis (
4.1
)
to a local orthonormal frame field
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
on a neighbourhood
U
of
p\in M^{2}
that satisfies (
4.3
)
and (
4.4
). We omit the proof and refer to
[
6
]
for further details.

This completes the proof of Lemma 4.2.

\Box

In the following subsections, we will give a local classification of Calabi surfaces satisfying specific conditions,
using the two distinct frame fields provided by Lemma 4.2.

4.1. The classification of affine maximal type surfaces

In this subsection, we aim to find a class of new complete solutions to the following nonlinear,
fourth-order PDE for a convex function
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
defined on a convex domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(4.8)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f^{ij}(D^{a})_{ij}=0,\;\;\;D=\det\left(\tfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right),

where
a\neq 0
is a constant. Following
[
7
]
, we call the PDE (
4.8
)
affine maximal type equation
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
x:M^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a Calabi hypersurface whose graph function
f
satisfies the PDE (
4.8
).
Then the trace of the Tchebychev operator
\mathcal{T}
satisfies

(4.9)

\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{T}=n(2a+1)|T|^{2}.

Proof.
From (
4.8
), we obtain

(4.10)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f^{ij}\left[(a-1)\tfrac{D_{i}D_{j}}{D^{2}}+\tfrac{D_{ij}}{D}\right]=0.

On the other hand, the trace of
\mathcal{T}
is computed as

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{T}=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f^{ij}(\ln{D})_{i,j}=-\tfrac{1}{2n}\Delta\ln{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f^{ij}\left[(\ln{D})_{ij}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\ln{D})_{i}(\ln{D})_{j}\right]

(4.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f^{ij}\left(\tfrac{D_{ij}}{D}-\tfrac{3}{2}\tfrac{D_{i}D_{j}}{D^{2}}\right).

A combination of (
4.10
) and (
4
) yields (
4.9
).
\Box

From (
4.9
), it follows directly that when
a=-\tfrac{1}{2}
, the PDE (
4.8
) is equivalent to the PDE (
3.1
).

We now consider the case
n=2
. Since quadratic polynomials are trivial solutions to the PDE (
4.8
),
we focus on non-trivial solutions thus assuming the Tchebychev vector field satisfies
T\neq 0
.
By Theorem 5.8.1 and Proposition 4.5.2 in
[
14
]
, we know that it is a natural condition
that
|T|
is a positive constant to find new complete non-trivial solutions of the PDE (
4.8
).

We begin by classifying the case in which
|T|
is a positive constant.
It is shown that, under this condition,
the only solutions are the surfaces
Q(c;2)
defined in
[
28
]
and the surfaces obtained in
[
24
]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an affine maximal type surface with
|T|=c>0
.
Then
x(M^{2})
is Calabi affinely equivalent to an open part of one of the following three types of surfaces:

(i)

x_{3}=-\tfrac{1}{4c^{2}}\ln x_{1}+\tfrac{x_{2}^{2}}{2}
;

(ii)

x_{3}=-c_{1}\ln x_{1}-c_{2}\ln x_{2},\;\;\tfrac{1}{c_{1}}+\tfrac{1}{c_{2}}=4c^{2},\;\;c_{1},c_{2}>0;

(iii)

x_{3}=-\tfrac{9}{16c^{2}}\ln{(x_{1}-\tfrac{x^{2}_{2}}{2})},\;\;\;\;\;x_{1}>\tfrac{x^{2}_{2}}{2}
;

(iv)

x_{3}=-\tfrac{9}{16c^{2}}\ln{x_{1}}+\tfrac{x^{2}_{2}}{2x_{1}},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;x_{1}>0
.

Here, the cases (i)(ii), (iii) and (iv) correspond to
a=-\tfrac{1}{2}
,
a=-\tfrac{2}{3}
, and
a=-\tfrac{1}{3}
in the PDE (
4.8
), respectively.

Proof.

Choose an orthonormal frame field
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
on
M^{2}
. Since
|T|^{2}
is a constant, we have

\langle\hat{\nabla}_{E_{i}}T,T\rangle=0
for
i=1,2
, which implies that
\sum_{j=1}^{2}T_{j,i}T_{j}=0
, that is,

\left\{\begin{aligned} T_{1,1}T_{1}+T_{2,1}T_{2}=0,\\
T_{1,2}T_{1}+T_{2,2}T_{2}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Regarding this as a homogeneous system of linear equations in
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, a nonzero solution exists if and only if
the determinant of the coefficient matrix vanishes, i.e.,

(4.12)

T_{1,1}T_{2,2}-(T_{1,2})^{2}=0.

Then we obtain

(4.13)

|\hat{\nabla}T|^{2}=(T_{1,1})^{2}+(T_{2,2})^{2}+2(T_{1,2})^{2}=(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{T})^{2}.

From (
4.9
), (
4.13
), (
2.11
) and the fact that
|T|=\text{const}>0
, we obtain the Gaussian curvature

(4.14)

K=-4(2a+1)^{2}|T|^{2}\leq 0.

We now consider the following two cases separately.

Case (1)
:
a=-\tfrac{1}{2}.

In this case, (
4.14
) implies
K\equiv 0
. It follows from (
2.11
) that
\hat{\nabla}T=0
.
Then by (
2.10
) and (
2.12
), we have
\hat{\nabla}A=0
, namely,
x(M^{2})
is a canonical Calabi surface.
From Theorem 1.3 in
[
29
]
, we obtain that
x(M^{2})
is Calabi affinely equivalent to an open part of
Q(c_{1},\ldots,c_{b};2)
for
b=1,2
.

Case (2)
:
a\neq-\tfrac{1}{2}.

In this case,
K
is a negative constant. Let
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
be the orthonormal frame field described in Lemma 4.2.
Consider case (i) of Lemma 4.2, in which the Fubini-Pick tensor satisfies (
4.2
).
Inserting (
4.14
) into (
4.2
) and using
|T|=c
, we know that

a=-\frac{2}{3}
and
a=-\frac{1}{3}
. Following
[
24
]
, we have
x(M^{2})
is Calabi affinely equivalent
to an open part of one of the following surfaces:

(4.15)

x_{3}=-\tfrac{9}{16c^{2}}\ln{(x_{1}-\tfrac{x^{2}_{2}}{2})},\;\;\;\;\;x_{1}>\tfrac{x^{2}_{2}}{2},

or

(4.16)

x_{3}=-\tfrac{9}{16c^{2}}\ln{x_{1}}+\tfrac{x^{2}_{2}}{2x_{1}},\;\;\;\;\;x_{1}>0.

For case (ii) of Lemma 4.2, the Fubini-Pick tensor satisfies (
4.3
).
Since
f_{1}>2g
and
K<0
, it follows from (
4.4
) that

(4.17)

f_{1}>2\sqrt{-K},\quad 0<g=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(f_{1}-\sqrt{f_{1}^{2}+4K}\right)=\tfrac{-2K}{f_{1}+\sqrt{f_{1}^{2}+4K}}<\sqrt{-K}.

Put
\hat{\nabla}_{E_{i}}E_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}E_{k}
, where
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}+\Gamma_{ik}^{j}=0
for
1\leq i,j,k\leq 2
.
Substituting
Z=E_{1},X=E_{2},W=E_{1}
into the Codazzi equation (
2.6
) and using (
4.3
), we derive

(4.18)

E_{2}\left(f_{1}\right)=(f_{1}-2g)\Gamma_{11}^{2},\quad E_{1}\left(g\right)=(f_{1}-2g)\Gamma_{21}^{2}+f_{2}\Gamma_{11}^{2}.

From (
4.4
), we obtain

(4.19)

f_{1}=\tfrac{g^{2}-K}{g}.

Thus, in the subsequent calculations, we express
f_{1}
in terms of
g
and
K
. Equation (
4.18
) thus implies

(4.20)

E_{2}(g)=-g\Gamma_{11}^{2},\;\;E_{1}(g)=-\tfrac{g^{2}+K}{g}\Gamma_{21}^{2}+f_{2}\Gamma_{11}^{2}.

Similarly, substituting
Z=E_{1},X=E_{2},W=E_{2}
into (
2.6
), we derive

(4.21)

E_{1}(f_{2})=-4g\Gamma_{11}^{2}-f_{2}\Gamma_{21}^{2}.

From (
4.3
) and (
4.19
), we obtain the Tchebychev vector field

(4.22)

T=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{2g^{2}-K}{g}E_{1}+f_{2}E_{2}\right).

As

|T|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}\left[\left(\tfrac{2g^{2}-K}{g}\right)^{2}+f_{2}^{2}\right]

is a constant, its derivatives along
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
vanish. Therefore, using (
4.20
) and (
4.21
), we obtain

(4.23)

\tfrac{f_{2}K^{2}}{g^{3}}\Gamma_{11}^{2}+\tfrac{f_{2}^{2}g^{4}+(4g^{4}-K^{2})(g^{2}+K)}{g^{4}}\Gamma_{21}^{2}=0,\quad-\tfrac{4g^{4}-K^{2}}{g^{2}}\Gamma_{11}^{2}+f_{2}E_{2}(f_{2})=0.

From (
4.9
), we obtain

\langle\hat{\nabla}_{E_{1}}T,E_{1}\rangle+\langle\hat{\nabla}_{E_{2}}T,E_{2}\rangle-2(2a+1)|T|^{2}=0.

It follows that

(4.24)

\displaystyle\tfrac{f_{2}(g^{2}+K)}{g^{2}}\Gamma_{11}^{2}+E_{2}(f_{2})-\tfrac{K(4g^{2}+K)}{g^{3}}\Gamma_{21}^{2}-(1+2a)[(\tfrac{2g^{2}-K}{g})^{2}+f_{2}^{2}]=0.

Our following discussions are divided into two subcases:

Case (2.1)
:
f_{2}\equiv 0.

Substituting
f_{2}\equiv 0
into (
4.23
), as
2g^{2}-K>0
and
g^{2}+K<0
, we obtain

(2g^{2}+K)\Gamma_{21}^{2}=0,\quad(2g^{2}+K)\Gamma_{11}^{2}=0.

If
2g^{2}+K\neq 0
, then
\Gamma_{11}^{2}=\Gamma_{21}^{2}=0
. If
2g^{2}+K=0
, i.e.,
K=-2g^{2}
, then
g
is a constant. From (
4.20
),
we still obtain
\Gamma_{11}^{2}=\Gamma_{21}^{2}=0
, it follows that
K=0
, which is a contradiction.
Hence,
Case (2.1)
does not occur.

Case (2.2)
:
f_{2}\neq 0.

From (
4.23
), we obtain

(4.25)

\Gamma_{11}^{2}=-\tfrac{f_{2}^{2}g^{4}+(4g^{4}-K^{2})(g^{2}+K)}{gf_{2}K^{2}}\Gamma_{21}^{2},\quad E_{2}(f_{2})=\tfrac{4g^{4}-K^{2}}{f_{2}g^{2}}\Gamma_{11}^{2}.

Substituting (
4.25
) into (
4.24
), we obtain

(4.26)

\Gamma_{21}^{2}=-\tfrac{(1+2a)f_{2}^{2}gK^{2}}{(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})}.

Substituting (
4.26
) into (
4.25
), we obtain

(4.27)

\Gamma_{11}^{2}=\tfrac{(1+2a)f_{2}(f_{2}^{2}g^{4}+(4g^{4}-K^{2})(g^{2}+K))}{(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})},\;\;E_{2}(f_{2})=\tfrac{(1+2a)(4g^{4}-K^{2})(f_{2}^{2}g^{4}+(4g^{4}-K^{2})(g^{2}+K))}{g^{2}(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})}.

Substituting (
4.26
) and (
4.27
) into (
4.20
) and (
4.21
), we obtain

(4.28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{1}(g)&=\tfrac{(1+2a)f_{2}^{2}g^{4}(f_{2}^{2}+4g^{2}+4K)}{(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})},\quad E_{2}(g)=-\tfrac{(1+2a)f_{2}g(f_{2}^{2}g^{4}+(4g^{4}-K^{2})(g^{2}+K))}{(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})},\\
E_{1}(f_{2})&=-\tfrac{(1+2a)f_{2}g(f_{2}^{2}+4g^{2}+4K)(4g^{4}-K^{2})}{(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})}.\end{split}

As the Levi-Civita connection is torsion-free, we have

(4.29)

E_{1}(E_{2}(g))-E_{2}(E_{1}(g))=(\hat{\nabla}_{E_{1}}{E_{2}})(g)-(\hat{\nabla}_{E_{2}}{E_{1}})(g).

By using (
4.26
)–(
4.28
), we obtain

(4.30)

-\tfrac{4f_{2}g^{2}(1+2a)^{2}((2g^{2}-K)^{2}(g^{2}+K)+f_{2}^{2}(K^{2}-g^{2}K+g^{4}))}{(g^{2}+K)(f_{2}^{2}g^{2}+(2g^{2}+K)^{2})}=0.

Then we get

(4.31)

f_{2}^{2}=-\tfrac{(2g^{2}-K)^{2}(g^{2}+K)}{K^{2}-g^{2}K+g^{4}}.

Substituting (
4.31
) into the first and third equations of (
4.28
), we obtain

(4.32)

E_{1}(g)=-\tfrac{3(1+2a)g^{4}(g^{2}+K)(2g^{2}-K)^{2}}{(K^{2}-g^{2}K+g^{4})(K^{2}+2g^{2}K+4g^{4})},\quad E_{1}(f_{2})=-\tfrac{3(1+2a)f_{2}g(4g^{4}-K^{2})}{K^{2}+2g^{2}K+4g^{4}},

where
K^{2}+2g^{2}K+4g^{4}=(K+2g^{2})^{2}-2g^{2}K>0
.

Differentiating both sides of (
4.31
) with respect to
E_{1}
and using (
4.31
) and (
4.32
), we obtain

(4.33)

\tfrac{6(1+2a)gK^{2}(g^{2}+K)^{3}(2g^{2}-K)^{4}}{(K^{2}-g^{2}K+g^{4})^{3}(K^{2}+2g^{2}K+4g^{4})}=0,

which is a contradiction. Hence,
Case (2.2)
does not occur.

This completes the proof of Theorem 4.4.
\Box

Theorem 4.4 classifies affine maximal type surfaces with
|T|=\text{const}>0
, yet it yields no new examples.
In the following, we turn to the condition that
T
is auto-parallel, i.e.,
\hat{\nabla}_{T}T=0
.
However, as shown in the following proposition, this reduces to the case
|T|
is a constant.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an affine maximal type surface with
\hat{\nabla}_{T}T=0
,
then the norm of the Tchebychev vector field
|T|
is a constant.

Proof.
If the Tchebychev vector field
T\equiv 0
, then the proposition holds.
Thus we assume that
T\neq 0
on
M^{2}
. Let
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
be the orthonormal frame field described in Lemma 4.2.

For case (i) of Lemma 4.2, the Fubini-Pick tensor satisfies (
4.2
), which implies that
|T|
is a positive constant.

For case (ii) of Lemma 4.2, the Fubini-Pick tensor satisfies (
4.3
). Since
\hat{\nabla}_{T}T=0
,
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{2}T_{i}\hat{\nabla}_{E_{i}}T=0
, which yields

(4.34)

\left\{\begin{aligned} T_{1}T_{1,1}+T_{2}T_{1,2}=0,\\
T_{1}T_{2,1}+T_{2}T_{2,2}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The analysis is divided into two cases according to whether the Gaussian curvature
K
vanishes.

Case (1)
:
K=0
.

From (
4.4
), we obtain
g=0
. Then (
4.3
) reduces to

(4.35)

\left\{\begin{aligned} A_{E_{1}}E_{1}&=f_{1}E_{1},\ A_{E_{1}}E_{2}=0,\quad f_{1}>0,\\
A_{E_{2}}E_{2}&=f_{2}E_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Substituting
Z=E_{1},X=E_{2},W=E_{1}
into the Codazzi equation (
2.6
), we derive

(4.36)

E_{2}(f_{1})=f_{1}\Gamma_{11}^{2},\quad\Gamma_{21}^{2}=-\tfrac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}\Gamma_{11}^{2}.

Substituting
Z=E_{1},X=E_{2},W=E_{2}
into (
2.6
), we obtain

(4.37)

E_{1}(f_{2})=\tfrac{f_{2}^{2}}{f_{1}}\Gamma_{11}^{2}.

Combining (
4.9
) with (
4.35
) and (
4.36

Centroaffine Bernstein problems I & IV

Five centroaffine Bernstein problems for locally strongly convex centroaffine extremal hypersurfaces were proposed by Li-Li-Simon

Recently, Lei-Xu-Zhao

We begin by recalling the five centroaffine Bernstein problems, for details see
[

Definition 5.1

Euclidean completeness,
that is the completeness of the Riemannian metric on

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

16

Centroaffine completeness
is the completeness of the centroaffine metric

Centroaffine Bernstein problem I.
Let

be a centroaffine
extremal hypersurface satisfying one of the completeness conditions from Definition 5.1. Is

Centroaffine Bernstein problem II.
Does the class of centroaffine extremal hyperbolic graphs over

\mathbb{R}^{n}
contain other examples as the ones given in Examples 3.1 and 3.3?

Centroaffine Bernstein problem III.
Do there exist extremal centroaffine hypersurfaces with complete
centroaffine metric which cannot be represented as graphs over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
?

Centroaffine Bernstein problem IV.
Do there exist extremal centroaffine hypersurfaces satisfying one
of the completeness conditions such that the Tchebychev field does not have constant norm?

Centroaffine Bernstein problem V.
Do there exist extremal elliptic centroaffine hypersurfaces satisfying
one of the completeness conditions which are not hyperellipsoids?

T=\tfrac{4}{3}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}+\tfrac{2}{3}(u_{1}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{1}}-u_{2}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u_{2}}),

and the norm is

(5.7)

|T|^{2}=\tfrac{4}{9}(4+u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}).

This not only positively answers centroaffine Bernstein problem IV but also provides a counterexample to
centroaffine Bernstein problem I, while also yielding a new example to centroaffine Bernstein problem V.
If we consider the curve

\gamma(t)=(e^{t},0,0,te^{t}),\qquad t\rightarrow-\infty,

on the centroaffine extremal hypersurface (
5.4
),
then it is easy to see that the hypersurface
y
has a Euclidean boundary point
(0,0,0,0)
. Therefore, it is not Euclidean complete,
which yielding a new example to centroaffine Bernstein problem III.

6.
Centroaffine Bernstein problem II

In Sections 4 and 5, using typical orthonormal frame fields and the Codazzi equations, we obtained a
complete Calabi affine maximal surface. Applying it and the type II Calabi product, we construct a centroaffine
complete elliptic centroaffine extremal hypersurface. It answers centroaffine Bernstein problems I, III, IV and V.
In order to solve centroaffine Bernstein problem II, we now directly use the similar method
in the centroaffine differential geometry.

Consider a hyperbolic centroaffine surface
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
(for further details of
centroaffine geometry, see
[
15
,
6
]
). We assume that
x
has constant negative Gaussian curvature
and its Tchebychev vector field
T\neq 0
on
M^{2}
. Then we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
x:M^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a hyperbolic centroaffine surface with Gaussian curvature
-1
.
Then, for any
p\in M^{2}
, there exists a local orthonormal frame field
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
on a neighbourhood

U
of
p
such that the difference tensor
K
takes the following form:

(6.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} K_{E_{1}}E_{1}&=f_{1}E_{1},\;\;\;\;f_{1}>0,\\
K_{E_{1}}E_{2}&=0,\quad K_{E_{2}}E_{2}=f_{2}E_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.
For any point
p\in M^{2}
, note that Tchebychev vector field
T(p)\neq 0
, thus we have that there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
T_{p}M^{2}

such that the following holds at
p
:

(6.2)

K_{e_{1}}e_{1}=\lambda e_{1},\quad K_{e_{1}}e_{2}=\mu e_{2},

where
\lambda>0,\lambda\geq 2\mu
. Moreover, if
\lambda=2\mu
, then
h(K_{e_{2}}e_{2},e_{2})=0
,
where
h
denotes the centroaffine metric induced by
x
.

Denote by
\hat{R}
the Riemannian curvature tensor of the centroaffine metric
h
. From the Gauss equation (2.8) in
[
6
]
, we have

(6.3)

R_{ijkl}=\sum_{m}(K_{jk}^{m}K_{il}^{m}-K_{ik}^{m}K_{jl}^{m})+\varepsilon(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-\delta_{jk}\delta_{il}).

Letting
i=k=1
,
j=l=2
in (
6.3
), and using the facts that
\varepsilon=-1
and the Gaussian curvature is
-1
,
together with (
6.2
), we obtain

(6.4)

\mu^{2}-\lambda\mu-1=-1.

It follows that
\mu=0
.

Let
\{\tilde{E}_{1},\tilde{E}_{2}\}
be an arbitrary local differentiable orthonormal frame field,
defined on a neighbourhood
\tilde{U}
of
p
such that
\tilde{E}_{i}(p)=e_{i}
for
i=1,2.

Define a mapping

\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\tilde{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\ (a_{1},a_{2},q)\mapsto(y_{1},y_{2}),

where

(6.5)

y_{k}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{i}a_{j}h(K_{\tilde{E}_{i}}\tilde{E}_{j},\tilde{E}_{k})-\lambda a_{k},\quad k=1,2.

Then we have

y_{k}(1,0,p)=0,\;\;k=1,2,

and

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial y_{k}}{\partial a_{i}}|_{(1,0,p)}=\begin{cases}\lambda>0,\quad\quad k=i=1,\\
-\lambda<0,\quad\ k=i=2,\\
0,\quad\quad\qquad k\neq i.\end{cases}

Thus the matrix
(\tfrac{\partial y_{k}}{\partial a_{i}})
at the point
(1,0,p)
is invertible.
By the implicit function theorem, there exist differential functions
a_{1}(q),a_{2}(q)
defined on a neighbourhood

\tilde{U}^{\prime}\subset\tilde{U}
of
p
such that
a_{1}(p)=1
,
a_{2}(p)=0
, and

y_{k}(a_{1}(q),a_{2}(q),q)\equiv 0,\ \ k=1,2,\quad\forall\>q\in\tilde{U}^{\prime}.

Let
V:=\sum_{i=1}^{2}a_{i}\tilde{E}_{i}
, then
V(p)=e_{1}
and
K_{V}V=\lambda V
. As
|V(p)|=1
,
there exists a neighbourhood
U\subset\tilde{U}^{\prime}
of
p
such that
V\neq 0
on
U
.
Hence the unit vector field
E_{1}
defined by
E_{1}:=\tfrac{V}{|V|}
on
U
satisfies

K_{E_{1}}E_{1}=f_{1}E_{1},\ f_{1}=\tfrac{\lambda}{|V|}.

This implies that the distribution
\{E_{1}\}^{\perp}
is
K_{E_{1}}
-invariant on
U.

For any
q\in U
, let
E_{2}(q)\in\{E_{1}(q)\}^{\perp}
be the unit eigenvector of
K_{E_{1}(q)}

with associated eigenvalue
\alpha
such that
K_{E_{1}(q)}E_{2}(q)=\alpha E_{2}(q)
.
By the conditions that the Gaussian curvature is
-1
and
\varepsilon=-1
, (
6.3
) yields

\alpha^{2}-f_{1}(q)\alpha-1=-1.

It follows that
K_{E_{1}(q)}
has at most two distinct eigenvalues:
f_{1}(q)
,
0.

By the continuity of the eigenvalue functions of
K_{E_{1}}
, we see that the eigenvalue functions of
K_{E_{1}}
can only be
f_{1}
and
0
.
Then for
E_{2}\in\{E_{1}\}^{\perp}
we get (
6.1
).
Since
f_{1}(p)>0
and
f_{1}
is a continuous function, we have
f_{1}>0
on a neighborhood
U
of
p
.

This completes the proof of Lemma 6.1.

\Box

In the following, we consider a special class of centroaffine extremal surfaces whose difference tensor satisfies (

) with
f_{2}\equiv 0
on

Remark 6.1
.

These centroaffine extremal surfaces of Theorem 6.2 are all the level set of homogeneous functions of degree 2 in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In some sense, the hyperboloid

(6.43)

x_{2}x_{3}-x_{1}^{2}=1

can be viewed as the limit case of (ii) of Theorem 6.2.
The centroaffine extremal surfaces (iii)–(v) of Theorem 6.2 are neither centroaffine complete
nor Euclidean complete. By Calabi products defined in
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, we know that there are lots of
centroaffine complete centroaffine extremal hypersurfaces, which is quite different to the case of affine complete
affine maximal hypersurfaces in the equiaffine geometry.
