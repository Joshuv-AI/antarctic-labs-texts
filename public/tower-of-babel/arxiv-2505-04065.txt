Accelerated Gradient Methods Through Variable and Operator Splitting
Long Chen, Hao Luo, Jingrong Wei

Abstract
This paper introduces a unified framework for accelerated gradient methods through the variable and operator splitting (VOS). The operator splitting decouples the optimization process into simpler subproblems, and more importantly, the variable splitting leads to acceleration. The key contributions include the development of strong Lyapunov functions to analyze stability and convergence rates, as well as advanced discretization techniques like Accelerated Over-Relaxation (AOR) and extrapolation by the predictor-corrector methods (EPC). For convex case, we introduce a dynamic updating parameter and a perturbed VOS flow. The framework effectively handles a wide range of optimization problems, including convex optimization, composite convex optimization, and saddle point systems with bilinear coupling.

Unconstrained convex optimization, First order method, Dynamical system, Lyapunov function, Gradient flow, Proximal gradient method, Acceleration, Minimax problem

Introduction

Optimization methods form the backbone of various fields, ranging from data science to computational physics. Among these, first-order methods like gradient descent and its accelerated variants are widely used due to their simplicity and efficiency. However, designing and analyzing accelerated gradient methods for various optimization problems remains a challenge.

This work presents a unified framework for accelerated gradient methods to solve the operator equation arising from optimization problems:

\mathcal{L}(x)=\nabla F(x)+N(x)=0,

where
F
is convex and smooth, and
N
is
\mu
-strongly monotone:

(N(x)-N(y),x-y)\geq\mu\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\mu>0.

Denote by
x^{\star}
the solution to
\mathcal{L}(x^{\star})=0
. One way to obtain the solution
x^{\star}
is through the (generalized) gradient flow:

x^{\prime}=-(\nabla F(x)+N(x)),

(1)

where
x(t)
is treated as a function of an artificial time variable
t
. Gradient descent (GD) and the proximal point algorithm (PPA) correspond to the explicit Euler and implicit Euler discretizations of (
1
), respectively.

The operator splitting method is a broad class of methods that breaks down a complex problem into simpler subproblems
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
In addition to the operator splitting
\mathcal{L}=\nabla F+N
, we introduce a new variable
y
, which converges to
x
but acts as a separate variable, and propose the following
variable and operator splitting
(VOS) flow:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,&&\text{(variable splitting)}\\
y^{\prime}&=-\frac{1}{\mu}(\nabla F(x)+N(y)).&&\text{(operator splitting)}\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

The equilibrium point is
x=y=x^{\star}
solving the operator equation
\mathcal{L}(x^{\star})=\nabla F(x^{\star})+N(x^{\star})=0
.

The operator splitting decouples the optimization process into simpler subproblems, and more importantly, the variable splitting leads to acceleration. We will demonstrate this acceleration using Lyapunov analysis.

Consider the dynamical system defined by the ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\bm{z}^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}(t)),\quad t>0,\quad\bm{z}(0)=\bm{z}_{0},

(3)

where
\bm{z}
is a vector-valued function of the time variable
t
, e.g.
\bm{z}=(x,y)^{\intercal}
, and
\mathcal{G}
is a vector field, which could represent the negative gradient or a reasonable alternative. In what follows, assume
\bm{z}^{\star}
is an equilibrium point of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}^{\star})=0
.

The Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z})
associated to the ODE (
3
) usually satisfies

•

(Non-negativity)
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z})\geq 0
for all
\bm{z}\in V
;

•

(Optimality)
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z})=0
if and only if
\bm{z}=\bm{z}^{\star}
;

•

(Lyapunov condition)
-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z})\rangle\geq 0
for all
\bm{z}\text{ near }\bm{z}^{\star}
.

The Lyapunov condition implies that
\mathcal{G}
serves as a descent direction for minimizing
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z})
. Consequently, the (local) decay property of
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}(t))
along the trajectory
\bm{z}(t)
of the system (
1
) can be immediately derived:

\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}(t))=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\bm{z}^{\prime}(t)\rangle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z})\rangle\leq 0.

To establish the convergence rate of
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}(t))
, we will use the
strong Lyapunov property
introduced in
[
9
]
. In its simplest form,
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z})
is a Lyapunov function and satisfies:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z})\rangle\geq c\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\quad\forall\bm{z}\in V,

(4)

where
c>0
is a constant. Then

\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}(t))=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\bm{z}^{\prime}(t)\rangle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z})\rangle\leq-c\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}),

and the exponential stability then follows:

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}(t))\leq\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{0})e^{-ct}.

As shown in
[
9
]
, the strong Lyapunov function offers a unified approach to analyzing various optimization flows and discretization schemes.

For the VOS flow (
2
), a suitable Lyapunov function is:

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2},

where
D_{F}(x,x^{\star})
is the Bregman divergence (cf. Section
2.2
) associated with the convex function
F
. By direct calculations, the strong Lyapunov property holds:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle\geq\mathcal{E}(x,y)+D_{F}(x^{\star},x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

From the exponential stability of the equilibrium point in the continuous level to the accelerated convergence rate of a discretization, the journey is nontrivial. However, by employing the strong Lyapunov property and convexity, the linear convergence of the implicit Euler method

\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}=\alpha\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\quad\alpha>0,

can be established in just a few lines:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{(convexity of }\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle

\displaystyle\text{(implicit Euler method)}

\displaystyle\leq-c\,\alpha\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}).

\displaystyle\text{(strong Lyapunov property})

From which, the linear convergence for arbitrary step size
\alpha>0
follows:

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{1+c\,\alpha}\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k}),\quad\forall\alpha>0.

The implicit Euler method is impractical, necessitating an explicit treatment of certain terms in the flow. To obtain explicit schemes, we introduce two discretization techniques: Accelerated Over-Relaxation (AOR), originally developed for solving linear algebraic systems
[
23
]
, and Extrapolation by the Predictor-Corrector (EPC) method
[
16
, Section 6.2.3]
, commonly used for solving ODEs. These techniques serve as the foundation for deriving accelerated gradient methods.

Here we use AOR to illustrate the idea and refer to Fig.
1
for the illustration of EPC. For the VOS flow (
2
), an implicit-explicit (IMEX) discretization is given by

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2y_{k+1}-y_{k}-x_{k+1},

(5a)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla F(x_{k})+N(y_{k+1})\right).

(5b)

Here,
y
is updated first by (
5b
), followed by
x
through (
5a
). The AOR technique approximates
y\approx 2y_{k+1}-y_{k}
, aiming to symmetrize the difference equation of the Lyapunov function.

The convergence analysis becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})={}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\alpha(\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\star},\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k})_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}},

where
\mathcal{A}^{\sym}
is a symmetric but might be indefinite operator.
With this symmetrization, the troublesome cross terms can be expanded using the identity of squares:

\displaystyle\alpha(\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\star},\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k})_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}+\|\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{k}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}\right),

These squares are then absorbed into the Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z})=\mathcal{E}(\bm{z})\pm\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}
. If
\alpha\leq\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
, where
L_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla F
, then
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z})
is a valid Lyapunov function, leading to accelerated convergence

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k}),\quad\text{ with }\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{L_{F}}}.

Accelerated convergence rate for the original Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})
can be derived afterwards.

Our framework is not restricted to smooth convex optimization but also extends to saddle-point systems. Specifically, we demonstrate the following cases:

1.

Strongly convex optimization
\min_{x}f(x)
:

\nabla F(x)=\nabla f(x)-\mu x,\quad N(y)=\mu y.

2.

Composite convex optimization
\min_{x}f(x)+g(x)
:

\nabla F(x)=\nabla f(x)-\mu x,\quad N(y)=\partial g(y)+\mu y.

3.

Saddle-point problem with bilinear coupling:

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{H}(u,p)=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu,p),

where
f
and
g
are strongly convex functions with constants
\mu_{f}>0
and
\mu_{g}>0
, and
B
is a bilinear coupling operator. The splitting framework provides the following decomposition:

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f(u)-\mu_{f}u\\
\nabla g(p)-\mu_{g}p\end{pmatrix}}_{\nabla F}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&B^{\intercal}\\
-B&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v\\
q\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{f}v\\
\mu_{g}q\end{pmatrix}}_{N}.

4.

When
\mathcal{N}:=N-\mu I
is linear and skew-symmetric, VOS leads to the Accelerated Gradient and Skew-Symmetric Splitting Methods (AGSS)
[
10
]
.

Previously we consider
\mu>0
. On the convex case, i.e.,
\mu=0
, we also propose two techniches. One is dynamically scaling a parameter
\gamma
that serves as a rescaling of time:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,\\
y^{\prime}&=-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\nabla F(x)+N(y)),\\
\gamma^{\prime}&=-\gamma.\end{aligned}\right.

The lack of convexity is compensated by the decay of
\gamma
in the Lyapunov function:

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Alternatively, we can use a perturbation of the flow:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,\\
y^{\prime}&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big(\underbrace{{\epsilon(x-y)}}_{\rm perturbation}-\nabla F(x)-N(y)\big).\end{aligned}\right.

with the corresponding Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

The convergence analysis is achieved through a perturbed strong Lyapunov property. Unlike traditional perturbation arguments
[
24
, Section 5.4]
,
[
35
]
, the variable splitting approach ensures that the perturbation (adding
\epsilon(x-y)
to the vector field) does not alter the equilibrium points.

The structure of this paper is as follows. Section
2
reviews fundamental properties of convex functions and establishes key bounds. Section
3
introduces strong Lyapunov functions and examines their role in convergence analysis. Section
4
studies the gradient flow and Euler methods. Section
5
presents variable and operator splitting (VOS) flow, while Section
6
describes advanced discretization schemes for VOS flow. Applications to convex and saddle point problems are discussed in Section
7
. Section
8
addresses the convex case.

Bounds on Convex Functions

This section gives a quick review of several bounds on convex functions
[
27
, Chapter 2]
. Let
V
be a Hilbert space with the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
and let the dual space be denoted by
V^{*}
with the bracket
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the dual paring between
V^{*}
and
V
. Throughout, both smooth convex functions over the entire space
V
and extended-value function
f:V\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
are considered. For the latter, the effective domain of
f
is denoted by
{\bf dom\,}f:=\{x\in V:f(x)<\infty\}
.

2.1
Convex functions

A continuous function
f
is called
convex
if

f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\leqslant\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y)\quad\forall\,\,x,y\in V,

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. It is called
strictly convex
if the above inequality holds strictly for
x\neq y
.
A convex function is called
\mu
-strongly convex
with parameter
\mu>0
if

f_{-\mu}(\cdot):=f(\cdot)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}

is convex. The notation
f_{-\mu}
can also be extended to
f_{+\mu}
.

The function
f
is
coercive
if
f(x)\to\infty
when
\left\lVert{x}\right\rVert\to\infty
. If
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu>0
, then it is not hard to see
f
is coercive. But convexity itself cannot imply the coercivity, e.g.
f(x)=e^{-x}
. The following results are classical, and proofs, which are skipped for the sake of brevity, can be found in
[
14
, Proposition 1.2]
or
[
12
, Theorem. 8.2.2]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
f
is convex and coercive, then
\min_{x\in V}f(x)
admits at least one solution
x^{\star}\in V
, which is unique if we assume further
f
is strictly convex.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{1}
consist of all continuous differentiable functions on
V
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{L}^{1,1}
the set of all
\mathcal{C}^{1}
functions, the gradients of which are Lipschitz continuous with constant
0<L<\infty
:

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{*}\leqslant L\|x-y\|\quad\forall\,x,y\in V,

where, for
g\in V^{*}
, the dual norm is

\|g\|_{*}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\langle g,v\rangle=\sup_{v\in V\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\langle g,v\rangle}{\left\lVert{v}\right\rVert}.

We now introduce several function classes of convex functions.
For
\mu>0
, we use

\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mu}
to denote the set of all
\mu
-strongly convex functions, and
\mathcal{S}^{0}_{0}
for convex functions, where the superscript
0
indicates the function is only continuous and may not be differentiable. Also, any
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{0}
is assumed to be closed (lower-semicontinuous) and proper (
{\bf dom\,}f\neq\emptyset
). Moreover, for all
\mu\geqslant 0
we set
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{1}:=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{0}\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}
.
For constants
0\leqslant\mu\leqslant L<\infty
, we introduce the function class

\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu,L}:=\{f\in\mathcal{S}_{0}^{1}:\mu\|x-y\|^{2}\leqslant\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\leqslant L\|x-y\|^{2}\,\forall\,x,y\in V\}.

Set
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1,1}=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{1}\cap\mathcal{C}_{L}^{1,1}
. It can be shown that
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1,1}=\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu,L}
; see
[
25
]
. The ratio
\kappa=L/\mu
is called the condition number of
f
.

Notice that the dual norm is metric related. Recall that
V
is a Hilbert space with the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
. Let
A
be a self-adjoint and positive definite operator defined on
V
. Then

(x,y)_{A}:=(Ax,y)

defines a new inner product and the induced norm is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{A}
. The dual norm with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{A}
will be
\|\cdot\|_{A^{-1}}
. The convexity and Lipschitz constants can be changed to

\mu_{A}\|x-y\|_{A}^{2}\leqslant\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\leqslant L_{A}\|x-y\|_{A}^{2}\quad\forall\,x,y\in V.

Choosing a correct inner product may reduce the condition number
\kappa_{A}=L_{A}/\mu_{A}
.

The product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{A}
can be defined for any symmetric operator
A
. Although it may not be positive, the identity of squares always holds

(x,y)_{A}=\frac{1}{2}\|x+y\|_{A}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{A}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|y\|_{A}^{2}\quad\forall\,x,y\in V.

2.2
Bregman divergence and various bounds

For
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
, define

D_{f}(y,x):=f(y)-f_{l}(y;x)=f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle,

where
f_{l}(y;x):=f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle
is its linear Taylor expansion at
x
. If
f
is convex, then
D_{f}(y,x)\geqslant 0
. When
f
is strictly convex,
D_{f}(y,x)=0
iff
x=y
, and
D_{f}(y,x)
is called the
Bregman divergence
associated with
f
.

By definition,
D_{f}
is linear with respect to
f
, i.e.
D_{\alpha f+\beta g}=\alpha D_{f}+\beta D_{g}
.
Treating
D_{f}(\cdot,x)
as a function of the first variable, we have

\nabla D_{f}(\cdot,x)=\nabla f(\cdot)-\nabla f(x).

The Bregman divergence of function
D_{f}(\cdot,x)
is

\displaystyle D_{D_{f}(\cdot,x)}(z,y)=D_{f}(z,x)-D_{f}(y,x)-\langle\nabla D_{f}(y,x),z-y\rangle.

By direct calculation, we get
D_{D_{f}}(z,y)=D_{f}(z,y)
, and thus obtain the identity

\langle\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x),z-y\rangle=D_{f}(z,x)-D_{f}(y,x)-D_{f}(z,y),

(7)

which is known as a three-point identity of Bregman divergence
[
8
]
.
When
f
is quadratic,
D_{f}(y,x)=D_{f}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}f}
. Identity (
7
) reduces to the identity of squares (
6
).
In general, Bregman divergence is not symmetric, i.e.,
D_{f}(y,x)\neq D_{f}(x,y)
.
We then introduce its symmetrization and call it the symmetrized Bregman divergence:

2M_{\nabla f}(x,y):=D_{f}(y,x)+D_{f}(x,y)=\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus

\displaystyle D_{f}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(x+t(y-x))-\nabla f(x)\,{\rm d}t,\,y-x}\right\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}2M_{\nabla f}(x_{t},x)\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t},\quad x_{t}:=x+t(y-x).

Based on (
8
), the bound for
D_{f}(y,x)
can be shifted to
M_{\nabla f}(x,y)
, and vice versa.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
f\in\mathcal{C}_{L}^{1,1}
, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\max\{D_{f}(y,x),M_{\nabla f}(x,y)\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{L}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(9)

For
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{1}
with
\mu\geqslant 0
, we have the (convexity) lower bound

\displaystyle\min\{D_{f}(y,x),M_{\nabla f}(x,y)\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(10)

All the above inequalities hold for all
x,y\in{\bf dom\,}f
.

Proof 2.3
.

The bound (
9
) for
M_{\nabla f}(x,y)
is a consequence of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Then apply it in (
8
) and integrate
\|x_{t}-x\|^{2}=t^{2}\|y-x\|
to get bound for
D_{f}(x,y)
. The bound (
10
) for
D_{f}(x,y)
is from the fact
D_{f_{-\mu}}(y,x)=D_{f}(y,x)-\mu\|y-x\|^{2}/2\geq 0
as
f_{-\mu}
is convex. Summing the bound for
D_{f}(y,x)
and
D_{f}(x,y)
to obtain the lower bound of
M_{\nabla f}(x,y)
.

\Box

2.3
Conjugate function

For any convex and continuous function
f
, we introduce its
conjugate function
or
dual
as follows

f^{*}(\chi):=\sup\limits_{x\in V}\left\{\left\langle{\chi,x}\right\rangle-f(x)\right\}\quad\forall\,\chi\in V^{*}.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1,1}_{\mu,L}
, we can show
f^{*}\in\mathcal{S}^{1,1}_{1/L,1/\mu}

[
4
, Theorem 5.26]
and the duality of the variables
(x,\chi)

\nabla f^{*}(\chi)=x,\quad\nabla f(x)=\chi.

Then, for
x=\nabla f^{*}(\chi),y=\nabla f^{*}(\eta)
,

\left\langle{\nabla f^{*}(\chi)-\nabla f^{*}(\eta),\chi-\eta}\right\rangle=\left\langle{x-y,\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}\right\rangle,

which can be simply written as

{}M_{\nabla f^{*}}(\chi,\eta)=M_{\nabla f}(x,y).

(11)

By direct calculations, we have the relation, for
x=\nabla f^{*}(\chi),y=\nabla f^{*}(\eta)
,

D_{f}(y,x)=D_{f^{*}}(\chi,\eta).

(12)

Notice that the order of variables are switched in (
12
).

We apply the bounds in Lemma
2.2
to
f^{*}\in\mathcal{S}^{1,1}_{1/L,1/\mu}
and switch back to
f
by the identity (
12
) and (
11
) to obtain bounds in terms of the difference of gradients.

Lemma 2.4
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{0,L}
, we have the (co-convexity) lower bound

\displaystyle\min\{D_{f}(y,x),M_{\nabla f}(x,y)\}{}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.

(13)

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu}
with
\mu>0
, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\max\{D_{f}(y,x),M_{\nabla f}(x,y)\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.

All the above inequalities hold for all
x,y\in{\bf dom\,}f
.

Next we prove a refined lower bound.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu,L}
with
\mu\geqslant 0
, we have, for all
x,y\in{\bf dom\,}f
,

\left\langle{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y}\right\rangle\geqslant\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}\|x-y\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu+L}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{*}^{2}.

(14)

Proof 2.6
.

The case
L=\mu
is a simple convex combination of (
10
) and (
13
) with appropriate weights. For
L>\mu
, we apply the co-convexity (
13
) to
f_{-\mu}(x)=f(x)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^{2}
:

\left\langle{\nabla f_{-\mu}(x)-\nabla f_{-\mu}(y),x-y}\right\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{L-\mu}\left\lVert{\nabla f_{-\mu}(x)-\nabla f_{-\mu}(y)}\right\rVert^{2}.

(15)

Expand (
15
) in terms of quantity of
f
to get the desired result (
14
).

\Box

2.4
Bounds involving a global minimum

We list specific examples of inequalities when one variable is
x^{\star}
for which
\nabla f(x^{\star})=0
. Then
D_{f}(x,x^{\star})=f(x)-f(x^{\star})
is the so-called optimality gap and
2M_{\nabla f}(x,x^{\star})=\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle
.

Corollary 2.7
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{0,L}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\leqslant f(x)-f(x^{\star})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{L}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\leqslant\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant L\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu}
with
\mu>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant f(x)-f(x^{\star})\leqslant\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle\mu\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2},

(17)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

(18)

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu,L}
with
\mu\geqslant 0
, we have

\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle\geqslant\frac{\mu L}{\mu+L}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu+L}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.

(19)

All the above inequalities hold for all
x\in{\bf dom\,}f
.

We extend those bounds to the non-smooth convex functions. Recall that the sub-gradient
\partial f
of a proper and convex function
f
is a set-valued function and can be defined as follows

\partial f(x):=\left\{p\in V^{*}:\,f(y)-f(x)\geqslant\left\langle{p,y-x}\right\rangle\quad\forall\,y\in V\right\}.

Any
p\in\partial f(x)
will be also called a
sub-gradient
of
f
at
x
.

Corollary 2.8
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\mu}
with
\mu>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant f(x)-f(x^{\star})\leqslant\frac{1}{2\mu}\|p\|_{*}^{2},

\displaystyle\mu\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\langle{p,x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu}\|p\|_{*}^{2},

\displaystyle\left\langle{p,x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},

where
p\in\partial f(x)
and
x\in{\bf dom\,}f
.

3
Strong Lyapunov Functions

In this section, we consider the dynamical system defined by the ODE

x^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{G}(x(t)),\quad t>0,

(20)

where
x:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to V
and
\mathcal{G}:V\to V^{*}
is a vector field. Let
x^{\star}
denote an equilibrium point of the dynamical system (
20
), i.e.,
\mathcal{G}(x^{\star})=0
. Our primary interest lies in analyzing the convergence of
x(t)
to
x^{\star}
as
t\to\infty
.

3.1
Strong Lyapunov condition and decay property

The Lyapunov function was originally introduced to study the stability of equilibrium points. To analyze convergence rates, however, we require a stronger condition than
-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle\geq 0
.

Specifically, suppose there exists a compact subset
W\subseteq V
, a positive constant
c>0
, a constant
q\geqslant 1
, and a function
p(x):V\to\mathbb{R}
, such that
\mathcal{E}(x)\geqslant 0
and

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle\geqslant c\,\mathcal{E}^{q}(x)+p^{2}(x)\quad\forall\,x\in W.

(21)

Under these conditions, we call
\mathcal{E}
a locally strong Lyapunov function if
W\subset V
or a globally strong Lyapunov function if
W=V
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{E}(x)
satisfies the strong Lyapunov property (
21
). If the trajectory
x(t)
of (
20
) satisfies
\{x(t):t\geqslant 0\}\subset W
, then for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathcal{E}(x(t))+\int_{0}^{t}e^{c(s-t)}\|p(x(s))\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leqslant\mathcal{E}_{0}\exp(-c\,t),\quad\text{ for }q=1.

(22)

and

\mathcal{E}(x(t))\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{(q-1)c\,t+\mathcal{E}_{0}^{1-q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q-1}},\quad\text{ for }q>1,

(23)

where
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{E}(x(0))
.

Proof 3.2
.

By assumption, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(x(t))&=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x(t)),x^{\prime}(t)\rangle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x(t)),\mathcal{G}(x(t))\rangle\\
&\leqslant-c\,\mathcal{E}^{q}(x(t))-\|p(x(t))\|^{2}\end{split}

(24)

For the case
q=1
, integrating (
24
) immediately gives the desired result.

Now consider
q>1
. From (
24
), we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}^{1-q}(x(t))=(1-q)\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x(t))}{\mathcal{E}^{q}(x(t))}\geqslant c(q-1).

Integrating this inequality yields

\mathcal{E}^{1-q}(x(t))-\mathcal{E}^{1-q}(x(0))\geqslant c(q-1)t,\quad t\geqslant 0.

Rearranging gives

\mathcal{E}(x(t))\leqslant\left((q-1)c\,t+\mathcal{E}^{1-q}_{0}\right)^{1/(1-q)},

which completes the proof.

\Box

Generally, the field
\mathcal{G}
can be a set-valued mapping, which occurs when
f
is convex but non-smooth. This scenario leads to the differential inclusion:

x^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{G}(x(t)),\quad t>0.

(25)

To emphasize the dependence of the sub-gradient
\partial f
, we refine the notation
\mathcal{G}(x)
to
\mathcal{G}(x,d(x))
, where
d(x)\in\partial f(x)
represents a particular direction. Under this notation, (
25
) can be expressed as

x^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{G}(x(t),d(x(t))).

Similarly, a Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x)
may not be smooth. For a specific direction
d\in\partial f(x)
,
\partial\mathcal{E}(x,d)
is assumed to be a single-valued vector function.

The strong Lyapunov condition can be extended to the non-smooth setting as follows:

-\langle\partial\mathcal{E}(x,d),\mathcal{G}(x,d)\rangle\geqslant c\mathcal{E}^{q}(x)+p^{2}(x),\quad\forall x\in W.

(26)

When verifying the strong Lyapunov property (
26
) for non-smooth functions, it suffices to identify a single sub-gradient
d\in\partial f(x)
that satisfies the inequality.

In most cases, we consider the setting where
W=V
and
q=1
.

3.2
Linear convergence of the implicit Euler method

The strong Lyapunov property ensures the straightforward establishment of the linear convergence of the implicit Euler method for solving
x^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}(x)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume
\mathcal{E}(x)
is a convex Lyapunov function satisfying the strong Lyapunov property for some
c>0
:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle\geqslant c\,\mathcal{E}(x),\quad\forall x\in V.

Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by the implicit Euler method, starting from a given
x_{0}
, for
k=0,1,\ldots

x_{k+1}-x_{k}=\alpha_{k}\mathcal{G}(x_{k+1}).

Then, for
k\geq 0
, the sequence satisfies the linear contraction:

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+c\,\alpha_{k}}\mathcal{E}(x_{k}).

Proof 3.4
.

For brevity, denote
\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}(x_{k})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{ (convexity of }\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(x_{k+1})\rangle

\displaystyle\text{ (implicit Euler method})

\displaystyle\leq-c\,\alpha_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}.

\displaystyle\text{(strong Lyapunov property at }x_{k+1})

Rearranging terms to get the desired linear convergence.
\Box

4
Gradient Flow and Euler Methods

In this section, we study the gradient flow and its connection to gradient descent methods. Convergence analysis is derived based on the strong Lyapunov property for various Lyapunov functions.

4.1
Gradient Flow

The simplest dynamical system associated with the unconstrained minimization problem

\min_{x\in V}f(x)

is the gradient flow:

x^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla f(x(t)),

(27)

with the initial condition
x(0)=x_{0}
. Hence
\mathcal{G}(x)=-\nabla f(x)
for the gradient flow.

4.1.1
Strongly convex case

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{1}
with
\mu>0
and let
x^{\star}
be the global minimizer of
f
.
A natural choice for the Lyapunov function is the so-called optimality gap:

\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star}).

We verify the strong Lyapunov property using (
16
):

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle=\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\geq\mu\,\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.

Notice we split the
\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}
to have an additional positive term
p^{2}=\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}/2
.

We can consider alternative candidates for strong Lyapunov functions besides the optimality gap. Two notable examples are presented below. One is the squared distance to the minimizer:

\mathcal{E}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

If
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1}
with
0<\mu\leq L<\infty
, then using (
19
), we obtain:

\begin{split}-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle&=\langle x-x^{\star},\nabla f(x)\rangle\geq\frac{2\mu L}{L+\mu}\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{L+\mu}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.\end{split}

Another effective candidate is a combination of the optimality gap and squared distance:

\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

By (
18
), the following strong Lyapunov property holds:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle=\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}+\mu\langle x-x^{\star},\nabla f(x)\rangle\geq\mu\mathcal{E}(x)+\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.

We summarize the results in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1}
with
0<\mu\leq L\leq\infty
. For the gradient flow
x^{\prime}(t)=-\nabla f(x(t))
, we have the following strong Lyapunov functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)

\displaystyle=f(x)-f(x^{\star}),\quad-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle\geq\mu\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2},

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},\quad-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle\geq\frac{2\mu L}{L+\mu}\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{L+\mu}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2},

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)

\displaystyle=f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},\quad-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle\geq\mu\mathcal{E}(x)+\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}.

(30)

Consequently Theorem
3.1
guarantees the exponential decay
\mathcal{O}(e^{-ct})
for both
\mathcal{E}(x)
and
\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}
along the trajectory of the gradient flow (
27
).

When verifying the strong Lyapunov property, we retain an extra positive term
p^{2}=C\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}
, which is useful for analyzing the gradient descent method.

4.1.2
Convex case

When
\mu=0
, the strong Lyapunov properties mentioned earlier degenerate. Define the sub-level set of
f
for a given constant value
c
as:

S_{c}(f)=\{x\colon f(x)\leq c\}.

Since
f
is convex, the sub-level set
S_{c}(f)
is also convex. The set of minimizers of
f
, where it attains its minimum value
f_{\min}
, can be expressed as
S_{f_{\min}}(f)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f
be convex and coercive. For a given finite value
f_{0}
, there exists a constant
R_{0}
such that

\max_{x^{\star}\in{\mathrm{argmin}\,}f}\max_{x\in S_{f_{0}}}\|x-x^{\star}\|\leq R_{0}.

(31)

Proof 4.3
.

Since
f
is coercive, we claim that
S_{f_{0}}
is bounded. If
S_{f_{0}}
were unbounded, we could construct a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
such that
f(x_{n})\leq f_{0}
but
\|x_{n}\|>n
for
n=1,2,\ldots
, which would contradict the coercivity of
f
.

As
{\mathrm{argmin}\,}f\subseteq S_{f_{0}}
,
{\mathrm{argmin}\,}f
is also bounded and convex. Thus, (
31
) holds.

\Box

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
f
be convex and coercive. For
\mathcal{G}(x)=-\nabla f(x)
, we have the following strong Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})
, where
x^{\star}
is an arbitrary but fixed point in the minimum set
{\mathrm{argmin}\,}f
,

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle=\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{2}(x)\quad\forall x\in S_{f_{0}}(f),

(32)

where
R_{0}
is defined by (
31
) and
f_{0}=f(x_{0})
. Consequently the trajectory of the gradient flow
x(t)
satisfies

f(x(t))-f(x^{\star})\leq\frac{R_{0}^{2}}{t+R_{0}^{2}/\mathcal{E}(x(0))},\quad\forall t>0.

(33)

Proof 4.5
.

For
\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})
assuming coercivity and convexity of
f
, we have

f(x)-f(x^{\star})\leq\langle\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}\rangle\leq R_{0}\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}\quad\forall x\in S_{f_{0}}(f),

(34)

which is equivalent to the strong Lyapunov property (
32
).

Since
-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x),\mathcal{G}(x)\rangle=\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\geq 0
, the trajectory of the gradient flow
x(t)
satisfies
x(t)\in S_{f_{0}}(f)
. Applying Theorem
3.1
, we conclude that the optimality gap
f(x(t))-f(x^{\star})
decays at the sublinear rate
O(1/t)
along the trajectory of the gradient flow.

\Box

4.2
Proximal Point Algorithm

When
\mathcal{G}(x)=-\nabla f(x)
, the implicit Euler method applied to the gradient flow:

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha\nabla f(x_{k+1})

(35)

can be reformulated as:

x_{k+1}=\operatorname{prox}_{\alpha f}(x_{k}):=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{x}\left\{f(x)+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\},

(36)

which is known as the proximal point algorithm (PPA)
[
31
]
.

With the strong Lyapunov property, the convergence of the proximal point algorithm is straightforward by combining Theorem
3.3
and Proposition
4.1
for
\mu
-strongly convex case. Even
\mu=0
, linear convergence of PPA can be still obtained with a refined Lyapunov function; see Theorem
8.1
.

4.3
Gradient Descent Methods

Next, we present the convergence analysis for the explicit Euler method applied to the gradient flow, which corresponds to the gradient descent method.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1,1}
with
0<\mu\leq L<\infty
. Let
\{x_{k}\}
be the sequence generated by

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}).

(37)

For
\alpha_{k}\leq 2/(L+\mu)
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq(1-\mu\alpha_{k})\mathcal{E}_{k},

where
\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}(x_{k})
.

The optimal value
\alpha_{k}=2/(L+\mu)
gives

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq\frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}\,\mathcal{E}_{k},

and the quasi-optimal value
\alpha_{k}=1/L
gives

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leq(1-\mu/L)\mathcal{E}_{k}.

Proof 4.7
.

We have verified the strong Lyapunov property in (
30
) with an additional term
\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}
. Note that
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{2\mu,L+\mu}
. Using the definition of Bregman divergence, the upper bound of
D_{\mathcal{E}}
, and the strong Lyapunov condition at
x_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+D_{\mathcal{E}}(x_{k+1},x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x_{k}),\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle+\frac{L+\mu}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\mu\,\alpha_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k}-\alpha_{k}\left(1-\frac{L+\mu}{2}\alpha_{k}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|_{*}^{2}.

For
\alpha_{k}\leq 2/(L+\mu)
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq-\mu\,\alpha_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k}
, and the linear convergence follows.

\Box

One can also choose

\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})\quad\text{or}\quad\mathcal{E}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2},

and prove the linear convergence of the gradient descent method using the strong Lyapunov property. Here, we use
\mathcal{E}(x)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})
to show a sufficient decay property of the function values.

Proposition 4.8
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1,1}
with
0\leq\mu\leq L<\infty
.
For the gradient descent method with
\alpha=1/L
:

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(x_{k}),

we have the function decay estimate

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq-\frac{1}{2L}\left(\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|^{2}+\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}\right).

(38)

Consequently, for
\mu>0
, we have the linear convergence

f(x_{k+1})-f(x^{\star})\leq\frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}\big(f(x_{k})-f(x^{\star})\big).

(39)

For
\mu=0
, we have the sub-linear convergence

f(x_{k})-f(x^{\star})\leq\frac{LR_{0}^{2}}{k+LR_{0}^{2}/\mathcal{E}(x_{0})}.

(40)

Proof 4.9
.

Using the Bregman divergence and the identity of squares, we expand the difference:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle-D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=-\alpha\langle\nabla f(x_{k+1}),\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle-D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|_{*}^{2}+\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|_{*}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_{k})\|_{*}^{2}-D_{f}(x_{k},x_{k+1}).

Then, using the co-coercivity property (
13
) for
\alpha=1/L
, we cancel the positive term, obtaining (
38
).

For
\mu>0
, we use the strong Lyapunov property
\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\geq 2\mu\mathcal{E}(x)
, cf. (
16
) to derive (
39
).

For
\mu=0
, we use the strong Lyapunov property (
32
)
\|\nabla f(x)\|_{*}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{R_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{2}(x)
and rewrite the inequality as

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq-\frac{1}{2LR_{0}^{2}}(\mathcal{E}^{2}_{k+1}+\mathcal{E}^{2}_{k})\leq-\frac{1}{LR_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\mathcal{E}_{k},

which is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{k+1}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{LR_{0}^{2}}.

(41)

Summing (
41
) leads to (
40
).

\Box

5
Variable and Operator Splitting

In this section we combine variable splitting, where we introduce a new variable
y
that converges to
x
, with operator splitting, where we decompose
\mathcal{L}(x)
into
\nabla F(x)+N(y)
, to study accelerated gradient methods. We then apply to strongly convex optimization, composite convex optimization, and saddle point problems with bilinear coupling.

5.1
Flow for variable and operator splitting

Consider the operator equation

\mathcal{L}(x)=\nabla F(x)+N(x)=0,

where
F
is convex, and
N
is
\mu
-strongly monotone:

\big(N(x)-N(y),x-y\big)\geq\mu\|x-y\|^{2}.

(42)

If instead
F
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
N
is monotone only, we can set

F(x)\leftarrow F(x)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^{2},\quad N(x)\leftarrow N(x)+\mu x.

This transformation moves the
\mu
-convexity from
F
to
N
so that the conditions required are satisfied. We will deal with the convex case
\mu=0
in Section
8
.

Besides operator splitting, we introduce a variable splitting technique. This method introduces a new variable
y
, which converges to
x
but remains distinct as a separate variable. In the operator splitting, one operator
\nabla F(x)
is associated with
x
, while the other,
N(y)
, is associated with
y
. This splitting leads to the following flow dynamics, referred to as the Variable and Operator Splitting (VOS) flow:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,&&\text{(variable splitting)}\\
y^{\prime}&=-\frac{1}{\mu}\big(\nabla F(x)+N(y)\big).&&\text{(operator splitting)}\end{aligned}\right.

(43)

The equilibrium point satisfies
x=y=x^{\star}
and
\mathcal{L}(x^{\star})=\nabla F(x^{\star})+N(x^{\star})=0
.

Define
\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\big(y-x,-\frac{1}{\mu}\big(\nabla F(x)+N(y)\big)\big)^{\intercal}
. Then,
(x^{\star},x^{\star})
is an equilibrium point of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}(x^{\star},x^{\star})=0.

Therefore we can rewrite
\mathcal{G}(x,y)
as follows, without explicitly requiring knowledge of
x^{\star}
:

\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}y-x^{\star}-(x-x^{\star})\\
-\mu^{-1}\big(\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star})\big)-\mu^{-1}\big(N(y)-N(x^{\star})\big)\end{pmatrix}.

5.2
Strong Lyapunov Functions

To analyze the stability and convergence of the system (
43
), we introduce the Lyapunov function:

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2},

(44)

where
D_{F}(x,x^{\star})
is the Bregman divergence associated with the convex function
F
.

To illustrate, consider the linear case where
\nabla D_{F}(x,x^{\star})=A(x-x^{\star})
with
0\leq\lambda(A)\leq L_{F}
and
N
is a matrix with
y^{\intercal}(\sym N)y\geq\mu\|y\|^{2}
. To verify the strong Lyapunov property, we calculate:

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}x-x^{\star}\\
y-x^{\star}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\mu I\end{pmatrix}}_{\nabla\mathcal{E}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
\mu^{-1}A&\mu^{-1}N\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x-x^{\star}\\
y-x^{\star}\end{pmatrix}}_{-\mathcal{G}}=\bm{e}^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}A&-A\\
A&N\end{pmatrix}\bm{e},

where
\bm{e}=(x-x^{\star},y-x^{\star})^{\intercal}
.
For a quadratic form,
\bm{e}^{\intercal}M\bm{e}=\bm{e}^{\intercal}{\rm sym}(M)\bm{e}
. So its symmetric part is:

\displaystyle\bm{e}^{\intercal}\mathrm{sym}\begin{pmatrix}A&-A\\
A&N\end{pmatrix}\bm{e}=\bm{e}^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\mathrm{sym}N\end{pmatrix}\bm{e}\geq\bm{e}^{\intercal}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\mu I\end{pmatrix}\bm{e}=2\mathcal{E}.

The linear case can be helpful for the design of a new system and guide the verification of the strong Lyapunov condition for the nonlinear case.

We will use a linear example to explain the acceleration through the change in the spectral radius. Consider
N=\mu I
and write
A=A+\mu I-\mu I=A_{+\mu}-\mu I
. Then the vector field is defined by the following matrix

G=\begin{pmatrix}-I&I\\
I-A_{+\mu}/\mu&-I\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\kappa=\kappa(A_{+\mu})\geq 1
. The spectral radius of matrix
G
will be given by that of the following matrix

R=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
1-\kappa&-1\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalue
\lambda
of
R
satisfies the equation

\lambda^{2}-(\operatorname{tr}R)\,\lambda+\det R=0.

Since
\operatorname{tr}R^{2}\leqslant 4\det R
, the eigenvalues come in complex conjugate pairs. For complex eigenvalues,
\lambda\bar{\lambda}=|\lambda|^{2}=\det R=\kappa
and
\lambda+\bar{\lambda}=2\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda)=\operatorname{tr}R
. Thus,

\lambda=\sqrt{\det R}=\sqrt{\kappa},\quad\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda)=\operatorname{tr}R/2<0.

The negative real part ensures the exponential decay. The spectral radius of
G
is now reduced to
\sqrt{\kappa}
.

We are aware of the limitations of the spectral analysis for the linear system near
x^{\star}
. For nonlinear ODE systems, transient growth or instability in perturbed problems can easily lead to nonlinear instabilities. This is why we employ Lyapunov analysis and the strong Lyapunov property.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the Lyapunov function (
44
), and let
\mathcal{G}
be the vector field of the VOS flow (
43
). The following strong Lyapunov property holds:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle\geq\mathcal{E}(x,y)+D_{F}(x^{\star},x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2},

(45)

which ensures the exponential decay of the Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}e^{-t}
.

Proof 5.2
.

By direct calculation, the cross term
\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle
cancels due to the sign change. Therefore,

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle={}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle+\langle y-x^{\star},N(y)-N(x^{\star})\rangle

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x,y)+D_{F}(x^{\star},x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

This completes the proof.
\Box

Notice that the Lyapunov function does not enter the flow. For the same vector field
\mathcal{G}
, we may design other Lyapunov functions. For example, consider

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{f}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2},

(46)

where
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}^{1}
satisfies

\nabla f(x)=\nabla F(x)+\mu x,\quad\iff\nabla F(x)=\nabla f(x)-\mu x.

We have the following strong Lyapunov property.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the Lyapunov function (
46
), and let
\mathcal{G}=\big(y-x,-\mu^{-1}\big(\nabla f(x)-\mu x+N(y)\big)\big)^{\intercal}
be the vector field of the VOS flow (
43
). The following strong Lyapunov property holds:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle\geq\mathcal{E}(x,y)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},

(47)

which ensures the exponential decay of the Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}(x,y)\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}e^{-t}
and the exponential decay of variable difference
\|y-x\|^{2}\leq C_{0}e^{-t}
.

Proof 5.4
.

The cross term
\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle
is canceled as before, but this time additional cross terms arise:

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle={}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle+\langle y-x^{\star},N(y)-N(x^{\star})\rangle

\displaystyle-\mu\langle y-x^{\star},x-x^{\star}\rangle.

Using the identity of squares, the additional cross term expands as:

-\mu\langle y-x^{\star},x-x^{\star}\rangle=-\frac{\mu}{2}\left(\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\|x-y\|^{2}\right).

The symmetric Bregman divergence is bounded below as:

\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle\geq D_{f}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Finally, the
\mu
-monotonicity of
N
ensures that
-\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}
cancels out. This yields the desired inequality (
47
).

\Box

Again the calculation is more clear when
\nabla f(x)=Ax
is linear and
N
is also linear. Then
-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle
is a quadratic form
\bm{e}^{\intercal}M\bm{e}=\bm{e}^{\intercal}(\sym M)\bm{e}
with
\bm{e}=(x-x^{\star},y-x^{\star})^{\intercal}
. We calculate the matrix
M
as follows

\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\mu I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
-I+\mu^{-1}A&\quad\mu^{-1}N\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A&-A\\
-\mu I+A&\;N\end{pmatrix}.

Its symmetric part is

\displaystyle\sym\begin{pmatrix}A&-A\\
-\mu I+A&\;N\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A&-\mu I/2\\
-\mu I/2&\sym N\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\;\mu I\end{pmatrix}+\frac{\mu}{2}\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\
-I&\;I\end{pmatrix},

where in the last step we use the convexity
A\geq\mu I
and
\sym N\geq\mu I
. For two symmetric matrices
A
and
M
,
A\geq M
if
x^{\intercal}(A-M)x\geq 0
for all
x
. Then (
47
) follows.

5.3
Examples

We present several examples arising in optimization problems including strongly convex optimization, composite convex optimization, and saddle point problems with bilinear coupling.

5.3.1
Strongly Convex Optimization

Strongly convex optimization problems arise in many practical settings, including machine learning, control systems, and signal processing
[
6
,
7
]
. For the strongly convex optimization problem

\min_{x\in V}f(x)\quad\text{with }f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu},

the VOS flow can be expressed as:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y-x,

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}(\underbrace{\nabla f(x)-\mu\,x}_{\nabla F(x)}+\underbrace{\mu\,y}_{N(y)}).

Eliminating
y
will lead to a Heavy-Ball (HB) momentum flow:

x^{\prime\prime}+2x^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x)=0.

(48)

Remark 5.5
.

As
x
and
y
represent the same variable, we can add more terms involving the difference
y-x
which will not affect the equilibrium point but may accelerate the flow. For example, a generalized VOS flow is

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y-x,

(49)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla f(x)-\mu\,x+\mu\,y+\theta\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x)\right),

with
\theta\in(0,\mu/L)
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the vector field of (
49
) and the Lyapunov function
\mathcal{E}
be defined by (
44
). The following strong Lyapunov property can be proved

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle\geq(1-\theta L/\mu)\mathcal{E}(x,y)+\frac{\theta\mu}{2}\left\lVert{y-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\mu(1+\theta)}{2}\left\lVert{y-x}\right\rVert^{2}.

(50)

\Box

5.3.2
Composite Convex Optimization

Composite convex optimization problems are widely encountered in scenarios where a regularization term is added to improve generalization or enforce certain properties (e.g., sparsity
[
38
,
40
]
). For a composite optimization problem

\min_{x\in V}f(x)+g(x),

where
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, and
g
is a convex but may be non-smooth function. Here we write the flow for smooth
g
:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=y-x,

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}(\underbrace{\nabla f(x)-\mu x}_{\nabla F(x)}+\underbrace{\mu y+\nabla g(y)}_{N(y)}).

This framework allows the incorporation of nonsmooth
g(x)
, which we will address in Section 6.5 with appropriate notation adjustments.

5.3.3
Saddle Point Systems with Bilinear Coupling

Saddle point problems with bilinear coupling frequently appear in game theory, optimization with constraints, and large-scale machine learning, such as adversarial training
[
13
]
or Wasserstein distance computation
[
15
]
. Here we consider the saddle point system with bilinear coupling:

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{H}(u,p)=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu,p),

where
f
and
g
are strongly convex functions with constants
\mu_{f}>0
and
\mu_{g}>0
, and
B
is a matrix. Denoted by
B^{\intercal}
its transpose.

The splitting framework provides the following decomposition:

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{u}\mathcal{H}(u,p)\\
-\partial_{p}\mathcal{H}(u,p)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f&B^{\intercal}\\
-B&\nabla g\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f&0\\
0&\nabla g\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix}}_{\nabla F_{+\mu}}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&B^{\intercal}\\
-B&0\end{pmatrix}}_{N_{-\mu}}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\bm{x}=(u,p)^{\intercal},\bm{y}=(v,q)^{\intercal}
, and
\bm{\mu}={\rm diag}\{\mu_{f}\,I,\mu_{g}\,I\}
. Then we have the flow:

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bm{y}-\bm{x},

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}-\bm{y}-{\bm{\mu}}^{-1}(\nabla F_{+\mu}(\bm{x})+N_{-\mu}(\bm{y})).

In its component form, with separated constants
\mu_{f}
and
\mu_{g}
, the flow becomes:

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v-u,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=u-v-\frac{1}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla f(u)+B^{\intercal}q),

\displaystyle p^{\prime}

\displaystyle=q-p,

\displaystyle q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p-q-\frac{1}{\mu_{g}}(\nabla g(p)-Bv).

We shall study discretizations of these flows, which lead to accelerated gradient methods, and provide convergence analyses to ensure accelerated rates in the next section.

6
Accelerated Gradient Methods

We shall derive accelerated gradient methods based on several discretizations of the Variable and Operator Splitting (VOS) flow (
43
). As mentioned earlier, with the strong Lyapunov property, the linear convergence of the implicit Euler method is straightforward. We will use the implicit Euler method as a reference and propose several discretization techniques to control the discrepancy.

6.1
Accelerated Over-Relaxation

We propose an implicit-explicit discretization (IMEX) with an accelerated over-relaxation (AOR) technique:

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2y_{k+1}-y_{k}-x_{k+1},

(51a)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}(\nabla F(x_{k})+N(y_{k+1})).

(51b)

The operator
N
is treated implicitly, and
y
is updated first by solving (
51b
), and then
x
using (
51a
). The AOR technique is applied in (
51a
) such that
y\approx 2y_{k+1}-y_{k}
.

The Lyapunov function is given by

\mathcal{E}(x,y)=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

(52)

The strong Lyapunov property (
45
) has been established in Theorem
5.1
.

A sharp convergence analysis requires the modified Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(x,y)-\alpha\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle,

(53)

where the cross term
\alpha\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle
is included. To simplify notation, introduce
\bm{z}=(x,y)^{\intercal}
and
\bm{z}^{\star}=(x^{\star},x^{\star})^{\intercal}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume
F
is convex and
L_{F}
smooth. For any two points
(x,y)
,
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
, and
\mu>0
, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle|\langle\hat{y}-y,\nabla F(\hat{x})-\nabla F(x)\rangle|\leq~\sqrt{\frac{L_{F}}{\mu}}\left(\min\{D_{F}(x,\hat{x}),D_{F}(\hat{x},x)\}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-\hat{y}\|^{2}\right).

Proof 6.2
.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Young’s inequality, and the co-convexity bound (
13
) for
F\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{0,L_{F}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\langle\hat{y}-y,\nabla F(\hat{x})-\nabla F(x)\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu L_{F}}}\|\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(\hat{x})\|_{*}^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{L_{F}\mu}}{2}\|y-\hat{y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{L_{F}}{\mu}}\left(\min\{D_{F}(x,\hat{x}),D_{F}(\hat{x},x)\}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-\hat{y}\|^{2}\right).

\Box

We use the linear case to illustrate the convergence proof. Denote

\mathcal{D}=\begin{pmatrix}A&\;0\\
0&\;\mu I\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{A}^{\sym}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\;A\\
A&\;0\end{pmatrix}.

The Lyapunov functions are in the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}-\alpha\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}.

The AOR is introduced to symmetrize the cross term in

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle=\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle+\alpha\langle\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\star},\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}.

With this symmetrization, we can use the identity of squares:

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\star},\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}+\|\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}-\|\bm{z}_{k}-\bm{z}^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2}\right).

These squared terms can be absorbed into
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
, resulting in the identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k})=

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\|_{\mathcal{D}-\alpha\mathcal{A}^{\sym}}^{2},

(54)

which holds for any
\alpha
. Finally, Lemma
6.1
is used to control the step size and strong Lyapunov property is used to obtain the linear convergence.

Theorem 6.3

(Convergence of AOR-VOS method)
.

Suppose
F
is convex and
L_{F}
-smooth, and
N
is
\mu
-strongly monotone. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by scheme (
51
) with the initial value
(x_{0},y_{0})
and step size
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
. Then,
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y)
serves as a Lyapunov function, and the method achieves the accelerated linear convergence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad k\geq 0,

and

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leq\sqrt{\frac{L_{F}}{\mu}}\left(\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad k\geq 0.

Proof 6.4
.

We rewrite (
51
) as a corrected implicit Euler discretization of (
43
):

\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}=\alpha\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})+\alpha\begin{pmatrix}y_{k+1}-y_{k}\\
\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Substituting this expression into the difference equation for
\mathcal{E}
gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})

(55)

\displaystyle=\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\begin{pmatrix}y_{k+1}-y_{k}\\
\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\right)\end{pmatrix}\rangle.

Expanding the last term in the component form, we obtain:

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle+\alpha\langle y_{k+1}-x^{\star},\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\rangle+\alpha\langle y_{k+1}-y_{k},\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle

\displaystyle-\alpha\langle y_{k}-x^{\star},\nabla F(x_{k})-\nabla F(x^{\star})\rangle.

Substituting this back into (
55
) and rearranging terms, we obtain the identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k})=

\displaystyle\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle

(56)

\displaystyle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})+\alpha\langle y_{k+1}-y_{k},\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})\rangle.

We express the Bregman divergence term as:

D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})=D_{F}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k}-y_{k+1}\|^{2}.

By Lemma
6.1
, the second line of (
56
) is negative for
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
which can be then dropped in the following inequality. Applying the strong Lyapunov property (
45
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha\left(\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})+D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k+1})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha\,\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1}).

The extra positive terms
D_{F}(x^{\star},x_{k+1})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
from the strong Lyapunov property (
45
) bounds the cross terms (by Lemma
6.1
) and facilitates switching from
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})
to
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})
. The step size
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
ensures
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z})\geq 0
.

Hence, the global linear convergence of
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}
follows:

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{0}),\quad k\geq 0.

Moreover, (
57
) implies:

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k})-\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\right)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{0}).

\Box

Remark 6.5
.

Another discretization of (
43
) givens the AOR-HB method introduced recently in
[
37
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=y_{k}-x_{k+1},

(58a)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}(2\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x_{k})+N(y_{k+1})).

(58b)

The scheme is symmetric to (
51
), and the convergence analysis is identical up to a sign change in
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}(x,y)+\alpha\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle
.

\Box

6.2
Extrapolation by Predictor-Corrector Methods

We start from the IMEX scheme, where
x_{k+1}
is computed first and then used to update
y_{k+1}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k}-x_{k+1},\\
\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}&=-\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla F(x_{k+1})+N(y_{k+1})\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(59)

Lemma 6.6
.

For the scheme (
59
) and the Lyapunov function (
52
), the following inequality holds:

(1+\alpha)\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\leqslant\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})-\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}-\alpha\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle.

Proof 6.7
.

Again we begin by expressing the iteration as a correction to the implicit Euler method:

\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}=\alpha\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\alpha\begin{pmatrix}y_{k+1}-y_{k}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Substituting this into the difference equation for
\mathcal{E}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\alpha\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1}),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle.

Using the strong Lyapunov property and part of
D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{z}_{k+1})
, i.e.
\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}
, we obtain:

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})\leqslant-\alpha\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}-\alpha\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle.

Rearranging terms gives the desired result.

\Box

The cross term
\alpha\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle
is challenging to control. We consider the predictor-corrector method for ODE solvers
[
16
, Section 6.2.3]
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k}-\tilde{x}_{k+1},\\
\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}&=-\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\nabla F(\tilde{x}_{k+1})+N(y_{k+1})\right),\\
\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(60)

Here,
\tilde{x}_{k+1}
represents the predictor produced by an explicit scheme for
x^{\prime}=y-x
, and
x_{k+1}
is the corrector obtained using an implicit scheme. Therefore
(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})
is more faithful to the VOS flow.

Subtracting the first equation from the third equation of (
60
), we derive the relationship:

x_{k+1}-\tilde{x}_{k+1}=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}(y_{k+1}-y_{k}),

(61)

which is illustrated in Fig.
1
. Namely
x_{k+1}
is an extrapolation of
(x_{k},y_{k})
through predictor-corrector methods and thus (
60
) will be named EPC-VOS scheme.

Figure 1
:
Extrapolation by the predictor-corrector methods. Triangle
\Delta(x_{k},\tilde{x}_{k+1},x_{k+1})
and triangle
\Delta(x_{k},y_{k},y_{k+1})
are similar.

Theorem 6.8
.

Suppose
F
is convex and
L_{F}
-smooth, and
N
is
\mu
-strongly monotone. Then for the predictor-corrector
scheme (
60
) with
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
, we have

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}}\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k}),\quad k\geq 0,

where
\mathcal{E}
is defined by (
52
).

Proof 6.9
.

Let
\tilde{\bm{z}}_{k+1}=(\tilde{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})
. By Lemma
6.6
, we have

\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})}{1+\alpha}-\frac{\mu}{2(1+\alpha)}\left\lVert{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left\langle{\nabla F(\tilde{x}_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}}\right\rangle.

Then we consider the difference
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{k+1})
. We use the upper bound of
D_{F}(x_{k+1},\tilde{x}_{k+1})\leq L_{F}\left\lVert{x_{k+1}-\tilde{x}_{k+1}}\right\rVert^{2}/2
and the relation (
61
) to get

\begin{split}{}&\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\bm{z}}_{k+1})=D_{F}(x_{k+1},x^{\star})-D_{F}(\tilde{x}_{k+1},x^{\star})\\
\leqslant{}&\left\langle{\nabla F(\tilde{x}_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x_{k+1}-\tilde{x}_{k+1}}\right\rangle+\frac{L_{F}}{2}\left\lVert{x_{k+1}-\tilde{x}_{k+1}}\right\rVert^{2}\\
={}&\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\left\langle{\nabla F(\tilde{x}_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}}\right\rangle+\frac{L_{F}\alpha^{2}}{2(1+\alpha)^{2}}\left\lVert{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

Adding those two inequalities to cancel the cross term, we obtain

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})+\left(\frac{L_{F}\alpha^{2}}{2(1+\alpha)^{2}}-\frac{\mu}{2(1+\alpha)}\right)\left\lVert{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

The second term can be dropped if the step size satisfies
L_{F}\alpha^{2}\leq\mu(1+\alpha)
which is true for
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
.

\Box

Compared with AOR-VOS, the EPC-VOS can be also applied to strong Lyapunov functions (
63
), since we do not use the extra positive terms in the strong Lyapunov property.

6.3
One extra gradient step

When consider the strongly convex optimization problem
\min_{x}f(x)
, there is another fix of the cross term. The discretization is

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k}-x_{k+1},\\
\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}&=x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x_{k+1}).\end{aligned}\right.

(62)

To analyze the convergence properties, we use the Lyapunov function:

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})=f(x_{k})-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2},

(63)

where
\bm{z}_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k})
.
The strong Lyapunov property of
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k})
follows as a direct consequence of Theorem
5.3
.

We now incorporate one gradient descent step:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k}-\tilde{x}_{k+1},\\
\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}&=\tilde{x}_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1}),\\
x_{k+1}&=\tilde{x}_{k+1}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1}).\end{aligned}\right.

(64)

As
\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})
has been evaluated in the second step, the computational cost of the third step is neglectable.

Theorem 6.10
.

Assume
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}^{1}
with
0<\mu\leqslant L<\infty
. Then, for the scheme (
64
) with
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L}
,

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\mu/L}}\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k}),\quad k\geq 0,

(65)

where
\mathcal{E}
is defined by (
63
).

Proof 6.11
.

As before, we have shown that

(1+\alpha)\mathcal{E}(\tilde{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leqslant\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k})-\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}-\alpha\langle\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young inequalities to cancel
\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}
, we get:

(1+\alpha)\mathcal{E}(\tilde{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leqslant\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k})+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\mu}\|\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})\|_{*}^{2}.

(66)

Due to the extra gradient descent step, we have the decay property (
38
):

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\tilde{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})=f(x_{k+1})-f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})\leqslant-\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})\|_{*}^{2}.

(67)

Multiplying (
67
) by
1+\alpha
and adding it to (
66
), we obtain:

(1+\alpha)\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leqslant\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k})+\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\mu}-\frac{1+\alpha}{2L}\right)\|\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})\|_{*}^{2}\leqslant 0,

as
L\alpha^{2}\leqslant\mu(1+\alpha)
. This implies (
65
).

\Box

Remark 6.12
.

The final gradient descent step can be replaced by any iteration satisfying the sufficient decay property (
67
). For example, if we let
p_{k+1}=-\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})
, the last step in (
64
) can be replaced with one dimensional problem:

x_{k+1}=\arg\min_{t>0}f(\tilde{x}_{k+1}+tp_{k+1}).

(68)

If
f(x_{k})\leq f(x_{k+1})
, we can retain
x_{k}
by resetting
x_{k+1}=x_{k}
. This ensures the monotonic decrease of
f(x_{k})
while preserving the accelerated linear convergence rate. Note that for the scheme (
64
), we have
f(x_{k+1})\leq f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})
, but
f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})
may not be less than
f(x_{k})
.

\Box

6.4
Examples

We consider the application of the previous schemes and convergence analysis to two well-known accelerated gradient methods: the Heavy-Ball (HB) momentum method and accelerated proximal gradient (APG) methods. Our framework enables the derivation and convergence analysis of these accelerated schemes in a straightforward manner.

6.4.1
Heavy-Ball methods

Consider the strongly convex case
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu,L}
with
\mu>0
. In 1960s, Polyak
[
30
]
introduced the HB model

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}+\eta\,x^{\prime}+\theta\nabla f(x)=0,

and its discretization

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\gamma\nabla f(x_{k})+\beta(x_{k}-x_{k-1}).

(69)

Using the asymptotic bound between the matrix norm and the spectral radius, Polyak
[
30
, Theorem 9]
established local convergence of (
69
) (under the stronger smoothness condition
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
) via spectral analysis, obtaining the minimal spectral radius:

\rho^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}},\quad\text{ with }\beta^{*}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{L}-\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu}}\right)^{2},\ \gamma^{*}=\frac{4}{(\sqrt{L}+\sqrt{\mu})^{2}}.

However, Lessard, Recht, and Packard
[
25
]
demonstrated that HB with parameters optimized for linear ODEs does not guarantee global convergence for general nonlinear objectives, highlighting the limitations of spectral analysis. They presented a counterexample where Polyak’s parameter choice fails for strongly convex problems. Subsequent works
[
17
,
34
,
32
,
33
]
modified
(\beta,\gamma)
to establish global linear convergence for HB. Yet, even the best rate,
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\kappa(f))
, achieved by
[
33
]
, matches gradient descent and does not attain the accelerated rate
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\kappa(f)})
.

The lack of acceleration is not merely an analytical challenge. Goujaud, Taylor, and Dieuleveut
[
21
]
recently showed that HB in the form of (
69
) inherently fails to achieve accelerated rates for smooth and strongly convex optimization. Specifically, for any positive
(\beta,\gamma)
, either there exists an
L
-smooth,
\mu
-strongly convex function and initialization such that HB fails to converge, or for quadratic objectives, HB’s convergence rate remains non-accelerated at
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\kappa(f))
.

Recently, Wei and Chen
[
37
]
introduced the AOR-HB method (cf. (
58
)) and rigorously proved that AOR-HB achieves global linear convergence with an accelerated rate, closing a long-standing theoretical gap in optimization theory.

We will show that AOR-HB is a special case of AOR-VOS methods. The flow is presented in Section
5.3.1
with
\nabla F(x)=\nabla f(x)-\mu x
and
N(y)=\mu y
, which is equivalent to an HB model; see (
48
).

Eliminating
y_{k}
in AOR-VOS scheme (
51
) leads to the formulation

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(1+\alpha)^{2}\mu}\left[2\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k-1})\right]+\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{(1+\alpha)^{2}}(x_{k}-x_{k-1}).

(70)

Setting
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}=\sqrt{\mu/(L-\mu)}
, we get the triple-term formula:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\gamma(2\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k-1}))+\beta(x_{k}-x_{k-1}),

where the parameters are

\gamma=\frac{1}{L+2\sqrt{\mu(L-\mu)}},\quad\quad\beta=L\gamma.

(71)

The most notable and straightforward change in (
70
) is replacing
\nabla f(x_{k})
with
2\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k-1})
to approximate
\nabla f(x)
. The parameter choice (
71
), derived from AOR-VOS, offers a slight improvement over AOR-HB, which was recently developed in
[
37
]
and the convergence analysis is also improved by removing the factor
1/2
in front of
\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}
.

The modified Lyapunov function in Theorem
6.3
is:

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y)=\mathcal{E}(x,y)-\alpha\langle\nabla f_{-\mu}(x)-\nabla f_{-\mu}(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle,

which expands to:

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y)=f(x)-f(x^{\star})-\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\alpha\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x^{\star})-\mu(x-x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle.

This function contains negative squared terms and a challenging cross term, making the proof of its accelerated linear convergence rate

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\mu/L_{F}}}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{k},y_{k}),\quad k\geq 0,

non-trivial without using our framework.

Remark 6.13
.

One can also derive (
70
) by considering the flow (
49
) where

\displaystyle\theta\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x)={}

\displaystyle\theta\nabla^{2}f(x)x^{\prime}=\theta\frac{\rm d}{\rm dt}\nabla f(x)\approx{}\theta\cdot\frac{\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_{k})}{\theta}=\nabla f(x_{k+1})-\nabla f(x_{k}).

\Box

6.4.2
Accelerated Proximal-Gradient Method

Consider the composite optimization problem:

\min_{x}(f(x)+g(x)),

where
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, and
g
is convex but might be non-smooth. The flow is presented in Section
5.3.2
with
\nabla F(x)=\nabla f(x)-\mu x,
and
N(y)=\partial g(y)+\mu y.

For the ease of notation, we first present AOR-VOS for smooth
g
:

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2y_{k+1}-y_{k}-x_{k+1},

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(x_{k})+x_{k}-y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla g(y_{k+1}).

Given the proximal operator of
g
:

{\bf prox}_{\lambda g}(x)=\arg\min_{v}\left(g(v)+{\frac{1}{2\lambda}}\|v-x\|^{2}\right),

we can solve
y_{k+1}
from the second equation of (
72
) and then update
x_{k+1}
to get an equivalent scheme

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle={\bf prox}_{\frac{\alpha}{(1+\alpha)\mu}g}(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}y_{k}+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\frac{1}{\mu}(-\nabla f(x_{k})+\mu x_{k})),

(73)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(x_{k}+2\alpha y_{k+1}-\alpha y_{k}).

The equivalent form (
73
) can be applied to a non-smooth convex function
g
as well.

The EPC-VOS scheme (
60
) is

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{k+1}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(x_{k}+\alpha y_{k}),

\displaystyle y_{k+1}={}

\displaystyle\bf{\displaystyle prox}_{\frac{\alpha}{(1+\alpha)\mu}g}(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}y_{k}+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\frac{1}{\mu}(-\nabla f(\tilde{x}_{k+1})+\mu\tilde{x}_{k+1}))

\displaystyle x_{k+1}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(x_{k}+\alpha y_{k+1}).

The convergence proof is identical to the strongly convex optimization with
f
only as
g
is not in the Lyapunov function (by treating
\nabla g
implicitly).
For the non-smooth convex function
g
, the only difference in the analysis is when verifying the strong Lyapunov property, use

\langle\chi-\eta,x-y\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ and }\chi\in\partial g(x),\eta\in\partial g(y).

The numerical examples in
[
37
]
shows AOR-VOS schemes outperforms the fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm (FISTA)
[
5
]
and the accelerated proximal gradient (APG) algorithm proposed in
[
26
]
when applied to LASSO problem
[
36
]
.

7
Accelerated Gradient and Skew-Symmetric Splitting Methods

In this section, we consider a class of monotone operator equations:

\mathcal{L}(x)=\nabla f(x)+\mathcal{N}x=0,

(74)

with a
\mu
-strongly convex function
f\in\mathcal{S}^{1}_{\mu,L}
, and a linear and skew-symmetric operator
\mathcal{N}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}^{\intercal}=-\mathcal{N}
. Based on the structure of
\mathcal{N}
, we can develop full explicit schemes.

7.1
Flow and Lyapunov function

It fits the variable and operator splitting framework with
F(x)=f_{-\mu}(x)
and
N(y)=\mathcal{N}y+\mu y
. The VOS flow is

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,\\
y^{\prime}&=-\mu^{-1}(\nabla f(x)-\mu x+\mu y+\mathcal{N}y).\end{aligned}\right.

(75)

Since the gradient and the skew-symmetric matrix split, we name discretization of (
75
) as Accelerated Gradient and skew-Symmetric Splitting (AGSS) methods
[
10
]
.

We will use the Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}(x,y):=D_{f_{-\mu}}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}

and denote the vector filed of (
75
) as
\mathcal{G}(x,y)=(\mathcal{G}^{x}(x,y),\mathcal{G}^{y}(x,y))
. The strong Lyapunov property is a consequence of Theorem
5.1
.

7.2
Explicit-Implicit AGSS scheme

If we treat
\mathcal{N}
implicitly, then the algorithm and convergence analysis is the same as the previous section. For example, the AOR-VOS is:

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2y_{k+1}-y_{k}-x_{k+1},

(76)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\mu^{-1}\left(\nabla f(x_{k})-\mu x_{k}+\mathcal{N}y_{k+1}+\mu y_{k+1}\right).

The scheme (
76
) is implicit for
y_{k+1}
and each iteration needs to solve a linear equation

(\beta I+\mathcal{N})y_{k+1}=b(x_{k},y_{k})

(77)

associated to a shifted skew-symmetric system with
\beta=\mu(1+1/\alpha)
.

With the modified Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y):

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(x,y)-\alpha\langle\nabla f_{-\mu}(x)-\nabla f_{-\mu}(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle,

(78)

we obtain the following convergence rate as the consequence of Theorem
6.3
. EPC-VOS can be also applied and achieve a similar accelerated rate.

Proposition 7.1

(Convergence rate of Explicit-Implicit AGSS)
.

Assume
f\in S^{1,1}_{\mu,L}
and
\mathcal{N}
is a skew-symmetric matrix. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the iteration of scheme (
76
) with the initial value
(x_{0},y_{0})
and step size
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu/(L-\mu)}
. Then,
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y)
defined as (
78
) serves as a Lyapunov function, and the method achieves accelerated linear convergence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad k\geq 0,

and

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad k\geq 0.

(79)

When the operator
\mathcal{L}
is linear, we have the following decomposition:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}^{\rm s}+\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{A}^{\rm s}=(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{\intercal})/2
is the symmetric (Hermitian for complex matrices) part and
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{\intercal})/2
is the skew-symmetric part. The condition
\mathcal{L}
is
\mu
-strongly monotone is equivalent to
\mathcal{A}^{\rm s}
is Hermitian and positive definite and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})\geq\mu
. Bai, Golub, and Ng in
[
2
]
proposed the Hermitian/skew-Hermitian splitting method (HSS) for solving general non-Hermitian positive definite linear systems
\mathcal{L}x=b
:

\displaystyle(\alpha I+\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})x_{k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=(\alpha I-\mathcal{N})x_{k}+b

(80)

\displaystyle(\alpha I+\mathcal{N})x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\alpha I-\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})x_{k+\frac{1}{2}}+b.

The iterative method (
80
) solves the equations for the symmetric (Hermitian) part and skew-symmetric (skew-Hermitian) part alternatively. For the HSS method (
80
), efficient solvers for linear operators
(\alpha I+\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})^{-1}
and
(\alpha I+\mathcal{N})^{-1}
are needed. A linear convergence rate of
\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa(\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})}+1}
can be achieved for an optimal choice of parameter
\alpha
. Several variants of HSS are derived and analyzed in
[
1
,
3
]
.

For linear systems, compared with HSS, AGSS achieves the same accelerated rate without treating the symmetric part implicitly, i.e., no need to compute
(\alpha I+\mathcal{A}^{\rm s})^{-1}
, and therefore significantly improve the efficiency; see
[
10
]
for numerical examples. More importantly, AGSS can handle non-linear problems while HSS is restricted to linear algebraic systems only.

7.3
Explicit AGSS scheme

As
\mathcal{N}
is skew-symmetric, we can write

\mathcal{N}=B^{\intercal}-B,\quad\text{with }B^{\intercal}={\rm upper}(\mathcal{N}).

Let
B^{\sym}=B+B^{\intercal}
be a symmetrization of
B
. We can split
\mathcal{N}
as

\mathcal{N}=B^{\sym}-2B,\quad\text{and }\ \mathcal{N}=2B^{\intercal}-B^{\sym}.

To avoid computing
(\beta I+\mathcal{N})^{-1}
, we develop an explicit AGSS scheme by applying AOR technique to
\mathcal{N}
as well:

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2y_{k+1}-y_{k}-x_{k+1},

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-y_{k+1}-\mu^{-1}\left(\nabla f(x_{k})+B^{\rm sym}y_{k}-2By_{k+1}\right).

Notice that as
B
is lower triangular,
y_{k+1}
can be computed by inverting
(\beta I+B)^{-1}
which is a forward substitution. We denote
L_{B^{\rm sym}}=\|B^{\rm sym}\|
.

We consider the modified Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y):=D_{f_{-\mu}}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}_{\mu I-\alpha B^{\rm sym}}-\alpha(\nabla f_{-\mu}(x)-\nabla f_{-\mu}(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}),

(82)

and bound the cross terms similar to Lemma
6.1
and skip the similar proof here.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth. For any two points
(x,y)
and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
and any
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle|\langle\nabla f_{-\mu}(\hat{x})-\nabla f_{-\mu}(x),\hat{y}-y\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{y}-y\|_{B^{\sym}}^{2}|-\frac{L_{B^{\sym}}}{2}\|\hat{y}-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sqrt{\frac{L-\mu}{\beta\mu}}\left(\min\{D_{f_{-\mu}}(x,\hat{x}),D_{f_{-\mu}}(\hat{x},x)\}+\frac{\beta\mu}{2}\|\hat{y}-y\|^{2}\right).

Theorem 7.3
.

Suppose
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth. For the step size

\displaystyle\alpha=\max_{\beta\in(0,1)}\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\beta\mu}{L-\mu}},\frac{(1-\beta)\mu}{L_{B^{\rm sym}}}\right\},

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x,y)
defined as (
82
) serves as the Lyapunov function and we obtain the accelerated linear convergence for scheme (
81
):

\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad k\geq 0,

and

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(x_{0},y_{0}),\quad k\geq 0.

Proof 7.4
.

The proof follows with that of Theorem
6.3
with minor modifications. In the error equation, we will have one more cross term

\displaystyle\alpha(y_{k+1}-y^{\star},y_{k+1}-y_{k})_{B^{\sym}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|y_{k+1}-y^{\star}\|_{B^{\sym}}^{2}+\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|_{B^{\sym}}^{2}-\|y_{k}-y^{\star}\|_{B^{\sym}}^{2}\right).

By Lemma
7.2
, we use part of the quadratic term, i.e.,
\frac{\beta\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}
to control the cross term of
\nabla f_{-\mu}
for
\alpha\leq\sqrt{\frac{\beta\mu}{L-\mu}}
and rest
\frac{(1-\beta)\mu}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2}
to control
\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y-y^{\star}\|_{B^{\sym}}^{2}
which requires
\alpha\leq(1-\beta)\mu/L_{B^{\rm sym}}
. We can solve for an optimal
\beta
by the step size formulae on
\alpha
.

\Box

Remark 7.5
.

Compared to the scheme (
76
) implicit in
\mathcal{N}
, the coupling of
\mathcal{N}
and
\nabla F
is through
\mu/L_{B^{\sym}}
. In case
\mu/L_{B^{\sym}}\ll\sqrt{\mu/L}
and evaluation of
\nabla f
is more costly than solving the linear equation, we can use the explicit-implicit scheme (
76
) as an outer iteration and solve the linear equation (
77
) inexactly. The tolerance of the inner linear solver can be dynamically decreasing.

\Box

7.4
Application to saddle point systems

Consider the saddle point problem:

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{H}(u,p)=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu,p),

where
f
and
g
are strongly convex functions with constant
\mu_{f}>0
and
\mu_{g}>0
, and
B
is a bilinear coupling operator. The splitting framework provides the following

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{u}\mathcal{H}(u,p)\\
-\partial_{p}\mathcal{H}(u,p)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f&B^{\intercal}\\
-B&\nabla g\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\nabla f&0\\
0&\nabla g\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix}}_{\nabla F_{+\mu}}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&B^{\intercal}\\
-B&0\end{pmatrix}}_{N_{-\mu}}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix}

where
F(x):=f(u)-\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\|u\|^{2}+g(p)-\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\|p\|^{2}
with a component splitting of
\mu
using
\mu_{f}
and
\mu_{g}
respectively. Here we flip the sign of
\partial_{p}\mathcal{H}
so that: for all
x=(u,p)^{\intercal},y=(v,q)^{\intercal}

\langle\nabla F(x)+N(x)-(\nabla F(y)+N(y)),x-y\rangle\geq\min\{\mu_{f},\mu_{g}\}\|x-y\|^{2}.

The strong monotonicity has essential similarity to strongly convexity. Comparing to the convex optimization, one extra difficulty is the bilinear coupling
(Bu,p)
which can be again handled by the AOR technique in discretization.

Recall the component form of VOS flow is

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=v-u,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=u-v-\frac{1}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla f(u)+B^{\intercal}q),

\displaystyle p^{\prime}

\displaystyle=q-p,

\displaystyle q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=p-q-\frac{1}{\mu_{g}}(\nabla g(p)-Bv).

By treating
\mathcal{N}
implicitly, we have the component form of (
51
) and call it AOR-VOS-Saddle-I(mplicit).

\displaystyle\frac{u_{k+1}-u_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2v_{k+1}-v_{k}-u_{k+1}

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{k+1}-p_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2q_{k+1}-q_{k}-p_{k+1}

\displaystyle\frac{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=u_{k}-v_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla f(u_{k})+B^{\intercal}q_{k+1}),

\displaystyle\frac{q_{k+1}-q_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=p_{k}-q_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu_{g}}(\nabla g(p_{k})-Bv_{k+1}).

The variable
(v_{k+1},q_{k+1})
are coupled together and can be computed by inverting the linear saddle point system

\begin{pmatrix}(1+\alpha)I&\frac{\alpha}{\mu_{f}}B^{\intercal}\\
-\frac{\alpha}{\mu_{g}}B&(1+\alpha)I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{k+1}\\
q_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{k}+\alpha u_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{\mu_{f}}\nabla f(u_{k})\\
q_{k}+\alpha p_{k}-\frac{\alpha}{\mu_{g}}\nabla g(p_{k})\end{pmatrix}.

It is sufficient to compute
\left((1+\alpha)^{2}I+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}}BB^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}
or
\left((1+\alpha)^{2}I+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}}B^{\intercal}B\right)^{-1}
, whichever is a relative small size matrix and can be further replaced by an inexact inner solver.
It is preferable when the size
BB^{\intercal}
or
B^{\intercal}B
is small. After
(v_{k+1},q_{k+1})
is computed, we update
(u_{k+1},p_{k+1})
by the first two equations.

The Lyapunov function expands as

\mathcal{E}(u,p,v,q):=D_{f_{-\mu_{f}}}(u,u^{\star})+D_{g_{-\mu_{g}}}(p,p^{\star})+\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\|v-u^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\|q-p^{\star}\|^{2}.

The following proposition shows the convergence rate of AOR-VOS-saddle-I method which is an easy variant of Theorem
6.3
and Proposition
7.1
.

Proposition 7.6

(Convergence of AOR-VOS-saddle-I method)
.

Suppose
f
is
\mu_{f}
-strongly convex and
L_{f}
-smooth,
g
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex and
L_{g}
-smooth. Let
(u_{k},v_{k},p_{k},q_{k})
be generated by (
83
) with initial value
(u_{0},v_{0},p_{0},q_{0})
and step size

\alpha=\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{f}}{L_{f}-\mu_{f}}},\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{g}}{L_{g}-\mu_{g}}}\right\}.

Then for the modified Lyapunov function defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u,p,v,q)

\displaystyle:=D_{f_{-\mu_{f}}}(u,u^{\star})+D_{g_{-\mu_{g}}}(p,p^{\star})+\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\|v-u^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\|q-p^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\alpha(\nabla f(u)-\nabla f(u^{\star})-\mu_{f}(u-u^{\star}),v-u^{\star})

\displaystyle-\alpha(\nabla g(p)-\nabla g(p^{\star})-\mu_{g}(p-p^{\star}),q-p^{\star}),

we have the linear convergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{k+1},p_{k+1},v_{k+1},q_{k+1})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{0},p_{0},v_{0},q_{0}),\quad k\geq 0

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{k+1},p_{k+1},v_{k+1},q_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{0},p_{0},v_{0},q_{0}),\quad k\geq 0.

When the linear saddle point problem is not easy to compute, we use the following explicit scheme by applying AOR to the skew-symmetric matrix.

\displaystyle\frac{u_{k+1}-u_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2v_{k+1}-v_{k}-u_{k+1}

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{p_{k+1}-p_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2q_{k+1}-q_{k}-p_{k+1}

\displaystyle\frac{v_{k+1}-v_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=u_{k}-v_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu_{f}}(\nabla f(u_{k})+B^{\intercal}q_{k}),

\displaystyle\frac{q_{k+1}-q_{k}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=p_{k}-q_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\mu_{g}}(\nabla g(p_{k})-2Bv_{k+1}+Bv_{k}).

Convergence analysis is a variant of Lemma
7.2
and Theorem
7.3
with separated estimate using
\mu_{f}
and
\mu_{g}
.

We consider the modifed Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u,p,v,q)

\displaystyle:=D_{f_{-\mu_{f}}}(u,u^{\star})+D_{g_{-\mu_{g}}}(p,p^{\star})+\frac{\mu_{f}}{2}\|v-u^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\|q-p^{\star}\|^{2}

(85)

\displaystyle-\alpha(\nabla f(u)-\nabla f(u^{\star})-\mu_{f}(u-u^{\star}),v-u^{\star})

\displaystyle-\alpha(\nabla g(p)-\nabla g(p^{\star})-\mu_{g}(p-p^{\star}),q-p^{\star})-\alpha(B(v-u^{\star}),q-p^{\star}).

We use the estimate

2|(Bu,p)|\leq 2\|B\|\|u\|\|p\|\leq\frac{\|B\|}{\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}}}(\mu_{f}\|u\|^{2}+\mu_{g}\|p\|^{2}),\quad\forall(u,p).

to control the extra cross term
(B(v-u^{\star}),q-p^{\star})
.

Theorem 7.7

(Convergence of AOR-VOS-saddle method)
.

Suppose
f
is
\mu_{f}
-strongly convex and
L_{f}
-smooth,
g
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex and
L_{g}
-smooth. Let
(u_{k},v_{k},p_{k},q_{k})
be generated by (
84
) with initial value
(u_{0},v_{0},p_{0},q_{0})
and
step size

\alpha=\max_{\beta\in(0,1)}\min\left\{\sqrt{\beta}\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{f}}{L_{f}-\mu_{f}}},\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{g}}{L_{g}-\mu_{g}}}\right\},(1-\beta)\frac{\sqrt{\mu_{f}\mu_{g}}}{\|B\|}\right\}.

Then we have the accelerated linear convergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{k+1},p_{k+1},v_{k+1},q_{k+1})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{0},p_{0},v_{0},q_{0}),\quad k\geq 0

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{k+1},p_{k+1},v_{k+1},q_{k+1})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}(u_{0},p_{0},v_{0},q_{0}),\quad k\geq 0

The convergence rate in Theorem
7.7
is global and accelerated, meaning that to obtain
\|(v_{k},q_{k})-(u^{\star},p^{\star})\|\leq\epsilon
, we need at most

O\left(\sqrt{L_{f}/\mu_{f}+L_{g}/\mu_{g}+\|B\|^{2}/(\mu_{f}\mu_{g})}|\log\epsilon|\right)

iterations. The iteration complexity is optimal for first-order methods for saddle point problems
[
39
]
.

Remark 7.8
.

The current framework, however, cannot handle the case
\mu_{g}=0
and
\mu_{f}>0
. Transformed primal dual method for saddle point problems are recently developed
[
11
]
and can be combined with the acceleration technique to deal with this case.

\Box

8
Convex Optimization

In this section we focus on the convex case, i.e.,
\mu=0
. We introduce a dynamically updating parameter and a perturbed VOS flow. For both modified flows, EPC-VOS method can achieve the accelerated convergence rate.

8.1
Dynamically Scaled Gradient Flow

Consider the dynamically scaled gradient flow:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=-\nabla f(x)/\gamma,\\
\gamma^{\prime}&=-\gamma.\end{aligned}\right.

(86)

with arbitrary initial values
x(0)=x_{0}
and
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0}>0
.
Introducing the parameter
\gamma
, governed by the equation
\gamma^{\prime}=-\gamma
, results in a time rescaling effect:
\gamma=\gamma_{0}e^{-t}
or equivalently
t=\ln\gamma_{0}-\ln\gamma
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(x,\gamma)
be the vector field of ODE (
86
). We introduce a Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}(x,\gamma):=f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert{x-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(87)

Let us verify the strong Lyapunov property as follows

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,\gamma),\mathcal{G}(x,\gamma)\rangle={}

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla f(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert{x-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x)}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant{}

\displaystyle f(x)-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert{x-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x)}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x,\gamma)+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x)}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}.

(88)

Hence
\mathcal{E}
is a strong Lyapunov function of
\mathcal{G}
with extra term
p^{2}(x,\gamma)=\left\lVert{\nabla f(x)}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}/\gamma
. By Theorem
3.1
, we have

\mathcal{E}(x(t),\gamma(t))\leqslant e^{-t}\mathcal{E}(x_{0},\gamma_{0}),\quad\,t\geqslant 0.

(89)

Note that even for
\mu=0
, we can still achieve the exponential stability. In the continuous level, exponential decay can be always obtained by a time rescaling.

8.2
Proximal Point Algorithm

Convergence analysis of the implicit Euler methods for smooth or non-smooth convex functions are almost identical. Therefore in the following we present the smooth case only.

Given any time step size
\alpha_{k}>0
, the implicit Euler method reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}&\mathcal{G}^{x}(x_{k+1},\gamma_{k}):=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}\nabla f(x_{k+1})\\
\frac{\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}&\mathcal{G}^{\gamma}(x_{k},\gamma_{k+1}):=-\gamma_{k+1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(90)

Denoted by
t_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\gamma_{k},
the update of
x_{k+1}
in (
90
) can be written using the proximal operator

x_{k+1}={\bf prox}_{t_{k}f}(x_{k}):=\mathop{{\mathrm{argmin}\,}}\limits_{x}\left\{f(x)+\frac{1}{2t_{k}}\left\lVert{x-x_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}\right\}.

(91)

which can be applied to non-smooth
f
as well.

We introduce the discrete Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}_{k}:=\mathcal{E}(x_{k},\gamma_{k})=f(x_{k})-f(x^{\star})+\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\lVert{x_{k}-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(92)

In (
91
), only the step size
t_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\gamma_{k}
enters the algorithm. Therefore we estimate the convergence rate by
t_{k}
. The following result recovers the classic estimate of PPA in
[
22
]
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Assume
f
is convex. Then for
(x_{k},\gamma_{k})
produced by the scheme (
90
) with any
\alpha_{k}>0
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}\,\mathcal{E}_{k},

(93)

where
\mathcal{E}_{k}
is defined in (
87
).
Consequently for any
\gamma_{0}>0
,

\max\left\{f(x_{k})-f(x^{\star}),t_{k}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}\right\}\leqslant\mathcal{E}_{k}\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{1+\gamma_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}t_{i}},

(94)

where
t_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\gamma_{k}
is the step size for the gradient descent step.

Proof 8.2
.

We split the difference as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}={}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},\gamma_{k})-\mathcal{E}(x_{k},\gamma_{k})}_{{\rm I}_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},\gamma_{k})}_{{\rm I}_{2}}.

As for fixed
\gamma>0
, the function

\mathcal{E}(\cdot,\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{0}
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle{\rm I}_{1}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\left\langle{\partial_{x}\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},\gamma_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}}\right\rangle-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\lVert{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}-\alpha_{k}\left\langle{x_{k+1}-x^{\star},\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rangle-\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}}{2\gamma_{k}}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}(f(x_{k+1})-f(x^{\star}))-t_{k}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}.

Notice that as
f
is convex only, we only get part of the Lyapunov function
f(x_{k+1})-f(x^{\star})
.
The quadratic part will be obtained by the change of parameters:

{\rm I}_{2}={}\left\langle{\partial_{\gamma}\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1}),\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}}\right\rangle={}-\alpha_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Adding all together, we get the discrete strong Lyapunov property

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{k}\leqslant{}&-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{E}_{k+1}-t_{k}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{*}^{2},\end{split}

which leads to (
93
) and
t_{k}\left\lVert{\nabla f(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{*}^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}_{k}
.

By recursion, we have

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1+\alpha_{i})}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\gamma_{i+1}}{\gamma_{i}}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{0}}.

We solve for
\gamma_{k}
by summing the identity

\frac{1}{\gamma_{k+1}}-\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}=t_{k},

and get the rate (
94
).

\Box

For explicit methods, sub-linear rate is expected for
\mu=0
. The implicit Euler method, however, retains the linear rate uniformly for all
\mu\geqslant 0
. The larger is step size
t_{k}
, the better is the convergence rate. On the other hand, uniform bound
\alpha_{k}\geqslant\alpha>0
implies the exponential increasing of
t_{k}
and the proximal operator is harder to evaluate. In the limiting case
t_{k}=\infty
, it goes back to the original optimization problem.

8.3
Accelerated Gradient methods

We consider the operator equation

\mathcal{L}(x)=\nabla F(x)+N(x)=0

and assume
F
is convex and
N
is monotone. Consider the VOS flow.

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}={}&y-x,\\
y^{\prime}={}&-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\nabla F(x)+N(y)),\\
\gamma^{\prime}={}&-\gamma.\end{aligned}\right.

(95)

For
\bm{z}=(x,y)
, we use the Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}(\bm{z},\gamma):=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert{y-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2},

(96)

and denote by
\mathcal{G}(\bm{z},\gamma)
the right hand side of (
95
).

As
\mu=0
, we will use the dynamical updated parameter
\gamma
which acts like the parameter
\mu
as before. The extra quadratic term in the strong Lyapunov property is obtained by the changing of
\gamma
.
A direct computation gives

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bm{z},\gamma),\mathcal{G}(\bm{z},\gamma)\rangle={}\left\langle{\nabla F(x),x-x^{\star}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\lVert{y-x^{\star}}\right\rVert^{2}\geqslant{}\mathcal{E}(\bm{z},\gamma).

(97)

Hence
\mathcal{E}(\bm{z},\gamma)
defined by (
96
) is a strong Lyapunov function.

A semi-implicit discretization of (
95
) is

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}&y_{k}-x_{k+1},\\
\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}&-\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}(\nabla F(x_{k+1})+N(y_{k+1})),\\
\frac{\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}&-\gamma_{k+1},\end{aligned}\right.

(98)

where
\alpha_{k}>0
is the time step size.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
\bm{z}=(x,y)
. For the scheme (
98
), we have

(1+\alpha_{k})\mathcal{E}({\bm{z}}_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1})\leqslant\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k},\gamma_{k})-\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2},

(99)

where
\mathcal{E}
is defined in (
96
).

Proof 8.4
.

We split the difference as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k},\gamma_{k})={}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1},\gamma_{k})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k},\gamma_{k})}_{{\rm I}_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1})-\mathcal{E}(\bm{z}_{k+1},\gamma_{k})}_{{\rm I}_{2}}.

The first term is

\displaystyle{\rm I}_{1}=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\bm{z}}\mathcal{E}({\bm{z}}_{k+1},\gamma_{k}),{\bm{z}}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},{\bm{z}}_{k+1};\gamma_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\rangle-\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k}\langle y_{k+1}-x^{\star},N(y_{k+1})-N(x^{\star})\rangle-D_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{z}_{k},{\bm{z}}_{k+1};\gamma_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}D_{F}(x_{k+1},x^{\star})-\alpha_{k}\langle\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y_{k+1}-y_{k}\rangle-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2}.

While the second term
{\rm I}_{2}
will be the change of the parameter:

{\rm I}_{2}=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}=-\alpha_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

Adding these two inequalities and rearranging terms to get the desired result.

\Box

Then we can apply EPC
with
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}/L}
to get the convergence. We summarize the algorithm and convergence in the following theorem.

Theorem 8.5
.

Suppose
F
is convex and
L_{F}
-smooth, and
N
is monotone. Consider the scheme, with
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}/L_{F}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}

\displaystyle y_{k}-\tilde{x}_{k+1},

(100)

\displaystyle\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}\left(\nabla F(\tilde{x}_{k+1})+N(y_{k+1})\right),

\displaystyle\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-x_{k+1},

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}={}

\displaystyle-\gamma_{k+1}.

Then we have the convergence

\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}\,\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\gamma_{k}).

(101)

Consequently for any
\gamma_{0}>0
, with
c_{0}=\sqrt{\gamma_{0}/L_{F}}/(\sqrt{\gamma_{0}/L_{F}+1}+1)
,

\mathcal{E}({\bm{z}}_{k},\gamma_{k})\leqslant\frac{1}{\left(c_{0}\ k+1\right)^{2}}\mathcal{E}({\bm{z}}_{0},\gamma_{0}).

(102)

Proof 8.6
.

Based on (
99
), the contraction (
101
) can be proved as that in Theorem
6.8
if the step size satisfies
L_{F}\alpha_{k}^{2}\leq\gamma_{k}(1+\alpha_{k})
, which is true for
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}/L_{F}}
.
By recursion, we have

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1+\alpha_{i})}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\gamma_{i+1}}{\gamma_{i}}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{0}}.

So it remains to estimate the decay rate of
\rho_{k}:=\gamma_{k}/\gamma_{0}
. By the third equation of (
100
),
\{\rho_{k}\}
satisfies the relation

\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}=-\alpha_{k}\rho_{k+1},\quad i.e.\quad\rho_{k+1}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{k}}\rho_{k},

(103)

which implies
\rho_{k}
is monotone decreasing and
\rho_{k}<\rho_{0}=1
for all
k\geq 1
.

Consider the difference of
1/\sqrt{\rho_{k}}
and use (
103
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{k+1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{k}}}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{k}-\rho_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\rho_{k}\rho_{k+1}}(\sqrt{\rho_{k}}+\sqrt{\rho_{k+1}})}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\sqrt{\rho_{k}}(1+\sqrt{1+\alpha_{k}})}.

As
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}/L_{F}}=\sqrt{\rho_{k}}c\leq c
, with
c=\sqrt{\gamma_{0}/L_{F}}
. Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{k+1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{k}}}=\frac{c}{1+\sqrt{1+\alpha_{k}}}\geqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{c+1}+1}.

Summing from
k=0,1,\ldots
, we obtain

\rho_{k}\leqslant\frac{1}{\left(c_{0}k+1\right)^{2}},\quad c_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{0}/L_{F}}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{0}/L_{F}+1}+1}.

(104)

\Box

Remark 8.7
.

According to the proof, the equality
\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}=-\alpha_{k}\gamma_{k+1}
to update the parameter can be relaxed to an inequality

\gamma_{k+1}-\gamma_{k}\leq-\alpha_{k}\gamma_{k+1}.

(105)

We can thus choose the parameters in a simple form

\alpha_{k}=\frac{2}{k+1},\quad\gamma_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{2}L_{F}=\frac{4}{(k+1)^{2}}L_{F},

which satisfies (
105
) by direct calculation, and obtain the convergence rate

\mathcal{E}_{k}\leq\frac{c_{0}\mathcal{E}_{0}}{(k+1)^{2}},\quad c_{0}=4L_{F}/\gamma_{0}.

\Box

By applying the operator splitting to examples in Section
6.4
, we thus recover the accelerated gradient method for convex optimization (NAG), for composite convex optimization (FISTA), and for the saddle point system but implicit in
N
(which requiring solve a linear saddle point system; cf. (
83
)). To achieve the accuracy
\epsilon
, the iteration complexity is
O(\sqrt{L_{F}/\epsilon})
.

8.4
A Perturbed Lyapunov Analysis

We present a perturbation argument to establish that a solution with accuracy
\epsilon
can be obtained with an iteration complexity of
O(\sqrt{L/\epsilon})
.

Fix some
\epsilon>0
, the perturbed VOS flow is

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=y-x,\\
y^{\prime}&=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big(\underbrace{{\epsilon(x-y)}}_{\rm perturbation}-\nabla F(x)-N(y)\big),\end{aligned}\right.

(106)

where
F
is convex and
N
is monotone. Due to the variable splitting, the perturbation of adding
\epsilon(x-y)
to the vector field will not change the equilibrium points.

Consider the perturbed Lyapunov function

\mathcal{E}(x,y):=D_{F}(x,x^{\star})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|y-x^{\star}\|^{2},

(107)

where
\epsilon>0
and
x^{\star}
is one but fixed solution of the operator equation
\nabla F(x^{\star})+N(x^{\star})=0
. As the Lyapunov function depends on
\epsilon
, we shall denote it as
\mathcal{E}(x,y;\epsilon)
, where
\epsilon
is considered as a parameter and thus
\nabla\mathcal{E}
is taking respect to
x
and
y
.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the Lyapunov function (
107
), and let
\mathcal{G}
be the vector field of the perturbed VOS flow (
106
). The following perturbed strong Lyapunov property holds:

-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y;\epsilon),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle\geq\mathcal{E}(x,y;\epsilon)+D_{F}(x^{\star},x)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

(108)

Proof 8.9
.

By direct calculations, the cross term
\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),y-x^{\star}\rangle
cancels due to the sign change. Therefore,

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla\mathcal{E}(x,y;\epsilon),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle={}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\rangle+\langle y-x^{\star},N(y)-N(x^{\star})\rangle

\displaystyle+\epsilon(y-x,y-x^{\star})

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x,y;\epsilon)+D_{F}(x^{\star},x)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}.

\Box

Previously if
F
is
\epsilon
-convex,
2D_{F}(x^{\star},x)\geq\epsilon\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}
which implies the strong Lyapunov property. Now we leave
\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}
term here.

Consider EPC-VOS scheme for solving (
106
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde{x}_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k}-\tilde{x}_{k+1},\\
\frac{y_{k+1}-y_{k}}{\alpha}&=\tilde{x}_{k+1}-y_{k+1}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\nabla F(\tilde{x}_{k+1})+N(y_{k+1})\right),\\
\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\alpha}&=y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(109)

For saddle point problems, we can further combine the AOR technique to discretize the skew-symmetric term
[
10
, Section 5.3.3]
.

Theorem 8.10

(Convergence of perturbed EPC-VOS method)
.

Suppose
F
is convex and
L_{F}
-smooth, and
N
is monotone. Let
(x_{k},y_{k})
be the sequence generated by scheme (
109
) with the initial value
(x_{0},y_{0})
and step size
\alpha=\sqrt{\epsilon/L_{F}}
. Assume that there exists
R>0
such that

\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|\leq R,\quad\forall k\geq 0.

(110)

Then we have the linear convergence with perturbation

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon)\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{\epsilon/L_{F}}}\right)^{k}\mathcal{E}(x_{0},y_{0},\epsilon)+\epsilon R^{2},\quad k\geq 0.

(111)

Proof 8.11
.

The proof is almost identical to that of Theorem
6.8
. The only difference is to use the perturbed strong Lyapunov property (
108
) and the bound
\|x_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq R^{2}
which leads to the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1},\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon)+\frac{\alpha\epsilon}{1+\alpha}R^{2}

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{k+1}\mathcal{E}(x_{0},y_{0},\epsilon)+\frac{\alpha\epsilon R^{2}}{1+\alpha}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(1+\alpha)^{i}}.

Summing up the geometric series we get the desired result.

\Box

The assumption (
110
) can be verified in the special case where
N=0
and
F
are coercive. In this scenario, we can use another Lyapunov function
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(x,y;\epsilon)=F(x)-F(x^{\star})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}
. Through direct calculation, we have

-\langle\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(x,y;\epsilon),\mathcal{G}(x,y)\rangle\geq\epsilon\|y-x\|^{2}\geq 0.

As a result,
F(x(t))-F(x^{\star})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
for
t>0
, and Lemma
4.2
implies the bound
\|x(t)-x^{\star}\|\leq R_{0}
. The discrete case can be established analogously.

To achieve an accuracy
\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k})=O(\epsilon)
, the number of iterations is bounded by

\left(1+\sqrt{\epsilon/L_{F}}\right)^{-k}=O(\epsilon)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{L_{F}}{\epsilon}}|\ln\epsilon|\right).

Compared to the dominant complexity
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
, the logarithmic factor
O(|\ln\epsilon|)
is negligible. This establishes the nearly optimal complexity of accelerated gradient methods.

Algorithm 1

EPC-VOS with perturbation.

1:

Parameters: Inititial value and tolerance
(x_{0},y_{0},\epsilon_{0})
and termination tolerance
\epsilon
.

2:

Set
k=0
and
m_{0}=(\sqrt{L_{F}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}})\ln(2(R^{2}+1))\epsilon_{0}^{-1/2}

3:

while

\epsilon_{k}>\epsilon

do

4:

\epsilon_{k+1}=\epsilon_{k}/2,\quad m_{k+1}=\sqrt{2}\,m_{k}

5:

Apply EPC-VOS scheme (
109
) with the initial value
(x_{k},y_{k})
, the parameter
\epsilon_{k+1}
and the step size
\alpha=\sqrt{\epsilon_{k+1}/L_{F}}
for
m_{k+1}
iterations to get
(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})

6:

k=k+1

7:

end

while

8:

return

(x_{k},y_{k})

Since
\epsilon_{k+1}
is strictly decreasing and the perturbation will not change the equilibrium point
x^{\star}
, we can use the homotopy argument to remove the
|\ln\epsilon|
dependence. This is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 8.12
.

Choose
(x_{0},y_{0})
and
\epsilon_{0}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(x_{0},y_{0},\epsilon_{0})\leq(R^{2}+1)\epsilon_{0}
.
Then for
(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k})
generated by Algorithm
1
, we have

\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k})\leq(R^{2}+1)\epsilon_{k}\quad\forall\,k\geq 0,

(113)

Moreover, let
M_{k}:=\sum_{i=0}^{k}m_{i}
be the overall iteration steps after the
k
-th outer iteration and
C=\frac{(\sqrt{2}-1)}{(\sqrt{2L_{F}}+\sqrt{2\epsilon_{0}})\ln(2(R^{2}+1))}
, then we have the global convergence rate

\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k})\leq\frac{R^{2}+1}{\left(CM_{k}+\epsilon_{0}^{-1/2}\right)^{2}}\quad\forall\,k\geq 0.

(114)

Thus, it takes
M_{k}=O(\sqrt{L_{F}/\epsilon})
iterations to get the accuracy
\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k})=O(\epsilon)
.

Proof 8.13
.

We prove (
113
) by induction. For
k=0
, it holds by choosing
\epsilon_{0}=D_{F}(x_{0},x^{\star})
. Now suppose that
\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k})\leq(R^{2}+1)\epsilon_{k}
and let us consider the
k+1
-th iteration. Since
0<\alpha\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/L_{F}}
and
(1+\alpha)^{-1}\leq 1-\alpha/(1+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/L_{F}})
, the number of inner iterations
m_{k+1}=(\sqrt{L_{F}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}})\ln(2(R^{2}+1))\epsilon_{k+1}^{-1/2}

is chosen so that

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)^{-m_{k+1}}\leq{}

\displaystyle(1-\alpha/(1+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/L_{F}}))^{m_{k+1}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\alpha m_{k+1}/(1+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}/L_{F}})\right)=(2(R^{2}+1))^{-1}.

Therefore, by (
8.10
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x_{k+1},y_{k+1},\epsilon_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2(R^{2}+1)}\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k+1})+\epsilon_{k+1}R^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2(R^{2}+1)}\mathcal{E}(x_{k},y_{k},\epsilon_{k})+\epsilon_{k+1}R^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{k}+\epsilon_{k+1}R^{2}=(R^{2}+1)\epsilon_{k+1}.

Since
\epsilon_{k}=\epsilon_{0}2^{-k}
, after the
k
-th outer iteration, the overall iteration steps is

\displaystyle M_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}m_{i}={}

\displaystyle(\sqrt{L_{F}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}})\ln(2(R^{2}+1))\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(\sqrt{L_{F}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}})\ln(2(R^{2}+1))\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1}\left(\epsilon_{k}^{-1/2}-\epsilon_{0}^{-1/2}\right).

Calculating
\epsilon_{k}
from this and plugging it into (
113
) proves (
114
). As a result, the iteration complexity bound
O(\sqrt{L_{F}/\epsilon})
follows easily.

\Box

In summary, the EPC-VOS method achieves the following complexity:

•

For convex minimization problem
\min f(x)
where
f
is convex and
L
-smooth, the iteration complexity to achieve
f(x)-\min_{x}f(x)\leq\epsilon
is
O\left(\sqrt{\frac{L}{\epsilon}}\right)
.

•

For composite convex minimization problem
\min(f(x)+g(x))
where
f
is convex and
L
-smooth,
g
is convex, non-smooth and the proximal operator is given, and
x^{\star}\in{\mathrm{argmin}\,}f(x)+g(x)
, the iteration complexity to achieve
f(x)-f(x^{\star})+g(x)-g(x^{\star})\leq\epsilon
is
O\left(\sqrt{\frac{L}{\epsilon}}\right)
.

•

For strongly-convex-concave saddle point problem
\min_{u}\max_{p}\mathcal{H}(u,p)=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu,p)
where
f
is
\mu_{f}
-strongly convex and
L_{f}
-smooth,
g
is convex and
L_{g}
-smooth and
(u^{\star},p^{\star})\in\arg\min_{u}\arg\max_{p}L(u,p)
, the iteration complexity to achieve
\mathcal{H}(u,p^{\star})-\mathcal{H}(u^{\star},p)\leq\epsilon
is
O\left(\sqrt{\frac{L_{g}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\|B\|^{2}}{\mu_{f}\epsilon}}\right)
.

•

For convex-concave saddle point problem
\min_{u}\max_{p}\mathcal{H}(u,p)=f(u)-g(p)+(Bu,p)
where
f
is convex and
L_{f}
-smooth,
g
is convex and
L_{g}
-smooth and
(u^{\star},p^{\star})\in\arg\min_{u}\arg\max_{p}L(u,p)
, the iteration complexity to achieve
\mathcal{H}(u,p^{\star})-\mathcal{H}(u^{\star},p)\leq\epsilon
is
O\left(\frac{\|B\|}{\epsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{L_{f}}{\epsilon}+\frac{L_{g}}{\epsilon}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\|B\|}{\epsilon}\right)
.

To the best of our knowledge, these match the optimal complexity for first-order methods
[
29
,
28
]
.
