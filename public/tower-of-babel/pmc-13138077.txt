IGF2BP3 promotes gastric cancer progression by inhibiting ferroptosis through ETV4-mediated regulation of GCH1
Authors: Kan Li, Hui Wei, Fang Li, Ya Zheng, Yongning Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFerroptosis can be inhibited by insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) in cancers. ETS variant transcription factor 4 (ETV4) is aberrantly expressed in various cancers. Elevated transcription of guanosine triphosphate cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1) contributes to tumor malignancy. This study investigated the involvement of IGF2BP3, ETV4, and GCH1 in ferroptosis in gastric cancer (GC).MethodsGC cells and tissue samples were used to detect IGF2BP3, ETV4, and GCH1 expression. The relationships between IGF2BP3, ETV4, and GCH1 were assessed using RNA immunoprecipitation assay, chromatin immunoprecipitation assay, and dual-luciferase reporter assay. BALB/c nude mice were utilized to establish GC tumor xenografts. Cell cloning and Transwell were used to detect the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cells.ResultsIGF2BP3 and ETV4 were upregulated in GC. IGF2BP3 regulated ETV4 protein level by mediating its mRNA stability. Knockdown of ETV4 inhibited GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and promoted their ferroptosis. ETV4 also promoted the transcription of GCH1 by directly binding to its promoter region. GCH1 overexpression diminished the facilitating effect of ETV4 knockdown on ferroptosis in GC. Overexpression of GCH1 also eliminated the promoting impact of IGF2BP3 knockdown on GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Lastly, inhibition of GCH1 reversed the promoting effect of IGF2BP3 overexpression on GC tumor growth in vivo.ConclusionsIGF2BP3 promotes tumor growth and inhibits ferroptosis in GC by regulating ETV4, while ETV4 promotes GCH1 expression by direct interaction with its promoter. GCH1 overexpression counteracts the effects of ETV4 and IGF2BP3 on GC.

Introduction

The pathogenesis of gastric cancer (GC) is a complicated, multistep process featured by a series of genetic and epigenetic alterations that contribute to the imbalance between tumor suppressor and oncogenic signaling pathways.1 Cell death is a natural physiological event tightly regulated by both intracellular and extracellular signals, playing a critical role in varied biological functions, such as homeostasis, development, and disease progression.2 The disrupted balance between cell death and proliferation serves as a fundamental mechanism underlying the formation and growth of malignant tumors.3 Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent cell death with distinct biochemical, morphological, and genetical characteristics compared to other cell death modalities (e.g. necrosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis).4 It is triggered by lipid peroxidation and excessive presence of lipid reactive oxygen species (lipid-ROS), which finally leads to fatal cellular injury.5 Ferroptosis has been found to exhibit tumor-suppressor function in a broad spectrum of pathophysiological conditions, with gastric cancer (GC) being one of them.6

Insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3), previously termed as IMP3, is an m6A reader responsible for interpreting information related to RNA methylation modification and participating in the translation and degradation of downstream RNAs.7 Following its initial discovery due to elevated expression levels in pancreatic cancer, IGF2BP3 has been rapidly recognized as a prominent overexpressed protein across various types of cancers.8 Aberrantly high levels of IGF2BP3 in gastric tumors suggest its potential oncogenic role in GC development.9 Mechanistically, IGF2BP3 exerts oncogenic effects by stabilizing m6A-modified target mRNAs and enhancing their protein expression. Notably, Zhang et al. reported that IGF2BP3 inhibited ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells by enhancing Nrf2 mRNA stability via an m6A-dependent pathway, leading to a compromised therapeutic impact of sorafenib in model animals.10 These findings suggest that IGF2BP3 may participate in ferroptosis regulation through mRNA stability control, although this mechanism remains unclear in GC.

ETS variant transcription factor 4 (ETV4) is a member of the ETS transcription factor family that binds to the GGAA/T core DNA motif and regulates genes involved in proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.11 Overexpression of ETV4 is positively corrected with poor prognosis in different types of malignancies, such as lung adenocarcinoma,12 breast cancer,13 as well as gastric adenocarcinoma.14,15 Additionally, ETV4 enhanced cancer growth, drug resistance, and metastasis mainly by activating the transcription of its target genes.16 For instance, ETV4 stimulates metastasis of breast cancer cells by activating the transcription of epithelial-mesenchymal transition inducers and matrix metalloproteinases.17 As a transcription factor, ETV4 may mediate downstream biological effects of post-transcriptional regulators like IGF2BP3. However, whether ETV4 links IGF2BP3 activity to ferroptosis-related pathways in GC has not been explored.

Guanosine triphosphate cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1) is the rate-limiting enzyme in the process of de novo synthesis of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4).18 It not only acts as a cofactor in neurotransmitter and nitric oxide production but is also recognized as an oncogene in breast cancer.19 Altered BH4 metabolism in the tumor microenvironment can significantly impact tumor resistance to immunotherapy.20 Additionally, GCH1-mediated BH4 synthesis induces lipid remodeling in fibrosarcoma and prevents these tumor cells against ferroptosis.21 The efficacy of BH4 biosynthesis inhibitors on tumor suppression has been widely tested.22 Despite these findings, whether GCH1 could function as a ferroptosis inhibitor in GC warrants further investigation.

Taken together, these observations suggest a potential regulatory axis involving IGF2BP3, ETV4, and GCH1 in cancer-related processes. Here, we hypothesized that IGF2BP3 enhances the translation of ETV4, thereby promoting GCH1, inhibiting ferroptosis, and eventually promoting the progression of GC. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the interplay between IGF2BP3, ETV4, and GCH1 in the ferroptosis of GC cells. By elucidating these molecular relationships, our study aims to provide new insights into GC pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets for this malignancy.

Materials and methods

Gene expression in GC samples

The gene expression data obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and the GSE184336 database were used to compare IGF2BP3 and ETV4 expressions in GC and normal tissues. The survival probability of patients with high or low IGF2BP3 and ETV4 expressions was shown by Kaplan-Meier curves.

Cell culture and treatments

GC cell lines, including MKN-45 (#SCSP-5473), NCI-N87 (#SCSP-534), MKN-7 (#TCHu283), AGS (#SCSP-5262), HGC-27 (#HGC-27), and SNU-5 (#TCHu242), and human gastric epithelial cells GES-1 (#GNHu65), were obtained from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (China) and cultivated as per the standard cell culture protocol provided by the supplier. The culture conditions for these cells were as follows: RPMI-1640 medium plus 10% fetal bovine serum (both from Gibco, USA), maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Overexpression and knockdown cells were established by transfecting cells under a density of approximately 70% using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Several short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) or overexpression plasmid vectors (Gene Pharma, China) were used, including shRNAs targeting ETV4 (sh-ETV4-1, 5′-CCCTGTGTACATATAAATGAA-3′; sh-ETV4-2, 5′-GCTCCGATACTATTATGAGAA-3′; sh-ETV4-3, 5′-CCAGGATCTAAGTCACTTCCA-3′) and GCH1 (sh-GCH1, 5′-GCGAGGATTGTAGAAATCTAT-3′), plasmid vectors overexpressing GCH1 (oe-GCH1), IGF2BP3 (oe-IGF2BP3), and ETV4 (oe-ETV4), and their corresponding negative controls (sh-NC and oe-NC). Following the transfection, cells were cultured for 48 h. Then, transfection efficiency was evaluated using Western blot and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR).

Following transfection, designated groups of cells were challenged with 20 μM of pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (#Cat. No: HY-16658B) to block apoptosis; 20 μM of necrostatin-1 (Nec-1,#Cat. No: HY-15760), an inhibitor of necroptosis; 60 μM of 3-methyladenine (3-MA, #Cat. No: HY-19312), a known autophagy inhibitor that prevents the formation of autophagic vesicles; 10 μM of ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, #Cat. No: HY-100579); or an equal volume of DMSO, used as the solvent control, for 24 h. All reagents were purchased from Merck Millipore (USA). The drugs were administered based on previously published literature and preliminary experiments suggesting these concentrations effectively modulate the respective pathways without affecting cell viability significantly.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The Imprint RIP Kit (Merck Millipore) was utilized to explore IGF2BP3 and ETV4 interaction in GC cells. Cells were treated with a lysis buffer for 25–30 min at 4 °C. Then, cell lysates were harvested and mixed with magnetic beads (Merck Millipore) bound with anti-immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody (Abcam, USA) or anti-IGF2BP3 antibody (Abcam, USA) at 4 °C. Following an incubation for 4 h, the beads were washed and eluted. To verify the efficacy of the RIP procedure, appropriate positive and negative controls were employed. A known RNA that interacts with IGF2BP3 was used as a positive control to validate the specificity of the interaction. As a negative control, an anti-IgG antibody was used, providing an assessment of the non-specific binding. The immunoprecipitated RNAs were isolated to assess ETV4 expression using real-time quantitative PCR. The primer sequences used were as follows: Forward, 5′-AAGGAGTGAGGAGGAAGAGG-3′; Reverse, 5′-CCTGTTGAGAGAGGTGAGG-3′.

mRNA stability assay

To explore the effects of IGF2BP3 on ETV4 mRNA stability, GC cells were transfected with sh-IGF2BP3 or sh-NC. After 48 h, cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 106 per dish and treated with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (Selleck Chemicals, USA) to inhibit de novo RNA synthesis. Cells were harvested at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h following actinomycin D treatment. At each time point, approximately 1 × 106 cells were collected for total RNA extraction. ETV4 mRNA levels were quantified by RT-PCR and normalized to GAPDH expression. The relative ETV4 mRNA abundance (%) at 1-h time point was defined as 100%, and remaining mRNA levels at subsequent time points were expressed as a percentage of this value.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)-quantitative PCR (qPCR)

MeRIP-qPCR was employed to quantify m6A modification on the ETV4 gene as previously described.23 Briefly, RNA was extracted and shredded into fragments using a Seq-Star™ poly(A) mRNA Isolation Kit (cat no. AS-MB-006-01, Arraystar Inc., USA). RNA samples were mixed with magnetic beads and added with anti-rabbit IgG antibody (cat no. 11203D, Invitrogen) or affinity-purified rabbit polyclonal anti-m6A antibody (cat no. 202003, Synaptic Systems, Germany), followed by overnight-incubation with RNase inhibitor (Promega, USA) at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were cleaned with immunoprecipitation buffer to eliminate any non-specifically bound RNA, and the m6A-modified RNA was then eluted. The RNA was subsequently extracted and analyzed using qPCR to quantify the level of m6A modification on the ETV4 gene. The following primers were used: ETV4 forward: 5′-GAGAGGAGGAGAGGAAGAGG-3′; ETV4 reverse: 5′-CCTGTTGAGAGAGGTGAGG-3′. These primers were designed based on the ETV4 gene sequence (Ensembl ID: ENSG00000116207), specifically targeting regions within the coding sequence where m6A modifications occur.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated as aforementioned. Twelve hours later, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) was added directly into each well, and cells were maintained in the same environment for an additional 2 h. The optical density (OD) values at 450 nm were read at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h with a microplate reader, and cell proliferation curves were drawn.

Colony formation analysis

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were treated with 0.25% trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to obtain single cells and resuspended in complete culture medium. Then, cell suspension was diluted, and a total of 100 cells were seeded into each culture dish. Following 2-week incubation at 37 °C, the media were discarded, and cells were fixed with 5 mL of pure methanol for 15 min, followed by 30-min staining with Giemsa solution (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the dishes were covered with a transparent film with grids for colony counting under a light microscope.

Transwell assay

To examine cell migration capacity, 1 × 105 GC cells were plated into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (Corning Inc., USA; 8 µm pore size). The inserts were then placed into a 24-well plate containing 500 µL of culture media in each well. After 24-h incubation at 37 °C, cells remaining on the membrane upper surface were removed, while those that had migrated to the lower place were fixed for 15 min with 95% ethanol and stained 10 min with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich). Stained cells were counted from five randomly chosen fields. The procedure of the invasion assay was similar, except that the membrane was pre-coated with diluted Matrigel (Corning Inc.) before adding cells. Both assays were performed with the presence of 10% FBS serving as a chemoattractant to drive the migratory and invasive behavior.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The intracellular content of glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) was determined using the GSH and MDA ELISA Kit (Abcam, USA), respectively.

Detection of intracellular lipid-reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

To measure intracellular lipid-ROS production, GC cells were incubated with Hanks' Balanced Salts Solution containing 5 μM of C11-BODIPY fluorescent probe (excitation wavelength: 581 nm, emission wavelength: 591 nm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for half an hour at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were subjected to flow cytometry using the NovoCyte Quanteon flow cytometer (USA). The fluorescence intensity of the control group was set as 1.

Immunofluorescence detection of intracellular Fe2+ levels

A volume of 200 μL GC cells (8 × 104) were plated on a dish for fluorescence imaging and cultured overnight. After treatment with DMSO and Fer-1, culture medium was removed, and fresh medium containing 1 μM of FerroOrange (cat no. F374, Dojindo Laboratories) and 0.5 μM Hoechst (cat no. 4082, Cell Signaling, USA) were added to cells for staining. Following 30-min incubation at 37 °C in a light-free room, the staining results were examined under a confocal microscope (Model: LSM 980, ZEISS, Germany). For analysis, the images were processed using ZEN Software (USA). Fluorescent images were captured with the appropriate settings for both FerroOrange (Fe2+) and Hoechst (nucleus). The intensity of the FerroOrange signal was measured to quantify Fe2+ levels inside the cells. The Hoechst signal was used to identify cells and correct for background fluorescence. The FerroOrange intensity was then normalized to Hoechst to account for any differences in cell size or number.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

A Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay system (Promega) was employed to investigate the transcriptional regulation of GCH1 by ETV4. Gastric adenocarcinoma cells NCI-N87 and AGS were plated in a 96-well plate and transiently transfected with a reporter plasmid containing the wild-type (WT) binding site (site 1 or site 2) of GCH1 or a mutant sequence (MUT) and oe-ETV4 or oe-NC. After 48 h, the relative luciferase activity was examined.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

To validate the binding between ETV4 and the GCH1 gene, ChIP assay following qPCR was conducted using NCI-N87 and AGS cells and ChIP-IT High Sensitivity Kit (cat no. 53040, Active Motif, USA). ChIP samples were eluted twice with 60 μL of elution buffer and subjected to PCR with GCH1 promoter-specific primers. The GCH1 promoter-specific primers were designed based on the Ensembl gene database to amplify a region located approximately 200 base pairs upstream of the GCH1 transcription start site. The following primer sequences were utilized: Forward, 5′-ATGACCGTGACCAGGAGGAA-3′; Reverse, 5′-AGGGATGAGAGGGGAGGAGT-3′.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation of mitochondrial morphology

The ultrastructure of mitochondria in GC cells was observed using TEM. In brief, harvested cells were incubated at 4 °C with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight. Then, they were rinsed with 0.1 M PBS and incubated at 4 °C with 1% osmium tetroxide for 3 h. Then, cells were dehydrated, embedded, and polymerized at 70 °C overnight, and sliced into 70-nm sections. These samples were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate at 80 °C for 15 min and then observed using TEM (Model: JEM-F200Multi-purpose, JEOL, Japan).

Patient sample collection

Gastric tissue samples, both cancerous and non-cancerous adjacent tissues (i.e. >5 cm away from the tumor site), were collected using endoscopy from 10 GC patients admitted to our hospital between December, 2023 and April, 2024. The average age of these patients was 56 y, with seven were males. GC was confirmed by pathological examination. The tumors were classified into stages I–IV based on the extent of regional lymph node involvement and the presence of distant metastasis. The histological type was further characterized according to the Lauren classification, which classifies gastric cancer into intestinal-type, diffuse-type and mixed-type based on the pattern of cell growth and histological appearance. There were four patients with stage II, one with stage III, and five with stage IV gastric cancer. Additionally, two patients had the diffuse Lauren histological type, while five had the intestinal type, also three had mixed type. Patients received no treatment, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or surgery, before sample collection. Specimens were kept at 4 °C in RNAlater (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight for RNA preservation and then stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University, ethical approval number is LDYYLL2025-308. All procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

A total of 10 paired samples were used for RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Paraffin-embedded sections (4 µm) from 10 paired samples were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking, sections were incubated with antibodies from Abcam against IGF2BP3, ETV4, and GCH1, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB chromogen. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and observed under a light microscope.

Tumor xenografts

Male BALB/c nude mice (6-week-old) were obtained from Hunan Slake Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. and housed in a standard specific pathogen‐free environment. Animals were then randomly assigned into four groups (n = 5 per group). To establish tumor xenograft models, mice in the oe-IGF2BP3 and oe-NC groups were injected subcutaneously with AGS cells (5 × 106) with normal or excessive expression of IGF2BP3, respectively, into the right axilla. To investigate the function of 2, 4-diamino-6-hydroxypyrimidine (DAHP), mice in the oe-IGF2BP3 + DAHP group were transplanted with GC cells overexpressing IGF2BP3 and intraperitoneally administered with DAHP (dissolved in 50% normal saline + 40% polyethylene glycol + 5% DMSO + 5% Tween 80) at a dosage of 80 mg/kg once every other day until the end of the experiment. The Control group remained untreated. Tumor volume was recorded every week (volume = ½ [length × width2]). Twenty-eight days after inoculation, mice were euthanized, and their tumors were harvested, weighed, and imaged. A portion of tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for IHC staining using antibodies (Abcam) against Ki-67, ETV4, IGF2BP3, and GCH1, while the remainder were used for Western blot analysis.

All animal handlings were approved by the Ethics Committees of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University and conducted following the Ethical Guidelines for the Use of Animals in Research, ethical approval number is LDYYLL2025-496. In addition, all anesthesia and euthanasia procedures followed applicable veterinary guidelines, including the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Mice were euthanized using CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, ensuring rapid loss of consciousness and minimizing pain or distress.

Western blot

GC cells and xenograft tissues, as well as clinical GC and adjacent normal tissues, were lysed with RIPA buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). BCA Protein Assay Kit (Biorbyt LLC, United Kingdom) was used to quantify protein concentration of each sample. Cell or tissue lysates (30 μg per sample) were loaded on 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore Corp, USA). After blocked with Pierce™ Protein-Free Blocking Buffer (Fisher Scientific) for 35 min, membranes were stained with primary antibodies (Abcam) overnight at 4 °C: ETV4 (1:1000; cat no. ab189826), IGF2BP3 (1:1000; cat no. ab177477), GCH1 (1:1000; cat no. ab236387), and β-actin (1:5000 dilution; cat no. 8226). After washing with TBST buffer and incubating with a secondary antibody for 120 min, the enhanced chemiluminescence method was used for protein detection. The protein bands were visualized by the Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Model: Tanon-5200; Tanon Science and Technology, China) and quantified using ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from GC cells, clinical GC tissues, and adjacent normal tissues using 1 mL of TRIzol solution (Sigma-Aldrich) and dissolved in RNase-free water. The QuantiTect® SYBR® Green RT-PCR Kit (Qiagen, USA) was utilized for RT-qPCR in the ABI PRISM 7300 system (Applied Biosystems Inc., USA). The following primers (5′–3′) were used: ETV4 forward, GAAAAACAAGTCGGTGCGCT and reverse, GGGGGCCGAGACCTGA; GCH1 forward, CCTACTCGTCCATCCTGAGC and reverse, GGACCTTTCCAACAAATGGA; IGF2BP3 forward, CTAAACTTCCATGTTGGCTATTATTG and reverse, CAATATTTGGTTTCTTATCCCAAAG; and GAPDH (internal control) forward, AGCCACAATCGCTCAGACAC and reverse, GCCCAATACGACCAAATCC.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times and conducted in triplicate to confirm the consistency of the findings. All data exhibited a normal distribution, thus presenting as mean ± standard deviation. The SPSS software (version 24.0) was used for all data analysis, with one-way analysis of variance utilized to determine significant difference among groups. Multiple group comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction to minimize the risk of false-positive results. Pearson's correlation coefficient was employed to analyze the relationship between different gene expressions. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were drawn to visualize the survival probabilities in high- and low-expression groups. The log-rank test calculates a chi-square statistic to determine whether the observed survival differences are significant. p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

IGF2BP3 and ETV4 were upregulated in GC specimens and cells

Gene analysis using the GSE184336 database revealed both IGF2BP3 and ETV4 were highly expressed in GC tissues compared to normal non-cancerous samples (Figure 1A). Gene analysis using TCGA database showed a similar trend, with IGF2BP3 and ETV4 being highly expressed in GC samples (Figure 1B). Furthermore, the expression of IGF2BP3 and ETV4 in GC tissues was positively correlated (Figure 1C). Additionally, GC patients with high expression of IGF2BP3 or ETV4 had a significantly lower survival probability compared to their counterparts with low expression (Figure 1D). Subsequently, we compared the protein and mRNA expressions of IGF2BP3 and ETV4 between different GC cells and human gastric epithelial cell line GES-1. Upregulation of IGF2BP3 and ETV4 was observed in all GC cell lines compared to GES-1 cells, with the greatest difference seen in NCI-N87 and AGS cells (Figure 1E and F). The above results confirmed the high expression of IGF2BP3 and ETV4 in GC.

(B) Expressions of IGF2BP3 and ETV4 in GC versus normal samples obtained from TCGA database. (C) Correlation between the expressions of IGF2BP3 and ETV4 in GC samples. IGF2BP3 regulated ETV4 protein expression by mediating its mRNA stability

Bioinformatics analysis using RMBase predicted that the ETV4 gene harbors a putative binding site for IGF2BP3 (Figure 2A). Moreover, the SRAMP website predicted the presence of multiple m6A modification sites in ETV4 (Figure 2B). The binding of IGF2BP3 to ETV4 was confirmed by RIP assay (Figure 2C). High m6A levels in the ETV4 gene were observed in both GC cell lines (Figure 2D). To examine whether IGF2BP3 regulates ETV4 at the post-transcriptional level, we examined the stability of the ETV4 mRNA following IGF2BP3 knockdown. GC cells transfected with sh-IGF2BP3 or sh-NC were treated with actinomycin D to block transcription, and ETV4 mRNA decay was monitored over time. As shown in Figure 2E, depletion of IGF2BP3 effectively accelerated the degradation of ETV4 compared to the controls, resulting in a markedly shorter mRNA half-life. These results indicate that IGF2BP3 positively regulates ETV4 expression by stabilizing its mRNA in GC cells. Further Western blot analysis demonstrated that, in both GC cell lines, IGF2BP3 knockdown led to a significant decline in the protein level of ETV4, while IGF2BP3 overexpression markedly increased ETV4 protein expression (Figure 2F). These data collectively suggested that IGF2BP3 regulated ETV4 protein level by mediating its mRNA stability.

Knockdown of ETV4 inhibited GC cells

To further investigate the roles of ETV4 in GC, we utilized three different shRNA sequences to knock down ETV4 in GC cells. The one exhibiting the most robust transfection efficacy (sh-ETV4-1) was selected for further experiments (Figure 3A). ETV4 knockdown significantly reduced the viability (Figure 3B), diminished the colony-forming ability (Figure 3C), and inhibited migration and invasion (Figure 3D) in both NCI-N87 and AGS cells. These data implied that ETV4 knockdown may play a tumor suppressor role in GC.

Effect of ETV4 knockdown on GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. AGS and NCI-N87 cells were transfected with shRNAs targeting ETV4 (sh-ETV4-1, -2, or -3) or sh-NC. (A) Transfection efficiency was evaluated at 48 h post-transfection using RT-qPCR. (B) Cell viability at 24–96 h post-transfection was evaluated by CCK-8 assay. (C) Colony formation assay was conducted to determine cell proliferation. (D) The migration and invasion capacities of different groups of cells were assessed using Transwell assay. (A–D), n = 3; p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Knockdown of ETV4 promoted ferroptosis in GC

Ferroptosis is highly involved in the formation of the GC microenvironment.24 To examine the potential effect of ETV4 in this process, we first treated cells with a ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1, an autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, a pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, and a necroptosis inhibitor Nec-1. The CCK-8 assay revealed that only Fer-1 reversed the inhibitory impact of ETV4 knockdown on GC cell viability (Figure 4A). ELISA showed that knockdown of ETV4 inhibited the production of GSH and increased MDA levels in GC cells, whereas the addition of Fer-1 restored GSH and reduced MDA secretion (Figure 4B and C). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that the ROS levels were markedly increased in ETV4-knockdown cells, while incubation with Fer-1 almost completely inhibited ROS production (Figure 4D). Moreover, the immunofluorescence staining using the FerroOrange probe revealed that silencing the ETV4 gene led to an increase in intracellular Fe2+ content, while the addition of Fer-1 could reverse this effect (Figure 4E). The JASPAR database predicted that the promoter regions of the ferroptosis repressors, GCH1 and DHODH, harbor putative binding sites for ETV4 (Figure 4F). Knockdown of ETV4 downregulated the mRNA expression of GCH1 in NCI-N87 and AGS cells but did not affect the expression of DHODH. Conversely, overexpression of ETV4 (oe-ETV4) significantly increased GCH1 mRNA levels compared with the control vector (oe-NC) in both cell lines (Figure 4G), confirming that ETV4 positively regulates GCH1 transcription. Based on the above data, we speculated that ETV4 knockdown may promote ferroptosis in GC cells by mediating GCH1 expression.

ETV4 promoted GCH1 transcription

To test our hypothesis, we first identified the binding sites between ETV4 and GCH1. Dual-luciferase reporter assay revealed that ETV4 interacted with the promoter site 1 of GCH1 (Figure 5A). ChIP assay further confirmed the binding of ETV4 to the site 1 of the GCH1 promoter (Figure 5B). Gene analysis of TCGA samples showed that GCH1 is highly expressed in GC (Figure 5C). RT-qPCR analysis of clinical GC samples versus paired adjacent non-cancerous tissues also showed that GCH1 was upregulated in GC (Figure 5D). Furthermore, knockdown of ETV4 suppressed GCH1 expression at both mRNA (Figure 5E) and protein (Figure 5F) levels. These results indicated that transcription factor ETV4 promoted the transcription of ferroptosis-related protein GCH1 and further enhanced its protein expression.

GCH1 overexpression diminished the promoting effect of ETV4 knockdown on ferroptosis in GC

To ascertain the regulatory effect of ETV4 on GCH1 in GC, we co-transfected ETV4-knockdown cells with plasmid vectors overexpressing GCH1. The results showed that knockdown of ETV4 inhibited the RNA levels of both ETV4 and GCH1, and overexpression of GCH1 reversed the inhibitory effect of knockdown of ETV4 on the expression of GCH1 (Figure 6A). Knockdown of ETV4 reduced the GSH level and promoted the MDA level, while after overexpression of GCH1, the GSH levels in both GC cell lines were restored, and the production of MDA was inhibited (Figure 6B and C). The overexpression of GCH1 also inhibited the increase in ROS caused by ETV4 knockdown (Figure 6D) and reversed the increase in Fe2+ in gastric cancer cells caused by ETV4 gene knockout(Figure 6E). Moreover, knockdown of ETV4 resulted in increased mitochondrial membrane density, reduced mitochondrial volume, disappearance of cristae structure, and outer membrane rupture, while overexpression of GCH1 reversed these effects (Figure 6F). These findings suggested that overexpression of GCH1 can reverse the ETV4 knockdown-induced ferroptosis in GC cells.

Knockdown of GCH1 or ETV4 eliminated the enhancing impact of IGF2BP3 overexpression on GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To examine the effects of ETV4 and GCH1 on IGF2BP3-mediated GC progression, we further co-transfected IGF2BP3-overexpressing cells with sh-ETV4 or sh-GCH1. Overexpression of IGF2BP3 increased ETV4 and GCH1 protein expression in GC cells, while knockdown of ETV4 reversed IGF2BP3-induced upregulation of ETV4 and GCH1, and knockdown of GCH1 only reversed the upregulation of GCH1 (Figure 7A and B). Knockdown of ETV4 or GCH1 reversed IGF2BP3-induced increase in GC cell viability (Figure 7C) and colony-forming ability (Figure 7D). Additionally, Transwell assay demonstrated that knockdown of ETV4 or GCH1 partially impeded IGF2BP3-induced migration and invasion of both NCI-N87 and AGS cells (Figure 7E). Taken together, either GCH1 or ETV4 knockdown successfully reversed IGF2BP3-induced GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Inhibition of GCH1 in vivo reverses the promoting impact of IGF2BP3 overexpression on GC tumor growth

Lastly, a GC tumor xenograft model was developed to examine the involvement of GCH1 and IGF2BP3 in gastric carcinogenesis in vivo. Over 28 d, overexpression of IGF2BP3 promoted tumor progression in nude mice, leading to increased tumor size, while GCH1 inhibitor (DAHP) counteracted the oncogenic effect of IGF2BP3 (Figure 8A–C). Western blot showed that overexpression of IGF2BP3 led to an increase in the protein levels of IGF2BP3, ETV4 and GCH1 in tumor tissues, while DAHP fundamentally suppressed GCH1expression (Figure 8D). Consistently, IHC staining revealed excessive tumor expressions of Ki67 and GCH1 in mice inoculated with IGF2BP3-overexpressing GC cells, whereas GCH1 inhibitor suppressed the expressions of both Ki67 and GCH1 (Figure 8E). The in vivo data indicate that overexpression of IGF2BP3 promotes GC tumor growth, while GCH1 inhibition can reverse this process.

Discussion

Ferroptosis is a tumor-suppressing process that influences tumor formation and drug resistance by mediating various biological features of cancer development, including oncogenic mutations, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, immune responses, and epigenetic alterations.25 Therefore, targeting ferroptosis has been considered a potential anticancer strategy for clinical application.26 In the present study, we observed that IGF2BP3 inhibited ferroptosis in GC cells and promoted tumor growth by mediating GCH1 through the mediation of ETV4 translation. These findings contribute to future research on the potential mechanism and application of IGF2BP3-mediated ferroptosis in GC treatment.

High IGF2BP3 expression is associated with multiple unfavorable pathological characteristics in GC, such as perineural invasion, lymph node metastasis, and lymphovascular invasion.27 As an m6A reader, IGF2BP3 has been reported to induce GC progression by enhancing the RNA stability and translation efficiency of its target genes via an m6A-dependent way. For instance, IGF2BP3 promoted GC cell invasion and migration by facilitating PKMYT1 mRNA stability via its m6A modification site.28 In addition, IGF2BP3 induced angiogenesis, tumor growth, and liver metastasis in GC by directly recognizing and binding to the m6A site of HDGF mRNA and enhancing its stability.29 On the contrary, circular RNA-TNPO3 functions as a protein decoy for IGF2BP3, diminishing its ability to stabilize MYC mRNA, thereby suppressing the migration and proliferation of GC cells.30 Here, we observed significantly elevated expression of IGF2BP3 in both GC tissues versus non-cancerous tissues and GC cells versus normal gastric epithelial cells. Moreover, IGF2BP3 knockdown in GC resulted in a significant decrease in the protein level of ETV4, and this effect was achieved by reducing the stability of ETV4 mRNA. It is worth noting that the GC cell lines used in this study represent different histopathological subtypes of gastric cancer, including gastric carcinoma, gastric tubular adenocarcinoma, and gastric adenocarcinoma. This heterogeneity may contribute to the observed differences in baseline IGF2BP3 and ETV4 expression among the cell lines.

Aberrant upregulation of ETV4 has been observed in 28 human tumors, with high expression linked to poor overall survival, progression-free interval, and disease-specific survival.31 Consistently, Cai et al. found that GC patients with high ETV4 levels had a higher occurrence rate of lymph node or distant metastasis than those with low ETV4 expression.32 They also found that the metastatic capacity of GC cells with insufficient ETV4 expression was remarkably decreased when compared with the controls, suggesting that knockdown of ETV4 reversed the malignant progression of GC cells. Consistent with these findings, our data showed that ETV4 knockdown effectively reduced viability, suppressed colony-forming ability, and inhibited migration and invasion in both GC cell lines. The oncogenic role of ETV4 in cancer development by mediating ferroptosis has also been reported. Wang et al. reported that, in papillary thyroid cancer cells, ETV4 binds directly to the promoter region of a key factor responsible for cysteine uptake in ferroptosis, upregulating its transcription and thereby reducing ferroptosis, while ETV4 knockdown inhibited the progression of papillary thyroid cancer by promoting ferroptosis.33 In this study, ETV4 directly binds the promoter of GCH1, promoting its transcription. GCH1 overexpression can reverse the effects of ETV4 knockdown on ferroptosis, and also rescues the inhibitory effects of IGF2BP3 knockdown on GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. While cell invasion and migration are mechanistically independent of ferroptosis, they are functionally linked through the IGF2BP3-ETV4-GCH1 regulatory axis. Collectively, these results highlight that this axis exerts its oncogenic role by simultaneously regulating GC cell ferroptosis and malignance.

As a transcription factor, ETV4 is capable of facilitating tumor growth by promoting transcription of its target genes by binding to their promoter regions.34 In breast cancer, ETV4 contributes to the maintenance of cancer stemness by transcriptionally upregulating the chemokine receptor CXCR4.13 In gastric cancer, ETV4 induced proliferation and suppressed apoptosis of GC cells by upregulating KIF2A transcription, a microtubule depolymerase, via direct binding to its promoter.35 Here, we found that ETV4 interacted with the promoter site of GCH1 and promoted its transcription, while knocking down ETV4 suppressed both GCH1 mRNA and protein expression. Furthermore, GCH1 overexpression reversed the ETV4 knockdown-induced ferroptosis in GC cells. GCH1 is the rate-limiting enzyme responsible for converting GTP into dihydroneopterin triphosphate.36 It can drive epithelial-mesenchymal transition in breast cancer.37 In addition, an animal study conducted by Liu et al. showed that upregulating BH4 biosynthesis by inducing the transcription of GCH1 in GC cells contributed to the progression of tumor malignancy.19 Our data illustrated from the opposite perspective that inhibition of GCH1 reversed GC tumor growth both in cell culture and nude mice.

In summary, IGF2BP3 facilitated tumor cell growth and suppressed ferroptosis in GC by regulating ETV4 translation in an m6A-dependent manner. Furthermore, ETV4 promoted GCH1 expression by directly interacting with the GCH1 promoter. Overexpression of GCH1 reversed the effects of both ETV4 and IGF2BP3 knockdown on GC tumor growth. Although this study has some limitations, such as a small sample size and not including all types of GC cell lines, these results highlight the regulatory importance of the IGF2BP3/ETV4/GCH1 axis in ferroptosis. It suggests promising therapeutic implications for targeting this axis in GC treatment. Exploring additional ferroptosis-related pathways involved in IGF2BP3-mediated GC progression is essential for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies. Based on the IGF2BP3–ETV4–GCH1 regulatory axis identified in this study, future research is also needed to explore the combined anti-tumor effects of IGF2BP3 inhibitors with ferroptosis-inducing drugs, providing a potential new strategy for clinical therapy.
