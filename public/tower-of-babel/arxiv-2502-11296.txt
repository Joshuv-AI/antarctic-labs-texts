Active solids: defect self-propulsion without flow
Fridtjof Brauns, Myles O’Leary, Arthur Hernandez, Mark J. Bowick, M. Cristina Marchetti

Abstract
The self-propulsion of + 1 / 2 +1/2 topological defects is a hallmark of active nematic fluids , where the defects are advected by the flow field they themselves generate. In this paper we propose a minimal model for defect self-propulsion in a nematic active solid : a linear elastic medium with an embedded nematic texture that generates active stress and associated elastic strains. We show that such coupling gives rise to self-propelled + 1 / 2 +1/2 defects that move relative to the elastic medium by local remodeling of the nematic texture without advection. This mechanism is fundamentally different from the fluid case and can lead to unbinding of defect pairs and stabilization of + 1 +1 defects. Our findings might help explain how orientational order, of, for example, muscle fibers, is reconfigured during morphogenesis in solid-like tissues. The proposed mechanism may, for instance, control motility and merging of + 1 / 2 +1/2 defects, which play a crucial role in setting up the body axis during Hydra regeneration.

In this paper we examine topological defects in an active nematic elastomer, where forces are generated internally by active processes. We propose a minimal model for a linear elastic medium with an embedded nematic texture that generates active stress and in turn is coupled to elastic strain. We show that such coupling gives rise to self-propelled
+1/2
defects that move relative to the elastic medium by local remodeling. If the self-propulsion is sufficiently large,
+1/2
defects can escape the Coulomb-like attraction to a
-1/2
defect.
In confined systems where defects are enforced through boundary anchoring, the elasto-nematic coupling gives rise to rich dynamics, including orbiting
+1/2
defects, and stabilized
+1
defects that can spontaneously deform from aster/vortex configurations to spirals.

Minimal elasto-nematic sheet model.

The main motivation for our model is epithelial tissue with nematically organized acto-myosin fibers, as found for instance in the ecto- and endoderm of
Hydra

Philipp et al. 2009
;
Aufschnaiter et al. 2017
;
Maroudas-Sacks et al. 2021
.
We denote the displacement field by
u_{i}
and the (linear) strain tensor as
u_{ij}=(\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{i})/2
.
Nematic order is described in terms of the symmetric, traceless tensor order parameter
Q_{ij}=S(n_{i}n_{j}-\delta_{ij}/2)
, where
S
is the magnitude of order and the director
\mathbf{n}\equiv-\mathbf{n}
,
|\mathbf{n}|\equiv 1
, describes the orientation of the nematic.
The free energy includes the usual Landau–de Gennes term for the isotropic–nematic transition, Frank elastic coupling in the single elastic constant
K
approximation, and linear elasticity parametrized by the shear modulus
\mu
and the bulk modulus
B
:

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2}\int\!\mathrm{d}r^{2}\,\Big[-\left(1+\chi_{B}u_{kk}\right)Q_{ij}Q_{ij}+(Q_{ij}Q_{ij})^{2}\\
+K(\nabla_{i}Q_{jk})^{2}+2\tilde{u}_{ij}(\mu\tilde{u}_{ij}-\chi Q_{ij})+Bu_{kk}^{2}\Big],

(1)

where we have chosen coefficients such that
2Q_{ij}Q_{ij}=S^{2}=1
for the uniform equilibrium when
\chi=\chi_{B}=0
.
The coefficient
\chi
accounts for strain alignment of nematic order along the direction of deviatoric strain
\tilde{u}_{ij}
, aligning them when
\chi>0
and anti-aligning them for
\chi<0
.
The coefficient
\chi_{B}
describes the coupling of the nematic order to isotropic strain
u_{kk}
. We call this “strain ordering.” For
\chi_{B}>0
, nematic order decreases (“melts”) in regions of compressive strain (
u_{kk}<0
).
Both strain alignment and strain ordering can either be of passive origin, or active, due to feedback-mediated regulation of the cytoskeletal organization in response to strain

He et al. 2015
;
Gupta et al. 2015
;
Maroudas-Sacks et al. 2024a
.

We consider adiabatic dynamics in a regime of quasi-static force balance where the nematic texture remodels on a time scale much slower than that of force equilibration through deformations.
This assumption is justified in a tissue where elastic forces balance on short time scales (seconds to minutes) compared to the remodeling of the actomyosin fibers that form the nematic texture (hours).
Force balance within the elastic sheet requires
\partial_{i}\sigma_{ij}=0
, where the total stress
\sigma_{ij}=\sigma^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}+\sigma^{\mathrm{a}}_{ij}
includes the passive elastic stress
\sigma^{\mathrm{el}}_{ij}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta u_{ij}}
and active isotropic and deviatoric stresses

\sigma^{\mathrm{a}}_{ij}=\frac{\alpha_{B}}{4}S^{2}\delta_{ij}+\alpha Q_{ij}
.
Positive (negative) signs of
\alpha_{B}
and
\alpha
correspond to contractile (extensile) activity.
Together, this gives

\sigma_{ij}=2\mu u_{ij}+(B-\mu)u_{kk}\delta_{ij}+\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{B}}{4}S^{2}\delta_{ij}+\tilde{\alpha}Q_{ij}\;,

(2)

where we have introduced the effective active stress strengths
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}=\alpha_{B}-\chi_{B}
and
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha-\chi
that capture the total isotropic and deviatoric stresses exerted by the nematic. We neglect passive stresses due to the nematic molecular field which are higher order in gradients than the active nematic stresses.
Throughout the manuscript, we set
K=\mu=B/2=1
. Unless specified otherwise, we set
\chi_{B}=-2
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}=0
.

The nematic texture evolves through relaxational dynamics to minimize the free energy,

\gamma\partial_{t}Q_{ij}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta Q_{ij}}\,,

(3)

where

\gamma
is the rotational viscosity. We non-dimensionalize time such that
\gamma=1
.
We assume a stiff elastic material, such that displacements are small, allowing us to neglect convective and co-rotational terms.

The effective active stresses induce elastic stresses which can relax through remodeling of the nematic texture.
Geometrically, the induced elastic stresses can be understood as a consequence of metric incompatibility (intrinsic curvature) as is briefly discussed in Appendix
A
.
As the texture remodels, the
active
stresses will, however, lead to further incompatibility. In other words, activity makes the coupling between elastic strain and nematic order non-reciprocal, thus allowing for the emergence of dynamic steady states
You et al. 2020
;
Saha et al. 2020
;
Fruchart et al. 2021
, as shown below.

\mathbf{Q}_{+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{2}S_{1/2}(r)\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\theta}&\sin{\theta}\\
\sin{\theta}&-\cos{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(4)

where
(r,\theta)
denote polar coordinates. Equation (
4

+1/2
defect with
\chi=\chi_{B}=0

The magnitude of order
S_{1/2}(r)
vanishes at the defect core

and is
1

r\gg\sqrt{K}

Let us first consider the case of purely deviatoric induced stress (
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}=0
).
Taking the divergence of the force balance equation,
\partial_{i}\sigma_{ij}=0
gives an equation for the isotropic strain

(B+\mu)\nabla^{2}u_{kk}=-\tilde{\alpha}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Q_{ij}\,,

(5)

which for
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}_{+\frac{1}{2}}
has the approximate solution
u^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{kk}(\mathbf{r})\approx-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}S_{1/2}(r)}{2(B+\mu)}\frac{x}{r}
(see SI).
For contractile active stress (
\tilde{\alpha}>0
), as expected for instance in a tissue like a
Hydra’s
ectoderm
West 1978
, this strain field is tensile in front of the defect and compressive behind it [see Fig.
1
(d)].
In our model, this strain gradient will lead to a free energy density gradient across the defect through the strain-ordering coupling
\chi_{B}
.
Thus, remodeling of the nematic texture, Eq. (
3
), will propel the defect in the direction that reduces this free energy.
As the defect moves, the strain field generated by active stresses shifts along with it such that there will always be a strain gradient across the defect core, sustaining the effective force acting on the defect.
This propulsion mechanism is markedly different from the fluid case, where the defect is advected by the velocity field it actively induces
Giomi et al. 2013
;
Pismen 2013
.

To estimate the defect propulsion force, we formulate Eq. (
3
) variationally and make the ansatz
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{r},t)=\mathbf{Q}_{+\frac{1}{2}}[\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}(t)]
, which yields (see Appendix
B
for details)

\gamma_{+\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{r}_{0}=\bm{f}_{\chi_{B}}^{+\frac{1}{2}}+\bm{f}_{\chi}^{+\frac{1}{2}}+\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ext}},

(6)

with the effective friction (inverse mobility)
\gamma_{+\frac{1}{2}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}r^{2}(\nabla_{j}Q_{ik})^{2}
, and
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}
accounting for any external effective forces acting on the defect (e.g. from interaction with other defects). The self-propulsion forces are given by

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\chi_{B}}^{+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=-\chi_{B}\int\!\mathrm{d}r^{2}\,\nabla u_{kk}^{+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{r})\left[1-S_{1/2}^{2}(r)\right],

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\chi}^{+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=-\chi\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\,\tilde{u}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{ij}(\mathbf{r})\nabla Q^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{ij}(\mathbf{r}).

(8)

In the SI, we show that
\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\chi}
is independent of
\tilde{\alpha}
and proportional to
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}
, i.e. it vanishes for
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}=0
.
The factor
(S^{2}_{1/2}-1)
in
\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\chi_{B}}
is nonzero only inside the defect core (
r\lesssim\sqrt{K}
), in agreement with the intuition that the strain gradient across the defect core drives defect propulsion.
For and isolated defect (
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
) and
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}=0
, numerical integration yields

\mathbf{v}_{0}=\frac{\bm{f}_{\chi_{B}}^{+\frac{1}{2}}}{\gamma_{+\frac{1}{2}}}\approx 0.4\,\frac{\tilde{\alpha}\chi_{B}\sqrt{K}}{\gamma_{+\frac{1}{2}}(B+\mu)}\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}\;.

(9)

Importantly, the direction of propulsion of the
+1/2
is determined by both the sign of the active stress
\alpha
and that of the coupling parameter
\chi_{B}
.
In particular, this allows a system with contractile activity to have a defect move towards its head.
This is in contrast to active fluids, where the sign of activity alone controls the direction of propulsion such that
+1/2
defects move towards their tail for contractile activity.

To numerically validate the analytic estimate for
\bm{f}_{\chi_{B}}^{+\frac{1}{2}}

+1/2
defect in disk with suitable anchoring boundary conditions. Boundary anchoring leads an effective force
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}=-\frac{\pi}{2}K\mathbf{r}_{0}/(R^{2}-r_{0}^{2})
that pushes the defect towards the disk center and can be calculated by placing a
+1/2
image charge at
(R^{2}/r_{0}^{2})\mathbf{r}_{0}

Equation (

Role of substrate traction.

The ectoderm of
Hydra
is separated by the endoderm by a layer of extracellular matrix that serves as a substrate to the tissue. The role of a rigid substrate can be incorporated by introducing a spring-like traction force
-ku_{i}
which modifies force balance to read
\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij}=ku_{i}
. Long-ranged elastic interactions then become screened, with a screening length
\ell_{\mathrm{el}}\sim\sqrt{(B+\mu)/k}
. Such a screening plays a role analogous to that of the viscous screening length in active fluids
Rønning et al. 2022
and eliminates system-size dependence arising form the long-range nature of elastic forces (e.g., in the stability condition for
+1
defects Eq. (
26
), see Appendix
D
).

Importantly, traction provides an additional mechanism for feedback between mechanics and nematic order, as it allows a term
\lambda u_{i}u_{j}Q_{ij}
in the free energy Eq. (
1
) describing alignment of
Q_{ij}
to the displacement field. This may
represent, for instance, alignment of actin fibers along the direction of traction force. In the limit of strong coupling to the substrate, we can neglect elastic stresses and solve the force balance equation for the displacement field as
u_{i}=\frac{\alpha}{k}\partial_{j}Q_{ij}
. Alignment with displacement then gives a contribution
(\lambda\alpha^{2}/k)\partial_{k}Q_{ik}\partial_{l}Q_{jl}
to the dynamics of
Q_{ij}
. Interestingly, this term generates a defect propulsion with direction independent of the sign of activity and determined entirely by the sign of the alignment interaction. For parallel alignment of nematic order and traction,
+1/2
defects will always move towards their head.

Discussion.

In active nematics
+1/2
, defects are generically expected to self-propel due to their polar symmetry. Such motility is transient in equilibrium, but becomes sustained in active systems. It has been studied extensively in
fluids
where defects are advected by the active flows they generate. Here we have proposed new mechanisms for self-sustained defect propulsion in active nematic
solids
where defects move relative to the elastic medium via local remodeling of the nematic texture.

Our work opens up many directions of future investigations.
Additional elasto-nematic couplings that make the bend and splay Frank elastic constants differentially dependent on isotropic strain could mediate defect propulsion due to the bend-splay gradient across the
+1/2
defect.
This is in fact the more likely scenario for defect propulsion in
Hydra
, where no melting of order ahead of
+1/2
defects has yet been observed.

It would also be interesting to examine the effect of noise, which we expect will lead to spontaneous defect unbinding analogously to the active fluid case
Shankar et al. 2018
;
Shankar and Marchetti 2019
.
In biological contexts defect creation and unbinding might also result from coupling to morphogen concentration fields
Wang et al. 2020
. An important direction for future work is therefore to augment the current model by including such morphogen concentration fields which can, in turn, be sourced by the concentrated stresses near topological defects
Ferenc et al. 2020
;
Maroudas-Sacks et al. 2024a
. Such a feedback loop may provide an alternate mechanism for the stabilization of the
+1
defect at the head of
Hydra

Wang et al. 2020
;
Maroudas-Sacks et al. 2024a
.

Finally, self-propulsion through local melting may provide an explanation for a puzzling observation in cell monolayers, where defect motion often suggests extensile activity despite contractile forces at the cell scale
Saw et al. 2017
;
Kawaguchi et al. 2017
;
Balasubramaniam et al. 2021
. Several explanations have been proposed
Vafa et al. 2021
;
Killeen et al. 2022
;
Bonn et al. 2022
;
Dedenon and Kruse 2024
, including most recently that cell motion is primarily driven by propulsive traction with the substrate and that the nematic texture of cell shapes and associated topological defects is a passive response
Nejad et al. 2024
;
Bera et al. 2025
. The mechanism proposed here provides an alternative explanation, that does not require cell motility. The telltale sign of this mechanism is that defects move relative to the cells, as it is observed in regenerating
Hydra

Maroudas-Sacks et al. 2021
. It will be interesting to investigate this in other tissues via the simultaneous use of defect tracking and Particle Image Velocimetry.

Appendix A
Connection to metric elasticity and shape programming

Our model has important connections to activated nematic elastomers which are often modeled using the framework of metric elasticity. There, the internal conformation change of the elastomer is described through a “target strain” that deforms the internal reference configuration of the material
Griniasty et al. 2019
;
Duffy and Biggins 2020
;
Warner 2020b
.
In the free energy Eq. (
1
), these target strains are captured by the elasto-nematic couplings
\chi_{B}
and
\chi
. Indeed, these terms can be rewritten as target strains by completing the square
Maitra and Ramaswamy 2019
.
When the target strains are geometrically incompatible with a flat realized configuration, i.e. when they have have intrinsic curvature
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Q_{ij}\neq 0
, residual elastic stresses remain even after the elastic energy has been minimized. When the elastic sheet is allowed to deform out of plane, the residual stresses lead to buckling. This is the mechanism used for shape programming in nematic elastomers
Griniasty et al. 2019
;
Duffy and Biggins 2020
.
In the language of geometric charges,
\tilde{\alpha}Q_{ij}
plays the role of a curvature quadrupole
Lemaître et al. 2021
.

Appendix B
Variational ansatz for defect propulsion

We use a variational formulation to find the dynamics of a
+1/2
defect due to elasto-nematic coupling.
To this end, we introduce the Rayleigh dissipation functional

\mathcal{R}\bigl[\dot{\mathbf{Q}}\bigr]=\frac{\gamma}{2}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\left(\partial_{t}Q_{ij}\right)^{2},

(10)

such that we can write the
\mathbf{Q}
dynamics Eq. (
3
) in a variational form

\gamma\partial_{t}Q_{ij}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{R}}{\delta(\partial_{t}Q_{ij})}=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta Q_{ij}}\,.

(11)

Physically, this equation expresses the power balance between the change of free energy and dissipation.
To find the approximate equation of motion for the
+1/2
defect, we substitute the ansatz
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{r},t)\approx\mathbf{Q}^{+\frac{1}{2}}[\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}(t)]
into the variational equation, to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{R}}{\delta\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{0}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mathbf{r}_{0}}\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\,(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{0}\cdot\nabla Q_{ij}^{+\frac{1}{2}})\nabla Q_{ij}^{+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\left.\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta Q_{ij}}\right|_{\mathbf{Q}^{+\frac{1}{2}}}\!\nabla Q^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{ij}\,.

(13)

Evaluating the left-hand side yields the effective friction coefficient

\int_{0}^{R}\!\mathrm{d}r\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!r\mathrm{d}\theta\left[\left(S_{1/2}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j}+\frac{S_{1/2}^{2}}{r^{2}}\hat{\theta}_{i}\hat{\theta}_{j}\right]\\
=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}\int_{0}^{R}\!\mathrm{d}r\left[\frac{S_{1/2}^{2}}{r}+r(S_{1/2}^{\prime})^{2}\right]=:\gamma^{+\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{ij}\;,

(14)

The effective friction (inverse mobility) of a defect diverges logarithmically with increasing system size because defect motion requires global remodeling of the nematic texture.
In a system with multiple defects, the dissipation rate depends on the locations of the other defects and is “screened” on the scale of the typical defect separation
Denniston 1996
;
Tóth et al. 2002
.

To evaluate the right-hand side of Eq. (
13
), we use integration by parts and the fact that
\mathbf{Q}^{+\frac{1}{2}}
remains a free energy minimum of
\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{(0)}
under translation. Therefore, only the terms coupling
\mathbf{Q}
to strain remain:

\gamma^{+\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{r}_{0}=\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}:=\underbrace{{}-\chi_{B}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\,\left[1-S_{1/2}^{2}(r)\right]\nabla u_{kk}(\mathbf{r})}_{\displaystyle\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\chi_{B}}}\\
\underbrace{{}-\chi\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\,\tilde{u}_{ij}(\mathbf{r})\nabla Q_{ij}^{+\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{r})}_{\displaystyle\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\chi}}.

(15)

Alternatively, this equation can be obtained through a systematic perturbation ansatz as in Ref.
Shankar et al. 2018
.
First, rewrite Eq. (
3
) as

\gamma\partial_{t}Q_{ij}=F^{(0)}_{ij}+F^{(1)}_{ij}\;,

(16)

where
F^{(0)}_{ij}
and
F^{(1)}_{ij}
collect terms to zeroth and first order in
\chi,\chi_{B}
.
Using the fact that
\nabla Q_{ij}^{+\frac{1}{2}}
is in the nullspace of the linearization of
F^{(0)}_{ij}
yields the solvability condition

\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\left[F^{(1)}_{ij}-\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{0}\cdot\nabla Q_{ij}^{+\frac{1}{2}}\right]\nabla Q^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{ij}=0\,,

(17)

which recovers the equation of motion of the defect position Eq. (
15
) we obtained using the variational ansatz.

Appendix C
Strain-alignment mediated defect propulsion

Strain alignment drives defect an propulsion force
\bm{f}_{\chi}^{+\frac{1}{2}}
if the alignment energy density
\chi\tilde{u}_{ij}Q_{ij}
.
To calculate this force, we consider the contributions from deviatoric active stress (
\sim\tilde{\alpha}
) and isotropic active stress (
\sim\tilde{\alpha}_{B}
) separately.
In the SI, we show that the former contribution vanishes identically.
To find the contribution due to isotropic active stress, first observe that the stress profile is azimuthally symmetric around the defect core. Therefore the induced elastic strain will have the same symmetry and its principal axes will be radial and azimuthal as illustrated in Fig.
4
(a).
We use the Airy stress formalism (see SI) and the approximation
S_{1/2}(r)\approx\Theta(r-\sqrt{K})
, to find the deviatoric strain

\tilde{u}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{rr}\approx-\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{B}K}{8(B+\mu)\,r^{2}}\,\Theta(r-\sqrt{K})\,.

(18)

Substituting into the expression for
\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\chi}
[cf. Eq.
15
] yields

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\chi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi\chi}{2}\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,\int_{0}^{R}\!\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,\tilde{u}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{rr}(r)\,\partial_{r}\!\left(\frac{S_{1/2}(r)}{r}\right)\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\pi}{16}\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{B}\chi\sqrt{K}}{B+\mu}\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,.

(20)

This result agrees well with numerics; see Fig.
4
(b).

Taken together, we have

\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}\approx\left(0.4\,\tilde{\alpha}\chi_{B}-\frac{\pi}{16}\tilde{\alpha}_{B}\chi\right)\frac{\sqrt{K}}{B+\mu}\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(21)

showing the “duality” of the two defect propulsion mechanisms: the first operating through the combination of deviatoric nematogen-induced stress
(\sim\tilde{\alpha})
and strain ordering
(\sim\chi_{B})
; the second operating through the combination isotropic nematogen-induced stress
(\sim\tilde{\alpha}_{B})
and strain alignment
(\sim\chi)
.

) agrees well with numerics. The mechanical boundary conditions (clamped: red circles; free: blue circles) plays only a minor role.

.)

Note that in the purely passive case
\alpha=\alpha_{B}=0
(i.e.
\tilde{\alpha}=-\chi
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{B}=-\chi_{B}
), the propulsion force for a defect at the center of a disk-shaped domain does not vanish:

\bm{f}^{+\frac{1}{2}}_{\mathrm{passive}}\approx 0.2\,\frac{\chi\chi_{B}\sqrt{K}}{B+\mu}\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}\,.

(22)

In this case the defect moves away from the domain center by extracting elastic energy from the elastic medium. Of course, this passive motion is transient and ends when the free energy
\mathcal{F}
has reached a minimum.
Calculating this minimum-energy position analytically is cumbersome because one can no longer exploit azimuthal symmetry.

Appendix D
+1 stability criterion

The isotropic strain concentrated at the defect core is sufficiently strong to completely “melt” the nematic order there via isotropic elasto-nematic coupling.
In this case it is energetically favorable to localize the entire topological charge in the melted region.
We can therefore estimate the active stress required to stabilize a
+1
defect by requiring that the core is in the isotropic regime, i.e.
\chi_{B}u_{kk}(r=\sqrt{K})<-1
.

There is a family of
+1
defects parametrized by the “phase”
\psi_{0}

\mathbf{Q}^{+1}(\mathbf{r};\psi_{0})=\frac{1}{2}S_{1}(r)\begin{pmatrix}\cos(2\psi_{0}+2\theta)&\sin(2\psi_{0}+2\theta)\\
\sin(2\psi_{0}+2\theta)&-\cos(2\psi_{0}+2\theta)\end{pmatrix},

(23)

where
\psi_{0}=0,\pi/2
correspond to an aster and vortex respectively. For other values of
\psi_{0}
, the texture is a spiral with pitch angle
\psi_{0}
.
Substituting into Eq. (
5
) yields

\displaystyle(B+\mu)\nabla^{2}u^{+1}_{kk}\approx-2\pi\tilde{\alpha}\cos(2\psi_{0})\,\delta^{2}(\mathbf{r})\,,

(24)

where we have made a far-field approximation (monopole term in multipole expansion) for
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}Q^{+1}_{ij}
(see SI for details).
Note that the induced elastic strain vanishes for the
\psi_{0}=\pi/4
spiral. In this case, the “target strain” induced by active stress and strain-alignment is geometrically compatible, i.e. has no intrinsic curvature.

This is solved by the Green’s function for the Laplacian

(B+\mu)u^{+1}_{kk}(r)=-\tilde{\alpha}\cos(2\psi_{0})\log\frac{r}{R}\,,

(25)

where we assume that isotropic strain (and hence pressure) vanish at the boundary.
Substituting into
\chi_{B}u_{kk}(r=\sqrt{K})<-1
gives the estimated stability condition for
+1
defects

\frac{\tilde{\alpha}\chi_{B}}{\mu+B}\cos(2\psi)<-\frac{1}{\log(R/\sqrt{K})}\,.

(26)

Comparing to numerics shows that Eq. (
26
) slightly underestimates the magnitude of
\tilde{\alpha}
required to stabilize the
+1
defect.
Nonetheless, Eq. (
26
), provides several qualitative insights.
First, in the region where the texture spontaneously twists into a spiral
+1
defects can only be stabilized when the spiral pitch angle
\psi_{0}
is sufficiently small. Indeed, there is only a very small regime with stable
+1
spirals near the onset of spiral formation [see (
ii
) in Fig.
3
].
Second, a
+1
defect stabilizes more readily for a larger domain, since more active stress builds up over a larger area.
Thus, the defect stabilization through elasto-nematic coupling is very different from stabilization through spatial confinement alone, where the latter requires a small domain size on the order of the nematic coherence length
\sqrt{K}
.
In the presence of spring-like attachment to a substrate, elasticity becomes screened such that the system size in Eq. (
26
) is replaced by the screening length (see SI).
