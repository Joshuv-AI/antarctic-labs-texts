A novel sono-activatable nanoreactor induces precision intratumoral juglone generation and caspase 3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis for treatment of bladder cancer
Authors: Yuanfeng Zhang, Enguang Yang, Xinyu Zhang, Yibo Shi, Guangrui Fan, Yuhan Wang, Junqiang Tian, Hanzhang Wang, Zhilong Dong, Yingru Wang, Liang Cheng, Zhiwen Zhao, Yonghai Zhang, Baodui Wang, Zhiping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJuglone, a natural naphthoquinone compound, exhibits potent anticancer activity but faces clinical limitations due to poor solubility, low bioavailability, and systemic toxicity. While tumor microenvironment-responsive drug activation strategies offer a promising solution, achieving precise spatial control over the conversion of non-toxic precursors into cytotoxic agents remains a significant challenge.MethodsWe developed DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, a platinum (Pt)-methylene blue (MB)- based nanoscale sonosensitizer, to enable ultrasound-triggered on-demand juglone synthesis within tumors. The system was evaluated in bladder cancer models including cell lines, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and patient-derived tumor xenograft (PDX) models, assessing tumor uptake, intracellular distribution, hypoxia modulation (“H2O2-O2-¹O2” cascade), oxidative stress markers (SOD, GSH, MDA, GSSG), and cell death pathways (apoptosis/pyroptosis). Transcriptome sequencing was performed to elucidate molecular mechanisms.ResultsDHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were internalized by UMUC-3 and T24 cells and primarily accumulated within the mitochondrial compartments. The platinum-based components catalytically exhausted GSH via redox cycling. DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs triggered the production of O2 and ¹O2 after local US irradiation, alleviating hypoxia and selectively converting dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN) to juglone in tumors, which reducing the systemic toxicity of juglone. The nanosystem further disrupted cellular redox balance by triggering depletion of antioxidant defenses (SOD and GSH) and reducing the ratio of GSH/GSSG. In vitro cell experiments and PDOs experiments demonstrated that DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs combined with US irradiation can significantly inhibit bladder cancer cell proliferation, induce apoptosis, and regulate pyroptosis via the ROS-mediated caspase 3/GSDME signaling pathway. In the PDX model, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs accumulated at the tumor site and significantly inhibited tumor growth under US irradiation.ConclusionsAs a novel sonosensitizer and pyroptosis inducer, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs achieve a precise synergistic effect of chemotherapy and sonodynamic therapy within cells, while minimizing adverse effects on normal cells and overcoming the limitations associated with natural chemotherapeutic agents. Introduction

Bladder cancer (Bca) ranks among the top 10 malignancies globally, with 573,000 new cases and 212,000 deaths reported in 2020 [1, 2]. In recent years, despite advances in immunotherapy and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), limitations in cost and efficacy persists [3]. Therefore, there is a need to find and develop new technologies or medicines to improve the therapeutic efficacy of Bca.

Natural naphthoquinones have attracted great interest due to their ability to modulate multiple signaling pathways, often exhibiting specific biological activities and high selectivity in recent years. Juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone), from walnut trees, show promising antitumor effects by inhibiting cancer cell growth and triggering ROS-mediated apoptosis [4]. However, juglone’s clinical use is limited by poor solubility, low bioavailability, and toxicity [5, 6]. Direct introduction of chemotherapeutic or cytotoxic agents can lead to poor distribution of drugs in the organism, which can damage normal tissue. Non-toxic small molecule prodrugs such as banoxantrone and 5-dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN) are uptaken by tumor cells and can be specifically synthesized into highly toxic small molecule chemotherapeutic drugs, resulting in a high therapeutic efficacy while minimizing damage to normal tissues and/or organs [7].

Dynamic therapies (DT) are becoming one of the attractive cancer treatment modalities in the past decade. The clinical application of photodynamic therapy (PDT) in the treatment of Bca has been reported in relevant literature. However, PDT for Bca has limitations such as shallow tissue penetration (< 5 mm), uneven light distribution, and the need for prolonged photosensitivity precautions. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) overcomes these issues by utilizing ultrasound waves with deeper penetration, eliminating light avoidance requirements and improving safety. Additionally, ultrasound cavitation enhances drug penetration and triggers stronger reactive oxygen species(ROS)-mediated tumor killing, making it a promising alternative for Bca therapy [8–10].

Building upon these advances, sophisticated sensitizer design is now crucial. Coordination polymers (CPs) have emerged as promising candidates, offering structural advantages for enhanced ROS generation in DT. Recent studies show that well-designed CP-based photosensitizers enable efficient O₂ activation. Their architectures facilitate photosensitizer (PS) isolation, reducing aggregation-induced quenching (AIQ) and boosting singlet oxygen (¹O₂) production [11, 12]. Moreover, the constructed CP-based photosensitizer systems can efficiently decrease the singlet-triplet energy gap (ΔEₛₜ) between the sensitizer’s lowest-energy excited singlet (S₁) and triplet (T₁) states [13, 14].

Tumor heterogeneity often limits the efficacy of single-mechanism therapies, underscoring the need for multi-pathway synergistic strategies. In our previous work, a coordination polymer, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was developed, which integrated the photosensitizer methylene blue (MB), a platinum (IV) prodrug, and non-toxic DHN to enhance antitumor efficacy through PDT and multi-mechanistic synergy [15]. Unlike conventional PDT systems that solely rely on ROS-mediated cytotoxicity, our system not only demonstrated significantly enhanced ¹O₂ production, but also achieved dual oxidative damage through selective DHN oxidation to generate the cytotoxic compound juglone. Furthermore, it exhibits spatiotemporal controllability and superior tissue penetration capability. Moreover, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs further induced ROS-mediated caspase 3/GSDME-dependent apoptosis/pyroptosis in Bca under ultrasound activation. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models confirmed enhanced oxidative stress and tumor suppression (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Juglone (98%) from AbMole (USA); DTNB and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F-12 (DMEM-F12) from Thermo Scientific (USA); RPMI-1640 medium, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) from GIBCO (USA); Cell permeable probes (Mito-Tracker Green, C1048; caspase 3 inhibitor (Z-DEVD-FMK), ROS inhibitor (N-Acetylcysteine, NAC) from MCE (China); superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay kit, reduced glutathione (GSH) colorimetric assay kit, oxidized glutathione (GSSG) colorimetric assay kit, and malondialdehyde (MDA) colorimetric assay kit from Thermo Scientific (USA); anti-cleaved caspase-1 antibody (HY-P80622) from MCE (China); anti-GSDMD antibody (ab219800), anti-N-terminal GSDME antibody (ab215191), anti-N-terminal GSDMD antibody (ab215203), and Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Assay Kit (ab102526) from Abcam (USA).

Preparation of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs

Following our previous method, Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were synthesized, and DHN was subsequently loaded to obtain DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs [15]. A reaction vessel was filled with 1 mL of 1.0 mM MB aqueous solution, 10 mg of PVP and 10 mL of 0.4 mM H2PtCl6 aqueous solution. Reaction mixtures were irradiated with visible light (420 nm < λ < 780 nm filter) in a constant temperature water bath at 37 °C. After 1 min of irradiation, Pt4+ and MB rapidly coordinated and self-assembled into shuttle-shaped PtMBCPsNSs. When the irradiation time was further extended to 30 min, stable Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were obtained, which were then collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm) and repeatedly washed with ethanol. The DHN loading efficiency was calculated based on established protocols [16, 17]. Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (2.0 mg) were dispersed in 2 mL of DHN/ethanol solution (2 mg/mL) and magnetically stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 5 min) and washed with ethanol 3 times.

Characterization of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs

Sample morphology was analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI, Sirion 200) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI, Sirion 200). The spatial distribution of elemental components within the nano-spindle structure was determined using high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) coupled with elemental mapping. Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Nicolet 170SX) and ultraviolet -visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry were used for chemical and optical characterization. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed, with spectra fitted using the Gauss-Lorentz method and Shirley background correction. Photosynthesis was performed using a xenon lamp (HSX-F/UV 300), equipped with 400–780 nm filters.

Stability of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs

A freshly prepared DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs suspension was diluted and vortex-mixed thoroughly, followed by measurement of hydrodynamic diameter using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Brookhaven Instruments Corporation, USA). Zeta potential measurements were performed using a particle size analyzer.

The stability of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was evaluated by incubating them in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at different pH values (5.5,6.0,7.4), saline, RPMI-1640 medium, and RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The experiments were conducted under constant temperature conditions at 37 °C. DLS was employed to monitor changes in particle size of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs after 24 h of incubation, while TEM was used to examine morphological alterations. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV2550 spectrophotometer, while fluorescence spectra were obtained using a Hitachi F7000 fluorometer.

Ultrasound irradiation system

The ultrasound (US) irradiation was performed with the LIPUSTIM® 330 Sonodynamic Therapy System (Sxultrasonic, China). Ultrasound with a frequency of 1.0 MHz, a duty cycle of 50%, and a power intensity of 500 mW/cm²was selected for subsequent experiments (The screening process for US irradiation intensity and duration was detailed in Figure S1).

Detection of oxygen (O2) generation and singlet oxygen (1O2) generation

A total of 40 mL of H2O2 (10 mM) PBS buffer solution (pH 6.0) and 100 µL of 10 mg/mL Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were mixed in a 50 mL flask and bubbled with nitrogen for 15 min to remove dissolved oxygen (O2) from the solution, after which the O2 production was measured by a Dissolved Oxygen Meter (JPBJ-609 L, China) over a period of 30 min at 37℃. In addition, the O2 production of PBS buffer solution alone (pH 6.0) and Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was also tested under the same conditions. Finally, continuous O2 production was measured after the addition of H2O2 (10 mM) to the Pt/PtMBCPsNSs dispersion at 30 min intervals.

DPBF served as a sensitive detection probe for singlet oxygen (1O2). A total of 100 µL of 10 mg/mL Pt/PtMBCPsNSs solution was added to a PBS buffer system (pH 6.0) containing 0.28 mg/mL DPBF. The UV-visible absorption spectra of DPBF was measured in the presence/absence of H2O2 upon US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle).

GSH-triggered DHN release, GSH depletion, and catalytic oxidation of DHN

DNH@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (2 mg) in a dialysis bag (MWCO 3500 Da) were incubated with 15 mL PBS (pH 6.0 and 7.4) with/without 10 mM GSH at 37℃. Released DHN was quantified by UV-Vis at timed intervals.

GSH levels were determined using the DTNB assay. Equal volumes (500 µL) of 10 mM GSH and DTNB solutions were mixed with Pt/PtMBCPsNSs suspension, and absorbance at 412 nm was monitored over 60 min.

A reaction system containing Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (100 µL, 10 mg/mL) and DHN (30 mL, 100 µM in DMSO/PBS, pH 6.0) was sonicated. DHN consumption (298 nm, ε = 7664 M⁻¹cm⁻¹) and juglone formation (419 nm, ε = 3500 M⁻¹cm⁻¹) were tracked by UV-Vis at various time points. Conversion efficiency was calculated as described [18, 19].

Cell lines and animals

Human bladder cancer cell lines UMUC-3 and T24 cells (from Chinese Academy of Sciences) were maintained in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS and antibiotics at 37 °C.

Male nude mice (3–4 weeks, 18–20 g, Cancer Derived Xenograft, CDX) and male NCG mice (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) (3–4 weeks, 18–20 g, Patient-Derived Xenograft, PDX) from GemPharmatech (China) were housed under standardized conditions. All procedures were approved by Lanzhou University Second Hospital Ethics Committees (Human: No.2024 A-804; Animal: D2024-917, D2024-918) and complied with NIH guidelines.

Cellular uptake and intracellular distribution

To evaluate the cellular uptake efficiency of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, UMUC-3 and T24 cells (8 × 104/well in 12-well plates) were treated with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs. After 0.5, 1, and 2 h incubation, uptake was visualized by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Zeiss) and quantified using the ImageJ.

To further investigate the subcellular localization, specific organelles were labeled with two fluorescent probes targeting the lysosomes (Lyso), and mitochondria (Mito). UMUC-3 and T24 cells treated with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (40 µg/mL, 2 h), while mitochondria were stained with MitoTracker Green (37 °C, 40 min) and lysosomes were stained with LysoTracker Green DND-26 (37 °C, 30 min), followed by analysis using CLSM.

Intracellular O2 assay and ROS assay

UMUC-3 and T24 cells (8 × 105/well in 6-well plates) were cultured for 24 h, then treated under hypoxia (1% O2, 5% CO2 and 94% N2) for 24 h with PBS (control), Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (40 µg/mL), DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (40 µg/mL). Intracellular hypoxia was measured using Ru(dpp)3Cl₂ (λex = 455 nm) by fluorescence microscopy.

Cells treated with nanoparticles (NPs) (40 µg/mL, 2 h) received US irradiation for 1 min (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle). ROS was detected using DCFH-DA, with nuclei counterstained by Hoechst. Analysis was performed by CLSM and flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Cytotoxicity and apoptosis assay in vitro

UMUC-3 and T24 cells were treated with various NPs for 24 h, followed by 1 min US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle). Groups included: (1) saline, (2) DHN, (3) US, (4) Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, (5) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, (6) DHN + US, (7) Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US, and (8) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US. The saline served as the control group. The concentrations of both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were all at a concentration of 40 µg/mL. Additionally, cells were also incubated with juglone (0–40 µM) for 24 h.

To evaluate the the cytotoxic effects of the NPs under different conditions, cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 and Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (AM/PI) live-dead staining kit. Apoptosis was analyzed with an Annexin V-FITC kit and quantified by flow cytometry.

Evaluation of cellular oxidative stress and cellular pyroptosis

UMUC-3 and T24 cells were seeded in 24-well (6 × 104 cells/well) or 12-well plates (8 × 105 cells/well) and divided into groups: (1) saline, (2) Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, (3) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, (4) Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US, (5) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US, (6) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US + Z-DEVD-FMK (caspase 3 inhibitor, 50 µM, pre-treatment), and (7) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US + NAC (ROS inhibitor, 2.5 mM, pre-treatment). NPs (40 µg/mL) were applied, and US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle, 1 min) was used where indicated. Besides, cells were treated with juglone (0–40 µM) for 24 h.

Intracellular levels of SOD, GSH, GSSG, and MDA were measured. Morphological changes were observed by microscopy, and the TEM and SEM was performed after glutaraldehyde fixation. Pyroptosis-related proteins (NLRP3, caspase 1, caspase 3, GSDMD, GSDME) were analyzed by western blot, while LDH levels were determined through colorimetric analysis. The levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the cell culture supernatant were measured using the ELISA method. Cell death rates were assessed by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry.

Construction of Bca-PDOs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs-mediated antitumor effects

We established Bladder cancer Patient-derived organoids (Bca-PDOs) following the protocols described by Lee and Shi et al. [20, 21]. The study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Lanzhou University Second Hospital, Lanzhou, China (No.2024 A-804), and written informed consent was obtained from patient. The 68 years male patient, diagnosed with muscle invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), received four cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine + cisplatin) before robotic assisted radical cystectomy (RARC), resulting in tumor shrinkage (Figure S2).

Bca-PDOs were maintained in DMEM-F12 medium containing 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), 5% FBS, 10 µM Y-27,632, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin/amphotericin B, cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Fresh tumor specimens were kept on ice during transport, then washed, finely minced, and enzymatically digested using collagenase/hyaluronidase. Following centrifugation and resuspension, the tissue fragments were further dissociated with TrypLE Express, passed through a 100 μm cell strainer, combined with Matrigel, and plated as 20–30 µL droplets in 24-well plates. The Matrigel droplets were allowed to polymerize for 30 min at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ before adding 1.5 mL of complete organoid culture medium to each well, with medium changes performed every 3 days. Organoids were passaged weekly (1:3 − 1:6 ratio) upon reaching > 100 μm. The protocol comprised: collagenase type 4 digestion (1 mg/mL, 37 °C, 30 min), centrifugation (400 g, 3 min), Trypsin-EDTA treatment (0.25%, 10 min), FBS-neutralization, and resuspension in fresh collagen matrix for replating.

After establishing stable Bca-PDOs, the PDOs were co-incubated with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (40 µg/mL) for 2 h, followed by 1-min US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle). Morphological changes in PDOs were monitored by bright-field microscopy, while cell viability was assessed via AM/PI assay.

Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-sequencing)

UMUC-3 cells were planted into a 6-well plate at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well. In the experimental group, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were co-incubated for 2 h, followed by US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle) for 1 min. In the control group, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were replaced with PBS only, while the other treatments remained the same as those in the experimental group. Cells were collected for RNA sequencing after 24 h (frasergen, China).

Establishment of xenograft models and evaluation of antitumor activity and biosafety

CDX Model: Male Nude mice aged 3 ~ 4 weeks were injected intravenously into the vein of the tail with 1 × 106 UMUC-3 cells. When tumor volumes reached 80 ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly allocated into 5 groups (n = 5): (1) saline, (2) Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, (3) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, (4) Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US, and (5) DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US. Treatments were administered via the vein of tail injection every 2 days (100 µL injection volume, with both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs at 40 µg/mL concentration). US irradiation was performed at 24 and 48 h post-injection (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle, 3 min).

PDX Model: Male NCG mice aged 3 ~ 4 weeks were implanted with patient-derived tumor fragments. The human tumor samples were obtained from the same patients used for PDOs generation and were employed to establish PDX models in NCG mice. Freshly resected bladder tumor tissues were placed in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, then transported on ice to the laboratory. Tissues were minced into 3 × 2.5 mm fragments and incubated in Matrigel for 10 min. Fragments were then subcutaneously implanted into the axilla of mice as first-generation PDX (Passages 1, P1). When tumor xenografts reached 80 ~ 100 mm3, they were excised and transplanted into new NCG mice to generate second-generation PDX (Passages 2, P2). Subsequently, the same procedure was repeated to establish third-generation PDX (Passages 3, P3) for subsequent experiments [22]. The PDX tumor was validated through immunohistochemistry (IHC) of urothelial plate proteins II (UPK2) and urothelial plate proteins III (UPK3B) [23]. Mice (P3) were randomly allocated into 3 groups (n = 4): (1) control, (2) SDT (DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US), (3) juglone. Control group mice received saline via intraperitoneal injection. The juglone group was intraperitoneally administered juglone (3 mg/kg) every other day. The SDT group received DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (40 µg/mL) through the vein of tail injection every two days, followed by US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle, 3 min duration) at 24 and 48 h post-injection.

Tumor volumes (mm3) were determined using the formula: 0.5 × length × width2. Both tumor size and body weight were measured every 48 h. Humane endpoints were implemented when tumors exceeded 1000 mm3. Tumor tissues, blood samples, and major organs (including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and small intestine) were harvested for toxicity evaluation and subsequent analysis. Processed blood samples (PDX model) were analyzed for biochemical markers of hepatic and renal function, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), as well as routine hematological parameters such as white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (HGB), red blood cell count (RBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and platelet count (PLT). Additionally, tissue sections from the harvested organs were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Tumor tissues were further analyzed through H&E staining, Ki67 staining, TUNEL assay, and IHC.

Additionally, we conducted a hemolysis assay using fresh rat red blood cells (RBCs). We performed the hemolysis assay according to the previously established experimental protocol [24]. RBCs (4% suspension) were incubated with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (37 °C, 2 h), centrifuged (3000 RPM, 15 min), and hemolysis (%) was calculated by the following formula: hemolysis (%)=[(ODsample - ODnegative)/(ODpositive - ODnegative)] × 100%. Hemolysis acceptability criterion: An hemolysis ratio (HR) < 5% confirms non-hemolytic properties, meeting biomedical material standards.

Biodistribution of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs in vivo

Animal in vivo fluorescence imaging system (Fluorvivo) was used for the detection of drug distribution in animals. UMUC-3 cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3%) for in vivo fluorescence imaging. DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs (20 mg/kg) were intravenously administered, and biodistribution was tracked using IVIS Xenogen system (Caliper Life Sciences) at various time points. Mice were euthanized at 24/36 h post-injection and fluorescence intensities in major organs (hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys) and tumors were quantified via Living Image software (V3).

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed with the software SPSS 21.0, continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (), and comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Two-by-two comparisons were made between groups. The LSD-t test was applied if the variance was homogeneous, and Tamhane’s T2 was applied if the variance was not homogeneous. P-values < 0.05 were taken to indicate that the difference was statistically significant. Significance levels were denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Characterization and in vitro evaluation of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs

The photochemical synthesis produced PtMBCPs with shuttle-like nanostructures as templates, with these prefabricated nanoshuttles being transformable into Pt/PtMBCPsNSs under visible light exposure. The synthesis process of PtMBCPsNSs and Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was depicted in Figure S1. Under visible light irradiation for 30 min, the Pt/PtMBCPs structure remained intact, preserving its shuttle-like shape (Figure S3). SEM revealed a spindle-like morphology with a length of 151.63 ± 3.55 nm and a width of 53.04 ± 4.7 nm (Fig. 2A-a, S4). TEM further confirmed the shuttle-shaped structure of Pt/PtMBCPs, while high resolution transmission electronmicroscopy (HRTEM) demonstrated the distribution of platinum elements on the NPs (Fig. 2A-b, c). The HAADF-STEM image and elemental mapping of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs demonstrated that Pt, C, N, and S are uniformly distributed within the nano-shuttle (Fig. 2B). Detailed UV-Vis absorption spectra and FT-IR spectral features of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were presented in Figure S5A. For reference, the FT-IR spectra, XPS survey spectra and high-resolution XPS spectra of MB can be found in our previous work [15]. In contrast to MB, Pt/PtMBCPsNSs displayed a distinct new absorption band at 414 cm− 1, which can be attributed to the coordination interaction between the nitrogen atoms of MB and Pt centers (Figure S5B). Figure 2C showed the FTIR spectra of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, where the characteristic peaks of phenolic hydroxyl and carbonyl groups confirmed the successful loading of DHN.

The XPS spectra not only revealed characteristic peaks of C, N, S, Cl and O, but also demonstrated the emergence of distinct Pt 4f peaks in Pt/PtMBCPsNSs compared with MB, which conclusively confirmed the successful synthesis of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs. The corresponding high-resolution XPS spectra were provided in Figure S6: Pt⁰ (71.4 eV) and Pt4⁺ (73.8/77.0 eV) species coexisted, with N 1 s (398.4 eV vs. MB 396.9 eV) and S 2p (163.1/164.7 eV vs. MB 161.4/162.9 eV) shifts indicating Pt4⁺-ligand coordination. BJH analysis revealed that Pt/PtMBCPsNSs predominantly exhibit a mesoporous structure (Figure S7A). Similar to other mesoporous materials, Pt/PtMBCPsNSs demonstrated potential for adsorbing prodrug molecules. UV-vis spectroscopy (characteristic peak at 298 nm) confirmed the successful adsorption of DHN onto the material (Figure S7B). Besides, the drug loading capacity of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was calculated to be 0.20 mg/mg, indicating its promising potential as an efficient nanocarrier for DHN delivery.

The zeta potentials of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs were measured to be −25.678 mV and − 23.805 mV, respectively in physiological buffer (Fig. 2D and Figure S8). The physiological stability of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was systematically evaluated by incubating the samples in various media, including PBS at different pH values (5.5, 6.0, 7.4), saline, RPMI-1640 medium, and serum, for 24 h. As shown in Figure S9, the shuttle-like morphology of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs remained intact under all conditions. Furthermore, Figure S10 demonstrated that the hydrodynamic diameter of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs in serum exhibited no significant changes across different time points (0, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h). Similarly, after 24 h incubation in other solvent systems, the hydrodynamic diameter remained stable (Figures S11).

The O₂ generation capacity of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was quantitatively evaluated using dissolved oxygen meter. As shown in Fig. 2E, rapid O₂ release occurred upon addition of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs to H₂O₂ solution, whereas negligible production was detected in their absence. Moreover, the catalyst exhibited excellent recyclability and stability across four consecutive cycles (Figure S12). The DPBF probe was used to measure ¹O2 generation by Pt/PtMBCPsNSs. Under US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle), Pt/PtMBCPsNSs showed significantly higher ¹O2 production than DPBF alone (Fig. 2F). H2O2 was added to simulate the tumor microenvironment (TME). Pt/PtMBCPsNSs exhibited enhanced ¹O2 generation in H2O2-containing solution (Figure S13). This is attributed to the cascade reactions: H₂O₂ → O₂ → ¹O₂ (ROS generation cascade).

DHN controlled release studies were performed under varying physiological conditions. The controlled release of DHN exhibited significant dependence on both pH and GSH concentration. Under weakly acidic conditions (pH 6.0) with 10 mM GSH, the cumulative release reached nearly 80%, whereas only 30.5% release was observed in the absence of GSH. When the GSH concentration was maintained at physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the release efficiency further decreased to below 20% (Fig. 2G), which can be inferred that the drug release of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs was caused by the decomposition of Pt/PtMBCPsNSs in the presence of GSH.

Besides, in light of the redox reaction (2Pt⁴⁺ + 4GSH → 2Pt²⁺ + GSSG + 4 H⁺), UV-Vis spectroscopic monitoring at 412 nm revealed substantial consumption of GSH (Figure S14); Under US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle) in PBS buffer (pH 6.0), the characteristic absorption intensity of DHN at 298 nm progressively decreased, while a synchronous increase in absorbance of juglone at 419 nm was observed (Fig. 2H-a). This characteristic bathochromic shift in the UV-Vis spectra unequivocally demonstrated the oxidative conversion process. In contrast, control experiments without US irradiation showed absence of oxidation products (Fig. 2H-b). The DHN photooxidation showed a reaction rate constant (k) of 0.041 min⁻¹ and achieved a juglone yield of 87.3%, with subsequent four cyclic catalytic yields remaining largely stable (Figure S15).

When incubated with the cells, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs exhibited a gradual increase in intracellular red fluorescence intensity from 0.5 to 2 h, indicating efficient endocytic uptake by the tumor cells (Fig. 3A).

Anti-tumor effects in vitro

The CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay showed that the viability of UMUC-3 cells treated with saline, US, DHN, or DHN + US remained above 90% (Fig. 4A). While Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs showed concentration-dependent toxicity without US (Fig. 4B), significant cell death occurred only after 48–72 h (Figure S16A). Both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, when combined with US, significantly enhanced cytotoxicity, with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs showing superior efficacy. Additionally, the two treatment groups exhibited comparable cytotoxic effects on UMUC-3 and T24 cells after 48–72 h of incubation (Figure S16B). This equivalence originates from the GSH-mediated reduction of Pt (IV) to active Pt (II) in Pt/PtMBCPsNSs.

The synergistic therapeutic effect was subsequently evaluated using a live/dead cell staining assay. Bright green fluorescence was observed in the control. UMUC-3 and T24 cells treated with Pt/PtMBCPsNSs or DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs exhibited partial red fluorescence. However, after 1-min US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 500 mW/cm2, 50% Duty Cycle), tumor cells treated with Pt/PtMBCPsNSs or DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs showed a significantly higher proportion of red fluorescence, consistent with the CCK-8 assay results, demonstrated the significant antitumor effect of US-triggered SDT on Bca cells (Fig. 4C).

The H&E results demonstrated consistent expression patterns between Bca-PDOs and their parental tumor tissues (Fig. 4D). The Bca-PDOs in the control group maintained intact morphological structure, clear boundaries, and tightly arranged cells. After 2 h of incubation with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs followed by US irradiation, the cell membranes of PDOs began to rupture, their originally distinct structural boundaries gradually blurred, and cells started detaching from the PDOs. By day 5, the overall structure of PDOs became loose, tissue integrity was lost, cells appeared disordered and scattered, accompanied by content leakage and abundant cellular debris in the surrounding area, ultimately leading to complete disintegration (Fig. 4E). Besides, AM/PI staining revealed only trace red fluorescence inside the control PDOs. Following DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US intervention, the PDOs exhibited mixed red-green fluorescence, with detached cells showing significantly more intense red fluorescence (dead cells) and markedly reduced green fluorescence (live cells) (Fig. 4F), confirming potent US-activated antitumor activity.

Anti-tumor effects in vivo

To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of nanoparticle-mediated SDT in vivo, a UMUC-3 cell-derived xenograft (CDX) mouse model was established (Fig. 5A). Both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs alone showed modest tumor suppression, consistent with in vitro live/dead staining results. Notably, US irradiation significantly enhanced tumor regression in both NPs groups (Fig. 5B). By the endpoint, US-treated groups exhibited markedly reduced tumor volume and weight, with the DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US combination showing the strongest inhibition (Fig. 5C, D). H&E staining revealed typical necrotic features with nuclear condensation, fragmentation, and dissolution in both US-activated nanoparticle groups. IHC analysis revealed significantly reduced proliferative activity as evidenced by decreased Ki-67 staining. Furthermore, TUNEL assays demonstrated extensive apoptotic cell death in SDT groups (Figure S17). These results robustly confirmed the excellent anti-tumor performance of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs upon US irradiation.

A patient-derived xenograft (PDX) animal model was established to compare the anti-tumor effects of juglone and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNS + US (SDT). DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US induces apoptosis in bladder cancer cells

Flow cytometry apoptosis staining showed that more than 50% of the cells in the Pt/PtMBCPsNSs group underwent apoptosis after US irradiation. Of these, the DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US group demonstrated maximum cell apoptosis of more than 70%, while some apoptosis was observed in the Pt/PtMBCPsNSs group and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs group (Figure S20). DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US demonstrated a significantly enhanced therapeutic effect compared to other treatment groups.

DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US induces pyroptosis in bladder cancer cells

During the experimental observations, UMUC-3/T24 cells treated with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US exhibited distinct morphological alterations with light microscopy: cell swelling, visible “blebs” or “balloon-like” changes at the cell membrane periphery (arrow), and subsequent cytoplasmic content release which closely resembled the morphological changes characteristic of pyroptosis [25] (Fig. 7A). TEM observations revealed that tumor cells treated with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US for 6 h exhibited mild mitochondrial swelling accompanied by cell membrane perforation. After 12 h of treatment, significant mitochondrial swelling was observed along with cell membrane rupture, displaying a pyroptotic morphology (red arrows for swollen mitochondria; red circles highlight perforation and rupture of the cell membrane) (Fig. 7B). SEM further confirmed the formation of pores in the cell membrane and the release of cytoplasmic contents (Figure S21). The colorimetric assay revealed significant LDH release from tumor cells into the tumor microenvironment (TME) in both US-irradiated groups (Fig. 7C). ELISA results confirmed that the levels of IL-1β and IL-18 were significantly elevated in the Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US groups, supporting the induction of pyroptosis (Fig. 7D).

DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US triggers pyroptosis via the caspase 3/GSDME pathway rather than the canonical NLRP3/caspase 1 axis

The western blot results demonstrated comparable expression levels of NLPR3, caspase 1, GSDMD, and N-GSDMD across different groups in both UMUC-3 and T24 cells, suggesting the NLPR3/caspase 1/GSDMD pathway might not be involved in this process (Fig. 7E). We subsequently investigated the expression patterns of caspase 3, GSDME, and its activated form N-GSDME in tumor cells subjected to various treatments. Western blot analysis results revealed both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US treatments markedly enhanced the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and N-GSDME fragments (Fig. 7F). These findings suggested that DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs can effectively induce pyroptosis in Bca cells through the caspase 3/GSDME signaling cascade upon US irradiation.

RNA sequencing on UMUC-3 cells treated with SDT was performed to elucidate the mechanism of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs in anti-tumor activity. Compared with the control, the RNA sequencing revealed 3,232 differentially expressed genes (with 2,188 transcripts upregulated and 1,044 transcripts downregulated). After performing principal component analysis (PCA), the results of the DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US group showed significant differences compared to the control group (Figure S22A). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses indicated that pathways related to cytokine/TNF/NOD-like receptor pathways in SDT, supporting pyroptosis (Fig. 7G, H). The Volcano plots (Figure S22B), heatmaps (Fig. 7I), and GSEA (Figure S22C) further supported pyroptosis-associated gene expression and inflammatory factor release trigger by DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US.

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of juglone on UMUC-3 cells was 21.34 µM, and T24 cells was 17.98 µM (24 h) (Fig. 7J). Western blot analysis demonstrated that juglone treatment induced significant alterations in the key apoptotic proteins BAX/BCL-2 in both UMUC-3 and T24 cells. While the expression of cleaved caspase-3 showed dose-dependent upregulation with increasing juglone concentrations, the protein levels of GSDMD, N-GSDMD, GSDME, and N-GSDME remained unaltered (Fig. 7K). Although microscopic observation confirmed juglone’s cytotoxic effects on tumor cells, no characteristic pyroptotic morphological changes were observed (Figure S23). These findings indicated that while juglone can effectively induce apoptosis in Bca cells, it fails to trigger pyroptosis.

DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US-induced pyroptosis is mediated by ROS-dependent activation of the caspase 3/GSDME pathway

DEVD-FMK (a caspase 3 inhibitor) was employed to further verify whether DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US induces pyroptosis through the caspase 3/GSDME pathway. The results demonstrated that Z-DEVD-FMK effectively suppressed the pyroptotic morphological changes induced by DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US (Fig. 8A) (the red arrows for apoptosis; the yellow arrows for pyroptosis). Western blot analysis demonstrated that DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US (SDT) upregulated the expression of cleaved caspase-3 and N-GSDME in Bca cells. Compared to SDT, pretreatment with Z-DEVD-FMK significantly downregulated the levels of both cleaved caspase-3 and N-GSDME (Fig. 8B), confirming that inhibition of caspase 3 activation impairs GSDME cleavage and N-GSDME generation, thereby suppressing pyroptosis. Additionally, when tumor cells were pre-treated with Z-DEVD-FMK before SDT, the level of LDH, IL-1β and IL-18 release was significantly reduced (Fig. 8C-E). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US potently induce apoptosis in Bca cells, whereas pre-treatment with Z-DEVD-FMK markedly decreased the percentage of dead cells (Figure S24).

Discussion

Bladder cancer is one of the most common malignancies in the urinary system. Although current clinical treatments, including surgical resection, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, are widely used, their therapeutic efficacy remains significantly limited. Tumor progression involves a complex regulatory network of multiple pathways and mechanisms. In recent years, multi-mechanism synergistic strategies, integrating chemotherapy, SDT, PDT, and immunotherapy, have emerged as promising approaches to overcome the limitations of conventional cancer treatments. In this context, nanomedicines, by exploiting their advantages in precise drug administration, controlled release, and multifunctional integration, give unique insights for Bca treatment.

Juglone belongs to the natural naphthoquinone, as the glycoside of hydrogenated nutraquinone (trihydroxynaphthalene), which has a variety of pharmacological effects, among which anti-tumor effects are particularly prominent. Nevertheless, due to the relative complexity of the extraction and purification process, poor water solubility and systemic toxic side effects of juglone, it has not yet been able to access the clinical application. Our previous study found that the strategy of converting non-toxic drugs into cytotoxic drugs showed good results in the PDT of tumors. DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs which served as a photosensitizer capable of generating juglone under laser irradiation while producing abundant ROS to induce apoptosis in HeLa cells [15]. Compared with PDT, SDT can prevent photoallergic reactions and also provides better penetration, which makes it possible to non-invasively treat diseased tissues at deeper sites and in wider areas. In recent years, MB as a sonosensitizer in SDT has also demonstrated significant efficacy [27, 28]. This study further investigates the application of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs as a sonosensitizer in the treatment of Bca.

In this study, US irradiation activated a cascade reaction via SDT within the nanosystem. This process depleted GSH and initiated an oxidative stress cascade in response to the TME, thereby alleviating tumor hypoxia and enhancing SDT efficacy.

Both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs alleviated tumor hypoxia via O₂ generation, thereby enhancing SDT efficacy. Additionally, both Nps exhibited concentration-dependent cytotoxicity against Bca cells, attributable to GSH-mediated reduction of Pt (IV) to Pt (II). When combined with US irradiation, both Pt/PtMBCPsNSs and DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs showed significantly enhanced cytotoxicity, with DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs demonstrating superior efficacy. This enhanced effect is attributed to the excessive production of ¹O₂ by DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs, which promotes the oxidation of non-toxic DHN into cytotoxic juglone, concurrently inducing both apoptosis and pyroptosis. To elucidate the pyroptosis mechanism, we examined relevant pathways. Although the NLRP3/caspase 1/GSDMD axis represents the classical pyroptosis route [25, 29, 30], non-canonical mechanisms also play a role in SDT-induced pyroptosis [31]. Western blot analysis excluded NLRP3/caspase 1/GSDMD involvement under DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US treatment, instead identifying caspase 3/GSDME signaling as the core mechanism. This aligns with recent studies implicating caspase 3 and GSDME as key regulators of pyroptosis downstream of apoptotic stimuli and robust ROS generation [32–34]. These results collectively establish that ROS acts as the upstream activator of the caspase 3/GSDME pathway [31, 35], mediating both pyroptosis and apoptosis within this therapeutic system. To evaluate translational potential, therapeutic effects were systematically assessed in clinically relevant models, including cell-based assays, organoids, and patient-derived xenograft (Bca-PDX) models. The PDX models utilized tumor tissues from chemotherapy-treated MIBC patients.As such MIBC cases are widely considered clinically representative, the significant suppression of tumor growth observed in this study supports the potential of this strategy as a promising therapeutic option for bladder-preserving treatment in MIBC, warranting further investigation in future studies.

The significant SDT efficacy observed in this study is likely closely associated with the unique subcellular distribution of DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs. Their negatively charged surface may promote cellular uptake via charge-mediated endocytosis. Quantitative colocalization analysis demonstrated that the NPs efficiently accumulated in the mitochondria, demonstrating a strong spatial association, with high Pearson’s and Mander’s coefficients, but low lysosomal colocalization (Fig. 3). It is hypothesized that the NPs may possess the ability to escape from the endolysosomal compartment. Acidic pH triggers polymer dissociation, releasing positively charged MB molecules that electrostatically target the negatively charged mitochondrial membrane. Another possibility is that NPs may directly damage mitochondria, thereby inducing mitophagy, followed by lysosomal engulfment. This inadvertent mitochondrial localization is highly beneficial for SDT, as it positions the sonosensitizer within the powerhouse of the cell and at the core of ROS-mediated apoptosis.

This study established a multimodal antitumor strategy combining SDT with chemotherapy. The potential mechanisms included: (1) In situ chemotherapeutic drug activation: The SDT-triggered cascade reaction induces specific oxidation of the prodrug DHN to juglone within the TME, achieving localized chemotherapeutic effects. This spatiotemporally controlled drug release process circumvented systemic toxicity (e.g., hepatorenal impairment) associated with conventional juglone delivery while enhancing antitumor efficacy. (2) Oxidative Stress Modulation: Pt (IV) in the nano-sonosensitizer catalyzed H₂O₂ decomposition in TME, alleviating hypoxia while inducing ROS accumulation via ¹O₂ generation and GSH depletion. Excessive ROS triggered multiple cell death pathways including apoptosis and pyroptosis. Elevated ROS levels can cause mitochondrial damage and activate oxidative stress-related signaling pathways, creating an “oxidative storm”-like cytotoxic effect. Our results showed DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US significantly increased ROS levels. NAC treatment rescued cell viability, suppressed apoptosis, and reduced pyroptosis by inhibiting caspase 3 cleavedage, confirming ROS-mediated pyroptotic signaling activation in Bca cells post-SDT. (3) Coordinated Programmed Cell Death: DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US concurrently triggered apoptosis (caspase 3-dependent) and pyroptosis (caspase 3/GSDME), potentially amplifying each other via ROS for enhanced cytotoxicity. Future studies should clarify the relative contributions of these two pathways in anticancer effects of SDT and identify the specific molecules involved. Furthermore, both NAC and Z-DEVD-FMK were shown to counteract SDT effects, though Z-DEVD-FMK’s incomplete reversal compared with NAC indicated SDT likely exerts additional ROS-activated anticancer pathways, for instance, studies have shown juglone can also generate ROS to combat tumors [36, 37]. Although juglone can induce apoptosis in Bca cells [38, 39], our results suggested that it does not trigger pyroptosis in Bca, indicating that DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US possesses broader anticancer mechanisms than juglone alone. This multimodal mechanism integrated in situ drug activation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and pyroptosis, achieving spatiotemporally controllable and synergistic multi-mode anti-bladder cancer effects.

Leveraging the EPR effect, DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs achieved significant tumor accumulation while showing markedly reduced distribution in normal organs. Its unique TME-responsive mechanism ensured precise drug release, while US-triggered intratumoral catalytic conversion of DHN to juglone effectively circumvents systemic toxicity. Safety evaluations revealed stable body weight, normal hepatic/renal function indices, and absence of significant pathological damage in major organs in the DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs + US group outcomes superior to the juglone group. This “local activation, systemic safety” profile underscored its strong clinical potential for Bca therapy. This study is subject to certain limitations. While the biodistribution data were corroborated by ICP-AES, the semi-quantitative fluorescence imaging results warrant further validation using more sensitive approaches such as ICP-MS. Further challenges also remain, particularly in standardizing ultrasound parameters, mitigating efficacy variability attributable to tumor heterogeneity, and optimizing manufacturing processes for large-scale production. Furthermore, future studies should focus on optimizing the synergistic effects between ultrasound parameters and nanoformulation composition, as well as developing multimodal combination strategies for clinical bladder cancer treatment.

Conclusion

This study developed DHN@Pt/PtMBCPsNSs as a multifunctional sensitizer for SDT, capable of inducing both pyroptosis and apoptosis in bladder cancer. The system exhibited catalase-like activity, alleviated tumor hypoxia via a “H₂O₂→O₂→¹O₂” cascade, and enhanced oxidative stress while reducing GSH/GSSG ratios under ultrasound. Additionally, ¹O₂-mediated oxidation of DHN enabled in situ generation of juglone, enabling localized cytotoxicity and improved biosafety. Beyond juglone-induced apoptosis, the platform activated ROS-mediated caspase 3/GSDME pyroptosis, offering a superior antitumor mechanism. This work presents a spatiotemporally controllable SDT strategy combined with in situ drug activation, providing a promising approach for clinical translation of natural anticancer agents and novel SDT.
