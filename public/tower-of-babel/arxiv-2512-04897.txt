In search of the electron-phonon contribution to total energy
Samuel Poncé, Xavier Gonze

Abstract
The total energy is a fundamental characteristic of solids, molecules, and nanostructures. In most first-principles calculations of the total energy, the nuclear kinetic operator is decoupled from the many-body electronic Hamiltonian and the dynamics of the nuclei is reintroduced afterwards. This two-step procedure introduced by Born and Oppenheimer (BO) is approximate. Energies beyond the electronic and vibrational (or phononic) main contributions might be relevant when small energy differences are important, such as when predicting stable polymorphs or describing magnetic energy landscape. We clarify the different flavors of BO decoupling and give an exact formulation for the total energy in the basis of BO electronic wavefunctions. Then, we list contributions, beyond the main ones, that appear in a perturbative expansion in powers of M 0 − 1 / 4 M_{0}^{-1/4} , where M 0 M_{0} is a typical nuclear mass, up to sixth order. Some of these might be grouped and denoted the electron-phonon contribution to total energy, E elph E^{\textrm{elph}} , that first appears at fourth order. The electronic inertial mass contributes at sixth order. We clarify that the sum of the Allen-Heine-Cardona zero-point renormalization of eigenvalues over occupied states is not the electron-phonon contribution to the total energy but a part of the phononic contribution. The computation of the lowest-order E elph E^{\textrm{elph}} is implemented and shown to be small but non-negligible (3.8 meV per atom) in the case of diamond and its hexagonal polymorph. We also estimate the electronic inertial mass contribution and confirm the size-consistency of all computed terms.



I
Introduction

The total energy is a cornerstone of modern solid-state physics and quantum chemistry.
It governs material stability
44
, phase transition
95
, crystal structure predictions
59
, or defect formation energy
25
.
Density functional theory (DFT) is often the method of choice when computing the total energy of solids
41
;
46
.
Indeed, if we had access to the exact exchange-correlation functional, DFT would give the exact electronic total energy of crystals.
However, DFT rests on the Born-Oppenheimer (BO) decoupling of nuclear and electronic degrees of freedom
14
;
13
.
Often, the first-principles BO nuclear dynamic contribution is added to the electronic energy.
Phononic (or vibrational) degrees of freedom are taken into account through Bose-Einstein statistics in the case of harmonic solids
9
, or through the quasiharmonic approximation to obtain thermal expansion
73
;
75
;
76
or using effective interatomic force constants to include anharmonic (phonon-phonon) corrections
40
;
56
;
45

However, the BO decoupling leaves away numerous contributions to the total energy, beyond the dominant electronic (static nucleus) energy and the phononic harmonic, quasi-harmonic, or anharmonic contributions.
So, one can wonder whether electron-phonon contributions to the total energy exist, how to characterize them, and how to compute them.

As a justification for their approach, Born and Oppenheimer
14
relied on a perturbation expansion, using
M_{0}^{-1/4}
as a small parameter, where
M_{0}
is a typical nuclear mass.
In the equilibrium BO geometry, the total energy is obtained for frozen nuclei, so they do not depend on
M_{0}
, while the first-order contribution vanishes.
The second-order contribution, scaling as
M_{0}^{-1/2}
, is the phononic contribution.
The third-order correction also vanishes.
Additional corrections come at the fourth order, with
M_{0}^{-1}
scaling.
Among these, two contributions are of the anharmonic phonon-phonon type, and can nowadays be included in first-principles calculations of total energy
40
;
56
;
45
.

In this work, we observe that the other BO expansion fourth-order correction can be computed approximately (within DFT) on the basis of the electron-phonon matrix elements.
Thanks to recent advances in efficient calculations of electron-phonon interaction
28
, mostly relying on density functional perturbation theory
10
;
37
;
32
;
9
;
33
;
34
, electron-phonon matrix elements are readily available when computing electronic
65
or thermal transport
4
;
49
properties, superconductivity
52
, polaron formation
85
;
92
, or the temperature-dependence of the band gap and its zero-point renormalization
63
.
Several terms of even higher order in the small parameter
M_{0}^{-1/4}
fall into that category.
All could be grouped as “electron-phonon contributions to the total energy”.
It is expected that such contributions are small compared to electronic and phononic contributions but they might be crucial for the study of defects and surfaces
26
, magnetic configurations
22
, chemical reactions and bonding
96
, or accurate phase transition
16
where small energy differences matter.

Given the recent advances in the computation of electron-phonon matrix elements, several recent works also discussed electron-phonon contributions to total energy.
In 2020, P. B. Allen
5
suggested that this contribution could be calculated using ingredients available when electron-phonon band-gap renormalization calculations are performed.
His approach is based on the summation over occupied states of partial Fan-Migdal and Debye-Waller self-energies
2
.
Recently, S. Paul
et al.

58
have performed first-principles calculations based on this idea.
They argued that such formulation violates size consistency, in which the total energy would depend on the size of the unit cell.
In the present work, we show that the formulation of Allen is well defined and size-consistent.
Crucially, we show that the approach proposed by Allen does not deliver the fourth-order
M_{0}^{-1}
electron-phonon contribution term but is already part of the second-order phononic
M_{0}^{-1/2}
contribution to the total energy, and should not be added to total energy calculations.

We first present in Section
II
the BO Hamiltonian, and approximations based on BO wavefunctions.
We also derive an expression for the exact total energy that includes contributions beyond the BO wavefunctions.
We then perform a detailed mass-scaling analysis of all the terms entering the total energy in Section
III
.
From this mass scaling analysis, we identify which terms are phononic contributions, Section
IV
, while the remaining ones are detailed in Section
V
.
In particular, we report, for the first time explicitly, the lowest-order electron-phonon contribution to the total energy.
In Appendix
A
, the BO perturbation expansion is derived and expressed in normal mode coordinates.
We then show in Section
VI
the extension to solids with practical formula for implementation in first-principles software.
In Section
VII
, we show that Allen’s energy is not the electron-phonon contribution to the total energy but a part of the phononic contribution to the total energy.
We then recast in Section
VIII
Allen’s formula into the standard Allen-Heine-Cardona (AHC)
2
;
3
theory for band renormalization thanks to an identically null term.
In Section
IX
, we present a detailed analysis of the energy terms at the phonon zone-center, comparing a frozen-phonon approach and perturbation theory.
We find excellent agreement for all terms, below 0.5% differences.
In Section
X
, we show size consistency by computing the diamond band renormalization using the
Abinit
code
30
;
31
;
74
;
94
and an interpolation of the perturbed potential which includes dipole and quadrupole contributions
20
;
19
.
We show that the same results are obtained between the primitive and a supercell of diamond when no crystal symmetries are used.

Finally, in Section
XI
we compute the (fourth order) electron-phonon contribution to the total energy and approximate the effect of electron mass rescaling (sixth order), for two allotropes of carbon: the stable face-centered cubic diamond and the high-pressure metastable hexagonal diamond called lonsdaleite which is typically found in meteorites.
For both materials, we report their converged total energy with momentum integration.
We compute the electron-phonon forces and the new equilibrium lattice parameters of diamond and internal parameter of lonsdaleite.
At 0 K, we find that diamond is 52.7 meV/(2 atoms) more stable than lonsdaleite.

II
Born-Oppenheimer-based approximations

We first consider finite systems and extend the theory to periodic crystals in Sec.
VI
.
The total Hamiltonian of a system composed of electrons and nuclei can be expressed as
14
;
15

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}+\hat{H}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\hat{P}^{2}_{\kappa\alpha}}{2M_{\kappa}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{M_{\kappa}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}^{2}},

(2)

where
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
denotes the set of nuclear positions,

\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}
is the nuclear kinetic operator,

\hat{P}_{\kappa\alpha}\equiv-i\partial/\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}
is the canonical momentum operator for nuclei, with
M_{\kappa}
the mass of nucleus
\kappa
and
\alpha
an index for cartesian axes.
Electronic coordinates are implicit, unlike the nuclear ones.
The global Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) is written in calligraphic style as well as the forthcoming associated total energies, that are independent of
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
.
Hartree atomic units are used throughout.

The Born-Oppenheimer (BO) Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
, is defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\rm e}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}+V^{\rm NN}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\rm e}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\rm e}+\hat{V}^{\rm ee}+\hat{V}^{\rm eN}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(4)

where
\hat{H}^{\rm e}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
is the electronic Hamiltonian,
V^{\rm NN}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
the bare nucleus-nucleus interaction,
\hat{T}^{\rm e}
the electronic kinetic operator,
\hat{V}^{\rm ee}
the electron-electron operator, and
\hat{V}^{\rm eN}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
the electron-nucleus operator.
The Schrödinger equation associated to the total Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\ket{\psi_{i}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}},

(5)

with ground state labeled
i=0
.
The Schrödinger equation for the BO Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}=

\displaystyle E^{\rm BO}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}},

(6)

with ground state for each configuration
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
labeled
j=0
.
The wavefunction
\psi_{i}
is normalized when integrated over electronic
\mathbf{r}
and nuclear
\mathbf{R}
coordinates, while the BO wavefunction
\phi_{j}
is normalized for each nuclear configuration
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
when integrated over electronic coordinates only.
The Dirac bracket notation is used for the electronic degrees of freedom.

The denominations
“Born-Oppenheimer Hamiltonian”
,
“Born-Oppenheimer wavefunction”
and
“Born-Oppenheimer eigenenergy”

for quantities in Eqs. (
3
) and (
6
) are well-established and consistent in the literature, but there is some confusion
about the meaning of
“Born-Oppenheimer approximation”
and
“adiabatic approximation”

8
;
57
;
38
;
89
;
84
;
23
.
In many publications, these are used interchangeably while in other they have different meanings.
For example, in their 1972 publication, Ballhausen and Hansen
8
discuss various adiabatic approximations including
“Born-Oppenheimer”
,
“Born-Huang”
,
“Herzberg-Teller”
, and
“crude”
, based on wavefunctions of the type

\ket{\psi_{i}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}=\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\ket{\zeta_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}},

(7)

that is, a simple product of a configuration-dependent (normalized) function of the nuclear configuration only
\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
, with a single normalized electronic wavefunction, also possibly configuration-dependent.
The electronic wavefunctions in these schemes change smoothly with the configuration.
With such definition, the wavefunction
\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
defines the nuclear dynamics.
Normalization of
\ket{\psi_{i}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}
, combined with the electronic normalization of
\ket{\zeta_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}
implies that
\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
is normalized with respect to integration over
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
configurations:

\langle\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}|\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\rangle_{{\mathbf{R}}}\equiv\int\textrm{d}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\chi_{ij}^{*}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=1,

(8)

where we introduce the notation
\langle.|.\rangle_{\mathbf{R}}
to indicate that we evaluate the scalar product by integrating functions on all nuclear configurations
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
.
Scalar products without such a subscript are implicitly only for electronic degrees of freedom.

However, beyond the different
“adiabatic approximations”
used by Ballhausen and Hansen, the recent

“exact factorization”

1
;
84
;
23
approach also has the form of Eq. (
7
) but is exact in that case.
Therefore, it is not helpful to define the
“adiabatic approximation”
as being based on Eq. (
7
), which is too general.
Instead, if one considers that
\ket{\zeta_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}
is a BO wavefunction
\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}
, a meaningful set of approximations follows.

We choose a reference configuration
\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm ref}\}
and the associated reference BO wavefunction
\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm ref}\}}
.
We impose unicity and that
\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}
changes smoothly as a function of
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
around
\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm ref}\}
.
We note that such an imposition might be difficult when there are degeneracies around
\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm ref}\}
or when the BO wavefunction changes abruptly.
Moreover, the arbitrary phase of the BO wavefunction should be taken consistently to reach smoothness.
We also use the index
j
to label this set of BO wavefunctions, even beyond the neighborhood where the smoothness is established.
We will assume in Sec.
III
that

\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm ref}\}
is the configuration of a (local or global) minimum of a BO hypersurface, denoted by
\{{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{0}\}

one deduces

\Big(\hat{T}^{\textrm{vec}}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}+E^{\rm opt}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\\
+\hat{\Sigma}_{j}(\mathcal{E}_{i},\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\Big)\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\mathcal{E}_{i}\chi_{ij}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(30)

where

\hat{\Sigma}_{j}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\equiv\hat{\cal{T}}^{\textrm{N},\dagger}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\hat{\cal{T}}^{\textrm{N}}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}

(31)

is an operator similar to self-energy operators found in the many-body perturbation theory context
51
.

Importantly, Eq. (
30
) is an exact version of the approximate Eqs. (
9
), (
13
), (
20
),
(
21
) and focuses only on nuclear dynamics where all the electronic degrees of freedom are condensed in the self-energy operator.
The Green’s function of the full Hamiltonian fulfills a Dyson-type equation:

\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\!=\!\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\Big(\hat{1}\!+\!\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\Big),

(32)

where
\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})
is the BO Green’s function defined as:

\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\equiv\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}\Big(z-\hat{H}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\Big)^{-1}\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}.

(33)

Inserting Eq. (
32
) into Eq. (
31
) can yield further approximate treatments.
For example, motivated by the inverse mass of nuclei that is present in
\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}
, one might neglect
\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})
with respect to
\hat{1}

in Eq. (
32
),
and set

\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\})\approx\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,\{{\mathbf{R}}\}).

(34)

We will study this approximation in Sec.
III
on the basis of its mass-scaling behavior.

As a final comment in this section, let us now focus on the ground state.
The variational freedom of the wavefunction Eq. (
23
) is complete, producing the lowest possible value of the energy of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
).
So, restricting the trial wavefunction to the BO-wavefunction subspace, which corresponds to the solution of Eq. (
13
), or correspondingly to the neglect of the self-energy in Eq. (
30
), must come with an increase of the energy such that
\mathcal{E}_{0}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\textrm{opt}}
.

III
Mass-scaling analysis

In the original BO work
14
, it is shown how to formulate a perturbation expansion around a minimum (global or local) of a chosen BO energy hypersurface with index
j
, taken as reference configuration, when the nuclei masses are much larger than the electronic masses.
The small parameter of this expansion is here denoted
\lambda
.
Taking
M_{0}
as a characteristic nuclear mass, expressed in units of electronic mass, one defines relative nuclear masses

m_{\kappa}\equiv M_{\kappa}/M_{0}.

(35)

Then, the masses become
\lambda
-dependent and are scaled together,

M_{\kappa}(\lambda)=m_{\kappa}\lambda^{-4}.

(36)

For the value

\lambda=\lambda_{0}\equiv M_{0}^{-1/4}
the physical masses are recovered.
We note that
M_{0}^{-1/4}
is small and at most 0.153 in the case of the hydrogen nucleus.
The choice of Eq. (
36
) will be explained later by the observation that the average fluctuations of nuclear displacements with respect to a reference configuration chosen to be a local BO energy minimum
\{{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{0}\}
, explicitly

\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}=R_{\kappa\alpha}-R_{\kappa\alpha j}^{0},

(37)

are proportional to
\lambda
at the lowest order.
Similarly, the average momenta are inversely proportional to
\lambda
.
Also, when
\lambda
tends to zero, all masses become infinite so that there is no nuclear dynamics.

The Schrödinger equation, Eq. (
5
), becomes

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\ket{\psi_{i\lambda}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}=\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}\ket{\psi_{i\lambda}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}},

(38)

with a
\lambda
-dependent Hamiltonian replacing Eqs. (
1
)-(
2
),

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\lambda^{4}\hat{P}^{2}_{\kappa\alpha}}{2m_{\kappa}}+\hat{H}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}.

(39)

Quantities that depend on
\lambda
(the wavefunctions, Hamiltonian, total energy, and Green’s function) can be expanded in a Taylor series in
\lambda
as follows, generically:

\displaystyle X(\lambda)=

\displaystyle X^{(0)}+\lambda X^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}X^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}),

(40a)

\displaystyle X^{(n)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}X}{\partial\lambda^{n}}_{|\lambda=0}.

(40b)

For
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
, the scaled Hamiltonian is equal to the original Hamiltonian, so that the series

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda_{0}}=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(0)}+\lambda_{0}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(1)}+(\lambda_{0})^{2}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(2)}+{\cal{O}}(\lambda_{0}^{3})

(41)

delivers the exact total energy.

Quantities that depend on the nuclei coordinates (the wavefunctions, different potentials, Hamiltonians, and Green’s functions) can also be expanded in another series, now considering atomic displacements, generically:

X\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=X_{j|\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\partial X}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}}_{j|\mathbf{0}}\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial^{2}X}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}_{j|\mathbf{0}}\!\!\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}\Delta R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}j}\!+\!\mathcal{O}\big(\Delta{\mathbf{R}}^{3}_{j}\big),

(42)

where the
j|\mathbf{0}
subscript of
X_{j|\mathbf{0}}
indicates that the quantity
X
is evaluated at
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\{{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{0}\}
.
For conciseness, we also introduce a compact notation for the terms of such Taylor series:

\displaystyle X\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=

\displaystyle X_{j|\mathbf{0}}+X^{\{\mathbf{1}\}}\cdot\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}+X^{\{\mathbf{2}\}}\cdot\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\big(\Delta{\mathbf{R}}^{3}_{j}\big),

(43a)

\displaystyle X^{\{\mathbf{n}\}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}X}{\partial R_{\kappa_{s_{1}}\alpha_{s_{1}}}\dots\partial R_{\kappa_{s_{n}}\alpha_{s_{n}}}}_{j|\mathbf{0}},

(43b)

\displaystyle\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{n}\equiv

\displaystyle\prod_{s=1}^{n}\Delta R_{\kappa_{s}\alpha_{s}j},

(43c)

where the dot product in Eq. (
43
) is defined as
\cdot\equiv\sum_{\kappa_{1}\alpha_{1}}\dots\sum_{\kappa_{n}\alpha_{n}}
.
Curly brackets are used as superscripts in this notation, instead of the superscript parentheses of Eq. (
40a
).
Being at the BO equilibrium positions, the first-order derivatives of the BO energy vanish, so that

E_{j}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=E^{\rm BO}_{j|\mathbf{0}}+E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{2}\}}_{j}\cdot\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{2}+\mathcal{O}\big(\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{3}\big).

(44)

with associated nuclear wavefunctions,

of the Hamiltonian will be explained later, see Eq. (

is even with respect to a sign change of

In Appendix
B
, we show that the Schrödinger equation, Eq. (

), is satisfied up to the second order in the displacements by the following wavefunction,

\ket{\Psi_{i\lambda}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}\equiv N_{\lambda}\vartheta_{\jmath j}\{\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}/\lambda\}\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}},

that has the form of Eq. (

) and where we have associated the index

to a specific pair
\jmath
and

.

.
The nuclear displacements are scaled by the inverse of
\lambda
in the first factor, describing the nuclear dynamics, while they are not scaled in the second factor, describing the electronic BO wavefunctions.
In Appendix
B
, it is also shown that
\mathcal{E}^{(2)}_{i}=\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[0]}
, so that

\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}=E^{\rm BO}_{j|\mathbf{0}}+\lambda^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[0]}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}),

(48)

where the first term is independent of the characteristic mass and the second term scales as
M_{0}^{-1/2}
.
In Eq. (

), the second-order energy is the correction with respect to a zero-order energy that depends on the index
j
.

Born and Oppenheimer
14
have analyzed anharmonic corrections, expanding
E_{j}^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}

beyond the second order in the nuclear displacements, up to the fourth order in
\lambda
.
We proceed with the analysis of the mass scaling of other corrections beyond those examined by them, up to the sixth order in
\lambda
.
In order to do this, inspired by Eq. (
47
) we define scaled nuclear displacements with respect to the reference configuration, and express the nuclear dynamics in terms of these.
We define

\displaystyle\rho_{\kappa\alpha j}\equiv

\displaystyle\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}/\lambda

(49)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}\equiv

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{P}_{\kappa\alpha}.

(50)

The associated notation for functions of the configuration argument is

X[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv X\{{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{0}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}\},

(51)

with the shift by
{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{0}
being taken into account and square brackets being used.
The square bracket notation for functions that depend separately and explicitly on
\lambda
and
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
does not follow the above simple correspondence and will be specified in their definition.
In particular, we will favor a convention in which the dominant
\lambda
power is factored outside of such functions.

Importantly, the
derivatives
of the

present in

, Eq. (
11
), in

, Eq. (

),

, Eq. (

), remain derivatives in terms of the nuclear configurations.
This is due to the expression of the second factor in Eq. (
47
), which is still expressed in nuclear configurations, not in scaled displacements.
However, these functions are evaluated in the configuration expressed equivalently by
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
or by
[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}]
.
So, one has

\displaystyle A_{\kappa\alpha j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=

\displaystyle A_{\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

(52)

\displaystyle B_{\perp\kappa\alpha j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=

\displaystyle B_{\perp\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}].

(53)

Also, referring to Eq. (
27
), we define

\lambda^{3}\hat{\cal{T}}^{\textrm{N}}_{\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv\lambda^{3}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}}{2m_{\kappa}}\\
\times\Bigg[-\lambda\bigg|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}^{2}}_{|[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]}\bigg\rangle-2i\bigg|\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}}_{|[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]}\bigg\rangle\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}\Bigg].

(54)

Transformed to the mass-scaled coordinates, the
\lambda
-dependent Hamiltonian, Eq. (

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\hat{H}^{\rm BO}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

(55)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\hat{\pi}^{2}_{\kappa\alpha}}{2m_{\kappa}}.

(56)

The corresponding Schrödinger equation is

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\ket{\psi_{i\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]}=\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}\ket{\psi_{i\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]}.

(57)

We now examine the mass-scaling behavior of the different approximations presented in Sec.
II
.
Similarly to Eqs. (
22a
)-(

), we express the total wavefunction using scaled displacements
[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
instead of real space coordinates:

\ket{\psi_{i\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]}=\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\ket{\phi_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]}.

(58)

The optimal approximation of Eq. (
13
) becomes

\Big(\lambda^{2}\hat{T}^{\textrm{vec}}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+E^{\rm opt}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big)\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}^{\rm opt}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]={\mathcal{E}}^{\rm opt}_{i\lambda}\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}^{\rm opt}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}],

(59)

with

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{j\lambda}^{\textrm{vec}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\Big(\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}+\lambda A_{\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big)^{2}}{2m_{\kappa}}

(60a)

\displaystyle E^{\rm opt}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]=

\displaystyle E^{\rm BO}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\lambda^{4}E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

(60b)

\displaystyle E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{m_{\kappa}}B_{\perp\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}].

(60c)

In the scalar approximation, one sets
\lambda A_{\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
and
E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

to zero, while
\lambda A_{\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
is retained in the “hyb1” approximation, and
E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
is retained in the “hyb2” approximation.
The exact equation for the energy
\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}
of the full Hamiltonian, Eq. (
57
), is now expressed in term of the self-energy, Eq. (
30
):

\Big(\hat{T}^{\textrm{vec}}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+E^{\rm opt}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\lambda^{6}\hat{\Sigma}_{j\lambda}(\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda},[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])\Big)\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\\
=\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}],

(61)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{j\lambda}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])=

\displaystyle\hat{\cal{T}}^{\textrm{N},\dagger}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])\hat{\cal{T}}^{\textrm{N}}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}],

(62)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j\lambda}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])=

\displaystyle\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}\Big(z-\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])\Big)^{-1}\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}.

(63)

Referring to Eq. (
32
), the latter Green’s function can equivalently be expressed in terms of the BO Green’s function as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j\lambda}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])=

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])

\displaystyle\times\Big(\hat{1}+\lambda^{2}\hat{T}^{\textrm{N}}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{\perp j\lambda}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big),

(64)

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])=

\displaystyle\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}\Big(z-\hat{H}^{\rm BO}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big)^{-1}\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}.

(65)

In Eq. (
61
), the reference BO energy
E^{\rm BO}_{j|\textbf{0}}
can be subtracted from both
E^{\rm opt}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
and
\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}
, leaving quantities that are at least second-order in
\lambda
.

\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}
and to find the wavefunction
\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
.
Explicitly, Eq. (
61
) becomes

\Big(\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[0]}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big)\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]=\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j\lambda}\chi_{\jmath j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}],

with

and corrections to this Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}^{[1]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(1)}}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\lambda^{2}\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(2)}}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}\lambda^{n}E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{n+2}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{n+2}

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\lambda^{4}\hat{\Sigma}_{j\lambda}(\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda},[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]),

(68)

where

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(1)}}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}A_{\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+A_{\kappa\alpha j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}}{m_{\kappa}}

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(2)}}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{(A_{\kappa\alpha}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])^{2}}{m_{\kappa}}.

(70)

In Eq. (
III
) and in later equations, the functions of the argument

, Eq. (

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}^{[1]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv\lambda E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{3}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{3}

\displaystyle+\lambda^{2}\Big(\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(1)\lx@text@lbrace{\bf{1}}\lx@text@rbrace}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}+E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{4}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{4}+E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\lambda\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big)

We treat Eq. (

-dependent Hamiltonian

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}([\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}],\Lambda)=\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[0]}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]+\Lambda\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}],

(77)

and

For the energy, one has

\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}-E_{j|\textbf{0}}^{\textrm{BO}}=\lambda^{2}\Big(\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[0]}+\Lambda\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j\lambda}^{[1]}+\Lambda^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j\lambda}^{[2]}\\

(78)

where

As previously observed,
\vartheta_{\jmath j}
is even or odd with respect to a change of sign of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}
(also changing sign of the associated momentum).
Moreover, all terms of
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}

\lambda

Hence, the
\lambda

At this stage, let us examine the contributions to
\mathcal{E}_{i\lambda}-E_{j|\textbf{0}}^{\textrm{BO}}
up to order five in
\lambda
.
We already have the zero- and second-order contributions, see Eqs. (

)-(

), while the odd-
\lambda
terms vanish.
So, we should compute
\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{(2)}
, which amounts to treating the effect of
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}^{[1]}
up to second order in
\Lambda
.
As usual in perturbation theory, the Hellmann-Feynman theorem allows one to compute the first-order contribution of
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}^{[1]}

in
\Lambda
by taking the expectation value of the Hamiltonian correction for the
unperturbed wavefunction,

\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j\lambda}^{[1]}=\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}=\lambda^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[1](2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}),

(79)

with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[1](2)}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(4),\text{vec}}+\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{4}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{4}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}

\displaystyle+\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|E^{\rm Born}_{j}[\bf{0}]|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}.

(80)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(4),\text{vec}}=\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(1)\lx@text@lbrace{\bf{1}}\lx@text@rbrace}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}.

(81)

The second-order contribution of
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}^{[1]}
in
\Lambda
can be obtained by a sum-over-state formulation, using a Sternheimer equation, or using a Green’s function approach, like for the case of Eqs. (

)-(

).
We will use the latter.
We define the Green’s function of the auxiliary harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j}^{[0]}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
, see Eq. (

), as

\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\perp\jmath j}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])\equiv\hat{\cal{P}}_{\perp\vartheta_{\jmath j}}\Big(z-\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[0]}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\Big)^{-1}\hat{\cal{P}}_{\perp\vartheta_{\jmath j}},

(82)

with the term between parenthesis being a pseudo-inverse, limited to the subspace perpendicular to
\vartheta_{\jmath j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
.
The Green’s function evaluated at
\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[0]}
is labeled with a
[0]
superscript:

\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\perp\jmath j}^{[0]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\equiv\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\perp\jmath j}(\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[0]},[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])

(83)

Then,

\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j\lambda}^{[2]}=\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\perp\jmath j}^{[0]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}.

(84)

Considering this expression up to second order in
\lambda
gives

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j\lambda}^{[2]}=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[2](2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}),

(85a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[2](2)}=

\displaystyle\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1](1)}_{j}\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\perp\jmath j}^{[0]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1](1)}_{j}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}},

(85b)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1](1)}_{j}=

\displaystyle E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{3}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{3}.

(85c)

With the explicit expressions Eqs. (
III
) and (

), the mass-scaling expansion of the total eigenenergies obtained from the different approximations presented in Sec.

can be established.
They all share the same zero- and second-order terms, see Eq. (

).
The fourth-order terms are the sum of contributions from Eqs. (
III
) and (
85b
), with all terms being present for the optimal approximation,

\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\rm(4)}=\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[1](2)}+\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[2](2)},

(86)

which is the same as the expansion of the exact energy
\mathcal{E}_{i}
up to order 5 included.
For the scalar approximation, all vector contributions and the Born contribution are neglected, which gives:

\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\rm(4),anh}=\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{4}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{4}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}\\
+\langle\vartheta_{\jmath j}|E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{3}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{3}\hat{\mathfrak{G}}_{\perp\jmath j}^{[0]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{3}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{3}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}},

(87)

that is, only the BO anharmonic terms are kept.
For the “hyb1” approximation, the Born contribution in

\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[1](2)}
is neglected, while for the “hyb2”, the vector potential contribution in
\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[1](2)}
is neglected.

Now that we have established the mass scaling of the four adiabatic approximations up to order five, with the definition of an auxiliary harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian, we can carry on our search for the electron-phonon contribution to the total energy.

IV
What is the phonon contribution to the total energy ?

To identify terms that can be called electron-phonon contributions to the total energy, we first identify the terms that can be called phonon contributions to the total energy.
At first sight, the second-order contribution
\mathcal{E}^{(2)}_{i}=\mathfrak{E}_{\jmath j}^{[0]}
is precisely the phonon contribution, solution of the auxiliary harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian for the nuclear dynamics, with
the well-known
M_{0}^{-1/2}
scaling, in which case higher-order terms should not be labeled as phonon contributions.

However, this choice has three drawbacks.
The first concerns the electronic inertial mass, especially the one of the core electrons.
The core electrons follow rigidly the nuclei movement.
Therefore, the mass of core electrons should be added to the bare nuclear mass in Eq. (
2
).
The second drawback comes from the vector potential in Eq. (
14
).
In fact, in the classical equation of motion that stems from the harmonic
approximation, a spatially homogeneous Berry curvature simply generates a force-velocity coupling, which does not prevent one to obtain normal modes of vibrations, with well-characterized frequencies
70
;
81
;
11
;
82
;
12
;
72
;
80
.
The third drawback stems from the fact that the optimal geometry (the average expectation of the nuclear positions for the exact wavefunction) is not exactly the one of the minimum of the BO hypersurface.
The BO curvature at the optimal geometry will differ from the one at the BO geometry and would be better suited to define the phonon contribution to the total energy.

In these three cases, the contribution from phonons will not have a pure
M_{0}^{-1/2}
scaling.
For example, the electronic inertial mass adds to the bare nuclear mass

M_{\kappa}
a
n_{e\kappa}m_{e}
correction, where
n_{e\kappa}
is the number of electrons moving with the nucleus
\kappa
, while
m_{e}
is the electronic mass (so, 1 in atomic units).
One can take such modification into account by updating accordingly the value of
m_{\kappa}
, with a factor
M_{0}^{-1}
, that is
\lambda^{4}
.
Coupled with the
\lambda^{2}
scaling of the phonon energy, one sees that this contribution should appear in a
\lambda^{6}
term in the perturbation expansion of Sec.
III
.

Indeed, the lowest-order
\lambda
contribution to the total energy by the self-energy operator
\hat{\Sigma}_{j\lambda}
contains such an inertial electronic mass correction.
Using Eqs. (
54
), (
62
),
(
63
), (
III
), and (
65
), we find

\hat{\Sigma}_{j\lambda}(z,[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}\Delta m_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}^{\textrm{elm},-1}(z)\hat{\pi}_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda),

(88)

where

\!\!\!\!\Delta m_{\kappa\alpha,\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}^{\textrm{elm},-1}(z)=\bigg\langle\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}}_{|\mathbf{0}}\bigg|\frac{2\hat{G}_{\perp j}^{\rm BO}(z,[\mathbf{0}])}{m_{\kappa}m_{\kappa^{\prime}}}\bigg|\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}_{|\mathbf{0}}\bigg\rangle

(89)

is the correction to the inverse electronic mass.
Taking into account that this is only the lowest-order correction to the electronic mass, this expression matches the one obtained using the exact factorization technique, see Eqs. (13), (14), (15) and (B10) of Ref.
83
.
We note that Eq. (
88
) is quadratic in the scaled momenta, and does not depend on the scaled displacements
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}
.
Such a quadratic contribution can be transferred from

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
as defined in Eq. (
III
) or (
III
) to

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[0]}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
defined in Eq. (
III
) without changing the fact that the latter is an harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian.
It simply becomes
\lambda
-dependent.

Similarly, quadratic contributions in the scaled displacements
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}

can be transferred from
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

to
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[0]}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
, as well as mixed contributions that include the scaled momenta and the scaled displacements such as

\hat{T}_{j}^{\textrm{vec(1)\lx@text@lbrace{\bf{1}}\lx@text@rbrace}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}=\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{m_{\kappa}}A_{\kappa\alpha j}^{\{\bf{1}\}}\cdot(\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}\hat{\pi}_{\kappa\alpha}).

(90)

Moreover, some contributions might be extracted from the terms in
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[1]}_{j\lambda}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
that are higher-order in the combined scaled displacements and scaled momenta, such as
\lambda E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{3}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{3}
, and
might be transferred to
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}^{[0]}_{j}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]
, in connection to a
\lambda
-dependent change of reference configuration.
These are so-called “quasi-harmonic” contributions.
Let us consider Eq. (
87
).
Changing the reference configuration from
\{{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{0}\}
to some nearby configuration
\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm ref}\}
will increase the BO energy contribution to the total energy, with a dominant effect proportional to the square of the change of configuration.
However, such a change of configuration might induce a linear change of
\lambda E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{3}\}}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}^{3}|\vartheta_{\jmath j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}
that is present twice in Eq. (
87
).
The net result might be a gain in energy.
This possibility is especially relevant when one examines the behavior of the optimized volume of the primitive cell.
The standard BO approach starts from the volume that minimizes the BO energy.
The derivative of the BO energy with respect to a volume change is zero.
However, the zero-point energy of phonons creates a modification of this energy, which is incorporated in the total energy.
The gradient of the zero-point energy of phonons with respect to a volume change will usually not vanish at the BO optimized configuration.
Hence, the re-optimized volume will be linear in the zero-point energy, that is
\mathcal{\lambda}^{2}
.
A
\mathcal{\lambda}^{2}
change in volume will create a
\mathcal{\lambda}^{4}
change in total energy.

It is possible to establish a BO expansion both with the BO optimized configuration as reference, as we have done until now, or with a
\lambda
-dependent optimized configuration as reference as obtained in such quasiharmonic approximation.
Since the reference configuration changes, the terms of both series will not be identical but the series summation gives the same final result.

However, some terms cannot be eliminated by a modification of the parameters of the auxiliary harmonic oscillator,
those of order lower than quadratic in scaled displacements and momenta, coming from the Born energy and from the self-energy.
They will be examined in the next section.

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{j\lambda}^{[1]}[\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}]

VI
Semiconductors and insulators treated within density-functional theory

In Sec.
II
, we introduced
\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
as the many-body electronic for a fixed nuclear configuration, in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, see Eq. (
6
).
Such a many-body wavefunction can only be computed in the simplest systems.
In this section, one approximates it by a Slater determinant formed from the combination of density-functional theory (DFT) one-electron Kohn-Sham wavefunctions.
Moreover, the application to gapped periodic solids is considered.
As mentioned in the previous section, the obtained results rely on non-degenerate perturbation theory, with an energy separation between the ground-state energy and the first excited-state energy.
This justifies our consideration of gapped solids only.
Modifications would be needed to deal with metallic periodic solids.

For periodic solids, eigenfunctions are Bloch waves, made of a periodic part times a phase.
A band index
n
and a crystalline momentum
{\mathbf{k}}
characterize each wavefunction
\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
.
For simplicity, the spin index is not explicitly noted and the summations over bands are to be understood as being on bands and spin channels if the two spin channels are treated separately.
The dependence on the nuclear configurations is not explicitly noted for the wavefunctions of the charge density, or for entities that depend on such configurations only indirectly.

The ground-state Born-Oppenheimer DFT energy for solids includes
53

a kinetic energy term, a one-electron atomic potential energy term, the Hartree-exchange-correlation (Hxc) energy term, and a nucleus-nucleus interaction energy term, as follows:

E^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=T+E^{\textrm{e-N}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}+E^{\textrm{Hxc}}+E^{\textrm{NN}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(94)

where we now assume to be in the ground state
j=0
and therefore omit the
j
index.
The DFT Kohn-Sham eigenenergies are obtained from the expectation value of the kinetic, one-electron nuclear potential and Hxc potential operators for the Kohn-Sham wavefunctions,

\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\hat{T}\!+\!\hat{V}^{\textrm{e-N}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\!+\!\hat{V}^{\textrm{Hxc}}|\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}},

(95)

where
\hat{V}^{\textrm{e-N}}
and
\hat{V}^{\textrm{Hxc}}
are periodic, and the Kohn-Sham wavefunctions are normalized to one in the unit cell.
The one-electron nuclear potential can be considered in an all-electron, pseudopotential, or projector-augmented wave framework.
In the latter two frameworks, the potential operator has local and non-local contributions.
Also, the nucleus-nucleus energy includes an Ewald energy term from periodic positively charged particles placed in a negatively charged homogeneous background, but also possibly a pseudo-core energy
39
for the pseudopotential and projector-augmented wave frameworks.

The ground-state Born-Oppenheimer DFT energy per unit cell can be written in terms of its eigenenergy component,
E^{\textrm{e}}
, plus other contributions, denoted
E^{\textrm{other}}
,

E^{\rm BO}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=E^{\rm e}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}+E^{\rm other}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(96)

with the eigenenergy component per unit cell being

E^{\rm e}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{n}f_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(97)

\Omega^{\rm BZ}
is the Brillouin-Zone volume and
f_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
is the Fermi-Dirac occupation function.
In this work, zero temperature is considered, so that the occupation function is one or zero.
Moreover, for gapped solids, the occupation function for all energy bands below the gap is one and zero for bands above the gap.
We therefore write Eq. (
97
) as:

E^{\rm e}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{n}^{\textrm{occ}}\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(98)

and the other contributions are

\displaystyle E^{\textrm{other}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=

\displaystyle E^{\textrm{Hxc}}-V^{\textrm{Hxc}}\!+\!E^{\textrm{NN}}\{{\mathbf{R}}\},

(99a)

\displaystyle V^{\textrm{Hxc}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{n}^{\textrm{occ}}\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\hat{V}^{\textrm{Hxc}}|\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}.

(99b)

Returning to the different orders of the Taylor expansion of the total ground-state energy, Eq. (
41
) becomes:

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}=E^{\rm BO}_{|\mathbf{0}}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{E}^{(2)}+\lambda^{4}\mathcal{E}^{(4)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{5}),

(100)

where one has to write expressions for the second-order and fourth-order energies in the case of DFT for a solid.
The physical total energy is obtained with
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
.
We then perform a transformation to mode coordinate, see Appendix
A
, and express the second-order contribution to the total energy per unit cell resulting from atomic vibrations (or phonons), Eq. (
150
), as:

E^{\textrm{ph}}\equiv\lambda_{0}^{2}\mathcal{E}^{(2)}=\lambda_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{E}_{0}^{\rm ph}=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{\nu}\frac{\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}{2},

(101)

where
\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
is the phonon frequency for mode
\nu
and momentum
{\mathbf{q}}
.

The fourth-order contribution to the total energy due to phonons from Eq. (
151
) can be written in different ways.
Considering normal mode derivatives of the wavefunctions yields

\displaystyle E^{\rm elph}\equiv

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{4}\mathcal{E}^{(4),\rm elph}

(102a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\rm BZ}\!\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{(\Omega^{\rm BZ})^{2}}\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\sum_{\nu}\bigg\langle\frac{\partial\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial\eta_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\bigg|\frac{\partial\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial\eta_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\bigg\rangle.

(102b)

The relation between nuclei displacements
\Delta R_{l\kappa\alpha}
and normal mode amplitude

\eta_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
, in the case of periodic solids is given by

\Delta R_{l\kappa\alpha}=\sum_{\nu}\eta_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}},

(103)

where
\mathbf{R}_{l}
is the lattice vector of the cell
l
, or alternatively by

\Delta R_{l\kappa\alpha}=\sum_{\nu}\eta_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}},

(104)

where
\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}
is the position vector of the atom
\kappa
in cell
l
.
The phase difference between these two possibilities does not impact observables as they cancel in the scalar product present in Eq. (
102a
).
In order to derive Eq. (
102a
), the orthogonality of first-order perturbed wavefunctions to the occupied subspace (parallel transport gauge) has been used
35
.

Direct derivatives of wavefunctions with respect to the atomic displacements of a specific sublattice can also be used.
Defining

\Delta R_{l\kappa\alpha}\equiv R_{\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}},

(105)

where
R_{\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}=\sum_{\nu}\eta_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}
, gives

E^{\rm elph}=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\\
\times\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{M_{\kappa}}\bigg\langle\frac{\partial\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}}\bigg|\frac{\partial\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}}\bigg\rangle,

(106)

with a possible spin doubling in the unpolarized spin case.

A third possibility to express the fourth-order contribution to the total energy due to electron-phonon interaction relies on the usual electron-phonon matrix elements and a sum over unoccupied states. This expression reads

E^{\rm elph}=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\\
\times\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\sum_{m}^{\rm unocc}\sum_{\nu}\frac{2\omega_{\nu\mathbf{q}}|g_{mn\nu}({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{q}})|^{2}}{(\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}-\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}})^{2}},

(107)

where

g_{mn\nu}({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{q}})=\braket{\phi_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}|\Delta V_{{\mathbf{q}}\nu}|\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}
are the electron-phonon matrix elements in which

\Delta V_{{\mathbf{q}}\nu}
is the first-order change to the Kohn-Sham potential induced by a phonon mode
\nu
.
The first-order potential in the mode basis is expressed in terms of the movement of atoms
\kappa
in the Cartesian direction
\alpha
as

\Delta V_{{\mathbf{q}}\nu}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Big[\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu\mathbf{q}}}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\kappa\alpha l}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}\\
\times\frac{\partial V_{\kappa\alpha}(\mathbf{r}+{\mathbf{R}_{l}},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{R}_{l}})}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+\mathbf{R}_{l})}.

(108)

In the
Abinit
software, for the purpose of the present work, Eqs. (
106
) and (
107
) have been implemented.
As mentioned earlier, the spin index is omitted and the summations over bands are to be understood as being on bands and spin channels if the two spin channels are treated separately.
In the spin-unpolarized case, the summation might be done over the (non-spin-polarized) wavefunctions, and a factor of two might be included afterward.

VII
Allen’s electron-phonon contribution to the total energy

In Ref.
6
, Allen proposed that the contribution from the electron-phonon interaction to the total energy could be computed from the same ingredients as for the zero-point renormalization of the electronic eigenenergies due to the electron-phonon interaction.
Using the notation of the present paper, Eq. (4) of Ref.
6
becomes

E^{\rm Allen}=\iint_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{n}f_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{\nu}\Big[\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\Delta_{2}V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}|\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}\\
+\sum_{m}\frac{|\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\Delta V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}|\phi_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}}|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}}(1-f_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}})\Big],

(109)

where the expression for the second-order potential
\Delta_{2}V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
can be found in Ref.
28
(there, it is denoted
V^{(2)}_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
).
It is asserted in Ref.
6
that this expression has the same structure as the sum of the electron-phonon energy shifts
\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
of the occupied states, but that it differs from the latter because of the presence of the

(1-f_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}})
factor in Eq. (
109
).
For comparison, such shifts write

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\textrm{d}{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{\nu}\Big[\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\Delta_{2}V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}|\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}\\
+{\sum_{m}}^{\prime}\frac{|\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\Delta V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}|\phi_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}}|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}}\Big],

(110)

where the prime indicates that the intraband contribution (
m=n
) is omitted for
{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}
.

Interestingly, the
(1-f_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}})
factor can be suppressed from Eq. (
109
).
Indeed, due to the change of sign in the denominator
\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}

upon exchange between
n{\mathbf{k}}
and
m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}
, one has

\iint_{\rm BZ}\frac{\textrm{d}{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{\textrm{d}{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}{\sum_{\nu nm}}^{\prime}f_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{|\braket{\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}|\Delta V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}|\phi_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}}|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}}f_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}=0.

(111)

Thus, without any approximation,

E^{\rm Allen}=\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{n}f_{n{\mathbf{k}}}\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}},

(112)

which at 0 K reduces to

E^{\rm Allen}=\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\text{d}{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}.

(113)

In a subsequent work
71
, Varma
et al.
computed the right-hand side of Eq. (
113
), and argued that it approximates well Eq. (
109
), the electron-phonon contribution to the total energy.
Like Allen, they did not mention that both quantities are equal.
We also remark that the eigenvalue renormalization in Eq. (
110
) is expressed using the adiabatic approximation, but the nonadiabatic version of
\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
should be used for infra-red active materials
63
;
55
.

Let us proceed with the scaling analysis of
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
.
Relying on the analysis presented in Sec.
III
, in particular the fact that the fluctuations of nuclear displacements are proportional to
\lambda
, one finds that
\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
scales as
\lambda^{2}
.
Actually,
\Delta V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
scales as
\lambda
, and is present twice in Eq. (
110
), while
\Delta_{2}V_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
scales like
\lambda^{2}
, see Eqs. (34) and (40) of Ref.
28
.
Hence,
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
also scales as
\lambda^{2}
.
It is of second order in the perturbation, not of fourth order.
However, the perturbation approach of the previous sections shows that the second-order term gives the phonon contribution to the total energy, no second-order contribution in

\lambda
can be called an electron-phonon contribution.
As we will show, this alleged electron-phonon contribution to the total energy is actually a part of the phonon contribution to the total energy, previously mentioned,
E^{\rm ph}
.
Hence
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
should not be added to
E^{\rm BO}_{|\mathbf{0}}
and
E^{\rm ph}
.
The correct expressions for the lowest-order electron-phonon contribution to the total energy, if any,
E^{\rm elph}
, are given by Eqs. (
102a
), (
106
), and (
107
).

To prove that
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
is a contribution to
E^{\rm ph}
and not to
\lambda_{0}^{4}\mathcal{E}^{(4)}
, we further start from Eqs. (1) and (2) of Ref.
63
:

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\\
\times\sum_{\kappa\alpha\kappa^{\prime}\beta l}\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}}{\partial R_{0\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{l\kappa^{\prime}\beta}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\beta{\mathbf{q}}}.

(114)

This expression is inserted in Eq. (
113
) and combined with Eq. (
98
) to give

E^{\rm Allen}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\\
\times\sum_{\kappa\alpha\kappa^{\prime}\beta l}\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\rm e}}{\partial R_{0\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{l\kappa^{\prime}\beta}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\beta{\mathbf{q}}},

(115)

where the dependence of the electronic energy
E^{\rm e}
on
\phi
and
\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
is omitted for conciseness.
In addition, the second-order derivative of the BO total energy is the interatomic force constant, see Eq. (
139
), which extends for the periodic case to

\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}^{2}=\sum_{\kappa\alpha\kappa^{\prime}\beta l}\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\rm BO}}{\partial R_{0\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{l\kappa^{\prime}\beta}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\beta{\mathbf{q}}}.

(116)

This is the same expression as the second summation in Eq. (
115
) when
E^{\rm e}
is replaced by
E^{\rm BO}
.
Dividing Eq. (
116
) by
2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}
and inserting it into Eq. (
101
) gives

E^{\rm ph}=\sum_{\nu}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\\
\times\sum_{\kappa\alpha\kappa^{\prime}\beta l}\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\rm BO}}{\partial R_{0\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{l\kappa^{\prime}\beta}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\beta{\mathbf{q}}}.

(117)

The decomposition of the BO energy had been introduced in Eq. (
96
) and allows one to split the second-order phononic contribution to the total energy, delivering

E^{\rm ph}=2E^{\textrm{Allen}}+E^{\textrm{ph,other}},

(118)

with

E^{\textrm{ph,other}}=\sum_{\nu}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\textrm{d}{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\\
\times\sum_{\kappa\alpha\kappa^{\prime}\beta l}\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\rm other}}{\partial R_{0\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{l\kappa^{\prime}\beta}}e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\beta{\mathbf{q}}}.

(119)

As announced,
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
is a contribution to
E^{\rm ph}
.
The factor two in front of
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
in Eq. (
118
) might seem surprising.
It comes from the fact that the zero-point energy of phonons includes both a kinetic energy and a potential energy contribution, which are actually equal, while the zero-point renormalization of eigenvalues, Eq. (
114
), does not include a kinetic contribution but contains the curvature of
\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
, which acts as a potential energy.

Therefore, Allen’s energy defined in Ref.
6
is not an electron-phonon energy but a part of the phonon contribution to the total energy.
We can inquire about the origin of the problem in Ref.
6
.
Apparently, the issue stems from the use of an effective Hamiltonian, with separate electronic and phononic terms fixed
a priori
, to which an electron-phonon interaction is added, changing the total energy.
As derived in the present work, following the BO approach, the first-principles BO-DFT phonons come from the BO curvature,
which already includes the electronic energy curvature
, in addition to nuclear-nuclear energy curvature, and Hartree and exchange-correlation corrections.
Hence, phonons cannot be separated from electrons
a priori
, unlike in the effective Hamiltonian of Ref.
6
.

VIII
Connection with the Allen-Heine-Cardona theory

Despite
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
not being the desired electron-phonon interaction energy, one can nonetheless study it and examine its connection with the Allen-Heine-Cardona (AHC) theory of zero-point renormalization.
Using Ref.
63
,
\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}
in Eq. (
113
) can be decomposed as:

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm AHC}+\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm NRIA},

(120)

where
\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm AHC}
is obtained from the AHC theory
2
;
3
with the rigid-ion approximation
62
to describe the Debye-Waller term.

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm AHC}
has the following sum-over-state expression
29
;
63

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm AHC}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\sum_{\kappa\alpha\kappa^{\prime}\beta}\bigg[U_{\kappa\alpha\nu{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\kappa^{\prime}\beta\nu{\mathbf{q}}}\\
\times\sum_{m}{}^{{}^{\prime}}\frac{g_{mn\kappa\alpha}^{*}({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{q}})g_{mn\kappa^{\prime}\beta}({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{q}})}{\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{q}}}+i\eta}\\
-\frac{1}{2}[U_{\kappa\alpha\nu{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\kappa\beta\nu{\mathbf{q}}}+U_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha\nu{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\kappa^{\prime}\beta\nu{\mathbf{q}}}]\\
\times\sum_{m}{}^{{}^{\prime}}\frac{g_{mn\kappa\alpha}^{*}({\mathbf{k}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})g_{mn\kappa^{\prime}\beta}({\mathbf{k}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})}{\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}-\varepsilon_{m{\mathbf{k}}}+i\eta}\bigg],

(121)

where
\eta
should be a positive infinitesimal in the AHC theory.
Beyond AHC,
\eta
would effectively describe the lifetime of the quasiparticle state
48
.
In this work, without computing
\eta
, it is chosen to be 5 meV.

The non-rigid-ion approximation (NRIA)
\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm NRIA}
terms in Eq. (
120
) have been shown to be small in solids
62
;
66
.
Computing them requires the second-order derivative of the Hamiltonian, not available with current DFPT implementation, that reads:

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm NRIA}=\sum_{\nu}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\rm d{\mathbf{q}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\!\sum_{l\alpha\beta,\kappa\neq\kappa^{\prime}}\!\frac{e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}}{4\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\beta{\mathbf{q}}}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha{\mathbf{q}}}\\
\times\int\textrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}\,\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}({\mathbf{r}})\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}({\mathbf{r}})\frac{\partial^{2}V^{\rm Hxc}({\mathbf{r}})}{\partial R_{0\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{l\kappa^{\prime}\beta}},

(122)

where
V^{\rm Hxc}({\mathbf{r}})
is the Hartree and exchange-correlation potential.
Therefore, at the DFPT level, one cannot currently compute
E^{\textrm{Allen}}
, but only its AHC approximation,

E^{\rm Allen,AHC}\equiv\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\text{d}{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm AHC},

(123)

with possible spin doubling in the unpolarized case.

IX
Validation for diamond at the phonon zone-center

In this section, we compute different quantities mentioned in Secs.
VI
-
VIII
using diamond as an example, and compare results from perturbation theory with those from finite differences (FD - frozen phonon approach).
Although numbers will first be obtained for a single wavevector at the zone center, the procedure
is defined for a grid of wavevectors compatible with a supercell of the primitive cell.
Let
N
be the size ratio between the primitive cell and the supercell, the latter being commensurate with the former, and consider similarly that there are
N
allowed
{\mathbf{q}}
wavevectors that match periodic boundary conditions in the supercell.
We define
E^{\rm BO,sup}
, the BO energy of the supercell, that will be evaluated with atoms collectively displaced from their equilibrium position.
Precisely, the atom labeled
\kappa
in the cell
l
, with equilibrium
position
\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}
, will be displaced by
h
times the eigendisplacement

\boldsymbol{U}_{\nu\kappa{\mathbf{q}}}
, modulated by the phase
e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}
, giving the displaced position

\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h)=\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}+h\boldsymbol{U}_{\nu\kappa{\mathbf{q}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}}.

(124)

We refer the reader to Ref.
61
, Eqs. (70) and (71),
for the treatment of complex displacements, using linear combinations of such displacements.
The phonon frequencies are obtained using the following FD formulation:

\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{Nh^{2}}\Big(E^{\rm BO,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h)\}]\\
+E^{\rm BO,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h)\}]-2E^{\rm BO,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}\}]\Big).

(125)

This FD value can be introduced in a discretized version of Eq. (
101
) to obtain
E^{\rm ph}
.
Alternatively,

E^{\rm ph}=\sum_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}N^{2}h^{2}}\Big(E^{\rm BO,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h)\}]\\
+E^{\rm BO,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h)\}]-2E^{\rm BO,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}\}]\Big).

(126)

In this expression, one of the factors
N
comes from the number of
{\mathbf{q}}
vectors, while the other comes from the reduction of
E^{\rm BO,sup}
to the primitive cell.
Similarly, the change of eigenvalues due to electron-phonon interaction can be computed with FD as

\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}}\frac{1}{h^{2}}\Big(\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h)\}]\\
+\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h)\}]-2\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}\}]\Big).

(127)

Allen’s total energy has two equivalent formulations using FD:

\displaystyle E^{\rm Allen}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}N^{2}h^{2}}\Big(E^{\rm e,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h)\}]

\displaystyle+E^{\rm e,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h)\}]-2E^{\rm e,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}\}]\Big)

(128)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\int\frac{\text{d}{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}},

(129)

with a possible spin doubling in the unpolarized case for the last equation.
The remaining contribution is:

E^{\rm ph,other}=\sum_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}N^{2}h^{2}}\Big(E^{\rm other,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h)\}]\\
+E^{\rm other,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h)\}]-2E^{\rm other,sup}[\{\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}^{0}\}]\Big).

(130)

Finally, Eq. (
122
) can also be computed using finite differences.
We first define

\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h_{1},\kappa_{1},h_{2},\kappa_{2})\equiv\mathbf{R}^{0}_{l\kappa}\\
+h_{1}\delta_{\kappa\kappa_{1}}\boldsymbol{U}_{\nu\kappa_{1}{\mathbf{q}}}+h_{2}\delta_{\kappa\kappa_{2}}\boldsymbol{U}_{\nu\kappa_{2}{\mathbf{q}}}e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{l}},

(131)

and obtain

\displaystyle\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm NRIA}=\sum_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\omega_{\nu{\mathbf{q}}}4N^{2}h^{2}}\sum_{l\kappa_{1}\neq\kappa_{2}}\int_{\textrm{sup}}\!\textrm{d}{\mathbf{r}}\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{*}({\mathbf{r}})\phi_{n{\mathbf{k}}}({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle\times\Big\{\,\,\,\,

\displaystyle V^{\rm Hxc}[\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h,\kappa_{1},h,\kappa_{2})]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle V^{\rm Hxc}[\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(h,\kappa_{1},-h,\kappa_{2})]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle V^{\rm Hxc}[\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h,\kappa_{1},h,\kappa_{2})]({\mathbf{r}})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle V^{\rm Hxc}[\mathbf{R}_{l\kappa}(-h,\kappa_{1},-h,\kappa_{2})]({\mathbf{r}})\Big\},

(132)

where the two arguments of
V^{\rm Hxc}
indicate that two nuclei are moved in the FD approach, and where the integral is performed in the supercell.
This term gives the corresponding total energy:

E^{\rm Allen,NRIA}\equiv\sum_{n}^{\rm occ}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{\text{d}{\mathbf{k}}}{\Omega^{\rm BZ}}\Delta\varepsilon_{n{\mathbf{k}}}^{\rm NRIA},

(133)

with possible spin doubling in the unpolarized case.

XII
Conclusions

In this work, we have examined which terms in the BO expansion might be called
“electron-phonon contribution to the total energy”
.
The lowest-order possibility appears at fourth order.
This contribution, not yet computed from first-principles to our knowledge, might become relevant when addressing small energy differences between competing phases, magnetic configurations, defects, or surfaces.
We actually derive all the contributions in the BO expansion to the sixth order, even including the effect of time-reversal symmetry breaking with the appearance of a vector potential.
We clarified that the expression proposed by Allen in 2020, albeit called the electron-phonon contribution to the total energy, is in fact part of the second-order phonon contribution and should not be added independently.
Building on this understanding, we derived and implemented a correct expression for the fourth-order electron-phonon correction, which we show to be size-consistent and compatible with standard density-functional theory workflows.
Our implementation is validated against finite-difference calculations or the sum-over-states formulation and applied to the case of diamond and its hexagonal polymorph, lonsdaleite.
We find that although the fourth-order electron-phonon contribution is small in absolute terms, it is non-negligible and on the order of several meV per atom.
However, there is only a marginal difference between its values for the two allotropes.
Overall, this work provides a practical and accurate route to include the lowest-order electron-phonon contribution to the total energy in first-principles simulations, enabling more precise energy comparisons in systems where quantum lattice effects play a significant role.

Appendix A
Normal mode coordinates

In this appendix, our goal is to obtain the lowest-order electron-phonon contribution to the total energy,

\mathcal{E}^{(4),\textrm{elph}}_{i}
, in terms of derivatives of the BO electronic
eigenfunctions with respect to normal mode coordinates, instead of derivatives with respect to nuclei displacements, as in Eq. (
93
).

Normal mode coordinates refer to the eigenmodes of the phononic Hamiltonian with physical masses, in which the Hamiltonian becomes separable.
In the main text, the formulation of the BO perturbation expansion refers to the auxiliary harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian Eq. (
45
), in which relative nuclear masses are present instead of the physical ones.
There is therefore only a simple scaling factor between these Hamiltonians.
In this appendix, we use the standard definition of the normal modes.
The normal modes are obtained by performing a Taylor expansion of the ground-state BO energy around the BO equilibrium positions
\{{\mathbf{R}}^{0}\}
.
With the notation of Eq. (
42
) instead of the more compact Eq. (
44
) notation,

E^{\rm BO}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=E^{\rm BO}_{j|\mathbf{0}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\rm BO}_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}_{|\mathbf{0}}\!\!\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}\Delta R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}j}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta{\mathbf{R}}^{3}).

(136)

We then define the phononic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{j}^{\rm ph}
as the nuclear kinetic operator plus the second-order BO Hamiltonian

\hat{H}^{\rm BO}
:

\hat{H}_{j}^{\rm ph}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}\equiv-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{M_{\kappa}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}^{2}}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{H}_{j}^{\rm BO}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}_{|\mathbf{0}}\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}\Delta R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}j},

(137)

so that

\hat{H}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}=\hat{H}^{\rm BO}_{j|\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\partial\hat{H}^{\rm BO}_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}}_{|\mathbf{0}}\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}\\
+\hat{H}_{j}^{\rm ph}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta{\mathbf{R}}^{3}).

(138)

Note that the first-order BO Hamiltonian is not included in
\hat{H}_{j}^{\rm ph}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
but in
\hat{H}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}
.
Indeed, the expectation value of the first-order BO Hamiltonian for a reference state at the minimum of the
j
-BO hypersurface vanishes due to the Hellman-Feynman theorem.

Next, we describe the nuclear vibrations with multi-dimensional harmonic oscillators.
Using the secular equation and following the notation from Ref.
62
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}C_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha j\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}j}=

\displaystyle M_{\kappa}\omega_{\nu j}^{2}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j},

(139)

\displaystyle C_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha j\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\rm BO}_{j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}\partial R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}_{|\mathbf{0}},

(140)

where
C
are the interatomic force constants and
\omega_{\nu j}^{2}
the vibrational energy of mode
\nu
from the
j
BO hypersurface.
The eigenfunctions of Eq (
139
) satisfy the completeness and orthonormalization conditions

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\sqrt{M_{\kappa}M_{\kappa^{\prime}}}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j}^{*}U_{\nu\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}j}=

\displaystyle\delta_{\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}},

(141)

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}M_{\kappa}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j}^{*}U_{\nu^{\prime}\kappa\alpha j}=

\displaystyle\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}},

(142)

where
U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j}
are the eigendisplacement vectors of the harmonic equation of motion.

The transformation to normal mode coordinates
\eta_{\nu j}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\eta_{\nu j}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j},

(143)

\displaystyle\eta_{\nu j}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}M_{\kappa}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j}\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}.

(144)

Using the relationships Eqs. (
141
) and (
142
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{M_{\kappa}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}^{2}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{1}{M_{\kappa}}\frac{\partial}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}}\sum_{\nu}\frac{\partial\eta_{\nu j}}{\partial R_{\kappa\alpha}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{\nu j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\nu^{\prime}\\
\kappa\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{M_{\kappa}}M_{\kappa}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j}M_{\kappa}U_{\nu^{\prime}\kappa\alpha j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{\nu^{\prime}j}\partial\eta_{\nu}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{\nu j}^{2}},

(145)

and similarly using Eqs. (
139
), (
143
), and (
142
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa\alpha}C_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\alpha j\\
\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\Delta R_{\kappa\alpha j}\Delta R_{\kappa^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}j}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\nu^{\prime}\\
\kappa\alpha\end{subarray}}\eta_{\nu}U_{\nu\kappa\alpha j}M_{\kappa}\omega_{\nu j}^{2}U_{\nu^{\prime}\kappa\alpha j}\eta_{\nu^{\prime}j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\eta^{2}_{\nu j}\omega_{\nu j}^{2}.

(146)

The phononic Hamiltonian, Eq. (
137
), becomes

\hat{H}^{\rm ph}_{j}[{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}]=\sum_{\nu}\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{\nu j}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}_{\nu j}\eta_{\nu j}^{2}\Big).

(147)

The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\rm ph}_{j}[{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}]
is separable.
The eigen-equation

\hat{H}^{\rm ph}_{j}[{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}]\chi_{\jmath j}[{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}]=E_{\jmath j}^{\rm ph}\chi_{\jmath j}[{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}],

(148)

has the following ground-state solution, stemming from the treatment of the quantum
harmonic oscillator:

\displaystyle\chi_{0j}[{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}]=

\displaystyle\Pi_{\nu}\Big(\frac{\omega_{\nu j}}{\pi}\Big)^{1/4}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}_{\nu j}\omega_{\nu j}},

(149)

\displaystyle E_{0j}^{\rm ph}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\nu j}.

(150)

Similarly, the fourth-order electron-phonon contribution to the total energy
\lambda_{0}^{4}\mathcal{E}^{(4),\textrm{elph}}_{i}
, see Eq. (
93
), becomes

E^{(4),\textrm{elph}}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu}\bigg\langle\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial\eta_{\nu j}}_{|{\mathbf{0}}}\bigg|\hat{\cal{P}}^{\textrm{BO}}_{\perp j}\bigg|\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial\eta_{\nu j}}_{|{\mathbf{0}}}\bigg\rangle

(151)

in normal mode coordinates.
This result can be deduced from Appendix VII of Ref.
13
, Eq. (VII.24) at page 405.

(dotless

\displaystyle=\Bigg(E^{\rm BO}_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}+\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\lambda^{4}\hat{P}^{2}_{\kappa\alpha}}{2m_{\kappa}}\Bigg)N_{\lambda}\vartheta_{\jmath j}\{\frac{\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}}{\lambda}\}\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}

\displaystyle\approx\Bigg(E^{\rm BO}_{j|\mathbf{0}}+E^{\rm BO\{\mathbf{2}\}}_{j}\!\cdot\!(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{j})^{2}+\sum_{\kappa\alpha}\frac{\lambda^{4}\hat{P}^{2}_{\kappa\alpha}}{2m_{\kappa}}\Bigg)N_{\lambda}\vartheta_{\jmath j}\{\frac{\Delta{\mathbf{R}}_{j}}{\lambda}\}\ket{\phi_{j}\{{\mathbf{R}}\}}

where the second equality comes from Eq. (
6

The normalization factor
