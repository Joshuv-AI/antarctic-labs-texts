Using Geometric Symmetries to Achieve Super-Smoothness for Cubic Powell–Sabin Splines
Jan Grošelj, Hendrik Speleers

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate C 2 C^{2} super-smoothness of the full C 1 C^{1} cubic spline space on a Powell–Sabin refined triangulation, for which a B-spline basis can be constructed. Blossoming is used to identify the C 2 C^{2} smoothness conditions between the functionals of the dual basis. Some of these conditions can be enforced without difficulty on general triangulations. Others are more involved but greatly simplify if the triangulation and its corresponding Powell–Sabin refinement possess certain symmetries. Furthermore, it is shown how the C 2 C^{2} smoothness constraints can be integrated into the spline representation by reducing the set of basis functions. As an application of the super-smooth basis functions, a reduced spline space is introduced that maintains the cubic precision of the full C 1 C^{1} spline space.



1
Introduction

Smooth polynomial spline spaces over general triangulations are known for their dependency on the mesh geometry which complicates the identification of degrees of freedom, entangles the analysis of splines, and often jeopardizes their stability. One of the standard approaches for constructing splines of this type is by using macro-element techniques: the above notorious problems can be avoided by applying additional restrictions to the general definition, e.g., extra refinement of the triangulation, super-smoothness in certain regions of the domain, elimination of derivative degrees of freedom, and combinations of these constraints. An important feature of macro-element methods is that splines are constructed locally over each triangle of the triangulation with little or no dependence on the neighbouring triangles.

A majority of macro-element techniques have emerged from the finite element method for numerically solving partial differential equations via the variational approach (see, e.g.,
Ciarlet (2002)
). The geometric interpretation of these techniques in terms of the Bernstein–Bézier representation has simplified the analysis of splines and enabled many of their generalizations, especially to higher smoothness orders (see, e.g.,
Lai and Schumaker (2007)
). Recently, efforts have been made to describe such spline spaces by B-spline basis functions which automatically incorporate smoothness conditions, are locally supported, form a nonnegative partition of unity, and are distinguished by excellent stability properties.

A macro-element technique that has proven to be well suited for the development of B-spline methods is the refinement of the triangulation by the Powell–Sabin 6-split
Powell and Sabin (1977)
. For the standard
C^{1}
quadratic splines over such a refinement, a B-spline basis was constructed in
Dierckx (1997)
and influenced several extensions to higher polynomial degrees and smoothness orders. Among these are the specifically tailored B-spline representations of
C^{2}
quintic splines
Speleers (2010)
;
Speleers (2012)
and almost
C^{2}
quartic splines
Barrera et al. (2021)
;
Grošelj and Krajnc (2016b)
as well as more general constructions of B-spline bases for splines of arbitrary smoothness
Speleers (2013)
;
Speleers (2015a)
and degree
Grošelj (2016)
.

Special attention has been lately dedicated to
C^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin splines. B-spline representations for specific subspaces were presented in
Lamnii et al. (2014)
and
Speleers (2015b)
. A B-spline basis for the full space with certain geometric constraints on the underlying triangulation was constructed in
Grošelj and Krajnc (2016a)
. Finally, a nonrestrictive
C^{1}
B-spline representation was introduced in
Grošelj and Speleers (2017)
. The latter provides a general framework for describing specific super-smooth cubic Powell–Sabin splines, quadratic Powell–Sabin splines, and even cubic Clough–Tocher splines
Clough and Tocher (1965)
. The potential of this framework in approximation of functions and numerical solution of partial differential equations was explored in
Grošelj and Speleers (2018)
and
Grošelj and Speleers (2023)
.

This paper investigates
C^{2}
smoothness constraints on
C^{1}
cubic splines over a Powell–Sabin refinement by exploiting their B-spline representation and its dual characterization derived in
Grošelj and Speleers (2017)
. The dual basis consists of functionals that can be expressed via blossoming of the polynomials obtained by restricting a spline to certain triangles of the refinement. Since smoothness constraints between polynomials can be elegantly described by blossoming identities, this offers a powerful tool for the study of super-smoothness that is more transparent than resolving relations acquired through the Bernstein–Bézier methods.

It is known from
Grošelj and Speleers (2017)
;
Grošelj and Speleers (2018)
that the B-spline representation of
C^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin splines allows a straightforward imposition of certain
C^{2}
smoothness constraints inside triangles of the original triangulation.
In this paper, we further analyze smoothness constraints of such splines and, in order to conceal their macro-structure, aim at the full
C^{2}
smoothness inside a macro-triangle.
We show that this can be locally achieved when the triangle 6-split is symmetric in the sense that certain edges of the refinement lie on the same line. This result is not practically irrelevant as triangulations are often at least partially structured and as such allow symmetric refinement of a possibly significant number of triangles.

This paper also addresses the implementation of the
C^{2}
smoothness conditions into the B-spline representation by recombining the original B-spline functions and reducing their number. The procedure resembles the one presented in
Grošelj and Speleers (2021)
for three-directional triangulations. It offers much more versatility, however, as it can be used on triangulations that are only partially structured. This is thanks to a more general recombination technique which permits the transition from the original B-spline representation in regions that are partitioned in an unstructured way to the new reduced B-spline representation on symmetrically refined parts of the triangulation.

Moreover, the reduction process ensures that the new basis functions still reproduce cubic polynomials, which is crucial for obtaining optimal convergence rates in approximation methods. In this way a better accuracy with respect to the number of degrees of freedom can be achieved as it has been recently demonstrated in
Grošelj and Speleers (2023)
for uniformly refined triangulations — these are a specific setting in which the methods presented in this paper are applicable. The reduced B-spline basis also preserves all other important features of the original one such as the properties of local support, nonnegativity, and partition of unity, making it a viable option for computer aided geometric design.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we review the Powell–Sabin refinement and the properties of the B-spline representation of
C^{1}
cubic Powell–Sabin splines. In Section
3
we study
C^{2}
super-smoothness conditions for a spline in such a representation. Section
4
is dedicated to the reduction of the B-spline functions, and Section
5
presents the reduced spline space spanned by the derived basis functions. We conclude with some remarks in Section
6
.

Spline Space

In this section we review how a given triangulation
\Delta

2.1
Powell–Sabin Refinement

A triangulation
\Delta=(V,E,T)
of a polygonal domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is determined by a finite set of vertices
V=\left\{v_{i}\right\}_{i}
, a finite set of edges
E=\left\{e_{ij}\right\}_{ij}
, and a finite set of triangles
T=\left\{t_{ijk}\right\}_{ijk}
. Every vertex
v_{i}\in V
corresponds to a point in
\Omega
, every edge
e_{ij}\in E
corresponds to a convex hull
[v_{i},v_{j}]
of two different vertices
v_{i},v_{j}\in V
, and every triangle
t_{ijk}\in T
corresponds to a convex hull
[v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}]
of three noncollinear vertices
v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}\in V
. In this notation, we identify the edges and triangles by the same set of indices, i.e., the order of indices does not matter. We assume that the union of triangles in
T
equals
\Omega
and the intersection of any two triangles from
T
is an empty set, an element of
V
, or an element of
E
. Naturally, we require that every
v_{i}\in V
is a vertex and every
e_{ij}\in E
is an edge of at least one triangle in
T
. We denote by
E^{b}\subseteq E
the set of all edges that lie on the boundary of
\Omega
.

A Powell–Sabin refinement
\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}}=(V_{\mathrm{PS}},E_{\mathrm{PS}},T_{\mathrm{PS}})
of
\Delta
is a triangulation obtained by splitting each triangle
t_{ijk}\in T
into six smaller triangles
Lai and Schumaker (2007)
;
Powell and Sabin (1977)
. This is achieved by choosing a triangle split point
v_{ijk}
inside
t_{ijk}
such that for an interior edge
e_{ij}\in E\setminus E^{b}
that is shared by triangles
t_{ijk},t_{ijk^{\prime}}\in T
, the intersection point
v_{ij}
between
e_{ij}
and
[v_{ijk},v_{ijk^{\prime}}]

lies inside
e_{ij}
. The point
v_{ij}
is an edge split point. For a boundary edge
e_{ij}\in E^{b}
, we choose the edge split point
v_{ij}
as an arbitrary point inside
e_{ij}
. Together with the vertices from
V
, these split points constitute
V_{\mathrm{PS}}
. For each
t_{ijk}\in T
we define the triangles

\displaystyle t_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle=[v_{i},v_{ij},v_{ijk}],\quad

\displaystyle t_{j,k,i}

\displaystyle=[v_{j},v_{jk},v_{ijk}],\quad

\displaystyle t_{k,i,j}

\displaystyle=[v_{k},v_{ki},v_{ijk}],

\displaystyle t_{j,i,k}

\displaystyle=[v_{j},v_{ij},v_{ijk}],

\displaystyle t_{k,j,i}

\displaystyle=[v_{k},v_{jk},v_{ijk}],

\displaystyle t_{i,k,j}

\displaystyle=[v_{i},v_{ki},v_{ijk}].

These triangles constitute
T_{\mathrm{PS}}
. Notice that here the indices are separated by commas, indicating that their order is important. Moreover, notice that
t_{i,j,k}\subset t_{ijk}
in this notation. For each
t_{ijk}\in T
we also define the edges

\displaystyle e_{i,j}

\displaystyle=[v_{i},v_{ij}],\quad

\displaystyle e_{j,k}

\displaystyle=[v_{j},v_{jk}],\quad

\displaystyle e_{k,i}

\displaystyle=[v_{k},v_{ki}],

\displaystyle e_{j,i}

\displaystyle=[v_{j},v_{ij}],

\displaystyle e_{k,j}

\displaystyle=[v_{k},v_{jk}],

\displaystyle e_{i,k}

\displaystyle=[v_{i},v_{ki}].

Again, notice that the indices are separated by commas and that
e_{i,j}\subset e_{ij}
.
Together with
[v_{i},v_{ijk}]
,
[v_{j},v_{ijk}]
,
[v_{k},v_{ijk}]
and
[v_{ij},v_{ijk}]
,
[v_{jk},v_{ijk}]
,
[v_{ki},v_{ijk}]
, these edges constitute
E_{\mathrm{PS}}
. We denote by
E_{\mathrm{PS}}^{b}\subset E_{\mathrm{PS}}
the set of all edges that lie on the boundary of
\Omega
.

For any triangulation
\Delta
, there exists a valid triangulation
\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}}
. It can be constructed by choosing
v_{ijk}
as the incenter of
t_{ijk}\in T
. An example of a Powell–Sabin refinement of a triangulation is shown in Figure

2.2
Blossoming of a Cubic Polynomial

Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
denote the space of bivariate polynomials of total degree at most
3
. The blossom of a polynomial
P\in{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
is a mapping
\mathcal{B}[P]:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
that is

•

symmetric, i.e., for every
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and any permutation
\pi
of
\left\{1,2,3\right\}
,

\mathcal{B}[P](p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=\mathcal{B}[P](p_{\pi(1)},p_{\pi(2)},p_{\pi(3)});

•

affine in the first (and hence each) argument, i.e., for every
p,q,p_{2},p_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and every
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathcal{B}[P]((1-\alpha)p+\alpha q,p_{2},p_{3})=(1-\alpha)\mathcal{B}[P](p,p_{2},p_{3})+\alpha\mathcal{B}[P](q,p_{2},p_{3});

•

and for every
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,

\mathcal{B}[P](p,p,p)=P(p).

By the blossoming principle, for each
P\in{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
, the mapping
\mathcal{B}[P]
is unique. We refer the reader to, e.g.,
Seidel (1993)
;
Speleers (2011)
for more details.

Blossoming is a convenient tool for expressing smoothness conditions of joints of two or more polynomials.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
t_{1},t_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are two nondegenerate and nonoverlapping triangles with a common edge
e
. Let
S:t_{1}\cup t_{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a
C^{0}
smooth spline

S(p)=\begin{cases}P_{1}(p)&\text{if }p\in t_{1},\\
P_{2}(p)&\text{if }p\in t_{2},\end{cases}

determined by polynomials
P_{1},P_{2}\in{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
. Let
p_{1},p_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be any pair of different points on the line containing
e
.

1.

Let
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be any point not on the line containing
e
. Then,
S
is
C^{1}
smooth across
e
if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P_{1}](p_{1},p_{1},q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P_{2}](p_{1},p_{1},q),

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P_{1}](p_{1},p_{2},q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P_{2}](p_{1},p_{2},q),

(1b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P_{1}](p_{2},p_{2},q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P_{2}](p_{2},p_{2},q).

(1c)

2.

Suppose that
S
is
C^{1}
smooth, and let
q_{1},q_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be any pair of (possibly equal) points not on the line containing
e
. Then,
S
is
C^{2}
smooth across
e
if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P_{1}](p_{1},q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P_{2}](p_{1},q_{1},q_{2}),

(2a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P_{1}](p_{2},q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P_{2}](p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}).

(2b)

Proof.

According to
(
Ramshaw, 1987
, Proposition 21.1)
, the spline
S
is
C^{r}
smooth,
r\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, across
e
if and only if

\mathcal{B}[P_{1}](a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\mathcal{B}[P_{2}](a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

(3)

for every three points
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of which at least
3-r
lie on the line containing
e
.

First, we consider
r=1
. Clearly, if
S
is
C^{1}
smooth, the equalities in (
1
) hold due to (
3
). Conversely, let
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be any three points. Since the blossom is symmetric and
S
is
C^{0}
smooth, we can assume that
a_{1},a_{2}
lie on the line containing
e
and
a_{3}
does not. Then,
a_{1},a_{2}
can be expressed as affine combinations of
p_{1},p_{2}
and
a_{3}
as an affine combination of
p_{1},p_{2},q
. As the blossom is affine in each argument and
S
is
C^{0}
smooth, the equalities in (
1
) imply (
3
).

For
r=2
, we argue similarly. The
C^{2}
smoothness of
S
clearly implies (
2
). Conversely, it is sufficient to consider a point
a_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
lying on the line containing
e
and points
a_{2},a_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
not lying on this line. Then,
a_{1}
can be expressed as an affine combination of
p_{1},p_{2}
and
a_{2}
as an affine combination of
p_{1},p_{2},q_{1}
and
a_{3}
as an affine combination of
p_{1},p_{2},q_{2}
. As the blossom is affine in each argument and
S
is
C^{1}
smooth, the equalities in (
2
) imply (
3
).
∎

2.3
Basis Functions

The space of

Given a triangle

\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}}
attached to the edge
[v_{ij},v_{ijk}]\in E_{\mathrm{PS}}

is

if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{i,j,k}}](v_{ijk},v_{i},v_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{j,i,k}}](v_{ijk},v_{i},v_{j}),

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{i,j,k}}](v_{ij},v_{i},v_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{j,i,k}}](v_{ij},v_{i},v_{j}).

(6b)

More precisely, we obtain (
6

Proposition 4

\left\{\begin{aligned} \beta_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}(S)&=\beta_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}(S)&&\text{if}\ e_{ij}\in E\setminus E^{b},\\

Remark 5

The result presented in Proposition

and analogous conditions are satisfied for the edges

3.2

[v_{i},v_{ijk}]

We now consider the triangles

of

attached to the edge

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{i,k,j}}](v_{i},v_{j},v_{k}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{i,j,k}}](v_{ijk},v_{j},v_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[{S}|_{t_{i,k,j}}](v_{ijk},v_{j},v_{k}).

(8b)

More precisely, we obtain (

Lemma 6

5.3
Properties of the Reduced Basis Functions

The reduction of
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{R}}
is designed in a way that the functions in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{R}}
preserve the main properties of the functions comprising
\mathcal{S}

Theorem 23
.

The space
{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
is a subspace of
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
.

Proof.

Suppose
P\in{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
. Clearly,
P\in{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{1}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
, and hence it can be expressed as described in Theorem
2
. Let
e_{ij}\in E
be an edge of
\Delta
. We consider only the case when
e_{ij}\in E\setminus E^{b}
, i.e.,
e_{ij}
is an interior edge of
\Delta
, as the reasoning for a boundary edge is analogous. Let
t_{ijk},t_{ijk^{\prime}}\in T_{ij}^{e}
be the triangles of
\Delta
attached to
e_{ij}
. By (
4b
),
\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}(P)=\beta_{j,i,k}^{t}(P)
and
\beta_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}(P)=\beta_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}(P)
. Then,

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}(P)B_{i,j,k}^{t}+\beta_{j,i,k}^{t}(P)B_{j,i,k}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},v_{ijk})\left(B_{i,j,k}^{t}+B_{j,i,k}^{t}\right),

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}(P)B_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}+\beta_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}(P)B_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},v_{ijk^{\prime}})\left(B_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}+B_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}\right).

Moreover, we can use (
10
) and the affinity of the blossom in the third argument to express

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},v_{ijk})

\displaystyle=(1-\omega_{ij,k})\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k})+\omega_{ij,k}\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},v_{ijk^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=(1-\omega_{ij,k^{\prime}})\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k^{\prime}})+\omega_{ij,k^{\prime}}\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k}).

Hence,

\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}(P)B_{i,j,k}^{t}+\beta_{j,i,k}^{t}(P)B_{j,i,k}^{t}+\beta_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}(P)B_{i,j,k^{\prime}}^{t}+\beta_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}(P)B_{j,i,k^{\prime}}^{t}

is by (
11
) equal to

\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k})B_{ij,k}^{e}+\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k^{\prime}})B_{ij,k^{\prime}}^{e}.

Since

\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k})=\begin{cases}\beta_{ijk}^{t}(P)&\text{if}\ t_{ijk}\in T_{\mathrm{S}},\\
\beta_{ij,k}^{e}(P)&\text{if}\ t_{ijk}\in T\setminus T_{\mathrm{S}},\end{cases}

and

\mathcal{B}[P](v_{i},v_{j},w_{ij,k^{\prime}})=\begin{cases}\beta_{ijk^{\prime}}^{t}(P)&\text{if}\ t_{ijk^{\prime}}\in T_{\mathrm{S}},\\
\beta_{ij,k^{\prime}}^{e}(P)&\text{if}\ t_{ijk^{\prime}}\in T\setminus T_{\mathrm{S}},\end{cases}

the statement follows from the definitions of
B_{ijk}^{t}
and
B_{ijk^{\prime}}^{t}
.

5.4
Comparison of the Number of Degrees of Freedom

The advantage of the basis
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{R}}
is that it provides the same quality of approximation as
\mathcal{S}
with a considerably smaller number of degrees of freedom. To argue that, let us approximately compare the dimensions of
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
and
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{1}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
. We use the estimates
|E|\approx 3|V|
and
|T|+|E^{b}|\approx 2|V|
; see, e.g.,
Lai and Schumaker (2007)
.

The dimension of
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{1}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
is
3|V|+4|E|\approx 15|V|
. On the other hand, the dimension of
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
depends on
|T_{\mathrm{S}}|
, the number of symmetrically refined triangles. If
T_{\mathrm{S}}=\emptyset
, then the dimension of
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
is
3|V|+2|E|\approx 9|V|
. In this case, the space corresponds to the Powell–Sabin spline space studied in
Speleers (2015b)
and the basis
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{R}}
matches the one proposed therein. If
T_{\mathrm{S}}=T
, then the dimension of
{\mathbb{S}}_{3}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Delta_{\mathrm{PS}})
is
3|V|+|T|+|E^{b}|\approx 5|V|
. If the triangulation
\Delta
is three-directional, the basis
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{R}}
agrees with the constructions presented in
Grošelj and Speleers (2021)
, except for the basis functions associated with the boundary edges.

Example 25
.

Let us consider the reduced spline space over a partially structured triangulation, an excerpt of which is shown in Figure
9
(left). The super-smooth basis functions on the region determined by red colored triangles are partially
C^{2}
smooth in the interior of these triangles and match the basis functions introduced in
Speleers (2015b)
. The green color indicates symmetrically refined triangles and so in their interior the super-smooth basis functions are
C^{2}
smooth. Since in this case the triangulation determined by the green colored triangles is three-directional, the basis functions correspond to the basis functions investigated in
Grošelj and Speleers (2021)
(with a possible exception of the basis functions associated with the vertices that are not shared only by green colored triangles). The triangles colored in blue represent the transition area. Following the previous discussion, we can expect an approximate reduction in the number of basis functions by a factor between
\frac{5}{3}
and
3
compared with the number of functions in the basis
\mathcal{S}
of the full
C^{1}
spline space.

Example 26
.

Starting from an arbitrary triangulation, application of one or more uniform refinements results in (local) symmetric configurations inside each of the original triangles; see Figure
9
(right) for an example. The green colored triangles are symmetrically refined, and the blue colored triangles are part of the transition area. Such refined triangulations were analyzed in
Grošelj and Speleers (2023)
. The corresponding (reduced) cubic spline spaces were tested in the context of least squares approximation and finite element approximation for second and fourth order boundary value problems. Note that the definition of the set of triangles that are part of the symmetric configurations in
Grošelj and Speleers (2023)
, denoted by
T_{\mathrm{sym}}
, is more restrictive than the definition of
T_{\mathrm{S}}
used here. Indeed, it holds
T_{\mathrm{sym}}\subseteq T_{\mathrm{S}}
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have analyzed
C^{2}
super-smoothness conditions of a previously established
C^{1}
cubic spline space on a Powell–Sabin refined triangulation. This has been done by employing a B-spline basis for
C^{1}
splines with a convenient dual representation developed in
Grošelj and Speleers (2017)
. The representation is based on blossoming of polynomials obtained by restricting a spline to the triangles of the Powell–Sabin refinement.

Blossoming offers an elegant and concise way of expressing the smoothness conditions between polynomial joints. Here this technique has been utilized to identify the
C^{2}
smoothness conditions between the functionals of the dual basis. Some of these conditions can be enforced without difficulty on general triangulations. Others are more involved but greatly simplify if the triangulation and its corresponding Powell–Sabin refinement possess certain symmetries.

It has also been shown how the
C^{2}
smoothness constraints can be integrated into the spline representation by reducing the set of basis functions. The proposed super-smooth B-spline basis functions are obtained by recombination of the original
C^{1}
basis functions and incorporate the
C^{2}
smoothness conditions to an extent that depends on the geometry of the underlying refinement.

The reduced cubic spline space obtained as the span of the constructed super-smooth B-spline basis functions contains all polynomials up to degree three and maintains the cubic precision of the full
C^{1}
spline space. Such space offers the usual flexibility of unstructured spline methods for handling irregularly partitioned parts of the domain, while the partial structuredness of the triangulation, which is common in practice, is exploited by reducing the number of degrees of freedom.

Starting from an arbitrary triangulation, application of one or more uniform refinements results in (local) symmetric configurations inside each of the original triangles. The implementation of reduced cubic spline spaces on such triangulations was investigated in
Grošelj and Speleers (2023)
. It was tested in the context of least squares approximation and finite element approximation, and the optimal convergence order was illustrated for those reduced spaces. In this paper, we have provided a more general framework to achieve space reductions by imposing super-smoothness. As such, we envision it as a versatile tool for approximation purposes.
