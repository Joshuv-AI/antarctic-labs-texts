Central Bone Mineral Density Is Not a Useful Tool to Predict Bone Strength of the Distal Femur for Cementless Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Dongwhan Suh, Dai-Soon Kwak, Yong Deok Kim, Seokjae Park, Nicole Cho, In Jun Koh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudThe increasing prevalence of cementless total knee arthroplasty (TKA) necessitates a reliable assessment of bone quality. Central bone mineral density (BMD), measured by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) in the lumbar spine and hip, is conventionally used to estimate bone quality. However, its effectiveness in predicting the actual bone strength at the knee, which is crucial for cementless TKA, is under scrutiny. This study investigated the relationship between central BMD and actual bone strength at the knee.MethodsThis prospective study included 191 knees undergoing standard posterior-stabilized TKA between November 2021 and March 2023. Central BMD was assessed 3 months before TKA, and the failure load of bone fragments collected during box preparation was directly measured using an indentation test. Relationships between central BMD and failure load as a measure of the actual bone strength at the knee were analyzed.ResultsLinear regression analysis revealed a weak correlation between central BMD and the actual bone strength at the knee (R2 = 0.146 in all patients; < 0.001 in osteoporosis group; 0.126 in non-osteoporosis group). The correlation suggested by the regression models was particularly insignificant in the osteoporosis subgroup, showing that central BMD is not a reliable predictor of bone strength for cementless TKA.ConclusionsCentral BMD measurements have limited utility in accurately predicting the real bone strength at the knee for cementless TKA. This study highlights the need for more specific and direct methods of assessing bone quality at the knee to ensure the success of cementless TKA.Keywords: Bone quality, Bone mineral density, Osteoporosis, Total knee arthroplasty, Cementless

The increasing prevalence of cementless total knee arthroplasty (TKA), now accounting for 18% of TKA utilization in the United States, signifies a shift in patient demographics and surgical preferences.1,2) While advancements in implant design and material manufacturing have addressed some of the initial fixation challenges faced by earlier generations of cementless implants,3,4,5) concerns about migration of components or early aseptic loosening persist.6,7) This necessitates further investigation into factors influencing implant stability, particularly bone quality.

Bone quality, influenced by factors such as sex, age, and race, is conventionally evaluated in clinical setting through bone mineral density (BMD) of the central skeleton including the lumbar spine and hip8,9) (Fig. 1). However, BMD measurements, primarily obtained via dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), offer an incomplete picture as they do not account for bone structure.10) Nevertheless, several studies have claimed that among various techniques for assessments, BMD still is the single most important predictor of the bone strength.11) The central BMD by DEXA has shown to correlate with peripheral BMD and with bone quality, albeit with limitations.9,12,13) Despite claims of BMD being a primary predictor of bone strength, its correlation with actual bone strength at the knee, which is critical for cementless TKA, remains questionable.

Recently, the concept of preoperative bone health optimization that aims to identify and address osteoporosis before surgery to enhance postoperative outcomes is emerging.14) This approach becomes increasingly relevant as the use of cementless TKA implants grows.15,16,17) The success of these implants hinges on the ability of bone to support ingrowth and prevent initial micromotion, making the assessment of bone quality pivotal.15) However, as no gold standard for determining adequate bone quality specific to cementless TKA nor for evaluating bone quality at the knee has been established, surgeons face a significant challenge to select optimal bone quality before cementless TKA.

This study was conducted to determine (1) whether central BMD measured before cementless TKA as a screening tool for assessing bone quality correlates with the actual bone strength at the knee measured by an indentation test; (2) whether the distribution of actual bone strength of the knee is different between osteoporosis and non-osteoporosis patients defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) working group. We hypothesized that central BMD would not correlate with the actual bone strength of the knee and that the distribution of actual bone strength of the knee would not be influenced by the severity of osteoporosis.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital (IRB No. PC22OISI0068) and conducted with informed consent from all participants.

In this prospective study, we chose to investigate 191 knees undergoing standard posterior-stabilized (PS) prosthesis TKA (Triathlon, Stryker) between November 2021 and March 2023. We included only patients with primary osteoarthritis who underwent TKA and agreed to participate. Medical records and radiological results were reviewed. All radiography and DEXA (Horizon DXA system) were taken 3 months before TKA. The results of DEXA are shown as T-scores (Table 1).

To directly assess bone quality, we employed an indentation test that has been well established from previous literatures on various anatomic sites.18) Contrary to DEXA that provides indirect imaging information, it provides a direct measurement of bone strength, crucial for evaluating the suitability of a patient for cementless TKA. The resected bone specimen was acquired during PS implant box preparation. The specimen was stored in −70 ℃ after acquisition and then defrosted the day before the experiment. With a linear precision saw (IsoMet 5000), 2 parallel diamond blades (gap 6 mm) were set to make a parallel bone cut to prepare the bone specimen for the indentation test (Fig. 2). The bone specimen was made 6 mm in thickness and then placed on the servohydraulic machine (Instron 5567) for indentation test machine, which used a 6-mm-diameter flat punch indenter with an area of approximately 28.3 mm2. The indenter was set in contact with the surface under a force of 2 N as the initial position, and an indentation experiment was conducted to be a depth of 2 mm at a speed of 1.0 mm/min. The indentation depth and load values were recorded at 30 Hz, and the results were analyzed. The stiffness was calculated based on the slope of the linear increase portion before the first peak, and both the initial yield load and the maximum yield load were determined. The calculation of stiffness, initial yield load, and maximum yield load was carried out using the testing machine software (Instron Bluehill version 4.23) (Fig. 3).

Statistical Analysis

Linear regression analysis models were implemented to assess the relationship between central BMD and actual bone strength, measured through indentation testing. Central BMDs used in regression analyses were T-scores of the lumbar spine, which were calculated by averaging the BMDs of L1–L4 and femur neck. This analysis primarily focused on 2 aspects of bone strength: the initial failure load of the bone specimen and its stiffness. The study examined these parameters in 3 distinct groups: (1) the entire cohort, (2) a subgroup of osteoporotic patients, and (3) a subgroup of non-osteoporotic patients. All statistical analyses were conducted using Rex-Pro (Rex: Excel-based statistical analysis software, version 3.6.1.0; RexSoft Inc.; https://rexsoft.org/). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The 95% CI was reported to 4 decimal places using rounding.

RESULTS

Of the 191 patients, 157 (82%) were female and 34 were male. The mean age was 68 years (range, 53–86 years), and the average body mass index (BMD) was 27.2 kg/m2 (range, 19.6–36.4 kg/m2). Regarding the central BMD assessed at the lumbar spines and hip, the mean T-scores were −0.5 for the lumbar spine total (range, −3.6 to 4.9) and −1.2 for the femur neck (range, −3.5 to 2.8). Among the patients, osteoporosis was identified in 28 patients (14.7%) according to the WHO working group’s definition (a T-score < −2.5 from DEXA) (Table 1). There was no statistically significant relationship between the actual bone strength and age (p = 0.303) and the logistic regression analysis for binomial analysis revealed that the actual bone strength of the knee was weakly correlated with sex (regression coefficient, 0.014; p < 0.01).

There was a weak correlation between central BMD and bone strength at the knee in all patients. The regression coefficient estimates for the first failure load of the bone fragment were 9.031 for the lumbar spine (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A) and 1.136 for the femur neck (p = 0.736) (Fig. 4B). In addition, the model yielded a low R2 value of 0.146, indicating a minimal correlation between central BMD and bone strength of the knee.

Plots of bone mineral density of the lumbar spine (A) and femur neck (B) versus the first failure load of the bone fragment in all patients. The coefficient estimates were 9.031 for the lumbar spine (p < 0.01, R2 = 0.146) and 1.136 for the femur neck (p = 0.736).

Central BMD was not related to actual bone strength of the knee in osteoporosis patients but showed a weak correlation in non-osteoporosis patients. In the osteoporosis patients, the regression coefficients for the first failure load were −0.633 for the lumbar spine (p = 0.912) (Fig. 5A) and −1.010 for the femur neck (p = 0.928) (Fig. 5B). The R2 value for this subgroup was < 0.001, even lower than that of the overall patient group. In the non-osteoporosis patients, the regression coefficients were 10.068 for the lumbar spine (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6A) and 0.046 for the femur neck (p = 0.991) (Fig. 6B). The R2 value was 0.126.

DISCUSSION

The key finding of this study is that central BMD exhibited a weak correlation with the actual bone strength of the knee across all patients. Importantly, it was not associated with the actual failure load of the knee in osteoporosis patients, for whom accurate bone strength assessment is critical in deciding whether to proceed with cementless TKA. While central BMD is conventionally used for pre-operative osteoporosis assessment, discrepancies between central BMD and the actual bone strength of the knee are not uncommon in clinical practice. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between preoperative central BMD and the actual bone strength of the knee, as well as to assess the feasibility of central BMD as a predictive screening tool for determining appropriate bone strength before cementless TKA.

The results of this study indicated that central BMD should not be used for the estimation of the actual bone strength of the knee before cementless TKA. In this study, the R2 values, which indicate the explanatory power of the linear regression model, were found to be 0.146 and this suggests that the regression model does not adequately fit the data to observe a correlation between central BMD and the first failure load of the knee. Previously, central BMDs assessed at the lumbar spines and hip were reported to be strongly correlated with the BMDs of peripheral sites such as the knee, forearm, and distal radius9,12) and BMD was generally known to be correlated with mechanical properties of a retrieved bone sample.19,20) However, our findings do not concur with those of previous studies advocating that central BMD might predict the actual bone strength of the knee. It is difficult to compare our study with previous studies because our study is, to our knowledge, the first study that investigated the relationship between central BMD and actual bone strength of the knee. Thus, the reason why the results of this study were different from those of previous studies remain unclear. Further research focusing on the relationship between central BMD and peripheral bone strength is urgently needed to ascertain whether central BMD is appropriate to be a screening test for peripheral bone procedures.

This study demonstrated that central BMD provides unreliable information on the actual bone strength of the knee, especially in osteoporosis patients. We found in this study that the low R2 values in the osteoporosis subgroup indicate that the model’s ability to explain the variation in bone strength based on central BMD is limited and not statistically reliable for this purpose. By comparison, central BMD in non-osteoporosis patients showed weak correlation. On the other hand, it is well documented that poor bone quality of the knee is strongly associated with early postoperative migration of component.16,21) Our findings, taken with previous studies, underscore the urgent need for more specific intraoperative or preoperative radiographic assessment tools for screening osteoporosis of the knee. Traditionally, intraoperative assessment has relied on surgeon intuition, including factors such as ease of bone sawing, manual palpation, and fit of the trial.4) However, recent advancements in imaging techniques, such as conventional knee computed tomography (CT) or dual-energy CT, have shown promising diagnostic value in knee joint-specific evaluation.22) Further research should focus on validating intraoperative bone quality assessment methods and exploring the relationship between the level of Hounsfield units in conventional knee CT or volumetric BMD of dual-energy knee CT and the actual bone strength of retrieved bone samples from the knee joint.

Up to date, there is no standard definition or assessment tool for determining optimal bone strength that can avoid early failure after cementless TKA. Theoretically, the results of our indentation test are definitely helpful to establish the minimum bone strength for preventing migration after cementless TKA. During typical daily activities, the peak stress exerted on the knee joint is approximately 2.5 times the body weight.23) Consequently, knowing the patient’s body weight enables the calculation of the minimum strength required to withstand activities of daily living (ADL). In this study, the first failure load was defined as the force at which a crack appears when pressed with a 6-mm diameter punch and the area of this punch corresponded to 28.3 mm2. Therefore, if we know the cross-sectional area of the femur component during standing, this can be converted into a ratio relative to our punch area for estimation. Consequently, using the measurements from this experiment, it would be possible to approximate the suitability of using a cementless implant based on the patient’s weight and the size of the femur component. We can assume if the estimated bone strength based on patient’s weight and femoral component size is greater than the minimum required strength for ADL, then cementless TKA would be suitable and vice versa. Based on our dataset, 36% of osteoporosis patients who were classified by the central BMD showed sufficient bone strength for cementless TKA (under-estimating) and 42% of non-osteoporosis patients had insufficient bone strength for cementless TKA (over-estimating). Thus, central BMD is highly unpredictable for estimating the actual bone strength of the knee without any definite tendency of over-estimating or under-estimating. Further research is necessary to establish the minimum bone strength for preventing initial migration after cementless TKA.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, there was a predominance of female patients, which was challenging to control given the higher prevalence and incidence of advanced osteoarthritis in women in our country.24,25,26) Additional research is required to determine how the balance of the sex ratio may affect the bone strength of the knee. Second, as the aim of this study was to determine whether the central BMD would be a useful tool to predict bone strength of the knee, this study only investigated the relationship between the BMD of central skeleton and the failure load of a bone fragment from the knee joint. Although the relationship between central and knee BMD may provide additional information, BMD is only an indirect imaging value that does not represent the actual bone strength and the diagnostic value of peripheral BMD remains unclear.27) Third, as the BMD of periarticular areas was affected by weight loading,28) a limited portion of bone specimens that was located at box preparation may not be representative of bone strength of the knee. However, this non-weight-bearing metaphyseal bone may reduce confounding factors that were affected by weight load or arthritis. Finally, this study did not assess bone structure but only measured the first failure load representing bone strength. As adequate bone structure is also required for proper bone ingrowth, which is another essential factor for preventing early failure after cementless TKA, further study dealing with evaluating proper bone structure is required. Nevertheless, given the limited clinical modalities available and the critical issue of component migration in cementless TKA, we believe that this study provides valuable information on an adequate method to screen bone quality before cementless TKA.

In conclusion, central BMD showed a weak correlation with actual failure load, making it unreliable for assessing bone strength of the knee. Notably, in the osteoporosis group, where bone strength assessment is crucial, there was no correlation between central BMD and failure load. Therefore, a more precise method for measuring knee bone quality is essential to accurately identify suitable candidates for cementless TKA.
