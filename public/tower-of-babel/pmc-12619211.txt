Does polypharmacy affect epigenetic aging in older people? Evidence from a longitudinal epigenome-wide methylation study
Authors: Alessandro Gialluisi, Alfonsina Tirozzi, Simona Costanzo, Danilo Licastro, Augusto Di Castelnuovo, Miriam Shasa Quiccione, Stefania Falciglia, Margherita Degasperi, Teresa Panzera, Sara Magnacca, Mariarosaria Persichillo, Fabrizia Noro, Chiara Cerletti, Maria Benedetta Donati, Giovanni de Gaetano, Licia Iacoviello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolypharmacy, defined as taking ≥ 5 different daily medications, is common in older adults and has been linked with neuropsychiatric/neurological and other health conditions. To clarify the potential molecular implications, we tested the hypothesis that polypharmacy may influence DNA methylation (DNAm) patterns in aging, in a longitudinal Italian cohort (N = 1,098; mean (SD) age at recruitment: 58.8 (5.6) years, 51.3% women; median (IQR) follow-up 12.6 (1.1) years).ResultsWe tested associations of polypharmacy with several DNAm aging clocks (Hannum, Horvath, GrimAge, DNAmPhenoAge, DunedinPACE), through linear mixed models incrementally adjusted for age, sex, education, prevalent health conditions and lifestyles, leukocyte counts and residual batch effects. This revealed significant positive associations of GrimAge acceleration and DunedinPACE with the switch to polypharmacy status during follow-up (Beta (SE): 0.024 (0.008) and0.0012 (0.0004)). While the association of GrimAge was driven by a DNAm-based surrogate of tissue inhibitor metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP-1), no significant association was detected for component CpGs of DunedinPACE. When we tested associations of polypharmacy with 668,413 CpGs epigenome-wide, we observed no statistically significant findings (top hit: cg07675998; chr11q13.1; Beta (SE) = 0.009 (0.002); p = 1.5 × 10–6). However, these showed significant enrichments of several biological functions and pathways related to renal tissue, lipoproteins, inflammatory and immune response.ConclusionsThese findings suggest an influence of polypharmacy on accelerated epigenetic aging and on altered methylation patterns in the genome, suggesting a potential implication of pathways related to renal tissue development, lipoproteins and cholesterol homeostasis, inflammatory and immune response, in line with previous proteomic analyses of polypharmacy mouse models. These observations also suggest potential targets for mitigating disruptive effects of polypharmacy on elderly health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02007-7.

Introduction

Polypharmacy, which is usually defined as taking five or more different medications daily, is common in older adults, which are typically affected by multimorbidity [1]. Indeed, the prevalence of polypharmacy in older populations (aged 65 years or older) is much higher than the one detected among younger populations (45% vs 27% [2]). Although guidelines recommend polypharmacy for the treatment of multiple comorbid conditions, an important side aspect of this ever-increasing trend is often neglected, namely the potentially adverse health outcomes in older people [3]. Indeed, a significant influence of polypharmacy on heightened mortality and hospitalization risk has been observed in aging cohorts from different countries, such as Italy [4], the UK [5] and South Korea [6]. Similarly, intensive drug therapy was also associated with the onset of age-related health conditions and complications, like macrovascular outcomes in older people with diabetes and hypertension, one of the most represented categories among subjects under chronic polypharmacotherapy [7]. Moreover, other adverse age-related outcomes have been associated with polypharmacy, including cognitive and functional impairment [8], frailty, falls and disability, particularly among older adults [1, 3]. Overall, despite often clinically necessary, polypharmacy is gaining more and more attention in the management of older people with multiple health conditions, due to the many associated adverse outcomes [3]. Still, whether these deleterious effects are due to adverse drug events, drug-drug or disease-drug interactions, or to a lower level of adherence to pharmacological treatments, is difficult to establish, both for the ethical hindrance on carrying out intervention studies in humans and for the scarcity of functional studies on polypharmacy in animal models [9]. This makes it difficult to establish what biological pathways are altered by polypharmacy, due to the shortage of experimental data on this aspect. While alterations of drug absorption and clearance pathways seem the most obvious candidates – prominently gut, liver and renal functions – some studies suggest disruptive effects on the central nervous system [10]. A so far neglected pathway through which polypharmacy may exert its deleterious effects is represented by epigenetic changes and in particular by DNA methylation, a process consisting in the addition of small methyl groups to cytosine residues in specific regions of the genome. As an organism ages, some regions accumulate methyl groups (hypermethylation) while others undergo a hypomethylation, which represents the rationale for the estimation of epigenetic (DNA methylation) aging clocks based on these patterns [11]. These indices of biological aging are usually based on the use of different regression models in order to estimate the “official” (chronological) age of subjects as precisely as possible (first generation clocks), health conditions and healthspan (second generation clocks), or alternatively the pace of aging of one or more systems/organs in the body, rather than a static biological age measure (third generation clocks) [12]. Most importantly, these composite markers well predict several health-related risks, including chronic disease conditions and mortality [13], and may well represent potential mediators of the deleterious effects of polypharmacy.

Here we aimed to test the hypothesis that polypharmacy may influence DNA methylation (DNAm) patterns and aging, in a longitudinal Italian aging cohort, over ⁓13 years of follow-up. To do so, we carried out a three-step analysis: first we tested different DNAm age acceleration estimators (hereafter called “epigenetic clocks”) for association with polypharmacy, then we tested surrogate DNAm-based markers and/or CpGs characterizing the associated clocks, and finally we expanded the analysis at the epigenome-wide level. The results of the epigenome-wide analysis were then tested for enrichment of specific biological functions and pathways based on publicly available databases, with the aim of identifying potential mechanisms explaining the effects of polypharmacy.

Materials and methods

Population of study

The Moli-sani Study is a prospective cohort study established in 2005–2010 with the enrolment of 24,325 men and women (aged ≥ 35 years) randomly recruited from the general population of Molise, a Central-Southern Italian region, to investigate risk and/or protective factors in the onset of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular and cancer diseases [14, 15].

Moli-sani participants who were not under any drug therapy at baseline recruitment, were ≥ 65 years old and were still resident in the Molise region upon recall of the cohort (N = 2,221) were re-examined between March 7, 2018 and January 31, 2020. A record linkage of the cohort study with the regional drug prescription register allowed us to update the information on drug therapy for the Moli-sani participants, identifying all prescriptions and polypharmacy therapy from baseline (2005–2010) through follow-up recruitment (2018–2020) (source: Farmastat; see URLs). This allowed us to identify those participants who were under no polypharmacotherapy – namely those taking daily medications < 5 different therapeutic groups [4]—at baseline visit (N = 1,164). These participants were further divided into two groups, based on whether they were exposed to polypharmacy (845) or not (319) during follow-up, which represented the main exposure in downstream analyses. Further details on participants selection are reported in Fig. 1 and described in [4]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the IRCCS Neuromed (28/02/2018). Blood samples

The same procedures for blood collection, sample storage and biomarkers analyses were used at baseline (2005–2006) and at the re-examination (2018–2020). Venous blood samples were obtained between 7 and 9 AM from participants who had fasted overnight and had refrained from smoking for at least 6 h.

White blood cell counts (103/uL) and leukocyte (monocytes, lymphocytes and granulocytes) fractions (%) were estimated through a centralized cell counter (Coulter HMX, Beckman Coulter, IL Milan, Italy at baseline, and Siemens ADVIA 120 at re-examination), within 3 h from blood collection. Other details and full descriptives of blood markers are reported in [16–18].

Methylation analyses

Among the recalled participants, 1,098 subjects (301 under polypharmacy) underwent longitudinal methylation analyses through the Illumina© EPIC array v1 (865,918 CpGs), to investigate the relationship between polypharmacy and altered methylation patterns in the genome (Fig. 1). Specifically, for each participant two blood samples were retrieved from the Neuromed Biobanking Center—corresponding to the cohort recruitment (2005–2010, t0) and recall (2018–2020, t1) time-points in the study (Nsamples = 2,196). After DNA extraction through a salting out in-house protocol and bisulfitation using the EZ-96 DNA Methylation Kit (ZYMO RESEARCH®), quality Control (QC), normalization and batch effect removal of methylation (beta) signals were carried out in ChAMP v2.20.1 [19] (see [20] for further details). Briefly, probes were filtered out if they showed a detection p-value > 0.01 (94,779), fewer than 3 beads in more than 5% of all samples (517); if they were cross-reactive probes (10); if they were located on sex chromosomes (14,185); if they were not CpG dinucleotides (2,761) or showed biased DNA methylation signals due to Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms detected at the same site (85,343). The computed methylation signals underwent i) a beta-mixture quantile (BMIQ) normalization, ii) identification of batch effects through singular value decomposition (svd) to test associations between principal components, biological and technical factors, and iii) combat analysis to remove such effects (technical variables adjusted included plate, well, slide and array). Overall, 668,413 probes and all the samples (N = 2,196) passed QC, which were used to compute different DNAm aging clocks (see below). Both Beta values and M-values (namely log2(Beta)) were computed, since the formers are needed for computing DNAm ages and the latters are more suitable for testing methylation levels of single CpGs as outcomes in linear models [21].

DNA methylation aging clocks

We computed two first generation DNAm ages, namely the Hannum [22] and the Horvath DNAm clocks [22], as well as intrinsic and extrinsic DNAm age [23] [24], and two second-generation clocks, namely GrimAge [25] and DNAmPhenoAge [26]. Where feasible – for Hannum, Horvath, GrimAge and DNAmPhenoAge clocks – we computed principal component (PC)-based version of these epigenetic clocks as described by Higgins-Chen et al. (2022) [27], using the Github codes made available by the authors (see URLs), since this approach significantly improves the technical reliability and reproducibility of epigenetic age measures, which is even more important in a longitudinal study with repeated measures. All these clocks were computed as linear regression residuals of each epigenetic age vs CA of the participants. Intrinsic and extrinsic epigenetic age accelerations (IEAA and EEAA) were also computed, using the methylclock v1.12.0 package (see URLs), as the residuals of the regression of Horvath DNAm age vs CA (and estimated white blood cell counts for IEAA) [28, 29]. Finally, we computed a third-generation DNAm clock, the DunedinPACE, using the homonymous package (see URLs). This was developed to predict the pace of aging in a longitudinal cohort, based on the rates of change in 19 biomarkers of multi-organ system integrity, measured over four time points spanning 20 years of follow-up. Therefore, it is expressed as year of biological aging accelerations per year of chronological aging [30]. Covariates

Sociodemographic data were obtained by interviewer-administered questionnaires. Education was based on the highest qualification attained and was categorized as up to primary (≤ 5 years of study), lower secondary (≤ 8 years), upper secondary school (> 8 and ≤ 13 years) and postsecondary level (> 13 years).

Personal history of cardiovascular disease (CVD: angina, myocardial infarction, revascularization procedures, peripheral artery diseases and cerebrovascular events) was self-reported and confirmed by medical records – as was history of cancer—and therapy in use. Participants were considered to have type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension or hyperlipidaemia if they were taking disease-specific drugs, as assessed by the interviewer. Subjects were also classified as never, current, or former smokers (reported not having smoked at all over the previous 12 months or more). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by squared height (kg/m2) of the participants.

Habitual food intake was assessed through the semi-quantitative EPIC food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), validated and adapted to the Italian population [31] to evaluate the participants' diet in the previous 12 months. The FFQ includes a total of 188 foods that were classified into 45 predefined food groups based on similar nutritional characteristics or culinary use. Using a specifically designed software [32], the frequencies and quantities of each food were linked to the Italian Food Tables [33] to obtain estimates of the daily intake of macro and micronutrients, energy (Kcal/day) and alcohol (g/day) [34]. Leisure-time physical activity was assessed through a structured questionnaire and expressed as daily energy expenditure in metabolic equivalent task-hours (MET-h/day) [35].

All the covariates showed a missing rate below 10% (Table S1). Missing observations were imputed through a k-nearest neighbours algorithm, implemented in the knn function of the R package VIM [36], with k = 10, before the analysis. This way, final analyses were performed on the totality of samples with epigenetic data available (N = 2,196, i.e. 1,098 participants with two samples each).

Statistical analyses

All the statistical analyses were carried out in R v4.1.0 to v4.4.0 (see URLs), through a three-step association analysis. First, we tested epigenetic clocks for association with polypharmacy in linear mixed effect models incrementally adjusted for: i) age and sex (Model 1); ii) Model 1 + education level completed (Model 2); iii) Model 2 + prevalent health conditions like CVD, T2D, hypertension, cancer and hyperlipidemia, which may affect both DNA methylation levels in the genome and medications in use (Model 3); and iv) Model 3 + lifestyles or their proxies, including daily alcohol (g/day) and caloric intake (Kcal/day), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), smoking status and daily level of physical activity (met-h/day) (Model 4), since these may also act as confounders in the relationship between chronic conditions (hence polypharmacy) and methylation patterns. All models were adjusted for white blood cells count, lymphocytes, granulocytes and monocytes fractions from both baseline and follow-up assessment, as well as for the first eight principal components derived from singular value decomposition of M-values, to account for residual batch effects, where appropriate. Associations were considered significant when the time-by-polypharmacy interaction term showed fixed effect associations with p < 0.01 (after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing of five different aging clocks) in the most conservative model (Model 4), and concordant effect sizes were detected across all the Models tested.

When a significant association with any epigenetic clock was observed, all the CpGs included in that clock were further tested for association with polypharmacy, using the most enriched model above (Model 4), applying Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing of the different CpGs analysed. Alternatively, for second generation clocks like GrimAge [25], DNAm-based surrogate component markers were computed, using the same PC-based approach described in [27] (see URLs).

Finally, a longitudinal epigenome-wide association scan was performed for all the 668,413 autosomal CpGs passing QC, using Model 4 as the main analysis model, to identify potential changes in methylation patterns of CpGs other than those tested within the associated epigenetic clocks. In this analysis, an epigenome-wide significance threshold was set to α = 9 × 10−8, as advised for analyses based on the EPIC array [37]. A gene set enrichment analysis of CpG associations was also performed, using the methylglm function within the methylGSA R package. This function uses generalized linear models to test for enrichment single gene sets and pathways while accounting for gene length bias, through a functional class scoring approach [38]. Specifically, we tested all the gene sets and pathways available in the Gene Ontology (GO) terms [39], in the REACTOME [40] and in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [41] databases, with a number of genes ≥ 10 and ≤ 1,000. For all three analyses, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using FDR correction, accounting for 9,293 GO terms, 192 KEGG and 1,512 REACTOME pathways tested. An enrichment was considered significant if the FDR was < 0.05.

Results

Compared to participants who were never under polypharmacy therapy during the study (n = 797), subjects who were prescribed at least five different medications (n = 301) were generally older (p < 0.001), less educated (p < 0.01), more frequently affected by comorbidities like CVD, T2D, hyperlipidemia and hypertension (p < 0.01), and showed a lower white blood cell count (p < 0.001), both at baseline and at follow-up recruitment (Table S2, S3). Similarly, their BMI was higher (p < 0.001), but the daily caloric intake was lower, at both time points (p < 0.01). The only characteristics which showed inconsistent discrepancies between the two time points were the level of physical activity and of alcohol intake—which were significantly lower at follow-up but not at baseline recruitment (p < 0.001) – and the percentage of lymphocytes and granulocytes over the total count of white blood cells, with the former being lower and the second being higher in those under polypharmacy at follow-up, despite their comparability between the groups at baseline (p ≤ 0.01).

A similar analysis was carried out for the DunedinPACE clock’s components: out of 173 CpGs used for its computation, only 146 were available in our study, due to other sites being filtered out during QC. Discussion

In this study, we analysed the unexplored relationship between polypharmacy, epigenetic aging and DNA methylation patterns in the epigenome, in a longitudinal aging cohort. The analysis of a broad spectrum of DNAm age accelerations, including first (Hannum’s, Horvath’s, IEAA and EEAA), second (GrimAge, DNAmPhenoAge) and third generation clocks (DunedinPACE), revealed subtle but significant positive associations of GrimAge and DunedinPACE with the switch to polypharmacy status, over a median follow-up of 12.6 years. These translate into an average acceleration of biological aging for these two clocks – for participants switching to polypharmacy compared to those who were stably in the non-polypharmacy status—by ⁓0.24 years (equivalent to about 9 days) in terms of mortality risk (GrimAge) and by ⁓0.001 years (equivalent to about 9 h) per year of chronological aging in terms of pace of aging (DunedinPACE). Interestingly, this positive trend was consistent between GrimAge and DunedinPACE and in general with the other aging clocks tested, despite these did not show statistically significant associations across all the incremental models tested. This slight discrepancy may be explained by several potential reasons, the main being that second- and third-generation clocks mat be more sensitive to the effects of polypharmacy compared to first-generation clocks. Indeed, due to the composition of markers which were used for their training, by construction they account for the integrity of multiple organ systems and aging domains in the body, providing a more holistic view of the aging process compared to clocks that may focus on specific tissues or systems [25, 30].

While the observed effect sizes are quite small and should be interpreted with caution, this represents the first consistent evidence that polypharmacy may affect the biological aging and particularly of epigenetic aging. Indeed, the deleterious effects of polypharmacy on human health have been increasingly reported in the last years, with several age-related adverse outcomes being associated. Our group recently observed a significant influence of polypharmacy on heightened mortality and hospitalization risk in the Moli-sani cohort, for both all and cardiovascular causes [4], in line with independent studies from other populations [5, 6, 42, 43]. While it would be interesting to test the hypothesis that an accelerated epigenetic aging may explain part of this influence, the current sample size with available epigenetic data do not allow us to do so.

Among the different aging domains investigated, one in particular – tagged by the DNAm-based surrogate of tissue inhibitor metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP-1)—showed to drive the association detected between polypharmacy and GrimAge acceleration. TIMP-1 is a regulatory hub within a large protein-interaction network and a fundamental player in several biological processes related to aging, prominently in regulating the extracellular matrix turnover and remodelling, tissue fibrosis, cell proliferation and apoptosis, and in modulating the activity of both innate and adaptive immune cells, as a key pro-inflammatory cytokine [44–47]. All these processes represent suggestive candidates in potentially mediating the effects of polypharmacy on mortality, warranting functional validation of these findings.

When we carried out analyses at the single CpG level, we did not observe any significant association, neither when testing all the CpGs available from DunedinPACE nor at the epigenome-wide level, where the top hit was located at cg07675998. Interestingly, this site was among the top genome-wide significant associations in a large EWAS of blood C-reactive protein levels, one of the inflammatory markers most frequently used in daily clinical practice, and has been previously associated in an EWAS including several age-related conditions, including prevalent ischemic heart disease and incident chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), despite these associations did not survive in models adjusted for lifestyles and education [48]. Based on publicly available functional genomic database information integrated within the UCSC Genome Browser (see URLs), this site lay within a histone H3K27ac (acetylation) mark, a well-recognized marker for active enhancers [49], which may be associated with the (RNA-seq) expression levels of the long noncoding RNA AP005273.1 (ENSG00000232500.1), prominently expressed within testis and brain tissues (GTEx release V8, August 2019; see URLs). A search for Expression Quantitative Trait Methylation (eQTM) sites at cg07675998 through the interrogation of common platforms like the eQTM Atlas, BIOS QTL and iMethyl (see URLs), did not reveal any significant association of the detected CpG with the expression levels of any gene, not even of AP005273.1. Moreover, despite previous evidence reporting this transcript to have downregulated expression during the osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow‑derived mesenchymal stem cells from patients suffering from osteonecrosis of the femoral head [50], the gene is poorly characterized and, to the best of our knowledge, its expression levels are not altered by any drug or small molecule. Therefore, a speculation on the potential role of AP005273.1 and on the drugs potentially responsible for specific alterations of methylation and expression levels in this region is too premature and warrants further replications and deeper investigation of our findings.

The analysis of the epigenome-wide CpG associations revealed enrichments for several biological functions and pathways related to renal tissue development, lipoproteins and cholesterol homeostasis and biosynthesis, inflammatory and immune response, suggesting important insights into mechanisms potentially implicated in the deleterious effects of polypharmacy. Indeed, polypharmacy has been reported to affect several body domains both in clinical and preclinical studies, such as alterations of the gut microbiota diversity and composition [51] and of the circulating levels of inflammatory markers like interleukin-8 and chemokine ligand 3 (although this evidence was not confirmed in animal models [52]). Some potential links with a reduced capacity of the adaptive immune response have also been reported [53, 54], although evidence from preclinical models is currently scarce, to our knowledge. Of note, a recent proteomic analysis of liver tissues in aging mouse models exposed to drug treatments revealed a unique signature of protein expression changes in polypharmacy compared to monotherapy status [55]. Indeed, polypharmacy altered the expression of proteins involved in both innate and adaptive immunity, as well as in drug, cholesterol, and amino acid metabolism, in line with the enriched pathways and functions detected in the present study. Most of the above-mentioned effects of polypharmacy have been reported to be reversible under deprescribing in animal models [10, 51, 55].

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of the present study include its novelty – since it represents the first study testing the influence of polypharmacy on epigenetic aging measures – testing different generations of epigenetic clocks—and on methylation patterns in the genome – and the longitudinal design, which allows to establish temporal directions in the analysed associations. Moreover, the wealth of covariates available allowed us to build conservatively adjusted models.

However, our study also presents some limitations. First, it is limited to a relatively small sub-cohort of elders from a small region in central-Southern Italy, which limits the generalizability of findings. While the peculiarity of the exposure made it unfeasible to attempt replication of our findings in independent aging cohorts, such replication is warranted (see Data and Coding availability for groups potentially interested in requests to share the analysis protocol and pipelines). Second, while it would be more appropriate to remove participants with prevalent health conditions, so to rule out any potential reverse causality bias on epigenetic aging, the definition of polypharmacy did not allow us to do so and we are confident that adjustment for these conditions allowed us to isolate the pure influence of polypharmacy on epigenetic aging and methylation changes. Third, potentially inappropriate prescriptions (PIPs) were only available at baseline, as computed by a specific tool implemented in the regional drug prescription register [4]. This lack of updated information, along with the lack of repeated blood draws and epigenetic data for each year of the follow-up, makes it unfeasible to test potential influences of PIPs on methylation patterns. This may allow us to investigate in detail whether PIPs drive the association between polypharmacy and epigenetic aging and the methylation changes detected, which we plan as a future development in a larger longitudinal cohort. Moreover, repeated follow-up measures may make it possible to provide a detailed breakdown of the age ranges where polypharmacy is more influent. Last, a randomized clinical trial would be the ideal study design to potentially test whether discontinuation or modifications of pharmacological treatments influence changes in methylation patterns. This represents a potentially novel frontier in human studies, to investigate how to revert or mitigate the disruptive effects of polypharmacy on the epigenome.

Conclusions

Overall, these findings further corroborate the idea that, despite sometimes necessary, polypharmacy should be avoided as much as possible, especially in old people, suggest potential molecular targets for functional validation and deprescribing as a potential public health action to further investigate, in order to mitigate the disruptive effects of polypharmacy on elderly health.
