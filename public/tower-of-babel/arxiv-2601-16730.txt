Torsion Obstruction for Conclusive Posets
Bekir DANIŞ, İsmail Alperen ÖĞÜT

Abstract
We give a counterexample to a conjecture made by Cigler, Jerman and Wojciechowski stating that all posets are conclusive. We also provide combinatorial characterizations for conclusiveness of finite posets and the existence of outer derivations. 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 06A11, Secondary 06A07. Keywords: conclusive poset, derivation, minimal finite model, real projective plane, torsion.



1
Introduction

Let
P
be a finite poset,
I(P,k)
its incidence algebra over some commutative ring
k
. A classical result states that the cohomology group
H^{n}(I(P,k))
vanishes for all degrees (
n\geq 1
) if
P
does not contain any crown subposets (
[
7
]
,
[
9
]
). This is an obstruction for the non-vanishing of the cohomology that is independent of the ring
k
.

In a recent paper by Cigler, Jerman and Wojciechowski (
[
5
]
, Conjecture
3.1
), it was conjectured that the non-vanishing of the first cohomology, assuming
k
is non-trivial, is a property that is independent of
k
(the precise statement is different, we shall explain why it is equivalent). This independence is named as being
conclusive
. The authors verify the conjecture for posets with at most
10
elements (
[
5
, Corollary
6.7
]
). The main purpose of this paper is to provide a counterexample to this conjecture.

If a poset admits torsion in its first homology group, then the vanishing of its first cohomology depends on whether
k
possesses the respective torsion, thus a counterexample to the conjecture can be obtained. In fact, the minimal finite model of
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
(see Figure
2
), admits
2
-torsion in homology. Moreover, in
[
1
]
, through computer verification, it was proven by Adamazsek that the smallest poset whose homology admits torsion has
13
elements, which increases the above bound to
12
. A more conceptual proof which avoids the reliance on calculations is given by Cianci and Ottina in
[
4
]
.

Although the topological machinery is very powerful, we believe that a purely combinatorial approach is necessary to develop better insight as to why the conjecture fails. For this reason, we give a combinatorial proof of the following theorem that does not rely on topological facts regarding
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

The minimal finite model of
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
is inconclusive.

We also investigate certain conditions that guarantee a poset to be conclusive which avoid homological calculations. We provide a characterization for the existence of outer derivations in terms of the number of edges, vertices and the
k
-rank of a particular matrix. Hence, a poset is conclusive if and only if this rank does not depend on
k
. We also provide a generalization for the above classical result regarding exclusion of a crown being an obstruction for the existence of outer derivations.

Another condition for conclusiveness arises from the machinery developed in
[
4
]
which provides a combinatorial way of deciding whether the homology of the poset admits torsion. The results assume posets to not include any beat points, that is, they must be
minimal finite spaces
. As shown in
[
3
]
, the number of elements these posets have must be at least twice of their height. We combine these two combinatorial results to obtain a characterization of conclusiveness in terms of basic invariants of posets.

2
Preliminaries and Homological Tools

Let us briefly recall the terminology used in
[
5
]
. Let
R
be a relation on a finite set
S
(seen as a subset of
S\times S
),
k
a commutative ring. A function
f:R\rightarrow k
is called
transitive
if for every
(x,y),(y,z)\in R
one has

f(x,z)=f(x,y)+f(y,z).

Moreover,
f
is called
potential
if there exists a function
\varphi:S\rightarrow k
such that for every
(x,y)\in R
, one has

f(x,y)=\varphi(y)-\varphi(x).

Let
P
be a finite poset on
S
. Clearly
P
can be seen as a relation on
S
. The quadruple
(S,P,k,f)
is called a
transitive system
(the original definition only requires
k
to be an Abelian group, so the whole ring structure is not necessary). If
f
is a potential function, then it is said that the system can be
completed
. The poset
P
is called
soluble
if for every
G
and
f
, the system
(S,P,G,f)
can be completed and is called
defective
if there exists an
f
such that for every nontrivial
G
, the system
(S,P,G,f)
can not be completed. Moreover
P
is called
conclusive
if it is defective or soluble. In
[
5
]
, authors conjectured that every poset on a finite set is conclusive (Conjecture
3.1
).

There exists a one to one correspondence between transitive functions on a poset and derivations of its incidence algebra (the codomain of the transitive function is the commutative ring over which we consider the incidence algebra). The same correspondence induces a one to one correspondence between the potential functions and the inner derivations (See
[
10
, Section 7]
for a compherensive discussion). Hence, being soluble means every derivation of the poset over any commutative ring is inner, whereas being defective is equivalent with the poset admitting outer derivations over every non-trivial commutative ring.

One can see that being conclusive is thus equivalent with the fact that the existence of outer derivations is a property that is independent of the commutative ring. Posets where there exists an element that is comparable with every other element do not admit any outer derivation, thus are conclusive (
[
5
, Theorem
1.7
]
,
[
10
, Theorem
7.1.9
]
). An algebraic approach that provide a way to obtain soluble posets via several operations including sum and cartesian product of posets is given in
[
6
]
, which provide another way of obtaining classes of conclusive posets.

Recall that
I(P,k)
denotes the incidence algebra of the poset
P
over a commutative ring
k
. From the above discussion, the solubility of
P
is equivalent with the vanishing
HH^{1}(I(P,k))=0
of the first Hochschild cohomology group for every
k
whereas
P
being defective can be formulated as
HH^{1}(I(P,k))\not=0
for every non-trivial commutative ring
k
. This point of view enables the utilization of topological machinery. Write
\Delta(P)
denote the order complex of
P
. The following provides a complete characterization for the notion of conclusiveness.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
P
be a poset on a finite set. Then
P
is conclusive if and only if
H_{1}(\Delta(P),\mathbb{Z})
is torsion free.

Proof.

Let
k
be any commutative ring. We have
HH^{1}(I(P,k))\cong H^{1}(\Delta(P),k)
. The Universal Coefficient Theorem yields

H^{1}(\Delta(P),k)\cong\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\Delta(P),\mathbb{Z}),k).

First assume that
P
is conclusive. Since
P
is finite,
H_{1}(\Delta(P),\mathbb{Z})
is finitely generated, so there exists a prime
p
where the homology does not contain any
p
-torsion. Suppose that for some integer
n\geq 2
it contains
n
-torsion. Then
H^{1}(\Delta(P),\mathbb{Z}_{p})=0
whereas
H^{1}(\Delta(P),\mathbb{Z}_{n})\not=0
, contradicting the conclusivity of
P
.

Now suppose the converse, that is,
H_{1}(\Delta(P),\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for some
r\geq 0
. The fact that
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{r},k)\cong k^{r}
for any commutative ring
k
yields the result.
∎

3
Conditions for Solubility

In this section, we will provide the graph theoretical characterizations for the existence of outer derivations. Let
P
be a poset on a finite set
S
. For
0\leq i\leq r,0\leq j\leq s
, let
x_{i},y_{j}\in P
. The pair
(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})
and
(y_{0},y_{1},\cdots,y_{s})
of ordered tuples of distinct elements is called a
parallel path
if
x_{0}=y_{0},x_{r}=y_{s}
and
x_{i}<x_{i+1},y_{j}<y_{j+1}
for
0\leq i<r,0\leq j<s
. The
consistency relation
dictated by any transitive function
f
for this pair of parallel paths is the equation

f(x_{0},x_{1})+f(x_{1},x_{2})+\cdots+f(x_{r-1},x_{r})-f(y_{0},y_{1})-f(y_{1},y_{2})-\cdots-f(y_{s-1},y_{s})=0.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{P}
denote the matrix where the columns are indexed by the edges of the Hasse diagram of
P
and the rows are the consistency relations for parallel paths that can be constructed by these edges. Write
C(P)
for the number of connected components of
P
and let
E(P)
and
V(P)
denote the edge and the vertex sets of the Hasse diagram of
P
, respectively. Also let
\mathrm{Pot}(P,k)
and
\mathrm{Der}(P,k)
denote the left
k
-modules of the potential and transitive functions on
P
, respectively. Notice that
\mathrm{Pot}(P,k)\subseteq\mathrm{Der}(P,k)
. The following is a complete characterization of the existence of outer derivations.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
P
be a finite poset,
k
a commutative ring. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathrm{Pot}(P,k)=\mathrm{Der}(P,k)
.

(ii)

E(P)-\mathrm{rank}_{k}(\mathcal{M}_{P})=V(P)-C(P).

Proof.

We will show that when
C(P)=1
, one gets
\mathrm{dim}_{k}(\mathrm{Pot}(P,k))=|V(P)|-1
. The result for this case will then follow from the fact that
\mathrm{dim}_{k}(\mathrm{Der}(P,k))=E(P)-\mathrm{rank}_{k}(\mathcal{M}_{P})
.

Let
M
be the
k
-module of all functions on
V(P)
. The
k
-dimension of
M
is clearly
|V(P)|
. Any element
\varphi\in M
yields a potential derivation
\delta_{\varphi}
where

\delta_{\varphi}(x,y)=\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)

for all
x,y\in P,x<y
. The restriction map from
M
to
\mathrm{Pot}(P,k)
is a homomorphism of the
k
-modules. Its kernel is generated by functions
f
on
P
for which
f(x)=f(y)
for all
x,y\in P
so the kernel has dimension one, whence the formula for
C(P)=1
case follows. The statement for
C(P)>1
can be obtained by applying the obtained result to each component.
∎

Every graph
G
with
V
vertices,
E
edges and
C
connected components satisfies
E\geq V-C(P)
and the equality hold if only if
G
is acyclic. This yields the following known result (
[
8
, Corollary 3.5]
), which originally was obtained via different approach.

Corollary 3.2
.

A poset with no parallel paths admits no outer derivations if and only if it is acyclic.

A
crown
is a poset
C_{n}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{n}\}
where
n\geq 2
and for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we have
x_{i}<y_{i},x_{i}<y_{i}+1
and
x_{n}<y_{n},x_{n}<y_{1}
. The crown
C_{n}
can be visualized as follows.

\dots

A cycle in a poset
P
is a set
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
of elements where
x_{i}\in P
such that
x_{n}=x_{1}
and for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
,
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
are comparable.
Given a transitive function
f
, define

\tilde{f}(x,y)=\begin{cases}f(x,y),&\text{if }x<y\\
-f(x,y),&\text{if }y<x.\end{cases}

For a cycle
C=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})
the circulation
v_{f}(C)
of
f
on
C
is defined as

v_{f}(C)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\tilde{f}(x_{i},x_{i+1}).

It is known that containment of a crown is a requirement for the non-vanishing of the first cohomology. In the context of transitive systems, this fact is given as
[
5
, Theorem
1.12
]
. The following is a generalization.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that
P
is a finite, defective poset. Then there exists a crown in
P
which does not have a join and a meet in
P
.

Proof.

Let
f
be an outer derivation of
P
over some commutative ring. Then there exists a cycle, over which
f
has a non-zero circulation. Let
C
be a cycle of minimal length such that
v_{f}(C)\not=0
. By minimality, no triple
x_{i},x_{i+1},x_{i+2}
of consecutive elements of
C
can form a chain, hence
C
is a crown. Let
C=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{2k+1})
where
x_{1}=x_{2k+1}
. Now suppose the join
M
of elements of
C
exists in
P
. Then for
i=1,2,\cdots,2k-1
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(x_{i},x_{i+1})

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(x_{i},M)+\tilde{f}(M,x_{i+1})=f(x_{i},M)-f(x_{i+1},M),

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2k})

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},M)-f(x_{2k},M).

This yields

\displaystyle v_{f}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\tilde{f}(x_{i},x_{i+1})

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},M)-f(x_{2},M)+f(x_{2},M)-f(x_{3},M)+\cdots+f(x_{2k},M)-f(x_{2k+1},M)

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},M)-f(x_{2k+1},M)=0.

So the circulation must vanish, which is a contradiction. Hence, the join
M
does not exist for the crown
C
.
∎

Conditions for Conclusiveness

The result that the smallest poset which admits torsion in homology has
13
elements is originally obtained via computer verification (
[
1
]
, Theorem
1.2
). A conceptual proof which avoids extensive calculations were given in
[
4
]
. It is obtained by a mechanism called

. Let us briefly review the notion and relevant results as it will enable us to uncover combinatorial characterizations for the conclusivity of a poset.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
4
]
, Proposition
3.1
)
.

Let
X
be a finite, connected poset. Suppose that there exists subposets
C
and
D
of
X
such that
X=C\cup D
. If the morphisms
H_{1}(C)\rightarrow H_{1}(X)
and
H_{1}(D)\rightarrow H_{1}(X)
induced by the inclusions are trivial, then
H_{1}(X)
is torsion-free, thus
X
is conclusive.

The authors provide a combinatorial way to check the triviality of the induced morphisms. Let
m_{X}
and
n_{X}
denote the number of maximal and minimal elements in
X
, respectively, and write
l_{X}
for element that are neither maximal, nor minimal.
A
beat
point in
X
is an element which is covered by a unique element or which cover a unique element.
Below we gather the conditions
[
4
, Lemma
5.4,5.5
]
that guarantee the triviality of the induced morphisms, hence ensure the poset to be conclusive.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
4
]
Lemma 5.4, 5.5)

A poset
P
is without beat points is conclusive if

l_{X}<\dfrac{n_{X}m_{X}}{(n_{X}-2)(m_{X}-2)}+\delta

where

is the logical value of

.
