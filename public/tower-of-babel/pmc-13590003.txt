Psychosocial Support as a Multidisciplinary Asset in Hypertension Care: A Systematic Review of Medication Adherence, Blood Pressure Outcomes, and Social Context Exposures
Authors: Reni Afriana, Ati Surya Mediawati, Maria Komariah, Muhammad Afiif Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension remains a major global health challenge that requires care strategies extending beyond pharmacological treatment. Psychosocial support may contribute to long-term self-management, medication adherence, and care coordination, but evidence remains dispersed across different support domains, social-context exposures, settings, and outcome measures. This systematic review aimed to synthesize recent evidence on psychosocial support, adverse social-context exposures, medication adherence, and blood pressure outcomes among adults with hypertension.MethodsThis systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 statement. A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and selected EBSCOhost databases for English-language empirical studies published from January 2021 to February 2026. Studies were included if they examined direct psychosocial support resources, structured support interventions, or related adverse social-context exposures among adults with hypertension and reported quantitative medication adherence or blood pressure outcomes. Data were synthesized using a structured narrative approach.ResultsEighteen studies involving 25,639 participants from 12 countries were included. Direct psychosocial support was generally associated with better medication adherence, although the strength and consistency of evidence varied across study designs, support definitions, measurement instruments, and analytical approaches. Evidence on blood pressure outcomes was less consistent. Some studies reported associations between low support or adverse social contexts and uncontrolled blood pressure, while intervention studies showed mixed findings. Adverse social-context exposures, including living alone, socio-familial risk, limited social contact, and cumulative social adversity, appeared distinct from direct psychosocial support and showed varying associations with hypertension management outcomes.ConclusionPsychosocial support appears to be an important community and multidisciplinary care resource for hypertension management, particularly in supporting medication adherence. Routine psychosocial assessment should be integrated into hypertension care pathways involving nurses, physicians, pharmacists, and community health workers. Future research should use longitudinal and controlled intervention designs with standardized measures to clarify how partnership-driven care models improve adherence and long-term blood pressure outcomes.Keywords: hypertension, medication adherence, psychosocial support, blood pressure, multidisciplinary care, community health

Introduction

Hypertension remains a major global health challenge because of its close relationship with cardiovascular disease, stroke, and kidney complications.1 In 2024, approximately 1.4 billion adults aged 30 to 79 years were living with hypertension worldwide, yet only about 23% had achieved adequate blood pressure control. Although antihypertensive medications are widely available and clinically effective, many patients experience difficulty maintaining long-term adherence. Previous evidence has also shown substantial regional variation in medication non-adherence, and non-adherent patients have higher odds of suboptimal blood pressure control (OR 2.15; 95% CI: 1.84 to 2.50).2 These findings indicate that hypertension management requires more than pharmacological treatment alone. Global hypertension guidance also emphasizes standardized treatment protocols, continuity of care, reliable medication access, and team-based service delivery as important components of effective hypertension control.3,4

Psychosocial support may contribute to hypertension self-management, particularly in relation to medication adherence. In this review, psychosocial support refers to emotional, instrumental, informational, family, peer, and community-based resources that may help patients follow treatment recommendations and manage daily care demands.5,6 Related adverse social-context exposures, such as loneliness, social isolation, living alone, socio-familial risk, and cumulative social adversity, are considered separately because they do not directly represent the receipt of support. Loneliness reflects a subjective gap between desired and actual social connection, while social isolation refers to an objective lack of sufficient relationships.1 Distinguishing these constructs is important because support resources and adverse social conditions may influence hypertension outcomes through different emotional, behavioral, and practical pathways.

The Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness provides a relevant conceptual framework for understanding how psychosocial support may contribute to long-term hypertension management.7 The theory conceptualizes self-care as involving maintenance, monitoring, and management behaviors. Self-care maintenance includes routine behaviors intended to preserve health, such as medication adherence and adherence to recommended lifestyle practices. Self-care monitoring involves the ongoing observation of blood pressure, medication-taking patterns, treatment-related symptoms or adverse effects, and changes in health status. Self-care management refers to the evaluation of these observations and the actions taken in response, including seeking professional assistance or adjusting care following clinical advice. Within this framework, psychosocial support can be positioned as a social and community-level resource that may help patients maintain treatment routines, undertake regular monitoring, communicate relevant changes to healthcare professionals, and respond appropriately to barriers encountered in daily life.

A previous systematic review reported a positive association between social support and medication adherence among patients with hypertension.6 Since that review, additional studies have examined psychosocial support and hypertension-related outcomes across diverse healthcare and sociocultural settings, including China, Nepal, Saudi Arabia, and other regions.8–11 However, the available evidence remains heterogeneous in terms of support definitions, study designs, measurement instruments, and outcome indicators. Direct psychosocial support and adverse social-context exposures are also often discussed together, although they represent conceptually different dimensions of the patient’s social environment. This creates a need for a structured synthesis that clarifies how these factors relate to medication adherence and blood pressure outcomes in hypertension care.

Therefore, this systematic review aimed to synthesize recent evidence on psychosocial support, medication adherence, and blood pressure outcomes among adults with hypertension. This review also examined adverse social-context exposures, including loneliness, social isolation, living alone, socio-familial risk, and cumulative social adversity, as related but distinct constructs. By organizing the evidence across support resources, adverse social conditions, and outcome domains, this review seeks to inform multidisciplinary hypertension care strategies involving nurses, physicians, pharmacists, and community health workers. This multidisciplinary framing is important because psychosocial barriers to hypertension management require coordinated responses across clinical assessment, medication counseling, patient education, follow-up, and community linkage.

Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted as a systematic review with structured narrative synthesis. The review aimed to synthesize recent evidence on psychosocial support resources, related adverse social-context exposures, medication adherence, and blood pressure outcomes among adults with hypertension. The review followed the PRISMA 2020 statement to support transparent reporting of the search process, screening, eligibility assessment, and study selection.12 Studies from diverse geographical and healthcare settings were included to capture variations in psychosocial support, community-clinical linkages, and adverse social contexts across different populations.

A structured narrative synthesis was used because the included studies differed in design, exposure definitions, intervention characteristics, outcome measures, effect estimates, and assessment methods. Direct psychosocial support resources were analyzed separately from adverse social-context exposures because these constructs represent different dimensions of patients’ social environments. Medication adherence and blood pressure outcomes were also synthesized separately to avoid combining clinically and methodologically distinct outcomes. The synthesis was guided by the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis approach, with findings organized by exposure category, outcome domain, study design, and direction of association.13 Adjusted effect estimates were prioritized where available, while unadjusted estimates, correlation coefficients, mean differences, and reviewer-calculated estimates were reported descriptively and interpreted with caution.

Eligibility Criteria

The eligibility criteria were developed using the Population, Exposure or Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study Design (PECOS) framework. The population included adults aged 18 years or older with hypertension. Studies involving participants with comorbid conditions were eligible when hypertension-specific data were reported separately. Eligible exposures or interventions included psychosocial support resources, such as emotional, instrumental, informational, family, peer, community, or multidimensional support, as well as structured psychosocial support interventions. Related adverse social-context exposures, including limited social contact, social isolation, living alone, socio-familial risk, and cumulative social adversity, were also eligible but were analyzed separately from direct support resources. Eligible comparators included usual care, higher versus lower levels of psychosocial support, adequate versus inadequate support, presence versus absence of adverse social conditions, exposed versus unexposed groups, and pre-intervention versus post-intervention measurements where applicable.

Eligible outcomes included quantitative measures of medication adherence or blood pressure outcomes. Medication adherence had to be assessed using validated instruments, objective measures, or clearly defined operational criteria. Blood pressure outcomes included controlled or uncontrolled blood pressure status, systolic blood pressure, or diastolic blood pressure. Eligible study designs included analytical cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, secondary analyses of cohort or survey data, post-hoc subgroup analyses, randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, and single-arm pre-post intervention studies. Articles were included if they were peer-reviewed, published in English, and available from January 1, 2021, to the final database search date, with no geographical restrictions. Studies were excluded if they focused entirely on participants younger than 18 years, pregnant women with gestational hypertension, patients with secondary hypertension, mixed clinical populations without extractable hypertension-specific data, unclear psychosocial exposure definitions, non-quantitative outcome reporting, or no analysis linking psychosocial exposure with the predefined outcomes. Qualitative studies, previous reviews, evidence syntheses, case reports, case series, protocols, editorials, expert opinions, letters, and conference abstracts were also excluded.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and EBSCOhost on 25 February 2026. The search included peer-reviewed articles published or available online ahead of print between 1 January 2021 and the final search date and was restricted to English-language publications. The 2021 start date was prespecified to focus the review on contemporary evidence and to update the evidence base following the systematic review published by Shahin et al in 2021.6 This focused publication window was not intended to suggest that evidence published before 2021 was irrelevant. Rather, it defined the review as a synthesis of recent evidence and preserved consistency with its intended scope.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary, where available, with free-text terms representing three core domains: hypertension; psychosocial support resources and related adverse social-context exposures; and hypertension-management outcomes, including medication adherence and blood pressure outcomes. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Title/Abstract terms were used in PubMed. The search syntax was adapted to the TITLE-ABS-KEY fields in Scopus and the Title and Abstract fields in EBSCOhost. The complete database-specific search strategies, including the exact search strings, applied limits, final search dates, and numbers of records retrieved, are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

All retrieved records were exported to Mendeley for reference management and deduplication. Potential duplicate records were identified using Mendeley’s duplicate-detection function and subsequently verified manually to minimize erroneous removal. Following deduplication, the remaining unique records were screened against the predefined eligibility criteria.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

After deduplication, study selection was conducted in two stages. First, two reviewers (RA and MAA) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the predefined eligibility criteria. Records that appeared eligible or had insufficient information for exclusion were advanced to full text assessment. Second, the same reviewers independently assessed the full texts and recorded the reasons for exclusion. Disagreements at each stage were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data were extracted using a standardized electronic form that had been piloted before use. Extracted data included author, year of publication, country, study setting, study design, follow-up period where applicable, sample size, participant characteristics, psychosocial support resources or related adverse social-context exposures, comparator, measurement instrument, outcome definition, and main findings. For medication adherence, extracted data included the reported effect estimate, uncertainty measure, and adjustment status where available. For blood pressure outcomes, extracted data included controlled or uncontrolled blood pressure status and changes in systolic or diastolic blood pressure. When an eligible study provided sufficient contingency table data but did not report an odds ratio, a crude odds ratio and 95% confidence interval were calculated for descriptive reporting and clearly identified as reviewer-calculated estimates. These estimates were not pooled. One reviewer performed the initial data extraction, and a second reviewer verified the extracted data for accuracy and completeness. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Methodological Quality and Risk-of-Bias Assessment

Two reviewers (RA and MAA) independently assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias of all included studies using design-specific appraisal tools. Analytical cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies, whereas the cohort study was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies. The randomized controlled trial was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, and single-arm pre–post intervention studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Quasi-Experimental Studies. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

The appraisal findings were reported separately according to study design because the relevant methodological domains differed between observational and interventional studies. For intervention studies, interpretation also considered design-specific limitations, including the practical difficulty of participant blinding and, where applicable, the absence of a concurrent comparator group. The appraisal results were used to contextualize the structured narrative synthesis and inform the interpretation of the strength, consistency, and limitations of the evidence rather than as criteria for study exclusion. Numerical composite quality scores were not calculated because the studies differed in design and were assessed using design-specific appraisal tools.

Detailed item-level assessments are presented according to study design: analytical cross-sectional studies in Supplementary Table S2A, the cohort study in Supplementary Table S2B, the randomized controlled trial in Supplementary Table S2C, and single-arm pre–post intervention studies in Supplementary Table S2D.

Data Synthesis

A structured narrative synthesis was conducted for all included studies and organized by outcome domain, psychosocial exposure category, and study design. Direct psychosocial support resources were synthesized separately from adverse social-context exposures because these constructs represent different dimensions of patients’ social environments. Medication adherence and blood pressure outcomes were also analyzed separately to avoid combining clinically and methodologically different outcomes. The synthesis followed the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis approach and considered the direction, consistency, and strength of findings across studies.13

Reported effect estimates were extracted and presented descriptively according to the measures used in the original studies, including odds ratios, adjusted odds ratios, prevalence ratios, adjusted prevalence ratios, mean differences, and correlation coefficients. Adjusted estimates were prioritized in the interpretation where available. Unadjusted estimates and reviewer-calculated crude odds ratios were clearly identified and interpreted with caution. No statistical pooling was conducted because the included studies differed in exposure definitions, study designs, intervention components, outcome measures, analytical approaches, and reported effect estimates. Geographic variation was described narratively where relevant.

Results

Study Selection

Initial database searches yielded 1,335 records, including 403 from PubMed, 743 from Scopus, and 189 from selected EBSCOhost databases. After removing 386 duplicates, 949 unique records were screened by title and abstract. During this stage, 925 records were excluded because they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. Full-text retrieval was attempted for 24 potentially eligible reports, but four reports could not be accessed.

The remaining 20 reports were assessed for eligibility. Two reports were excluded at the full-text stage: one did not report a relevant outcome, and one did not provide extractable hypertension-specific data. Consequently, 18 studies were included in the systematic review. All included studies were synthesized using a structured narrative approach and organized by psychosocial exposure category, outcome domain, and study design. Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 18 studies involving 25,639 participants were included in this systematic review. The studies were conducted across 12 countries: the United States, China, Indonesia, Taiwan, Cambodia, Ethiopia, Nigeria, Colombia, India, Ecuador, South Africa, and Vietnam. Most studies used cross-sectional designs (n = 14). The remaining studies included one post hoc subgroup analysis of a prospective cohort, one randomized controlled trial, and two single-arm pre-post intervention studies. The prospective cohort study met most JBI appraisal criteria and established an appropriate temporal sequence between exposure and outcome measurement. Nevertheless, its post hoc subgroup analysis, nonrandom participant selection, and potential residual confounding warranted cautious interpretation. The RoB 2 assessment of the randomized controlled trial was specific to the comparison between the social-support and usual-care groups for systolic blood pressure at the four-month end-of-study assessment. The trial was judged to have some concerns overall, primarily because completion of the final follow-up differed across study arms and the research staff conducting the end-of-study outcome assessment were not blinded. The two single-arm pre–post intervention studies lacked concurrent comparator groups and evaluated multicomponent interventions. Consequently, their findings were interpreted as preliminary evidence of feasibility or potential benefit rather than evidence of causal effectiveness attributable specifically to psychosocial support. These design-specific considerations informed the interpretation of the strength, consistency, and limitations of the evidence in the structured narrative synthesis.

Psychosocial Support and Medication Adherence

Seven observational studies examined direct psychosocial support resources in relation to medication adherence. In Cambodia, good family support was associated with higher odds of medication adherence than poor family support (AOR = 2.16; 95% CI: 1.49 to 3.14).17 Similarly, adequate social support was associated with higher odds of adherence among adults with hypertension in Ethiopia (AOR = 1.86; 95% CI: 1.13 to 3.08).31 These adjusted estimates showed a consistent positive direction, suggesting that stronger psychosocial support may be linked to better medication adherence.

Other observational studies reported mixed but mostly supportive findings. Chu et al18 identified social support as one of several factors associated with adherence, alongside health beliefs, disease duration, and self-efficacy. Positive correlations between social support and medication adherence scores were reported in Vietnam (Spearman’s rho = 0.18; p < 0.01) and Nigeria (r = 0.302; p < 0.001).14,29 In contrast, family support was not significantly associated with adherence in an Indonesian study (Spearman’s rho = 0.114; p = 0.39).27 Ikurayeke et al23 reported a significant association between perceived family support and adherence (p = 0.0409), but a reliable effect estimate could not be extracted because of internal inconsistencies in the reported category totals.

Two single-arm intervention studies reported improved adherence after structured support interventions. In China, medication adherence scores increased after a three-month personalized informational support intervention (MD = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.38 to 0.91; p < 0.001).22 In the United States, the K-Wood-MAS-4 score decreased from 2.19 to 1.58 after an eight-week multicomponent intervention involving self-measured blood pressure monitoring, electronic messaging, education, and community-clinical linkages (p = 0.0001).20 These findings suggest potential adherence benefits from structured support interventions, but they should be interpreted cautiously because neither study included a concurrent comparator group and both involved multicomponent interventions.

Psychosocial Support and Blood-Pressure Outcomes

Six studies examined psychosocial support resources or structured support interventions in relation to blood pressure outcomes. In Ethiopia, low perceived social support was associated with a higher prevalence of uncontrolled blood pressure than high social support after adjustment for relevant covariates (APR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.01 to 1.54; p = 0.048).19 In contrast, Galvis-Acevedo et al21 found no significant association between instrumental support from cohabitants and uncontrolled blood pressure among older adults in Colombia after adjustment (AOR = 1.15; 95% CI: 0.96 to 1.39; p = 0.117). In Taiwan, social support was negatively correlated with systolic blood pressure (r = −0.25; p < 0.01) and diastolic blood pressure (r = −0.15; p < 0.05) in bivariate analysis. However, social support did not show a direct association with blood pressure in the final path model and was directly associated only with lower anxiety symptoms Chen et al.16

Findings from intervention studies were mixed. In a randomized controlled trial, adding social-support partner feedback to a remote monitoring intervention did not significantly improve systolic blood pressure compared with usual care (adjusted MD = −0.91 mmHg; 95% CI: −6.37 to 4.55; p = 0.74). The difference in diastolic blood pressure was also not significant (adjusted MD = −0.63 mmHg; 95% CI: −3.77 to 2.51; p = 0.70).25 In a single-arm feasibility study, personalized informational support was followed by reductions in nocturnal systolic blood pressure (MD = −5.07 mmHg; 95% CI: −8.12 to −2.01; p = 0.001) and nocturnal diastolic blood pressure (MD = −3.39 mmHg; 95% CI: −5.12 to −1.67; p < 0.001), although office and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure measures did not change significantly.22 Ferdinand et al20 also reported a reduction in office systolic blood pressure after an eight-week multicomponent intervention involving self-measured blood pressure monitoring, electronic messaging, education, and community-clinical linkages (MD = −10.50 mmHg; p = 0.0027), while office diastolic blood pressure did not change significantly (MD = −1.71 mmHg; p = 0.1337).

Overall, evidence on blood pressure outcomes was less consistent than evidence on medication adherence. The included studies differed in exposure definitions, outcome measures, study designs, and intervention components. These differences support the use of structured narrative synthesis to interpret the findings. Results from single-arm intervention studies should be interpreted cautiously because these studies lacked concurrent comparator groups and used multicomponent interventions, making it difficult to determine the independent contribution of psychosocial support.

Adverse Social-Context Exposures and Hypertension Outcomes

Adverse social-context exposures were examined in relation to hypertension management outcomes across five studies. These exposures were synthesized separately from direct psychosocial support resources because living arrangements, cumulative social adversity, socio-familial risk, and limited social contact do not directly represent the receipt of emotional, informational, instrumental, or practical support. For blood pressure outcomes, living alone was associated with higher odds of uncontrolled hypertension among adults older than 50 years in India (OR = 9.50; 95% CI: 3.36 to 27.20; p < 0.0001).24 In a secondary analysis of REGARDS baseline data, a greater cumulative burden of adverse social determinants of health was associated with a progressively higher prevalence of uncontrolled blood pressure.15 However, individual social-context indicators did not show a uniform direction of association. Participants who reported seeing no friends or relatives during the previous month had a lower prevalence of uncontrolled blood pressure than those who saw more than 10 people monthly (adjusted PR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.73 to 0.96), whereas having no person available to provide care during illness or disability was not significantly associated with uncontrolled blood pressure (adjusted PR = 1.04; 95% CI: 0.97 to 1.12).15 These findings should be interpreted cautiously because isolated indicators may not fully capture the quality, function, or stability of patients’ social relationships.

For medication adherence, participants in the highest social risk group had lower odds of antihypertensive medication coverage throughout follow-up than those with the fewest social risks (AOR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.53 to 0.78).32 Neira et al26 also reported an association between socio-familial risk and therapeutic adherence among older adults (p = 0.047). However, the reviewer-calculated crude OR from the reported contingency table was not statistically significant (OR = 1.85; 95% CI: 0.75 to 4.55), so this finding was interpreted descriptively.

As related contextual evidence, attendance at an adult day care centre was associated with lower odds of uncontrolled blood pressure in a South African study (reviewer-calculated crude OR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.26 to 0.72).28 This result should be interpreted descriptively because the analytical interpretation was limited by the relatively small exposed group. Overall, these findings suggest that patients’ social environments may be relevant to community health and hypertension management, although the direction and magnitude of associations varied across exposure definitions, outcome measures, and study designs.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 18 studies involving 25,639 adults with hypertension across different geographical and healthcare settings. The findings suggest that psychosocial support is relevant to hypertension management, particularly in relation to medication adherence. However, the evidence varied in study design, exposure definition, outcome measurement, and analytical approach. Most included studies used cross-sectional designs, so the findings should be interpreted mainly as associations rather than causal effects.

The narrative synthesis showed that direct psychosocial support was generally associated with better medication adherence among adults with hypertension. Two studies reporting adjusted odds ratios showed a consistent positive direction of association, with good family support in Cambodia and adequate social support in Ethiopia both linked to higher medication adherence.17,31 Other studies also reported positive correlations between social support and adherence in Vietnam and Nigeria.14,29 In contrast, an Indonesian study found no significant association between family support and adherence, while another Nigerian study reported a significant association but did not provide a reliable extractable effect estimate because of inconsistencies in the reported category totals.23,27 These differences may reflect variation in measurement instruments, population characteristics, healthcare access, and operational definitions of psychosocial support. More broadly, antihypertensive medication adherence is shaped by interacting patient-related, treatment-related, healthcare, and socioeconomic factors rather than a single determinant.33

Evidence on blood pressure outcomes was less consistent than evidence on medication adherence. Low perceived social support was associated with a higher prevalence of uncontrolled blood pressure in Ethiopia, whereas instrumental support from cohabitants was not significantly associated with uncontrolled blood pressure in Colombia after adjustment.19,21 In Taiwan, social support was negatively correlated with systolic and diastolic blood pressure in bivariate analysis, but the final path model suggested that support was more directly related to lower anxiety symptoms than to blood pressure itself.16 Intervention findings were also mixed. Adding social-support partner feedback to a remote monitoring intervention did not significantly improve blood pressure in a randomized controlled trial.25 In contrast, personalized informational support and a community-linked multicomponent intervention were followed by improvements in selected blood pressure measures.20,22 These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the intervention studies differed in design, duration, components, and outcome assessment. Recent reviews similarly indicate that digital health and nurse-led interventions may support hypertension management, although their effects vary by intervention content, delivery context, and follow-up duration.34–36

A key contribution of this review is the distinction between direct psychosocial support resources and adverse social-context exposures. Living alone, socio-familial risk, cumulative social adversity, and limited social contact describe related aspects of patients’ social environments, but they do not directly represent the receipt of emotional, informational, instrumental, or practical support. Living alone was associated with higher odds of uncontrolled hypertension, while lower adherence was identified among patients with the highest social risk burden.24,32 A more complex pattern was reported in the REGARDS analysis, where cumulative social disadvantage was associated with uncontrolled blood pressure, but individual social-context indicators showed different directions of association.15 These findings suggest that the quality, function, and stability of social relationships may be more informative than the presence or absence of a single social indicator.

The findings can also be interpreted in relation to the monitoring component of the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness.7 Family members, peers, and community health workers may support monitoring by assisting with home blood pressure measurement, recognizing missed medication doses or warning signs, documenting changes in health status, and facilitating timely communication with healthcare professionals. Monitoring therefore functions as a link between self-care maintenance and management: information generated through observation becomes clinically meaningful when it prompts behavioral adjustment, professional consultation, referral, or treatment review. Effective monitoring requires not only the collection of blood pressure readings or adherence information but also clearly defined feedback and response mechanisms involving patients, their support networks, and healthcare professionals.

Nevertheless, monitoring was not consistently measured as an independent outcome in the included studies. Several interventions incorporated blood pressure measurement, electronic communication, reminders, or structured follow-up as components of broader support programs, but their specific effects on self-care monitoring could not be isolated. Accordingly, the findings support the conceptual and practical relevance of monitoring but do not establish that psychosocial support independently improves monitoring behavior or subsequent blood pressure outcomes. Future studies should evaluate monitoring using clearly defined measures and examine whether it mediates the relationship between psychosocial support, medication adherence, and blood pressure control.

Implications for Multidisciplinary Practice, Nursing Management, and Health Policy

The findings support the integration of structured psychosocial assessment into routine hypertension care, particularly because stronger family or social support was associated with better medication adherence in several included studies.17,31 Nursing managers can consider brief and standardized screening protocols to identify social support resources, living arrangements, medication-taking barriers, and related social risks during outpatient visits or community follow-ups. Screening results should be documented in electronic health records and linked to clear referral pathways. Patients with limited support networks may benefit from individualized education, structured follow-up, family engagement, and coordinated support involving nurses, physicians, pharmacists, and community health workers. Digital tools may support monitoring and communication, but they should complement interpersonal and team-based care rather than replace it.34–36

At the organizational level, nurse leaders and healthcare managers can strengthen hypertension-care programs by defining multidisciplinary responsibilities, establishing follow-up protocols, and developing explicit monitoring and escalation pathways. Patient-level monitoring may include home blood pressure trends, medication-taking patterns, treatment-related symptoms, and barriers to self-management. Service-level indicators may include completion of psychosocial-risk screening, follow-up attendance, referral completion, continuity of care, and patient engagement. Nurses and community health workers may support ongoing assessment and follow-up, pharmacists may identify medication-related barriers, and physicians may evaluate clinically relevant changes and modify treatment where appropriate. Monitoring data should therefore be linked to clearly defined feedback, referral, and clinical-response mechanisms rather than collected without subsequent action.

At the policy level, psychosocial assessment should be considered as part of integrated primary care pathways for hypertension management. Health policies can promote team-based care, task sharing, accessible follow-up mechanisms, and simple interoperable information systems that help healthcare professionals identify patients who need additional support. These priorities are consistent with global hypertension care recommendations that emphasize standardized protocols, multidisciplinary collaboration, patient-centred services, reliable treatment access, and monitoring systems.3,4 Because the available evidence remains heterogeneous, policy implementation should be accompanied by routine evaluation to determine which collaborative strategies are feasible, acceptable, and useful across different healthcare settings.

Strengths and Limitations

This systematic review has several methodological strengths. It synthesized evidence from 18 studies involving 25,639 adults with hypertension across diverse geographical and healthcare settings. Direct psychosocial support resources were distinguished from related adverse social-context exposures, thereby avoiding the inappropriate combination of conceptually different constructs. Medication-adherence and blood pressure outcomes were also synthesized separately because they differed clinically and methodologically. Design-specific appraisal tools were applied to observational and interventional studies, adjusted effect estimates were prioritized where available, and unadjusted or reviewer-calculated estimates were clearly identified and interpreted cautiously. A structured narrative synthesis was appropriate because the included studies differed substantially in their designs, definitions of psychosocial support, intervention components, outcome measures, analytical approaches, and reported effect estimates.

Several study-level limitations should be considered. Most included studies used cross-sectional designs, restricting causal interpretation and raising the possibility of reverse causality. Psychosocial support was measured using heterogeneous instruments and operational definitions, which limited direct comparison across studies. Several studies relied on self-reported medication adherence, unadjusted bivariate analyses, or effect estimates that were not directly comparable. The single-arm intervention studies lacked concurrent comparator groups, while several interventions included multiple components, making it difficult to isolate the independent contribution of psychosocial support. Self-care monitoring was also not consistently evaluated as a distinct exposure, process, or outcome. Therefore, although monitoring is theoretically and clinically relevant, the available evidence cannot establish whether psychosocial support independently improves monitoring behaviors or whether monitoring mediates subsequent medication-adherence or blood pressure outcomes.

Review-level limitations also affect the comprehensiveness and applicability of the findings. Restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language and selection bias by excluding potentially relevant evidence published in other languages. Although the included studies represented several geographical settings, geographical diversity alone does not ensure generalizability across populations and healthcare systems with different cultural norms, family structures, socioeconomic conditions, demographic characteristics, healthcare access, and service-delivery models. In addition, the publication window was limited to January 2021 through February 2026 to provide a focused synthesis of contemporary evidence following the systematic review published by Shahin et al in 2021.6 Nevertheless, excluding studies published before 2021 may have omitted relevant earlier evidence, including research reflecting the initial and acute phases of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, when healthcare access, clinical practices, resource availability, social-contact patterns, and patient outcomes may have differed. The findings should therefore be interpreted as a synthesis of recent evidence rather than a comprehensive historical account, and their application should be adapted to the cultural and healthcare contexts of the populations concerned. Publication bias also cannot be excluded because the review focused on peer-reviewed evidence.

Conclusion

Psychosocial support appears to be a relevant community and multidisciplinary care resource for adults with hypertension, particularly in relation to medication adherence. Stronger family or social support was generally associated with better adherence; however, the strength and consistency of these associations varied across study designs, measurement instruments, and analytical approaches. Evidence concerning blood pressure outcomes was less consistent, and the predominance of cross-sectional studies prevents causal conclusions regarding the effects of psychosocial support on hypertension management.

This review also demonstrates the importance of distinguishing direct psychosocial support resources from adverse social-context exposures, including living alone, socio-familial risk, limited social contact, and cumulative social adversity. These constructs represent different dimensions of patients’ social environments and may influence self-care through different emotional, behavioral, and practical pathways. Within the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness, psychosocial support may contribute to self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management by supporting medication routines, home blood pressure observation, recognition of treatment-related problems, communication with healthcare professionals, and appropriate responses to changes in health status. Nevertheless, monitoring was not consistently evaluated as a distinct process or outcome; therefore, its independent contribution could not be established.

Routine psychosocial assessment may be incorporated into multidisciplinary hypertension-care pathways involving nurses, physicians, pharmacists, and community health workers. Monitoring activities should be linked to clearly defined feedback, referral, and clinical-response mechanisms rather than being limited to the collection of blood pressure or adherence information. These implications should be adapted to local cultural, socioeconomic, demographic, and healthcare-system contexts because the English-language restriction, uneven geographical representation, and limited 2021–2026 publication window constrain the universal applicability and historical comprehensiveness of the findings. Future research should use longitudinal and controlled intervention designs, standardized measures of psychosocial support and self-care monitoring, and culturally diverse populations to clarify whether monitoring mediates the relationship between psychosocial support, medication adherence, and long-term blood pressure outcomes.
