Diagrammatic expressions for steady-state distribution and static responses in population dynamics
Koya Katayama, Ryuna Nagayama, Sosuke Ito

Abstract
One of the fundamental questions in population dynamics is how biological populations respond to environmental perturbations. In population dynamics, the mean fitness and the fraction of a trait in the steady state are important because they indicate how well the trait and the population adapt to the environment. In this study, we examine the parallel mutation-reproduction model, which is one of the simplest models of an evolvable population. As an extension of the Markov chain tree theorem, we derive diagrammatic expressions for the static responses of mean fitness and the steady-state distribution of the population. For the parallel mutation-reproduction model, we consider self-loops, which represent trait reproduction and are excluded from the Markov chain tree theorem for the linear master equation. To generalize the theorem, we introduce the concept of rooted 0 0 / 1 1 loop forests, which generalize spanning trees with loops. We demonstrate that the weights of rooted 0 0 / 1 1 loop forests yield the static responses of mean fitness and the steady-state distribution. Our results provide exact expressions for the static responses and the steady-state distribution. Additionally, we discuss approximations of these expressions in cases where reproduction or mutation is dominant. We provide numerical examples to illustrate these approximations and exact expressions.

Introduction

Understanding how biological populations respond to environmental changes is fundamental to biology because evolution itself can be viewed as a long-term response to these changes

;

Although the parallel mutation-reproduction model is important, its mathematical treatment is less developed than that of the linear master equation because it is nonlinear. For example, computing the fraction of a trait in the steady state, that is the steady-state distribution, and the static response of mean fitness requires considering the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix, as discussed in Ref.
Hermisson et al. 2002

;
Saakian et al. 2008
;
Muñoz et al. 2009
;
Semenov and Novozhilov 2015
have assumed specific functional forms for the fitness landscape or the mutation rate.

In contrast, the steady-state distribution and the static responses in the steady state are well understood for the linear master equation. For example, the steady-state distribution can be described by a canonical distribution if the detailed balance condition is satisfied
Van Kampen 1992
. Even when the detailed balance condition is not satisfied, the steady-state distribution can be graphically described using the weights of the spanning tree on Markov networks, thanks to the Markov chain tree theorem

;
Chaiken 1982
;

;

;

;

;

;

Thus, due to the absence of a mathematical treatment similar to that established for the linear master equation in the parallel mutation-reproduction model, it has been difficult to handle analytically the steady-state distribution and the static responses of the mean fitness in the steady state for general cases, despite the importance of these quantities in population dynamics.

To address this issue, we extend the Markov chain tree theorem to the parallel mutation–reproduction model, and derive exact expressions for the steady-state distribution and the static responses of the mean fitness in the steady state. To achieve this, we propose a graph called

1

0
/
1

Setup

Here, we introduce the parallel mutation-reproduction model, as well as quantities such as the steady-state distribution and the static responses of the mean fitness.

II.1
The parallel mutation–reproduction model

Schematic illustration of the model. (a) Each individual reproduces, and its offspring may mutate into a different trait.
(b) In the long-time limit, the total number of individuals in the population grows exponentially with time, and

We explain the parallel mutation-reproduction model
Crow 2017
that we are dealing with in this paper.
Suppose that a population consists of
N
subpopulations, and each subpopulation has a different trait.
Each individual in the population reproduces itself and mutates into a different trait (Fig.
1
(a)).
Although other factors such as migration or predation may affect the dynamics of the population, we neglect them for simplicity.
We let
n_{i}(t)\geq 0
denote the number of individuals in the subpopulation with the trait
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
at time
t
.
In the following, we assume that
n_{i}(t)
is large enough to be treated as continuous number.
We let denote the reproductive rate (or the fitness) of trait
i
as
R_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and the mutation rate from trait
j
to trait
i
as
M_{ij}\geq 0\,(i\neq j)
.
Then, we can write down the equation that describes the dynamics of
n_{i}(t)
as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}n_{i}(t)=R_{i}n_{i}(t)+\sum_{j(\neq i)}(M_{ij}n_{j}(t)-M_{ji}n_{i}(t)).

(1)

As explained in Appendix
A
, this model can incorporate changes in the population due to reproduction and death, as well as mutations during reproduction.
If there is competition for food or predator-prey interaction between individuals,
\{R_{i}\}_{i}
may depend on
\bm{n}(t):=(n_{1}(t),\ \cdots,\ n_{N}(t))^{\top}
.
However, in this paper we assume that
\{R_{i}\}_{i}
are independent of
\bm{n}(t)
.
We also assume that all
R_{i}
and
M_{ij}
do not depend directly on time
t
, which means that the environment is stationary.
For simplicity, we rewrite Eq. (
1
) in the vector form.
To do this, we define
M_{ii}:=-\sum_{j(\neq i)}M_{ji}
,
\mathsf{M}:=(M_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and
\mathsf{R}:=\mathrm{diag}(R_{1},\ \cdots,\ R_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denotes the set of all

N\times N
real matrices.
Then, Eq. (
1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{n}(t)=(\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M})\bm{n}(t).

(2)

We also assume that the matrix
\mathsf{M}
is irreducible, which means that for any pair of
i
and
j
, trait
i
can mutate into trait
j
in finite steps.

In this paper, we focus on the
distribution
of the traits rather than the number of them.
Defining the total number of the population as
n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t):=\sum_{i}n_{i}(t)
, the distribution of the traits is defined as
\bm{p}(t):=(p_{1}(t),\ \cdots,\ p_{N}(t))^{\top}
with
p_{i}(t):=n_{i}(t)/n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)
.
From Eq. (
2
), we obtain the equation that describes the dynamics of
\bm{p}(t)
as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{p}(t)=\left(\mathsf{R}-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{M}\right)\bm{p}(t),

(3)

where
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}:=\sum_{i}R_{i}p_{i}(t)
is the
mean fitness
of the population at time
t
, and
\mathsf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the identity matrix. Here, Eq. (
2
) is considered a nonlinear master equation because the term
-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}\bm{p}(t)
is a nonlinear function of
\bm{p}(t)
.

Note that the mean fitness
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}
can be interpreted as the reproductive rate of the total number of individuals in the population because the equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t),

(4)

is obtained by summing Eq. (
1
) over
i
.

We note that Eq. (
3
) reduces to the linear master equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{p}(t)=\mathsf{M}\bm{p}(t),

(5)

when all reproductive rates are equal because
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}=R_{i}
holds for any
i
and
\bm{p}(t)
, and
\mathsf{R}=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}\mathsf{I}
is obtained.

II.2
Steady-state distribution

We focus on the steady state of the population, which is one of the main topics of this paper. An expression for the steady-state distribution and its properties are obtained by applying the Perron–Frobenius theorem
Pillai et al. 2005
(see also Appendix
B
).

We clarify what is the steady state in the parallel mutation-reproduction model.
Equation (
4
) implies that the total number of the population could diverge in the limit of
{t\to\infty}
due to the reproduction (Fig.
1
(b)).
In contrast, the irreducibility of
\mathsf{M}
and the Perron–Frobenius theorem ensure that the distribution of the population
\bm{p}(t)
converges to the unique distribution
\bm{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which is the
steady-state distribution
(Fig.
1
(c)),

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bm{p}(t).

(6)

For the details on the existence and uniqueness of
\bm{\pi}
, see Appendix
B
. Equation (
6
) implies that the mean fitness also converges to the unique value
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
:
\lim_{t\to\infty}\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
.
This
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
can be interpreted as follows.
The solution of Eq. (
4
) is obtained as
n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=n_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)\exp(\int_0^{t}\ev{R}_{\bm{p}(t')} dt')
. By considering the long-time limit, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\frac{n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)}{n_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)}=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}.

(7)

Roughly speaking, this equality means that the total number of the population
n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)
increases exponentially as
n_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\simeq n_{\mathrm{tot}}(0)e^{\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}t}
in the long-time limit (Fig.
1
(b)).
Therefore, we can interpret the mean fitness
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
as the reproductive rate of the total number of the individuals in the population in the long-time limit.

Perron–Frobenius theorem also tells us that every component of
\bm{\pi}
is strictly positive, i.e.,
\pi_{i}>0

for any
i
(see Appendix
B
for the details). This fact means that no trait will go extinct in the long-time limit if
\mathsf{M}
is irreducible.

The steady-state distribution
\bm{\pi}
can be regarded as the eigenvector of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
.
In fact, combining Eq. (
3
) and the fact that
\bm{\pi}
is time-independent, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M})\bm{\pi}=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}\bm{\pi},

(8)

which means that
\bm{\pi}
is the eigenvector of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
with eigenvalue
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
.

The left eigenvector of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
also plays an important role in population dynamics.
The Perron–Frobenius theorem implies that there exists the unique vector
\bm{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
which satisfies the following equations (see also Appendix
B
for the details),

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}^{\top}(\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M})

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}\bm{\zeta}^{\top},

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}^{\top}\bm{\pi}

\displaystyle=1,

(10)

and
\zeta_{i}>0
for any
i
.
In other words,
\bm{\zeta}
is the left eigenvector of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
with eigenvalue
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
normalized by
\bm{\pi}
and whose components are all positive.
Note that, in the case of the linear master equation [Eq. (
5
)],
\bm{\zeta}
is the vector whose every component is
1
, i.e.,
\bm{\zeta}=\bm{1}:=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}
.
In fact,
\bm{1}^{\top}\mathsf{M}=\bm{0}^{\top}=\bm{\zeta}^{\top}(\mathsf{R}-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}})
is satisfied because
\sum_{i}M_{ik}=0
for any
k
.
\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\pi}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}=1
is also satisfied. The positivity of the elements of
\bm{1}
is also obviously satisfied.

II.3
Static responses of the mean fitness

Here we discuss the static responses of the mean fitness in the steady state, which are also the main topics of this paper.
Since Eq. (
1
) contains two kinds of parameters,
R_{i}
and
M_{ij}
, we can consider two kinds of the static responses
\partial_{R_{i}}\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}:=\partial\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}/\partial R_{i}
and
\partial_{M_{ij}}\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
, which are important quantities that explain the variation in the mean fitness due to changes in the parameters.
These static responses are related to the left and right eigenevectors with the eigenvalue
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
.
Interestingly, Ref.
Hermisson et al. 2002
shows that the following equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}}{\partial R_{i}}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{i}\pi_{i},

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}}{\partial M_{ij}}

\displaystyle=(\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{j})\pi_{j},

(12)

hold for
i\neq j
(see also Appendix
C
).
While these expressions give the rigorous forms of the static responses of the mean fitness, it is difficult to express these static responses explicitly by using only the given parameters,
\{R_{i}\}
and
\{M_{ij}\}
.
This is because we need to solve the eigenvalue problem of the matrix
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
to obtain the explicit forms of
\zeta_{i}\pi_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}\pi_{j}
.
However, in this paper we will give a diagrammatic expression for the static response functions. The diagrammatic expression includes the terms not only
\{R_{i}\}
and
\{M_{ij}\}
but also the mean fitness
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
.

Note that
\{\partial_{R_{i}}\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}\}
, or equivalently
\{\zeta_{i}\pi_{i}\}
, can be regarded as a probability distribution.
From Eq. (
10
) and the positivity of
\pi_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
, we obtain
\zeta_{i}\pi_{i}>0
and
\sum_{i}\zeta_{i}\pi_{i}=1
.
Therefore,
\{\zeta_{i}\pi_{i}\}
is referred to as the
ancestral distribution

Hermisson et al. 2002
.
Combining Eq. (
11
) and the positivity of
\zeta_{i}\pi_{i}
, we find
0<\partial_{R_{i}}\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}<1
for any
i
.
Therefore, the mean fitness in the long-term limit is monotonically increasing with respect to any increase in the reproductive rate.

III.1
Basic graph

We introduce a

The construction of a basic graph, denoted by

The vertex set of

, is defined as the set of the traits in the population, i.e.,
V(G):=\{1,\cdots,N\}
.
In other words, each vertex of
G
represents a trait in the population.
Next, we define the edge set of
G
, denoted by

, as follows.
Let
(i\leftarrow j)
be a directed edge from vertex
j
to vertex
i
.
In particular,
(i\leftarrow i)
represents a loop at vertex
i
.
Using this notation,
E(G)
is defined as

\displaystyle E(G):=

\displaystyle\{(i\leftarrow i)\mid i\in V(G)\}

\displaystyle\cup\{(i\leftarrow j)\mid M_{ij}\neq 0,\,i\in V(G),\,j(\neq i)\in V(G)\}.

(13)

A loop at vertex
i
represents the reproduction of trait
i
, and a directed edge from vertex
j
to
i
represents the mutation from trait
j
to
i
.
Note that if the mutation from trait
j
to
i
never occurs, i.e.,

holds, then
(i\leftarrow j)
does not belong to

.

III.2
Cycle

We introduce the concept of a
cycle

Godsil and Royle 2013

(e_{1},\ \cdots,e_{L})\,(L\geq 3)
is called a cycle if starting from a vertex, it is possible to return to the same vertex by following all the edges
(e_{1},\ \cdots,e_{L})
sequentially without visiting the same vertex twice.
Here, “following” an edge
e
means moving along it either in the given direction or in the opposite direction.
Thus, edge directions are not taken into account when defining a cycle, and a loop is not included in a cycle.
See Fig.
2
(c) for an example.

Subgraph and component

We introduce a

.
A subgraph of a graph

and

, and we write

An

Given a graph

and whose edge set consists of all edges in

In other words,

.

A

(see Fig.
2

Roughly speaking, a component of

We explain the union of graphs.
Suppose that there are
M

.
The

is defined as the graph with the union of the vertices and the union of the vertices,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{M}H^{(\alpha)}:=\left(\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{M}V(H^{(\alpha)}),\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{M}E(H^{(\alpha)})\right).

We also use the notation

for the union of

and

, which is defined as
H^{(1)}\cup H^{(2)}:=(V(H^{(1)})\cup V(H^{(2)}),E(H^{(1)})\cup E(H^{(2)}))
. Moreover, when a graph

, we sometimes identify

with

for the union of graphs expressed by the edges

III.5
Rooted spanning tree

We explain
rooted spanning trees
, which play a key role in the analysis of the linear master equation.
Given a basic graph
G
and its subgraph
H
, we call
H
a
spanning tree of
G
rooted at vertex
i
if
H
satisfies all of the following conditions
Schnakenberg 1976
:

1.

H
contains no cycles.

2.

H
contains no loops.

3.

V(H)=V(G)
.

4.

H
is connected.

5.

All edges in
H
are directed toward vertex
i
.

If
H
satisfies all of the above conditions except the fifth condition, then we call
H
a
spanning tree
.
A graph
H
is simply called a
rooted spanning tree
if it is a spanning tree rooted at a certain vertex.

Weight of a graph

We introduce the

of a graph which is applicable to a rooted

We define the weight of a graph

\displaystyle w(H):=\prod_{e\in E(H)}\tilde{w}(e),

\tilde{w}(e):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-(R_{i}-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}})&&\text{if}\>e=(i\leftarrow i),\\
&M_{ij}&&\text{if}\>e=(i\leftarrow j),\,i\neq j.\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

Thus, the weight of a graph

is given by the product of the weights of all its edges

. See Fig.

IV.1
Generalization of the Markov chain tree theorem

We first state a lemma that plays an important role in the derivation of the main results. The lemma is a generalization of the Markov chain tree theorem
Hill 1966
;
Schnakenberg 1976
, and provides a diagrammatic expression for
\zeta_{j}\pi_{i}
.
As discussed in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
),
\zeta_{j}\pi_{i}
characterizes the static response of the mean fitness.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that the basic graph
G
is given.
Then, for any
i\in V(G)
and any
j\in V(G)
,
\zeta_{j}\pi_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle\zeta_{j}\pi_{i}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)}w(H),

(18)

where
Z:=\sum_{i\in V(G)}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)}w(H)
.

See Appendix
E
for the proof of this lemma.

As shown in Appendix
F
, the number of elements
|\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)|
is at most
2(N+1)^{N-2}
with
N=|V(G)|
. Thus, the number of terms appearing in
\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)}w(H)
is at most
2(N+1)^{N-2}
because
|\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)|\geq|\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)|
.
The number of terms appearing in
Z
is at most
2N(N+1)^{N-2}
.

When all reproductive rates are equal (
\mathsf{R}=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{p}(t)}\mathsf{I}
), Lemma
1
reduces to the Markov chain tree theorem as follows.

Markov chain tree theorem.

Suppose that the basic graph
G
is given and
R_{i}=R_{j}
holds for any
i\in V(G)
and any
j\in V(G)
.
Then, for any
i\in V(G)
, the steady-state distribution
\pi_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{T}_{i}(G)}w(H),

(19)

where
Z^{\prime}:=\sum_{i\in V(G)}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{T}_{i}(G)}w(H)
.

This theorem is proved from Lemma
1
as follows.
When
R_{i}=R_{j}
holds for any pairs
(i,j)
,
\bm{\zeta}=\bm{1}^{\top}
holds as mentioned in Sec.
II.2
.
Thus, the left-hand side of Eq. (
18
) reduces to
\pi_{i}
.
In this case,
R_{i}-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}=0
also holds for any
i
.
Therefore, if
H\in\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)
has at least one loop, the weight of
H
vanishes, i.e.,
w(H)=0
. Only the graphs without loops contribute to the right hand side of Eq. (
18
), and such graph
H
in
\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)
or
\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)(\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{i}(G))
is regarded as a rooted spanning tree

because Eq. (

) implies that a graph

should be a rooted spanning tree if

Thus, we obtain
\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)}w(H)=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{T}_{i}(G)}w(H)
and

19

V
Applications for two limiting cases

While our results provide exact expressions for the static responses and steady-state distributions, the computation with counting graphs can be complicated. Here, we consider approximations for special situations where either reproduction or mutation is dominant.

V.1
Selection-dominant case

We consider the case where the effect of natural selection dominates that of mutation, and derive approximate expressions for the steady-state distribution and the static responses of the mean fitness.
We define the “fittest” trait in the population
i_{*}
as
i_{*}:={\operatorname{argmax}}_{i}\{R_{i}\}
, and assume that this trait
i_{*}
is unique.
Let
\epsilon
be a small parameter satisfying
0<\epsilon\ll\min_{j(\neq i_{*})}\{R_{i_{*}}-R_{j}\}
.
We then assume that
M_{ij}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
for any
i
and
j(\neq i)
, where
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
denotes big O notation.
In other words, we assume that the effect of mutation is negligible compared to the differences in reproductive rates.
We call this the selection-dominant case.

We rewrite this condition so that it is suitable for our diagrammatic expressions.
In our diagrammatic expressions, we use the following inequality:

\displaystyle R_{i}-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}<-M_{ii},

(25)

which holds for any
i
.
This inequality [Eq. (
25
)] always holds and
can be derived as follows.
Since
\bm{\pi}
is the right eigenvector of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
with eigenvalue
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
,
it satisfies
(R_{i}+M_{ii})\pi_{i}+\sum_{j(\neq i)}M_{ij}\pi_{j}=\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}\pi_{i}
.
Dividing both sides by
\pi_{i}>0
and rearranging yields
R_{i}-\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}+M_{ii}=-\sum_{j(\neq i)}M_{ij}\pi_{j}/\pi_{i}<0
, which leads to Eq. (
25
).
By setting
i=i_{*}
in Eq. (
25
) and subtracting
R_{j}
from both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}-R_{j}>R_{i_{*}}-R_{j}+M_{i_{*}i_{*}}

(26)

for any
j(\neq i_{*})
.
Combining this inequality with the assumption
-M_{i_{*}i_{*}}=\sum_{j(\neq i_{*})}M_{ji_{*}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)\ll R_{i_{*}}-R_{j}
, we find
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}-R_{j}>0
for any
j(\neq i_{*})
.
Moreover, combining Eq. (
26
) with
M_{ij}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0<\frac{M_{kl}}{\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}-R_{j}}<\frac{M_{kl}}{R_{i_{*}}-R_{j}+M_{i_{*}i_{*}}}

(27)

for any
j(\neq i_{*}),k
, and
l(\neq k)
.
Since
M_{i_{*}i_{*}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and
M_{kl}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{M_{kl}}{\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}-R_{j}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)

(28)

for any
j(\neq i_{*}),k
, and
l(\neq k)
.
This equation means that the weight of the loop at vertex
j(\neq i_{*})
dominates the weight of a directed edge from vertex
l
to vertex
k
.

By applying Eq. (
28
), we approximate
\bm{\pi}
.
We first approximate
\pi_{j}/\pi_{i_{*}}
for each
j(\neq i_{*})
.
From Theorem
3
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{i_{*}}}=\frac{\sum_{H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{j\leftarrow i_{*}}(G)}w(H^{\prime})}{\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i_{*}}(G)}w(H)}.

(29)

Note that no graph in
\mathcal{F}_{i_{*}}(G)
or
\mathcal{F}_{j\leftarrow i_{*}}(G)
contains the loop at vertex
i_{*}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{(\alpha)}_{i}(G)(\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{i}(G))
and
\mathcal{F}^{(\alpha)}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)(\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G))
be the set of graphs in
\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)
and
\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)
that contain exactly
\alpha
loops, respectively. Equation (
28
) implies that the more loops in
H
, the heavier the weight
w(H)
.
Thus, the leading term in the denominator of Eq. (
29
) is

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}^{(N-1)}_{i_{*}}(G)}w(H)
with
N=|V(G)|
, and
\mathcal{F}^{(N-1)}_{i_{*}}(G)
consists of only one graph

\bigcup_{k\neq i_{*}}(k\leftarrow k)
.
The leading term in the numerator of Eq. (
29
) is

VI
Summary and discussion

In this paper, we extend the Markov chain tree theorem to the parallel mutation-reproduction model [Lemma
1
] and derived diagrammatic expressions for the static responses of the mean fitness [Theorems
1
and
2
] and the steady-state distribution [Theorem
3
and Corollary
1
].
We applied our results to two limiting cases and provided a constructive approach for approximating the static responses of the mean fitness and the steady-state distribution.
Specifically, Theorems
1
and
2
enable the prediction of the static responses of the mean fitness based solely on information about the current state of the population.

Approximate and exact expressions of the steady-state distribution and static responses of mean fitness may both be useful for controlling evolutionary dynamics. Because these expressions are given by the parameters
\mathsf{M}
,
\mathsf{R}
and
\expectationvalue{R}_{\bm{\pi}}
, they provide guidelines for altering the environment to achieve the desired steady state and static responsiveness by changing the mutation rates
\mathsf{M}
and the fitness
\mathsf{R}
. Controlling such evolution and population dynamics will likely be useful from both an epidemiological and a biotechnological perspective.

Similar to how the conventional Markov chain tree theorem is used to analyze the static properties of systems in stochastic thermodynamics
Schnakenberg 1976
;
Maes and Netočnỳ 2013
;
Polettini and Esposito 2017
;
Owen et al. 2020
;
Khodabandehlou et al. 2022
;
Fernandes Martins and Horowitz 2023
;
Dal Cengio et al. 2023
;
Chun and Horowitz 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024
;
Harunari et al. 2024
, our result may be useful for investigating the static properties of the parallel mutation-reproduction model. In stochastic thermodynamics, various inequalities have been obtained using the Markov chain tree theorem. It may be possible to find similar inequalities in population dynamics using our generalization of the Markov chain tree theorem.

Our results may be of interest from a graph theory perspective. In order to obtain diagrammatic expressions for the steady-state distribution and static response of mean fitness, we must introduce a generalization of a rooted spanning tree, that is a rooted 0/1 loop forest. Since a rooted
0
/
1
loop forest generalizes a rooted spanning tree, it may be possible to generalize some concepts in graph theory related to spanning trees, such as cycles and cyclic affinities
Schnakenberg 1976
, for the parallel mutation-reproduction model. Interestingly, we indeed obtain a generalization of Cayley’s
formula for the maximum number of rooted
0
/
1
loop forests in Appendix
F
.

Appendix A
The details of Eq. (
1
)

Here, we explain the details of Eq. (
1
). We assume that reproduction and death occur independently, and that mutation to another trait occurs during reproduction.
Let
r_{i}
and
d_{i}
be the probabilities that trait
i
will reproduce and die in unit time, respectively.
Furthermore, let
P_{ij}
be the probability that the immediate descendants of trait
j
will have trait
i
. Note that
P_{ij}
satisfies
\sum_{i}P_{ij}=1
.
Then, we can write down the number of trait
i
at time
t+\Delta t
as follows,

\displaystyle n_{i}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=(1-d_{i}\Delta t)n_{i}(t)+\Delta t\sum_{j}P_{ij}r_{j}n_{j}(t),

(42)

The first term represents the individuals with trait
i
that do not die.
The second term represents the mutation to trait
i
from the other traits
j\neq i
and the reproduction of trait
i
.
Taking the limit
\Delta t\to 0
in Eq. (
42
) and using
P_{ii}=1-\sum_{j(\neq i)}P_{ji}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}n_{i}(t)=(r_{i}-d_{i})n_{i}(t)+\sum_{j(\neq i)}(P_{ij}r_{j}n_{j}(t)-P_{ji}r_{i}n_{i}(t)).

(43)

Therefore, defining
R_{i}:=r_{i}-d_{i}
and
M_{ij}:=P_{ij}r_{j}\,(i\neq j)
reproduces Eq. (
1
).

Appendix B
Relationship between the steady state and the Perron-Frobenius theorem

We show how the Perron–Frobenius theorem provides an expression for the steady state and its mathematical properties in Sec.
II.2
. The statement of the Perron–Frobenius theorem is as follows.

Perron–Frobenius theorem
Pillai et al. 2005
.

Let
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be an irreducible matrix whose all components are real and non-negative,
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\cdots,\lambda_{N}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{A}
that are ordered as
{\rm Re}\lambda_{1}\geq{\rm Re}\lambda_{2}\cdots\geq{\rm Re}\lambda_{N}
. Then, the following statements hold:

1.

\lambda_{1}
is real and positive, and satisfies

\displaystyle|\lambda_{i}|\leq\lambda_{1},\quad i=2,\cdots,N.

(44)

2.

\lambda_{1}
is simple, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{i},\quad i=2,\cdots,N.

(45)

3.

There exists an right eigenvector
\bm{u}^{(1)}
of
\mathsf{A}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
such that all components are positive.

To apply the Perron–Frobenius theorem to our model, we define
\mathsf{A}
as
\mathsf{A}:=\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}+a\mathsf{I}
with
a=\max_{i}[-(R_{i}+M_{ii})]
.
For any eigenvalue of
\mathsf{A}
denoted by
\lambda
, the corresponding eigenvector
\bm{u}
satisfies

\displaystyle(\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M})\bm{u}=(\lambda-a)\bm{u},

(46)

which implies that the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
are shifted by
-a
from the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{A}
while the eigenvectors of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
are the same as the eigenvectors of
\mathsf{A}
.
Since the matrix
\mathsf{A}
is an irreducible matrix whose components are all real and non-negative, we can apply the Perron–Frobenius theorem to the matrix
\mathsf{A}
. We thus obtain the following corollary:

Corollary B.1
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\cdots,\mu_{N}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
that are ordered as
\real\mu_{1}\geq\real\mu_{2}\cdots\geq\real\mu_{N}
.
Then, the following statements hold:

1.

\mu_{1}
is real, and satisfies

\displaystyle\real\mu_{i}<\mu_{1},\quad i=2,\cdots,N.

(47)

2.

There exists an right eigenvector
\bm{u}^{(1)}
of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
with eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
such that all components are positive.

The first statement of Corollary
B.1
is derived as follows.
From the first and second statements of the Perron–Frobenius theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\real(\lambda_{i}-a)\leq|\lambda_{i}|-a\leq\lambda_{1}-a,\quad i=2,\cdots,N,

(48)

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\cdots,\lambda_{N}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{A}
ordered as
{\rm Re}\lambda_{1}\geq{\rm Re}\lambda_{2}\cdots\geq{\rm Re}\lambda_{N}
.
Combining Eq. (
48
) with Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
), we find that
\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}-a\in\mathbb{R}
and Eq. (
47
) hold.
The second statement is derived by combining Eq. (
46
),
\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}-a
, and the third statement of the Perron–Frobenius theorem.

From Corollary
B.1
, we can prove an expression for the steady-state distribution in Sec.
II.2
as follows.
For simplicity, we discuss the case where
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
is diagonalizable, i.e., there exists a unitary matrix
\mathsf{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\mu_{N}\end{pmatrix}\mathsf{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}^{(1)}&\cdots&\bm{u}^{(N)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\mu_{N}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left({\bm{v}^{(1)}}\right)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\left({\bm{v}^{(N)}}\right)^{\top}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\bm{u}^{(i)}\left(\bm{v}^{(i)}\right)^{\top},

(49)

where
\mathsf{U}^{\dagger}
is the Hermite conjugate of
\mathsf{U}
.
Here,
\bm{u}^{(i)}
and
\bm{v}^{(i)}
are the right and left eigenvectors of
\mathsf{R}+\mathsf{M}
with eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
respectively. Since
\mathsf{U}
is a unitary matrix,

\bm{u}^{(i)}
and
\bm{v}^{(i)}
satisfy
\left(\bm{v}^{(i)}\right)^{\top}\bm{u}^{(j)}=(\mathsf{U}^{\dagger}\mathsf{U})_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
.

\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})\neq 0

We consider the conditions for
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})\neq 0
because
\varepsilon
satisfying
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})\neq 0
only contributes to
\sum_{\varepsilon}\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})
in Eq. (
65
).
Here, we show that
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if the corresponding graph
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy any of
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
. Note that
H(\varepsilon)
always satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{7}
since
\varepsilon
consists of
N-1
edges with
N=|V(G)|
.
This fact allows us to restrict the sum to those
\varepsilon
such that the corresponding graph
H(\varepsilon)
satisfies these conditions, which are related to
\mathcal{F}_{j\leftarrow i}(G)
.

E.4.1
\mathrm{C}_{1}

We show that
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{1}
. If
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{1}
, it means that
H(\varepsilon)
contains a cycle. Here, we introduce the notation
E(H(\varepsilon))=\varepsilon=\{e_{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,e_{\alpha_{N-1}}\}
where
e_{\alpha_{k}}
corresponds to the
k
-th column of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
(or
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
).
Suppose that
H(\varepsilon)
contains a cycle
(e^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,e^{\prime}_{L})
where
e^{\prime}_{k}\in E(H(\varepsilon))
for
k=1,\dots,L
and
L\geq 3
. Note that a cycle does not contain a loop, and the target vertex of the edge
e^{\prime}_{k}
is different from the source vertex of the edge
e^{\prime}_{k}
(
\mathrm{s}(e^{\prime}_{k})\neq\mathrm{t}(e^{\prime}_{k})
). We introduce
-e:=\left(\mathrm{s}(e)\leftarrow\mathrm{t}(e)\right)
as the reverse of the edge
e=\left(\mathrm{t}(e)\leftarrow\mathrm{s}(e)\right)
.
By appropriately considering the reverse of some edges in the cycle
(e^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,e^{\prime}_{L})
, we obtain a
directed cycle

\mathcal{C}=(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,\bar{e}^{\prime}_{L})
satisfying
\mathrm{s}(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{k+1})=\mathrm{t}(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{k})
for any
k=1,\dots,L
where
\mathrm{s}(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{L+1}):=\mathrm{s}(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{1})
.
Using the directed cycle
\mathcal{C}
, we define the vector
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
as

x_{k}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\text{if}\>e_{\alpha_{k}}\in\mathcal{C},\\
&-1&&\text{if}\>-e_{\alpha_{k}}\in\mathcal{C},\\
&0&&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(68)

Therefore, the following relation for the directed cycle and the incidence matrix
West et al. 2001
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}\bm{x}=\bm{0},

(69)

is satisfied (see also Fig.
12
(a) for an example) because the contributions of loops do not exist in
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}\bm{x}
, i.e.,
x_{k}=0
if
e_{\alpha_{k}}\in E_{R}
, and we only consider the contribution by the conventional incidence matrix for
e_{\alpha_{k}}\in E_{M}
. We can also verify Eq. (
69
) directly using Eqs. (
61
) and (
68
), and
\mathrm{t}(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{k})=\mathrm{s}(\bar{e}^{\prime}_{k+1})
for
k=1,\dots,L
.
Equation (
69
) implies that the columns of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
are linearly dependent and thus
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})=0
. Therefore,
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{1}
.

E.4.2
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)

We show that
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
and satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
. We assume that
H(\varepsilon)
satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
, since otherwise
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
already holds, as discussed in
E.4.1
. Therefore, we assume that the component does not contain cycles.

There are two cases where
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
is not satisfied.
The first case is that
H(\varepsilon)
contains a component with at least two loops, where this component does not contain vertex
j
.
The second case is that
H(\varepsilon)
contains a component without loops, where this component does not contain vertex
j
.
We show
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
in both cases.

First, we consider the first case.
We arbitrarily choose such a component and let
V^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
denote the set of vertices and the set of edges for this component, respectively.
We can show
|E^{\prime}|-1\geq|V^{\prime}|
because the number of edges in a component without cycles and with
n_{\rm l}(\geq 2)
loops is
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|-1+n_{\rm l}
. Here, the number of edges that are not loops for a component without cycles is
|V^{\prime}|-1
because the component without cycles becomes a spanning tree for
V^{\prime}
with
|V^{\prime}|-1
edges when we remove the loops from the component. Using
|E^{\prime}|-1\geq|V^{\prime}|
, we can show that the columns of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
are linearly dependent as follows.
Note that all rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}
are present in
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
because the component we are now considering does not contain vertex
j
.
We arbitrarily label the edges in
E^{\prime}
as
E^{\prime}=\{e^{(1)},\cdots,e^{(|E^{\prime}|)}\}
. Let
\bm{w}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V(G)|-1}
be the column of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to
e^{(k)}
(see also Fig.
12
(b) for examples of
\bm{w}^{(k)}
).
By the definition of
\mathsf{\Gamma}
in Eq. (
62
),
(\mathsf{\Gamma})_{i\alpha}\neq 0
if vertex
i
is the target vertex or the source vertex of
e_{\alpha}
.
Therefore, the element of
\bm{w}^{(k)}
can be nonzero only if the corresponding node is in
V^{\prime}
.
In other words, each column of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to an edge in
E^{\prime}
can have nonzero elements only in the rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}
.
Combining this fact with
|E^{\prime}|-1\geq|V^{\prime}|
, we find that
\bm{w}^{(k)}
has at most
|E^{\prime}|-1
nonzero elements in the rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}
.
Thus, the
|E^{\prime}|
vectors,
\bm{w}^{(1)},\cdots,\bm{w}^{(|E^{\prime}|)}
, are linearly dependent.
As a result, the columns of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
are linearly dependent, and hence
\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
(see also Fig.
12
(b) for an example).
Thus, we conclude
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
in the first case.

Next, we consider the second case.
Let
V^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
denote the set of vertices and the set of edges for this component, respectively.
We have
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|-1
because the component does not contain loops and cycles, and the component is a spanning tree for
V^{\prime}
with
|V^{\prime}|-1
edges.
Using
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|-1
, we can show that the rows of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
are linearly dependent as follows.
Note that all rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}
are present in
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
.
These rows can have nonzero elements only in the columns corresponding to
E^{\prime}
.
Combining this fact with
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|-1
, we find that each row of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to an element of
V^{\prime}
can have at most
|V^{\prime}|-1
nonzero elements in the columns corresponding to
E^{\prime}
.
Thus, the
|V^{\prime}|
rows of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to the set
V^{\prime}
are linearly dependent.
Therefore, we conclude
\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
, and thus
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
in the second case.

Combining the results of the two cases, we conclude that
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
holds when
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
and satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
.

E.4.3
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)

We show that
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
and satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
. If
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
, then
H(\varepsilon)
contains a component with at least one loop and vertex
j
.
Let
V^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
be the set of vertices and the set of edges for this component, respectively.
We can show
|E^{\prime}|\geq|V^{\prime}|
because the component with
n_{\rm l}(\geq 1)
loops and without cycles, the number of the edges is
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|-1+n_{\rm l}
.
Using
|E^{\prime}|\geq|V^{\prime}|
, we show that the columns of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
are linearly dependent as follows.
Note that the component contains vertex
j
, while
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
does not have the row corresponding to vertex
j
.
Therefore, each column of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to an edge in
E^{\prime}
can have nonzero elements only in the rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}
.
Since
|E^{\prime}|\geq|V^{\prime}|
implies
|E^{\prime}|-1\geq|V^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}|
, the
|E^{\prime}|
columns of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to
E^{\prime}
can have at most
|E^{\prime}|-1
nonzero elements in the rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}\setminus\{j\}
.
Thus, these
|E^{\prime}|
columns of
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponding to the set
E^{\prime}
are linearly dependent (see also Fig.
12
(c) for an example). Therefore, we conclude
\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
, and thus
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
and satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
.

E.4.4
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)

We show that
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
and satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
. We assume that
H(\varepsilon)
satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
, since otherwise
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
already holds.
Since
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
is trivially satisfied when
j=i
, we consider only the case
j\neq i
.
If
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
with
j\neq i
, then vertex
i
and vertex
j
belong to different components.
We focus on the component that contains vertex
i
and does not contain vertex
j
.
Let
V^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
be the set of vertices and the set of edges for this component, respectively. Since
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
are satisfied, this component has a loop and no cycles.
We can show
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|
because the component is connected and contains a loop and no cycles.
Using
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|
, we can show that the columns of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
are linearly dependent as follows.
The
|E^{\prime}|
columns of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to
E^{\prime}
can only have nonzero elements in the rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}
, since
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
does not have the row corresponding to vertex
i
.
Since
|E^{\prime}|=|V^{\prime}|
implies
|E^{\prime}|-1=|V^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}|
, each column corresponding to an edge in
E^{\prime}
can have at most
|E^{\prime}|-1
nonzero elements in the rows corresponding to
V^{\prime}\setminus\{i\}
.
Therefore, the
|E^{\prime}|
columns of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to the set
E^{\prime}
are linearly dependent (see also Fig.
12
(d) for an example). Therefore, we conclude
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})=0
, and thus
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})=0
if
H(\varepsilon)
does not satisfy
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
and satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
.

(70)

satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,

,

,
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{7}
.

E.5
Block diagonalization of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}

We show that
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
can be transformed into block diagonal matrices if
H(\varepsilon)
satisfies all the conditions
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{7}
, which are necessary conditions for
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})\neq 0
.
The block diagonalization makes it easy to compute nonzero values of
\det(\BB^\varepsilon_{\backslash(i, \cdot)})\det(\GGamma^\varepsilon_{\backslash(j, \cdot)})
.

If
H(\varepsilon)
satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{7}
, we can introduce a procedure that transforms
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
into block diagonal matrices by swapping rows and columns as follows,

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(N_{\rm c})}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}\end{pmatrix},

(71)

\displaystyle\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(N_{\rm c})}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}\end{pmatrix},

(72)

(see also Fig.
13
for an example). Note that
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
or
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
corresponds to the
\alpha
-th component
H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)}
where
H(\varepsilon)
satisfies
\mathrm{C}_{1}
,
\mathrm{C}_{3}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{4}(j)
,
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
and
\mathrm{C}_{7}
. Thus,
N_{\mathrm{c}}
is the number of components in
H(\varepsilon)
.
If
i=j
, the rows and columns are swapped in the same way for both matrices
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
. If
i\neq j
, the columns are swapped in the same way for both matrices
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
, and the rows except the rows corresponding to
i
and
j
are swapped in the same way for both matrices
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
and
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
. The row corresponding to
j
for the matrix
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
is placed in a row corresponding to the first block
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
, and the row corresponding to
i
for the matrix
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
is placed in the same row as the row corresponding to
j
for the matrix
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
. Thus, the row corresponding to
i
for the matrix
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
is placed in a row corresponds to the first block
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
. The first component corresponding to
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
contains the vertcies
i
and
j
because of the condition
\mathrm{C}_{6}(i,j)
. Similarly, the first component corresponding
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
contains the vertcies
j
and
i
.

First, we discuss a procedure for the block diagonalization of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
in Eq. (
71
) more precisely. Let
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
be the set of vertices for the
\alpha
-th component corresponding to
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
or
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
. Let
E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
be the set of edges for the
\alpha
-th component corresponding to
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
or
\mathsf{\Gamma}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(j,\cdot)}
. For the first component, we have
|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})|=|V(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})|-1
because
\mathrm{C}_{1}
and
\mathrm{C}_{4}(i)
imply that the first component is a spanning tree. We swap the
|V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|-1
rows of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})\setminus\{i\}
with the top
|V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|-1
rows, preserving their relative order in
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
. We also swap the columns of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to
E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})
with the first
|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|
columns, preserving their relative order in
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
. Note that the rows of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})\setminus\{i\}
can have nonzero elements only in the columns corresponding to
E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})
, and the columns of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to
E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})
can have nonzero elements only in the rows corresponding to
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})\setminus\{i\}
. Because
|V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})\setminus\{i\}|=|V(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|-1=|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|
, we obtain a
|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|\times|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(1)})|
matrix, that is
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(1)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
. For the
\alpha
-th component with
\alpha\neq 1
, we have
|V(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})|=|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})|
because
\mathrm{C}_{1}
and
\mathrm{C}_{3}(i)
imply that the
\alpha
-th component contains no cycles and contains a loop with
\alpha\neq 1
. Note that
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}

contains all the rows corresponding to
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
since the
\alpha
-th component does not contain vertex
i
with
\alpha\neq 1
. We swap the rows corresponding to
E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
with the rows in the range from
(\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{\alpha-1}|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha^{\prime})})|+1)
-th row to
\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{\alpha}|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha^{\prime})})|
-th row, preserving their relative order in
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
. We also swap the columns corresponding to
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
with the columns in the same range, preserving their relative order in
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
. This swapping results in a
|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})|\times|E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})|
square matrix, which is
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon(\alpha)}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
with
\alpha\neq 1
. Note that the rows of
\mathsf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{\backslash(i,\cdot)}
corresponding to
V(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
can have nonzero elements only in the columns corresponding to
E(H(\varepsilon)^{(\alpha)})
, and the columns of

We determine the maximum number of rooted
0
/
1

The number of graphs in
\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)
is bounded by

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)|\leq 2(N+1)^{N-2},

(86)

with
N=|V(G)|
.
Since
\mathcal{F}_{i\leftarrow j}(G)
and
\mathcal{F}_{i\not\leftarrow j}(G)
are subsets of
\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)

.
Note that the number of rooted spanning trees with
N

, i.e.,
|\mathcal{T}_{i}(G)|\leq N^{N-2}
holds with
N=|V(G)|
, which is known as Cayley’s formula
West et al. 2001
.

The inequality (
86
) is proved as follows.
To derive the upper bound on

, we consider the

with
N

, which is defined as a basic graph where each vertex has a loop and there are edges in both directions between each pair of vertices.
The set of
0
/
1

\mathcal{F}_{i}(G^{\mathrm{comp}}_{N})
satisfies
\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{i}(G^{\mathrm{comp}}_{N})
for any basic graph
G
with
N
vertices.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{F}_{i}(G^{\mathrm{comp}}_{N})|=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i}(G^{\mathrm{comp}}_{N})}1.

(87)

If we compare the right side of this inequality with the right side of Eq. (
84
) when
i=j
, we notice that
\sum_{H\in\mathcal{F}_{i}(G^{\mathrm{comp}}_{N})}1
can be obtained by replacing every

with
-1
and every

with 1 for

, because this replacement turns every

in eq. (

.
Because

is replaced with

, applying this replacement transforms

into

, which is defined as

if

, and
\tilde{L}_{kl}:=-1
if

.
Then, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{i}(G)|

\displaystyle\leq\det(\tilde{\mathsf{L}}_{\backslash(i, i)})

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}N&-1&\cdots&\cdots&-1\\
-1&N&-1&\cdots&-1\\
\vdots&-1&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
-1&-1&\cdots&-1&N\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}2&-1&\cdots&\cdots&-1\\
2&N&-1&\cdots&-1\\
\vdots&-1&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\

\displaystyle=\begin{vmatrix}2&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\

\vdots&0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\

\displaystyle=2(N+1)^{N-2}.

In the third line, we added the columns from the second to the
(N-1)
-th column to the first column.
In the fourth line, we added half of the first column,

, to each of the columns from the second column to the
(N-1)
-th column.
