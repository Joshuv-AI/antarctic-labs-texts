Early post‐operative outcomes in adults undergoing minimally invasive versus open emergency colorectal surgery: A retrospective cohort study of the ACS‐NSQIP 2016–2022
Authors: Tyler McKechnie, Sara Keshavjee, Victoria Shi, Olivia Kuszaj, Sahaar Rattansi, Dennis Hong, Aristithes Doumouras, Cagla Eskicioglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimMinimally invasive surgery (MIS) has become common practice in elective colectomy; however, in emergent settings, this is less common and requires further research on safety and efficacy. The aim of this study was to compare MIS and open approaches for emergency colectomy.MethodAdults who underwent emergency colectomy for diverticular disease, colorectal malignancy or inflammatory bowel disease between 2016 and 2022 were identified in the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program. The primary outcome was overall 30‐day post‐operative major morbidity—a composite outcome that included variables such as mortality and venous thromboembolism. Secondary outcomes were 30‐day post‐operative mortality, septic complications, wound complications and length of stay (LOS). Multivariable regression models were fit for each outcome.ResultsIn total, 82,142 patients were identified who underwent emergency colectomy, of which 24,050 patients underwent MIS (29.4%). In total, 27,206 patients (33.1%) experienced the primary outcome of overall in‐hospital post‐operative morbidity, with significantly more patients in the open group experiencing this outcome (39.3% vs. 18.3%, odds ratio [OR] 0.57, 95% confidence intervals [CI] 0.54–0.59, p < 0.001). MIS patients had significantly lower risk of mortality (OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.48–0.60), septic complications (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.54–0.61) and wound complications (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.51–0.56). Post‐operative LOS was 2.22 days shorter in the MIS group (95% CI −2.32 to −2.13, p < 0.001).ConclusionEmergency colectomy via MIS is associated with a decreased risk of post‐operative morbidity and mortality as compared to open surgery. While MIS is increasingly prevalent in elective settings, these data illustrate potential benefits in the emergent setting.Keywords: colectomy, colorectal surgery, emergency surgery, minimally invasive surgery

What does this paper add to the literature?

We performed an updated comparison of minimally invasive (MIS) and open emergency colectomy with a 2016–2022 cohort of the ACS‐NSQIP database. This large dataset provides new information on the utility of MIS in emergency colectomy, illustrating a decreased risk of post‐operative morbidity and mortality as compared to open surgery.

INTRODUCTION

There has been a discernible surge in the number of presentations to the emergency department (ED) attributed to colorectal‐related emergencies, encompassing issues such as obstruction, haemorrhage, perforation and ischaemia [1, 2, 3]. This notable increase is attributable to a spectrum of aetiologies, including both benign and malignant factors. A significant proportion of newly diagnosed colorectal cancer (CRC) may present emergently with intestinal obstruction (8%–29% of cases) or perforation (3%–8% of cases) [4]. For benign disease, such as acute diverticulitis, the emergency department is a common point of entry for most patients [5]. In the early 2000s, there was a 40% increase in the number of hospital admissions related to acute colonic diverticulitis and it has since become the third most common gastrointestinal discharge diagnosis from EDs in North America [6]. As a result, the acuity and severity of these colorectal pathologies may necessitate operative interventions urgently or emergently.

Minimally invasive colorectal surgery has emerged as a preferred approach in elective settings due to its distinct advantages over traditional open colorectal surgery. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has been shown to offer patients a range of benefits such as reduced post‐operative morbidity, more easily controlled post‐operative pain, shorter hospital stays, quicker post‐operative recovery of bowel function and improved cosmetic outcomes [7, 8, 9]. The benefits of MIS are accentuated in the setting of obesity, a condition becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide [10]. Large randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the efficacy of MIS in achieving comparable oncologic outcomes to open surgery, thereby ensuring the maintenance of high‐quality patient care [11, 12]. Uptake into clinical practice has been rapid over the past two decades [13, 14], yet in the emergent setting, MIS has been less extensively implemented into clinical practice [15].

There have been previously published data, however, suggesting that minimally invasive approaches for emergency colorectal surgery may benefit this vulnerable patient population through improved short‐term post‐operative outcomes [16, 17]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis published in 2021 included 28 observational studies comparing minimally invasive and open approaches, 50% of which evaluated fewer than 100 patients and 75% of which evaluated fewer than 1000 patients [16]. This meta‐analysis concluded in pooled‐analysis that MIS patients had reduced post‐operative morbidity (odds ratio [OR] 0.53, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.43–0.65) and mortality (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.35–0.54) [16]. This study did highlight the need for larger studies in order to analyse rarer events, such as mortality [16]. In 2023, Jecius et al. utilized a 2012–2015 cohort of the American College of Surgeons (ACS)—National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) database (n = 1,855) to compare patients undergoing minimally invasive and open emergent colectomy for CRC [17]. Similarly, post‐operative morbidity (16.7% vs. 28.1%) and mortality (3.8% vs. 6.7%) were significantly reduced in the MIS group [17]. In summary, the majority of the data included in the aforementioned systematic review and meta‐analysis were from small, confounded observational studies, while the most recent ACS‐NSQIP database study addressing this question was performed with an older cohort, limited to CRC patients. Therefore, we designed the present study to perform an updated comparison of minimally invasive and open emergency colectomy with a 2016–2022 cohort of the ACS‐NSQIP database. We hypothesize that adult patients receiving minimally invasive emergent colorectal surgery will have decreased odds of post‐operative morbidity and mortality compared to adult patients receiving open surgery.

METHOD

Study design

This was a retrospective population‐based cohort study. This study adhered to STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Appendix S1) [18]. Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board approval was obtained (Project ID 17117).

Data source

This study was performed utilizing the ACS‐NSQIP database, a database curated with the intention of providing data for quality improvement, from January 1st, 2016 to December 31st, 2022. With data accrued from over 700 hospitals, it stands as one of the largest and most detailed surgical databases globally. Data collection is performed by trained clinical reviewers, who capture detailed information on over 150 variables pertaining to patient demographics, comorbidities, perioperative variables and 30‐day post‐operative outcomes for patients undergoing both inpatient and outpatient major surgical procedures [19]. Data are abstracted by trained Surgical Clinical Reviewers (SCRs) from patient medical charts [19]. The quality of data abstraction is upheld through regular Inter‐Rater Reliability (IRR) Audits of participating sites [19]. These data are stored annually in Participant Use Files (PUFs) which are Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant data files [19]. Smaller hospitals will collect all ACS‐NSQIP eligible cases at their hospitals. Larger institutions follow a systematic Eight‐Day Cycle Schedule sampling process to ensure a representative sample of approximately 10%–20% of eligible cases for data abstraction [19]. The ACS‐NSQIP database excludes data on patients under the age of 18 years and patients who have undergone an ACS‐NSQIP eligible procedure within the past 30 days, excluding any returns to the operating room for complications of a prior procedure [19]. Hospitals with an IRR Audit disagreement of greater than 5% and hospitals with issues consistently reporting 30‐day follow‐up are excluded [19].

Cohort selection

The ACS‐NSQIP classifies procedures with Current Procedure Terminology (CPT) codes and disease processes with International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10). After excluding cases that were elective, all emergent and urgent cases with corresponding CPT codes were used to identify a cohort of patients undergoing emergent colectomy. The study cohort was further narrowed through ICD‐10 codes used to identify only patients within the emergency colectomy cohort with a primary admission diagnosis of colon cancer, acute diverticulitis or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The procedure and diagnosis codes were drawn from previous similar studies [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Patients were excluded if they had an assigned American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class 5 or if they were undergoing elective surgery. Additionally, patients were excluded if they were noted to be mechanically ventilated preoperatively. Patients were dichotomized into open and MIS groups based on CPT codes. Minimally invasive surgeries converted to open were classified as MIS. Appendix S2 includes detailed CPT and ICD‐10 codes utilized to define the study cohort.

Patient and disease characteristics

The patient characteristics extracted included age, sex, ethnicity, race, body mass index (BMI), ASA class, smoking status, comorbidities (i.e., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, renal failure, ascites, immunosuppression) and functional status. The modified frailty index 5 (mFI‐5) was calculated for each patient as per previously described methods [25]. For patients with CRC, disease was characterized based on location and presence of metastases. Diverticulitis was classified as either uncomplicated or complicated. Type of IBD was specified for each applicable patient as either Crohn's Disease, Ulcerative Colitis or Indeterminant Colitis. Preoperative sepsis was described for each patient (i.e., none, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, septic shock). Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) wound classifications were available for all patients prior to 2021 [26]. Preoperative lab values were defined as the values drawn closest to the start of the operation and within three days of the start of the operation. These included leukocyte count, haematocrit, platelets and international normalized ratio test. Operative time in minutes and perioperative blood transfusions were collected where applicable.

Intervention

The intervention of interest was the minimally invasive operative approach. This was defined according to CPT codes for the index operative intervention (Appendix S2). Patient, disease and treatment characteristics were stratified according to the operative approach.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 30‐day major post‐operative morbidity. This was a composite of the following 30‐day post‐operative outcomes that are captured in the ACS‐NSQIP database based on previous literature: deep surgical site infections (SSIs), organ‐space SSIs, wound dehiscence, reintubation, ventilator dependence or failed wean from ventilation for over 48 h, sepsis, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, progressive renal insufficiency, cardiac arrest, mortality, or reoperation [27, 28, 29].

The secondary outcomes included 30‐day post‐operative mortality, post‐operative sepsis complications (i.e., sepsis, septic shock), wound complications (i.e., superficial SSI, deep SSI, organ‐space SSI, dehiscence), respiratory complications (pneumonia, reintubation, ventilator dependence or failed wean from ventilation for over 48 h), cardiovascular complications (myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident), venous thromboembolic complications (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism), genitourinary complications (urinary tract infection, renal failure, post‐operative dialysis), post‐operative length of stay (LOS), prolonged LOS, reoperation within 30‐days, readmission within 30‐days and discharge destination [29]. Prolonged LOS was defined as LOS exceeding the 75th percentile for the overall cohort, which is a commonly used cut‐off in other ACS‐NSQIP studies focused on post‐operative outcomes [30, 31]. Each postoperative complication is coded based on medical record abstraction by a trained SCR [19].

Sample size estimation

An event‐per‐variable (EPV) of 10 is often regarded as a minimum for powering logistic models [32]. We aimed to include 20 covariates in our logistic regression model, which corresponds with a minimum of 200 outcome events. Based on a large systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluating post‐operative morbidity following emergency colectomy, the expected proportion of patients experiencing post‐operative morbidity was estimated at 0.40 [16]. Using this presumed proportion, we calculated that a minimum of 500 patients would be required to adequately power our multivariable model.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample population. Continuous variables were described as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Missing data were assumed to be missing at random and were handled with pairwise deletion for all statistical analyses [33]. Multivariable logistic regression models were fit for the primary and secondary outcomes to determine baseline covariates associated with the outcomes. The following baseline variables were included in the final model a priori based on previous literature, physiology and clinical expertise: operative approach (i.e., minimally invasive vs. open), age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, ASA class, mFI‐5, smoking status, functional dependence, immunosuppression, preoperative dialysis, preoperative blood transfusions, preoperative sepsis, preoperative leukocytosis, preoperative anaemia, disease type, type of operation, operative time in minutes, intraoperative blood loss and year of treatment [23, 34, 35, 36, 37]. A multivariable linear regression model was fit for post‐operative LOS adjusting for the same baseline covariates. Normality was tested with q–q plots and homoskedasticity was assessed by fitting model residuals against independent variables. Cook's distance was used to identify influential outliers. Observations with a Cook's distance of greater than 0.1 were dropped from our final multivariable linear regression model. Model fit was evaluated with the pseudo R 2 and Hosmer‐Lemeshow test [38]. The variation inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to exclude multicollinearity. A VIF of greater than five was assumed to represent significant multicollinearity and the corresponding variable was removed from the model [39]. A subgroup analysis was performed by stratifying multivariable logistic regressions for the primary outcome according to underlying disease process (i.e., colon cancer, diverticular disease, IBD). A sensitivity analysis was performed by removing patients who underwent emergent colectomy for uncomplicated diverticular disease. Findings from logistic regression models were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and mean difference (MD) with associated 95% CIs for dichotomous and continuous outcomes, respectively. All statistical analysis was performed using STATA (StataCorp version 18; College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Patient demographics

There were 82,142 patients who underwent emergency colectomy between 2016 to 2022 who met inclusion criteria. After stratifying based on MIS versus. open surgical approach, there were 24,050 patients (29.3%) who underwent MIS and 58,092 (70.7%) who underwent open surgery. The MIS patients were younger, less likely to be white (63.4% vs. 70.2%) and less likely to have higher BMIs (8,248 (34.3%) vs. 22,353 (35.5%) with BMI > 30). Patients in the open surgery group were more likely to be ASA 4 (19.9% vs. 12.5%). Other baseline characteristics stratified according to operative approach are presented in Table 1.

Disease and treatment details

The majority of patients receiving emergency colectomy were presenting with CRC (38,454, 46.8%) or diverticular disease (32,605, 39.7%), with a smaller subset presenting with IBD (11,083, 13.5%). Patients with diverticular disease were more likely to undergo open surgery (27,324 (83.8%)) versus. those with colorectal cancer (24,527 (63.8%)) or IBD (6,241 (56.3%)). For those in the MIS group, the majority of patients underwent colectomy with primary anastomosis (73.7%), whereas in the open surgery group, 43.5% underwent colectomy with primary anastomosis and 46.6% underwent Hartmann's procedure. The operative time was significantly longer in the MIS group at 167.27 min (SD 76.83) versus 149.10 min (SD 74.60) in the open surgery group.

More patients in the open surgery group had preoperative sepsis, SIRS and septic shock. Patients in the open surgery group had a higher percentage of dirty (Class IV) wounds (47.6% vs. 18.7%) and a lower percentage of clean contaminated wounds (37.1% vs. 65.2%). Post‐operative morbidity and mortality

In total, 27, 206 patients (33.1%) experienced the primary outcome of overall in‐hospital post‐operative morbidity. According to our multivariable logistic regression model (Table S1), which included all of the a priori defined variables aside from intraoperative blood loss as this was not available in the ACS‐NSQIP, patients in the MIS group had a 43% decreased odds of experiencing major post‐operative morbidity within 30‐days of their index operation (18.3% vs. 39.3%, OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.54–0.59, p < 0.001), as well as a 46% decreased odds of 30‐day mortality (2.4% vs. 6.1%, OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.48–0.60, p < 0.001). Regarding specific sources of morbidity, patients in the MIS group had 43% decreased odds of experiencing post‐operative sepsis (8.6% vs. 25.2%, OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.54–0.61, p < 0.001), 47% decreased odds of overall wound complications (10.0% vs. 19.8%, OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.51–0.56, p < 0.001) and 47% decreased odds of respiratory complications (4.1% vs. 11.0%, 0.53, 95% CI 0.48–0.57, p < 0.001). DISCUSSION

This population‐level retrospective cohort study of 82,142 patients illustrates that those undergoing emergency minimally invasive colectomy have lower odds of post‐operative morbidity, mortality, septic complications, wound complications and reoperation as compared to open colectomy. Patients undergoing MIS also have lower risk of readmission and discharge to location other than home, with decreased post‐operative LOS. Outcome data were similar regardless of whether patients were undergoing surgery for colon cancer, diverticular disease or IBD.

There has been a clear temporal shift in findings from studies also using the NSQIP database. Earlier studies, such as that by Ballian et al. examining emergent laparoscopic versus open colectomy outcomes in patients from 2005 to 2008 showed no significant difference in post‐operative morbidity, mortality or reoperation rates [40]. Notably, they did show a significantly longer operating time and a shorter LOS in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery in their cohort [40]. However, studies including data from a more recent timeframe illustrate results that are quite comparable to those of this study. Patel et al. examined a cohort of 11,484 cases from the NSQIP database, which included laparoscopic and open cases propensity matched for demographic and comorbidity data, between 2005 and 2018 [41]. They found that open cases were associated with higher odds of failure to rescue (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.42–2.08), post‐operative mortality (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.44–2.07), morbidity (OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.60–1.88) and LOS (12.3 vs. 10.4, p < 0.0001) [41], which was in line with the results of this study. Another study by Jecius et al. also examined 1,855 patients undergoing open versus. minimally invasive colectomy for CRC in the NSQIP database, but in an unmatched cohort between 2012 to 2015 [17]. Similar to our study, they found the open surgery group had increased mortality rates (6.7% vs. 3.8%, p < 0.01), increased 30‐day complications (28.1% vs. 16.7%, p < 0.01) and increased LOS (14 ± 10 days vs. 11 ± 8 days, p < 0.01), compared to those undergoing MIS [17]. Ideally, randomized data would be produced on this topic in the emergent setting, as shown feasible by the laparoscopic versus open colorectal surgery in the acute setting (LaCeS) feasibility trial (ISRCTN 15681041) [42]. In this feasibility trial, there was a trend towards decreased morbidity and mortality in the laparoscopic arm, but formal statistical analysis was not yet completed [42]. Unfortunately, the subsequent LaCeS2 trial based on the initial feasibility study closed due to lack of recruitment after over 2 years in 2024 [43]. Another randomized clinical trial with a similar objective taking place in Finland, the LapEmerge trial (NCT 05005117), is currently undergoing recruitment with expected completion in September 2025 [44]. Given the difficulty in obtaining randomized data in the emergent setting, as well as previous trends towards improved outcomes in MIS, our study of 82,142 patients with colorectal surgical emergencies across cancer, diverticular disease and IBD was essential in updating and expanding upon available data.

Given the observational nature of this study, it is clear that the choice of minimally invasive versus open surgery is impacted by clinician's choice and patient presentation, ideally aimed at providing optimal, individualized patient care. Despite relative contra‐indications for MIS in patients with certain severe comorbidities, there has been evidence of safety in colorectal surgery patients with high age‐adjusted Charleston comorbidity index [45]. Notably, previous studies in colorectal surgical patients have shown consistently that MIS approaches are associated with longer operative time [40, 45, 46]. This is an important consideration in patients undergoing emergency surgery, where patient's hemodynamics may mean inability to tolerate longer operative times. Additionally, in cases of obstruction, MIS may be routinely avoided in some institutions, yet there are observational data showing safety, improved post‐operative outcomes and possibly a reduced rate of iatrogenic injury with a MIS approach in select patients [47, 48]. Importantly, all such data are observational and at high risk of patient selection bias, as well as suffering from small sample sizes, as the NSQIP database does not record intraoperative complications (e.g., missed enterotomies, intraoperative haemorrhage). With increased data on the safety of MIS, as well as changes in surgeon training over time, evidently there has been an increase in the frequency of minimally invasive approaches for emergency colorectal surgery cases in our cohort from 2016 to 2022 with 29.3% MIS as compared to the cohort in Ballian et al. from 2005 to 2008 with only 9.6% [40].

The results of this study also highlight the improved healthcare resource utilization associated with MIS approaches. Notably, decreased length of stay, decreased rates of reoperation and readmission at 30 days post‐operatively and a decreased number of patients from home being discharged to other locations. Trends over time have shown that despite the initial concern over increased cost per case in MIS, the gap is closing, and longer term cost‐savings may outweigh upfront costs [49, 50]. One American study on resource utilization for open versus minimally invasive colectomy in over 4,000 patients found that open surgery was associated with 1.26 times higher overall costs in the first 90 days post‐operatively, with increased usage of healthcare services and increased days off work [49]. Evidently, increased use of MIS may lead to decreased long‐term overall costs on a healthcare system.

This study has advantages due to a large sample size, including patients from both urban and rural sites, as well as academic and community hospitals. There is strong generalizability from this study due to a diversity of presenting pathologies necessitating emergency colorectal surgery. However, there were important confounders that were unaccounted for in the NSQIP database, including measures of nutritional status, prior surgical history or Hinchey class in cases of diverticular disease, all of which may help to further explain differences in post‐operative outcomes [51]. The database also does not account for surgeon experience and surgeon subspecializing, which adds residual confounding. Moreover, there is a high rate of missingness with preoperative blood work and wound class data in the ACS‐NSQIP, and thus, these data were not included in our multivariable models, potentially contributing to residual confounding as well. The database also does not specify reasons for reoperation, as well as intraoperative iatrogenic injuries, which may have helped delineate key differences in post‐operative outcomes between patients undergoing MIS versus open colectomy. The database also fails to account for long‐term outcomes such as post‐discharge wound infections or rates of incisional or parastomal hernias. Finally, despite statistical analyses to account for baseline patient characteristics, such as severity of disease, and exclusion of patients who were ASA class 5 and ventilator‐dependent preoperatively, both of which would influence the choice to proceed preferentially with open surgery, there remains the potential for selection bias to have impacted the observed findings.

Overall, the results of this study illustrate that emergency MIS colectomy may provide key advantages over open colectomy, including lower morbidity and mortality, and improved healthcare resource utilization. The use of MIS in the emergent setting should be considered whenever feasible, particularly in those with less severe disease, as a means to improve outcomes in emergency colectomy patients with varying pathologies, including diverticular disease, malignancy or IBD.
