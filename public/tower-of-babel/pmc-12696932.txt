Morphological and cortical bone assessment of the edentulous posterior mandible using CBCT Implications for implant planning and mandibular cortical index evaluation
Authors: Ozgun Yildirim, Hacer Eberlikose, Orhan Gulen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between posterior mandibular ridge morphology, cortical bone characteristics, and the Mandibular Cortical Index (MCI), and to assess their potential impact on the complexity of dental implant placement. Understanding these parameters is essential to optimize implant planning, minimize surgical complications, and improve clinical outcomes, particularly in regions where anatomical variations pose increased risks.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study, 100 cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans of edentulous mandibles were analyzed in Turkish population. Each mandible was evaluated through 10 sagittal cross-Sect. (5 from each side), resulting in 1000 sections. The sample consisted of equal numbers of male and female patients aged between 32 and 79 years. Morphological parameters, including lingual concavity and convexity, bucco-lingual width, cortical bone thickness (buccal and lingual), alveolar crest height, and ridge morphology types, were evaluated in sagittal sections. Ridge shapes were categorized as saddle, kidney, pen shape, toucan beak, straight, hourglass, or basal. The MCI was classified as C1 (normal cortex), C2 (moderate erosion), or C3 (severe erosion) based on cortical integrity. Statistical analyses were conducted to determine the relationships between MCI, ridge morphology, and bone parameters.ResultsA statistically significant relationship was observed between MCI groups and ridge morphology types (p < 0.001). The C3 group exhibited significantly thinner lingual cortical bone (0.81 ± 0.22 mm), lower alveolar crest heights, and increased bucco-lingual width compared to the C1 group (1.29 ± 0.26 mm, p < 0.001), along with lower alveolar crest heights and increased bucco-lingual width (p < 0.05). Basal-shaped ridges showed the widest bucco-lingual dimensions but the lowest alveolar crest heights, suggesting advanced vertical bone loss. Conversely, straight and pen-shaped ridges demonstrated greater lingual cortical thickness. Lingual concavity was more pronounced in cases with lower MCI.ConclusionCBCT-based evaluation of mandibular ridge morphology and cortical bone integrity, supported by appropriate statistical analyses (ANOVA, Chi-square, Kruskal-Wallis), offers clinically valuable insights for implant planning. Incorporating these assessments helps identify patients at greater risk for complications, guides the need for augmentation procedures, and supports safer and more predictable implant placement. These findings underscore the clinical relevance of ridge morphology and MCI in preoperative risk assessment, helping clinicians tailor implant planning strategies to anatomical variations and minimize complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06951-x.Keywords: Mandibular cortical index, Cone beam computed tomography, Ridge morphology, Dental implant planning, Posterior mandible

Introduction

Implant therapy is an efficacious treatment method that improves dental health and quality of life, increasingly favored by those who have suffered tooth loss for many reasons [1]. The first mandibular molars are the most frequently extracted teeth, usually due to significant carious lesions, periodontal disease, or endodontic complications, necessitating rehabilitation with dental implants to restore function and aesthetics [2, 3]. This jaw area is therapeutically important because of its unique morphology and closeness to specific structures, including the submandibular salivary gland cavity, the submandibular fossa, and the inferior alveolar nerve, which provide risks during implant placement [4]. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and analysis of the remaining bone ridge shape might yield valuable insights for formulating an appropriate treatment plan and identifying the ideal implant placement position [1]. Exceeding the alveolar housing with a dental implant in the posterior mandibular area may cause harm to essential anatomical structures. Accidental perforation of the lingual cortical plate into the floor of the mouth during drilling can lead to vascular injury and significant hemorrhage, perhaps culminating in the formation of a sublingual or submandibular hematoma [5].

Watanabe et al. classified the cross-sectional morphology of the jaw, revealing that lingual concavity occurs in 36–39% of the sample group. While optimal bucco-lingual implant angulation should align with the long axis of occlusal forces for biomechanical efficacy, neglecting a lingual undercut may increase the risk of perforating the lingual cortical plate, resulting in significant surgical complications [6].

Conventional radiographic techniques, including panoramic and periapical radiographs, are limited in assessing bucco-lingual dimensions and morphology because of their two-dimensional format. Recent developments in cross-sectional imaging techniques, particularly CBCT, have become increasingly relevant [7]. While CBCT offers high-resolution, three-dimensional imaging that enhances diagnostic accuracy, it is important to acknowledge its potential limitations, including higher radiation exposure compared to conventional two-dimensional radiography. Therefore, its use should be justified by clinical necessity and adherence to the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle.

Tözüm et al. investigated the relationship between mandibular ridge morphology and the complexity of dental implant placement in the posterior mandible using CBCT, emphasizing the importance of understanding ridge variations for effective implant planning [8]. Similarly, Goudarzi et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis of posterior mandibular ridge morphology, categorizing it into 14 distinct types based on shape and features, providing a detailed framework for clinical evaluation [1]. Despite these contributions, no prior research has thoroughly and comprehensively described the mandibular ridge in a manner that encompasses all of its dimensions and classifications, particularly in the edentulous posterior mandible.

This information gap has direct clinical consequences. The lack of a standardized and detailed morphological classification complicates preoperative diagnostic accuracy and decision-making in implant planning. Without clearly defined reference points for ridge width, concavity depth, or cortical thickness, clinicians may underestimate risks such as lingual plate perforation, insufficient primary stability, or poor osseointegration. These limitations can negatively impact implant success, increase postoperative morbidity, and raise the likelihood of life-threatening complications. Therefore, mapping and classifying these anatomical features using CBCT may significantly support surgical decision-making, guide the need for augmentation procedures, and reduce the incidence of intraoperative errors.

Additionally, the Mandibular Cortical Index (MCI) is a valuable tool for assessing alterations in the cortical bone of the mandible using panoramic radiography, offering insights into bone health and structural adaptations [9]. Together, these approaches highlight the critical role of precise morphological and structural assessment in improving diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes in mandibular surgeries and implantology [10]. However, planning using CBCT images offer significant benefits even though they are not yet commonly used in clinical practice. Their broader adoption has been hampered primarily by high costs, limited availability, and radiation exposure concerns.

A comprehensive understanding of posterior mandibular ridge morphology and cortical bone characteristics, as assessed by CBCT and the MCI, plays a pivotal role in improving implant outcomes and ensuring patient safety. Anatomical variations, such as prominent lingual concavities or a deep submandibular fossa, significantly increase the risk of lingual cortical plate perforation during osteotomy preparation, potentially resulting in life-threatening hemorrhage or submandibular hematoma. Furthermore, reduced cortical bone thickness—detectable via MCI—may compromise primary stability and long-term osseointegration. Integrating these parameters into preoperative evaluation can guide clinicians in selecting the optimal implant angulation and position, determining the need for bone augmentation, and avoiding critical anatomical zones. A detailed understanding of ridge morphology and MCI may thus help reduce implant failures, prevent severe complications, and serve as a valuable component in clinical decision support systems and evidence-based treatment protocols. This study aims to evaluate the posterior mandibular morphology and cortical bone condition using CBCT, and to investigate their potential implications for implant planning, complication risk reduction, and surgical decision-making.

No prior research has thoroughly and comprehensively described the mandibular ridge in a manner that encompasses all of its dimensions and classifications. Furthermore, the detailed morphology and frequency of ridge types in dentulous regions, particularly suitable for immediate implant placement, remain insufficiently explored. Understanding these variances is important because they may affect cortical bone health, measured by the Mandibular Cortical Index. The MCI complements ridge morphology research by revealing mandibular cortical porosity and structural modifications. These methods may improve diagnostic accuracy and implant placement and surgical planning.

The null hypothesis of this research is that there is no statistically significant association between the morphological characteristics of the edentulous posterior mandible, as assessed by CBCT, and the complexity of dental implant planning.

All images were analysed by one researcher with 20 years of experience as a maxillofacial radiologist (O.G.). The researcher worked 5 days a week, between 13.00 and 15.00, to avoid getting tired. After two weeks, 20% of the evaluation was re-evaluated.

Processing of CBCTs and radiographic measurements

All CBCTs were obtained using an HDX brand Dentri-Sα (South Korea) device. The irradiation parameters were 80-90kVp and 7-10mA, and the imaging area was 16x14.5- 16x8 mm (wied FOV). The CBCT images were inspected with Kodak Carestream Version 3.8.6.0 on a 15.6 inch screen with 1920 x 1080 resolution in a quiet room. A total of 10 sagittal sections were taken for the right and left mandible, 6 mm apart, starting from the distal of the mental foramen (Figure 1). For each patient, a total of 10 sagittal sections (5 from the right and 5 from the left posterior mandible) were obtained, resulting in a comprehensive dataset of 1000cross-sectional images derived from 100 patients. This approach ensured sufficient data density to meet the requirements of the power analysis and allowed for a detailed evaluation of bilateral mandibular morphology. These sections were aligned to approximately correspond with the anatomical positions of the premolar and molar teeth (i.e., 34–38 and 44–48), ensuring mesiodistal symmetry between the right and left mandibular regions for accurate comparative analysis. 3. Measurement Tools and Software

All measurements were made using Planmeca Romexis CBCT software. Measurements were made with 0.1 mm precision using digital calipers. To ensure consistency in image acquisition, ten sagittal cross-sections were obtained for each patient at 6-mm intervals starting from the distal aspect of the mental foramen bilaterally. The reference point for sectioning was the mandibular canal, and the slices were aligned perpendicular to the occlusal plane in the edentulous region. Section orientation was standardized by aligning the axial and coronal planes to the mandibular base and midline, respectively. This ensured reproducibility of section location and orientation across all scans.

Classification criteria

The shape of the mandibula was evaluated bilaterally on CBCT scans by examining the region distal to the mental foramen. Based on the classification system described by Klemetti et al.[13] each case was assigned to one of three categories[14]. Classification thresholds for submandibular fossa morphology were adapted from previously published studies. Submandibular fossae were categorized as shallow (< 2 mm; Type I), moderate (2–3 mm; Type II), or deep (> 3 mm; Type III) based on CBCT measurements as described by Bayrak et al.[15]

Anatomical reference definitions

In this study, the midsagittal reference was determined based on the radiographic midline extending through the mandibular symphysis. The alveolar crest was defined as the highest point of the residual ridge visualized distal to the mental foramen. The mandibular canal trajectory was traced bilaterally, and cortical measurements—such as mandibular cortical width—were obtained along a line perpendicular to a tangent drawn along the inferior mandibular border, intersecting the inferior aspect of the mental foramen. These reference definitions aimed to ensure geometric standardization and reduce operator-dependent variability in linear assessments on CBCT imaging.

Power analysis

The data obtained were subjected to a post hoc power analysis using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7)[16] based on the comparison of three independent groups (N = 100). Using the observed effect size (Cohen’s f = 1.027)[17], a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a total sample size of 100, the calculated statistical power was 1.000. The final sample size was comparable to or larger than those used in similar studies employing cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) for mandibular assessments; for example, Gomes et al.[18] used a sample size of 44 participants to evaluate mandibular indices. However, this post hoc power analysis was based on a simplified one-way ANOVA model, and did not account for the increased Type I error risk due to multiple comparisons or the potential hierarchical structure of the data (e.g., clustering of measurements within individuals). Therefore, the obtained power value may be overestimated, and these methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

Statistical analysis

In this study, descriptive statistics (number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum) were presented according to the type and distribution of variables. The assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was checked using the Levene test. One-way ANOVA was used to compare three or more independent groups when both assumptions were met. Otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Post hoc Bonferroni or Tamhane’s T2 tests were used based on the equality of variances after ANOVA, while Corrected Bonferroni test was used following Kruskal-Wallis analysis. For the comparison of categorical variables, Pearson’s Chi-Square test was used when all expected cell counts were greater than 5; otherwise, Fisher’s Exact test was applied. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.

Intra-inter observer repeatability

To assess measurement reliability, both intra-observer and inter-observer agreement analyses were conducted. For intra-observer reliability, 20% of the CBCT sections were randomly selected and re-evaluated two weeks after the initial assessment. Inter-observer agreement was evaluated by having a maxillofacial surgeon (O.Y.), blinded to the initial findings, repeat measurements on the same subset. Kappa statistics were calculated for categorical variables (e.g., MCI classification and ridge morphology), and Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were computed for continuous variables The intra-observer kappa values ranged from 0.759 to 0.900 and correlation coefficients ranged from 0.954 to 0.999 (p<0.05), indicating excellent repeatability. Inter-observer agreement was also high, with kappa values between 0.721 and 0.881 and correlation coefficients between 0.931 and 0.985 (p<0.05), supporting the reproducibility and validity of the measurement protocol[19].

According to Table 4, statistically significant differences in alveolar crest height were observed between MCI groups in the regions of teeth 34 and 35 (p<0.05). Based on the Tamhane post hoc test results, the alveolar crest height measurements in the moderate erosion group were significantly higher than those in the normal cortex group for these teeth. Upon examination of the overall findings, it concluded that decreasing MCI levels are associated with thinning of the lingual cortical bone and a significant reduction in alveolar crest height.

Upon examination of Table 5, it was found that the mean lingual cortical bone thickness in the C3 group was 0.81 ± 0.22 mm, which was significantly lower than that in the C1 group (1.29 ± 0.26 mm, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Since multiple statistically significant relationships were identified between the MCI, ridge morphology, and cortical bone characteristics (p<0.05), the null hypothesis, which posited that these factors do not significantly influence implant complexity, ridge morphology types, or cortical bone health, is rejected. The findings demonstrate that variations in MCI classification are associated with differences in lingual cortical thickness, bucco-lingual width, and alveolar crest height, indicating that MCI plays a crucial role in bone quality and morphology assessments[10, 11]. These results underscore the importance of considering MCI and ridge morphology during implant planning, as they directly affect bone availability, implant stability, and potential surgical complexity.

The submandibular fossa is an important anatomical structure located in the posterior region of the mandible and should be considered during implant surgery. Yıldız et al. revealed that the depth of the submandibular fossa is related to the risks encountered during lingual implant placement.[12] This study stated that the risk of perforation of the lingual cortical layer increased significantly as the depth of the fossa increased. The submandibular fossa's depth increases, while reducing implant insertion angles, potentially increasing the risk of implant breaching the lingual cortical layer and necessitating severe lingual angles[20–22].

The lingual concavity and convexity measurements included in our study are essential to evaluate the risk of lingual perforation. Deeper concavities are associated with the depth of the submandibular fossa. Deeper lingual concavities were predominantly observed in patients with thinner cortical plates, potentially reflecting lower MCI classifications. This link substantiates the correlation between the risk of lingual perforation and the depth of the submandibular fossa as examined in the study by Yıldız et al.[12]. The C3 group and deeper lingual concavities represent the high-risk category for the likelihood of lingual perforation.

This study revealed that patients classified as MCI-C1 had significantly greater lingual cortical thickness compared to those in the C3 group, suggesting a more robust bone structure in the former. This difference is clinically relevant, as increased cortical thickness has been directly correlated with higher implant stability values, including insertion torque and ISQ measurements, in previous studies[23, 24].

The present study revealed that lingual cortical thickness was significantly higher in straight, basal, and pen-shaped crests. This finding suggests that these crest morphologies may be associated with greater cortical bone preservation or reduced resorption on the lingual aspect. In particular, the basal and pen-shaped crests may provide additional support for prosthetic and implant applications due to their thicker lingual cortical bone. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of cortical bone thickness in determining implant stability. Increased cortical thickness contributes to higher primary stability and greater resistance to occlusal forces[25, 26].

In contrast, the highest buccolingual width was observed in the basal shape, which aligns with the structural characteristics of this crest type. A wider buccolingual dimension may reflect increased basal bone volume, offering more outstanding biomechanical support for dental implants or prostheses. However, despite the wider buccolingual dimension, the basal shape exhibited the lowest alveolar crest height in our analysis. This could indicate advanced vertical bone resorption, a critical factor in implant planning and prosthetic rehabilitation.

Although basal crests exhibit wider bucco-lingual dimensions, their reduced alveolar crest height may necessitate additional vertical bone augmentation during implant placement. Studies suggest that lower alveolar crest height is associated with higher implant failure rates and an increased need for augmentation procedures, such as guided bone regeneration or sinus lift[23, 26]. The clinical implication of this finding is that patients with basal-shaped crests may require bone grafting procedures or short implants to compensate for the limited vertical bone availability.

Although numerous international studies have investigated mandibular morphology using CBCT, there is a relative scarcity of comprehensive morphometric analyses focusing specifically on the Turkish population. Anatomical features such as mandibular ridge shape, cortical bone thickness, and lingual concavities may vary across populations due to ethnic, genetic, and environmental factors. Recent studies conducted in Turkish cohorts have provided region-specific data. For instance, Orhan et al. evaluated the vertical and horizontal position of the mandibular canal in a Turkish population, providing essential information for posterior mandibular surgeries.[27] Moreover, studies focusing on lingual foramina and vascular canal variations in Turkish dry mandibles have highlighted the anatomical variability relevant to surgical risk zones[28]. However, these studies have largely focused on dentate individuals or isolated anatomical structures. A population-specific assessment of the edentulous posterior mandible that integrates ridge morphology, cortical bone characteristics, and MCı remains lacking. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by providing a detailed CBCT-based morphometric analysis in a Turkish edentulous population, thereby contributing regionally relevant data that may support safer and more accurate implant planning tailored to this demographic.

The findings of this study offer several practical implications for preoperative implant planning in the posterior mandible. First, patients exhibiting a C3 MCI classification—characterized by severe cortical erosion and reduced lingual cortical thickness—should be considered at elevated risk for insufficient primary stability. In such cases, clinicians may opt for short or wider-diameter implants, utilize undersized osteotomy techniques, or plan for staged loading protocols to enhance stability. Second, ridge morphologies such as basal or saddle types, which are associated with reduced alveolar crest height despite greater bucco-lingual width, may necessitate vertical bone augmentation procedures (e.g., guided bone regeneration or interpositional grafting) prior to implant placement. Third, pronounced lingual concavities identified on CBCT, particularly in basal-shaped ridges or low MCI cases, indicate a higher risk of lingual plate perforation; therefore, modifications in drilling angulation, use of surgical stents, or alternative implant sites (e.g., more mesial or crestal) should be considered. Lastly, integrating MCI assessment from panoramic radiographs may serve as an initial, low-cost screening tool to identify patients who require detailed CBCT evaluation, particularly when ridge morphology suggests complex anatomy or bone resorption.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings. First, the retrospective design inherently limits control over image acquisition parameters and patient selection, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, although standardized measurement protocols were applied, certain classifications—such as ridge morphology and MCI grading—carry an element of subjectivity, which may affect inter-rater consistency. While intra- and inter-observer reliability were assessed in this study and demonstrated high agreement, classification systems themselves are still subject to interpretative variation. Third, the technical limitations of CBCT imaging must be considered. Despite its utility in three-dimensional bone evaluation, CBCT has relatively lower contrast resolution compared to medical CT and cannot assess bone density at a microstructural level. Furthermore, due to the higher radiation dose compared to two-dimensional imaging, CBCT use should comply with the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle. In this study, only existing diagnostic scans were used to avoid unnecessary exposure, yet the variability in voxel size and scan resolution across patients may still have affected measurement accuracy. Lastly, the lack of longitudinal clinical outcome data—such as implant success, insertion torque, or ISQ values—limits the direct translation of morphologic findings into predictive clinical decision-making. Future prospective studies incorporating surgical outcomes and histological validation are needed to strengthen the clinical applicability of these radiographic parameters.

Another limitation is the lack of direct implant outcome data, such as osseointegration success rates or peri-implant bone loss over time. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal designs, histomorphometric analyses, and clinical follow-up data to understand better how MCI-related variations influence implant longevity, prosthetic success, and long-term patient outcomes. Moreover, exploring the impact of systemic factors, such as osteoporosis or metabolic bone diseases, on MCI classification and ridge morphology could provide further insights into individualized treatment approaches for implant planning.

A further limitation of the study is that the samples were only derived from edentulous subjects. Physiological alterations in the morphology of the alveolar bone may occur following tooth extraction, and relying solely on edentulous patients may be inadequate to replicate the preoperative tissues. This constraint is especially pertinent considering the study's focus on rapid implant implantation, as post-extraction remodeling may modify alveolar bone shape, thus restricting the applicability of the findings to pre-extraction anatomical conditions.

Another limitation of this study lies in the 6-mm interval used between sagittal sections for morphological assessment. While this spacing enabled a comprehensive bilateral overview and reduced observer fatigue, it may have led to the omission of subtle anatomical variations, particularly in regions where ridge morphology changes abruptly over short distances. Structures such as lingual undercuts, submandibular fossa contours, or minor cortical thinning may not have been fully captured with this sampling density. Utilizing denser intervals (e.g., 1–2 mm) could improve spatial resolution and provide a more detailed characterization of cortical bone and ridge shape. However, this would also increase data processing time and may present challenges in terms of clinical feasibility. Future studies employing finer section intervals may yield higher anatomical fidelity and allow for more precise correlation between ridge morphology and implant planning complexity.

This study demonstrates a significant association between MCI, ridge morphology, and cortical bone characteristics, emphasizing their critical role in dental implant planning and surgical decision-making. The findings indicate that patients with lower MCI classifications (C3) exhibit reduced lingual cortical thickness, wider bucco-lingual dimensions, and lower alveolar crest heights, which may present challenges for implant placement and necessitate additional bone augmentation procedures. Future studies should further explore the long-term implications of MCI variations on implant success rates, integrating histological evaluations and clinical follow-ups to refine treatment protocols. Incorporating MCI classification into routine implant planning may enhance patient-specific treatment strategies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and long-term implant survival rates.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed CBCT-based morphometric assessment of the edentulous posterior mandible in a Turkish population, with a specific focus on ridge morphology and Mandibular Cortical Index (MCI) classifications. Beyond descriptive anatomical data, the findings have practical implications for clinical implant planning. For example, identifying ridge types with pronounced lingual concavities or reduced cortical thickness can alert clinicians to the heightened risk of lingual plate perforation and guide the adjustment of implant angulation or the consideration of pre-implant augmentation procedures. Moreover, the correlation between MCI classifications and ridge morphology may offer a radiographic screening tool for assessing underlying bone quality and potential surgical complexity prior to CBCT imaging. These insights may help clinicians stratify patients based on anatomical risk, tailor surgical protocols accordingly, and ultimately reduce intraoperative complications and improve implant success rates. Future studies are encouraged to validate these findings with clinical outcome data such as insertion torque, ISQ, and long-term survival.
