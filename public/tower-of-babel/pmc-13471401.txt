Lower-extremity muscle quality asymmetry in older adult fallers and non-fallers: a phase angle analysis
Authors: Phunsuk Kantha, Kulvara Lapanan, Makha Phongjit, Peerakan Inkhao, Rinrada Chanthorn, Wei-Li Hsu, Jakkrit Amornvit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls in older adults are a major public health concern associated with significant morbidity, psychological distress, and functional decline. Although lower-limb strength asymmetry is an established risk factor for falls, whether muscle quality asymmetry is associated with falls remains unclear. Phase angle (PhA), derived from multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (MF-BIA), is a validated biomarker of muscle quality. Therefore, this study aimed to compare segmental muscle quality and asymmetry between older adults with a recent history of falls (“fallers”) and their non-falling counterparts (“non-fallers”), and to evaluate its association with fall-related outcomes.MethodsThis study included 120 community-dwelling older adults, divided into non-fallers (n = 60; median age 69.5 years) and fallers (n = 60; median age 70.0 years). Participants completed fear of falling evaluations, physical performance tests (including chair stand and Timed Up and Go tests), and MF-BIA measurements. Segmental impedance (Z) at six frequencies (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, and 1,000 kHz) and reactance (Xc) at three frequencies (5, 50, and 250 kHz) were measured for the arms, legs, and trunk.ResultsCompared to non-fallers, fallers exhibited significantly poorer sit-to-stand performance (p = 0.03) and a higher percentage of whole-body fat (p = 0.01). No significant group differences were found for fear of falling or Timed Up and Go. While overall segmental PhA did not differ between groups, fallers showed significantly greater PhA asymmetry at 5 kHz in both the arms (p = 0.02) and legs (p = 0.02). Crucially, only leg PhA asymmetry was positively correlated with fall status at 5 kHz (rs = 0.29, p = 0.03) and negatively correlated with sit-to-stand performance at 5 kHz (rs = -0.32, p = 0.01) and 50 kHz (rs = -0.34, p = 0.04).ConclusionMuscle quality asymmetry in the lower extremities, identified by PhA asymmetry at 5 kHz, is modestly associated with fall status and reduced lower-limb performance in older adults. These findings suggest that leg PhA asymmetry may serve as an adjunctive outcome measure for assessing fall risk in geriatric populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07786-z.Keywords: Muscle quality, Phase angle, Bioelectrical impedance analysis, Falls, Older adults

Introduction

Falls are a major concern in geriatric health, representing a leading cause of injury, morbidity, and mortality among older adults worldwide [1]. Approximately one in three adults aged 65 and older experience a fall each year, with consequences ranging from fractures and traumatic brain injuries to a significant loss of independence and an increased need for long-term care [2]. Beyond the profound impact on overall health and quality of life, the economic burden of these events is substantial [3].

In addition to the physical impact, falls inflict a significant psychological burden, often manifesting as anxiety or a fear of falling [4]. This distress can initiate a vicious cycle: fear of falling leads to activity restriction and social withdrawal, which in turn causes physical deconditioning, muscle atrophy, and poorer balance [5, 6]. This interplay highlights that fall risk is a complex interaction between physical performance and psychological factors.

Muscle decline is a hallmark of age-related functional decline and is closely linked to fall-related outcomes [7]. For instance, sarcopenia—a syndrome defined by the progressive loss of muscle strength, mass, and physical performance—is a known risk factor for falls [8]. As lower-limb strength declines, an individual’s ability to maintain balance, react to unexpected perturbations, and execute protective stepping strategies is severely compromised [9]. Consequently, previous studies have focused on quantifying muscle mass and strength as primary indicators of fall risk [8–10]. Nevertheless, recent studies suggest that muscle quality and cellular health are more critical determinants of physical function than muscle mass alone [11].

Multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (MF-BIA) has emerged as a practical, non-invasive, and cost-effective tool for assessing body composition and cellular health [12]. MF-BIA applies currents across a spectrum of frequencies to differentiate between extracellular and intracellular water, providing detailed insights into fluid distribution and cellular-level properties rather than just muscle quantity [12, 13]. The phase angle (PhA), a parameter derived from these measurements, is considered a robust indicator of cell membrane integrity and overall muscle quality [14]. Because PhA reflects the capacitive properties of cell membranes, a higher PhA indicates healthy, intact membranes that effectively store charge. Conversely, a lower PhA signals compromised membrane integrity associated with malnutrition, inflammation, or atrophy [13].

Falls are dynamic events often caused by failure to recover from a trip or slip, requiring rapid limb coordination. Asymmetry in muscle strength and power between limbs is a well-documented risk factor for falls and mobility impairment, as it disrupts gait, balance, and the ability to execute compensatory steps [15, 16]. However, it remains unknown whether this asymmetry extends to the cellular level or impacts overall muscle quality.

To our knowledge, few studies have utilized segmental MF-BIA parameters to explore muscle quality asymmetry. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether these asymmetries are more pronounced in older adults who experience falls, or how these parameters correlate with specific fall-related outcomes. Thus, the objective of this study was to investigate whether PhA asymmetry differs between older adult fallers and non-fallers, and to examine its correlation with fall-related outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study used a cross-sectional, comparative observational design. Community-dwelling older adults were recruited from a community in Bangkok, Thailand. Data collection was conducted between June 2024 and June 2025 at the Chula Neuroscience Center, King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital.

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were 65 years or older, able to walk independently or with an assistive device, able to provide informed consent, and could understand and follow instructions for the assessments. Participants were excluded if they had an implant (e.g., pacemaker, knee replacements), a diagnosed neurological condition (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, stroke).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Chulalongkorn University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Protocol COA No. 0607/2024) and pre-registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06480279; registered June 13, 2024). All procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Participant screening and grouping

Definition of fall status

A “fall” was defined according to the Prevention of Falls Network Europe (ProFaNE) consensus as “an unexpected event in which the participant comes to rest on the ground, floor, or lower level” [17]. Fall history was obtained via a structured interview asking participants to recall the number of falls in the preceding 12 months. Based on this history, participants were categorized into two primary groups: “non-fallers” (reported zero falls) and “fallers” (reported one or more falls).

Data collection procedures

Each participant attended a single assessment session, and all measurements were performed by trained researchers following a standardized protocol. The session began with obtaining informed consent, followed by a demographic questionnaire (including fear of falling), the MF-BIA assessment, and the physical performance tests. Limb dominance was determined by asking participants, “Which hand would you use to write?” and “Which leg would you use to kick a ball?” [18].

Demographics

Participants completed a questionnaire to gather information on age, sex, height, weight, and fall status.

Falls Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I)

Fear of falling was quantified using the FES-I, a 16-item self-report questionnaire assessing concern about falling during various activities [19]. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = “Not at all concerned” to 4 = “Extremely concerned”), yielding a total score from 16 (no concern) to 64 (severe concern).

Physical performance assessment

Physical performance was evaluated using a standard armless chair. Prior to data collection, participants completed a single practice trial.

Chair Stand Test (CST): Participants began in a seated position with arms folded across their chest and were instructed to complete as many repetitions as possible. The total number of complete sit-to-stand repetitions performed within a 30-second interval was recorded. Fewer repetitions indicate poor sit-to-stand performance or lower leg power.

The total number of complete sit-to-stand repetitions performed within a 30-second interval was recorded. Fewer repetitions indicate poor sit-to-stand performance or lower leg power.

Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test: Participants were timed as they rose from the chair, walked 3 m at a self-selected, comfortable pace, turned, returned to the chair, and transitioned back to a seated position. A longer time indicates poorer mobility.

Multi-frequency Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (MF-BIA)

Body composition was assessed using the InBody S10 (InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), a portable, 8-point tactile electrode MF-BIA device. Participants were instructed to fast for at least 4 h, abstain from caffeine and alcohol for 12 h, avoid vigorous exercise for 12 h, and void their bladder before the test [20]. For the measurement, participants lay supine with electrode clips attached to their hands and feet at standard anatomical locations. The device measures impedance (Z) at six different frequencies (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, and 1,000 kHz) and reactance (Xc) at three different frequencies (5, 50, and 250 kHz) across five body segments (i.e., right arm, left arm, trunk, right leg, and left leg). The following MF-BIA parameters were derived by the software:

Body composition: protein mass, mineral mass, body cell mass (BCM), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), percent body fat (PBF), skeletal muscle index (SMI) and soft lean mass.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with the threshold for statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the data were found to be non-normally distributed, non-parametric methods were employed. Group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Chi-square (χ2) tests for categorical data. The relationships between MF-BIA parameters and fall-related outcomes (including fall status, fear of falling, and physical performance) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 120 community-dwelling older adults were recruited and completed all assessments. Participants were stratified into two groups: non-fallers (n = 60) and fallers (n = 60). Comprehensive demographics, fall histories, and physical performances are detailed in Table 1. Fallers demonstrated significantly higher weight (p = 0.04; z = -2.06) and BMI (p = 0.03; z = -2.23) compared to non-fallers. For physical performance, the faller group demonstrated significantly fewer CST repetitions compared to the non-faller group (p = 0.04; z = -2.01), indicating reduced lower-extremity power. However, no significant between-group differences were observed for fear of falling or mobility.

Correlation analyses

The relationships among all measured variables are illustrated in Fig. 5. Notably, CST performance demonstrated a weak negative correlation with whole-body PhA (rs = -0.28, p = 0.04, Fig. 5A). In contrast, MF-BIA parameters for the arms showed no significant correlation with any fall-related outcomes (p > 0.05; Fig. 5B).

Correlation coefficients of all variables for the (A) whole body, B dominant and non-dominant arms, C dominant and non-dominant legs, and D inter-limb asymmetry

When examining specific segments, PhA at 5 kHz in the dominant leg was moderately negatively correlated with CST performance (rs = -0.32, p = 0.03, Fig. 5C). Furthermore, leg PhA asymmetry at 5 kHz was positively correlated with fall status (rs = 0.29, p = 0.03, Fig. 5D), indicating that greater cellular asymmetry in the lower extremities is associated with a history of falls. Similarly, PhA asymmetry in the legs showed moderate negative correlations with CST performance at both 5 kHz (rs = -0.32, p = 0.01) and 50 kHz (rs = -0.34, p = 0.04, Fig. 5D). These findings suggest that a greater imbalance in muscle quality between the lower limbs is associated with diminished sit-to-stand performance.

Discussion

This study specifically compared MF-BIA parameters between fallers and non-fallers, focusing on muscle quality asymmetry measured by PhA. We investigated the relationships between these parameters and fall status, fear of falling, and physical performance in community-dwelling older adults. The findings revealed that older adult fallers exhibited significantly greater PhA asymmetry at 5 kHz in both their arms and legs compared to non-fallers. We also identified a moderate significant association of this asymmetrical muscle quality with both sit-to-stand performance and fall status. This provides a cellular-level perspective on functional decline, suggesting that a disparity in muscle quality between limbs is a relevant factor in fall risk.

The 5 kHz frequency is physiologically important because its current penetrates primarily through the extracellular fluid, as it is less able to pass through cell membranes [11, 22]. At this frequency, PhA is therefore sensitive to the properties of the extracellular space and the cell membrane’s capacitive resistance. Notably, we observed a lack of significant asymmetry at higher frequencies (50 and 250 kHz), which penetrate both intra- and extracellular compartments. Therefore, high PhA asymmetry specifically at 5 kHz suggests differences in extracellular integrity or localized fluid dynamics between limbs. A lower PhA in one limb relative to the other may indicate localized compromised membrane integrity, altered fluid distribution, or increased infiltration of non-contractile tissues (e.g., adipose or fibrosis). While our finding of higher whole-body fat in fallers provides some contextual support for altered tissue composition, it is important to note that whole-body metrics cannot directly confirm localized infiltration. Additionally, multi-frequency or segmental validation is necessary to confirm if this low-frequency asymmetry is a stable biomarker or a transient physiological shift.

We not only identified PhA asymmetry in fallers but also observed an association with functional performance, finding a negative correlation between leg PhA asymmetry at 5 kHz and leg power (measured by CST). These findings suggest that unbalanced cellular muscle quality, reflected by high PhA asymmetry, may be associated with reduced ability to generate the symmetrical power required to rise from a chair. This partially aligns with previous studies that have identified strength and power asymmetry as a major risk factor for falls and mobility limitations [15, 23]. We suggest that PhA asymmetry is a physiological correlate of this well-documented functional decline. A muscle with poorer cell integrity (lower PhA) is fundamentally less capable of efficient force generation, which may partly relate to poorer performance.

No significant differences were found between fallers and non-fallers in the PhA values for any single body segment. This critical finding highlights the limitations of traditional, non-asymmetrical assessments. While other research [24, 25], found that a low whole-body PhA was a significant predictor of incident falls, our results suggest a potential explanation for this discrepancy. An older adult could have one limb with high PhA and another with low PhA. While their average PhA might appear normal, our findings suggest the high degree of inter-limb asymmetry remains a potentially overlooked correlate of fall history. Consequently, this study suggests that the inter-limb asymmetry correlates compared to single-segment averages.

The finding that fallers performed worse on the CST aligns with existing literature, confirming their expected phenotype of reduced lower-limb power. Similarly, the faller group’s higher whole-body fat percentage is a known contributor to fall risk, as it increases mechanical load while reducing relative strength [26].

Regarding psychological factors, FES-I scores did not differ significantly between fallers and non-fallers. Based on the established cut-off score of < 28 [27], both groups exhibited low concern about falling. The absence of a significant FES-I difference may be attributed to the urban environment of this community-dwelling population. It is possible that extrinsic factors, such as home safety modifications, minimized the subjective concern about falling in this cohort [28]. Accordingly, our results suggest that the observed PhA asymmetry is more directly related to the physical mechanics of falling rather than psychological distress.

A notable characteristic of our study is the heavy skew toward female participants (83%). While this reflects the higher epidemiological prevalence of falls in older women [28] and volunteer recruitment bias, it has critical implications for our data’s interpretation. Given well-established sex differences in body composition [29] and age-related neuromuscular decline [30], sex likely acts as an effect modifier in these relationships. It is probable that the observed associations between PhA asymmetry and fall status in our study are influenced by female-specific aging trajectories. Consequently, these findings cannot be confidently generalized to older men, highlighting the need for future studies with larger sex-balanced groups.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, PhA asymmetry at 5 kHz may represent a useful adjunctive outcome measure for assessing fall risk in older adults. As MF-BIA devices are increasingly utilized in clinical and wellness settings for body composition assessment (e.g., fat mass, muscle mass), evaluating inter-limb asymmetry could add value to standard geriatric assessments. A high asymmetry index may identify at-risk individuals who might be missed by traditional methods or those presenting with normal unilateral segmental PhA. Furthermore, this quantifiable metric could serve as an objective longitudinal measure to track the efficacy of rehabilitation programs.

While current fall prevention programs focus on bilateral strength, balance, and gait training, our results suggest that the role of corrective symmetry interventions necessitates further prospective investigation. For an individual with high leg PhA asymmetry, rehabilitation might preferentially target the limb with lower cellular integrity to restore cellular and functional symmetry, rather than simply strengthening both legs. This could involve targeted unilateral strengthening exercises on the affected limb to improve muscle quality. Interventions might include resistance exercise with nutritional support or aerobic exercise to improve mitochondrial function, which may improve PhA [31].

Limitations

Several limitations must be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causality between PhA asymmetry and fall risk. It is plausible that prior falls resulted in injury, physical disuse, or protective offloading, subsequently leading to unilateral muscle atrophy and the observed PhA asymmetry. Second, our classification of fallers relied on a 12-month retrospective self-report; while common in fall research, this method is susceptible to recall bias and potential misclassification. Third, falls in older adults are multifactorial. To maintain the study’s scope, we did not collect or adjust for several key intrinsic and extrinsic fall-risk factors. It is highly plausible that evaluating PhA asymmetry within a comprehensive multivariate model incorporating other clinical and environmental covariates might attenuate its independent statistical weight. Fourth, selection bias is a concern because the sample was recruited from community centers, meaning these participants may be healthier or more motivated than the broader older adult population.

Conclusion

This exploratory cross-sectional study found that lower-extremity PhA asymmetry, particularly at 5 kHz, was greater in older adults with a history of falls and modestly associated with fall status and sit-to-stand performance. These findings suggest that the PhA asymmetry index obtained from MF-BIA could serve as an adjunctive measure in standard geriatric assessments to identify subtle changes in muscle quality. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether PhA asymmetry has predictive value for future falls.
