Emerging small molecule strategies target cancer stem cells through ferroptosis and metabostemness
Authors: Husni Farah, Waleed K. Abdulsahib, Sajida Hussein Ismael, R. Roopashree, Subhashree Ray, S. Usha Nandhini, Vimal Arora, Ashish Singh Chauhan, Dilnoz Buriyeva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer remains a formidable global health challenge, with therapeutic resistance and disease relapse frequently attributed to the persistence of a resilient subpopulation of cells known as cancer stem cells (CSCs). These cells, characterized by self-renewal capacity, continuous proliferation, and multi-lineage differentiation potential, drive tumorigenesis, metastasis, and evade conventional therapies. Traditional cancer treatments often fail to eradicate CSCs, leading to their enrichment post-therapy and subsequent tumor recurrence. This comprehensive review explores two innovative and interconnected therapeutic paradigms for targeting CSCs: ferroptosis and metabostemness. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death distinct from apoptosis, offers a promising avenue to overcome apoptosis resistance prevalent in many cancers, including CSCs, by inducing lethal lipid peroxidation. Concurrently, “metabostemness” describes the unique metabolic reprogramming of CSCs, encompassing alterations in glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, which are critical for maintaining their stemness, survival, and resistance to treatment. This review synthesizes the current understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis and metabostemness in CSCs, highlighting emerging small-molecule strategies designed to exploit these vulnerabilities. The discussion includes direct ferroptosis inducers and compounds that sensitize CSCs to ferroptosis by disrupting their aberrant metabolic pathways. Furthermore, the synergistic potential of combining ferroptosis induction with metabolic inhibitors to enhance therapeutic efficacy and circumvent drug resistance is explored. Finally, the significant challenges in clinical translation, including issues of specificity, systemic toxicity, and tumor heterogeneity, are addressed, while proposing advanced drug delivery systems and integrated multi-omics approaches as crucial future directions for realizing the full potential of these novel anti-CSC strategies in precision oncology.

Introduction

The relentless progression and recurrence of cancer, a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, underscore the urgent need for innovative therapeutic paradigms. Despite significant advancements in early detection and conventional treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, many cancers remain refractory, leading to high relapse rates and poor patient outcomes [1]. A pivotal factor contributing to this therapeutic recalcitrance is the existence of a distinct subpopulation of cells within tumors known as cancer stem cells (CSCs), also referred to as tumor-initiating cells [2]. These elusive cells, defined by their stem-like properties [3], are now understood to be the primary drivers of therapeutic failure; their inherent resistance to conventional treatments leads to their enrichment post-therapy, which in turn fuels tumor recurrence and distant metastasis [4].

This intrinsic resistance necessitates the exploration of novel therapeutic strategies that can selectively target and eliminate these critical drivers of malignancy.

In recent years, two distinct yet interconnected avenues have emerged as highly promising for overcoming CSC-mediated resistance: ferroptosis and metabostemness. Ferroptosis represents a unique, iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, biochemically and morphologically distinct from apoptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy [5]. Its mechanism involves the lethal accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent lipid peroxidation, making it an attractive strategy for cancers that have developed resistance to apoptosis-inducing agents [6]. Simultaneously, the concept of “metabostemness” recognizes the profound metabolic reprogramming that underpins the unique properties of CSCs [7]. Unlike their differentiated counterparts, CSCs exhibit remarkable metabolic plasticity, adapting their energy production and biosynthetic pathways—including glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism—to support their stemness, survival, and drug resistance within the dynamic tumor microenvironment [8]. This metabolic rewiring provides a novel framework for pharmacological intervention.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the current understanding and emerging small-molecule strategies that leverage the principles of ferroptosis induction and metabostemness targeting to effectively eradicate CSCs. The intricate molecular mechanisms governing these processes will be delineated, the vulnerabilities they present in CSCs will be explored, and specific small-molecule agents designed to exploit these weaknesses will be discussed. Furthermore, the synergistic potential of combining ferroptosis-inducing agents with metabolic inhibitors to enhance anti-cancer efficacy and overcome the formidable challenge of therapeutic resistance will be highlighted. Finally, the critical challenges in translating these promising preclinical findings into clinical practice, including issues of drug delivery, specificity, and tumor heterogeneity, will be addressed, while outlining future directions that could pave the way for a new era of precision oncology.

Cancer stem cells: the drivers of tumorigenesis and therapeutic relapse

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) constitute a critical subpopulation within heterogeneous tumors, distinguished by their unique stem cell-like properties of self-renewal, continuous proliferation, and the capacity to differentiate into various cancer cell phenotypes. This inherent ability maintains tumor heterogeneity and positions CSCs as the primary drivers of tumor initiation, growth, and progression [3, 4, 9]. This hierarchical organization, often referred to as the CSC model, posits that not all cancer cells are equal in their tumorigenic potential, with a small subset holding the exclusive ability to regenerate the entire tumor.

Defining characteristics and functional significance

CSCs possess an inherent self-renewal capacity, enabling them to indefinitely regenerate tumors. This has been rigorously demonstrated through serial transplantation experiments in immunocompromised mice, where implanted CSCs successfully recapitulate the cellular heterogeneity and complexity of the original patient tumor [10]. Beyond self-renewal, CSCs exhibit pluripotency, meaning they can differentiate into specialized cancer cell types, including vascular endothelial cells, pericytes, and erythroblasts, all of which contribute to the diverse cellular composition and functionality of a tumor [11]. This capacity for differentiation allows CSCs to drive tumor growth and adapt to various microenvironments.

A significant clinical observation is the enrichment of CSCs in advanced and aggressive tumors. These cells are frequently isolated from distant metastases, underscoring their pivotal role in metastatic dissemination [12]. This suggests that CSCs are not merely a component of the primary tumor but are actively involved in the spread of the disease. The identification and isolation of CSCs often rely on specific surface markers, such as CD44, CD133, and the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) [13]. Additionally, the side population (SP) technique, which leverages the activity of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters to efflux fluorescent dyes, also serves as a method for identifying CSCs [14]. Transcription factors, notably Oct4, are highly expressed in CSCs and play a crucial role in maintaining pluripotency and self-renewal. Oct4 also promotes tumorigenesis, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and contributes to therapeutic resistance [15]. Furthermore, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) represent a particular population of CSCs that can circulate in the peripheral blood and significantly influence various aspects of carcinogenesis, including EMT, angiogenesis, drug resistance, and metastasis [16]. Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASCs) are recognized as an interesting and accessible source of MSCs, further highlighting the diverse origins and roles of these critical cells [16].

Role in therapeutic resistance and relapse

A major challenge in contemporary cancer therapy stems from the inherent resistance of CSCs to conventional treatments, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy [17]. This resistance is not accidental but is attributed to several intrinsic mechanisms. CSCs often exist in a quiescent or slow-cycling state, making them less susceptible to cytotoxic agents that primarily target rapidly dividing cells [18]. They possess enhanced DNA repair capabilities, allowing them to quickly mend damage inflicted by genotoxic therapies [19]. The overexpression of anti-apoptotic proteins, such as X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), further protects them from programmed cell death [20]. Moreover, CSCs frequently exhibit increased drug efflux activity through ABC transporters, which pump chemotherapeutic agents out of the cell, thereby reducing intracellular drug concentrations [21]. The failure to eradicate CSCs during initial treatment leads to their subsequent enrichment within the tumor cell population. These residual CSCs then serve as the reservoir for tumor recurrence and distant metastasis [3]. This phenomenon is particularly evident in high-risk pediatric cancers like neuroblastoma, where relapse rates remain unacceptably high [22]. In neuroblastoma, this challenge is compounded by profound CSC plasticity, where cells can dynamically and reversibly switch between a highly adrenergic (ADRN) state and a less-differentiated, therapy-resistant mesenchymal (MES) state. These distinct CSC phenotypes possess unique metabolic and survival profiles, representing a moving target for therapy [23]. The persistence of such adaptable CSCs thus represents a fundamental bottleneck in achieving long-term remission.

Furthermore, CSCs can actively escape immune surveillance by altering immunomodulatory molecules, such as downregulating MHC class I molecules and antigen processing machinery, rendering them less visible and vulnerable to immune cell recognition and attack [24].

The dynamic nature of CSCs, characterized by “diversity and instability” and their ability to originate from various cell types, suggests a high degree of phenotypic plasticity [3, 10]. This adaptability extends to their metabolism, as they can adapt to microenvironmental changes and activate dormant pathways to evade therapy [18]. This implies that single-targeted therapies, while effective against bulk tumor cells, may inadvertently select for and enrich these highly plastic CSCs, driving adaptive resistance. Consequently, therapeutic strategies must account for this inherent plasticity, potentially requiring combination therapies that simultaneously target multiple vulnerabilities or induce non-adaptive cell death pathways to prevent the emergence of new resistant states.

The identification of CSCs as “tumor-initiating cells” and their responsibility for metastatic dissemination, tumor relapse, and drug resistance positions them as the fundamental “seed” of malignancy [25, 26]. If conventional therapies primarily target the bulk, differentiated tumor cells while leaving CSCs untouched, then tumor recurrence becomes an inevitable outcome. This highlights that eradicating CSCs is paramount for achieving long-term remission and preventing recurrence [27]. The focus of therapeutic development must therefore shift from merely reducing tumor bulk to effectively eliminating the regenerative source of the cancer.

The ability of CSCs to escape immune surveillance by downregulating MHC-I molecules and antigen processing machinery directly links these cells to immune evasion, a broader hallmark of cancer [28]. This implies that even advanced immunotherapies, which have revolutionized treatment for some cancers, might face significant challenges if they do not simultaneously address the immune-evasive properties inherent to CSCs. Combining CSC-targeting agents with immunotherapies could represent a powerful synergistic approach, potentially re-sensitizing CSCs to immune recognition or direct killing by immune effector cells (Fig. 1). Ferroptosis: a vulnerable achilles’ heel in cancer cells

Ferroptosis is a distinct, iron-dependent form of regulated cell death (RCD) that has garnered significant attention as a novel therapeutic strategy, particularly for overcoming the prevalent apoptosis resistance observed in many cancers [29]. Unlike apoptosis, which is primarily mediated by caspase proteases, ferroptosis proceeds through unique biochemical and morphological hallmarks.

Core molecular machinery and biochemical hallmarks

The defining features of ferroptosis are the lethal accumulation of iron and extensive lipid peroxidation. Iron, particularly redox-active ferrous iron (Fe2+), plays a pivotal role by generating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the Fenton reaction [30]. These highly reactive radicals then drive the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) embedded within cell membranes, leading to membrane damage and cell demise. This process is fundamentally driven by a redox imbalance, where the production of ROS overwhelms the cell’s capacity for lipid ROS detoxification [5, 31, 32].

Morphologically, ferroptotic cells exhibit characteristic changes that distinguish them from other forms of cell death. These include a loss of plasma membrane integrity, cytoplasmic swelling (oncosis), and distinct alterations in mitochondria. Specifically, mitochondria in ferroptotic cells often appear reduced in size, with increased membrane density, and a notable diminution or complete vanishing of their cristae [5, 33]. These unique cytological features provide clear indicators of ferroptotic engagement.

Key regulators and pathways

Several key molecular players and pathways precisely regulate ferroptosis:

Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4): This selenoprotein stands as a central regulator, powerfully inhibiting lipid oxidation. GPX4 functions by converting toxic phospholipid hydroperoxides into non-toxic lipid alcohols, thereby preventing the accumulation of damaging lipid ROS [34]. Its enzymatic activity is critically dependent on an adequate supply of glutathione (GSH), a major cellular antioxidant. Consequently, inhibition or genetic deletion of GPX4 is a primary and highly effective mechanism for inducing ferroptosis [34, 35]. System Xc- Antiporter (SLC7A11/SLC3A2): This cystine/glutamate antiporter is crucial for maintaining intracellular GSH levels. It facilitates the import of cystine (an essential precursor for GSH synthesis) into the cell while exporting glutamate [35, 36]. Inhibition of System Xc-, for instance by compounds like erastin, leads to a rapid depletion of intracellular GSH. This depletion, in turn, impairs GPX4 function, allowing lipid peroxidation to proceed unchecked and ultimately triggering ferroptosis [37]. Ferroptosis Suppressor Protein 1 (FSP1): Also known as AIFM2, FSP1 represents a glutathione-independent ferroptosis suppressor pathway [38]. It confers protection against ferroptosis by catalyzing the regeneration of ubiquinone (CoQ10) to its reduced form, ubiquinol, utilizing NAD(P)H as a reductant. Ubiquinol then acts as a radical-trapping antioxidant, scavenging lipid peroxyl radicals [38, 39]. The N-myristylation of FSP1 is crucial for its anti-ferroptotic activity, enabling its proper localization and function [40]. Interestingly, FSP1 has also been identified as a vitamin K reductase, adding another layer to its antioxidant defense mechanism [40]. Lipoxygenases (LOXs): These enzymes, particularly ALOX15, directly contribute to the generation of lipid peroxides, thereby enhancing the ferroptotic process [41]. Iron Metabolism Regulators: Proteins involved in iron homeostasis, such as transferrin, ferritin, hepcidin, and ferroportin, meticulously regulate systemic and cellular iron levels. Perturbations in these regulatory mechanisms directly influence a cell’s sensitivity to ferroptosis [42]. Notably, ferritinophagy, an autophagy-mediated degradation of ferritin (the primary iron storage protein), can lead to an increase in the labile iron pool, thereby triggering ferroptosis [43].

Exploiting ferroptosis in cancer therapy

The unique mechanisms of ferroptosis offer a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment, particularly for drug-resistant tumors that have developed mechanisms to evade apoptosis [44]. By inducing ferroptosis, it becomes possible to bypass the common apoptotic resistance pathways that render many conventional therapies ineffective. This approach has shown the ability to overcome acquired resistance to various conventional anticancer agents, including cisplatin [45]. Beyond direct cell killing, ferroptosis also plays a role in tumor suppression and can influence oncogenic pathways. For instance, tumor suppressors like P53 have been shown to induce ferroptosis, highlighting its inherent anti-tumorigenic potential [29].

A significant advantage of targeting ferroptosis is its ability to bypass apoptosis resistance. Many cancers develop robust mechanisms to evade apoptosis, which is the primary mode of cell death induced by most conventional chemotherapies [46]. Ferroptosis, however, is explicitly defined as a non-apoptotic form of cell death [47]. This fundamental distinction positions ferroptosis as a critical alternative pathway to induce cancer cell death when apoptosis is compromised. This offers a substantial therapeutic advantage, as inducing ferroptosis can circumvent a major mechanism of drug resistance, potentially re-sensitizing tumors that are otherwise unresponsive to conventional treatments that rely on apoptotic pathways. This represents a significant paradigm shift in strategies for inducing cancer cell demise.

Ferroptosis is fundamentally caused by a “redox imbalance” and “massive lipid peroxidation” [48]. The key regulators of ferroptosis, such as GPX4, FSP1, and System Xc-, are all intricately involved in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [49]. This suggests that disrupting the delicate balance of pro-oxidant and antioxidant systems within cancer cells is a broad and effective strategy for inducing ferroptosis. This could involve increasing the availability of labile iron, inhibiting the detoxification of lipid peroxides, or depleting key antioxidants like GSH. Furthermore, cancer cells, due to their often heightened metabolic activity, may already exist under a certain level of oxidative stress, potentially making them inherently more susceptible to ferroptosis induction compared to normal cells [50].

The iron-dependent nature of ferroptosis, coupled with the observation that cancer cells “require more iron for proliferation” and can exhibit “iron addiction”, creates a specific and exploitable vulnerability [51, 52]. This implies that modulating iron metabolism—for instance, by increasing the intracellular labile iron pool, inhibiting iron export, or promoting ferritinophagy—could be a highly potent and selective way to induce ferroptosis in cancer cells. Such strategies might have a more favorable therapeutic window, potentially impacting normal cells less if their iron homeostasis is not as severely perturbed. This also suggests that while iron chelators have historically been used, strategies that increase labile iron could be more therapeutically beneficial for inducing ferroptosis, rather than preventing it.

Metabostemness: metabolic reprogramming in cancer stem cells

The concept of “metabostemness” highlights that the unique properties of cancer stem cells (CSCs) are profoundly influenced, and even causally controlled, by their distinct metabolic profiles [53]. This metabolic reprogramming acts as a “starter dough” for cancer stemness traits, enabling differentiated cells to dedifferentiate and acquire CSC-like characteristics [7]. This framework suggests that metabolic parameters are not merely consequences of cancer but are active orchestrators of the genetic and epigenetic rewiring that drives the acquisition of aberrant stemness and resistance to differentiation.

Definition and causal link to CSC traits

Metabostemness refers to the specific metabolic parameters that functionally orchestrate the genetic reprogramming driving normal and non-CSC tumor cells towards less-differentiated, more aggressive CSC states. Research indicates that metabolic alterations can precede changes in stemness, suggesting that metabolic reprogramming is a fundamental event for cell-fate redirection [7, 54, 55].

This metabolic reprogramming acts as a “starter dough” for cancer stemness traits, a metaphor that requires clarification [7, 54, 55]. In traditional baking, the “starter dough” is not the final loaf but is the active, living engine containing the necessary culture (yeast and bacteria) to transform the entire mass of inert flour and water. Similarly, “metabostemness” proposes that a specific metabolic profile is not merely a passive byproduct of the CSC state but is its causal, transformative engine. This metabolic “starter” initiates the CSC state by providing the essential biochemical and bioenergetic foundation. For example, it produces the specific metabolites (e.g., acetyl-CoA, SAM, 2-hydroxyglutarate) that act as crucial co-factors and substrates for epigenetic enzymes (e.g., histone acetyltransferases, methyltransferases, and demethylases) [51, 56–58]. This actively “leavens” the cell, lowering epigenetic barriers and “kneading” the chromatin into a more plastic, dedifferentiated state. Once this state is achieved, the same metabolic profile is essential to maintain it, creating a self-sustaining loop where the metabolism supports the stemness-associated gene expression, and that gene expression, in turn, reinforces the unique metabolic profile [51, 56, 57]. For instance, this includes specific types of epigenetic modifications such as DNA hypomethylation (the removal of methyl groups) at the gene promoters of key metabolic enzymes. A classic example is the gene encoding Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2). In many cancers, the PKM gene promoter is hypomethylated, an epigenetic alteration that leads to the massive overexpression of the PKM2 enzyme. This specific event is a primary driver of the “Warburg effect,” or aerobic glycolysis, which is a foundational metabolic feature of metabostemness [59]. In other cases, histone modifications like acetylation or methylation can be altered at the gene loci of enzymes like Glutaminase (GLS), locking in the “glutamine addiction” phenotype characteristic of many CSCs.

Distinct metabolic profiles of CSCs

CSCs exhibit remarkable metabolic plasticity, adapting their energy production and biosynthetic pathways to the dynamic and often nutrient-poor tumor microenvironment [60]. While some studies suggest that CSCs predominantly rely on a glycolytic phenotype, others point to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as their principal energy source, indicating a context-dependent metabolic heterogeneity that allows them to thrive under diverse conditions [8, 61].

Glucose Metabolism: Many CSCs show enhanced glucose metabolism, characterized by higher glucose uptake, increased lactate production, and upregulated expression of key glycolytic enzymes such as GLUT1-4, hexokinase (HK), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) [51]. This phenomenon, akin to the Warburg effect observed in bulk tumor cells, enables CSCs to rapidly produce ATP [62]. Glucose is considered an essential nutrient for CSCs, with its deprivation leading to in vitro depletion of CSCs [18]. In hypoxic tumor niches, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) further stimulate the transcription of glycolytic enzymes, thereby reinforcing a glycolytic metabolism [51, 63]. Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS): Despite the prevalence of glycolysis, certain CSC subtypes are highly dependent on OXPHOS for their survival, growth, and drug resistance. This is particularly noted in hematologic malignancies and in cancer cells that have developed resistance to chemotherapy or targeted therapies [64]. OXPHOS activity is frequently upregulated in resistant cancer cells, often mediated by the activation of various oncogenic signaling pathways [65]. CSCs can utilize OXPHOS to adapt to fluctuating tumor microenvironments and are observed to be enriched after exposure to antitumor drugs, suggesting its role in post-treatment survival and relapse [65]. Lipid Metabolism: Lipid metabolism significantly contributes to CSC properties and drug resistance [66]. This includes enhanced lipid storage in lipid droplets (LDs), which serve as crucial energy reserves, particularly under metabolic stress conditions like glycolysis inhibition. Increased Fatty Acid Oxidation (FAO) is critical for CSC growth, survival, and drug resistance, as it efficiently produces ATP and maintains energy homeostasis [66, 67]. Key enzymes like carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 A (CPT1A) are central to this process. This reprogramming of lipid metabolism is not only a hallmark of metabostemness but also a critical determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity. The susceptibility to lipid peroxidation is dictated by the abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) within cellular membranes, which are the primary substrates for ferroptotic damage. The enzyme Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) is crucial for this process, as it activates PUFAs (e.g., arachidonic acid) for their incorporation into membrane phospholipids, thereby sensitizing cells to ferroptosis [68]. Conversely, CSCs often develop mechanisms to resist this. They can upregulate enzymes like stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), which converts saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs). This metabolic shift from PUFAs to MUFAs in membrane lipids reduces the available substrates for peroxidation, conferring significant resistance to ferroptosis [69]. Furthermore, the mevalonate (MVA) pathway, which is often hyperactive in CSCs for cholesterol synthesis [70, 71], plays a direct anti-ferroptotic role. The MVA pathway synthesizes the precursors for ubiquinone (CoQ10), a potent lipophilic antioxidant. This CoQ10 pool is then utilized by FSP1 (as discussed in the Ferroptosis section) to trap lipid radicals and halt peroxidation, directly linking the metabolic “metabostemness” signature of CSCs to a robust ferroptosis defense mechanism [72, 73]. Amino Acid Metabolism: Amino acid metabolism is crucial for maintaining CSC self-renewal and tumorigenicity [74]. Glutamine, in particular, is considered a “conditional essential amino acid” for tumor cells, with many exhibiting “glutamine addiction” due to a heightened demand for biosynthesis (e.g., nucleotides, fatty acids, other amino acids), energy generation (via TCA cycle anaplerosis), and redox homeostasis (through glutathione production) [75]. Other amino acids, such as lysine, serine, and tryptophan, also contribute to supporting CSC features and stemness [76].

The MVA pathway synthesizes the precursors for ubiquinone (CoQ10), a potent lipophilic antioxidant. This CoQ10 pool is then utilized by FSP1 (as discussed in the Ferroptosis section) to trap lipid radicals and halt peroxidation, directly linking the metabolic “metabostemness” signature of CSCs to a robust ferroptosis defense mechanism [72, 73].

Lipid Metabolism: Lipid metabolism significantly contributes to CSC properties and drug resistance [66]. This includes enhanced lipid storage in lipid droplets (LDs), which serve as crucial energy reserves, particularly under metabolic stress conditions like glycolysis inhibition. Increased Fatty Acid Oxidation (FAO) is critical for CSC growth, survival, and drug resistance, as it efficiently produces ATP and maintains energy homeostasis [66, 67]. Key enzymes like carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 A (CPT1A) are central to this process. This reprogramming of lipid metabolism is not only a hallmark of metabostemness but also a critical determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity. The susceptibility to lipid peroxidation is dictated by the abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) within cellular membranes, which are the primary substrates for ferroptotic damage. The enzyme Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) is crucial for this process, as it activates PUFAs (e.g., arachidonic acid) for their incorporation into membrane phospholipids, thereby sensitizing cells to ferroptosis [68]. Conversely, CSCs often develop mechanisms to resist this. They can upregulate enzymes like stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), which converts saturated fatty acids into monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs). This metabolic shift from PUFAs to MUFAs in membrane lipids reduces the available substrates for peroxidation, conferring significant resistance to ferroptosis [69]. This CoQ10 pool is then utilized by FSP1 (as discussed in the Ferroptosis section) to trap lipid radicals and halt peroxidation, directly linking the metabolic “metabostemness” signature of CSCs to a robust ferroptosis defense mechanism [72, 73].

Amino Acid Metabolism: Amino acid metabolism is crucial for maintaining CSC self-renewal and tumorigenicity [74]. Glutamine, in particular, is considered a “conditional essential amino acid” for tumor cells, with many exhibiting “glutamine addiction” due to a heightened demand for biosynthesis (e.g., nucleotides, fatty acids, other amino acids), energy generation (via TCA cycle anaplerosis), and redox homeostasis (through glutathione production) [75]. Other amino acids, such as lysine, serine, and tryptophan, also contribute to supporting CSC features and stemness [76].

Contribution to CSC maintenance and therapy resistance

These metabolic adaptations enable CSCs to survive and rapidly proliferate even in nutrient-poor tumor microenvironments [77]. The ability of CSCs to dynamically shift between metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis and OXPHOS, provides a remarkable metabolic flexibility that significantly contributes to their resilience against treatment [78]. Furthermore, disrupting the delicate balance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within quiescent CSCs, for example, by inhibiting glycolysis with agents like 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), may induce their differentiation into non-CSCs and lead to a loss of stemness, thereby sensitizing them to therapy [79].

The observation that CSCs can be glycolytic or OXPHOS-dependent, and that they “adapt their metabolism to microenvironmental changes by conveniently shifting energy production”, highlights metabolic plasticity as a key survival mechanism [8, 18, 80]. This dynamic metabolic state, rather than a fixed one, allows CSCs to maintain energy and biomass production even when a single metabolic pathway is inhibited. This underscores that therapeutic strategies should not rely on targeting a single metabolic pathway but rather necessitate combination therapies that target multiple, interconnected metabolic pathways or employ strategies that prevent this metabolic adaptation. Such multi-pronged approaches are crucial for effectively overcoming the inherent adaptability of CSCs.

The concept of “metabostemness” explicitly links “metabolic parameters” to the “epitranscriptional orchestration of the genetic reprograming that redirects normal and tumor cells toward less-differentiated cancer stem cell (CSC) cellular states” [7]. It further notes that “epigenetic modification of key metabolic enzymes seems to play a decisive role” [51]. This indicates a profound, bidirectional relationship where cellular metabolism influences epigenetic modifications, and these epigenetic changes, in turn, influence metabolic enzyme activity, both converging to maintain CSC stemness. This suggests that therapeutic strategies could effectively target this epigenetic-metabolic crosstalk. For instance, small molecules that subtly alter metabolism could disrupt epigenetic marks favorable for stemness, or epigenetic modifiers could be used to render CSCs metabolically vulnerable. This opens up a new class of therapeutic targets beyond direct enzyme inhibition.

CSCs exhibit specific, heightened nutrient demands, as evidenced by their “glutamine addiction” and the essentiality of glucose for their survival [81]. Their ability to adapt and proliferate even in “nutrient-poor tumor microenvironment” further emphasizes their metabolic resourcefulness [57]. This suggests that these specific nutrient dependencies represent exploitable vulnerabilities. Therapeutic approaches could involve nutrient deprivation strategies (e.g., dietary interventions, enzyme-based depletion) or the use of inhibitors targeting nutrient transporters or key metabolizing enzymes. The significant role of the tumor microenvironment in supplying these essential nutrients also highlights the necessity for therapies that modulate the TME, disrupting its supportive role for CSCs.

Small-molecule strategies for targeting cancer stem cells

The unique vulnerabilities of cancer stem cells (CSCs) through ferroptosis induction and metabolic reprogramming offer fertile ground for the development of novel small-molecule therapeutic strategies. These approaches aim to selectively eliminate CSCs, thereby overcoming drug resistance and preventing tumor relapse, which remains a significant challenge in oncology.

Ferroptosis-inducing small molecules

Small molecules designed to directly induce ferroptosis represent a promising class of anti-cancer agents, particularly against apoptosis-resistant CSCs.Direct Inducers: Erastin: This is a classical ferroptosis inducer that primarily acts by inhibiting the System Xc- antiporter [82]. This inhibition prevents the uptake of cystine, a crucial precursor for glutathione (GSH) synthesis, leading to a rapid depletion of intracellular GSH. The subsequent inactivation of GPX4, which relies on GSH for its antioxidant function, results in the lethal accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation [83]. For example, in vitro studies on breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) isolated from MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines (identified by CD44+/CD24- markers) have demonstrated that these CSCs are highly sensitive to erastin. This sensitivity was linked to a high baseline expression of iron-storage proteins, making them primed for ferroptosis upon GSH depletion [84, 85]. Erastin treatment has also been shown to cause mitochondrial dysfunction, further contributing to its cytotoxic effects [86]. However, the clinical translation of erastin itself is severely hampered by its poor pharmacokinetic (PK) properties, including low metabolic stability, poor water solubility, and rapid in vivo degradation [87]. Furthermore, erastin is a promiscuous compound, exhibiting significant off-target effects, most notably by binding to voltage-dependent anion channels (VDACs) [88]. These substantial limitations have largely relegated erastin to a foundational “tool compound” for preclinical research rather than a viable clinical candidate. RSL3: Another potent ferroptosis inducer, RSL3, directly inhibits GPX4 activity, leading to the accumulation of toxic lipid hydroperoxides [37]. Its efficacy can be enhanced by the activity of lipoxygenases, such as ALOX15, which contribute to lipid peroxidation [41]. Interestingly, RSL3 has also been observed to trigger pyroptosis, another form of inflammatory cell death, in certain cancer cell lines, suggesting potential crosstalk between different cell death pathways [89]. Similar to erastin, RSL3 is a first-generation inducer that also suffers from significant PK drawbacks, including poor bioavailability and a short half-life, which curtail its systemic efficacy [46]. Its development has been challenged by potential off-target toxicities, and its utility in vivo often requires specialized delivery systems (like nanoparticles) or direct intratumoral injection, limiting its broad therapeutic applicability [87]. Sorafenib and Sulfasalazine: These compounds are also recognized for their ferroptosis-inducing capabilities [50, 90]. Sulfasalazine, a drug initially approved for anti-inflammatory purposes, has demonstrated anti-cancer effects by inhibiting xCT activity, thereby retarding the growth of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) by inducing ferroptosis. Sorafenib, a multi-kinase inhibitor used for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), was shown to trigger ferroptosis by inhibiting System Xc- activity. This was demonstrated both in vitro in HCC cell lines (e.g., HepG2, Huh7) and in vivo in xenograft tumor models, where it led to GSH depletion and subsequent lipid peroxidation [91]. Similarly, Sulfasalazine, a drug for inflammatory conditions, has demonstrated anti-cancer effects by inhibiting xCT (System Xc-) activity, thereby retarding the growth of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) in vitro by inducing ferroptosis [77].Natural Products and Emerging Compounds: Curcumin: This natural compound has shown promise in inducing ferroptosis, particularly in CD133 + lung cancer stem cells. Curcumin achieves this by simultaneously inhibiting both the GSH-GPX4 pathway and the FSP1-CoQ10-NADH pathway, thereby suppressing the self-renewal potential of these CSCs [92]. This highlights the potential of natural compounds to target multiple anti-ferroptotic defense mechanisms. Marine Compounds: Recent research has identified specific diterpene derivatives and polyketide compounds derived from marine sources with ferroptosis-inducing properties. For example, Aucubins, extracted from peach leaf corals, can regulate GPX4 stability, leading to its degradation and ferroptosis [93, 94]. Plocabulin, a polyketide from deep-sea sponges, inhibits ACSL4 activity, which reduces the material basis for lipid peroxidation [93, 95]. These marine-derived compounds often exhibit multi-target regulatory properties, offering a unique advantage for overcoming adaptive resistance [93].

Metabolic inhibitors as ferroptosis sensitizers

Given the close association between ferroptosis and cellular metabolism [96], targeting CSC-specific metabolic vulnerabilities can effectively sensitize these resistant cells to ferroptosis induction.Glycolysis Inhibitors: 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG): As a glucose analog, 2-DG competitively inhibits glucose transport and glycolysis [97]. This disruption can re-sensitize quiescent CSCs by altering their ROS equilibrium, leading to differentiation and a loss of stemness markers, and can enhance their sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy [79]. Experimental work has confirmed this; in glioblastoma (GBM) stem-like cells (GSCs), which are heavily glycolytic, 2-DG treatment in vitro was shown to disrupt the CSC phenotype and potently sensitize the cells to temozolomide. This effect was validated in vivo, where the combination significantly reduced tumor burden in GSC-derived orthotopic xenografts [98]. Lonidamine (LND): This compound selectively inhibits aerobic glycolysis and energy metabolism in tumor cells [99].Other agents like phloretin, fasentin, and WZB117 also inhibit glucose uptake, thereby disrupting the energy metabolism crucial for CSC survival [62].OXPHOS Inhibitors: IACS-010759: This clinical-grade small molecule is a potent inhibitor of complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain [100]. Treatment with IACS-010759 robustly inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in models of brain cancer and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) that are reliant on OXPHOS. This is likely due to a combination of energy depletion and reduced aspartate production, which impairs nucleotide biosynthesis [101]. While IACS-010759 showed robust preclinical efficacy and possessed good oral bioavailability, its clinical development (e.g., NCT02882321) was ultimately discontinued. The molecule demonstrated a very narrow therapeutic index, resulting in severe on-target, off-tumor toxicities. Because OXPHOS is essential for healthy, high-energy-demand tissues (such as the gastrointestinal tract, heart, and nervous system), systemic inhibition led to dose-limiting toxicities, including severe nausea, vomiting, peripheral neuropathy, and potentially fatal lactic acidosis [102, 103]. This experience underscores the profound challenge of targeting a ubiquitous metabolic pathway like OXPHOS without inducing unacceptable systemic toxicity. Other OXPHOS inhibitors are also being explored for their ability to selectively eliminate CSCs [64]. Inhibition of desaturases, such as SCD1, has been shown to reduce cancer stemness and increase susceptibility to treatment [66, 105]. Similarly, inhibitors of ALDH1A1, a key enzyme in lipid metabolism, can decrease unsaturated lipid levels and suppress spheroid formation in CSCs [106]. For instance, targeting fatty acid oxidation (FAO), which is critical for CSC survival, has shown significant promise. Foundational studies in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) demonstrated that leukemic stem cells (LSCs) isolated from patient samples had significantly higher rates of FAO compared to their normal counterparts. OXPHOS Inhibitors: IACS-010759: This clinical-grade small molecule is a potent inhibitor of complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain [100]. Treatment with IACS-010759 robustly inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in models of brain cancer and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) that are reliant on OXPHOS. This is likely due to a combination of energy depletion and reduced aspartate production, which impairs nucleotide biosynthesis [101]. While IACS-010759 showed robust preclinical efficacy and possessed good oral bioavailability, its clinical development (e.g., NCT02882321) was ultimately discontinued. The molecule demonstrated a very narrow therapeutic index, resulting in severe on-target, off-tumor toxicities. Because OXPHOS is essential for healthy, high-energy-demand tissues (such as the gastrointestinal tract, heart, and nervous system), systemic inhibition led to dose-limiting toxicities, including severe nausea, vomiting, peripheral neuropathy, and potentially fatal lactic acidosis [102, 103]. This experience underscores the profound challenge of targeting a ubiquitous metabolic pathway like OXPHOS without inducing unacceptable systemic toxicity. Other OXPHOS inhibitors are also being explored for their ability to selectively eliminate CSCs [64]. Lipid Metabolism Inhibitors: Targeting the unique lipid metabolism of CSCs can significantly weaken their resilience and improve treatment outcomes [104]. Inhibition of desaturases, such as SCD1, has been shown to reduce cancer stemness and increase susceptibility to treatment [66, 105]. Similarly, inhibitors of ALDH1A1, a key enzyme in lipid metabolism, can decrease unsaturated lipid levels and suppress spheroid formation in CSCs [106]. For instance, targeting fatty acid oxidation (FAO), which is critical for CSC survival, has shown significant promise. Foundational studies in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) demonstrated that leukemic stem cells (LSCs) isolated from patient samples had significantly higher rates of FAO compared to their normal counterparts. Targeting this pathway with the CPT1 inhibitor etomoxir in vitro selectively induced apoptosis in LSCs while sparing normal hematopoietic stem cells [107].

Synergistic combination strategies

The combination of ferroptosis inducers and metabolic inhibitors represents a highly promising approach to enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome the formidable challenge of drug resistance [109]. This strategy leverages the inherent interconnectedness of ferroptosis and metabolism, where metabolic reprogramming can confer resistance to ferroptosis, and conversely, disrupting metabolism can sensitize cells to ferroptosis [96]. Beyond metabolic combinations, a profoundly important synergy exists between ferroptosis induction and anti-tumor immunity. Unlike apoptotic cell death, which is often immunologically “silent,” ferroptosis is an immunogenic form of cell death (ICD). Ferroptotic cancer cells release a specific profile of Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs), including ATP and HMGB1, as well as lipid-based mediators, which can act as a potent “find me” signal for the immune system. This process promotes the maturation and recruitment of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as dendritic cells, which then prime an adaptive, CD8 + T cell-mediated anti-tumor response [29].

This creates a powerful, bidirectional therapeutic opportunity. First, by inducing ferroptosis in CSCs—which are notoriously “cold” and immune-evasive [28]—it may be possible to re-engage the immune system and transform the tumor microenvironment (TME) from “cold” to “hot.” Second, this interaction is reciprocal: activated CD8 + T cells themselves are potent inducers of tumor cell ferroptosis. Upon recognizing a target cell, T cells release interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), which subsequently downregulates the expression of the cystine transporter SLC7A11 (System Xc-). This depletion of the cell’s primary antioxidant machinery sensitizes the cancer cell, pushing it below the ferroptotic threshold [110]. This synergistic loop—where a ferroptosis inducer initiates cell death, which recruits T cells that, in turn, enhance ferroptosis—provides a strong rationale for combining ferroptosis inducers with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to overcome CSC-mediated immune evasion and resistance.

The close relationship between ferroptosis and cellular metabolism implies that targeting one pathway can sensitize cancer cells to the other [111]. This suggests that combination therapies are not merely additive but can achieve synergistic effects, effectively overcoming adaptive resistance by hitting multiple, interdependent survival pathways. This approach is particularly effective against highly adaptable CSCs, which can otherwise compensate for single-pathway inhibition.

CSCs often develop multi-drug resistance (MDR) through mechanisms that include increased drug efflux and altered metabolic states [112]. Inducing ferroptosis and disrupting metabostemness offers a novel strategy to bypass these conventional resistance pathways, providing a viable option for treating refractory tumors. The observed ability of ferroptosis inducers to reverse acquired resistance to various anticancer agents underscores the significant advantage of this combined approach [45, 113].

While ferroptosis inducers can have broad cytotoxic effects, combining them with inhibitors of CSC-specific metabolic pathways (e.g., those involved in glutamine addiction or unique lipid metabolism enzymes) could significantly enhance selectivity and reduce off-target toxicity. This approach aligns with the principles of precision oncology by tailoring treatment to the unique metabolic fingerprint of CSCs, thereby maximizing therapeutic benefit while minimizing harm to healthy tissues.

Examples of such synergistic strategies include the combination of sorafenib with elesclomol-Cu to simultaneously induce ferroptosis and cuproptosis, exploiting the interconnectedness of iron and copper metabolism [42]. Another innovative approach involves dual-mode strategies, such as the use of Fe-Cu-SS metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) loaded with BAY876. This system simultaneously induces ferroptosis by targeting lipid peroxidation and triggers disulfidptosis by restricting glucose uptake, leading to NADPH depletion [114]. This dual targeting effectively counteracts resistance mechanisms, such as the adaptive upregulation of SLC7A11 expression observed during ferroptosis induction [114]. Furthermore, the induction of ferroptosis has been shown to be more effective in eliminating drug-resistant cancer cells during epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), suggesting that targeting the ferroptosis pathway can directly overcome drug resistance associated with this process [115]. Challenges in clinical translation and future directions

Despite the immense promise of targeting cancer stem cells (CSCs) through ferroptosis and metabostemness, significant challenges must be addressed to translate these preclinical findings into effective clinical therapies.

Challenges

Specificity and Systemic Toxicity: A primary concern for any small-molecule therapeutic is its specificity. Many ferroptosis inducers and metabolic inhibitors can have off-target effects, leading to toxicity in normal, healthy cells [116]. Achieving selective targeting of CSCs while sparing healthy tissues remains a significant hurdle, as some metabolic pathways are also essential for normal cell function. Translational Hurdles in Pediatric Oncology: While these strategies hold promise, their translation to pediatric malignancies like neuroblastoma (NB) and medulloblastoma (MB) requires navigating a distinct set of challenges. A primary concern is that these cancers are often “developmental” in origin, meaning their CSCs hijack normal developmental pathways. This raises the significant risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity in healthy, developing tissues—a critical consideration largely absent in adult oncology [117]. However, these unique developmental origins also create specific therapeutic opportunities. For instance, high-risk, MYCN-amplified NB exhibits a profound metabolic reprogramming, including a “glutamine addiction,” making its CSCs uniquely vulnerable to glutamine metabolism inhibitors (a clear “metabostemness” target) [118]. Similarly, both NB and specific subtypes of MB (e.g., Group 3) have shown preclinical sensitivity to ferroptosis induction, with some studies suggesting ferroptosis inducers can potentiate standard-of-care chemotherapy like cisplatin [119]. The primary challenges, therefore, involve not only data rarity [120, 121] but also overcoming the significant CSC plasticity (e.g., the ADRN-MES switch) [23] and the rapid emergence of acquired resistance to ferroptosis. Tumor Heterogeneity and Plasticity: The inherent heterogeneity within tumors, coupled with the remarkable phenotypic plasticity of CSCs, poses a formidable challenge. CSCs are not static entities; they can adapt and switch metabolic states or activate alternative survival pathways, leading to acquired resistance to targeted therapies [81, 122]. This dynamic adaptability means that a single therapeutic approach may only offer transient efficacy before resistance emerges. Drug Delivery and Pharmacokinetics: Many existing small-molecule ferroptosis inducers exhibit poor water solubility, instability, and low targeting ability, which severely limit their clinical application. Ensuring effective delivery to the tumor site, and specifically to the relatively rare CSC population, remains a complex pharmacokinetic challenge [35, 50, 123]. Inefficient delivery can lead to suboptimal drug concentrations at the target site and increased systemic exposure. Lack of Clinical Validation and Standardization: A substantial portion of promising strategies in this field are currently in early research phases, lacking robust clinical validation and standardized protocols for their application [124]. This is particularly pronounced in rare cancers like neuroblastoma, where the rarity of the disease and limitations in available patient data hinder the training of predictive models and the conduct of large-scale clinical trials [120, 121]. Data Privacy, Interpretability, and Algorithmic Bias in AI-driven Approaches: While artificial intelligence (AI) holds immense promise for integrating complex biological data, its application in clinical settings faces hurdles. The use of vast amounts of sensitive patient data raises significant concerns regarding privacy and security [125, 126]. Furthermore, many advanced AI models operate as “black boxes,” producing predictions without offering clear insights into their decision-making processes. This lack of interpretability is a barrier to clinical adoption, as clinicians require an understanding of how a system arrives at its conclusions, especially in high-stakes decisions like cancer diagnosis and treatment [127]. Ensuring generalizability and ethical implementation across diverse patient populations, free from algorithmic bias, is also crucial [125].

The transition from promising preclinical findings to established clinical utility requires rigorous validation and standardization. This points to the necessity of well-designed clinical trials that not only demonstrate analytical or clinical validity but also unequivocally show an improvement in patient outcomes [124, 138]. Addressing practical issues such as drug solubility, stability, and targeted delivery capabilities is paramount for the successful translation of these novel strategies into routine clinical practice.

Tumor heterogeneity and the inherent plasticity of CSCs mean that single-target therapeutic approaches are highly susceptible to the emergence of resistance. The comprehensive integration of multi-omics data with advanced AI algorithms provides a holistic view of the tumor’s dynamic biological landscape. This allows for the development of adaptive and personalized treatment strategies that can anticipate and effectively counteract resistance mechanisms. This paradigm shift moves beyond static treatment protocols towards dynamic, data-driven interventions that can continuously adjust to the evolving nature of the tumor.

Conclusions

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a formidable barrier to effective cancer eradication, driving therapeutic resistance, tumor recurrence, and metastasis due to their self-renewal capacity, phenotypic plasticity, and unique survival mechanisms. The persistence of these highly resistant cells necessitates innovative therapeutic approaches that move beyond conventional cytotoxic strategies.

This review highlights the burgeoning potential of two interconnected paradigms: ferroptosis induction and the targeting of metabostemness. Ferroptosis offers a compelling avenue to overcome the common apoptosis resistance in cancer by inducing iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, thereby providing an alternative, non-apoptotic cell death pathway. Simultaneously, the concept of metabostemness underscores the distinct metabolic reprogramming of CSCs—across glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism—which fundamentally underpins their stemness, survival, and resistance.

Emerging small-molecule strategies, including direct ferroptosis inducers like erastin and RSL3, as well as natural compounds such as curcumin and marine-derived agents, demonstrate the feasibility of exploiting these vulnerabilities. Crucially, the synergistic potential of combining ferroptosis-inducing agents with metabolic inhibitors (e.g., glycolysis inhibitors like 2-DG, OXPHOS inhibitors like IACS-010759, or glutamine metabolism inhibitors like CB-839) is becoming increasingly evident. This combinatorial approach leverages the interconnectedness of these pathways, offering a powerful means to overcome CSC-mediated multi-drug resistance by simultaneously disrupting multiple, interdependent survival mechanisms.

Despite these promising advances, significant challenges remain in clinical translation, including achieving selective targeting, mitigating systemic toxicity, navigating tumor heterogeneity and CSC plasticity, and establishing robust clinical validation. Future directions must therefore focus on developing advanced drug delivery systems, particularly nanotechnology-based targeted nanocarriers, to enhance drug specificity and efficacy. Furthermore, the integration of comprehensive multi-omics data with advanced artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms is paramount. This will enable a deeper, more dynamic understanding of CSC biology, facilitating the identification of novel biomarkers, predictive models, and the rational design of adaptive, personalized treatment strategies. By bridging the translational gap through rigorous validation and collaborative efforts, the strategic targeting of CSCs through ferroptosis and metabostemness holds immense promise to revolutionize precision oncology and significantly improve long-term outcomes for cancer patients.
