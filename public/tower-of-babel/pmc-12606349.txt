Learning from nature: phytochemical strategies to protect against UV-B damage
Authors: Mustafa Bulut, Takayuki Tohge, Wei Chen, Jie Luo, Zhirong Wang, Xu Huang, Meiliang Zhou, Alisdair R Fernie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe colonization of terrestrial ecosystems exposed plants to intensified solar radiation, particularly that of UV-B (280–315 nm), which induces DNA, RNA and protein damage. While historical ozone depletion exacerbated UV-B stress, recent stabilization efforts have mitigated its effects. However, independent fluctuations in UV-B levels continue to challenge crop performance, particularly in equatorial regions. Plants have evolved diverse UV-B-protective strategies, including producing specialized metabolites such as flavonoids, phenylpropanoids and ascorbate alongside lineage-specific compounds like mycosporine-like amino acids. These metabolites act as antioxidants, UV-B absorbers and stress mitigators, with their diversity driven by gene duplication and environmental adaptation. In keeping with this, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified key genetic loci, such as OsUVR8 and OsMYB44, regulating UV-B tolerance through enhanced flavonol glycoside and tryptamine derivatives biosynthesis. These combined data suggest that we should be able to fortify plants’ tolerance to UV-B by future breeding strategies. Advances in spatially resolved metabolomics and machine learning hold promise for identifying novel UV-B-protective compounds and facilitating precise metabolic engineering. In addition, modifications to cell wall composition, including lignin thickening and embedding UV-B-absorbing phenylpropanoids, offer another venue for protective mechanisms. Recent discoveries of natural variation in UV-B protectants across species like rice, barley and maize highlight the potential for breeding UV-resilient crops. Such innovations will not only bolster UV-B tolerance but also enhance crop nutritional value, as many UV-B-protective compounds also serve as essential nutrients as well as harbor antioxidant properties. This comprehensive understanding of UV-B responses, from molecular signaling to phytochemical strategies and structural adaptations, underscores the potential for leveraging genetic engineering and natural variation to mitigate UV-B-induced stress.Subject terms: Plant sciences, Systems biology, Plant stress responses

Introduction

With the conquest of terrestrial ecosystems, plants were exposed to higher irradiation and this persisted with the colonization of almost all ecosystems on the planet1,2. While extant plants are locally adapted to their habitats, ongoing climate change is having a detrimental impact on many ecosystems by rapid fluctuations resulting in considerable abiotic stress—a fact that also results in considerable loss in crop yields. Several studies have been performed to investigate crop security with respect to stress under climate change scenarios3,4. Among these stresses, UV-B stress is becoming more prevalent. UV-B, defined as the light portion with a wavelength of 280–315 nm, results in various hazardous reactions in plants, including protein, DNA, and RNA damage with the latter potentially resulting in mutagenesis5–7. Considering the biologically effective proportion, UV-BBE radiation for plants is concentrated within the 290–310 nm wavelength range, with peak effectiveness near 290–295 nm due to strong absorption by DNA and other cellular targets. However, wavelengths below ~295 nm are largely absorbed by stratospheric ozone, making 295–305 nm the most relevant range at the Earth’s surface. According to the biological weighting functions developed by Caldwell8 and refined by Flint and Caldwell9 wavelengths in this range contribute disproportionately to UV-induced effects such as DNA damage, photosynthesis inhibition, and the induction of UV-protective specialized metabolites.

Plants in natural environments seldom experience continuous UV-B fluxes of the magnitude used in many controlled experiments. For instance, solar UV-B in the High Andes may reach ~ 3.13 W m−2 (broadband) and ~ 0.144 W m−2 in the short-wavelength UV-B fraction under clear, high-altitude conditions during spring10–12. In contrast, Perilla frutescens plants grown under artificial UV-B lighting have been exposed to intensities of ~ 4–6 W m−2 for much of the day (16 h) or continuously, yielding UV-B treatments that match or exceed extreme natural peaks13. Moreover, some seedling studies apply lower intensities (0.05–0.2 W m−2) but still result in integrated daily UV-B energies (≈ 0.5–2.2 kJ m−2 day−1) that can be comparable to or higher than what many plants receive under non-ideal natural conditions (cloud cover, atmospheric attenuation) in mid-latitudes10,12,13. These differences in both instantaneous flux and duration/diurnal pattern likely influence the types of metabolic, protective, and stress responses observed. Thus, interpreting results from high-UV-B or continuous UV-B treatments requires caution when extrapolating to field or ecological settings. The UV-B RESISTANCE 8 (UVR8) photoreceptor, as discussed by Jenkins14, dissociates under UV-B into monomers, mediating specific photomorphogenic responses. However, it has been reported that UV-B radiation also has positive effects. There is now increasing evidence that appropriate UV-B exposure can enhance crop disease resistance and be utilized to manage pathogen infections15,16. It has been predicted that by 2100, UV radiation will have increased in the tropics but decreased in the polar latitudes17,18. In addition, the problem of enhanced UV-B radiation was worsening globally due to human activity causing the progressive thinning of the stratospheric ozone layer19,20, though more recent studies suggest local ozone stabilization and even recovery18,21.

Indeed, the levels of solar UV-B radiation have been monitored extensively in the last decades, using both ground-based and space-borne instrumentation such as the Brewer spectrophotometer or the Total Ozone Mapping Spectrometer, respectively. Ground-based monitoring stations are not by themselves able to provide coverage of global-scale long-term trends in UV-B radiation, but satellite-based measurements, although influenced by scattered reflections from the atmosphere and earth surface, can achieve this22. Since the launch of the AURA satellite in 2004, the so-called Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI) has become a major source of UV-B information, with validation with ground reference data revealing that it provides reliable measurements at relatively high spatial and temporal resolutions23,24. To improve on this Beckmann et al.25 developed a global set of surface UV-B radiation (glUV) data with a spatial resolution of 15 arc minutes based on daily measurements of remotely sensed UV-B levels collected by the Aura-OMI satellite mission in the period 2004–2013 (http://www.ufz.de/gluv). The mean annual UV-B radiation across this period is depicted in Fig. 1A. Perusal of this data reveals that the strongest UV-B levels are observed across the equatorial plane. By contrast, consolidating these findings with the study of Unger et al.26 highlights that an anthropocentric-driven increase in surface ozone results in global warming by reducing CO2 assimilation. Furthermore, Unger and co-workers utilized a global earth system model to compare the loss of gross primary production (GPP) by simulating the reduction of ozone emissions by 50%, resulting in the identification of major GPP losses in Eastern China, Eastern United States, and Europe (Fig. 1B, from the source article). When considered in the context of global cereal production (Fig. 1C), crops which provide the vast majority of our food and feed, the potential threat to food security is considerable. This suggests that given the Montreal protocol clearly had an excellent effect on ozone levels27 other ozone-independent increases in UV-B levels are likely behind the predicted changes. Thus, despite the fact that UV-B has been demonstrated to be beneficial to net plant photosynthesis28, the fluctuation in its levels renders UV-B stress a major factor in influencing future plant performance. Indeed, despite a degree of uncertainty in models of climate sensitivity29, the fact that either high or low levels of UV-B have dramatic effects on crop performance, suggests that precision cultivation of crops depending on their geographical context is crucial. In light of this, various clades of the green lineage have evolved distinct strategies to cope with this stress, including the production of different UV-B protectant phytochemicals with the capacity of absorbing in the UV-B wavelength range. These consist mainly of compounds exhibiting ring-like structures, such as ascorbate30,31, flavanol glycoside and anthocyanins 32–34 and other sinapoyl-esters5,35,36. In addition to these, plants accumulate pollen-specific UV-B absorbing phenylpropanoids such as phenolamides37–39 and flavonol-3-O-sophorosides38,40,41, which are postulated to have evolutionary significance and play important roles in plant fitness38. However, whilst most of these compounds are ubiquitous in plants, recent studies have revealed a number of more phylogenetically restricted phytochemical sunscreen,s while microalgae have evolved protection via the accumulation of mycosporine-like amino acids42.

Considering the geographic distribution—and thereby diverging ecosystems—of plant species, ecotypes within a species have been found to be adapted to their ecosystems in order to ensure robustness in plant fitness43. Early studies evaluated UV responses in Arabidopsis accessions, focusing on growth rate in one set of seven accessions and biomass in another set of seven accessions and their F1 hybrids44. A subsequent study revealed significant variation in the UV susceptibility of photosystem II among 224 Arabidopsis accessions45. Notably, this large-scale analysis uncovered a negative correlation between the latitude of origin and the rate at which UV defenses were activated. However, while an assessment of 345 Arabidopsis accessions under UV-B stress revealed substantial phenotypic variation and sensitivity, no significant association was observed between this variation and latitude, despite its role as a major geographic UV gradient46. It is known that physiological robustness in plant fitness can arise from plasticity in other traits47, for example, those underlying metabolic adjustments to regulate oxidative homeostasis48. To this end, recent discoveries focused on the natural variation of phytoprotective compounds provide insight into the convergent evolution of phytochemical tolerance to UV-B via both constitutive and inducible mechanisms in species including the model plant Arabidopsis49,50, rice51,52, barley53, wheat54, soybean55, and maize56. In the following sections, we will focus on major advances in our understanding of the biosynthesis of UV-B protective compounds across the green lineage. We will highlight innovations that we believe were important to the thriving of terrestrial plants as well as those that will be of importance for securing future crop yields in the face of future climate challenges.

UV-B responses in the green lineage

UV-B radiation directly damages the structure of plant DNA by disrupting nucleotide bases and the polynucleotide backbone. Plants possess intrinsic DNA repair mechanisms such as base excision repair and photoreactivation repair, but their repair capacity is limited by stress intensity. When radiation doses exceed a critical threshold, repair efficiency declines significantly, ultimately leading to programmed cell death. UV-B radiation has profound effects on photomorphogenic responses in plants57. These responses are mediated by UVR8, a UV-B-specific photoreceptor, occurring as an inactive homodimer in the cytoplasm. Upon UV-B exposure, UVR8 monomerizes and translocates to the nucleus. The monomeric UVR8 interacts with the CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1), inhibiting E3 ubiquitin ligase activity to stabilize the transcription factor ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), thereby promoting photomorphogenesis and specialized metabolite accumulation (ref. 58, Fig. 2B). UVR8 can also bind to the repressor of HY5 transcription, transcription factor WRKY DNA-BINDING PROTEIN 36 (WRKY36), resulting in the transcription of HY5 (ref. 59, Fig. 2B). Monomeric UVR8 also interacts and represses BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1) and BES1-INTERACTING-MYC-LIKE1 (BIM1) transcription factors, relieving the inhibition on the MYB gene and promoting the synthesis of photoprotective substances such as flavonoids and anthocyanins (refs. 60,61, Fig. 2B). Recent studies have identified a phosphorylation site on UVR8 at Serine 402, which facilitates binding to REPRESSOR OF UV-B PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS (RUP) proteins that negatively regulate UVR8 activity by accelerating UVR8 dimerization to terminate signal transduction and establish a dynamic balance. This phosphorylation site has also been implicated in modulating UVR8 activity and promoting the biosynthesis of phenylpropanoid-derived metabolites62. However, it was recently also demonstrated that naringenin chalcone can stabilize the active monomeric UVR8 photoreceptor independent of UV-B, thereby revealing how light-induced metabolites can modulate photoreceptor-mediated development beyond the canonical UV-B response63. Beyond UVR8, other regulatory factors involved in UV-B signal perception have been identified. For instance, UVR2, a photolyase, protects against UV-B-induced mutagenesis in the germline64. However, a detailed discussion of transcriptional signal transduction cascades is beyond the scope of this review. Readers are directed to other more comprehensive reviews on this topic57,65.

Evolution of metabolic diversity

Plants produce a vast array of specialized metabolites, including flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenoids. Several theories have been proposed to explain this remarkable diversity. One widely accepted hypothesis suggests that frequent gene duplications have driven the emergence of metabolic diversity by enabling sub-/neofunctionalization. This process allows enzymes to accept novel substrate combinations, catalyzing the production of new compounds and diversifying biosynthetic pathways (reviewed in refs. 66,67). Consequently, plants have evolved the ability to produce lineage-specific specialized metabolites. Another theory posits that specialized metabolite biosynthetic pathways originated and diversified as components of redox chemistry, primarily for scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS). According to this model, the presence of specific functional groups (e.g., accessible unsubstituted hydroxyls, particularly ortho-hydroxyls) is more critical than the overall molecular structure, allowing for significant structural diversification68. These theories offer insights into the structural diversity of pigments and flavonoids, some of which are produced in response to UV-B exposure in Arabidopsis50. The production of these compounds varies with growth conditions and their diverse specialized modifications likely enhance their functionality under specific stress conditions69. Regardless of the nature of UV phytoprotectants, ROS generation remains a central factor in plant stress responses.

UV-B radiation disrupts stress tolerance mechanisms and antioxidant systems by altering the physiological balance of ROS, leading to oxidative stress70. Without protective mechanisms, UV-B exposure at levels found in sunlight can cause severe DNA damage, cellular stress, and even cell or plant death. This disruption may have driven the evolution of flavonoids and the phenylpropanoid pathway as inducible stress tolerance mechanisms during the colonization of land71,72. Unlike aquatic environments, where water absorbs UV-B, land plants face greater exposure, making UV-B a significant stress factor. Notably, Charophyceae, a group of submerged algae, lack UV-B tolerance compounds, likely rather rely on water to absorb UV-B73. While some aquatic species produce UV-protective compounds such as mycosporine-like amino acids, including guadsol74, here we restrict discussion to chemical compounds predominant in terrestrial plants. To mitigate UV-B damage, most land plants produce among others phenylpropanoids, such as flavonoids, which are crucial for UV-B tolerance in liverworts and angiosperms33,75,76.

Highlighting of selected UV-B absorbing molecules

As highlighted, plants have evolved several mechanisms to cope with UV-B stress, including the production of a diverse array of chemical structures that absorb UV-B radiation (Supplementary Table 1). One such strategy involves the mitigation of UV-B-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation through the synthesis of potent antioxidants like ascorbate and other highly hydroxylated compounds capable of undergoing multiple oxidation cycles. Additionally, some compounds exhibit dual functions, for example, ascorbate not only serves as an antioxidant but also absorbs UV-B light77.

Other vitamins, such as retinol and carotenoids ((pro-)vitamin A), cholecalciferol and 7-dehydrocholesterol ((pro-)vitamin D3), and tocopherols (vitamin E), also contribute to UV-B resilience. Early studies revealed that short-term, intense UV-B exposure decreases α-tocopherol levels, whereas long-term UV acclimation increases tocopherol levels78. Synthesized in the inner envelope of chloroplasts, α-tocopherol accumulates in all chloroplast membranes and plays a critical role in deactivating ROS generated during photosynthesis and scavenging lipid peroxyl radicals. This action prevents lipid peroxidation in thylakoid membranes, thereby safeguarding the photosynthetic machinery. Similarly, vitamin D3, the so-called sunshine vitamin, and its provitamin forms absorb UV-B radiation, resulting in a range of photoproducts with diverse biological properties79. Retinyl esters, the storage form of vitamin A, also absorb UV radiation. For example, retinyl palmitate has been shown to prevent in vitro UV-induced fluorescence and photobleaching in fluorescent markers80.

Phenolic compounds derived from the shikimate pathway are notorious for their UV absorption properties. The sheer diversity of downstream products varies across plant species, leading to the synthesis of pigments such as tyrosine- and phenylalanine-derived betalains and phenylpropanoids, both of which have UV absorption capacities. The diversity and UV absorption rates of these compounds are substantial (see Supplementary Table 1). For example, hydroxycinnamic acid amides (HCAAs) are a class of specialized plant metabolites formed by the conjugation of hydroxycinnamic acids (e.g., ferulic, caffeic, and p-coumaric acids) with (poly)amines by BAHD acytransferases, a protein family named after the first four characterized enzymes, benzylalcohol O-acetyltransferase, anthocyanin O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase, anthranilate N-hydroxycinnamoyl/benzoyltransferase, and deacetylvindoline 4-O-acetyltransferase81,82. These compounds accumulate in the epidermal tissues of plants and contribute significantly to the attenuation of UV-B radiation, particularly within the biologically effective range (UV-BBE) of 290–315 nm. HCAAs absorb strongly in the UV-B spectrum due to their conjugated aromatic ring systems, functioning as effective sunscreens that protect underlying photosynthetic tissues from UV-induced damage83,84. Their biosynthesis is often upregulated in response to UV-B exposure via signaling pathways involving UVR8 and downstream transcriptional regulators such as HY5, which coordinate the expression of genes involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism31,85. In addition to UV protection, HCAAs play roles in oxidative stress mitigation and pathogen defense, rendering them multifunctional components of the plant’s adaptive response to UV-B stress86,87. In eudicotyledons, trihydroxycinnamoyl-spermidine accumulation in the pollen coat represents an evolutionarily conserved trait, but in the Asteraceae, this is replaced by tetrahydroxycinnamoyl-spermine, synthesized via neofunctionalized BAHD enzymes spermidine hydroxycinnamoyl transferase-like proteins 1 and 2 (CiSHT1 and CiSHT2) in chicory (Cichorium intybus)88,89. Phylogenetic, expression, and in vitro biochemical analyses confirmed that CiSHT2 primarily initiates N-coumaroylation of spermine and spermidine, while CiSHT1 completes formation of fully substituted tetracoumaroyl-spermine88. This metabolic innovation highlights a recent subfunctionalization within the SHT clade that may have contributed to the remarkable diversification and success of the Asteraceae lineage82,88,89. In combination with flavonoids, HCAAs enable complementary wavelength coverage of the UV spectrum leading synergistically improved UV resilience in plants90. Therefore, the accumulation of HCAAs in epidermal tissues represents a crucial photoprotective strategy that enhances plant survival under elevated UV-B conditions. While a plethora of aromatic compounds are promiscuous targets of several natural genetic variation studies, we omit to highlight them as these will be discussed in detail in the following sections. Among the shikimate pathway-derived aromatic compounds, tryptophan serves as a precursor for melatonin, known for its sunlight-absorbing properties91. It is capable of scavenge reactive oxygen species, promote DNA repair by stimulating repair enzymes, and affect expression of UV-B signal transduction pathway genes under UV-B stress in plant cells92–94. Collectively, the wide array of chemical structures with UV-B absorption properties highlights the potential to enhance plant resilience to UV-B radiation. These compounds also represent promising targets for commercial applications in agriculture and beyond.

Studies on plant natural variation in the levels of UV-B protectants

In the last couple of decades, research based on natural accessions exhibiting trait variation within a species has become an established route to identifying the genetic and sometimes also the metabolic basis for such variation95,96. With such studies now being commonplace in both model and crop species97,98. As mentioned above, early studies in Arabidopsis reported UV-B protective functions of ascorbate and various phenylpropanoids. For example, natural variation studies in Arabidopsis performed under high-light conditions elucidated a novel component, namely a PAS/LOV PROTEIN, in negatively regulating ascorbate levels by potential interaction with VITAMIN C DEFECTIVE249. Similarly, the Pna-10 accession of Arabidopsis was characterized to have abnormal floral development, pollination, and seed production phenotypes99. This accession was characterized to be a naturally occurring mutation of the SNG1 gene encoding a key gene in the biosynthesis of sinapoyl-malate (SM)100. Furthermore, the REDUCED EPIDERMAL FLOURESENCE (REF) and BRIGHT TRICHOMES 1 (BRT1) were identified yielding in reduced SM and overall sinapate esters101. Sinapoyl-esters, particularly SM, are phenolic compounds that play a critical role in protecting plants from UV radiation83. These esters are synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway and belong to the class of hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives102. In members of the Brassicaceae family, including Arabidopsis thaliana, SM is the predominant UV-absorbing compound in the adaxial epidermis of leaves103. From an evolutionary perspective, sinapoyl-esters appear to be a relatively recent innovation restricted primarily to the Brassicaceae lineage102,104. Indeed, comparative genomic and metabolic studies suggest that gene duplication and neofunctionalization of enzymes such as ferulate 5-hydroxylase (FAH1) and UDP-glucose:sinapate glucosyltransferase (UGT84A2) facilitated the relatively recent emergence of sinapate ester biosynthesis41,101,102. This specialization likely conferred a selective advantage in open and high-UV habitats, contributing to the ecological success of Brassicaceae species103,104.

Turning to crop species, studies of tomato transgenics containing up or downregulated levels of the hydoxycinnamate chlorogenic acid revealed that it was strongly related to UV-B tolerance105 with broad variance in the levels of this metabolite also noted across wild tomato species106 and in the S. pennellii introgression lines107. The latter of which additionally harbored quantitative trait loci for ascorbate108,109 and flavonoids107. Moreover, an early study directly assessing variability in polyphenols and UV-B tolerance reported a link between these features across 34 diverse rice cultivars without providing any mechanistic insight into the linkage110, while a study in grape suggested possible roles of terpenes and carotenoids based on similar observations111. In recent years, the ever-increasing adoption of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) has expanded our understanding of the genetic architecture of these compounds to Arabidopsis as well as a wide range of crops, including rice51,52, barley53, wheat54, soybean55, and maize56. The first of these studies was focused on a relatively small collection of 64 Arabidopsis accessions50. In this study, a total of 68 specialized metabolites, including 24 flavonoids, a handful of polyamine conjugates, and 18 unknown peaks subsequently identified as novel phenylacylated-flavonol glycoside and collectively called saiginols50, were studied. Intriguingly, the accessions deriving from high irradiance habitats (i.e., latitudes of between 16 and 43° N or altitudes in excess of 1000 m) produced saignols. Indeed, the additional phenylacylation rendered these compounds considerably more competent at UV-B absorption than their undecorated precursors (Fig. 2C). In subsequent UV-B treatment experiments, saiginol-producing lines showed higher silique and seed production. Structural analysis of the saiginols revealed that their high efficiency of UV-B absorbance likely derives from the bridge piled structure between the aromatic ring A and the additional phenylacylated phenolic ring. Surprisingly, a literature survey revealed that phenylacetylation, albeit at a different position, had previously been linked to UV-B tolerance in Scots Pine112. In follow-up studies, a survey of the KnapSac database113 revealed that phenylacylation whilst being relatively rare, occurs in a range of flavonoids that are decorated with hydroxycinnamoyl units, namely cinnamoyl, 4-coumaroyl, caffeoyl, feruloyl, and sinapoyl moieties and is widely distributed in the plant kingdom. Indeed currently, more than 400 phenylacylated flavonoids have been reported113. In a subsequent study, Jing et al.114, comprehensively analyzed the responses to UV-B radiation in four Brassicaceae species namely Arabidopsis thaliana, Brassica napus, Brassica oleracea, and Brassica rapa. The response to UV-B radiation was commonly characterized by increased production of functional precursors of specialized metabolites with UV-B shielding properties, indicating a targeted effort of plant metabolism to provide increased protection. The analysis of specialized metabolites and transcripts revealed a specific activation of the phenylpropanoid-acetate pathway115, leading to the production of specific classes of flavonoids and a cross-species increase in phenylacylated-flavonoid glucosides with sinapoyl glycoside decorations.

Research has additionally been carried out in more diverse species with notable studies carried out on rice, quinke, and Tartary buckwheat to date. In the first of these studies, which was contemporary to the discovery of the saiginols, the laboratory of Jie Luo studied the natural variation of flavones in rice and across a total of 14 diverse species52. In doing so, they revealed that the natural variation of rice flavones is mainly determined by OsUGT706D1 (flavone 7-O-glucosyltransferase) and OsUGT707A2 (flavone 5-O-glucosyltransferase) and that the product of these enzymes, namely glycosylated apigenin, kaempferol, and tricin, displays enhanced properties of UV-B absorbance. Moreover, UV-B exposure and transgenic evaluation demonstrate that their allelic variation contributes to UV-B tolerance in nature. Finally, the biochemical characterization of over 40 flavonoid UGTs reveals their differential evolution in angiosperms. In a more recent study, comprehensive metabolic profiling of leaves from 160 diverse rice accessions was performed under both UV-B and normal light conditions using a widely targeted metabolomics approach116. The results revealed substantial differences in metabolite accumulation between the two major rice subspecies, indica and japonica, especially after UV-B treatment, implying the possible role and mechanism of metabolome changes in subspecies differentiation and the stress response. Metabolite-based genome-wide association study (mGWAS), revealed 3307 distinct loci under UV-B stress. Moreover, functional validation of candidate genes shows that OsMYB44 regulates tryptamine accumulation to mediate UV-B tolerance, while OsUVR8 interacts with OsMYB110 to promote flavonoid accumulation and UV-B tolerance in a coordinated manner. OsUVR8 plays a crucial role in enhancing UV-B stress tolerance by promoting flavonoid accumulation. Specifically, natural variants within the OsUVR8 locus, particularly those in group A, were associated with increased UV-B resistance in rice116. This result is consistent with earlier reports linking natural variation in rice flavone accumulation to UV tolerance52. In addition to these broad studies, the receptor-like kinase OsRLCK160, acting in concert with the bZIP transcription factor contributes to both flavonoid accumulation and UV-B tolerance in rice117, while the UV-B responsive glycosyltransferase, OsUGT706C2, also modulates flavonoid metabolism in rice118. It will be interesting in the future to additionally evaluate the UV-B tolerance of pigmented rice collections, which have considerably higher flavonoid content119. Qingke (Tibetan hulless barley) is a particularly interesting plant to study with regard to UV-B stress, given that it has long been cultivated and exposed to strong, long-term UV-B radiation on the high-altitude Tibetan Plateau. Zhang and co-workers reported a comprehensive metabolite profiling and mGWAS using 196 diverse qingke and barley accessions. This study revealed both constitutive and induced accumulation and common genetic regulation, of metabolites from different branches of the phenylpropanoid pathway that are involved in UV-B protection, including tryptamine, sinapate glycoside, cyanidin glycoside, apigenin glycoside, and derivatives53. In total 90 significant mGWAS loci for these metabolites were identified in barley-qingke differentiation regions and a number of high-level metabolite trait alleles were found to be significantly enriched in qingke, suggesting co-selection of various phenylpropanoids. Key determinants controlling natural variation of phenylpropanoid content, included three novel proteins, a flavone C-pentosyltransferase, a tyramine hydroxycinnamoyl acyltransferase, and a MYB transcription factor. Intriguingly, this study, furthermore, demonstrated co-selection of both constitutive and induced phenylpropanoids for UV-B protection in Qingke. In a subsequent study, Jiang and co-workers determined the levels of total polyphenols, total flavonoids, total triterpenes, total polysaccharides, proanthocyanidins, and chlorophyll were determined and antioxidants capacity was evaluated by OH• and 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) scavenging ability in quince in response to exposure to short-term UV radiation120. Using a mathematical model, they were able to deduce that the major functional component with regard to antioxidant ability varied depending on both the tissue studied and the length of UV-B exposure highlighting that generating UV-tolerant plants will likely require multi-genic intervention. Similar to qinke, Tartary buckwheat also originated in the Himalayan region prior to spreading dramatically in parallel to the Mongol conquests a fact that can be confirmed by population genomics of a worldwide panel of Tartary buckwheat accessions121. Like other species, buckwheat utilizes phenlypropanoids in particular rutin as a molecular shield against UV-B. Moreover, other substances with aromatic rings of six carbon atoms have a similar function, including the buckwheat specific metabolite fagopyrin. These compounds are formed in the leaves and flowers of common and Tartary buckwheat at similar levels, yet the level of rutin in Tartary buckwheat is considerably higher122. Flavonoids have additional functions in protection from pests and disease and thus, given the necessary humidity rutin is converted to the bitter quercetin, which is a pest deterrent122. Furthermore, these flavonoids have important functions in the human diet counteracting several chronic diseases, including obesity, cardiovascular diseases, gallstone formation, and hypertension123,124. However, it is important to note that multifaceted roles have been demonstrated for a wide range of antioxidants.

Recently, a study on the biosynthetic gene clusters in Fagopyrum Genus has demonstrated that certain wild relatives of buckwheat, which grow at higher altitudes, exhibit variations in the UFGT3 gene cluster. These variations result in an increased accumulation of pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside, which can enhance the plants’ ability to better withstand UV-B radiation compared to cultivated varieties125. In addition, flavonoid biosynthesis, particularly rutin accumulation driven by natural variation in key enzymes such as FtFLS4 and FtRT1, underpins UV-B tolerance in buckwheat and provides molecular targets for developing UV-B-resilient crops126. A few further isolated studies are likely worthy of note for example, soybean, maize, and rice52,55,56,116,117. Moreover, a myriad of GWAS studies have been carried out in most of our major crop species, including maize, rice, wheat, barley, and tomato52,54–56,91,105,109,116,117,120, that assess the levels of multiple metabolites, including those such as ascorbate and polyphenolics, which have been implicated in UV tolerance. As such, it will likely be relatively facile to engineer this trait in future breeding programs.

Perspective

As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2E, several avenues for the metabolic engineering of plants aiming to improve UV-B resilience exist. The sheer number of metabolites with UV-B absorbing properties opens a wide range of routes for improved metabolic resistance to UV-B, including increasing the levels of various phenylpropanoids and vitamins. This, will additionally likewise boost the nutritional values of those crops Another potential route would be improving melatonin production91, as a few reports suggest improved UV-B absorption of this phytochemical. Finally, by contrast to the discussion above, considering that UV-B radiation activates plant defense mechanisms that enhance terpenoid indole alkaloid biosynthesis as an adaptive strategy means that it may additionally have benefits in certain circumstances. For example, hairy root cultures of Catharanthus roseus exposed to UV-B radiation showed increased accumulation of lochnericine, serpentine, and ajmalicine, while hörhammericine levels decreased127, making UV-B treatment as a potential agent to improve specialized metabolite production systems. In the remainder of this article, we will discuss how sub-cellular spatial dynamics of metabolites, intelligent regulation of metabolic networks, and enhancing cell wall composition may represent additional sources of insight into how best to elevate crop UV tolerance.

Sub-cellular spatial dynamics of metabolites

To date, most studies utilizing metabolic profiling of tissues exposed to UV-B radiation are performed on a bulk biological material for which no spatial resolution is provided. To untangle the fine nuances in the regulation and interconnectivity of tissue and cell types, high-resolution metabolic profiling is urged to elucidate the metabolic compositions in the respective cell types. In the last decades, large improvements have been made to improve the spatial resolution in metabolomics128, however, none of the methods provides an optimal solution, such as that provided by single-cell transcriptomics. However, given the intensity of efforts in this area, it is highly likely that in the near future, single-cell metabolomics will enable the untangling of the cell type-specific modulation of metabolic adjustments under UV-B radiation and ultimately enable precise metabolic engineering to enhance UV-B resilience in a more sophisticated manner. Based on the integrated technological advancements, NanoSIMS technology—leveraging its 50-nm scale spatial resolution and multi-isotope synchronous imaging capabilities—provides a pivotal tool for resolving the tissue and subcellular distribution of plant metabolites (e.g., flavonoids and melatonin, UV-B protectants). Notably, by combining nanoSIMS with ¹H NMR metabolomic data129, researchers have successfully deciphered the spatial distribution characteristics of metabolic profiles in tropical plant root systems. Building on this foundation, dynamic tracking via stable isotope labeling (like 13C-phenylalanine) enables the quantification of compartmentalized enrichment gradients of protectants and their physical shielding efficiency in plant tissues under UV-B stress. Further, correlating metabolic hotspots with organelle spatial localization through confocal microscopy can elucidate the directional transport mechanisms of substances such as flavonoid glycosides that are mediated by vacuolar membrane transporters. In the future, integrating single-cell metabolomics with spatial transcriptomics-guided CRISPR target screening will enable nanoSIMS to precisely localize flavonoid synthesis hotspots in living plant cells, thereby identifying candidate targets for subcellular-scale precision breeding of UV-B-resistant crops. Concurrently, advances in high-pressure freezing-assisted cryo-nanoSIMS technology will significantly enhance the capture precision of in situ distributions of water-soluble antioxidants such as ascorbic acid. Based on this integrated framework, temporal sampling strategies will facilitate the construction of dynamic subcellular metabolic atlases of plant UV-B responses, ultimately propelling stress-resilient crop breeding into a nanoscale-precision era.

Intelligent regulation of metabolic networks

AI-driven computational models, such as deep learning and cheminformatics, can efficiently mine plant multi-omics datasets to predict novel compound structures with UV-B-absorbing potential. Although this field remains at an early stage, as exemplified by a deep learning-based visual discriminant model for buckwheat flavonoid content enabling high-throughput prediction in natural populations130, the capacity to integrate high-dimensional metabolic data, including GWAS data and spatial metabolomics, offers a new paradigm for rapidly identifying plant-derived UV-B protectants. For instance, in metabolic pathway simulations graph neural networks GNNs can model nonlinear relationships between metabolite structures and UV absorption efficiency by incorporating GWAS data on rice OsUVR8-regulated flavonoid accumulation and barley phenylpropanoid co-selection thereby predicting novel glycosylated/acylated derivatives. By integrating multi-species dynamic phenotyping data as referenced in techniques from the Sub-Cellular Spatial Distribution and Regulations chapter migration learning constructs cross-species generalizable models while synchronously optimizing targeted metabolic engineering to provide candidate targets for UV-resistant crop breeding. AI holds significant potential in deciphering complex metabolic networks and future integration with in vitro spectroscopic validation and transgenic models represents a promising route for future studies.

Enhancing cell wall composition

From a morphophysiological perspective, it has been shown that leaf elongation is reduced in plants exposed to UV-B (Antarctic grass and lettuce). It is suggested that this is partially attributed to the accumulation of insoluble hydroxycinnamic acids (p-coumaric, caffeic, and ferulic acids), which cross-link the cell wall polysaccharide network and cell wall peroxidase, thereby limiting epidermal cell expansion and leaf elongation131,132. Among the effects of UV-B, the elasticity of the cell wall was also reduced in grapevine cells133. In cell wall isolates from pea leaves or citrus fruit pectin, UV-A or UV-B radiation generated hydroxyl radicals (OH•) in the presence of hydrogen peroxide. Pectin is capable of transforming (OH•) into superoxide (O2•−), which may have intracellular consequences, as it can pass through cellular membranes134. This suggests that pectin might be involved in the UV radiation signaling pathway as a source of superoxide134,135. In tomato pericarp, exposure to UV-C irradiation (230–280 nm) during postharvest slowed cell wall disassembly. This was primarily linked to the downregulation of transcriptional expression or the inhibition of several cell wall-degrading enzymes, including PME, PG, and cellulase, during ripening136,137.

Alterations in cell wall structure and composition under UV light may also lead to the formation of mechanical barriers and protective substances, such as specialized compounds like lignin138. In cucumber, cotyledons exposed to UV-B radiation showed increased lignin accumulation in the cell walls of the parenchyma of tracheid elements and in the intercellular space139,140. Phenylpropanoids, including hydroxycinnamic acids and their derivatives (sinapate esters), as well as flavonoids, may play complementary roles in UV-B protection. Additionally, in grapevine leaves, the expression of genes involved in the lignin biosynthetic pathway, such as PAL, C4H, 4CL, and CCoAOMT, were upregulated in response to UV-B radiation, while genes associated with cell wall loosening were downregulated141,142. Novel strategies in improving physical barriers against UV-B by thickening the lignin composition of the secondary cell wall structures as well as embedding known UV-B absorbing phenylpropanoids in the cutin, can be utilized to enhance plants.

Despite the fact that core factors for UV-B resilience have been identified through artificial UV exposure, particularly those involving the UVR8 photoreceptor, it is important to bear in mind that these may not accurately represent natural exposure scenarios. As such, it will be critical to return to UV-BBE measurements that account for wavelength-specific weighting based on physiological relevance143. Ultimately, this approach is essential to assess plant acclimation, photoprotection, and stress resilience in environments that mimic natural sunlight, ultimately bridging the gap between lab-based findings and field-level plant behavior. In our opinion, such studies are imperative in order to fully exploit the immense knowledge gathered concerning phytochemical resistance to UV stress. This is even more important in light of current climate change scenarios.
