Severe dengue fever with life-threatening gastrointestinal hemorrhage in a liver transplant recipient undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jihua Feng, Xiaoling Zhu, Xiaowen Zheng, Xiaoxin Luo, Chunling Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue fever poses a significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenge in immunocompromised hosts, particularly in solid organ transplant recipients. We report a severe case in a patient during the early post-operative period following liver transplantation for hepatocellular carcinoma, who was concurrently receiving adjuvant chemotherapy with oxaliplatin and fluorouracil. This unique confluence of iatrogenic immunosuppression and chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression resulted in a life-threatening presentation characterized by grade 3 bone marrow suppression, severe anemia, overt gastrointestinal hemorrhage from gastric ulcers and colonic erosions, and acute kidney injury, necessitating intensive care management. Diagnosis was confirmed via dengue virus RNA testing, which was critical in differentiating the etiology from other potential causes of fever and pancytopenia in this complex setting. Management required a delicate balance of aggressive multidisciplinary supportive care, including endoscopic hemostasis and judicious blood product support, alongside cautious adjustment of immunosuppressive therapy to control the viral infection without precipitating graft rejection. This case underscores the severe, multifaceted complications dengue can precipitate in patients with dual vulnerabilities from transplantation and oncological therapy, highlighting the imperative for high clinical suspicion, rapid molecular diagnostics, and a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach to navigate competing clinical priorities and achieve a successful outcome.Keywords: Dengue fever, Liver transplantation, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Immunosuppression, Gastrointestinal bleeding

Introduction

Dengue fever, a mosquito-borne viral illness caused by the dengue virus (DENV), represents a significant global public health burden, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Its clinical spectrum ranges from asymptomatic infection and classic dengue fever (DF) to severe forms such as dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS), with severe disease often associated with cytokine dysregulation and secondary infection [1, 2]. Solid organ transplant recipients, including liver transplant patients, constitute a uniquely immunocompromised population due to their chronic immunosuppressed state, which predisposes them to opportunistic infections and can alter the typical presentation and course of viral illnesses [3]. Concurrently, the management of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) recurrence post-liver transplantation often involves adjuvant chemotherapy, which introduces an additional layer of complexity by inducing bone marrow suppression, further compromising the host’s hematological and immunological defenses [4].

The diagnosis of dengue in an immunocompromised host presents considerable challenges. Typical symptoms like fever, headache, and myalgia may be attenuated or masked, while complications such as hemorrhage and severe cytopenias can be profound and multifactorial [5]. In a post-transplant patient receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy, the differential for fever, pancytopenia, and gastrointestinal bleeding is extensive, encompassing bacterial sepsis, drug toxicity, graft-versus-host disease, and recurrence of malignancy. This diagnostic ambiguity can lead to delays in specific management and inappropriate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Furthermore, gastrointestinal bleeding in this context requires careful etiological distinction between dengue-induced capillary leakage and coagulopathy, drug-induced mucosal injury (e.g., from chemotherapy or steroids), and other transplant-related complications [6]. Therefore, a high index of suspicion and prompt molecular confirmation via dengue virus RNA testing are critical for timely diagnosis and appropriate clinical guidance.

This case is of significant clinical value and merits reporting due to the exceptional confluence of high-risk factors and the severe, multifaceted presentation it illustrates. We describe a patient in the early post-operative period following liver transplantation for HCC who developed severe dengue fever while undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy with oxaliplatin and fluorouracil. This scenario represents a “double-hit” to the immune system: profound iatrogenic immunosuppression from transplant medications synergistically compounded by chemotherapy-induced bone marrow suppression. The ensuing clinical course was marked by life-threatening complications including grade 3 bone marrow suppression with severe anemia, overt gastrointestinal hemorrhage from gastric ulcers and colonic erosions, and acute kidney injury, necessitating intensive care management. Reporting this case provides crucial insights into the diagnostic pitfalls and complex management decisions required when managing a severe systemic viral infection in a patient with dual vulnerabilities from transplantation and oncological therapy. It highlights the imperative for rapid pathogen identification, the delicate balance of adjusting immunosuppression in the face of active infection and bleeding, and the pivotal role of aggressive multidisciplinary supportive care in achieving a successful outcome.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 60-year-old male with a history of hepatitis B-related HCC and decompensated cirrhosis underwent allogeneic liver transplantation in July. Post-transplant pathology revealed moderately differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma with tumor necrosis and no residual tumor in the largest nodule. He received induction immunosuppression with methylprednisolone and basiliximab, followed by maintenance therapy with tacrolimus (3 mg/q12h), mycophenolate sodium enteric-coated tablets (540 mg/q12h), and prednisone (10 mg/d). Adjuvant chemotherapy with oxaliplatin (100 mg), fluorouracil (750 mg), and calcium folinate (300 mg) was in September 24. On October 20, he was readmitted for further chemotherapy.

Clinical findings

Following admission, the patient developed a fever and experienced recurrent episodes of melena. Laboratory investigations revealed multiple abnormalities: elevated C-reactive protein at 61.77 mg/L; increased lactate dehydrogenase (541 U/L) and α-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (345 U/L); decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (0.87 mmol/L); impaired renal function with elevated creatinine (168 µmol/L) and low bicarbonate (20.7 mmol/L); hepatic synthetic dysfunction indicated by low total protein (59.2 g/L), albumin (34.1 g/L), and prealbumin (144.2 mg/L); significant hypokalemia (2.54 mmol/L), hypocalcemia (2.00 mmol/L), and hypomagnesemia (0.47 mmol/L); markedly elevated ferritin (3390.24 ng/ml); lymphopenia with an absolute CD45 + count of 743 cells/µL; elevated D-dimer (2803 ng/mL) and fibrinogen degradation products (22.16 µg/mL); trace proteinuria; positive fecal occult blood test; and pancytopenia with leukopenia (WBC 1.88 × 10⁹/L), anemia (HGB 67.00 g/L), and thrombocytopenia (PLT 97.00 × 10⁹/L). Dengue virus nucleic acid testing returned positive on October 25 (CT value: 13.35) and Targeted Next-Generation Sequencing also returned positive (Number of reads for dengue virus: 216,073) without any any other pathogens detected, confirming a diagnosis of dengue fever. Peripheral blood bacterial culture were negative. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy identified a gastric fundus ulcer (A1 stage), chronic non-atrophic antral gastritis with erosion, and anemic gastric mucosa. Colonoscopy findings included suspected small intestinal bleeding, an ulcerated and eroded ileocecal valve, multiple colonic erosions, and a sigmoid colon polyp; endoscopic hemostasis with powder spray and titanium clip application was performed.

Diagnostic assessment

The admission diagnoses were postoperative chemotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma and status post liver transplantation. The diagnostic workup during hospitalization, prompted by the onset of fever and melena, led to the identification of several complications. The positive dengue virus RNA test confirmed an acute dengue fever infection. The endoscopic findings, coupled with recurrent melena and positive fecal occult blood, established the diagnosis of gastrointestinal bleeding, with likely sources from the gastric ulcer, ileocecal valve ulceration, and colonic erosions. The profound pancytopenia (leukocytes 1.88 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin 67.00 g/L, platelets 97.00 × 10⁹/L) was consistent with grade 3 bone marrow suppression, potentially multifactorial due to chemotherapy, dengue infection, or immunosuppressive therapy. Renal insufficiency was evidenced by elevated serum creatinine (168 µmol/L). Hypoalbuminemia (ALB 34.1 g/L) and severe anemia (HGB 67.00 g/L) were also confirmed. The sigmoid colon polyp and multiple gastrointestinal erosions were documented endoscopic findings.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient was transferred to the Emergency Intensive Care Unit (EICU) for isolation and intensive management following the diagnosis of dengue fever. The therapeutic regimen was comprehensively adjusted to address the multiple complications. Immunosuppressive therapy was carefully modified (tacrolimus 2 mg/q12h, stopping mycophenolate sodium enteric-coated tablets and prednisone). The patient was placed on a nil-by-mouth regimen. Supportive measures included intravenous fluid resuscitation, nutritional support, proton pump inhibitors, and somatostatin analogs. Antimicrobial therapy was initiated with cefoperazone-sulbactam (3 g q8h from October 24 to 30) and later switched to piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g q8h from October 30 to November 4). Treatment for bone marrow suppression included leukocyte-raising agents (e.g., leucogen) and blood product transfusions to correct the severe anemia and support hematopoiesis. Measures to maintain electrolyte balance and correct renal dysfunction were implemented. Endoscopic hemostasis was achieved during the colonoscopy procedure.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient’s clinical condition gradually improved with the instituted therapies. The fever subsided by October 29, and the frequency of melena progressively decreased. Following stabilization, he was transferred back to the general ward on November 4 for continued care. Subsequent treatment led to the resolution of active gastrointestinal bleeding. The final discharge diagnoses encompassed the acute infection and the array of complicating conditions: Dengue Fever, Gastrointestinal Hemorrhage, Grade 3 Bone Marrow Suppression, Renal Insufficiency, Hypoalbuminemia, Sigmoid Colon Polyp, Multiple Gastrointestinal Erosions, Severe Anemia, Gastric Fundus Ulcer (A1 stage), status post Hepatocellular Carcinoma surgery, and status post Liver Transplantation.

Discussion

The presentation of dengue fever in solid organ transplant recipients, particularly liver transplant recipients, is a recognized but infrequently reported complication, often associated with severe outcomes due to the underlying immunosuppressed state [7]. While opportunistic infections like cytomegalovirus are well-documented in this population, dengue virus infection poses unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [8]. CMV typically manifests as interstitial pneumonia, hepatitis, or colitis, with disease expression closely linked to the net state of immunosuppression and primarily mediated by direct viral cytopathic effects and alloreactive immune responses. In contrast, dengue virus infection in the transplant population poses a uniquely perilous threat due to its propensity to induce systemic capillary leakage, profound thrombocytopenia, and a hemorrhagic diathesis. Unlike CMV, which generally permits a more gradual and targeted adjustment of immunosuppression, dengue mandates urgent and delicate management to avert life-threatening bleeding while preserving graft integrity. This contrast underscores why dengue, though less commonly considered in post-transplant infectious protocols, may represent a more immediately catastrophic threat in the early post-operative period, demanding a heightened index of suspicion and more aggressive supportive care than many conventional opportunistic viral infections. Previous literature has highlighted the increased risk of severe and atypical presentations of arboviral infections in immunocompromised hosts, where the dysregulated immune response can alter the typical clinical course [9]. However, the complexity of the present case extends beyond a singular opportunistic infection in a transplant recipient.

An essential consideration in this case was the determination of the likely source of dengue virus infection, as establishing the route of acquisition has significant implications for infection control and clinical management. In immunocompromised populations, particularly solid organ transplant recipients, the possibility of transfusion-transmitted infections or nosocomial transmission must be carefully excluded. The patient in this case had no history of blood product transfusion in the two months preceding symptom onset, effectively ruling out transfusion-associated dengue transmission, a rare but documented phenomenon in endemic regions. Furthermore, no other cases of dengue fever were identified among hospitalized patients or healthcare workers in the same unit during the admission period, making nosocomial acquisition highly unlikely. Notably, the patient resided in an area with known local dengue transmission, and his hospitalization coincided with an ongoing community epidemic. Collectively, these epidemiological and clinical clues strongly support community-acquired dengue infection. This distinction is clinically relevant, as it underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for arboviral illnesses in febrile transplant recipients presenting with cytopenias or hemorrhagic manifestations, especially during peak transmission seasons in endemic zones. Early recognition of community-acquired dengue in such patients facilitates timely implementation of appropriate isolation precautions, avoidance of unnecessary broad-spectrum antimicrobials, and prompt initiation of supportive care tailored to the unique vulnerabilities of the immunocompromised host.

This case is distinguished by the convergence of multiple high-risk factors occurring simultaneously. The patient was not only in the early post-transplant period with significant immunosuppression but was also undergoing active adjuvant chemotherapy for hepatocellular carcinoma, a combination that profoundly exacerbates myelosuppression [10]. This dual insult of pharmacologic immunosuppression and chemotherapy-induced bone marrow suppression created a uniquely immunocompromised host, leading to grade 3 myelosuppression and severe anemia, complications more severe than typically reported in transplant-related dengue cases [11]. The quantitative dengue virus RNA testing yielded a cycle threshold (CT) value of 13.35 and tNGS with 216,073 reads, which corresponds to an exceptionally high viral load. This finding strongly suggests that dengue-induced bone marrow suppression was the primary acute driver of the profound cytopenias observed during hospitalization, even if immunosuppression and chemotherapy provided the initial “first hit” to the hematopoietic reserve. Dengue virus is known to directly infect and suppress hematopoietic progenitor cells in the bone marrow, while also inducing a cytokine storm that further impairs hematopoiesis and promotes peripheral destruction of blood cells.In this patient, the superimposition of acute, high-level dengue viremia upon a background of chemotherapy-induced marrow hypoplasia created a catastrophic convergence, resulting in the rapid development of grade 3 myelosuppression and anemia. Furthermore, the clinical manifestation was dominated by life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding from multiple ulcerative and erosive lesions, necessitating intensive endoscopic intervention and EICU care. This severe hemorrhagic complication, while gastrointestinal bleeding can occur in various immunosuppressed states, underscores the potential for dengue infection to precipitate catastrophic events in an already compromised physiological milieu. The management required navigating the delicate balance between controlling a viral hemorrhagic fever, managing profound cytopenias, and maintaining adequate immunosuppression to prevent graft rejection, a therapeutic dilemma less commonly emphasized in reports of post-transplant dengue [12, 13].

The diagnostic challenge in this patient was multifaceted, primarily centered on differentiating the etiology of fever and pancytopenia in a complex immunocompromised host. In liver transplant recipients, the differential for these symptoms is broad, encompassing opportunistic infections, drug toxicity, graft-versus-host disease, and disease recurrence [14]. Dengue fever, especially outside of peak epidemic seasons, may not be the initial consideration, leading to potential delays in diagnosis and inappropriate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [15]. This case underscores the critical importance of rapid molecular diagnostics, such as dengue virus RNA testing, for timely and accurate identification, which is paramount for guiding appropriate supportive care and avoiding unnecessary antimicrobial exposure. Furthermore, the source of gastrointestinal bleeding required careful discernment between dengue-associated hemorrhagic diathesis, chemotherapy or immunosuppressant-induced mucosal injury, and other transplant-related pathologies, a process where endoscopic evaluation proved indispensable [16, 17].

The markedly elevated serum ferritin level of 3390.24 ng/mL observed in this patient warrants consideration of secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (sHLH), particularly given the concurrent findings of persistent pancytopenia, severe dengue infection, and underlying immunosuppression. Dengue-associated HLH is a well-documented but often underrecognized complication, especially in immunocompromised hosts, where an exaggerated inflammatory response may precipitate a cytokine storm and hemophagocytic activity [18]. While a definitive diagnosis of HLH typically requires fulfillment of established criteria such as the HLH-2004 guidelines [19]—including fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, hemophagocytosis in bone marrow, and elevated soluble CD25 or reduced NK cell activity—our patient exhibited several clinical and laboratory features consistent with this syndrome. The profound pancytopenia, extreme hyperferritinemia, and clinical severity beyond that expected from isolated dengue infection collectively raise the possibility that sHLH contributed significantly to the disease course. Although confirmatory studies such as bone marrow examination or soluble CD25 measurement were not performed in this case, the presence of these key features underscores the importance of considering sHLH in immunocompromised patients presenting with severe dengue and hyperferritinemia, as early recognition may have therapeutic implications, including the potential role of immunomodulatory agents such as corticosteroids or etoposide in selected case.

From a pathophysiological perspective, this case illustrates a potential synergistic suppression of hematopoiesis. Chemotherapeutic agents like oxaliplatin and fluorouracil are known myelosuppressants, and their effects were likely compounded by the direct impact of dengue virus infection on bone marrow progenitor cells and the cytokine-mediated inflammatory milieu [20]. The resultant severe pancytopenia created a precarious state, increasing bleeding risk and susceptibility to secondary infections. Concurrently, the use of calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus, essential for graft maintenance, may have contributed to renal dysfunction, complicating fluid management in a patient with a capillary leak syndrome characteristic of dengue [21]. The therapeutic strategy, therefore, hinged on meticulous supportive care—including judicious blood product transfusion, endoscopic hemostasis, and renal-protective fluid resuscitation—while cautiously adjusting immunosuppression to mitigate infection severity without precipitating acute rejection [22]. This intricate balance highlights the necessity of a multidisciplinary approach in managing such high-stakes scenarios where competing clinical priorities must be harmonized.

In conclusion, the successful management of this case hinged on the early molecular confirmation of dengue infection, which steered the therapeutic focus away from empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics and towards targeted supportive care. The decisive role of endoscopic intervention in controlling life-threatening hemorrhage and the careful, individualized titration of immunosuppression were pivotal in navigating the competing risks of graft rejection, uncontrolled infection, and bleeding diathesis. This case underscores that in dengue-endemic regions, a high index of suspicion for arboviral illness is imperative in febrile, immunocompromised transplant recipients presenting with cytopenias and hemorrhage, as prompt diagnosis is critical for appropriate management.

This report highlights the severe, multisystemic manifestation of dengue fever in a uniquely immunocompromised host subjected to dual immunosuppressive pressures. The primary limitations of this single-case description are its inherent lack of generalizability and the absence of serial virological or immunophenotyping data, which could have elucidated the dynamic host-pathogen interactions in this setting.The second limitation is the absence of platelet function analysis. Given the patient’s history of renal insufficiency and prior chemotherapy, platelet dysfunction may have preceded the decline in platelet count. Third, the case lacks serological profiling (dengue IgG/IgM), which is essential for determining whether this represented a secondary dengue infection—associated with increased risk of DHF/DSS via antibody-dependent enhancement—or a primary infection rendered severe solely by iatrogenic immunosuppression. Future studies are needed to better define optimal management strategies for balancing anti-rejection, anti-neoplastic, and supportive therapies in transplant recipients who contract severe systemic viral infections.
