SPION+ macrophage traffic out of the central nervous system with SIV infection
Authors: Xavier Alvarez, Zoey K Wallis, Cecily C Midkiff, Jaclyn Mallard, Pete J Didier, Kenneth C Williams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral nervous system (CNS) macrophages play key roles in viral neuropathogenesis and immune surveillance, yet their trafficking and trafficking dynamics between the CNS and peripheral lymphoid tissues remain poorly understood. To address this gap, we used intracisternal (i.c.) injection of fluorescent-superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPION) in SIV-infected macaques that we found labeled CNS CD68+CD163+CD206+ perivascular, meningeal, and choroid plexus (CP) macrophages. SPION+CD163+ macrophages were also detected in the optic nerves and cribriform area—potential routes of macrophage egress from the CNS. CD163+SPION+ macrophages labeled in the CNS were identified in the deep cervical lymph node (dCLN) and dorsal root ganglia (DRG). SPION+ CD163+ macrophages appeared in both the CNS and the periphery as early as 24 h post i.c. inoculation, decreasing over time in the CNS of non-infected animals but accumulating with SIV infection. A trend toward greater numbers of SPION + macrophages that traffic out of the CNS was observed in non-infected animals, a pattern not seen with SIV infection. Productively infected SPION+ cells were found in the CNS and dCLN of infected animals 7–28 days post i.c. injection. These findings support a model in which SPION + macrophages, some of which harbor virus, traffic between the CNS and peripheral lymphoid tissues. This is discussed with regard to HIV infection of the CNS and reseeding of the periphery with CNS virus.Keywords: Monocytes, Macrophage, HIV, SIV, Cell-traffic

Summary

SPION+ macrophages can leave the CNS in non-SIV infected animals and SIV- infected animals. SPION+ macrophages are in the CNS, at CNS borders, including optic nerve and cribriform area, and outside the CNS in deep cervical lymph node (dCLN) and dorsal root ganglia (DRG). SPION+SIV-RNA+ from the CNS are found in the dCLN underscoring the traffic of CNS macrophages to the periphery as a mechanism of viral reseeding.

Introduction

The central nervous system (CNS) is susceptible to human immune deficiency (HIV) both early and late following peripheral infection, even in individuals undergoing antiretroviral therapy(ART) [54, 56]. Such infection is associated with perivascular cuffing of macrophages and subsequently with macrophage accumulation in CNS lesions [4, 31, 54]. Meningeal and choroid plexus macrophages are also HIV and simian immune deficiency (SIV) infected, although they have been studied less extensively [41, 54]. Understanding the biology of these cells is crucial, considering the CNS infection as a reservoir for HIV and SIV, and the unresolved question of whether macrophages can traffic out. CNS macrophages are heterogeneous originating either from bone marrow-derived cells that normally traffic to the CNS or from embryonic stem cells (yolk sac-derived) that repopulate within the CNS. Therefore, the biology of CNS inflammation and trafficking of macrophages into and out of the CNS, both under normal conditions and during HIV infected are of critical interest.

The majority of HIV- and SIV-infected cells within the CNS are bone marrow-derived, perivascular macrophages, located at the blood-cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) interface. These macrophages are a key component of the CNS immune defense but, when infected, can become reservoirs for SIV and HIV [3, 18, 21, 29, 35, 54]. These cells can transport infected virus into the CNS, acting as “Trojan horse cells” or a “Trojan herd” thus facilitating CNS viral persistence and dissemination [45, 55]. The CSF provides another route for CNS viral dissemination, whereby infected monocytes/macrophages travel through the bloodstream and migrate into the CNS, either as part of normal repopulation or in response to infection [4, 8, 57]. While the trafficking of SIV- or HIV-infected macrophages into the CNS is well-documented, the question of whether they can traffic out, though speculated, remains poorly understood and has not been thoroughly investigated in a viral infection models using non-human primates.

Contrary to the long-held belief that the CNS lacked or had limited lymphatic drainage [42], it is now well-established that CNS fluids—and, in some instances, cells—do drain or exit into the periphery [2, 14, 19, 23, 28, 38, 42]. Radiolabeled proteins and fluorescence dyes injected intracisternally, as well as fluorescently-labeled antigen-presenting cells transplanted into the CNS, are detected in deep cervical lymph nodes (dCLN)[20], suggesting trafficking via meningeal lymphatics. The presence of macrophages and dendritic cells (antigen presenting cells) (APC)) in lymph nodes suggests that this trafficking pathway may be important for maintaining tolerance to CNS antigens and autoimmune responses [12, 32, 48]. Another alternative route for cells to exit the CNS involves the central canal and meninges of the spinal cord, which connect the inner layers of the peripheral nerve root sheaths as they emerge from the spinal cord [36, 37, 51]. This connection, at the subarachnoid angle, effectively links the CSF-filled subarachnoid space (SAS) of the brain and spinal cord with the perineural space of the nerve roots [24, 36, 37]. This path communicates with the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) at the interface between the CNS, the peripheral nervous system (PNS), and peripheral immune organs [22, 24, 40]. Overall, experimental studies of cell traffic out of the CNS are informative; however, they carry the caveat of potentially inducing CNS injury, breaching the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and/or causing resultant CNS inflammation [2, 19, 20]. To date, similar studies have not been conducted in humans or non-human primates with HIV or SIV infection.

In this study, we used intracisternally (i.c.) injected superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPION) in non-infected and SIV-infected monkeys to examine their uptake and distribution within the CNS, as well as trafficking of SPION+ CNS macrophages out of the CNS. Due to their size (0.86 μm average diameter), SPION do not passively diffuse out of the CNS; instead, they are taken up by CNS macrophages in perivascular spaces, the meninges, and the choroid plexus from which they can exit. SPION + macrophages are detected in the CNS within hours after i.c. inoculation in non-infected and SIV-infected animals. Furthermore SPION+ macrophages are found outside the CNS in dCLN and DRG, with varying numbers observed between non-infected and SIV-infected animals. SPION+-SIV-RNA+ and SPION+-gp41+ macrophages are detected in dCLN. This report is the first to utilize i.c. SPION injections in the context of SIV infection to comprehensively detail CNS labeling, macrophage accumulation, and trafficking out of CNS. Our findings underscore the ability of SPION+ macrophages, once labeled within the CNS, to traffic to peripheral lymph organs and the peripheral nervous system, including some that are infected. These data are discussed in the context of CNS inflammation and the role of HIV viral reservoirs.

Results

We injected SPION, and in select animals also dextran, intracisternally (i.c.) into non-infected and SIV-infected animals. We then studied their uptake within CNS macrophages two hours (SPION and dextran), 1 day (SPION and dextran) and 7, 14, and 28 days post injection (Fig. 1). SPION-labeled cells were detected using bright-field microscopy and Prussian blue iron staining, or by visualizing the internal fluorescence of the SPION (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6). Multiple SPION are taken up per labeled cells, which were distributed within perivascular spaces, the meninges, and the choroid plexus (Fig. 2, Fig. 3 bottom row). We do not find free (non-cell-associated) SPION in the CNS, likely due to their size (0.86 μm diameter). However, scattered free SPION were observed around the meninges surrounding the brain, in the DRG, within the subarachnoid space contiguous with the central canal of the spinal cord, and in CSF (not shown). SPION were consistently taken up by CD68+, CD163+, and CD206+ perivascular macrophages, meningeal macrophages, and choroid plexus (CP) macrophages (Fig. 3). We observed approximately > twofold more SPION+ meningeal macrophages compared to SPION+ perivascular macrophages in non-infected animals (Table 1, Figs. 2 and 3), which is consistent with the greater overall number of total meningeal versus perivascular macrophages in each respective compartment. Differential fluid dynamics of CSF in the meninges versus the perivascular space likely plays a role in this distribution [5, 38, 47]. Between 7 and 28 days post SPION inoculation, a trend toward decreased numbers of SPION+ perivascular (p = 0.06) and meningeal macrophages (p = 0.09) was observed in non-infected animals; however this did not reach statistical significance (Table 1). In contrast, a non-significant trend toward increased numbers of SPION+ macrophages in perivascular space and meninges was seen in with SIV-infected animals (Table 2).

Next, we examined SPION + macrophages exiting the CNS. We detected SPION+ macrophages in the optic nerve, nasal septum, cribriform plate, all located at the interface between the CNS and the periphery (Fig. 4, Table 2). Outside the CNS, SPION+ are in deep cervical lymph nodes dCLN, axillary lymph nodes (LN), spleen (Table 2), and dorsal root ganglia (DRG)(Fig. 4) 7, 14, and 28-days post-inoculation in non-infected and SIV-infected animals (Figs.4 and 5 and Table 2). To assess fluid and cell trafficking out of the CNS and distribution in LN, fluorescently labeled dextran was co-injected with SPION in two animals. One animal which received SPION and dextran i.c. and was sacrificed 2 h later, showed only dextran in the dCLN (data not show). In the second animal, which also received SPION and dextran, both were detected in different locations within the dCLN. This finding is consistent with CNS macrophage taking up SPION before migrating out of the CNS, and likely differential diffusion of dextran compared to cell-associated SPION (Fig. 5). We next examined peripheral sites outside the CNS between 7 and 28 days post i.c. SPION inoculation in non-infected and infected animals. SPION+ macrophages were detected in at least two of the three-time points in dCLN, optic nerve, cribriform area, axillary lymph nodes, and spleen (Table 2). Overall, SPION-labeled macrophages appeared to exit the CNS more efficiently in non-infected animals, while SIV infection might promote retention within the CNS with CNS inflammation.

Using the internal fluorescence of SPION and anti-SIVgp41 and SIV-RNA immune fluorescence probes, we detected CD163+SPION+, SPION+SIVgp41+ and SPION+SIV-RNA+ macrophages both within the CNS and among cells outside the CNS in dCLN of SIV-infected animals, including those with AIDS (Fig. 6A, B). In AIDS animals with SIVE (N = 2), we found SPION+SIV-RNA+ cells (ranging from 20 to 50 SPION + SIV-RNA + cells per dCLN section, 1.0 ± 1.7 SPION+SIV-RNA+/mm2) in the dCLNs (Fig. 6C). We have previously reported the presence of SPION + SIV-RNA + macrophages in the CNS of SIV infected animals with SIV encephalitis (SIVE). These current data align with the interpretation that SPION-labeled macrophages originated within the CNS and were productively SIV-infected. It remains undetermined whether the SPION+SIV-RNA+ cells found in the dCLN were infected within the CNS and subsequently carried SIV-RNA and DNA to the periphery, or whether they became infected after exiting the CNS.

Discussion

In this study, we used i.c. injected SPION to assess their distribution in macrophages within and outside the CNS of non-infected and SIV-infected animals. SPION were efficiently taken up by CD163+, CD206+, and CD68+ perivascular macrophages, as well as macrophages in the meninges, and CP. We observed a greater number of SPION-labeled macrophages in the meninges compared to perivascular macrophages. This is likely due to the higher overall density of macrophages in the meninges and the increased rate of CSF fluid flow within this compartment [5, 38, 47]. Furthermore, macrophages in the meninges consistently exhibited a higher SPION burden than perivascular macrophages, potentially influencing their subsequent trafficking dynamics [13, 25]. Our previous work indicated that the ratio of total macrophages to SPION+ macrophages in the meningeal and perivascular spaces remains relatively constant in both non-infected and SIV-infected animals (Wallis et. al., 2025a). Outside the CNS, SPION + macrophages were detected in both the dCLN and the DRG, with the highest numbers observed in the DRG. This finding aligns with the DRG’s anatomical continuity with the subarachnoid space of the spinal cord and its direct contact with CSF into which the SPION were injected [36].

In non-infected animals, we observed a non-significant trend toward increased numbers of SPION+ macrophages in the CNS 7 days post-injection, which normalized over time. However, in SIV-infected animals, the number of SPION+ macrophages in the CNS tended to increase, consistent with the presence of CNS inflammation, potentially leading to reduced macrophage egress. A limitation of this study is the low animal numbers per data point (n = 2) due to working with non-human primates. The variability in SPION+ macrophage counts within tissue samples also contributes to this limitation. Increasing the number of animals per data point and analyzing larger tissue regions would likely enhance our statistical power. Consistent with previous studies [6] [19, 20])) we did not observe evidence of SPION passively “leaking out” of the CNS Our findings suggest that SIV infection and associated macrophage accumulation within the CNS may promote the retention of SPION+ macrophages. Conversely, in non-infected animals, SPION+ macrophages appeared less likely to accumulate in the CNS and were found in greater numbers outside of the CNS. The signals regulating macrophage exit from the CNS under normal SIV-related inflammatory conditions remain undefined but likely involve macrophage activation, cellular function dependent on SPION uptake, and perhaps cell growth and death signals. Future studies should investigate whether similar signals are present in non-viral CNS inflammatory conditions such as multiple sclerosis, characterized by relapsing and remitting inflammation. Consistent with a role for ART in resolving CNS inflammation and promoting macrophage egress, we observed increased numbers of SPION+ macrophages outside the CNS in SIV-infected monkeys on ART compared to those with SIV encephalitis (SIVE) that are not receiving ART [50]. It remains unknown whether these cells can traffic SIV-DNA out of the CNS.

We detected SPION + macrophages both within the CNS and, importantly, outside the CNS in dCLN. Moreover, we demonstrated the presence of SPION+SIV-RNA+ and SPION+gp41+ cells within these dCLN. Technical limitations prevented us from performing immune-fluorescence 3-label in situ hybridization for SIV-RNA, SPION detection, and macrophage marker identification. However, our previous studies, utilizing double-label IHC for macrophage markers and Persian blue iron staining, have demonstrated that virtually all SPION+ cells in the CNS and periphery are macrophages [50, 51]. In this study we did not determine whether productively SIV infected SPION+ cells were infected within or instead outside the CNS. Gene flow analysis of SIV sequences, conducted by our group and others, has shown viral traffic from parenchymal tissues and blood early in infection, and from the CNS during later stages [33, 44]. Future studies should perform such analysis at a single-cell level, comparing SPION+SIV-RNA+ in the CNS and the periphery.

Our findings build upon previous rodent studies that utilized transplanted or injected cells and dyes to demonstrate immune cell egress from the CNS including DC, T cells, B cells, and monocytes [17, 19, 20, 27, 28, 30]. However, our study differs from these rodent models due to the use of i.c. SPION injection in conjunction with an SIV infection model [19, 20]. The pathways by which cells can traffic out of the CNS have been extensively described, including meningeal lymphatics and perineural routes via the cribiform plate [17, 19, 20] as well as novel perineural pathways [50] (reviewed [9, 11, 15, 26, 30, 34, 43]. Interestingly, we observed that i.c.-injected dextran was readily detected outside the CNS in the dCLN within hours after administration, while SPION+ macrophages were not detected until 24 h post-i.c. inoculation. This difference is likely due to size of the SPION, which limits their ability to passively leak out of the CNS [7, 10, 23]; instead they must be taken up by macrophages for transport, unlike dextran dyes, which readily diffuse. Furthermore, the differing distributions of SPION and dextran dye within the dCLN suggest distinct trafficking pathways of fluid versus myeloid cell traffic in the dCLN [16, 19, 32].

Consistent with previous reports from our group and others, perivascular macrophages in HIV-infected humans and SIV-infected monkeys represent a major site of productive infection within the CNS [1, 41, 54]. Our parallel work [50] indicates that ART effectively clears SIV infection from CNS perivascular macrophages, as well as the majority of CNS meninges and CP macrophages. Interestingly, we found that four weeks following ART interruption, CNS perivascular macrophages remained SIV-RNA negative; however, we observed a rebound in the number of SIV-RNA+ meningeal and CP macrophages [50]. Similarly, ART reduces plasma virus and SIV-RNA levels in the spleen and dCLN, but not in the DRG. Consistent with observations by us and others, the DRG appears less responsive to ART, exhibiting macrophage accumulation and persistent activation despite treatment [51, 52]. Our recent work using SPION in the CNS and periphery supports these findings, showing a similar trend of decreased SPION+-RNA+ cells in the CNS, with a less pronounced effect in the DRG following ART. The DRG, located at the interface between the CNS and the PNS, exhibits abundant SPION, consistent with its communication with the subarachnoid space of the spinal cord, and the subarachnoid angle surrounding the nerve sheath [22, 39]. We have previously shown that SPION-laden macrophages in the epineurium and within the nerve fascicles communicate with the DRG, suggesting a novel for macrophage trafficking from the CNS [51]. Importantly, macrophages harboring SIV in the peripheral nerves and DRG are less affected by ART than those in the spleen and dCLNs [51]. Therefore, the DRG as well as peripheral and cranial nerves, which remain affected by HIV in people living with HIV (PLWH) on suppressive ART, warrant further investigation to understand the mechanisms underlying persistent viral reservoirs and macrophage activity. It is also important to consider that Clements et al., show evidence of replication-competent virus in monocytes of PLWH on ART [1, 49].

In summary, this study investigated the distribution of SPION-labeled macrophages within the CNS of non-infected and SIV-infected animals over time. Our key findings include the presence of SPION-labeled macrophages in the perivascular space, meninges, and choroid plexus (CP), as well as a higher SPION burden in meningeal macrophages compared to perivascular macrophages. These data suggest that SIV infection promoted the accumulation of SPION + macrophages within the CNS, whereas macrophages in non-infected animals exhibit a greater propensity to traffic out of the CNS. The detection of SPION-labeled macrophages in the dCLNs underscores the potential for macrophages to traffic to peripheral immune organs. This observation underscores the need for further investigation to fully elucidate the role of macrophages in maintaining viral reservoirs, as well as the impact of ART on CNS inflammation and the subsequent reseeding of viral reservoir in peripheral tissues.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal work was approved by the Tulane National Primate Research Center Care and Animal Use Committee. The TNRPC protocol number is 3767 and the animal welfare assurance number is P0310.

Animals and viral infection

Sixteen rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) were used in this study (Fig. 1). The animals were born and housed at the Tulane National Primate Research Center in strict adherence to the "Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals”. Two animals were used in a mixed SPION and dextran experiment to study trafficking to dCLN. Both animals were chronically SIV-infected (310 and 310 days) without AIDS. One animal received SPION and dextran and was sacrificed 2 h post i.c. inoculation. The second animal received the same injection and was sacrificed 24 h post i.c. inoculation. Six animals were non-infected, and six were chronically infected with (310–609 days) and had not developed AIDS. These animals were used to study SPION + macrophages in the periphery at 7, 14 and 28 days post SPION inoculation (n = 2 per group). Two additional animals were CD8 lymphocyte-depleted and developed rapid AIDS with SIV encephalitis (SIVE). These animals were used to study SPION+SIV-RNA+ and SPION+SIV-RNA+ cells in the CNS and LN. All SIV-infected animals were intravenously inoculated (i.v.) with a bolus of the SIVmac251 viral swarm (20 ng of SIV gp41).

SPION and dextran

Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPION) were obtained from Bangs Laboratories Inc (Fishers, IN) (PSCOOHMag/Encapsulated, MEDG001). These SPION are composed of iron oxide nanospheres encapsulated in an inert polymer containing a photoreactive fluorophore. The SPION have an average particle size of 0.86 μm diameter and internal fluorescence (Dragon Green [480/520]). SPION were prepared under sterile conditions using 3 mL of stock solution and 7 mL of sterile, low-endotoxin phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). SPION were prepared under sterile conditions by diluting 3 mL of stock solution with 7 mL of sterile low-endotoxin. SPION were suspended in 1 mL of sterile PBS for a final concentration of 33 mg/mL. A subset of animals (n = 2) also received fluorescent dextran (RITC-Dextran, 10 KD, Invitrogen, CA) via an i.c injection at a concentration of 50 mg/ML. To minimize increased in intracranial pressure during i.c. injection, 1 mL of CSF was removed from the cisterna and inoculations of equivalent volumes(1 mL) of SPION and dextran were then injected over a 5-min time period.

Tissue collection and processing

Animals were anesthetized with ketamine-HCl and then euthanized with i.v. administration pentobarbital overdose, followed by exsanguination. Blood was collected, and heparin sulfate was administered i.v. and allowed to diffuse for 5 min. Sodium pentobarbital was then administered via intracardiac injection, and CSF was collected. Following CSF collection, the animals were perfused with 3L of 1XPBS. A board certified veterinary pathologist performed postmortem examination as previously described [33, 34, 52]. The following tissues were collected: Brain (frontal, temporal, occipital, parietal, and choroid plexus) and peripheral tissues (cranial nerves, cribriform plate, nasal septum, brachial plexus, spleen, dCLN, superficial LN, axillary LN, tonsil) These tissues were either: (i) collected in zinc-buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin, (ii) fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 4–48 h, sucrose protected and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound for SPION analysis, or (iii) snap frozen in OCT without fixation.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC was performed as previously described, using antibodies targeting CD163 (1:250, Leica (Deer Park, IL)), CD68 (1:100, DAKO (Carpinteria, CA)), CD206 (1:1000, R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN)), and IBA1 macrophages (1:100, Wako (Osaka, Japan)) [33, 34]. Briefly, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections were de-paraffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was then performed with a citrate-based Antigen Unmasking Solution (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) in a microwave (900 W) for 20 min. After cooling for 20 min, the sections were washed with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.05% Tween-20 for 5 min, followed by incubation with peroxidase block (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA) aprotein block (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA) for 30 min and finally incubation with primary antibody. Following incubation with a peroxidase-conjugated polymer, the slides were developed using a diaminobenzidine chromogen (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA) with Harris Hematoxylin (StatLab, McKinney, TX).

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence microscopy, antibodies targeting CD163 (NCL-CD163, Leica Biosystems (Deer Park, IL)) and gp41 envelope protein (KK41, NIH (Manassas, VA)) were used on 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixed frozen sections as previously described [41, 46, 53]. The 2% PFA, fixed frozen sections were thawed for 20 min at room temperature, unwrapped, submerged in a citrate-based Antigen Unmasking Solution, and microwaved for one minute and forty-five seconds before being allow to cool to room temperature. Slides were permeabilized in a solution of phosphate-buffered saline with 0.01% Triton X-100 and 0.02% fish skin gelatin (PBS-FSG-TX100) followed by a PBS-FSG wash, transferred to a humidified chamber and blocked with 10% normal goat serum (NGS) diluted in PBS-FSG for 40 min, followed by a 60-min primary antibody incubation, washes, and 40-min secondary antibody incubation. Following routine washes, a DAPI nuclear stain added for 10 min. The slides were mounted using a custom-formulated anti-quenching mounting media containing Mowiol (#475,904, Calbiochem; San Diego, CA) and DABCO (#D2522, Sigma: St. Louis, MO), allowed to dry overnight, and digitally imaged with a Zeiss Axio Scan.Z1. HALO software (HALO v3.4, Indica Labs; Albuquerque, NM) was used for quantification and analysis.

Detection and quantification of SPION + macrophages in tissues

SPIONs were detected in the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral tissues by 1) light microscopy by morphology of the amber SPION beads, 2) Prussian blue iron staining (Sigma Aldrich Iron Stain, St. Louis, MO), and 3) detection of the internal fluorescence of Dragon Green. SPION + cells where manually counted in whole tissue at 20 × by light microscopy (Plan-Apochromat 620/0.7, Olympus; Japan) in a blinded fashion. Whole section tiling and stitching were performed using a Zeiss Axio Imager M1 microscope (Zeiss;Oberkochen, Germany) with AxioVision (Version 4.8, Zeiss; Oberkochen, Germany) using Plan-Apochromat 620/0.8 and 640/0.95 Korr objectives. This was followed by manual annotation of the parenchyma from the meninges into separate regions of interest (ROIs) and tissue area reported as mm2.

Viral RNA detection

Ultrasensitive SIV-RNAscope with probes for SIVmac251 was used to detect SPION-containing SIV-RNA positive cells within and outside of the CNS as previously described [53]. Tissue sections were placed in a target antigen retrieval solution, heated, and treated with protease plus, and a hydrogen peroxide blocker, all according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA). SIVmac239 RNAscope probes (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA) were hybridized at 40 °C in the HybEZ II Hybridization System. The RNAscope 2.5 HD Assay amplification steps were applied according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Target RNA was visualized through the addition of chromogenic Fast Red A and Fast Red B (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich) and mounted using Vectamount (Vector Laboratories).
