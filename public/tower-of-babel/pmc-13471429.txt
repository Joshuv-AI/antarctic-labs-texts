Seroprevalence of canine distemper in free-roaming dogs in buffer zone of Bardiya National Park, Nepal
Authors: Madan Bhandari, Sirjan Bastola, Rajesh Gautam, Mitesh Shrestha, Ajiv Babu Yadav, Yam Bahadur Gurung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanine distemper (CD), caused by Morbilivirus canis of Paramyxoviridae family, is a contagious and potentially fatal viral disease affecting a wide range of domestic and wild canid species. This study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of Canine Distemper Virus (CDV) and associated risk factors among free-roaming dogs in the buffer zone of Bardiya National Park (BNP), Nepal. A total of 91 serum samples were collected from free-roaming dogs and tested for CDV-specific IgG antibodies using an iELISA kit. The apparent seroprevalence was found to be 52.75% (95% CI: 42.5–63.0%), while the Rogan–Gladen adjusted true prevalence was 53.28% (95% CI: 42.92–63.64%). Among seropositive samples, 29.17% had high, 31.25% medium, and 39.58% low antibody titers, indicating mixed stages of exposure. Although older dogs (> 72 months) showed higher seropositivity (80%), no statistically significant association was observed between CDV seropositivity and age, sex, body condition score, and location (p > 0.05). The findings suggest that CDV is enzootic in the free-roaming dog population around BNP, representing a potential reservoir for viral spillover to vulnerable wildlife species such as leopards and tigers.Keywords: Bardiya, Canine distemper, Wildlife, Buffer zone

Introduction

Canine distemper (CD) is a highly contagious and lethal disease caused by Morbillivirus canis [8]. It is transmitted through close contact with an infected animal, either via aerosolized droplets or contaminated body fluids [13]. Initially, CD was reported to affect only domestic dogs, but subsequent studies have confirmed its occurrence in a variety of wildlife species, like foxes, wolves [1]. Previous study revealed strains of Asia-5 lineage of CD in leopard(Panthera pardus) from different 4 location (Palpa, Parbat, Dolakha and Kathmandu) of Nepal [24].

The number of feral and free dogs is increasing, correlating with a rise in dog bite incidents in recent years [22]. This growing free-roaming dog population poses a potential risk for transmission of CDV to wildlife. Interactions between free-roaming dogs and wildlife species have been responsible for distemper virus spillover events, resulting in severe outbreaks among vulnerable wildlife populations [3, 9].

Bardiya National Park (BNP), Nepal’s largest lowland national park, covers 968 sq. km with a 507 sq. km buffer zone and is rich in biodiversity, hosting 61 mammalian species. Between 2009 and 2022, the tiger population in BNP increased sevenfold to 125 individuals, intensifying competition with leopards [2]. This rise in competition, coupled with habitat fragmentation and increasing human encroachment near the park, has driven leopards into buffer zones where they encounter domestic dogs more frequently [10, 26]. Reports from similar conservation areas in Nepal have highlighted the increased danger to wildlife posed by CDV-infected free-roaming dogs [19].

Some investigators report the prevalence of virus (CDV) in the Annapurna Conservation Area (ACA) with 70% seropositivity among dogs, with 13% also testing positive for the CDV.

Researchers from India [5] reported seroprevalence rates of over 72% for CDV in free-ranging dogs and 18% in sympatric Indian foxes, indicating possible interspecies transmission. Spillover from the dog population to wildlife carnivores is a major conservation threat due to high mortality and morbidity rates. Kapil and Yeary [15], . Understanding the current seroprevalence of CDV in free-roaming dogs in BNP’s buffer zones is essential for early detection, prevention, and control strategies aimed at safeguarding the park’s wildlife. So, this study aims for a cross-sectional approach to assess the circulation of canine distemper virus (Morbili Virus) in community dogs in the buffer region of Bardiya National Park and tries to find out the association of seroprevalence of CDV with various risk factors. It supports the development of targeted vaccination campaigns and sterilization programs, aimed at controlling the risk posed by free-roaming dogs to wild species.

Methodology

Study area

A cross-sectional serological study was conducted in the buffer zone areas adjoining Bardiya National Park of Nepal. Madhuwan municipality (Nepali: मधुवन) is one of the eight local municipities with area of 129.73 sq.km where, field study was carried (Fig. 1).

Map of study area

Sample size and sampling methods

Purposive sampling was done from a high dog-wildlife conflict area. Due to limited available resources, total of 91 free-roaming dogs of both sexes were within the study area excluding ill and injured. Pregnant bitches and young puppy aged less than 4 months are not used for the study. None individual owner was found; some volunteer caretaker provide food when available. Although dogs were free roaming, age was determined by observation of dental status, community cross question, and information based on local owner who feed the dog.

Sampling location and vaccination status

Due to free-roaming nature of dogs in buffer zone, they constantly move across zone, it is difficult to locate exact location. So, Dogs are classified on basis of availability during sampling date. No previous vaccination history against CDV was identified. However, some dogs were vaccinated against rabies virus by the effort of local municipal council annually.

Sample collection and analysis

3 ml of blood was collected from the cephalic vein of dogs using a sterile 5 ml syringe, immediately transferred to a clot-activated serum tube and stored in an ice box − 20 °C. The collected blood samples were then transferred to the Microbiology laboratory of the Institute of Agriculture and Animal Science. A non-toxic red crayon mark was applied to the dog to prevent repeat sampling. No any medication was given before, during or after blood collection. Blood serum was separated by centrifuging at 1500 rpm for 10 min. ELISA test was performed using Agrolabo Distemper IgG Ab EILSA kit (sensitivity of 99% and specificity of 100%) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Data analysis

The obtained data were analyzed by descriptive statistical methods using IBM © SPSS Statistics version 25 and MS-Excel 2013. The effect of sex, age, sex, body condition score, and location of dogs on the seroprevalence of Canine Distemper was evaluated by odds ratio (OR) and p-value (P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant) of each association was obtained using the epitool online software. Rogan-Gladen (1978) adjustment was applied to calculate exact confidence interval and sensitivity when sensitivity error < 1 [16].

Result

Overall prevalence

Out of 91 serum samples, 48 samples were found positive to CDV IgG, indicating 52.75% (9% CI: 42.50–63.00%), while the Rogan–Gladen adjusted true prevalence was 53.28% (95% CI: 42.92–63.64%).

Antibody titer

Among positive dog sample, based on optical density(OD) value of ELISA was carried out at 450 nm wavelength. Positive samples were classified into three categories based on antibody titer level (Table 1) Specifically, 14 dogs (29.17%) exhibited high, while medium and low antibody titers was reported in 15 (31.25%) and 19 (39.58%) samples respectively. A higher level of immune response may be due to recent exposure to a virus or infection [20].

Risk factors associated with CDV

Analysis of potential risk factors associated with CDV seropositivity is presented in Table 2. Age-wise distribution indicated that older dogs (> 6 years) had the highest prevalence (80%), with an odds ratio (OR) of 4.31 (95% CI: 0.77–24.14) compared to young dogs, although the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.14). Adult dogs (13–72 months) showed a prevalence of 50% (OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.43–2.71, p = 0.88), which is similar as compared to young dogs (< 12 months). With respect to sex, prevalence was slightly higher in females (59.38%) than in males (49.15%), but the difference was not significant (OR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.28–1.58, p = 0.35). Body condition score (BCS) also did not show a significant association with CDV seropositivity; the prevalence ranged from 40% in dogs with BCS 2 to 54.55% in obese dogs (BCS 5), with odds ratios containing unity and p-values remaining > 0.05 in every category. Location-wise analysis revealed that dogs from the buffer zone had a higher prevalence (55.93%) compared to those outside the buffer zone (46.88%) (OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 0.61–3.41, p = 0.41), but this difference was not statistically significant. Overall, none of the examined factors showed a significant association with CDV seropositivity in the study population.

Discussion

In comparison to previous finding of morbillivirus in Nepal, 80% seropositive cases in free-roaming dogs were reported in buffer areas of Chitwan National Park [19], Meanwhile 70% seropositivity was reported in domesticated dogs of Annapurna Conservation area [21]. Between 2020 and 2022 six leopard (P. Pardus fusca) were reported neurological signs, upon investigation 11% (3/28) tigers and 30% (6/20) leopard tested positive for CDV [6]. Prey and diet preference study of wild feline indicate Tigers were restricted to deep forest meanwhile, Leopard frequently prey from buffer region and boundary of Bardia national parks [27]. This indicates that free-roaming dogs living near protected parks as potential prey and act as persistent reservoirs of infection. The likelihood of dog–wildlife interaction in such transitional areas is high, 85% livestock are hunted by Leopard in BNP [28].

We observed lower level of prevalence in BNP as compared to other conservation areas of Nepal (CNP and ACA), we believe this is due to environmental factor (CDV is prone in colder climate, extended viability in lower temperature) [17], unlike subtropical climate of BNP.

The age-related trend observed in our dataset aligns with assumptions that exposure accumulates over time as animals age. Essentially, older dogs have had more opportunities to encounter the virus, so seropositivity gradually increases with age. For instance, adult dogs had roughly 14 times the odds of seropositivity compared to pups [19]. The high prevalence also reflects dogs’ ecology in the Bardiya buffer zone. Most dogs here are owned but roam freely, scavenging around communal waste sites, carcasses, or local markets [18].

No statistically significant differences were detected across sex, body condition, or sampling subregion (buffer zone vs. outside buffer zone). Other factor (age, sex, location) associated with antibody variation was not observed in this study. When more than half of a population carries antibodies, the contrast between subgroups becomes blurred. A weak trend toward higher prevalence in older dogs (odds ratio about four, p ≈ 0.14) was visible. This null result should not be over-interpreted as “no effect”; it more likely reflects the combination of uniformly high exposure and limited sample size. Therefore, domestic dogs are the most abundant in the landscape, and our data imply that a large reservoir of CDV is circulating at the edges of Bardiya NP. Even if dogs are not the sole reservoir, since jackals or feral canids may also carry the virus, the mobile dog population substantially amplifies the overall risk [4, 14]. CDV likely persists through a multi-host network, in which domestic and wild carnivores collectively maintain viral circulation [23, 29].

Vaccination campaigns in suburban areas focus exclusively on rabies prevention; meanwhile, DHPPIL vaccine (Canine Distemper, Canine Adenovirus, Canine Parvovirus, Canine Parainfluenza, Leptospira interrogans) is largely confined to urban-owned pets. Therefore, the antibodies detected in our study almost certainly indicate infection-derived immunity rather than vaccine-induced protection. Low antibody titration is due to nutritional deficiency [25], old age and geriatric dogs are prone to low antibody titration [7]. The coexistence of high seroprevalence and no vaccination history strongly supports the belief of continuous natural transmission among the free-roaming dogs surrounding of protected park, such incidence has been reported in Bhutan [12].

Addressing virus spillover is crucial for wildlife protection and conservation. This can be done by increasing pathogen surveillance in human wildlife interaction areas. Awareness of biosecurity is essential. Slaughtering or disposal of domestic animal near wildlife zone should restrict, vaccination is effective methods of disease control in buffer zone animal.

Conclusion

The study provides scientific insight into the epidemiology of CDV in a buffer area landscape where domestic and wild carnivores coexist closely. The combination of high true prevalence, diverse antibody titers, and parallels with other conservation areas paints a clear picture: CDV is enzootic within the free-roaming dog population bordering Bardiya National Park. Being cross-sectional, the design captures only a snapshot of exposure and cannot reveal when the infection occurred or whether animals were infectious at the time of sampling. To understand its broader ecological impact, future studies should integrate molecular detection, longitudinal sampling, and wildlife monitoring. Such coordinated approaches would clarify the movement of CDV across host species and help guide targeted vaccination and management strategies aimed at safeguarding both domestic and wild carnivores in Nepal’s protected ecosystems. The ELISA assay used was specific for IgG detection and therefore identifies past exposure rather than active infection.
