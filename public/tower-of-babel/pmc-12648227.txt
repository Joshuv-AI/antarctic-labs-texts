Barriers and facilitators affecting nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide in general hospitals in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Shafeah Aljedaani, Mona El-Bilsha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide is a growing public health concern in Saudi Arabia, where rates have shown a gradual increase over recent decades. Nurses working in general hospital settings often encounter patients at risk but may face barriers that limit their ability to assess and prevent suicide effectively. Understanding these barriers and facilitators is essential to improve nurses’ preparedness and strengthening institutional suicide prevention efforts.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the barriers and facilitators influencing nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide in general hospitals in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, and to identify factors associated with these perceptions.MethodsA quantitative, descriptive cross-sectional design was employed in three major government hospitals in Jeddah between April and May 2025. A structured self-administered questionnaire was developed based on the Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) framework and validated through exploratory factor analysis using a randomly split dataset (200 cases for instrument validation and 347 cases for main analysis). The two-factor structure (barriers and facilitators) demonstrated strong construct validity (KMO = 0.962, Bartlett’s χ2 = 9185.382, p <0.001) and explained 74.29% of the total variance. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and perceptions, while independent-samples t-tests and multiple linear regression analyses examined predictors of perceived barriers and facilitators.ResultsAmong 347 nurses in the main analysis, the highest-rated barriers were limited time and heavy patient load (M = 3.81, SD = 0.96) and inadequate training (M = 3.72, SD = 0.99). The strongest facilitator was adequate training and continuing education (M = 3.80, SD = 1.05). Regression analyses showed that education level, total years of experience, and years in the current position significantly predicted perceived barriers (F = 12.16, p <0.001), explaining 9.6% of the variance (R2 = 0.096), and of facilitators (F = 6.286, p <0.001), explaining 5.2% of the variance (R2 = 0.052). Nurses without suicide-prevention training perceived more barriers (p = 0.004), whereas those with prior experience caring for suicidal patients reported greater awareness of both barriers and facilitators (p <0.05).ConclusionNurses’ capability, opportunity, and motivation to engage in suicide prevention appear to be influenced by workload pressures, limited training, and organizational factors. Strengthening structured education, implementing standardized assessment protocols, promoting interdisciplinary collaboration, and improving staffing support may enhance nurses’ preparedness and confidence in suicide risk assessment and prevention within general hospital settings.

Background

Suicide is a significant global public health issue, with over 720,000 individuals dying by suicide each year worldwide (WHO, 2025). According to the latest official estimate by the World Health Organization (updated January 2024), the suicide mortality rate in Saudi Arabia was 0.9 per 100,000 population in 2021 based on data trends from 2000 to 2021 (WHO, 2024). Additionally, Saudi Arabia showed an average annual increase in suicide mortality of +2.8% from 2000 to 2019, placing it among the 26 countries with the most significant rise in that period (Ilic & Ilic, 2022). The Saudi National Mental Health Survey provides nationally representative data on suicide-related behaviors among citizens aged 15 to 65. According to the survey, the lifetime prevalence rates are 4.90% for suicidal ideation, 1.78% for suicide planning, and 1.46% for suicide attempts (Altwaijri et al., 2024). However, it is suggested that the actual number of suicide cases may be higher than reported due to underreporting or lack of systematic documentation, highlighting the need for more comprehensive surveillance systems (Altwaijri et al., 2024; Hodges et al., 2025).

Research indicates that a substantial proportion of individuals who die by suicide had recent contact with healthcare services, particularly in non-psychiatric settings such as general hospitals. Notably, approximately 60.9% had such contact in the month preceding their death (Kootbodien et al., 2024; Laanani et al., 2020). This indicates missed opportunities for early identification and intervention. Suicide has a profound impact on individuals, families, and communities, imposing substantial psychological, social, and economic burdens, including hospital costs, lost productivity, and significant emotional and professional strain on healthcare providers, often compounded by limited postvention support (Creuzé et al., 2022; Jupina et al., 2024).

These realities highlight the importance of equipping frontline professionals, particularly nurses, with education on inpatient suicide prevention, as it positively influences their knowledge, attitudes, and skills. In addition, providing appropriate tools and institutional support is essential to enable them to play an effective role in suicide prevention (Xue et al., 2020). Nurses in general hospitals are in a pivotal position to detect and respond to suicide risk; however, their ability to assess and prevent suicide is shaped not only by personal motivation or clinical experience but also by a complex interplay of barriers and facilitators at the individual, organizational, and systemic levels (Darnell et al., 2023; Vandewalle et al., 2020).

Understanding the interrelation of barriers and facilitators in suicide prevention practices among nurses is well supported by the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation—Behavior), which provides a robust framework for analyzing the dynamic relationships among these components. Developed by Michie et al. (2011), the COM-B model offers a theoretical foundation for understanding behavior change within healthcare contexts: Capability refers to their knowledge and skills related to suicide risk assessment; Opportunity encompasses external factors such as institutional support, available time, and resources; and Motivation includes personal attitudes, beliefs, and intentions towards suicide prevention (Johnson et al., 2024; Michie et al., 2011; West & Michie, 2020).

In general hospital settings, the COM-B model provides a valuable framework for examining the barriers and facilitators that affect nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide. Capability-related barriers may include limited mental health training, insufficient skills in suicide risk assessment, and persistent misconceptions—such as the erroneous belief that asking patients about suicidal thoughts may provoke suicidal behavior (Alonzo & Zapata Pratto, 2021; Bérdi & Cramer, 2024). Understanding these capability-related factors is essential to enhancing nurses’ engagement in effective suicide prevention practices.

Many nurses in general hospitals report feeling unprepared to manage instances of suicidal ideation, primarily due to gaps in undergraduate education and limited exposure to psychiatric settings (Moser et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2025). This sense of unpreparedness is particularly pronounced among emergency room nursing staff, who often feel unprepared to assist patients experiencing suicidal thoughts, underscoring the need for ongoing professional training in this area (Moser et al., 2020). In contrast to capability-related barriers, opportunity-related barriers are typically structural and environmental in nature. These include high patient loads, time constraints, lack of privacy for sensitive conversations, and the absence of institutional policies or protocols for suicide risk assessment (Turner et al., 2023).

From a motivational perspective, nurses’ attitudes and confidence are critical determinants of their engagement in suicide prevention. Intentions to intervene are shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. However, fears of legal liability, emotional burden, and the stigma associated with suicide may undermine motivation and lead to avoidant behaviors (Langster et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024). Despite these barriers, several facilitators exist that can enhance nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide. These include targeted training programs, ongoing professional education, the implementation of clear clinical protocols, interdisciplinary collaboration, and strong leadership support (Sufrate Sorzano et al., 2023). In addition, nursing interventions—such as the use of coping tools, behavioral strategies, and the development of therapeutic partnerships—have proven effective in managing and preventing suicidal behavior in patients. Moreover, direct experience with suicidal patients, sufficient knowledge, a positive attitude toward mental health care, and a supportive institutional culture further strengthen nurses’ motivation and confidence in caring for individuals at risk of suicide (Sufrate Sorzano et al., 2023; Van Bergen & Henseler, 2025).

A review of the literature reveals four critical gaps that this study intends to address. Most prior research has focused on psychiatric settings rather than general hospitals, where patients at risk may go unrecognized. There has also been a limited distinction between different types of barriers, restricting understanding of the specific factors that hinder effective suicide assessment and prevention. Furthermore, there is a lack of region-specific research in Saudi Arabia despite the increasing national concern about suicide (Altwaijri et al., 2024). Finally, insufficient attention has been given to the role of nurses in general hospital settings related to suicide assessment and prevention. In response to these gaps, this study focuses specifically on general hospitals, examines barriers in detail to enable more targeted interventions, and generates locally relevant evidence from general hospitals in Saudi Arabia to ensure applicability to the national healthcare context. Importantly, it considers both barriers and facilitators together, providing a comprehensive understanding that can guide the development of targeted interventions.

The present study, therefore, aims to explore the barriers and facilitators that affect nurses' ability to assess and prevent suicide among patients in general hospitals. The objectives are to identify the barriers that hinder nurses from effectively engaging in suicide risk assessment and prevention, determine the facilitators that support their involvement in these practices, and develop practical, evidence-based recommendations to guide the design of targeted interventions, educational strategies, and institutional policies aimed at enhancing nurses’ preparedness and competence in suicide prevention.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a quantitative, descriptive cross-sectional design to explore the barriers and facilitators influencing nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide in general hospital settings. The study was conducted in Jeddah, located in the Makkah region of Saudi Arabia. Data were collected from inpatient units across three major government hospitals: King Fahad Hospital, King Abdullah Medical Complex, and Rabigh General Hospital. Participating nurses were drawn from departments including Intensive Care, Emergency, Postpartum, Oncology, Psychiatry, Geriatrics, Dialysis, and Medical Units. These departments were selected because they are more likely to encounter patients experiencing emotional distress or suicidal ideation, conditions associated with psychological vulnerability such as serious illness, crises, or major life transitions (Nafilyan et al., 2023).

Sample/Participants

The study targeted registered nurses working in the hospitals mentioned above. The total nursing populations were 1,315 at King Fahad Hospital, 890 at King Abdullah Medical Complex, and 234 at Rabigh General Hospital. The minimum sample size required was calculated using the Raosoft sample size calculator (Raosoft, 2004), indicating a need for at least 332 participants. A convenience sampling method was used to recruit eligible nurses based on availability and willingness to participate (Dhabare & Mathew, 2021).

A total of 620 questionnaires were distributed, and 600 were returned. After excluding incomplete responses, 547 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 87.9% (N = 547). To enhance psychometric rigor and prevent overfitting, the dataset was randomly split into two subsets: 200 cases were used for exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and 347 cases were used for descriptive and inferential analyses. Inclusion criteria required participants to be registered nurses with at least six months of clinical experience, directly involved in inpatient care, and working in one of the selected units. Nurses in administrative, outpatient, or non-clinical roles and those without professional nursing qualifications were excluded.

Instruments

In this study, a structured, self-administered questionnaire was developed to assess nurses’ perceptions of suicide risk assessment and prevention in general hospital settings. The instrument comprised three parts: (1) demographic and professional characteristics, (2) a barriers scale with 20 items, and (3) a facilitators scale with 15 items. Item content was informed by prior literature and expert consultation to ensure content validity. The COM-B model was used as the conceptual framework to guide interpretation, emphasizing capability, opportunity, and motivation as determinants of behavior. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale. For the barriers scale, response options ranged from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating stronger perceived barriers. For the facilitators scale, options ranged from 1 (“Not helpful at all”) to 5 (“Extremely helpful”), with higher scores indicating stronger perceived facilitators.

Pilot Testing. A pilot study was conducted with 30 nurses who met the same inclusion criteria as the main study to assess clarity, feasibility, and reliability. Participants completed the questionnaire independently and provided feedback on item comprehension and relevance. Minor linguistic adjustments were made based on feedback to ensure cultural and contextual appropriateness. Preliminary reliability and validity analyses demonstrated excellent psychometric properties. Item–total correlations ranged from 0.710 to 0.929 for the barriers scale and from 0.811 to 0.949 for the facilitators scale, confirming strong construct validity. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.980 and 0.984 for the barriers and facilitators scales, respectively. Split-half reliability coefficients were 0.980 (Spearman–Brown) and 0.979 (Guttman) for the barriers scale, and 0.968 (Spearman–Brown) and 0.955 (Guttman) for the facilitators scale. The self-validity coefficients (√α) were 0.989 and 0.991, respectively, indicating excellent internal consistency across both scales.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation on the 200-case subsample to determine the factor structure of the instrument (Table 1). Sampling adequacy was confirmed by a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value of 0.962, which exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.60, indicating suitability for factor analysis. Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was significant (χ2(595) = 9185.382, p <0.001), confirming that correlations among items were sufficient for extraction.

Two components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained, together explaining 74.29% of the total variance. The first component (Barriers) accounted for 60.14% of the variance, and the second component (Facilitators) explained 14.15%. After rotation, the variance was distributed more evenly (Barriers = 37.73%, Facilitators = 36.56%), confirming a clear two-factor solution corresponding to the conceptual dimensions. Items 1–20 loaded strongly on Component 1 (Barriers), with loadings ranging from 0.679 to 0.846, while items 21–35 loaded strongly on Component 2 (Facilitators), with loadings ranging from 0.821 to 0.901. Minimal cross-loadings indicated distinct and independent constructs, confirming the instrument’s construct validity and factor stability. Although the exploratory factor analysis yielded two clearly defined components with excellent psychometric properties, it is important to note that the factor structure should ideally be confirmed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in future studies with independent samples to establish construct stability. The use of PCA with Varimax rotation was appropriate for preliminary exploration, but it does not test model fit. Both components assessing perceived barriers and facilitators also demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.98). The very high Cronbach’s alpha values indicate excellent internal consistency but may also reflect item redundancy. Future refinement could consider reducing highly overlapping items to enhance parsimony without compromising reliability.

Data Collection

Data were collected between 1 April and 30 May 2025 through both online and in-person distribution of the structured questionnaire under the supervision of the Research Department of the Jeddah Second Health Cluster. Eligible nurses were informed about the study’s purpose, confidentiality, and voluntary participation, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Anonymity and data security were strictly maintained throughout the research process.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Construct validity was assessed using item–total correlations, while reliability was examined using Cronbach’s alpha, split-half reliability, and self-validity coefficients. EFA with Varimax rotation was conducted to confirm factor structure. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic data and mean scale scores. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare mean scores of barriers and facilitators by prior suicide-prevention training and experience with at-risk patients. Multiple linear regression analyses examined whether education level, total years of nursing experience, and years in the current position predicted perceived barriers and facilitators. All statistical assumptions for t-tests and regression (including linearity, homoscedasticity, independence of errors, and normality of residuals) were checked and met the acceptable thresholds. No influential outliers were detected using standardized residuals (±3 SD).

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Directorate of Health Affairs, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (Approval Date: 13/02/2025; IRB Log No: A02156). Participation was voluntary, and all ethical standards for research involving human subjects were strictly followed.

Results

Among the 347 participating nurses, most were female (80.98%) and held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (61.67%). The largest proportion of participants worked at King Fahad Hospital (46.97%). More than half (57.64%) had worked in their current position for over five years, and approximately one-quarter (26.51%) reported more than 15 years of total nursing experience. The majority of nurses were staff nurses (73.20%) working across diverse departments, most frequently in medical (22.19%) and emergency (21.61%) units. Over half (56.48%) reported no prior experience working with patients at risk of suicide, and 61.96% had not received suicide risk management training in the past two years (Table 2).

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study explored the perceived barriers and facilitators influencing nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide in general hospitals. The findings highlight critical factors that impact suicide assessment and prevention practices in clinical settings. Nurses identified a range of elements that could function as either barriers or facilitators, depending on their presence or absence in the work environment. Education and training were perceived as central to effective suicide risk assessment and prevention. Participants reported that access to structured training and continuing education increased their confidence and competence. In contrast, inadequate training or limited exposure to suicide prevention education was stated as a major barrier, contributing to uncertainty and discomfort when managing suicidal patients. Nurses without prior suicide prevention training reported perceiving more barriers than those who had received such training. These findings align with previous studies emphasizing the importance of continuous education in strengthening nurses' capabilities for effective suicide assessment and prevention (Davidson et al., 2020; James et al., 2025; Litta et al., 2024).

Staffing and workloads emerged as dual-impact factors. When staffing levels were adequate and workloads manageable, nurses could devote sufficient time to thorough patient assessments and timely interventions. However, limited time and heavy patient loads cause significant difficulties in performing comprehensive suicide risk assessments. This concern was further compounded by staff shortages and high turnover rates, as workforce deficits have been linked to higher youth suicide rates (Hoffmann et al., 2023). This was cited by nurses as undermining continuity of care and reducing their ability to monitor patients consistently. Such findings echo those of Darnell et al. (2023), who found that time constraints, workload pressure, and staffing disruptions significantly hinder nurses’ capacity to assess suicide risk and deliver preventive interventions.

Organizational support and access to mental health professionals were also critical. Participants cited a lack of administrative support, limited access to specialists, and unclear referral pathways as significant barriers. However, when present, these factors served as strong facilitators—supporting Elzinga et al. (2020) who emphasis on integrated mental health services. Personal psychological strain, including burnout and compassion fatigue, was another reported barrier. Emotional exhaustion was considered a limiting factor in nurses’ readiness to conduct suicide risk assessments, especially in high-pressure environments. Similar concerns were noted by Kelly (2020), who described how emotional fatigue impairs performance in emotionally demanding clinical tasks.

Diagnostic challenges also hindered suicide risk assessment. Participants reported difficulty distinguishing suicidal ideation from symptoms of other mental health conditions, such as depression, anxiety, and self-harm. Symptom overlap can mask suicidal ideation, a finding supported by previous literature (Sarwar & Asmat, 2025; Vandewalle et al., 2020). The current study highlighted the availability of valid and reliable tools for assessing suicide risk as a critical facilitator in clinical practice. Nurses emphasized that standardized instruments reduce reliance on personal judgment, thereby improving the consistency and accuracy of suicide risk assessments. This view is supported by Coman et al. (2024), who noted that validated tools can significantly enhance the early identification and management of suicidal behaviors, especially among high-risk populations.

From a structural standpoint, the availability of standardized protocols and clinical guidelines was identified as a key facilitator, supporting consistent and effective suicide risk assessment and prevention. Participants confirmed its importance in ensuring uniform clinical procedures. This finding aligns with Rassy et al. (2022), who highlighted the utility of structured protocols in improving the quality and consistency of mental health interventions for individuals at risk of suicide in emergency departments. Although follow-up contact with patients after discharge was rated the lowest among facilitators, it still received a relatively high mean score (M = 3.68), reflecting strong agreement support for its role in suicide prevention. Post-discharge follow-ups are seen as an essential preventive measure to reduce the risk of suicide, as found by Kehoe et al. (2023), who demonstrated that post-suicidal care delivered through a multidisciplinary model can significantly enhance suicide prevention outcomes.

Demographic analysis revealed that education level and years of nursing experience were significantly associated with nurses’ perceptions of both barriers and facilitators. These findings are consistent with those of Gillette et al. (2020), who found that increased clinical exposure and educational attainment enhance awareness and understanding of factors influencing suicide risk assessment, regardless of job position. Finally, nurses with prior experience caring for suicidal patients reported significantly higher scores for both barriers and facilitators. This suggests that direct clinical experience heightens awareness of both the system-related barriers challenges and effective strategies in suicide risk assessment, as supported by Thangada and Kasoju (2024).

Limitations

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. Second, reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce social desirability bias, which can affect the accuracy of reported behaviors or attitudes. Third, the use of convenience sampling and the inclusion of only three government hospitals within the Jeddah Second Health Cluster restricts the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the cross-sectional and self-reported nature of the data limits the ability to infer causation, and the regression models explain only a modest amount of variance in perceived barriers and facilitators. Finally, ordinal predictors such as education level and years of experience were treated as continuous variables in regression analyses; although common in applied nursing research, this may slightly limit interpretive precision. Future studies should employ longitudinal or mixed-methods designs to explore additional predictors, such as organizational culture, psychological safety, and team climate, that may better account for observed differences.

Implications

The findings of this study provide important insights for healthcare administrators, nurse educators, and policymakers seeking to improve suicide prevention practices in general hospitals. The results highlight the need to prioritize both structural and educational strategies that address the specific barriers identified by nurses. First, institutional workload management should be addressed, as limited time and heavy patient load were the most frequently reported barriers. Optimizing staffing levels and ensuring equitable workload distribution would allow nurses sufficient time to conduct comprehensive suicide risk assessments.

Second, structured and continuous education should be integrated into professional development programs. Adequate training and continuing education were the strongest facilitators, underscoring the importance of ongoing skill development in suicide risk identification, communication, and culturally sensitive care. Educational interventions should include scenario-based simulations, case discussions, and refreshers to sustain confidence and competence over time. Third, standardized clinical protocols should be embedded in nursing practice. The availability of clear suicide risk assessment and management guidelines, standardized documentation tools, and post-discharge follow-up procedures can help ensure consistency and accuracy in care across units and shifts. Fourth, interdisciplinary collaboration with mental health professionals must be strengthened. Nurses reported that limited access to mental health specialists and unclear referral pathways impede effective risk management. Establishing structured consultation systems or liaison models could improve care coordination and support timely interventions for at-risk patients.

Finally, organizational and managerial support should be reinforced through strong leadership engagement, regular debriefing opportunities, and peer support programs. Such initiatives may mitigate burnout, compassion fatigue, and emotional strain—factors that nurses identified as barriers to effective suicide prevention. Collectively, these implications suggest that enhancing nurses’ capability (through education and tools), opportunity (through supportive systems and staffing), and motivation (through leadership and workplace culture) can strengthen their preparedness and confidence in assessing and preventing suicide, consistent with the COM-B model framework.

Conclusion

This study identified key barriers and facilitators influencing nurses’ ability to assess and prevent suicide in general hospitals in Saudi Arabia. The most prominent barriers included limited time and heavy patient load, inadequate training, and organizational constraints, while the most influential facilitators were adequate training, standardized protocols, and supportive leadership. Nurses’ education level, clinical experience, and tenure in their current positions were associated with differences in perceived barriers and facilitators, highlighting the importance of professional development and experiential learning. Overall, the findings suggest that nurses’ capability, opportunity, and motivation to engage in suicide prevention are shaped by a combination of individual competencies and systemic factors. Enhancing structured and ongoing education, implementing standardized clinical guidelines, strengthening interdisciplinary collaboration, and improving staffing and administrative support may collectively improve nurses’ readiness and confidence in conducting suicide risk assessments and preventive interventions. By addressing these interrelated components, healthcare institutions can create a more supportive environment that empowers nurses to play a proactive and effective role in suicide prevention within general hospital settings.
