Classification of dental implants using supervised deep learning from dental radiographs: A scoping review
Authors: Tamires Santos-Melo, Gabriela Costa da Silva, Lizandra Esper Serrano, Diana Amado Baptista dos Santos, Mayla Kezy Silva Teixeira, Daniel de Moraes Telles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis scoping review examined the evidence on the use of supervised deep learning models for the classification of dental implants using radiographic images.Materials and MethodsA preliminary search was conducted in PubMed, Google Scholar, PROSPERO, JBI Evidence Synthesis, and the Open Science Framework, identifying a small number of relevant records. A comprehensive search was subsequently performed across 7 databases using adapted strategies without filters. Studies were included if they evaluated implant classification using supervised deep learning models applied to panoramic or periapical radiographs. Studies were excluded if they did not involve implant classification, were review articles, involved children, were unavailable in full text, or did not apply artificial intelligence methods. Data extraction was conducted by 2 independent reviewers, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis.ResultsOf 274 records, 9 studies met the inclusion criteria. Studies published between 2020 and 2024 evaluated deep learning and machine learning approaches for dental implant identification and classification from radiographic images. A range of models was applied, predominantly convolutional neural networks. Dataset sizes ranged from 355 to 156,965 radiographs and included multiple implant brands. Few studies addressed ethical considerations related to recent data protection regulations.ConclusionThis scoping review indicates that most deep learning-based approaches to implant classification using dental radiographs primarily rely on implant brand or manufacturer as the labeling strategy. This reliance may limit model generalizability and long-term applicability due to the frequent discontinuation of implant systems. Future studies should focus on intrinsic radiographic characteristics, including macrogeometry and prosthetic connection types.

Introduction

The discovery of osseointegration by Per-Ingvar Brånemark marked a turning point in dentistry, establishing titanium implants as a widely adopted solution for oral rehabilitation.12 In recent years, the number and diversity of implant models and brands have increased substantially,3 making it increasingly difficult for clinicians to remain up to date as new systems are introduced.456 Consequently, the lack of effective techniques for implant identification has emerged as a significant concern in contemporary dental practice.7

Radiographic analysis for implant classification requires detailed evaluation of anatomical and geometric features, which is both time-consuming and dependent on advanced clinical expertise.89 In this context, supervised deep learning approaches, which rely on labeled data, have demonstrated promising performance, particularly in medical and dental image classification tasks.101112 In implantology specifically, these systems have shown a high capacity to identify and classify implants, in some cases outperforming less experienced clinicians.13

Given the emerging and heterogeneous nature of studies applying supervised deep learning to dental implant classification, a scoping review was considered the most appropriate methodological approach. Scoping reviews are particularly well suited to mapping existing evidence, clarifying key concepts, and identifying research gaps in areas where the literature is diverse and still evolving. Unlike systematic reviews, which aim to answer narrowly defined questions, scoping reviews allow for broader exploration of study designs, methodologies, and outcomes, thereby providing a comprehensive overview of the field.141516

In light of this, the primary objective of this scoping review is to examine the scientific evidence on the use of supervised deep learning models for the classification of dental implants using radiographic images.

Materials and Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews.17 The review protocol was registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/3GSXT).

The guiding question for this scoping review was developed using the PCC framework (Table 1): “What is the scientific evidence regarding the classification of dental implants using supervised deep learning models applied to dental radiographs?”

Study selection was guided by predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were included if they evaluated the classification of dental implants using dental radiographs and supervised deep learning models, operationally defined as the categorization of implants according to macrogeometric characteristics and/or prosthetic connection features identifiable on radiographic images. Studies were excluded if they did not meet these criteria, were review articles, involved children, were unavailable in full text, or did not specify the method used for implant identification.

A preliminary search was conducted in MEDLINE and Google Scholar. Subsequently, the OSF, PROSPERO, and JBI Evidence Synthesis platforms were searched for completed or ongoing reviews. A small number of records were identified as relevant to the topic of this review.

The main differences between this review and previously published reviews are the inclusion of a comprehensive literature search across 7 databases with strategies adapted to each database, the identification of an insufficient number of eligible studies to support a systematic review, and the emphasis on ethical considerations in artificial intelligence development. The results of this preliminary search, conducted on August 5, 2024, are presented in Table 2.

The search strategy was designed to identify both published and unpublished studies and followed a 3-step approach. First, an initial PubMed search was performed to identify relevant articles on the topic. The text words used in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, as well as the index terms used to describe them, were then analyzed to develop a comprehensive search strategy. The finalized strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was subsequently adapted for each of the following databases: EMBASE (Elsevier), Virtual Health Library (VHL/BVS), Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science (Clarivate), Dentistry and Oral Sciences Source (DOSS; EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), and Google Scholar. To structure the search strategies, the MeSH (PubMed) and DeCS tools were used to identify relevant keywords and indexed terms. With respect to filters, only Google Scholar required adjustment because of its limited advanced search functionality. The search yielded 4,980 results; however, Google Scholar results were restricted to the first 4 pages, yielding 37 records. This restriction was based on the platform’s relevance-based ranking, which prioritizes the most pertinent and highly cited studies, whereas results beyond these pages are typically less specific and more likely to be duplicates or irrelevant to the research question. To minimize potential selection bias, the search strategy was complemented by additional databases to ensure comprehensive coverage of the relevant literature.

The detailed search strategies for each database, together with the search dates and the number of records retrieved, are presented in Table 3 to illustrate the comprehensiveness of the literature search.

After the search, all identified citations were collated and uploaded into Zotero 6.0/2022 (Corporation for Digital Scholarship, Falls Church, USA), and duplicates were removed. Following a pilot test, 2 independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer resolved the discrepancy.

Potentially relevant sources were then retrieved in full text, and their citation details were imported into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, USA). The full texts of the selected studies were assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by 2 or more independent reviewers. The results of the search and selection process are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.18

Data extraction for this scoping review was performed by 2 independent reviewers (GCS and LES) using a data extraction spreadsheet developed by the research team in Microsoft Excel in 2024. The spreadsheet was specifically designed to systematically collect and organize study information aligned with the review objectives, including the research aim, title, study design, image-processing steps, number of images, artificial intelligence methods, and classification labels.

In accordance with JBI guidance for scoping reviews, no formal critical appraisal of study quality was conducted. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a mapping of the available evidence.

Results

The included studies were published between 2020 and 2024 and were conducted primarily in Korea (5 studies), Japan (3 studies), and India (1 study). Most studies applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs), including VGG16, VGG19, GoogLeNet Inception-v3, ResNet architectures, and YOLOv3, for dental implant classification. Dataset sizes varied widely, ranging from 355 to 156,965 radiographs, reflecting a general trend toward the use of large image datasets for model training. Across all studies, implant brand or manufacturer was the most frequently used labeling strategy.

Study selection was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram, which provided a transparent overview of each stage of the process. A total of 334 records were initially identified through database searches (Table 3). After duplicate removal, 274 studies remained for title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 211 articles that did not meet the eligibility criteria. During full-text review, 13 studies were excluded with justification, leaving 9 studies for inclusion in the final synthesis. A detailed summary of this process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The results of data extraction are presented in Tables 4 and 5.

Nine relevant studies published between 2020 and 2024 were identified, most of which were conducted in Korea and Japan, with 1 study from India. These studies evaluated the use of deep learning and machine learning techniques for the identification and classification of dental implants based on radiographic images.

A study conducted by Park et al. (2023)19 in Korea and published in Scientific Reports used a large multicenter dataset to assess the accuracy of an automated deep learning algorithm for identifying and classifying different types of dental implant systems. Another Korean study by Lee et al. (2024)20 investigated the effectiveness of an artificial intelligence model for recognizing implant systems in low-quality dental radiographs, likewise using a large multicenter dataset (Scientific Reports).

Additional studies by Lee et al. in 202013 explored the performance of deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in classifying dental implants using both panoramic and periapical radiographs. One pilot study by Lee and Jeong, published in Medicine (2020),21 focused on the algorithm’s effectiveness, while another study published in Diagnostics (2020)13 compared the performance of an automated deep learning model with that of dental professionals in a multicenter setting.

In Japan, Sukegawa et al.222324 conducted 3 distinct studies between 2020 and 2022. In 2020, they evaluated CNNs with transfer learning and fine-tuning for dental implant classification on panoramic radiographs (Biomolecules).22 In 2021, they proposed a multitask deep learning model designed to classify both implant brands and treatment stages (Biomolecules).23 In 2022, they examined the performance of an attention branch network integrated with CNNs for the same purpose (PLoS One).24

An additional Korean study by Kim et al. (2022),25 published in Imaging Science in Dentistry, investigated the use of transfer learning in deep CNNs for implant fixture classification in a pilot study.

Finally, Benakatti et al. (2021)26 from India published a descriptive study in the Journal of Indian Prosthodontic Society that applied machine learning to identify dental implants based on shape using panoramic radiographs.

Collectively, these studies highlight growing international interest in the application of artificial intelligence models in implant dentistry, particularly for improving the accuracy of implant identification using conventional radiographic imaging.

The studies analyzed employed a variety of artificial intelligence models, with a particular emphasis on classical and advanced CNNs, such as VGG16, VGG19,22 GoogLeNet Inception-v3,21 and ResNet-50,2024 as well as approaches such as YOLOv3,25 alongside traditional machine learning algorithms, including support vector machines, k-nearest neighbors, XGBoost, and logistic regression. CNNs predominated, reflecting their effectiveness in radiographic image analysis.

Image pre-processing and post-processing typically involved region-of-interest cropping, conversion to JPEG or PNG format, anonymization, and data augmentation techniques such as rotation and adjustments in brightness, contrast, noise, and saturation. Specific software tools, including Neuro-T, ImageJ, and LabelImg, were used for labeling.

The number of radiographs varied considerably. Some studies used large datasets, such as 156,965 images from multiple centers and private clinics, whereas others relied on smaller samples, such as the YOLOv3 study, which analyzed 355 images. The datasets encompassed a range of implant brands, including Osstem, Dentium, Straumann, Dentsply, and Nobel Biocare, with up to 27 implant systems classified according to brand, diameter, and length.

Labeling was performed either by experts, such as periodontists and radiologists, or manually using specialized software. Labels were defined according to manufacturer, brand, and implant characteristics. Model validation used metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the curve, as well as comparisons with human experts.

Notably, none of the studies explicitly described measures to ensure data security, such as procedures implemented to protect patient information. Although some studies referred to image anonymization, not all clearly stated whether anonymization was consistently applied.

Discussion

One of the main findings of this review is that most of the analyzed studies used implant brand as the primary feature for training artificial intelligence models. However, in the clinical and commercial context of implant dentistry, brands and models are often discontinued or withdrawn from the market, which may complicate the long-term maintenance of implants with long service lives. The literature has suggested that training artificial intelligence models on implant macrogeometric features—such as thread design, body shape, prosthetic platform, and other fundamental structures—may provide a more stable approach.26 These characteristics, grounded in principles established since the Brånemark era, continue to serve as key references in the practice and teaching of implant dentistry and have also been incorporated into artificial intelligence-assisted tools.

Image quality directly influences the diagnostic accuracy and performance of artificial intelligence models. High-resolution images enable clearer visualization and facilitate identification.27 By contrast, low-quality images impair the training of artificial intelligence models, which depend on sufficiently clear data to recognize patterns. For this reason, ensuring high-quality images is essential, particularly in contexts involving the use of artificial intelligence.19

According to Lee et al. (2024),20 low-quality and distorted radiographs cannot be evaluated effectively, highlighting the need to expand image datasets. Similarly, Kim et al. (2022)25 emphasized that both the quantity and quality of data are fundamental to the learning process of artificial intelligence systems.

Data volume also plays a crucial role in model development. Large databases provide greater image variability, which in turn strengthens algorithm performance. Thus, maintaining a balance between image quantity and quality is essential to ensure the scientific validity and practical applicability of artificial intelligence models.13

Artificial intelligence models have gained prominence in dentistry as tools that support diagnosis and clinical decision-making. Although specialists possess advanced clinical knowledge and interpretive skills, trained algorithms have demonstrated comparable or even superior accuracy in the automated identification of dental implants. This is particularly valuable in cases in which the clinician is unfamiliar with the implant system used by the patient.21

The study by Lee et al. (2020),13 which compared the performance of artificial intelligence with that of expert clinicians, reported that artificial intelligence achieved an accuracy of 95.05%, whereas 5 experts achieved a mean accuracy of 37.2% when classifying the analyzed implant systems.21

Automating this process not only reduces the time required for analysis, because manual identification often requires extensive comparison with catalogs, but also reduces the margin of error and improves efficiency in high-demand clinical settings. Recent studies have shown that artificial intelligence-based systems can identify implant brands and models with high accuracy, thereby contributing to both clinical efficiency and patient safety.22 Thus, artificial intelligence should be regarded as a tool that complements professional expertise and promotes greater integration between technology and clinical knowledge.

In addition to the technical challenges, the use of artificial intelligence in dentistry raises important ethical issues that remain insufficiently discussed in the literature. Macrì et al. (2024)28 emphasized the importance of anonymizing radiographic images, including metadata, to safeguard privacy and protect patient data. The lack of in-depth ethical reflection represents a concerning gap, particularly given the growing implementation of these technologies in clinical practice. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidance on Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health (2021),29 the ethical application of artificial intelligence must consider fundamental principles such as transparency, accountability, equity, protection of autonomy, explainability, and privacy. However, most studies to date have prioritized technical aspects, such as model accuracy and sensitivity, while giving limited attention to the ethical and social implications. Responsibility for the use of algorithms should be shared by developers and health professionals, with clear mechanisms for oversight and accountability in cases of failure or harm, as recommended by the WHO.

The WHO classifies machine learning models as supervised or unsupervised. In supervised models, the training data are labeled, enabling the model to learn predictions based on known outputs. In unsupervised models, the data are unlabeled, and the system independently identifies patterns. This review focused on supervised models, consistent with most of the available literature. For example, Sukegawa et al. (2022)24 applied supervised CNNs such as ResNet using images labeled by implant brand and model. Likewise, Park et al. (2023)19 trained algorithms on panoramic radiographs labeled according to manufacturer, brand, and prosthetic connection type. The widespread adoption of supervised models highlights both their effectiveness and their alignment with ethical principles, because the data can be anonymized throughout the collection, training, and validation phases. By contrast, unsupervised models, which handle large volumes of unstructured data from diverse sources, may present greater risks of misuse when applied without consent or adequate anonymization.

One limitation of this review is that, as shown in Table 3, the searches were conducted on 2 different dates. This was necessary to refine the search expressions in collaboration with the librarian because the initial strategies produced errors in the search results. In addition, the total number of retrieved studies remained low despite these adjustments. The authors suggest that this may be explained by the novelty of the research area, which still requires further development and additional studies.
