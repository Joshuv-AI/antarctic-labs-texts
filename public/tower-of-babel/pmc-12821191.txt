Inverse-designed plasmonic biosensors with LSPR-SPP-wood anomaly coupling enhanced for biomolecular analysis
Authors: Yihui Yang, Youqian Chen, Rui Li, Wen Li, Mingqian Chen, Yanan Li, Wenjun Hu, Gang Logan Liu, Liping Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Investigating molecular interactions is crucial for advancing biological research and therapeutic discovery. Traditional analytical techniques often face limitations in sensitivity, quantification accuracy, and simplicity. Metasurfaces support resonances that are widely explored both for far-field wavefront shaping and for near-field sensing. Here, we introduce an innovative inverse-designed multilayer metasurface plasmon resonance (IDMM-SPR) sensor that overcomes these challenges. Using a double-objective optimization method that combines numerical simulations and machine learning, we developed an IDMM-SPR sensor featuring an optimized periodic nanocup array. This design yields unparalleled sensitivity and stability by harnessing collective resonances—including localized SPR, Wood’s anomalies, and the Bloch wave SPR—which collectively enhance the sensing performance to enable the analysis of ultra-high affinity and low molecular weight interactions. The sensor achieves a figure of merit (FoM) of 26.3 and a detection limit (LOD) for C-reactive protein (CRP) as low as 0.84 ng/mL. Its compatibility with microplate absorbance readers and imaging detection makes it highly practical. The IDMM-SPR sensor shows exceptional promise for high-throughput direct molecular fishing, drug development, and disease diagnosis, offering a powerful tool for real-time, label-free affinity detection and quantitative analysis of biomolecular interactions.Graphical Abstract Inverse-Designed Plasmonic Biosensors with LSPR-SPP-Wood Anomaly Coupling Enhanced for Biomolecular Analysis. Introduction

Molecular interactions underpin every aspect of cellular life and higher-order biological processes [1]. Detection of molecular interactions is the foundation for many important biotechnology applications in society and industry, such as drug discovery, diagnostics, and DNA sequencing [2]. The above problems usually need to be addressed at different levels of molecular complexity. Conventional assays such as ELISA [3], Western blot [4], FRET [5] or HPLC [6] can reveal tight interactions, but cannot resolve individual association or dissociation rates, are labour-intensive, low-throughput and require multiple washing and labelling steps [7]. The label-free, real-time, and high-throughput molecular interaction analysis methods include biolayer interferometry (BLI) [8], surface plasmon resonance (SPR) [9], and localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) [10]. Among them, SPR is the most widely adopted platform for studying antigen–antibody, drug–target, protein–nucleic acid and protein–protein interactions. Nevertheless, existing label-free techniques still face major challenges when coping with ultra-high-affinity interactions (pM range, demanding exceptional sensor stability) or with small-molecule analytes (requiring superior sensitivity) [11, 12]. There is thus an urgent need for next-generation technologies that combine higher sensitivity, specificity and instrument compatibility to satisfy the evolving requirements of modern biology.

The SPR technology relies on attenuated total internal reflection to measure changes in the refractive index (RI) at the sensor surface, which are induced by variations in surface loading [13]. With the advancement and refinement of micro/nanofabrication technologies, materials science, and related theories, the field of micro-nano optics has witnessed numerous intriguing discoveries and applications. A growing number of novel devices based on optical and micro-nano technologies have been developed for various fields. Examples include plasmonic logic gates with dual optical communication bands designed on a dielectric–metal–dielectric structure [14], and directional coupler switches realized on monolithic lithium niobate for high-efficiency optical communication applications [15]. Additionally, a range of high-performance optical sensors with unique designs have been developed, such as resonator sensors based on low-cost silica–titania [16], nanosensors based on metal–insulator–metal (MIM) waveguides [17], and dielectric nanohole array metasurfaces [18], among others.

Metasurface plasmon resonance (Meta-SPR) technology is an innovative technique compared to SPR, leveraging metasurface structures and metamaterials to induce resonance of free electrons and manipulate light transmission. Thus, there is no need for special angles to excite surface plasmon resonance. Metasurfaces are sub-wavelength-spaced arrays of optical scatterers, which have been widely used to overcome the limitations of traditional optical devices [19]. The constituent materials of metasurfaces can be metals or dielectrics, which are classified as plasmonic or dielectric metasurfaces, respectively. The optical response of metal metasurfaces is typically controlled by local plasmon polariton resonances induced by incident electromagnetic waves [20]. Our previous research [21] shows that by transforming two-dimensional planar thin films into uniform nano cup structures with three-dimensional structures, biosensors are endowed with unique optical properties, thus overcoming the problems of traditional SPR requiring bulky and precise optical instruments. This makes it possible to create desktop, easy-to-use MetaSPR molecular interaction instruments to meet the requirements of individuals and laboratories. However, due to the sensor’s sensitivity, many of its application ranges are still limited.

In traditional sensor development, it is often necessary to repeatedly fabricate and test various sensor parameters to determine the optimal sensor specifications. To address this inefficiency, several machine learning-based approaches have been introduced to accelerate the design process [22–24]. Moreover, conventional surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensors typically rely on detecting peak shifts and resonance angles to collect signal responses [25], which necessitates the use of specialized detection equipment. This significantly increases the development costs and time. In this work, we designed and defined the target function to inversely design a plasmonic metasurface with high optical density (OD) response and peak shift response, based on multilayer metal materials and a periodic nanocup array using machine learning. The resulting high-performance metasurface sensing chip is compatible with virtually all commercial microplate absorbance readers and spectroscopic instruments. Moreover, the metasurface exhibits high sensitivity to resonance peak shifts, which also induces color changes. Therefore, it is also highly suitable for colorimetric sensing applications. As shown in Fig. 1a, we considerate the both response of OD response and peak shift response. Subsequently, we employed a combination of three-dimensional finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations and machine learning algorithms to search for the optimal parameters within the defined parameter space. This optimization process focused on key metasurface parameters, including the period, radius, height, and thickness of the multilayer metal materials (Fig. 1b). The excellent performance of the designed sensor was verified through manufacturing and experiments, and the optimal parameters were determined (Fig. 1c). Afterward, photolithography, nanoimprint lithography, electron beam evaporation, and chip functionalization techniques can be used on a large scale, quickly, and inexpensively to produce low-cost wafer-level IDMM-SPR chip sensors. By assembling with 96-well or 384-well plates (Fig. 1d), a detection device suitable for almost all microplate absorbance reader instruments can be produced, which can conveniently, quickly, high-throughput, and accurately detect molecular interactions and obtain real-time detection data (Fig. 1e). The protocol is validated through three ultra-high affinity antibodies and Quercetin affinity evaluation. Concurrently, the rapid, label-free detection of low concentrations of CRP has showcased the quantitative analysis capability of the developed sensor. Overall, these biosensors offer several advantages, including high selectivity and sensitivity to analytes, fast and in situ real-time monitoring, label-free operation, and reduced costs for target analysis. The research conducted by this institute will significantly enhance the application of metasurface sensors in areas such as drug development and disease diagnosis.

Simulation of multilayer metasurface nanocup array sensor chip

FDTD Solutions (Lumerical Inc., FTDT solutions, Canada) was used to conduct simulations of the optical experiments of our nanocup biosensor chip to explore the optimal structures and illustrate the experiment results. Cr, Ag, and Au three-layer metal layers were placed on the PET film to build the multilayer metasurface modeling. An x-direction polarization plane-wave light source placed on the side of the metal surface was used to excite surface plasmon polarization (SPP) of the metasurface nanocup array. Perfectly matched layer (PML) boundary conditions along the perpendicular z-axis and x- and y-axes (each parallel to the array edge) were used with periodic boundary conditions. The mesh grid size was set at 3 nm in all axes, which was verified by convergence tests and adequate accuracy for the model of the plasmonic effects of the metal. The simulation time was set to 1000 fs. The transmission intensity was monitored at a plane 800 nm from the surface of the metal film on the PET side. Then, we change the surface RI and calculate the sensitivity we defined to evaluate the sensing performance in different RI solutions. Additional model-validation steps are detailed in Supplementary Information S2 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Particle swarm optimization calculation

To design an ultrasensitive metasurface sensor, we employed the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method in conjunction with the particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm. PSO is a robust population-based stochastic evolutionary computation technique inspired by the social behavior and dynamic movements of animal groups (referred to as “particles”) as they search for optimal solutions in a given space. In this study, PSO was utilized to optimize the structural parameters of the multilayer nanocup metasurface sensor to achieve the highest figure of merit (FoM) as defined in Sect. "Materials". To simulate different refractive index environments, we selected two refractive indices, 1.3333 and 1.3397, for the optimization process. The optimization began by initializing 12 multilayer nanocup arrays with randomly distributed parameters within their respective parameter spaces. These arrays were then sent to the Lumerical FDTD platform to compute the optical density (OD) values for each structure. The FDTD platform returned the computed optical values to the PSO algorithm, which calculated the FoM and generated parameters for the next generation. After every FDTD call the returned transmission spectrum was post-processed by four hard filters that mitigate the numerical pitfalls of high-dimensional electrodynamic optimisation:

Fabrication of metasurface nanocup biosensor chip

First, Electron beam lithography processes the different parameters mask templates and then obtains the nanopillar structure templates on a silicon substrate through dry etching. Then, the periodic Ti-Ag-Au multilayer nanocup array device was prepared using a replica molding process. We evenly applied a thin layer of ultraviolet (UV)-curable polymer (NOA-61, Sigma Aldrich) onto the mold and covered it with a layer of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film. PET, also known as poly(ethylene terephthalate), is superior in heat resistance, chemical resistance, dimensional stability, and transparency in the visible and near-infrared spectral range. This PET film serves as the substrate for the chip, providing a robust and flat surface for the deposition of the metasurface structures without significantly affecting the optical properties of the sensor. After curing with ultraviolet light (105 mW/cm2) for 3 min, the periodic nanocup pattern substrate was carefully peeled off from the mold. Finally, a chromium layer with good adhesion, a Silver layer with good plasma resonance and low cost, and a gold layer with excellent stability and biocompatibility were deposited on the nanocup substrate using electron beam evaporation technology to fabricate the nanoplasma sensors.

The nanocup chip will be assembled with 96-well plates, which was fabricated by a 3D printer for convenient use in the future.

From Eqs. (7) and (8), it is evident that the sensor exhibits high sensitivity to the dielectric constants of the surrounding medium. When an analyte binds to the sensor surface, the resultant change in the surface refractive index induces a shift in the resonance spectrum, thereby enabling the monitoring of molecular interactions. However, the detailed detection of such spectral shifts typically necessitates high-resolution spectrometers, which can limit the practicality and accessibility of the sensing platform.

To reduce reliance on complex instruments and simplify spectral shift detection, we used the differential OD spectral response (see Supplementary Fig. S2). Specifically, we utilize the endpoint method to quantify these shifts, which simplifies the detection process and enhances the robustness of our sensor design. This approach allows for the effective monitoring of molecular interactions without compromising the sensitivity or resolution of the measurements.

The efficacy of SPR is largely contingent upon the properties of the metal film employed. To optimally harness the plasmonic effects on metasurfaces, the selection of appropriate coating materials is imperative. Silver and gold are the preferred plasmonic materials due to their low optical losses in the visible and near-infrared (NIR) spectra, with silver exhibiting superior performance in the visible range [31]. However, both silver and gold suffer from poor adhesion, necessitating the use of a chromium adhesive layer to ensure stable biosensor fabrication. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, we conducted simulation calculations on several common metals, which corroborated the aforementioned considerations. In summary, we have elected to utilize chromium as the adhesive layer, silver for its superior surface plasmon polariton (SPP) performance, and gold as the protective layer to ensure stability and biocompatibility.

Inverse design of multilayer metasurface SPR sensors

Our initial intention was to develop a metasurface that possesses both extremely high OD response and resonance peak shift response, enabling it to be highly compatible with commercial microplate absorbance readers or cameras, thereby achieving superior sensing performance. This approach addresses significant challenges in current molecular interaction sensors, such as insufficient sensitivity, poor stability, and high cost. As previously discussed, the combination of multilayer materials and periodic nanocup arrays offers distinct advantages. This geometric design allows the transformation of the SPR displacement response into an OD response. This method effectively reduces device dependency and enhances the resolution of RI measurements.

We designed the nanocup metasurface sensor based on multilayer nanocup arrays, combining FDTD simulations with an inverse design optimization algorithm. To simultaneously optimize the both metrics, we introduced a double-objective function to serve as the optimization criterion. The weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:v$$\end{document} were set to 3 and 1, respectively, based on preliminary calculations (Supplementary Fig. S1) that showed this weighting brings the two objectives to comparable magnitudes, ensuring the final design excels in both ΔdifOD and ΔPeak. The segmented function with OD = 3.8 as the threshold is based on our observation that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in commercial spectrometers significantly declines when OD exceeds 3.8. This decline directly impacts the reliability and accuracy of measurements. As discussed in Sect. "Definition of sensitivity and FoM of inverse design metasurface", balancing the thickness and SNR of metasurface sensors is crucial for optimizing sensing performance. When OD exceeds 3.8, the SNR deteriorates, necessitating the introduction of a penalty term to counteract this adverse effect. This penalty term, derived from empirical testing, indicates that exceeding a certain OD threshold compromises sensor performance. More precise definition of this penalty term can be explored in future work. Notably, this threshold should be instrument-specific—more advanced detection systems can extend the upper OD limit, enabling sensors with even higher performance.

Designing metasurface sensors with multilayer plasmonic materials requires consideration of both the geometric parameters of the metasurface and the thicknesses of the multilayer materials, significantly increasing the dimensionality of the parameter space. For instance, evaluating ten values for each parameter—such as period, radius, height, and thicknesses of three plasmonic layers—results in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} possible combinations. This complexity demands extensive parameter sweeps and specialized optimization techniques, which are both time-consuming and reliant on expertise in metasurface engineering. To efficiently identify the optimal parameter combination that maximizes the FoM within this high-dimensional space, we employed a Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm coupled with FDTD simulations (Fig. 3a). This approach utilizes a population-based optimization strategy, where a swarm of particles collectively explores the parameter space and iteratively improves the optimization objective through collaborative movement, ultimately maximizing the FoM.

We initialize the optimization process with 12 particles, each assigned a random set of initial parameter values. The corresponding FoM for each particle is evaluated using FDTD calculations. In subsequent generations, each particle updates its position based on its own experience and the collective experience of the swarm, moving towards parameter values that yield higher FoM values (Supplementary Fig. S4). As shown in Fig. 2b, after iterating through 15 generations, the average FoM of the swarm increases from 10.3 to 12.5, with the best-performing particle achieving an FoM of 14.1. During the optimization process, some particles exhibited an FoM of 0. This occurred when the penalty was so large that it rendered the FoM negative, in which case we set the FoM to 0. Additionally, we assigned an FoM of 0 to particles that produced abnormal results during the calculations, in order to prevent erroneous results from affecting the overall optimization process. The optimized sensor architecture obtained through this process is characterized by the following parameters: p = 475 nm, h = 400 nm, r = 140 nm, Cr = 5 nm, Ag = 90 nm, Au = 10 nm. The optimal results of each parameter in each generation are shown in Fig. 2c. For detailed results, please refer to Supplementary Figs. S5-S9. Additionally, we further investigate and validate the results of the PSO inverse design through a parametric sweep study. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S10 and S11, our proposed double-objective function inverse design approach effectively yields optimal design results that possess both high OD response and peak shift response. Moreover, this approach also enables us to assess whether the desired design outcomes can still be achieved within a certain range of manufacturing tolerances. At this point, the reverse engineered sensor can excite the SPR effect using only vertical light incidence (Supplementary Fig. S12), eliminating the need for precision equipment such as prism coupling and angle measurement.

To further verify the conclusions of the above numerical calculation, we manufactured chips with different periods and thicknesses at a diameter-to-period ratio of 0.60. Figure 2d shows the variation of OD response spectra of IDMM-SPR sensors under different period and thickness parameters at 4% sucrose concentration. The simulation and experimental results are highly consistent, confirming the accuracy of our design. Notably, the OD response increases with the period, and the resonance peak redshifts, indicating a strong correlation between structural parameters and optical performance. Under a fixed period, the OD response also increases with the increase of Ag layer thickness. These findings provide a basis for tuning the OD response and position in metasurface research.

Mechanism analysis based on LSPR, wood anomalies, and SPR resonance enhancement

Although inverse design has provided us with a nanocup array metasurface that exhibits optimal performance, the underlying enhancement mechanisms remain unclear. To elucidate the physical significance behind these parameters, further spectral and electric field analyses are required. We calculated and plotted the transmission spectra of the optimal parameters in different refractive index media, as shown in Fig. 3a. We observed that the spectral shifts in different refractive index media primarily occur in the 500–750 nm wavelength range. Within this range, two distinct transmission maxima and minima are evident, which we labeled in wavelength order as λ1, λ2, λ3, and λ4. To further identify the physical mechanisms of these resonance peaks, we plotted the electric field distributions of the four peaks, as shown in Fig. 3b. We observed complex field distributions at the shorter wavelength λ1, with enhanced electric fields around the nanocups and maxima located between the nanocups. This phenomenon is similar to the findings of F. A. A. Nugroho et al. [22], suggesting a significant contribution from Rayleigh anomalies (RA) to the resonance. At λ2, the electric field distribution is similar to that at λ1, but the field enhancement is directed inward toward the nanocups, indicative of a clear LSPR phenomenon. At λ3, we observed magnetic field stripes similar to those reported by Yanik et al. [32] in the context of Wood’s anomalies, and this position is also close to that described in Equation S6. At λ4, the electric field exhibits a pronounced enhancement, corresponding to the extraordinary optical transmission (EOT) phenomenon induced by the nanocup array metasurface BW-SPP, which aligns with the description in Equation S4. Through electric field analysis, we can confirm the presence of multiple complex optical effects within the nanocup array metasurface.

As shown in Fig. 3c, the period of the nanocup array significantly influences the sensing response. We extracted the response values using the method described in S2 and plotted them against the period, as shown in Fig. 3d, revealing a clear positive correlation between the sensing response and the period. To further understand the physical mechanisms behind this phenomenon, we plotted the cross-sectional electric field distributions for LSPR, Wood’s anomalies, and BW-SPP, which are the three dominant contributors to the metasurface sensing response, and calculated the proportion of the electric field strength greater than 2.5 in the enhanced region relative to the total area. As shown in Figs. 3e-j, the electric field responses of LSPR, Wood’s anomalies, and BW-SPP are all highly positively correlated with the period. This result is also supported by the derivations in Equations S4 and S6, where both Wood’s anomalies and BW-SPP are positively correlated with the period. As the period increases, the resonance wavelength is coupled to longer wavelengths, and the resonance at longer wavelengths causes the electric field to couple to regions farther from the interface, thereby increasing the sensing area and enhancing the sensing response. This result is consistent with Fig. 3d. Notably, as shown in Figs. 3e and i, LSPR and BW-SPP not only have larger sensing areas with increasing period but also exhibit stronger local electric field enhancements, with enhanced electric fields inside the nanocups. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 3g, the intensity of Wood’s anomalies remains relatively unchanged, and this change primarily occurs above the metasurface. Among these three effects, the electric field of BW-SPP dominates. It is these three effects that extend the electric field of light interacting with the metasurface beyond the interface, making it highly sensitive to the refractive index near the interface. Furthermore, we analyzed the electric field at the difOD maximum, as shown in Fig. 3k, and found distinct features of LSPR and Wood’s anomalies at this wavelength, with significantly enhanced electric fields. Therefore, this wavelength is likely a region where LSPR, Wood’s anomalies, and BW-SPP are coupled, resulting in the optimal sensing performance.

Characterization and validation of IDMM-SPR sensors

Following the completion of chip fabrication (the main process is shown in Supplementary Fig. S13), we employed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine the morphology of the IDMM-SPR chip and verify that its parameters align with our design specifications. Figure 4a presents SEM images of the chip surface at different periods, revealing a uniform arrangement of 3D nanocup structures on the metasurface. The images are taken at the same magnification, and it is evident that the nanocup period increases progressively from left to right. Figure 4b illustrates the cross-sectional view of the nanocup metasurface arrays, confirming that nanocups with a height of 450 nm are uniformly arranged. The SEM results indicate that the actual radius of the nanocup metasurface is slightly smaller than the designed radius. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to manufacturing and measurement errors, as well as the deposition of plasmonic materials on the surface. The depth discrepancy may arise from the nanoimprint lithography process. However, as discussed in Sect. "Software", our multilayer nanocup design allows for robust performance even within a certain range of fabrication errors, ensuring the high performance of the metasurface sensor.These results demonstrate that nano-etching and nanoimprinting processes can effectively produce metasurfaces that meet our design requirements. We then assembled the fabricated IDMM-SPR sensor with a 3D-printed 96-well plate to create a portable detection device. By placing sucrose solutions of varying concentrations into the wells and measuring their absorbance, we obtained differential OD spectra by subtracting the absorbance curves from that of the control group (water). Figure 4c shows typical differential OD responses of the four chips at different refractive indices (RIs), while Fig. 4d presents a strong correlation between OD response and RI (R² >0.99) for the four types of IDMM-SPR chips. These findings demonstrate that the designed IDMM-SPR biosensors exhibit exceptional capabilities for RI sensing and hold significant potential for applications in biological detection. Additionally, the results indicate that larger periods correspond to higher sensor responses and improved detection resolution. This conclusion is further supported by numerical simulations. This conclusion is further supported by numerical simulations. Supplementary Fig. S14a and b show that significant local electric field enhancement only occurs at the resonant wavelength. Moreover, the amplitude of this enhancement increases significantly with the period. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S14c, magnetic field stripes similar to those reported by Yanik et al. [32] in the context of Wood’s anomalies are observed. According to Supplementary Fig. S14c and d, under our optimal structure with a period of 475 nm, both Wood’s anomalies and bound-wave surface plasmon polaritons (BW-SPP) contribute to the sensing capability, with the SPP mode being predominant.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. S15, we further evaluated the quantitative detection sensitivity of the above parameter chips using the differential OD response of Protein A against immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies, and the results show a similar trend with the sucrose experiment. Meanwhile, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S16, we further calculated the noise and signal-to-noise ratio of different parameter metasurfaces to evaluate our parameter selection. Finally, we confirmed that the optimal thickness combination is 5 nm for Cr, 80 nm for Ag, and 10 nm for Au. According to the results of the simulation and experiment, when the thickness of Ag is greater than 100 nm, the differential OD response no longer increases significantly. On the contrary, the signal-to-noise ratio decreases due to the performance limitations of the instrument. And increasing the thickness of the metal will also increase manufacturing costs.

Figure 4e displays transmission microscopy images of four IDMM-SPR chips in the air, exhibiting distinct color changes that correspond to variations in resonance wavelengths induced by periodic structural differences. These color variations demonstrate the chips’ potential for wavelength-selective filtering. Given the differing periods of the four fabricated parameters, their resonance wavelengths are expected to vary, as predicted by Ebbesen’s formula [30]. To more intuitively illustrate the influence of period and thickness on transmitted light intensity within the designed metasurface, we present transmission microscopy images for all metasurfaces with periods ranging from 400 to 475 nm and Ag thicknesses from 60 to 140 nm in Supplementary Fig. S17. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. S17, the transmission images progressively darken with increasing thickness while the colors shift across different periods. The unprocessed OD spectrum of the sensors in Supplementary Fig. S18 also confirms this. Owing to our double-objective function design, which takes into account the resonance peak shift response, and given that peak shifts induce color changes, the inversely designed sensor is also highly suitable for colorimetric sensing in SPR imaging. We demonstrated the feasibility of using it for optical imaging colorimetric sensing in Supplementary Fig. S19. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S19a, the sensor surface exhibits distinct color changes under different sucrose concentrations. We further separated the RGB channel image data and found that the R channel plays a dominant role in this change, as the strong resonance and peak shift mainly occur in the red band of 620–720 nm (Supplementary Fig. S19b). Moreover, we extracted the mean values of the RGB channels and fitted them against sucrose concentration, revealing a high correlation between RGB channel data and sucrose concentration, with an R2 value exceeding 0.9. These findings demonstrate that the sensor developed using the double-objective inverse design method is not only compatible with commercial microplate readers but also suitable for camera-based sensors, enabling its application in a wider range of scenarios. However, considering that microplate absorbance readers have well-established commercial solutions that are more conducive to controlled conditions and rapid dissemination of our practical application value, this study focuses on showcasing the potential research scenarios and value based the perspective of OD response.

To evaluate and demonstrate the performance of the developed IDMM-SPR sensor, we also compared it with relevant reported works in Supplementary Table S1. Although FoM and sensitivity were not our optimization goals, the inverse design optimal sensor (p = 475 nm, h = 480 nm, r = 140 nm, Cr = 5 nm, Ag = 80 nm, Au = 10 nm.) we ultimately determined still achieved 812 nm ⁄ RIU (Refractive Index Unit) and 26.3 RIU− 1, respectively.

Application of IDMM-SPR sensor in ultra-high affinity antibody drugs development

Therapeutic antibodies have grown rapidly in recent years, becoming a dominant force in the therapeutics market [33]. The IDMM-SPR biosensing platform developed by our research institute provides real-time affinity data to evaluate the binding and dissociation rates between antibody components and target molecules, which can help understand drug mechanisms, screen lead compounds, optimize drug design, target fishing and validation, and accelerate the discovery process of new drugs.

The measurement of ultra-high affinity interactions typically involves rapid binding and slow dissociation processes, necessitating real-time detection capabilities and high sensor stability. Insufficient sensor stability and sensitivity can lead to baseline drift, thereby compromising data accuracy. Therefore, strategies to control this issue are required [34]. To address this, we further evaluated the anti-nonspecific adsorption capability of the IDMM-SPR biosensor (Supplementary Fig. S20). The results demonstrated that the sensor exhibits excellent anti-nonspecific adsorption performance when configured with a 10 nm Au overlayer. As shown in Supplementary Figs. S21 – S23, we optimized the blocking and detection systems for the sensing platform. A 1% BSA solution was selected as the blocking agent, while CBS and HBS-ET were used as the incubation and detection buffer systems, respectively. Additionally, Supplementary Fig. S24 presents the raw OD spectra and calculated differential OD data for each critical step in biological sample detection, confirming that the data accurately reflect the adsorption and desorption of biomolecules on the sensor surface. Finally, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S25, we detail the establishment of an affinity analysis platform based on IDMM-SPR sensors. Furthermore, in Supplementary Fig. S26, we validate the anti-non-specific adsorption properties, regeneration ability, and stability of the IDMM-SPR sensors. Notably, over nearly 60 regeneration experiments, the sensors demonstrated no significant signal attenuation, thereby confirming their robust regeneration capability and stability. These experiments collectively demonstrate the feasibility, accuracy, and stability of the affinity detection platform developed based on the IDMM-SPR sensor.

To further validate the applicability of our IDMM-SPR biosensor for ultra-high affinity molecular interaction analysis, we selected Programmed Death-1 (PD-1) and its ligand Sintilimab as a model system. Sintilimab is a safe and effective human IgG4 monoclonal antibody that binds to PD-1 with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\boldsymbol{D}}$$\end{document} value of 74 pM [35]. Given its ultra-high affinity, a prolonged dissociation phase is required to achieve sufficient dissociation for accurate data fitting and determination of the dissociation constant (typically observing a 5% decrease). Insufficient sensor sensitivity and stability may lead to drift in the dissociation curve during prolonged dissociation periods. This drift can cause the curve, which should ideally decline, to appear as a horizontal line or even show a slow increase, mimicking non-dissociation or spurious re-binding phenomena. These issues complicate data fitting and prevent accurate calculation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\boldsymbol{D}}$$\end{document}. Figure 5a illustrates the rapid binding kinetics between PD-1 and Sintilimab, with a swift increase in the binding curve (data normalized to a zero-concentration reference). Upon introducing buffer solution, the dissociation phase is notably slow, prompting a 30-minute dissociation period for adequate data collection. Figure 5b demonstrates the IDMM-SPR platform’s ability to stably monitor this slow dissociation process, characteristic of ultra-high-affinity interactions. Using the Langmuir model with 1:1 binding stoichiometry, we fitted the kinetic data (Fig. 5c) and obtained a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\boldsymbol{D}}$$\end{document} value of 70–127 pM, closely aligning with previously reported values [35, 36]. This result underscores the exceptional performance of our SPR sensor in ultra-high-affinity kinetic analysis and target validation.

Due to cost control rather than the limitations of the testing equipment we developed. Here, a monoclonal antibody drug (Adalimumab), a potential Antibody–Drug Conjugate (ADC) drug candidate (Polatuzumab) and an ADC drug (Sacituzumab govitecan) are used to demonstrate our simple, rapid, and high-throughput molecular fishing and affinity detection scheme based on the IDMM-SPR biosensor. This protocol includes sensor surface cleaning, functionalization, binding detection, and dissociation detection. Figure 4e illustrates a schematic of sample deposition on the sensor surface during the detection process. The entire measurement process can be completed in 20 min or less, enabling high-throughput detection.

To further validate the performance of the inverse-designed sensor, we compared it with an unoptimized sensor (parameters: p = 400 nm, h = 550 nm, r = 90 nm, Cr = 5 nm, Ag = 60 nm, Au = 5 nm). It is worth noting that the non-optimized sensor was fabricated near the optimal parameters to confirm our inverse-designed results, rather than being deliberately selected as the worst-case scenario. Despite this, the performance of the non-optimized sensor remains highly advanced compared to many previously reported works. Figures 5f–h show the detection kinetic curves obtained using the unoptimized sensor, while Figs. 5i–k present the corresponding curves for the optimized sensor. Although the unoptimized sensor performed well, the optimized sensor demonstrated significantly enhanced detection capabilities, particularly in distinguishing low-concentration samples. In Supplementary Fig. S27, we quantitatively fit the binding signals, further highlighting the difference between the two sensors.

Development of the quantification platform with C-reactive protein (CRP)

Quantitative detection of target analytes (biomarkers) holds significant importance in numerous fields, particularly in disease screening and diagnosis [40, 41]. Traditional methods for biomarker quantification, including immunoassays, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), Western blot (WB), and RT-PCR, are often limited by their sensitivity and accuracy. Moreover, these approaches are typically costly, intricate, and time-consuming, requiring sizable sample volumes [41]. CRP is an inflammatory biomarker whose levels are usually low in healthy individuals but can increase by 100 to 200 times or more in acute systemic inflammation [42]. CRP and other inflammatory markers have been measured in various clinical settings, and it has been shown that CRP levels are higher in patients with acute coronary syndrome, which can be used to predict the prognosis of these patients [42].

In this study, we demonstrate a CRP detection scheme based on the designed IDMM-SPR platform, which is ultra-sensitive, has a wide response range, and is fast and low-cost. This platform can be rapidly and efficiently deployed for CRP detection applications. As shown in Fig. 6a, to maximize the performance of the sensor, we self-assembled a monolayer of MUA on the chip surface to reduce interference and improve fixation efficiency. Subsequently, the signal was further amplified using the double-antibody sandwich method reported previously [21]. To test the effectiveness of the developed quantitative analysis platform, we also conducted experiments on specific recognition and non-specific adsorption. As shown in Fig. 6b, all test groups showed no significant signal changes like CRP at 0 concentration, which reflects the excellent ability of our developed quantitative analysis platform to resist non-specific adsorption and specifically recognize CRP (Refer to Supplementary Fig. S26d for the experiment of the 8 µg/mL CRP group). The 8 µg/mL CRP group, which was the experimental group without CRP antibody incubation, showed similar results to the 0 µg/mL CRP group, both lacking significant signal response, further validating the platform’s anti-nonspecific adsorption capability. Figure 6c shows the differential OD curve after the second addition of antibodies, and the results show good discrimination at different concentrations. To verify the effectiveness of the inverse design approach, we conducted identical experiments using both an unoptimized sensor and a sensor without any metasurface design. The results are plotted together in Fig. 6d, while Fig. 6e presents the fitting results of the response values relative to concentration. Figure 6f illustrates the three sensor configurations. The LOD for the optimized and unoptimized sensors was calculated to be 0.84 ng/mL and 124.02 ng/mL, respectively. This indicates that the detection performance of the sensor was enhanced by at least 147 times after inverse design optimization, and which is 60-fold lower than before [21]. In contrast, the sensor that was merely coated without any design could hardly distinguish the concentration of any samples in this application scenario. This clearly demonstrates the necessity of rational inverse design in the complex, multidimensional design of metasurface biosensors, which can significantly improve sensor performance.

Furthermore, we compared our quantitative analysis platform based on the IDMM-SPR sensor with several previously reported works. As shown in Table 2, our CRP detection scheme using the IDMM-SPR platform demonstrates significant advantages in terms of the limit of detection (LOD) compared to traditional methods. For instance, compared to the fiber optic SPR, our approach achieves a LOD that is nearly 260 times lower. Additionally, when compared to the colorimetric immunoassay based on gold nanoparticles, our method exhibits a LOD that is nearly 120 times lower. Moreover, the inverse-designed IDMM-SPR sensor shows a nearly 150-fold improvement in LOD compared to the unoptimized MetaSPR sensor. These comparisons highlight the substantial potential of inverse design in enhancing sensor performance.

The multilayer IDMM-SPR biosensor exhibits considerable potential for applications in clinical detection and diverse areas of bio-detection. Characterized by high sensitivity, rapid response, and cost-effectiveness, this detection platform is poised for broad application in clinical diagnostics and bio-detection, thereby providing robust support for early disease screening and precision medicine. Its advanced capabilities are expected to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of diagnostic processes, ultimately contributing to improved healthcare outcomes and advancements in biomedical research.

Conclusion

In this study, we have successfully developed an ultrasensitive plasmonic biosensor through inverse design, leveraging the unique optical properties of multilayer nanocup arrays. Our work not only demonstrates the effectiveness of inverse design in optimizing sensor performance but also unveils novel physical mechanisms underpinned by the synergistic effects of LSPR, Wood’s anomalies, and BW-SPP. These collective resonances significantly enhance the sensor’s sensitivity and stability, enabling it to address critical limitations in current molecular interaction analysis technologies, such as insufficient sensitivity for small molecules and ultra-high affinity interactions.

A important contribution of this work lies in the mechanistic analysis of the nanocup metasurface, where we identified and characterized the contributions of LSPR, Wood’s anomalies, and BW-SPP to the sensor’s performance. The electric field analysis revealed that larger periods of the nanocup array extend the sensing area and intensify local field enhancements, further amplifying the sensor’s response. This mechanistic insight not only deepens our understanding of plasmonic metasurfaces but also provides a foundation for future designs targeting specific resonance enhancements.

Another standout feature of our work is the successful application of inverse design, which combines numerical simulations with machine learning to optimize the sensor’s structural parameters. Traditional sensor development often involves iterative trial-and-error processes, which are time-consuming and costly. By employing a double-objective optimization function and the PSO algorithm, we efficiently identified the optimal configuration (e.g., p = 475 nm, Ag = 80 nm) that balances high OD response and resonance peak shift. This approach not only accelerates the design process but also ensures robust performance even within manufacturing tolerances, making the sensor highly practical for real-world applications.

From a practical standpoint, our IDMM-SPR sensor offers significant advantages over existing technologies. This metasurface requires only a vertically incident light source to achieve surface plasmon excitation, eliminating the need for complex and expensive detection equipment. This scalable production method, combined with the sensor’s compatibility with commercial microplate absorbance readers, positions it as a viable solution for high-throughput molecular interaction analysis. The sensor’s ability to perform real-time, label-free detection of ultra-high affinity interactions (e.g., PD-1/Sintilimab), high sensitive (0.84 ng/mL of CRP) and small molecules (e.g., Quercetin) further highlights its versatility in drug development and disease diagnosis.

Notably, this work also identifies several avenues for future exploration. First, expanding the IDMM-SPR platform to portable detection devices (e.g., smartphone-based systems) could enable point-of-care applications. Second, integrating emerging materials (e.g., graphene, van der Waals materials) or exploring advanced surface functionalization strategies may further enhance sensitivity and specificity. Third, systematic investigations into how metasurface nanostructures influence metal deposition conformality (e.g., step coverage on high-aspect-ratio sidewalls) and subsequent plasmonic mode excitation will help refine fabrication-performance relationships.

In summary, this study addresses critical limitations of conventional SPR and metasurface-based sensors by combining inverse design with deep mechanistic insights. The IDMM-SPR sensor not only achieves superior performance but also exemplifies how metal-based metasurfaces can overcome the limitations of dielectric counterparts in scalability and cost. We anticipate that this work will inspire further advancements in metasurface biosensors, paving the way for their widespread use in life sciences, drug discovery, molecular fishing, and clinical diagnostics.
