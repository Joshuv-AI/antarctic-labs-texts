Jabuticaba (Plinia cauliflora) as a Source of Bioactive Phenolics: Extraction and Microencapsulation by Spray Drying
Authors: Kelly Aparecida Dias, Lívya Alves Oliveira, Stephanie Michelin Santana Pereira, Valéria Silva de Lana, Júlia Francisquini D'Almeida, Izabela Maria Montezano de Carvalho, Hércia Stampini Duarte Martino, Italo Tuler Perrone, Elad Tako, Vitor Lacerda Sanches, Mauricio Ariel Rostagno, Ceres Mattos Della Lucia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTJabuticaba (Plinia cauliflora), a Brazilian berry rich in phenolic compounds and anthocyanins, shows antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory potential, but the instability and low bioavailability of these bioactive compounds limit their use, highlighting extraction and microencapsulation as preservation strategies. This study aimed to develop a spray‐dried microencapsulated jabuticaba extract using maltodextrin as a carrier, while optimizing the extraction of phenolic compounds to enhance encapsulation efficiency and the retention of bioactive compounds. The peel and whole fruit were evaluated under different conditions (in natura, freeze‐dried, oven‐dried) to determine the most promising source of bioactive compounds. Extraction conditions included hydroethanolic solvents (50:50 and 80:20 v/v, acidified or non‐acidified), solid concentrations (1%, 4%, and 15% w/v), and extraction times (2–24 h). The optimized extract was microencapsulated by spray drying using maltodextrin as the carrier. The freeze‐dried peel showed higher phenolic recovery and antioxidant capacity than the whole fruit, with optimal extraction obtained using a 50:50 hydroethanolic solution and 2 h extraction. The extract showed high concentrations of phenolic compounds (62.38 ± 3.71 mg GAE/g), monomeric anthocyanins (11.63 ± 0.34 mg/g), and antioxidant capacity (DPPH: 14,017 ± 135 µmol Trolox/g); UPLC analysis identified cyanidin‐3‐glucoside (657.43 ± 2.93 mg/100 mL) and ellagic acid (71.17 ± 0.35 mg/100 mL) as the major phenolic compounds. The microencapsulated extract with maltodextrin presented low water activity (0.199 ± 0.014), spherical morphology, and retention of phenolic compounds. This study provides optimized extraction conditions and demonstrates the effectiveness of spray‐drying microencapsulation using maltodextrin to stabilize jabuticaba phenolics, which may contribute to improving their stability and potential application as functional ingredients.

Introduction

Jabuticaba, a popular Brazilian fruit belonging to the Myrtaceae family and native to the Atlantic Forest, can be found throughout most of the country. Among the best‐known and most‐studied species, Plinia cauliflora and Plinia jaboticaba stand out (Mattos 1983). Considered a Brazilian berry, jabuticaba has attracted growing scientific interest due to its remarkable phytochemical profile, rich in polyphenols, anthocyanins, and other phenolic derivatives, and its recognized antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and antidiabetic effects (Inada et al. 2021; Rodrigues et al. 2021).

More than 80 phenolic compounds have been described in the whole fruit and its fractions (seed and peel), including anthocyanins, hydroxybenzoic acid derivatives (ellagitannins, gallotannins, and ellagic acid derivatives), hydroxycinnamic acids, flavonols, flavanols, flavanones, and flavones (Inada et al. 2021). The literature indicates that fruit peels are an important source of bioactive compounds and antioxidants, mainly due to their high content of secondary metabolites (Ignat et al. 2011). However, the recovery and preservation of these compounds are strongly influenced by processing conditions, extraction parameters, and drying methods, which may significantly affect their stability and bioactivity (Hariadi et al. 2024).

Drying methods, in particular, play a role in the preservation of phenolic compounds and antioxidants. The study by Mphahlele et al. (2016) demonstrated that freeze‐dried pomegranate peel presented total phenolic compounds, tannins, and flavonoids concentrations approximately 3 to 5 times higher than those observed in oven‐dried peel, regardless of the temperature applied (40, 50, or 60°C). Similarly, the study by Karaman et al. (2014) reported higher total phenolic content in freeze‐dried persimmon powder. In agreement with these findings, Zhang G et al. (2024) evaluated hot air drying (HAD), vacuum drying (VD), and vacuum freeze‐drying (VFD) applied to fresh mulberries and observed that drying methods significantly influenced sample quality. Compared to HAD and VD, VFD resulted in the highest contents of anthocyanins and total flavonoids, as well as superior antioxidant capacity, with DPPH (81.2%) and ABTS•+ (79.9%) radical scavenging activity.

Despite the broad health benefits of phenolic compounds, their industrial application is limited by low stability, degradation during processing and storage, and low bioavailability in biological systems (Tarone et al. 2020). These challenges highlight the need for standardized extraction procedures and protective technologies that maintain the integrity and functionality of phenolic compounds. In this context, microencapsulation emerges as a promising approach to improve the stability, functionality, and applicability of bioactive compounds in food systems (Castillo‐Barzola et al. 2025). Microencapsulation technologies allow for the protection of these compounds against oxidation, light, temperature, and interactions with other components of the food matrix, in addition to improving shelf life and facilitating incorporation into food (Sharif et al. 2020; Choudhary et al. 2023).

Evidence from recent studies indicates that spray drying processes using carriers such as maltodextrin can preserve nutritional attributes, as reported for Spirulina (Arthrospira platensis), where optimized conditions allowed for the maintenance of protein content and amino acid composition (Castillo‐Barzola et al. 2025). Similarly, the encapsulation of unstable compounds has been associated with increased thermal stability, reduced volatility, and extended shelf life, reinforcing its relevance for food preservation (Zhang J et al. 2024). Optimizing process variables, such as inlet air temperature and wall material concentration, is fundamental to determining encapsulation efficiency and the physicochemical and bioactive properties of the resulting powders (Gül 2025).

Among available encapsulation methods, spray drying has become one of the most widely applied techniques in the food industry due to its operational simplicity, relatively low cost, scalability, and ability to produce stable powders with good physicochemical properties (Gomes et al. 2021). The selection of appropriate carrier agents, such as maltodextrin, gum arabic, or modified starches, plays a crucial role in determining encapsulation efficiency, particle morphology, moisture stability, and the retention of phenolic compounds and anthocyanins (Hariadi et al. 2024; Zhang J et al. 2024). Furthermore, recent advances in microencapsulation technologies, such as those applied to α‐lipoic acid (Choudhary et al. 2023) and plant extracts (Hariadi et al. 2024), reinforce the growing interest in developing stable bioactive powders with functional potential for food and nutraceutical applications. Despite these advances, the application of approaches that combine these strategies to fruit matrices rich in phenolic compounds, such as jabuticaba, remains limited, mainly due to the challenges of preserving highly sensitive bioactive compounds, especially anthocyanins, to processing conditions, which may compromise their recovery and stability.

In addition, although jabuticaba has been investigated for its phytochemical composition and biological properties, studies integrating pre‐processing strategies, extraction optimization, and microencapsulation of phenolic‐rich extracts remain limited. In particular, the influence of different drying methods applied to jabuticaba fractions (peel and whole fruit) on the recovery of these bioactive compounds, as well as the stabilization of optimized extracts through spray‐drying microencapsulation, has not been explored. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a spray‐dried microencapsulated jabuticaba extract using maltodextrin as a carrier, while evaluating different drying methods (fresh, freeze‐dried, and oven‐dried samples) and optimizing hydroethanolic extraction conditions (solvent ratio, concentration, and time) to maximize the recovery of phenolic compounds.

Material and Methods

Raw Material

The Plinia cauliflora fruits (Figure 1) were sourced from rural producers in the city of Jequeri (20°31'32.2“S 42°39'11.2”W), Minas Gerais, Brazil, during the harvest season (September to November), from different trees to ensure a representative sample. After acquisition, the jabuticabas were sanitized and manually separated into (1) jabuticaba peel and (2) whole fruit. Both were analyzed in three conditions: in natura, freeze‐drying, and after oven drying with air circulation at 40°C for 72 h.

Jabuticaba tree and jabuticaba (Myrciaria Cauliflora) from the southeast region of Minas Gerais, Brazil.

Proximate Composition and Minerals Analysis

The proximate composition analyses of jabuticaba (whole fruit) and its peel were performed in triplicate, following the methodology of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC 2016). Moisture content was determined using a drying oven (Nova Ética, model 400/6ND, São Paulo, SP, Brazil) at 105°C, and ash content was measured in a muffle furnace (Quimis, model Q320M, Diadema, SP, Brazil) at 550°C. Protein concentration was obtained by the micro‐Kjeldahl method, total dietary fiber was determined using the gravimetric non‐enzymatic method, and lipid content was quantified by the Soxhlet extraction method. Total carbohydrates were calculated by difference, and the total energy value was estimated using the conversion factors of 4 kcal g− 1 for proteins and carbohydrates and 9 kcal g− 1 for lipids.

For mineral determination, samples were subjected to nitric‐perchloric acid digestion using a mixture of nitric acid (HNO3) and perchloric acid (HClO4) in a 4:1 ratio, as described by Sarruge et al. (1974). The concentrations of iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), potassium (K), phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), sodium (Na), and chromium (Cr) were quantified using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP‐OES, PerkinElmer, model Optima 8300 DV, Waltham, MA, USA).

Determination of the Total Phenolic Compounds Content

Obtaining Extracts

One gram of powdered samples (jabuticaba peel and whole fruit) was mixed with 10 mL of a 50% (v/v) ethanol‐water solution, according to Theodoro et al. (2024). For in natura samples of jabuticaba peel and whole fruit, five grams were extracted with 50 mL of the same solvent mixture. The suspensions were agitated in a metabolic bath (Marconi, model MA‐093, Piracicaba, São Paulo, Brazil) at 180 rpm for 10 min and subsequently centrifuged (Nüve, model NF 1200/1200R, Ankara, Turkey) for 10 min. The supernatants (extracts) were collected and stored in amber bottles at −18°C ± 1°C until analysis. Aliquots of these extracts were used to determine total phenolic content, monomeric anthocyanins, and antioxidant capacity, according to Bloor (2001).

Total Phenolic Compounds Content

The concentration of total phenolics in the extracts was determined using the Folin‐Ciocalteu colorimetric method, according to Singleton et al. (1999). Briefly, 500 µL of the extract were mixed with 500 µL of Folin‐Ciocalteu reagent (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) diluted to 20% (v/v) in water, followed by the addition of 500 µL of a 7.5% (v/v) sodium carbonate solution. The mixture was vortexed (BIOSAN, model Bio Vortex V1, Riga, Latvia) and allowed to react for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, model Evolution 60S, Waltham, MA, USA). A standard calibration curve was prepared using gallic acid solutions (0.005–0.10 mg/mL) (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and the total phenolic content was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of sample (mg GAE/g).

Determination of Monomeric Anthocyanins by the Differential pH Method

Monomeric anthocyanins were determined using the differential pH method proposed by Giusti and Wrolstad (2001). This method is based on the structural transformation of anthocyanin pigments in response to pH variation. For this analysis, two buffer solutions were prepared: 0.025 M potassium chloride (pH 1.0) and 0.4 M sodium acetate (pH 4.5). Aliquots of the extract were diluted separately in each buffer, and the absorbance was measured at 510 and 700 nm using a UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, model Evolution 60S, Waltham, MA, USA).

The monomeric anthocyanin (MA) content was expressed as cyanidin‐3‐glucoside equivalents (mg C3G/g of dry matter), calculated according to Equation (1): (1) MA=A510−A700pH1,0−A510−A700pH4,5·MW·DF·1000ε·1

In the equation, MW is the molecular weight of cyanidin‐3‐glucoside (449.2 g/mol), DF is the dilution factor, ε is the molar extinction coefficient (26,900 L·mol− 1·cm− 1), 1000 is the conversion factor from g to mg, and l is the optical path length of the cuvette (1 cm).

Determination of Antioxidant Capacity

Radical Removal Activity 1,1‐Diphenyl‐2‐Picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)

The radical scavenging activity (RSA) of the samples was determined using the 1,1‐diphenyl‐2‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, according to the method described by Bloor (2001), with minor adaptations. In this procedure, aliquots of the extract were mixed with a DPPH methanolic solution (0.1 mmol·L− 1) and allowed to react in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, model Evolution 60S, Waltham, MA, USA).

The results were expressed as millimoles of Trolox equivalent per 100 g of sample (mmol Trolox/100 g), based on a standard calibration curve constructed with Trolox solutions (0 ‐ 500 µmol·L− 1).

Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (TEAC) Test

The Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (TEAC) assay was performed according to Pavan et al. (2014), with minor modifications. The ABTS•+ (2,2′‐azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid) radical cation was generated by reacting 5 mL of ABTS•+ solution (7 mmol·L− 1) with 88 µL of potassium persulfate solution (140 mmol·L− 1), followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 12 h. The resulting radical solution was diluted with ethanol to obtain an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm.

For the assay, 250 µL of the ABTS•+ working solution was mixed with 50 µL of the extract. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, model Multiskan Go microplate spectrophotometer, Vantaa, Finland).

The ABTS•+ radical scavenging activity (RSA) was calculated as the percentage of inhibition, according to Equation (2): (2) %inhibition=ControlAbs−SampleAbsControlAbs×100

Trolox was used as the reference antioxidant standard, and the results were expressed as micromoles of Trolox equivalent per gram of sample (µmol TE/g).

Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) Test

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed according to the method described by Oyaizu (1986), with minor modifications. This method is based on the reduction of ferric ions (Fe3 +) to ferrous ions (Fe2 +) by antioxidant compounds present in the sample under acidic conditions.

The reaction mixture was prepared by combining sample extract aliquots with the FRAP reagent, which contained acetate buffer (300 mmol·L− 1, pH 3.6), TPTZ (2,4,6‐tripyridyl‐s‐triazine, 10 mmol·L− 1 in 40 mmol·L− 1 HCl), and FeCl3·6H2O (iron (III) chloride hexahydrate) (20 mmol·L− 1) in a 10:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio. The mixture was then incubated at 37°C for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a UV‐Vis microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, model Multiskan Go microplate spectrophotometer, Vantaa, Finland). The FRAP values were calculated from a Trolox calibration curve and expressed as micromoles of Trolox equivalents per gram of sample (µmol TE/g).

Ultra‐High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography With Photodiode Array (UPLC‐PDA) Analysis

The analyses were carried out using a UPLC‐PDA equipped with a quaternary solvent manager, an autosampler manager, and a column manager (Waters, Acquity H‐Class UPLC system, Milford, MA, USA), as previously mentioned by Theodoro et al. (2024), with modifications. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Kinetex C18 column (2.6 µm particle size, 100 mm × 4.6 mm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) using a binary mobile phase composed of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both acidified with formic acid (1%) in the following gradient mode: B (%): 0–1 min (3%–13 %), 1–2 min (13%–15%), 2–2.5 min (15%), 2,5–3 min (15%–18 %), 3–3.5 min (18%–20%), 3.5–4 min (20%–25 %), 4–4.5 min (25%–27%), 4.5–5 min (27%–28%), 5–6 min (28%–30%), 6–7 min (30), 7–9 min (30%–40%), 9–10 min (40%–60)%, 10–12 min (60%–3%). The injection volume was 10 µL, the column temperature was maintained at 47°C, the flow rate was 0.7 mL·min−1.

Samples were dissolved in methanol/water (50%/50%) using an ultrasonic bath (Elmasonic, model P60H, Elma Schmidbauer GmbH, Singen, Germany) for 7 minutes in sweep mode at 37 kHz and 100 W. Compounds were identified based on their UV‐Vis absorption spectra in the range of 200 ‐ 520 nm and by comparing retention times with those of standard solutions. Quantification was performed using external calibration curves of cyanidin‐3‐glucoside and ellagic acid standards, and results were expressed as mg of compound per 100 g or mL of sample. Detection wavelengths were set according to the maximum absorption of each compound (approximately 520 nm for anthocyanins and 254–270 nm for ellagic acid). Empower 3 software (Waters, Alliance HPLC system, Milford, MA, USA) was used for data processing.

Development of the Jabuticaba Peel Extract by Spray Drying

For the development of the extract, the freeze‐dried and oven‐dried jabuticaba peels were ground in a knife mill (Quimis, model Q298A, Diadema, SP, Brazil) until a homogeneous powder was obtained. The powdered peels were then added to a hydroethanolic solution (ethanol:water) following the procedure described by Albuquerque et al. (2020), with modifications. To determine the optimal extraction conditions, different extraction times, solvent ratios (ethanol:water), and solvent‐to‐solid ratios were tested (see Supplementary Material).

Aliquots were collected at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h of extraction, and the extracts were analyzed for total phenolic compounds and monomeric anthocyanins to establish the optimal extraction time. After extraction, the mixture was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min (Nüve, model NF 1200R centrifuge, Ankara, Turkey), and the supernatant was vacuum‐filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure at 40°C for 20 min using a rotary evaporator (Quimis, model Q344‐1, Diadema, SP, Brazil) connected to a vacuum pump. Subsequently, total soluble solids (°Brix) were determined using a digital refractometer (Instrutherm, model RTD‐45, São Paulo, SP, Brazil), and pH was measured with a benchtop pH meter (MS Tecnopon Equipamentos Especiais, model mPA‐210, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil). The concentrated extract was stored in amber bottles at −18°C until spray drying (SD).

For microencapsulation, maltodextrin (approximately 30%, m·m− 1) was used as the encapsulating agent, previously dissolved in distilled water and homogenized on a magnetic stirrer for 10 min. The maltodextrin solution was prepared by dissolving 396 g of maltodextrin in 804 mL of distilled water (32.6 °Brix; pH 5.18), resulting in a solid content of approximately 33% (w/w). For spray drying, 150 mL of jabuticaba peel extract (16.8 °Brix; pH 3.19) was added to 500 mL of the maltodextrin solution, resulting in a final feed mixture with 26.7 °Brix and pH 3.49. Based on these proportions, the extract‐to‐wall material ratio was approximately 1:6.55 (w/w, dry basis). The spray drying process was carried out in a spray dryer (LabMaq, model MSD spray dryer, Ribeirão Preto, SP, Brazil), equipped with a drying chamber and cyclone and a 1 mm diameter pressure nozzle. The operating parameters were: inlet air temperature of 170°C ± 1°C, outlet air temperature of 88.5°C ± 1.45°C, air flow rate of 2.2–2.8 m3·min− 1, and feed flow rate of 0.73 kg·h− 1, with a total drying time of approximately 55 min.

Technological Aspects

The freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel and whole fruit, as well as the jabuticaba peel extract microencapsulated with maltodextrin by spray drying using a chamber and cyclone system, were subjected to the analyses described below.

Water Activity (aw)

Water activity (aw) was measured at 25°C using a water activity analyzer (Novasina, model LabSwift‐aw, Lachen, Switzerland). For the microencapsulated extract obtained by spray drying, aw was determined in duplicate at the end of the process, as the equipment does not allow sample access during operation (Francisquini et al. 2024).

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The morphology of the freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel and whole fruit, as well as the microencapsulated extract, was evaluated in duplicate using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi, model TM3000, Tokyo, Japan) at a magnification of 300 × to characterize the surface microstructure, according to Piovezana‐Gomes et al. (2023), with modifications.

Particle Size Distribution in the Rehydrated by Laser Diffraction

Particle size distribution was determined using a laser diffraction analyzer (Beckman Coulter, model LS 13 320, Miami, FL, USA) equipped with a liquid module. Analyses were performed in duplicate using powdered samples (freeze‐dried jabuticaba whole fruit, jabuticaba peel, and microencapsulated extract) without prior rehydration. The powders were gradually added to the dispersion unit containing distilled water at room temperature until an adequate obscuration level was reached. Measurements were recorded in five series at 90 s intervals (1.5, 3.0, 4.5, 6.0, and 7.5 min), within a detection range of 0.375 ‐ 2000 µm. Particle size distribution was calculated using the Fraunhofer diffraction model, and results were expressed as the percentage of total volume occupied by particles as a function of their size (Piovezana‐Gomes et al. 2023). Data processing was performed using the Beckman Coulter Particle Characterization software (version 5.03).

Colorimetric Analysis

Colored powder samples were analyzed, in duplicate, using a spectrophotometer (Konica Minolta, model CM‐5, Osaka, Japan) operated according to the CIELab color system. This system defines three color coordinates: L* (lightness), a* (red‐green component), and b* (yellow‐blue component). L* values range from 0 (black) to 100 (white), a* values from −80 (green) to +100 (red), and b* values from −50 (blue) to +70 (yellow). The instrument operated in the SCI (specular component included) mode, using a planar diffraction grating for spectral separation, with a reflectance and wavelength range from 360 to 740 nm (Francisquini et al. 2024).

Statistical Analyses

Data was analyzed using a Student's t‐test to compare jabuticaba peel and whole fruit. A one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was applied to compare the means among the different processing methods (in natura, freeze‐dried, and oven‐dried samples of either jabuticaba peel or whole fruit). Prior to ANOVA, data were evaluated for normality by Shapiro‐Wilk and homogeneity of variances by Levene's test. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and Discussion

Proximate Composition

Jabuticaba (whole fruit) exhibited higher moisture and ash contents than the peel (p < 0.05) (Table 1), while lipid and protein contents did not differ among samples (p > 0.05). The higher moisture and ash contents in the whole fruit are associated with the greater proportion of pulp, which is richer in water and minerals.

The jabuticaba peel exhibited higher total dietary fiber, especially insoluble fiber, compared to the whole fruit (p < 0.05) (Table 1). This finding aligns with the structural role of the peel, which is rich in cell wall polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, contributing to its high fiber content and lower digestibility. However, both fractions can be considered good sources of dietary fiber (FDA 2013).

Carbohydrate content was higher in the whole fruit than in the peel (p < 0.05) (Table 1), reflecting the higher concentration of soluble sugars in the pulp. No significant difference was observed in total energy value between samples (p > 0.05) (Table 1), indicating that the higher fiber content in the peel and higher carbohydrate content in the pulp balanced the overall caloric contribution.

Mineral Content

Potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, and calcium were the predominant minerals in both jabuticaba whole fruit and peel (Table 2). The whole fruit showed higher levels of iron, manganese, copper, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, and sodium (p < 0.05), whereas the peel presented higher concentrations of zinc and calcium (p < 0.05).

These differences reflect the effect of fruit fraction on mineral distribution. The higher calcium content in the peel, per example, plays a key role in maintaining cell wall integrity through interactions with pectic substances, while zinc is often linked to the presence of phenolic compounds that can chelate this element (Gao et al. 2019; Czech et al. 2020). The high potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, and calcium contents observed in both fractions reinforce the nutritional importance of jabuticaba, whose consumption may contribute to the dietary intake of these essential minerals.

Total Phenolic Compounds Content and Monomeric Anthocyanins Content

The jabuticaba peel exhibited higher phenolic compound content than the whole fruit across all conditions (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A). On a dry basis, the in natura peel showed the highest concentration, followed by freeze‐dried and oven‐dried samples (p < 0.05). Similarly, anthocyanin levels were higher in the peel than in the whole fruit, regardless of processing (Figure 2B).

The drying method significantly affected the retention of bioactive compounds. Freeze‐drying preserved higher levels of phenolic compounds and monomeric anthocyanins in both peel and whole fruit compared to oven drying (p < 0.05). In contrast, oven‐dried samples showed reduced concentrations, indicating greater degradation during processing. Additionally, in natura whole fruit presented higher phenolic content than both dried samples (p < 0.05), reinforcing the sensitivity of these compounds to thermal processing.

Phenolic compounds, particularly flavonoids such as anthocyanins, play a key role in plant defense and exhibit strong antioxidant activity by neutralizing free radicals and preventing oxidative damage (Al‐Khayri et al. 2023). In the present study, this was evidenced by the high levels of phenolic compounds and monomeric anthocyanins observed in the peel, which can be attributed to their accumulation in the outer layers of the fruit. Furthermore, the superior performance of freeze‐drying, which retained higher levels of these compounds, can be explained by the use of low temperatures and reduced oxygen exposure, thereby minimizing thermal degradation and oxidation. In contrast, oven drying promotes degradation due to prolonged heat exposure. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that freeze‐drying preserves the structural integrity of plant tissues and reduces the oxidation of heat‐sensitive compounds (Mphahlele et al. 2016).

Antioxidant Capacity

The jabuticaba peel exhibited higher antioxidant capacity than the whole fruit in the DPPH and ABTS assays, regardless of processing (Figure 3). Among treatments, the in natura peel showed the highest antioxidant activity across all methods. In the ABTS and FRAP assays, freeze‐dried peel also presented higher antioxidant capacity than the oven‐dried sample (p < 0.05).

For the whole fruit, antioxidant capacity varied according to the analytical method. In the DPPH assay, in natura samples showed the highest antioxidant activity, whereas in the ABTS assay, both in natura and freeze‐dried samples exhibited higher values than oven‐dried ones (p < 0.05). In the FRAP assay, freeze‐dried whole fruit showed superior antioxidant capacity compared to the other treatments.

The higher antioxidant activity in the peel can be attributed to the accumulation of secondary metabolites, particularly phenolic compounds, in the outer layers of the fruit, as widely reported in the literature (Ignat et al. 2011). The superior performance of freeze‐drying is associated with the use of mild dehydration conditions, including low temperatures and reduced oxygen exposure, which contribute to the preservation of thermolabile phenolic compounds. In contrast, oven drying promotes greater degradation due to prolonged heat exposure and oxidative conditions.

It has been highlighted in the literature that fruit peels can be considered the fraction richest in antioxidants due to the presence of secondary metabolites (Ignat et al. 2011). Despite the relevant nutritional composition of jabuticaba and its peel, its high perishability limits the commercialization of in natura fruit. In addition, food processing and storage factors, as well as interactions with other nutrients found in food matrices, can lead to the degradation of its bioactive compounds. Thus, the extraction of these compounds and the application of technological processes may be a promising strategy (Sharif et al. 2020; Tarone et al. 2020).

Identification and Quantification of Phenolic Compounds by UPLC‐PDA

Cyanidin‐3‐glucoside was detected in all samples analyzed, although its concentration varied depending on the matrix. In the whole fruit, cyanidin‐3‐glucoside was present at lower concentrations (21.13 ± 0.02 mg/100 g), whereas higher concentrations were observed in the peel (724.26 ± 0.26 mg/100 g), confirming the peel as the anthocyanin‐rich fraction of jabuticaba. This difference indicates a compartmentalization of anthocyanins in the outer layers of the fruit, suggesting that tissue structure influences the distribution of these compounds. The liquid peel extract also exhibited a high cyanidin‐3‐glucoside content (657.43 ± 2.93 mg/100 mL), indicating an efficient transfer of this compound from the solid matrix to the extract (Table 3).

Ellagic acid was not detected in the whole fruit, while it was identified and quantified in both peel‐derived samples (Table 3). The jabuticaba peel presented ellagic acid concentrations of 22.32 ± 0.05 mg/100 g, whereas the liquid peel extract exhibited higher levels (71.17 ± 0.35 mg/100 mL), suggesting enhanced recovery of this phenolic acid during the extraction process. The absence of ellagic acid in the whole fruit may be associated with its dilution in the pulp fraction and/or its occurrence in bound forms that are not easily extractable. The higher concentration observed in the extract indicates that the extraction process likely promoted the hydrolysis of ellagitannins.

The higher concentrations of cyanidin‐3‐glucoside observed in the jabuticaba peel are consistent with previous evidence indicating that anthocyanins are predominantly localized in the outer fruit tissues, where they play a protective and pigmentation‐related role (Mattioli et al. 2020; Khoo et al. 2022). The recovery of this compound in the liquid peel extract demonstrates the efficiency of the extraction process in transferring anthocyanins from the solid matrix to the soluble fraction, in agreement with recent studies highlighting fruit peels as sources of extractable anthocyanins (Yang et al. 2023).

The detection of ellagic acid in jabuticaba peel and its liquid extract corroborates previous reports indicating that this phenolic acid is mainly associated with peel tissues, where it occurs either in free form or as a product of ellagitannin hydrolysis. The higher concentration observed in the liquid peel extract suggests that the extraction process enhanced the release and recovery of this compound, consistent with recent evidence showing that solvent‐based extraction improves the availability of ellagic acid from complex plant matrices (Sharifi‐Rad et al. 2022).

Optimization of the Extraction Method and Development of a Spray Drying Microencapsulated Extract

Considering the results obtained, which highlight the relevant nutritional composition of jabuticaba peel, this matrix was selected for extract development. Although the whole fruit also showed a relevant composition, the peel exhibited higher concentrations of phenolic compounds and anthocyanins. When comparing in natura, freeze‐dried, and oven‐dried peels, the in natura samples showed higher antioxidant capacity and phenolic content, likely due to the absence of degradation during processing. However, as all results were expressed on a dry basis, dried samples represent a higher concentration of bioactive compounds per mass and provide greater extraction yield due to water removal, which enhances solvent penetration. Therefore, to determine which jabuticaba peel would be ideal for conducting the process of extraction and recovery of bioactive compounds, only freeze‐dried and oven‐dried peels were considered.

Although numerous studies that conducted the extraction of bioactive compounds from jabuticaba peel have been found in the literature, which tested different extracting solutions, concentrations and extraction times, lack of consensus in the literature regarding optimal extraction conditions (Albuquerque et al. 2020; Rodrigues et al. 2021). Therefore, the present study conducted an optimization of the extraction process of phenolic compounds and anthocyanins from jabuticaba peel, in order to understand the best combination to effectively extract these compounds.

The analysis of the initial tests to optimize the extraction method showed that, for all concentrations tested (1%, 4%, and 15% jabuticaba peel powder), the solution with the best extraction efficiency for phenolic compounds was ethanol/water 50:50 (Figure 4). In addition, 1% jabuticaba peel powder presented the highest levels of these compounds, regardless of the solution used (Figure 4).

Ethanol was selected due to its efficiency in the extraction of these compounds, its intermediate polarity enables efficient extraction of both hydrophobic and hydrophilic compounds. Furthermore, its use in low concentrations is permitted in some food industry processes, as it is classified as GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by the FDA (Food and Drug Administration) (Dai and Mumper 2010; FDA 2013). In addition, the extraction of phenolic compounds can also be influenced by other factors, such as the ratio between the solvent and the solid material (Dai and Mumper 2010). Since solvents continue to extract the target compounds until reaching saturation or equilibrium with the sample, this ratio between the amount of sample solids and the volume of solvent is one of the main variables that can affect the extraction efficiency.

Overall, regarding monomeric anthocyanins, for the 1% concentration, the 50:50 ethanol/water solution presented the best extraction efficiency in all time intervals tested. For the 4% concentration, the 50:50 solution was more efficient at the 2 and 4 h time intervals, and for the 15% concentration at the 4 and 12 h time intervals. Only for the 4% concentration at the 12 h interval did the 80:20 solution present better extraction (Figure 5).

Among the tested concentrations of jabuticaba peel powder (1%, 4%, and 15% jabuticaba peel powder), the 1% concentration showed the highest levels of monomeric anthocyanins within the 2‐hour extraction time (Figure 5A), in both 50:50 and 80:20 solutions. In the acidified 80:20 solution, the 1% and 4% concentrations presented similar results. Furthermore, at the 4‐hour extraction time in the 50:50 solution, the 1% and 4% concentrations showed similar results, both showed higher levels of anthocyanins compared to the 15% concentration (Figure 5B).

At the 8‐hour extraction time, the jabuticaba peel powder at 1% concentration showed higher levels of monomeric anthocyanins compared to the 15% concentration in both the 50:50 solution and the acidified 80:20 solution (Figure 5C). Finally, at the 12 h extraction time, the 1% concentration showed higher levels of monomeric anthocyanins in the 50:50 solution (Figure 5D).

After defining the best extraction solution and the best concentration of jabuticaba peel powder, different extraction time intervals were tested for both freeze‐dried jabuticaba and oven‐dried jabuticaba peel powders, considering that extraction time is another factor capable of influencing the recovery of bioactive compounds from plant materials (Dai and Mumper 2010). Aliquots collected at different times were analyzed for the content of phenolic compounds (Figure 6A) and monomeric anthocyanins (Figure 6B).

Occurrence and concentration of phenolic compounds (A) and monomeric anthocyanins (B) in jabuticaba peel extract produced at different time intervals. Lowercase letters represent differences between different time intervals by ANOVA and Tukey's post hoc test. * represent differences between freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel extract and oven‐dried jabuticaba peel extract by t‐test.

The results showed that the extraction of phenolic compounds, as well as monomeric anthocyanins, was superior when extracted from the freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel powder, at all extraction times tested. Furthermore, among the different times tested, it was observed that for the freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel powder, the extraction time of 2 h presented better extraction efficiency of phenolic compounds, when compared to the times of 4, 6, 8, 10, and 24 h (Figure 6A). Prolonged extraction times increase the probability of oxidation of phenolic compounds (Dai and Mumper 2010). For monomeric anthocyanins, the extraction time of 2 hours showed better extraction efficiency than the times 6, 8, 12, and 24 (Figure 6B).

Furthermore, in freeze‐dried samples, phenolic compounds and monomeric anthocyanins showed a decreasing trend over extended extraction times, while oven‐dried samples remained relatively stable (Figure 6). This behavior can be explained by the high porosity and rapid solvent penetration in freeze‐dried matrices, which may initially release compounds quickly but also expose them to oxidation or degradation during prolonged extraction. In contrast, the denser structure of oven‐dried samples results in slower, more controlled extraction, maintaining stable compound levels throughout the evaluated intervals.

Thus, the developed liquid extract for the microencapsulation was obtained from the freeze‐dried peel using a 50:50 (v·v) hydroethanolic solution at a 1% jabuticaba peel powder concentration. This extract showed high concentrations of total phenolic compounds (62.38 ± 3.71 mgGAE/g), monomeric anthocyanins (11.63 ± 0.34 mg/g), and antioxidant capacity (DPPH: 14017 ± 135 µmol trolox/g; ABTS: 1232 ± 422 µmol trolox/g; FRAP: 1652 ± 64 µmol trolox/g).

Anthocyanins have high antioxidant potential, which has motivated a growing number of studies to investigate their effects on human health (Lu et al. 2024; Verediano et al. 2021). However, anthocyanins instability and food processing conditions limit their bioavailability and use. Microencapsulation emerges as a promising strategy to enhance their stability, bioavailability, solubility, and technological properties, supporting their application in food and nutraceuticals (Sharif et al. 2020; Tarone et al. 2020).

In this sense, to improve the stability and bioavailability of the bioactive compounds present in the extract developed from the freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel, we conducted a spray drying process, which is considered one of the most effective method for the microencapsulation of extracted anthocyanins (Sharif et al. 2020). Maltodextrin was selected as a carrier in this study due to its proven efficiency as a drying vehicle, contributing to the protection of anthocyanins. In addition, its wide commercial access and low cost make this polymer an economically viable option for industrial applications. The systematic review conducted by Sharif et al. (2020) on microencapsulation of anthocyanins indicated that maltodextrin, isolated or in association with other biopolymers, was widely used as a wall material in most of the studies analyzed.

Furthermore, its versatility favors application in industrial food production systems, where factors such as cost and availability of inputs play a crucial role in the technical and economic viability of the process. Thus, the choice of maltodextrin allowed for the evaluation of its effectiveness without the influence of other wall materials. Although other carriers (e.g., gum arabic, proteins, or inulin) may provide complementary functional properties, the aim of this study was to establish a scalable baseline using a widely adopted and industrially viable encapsulating agent.

Technological Aspects

Water Activity (aw)

The freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel and freeze‐dried whole fruit presented aw of 0.295 ± 0.003 and 0.282 ± 0.006, respectively. The microencapsulated jabuticaba peel extract processed by the spray dryer cyclone presented aw of 0.199 ± 0.014, while the extract dried by the chamber presented aw of 0.264 ± 0.004. This suggests that the powder obtained by the cyclone has greater stability, which is crucial to prevent the multiplication of microorganisms and delay non‐enzymatic browning reactions, preserving the bioactive compounds present.

A typical spray dryer consists of a reservoir and feed pump, an atomizer, a heater and drying gas supplier, a drying chamber, a cyclone for separation of dry particles, and an exhaust fan and filter for the drying gas outlet (Assadpour and Jafari 2019). Previous studies have reported compositional and physicochemical differences between powders recovered from the drying chamber and the cyclone (Francisquini et al. 2024). In this study, the water activity of the microencapsulated extract obtained from the cyclone was 0.199. Water activity is a quality attribute of dry powder products and is related to the maximum encapsulation capacity (Martins et al. 2019). Recent studies have shown that a water activity value around 0.200 corresponds to the ideal result for better conservation and extension of the shelf life of powder products (Lima de Paula et al. 2021; Zouari et al. 2020). Water activities below or above this range can result in: product degradation reactions, non‐enzymatic reactions, and hydrolysis, in addition to possible development of microorganisms such as fungi, bacteria, and yeasts (De Paula et al. 2020).

Particle Size Distribution

Particle size distribution in freeze‐dried jabuticaba peel and whole fruit indicates predominantly larger particles, based on Dv90 values and the volume of particles smaller than 1 µm, which was 0.24% and 0.82% for jabuticaba peel and whole fruit, respectively (Table 4). Microencapsulation by spray drying significantly reduced particle size compared to peel and whole fruit. The powder obtained by the cyclone showed smaller particles when compared to the powder obtained by the chamber, resulting in a product with a higher fraction of fine particles (< 1 µm), in addition to greater uniformity in the size of these particles (Table 4).

The higher fraction of fine particles in the powder extract obtained by the cyclone demonstrates a better rehydration capacity of the powder (d'Almeida Francisquini et al. 2020; Francisquini et al. 2024), which may provide a more efficient release of compounds during application. Typically, the chamber product exhibits a lower rehydration capacity compared to the cyclone product. This can be inferred from the number of particles below 1 micrometer, since the smaller particle size, as observed in the powder obtained by the cyclone, is associated with better rehydration capacity. Furthermore, the greater uniformity of the particles obtained by the cyclone, compared to those obtained by the chamber, is also beneficial, as it can improve the flowability and consistency of the powder, important characteristics for its applicability in food or pharmaceutical products.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The jabuticaba peel and the whole fruit presented similar morphological properties (Figure 7A, B), with more irregular characteristics, non‐spherical particles, and more broken particles, due to the freeze‐drying process. The jabuticaba peel extract microencapsulated with maltodextrin, obtained from the cyclone and the chamber, exhibited typical spherical particles from spray drying. The spray‐dried cyclone extract presented spherical (regular) morphology (Figure 7C). On the other hand, the jabuticaba peel extract microencapsulated from the chamber presented larger, more agglomerated, and more brittle particles, which is possibly associated with the longer exposure time of the powder to high temperatures (Figure 7D). This intensification of the heat treatment in the chamber therefore affects the morphological characteristics of the powder.

This can compromise the structural integrity of the final product, in addition to affecting the controlled release of bioactive compounds. Thus, the absence of cracks and fractures in the microspheres is crucial to ensure the protection and efficient retention of the microencapsulated extract, avoiding losses and instability in the material (Gomes et al. 2021). The formation of agglomerates, as observed in electron microscopy of the microencapsulated extract in the cyclone, represents a relevant property of the encapsulated material. This contributes to the stability of the system, as the external particles act as a protective barrier, preserving the internal compounds, including their pigments (Gomes et al. 2021).

In the ordinate analysis (b*) on the yellow‐blue axis, both samples are low, suggesting few yellowish hues. The chroma (c) of the freeze‐dried peel was 29.48 ± 0.38 and of the whole fruit was 24.06 ± 0.14, showing greater color intensity. Finally, the h angle was similar between the samples (13.00 ± 0.44 for the freeze‐dried peel and 13.06 ± 0.21 for the whole fruit), indicating that the predominant hue in both was similar, remaining within the reddish spectrum (Table 5).

The data from the microencapsulated jabuticaba peel extract samples processed by the cyclone chamber and spray dried showed that the cyclone extract had greater brightness (L*) (L = 61.46 ± 0.47) than the chamber extract (L = 51.92 ± 0.27), suggesting less thermal degradation of the pigments. Overall, the results indicate that jabuticaba peel is a suitable matrix for the recovery of phenolic compounds and anthocyanins, and that the selected extraction conditions (50:50 hydroethanolic solution, 1% solid concentration, and 2 h extraction time) were effective in maximizing their extraction. In addition, the spray drying process resulted in powders with low water activity, reduced particle size, and more homogeneous spherical morphology, suggesting good process performance and stability of the obtained material. However, some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. A multivariate experimental design for extraction optimization was not performed, which may limit the evaluation of interactions between variables. Furthermore, encapsulation efficiency was not determined, preventing a more precise assessment of the process's effectiveness. The bioavailability of the encapsulated compounds was not evaluated, restricting conclusions about their behavior after ingestion. Future studies could explore these aspects. Additionally, the long‐term stability of the microencapsulated powder under different storage conditions (e.g., temperature, humidity, and light exposure) remains to be explored to better support their industrial application.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of jabuticaba (Plinia cauliflora) peel as a source of dietary fiber, minerals, and bioactive compounds, particularly phenolics and anthocyanins. The results indicate that processing conditions play a role in the preservation and recovery of these compounds. Among the evaluated drying methods, freeze‐drying was the most effective to enhance extraction efficiency. Furthermore, the optimized hydroethanolic extraction (50:50, v/v) for 2 h yielded the greatest recovery of phenolics and anthocyanins.

This study also highlights the effectiveness of spray drying as a microencapsulation technique for producing stable jabuticaba peel extract powders. The resulting microparticles exhibited desirable physicochemical properties, including low water activity, spherical morphology, and good color retention. Notably, the fraction collected from the cyclone showed improved stability.

Taken together, these findings provide an approach for the valorization of jabuticaba peel, supporting its application as a functional ingredient in food and nutraceutical formulations. Moreover, this work advances current knowledge on the microencapsulation of phenolic‐rich extracts. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to deepen the understanding of the structural and functional properties of the microcapsules, as well as to evaluate their bioavailability, stability during storage, and performance in food systems. Investigations involving in vivo studies and sensory analyses will be essential to fully validate their applicability and consumer acceptance.
