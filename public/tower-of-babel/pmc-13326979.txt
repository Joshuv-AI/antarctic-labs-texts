Neuroimaging biomarkers in migraine with aura mimicking stroke: A qualitative and quantitative analysis of perfusion CT alterations
Authors: Lucía Gómez Martín de la Escalera, Santiago Trillo, Antonio Barbosa del Olmo, Carmen De Benavides Bernaldo de Queirós, Esther Valiente, Ana Beatriz Gago‐Veiga, Cristina Sanabria‐Gago, Florentino Nombela, Alicia Gonzalez‐Martinez

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOur main goal was to describe the clinical, analytical, and neuroimaging characteristics of a large series of patients with migraine with aura (MA) as stroke mimic (SM). As secondary goals, we evaluated factors associated with perfusion computed tomography (PCT) alterations in MA.BackgroundMA is a frequent SM in the acute setting. Increasing our knowledge and awareness of the clinical and radiological characteristics that may occur in the context of a MA as SM may improve diagnosis and reduce unnecessary treatments such as intravenous thrombolysis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational cross‐sectional study including patients with a final diagnosis of MA who were evaluated at a tertiary hospital and stroke center under the stroke code (SC) protocol activation ‐focal neurological symptoms occurring within 24 h‐ between January 1, 2015, and January 31, 2025. Demographic variables (age, sex), clinical features (history of migraine, vascular risk factors, aura type), and biochemical markers (potassium, glucose, and creatinine levels) were collected. The most frequent aura symptom was sensory (67%). PCT alterations were found in 15/68 (22%), with a hypoperfusion pattern mainly unilateral, corresponding to a non‐vascular territory. The automatic software analysis identified PCT alterations in five patients that did not show alterations in the qualitative analysis. Automatic RAPID software analysis may help identify PCT alterations that cannot be detected by qualitative analysis. Male sex and aphasia are associated with PCT alterations in MA, which may improve diagnosis in the acute setting. Keywords: biomarkers, migraine, neuroimaging, pharmacological

BACKGROUND

Stroke mimics (SM) represent a group of conditions that manifest with symptoms resembling those of acute stroke, which, after complete diagnosis workup, receive an alternative diagnosis. 1 They account for approximately one in every five strokes, and over 17% of SM are treated with thrombolysis. 1 , 2 Recent studies, including our group, have identified migraine with aura (MA) as one of the main conditions that may present as a SM, alongside seizures and psychiatric disorders. 3 , 4 MA is defined by the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD‐3) as recurring attacks that last minutes to hours, with unilateral and reversible symptoms such as visual, sensory, or speech disturbances, that gradually progress and are usually followed by holocranial or hemicranial headache, with accompanying symptoms like photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, or vomiting. 5 , 6 However, the sudden appearance of an aura, especially the first episode, may mimic a stroke and could challenge the diagnose of possible MA. 7 , 8 , 9

Regarding the physiopathology of migraine, the most accepted theory is cortical spreading depolarization (CSD), originally described in 1944. 10 , 11 It is based on a depolarization wave spreading through the neuronal and glia cell network that make up the cerebral cortex in an occipitofrontal direction, followed by a brain function suppression, and a full recovery afterward. This process leads to transient and reversible alterations in brain function involving neuronal, glial, and vascular activity. 11 , 12 At a vascular level, a transient vasodilatation would occur, producing a hyperemia wave, followed by a hypoperfusion period for hours that may take place during the aura phase, which in some cases may be followed by a transient hyperperfusion wave. 13 , 14 On the other hand, the CSD leads to a massive elevation of extracellular potassium and glutamate release. It is postulated that these biochemical changes may excite the meningeal–trigeminal and trigeminal–vascular system, participating in the headache onset. 15 Among the theories that seek to explain the pathophysiology of migraine are oxidative stress and the depletion of antioxidant compounds such as creatinine; however, there are few studies related to blood markers in migraine. 16 , 17

Moreover, cerebrovascular disease is the second most common cause of death. 18 It is considered a medical emergency, and so, the quick and proper recognition and treatment is fundamental. 19 Multimodal computed tomography (CT), including non‐contrast CT, angio‐CT, and perfusion CT (PCT), is a fast and accessible tool that allows a proper identification of ischemic strokes and ischemic penumbra widely used in stroke diagnosis. 20 In addition to its advantages of including it as part of the stroke code (SC) protocol, it has also allowed the detection of neuroimaging PCT qualitative alterations in patients with MA presenting as SM. 7 , 21 Moreover, in the past few years, automated software, such as Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion (RAPID) automated software has been developed to analyze imaging data mainly obtained from PCT studies to detect ischemic penumbra regions with greater specificity and sensitivity. 22 RAPID software has been implemented in more than 100 countries, and used in more than 2000 hospitals all around the world. 22 Finally, when it comes to treatment, one of the main time‐dependent treatments is intravenous thrombolysis. 19 Although the fibrinolytic therapy is usually safe in most SM cases, it is not completely exempt of risks such as intracranial bleeding. The proper diagnosis of SM such as MA could avoid administration of this treatment when it is not necessary and thus the presence of its associated complications. 23

For all the above, a detailed evaluation of the clinical, neuroimaging, and biochemical characteristics reflecting the physiopathology of MA as SM could improve diagnosis of this condition and avoid the administration of unnecessary treatments. The hypothesis of the present study is that there are clinical, laboratory, and PCT characteristics associated with patients with MA mimicking stroke. Therefore, the main goal is to describe the demographic, clinical, laboratory parameters, and radiological PCT characteristics of patients with MA presenting as SM. Secondary exploratory objectives include to evaluate potential clinical, demographic, and laboratory parameters associated with the presence of PCT alterations in patients with MA attended as a SC, as well as exploring quantitative PCT patterns derived from automated RAPID software analysis to identify alterations that may not be detected by qualitative assessment.

METHODS

Study design

We performed an observational retrospective cross‐sectional study including patients attended at Hospital Universitario de La Princesa in Madrid (stroke center) as SC, that is, focal neurological symptoms occurring within 24 h, with a final diagnosis of MA as SM, between January 1, 2015, and January 31, 2025.

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria included patients attended as SC who underwent multimodal CT (non‐contrast CT, angio‐CT, and PCT), who had a final diagnosis of MA according to the ICHD‐3 criteria, with accessible clinical and radiological data, and without ischemic evidence of infarct in the non‐contrast CT nor occlusion in the angio‐CT. Exclusion criteria include patients who were attended as SC, had incomplete multimodal CT scans or had a final diagnosis of stroke or an alternative SM after diagnosis workup.

Variables included in the study

Clinical‐demographic and laboratory data collection and variables

Data were obtained from the HCIS (Health Care Information System) database of La Princesa University Hospital. Variables collected were demographic and clinical data such as age, sex, vascular risk factors such as high blood pressure (HBP), diabetes mellitus (DM), dyslipidemia, smoking, alcohol; migraine‐related information such as personal history of migraine, type of aura (visual, sensory, or aphasic aura), time elapsed from the onset of aura symptoms to the multimodal CT scan performance, aura duration (over or under 60 min); laboratory parameters with <20% missing data such as sodium [136–145 mEq/L], potassium [3.5–5.1 mEq/L], glucose [74–106 mg/dL], and creatinine [0.7–1.2 mg/dL in males; 0.5–0.9 mg/dL in females]. Regarding PCT, we included all available qualitative analysis and quantitative analysis using RAPID since 2020 after implementation in clinical practice in our center.

PCT imaging analysis

The PCT analysis included the following maps: time to peak (TTP) or T max, cerebral blood flow (CBF), and cerebral blood volume (CBV). These evaluate the brain perfusion as normal, ischaemic, or ischaemic penumbra by the administration of iodinated contrast and the acquisition of sequential brain CT slices. The TTP map shows the time at which the maximum concentration of contrast is reached in the brain tissue; T max is the time at which maximum value of residue function occurs after deconvolution. The volume map (CBV) indicates the volume of blood per unit of brain mass (normal 4–6 mL/100 g). The flow map (CBF) indicates the volume of blood per unit of brain mass and the time (normal between 50 and 60 mL/100 g/min). 4 We considered that there were PCT alterations if there were abnormalities in either qualitative alterations and/or quantitative alterations characterized by RAPID software. 22

PCT qualitative patterns and type of perfusion alteration

PCT alterations were qualitatively classified by the visual analysis according to the previously established patterns: vascular territory or non‐vascular territory distribution, unilateral or bilateral, brain lobes affected and the alterations in time, flow, and volume maps. 4 Perfusion patterns we defined as hypoperfusion if there was an increase in mean transit time (MTT) or T max, with a normal/reduced CBF and/or CBV; and hyperperfusion as a normal/increased CBF and/or CBV, with a corresponding decrease in MTT or T max. 4

PCT quantitative evaluation using RAPID automatic software

RAPID automated software bases its analysis on: (a) calculating the range and volume of the ischemic core, based on CBF < 30% with regard to healthy tissue in PCT; (b) calculating the hypoperfused area, based on T max > 6 s in PCT; and (c) calculating the mismatch, considering the difference between the hyperfused area and the ischemic core in order to identify the ischemic penumbra tissue in case of a stroke. In the present study we only used the RAPID data concerning perfusion maps, as milliliters in T max > 4 s, T max > 6 s, CBF, and CBV. 22

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in RStudio (version 1.4.1717). Descriptive and univariate analyses were conducted using the compareGroups and ggplot2 packages. Assumptions required to interpret the statistical analyses were verified prior to analysis. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A descriptive analysis of the sample was performed, with continuous variables summarized as means and standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on normality assumptions, and categorical variables described as absolute counts with relative frequencies. Continuous variables were compared using independent‐samples t‐test, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with a 5% significance level. Quantitative PCT patterns derived from automatic RAPID software analysis were collected and analyzed as an exploratory objective to identify alterations not detectable by qualitative assessment. To evaluate factors associated with alterations in PCT, statistically significant differences considering p< 0.05 between the groups with and without PCT alterations were evaluated in the univariate analysis. No statistical power calculation was conducted prior to the study. The sample size was based on the data availability. Missing data were reported as absolute counts and percentages for all variables. Only variables with <20% missing values were included in the analyses, and a complete‐case analysis was performed without data imputation.

Study protocol approvals, registrations and patient consents

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board from the ethics committee (CEIm) from the Hospital Universitario de La Princesa (Registry number: 5833) and conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. 24 The informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study.

Demographic and clinical characteristics in patients with MA

A total of 68 patients presented in the emergency department had a diagnosis of MA after diagnosis workup. Among them, 46/68 (68%) were females and mean age was 41.8 years (SD 12.1). Regarding personal history, 42/68 (62%) patients had a previous history of migraine. The most frequent type of aura was sensory aura in 44/68 (65%) patients, followed by visual aura in 30/68 (44%) and aphasic aura in 16/68 (23%) patients. The aura duration was over 60 min in 66/68 (97%) patients. Twenty‐six patients (38%) had multiple auras.

Laboratory parameters in patients with MA

Urgent testing laboratory parameters showed that sodium levels were normal in 59/63 (93%) patients and altered in 4/63 (6%), in 1/63 (2%) it was elevated and in 3/63 (5%), decreased. Potassium levels were normal in 57/62 (92%) patients, and were decreased in 5/62 (8%). Regarding blood glyecemia, 23/64 (36%) patients had hyperglycaemia, and 2/64 (3%) had hypoglycaemia. The mean value of creatinine was 0.8 (SD 0.2). The main results of the evaluated laboratory parameters are represented in Table 2.

Qualitative visual analysis.

PCT alterations showed hypoperfusion findings represented as increased TTP in all 15 patients (100%), decreased CBF in 5/15 (33%) patients, and decreased CBV in 1/15 (7%). Regarding the distribution pattern, 13/15 patients (87%) presented a hypoperfusion pattern corresponding to non‐vascular territory, and the other 2/15 (13%) presented complete hemispheric hypoperfusion alteration.

All the patients with MA who presented PCT alterations had unilateral hypoperfusion with 13/15 (87%) involving the left hemisphere. Moreover, the presence of PCT alterations in the left hemisphere were associated with aphasic aura (p = 0.025).

Territorial distribution was based on the affected cerebral lobes, the most frequently affected lobe was the parietal 9/15 (60%) followed by the frontal and occipital lobes 6/15 (40%), and temporal lobe 3/15 (20%). Likewise, the presence of parietal lobe PCT alteration was associated with occurrence of sensory aura (p = 0.034).

The RAPID automatic software was available in 9/15 (60%) patients. Of these, 100% showed T max > 4 s elevation, and one patient also presented T max > 6 s elevation. Variables associated with PCT alterations in MA

Patients with MA and PCT alterations were predominantly males [10/15 (67%) males vs. 5/15 (33%) females; p = 0.003] presenting with aphasic aura [8/15 (53%) aphasic aura in presence of PCT alterations vs. 8/53 (15%) in unaltered PCT; p = 0.004]. We also found a statistically significant difference in the frequency of aura duration over 60 min, with higher proportion of patients with shorter lasting auras in the group of altered PCT [13/15 (87%) aura lasting >60 min in patients with abnormal PCT vs. 53/53 (100%) aura lasting >60 min in patients with normal PCT; p = 0.046]. There was no difference in the prevalence of multiple aura subtypes between patients with normal and abnormal PCT findings [21/53 (40%) vs. 5/15 (33%); p = 0.887].

Regarding laboratory parameter variables, a statistically significant difference was observed in glycemia levels between patients without and with PCT alterations [normal glycemia was present in 39/64 (61%) patients with normal PCT versus 5/15 (33%) with altered PCT; p = 0.028], with a tendency toward hyperglycemia in patients with PCT alterations; likewise, creatinine levels showed a statistically significant difference between the two groups [mean creatinine levels of 0.80 (SD: 0.2) in unaltered PCT vs. 0.95 (SD:0.24) in patients with altered PCT; p = 0.041]. Potassium levels showed a tendency toward hypokalemia in patients with altered PCT [potassium levels were decreased in 2/53 (4%) of patients with unaltered PCT vs. 3/15 (21%) patients within PCT alterations; p = 0.071].

DISCUSSION

Our study showed that MA represents approximately 1% of all SC with complete multimodal CT, affecting predominantly middle‐aged premenopausal females with previous history of migraine. Moreover, 22% of our patients with a final diagnosis of MA presented PCT alterations, mainly hypoperfusion pattern restricted to non‐vascular territories. In addition, our exploratory analysis showed that male sex and aphasic aura were the main factors associated with the presence of PCT alterations. Overall, these results provide novel and relevant multimodal information—clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging features—of patients with MA presenting as SM that could improve diagnosic accuracy in the acute setting.

Characteristics of patients with MA

In our series, MA represent 1% of SC activations with a complete multimodal CT, a similar frequency to what has been described to date. 7 , 25 , 26 , 27 Regarding the clinical characteristics, we found that more than half of them were females in the fourth decade of life with a previous history of migraine, which is concordant with the demographic characteristics of migraine in the general population affecting predominantly young females. 28 , 29 Interestingly, the most frequent aura was sensory (65% in our cohort), despite visual aura generally being the most common type of aura. 5 Aura duration exceeded 60 min in most cases, a prolonged course that likely prompted the use of PCT in the emergency setting. 5 These results corroborate previous studies' findings in which a predominance of female sex, sensory aura, and prolonged aura (more than 60 min) in patients assessed for suspected stroke and a MA diagnosis was observed. 7 , 27 , 30 In our study, presence of vascular risk factors was not high, generally under 15% in HBP, DM, dyslipidemia, and alcohol, with smoking the most frequent (23%), similar to what is described in previous studies. 7 , 27

Laboratory parameters in patients with MA

The study of laboratory parameters showed a higher prevalence of abnormal glycemia and higher creatinine levels (within normal range) in patients with MA. Hydro‐electrolyte disturbances such as abnormal blood glucose levels could also contribute to the modification of PCT alterations. 31 In physiopathology studies about MA, a massive potassium liberation to the extracellular space is described 15 ; however, it is described at a local level. Moreover, a systemic decrease in potassium levels could be related to preventative migraine medicine intake, such as topiramate, 32 or due to hydro‐electrolyte losses as a result of vomiting or diarrhea frequently associated with migraine, 33 which could also modify creatinine levels.

PCT alteration patterns MA patients

With regard to PCT alterations, qualitative analysis identified PCT alterations in approximately 15% of patients with MA, which is consistent with the prevalence observed in previous studies. 4 , 7 Notably, quantitative analysis demonstrated alterations in 22% of patients with MA presenting as SM. The increased percentage of alterations in patients with MA by using RAPID automated software highlights the potential use of RAPID and other automated software as tools to improve sensibility in the detection of PCT abnormalities and therefore in diagnosis of MA as a SM attended as SC, overcoming the limitations of an exclusively qualitative analysis. 26 Previous studies exist where other diagnosis software were used, 26 , 30 but, as far as we know, this is the first study that used RAPID automated software in the PCT analysis of patients with MA. Moreover, in terms of time elapsed between symptoms onset and multimodal CT performance, we observed a mean time of 271.2 (SD 194.9) minutes, superior to the time observed in other studies. 26 , 30 Previous studies identified alteration up to three hours since symptoms onset; however, it is possible that PCT is less sensible as time goes by since it may reflect the CSD phenomenon and that quantitative analysis may help extend the window in which PCT alterations can be detected. 6 , 11 , 21 , 34 Regarding perfusion patterns, we found hypoperfusion pattern not following a vascular territory, predominantly unilateral and left‐sided, congruent with anomalies detected in previous studies and the latest phase of migraine aura. 7 , 26

Variables associated with PCT alterations in MA presenting as SM

Our study shows that male sex and aphasic aura are associated with PCT alterations. In spite of migraine affecting predominantly females, males present PCT alterations more frequently. 5 This association could be related to the higher vascular risk and susceptibility males have compared to premenopausal females due to estrogen protective effects which may play a role in the presentation. 35 Of note, previous studies identified aphasia in association with PCT in SM, which can be a form of aura presentation in patients with migraine. 7 , 36 It has been related to hypoperfusion patterns in Wernicke's area in the left parietal lobe, compromising language processing and delivery. 37 In our study, an association of aphasia with left lobe alteration was found, although not all patients presented hypoperfusion in the parietal lobe at the time of the PCT performance. Future studies with a larger sample size could define the relevance of ionic alterations in migraine with aura.

LIMITATIONS

The main limitations to this study include a retrospective design and a unicenter data collection,with the inability to infer causality and potential for type II errors due to small sample size as the inherent bias of this type of study. The study population was restricted to patients with MA presenting to the emergency department with suspected stroke, and thus the clinical characteristics observed may not be generalizable to the broader MA population. Due to overlapping clinical presentations, some degree of diagnostic overlap between MA and acute ischemic stroke cannot be entirely excluded when relying solely on ICHD‐3 criteria; however, to mitigate this limitation, diagnoses were established by neurologists based on the overall clinical presentation, symptom evolution, imaging findings, and follow‐up. Although CT‐based imaging has limited sensitivity for small or early ischemic lesions and qualitative perfusion assessment may be subject to inter‐observer variability, these limitations were mitigated by a standardized multimodal CT approach including non‐contrast CT, CT angiography, and CT‐perfusion, complemented, when available, by automated software‐based analysis and review by experienced neuroradiologists; it is worth noting the novelty conferred by the quantitative data inclusion provided by RAPID, and the comparison with qualitative data. However, RAPID analysis was available in only nine out of 15 patients with PCT alterations, which may have introduced misclassification bias in the qualitatively defined group, despite the combined use of qualitative and quantitative assessments remaining the most applied approach in current clinical practice. Moreover, there is potential for residual confounders such as specific time from symptoms onset to the scan or a migraine history which have been collected. The small event rate precluded reliable multivariable adjustment; therefore, the analysis focused on univariable associations. Finally, despite the limitations, this is one of the largest sample size studies done to date including patients with MA. Future studies, multicenter, with a larger number of participants and multimodal data that incorporate automated software are necessary to corroborate these findings, supporting the role of PCT in the MA as SM diagnosis.

CONCLUSIONS

Our results showed that MA as an SM accounts for 1% of SC, affecting predominantly middle‐aged premenopausal females with a previous history of migraine. Around a fifth of patients presented PCT alterations, which were predominant in males with aphasic aura. The most common PCT alteration was a unilateral hypoperfusion in a non‐vascular territory. Automated software identified PCT alterations in MA not detected by qualitative visual assessment. Future studies that corroborate these findings and further investigate the differential alterations with other SM may allow for advancements in the knowledge of physiopathology and appropriate diagnosis and treatment of these patients.
