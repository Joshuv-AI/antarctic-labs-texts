Ultrasonographic findings of feline large intestinal adenocarcinoma and high-grade lymphoma
Authors: Joe Khodari, Agustina Anson, Dominique Penninck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim of this retrospective study was to describe the ultrasonographic findings of cats with large intestinal adenocarcinoma (ACA) and high-grade lymphoma (LSA).MethodsCats diagnosed with large intestinal neoplasia with a complete abdominal ultrasound were included in the study. Signalment, clinical history, physical examination, blood work and outcome data were collected. Tumor location, length, wall thickness and layering, echogenicity, lymphadenopathy, effusion, ulceration and obstruction were recorded.ResultsA total of 44 cats had ACA, with the colon affected in 28/44 cats and the ileocolic junction (ICJ) affected in 16/44 cats. The ACA tumors had a median length of 23 mm and a median wall thickening of 9 mm. Regional lymphadenopathy was noted in 29/44 cats, with a median thickness of 6.05 mm. Obstruction was observed in 30% of the cats with ACA. In total, 38 cats were diagnosed with high-grade LSA, primarily affecting the ICJ in 20/38 cats, the colon in 14/38 cats and multiple sites within the gastrointestinal tract in 4/38 cats. In addition, 7/38 cats had other organ involvement. The LSA tumors had a median length of 40.3 mm and a median wall thickening of 15 mm. Regional lymphadenopathy was noted in 31/38 cats, with a median thickness of 8.6 mm. Obstruction was present in 11% of the cats with LSA. Both tumor types commonly appeared as transmural hypoechoic thickening with loss of wall layering. The median survival time for cats with large intestinal ACA and LSA was 19 days and 23 days, respectively.Conclusions and relevanceCats with large intestinal ACA and LSA shared a similar clinical presentation. ACA and LSA share similar ultrasonographic features; however, LSA lesions are typically thicker and longer and are associated with more frequent and pronounced lymphadenopathy than ACA lesions. ACAs are more likely to cause obstruction, whereas LSA tumors tend to have a multicentric distribution. Because imaging findings overlap, cytologic or histologic sampling remains essential for definitive diagnosis.

Introduction

Cats with tumors of the large intestine generally present with non-specific clinical signs, such as vomiting, anorexia, weight loss, diarrhea, lethargy and constipation.1 –4 Ultrasonography is a useful, non-invasive diagnostic method in differentiating inflammatory intestinal disease from intestinal neoplasia in dogs and cats.5 –8 The ultrasonographic appearance of gastrointestinal (GI) neoplasia includes a poorly echogenic, transmural thickening of the intestinal wall with loss of layering, whereas inflammatory changes are often associated with less pronounced wall thickening and preserved or altered wall layering.5,8 –13 Adenocarcinoma (ACA) and lymphoma (LSA) are the most common intestinal neoplasia encountered in the cat; however, there are few reports describing large intestinal ACA and LSA.3,4 The purpose of this retrospective study was to review the ultrasonographic features of feline large intestinal neoplasia.

Materials and methods

Medical records from Foster Small Animal Hospital of the Cummings School of Veterinary Medicine, Tufts University were searched for cats with large intestinal neoplasia between January 2002 and May 2020. The inclusion criteria for this study consisted of cats that had a complete abdominal ultrasound (US) and a confirmed histologic or cytologic diagnosis of a large intestinal tumor. Cats being treated for neoplasia before ultrasonographic evaluation or with insufficient imaging data were excluded.

Signalment, clinical history, physical examination, and cytological and histopathological findings were recorded. When available, complete blood count and serum biochemistry which was performed within 7 days of presentation were evaluated. Clinical signs reported to be less than 1 month in duration were categorized as acute.

The US images, videos and reports were reviewed by board-certified radiologists (DP and AA). Lesions were evaluated according to primary location (ileocolic junction [ICJ], colon), size (maximum length and thickness), echogenicity (hypoechoic, hyperechoic, mixed), distribution (focal, multifocal, diffuse) and wall layering (normal, altered, lost). Measurements were obtained from the reports and/or measurements on the images or video clips. Colonic and cecal wall thicknesses were considered normal up to 1.5 mm and 3 mm, respectively. 10 If multifocal GI lesions were present, the largest one was measured and recorded, and when reported, multicentric lesions were noted. Additional findings such as ulceration (crater-like or irregular mucosal surface with gas pocket), 14 perforation, mechanical obstruction, free gas, free fluid and regional lymphadenopathy were recorded, if present. Regional lymph nodes (LNs) were considered abnormal if more than 5 mm thick, subjectively hypoechoic and/or rounded. 15

Histopathologic samples were obtained via US-guided transcutaneous biopsy, colonoscopy, surgery or necropsy, while cytology samples were obtained by US-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of the intestinal mass. Survival time was calculated from the time of the first US examination associated with clinical signs related to the large intestinal neoplasia until time of death.

Basic descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population and ultrasonographic findings. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarized as median and range because several variables were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables between ACA and LSA groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Associations between categorical ultrasonographic variables and tumor type were evaluated using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 92 cats met the inclusion criteria. Of them, 44 were diagnosed with large intestinal ACA, 38 cats with high-grade LSA, five with mast cell tumor, two with undifferentiated sarcoma, two with hemangiosarcoma and one with plasma cell tumor. Only cats with large intestinal ACA and LSA were included. The small population size of the other tumor types precluded statistical analysis and characterization of imaging appearance and were therefore excluded. Three cats were excluded because of insufficient imaging data.

Adenocarcinoma

The cats in the ACA group had a median age of 12.5 years (range 5.1–18.4) at the time of presentation. There were 23 female cats and 21 male cats. The breeds represented included 29 domestic shorthair (DSH) cats, nine domestic longhair (DLH) cats, three Maine Coon, one Balinese, one Abyssinian and one crossbreed. In total, 21 cats presented with acute clinical signs, 21 cats presented with chronic clinical signs and two cats did not have available data. Presenting signs included anorexia (56%), weight loss (40%), vomiting (36%), diarrhea (29%), hematochezia (27%), lethargy (24%), constipation (18%), tenesmus (7%) and inappropriate defecation (2%). Physical examination findings at the time of presentation included a palpable abdominal mass (41%), muscle wasting (29%), dehydration (27%), hypothermia (10%), painful abdomen (7%), doughy abdomen (7%) and distended abdomen (7%). Blood work was available for 39/44 cats. The most common abnormalities were azotemia (33%), anemia (26%), neutrophilia (26%) and hypermagnesemia (26%).

ACA affected the colon in 28/44 (64%) cats and the ICJ in 16/44 (36%) cats. All cats had a single tumor (Figure 1). The ACA tumors had a median length of 23 mm (range 9.4–56) and a median wall thickening of 9 mm (range 5–24.2) (Table 1). Ultrasonographically, the lesions exhibited a loss of wall layering (84%), altered wall layering (9%), or a combination of altered and lost wall layering (4%), and were primarily hypoechoic (84%), hyperechoic (9%) or showed a mixed echogenicity (7%).

There was ultrasonographic evidence of free peritoneal fluid in 14 (32%) cats, luminal obstruction in 13 (30%) cats, ulceration in six (14%) cats and perforation with free gas in two (5%) cats. Of the 13 cats, 10 with obstruction had a colonic mass, including one cat with an intussusception, and three cats had an ICJ mass. Another cat had a partially obstructive intussusception related to an ICJ mass. Regional lymphadenopathy was noted on US in 29/44 (66%) cats, with a median thickness of 6.05 mm (range 3.7–15.3) (Table 1). Large intestinal ACA was diagnosed using cytology (11%) and histology (89%), including 33 samples from surgery, three from necropsy, two from colonoscopy and one without a specified biopsy method. Three cats initially underwent FNA that failed to diagnose neoplasia. Sampling of affected LNs was available in 15/44 (34%) cats. Of these, three samples were obtained by cytology, 11 by biopsy and one at necropsy. Of the 15 LN samples, nine demonstrated metastatic or neoplastic involvement, three were reactive and three were normal.

Survival time and treatment data were available for 15/44 (34%) cats. Overall median survival time for cats with large intestinal ACA was 19 days (range 0–1378). Cats that received no treatment (n = 4/15), surgery only (n = 6/15), chemotherapy only (n = 1/15) and surgery followed by non-standardized adjuvant chemotherapy (n = 4/15) had a median survival time of 1 day (range 0–19), 13 days (range 3–1378), 3 days and 280 days (range 190–362), respectively.

Lymphoma

The 38 cats with high-grade LSA had a median age of 11.8 years (range 2.5–16.1) at the time of presentation. There were 19 female cats and 19 male cats. The breeds represented included 28 DSH cats, seven DLH cats, one domestic mediumhair cat, one Siamese and one Russian Blue. In total, 17 cats presented with acute clinical signs, 18 cats presented with chronic clinical signs, two cats had no GI clinical signs and one cat did not have the duration of time recorded. Presenting signs included anorexia (53%), weight loss (42%), lethargy (39%), vomiting (29%), diarrhea (32%), hematochezia (16%), constipation (11%), tenesmus (11%) and inappropriate defecation (3%). Physical examination findings at the time of presentation included a palpable abdominal mass (68%), muscle wasting (26%), dehydration (21%), painful abdomen (12%) and tense abdomen (9%). Blood work was available for 37/38 cats. The most common abnormalities were anemia (41%), neutrophilia (41%) and azotemia (24%).

LSA affected the ICJ in 20/38 (53%) cats and the colon in 14/38 (37%) cats; four cats had multifocal intestinal tumors (the ICJ and descending colon in one cat; the small intestine (SI) and colon in one cat; the SI, ICJ and colon in one cat; and the SI and ICJ in one cat). The large intestinal LSA tumors had a median length of 40.3 mm (range 13–70) and a median wall thickening of 15 mm (range 3.7–29.4) (Table 1, Figures 2 and 3). Tumors exhibited loss of wall layering (97%) or altered wall layering (3%) and appeared hypoechoic (92%) or showed a mixed echogenicity (8%).

Free fluid was detected in 18 (47%) cats, ulceration in eight (21%) cats, obstructing intussusception in four (11%) cats, free gas in three (8%) cats and perforation in two (5%) cats. Among the cats with obstructive intussusception, two had an ICJ mass, one had a colonic mass, and one had concurrent ICJ and colonic masses. Regional lymphadenopathy was noted in 31/38 (82%) cats, with a median thickness of 8.6 mm (range 4–35 mm) (Table 1). Large intestinal LSA was diagnosed by cytology (47%) and histology (53%), including nine samples from surgery, seven samples from US-guided transcutaneous biopsy, three from necropsy and one from colonoscopy. Sampling of affected LNs was available for 7/38 (18%) cats. Of these, six samples were obtained by cytology and one by biopsy. Of the seven LN samples, six demonstrated neoplastic involvement and one sample was non-diagnostic. Multicentric LSA involving the liver, mesentery, kidneys and bladder was reported in 7/38 (18.4%) cats.

Survival time and treatment data were available for 12/38 cats. Overall median survival time for cats with large intestinal LSA was 23 days (range 1–234). Cats that received no treatment (n = 5/12) or non-standardized chemotherapy (n = 7/12) had a median survival time of 4 (range 1–15) and 55 days (range 11–234), respectively.

Median lesion length, wall thickness and regional LN thickness differed significantly between the ACA and LSA groups. Lesions were significantly longer in cats with LSA than in cats with ACA (median 40.3 mm [range 13.0–70.0] vs 23.0 mm [range 9.4–56.0]; P <0.001). Intestinal wall thickness was also significantly greater in cats with LSA than in cats with ACA (median 15.0 mm [range 3.7–29.4] vs 9.0 mm [range 5.0–24.2]; P <0.001). Regional LN thickness was significantly greater in cats with LSA than in cats with ACA (median 8.6 mm [range 4.0–35.0] vs 6.05 mm [range 3.7–15.3]; P = 0.042).

No significant associations were identified between tumor type and the presence of ultrasonographic ulceration/erosion, perforation, obstruction, free gas, free fluid or steatitis (all P >0.05). However, obstruction tended to be more frequent in ACA than LSA, although this did not reach statistical significance (12/41 vs 4/36; P = 0.089). Echotexture differed significantly between tumor types (P = 0.034), with ACA more often heterogeneous and LSA more often homogeneous.

Discussion

Signalment, clinical signs, physical examination findings and initial blood work in our population of cats with large intestinal tumors were similar to data in previous studies on feline GI tumors1,4,7,16 –20 and did not provide distinctive features between ACA and LSA.

In our study, ACA tended to arise from the colon while LSA occurred more frequently at the ICJ, aligning with previous reports. With the ICJ being a region rich in lymphoid tissue and characterized by complex mucosal architecture, one can speculate a higher rate of lymphoid neoplasia.1,16,20 –23

The majority of ACA and LSA tumors in our study displayed similar ultrasonographic features, including wall thickening, loss of layering and reduced echogenicity, consistent with the previously reported ultrasonographic appearance of feline intestinal neoplasia.8 –11,22,24 In addition, in this cohort, LSA tumors had significantly greater mural thickness and length, with more pronounced regional lymphadenopathy than ACA tumors. These measurements, combined with the loss of wall layering and the poor echogenicity of the lesions, are useful features to provide a pertinent, prioritized differential diagnosis. These morphological findings likely reflect inherent biological differences between the two tumor types: although ACA originates from the mucosal epithelium and tends to remain relatively localized, LSA arises from submucosal lymphoid populations with a higher propensity for mural infiltration, circumferential spread and regional lymphatic involvement.21,23

Intestinal ACA tumors are often described as single, annular, constrictive lesions with luminal stenosis.1,18,23,25,26 In this study, intestinal obstruction without an intussusception was only noted in cats with ACA tumors (30%), consistent with previous reports linking the annular configuration of these tumors to a higher incidence of obstruction.1,8,20,23,25 –27 Contrary to ACA, LSA tumors are uncommonly obstructive, as seen in our study as well.

In our study, LSA was diagnosed by FNA in 47% of cases, while ACA was diagnosed by FNA in 11% of cases. In contrast, ACA was more likely to be diagnosed by a surgical procedure (75%). The US imaging features, such as presence of an obstruction, lesion size or presence of more than one lesion, contribute to the choice to pursue fine-needle sampling as opposed to other sampling modalities. Lesion exfoliative characteristics of each tumor type likely contributed to the discrepancy as well.

LSA tumors can also affect other abdominal locations, such as other parts of the GI tract, spleen, kidneys, mesentery and liver, as was noted in 28.9% of the cats in our study. Similar observations have been reported in gastric and small intestinal LSA.19,22,28

When intussusception occurs in older cats, underlying neoplasia is typically diagnosed, and LSA was the most common tumor type reported in the literature;17,29 –32 however, rare associations with intestinal ACA, mast cell tumor and adenoma have also been described.32,33 The ICJ is a common location for feline intussusception.3,17,29 –31 Six cats in our study (four LSA, two ACA) with large intestinal neoplasia had concurrent intussusception at the site of the tumor, with four of them involving the ICJ. Detecting the underlying tumor via ultrasound at the intussusception site may be challenging. 24 In our study, one of the LSA tumors associated with intussusception was not clearly identified; a limitation described in previous studies.17,32

Erosions and ulcerations are common changes associated with GI tumors.15,28 In this study, the detection was estimated at 14% in ACA and 21% on LSA. In a recent study centered on gastric LSA, ulceration/erosion was seen in 51% of cats. 28 The detection rate likely depends on several factors, such as the operator’s experience, quality of equipment and location of the mucosal irregularity/defects. Because of the limited clinicopathological data in our study, an association between anemia, hematochezia and ulceration on US warrants further investigation.

Peritoneal effusion was a common finding in cats with large intestinal ACA (32%) and LSA (47%) tumors, while perforation (ACA: 5%; LSA: 5%) and free gas (ACA: 5%; LSA: 8%) were uncommon. These observations are consistent with previous reports of feline GI neoplasia; however, they may also be present with other non-neoplastic GI disorders.8,9,17,19,23,24,28 More recently, reports of feline GI eosinophilic sclerosing fibroplasia 34 and of feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) 35 described intestinal mass lesion, often of mixed echogenicity, that can mimic tumors. In FIP, the prevalence of intestinal lesions was seen in 68% of cats, with 52% of them located at the ICJ or colon.

This study has several inherent limitations, primarily related to its retrospective design. Case inclusion was dependent on the availability and quality of medical records and ultrasonographic images, which may have introduced selection and information bias. Ultrasonographic evaluations were performed by different operators over an extended period, using variable equipment and imaging protocols, potentially affecting measurement consistency, image interpretation and precise colonic location assessment (ascending versus transverse vs descending). In addition, accurate assessment of colonic tumor length can be hindered by extension into the pelvic canal or excessive fecal content, leading to underestimation of lesion extent. Furthermore, treatment approaches and follow-up duration were highly variable, precluding meaningful assessment of survival or treatment outcome. Finally, the relatively small sample size and incomplete staging information limited the ability to identify subtle differences in biological behavior between tumor types.

Conclusions

Cats with large intestinal ACA and high-grade LSA in our study shared a similar signalment, clinical signs, physical examination findings and initial blood work. ACA and LSA tumors have ultrasonographic features in common, such as transmural thickening, with loss of wall layering and lymphadenopathy. However, LSA lesions in our study were generally longer, thicker lesions and were associated with more frequent and pronounced lymphadenopathy. In addition, LSA tended to have multicentric distribution while ACA were single, annular lesions that were more commonly associated with intestinal luminal obstruction. As there is an overlap in clinical and ultrasonographic findings, sampling of the ultrasonographic lesions is recommended to achieve a final diagnosis.
