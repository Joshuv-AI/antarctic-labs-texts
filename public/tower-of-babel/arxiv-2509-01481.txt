Ideal Optical Flux Lattices
Ophelia Evelyn Sommer, Nigel R. Cooper

Abstract
The realization of fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states in cold atomic gases is a long-standing goal in quantum simulation. Established approaches, including rapidly rotating gases and tight-binding lattices, are often hampered by low interaction energies and small many-body energy gaps. While optical flux lattices (OFLs) can achieve higher effective magnetic flux densities, standard two-state configurations generate highly non-uniform fields, and extensions to multi-state systems introduce significant experimental complexity. Here, we present a new paradigm for engineering robust FQH phases in OFLs using only two internal atomic states. We show that the introduction of an additional scalar potential provides a generic mechanism for creating Chern bands that are simultaneously essentially flat and “ideal.” Drawing on concepts from moiré materials, these desirable properties arise by tuning lattice parameters to certain N N -flat manifolds ( N = 1 , 2 , … ) (N=1,2,\dots) , where the 1 1 -flat manifold shares its origin with certain “magic-angle” conditions. We provide a variety of examples of how to design optical flux lattices, including dark-state OFLs, to achieve these goals. This method allows for precise tuning of band flatness and stabilizes both Abelian and non-Abelian FQH phases. Our scheme is compatible with existing experimental capabilities using vector polarizability, opening practical routes to exploring strongly correlated topological physics with cold atoms.



I
Introduction

One of the long-standing challenges in quantum simulation using cold
atomic gases is the realization of fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states.
The possibility of using experiments on cold atomic gases to explore FQH states of
bosons – in place of the fermions familiar from electronic matter –
was first discussed in the context of Bose-Einstein condensates in
harmonic trapping potentials, for which Landau level wavefunctions
emerge under rapid rotation
Cooper 2008
;
Fetter 2009
.
Remarkably, the bosonic Laughlin state can be the
exact

ground state at sufficiently high angular momentum
Wilkin et al. 1998
,
due to the short-range nature of typical interatomic interactions.
Subsequent theoretical work established the phases of short-range
interacting bosons as a function of the Landau level filling
factor,
\nu
Cooper et al. 2001
. In addition to conventional
hierarchy/composite fermion states, bosons form unusually stable
non-Abelian phases: including the Moore-Read phase
Moore and Read 1991

at
\nu=1
Cooper et al. 2001
;
Regnault and Jolicoeur 2003
, and the Read-Rezayi
phases
Read and Rezayi 1999
;
Rezayi et al. 2005
. Achieving these FQH phases in
experiments using rotating gases is extremely challenging, in part
due to the low flux densities and hence low interaction energies.
However, this may be mitigated by tight confinement using optical
tweezers
Lunt et al.
.

One way to increase the interaction energies of FQH states is
to place the atoms in tight-binding lattices, engineered (by
breaking time-reversal symmetry) to host narrow Chern bands
Cooper 2008
. The
Harper-Hofstadter model supports the
\nu=1/2
bosonic Laughlin
state
Sørensen et al. 2005
and
\nu=1
Moore-Read state
Palm et al. 2021
, as well as other FQH states
that are stabilized by the lattice itself and do not have continuum
analogues
Möller and Cooper 2009
. Interaction energies are boosted compared to
those in rotating gases, but they typically remain small. The state of
the art experiments
Léonard et al. 2023
have shown Laughlin
correlations for two particles, with a many-body energy gap of order

\sim 1\,$\mathrm{Hz}$
.

A separate approach to forming Chern bands for cold atoms uses
the possibility to couple internal (electronic or magnetic)
levels of atoms, to form so-called “optical flux lattices”
(OFLs)
Cooper 2011
. The main conceit of OFLs is that they
require state-dependent potentials that do not commute at different
points in space. In the deep-lattice limit, the energetically preferred
internal state at each point in real space forms a texture with a
varying direction. The Berry phase associated to this texture becomes an
Aharonov-Bohm phase for an emergent magnetic field associated with the
residual dynamics, that often persists even in the intermediate-shallow
lattice regime. Thus, OFLs stand in stark contrast to deep-lattice
tight-binding models, in that they are generally shallow lattice
continuum models. As a result, they can operate at higher flux densities
boosting the densities at which FQH states can form, and thereby also
the associated energy scales.

While the emergent magnetic field in OFLs has a nonzero average, it
is typically spatially varying, so these lattices cannot directly
reproduce the uniform continuum Landau levels. In particular, if only two
internal levels are coupled the local magnetic field must vanish
within the unit cell
Cooper 2011
, so the field must
have very large spatial variations. To mimic continuum Landau
levels more closely, OFLs have been proposed using
N>2
internal
states
Cooper and Moessner 2012
, which converge quickly to the
continuum Landau levels with increasing
N
. However, experimental
implementations
Cooper and Dalibard 2013
;
Lauria et al. 2022
quickly become
more cumbersome, requiring multiple frequencies of the light field to
control the various interspecies couplings, which can lead to enhanced
losses through light scattering.

In this paper, we take a different approach, and show how OFLs using just two
internal states can be designed to host robust Abelian and non-Abelian
FQH phases. We draw on theoretical work inspired by advances in two-dimensional electronic platforms, such as the moiré materials
formed from graphene or TMDs. The prevalence of non-trivial band geometry in these platforms has led to a deeper understanding
of the general properties of a single-particle band that are desirable in order to stabilize FQH states
Wang et al. 2021
;
Ledwith et al. 2023
;
Estienne et al. 2023
;
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Fujimoto et al. 2025
;
Ledwith et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Reddy et al. 2023
;
Jackson et al. 2015
;
Claassen et al. 2015
;
Ledwith et al. 2022
. A prominent notion emerging from these considerations is that of
“ideal” bands, which are “vortexable”, and consequently allow for
the appearance of exact FQH states for short-range interactions,
generalizing the Laughlin state in the lowest Landau level to exact
states in these bands. Our principal result is to present a
generic mechanism for creating essentially flat and ideal Chern
|C|=1

bands, upon introducing an additional scalar potential to OFLs. This
additional scalar potential can be used to tune the flatness of the
band to a high precision. These flat bands host fractional quantum Hall
phases in the presence of repulsive interactions, and can be realized
with currently existing experimental tools in optical lattices using
vector polarizability.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Sec.
II
we provide background on optical flux lattices, emphasizing their description as reciprocal space tight-binding models. We then describe the concepts of ideality in Sec.
III
, and explain how to use these to design ideal OFLs. We provide explicit examples of these design principles in Sec.
IV
, including also for dark-state optical flux lattices. In Sec.
V
, we demonstrate their utility for stabilizing Abelian and non-Abelian FQH states.

II
Background on Optical Flux Lattices

II.1
Real Space Viewpoint

Optical lattices are created by
coupling atoms to lasers that transfer
momentum. For optical flux lattices, we further allow the lasers to change the internal state of the atom. Let the center-of-mass position of the atom be denoted

\mathbf{r}
, the atomic mass
M
, and label the manifold of internal
energy levels that are coupled resonantly by the lasers
\ket{\alpha}
.
We will consider this manifold to be a subset of the ground-state
manifold of the atom, and ignore finite lifetime effects. Upon eliminating the photon dynamics by going
into a rotating frame, the dynamics for the atom are governed by
the familiar sum of kinetic and potential energy
\hat{V}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}
of a spinor particle

\hat{H}=-\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}}{2M}\hat{1}+\hat{V}(\mathbf{r})\,.

(1)

Despite atomic neutrality, this Hamiltonian can
give rise to a lowest band similar to a Landau level
Dalibard et al. 2011
;
Goldman et al. 2014
;
Cooper et al. 2019
. The key to
understanding this is the spinor nature of the particle, and it is
particularly clear in the adiabatic deep-lattice limit
M\to\infty
.
In this limit, the Hamiltonian reduces to the potential
energy
\hat{V}(\mathbf{r})
. The low-energy manifold consists of
the ground state of
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})
for each
\mathbf{r}
, which
forms a spinor texture
n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
. Consider now including the
leading
1/M
corrections to the dynamics, which amounts to projecting
(
1
) to the subspace of adiabatic wavefunctions of the
form
\braket{\mathbf{r},\alpha|\psi}=\psi^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=b(\mathbf{r})n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
where
b(\mathbf{r})
is
an arbitrary scalar wavefunction. Since
n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
is fixed,
the dynamics are purely those of the scalar
b(\mathbf{r})
. Under an infinitesimal translation the
total wavefunction picks up a Berry phase
Qua 1984
from the change in the spinor direction. This manifests as an emergent background gauge
field acting on
b(\mathbf{r})
, which is evident from projecting the
momentum to the space of adiabatic wavefunctions

-\mathrm{i}\bar{n}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\psi^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=[-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}-\mathrm{i}\bar{n}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})]b(\mathbf{r})\,.

(2)

Clearly this is the same form as a scalar particle
in the presence of a magnetic field with vector potential

\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{texture}}=\mathrm{i}\bar{n}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
, or equivalently magnetic field

B_{\mathrm{texture}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\bar{n}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\wedge\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\,.

(3)

The Berry phase of the spinor texture becomes the Aharonov-Bohm phase
Aharonov and Bohm 1959
of the effective magnetic
field felt by
b(\mathbf{r})
Cooper 2011
, and the astute
reader will recognize equation (
3
) as the expression
for the Berry curvature of
n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
. The Hamiltonian
governing
b
in the adiabatic limit takes the form

H^{\mathrm{adi}}=\frac{(-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{texture}})^{2}}{2M}+\frac{D(\mathbf{r})}{2M}+\bar{n}_{\alpha}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}n^{\beta}

(4)

where
D(\mathbf{r})=\nabla\bar{n}_{\alpha}(\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}-n^{\alpha}\bar{n}_{\beta})\nabla n^{\beta}

is the trace of the Fubini-Study metric of the
texture. This adiabatic Hamiltonian is valid whenever the
low-energy dynamics can be projected along a spin texture. This is
evidently justified in deep lattices, but even when
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}

is comparable to the typical kinetic energy, there are a plethora
of examples where this holds approximately.

An optical flux lattice (OFL) is defined to be an optical lattice whose low-energy
subspace has a texture
n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
with effective magnetic field

B_{\mathrm{texture}}
of nonzero average flux.

Perhaps the simplest example – for a two-level system – is the triangular OFL introduced in Ref.
Cooper 2011
, for which

\hat{V}_{\rm tri}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{\sigma}_{i}\cos(\mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r})\,,

(5)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{i=1,2,3}
are the Pauli matrices, and

\mathbf{q}_{i}
are three in-plane momentum at
120^{\circ}
to each other. We define them as

\mathbf{q}_{i}\equiv q\mathbf{e}_{x}\cos\tfrac{2\pi i}{3}+q\mathbf{e}_{y}\sin\tfrac{2\pi i}{3}

(6)

in terms of the orthogonal unit vectors
\mathbf{e}_{x,y}
and the overall momentum
q
.
It is readily found that
V(\mathbf{r})
is gapped at all positions, and the dressed state wraps the Bloch sphere to give nonzero flux of
B(\mathbf{r})
through the periodic real-space cell. The real-space lattice is triangular, with a high degree of translational symmetry such that
B(\mathbf{r})
and
D(\mathbf{r})
have translational symmetry over a cell with just
\pi
-flux of
B(\mathbf{r})
. (This is discussed further in Sec.
II.3
.)

Practical implementations for OFLs in cold atomic gases are likely to employ two-photon Raman coupling. A simple form of OFL using two-photon coupling was proposed in Ref.
Cooper and Dalibard 2011
. This uses three in-plane beams at frequency
\omega_{L}
along directions
\mathbf{q}_{i}
(
6
) to form Rabi couplings,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\cos\theta\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\sin\theta\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{z}\times\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i}}\,,

(8)

where
\kappa
and
\theta
are parameters. The transition between internal states involves one out-of-plane circularly polarized beam at frequency
\omega_{L}+\delta
with spatially uniform coupling
{\kappa}^{\prime}_{-}
. [See Fig.
2
(a).] From these, the potential is of the form

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\rm 2-photon}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{\rm tot}^{2}}{3\delta}\hat{1}+\frac{1}{3\delta}\left[(|\kappa_{+}|^{2}-|\kappa_{-}|^{2})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\right.

(9)

\displaystyle\left.+\bar{\kappa}^{\prime}_{-}\kappa_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{+}+{\kappa}^{\prime}_{-}\bar{\kappa}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\right]\,,

where
\kappa_{\rm tot}^{2}=\sum_{m}|\kappa_{m}|^{2}
, and
\delta
is the detuning between the two internal states. See Appendix
E
for details on the polarizability.
This again leads to a real-space unit cell that is triangular, in which the Bloch vector wraps the sphere, signaling that there is a net flux per unit cell. However, in this case the minimal translational symmetry encloses
2\pi
flux per unit cell.

II.2
Designing in reciprocal space

The definition of OFLs, and the above examples, were motivated by the properties in real space. However, given that optical lattices involve the use of lasers to write in momentum transfers (with or without changes in internal state), it is very instructive to view them in reciprocal space.
Indeed, this set of momentum transfers directly constructs a tight-binding model in reciprocal space.
In the adiabatic limit, the effective magnetic field is nothing but
the Berry curvature of
n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
which is the ground state of
this reciprocal-space tight-binding model
Cooper and Moessner 2012
. Hence considering any tight-binding
Chern insulator with Hamiltonian
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{k})
, there is
a dual optical flux lattice with potential
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})
.

To illustrate the reciprocal space mapping, consider the triangular OFL constructed for spin-
1/2

fermionic atoms in
Cooper and Dalibard 2011

9

The interspecies coupling involves one in-plane photon, from
\kappa_{\pm}

\tilde{\kappa}

8

\mathbf{q}_{i}

|\pm\rangle

\mathbf{G}_{2}=\mathbf{q}_{2}-\mathbf{q}_{1}
etc. These couplings give the next-nearest-neighbor bonds on the honeycomb lattice.
Overall the optical potential of the two-photon OFL of Ref.
Cooper and Dalibard 2011

2

II.3
Translational Symmetries

We shall make much use of symmetries in later sections of the paper.
It is therefore important to pause to consider in detail the symmetries of the three lattices just presented.

Arising from standing beams of light in a
periodic interference pattern, the potential is genuinely periodic.
Nevertheless, a finer translational symmetry can often be found by
letting the translation operators act on the spinor degrees of freedom.

For the dual triangular (square) model, the minimal unit cell under which

\hat{V}^{\triangle}
(

\Lambda^{\star}_{\mathrm{pot}}=2\mathbf{q}_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus 2\mathbf{q}_{2}\mathbb{Z}

\hat{T}_{a}\hat{H}=\hat{H}\hat{T}_{a}

III
Ideality

Having discussed several examples of OFLs, let us return to the adiabatic Hamiltonian (
4
). A flat Chern band is evidently furnished from the Landau levels when the magnetic field and potential
\frac{D}{2M}+\bar{n}_{\alpha}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}n^{\beta}
are both uniform. Perhaps surprisingly, as was recognized in
Aharonov and Casher 1979
, and later in the context of adiabatic moiré bands
Shi et al. 2024
, a flat lowest band can survive a non-uniform magnetic field
B_{\mathrm{texture}}(\mathbf{r})
with the same average, when the potential takes the value
\frac{D}{2M}+\bar{n}_{\alpha}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}n^{\beta}=-\frac{B_{\mathrm{texture}}}{2M}
. The Hamiltonian then reduces to the Aharonov-Casher (AC) form:

H^{\mathrm{AC}}=\frac{[-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{texture}}(\mathbf{r})]^{2}}{2M}-\frac{B_{\mathrm{texture}}(\mathbf{r})}{2M}\,.

(13)

To see how this occurs, let us introduce the following notation to draw similarities with the Landau levels. (A more complete review is given in Appendix
C
.) Denote the unit cell area of
\Lambda
containing
2\pi
flux by
2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}
, and
the associated cyclotron frequency
\omega_{c}=(M\ell_{B}^{2})^{-1}
. We use the complexified coordinate
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
, while the
complexified kinetic momentum for the corresponding uniform field (in symmetric gauge) is

\pi=-\mathrm{i}(\partial_{x}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{y})+\mathrm{i}\bar{z}/2\ell_{B}^{2}
. We will also need the
momenta of opposite chirality, which can be considered the ‘guiding-center’ momenta,
\tilde{\pi}=\pi-\mathrm{i}\bar{z}/\ell_{B}^{2}
. A non-uniform field with average
\ell_{B}^{-2}
can be described by the complexified vector potential
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{i}\bar{n}_{\alpha}(\partial_{x}+\mathrm{i}\partial_{y})n^{\alpha}=\mathrm{i}{z}/2\ell_{B}^{2}+\delta\mathcal{A}
. Thus the AC Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

H^{\mathrm{AC}}=\frac{(\pi-\delta\bar{\mathcal{A}})(\pi^{\dagger}-\delta\mathcal{A})}{2M}\,.

(14)

This Hamiltonian naturally has zero modes equal in quantity to the number of solutions to

[\pi^{\dagger}-\delta\mathcal{A}]b(\mathbf{r})=0.

(15)

When
\delta\mathcal{A}=0
, this is nothing but the usual lowest Landau level (LLL) condition, with solutions for each crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
in the Brillouin zone of
\Lambda^{\star}
given by

\braket{\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}}=\mathcal{N}\sigma(z-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}k)\mathrm{e}^{-(\ell_{B}^{-2}|z|^{2}+\ell_{B}^{2}|k|^{2})/4+\mathrm{i}\bar{k}z/2},

(16)

where
\sigma
is the modified Weierstrass
\sigma
-function
Haldane 2018
,
k=k_{x}+\mathrm{i}k_{y}
, and
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization constant. Furthermore, whenever
\delta\mathcal{A}
corresponds to a magnetic field modulation
\delta B_{\mathrm{texture}}=B_{\mathrm{texture}}-\ell_{B}^{-2}
with vanishing average, the generic solution to (
15
) takes the form of density modulated lowest Landau levels

b_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathrm{e}^{\phi(\mathbf{r})}\braket{\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}};\qquad\pi^{\dagger}\phi=\delta\mathcal{A}\,.

(17)

In the Coulomb gauge
\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{texture}}=0
the density modulation
\mathrm{e}^{\phi}
is the Kähler potential for the magnetic field modulation

-\nabla^{2}\phi=\delta B_{\mathrm{texture}}(\mathbf{r})\,.

(18)

The manifold of zero modes forms a flat band with Chern number
\mathcal{C}=-1
. Beyond being a flat Chern band, the LLL is remarkable for the ability to attach vortices within it, so that
b(\mathbf{r})\mapsto zb(\mathbf{r})
does not cause any excitation. Chern bands for which vortex attachment is possible are known as ideal or vortexable
Ledwith et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2021
. Wavefunctions belonging to many fractional quantum Hall phases in the LLL have exact parent Hamiltonian given by various zero-range repulsive interactions, with respect to which they have zero energy. For such parent interactions, ideal bands that are flat also give rise to zero energy ground states in the same phase as the LLL.
For scalar particles with
\mathcal{C}=-1
(and
2\pi
flux per unit cell), the AC wavefunctions (
17
) exhaust the possibilities for ideal wavefunctions
Estienne et al. 2023
.

Instead of approaching the design of OFL with a view towards mimicking a uniform field, a natural path to flat ideal bands is AC Hamiltonian mimicry. Although we will not be able to find an exact AC Hamiltonian, we find that perturbations to an AC Hamiltonian are well behaved, even when they are comparable in size to the typical gap
\sim\omega_{c}
.
Including an amplitude modulated spinor
\chi^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=n^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\mathrm{e}^{\phi(\mathbf{r})}
, a
\mathcal{C}=-1
ideal band in an OFL has wavefunction

\psi^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\chi^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\braket{\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}}\,.

(19)

A useful practical criterion for ideality is saturating the trace inequality, which we now describe.
To any wavefunction with crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
there is a periodic
Bloch wavefunction of the form

\braket{\mathbf{r}\alpha|{u_{\mathbf{k}}}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\psi^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
. From this we
may compute the Berry curvature
\Omega(\mathbf{k})=\mathrm{i}\braket{\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}|\wedge|\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}}
and Fubini-Study metric

g^{ab}(\mathbf{k})=\braket{\nabla^{a}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}|(1-\ket{u_{\mathbf{k}}}\bra{u_{\mathbf{k}}})|\nabla^{b}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}}
. For any band

\tr g(\mathbf{k})\geq|\Omega(\mathbf{k})|
with equality for all
\mathbf{k}
only if the band is ideal
Wang et al. 2021
. Noting that

\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\,\Omega(\mathbf{k})=2\pi\mathcal{C}
, a natural measure of
deviation from ideality is the trace violation

T=\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\tr g(\mathbf{k})\right]-|\mathcal{C}|\geq 0\,,

(20)

which takes the value
2n
for the
n
th Landau level, see Appendix
C.2
. For comparison with a different measure of ideality deviation used in
Nascimbene and Dalibard 2024
see Appendix
C.3
.

IV.2
Dark OFL

From the dual Haldane example it is clear that
1
-flat bands can be achieved by adding a suitably tuned scalar lattice even when the adiabatic Hamiltonian is far from the AC limit. We will now introduce an OFL that is an exact AC Hamiltonian in the adiabatic limit. Nevertheless adding a tunable scalar lattice still permits a huge decrease in the bandwidth.
As was shown in
Nascimbene and Dalibard 2024
, OFLs can be constructed
also for so-called “dark” lattices, for which the optically dressed state is exactly decoupled from the optical fields, and lies at the lowest energy with all other “bright” levels being energetically repulsed. Dark optical lattices can be understood
straightforwardly from their dual tight-binding models. Including both
N

ground states
\alpha
and
\tilde{N}
excited states
\tilde{\alpha}
,
a dark lattice arises when there are no potential couplings connecting
ground states
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})=0
, and the number of coupled
ground states exceeds the number of excited states
N>\tilde{N}
. Then
the off-diagonal part of the potential
V^{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})
must have a kernel of dimension
N-\tilde{N}>0
, which describes textures
with exactly zero potential energy, the “dark” states. When the dark-state texture
has net flux per unit cell, these are dark OFLs. It is well known

Chen et al. 2014
that flat bands of tight-binding models
cannot be energetically isolated and possess nonzero Chern number: coming from a tight-binding model in reciprocal space, the real-space texture in dark OFLs must be degenerate with a different
bright texture at least at one point in the unit cell. This makes the
adiabatic limit more subtle as the gap must be due to the zero-point
motion of the atom. As noted in
Nascimbene and Dalibard 2024
for
different dark OFLs, the bright-state gap generically vanishes quadratically
around the gapless point. If
V_{1}
is the characteristic size of the
potential, the bright state zero-point energy is
\propto\sqrt{MV_{1}}
. As
V_{1}\to\infty
the model will project
perfectly onto the adiabatic subspace of dark states, and be governed
by the adiabatic Hamiltonian (
4
) with geometric
potential contribution
D(\mathbf{r})/2M
. For most dark OFLs this potential
is significant as
D(\mathbf{r})\geq|B_{\mathrm{texture}}(\mathbf{r})|
, hence
D(\mathbf{r})/2M
is generically a fluctuating potential comparable to the natural
cyclotron energy of the problem. This means that even as
V_{1}\to\infty

a generic dark OFL will
not
lead to a flat Chern band, even
in the absence of diabatic corrections, which decay with the reciprocal
zero-point energy
\propto 1/\sqrt{V_{1}}
.

in

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

We have shown that optical flux lattices can generically support nearly flat and ideal bands through the addition of a suitable scalar potential. By tuning one potential parameter for lattices with
C_{6}

2\pi
-flux and
C_{4,6}

\pi
-flux symmetries, one can reach
1
-flat bands, for which the interaction strength
\tilde{g}
can readily be made big enough in experiment, to reach the Laughlin, Jain sequence, and Moore-Read states.
Due to the flexibility of this approach, the principal challenge remains to realize a suitable host OFL potential. In alkali-metal gases this is complicated by the fact that the dominant contributions to the polarizability typically arise from the
\mathrm{D}_{1,2}
lines, with opposite vector polarizability. To craft a state-dependent lattice potential, the laser frequency must be suitably close to these transitions, which necessarily begets photon scattering. However, for heavy atoms like Rb or Cs, this photon scattering need not be prohibitive for the lifetime. In Appendix
B
we give examples of experimental setups that can realize the dual Haldane and square models. The photon scattering rate in these setups is of order
1-6\times 10^{-4}\omega_{c}
. For
{}^{87}\mathrm{Rb}
, a wavelength of
\lambda=810\,$\mathrm{nm}$
yields a photon scattering rate around
0.8-1.6\,$\mathrm{s}$
. In the case of
{}^{133}\mathrm{Cs}
, with red detuned beams at wavelength
\lambda=950\,$\mathrm{nm}$
, it can be as low as
0.1\,$\mathrm{s}$
. The photon scattering can be further reduced using other atomic species (including Dy, Er, or Ti) where state-dependent Raman coupling can be achieved with far-detuned light. Instead of using far-detuned light, it is interesting to note that, absent many-body effects, photon scattering from (near) resonant levels can in principle be reduced arbitrarily in dark lattice setups by increasing the laser power, though to our knowledge, experimentally achieved dark-lattice lifetimes remain small
Wang et al. 2018
. To shape higher-shell Fourier components of the lattice, one particularly interesting technique, is the realization of optical potentials using programmable holographic techniques
Zupancic et al. 2016
. Beyond photon scattering, we caution that magnetic field noise will likely pose an important experimental obstacle, as it contributes a non-uniform perturbation to the system.
Irrespective of atomic species, we have provided a general approach by which to realize flat ideal Chern bands and robust fractional quantum Hall states. The ability to precisely tune band geometry will enable systematic studies of the transition into exotic topologically ordered phases, including the non-Abelian states sought for fault-tolerant quantum computation. Furthermore, this approach strengthens the connection between cold atoms and moiré materials, promising a new, highly controllable platform to probe the fundamental principles of strongly correlated systems and test theoretical models of ideal band structures in a clean, controllable, and, well-characterized experimental environment.

Appendix B
Experimental schemes

For state-dependent optical lattices, the choice of two internal states has large experimental implications on the preparation and lifetime of the experiment. In keeping with the nature of the flat bands, our considerations here are general, and should be considered merely an example for a possible experimental procedure. Consider a ground state manifold characterized by definite combined orbital and spin angular momentum
J
and nuclear spin
I
, so that in the absence of magnetic field the manifold splits further into levels of definite total angular momentum
F=|I-J|,\dots,I+J
. In the case of alkali-metal atoms, the ground state has
J=1/2
. For fermionic isotopes
F
is half-integer while for bosonic isotopes it is integer. Furthermore, applying a small quantizing magnetic field, which we take to be in the vertical
z
direction, the energy levels may be labeled
\ket{F,m_{F}}
.

Increasing the magnetic field until the quadratic Zeeman effect detunes the atomic transitions by energies in large excess of
\omega_{c}
, a two-level system can be furnished from a pair
\ket{\pm}=\ket{F_{\pm},m_{\pm}}
. Let us focus on the case of off-resonant lasers, so that the optical lattice potential arises from the second order light shift. This is controlled by the dynamic polarizability tensor operator
\alpha_{ab}
of the ground state manifold, that is characterized by a combination of scalar
\alpha_{S}
, vector
\alpha_{V}
, and tensor
\alpha_{T}
components. For each frequency
\omega
, this decomposition becomes

\displaystyle\alpha_{ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{S}(\omega)\delta_{ab}-\alpha_{V}(\omega)\frac{\mathrm{i}\epsilon_{abc}\hat{J}_{c}}{2J}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\alpha_{T}(\omega)\frac{3[\hat{J}_{a}\hat{J}_{b}+\hat{J}_{b}\hat{J}_{a}]-2\delta_{ab}\hat{\mathbf{J}}^{2}}{2J(2J-1)}

(45)

where for notational clarity the
\hat{}
have been added on angular momentum operators. Generically
\alpha_{ab}(\omega)
is predominantly real, but has a finite imaginary component. This is the remnant of the finite life time of the excited states which the light field mixes with the ground state.

B.1
Alkali Metals

Projected to within the
\ket{\pm}

Since the vector polarizability allow for off-diagonal matrix elements differing by at most
1

\ket{\pm}

V1

m_{+}=m_{-}+1

V2

Appendix C
Landau level review

For convenience we give a short review of Landau levels on a plane in the periodic basis
Haldane and Rezayi 1985
.
Consider the usual affine plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with coordinates
x^{1},x^{2}
and magnetic field

F_{ab}
, which is related to the flux
density
B
by the anti-symmetric tensor
F_{ab}=\epsilon_{ab}B
.
We consider identical charge
+1
particles moving on the plane. When these are transported around the plane, they acquire an Aharonov-Bohm phase. The generator of this translation is the kinetic momentum

\bm{\pi}
, which has the form
\pi_{a}=-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{a}-A_{a}
where

A_{a}
is the connection. The operator commutation relations take the form

[x^{a},x^{b}]=0\qquad[x^{a},\pi_{b}]=\mathrm{i}\delta^{a}_{b}\qquad[\pi_{a},\pi_{b}]=\mathrm{i}F_{ab}.

(94)

Even without specifying a particular Hamiltonian, it is natural to ponder the existence of operators that commute with both
\pi_{1,2}
. When
B
is constant (
94
) is a central extension of the usual Heisenberg algebra, and we may construct such operators, which are the guiding center momenta

\tilde{\pi}_{a}=\pi_{a}-F_{ab}x^{b}
. Their name is chosen since
R^{a}=\epsilon^{ab}\tilde{\pi}_{b}/B
are the typical guiding center operators. The two kinds of momenta commute

[\tilde{\pi}_{a},\pi_{b}]=\mathrm{i}F_{ab}-F_{ac}[x^{c},\pi_{b}]=\mathrm{i}F_{ab}-\mathrm{i}F_{ab}=0

(95)

while the guiding center momenta have opposite chirality to
\pi_{a}

[\tilde{\pi}_{a},\tilde{\pi}_{b}]=-\mathrm{i}F_{ab}

(96)

The guiding center momenta are distinct from the kinetic momenta, and
as such there are two natural notions of translation operators. The parallel
transport translations

t(\mathbf{d})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{d}\cdot\bm{\pi}}
and the guiding center translations
\hat{t}(\mathbf{d})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{d}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}}
. Since they have the same commutation
relations with the position operator
\mathbf{x}
, if
V(\mathbf{x})
is an operator

t^{\dagger}(\mathbf{d})V(\mathbf{x})t(\mathbf{d})=\hat{t}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{d})V(\mathbf{x})\hat{t}(\mathbf{d})=V(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{d})
. Their translation algebras are inherited from their
respective momentum algebras

t(\mathbf{d}_{1})t(\mathbf{d}_{2})=t(\mathbf{d}_{1}+\mathbf{d}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}F_{ab}d_{1}^{a}d_{2}^{b}}

(97)

whereas

\hat{t}(\mathbf{d}_{1})\hat{t}(\mathbf{d}_{2})=\hat{t}(\mathbf{d}_{1}+\mathbf{d}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}F_{ab}d_{1}^{a}d_{2}^{b}}.

(98)

Let us suppose magnetic field or potential inhomogeneities conspire so that only
translations along some lattice
\Lambda=\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbf{a}_{2}\mathbb{Z}
are
symmetries, with a unit cell that encloses
2\pi
flux
F_{ab}a_{1}^{a}a_{2}^{b}=2\pi
.
The dual lattice we denote
\Lambda^{\star}=\mathbf{g}_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbf{g}_{2}\mathbb{Z}
,
where
\mathbf{g}^{i}_{a}=\epsilon^{ij}F_{ab}\mathbf{a}^{b}_{j}
.
This ensures that the corresponding guiding center translations commute
\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{1})\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})=\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{1})
, hence they can be simultaneously diagonalized.
This motivates defining their common eigenstates, which are essentially the magnetic Bloch wave-functions, with crystal momenta
\mathbf{k}
by

\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{i})\ket{\mathbf{k}}=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{k}}\ket{\mathbf{k}}

(99)

in analogy with the usual translation symmetry. The choice of overall
-
sign
corresponds to a shift in the origin of the Brillouin zone, which is made since
it makes the resulting wave-functions more symmetric between real and momentum
space.
With this preamble, let us understand the magnetic Bloch wave-functions.
To write
these down explicitly, we must pick a gauge connection. For example the symmetric
gauge wherein

A_{a}=-\frac{1}{2}F_{ab}x^{b}
, and hence

\tilde{\pi}_{a}=-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}F_{ab}x^{b}
.
Consider first understanding
the eigenstates of
\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}
with eigenvalue
2\pi\phi_{1}
. These eigenstates
satisfy

2\pi\phi_{1}f_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=\left(-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\nabla-\frac{\epsilon_{1j}}{2}\mathbf{g}^{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}\right)f_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{x})

(100)

which can be directly integrated to yield

f_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{1}\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{1}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\epsilon_{12}}{4\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})}

(101)

up to a multiplicative function dependent only on
\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}
.
Since the guiding center translations by
\mathbf{a}_{1,2}
commute, we can form a
superposition of
f_{\phi_{1}+n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
states that are

\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})
eigenstates. It is straightforward to compute the action of

\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})
on
f_{\phi_{1}}

\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})f_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}}f_{\phi_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\phi_{1}+1}(\mathbf{x})

(102)

The most general eigenstate of
\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{1,2})
with eigenvalues
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi\phi_{1,2}}
must be a superposition of the form

\chi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{\phi_{1}+n}(\mathbf{x})h_{n}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})

(103)

where
h_{n}
is some set of functions.
To find conditions relating
h_{n}
we investigate the action of
\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})
. In particular

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi\phi_{2}}\chi=\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{2})\chi_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2}}=\sum_{n}f_{\phi_{1}+n+1}(\mathbf{x})h_{n}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi)

(104)

From the linear independence of the summand, it is clear
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi\phi_{2}}h_{n}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})=h_{n-1}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi)
, which immediately implies

h_{n}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi\phi_{2}n}\tilde{h}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi n)

(105)

for an arbitrary function
\tilde{h}
. In particular let us factor out
\tilde{h}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{2}\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}}h
. In terms of this arbitrary function, the simultaneous eigenfunctions take the form

\chi_{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{i}\mathbf{g}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi n)

This is naively very asymmetric between the two lattice vectors, but they can be
related by Poisson resummation, which exchange
\mathbf{g}^{1,2}
and replaces
h

with its Fourier transform. In particular the Fourier series of the Dirac comb
can be expressed

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta(x-2\pi n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nx}

(106)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}xy/2\pi}\delta(y-2\pi n)

(107)

Defining
\hat{h}(y)=\int\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}xy/2\pi}h(x)/2\pi
Poisson resummation relates

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}}h(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi n)

(108)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\theta\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}/2\pi}h(\theta)\delta(\theta-2\pi n)

(109)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\phi}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(\phi+\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})/2\pi}h(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\theta)\delta(\phi-2\pi n)

(110)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{h}(\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi n)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}}

(111)

so we could equally well write

\chi_{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{i}\mathbf{g}^{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{2})}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}n\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\hat{h}(\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi n)

(112)

The ordering of the lattice basis is arbitrary, so the
most natural basis to expand
h
in would be a self-dual basis under Fourier
transformation. Such a basis is provided by the eigenstates of the harmonic
oscillator with a free parameter
\tau
in the complex upper
half plane. They take the form of the Hermite functions

h_{\tau,N}(x)=H_{N}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}}x\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\tau}{4\pi}x^{2}}
, where
H_{N}
is the
N
th Hermite polynomial.
Under Fourier transform we find

\hat{h}_{\tau,N}(y)=\frac{\mathrm{i}^{N}}{2\pi}H_{N}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi\tau}}y\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{4\pi\tau}y^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}^{N}}{2\pi}h_{-1/\tau,N}(y)

(113)

The corresponding wavefunctions are
closely related to the Jacobi theta function
\theta(u,\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi n^{2}\tau+\mathrm{i}nu}
(with periods
2\pi,2\pi\tau
).
In particular for
h_{\tau,0}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\mathbf{g}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\tau}{4\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}+2\pi n)^{2}}

(114)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\tau n^{2}+\mathrm{i}n\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{g}^{1}+\tau\mathbf{g}^{2})+\mathrm{i}\frac{\tau}{4\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})^{2}}

(115)

\displaystyle=\theta\left(\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{g}^{1}+\tau\mathbf{g}^{2});\tau\right)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\tau}{4\pi}(\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})^{2}}

(116)

Since the complex numbers come with a natural metric, a choice for the free parameter
\tau
amounts to picking a metric on the plane. We complexify coordinates using the map:

z=\frac{\ell_{B}}{\sqrt{2\pi\Im\tau}}\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{g}^{1}+\tau\mathbf{g}^{2})

(117)

Under this map
a_{1}=\ell_{B}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Im\tau}}
and
a_{2}=\ell_{B}\tau\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Im\tau}}
, so the area of the unit cell is

\frac{1}{2}|a_{1}\bar{a}_{2}-\bar{a}_{1}a_{2}|=\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}\pi}{\Im\tau}|\tau-\bar{\tau}|=2\pi\ell_{B}^{2}

(118)

To convert from
\phi
to
\mathbf{k}
in the wavefunction, recall the minus sign of equation (
99
). First consider
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}=0
which corresponds to a corner of Brillouin zone:

\displaystyle\Theta(z;\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\pi}[(\mathbf{g}^{1}+\tau\mathbf{g}^{2})\cdot\mathbf{x}]\mathbf{g}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\theta\left(\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{g}^{1}+\tau\mathbf{g}^{2});\tau\right)

(119)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}z\Im z/2\ell_{B}^{2}}\theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi\Im\tau}}{\ell_{B}}z;\tau\right)

(120)

To center the wavefunction in the Brillouin zone translate it by
\mathbf{a}_{1}/2+\mathbf{a}_{2}/2
to obtain

\displaystyle\braket{\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}}

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}{(\mathbf{g}^{1}-\mathbf{g}^{2})\cdot\mathbf{x}}}\Theta(z+a_{1}/2+a_{2}/2)

(121)

\displaystyle\propto\sigma(z)\mathrm{e}^{-|z|^{2}/4\ell_{B}^{2}}

(122)

where
\sigma(z)
is the holomorphic (modified) Weierstrass sigma function
Haldane 2018
of
\Lambda
.
The
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{0}
case can be constructed by similar translations:

\displaystyle\braket{\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4\ell_{B}^{2}}|z-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}k|^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\sigma(z-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}k)

(123)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4}(\ell_{B}^{-2}|z|^{2}+\ell_{B}^{2}|k|^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\bar{k}z)}\sigma(z-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}k)

(124)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a
\mathbf{k}
independent normalization constant whose value is not important. In fact
\braket{\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k}}
is the lowest Landau level wavefunction on a torus.

C.1
Higher Landau levels and form factors

Another way of understanding the construction is to consider an operator that
commutes with
\hat{t}(\mathbf{a}_{1,2})
, and
split the
\mathbf{k}
sector by the spectrum of this operator. A natural choice
is the kinetic energy
\bm{\pi}^{2}
. The
n
th level of
\bm{\pi}^{2}
is the
n
th Landau level. Let
z=x+\mathrm{i}y
and define the corresponding Wirtinger derivatives
\partial z=1;\bar{\partial}z=0
. In terms of these, the complexified kinetic and guiding center momenta take the form

\pi=-2\mathrm{i}\partial+\frac{\mathrm{i}\bar{z}}{2\ell_{B}^{2}};\qquad\tilde{\pi}=\pi-\frac{\mathrm{i}\bar{z}}{\ell_{B}^{2}}.

(125)

They satisfy the commutation relations

[\pi,\pi^{\dagger}]=-2\ell_{B}^{-2}\qquad[\tilde{\pi},\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger}]=2\ell_{B}^{-2}

(126)

where all other commutators vanish. After a
\sqrt{2}\ell_{B}^{-1}
rescaling they therefore form two independent harmonic oscillator algebras. Furthermore
\bm{\pi}^{2}=\pi\pi^{\dagger}+2\ell_{B}^{-2}
, so we are in effect studying the harmonic oscillator. Note that
\pi^{\dagger}
annihilates holomorphic functions multiplying the Gaussian

\mathrm{e}^{-\ell_{B}^{-2}|z|^{2}/4}
of which the wavefunction
\braket{\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}}
is an example. The general Hermite functions from before are precisely the states that can be found by applying
\pi
to
\ket{\mathbf{k}}=\ket{n=0,\mathbf{k}}
, namely

\ket{n,\mathbf{k}}=\frac{\ell_{B}^{n}\pi^{n}}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}\ket{\mathbf{k}}

(127)

We use the guiding center translations to change momentum sectors:

\ket{n,\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{e}^{\ell_{B}^{2}(\bar{k}\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger}-{k}\tilde{\pi})/2}\ket{n,\mathbf{0}}=\hat{t}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp})\ket{n,\mathbf{0}}

(128)

hence the action of the translation operators becomes

\displaystyle\hat{t}(\mathbf{k}_{1}^{\perp})\ket{n,\mathbf{k}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}}{4}(\bar{k}_{1}k_{2}-\bar{k}_{2}k_{1})}\ket{n,\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{k}_{2}}

(129)

Noting the Bloch-condition, we deduce boundary conditions for the wavefunctions

\displaystyle\ket{n,\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{g}^{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}}{4}(\bar{k}g^{i}-\bar{g}^{i}k)}\hat{t}((\mathbf{g}^{i})^{\perp})\ket{n,\mathbf{k}}

(130)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}}{4}({k}\bar{g}^{i}-{g}^{i}\bar{k})}\ket{n,\mathbf{k}}

(131)

This basis is very suitable for numerical calculations.
The position operator takes the form
z=\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}(\pi^{\dagger}-\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger})
and
\bar{z}=\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}(\tilde{\pi}-\pi)
.
This implies that the plane wave operator factorizes

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=\mathrm{e}^{\ell_{B}^{2}(q\pi-\bar{q}\pi^{\dagger})/2}\mathrm{e}^{\ell_{B}^{2}(\bar{q}\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger}-q\tilde{\pi})/2}

(132)

Denote the canonical displacement operator

\displaystyle D^{n}_{m}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\braket{n|\mathrm{e}^{\alpha a^{\dagger}-\bar{\alpha}a}|m}

(133)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{n!}{m!}}\alpha^{m-n}L_{n}^{(m-n)}(|\alpha|^{2})&m\geq n\\
\sqrt{\frac{m!}{n!}}\bar{\alpha}^{n-m}L_{m}^{(n-m)}(|\alpha|^{2})&n\geq m\end{cases}

where
L^{(a)}_{b}(x)
are the associated Laguerre polynomials. With this in hand, suppose
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{k}_{1}-\mathbf{k}_{2}+\mathbf{G}
for some reciprocal lattice vector
\mathbf{G}\in\Lambda^{\star}
. Then the form factor takes the easily computable form:

\displaystyle\braket{n,\mathbf{k}_{1}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}}|m,\mathbf{k}_{2}}

(134)

\displaystyle=D^{n}_{m}(q\ell_{B}/\sqrt{2})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}\mathbf{q}\wedge\mathbf{k}_{2}/2}\braket{0,\mathbf{k}_{1}|0,\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{G}}

(135)

\displaystyle=D^{n}_{m}(q\ell_{B}/\sqrt{2})\eta_{\mathbf{G}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}\mathbf{q}\wedge\mathbf{k}_{2}/2+\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}\mathbf{G}\wedge\mathbf{k}_{1}/2}

(136)

where
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
is the parity of the reciprocal lattice vector such that
\eta_{\mathbf{G}}=1
if
\mathbf{G}\in 2\Lambda^{\star}
and
-1
otherwise.
We use these matrix elements for numerical computation involving
\tilde{H}
. To do this note that
\psi^{\alpha}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
obeys the Bloch periodicity
\psi^{\alpha}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}_{\alpha})\cdot\mathbf{R}}\psi^{\alpha}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
. Factoring out the LLL wavefunction with crystal momentum
\mathbf{k}
means that
\bar{\chi}
transforms as

{\chi}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{-2}\mathbf{R}\wedge\mathbf{r}/2}\chi^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
– it obeys the magnetic translation symmetry with opposite chirality to
\braket{\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}}
. Note the invariance of the algebra under the relabeling
\pi\to\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger}
and
\pi^{\dagger}\to\tilde{\pi}
. From this we can reuse the earlier matrix elements, by expanding
\chi^{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\bar{n}}c_{n\alpha}\braket{\mathbf{r}|\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}
, where
\ket{\overline{n\mathbf{k}}}
are the Landau levels of opposite chirality. In particular we define them by the properties
t(\mathbf{a}_{i})\ket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}\ket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}
,
\ket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}=\ell_{B}^{n}(\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{\overline{\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}/\sqrt{2^{n}n!}
, and
\braket{\mathbf{r}|\overline{\mathbf{k}}}=\overline{\braket{\mathbf{r}|-\mathbf{k}}}
. Including a spinor label we have basis states
\ket{\overline{n\mathbf{k}}\alpha}
.
The matrix elements

\frac{1}{2M}\braket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}|\tilde{\pi}\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger}|\overline{m\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}=(n+1)\omega_{c}\delta^{n}_{m}\,,

(137)

while for a potential of the form
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{G})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{q}_{\beta})\cdot\mathbf{r}}
(for some
\mathbf{q}_{\alpha},\mathbf{q}_{\beta}
which displace the Brillouin zones of the internal states) we have

\displaystyle\hskip-21.33955pt\braket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}\alpha|\hat{V}|\overline{m\mathbf{q}_{\beta}}\beta}

(138)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{G})\braket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{G}+\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{q}_{\beta})\cdot\mathbf{r}}|\overline{m\mathbf{q}_{\beta}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\mathbf{G})\eta_{\mathbf{G}}D^{n\alpha}_{m\beta}\left(\mathbf{G}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}\left[\mathbf{G}+\frac{\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{q}_{\beta}}{2}\right]\wedge\left[\frac{\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{q}_{\beta}}{2}\right]}

\displaystyle D^{n\alpha}_{m\beta}\left(\mathbf{G}\right)

\displaystyle=D^{n}_{m}\left([{\bar{G}+\bar{q}_{\alpha}-\bar{q}_{\beta}}]\ell_{B}/\sqrt{2}\right)\,.

For comparison between the optimal ideal and true wavefunctions we find it most convenient to find the latter by solving (
27
). To do this we need the matrix elements of
\hat{\zeta}_{\mathbf{k}}
which are

\displaystyle\braket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}|\hat{\zeta}_{\mathbf{k}}|\overline{m\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}

(139)

\displaystyle=-\omega_{c}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq\mathbf{0}}G^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}\mathbf{G}\wedge\mathbf{k}}\braket{\overline{n\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\tilde{\pi}^{\dagger}|\overline{m\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{c}\sqrt{2(m+1)}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}\mathbf{G}\wedge(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}_{\alpha})}}{\ell_{B}G}\eta_{\mathbf{G}}D^{n}_{m+1}(\bar{G}\ell_{B}/2)\,.

Using these pieces we can reproduce the same spectrum from
\tilde{H}+\hat{\zeta}_{\mathbf{k}}
as from a plane-wave expansion of the original Hamiltonian. In practice we truncate the single-particle space to include the first
100
Landau levels.

C.2
Trace violation for the
n
th Landau level

We claimed in the main text that the trace violation for the
n
th Landau level is
2n
, which can be succinctly shown using the technology we have developed so far in this Appendix. The Bloch wavefunctions of the lowest Landau level take the form

\displaystyle u_{0\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\braket{\mathbf{r}|\mathbf{k}}

(140)

\displaystyle=N\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{4}(\ell_{B}^{-2}|z|^{2}+\ell_{B}^{2}|k|^{2})-\mathrm{i}{k}\bar{z}/2}\sigma(z-\mathrm{i}\ell_{B}^{2}k)\,.

(141)

Except for the Gaussian
\mathrm{e}^{-\ell_{B}^{2}|k|^{2}/4}
, this is holomorphic in
k
. The
n
th Landau level Bloch wavefunction can be similarly computed by noting that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\pi=(\pi+\bar{k})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
, hence

\ket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}}=\frac{(\pi+\bar{k})^{n}}{2^{n/2}\sqrt{n!}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\ket{\mathbf{k}}\,,

(142)

from which it is immediately clear that

\partial_{\bar{k}}\ket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}}=\ell_{B}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}\ket{u_{n-1\mathbf{k}}}-\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}k}{4}\ket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}}\,.

(143)

The Berry curvature is uniform for each Landau level:

\displaystyle\Omega(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\partial_{k}\braket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}|\partial_{\bar{k}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}}-2\partial_{\bar{k}}\braket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}|\partial_{{k}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}}

(144)

\displaystyle=4\partial_{k}\braket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}|\partial_{\bar{k}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}}

(145)

\displaystyle=-\ell_{B}^{2}\,.

(146)

We see that it is always negative and results in Chern number
\mathcal{C}=-1
.
The corresponding trace violation is straightforwardly computed as

\displaystyle\tr g+\Omega

\displaystyle=4\bra{\partial_{k}u_{n\mathbf{k}}}(1-\ket{u_{n\mathbf{k}}}\bra{u_{n\mathbf{k}}})\ket{\partial_{\bar{k}}u_{n\mathbf{k}}}

(147)

\displaystyle=2n\ell_{B}^{2}\braket{u_{n-1\mathbf{k}}|u_{n-1\mathbf{k}}}

(148)

\displaystyle=2n\ell_{B}^{2}\,.

(149)

Hence
(2\pi)^{-1}\int\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}(\tr g+\Omega)=2n
as claimed.

C.3
Circular dichroism criteria

A natural alternative to the trace condition as a measure of proximity to ideality relates to the null vectors of the quantum geometric tensor
q^{ab}(\mathbf{k})=g^{ab}(\mathbf{k})+\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{ab}\Omega(\mathbf{k})/2
. For ideal bands (with sufficient rotational symmetry) either
w_{a}=(\mathbf{e}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{e}_{y})_{a}/\sqrt{2}
or its complex conjugate
\bar{w}_{a}
are null-vectors of
q^{ab}
for all
\mathbf{k}
. Consider measuring the magnitude

\displaystyle||q^{ab}w_{b}||^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(g^{ab}\bar{w}_{b}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{ab}\bar{w}_{b}\Omega}{2}\right)\left(g^{ac}w_{c}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{ac}w_{c}\Omega}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\Omega-\tr g)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(\tr g)^{2}-\det g\,.

(150)

Using
\frac{1}{4}(\tr g)^{2}\geq\det g
and taking
\Omega<0
we find

\displaystyle\frac{||q\bar{w}||^{2}}{||qw||^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tr g+\Omega)^{2}+[(\tr g)^{2}-4\det g]}{(\tr g-\Omega)^{2}+[(\tr g)^{2}-4\det g]}

(151)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\tr g+\Omega}{\tr g-\Omega}\right)^{2}\,,

(152)

or in short

{\frac{\sqrt{2\tr g(\tr g+\Omega)}}{\tr g-\Omega}}\geq\frac{||q\bar{w}||}{||qw||}\geq\frac{\tr g+\Omega}{\tr g-\Omega}\,.

(153)

Ignoring the covariance of the denominator and the numerator, and to leading order in the trace violation, the average of this ratio can be approximately bounded

\sqrt{\frac{T}{2|\mathcal{C}|}}\gtrsim\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{k}}{2\pi\ell_{B}^{-2}}\frac{||q\bar{w}||}{||qw||}\gtrsim\frac{T}{2|\mathcal{C}|}\,.

(154)

Whenever the trace violation is small, so too must this average ratio be, but precise numerical comparison between the two quantities is not possible. This makes direct comparison between the deviation from ideality cited in
Nascimbene and Dalibard 2024
with the trace violations we quote practically difficult, though qualitatively they show similar features. For example, it monotonically increases in the
n
th Landau level

\frac{||q\bar{w}||}{||qw||}={\frac{n}{n+1}}

(155)

though the rate is much gentler than the trace condition.

Appendix D
Fourier expansion of
\hat{\zeta}

In the main text we claimed the following identity regarding the periodic completion of the Weierstrass
\zeta
function
Haldane 2018

\hat{\zeta}(z)=\zeta(z)-\bar{z}/2\ell_{B}^{2}=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}}{\ell_{B}^{2}G}\,.

(156)

The most straightforward derivation is to consider the anti-holomorphic derivative of this function:

\bar{\partial}({\zeta}-\bar{z}/2\ell_{B}^{2})=\pi\sum_{\mathbf{R}\in\Lambda}\delta^{2}(\mathbf{r})-1/2\ell_{B}^{2}

(157)

where the Dirac comb follow from the pole locations of
\zeta
.
The Dirac comb can be Fourier expanded, showing that

\bar{\partial}\hat{\zeta}=\frac{1}{2\ell_{B}^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq\mathbf{0}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,.

(158)

Periodicity implies that there is a Fourier expansion with coefficients
c_{\mathbf{G}}
of
\hat{\zeta}

\hat{\zeta}(z)=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}c_{\mathbf{G}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

(159)

hence taking derivatives of this expression and matching terms we find
\mathrm{i}Gc_{\mathbf{G}}/2=1/2\ell_{B}^{2}
for
\mathbf{G}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Since
\hat{\zeta}(z)
has vanishing average, the claimed Fourier expansion follows.

Appendix E
Polarizability of Alkali Atoms

For completeness, let us derive the polarizability of alkali-metal atoms in the ground state. We label the internal atomic states, including the excited states by
\alpha
. In the dipole approximation, an external electric field of strength
\mathbf{E}(t)=\sum_{\omega}\mathbf{E}_{\omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}
modifies the Hamiltonian by

\delta H=-\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{E}(t)=\sum V_{\beta}^{\alpha}(\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}

(160)

where
V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\omega)=-\mathbf{d}^{\alpha}_{\beta}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\omega}
.
If the internal levels are governed by a reference Hamiltonian
H_{0}=\sum\omega_{\alpha}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}
, the perturbation in the
H_{0}
rotating frame becomes

\delta H^{\mathrm{rot}}=\sum_{\alpha\beta\omega}V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\omega-\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta})t}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}\,.

(161)

To recast this as a static problem, we use a time-dependent Schrieffer-Wolff transformation
\ket{\psi}=\mathrm{e}^{-S(t)}\ket{\tilde{\psi}}
where

S(t)=-\sum_{\alpha\beta\omega}\frac{V^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\omega)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\omega-\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta})t}}{\omega-\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta}}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}.

(162)

Let
H_{\mathrm{atom}}=\sum E_{\alpha}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}
be the true atomic Hamiltonian absent driving, and pick
H_{0}
so that
|E_{\alpha}-\omega_{\alpha}|\ll|\omega_{\alpha}-\omega_{\beta}|
for all
\alpha\neq\beta
that are coupled.
To second order in
\mathbf{E}
, the time evolution of
\ket{\tilde{\psi}}
is governed by

H^{(2)}=\sum_{\alpha}(E_{\alpha}-\omega_{\alpha})\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}+\delta H^{(2)}

(163)

where corrections to from the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation to the atomic piece have been neglected due to the proximity between
H_{\mathrm{atom}}
and
H_{0}
, and where

\displaystyle\delta H^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta})t}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\left[\frac{V^{\alpha}_{\gamma}(\omega_{1})V^{\gamma}_{\beta}(\omega_{2})}{\omega_{1}-\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\gamma}}-\frac{V^{\alpha}_{\gamma}(\omega_{1})V^{\gamma}_{\beta}(\omega_{2})}{\omega_{2}-\omega_{\gamma}+\omega_{\beta}}\right]\,.

(164)

Whenever the resonance condition
\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\omega_{\alpha}+\omega_{\beta}=0
is met, this results in a static term. We assume the rotating wave approximation where only these resonant terms are important, that is ignoring higher order light shifts and taking any off-resonant oscillations much faster than the dynamics of interest. Due to the reality of
\mathbf{E}(t)
, clearly
\mathbf{E}_{-\omega}=\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega}
. Furthermore, we consider the situation where the energy splitting of the states of interest is much smaller than the frequencies
\omega
, so that the only resonant contributions arise when
\omega_{1}<0<\omega_{2}
or conversely, and by focusing on ground states furthermore neglecting the contribution to the the polarizability from virtual levels formed below the ground state. Combining these considerations, the energy takes the form

\displaystyle\delta H^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}>0,\mathrm{res}}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\beta}\frac{(\bar{\mathbf{E}}_{\omega_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\alpha}_{\gamma})(\mathbf{E}_{\omega_{2}}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\gamma}_{\beta})}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega_{2}-\omega_{\beta}}

(165)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{K,q}(-)^{K-q}\alpha^{K,-q}[\bar{E}(\omega_{1})\otimes E(\omega_{2})]^{K,q}

(166)

where in the last line, we have transformed to the spherical tensor basis with
K=0,1,2
corresponding to scalar, vector, and tensor polarizabilities. This picture is particularly clear when the lifetimes of all states are infinite so that
H_{\mathrm{atom}}
is Hermitian. Unfortunately this is not true for the excited states where the imaginary part
\Im E_{\alpha}=-\Gamma_{\alpha}/2
measures the line-width. To account for this
H_{0}
should include the linewidth for states which are not resonant, so that
\omega_{\alpha}\mapsto\omega_{\alpha}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\alpha}/2
is complex. The mismatch between the true and reference energies
E_{\alpha}-\omega_{\alpha}
functions as a species-dependent chemical potential due to the lasers being off-resonant.

E.1
Spherical tensor decomposition

The electrons in the atom have orbital angular momentum
\mathbf{L}
, under which
\mathbf{d}
is a vector operator, as well as spin angular momentum
\mathbf{S}
. When the atomic reference Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is rotationally symmetric, eigenstates carry definite angular momentum
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}
. Consider a degenerate manifold of excited states
\gamma
with definite angular momentum
J_{\gamma}
, coupling to ground states with angular momentum
J
. We can work out the
K=0,1,2
polarizabilities using the Wigner-Eckart theorem, which is to say using the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
representation theory. We use the convention that the reduced matrix elements of a spherical tensor is related to the
3j
symbol as follows:

\braket{J_{1}m_{1}|T^{Kq}|J_{2}m_{2}}=(-)^{J_{1}-m_{1}}\braket{J_{1}\|T^{K}\|J_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}J_{1}&K&J_{2}\\
-m_{1}&q&m_{2}\end{pmatrix}

and that the tensor product of spherical tensors takes the form (where
[J]=2J+1
)

[A\otimes B]^{Kq}=\sum_{m_{a,b}}\sqrt{[K]}(-)^{K-q}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&K\\
m_{a}&m_{b}&-q\end{pmatrix}A^{m_{a}}B^{m_{b}}

(167)

A tedious but straightforward calculation then shows that

\displaystyle\braket{Jm_{\alpha}|\alpha^{K,q}|Jm_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{[K]}\sum_{\gamma}(-)^{m_{a}+m_{b}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&K\\
-m_{a}&-m_{b}&q\end{pmatrix}\frac{\braket{Jm_{\alpha}|d_{m_{a}}|J_{\gamma}m_{\gamma}}\braket{J_{\gamma}m_{\gamma}|d_{m_{b}}|Jm_{\beta}}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{[K]}\sum_{\gamma}(-)^{m_{a}+m_{b}-m_{\alpha}+m_{\gamma}}\frac{|\braket{J\|\mathbf{d}\|J_{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&K\\
-m_{a}&-m_{b}&q\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J&1&J_{\gamma}\\
-m_{\alpha}&m_{a}&m_{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{\gamma}&1&J\\
-m_{\gamma}&m_{b}&m_{\beta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{[K]}\sum_{\gamma}(-)^{J_{\gamma}-m_{\alpha}}\frac{|\braket{J\|\mathbf{d}\|J_{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}\begin{Bmatrix}J&J&K\\
1&1&J_{\gamma}\end{Bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J&J&K\\
m_{\beta}&-m_{\alpha}&q\end{pmatrix}

(168)

Hence the reduced matrix elements of the polarizability take the form

\braket{J\|\alpha^{K}\|J}=\sum_{\gamma}\sqrt{[K]}(-)^{J_{\gamma}-J}\frac{|\braket{J\|\mathbf{d}\|J_{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}\begin{Bmatrix}J&J&K\\
1&1&J_{\gamma}\end{Bmatrix}

(169)

Of particular interest to the alkali metals is the
J=1/2
case, which represents the ground state

\displaystyle\braket{1/2\|\alpha^{K}\|1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma}\frac{|\braket{1/2\|\mathbf{d}\|J_{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}\times\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}&K=0\\
-\frac{11-4J_{\gamma}(J_{\gamma}+1)}{8\sqrt{3}}&K=1\end{cases}

(170)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma}\frac{|\braket{1/2\|\mathbf{d}\|J_{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}\times\begin{cases}\frac{\braket{1/2\|1\|1/2}}{2\sqrt{3}}&K=0\\
\frac{11-4J_{\gamma}(J_{\gamma}+1)}{12\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{i}\braket{1/2\|\mathbf{J}\|1/2}&K=1\end{cases}

(171)

To rewrite this in the usual form, we note that with our convention

\displaystyle[\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\otimes\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})]^{Kq}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\cdot\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})}{\sqrt{3}}&K=0\\
\frac{[\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\times\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})]_{q}}{\sqrt{2}}&K=1\end{cases}

(172)

For alkali metals, the dominant sources of polarizability are the
D_{1,2}
lines connecting the ground state
S_{1/2}
to the
P_{1/2,3/2}
levels at energy
\omega_{1/2,3/2}
, where
\braket{S_{1/2}|\mathbf{d}|P_{3/2}}=\sqrt{2}\braket{S_{1/2}|\mathbf{d}|P_{1/2}}=\sqrt{2}d
. Hence the second order Hamiltonian takes the form

\displaystyle\delta H^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\gamma,\omega_{1,2}\,\mathrm{res}}\frac{|\braket{S_{1/2}\|\mathbf{d}\|J_{\gamma}}|^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}-\omega}\left[\frac{\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\cdot\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})}{6}+\frac{\mathrm{i}(11-4J_{\gamma}(J_{\gamma}+1))\mathbf{J}\cdot[\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\times\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})]}{24}\right]

(173)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega_{1,2}\,\mathrm{res}}\frac{d^{2}\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\cdot\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})}{6}\left[\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{1/2}}+\frac{2}{\omega-\omega_{3/2}}\right]+\frac{\mathrm{i}d^{2}\mathbf{J}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{E}}(\omega_{1})\times\mathbf{E}(\omega_{2})}{3}\left[\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{1/2}}-\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{3/2}}\right]\,.

(174)

The most important feature is that the vector polarizability from the two lines destructively add when far detuned. In this regime, when
\omega\ll\omega_{1/2},\omega_{3/2}
or
\omega\gg\omega_{1/2},\omega_{3/2}
, we find

\alpha_{V}\to\frac{d^{2}(\omega_{3/2}-\omega_{1/2})}{3(\omega-\omega_{1/2})^{2}}.

(175)

As discussed, the imaginary part of the polarizability can be obtained by
\omega_{1/2,3/2}\to\omega_{1/2,3/2}-\mathrm{i}\Gamma/2
. When far detuned the imaginary part of the scalar potential scales in the same way as
\alpha_{V}
, namely

\mathrm{Im}\alpha_{S}\to\frac{d^{2}\Gamma}{4(\omega-\omega_{1/2})^{2}}.

(176)
