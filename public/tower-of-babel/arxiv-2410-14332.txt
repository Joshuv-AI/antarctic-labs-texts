Croc : Pretraining Large Multimodal Models with Cross-Modal Comprehension
Yin Xie, Kaicheng Yang, Ninghua Yang, Weimo Deng, Xiangzi Dai, Tiancheng Gu, Yumeng Wang, Xiang An, Yongle Zhao, Ziyong Feng, Roy Miles, Ismail Elezi, Jiankang Deng

Abstract
We introduce a novel pretraining stage for Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) aimed at improving their visual comprehension. We approach this problem using the task of generating masked visual tokens. However, we find that this task has some significant limitations in the context of multi-modality learning, which will limit the LLMs efficacy in learning a strong multi-modal representation. To naturally address this problem, we propose to use a dynamically learnable prompt token pool and the Hungarian algorithm for replacing the masked visual tokens with the most relevant visual prompt tokens. Next, we propose a mixed attention mechanism with bidirectional visual attention to encourage more cross-modality collaboration. Finally, we integrate a detailed caption generation task, leveraging rich descriptions to facilitate LLMs in understanding visual semantic information. After pretraining on 1.5 million publicly accessible data, we present a new foundation model called Croc . Croc reaches state-of-the-art results improving over LLaVA-v1.5-7B by 3.3 % 3.3\% , 5.5 % 5.5\% , and 8.5 % 8.5\% on the MMBench, SEED, and LLaVA-Wild benchmarks, respectively. To support reproducibility and facilitate further research, we release the training code and pre-trained model weights at https://github.com/deepglint/Croc .

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Achiam et al. 2023
have made significant progress in text understanding and generation, largely due to their foundation in autoregressive causal-Transformer architectures. These models have demonstrated strong scalability with computation and data. However, LLMs are primarily still limited to processing text. To overcome this limitation, there is a growing focus on Large Multimodal Models
(
Yin et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2023b
)
, which aim to process and integrate information across multiple modalities.

A key milestone in LMM research is LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
, which converts images into token representations using vision encoders such as CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
, and subsequently maps these tokens into LLM space, treating them as language tokens. LLaVA also leverages GPT-4’s language capabilities
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
to generate multimodal language-image instruction-following datasets. Other works
(
Li et al. 2023a
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
have explored more advanced projection architectures, such as QFormers and experts, to improve performance. These approaches highlight the complexity of multimodal tasks, but also the challenges in integrating visual and textual information.

Figure 2
:
The training pipeline of our proposed Croc model. In contrast to LLaVA-1.5
(
Liu et al. 2024a
)
, we introduce an additional pre-training stage that involves visual token reconstruction and detailed caption generation. This stage is essential for improving cross-modal comprehension.

In parallel, there has been increasing emphasis on pretraining strategies for LMMs. Integrating pre-trained vision and language models with multimodal web corpora that combine text and image has proven effective in improving performance across a range of tasks, such as captioning, visual dialogue, and visual question-answering. Notably, the VILA framework
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
demonstrates that pretraining can significantly enhance model capabilities, though it also introduces substantial computational overhead. Another line of research
(
Jin et al. 2023
)
has introduced novel visual tokenizers, which transform non-linguistic images into discrete token sequences, allowing LLMs to interpret them as foreign languages.
However, there is still a disparity in the causal interaction between visual and text tokens, which makes cross-modal comprehension still very challenging.

To address these challenges, we propose to introduce a novel pretraining paradigm for LMMs that enhances visual comprehension through a cross-modal reconstruction stage. Our approach involves designing a dynamically learnable prompt token pool, which allows for the selective replacement of certain visual tokens with the most relevant prompt tokens using the Hungarian algorithm.
To improve the LLM’s capacity to understand these visual tokens, we then propose a mixed attention mechanism that combines bidirectional visual attention with unidirectional textual attention.
Additionally, we integrate a detailed caption generation task, which uses rich image descriptions to facilitate a deeper understanding of visual semantics.
Our experimental results show that this method achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple benchmarks, demonstrating superior visual understanding and reasoning capabilities.
In summary, our
contributions
are the following:

•

We

a new pretraining paradigm designed to enhance visual comprehension, incorporating a novel cross-modal comprehension stage that integrates visual token reconstruction and detailed caption generation.

•

For visual token reconstruction, we

We also propose a mixed attention mechanism that combines bidirectional visual attention and unidirectional textual attention for more comprehensive visual token understanding.

•

We
demonstrate
that our proposed model achieves new state-of-the-art performance across various benchmarks, outperforming LlaVA-v1.5-7B by up to

showing robust visual understanding and reasoning capabilities.

Related Work

Large Multimodal Model Pre-training.
LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
uses a subset of the CC3M
(
Changpinyo et al. 2021
)
dataset for a more balanced coverage of concepts.
Both the visual encoder and LLM are then frozen while the projection layer is trained to align the visual features and language tokens.
However, relying solely on this approach will lead to a limited deep feature integration between the visual encoder and the LLM, mainly due to the restrictions imposed by the projection layer.
To address this limitation, CogVLM
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
introduce a trainable visual expert module into the attention and feed-forward network layers of the language model.
Despite this additional module, the LLM still remains limited by its frozen state and will continue to struggle interpreting the visual tokens.
Recently, LaVIT
(
Jin et al. 2023
)
introduced a new visual tokenizer to convert images into a sequence of discrete tokens. However, directly inputting visual tokens into an LLM to enhance visual understanding with next-token prediction still presents significant difficulties.
VILA
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
proposes an interleaved pretraining stage to augment the LLM to support visual input, but it relies on a 50M pertraining dataset, requiring considerable computational resources. In contrast, we efficiently enable LLMs to understand visual inputs with just 1.5M pretraining dataset and without any modifications to the underlying LLaVA architecture itself.

Cross-Modal Comprehension.
Masked Image Modeling (MIM)
Hondru et al. 2024
is now a common pre-training strategy for improving visual comprehension.
Both 4M
(
Mizrahi et al. 2023
)
and MVP
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
propose to integrate this idea in the context of multi-modality learning.
In contrast, MILAN
(
Hou et al. 2022
)
propose to reconstruct the image features infused with semantic content derived from caption supervision.
UnMasked Teacher
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
selectively masks video tokens exhibiting low semantic content and aligns the remaining unmasked tokens through a linear projection to their counterparts from the teacher model.
In a recent study, RILS
(
Yang et al. 2023a
)
introduced a novel pre-training framework that employs masked visual reconstruction within a language semantic space. This framework facilitates the extraction of structured information by vision models through the accurate semantic prediction of masked tokens.
Meanwhile, EVA
(
Fang et al. 2023
)
demonstrates that reconstructing the masked tokenized semantic vision features is an efficient strategy for vision-centric representation learning, removing the need for semantic feature quantization or any additional tokenization steps.
In contrast to all these prior works, we show that masked image modeling has some significant limitations in the context of large multi-modal models.
To address these limitations we propose a novel dynamic token pool to enable effective learning at high mask ratios, alongside a detailed semantic caption generation for strong cross-modal collaboration.

Cross-Modal Reconstruction

Preliminaries.
LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2024b
)
introduces a method for generating multimodal language-image instruction-following data using language-only GPT-4
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
. Its framework consists of three main components: a Visual Encoder, which converts input images into visual embeddings; a Projector, which maps these visual embeddings into a textual embedding space; and a Large Language Model (LLM) that processes both visual and textual tokens to generate responses. LLaVA uses a two-stage instruction-tuning process. In the first stage, image-text pairs are converted into single-turn conversations where the assistant describes the image. With visual tokens
T_{v}
and textual tokens
T_{t}
as input, the LLM produces a coherent response, with the original caption
T_{c}
serving as the ground-truth answer.
Despite the importance of vision-to-text alignment as a pre-training stage, it is insufficient to enable strong visual comprehension
Tong et al. 2024
.

Croc.
To address this limitation we propose an additional pre-training stage, which we describe as
visual token reconstruction
.
In Section
3.1
, we explain the need for visual token reconstruction to enhance visual comprehension, noting that a simple masked reconstruction of visual tokens is ineffective due to its tendency to produce degenerate or poorly generalizable features. Thus, we propose to extend masked reconstruction in section
3.2
through using a dynamic token pool, that provides the necessary contextual cues for vision token reconstruction. To encourage a better exploration of the token space provided by the token pool, we use Hungarian matching
Kuhn 1955
.
Finally, we tie all these ideas together in section
3.3
defining the additional pre-training stage and give the full training pipeline in section
3.4
.

3.1
Visual Token Reconstruction

The process of visual token reconstruction reflects the way in which LLMs fill in missing words, but here it involves learning to predict visual content based on contextual cues from the visible portion of the image. While the previous image-to-text alignment stage helps the model associate textual representations with visual features, it is insufficient for the model to fully grasp the fine-grained details and spatial relationships of an image. By training on the task of visual token reconstruction, the model can learn to focus on low-level visual features and develop a holistic representation of the image, including texture, shape, and context.

To implement this process, we use the LLM to reconstruct the masked visual tokens conditioned on the visible image tokens and its associated instruction. Given an image
I
, we first extract visual features using the image encoder
F_{v}=E_{v}(I)
. Following LLaVA, we then use a visual projector to translate these features into visual tokens
T_{v}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
. We then mask these tokens before feeding them into the LLM and having a loss for reconstructing the unmasked tokens. This encourages the LLM to learn a robust representation of the full image in order to reconstruct the masked visual tokens correctly. However, while this idea is promising, we find it has a few limitations in practice which we address in the following section.

3.2
Masked Reconstruction Is Not Enough

Masking and reconstructing the visual tokens can lead to poor visual comprehension. If the mask ratio is too high, the model will have insufficient contextual visual information, leading to a poorly robust representation of the image. Conversely, if the mask ratio is too small, the model will learn a degenerate solution using shortcuts such as feature-wise interpolation. We find that this level of fidelity in the masking ratio (see section
4.2
) makes its adoption difficult in practice and is in line with observations from prior work
Li et al. 2023b
;
Assran et al. 2023
.

Dynamic Token Pool.
Instead of proposing any hand-crafted masking or annealing strategies
Assran et al. 2023
, we propose to rectify the problem using a dynamic token pool. This pool of tokens provides sufficient contextual visual cues at high masking ratios, where each masked token is replaced with a learnable token from the dynamic pool. We then vary the size of this pool to control the level of difficulty for the generation task.
We denote the prompt token pool as
T_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
, where
N
and
D
represent the number of prompt tokens and the feature dimension respectively. With a masking ratio of
\gamma
, we obtain the set of masked visual tokens
\widetilde{T}_{v}
. The masked tokens
\widetilde{T}_{v}
are first padded with
\varnothing
into a set of size
N
before awaiting assignment.

Token Assignment.
We explore two different options for assigning tokens from the token pool. The first is using a simple nearest neighbors lookup, however, we find that this can lead to an over-dependence on a small subset of the token pool and thus a poor utilization of the entire token space (see section
4.2
). Fortunately, this assignment problem has been widely studied in the field of combinatorics, with the Hungarian algorithm
Kuhn 1955
providing a polynomial solution with many efficient GPU implementations
Papadimitriou and Steiglitz 1998
;
Crouse 2023
. Its generality has subsequently seen its adoption in many other practical applications, such as object detection
Carion et al. 2020
.
The objective we have is to find a bipartite matching between
\widetilde{T}_{v}
and
T_{p}
such that the total cost is minimized - where this cost is the L2 distance between the replaced and original visual token. This objective can be solved by finding a permutation of
N
elements
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
that minimizes the following:

\hat{\sigma}=\underset{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}{\arg\min}\sum_{i}^{N}\|\widetilde{T}_{v}^{i}-T_{p}^{\sigma(i)}\|_{2}

(1)

As an illustration, the Hungarian algorithm solves this assignment through iteratively subtracting the minimum entries of the rows and columns. We find that this optimisation step is very cheap and accounts for less than 5% of the forward pass wall-clock time during training.

3.3
Detailed Caption Generation

Finally, to improve the understanding of visual tokens, we propose to use detailed caption data for pre-training. As shown in Fig.
4
, these detailed captions contain a greater wealth of semantic information, thus providing better guidance for jointly learning and utilizing the visual tokens.
We introduce two specific objectives: Visual Token Reconstruction (VTR) and Detailed Caption Generation (DCG).
In the prompt visual token generation step, we randomly remove and then replace some of the original visual tokens
T_{v}=\{v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{n}\}
with tokens from our prompt token pool, thus obtaining our prompt visual tokens
\hat{T}_{v}
. We then concatenate these prompt visual tokens
\hat{T}_{v}
with the instruction text tokens
T_{t}=\{t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{m}\}
and feed them into the LLM for generating a response of length
L
.

Improving Cross-Modal Collaboration.
Since the causal interactions between visual tokens are significantly weaker than those observed between textual tokens, we rethink the uni-directional attention mechanism during this stage. Inspired by UNILM
(
Dong et al. 2019
)
, we propose to use a mixed cross-modal attention mechanism that enables both the vision and text tokens to attend to all the vision tokens (see figure
3
). We find that this simple modification can greatly enhances the LLMs visual understanding and can be straightforwardly implemented by just modifying the attention mask.

Training Objectives.
After undergoing a forward pass, using this mixed attention strategy, the probability of generating a response
T_{r}
=
\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{L}
will be given as:

p(T_{r}|{\hat{T}_{v}},{T_{t}})=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p(r_{i}|{\hat{T}_{v}},{T_{t,{{<i}}}},{T_{r,{{{<i}}}}}).

(2)

After receiving the response
T_{r}
, we reserve the first 576 tokens
{T}_{rv}
for computing the visual token reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VTR}}
and the remaining tokens
{T}_{rt}
to compute the detailed caption generation loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCG}}
.
The visual token reconstruction loss is calculated as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VTR}}=\sum_{i\in\Theta}\|{T}_{rv}^{i}-T_{v}^{i}\|,

(3)

where
\Theta
represents the index set of replaced visual tokens.
We maximize the likelihood of text tokens
{T}_{rt}
by employing the auto-regressive language modeling objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCG}}=\sum_{i}\log p\left(t_{i}\mid\hat{T}_{v},t_{1},\cdots,t_{i-1}\right)

(4)

The overall loss is the sum of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VTR}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCG}}
:

\mathcal{L}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VTR}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCG}}

(5)

where
\alpha
is used to balance the influence of different losses.

3.4
Training Pipeline

The overall training pipeline of our Croc model is shown in Fig.
2
. Building upon LLaVA-1.5, we propose a two-stage pre-training procedure followed by instruction tuning.

Stage 1: Cross-modal Alignment.

Following LLaVA-1.5, we first pretrain the projection layer with the identical 558K pretraining dataset used in LLaVA-1.5 to align image features to the LLM embedding space. During training, we keep both the visual encoder and the LLM weights frozen.

Stage 1.5: Cross-Modal Reconstruction.

Building on the cross-modal alignment stage, we introduce the cross-modal reconstruction phase as a pre-training stage. To facilitate comprehensive learning of visual tokens by the LLM, we pretrain the projection layer as well as the LLM. We select 1.2M detailed image-text pairs from the ShareGPT4V
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
dataset, as detailed captions (Fig.
4
) enhance the visual token learning of the LLM. To prevent the degradation of the LLM capabilities, we also incorporate 300K pure text data. Please refer to the appendix for more details.

Stage 2: Instruction Tuning.

Following LLaVA-1.5, we freeze the visual encoder weights and update both the pre-trained weights of the projection layer and LLM to improve its visual question answering capabilities. We employ the identical 665K instruction dataset used in LLaVA-1.5.

Prompt Token Pool Size.

The prompt token pool provides a set of jointly learnable visual tokens to replace the masked visual tokens. The tokens in the pool can capture global visual context, thus making the task of reconstruction easier at high masking ratios.
This level of difficulty can be further refined using the token pool size.
As shown in Fig.
6
, we find that the larger the number of tokens in the pool, the easier it is to generate detail captions, resulting in a lower training loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCG}}
). We show in Tab.
, training loss saturates when the pool size is 2,048.
In contrast, with pool size of 1, this is functionally equivalent to standard masked generation, which is too difficult and subsequently impeding learning.
We observe that increasing the token pool from 1 to 2,048 tokens almost linearly improves performance across all benchmarks.
However, expanding the pool to 4,096 tokens, makes the visual token reconstruction too easy, thus degrades the performance.

Visual Token Mask Ratio.

The mask ratio of visual tokens directly affects the difficulty of the visual token reconstruction task, and thus significantly affects the effectiveness of pretraining.
In Table.
, we report the results of experiments with different mask ratios.
Similar to the observations with MAE
(
He et al. 2022
)
, we find that using a 75% masking ratio yields optimal results in several downstream benchmarks.
Lower ratios, e.g., 0%, make the pre-training task too easy, while higher ratios, e.g., 90%, make it too difficult.
However, even though a 75% masking ratio is optimal, it must be coupled with an appropriately sized token pool size which is discussed in the previous section.

Figure 6
:
Visualization of the pre-training loss curves for different prompt token pool sizes. Setting the pool size to 1 means that only one learnable token is used to replace all masked visual tokens.

Nearest Neighbors v.s. Hungarian Matching.

To improve the utilization rate of the tokens in the prompt token pool, we use the Hungarian Matching algorithm. This step is important to avoid an over-dependence on a small subset of the token pool, while also enabling a sufficient exploration of the token space. In Fig.
7
, we present a comparative analysis of token utilization using both Nearest Neighbors and Hungarian Matching. Due to the Hungarian Matching requirement to select distinct prompt tokens, we observe a significant improvement in the overall utilization of prompt token, leading to a significant improvement across many evaluation benchmarks (see Tab.
).

Unidirectional Attention v.s. Mixed Attention.

To verify the impact of the mixed attention mechanism, we conduct experiments comparing mixed attention with unidirectional attention used in LLaVA-1.5. As shown in Tab.

Croc-7B model exhibits significantly higher attention scores for visual tokens, as indicated by the brighter region in the visual token part of the heatmap, suggesting that it puts more emphasis on understanding and attending to the image content.

Ablation on Stage, Data, and Objectives.
In Tab.
, we show the model’s performance without cross-modal alignment pretraining. The feature misalignment between the LLM and visual encoder makes the visual token reconstruction task more challenging, leading to reduced performance.
In Tab.
5
, we present ablation studies on the effects of pretraining data and different training objectives. First, we expand the 558K pretraining dataset of LLaVA-1.5 with an additional 1.5M samples, applying the same training method. This addition improves performance in 4 of 9 datasets, but causes declines in the others, likely due to the altered distribution of the original data.
Next, we add a visual token reconstruction task to enhance the integration of the LLM and visual tokens, improving performance in 5 of 9 datasets.
Further, using detailed caption descriptions improves performance in 8 of 9 datasets. Finally, combining visual token reconstruction with detailed caption generation significantly increases the LLM’s understanding of visual features, reaching the best overall performance.

VILA v.s. Croc.

As VILA incorporates an additional one million instruction data, we construct an 802K dataset for the instruction tuning stage to facilitate a more equitable comparison. Specifically, we expand the identical 665K instruction data used in LLaVA-1.5 to 802K by sampling an additional 142K instances from publicly accessible datasets. Please refer to the appendix for more details. As shown in Tab.
6
, although our model only utilizes 1/25 of the pre-training data compared to VILA, Croc-7B achieves significant performance improvements in 8/10 of the benchmarks. Similarly, Croc-13B achieves significant performance improvements in 9/10 of the benchmarks. These performance improvements demonstrate that our proposed cross-modal comprehension pretraining stage can facilitate the learning of visual tokens by LLMs, substantially improving the visual comprehension and reasoning of LMMs.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose Croc, a pretraining method aimed at improving the visual understanding of LMMs. We introduce a cross-modal comprehension stage that bridges the visual and textual domains, and design a dynamically learnable prompt token pool, which utilizes the Hungarian algorithm to replace some visual tokens with relevant prompts. For better understanding, we combine bidirectional visual attention with unidirectional textual attention. Additionally, we incorporate a caption generation task to improve the model’s grasp of visual semantics. Croc outperforms state-of-the-art by up to
8.5\%
across a wide range of evaluation benchmarks and without any architectural modifications.

3
Limitation Discussion

In this work, we have focused solely on image token reconstruction, which limits the scope to static images. However, for comprehensive video understanding, it is essential to consider both spatial and temporal token reconstruction. This would allow us to capture the dynamic changes that occur across frames and enhance the model’s ability to process and interpret video sequences more effectively. Expanding our approach to include spatial-temporal token reconstruction is a necessary step for future improvements in video analysis.

4
Ethic Discussion

Even though large multimodal models represent a major technological advancement with broad applications, their development and deployment bring significant ethical responsibilities. To ensure that the proposed Croc model benefits society while minimizing potential harm, it is essential to address key concerns such as bias, privacy, misinformation, environmental impact, intellectual property, accountability, and human oversight. These challenges are not unique to Croc but are pressing issues faced by the entire AI research and development community.
