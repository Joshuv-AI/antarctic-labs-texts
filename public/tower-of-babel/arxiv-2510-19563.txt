Local limits of determinantal processes
Asaf Nachmias, Yuval Peled

Abstract
Let H n H_{n} be the row space of a signed adjacency matrix of a C 4 C_{4} -free bipartite bi-regular graph in which one part has degree d ⁡ ( n ) → ∞ d(n)\to\infty and the other part has degree k + 1 k+1 where k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 is a fixed integer. We show that the local limit as n → ∞ n\to\infty of the determinantal process corresponding to the orthogonal projection on H n H_{n} is a variant of a Poisson ( k ) (k) branching process conditioned to survive. This setup covers a wide class of determinantal processes such as uniform spanning trees, Kalai’s determinantal hypertrees, hyperforests in regular cell complexes, discrete Grassmanians, incidence matroids and more, as long as their degree tends to ∞ \infty .

Introduction

We study the local limits of determinantal processes. To do this one must have an adjacency relation between the process’ elements which general determinantal processes do not posses. Hence we restrict our setting to processes which naturally have such a structure. This restricted setting may seem unfamiliar at a first glance, but in fact it includes many well studied examples such as the uniform spanning tree, Kalai’s determinantal hypertrees
[
10
]
and many more (see
Section

1.2
).

In this setting, the determinantal process is a probability measure on subsets of cardinality
r
of some finite ground set
U
. We think of
U
as the right sided vertex subset of a bipartite graph
G=(V,U,E)
where
V\uplus U
are the left and right vertex subsets, respectively, and
E
is the edge set. For a random sample
\mathcal{T}\subset U
drawn according to this measure, we study the induced subgraph
G[\mathcal{T}]
consisting of
\mathcal{T}
together with all its neighbors in
G
. Assuming that
G
is
C_{4}
-free and
(d,k+1)
-bi-regular with fixed
k
and
d\to\infty
, we prove that the rooted random graph
(G[\mathcal{T}],o)
, where
o\in V
is chosen uniformly at random, converges in distribution with respect to the local topology of rooted graphs to a variant of a Poisson
(k)
branching process, which we denote by
\mathbb{T}_{k}
. Notably, the limit depends only on
k
.

Our main result (
Theorem

1.1
) recovers the main result of
[
22
]
regarding the local limit uniform spanning trees of
d
-regular graphs and significantly extends the result in
[
15
]
on the local limit of Kalai’s determinantal hypertrees to include determinantal hyperforests of any high-degree cell complex. More importantly, we provide a unified linear algebraic approach to proving such local limits which include a very wide class of determinantal processes, see
Section

1.2
below for examples.

1.1
Setup and main result

Suppose that
G_{n}=(V_{n},U_{n},E_{n})
is a sequence of simple bipartite graphs where
V_{n}\uplus U_{n}
is the vertex set and
E_{n}
is the edge set. For notational convenience we frequently drop the subscript
n
and denote
G=(V,U,E)
instead of
G_{n}=(V_{n},U_{n},E_{n})
. We assume that
|V|=n
and
|U|=m
and that
G
is
C_{4}
-free (that is, any two vertices
G
have at most one common neighbor) and that it is a
(d,k+1)
-bi-regular graph, i.e., the degree of
v\in V
is
d
and the degree of
u\in U
is
k+1
. So
m=nd/(k+1)
. We assume that
k\geq 1
is a fixed integer and that
d\to\infty
as
n\to\infty

Let
B
be a
n\times m
signed adjacency matrix of
G
, i.e.,
B_{u,v}\in\{1,-1\}
if
uv\in E
and
B_{u,v}=0
otherwise. Denote
r=\operatorname{rank}(B)\leq\min(n,m)
. Let
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the row space of
B
and denote by
P_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to H
the orthogonal projection operator onto
H
which we treat as an
m\times m
matrix represented in the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Given a subset
\mathcal{T}\subset U
, we follow Lyons’ notation
[
12
]
, and denote
P_{H}\res\mathcal{T}
for the submatrix of
P_{H}
whose rows and columns are indexed by
\mathcal{T}
. The determinantal process associated with
H
is a probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{H}
on subsets
\mathcal{T}\subset U
of size
|\mathcal{T}|=r
given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{H}(\mathcal{T})=\det(P_{H}\res\mathcal{T})\,.

To see that (
1.1
) defines a probability measure let
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{r}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
H
viewed as row vectors and let
C=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{r})
be an
r\times m
matrix; then applying Cauchy-Binet on the left hand side of the equality
\det(CC^{T})=1
yields that
\sum_{|\mathcal{T}|=r}\mathbb{P}^{H}(\mathcal{T})=1
. Also, the
r\times r
matrix
P_{H}\res\mathcal{T}
is nonsingular only when the columns of
B
indexed by
\mathcal{T}
span an
r
-dimensional space. Hence, the measure
\mathbb{P}^{H}
is supported on subsets
\mathcal{T}\subset U
such that these columns form a basis of the column space of
B
. In particular, if
\mathcal{T}\subset U
is such that
\mathbb{P}^{H}(\mathcal{T})>0
, then every
v\in V
has a neighbor in
\mathcal{T}
(in other words, such
\mathcal{T}
must be
spanning
).

Given a subset
\mathcal{T}\subset U
we denote by
G[\mathcal{T}]
the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertices
N_{G}(\mathcal{T})\cup\mathcal{T}
, where
N_{G}(\mathcal{T})
is the subset of
V
of neighbors of
\mathcal{T}
in
G
. Our goal is to study the local limit (see below) of
G[\mathcal{T}]
as
n\to\infty
. The limiting object
\mathbb{T}_{k}
, first studied by Linial and the second author
[
11
]
, is a variant of a Poisson
(k)
branching process. Formally
\mathbb{T}_{k}
is an infinite random rooted tree that is drawn according multi type branching process with
4
types:
\mathfrak{a}
-even,
\mathfrak{a}
-odd,
\mathfrak{b}
-even and
\mathfrak{b}
-odd. The symbol odd/even only represents the parity of the distance from the root (intuitively, the even vertices correspond to
V
and the odd to
U
). The root
\rho
is of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even, and the distribution of the number of progeny and their types for a given type is given by the following rules, where all the Poisson
(k)
random variables below are independent:

•

A particle of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even has

1
child of type
\mathfrak{a}
-odd and Poisson
(k)
children of type
\mathfrak{b}
-odd.

•

A particle of type
\mathfrak{b}
-even has Poisson
(k)
children of type
\mathfrak{b}
-odd.

•

A particle of type
\mathfrak{a}
-odd has
k
children all of them of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even.

•

A particle of type
\mathfrak{b}
-odd has
k-1
children of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even and
1
child of type
\mathfrak{b}
-even.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer,
d(n)\to\infty
an integer sequence,
G_{n}=(V_{n},U_{n},E_{n})
a sequence of simple
C_{4}
-free bipartite graphs that are
(d(n),k+1)
bi-regular,
B_{n}
a
|V_{n}|\times|U_{n}|
signed adjacency matrix of
G_{n}
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be drawn according to the determinantal probability measure (
1.1
) associated with the row space of
B_{n}
, and
o_{n}\in V_{n}
is chosen uniformly at random independently. Then the rooted graphs
(G[\mathcal{T}_{n}],o_{n})
converge in distribution with respect to the local topology to
(\mathbb{T}_{k},\rho)
.

The only notion left to recall in order to parse
Theorem

1.1
is the
local rooted graph topology
, first introduced in the influential paper of Benjamini and Schramm
[
2
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
be the set of all locally finite rooted graphs
(G,o)
viewed up to root preserving graph isomorphism. Given a rooted graph
(G,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
and an integer
r\geq 0
we write
B_{G}(o,r)
for the induced graph of
G
on the vertices of graph distance at most
r
from
o
; it is also an element of
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
. We endow
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
with a distance: the distance between
(G_{1},o_{1})
and
(G_{2},o_{2})
is
2^{-r}
where
r\geq 0
is the largest integer so that there is a root preserving graph isomorphism between
B_{G_{1}}(o_{1},r)
and
B_{G_{2}}(o_{2},r)
. This metric endows what is known as the
local topology
on
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
and with it
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
is a polish space and the usual notions of convergence in distribution and tightness are viable. In particular, the conclusion of
Theorem

1.1
is equivalent to the statement that for any
r\geq 0
the random rooted graphs
B_{G[\mathcal{T}_{n}]}(o_{n},r)
converges in distribution to
B_{\mathbb{T}_{k}}(\rho,r)
, see
[
3
, Section 1.2]
.

Mészáros
[
15
, Lemma 1.4]
computed a useful explicit expression for the distribution of
B_{\mathbb{T}_{k}}(\rho,r)
for every fixed integer
r
. Let
(T,o)
be a finite rooted tree. The
height
of a vertex in
T
is its graph distance from
o
and the height of
T
is the maximal height of a vertex in
T
. We say that
(T,o)
is
valid
if its height is even and every vertex at odd height has degree
k+1
. We call vertices of
(T,o)

even
or
odd
according to the parity of their height. Let
V(T)
and
U(T)
be the sets of even and odd vertices of
(T,o)
, respectively, and let
I(T)\subset V(T)
be the even vertices of height
strictly
smaller than the height of
T
. We also denote by Aut
(T,o)
the set of root preserving automorphisms of
(T,o)
. Lastly, for
K\subset V(T)
we write
m(K,T)
for the number of matchings in
T
in which the vertices
K\cup U(T)
are saturated. Lemma 1.4 of
[
15
]
states that for any valid finite rooted tree
(T,o)
of height
r
we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathbb{T}_{k}}(\rho,r)\cong(T,o))=\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}(k!)^{|U(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\,,

where
\cong
is the rooted graph isomorphism relation. In
Section

1.3
we give an alternate proof for this equality based on the
1
-out model of Linial and the second author
[
11
]
. It is not used in the main results of this paper, but we believe it sheds more light on the distribution described in (
1.2
).
Since
\mathbb{T}_{k}
is always infinite and locally-finite, (
1.2
) completely described the distribution of
(\mathbb{T}_{k},\rho)
.

Due to (
1.2
), the following theorem is equivalent to (and is a more concrete version of)
Theorem

1.1
. For
v\in V
and an integer
r\geq 0
, we abbreviate
B_{\mathcal{T}}(v,r)
for
B_{G[\mathcal{T}]}(v,r)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

1.1
, let
(T,o)
be a valid finite rooted tree of height
r
and let
v_{n}
denote a uniform drawn vertex of
V_{n}
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(v_{n},r)\cong(T,o))=\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}(k!)^{|U(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\,,

where
\cong
is the rooted graph isomorphism relation.

We also obtain a stronger quenched version of
Theorem

1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

1.1
, let
(T,o)
be a valid finite rooted tree of height
r
and let the random variable
Y_{n}=Y_{n}(T,o)
denote the number of vertices
v_{n}\in V_{n}
for which

B_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(v_{n},r)\cong(T,o)\,.

Then

\frac{Y_{n}}{|V_{n}|}\to\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}(k!)^{|U(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\,,

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

1.2
Examples

We present examples of determinantal processes of interest that fall into the setting of
Theorem

1.1
.

1.2.1
Uniform spanning trees

It is well-known that the uniform spanning tree (UST) of a connected graph
\Gamma
is the determinantal process associated with the rows-space of its oriented incidence matrix. To formulate it precisely in our setup, let
V=V(\Gamma)
,
U=E(\Gamma)
, choose an arbitrary orientation of the edges and set
B
accordingly. That is,

B_{v,e}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{$v$ is the target of $e$,}\\
-1&\mbox{$v$ is the source of $e$,}\\
0&v\notin e.\end{cases}

Then, the rank of
B
is
|V(\Gamma)|-1
and
\mathbb{P}^{H}
is the uniform measure on the edge-sets of spanning trees of
\Gamma
.
In
[
22
]
, it is shown that if
\Gamma
is a
d
-regular graph, and
d\to\infty
, then the local limit of the UST of
\Gamma
is the Poisson
(1)
branching process conditioned to survive. The special case
\Gamma=K_{n}
was established by Grimmet
[
6
]
.

Note that this setting falls into our setup with
k=1
, hence
Theorem

1.1
applies. In addition, for every
\mathcal{T}\subset U=E(\Gamma)
, the graph
G[\mathcal{T}]
is obtained from the subgraph
(N_{G}(\mathcal{T}),\mathcal{T})
of
\Gamma
by edge-subdivisions — replacing each edge with a path of length
2
. Indeed, the old vertices are
N_{G}(\mathcal{T})
and the new vertices represent
\mathcal{T}
.
Similarly, observe that the limit object
\mathbb{T}_{1}
is obtained by a similar edge-subdivisions of the infinite path with an unconditional Poisson
(1)
branching process “hanging” on each vertex of the path. This is precisely the Poisson
(1)
branching process conditioned to survive. Hence,
Theorem

1.1
recovers the result of
[
22
]
. In fact, some parts of our proof extend their methods, which rely of effective resistance in electrical networks, to a more general linear-algebraic setting.

1.2.2
Kalai’s hypertrees

Kalai
[
10
]
extended the well-known Cayley’s formula for the number of labelled trees to
k
-dimensional simplicial complexes with a complete
(k-1)
-dimensional skeleton. He showed that

(1.3)

\sum_{\mathcal{T}}|H_{k-1}(\mathcal{T})|^{2}=n^{\binom{n-2}{k}}\,,

where
H_{k-1}
denote the
(k-1)
-dimensional homology group with integral coefficients, and the summation is over all
n
-vertex
k
-dimensional simplicial complexes
\mathcal{T}
satisfying:

(1)

\mathcal{T}
has a complete
(k-1)
-dimensional skeleton, i.e., it contains all simplices of dimension less than
k
spanned by its
n
vertices,

(2)

H_{k-1}(\mathcal{T};\mathbb{R})=0,
and

(3)

H_{k}(\mathcal{T};\mathbb{R})=0
.

When
k=1
, these conditions force
\mathcal{T}
to be a spanning tree, and (
1.3
) recovers Cayley’s tree enumeration formula. For general
k\geq 1
, complexes with properties (1)–(3) are called
k
-hypertrees
. It can be shown (see
[
10
]
) that each
k
-hypertree contains precisely
\binom{n-1}{k}

k
-faces.

Equation (
1.3
) induces a natural probability measure on
k
-hypertrees of the complete
k
-dimensional simplicial complex with
n
vertices,

(1.4)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{|H_{k-1}(\mathcal{T})|^{2}}{n^{\binom{n-2}{k}}}\,.

This is a determinantal probability measure. Indeed, if
V=\binom{[n]}{k}
and
U=\binom{[n]}{k+1}
are the sets of all
(k-1)
-faces and all
k
-faces of the complete complex, respectively, and
B
is the
V\times U
matrix form of the
k
-dimensional boundary operator, then the measure
\mathbb{P}^{H}
associated with the row-space
H
of
B
is supported on
k
-hypertrees and satisfies (
1.4
). This follows directly from
[
10
]
, see also
[
13
]
.

Many properties of Kalai’s determinantal hypertree have been studied
[
9
,
25
,
16
,
21
,
18
,
17
,
19
]
. In particular, Mészáros
[
15
]
proved that the random bipartite incidence graph between the
(k-1)
-faces and the
k
-faces of
\mathcal{T}
, rooted at a uniformly random
(k-1)
-face converges in distribution to
\mathbb{T}_{k}
with respect to the local topology. This result extends the aforementioned Grimmet’s local weak limit of the uniform spanning tree of the complete graph
K_{n}
to higher dimensions.

In our setup (see
Section

1.1
), this incidence bipartite graph is exactly
G[\mathcal{T}]
so Mészáros’ result is another special case of
Theorem

1.1
. In fact,
Theorem

1.1
significantly extends
[
15
]
as it applies to general regular high-degree cell complexes (and not just the complete simplicial complex), see
Corollary

1.4
below.

1.2.3
Hyperforests in high-degree cell complexes

Counterparts of Kalai’s enumeration formula for more general simplicial and cell complexes
X
have been studied extensively (see the survey of Duval, Klivans and Martin
[
5
]
and the references therein). Every such enumeration formula gives rise to a corresponding determinantal measure on subcomplexes of
X
. As we explain below, our result is applicable to this measure provided that the underlying complex
X
is regular and of high-degree.

Let
\ell\geq 1
be an integer and
X
be an
\ell
-dimensional cell complex. A subcomplex
\mathcal{T}\subset X
is called an
\ell
-hyperforest
(referred to as a
maximal spanning forest
in
[
5
]
) if

(1)

\mathcal{T}
contains all the cells of
X
in dimensions less than
\ell
,

(2)

H_{\ell-1}(\mathcal{T};\mathbb{R})=H_{\ell-1}(X;\mathbb{R})
, and

(3)

H_{\ell}(\mathcal{T};\mathbb{R})=0
.

In the case
\ell=1
of graphs, theses conditions force
\mathcal{T}
to be a maximal spanning forest, that is, a spanning tree in each of the connected components of the graph
X
. In addition, assuming condition (1), conditions (2)–(3) are equivalent to the condition that the columns of
\mathcal{T}
in the
\ell
-dimensional boundary operator
B
of
X
are a basis for the column-space of
B

[
5
]
. Therefore, the determinantal measure associated with the row-space
H
of
B
is supported on (top dimensional cells of)
\ell
-hyperforests of
X
. In addition, Lyons showed in
[
13
]
that
\mathbb{P}^{H}(\mathcal{T})
is proportional to the squared-size of the torsion subgroup of
H_{\ell-1}(\mathcal{T})
. We refer to a complex drawn from this measure as the
determinantal
\ell
-hyperforest of
X
. The next result follows immediately from
Theorem

1.1
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\ell,k\geq 1
be fixed integers,
d(n)\to\infty
an integer sequence, and
X_{n}
a sequence of cell complexes of dimension
\ell
such that

•

the boundary of every
\ell
-cell contains precisely
k+1
cells of dimension
\ell-1
,

•

every
(\ell-1)
-cell is contained in precisely
d
cells of dimension
\ell
, and

•

(“
C_{4}
-free”) no pair of
(\ell-1)
-cells is contained in more than one
\ell
-cell.

Suppose that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is the determinantal
\ell
-hyperforest of
X_{n}
,
o_{n}
a uniformly drawn random
(\ell-1)
-cell of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and let
G[\mathcal{T}_{n}]
be the bipartite incidence graph between
(\ell-1)
-cells and
\ell
-cells of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Then
(G[\mathcal{T}_{n}],o_{n})
converges in distribution to
(\mathbb{T}_{k},\rho)
with respect to the local topology.

We note that if
X
is simplicial then
\ell=k
and “
C_{4}
-freeness” is trivial. We give here two concrete examples — apart from the complete simplicial complex — where Kalai-type enumeration had been studied and for which
Corollary

1.4
applies.

1.

Take
X_{n}
to be the complete balanced
r
-partite (colorful)
\ell
-dimensional simplicial complex
K_{n/r,\ldots,n/r}
, where
r\geq\ell+1
.
Since
X
is simplicial, we have
k=\ell
and
d=n(1-k/r)\to\infty
. Adin established a Kalai-type enumeration formula for colorful simplicial complexes in
[
1
]
.

2.

Consider the cubical complex
X_{n}
, given by the
\ell
-skeleton of the
n
-dimensional hypercube
Q_{n}=[0,1]^{n}
. In this case,
d=n-\ell+1\to\infty
and
k=2\ell-1
. A Kalai-type enumeration in this setting is given in
[
4
]
.

1.2.4
Discrete Grassmanian

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of order
q
and denote by
\mathrm{Gr}_{\ell}(n)
the set of linear subspaces of dimension
\ell
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. We fix
q
and
\ell
and let
V=\mathrm{Gr}_{\ell}(n)
and
U=\mathrm{Gr}_{{\ell}+1}(n)
. We consider a bipartite graph with vertex set
V\uplus U
by putting an edge
(v,u)
with
v\in V
and
u\in U
if and only if
v\subset u
, and set
B_{v,u}=1
, and
B_{v,u}=0
otherwise. If
{\ell}+1<n/2
, then
B
has full row rank, see
[
23
]
. Homology groups over finite fields that correspond to these matrices were studied in
[
20
,
24
]
.
Denote the size of
\mathrm{Gr}_{\ell}(n)
by

\binom{n}{q}_{\ell}:={(q^{n}-1)(q^{n}-q)(q^{n}-q^{2})\cdots(q^{n}-q^{{\ell}-1})\over(q^{\ell}-1)(q^{\ell}-q)(q^{\ell}-q^{2})\cdots(q^{\ell}-q^{{\ell}-1})}\,.

We have that
|V|=\binom{n}{q}_{\ell},~|U|=\binom{n}{q}_{{\ell}+1}
and
k+1=\binom{{\ell}+1}{\ell}_{\ell}
. In addition

d(n)={q^{n-{\ell}}-1\over q-1}\to\infty\,,

as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, for any fixed
v\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\ell}(n)
, each
u\in\mathrm{Gr}_{\ell+1}(n)
containing
v
corresponds to a one dimensional subspace of the quotient space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}/v
which has dimension
n-\ell
.
Note that
G=(V,U,E)
is
C_{4}
-free since there is at most one
({\ell}+1)
-dimensional subspace containing two distinct
{\ell}
-dimensional subspaces.

We are not aware of a description of the bases
\mathcal{T}
of the column-space of
B
, nor of previous work studying the determinant of
B\res\mathcal{T}
. Nevertheless,
Theorem

1.1
obtains the distributional limit of
G[\mathcal{T}]
that is sampled proportionally to this determinant with respect to the local topology.

1.2.5
Incidence matroids

Let
V=\binom{[n]}{l}
and
U=\binom{[n]}{r}
for some fixed
l<r\leq n
, and
B
be the unsigned incidence matrix (all
+1
). It is known
[
23
]
that
B
has a full row rank, but we are not aware of a characterization of the bases of the corresponding matroid or their determinants in general.
In the special case
l=1,r=2
, the bases
\mathcal{T}
are
n
-edge graphs with no even cycles and only unicyclic or acyclic components, and
\mathbb{P}^{H}(\mathcal{T})
is proportional to
4^{C}
, where
C
is the number of cyclic components in
\mathcal{T}

[
26
]
. Our result shows that the local weak limit of such a random graph is the Poisson
(1)
branching process conditioned to survive.

1.3
The distribution of
(\mathbb{T}_{k},\rho)

In this section we give an alternate proof that the distribution of
(\mathbb{T}_{k},\rho)
is given by (
1.2
); the original proof is due to Mészáros, see
[
15
, Lemma 1.4]
.
Our argument is related to the motivation for the original definition by Linial and the second author of
\mathbb{T}_{k}
in
[
11
]
as the local limit of a random
1
-out
k
-dimensional simplicial complex.

Let
k
and
d
be integers, and consider the following infinite tree
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}_{k,d}
rooted at a vertex
\mathbf{v}
. As before, the vertices of
\mathbf{T}
are split into
V(\mathbf{T})
and
U(\mathbf{T})
— the vertices of even and odd height respectively. In addition, every vertex from
V(\mathbf{T})
has
d
children, and every vertex from
U(\mathbf{T})
has
k
children. For a vertex
v\in V(\mathbf{T})
, we denote by
N_{\mathbf{T}}(v)
the neighbor set of
v
. We stress that
N_{\mathbf{T}}(v)
include the
d
children of
v
and also its parent if
v
has one. Let each vertex
v\in V(\mathbf{T})
independently
draw
a uniform random vertex
u_{v}\in N_{\mathbf{T}}(v)
. Additionally, let
\mathcal{O}=\{u_{v}\mid v\in V(\mathbf{T})\}
and
\mathbf{T}[\mathcal{O}]
be the random subgraph of
\mathbf{T}
induced by
V(\mathbf{T})\cup\mathcal{O}
, and abbreviate
B_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{v},r)
for
B_{\mathbf{T}[\mathcal{O}]}(\mathbf{v},r)
for an integer
r\geq 0
.

Lemma 1.5
.

Fix an integer
k
and let
(T,o)
be a valid rooted tree of height
r\geq 0
. Then,

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathbb{T}_{k}}(\rho,r)\cong(T,o))=\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{v},r)\cong(T,o))=\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}(k!)^{|U(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\,.

Proof.

The first equality follows by viewing the component of the root
\mathbf{v}
in
\mathbf{T}[\mathcal{O}]
as a multi type branching process. One readily verifies that, as
d\to\infty
, the progeny number and type distribution converges weakly to those of
\mathbb{T}_{k}
. Hence,
(\mathbf{T}[\mathcal{O}],\mathbf{v})
converges in distribution with respect to the local topology to
(\mathbb{T}_{k},\rho)
.

The types in the process are
\mathfrak{a}
-even,
\mathfrak{b}
-even, and
j
-odd for
0\leq j\leq k
, where the root
\mathbf{v}
is of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even. Intuitively,
v\in V(\mathbf{T})
is of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even if
u_{v}
is his child and is of type
\mathfrak{b}
-even if
u_{v}
is his parent.
Note that we have more odd types here than in
\mathbb{T}_{k}
since we need to account for the possibility that a vertex in
U(\mathbf{T})
is drawn by more than one of its neighbors. Namely, a vertex
u\in U(\mathbf{T})
is of type
j
-odd if it was drawn by
j
of his children. In particular, type
0
-odd corresponds to
\mathfrak{a}
-odd in
\mathbb{T}_{k}
and type
1
-odd to
\mathfrak{b}
-odd.

To sample the branching process we use the following source of randomness. For each even particle
v
we encounter, we sample independent random variables
X_{1},...,X_{d}
that are binomially distributed with
k
trials and success probability
1/(d+1)
. One should view
X_{i}
as the number of children of
u_{i}
— the
i
-th child of
v
— that drew
u_{i}
. Here is the precise description of the process:

•

A particle of type
j
-odd, for
0\leq j\leq k
, has
j
children of type
\mathfrak{b}
-even and
k-j
children of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even.

•

A particle of type
\mathfrak{a}
-even has one child of type
X_{1}
-odd, and
n_{j}
children of type
j
-odd, where

n_{j}=|\{2\leq i\leq d\mid X_{i}=j\}|,

for
1\leq j\leq k
. Intuitively, the first child, of type
X_{1}
-odd, is the one drawn by the
\mathfrak{a}
-even particle.

•

A particle of type
\mathfrak{b}
-even has
m_{j}
children of type
j
-odd, where

m_{j}=|\{1\leq i\leq d\mid X_{i}=j\}|,

for
1\leq j\leq k
.

It is clear that this branching process and the component of
\mathbf{v}
in
\mathbf{T}[\mathcal{O}]
have the same distribution. In addition, the convergence to the law of
\mathbb{T}_{k}
is also straightforward. Indeed,
X_{1},n_{2},...,n_{k},m_{2},...,m_{k}
all converge to
0
in distribution as
d\to\infty
, and
n_{1},m_{1}
converge to Poisson
(k)
.

To prove the second equality, denote by
c(v)
the number of children of
v\in V(T)
in the rooted tree
T
. Then, the number of rooted-graph embeddings of
(T,o)
into
(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{v})
is

\left(\prod_{v\in I(T)}d(d-1)\cdots(d-c(v)+1)\right)\cdot(k!)^{|U(T)|}=(1+O(d^{-1}))\cdot(d\cdot k!)^{|U(T)|}\,,

where the last equality uses
\sum_{v\in I(T)}c(v)=|U(T)|
. Therefore, there are

(1+O(d^{-1}))\cdot\frac{(d\cdot k!)^{|U(T)|}}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}

different rooted subgraphs of
(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{v})
that are isomorphic to
(T,o)
.

Fix such a rooted subgraph
T^{\prime}
. The event
E
that
B_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{v},r)=T^{\prime}
occurs if and only if (i)
U(T^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{O}
and (ii)
N_{\mathbf{T}}(v)\cap\mathcal{O}\subset U(T^{\prime})
for every
v\in I(T^{\prime})
. Note that condition (i) is equivalent to
U(T^{\prime})\subset\{u_{v}:v\in V(T^{\prime})\}
since
T
is valid.

Denote by
E^{\prime}
the event that there exists a vertex
u\in U(T^{\prime})
that is chosen by at least two vertices in
V(T^{\prime})
. If
E\cap E^{\prime}
occurs then there are at least
|U(T)|+1
vertices in
V(T^{\prime})
that chose a vertex in
U(T^{\prime})
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(E\cap E^{\prime})\leq\left(|V(T^{\prime})|\cdot|U(T^{\prime})|\cdot\frac{1}{d}\right)^{|U(T^{\prime})|+1}=O\left(d^{-(|U(T)|+1)}\right)\,.

In addition, for every matching
M
in
T^{\prime}
in which the vertices
I(T^{\prime})\cup U(T^{\prime})
are saturated, consider the event
E_{M}\subset E\setminus E^{\prime}
in which

•

every vertex
v\in V(T^{\prime})
that is saturated in
M
drew its pair in
M
as
u_{v}
,

•

N_{G}(v)\cap\mathcal{O}\subset U(T^{\prime})
for every
v\in I(T^{\prime})
, and

•

u_{v}\notin U(T^{\prime})
for every vertex
v\in V(T^{\prime})
that is unsaturated by
M
.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{M})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{d+1}\right)^{|U(T)|-1}\cdot\prod_{v\in I(T^{\prime})}\left(\frac{d}{d+1}\right)^{k(d-c(v))}\cdot\left(\frac{d}{d+1}\right)^{|V(T)|-|U(T)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+O(d^{-1}))\cdot d^{-|U(T)|}\cdot e^{-k|I(T)|}\,.

The first inequality is obtained as follows. The first term accounts for the choice of the root (which has only
d
neighbors), the second term corresponds to the choices of the other vertices in
V(T^{\prime})
that are saturated in
M
, the third term reflects the requirement that
N_{G}(v)\cap\mathcal{O}\subset U(T^{\prime})
for every
v\in I(T^{\prime})
, and the last term reflects the requirement that the unsaturated vertices in
V(T^{\prime})
(which must be leafs) do not choose their parent.

We claim that
E\setminus E^{\prime}
is a disjoint union of the
m(I(T),T)
events of the form
E_{M}
. Indeed, suppose that
E\setminus E^{\prime}
occurs, and consider a matching in
T^{\prime}
that pairs
(v,u)
if
u=u_{v}
. The assumption that
E^{\prime}
does not occur guarantees that this is a well-defined matching, and the occurrence of
E
implies that
I(T^{\prime})\cup U(T^{\prime})
are saturated. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(E)=(m(I(T),T)+O(d^{-1}))\cdot d^{-|U(T)|}\cdot e^{-k|I(T)|}\,.

In conclusion, by summing over the different rooted subgraphs
T^{\prime}
of
(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{v})
that are isomorphic to
(T,o)
, we find that

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbf{v},r)\cong(T,o))=(m(I(T),T)+O(d^{-1}))\cdot\frac{(d\cdot k!)^{|U(T)|}}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\cdot d^{-|U(T)|}\cdot e^{-k|I(T)|}\,,

and the proof is derived directly.
∎

1.4
Some basic facts about determinantal probability measures

We describe here some useful facts about the determinantal probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{H}
defined in (
1.1
), all can be found in Lyons’ seminal paper
[
12
]
. Even though
\mathbb{P}^{H}
description in (
1.1
) is complete, it is sometimes more convenient and common to describe its finite marginals: for any subset
E\subset U
we have that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{H}(E\subset\mathcal{T})=\det(P_{H}\res E)\,.

We will also frequently use the
negative correlation
property of
\mathbb{P}^{H}
, that is,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{H}(U_{1}\uplus U_{2}\subset\mathcal{T})\leq\mathbb{P}^{H}(U_{1}\subset\mathcal{T})\mathbb{P}^{H}(U_{2}\subset\mathcal{T})\,,

for every disjoint subsets
U_{1},U_{2}\subset U(G)
,
see
[
12
, Theorem 6.5]
or
[
8
, Theorem 7.8.9]
. Next, if
H^{\prime}\subset H
is a linear subspace of
H
, then

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{H^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{P}^{H}\,,

where by
\mathbb{P}^{H^{\prime}}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{P}^{H}
we mean that
\mathbb{P}^{H}
stochastically dominates
\mathbb{P}^{H^{\prime}}
. Lastly, conditioning on the event that some elements belong to
\mathcal{T}
and others do not results in another determinantal probability measure corresponding to a certain linear subspace. In particular, for two disjoint subsets
A,B
of
U
, it is shown in
[
12
, (6.5)]
that

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{H}(\cdot\mid A\subset\mathcal{T},B\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset)=\mathbb{P}^{((H\cap[A]^{\perp})+[A]+[B])\cap[B]^{\perp}}(\cdot)\,,

where by
[A]
we mean the linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
having non-zero values only on the coordinates of
A
.
In words, the subspace corresponding to conditioning that

B\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
is the projection of
H
onto the orthogonal complement of
B
.
On the other hand, conditioning that
A\subset\mathcal{T}
is
obtained by taking the direct sum of
[A]
with the intersection of
H
and the orthogonal complement of
[A]
.

Proofs

We begin with some definitions and notations central to the proof. Recall that
G=(V,U,E)
is a bipartite graph that is
(d,k+1)
-bi-regular, that
d\to\infty
and that
B
is a
|V|\times|U|
signed adjacency matrix of
G
. We denote
n=|V|
and
m=|U|
.

Let
L^{+}:=\frac{1}{d}BB^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
L^{-}=\frac{1}{d}B^{T}B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be the two natural “Laplacian” operators. Both operators are positive semi-definite and have the same set of positive eigenvalues denoted by
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{r}
where
r\leq\min(n,m)
is the rank of
B
. We write
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for a choice of orthogonal unit eigenvectors of
L^{-}
corresponding to
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
. We fix this choice throughout the rest of the proof. The vectors
\{\psi_{1}^{T},\ldots,\psi_{r}^{T}\}
form an orthonormal basis of the row space
H=\ima(B^{T})
of
B
, hence the orthogonal projection
P_{H}
onto the
H
is

P_{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{T}\,.

Next we set parameters
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n)>0
and
\delta=\delta(n)>0
that satisfy

(2.1)

\varepsilon\to 0,\;\varepsilon d\to\infty,\;\delta d\to 0,\;\mbox{and }\varepsilon\delta d^{2}\to\infty\,,

as
n\to\infty
(for instance,
\varepsilon=d^{-1/2}
and
\delta=d^{-5/4}
will do). We will use these parameters throughout the proof. The following definition will be crucial.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a vertex
u\in U
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-structured
, if

(2.2)

\sum_{i:(\lambda_{i}-1)^{2}>\varepsilon}\psi_{i}(u)^{2}>\delta\,.

We denote by
U_{\varepsilon,\delta}\subset U
the set of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-structured vertices.

Informally,
u
is structured if there is a non-negligible
\ell_{2}
mass on it from the eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalues far from
1
. Intuitively this captures elements of the determinantal process that have “structure” around them. For example, in the UST example (see
Section

1.2.1
), edges that disconnect the graph into at least two linear sized components can be shown to be structured. We will show that their number is small, namely
o(m)
(see
2.4
).

2.1
Tightness and local lack of structure

Recall the definition of
B_{\mathcal{T}}(v,r)
appearing above
Theorem

1.2
. The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
o
be a uniformly drawn vertex of
V
that is independent from
\mathcal{T}
. Then for any integer
r>0
we have

1.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)|>t)=0
,
and,

2.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)\mathrm{\ is\ a\ tree})=1
, and,

3.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)\cap U_{\varepsilon,\delta}\neq\emptyset)=0.

We first show that most of the
r
positive eigenvalues of
L^{-}
are close to
1
. Let

I:=\{i\in[r]~:~(\lambda_{i}-1)^{2}\leq\varepsilon\}\,,

and denote by
H_{I}
be the span of
\{\psi_{i}:i\in I\}
.

Claim 2.3
.

We have that

(1-k(\varepsilon d)^{-1})n\leq|I|\leq n\,.

Proof.

The upper bound is obvious since
r\leq\min(n,m)
. For the lower bound we consider the operator
(L^{+}-\id_{n})^{2}
where
\id_{n}
is the identity operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
On the one hand,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Tr((L^{+}-\id_{n})^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\lambda_{i}-1)^{2}+n-r\geq(r-|I|)\varepsilon+n-r\geq(n-|I|)\varepsilon\,,

since
r\leq n
. On the other hand, for every
v_{1},v_{2}\in V

(L^{+}-\id_{n})_{v_{1},v_{2}}=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&v_{1}=v_{2}\,,\\
\pm\frac{1}{d}&N(v_{1})\cap N(v_{2})\neq\emptyset\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{matrix}\right.

since
N(v_{1})\cap N(v_{2})\in\{0,1\}
because
G
is
C_{4}
-free. Due to the same reason, the set of vertices
v_{2}
such that
N(v_{1})\cap N(v_{2})=1
is of size precisely
kd
. Hence
\Tr((L^{+}-\id)^{2})=kn/d
. Together with (
2.3
) the claim follows directly.
∎

Claim 2.4
.

We have that

|U_{\varepsilon,\delta}|\leq{mk(k+1)\over d^{2}\varepsilon\delta}\,.

Proof.

The sum
\sum_{i\notin I}\|\psi_{i}\|^{2}=r-|I|\leq nk(\varepsilon d)^{-1}
by
2.3
. On the other hand, every
u\in U_{\varepsilon,\delta}
contributes at least
\delta
to that sum. Hence
|U_{\varepsilon,\delta}|\leq nk(d\varepsilon\delta)^{-1}
and since
n=m(k+1)/d
the claim follows.
∎

Let
P_{H_{I}}
denote the orthogonal projection operator onto
H_{I}=
span
\{\psi_{i}:i\in I\}
, i.e.,

(2.4)

\displaystyle P_{H_{I}}=\sum_{i:(\lambda_{i}-1)^{2}\leq\varepsilon}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{T}\,.

We denote the corresponding determinantal process by
\mathbb{P}^{H_{I}}
so that

\mathbb{P}^{H_{I}}(F)=\det(P_{H_{I}}\res F)\,,

and we write
\mathcal{F}
for a sample of
\mathbb{P}^{H_{I}}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any fixed integer
r>0
and any
v\in V
we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(|B_{\mathcal{F}}(v,r)|>t\Big)=0\,,

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(B_{\mathcal{F}}(v,r)\mathrm{\ is\ a\ tree}\Big)=1\,.

Proof.

Using (
1.5
) and (
2.4
) and that
\lambda_{i}\geq 1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for all

we get that for every

(2.5)

\mathbb{P}(u\in\mathcal{F})=\sum_{i\in I}\psi_{i,u}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i,u}^{2}={L^{-}_{u,u}\over 1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}=\frac{k+1}{(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})d},

where in the last equality we used that

since every column of

has precisely

nonzero entries all of which are
\pm 1
.

Next observe that that if all the vertex degrees of

bounded above by some

, then

. Hence, if

, then there must be a vertex of degree at least

are

. For such a path to be included in

we must have that all its

and additional

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|B_{\mathcal{F}}(v,r)|>t)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r^{\prime}\leq r}(dk)^{r^{\prime}/2}\binom{d}{t^{\prime}-1}\left(\frac{k+1}{(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})d}\right)^{r^{\prime}/2+t^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r^{\prime}\leq r}(k+1)^{r^{\prime}}\left(\frac{e(k+1)}{t^{\prime}}\right)^{t^{\prime}-1}(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})^{-r^{\prime}/2-t^{\prime}}\,,

Next, if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}\leq r\\

as

By (

Lemma 2.6
.

Denote by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)\cap W\neq\emptyset)=0\,.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)\cap W\neq\emptyset,|B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,2r)|\leq t)\leq{t\mathbb{E}|W|\over n}\,.

\mathbb{E}|W|\leq(k+1)\mathbb{E}\big[|\mathcal{T}|-|\mathcal{F}|+|\mathcal{F}\cap U_{\varepsilon,\delta}|\big]\,.

We use this to bound the left hand side of (

t\geq 1
we get that

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)\cap W\neq\emptyset)\leq\mathbb{P}(|B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,2r)|>t)+{kn\over\varepsilon d}+{tk^{2}\over(1-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\varepsilon\delta d^{2}}\,.

Hence, by choosing
t
large enough and using
Lemma

2.5
we obtain the desired result.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

Since
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{T}
we always have that
B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)\subset B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)
. This containment is strict only if
B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)
contains a vertex
v\in V
that is incident to a vertex
u\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{F}
. The latter occurs with probability
o(1)
by
Lemma

2.6
. Hence for any integer
r>0

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\mathbb{P}\Big(B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)=B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)\Big)=1\,,

which together with
Lemma

2.5
implies the first two assertion of the theorem. The third assertion also follows since
B_{\mathcal{F}}(o,r)\cap U_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\emptyset
with probability
1-o(1)
by
Lemma

2.6
.
∎

2.2
Inclusion

For any subgraph
T
of
G=(V,U,E)
we write
V(T)
and
U(T)
for the vertices of
T
belonging to
V
and
U
, respectively. For such a subgraph and
K\subset V(T)
, recall that we write
m(K,T)
for the number of matchings in
T
saturating
K\cup U(T)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(T,o)
be a valid rooted finite tree which is a subgraph of
G
and
o\in V
. Assume that
U(T)\cap U_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\emptyset
. Then

\mathbb{P}(U(T)\subset\mathcal{T})\leq(1+o(1))d^{-|U(T)|}m(\emptyset,T)\,.

Proof.

For notational convenience we write
S=U(T)
. Recall that
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{r}
are orthogonal unit eigenvectors of
L^{-}
corresponding to the non-zero eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
and denote by
\psi_{i,S}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
their restriction to the entries indexed by
S
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S\subset\mathcal{T})=

\displaystyle~\det\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\psi_{i,S}\psi_{i,S}^{T}\right)

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\det\left(\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i,S}\psi_{i,S}^{T}+\sum_{i\notin I}\psi_{i,S}\psi_{i,S}^{T}\right)\right)\,,

where we used the fact that
\lambda_{i}\geq 1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}
for all
i\in I
and
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\not\in I
together with the inequality
\det(A+B)\geq\det(A)
whenever
A
and
B
are two positive definite matrices which follows by Minkowski’s determinanat inequality, see
[
8
, Theorem 7.8.21]
.
We also have that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i,S}\psi_{i,S}^{T}=L^{-}\res S
, hence the Cauchy-Binet formula implies that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\det\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i,S}\psi_{i,S}^{T}\Big)=\det(L^{-}\res S)=d^{-|S|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\subset V\\
|Q|=|S|\end{subarray}}\det(B_{Q,S})^{2}=d^{-|S|}m(\emptyset,T)\,,

since
\det(B_{Q,S})\in\{-1,1\}
if and only if there is a matching in
T
saturating
Q\cup S
, and otherwise
\det(B_{Q,S})=0
. Such a matching must be counted in
m(\emptyset,T)
and conversely each matching in
m(\emptyset,T)
corresponds to such
Q
. Next, since
L^{-}\res S
is invertible the generalized matrix determinant lemma
[
7
, Theorem 18.1.1]
states that

\det(L^{-}\res S+CC^{T})=\det(\id_{\ell}+C^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1}C)\det(L^{-}\res S)\,,

where
C
is any
|S|\times\ell
matrix with any
\ell\geq 1
. We apply this with
C
the
|S|\times|I^{c}|
matrix whose columns are the vectors
\psi_{i,S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
for
i\in I^{c}
so that
CC^{T}=\sum_{i\notin I}\psi_{i,S}\psi_{i,S}^{T}
. This together with (
2.2
) and (
2.10
) yields

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S\subset\mathcal{T})\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)^{|S|}d^{-|S|}m(\emptyset,T)\det(\id_{|I^{c}|}+C^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1}C)\,.

Since
\varepsilon\to 0
, it suffices to show that that the determinant on the right hand side is
1+o(1)
. For any square matrix
Q
for which all its eigenvalues are real and at least
-1
we may bound
\det(\id+Q)\leq\exp(\Tr(Q))
since
\prod_{i}(1+x_{i})\leq e^{\sum_{i}x_{i}}
for any numbers
x_{i}\geq-1
. Hence,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\det(\id_{|I^{c}|}+C^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1}C)\leq\exp(\Tr(C^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1}C))=\exp(\Tr(CC^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1}))\,,

where
\Tr(AB)=\Tr(BA)
implies the second inequality. We expand

(2.13)

\displaystyle\Tr(CC^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1})=\sum_{u_{1},u_{2}\in S}(CC^{T})_{u_{1},u_{2}}((L^{-}\res S)^{-1})_{u_{2},u_{1}}\,.

We estimate each term in the sum above. Firstly, by Cauchy-Schwarz

|(CC^{T})_{u_{1},u_{2}}|=\left|\sum_{i\notin I}\psi_{i}(u_{1})\psi_{i}(u_{2})\right|\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\notin I}\psi_{i}^{2}(u_{1})\sum_{i\notin I}\psi_{i}^{2}(u_{2})}\leq\delta

since
u_{1},u_{2}\in S
and
S\cap U_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\emptyset
. Secondly, let
M_{u_{1},u_{2}}
be the corresponding minor of
L^{-}\res S
. By Cauchy-Binet,

|M_{u_{1},u_{2}}|=\Big|\det(L^{-}\res_{S\setminus\{u_{1}\},S\setminus\{u_{2}\}})\Big|\leq\Big(\frac{k+1}{d}\Big)^{|S|-1}

since there at most
(k+1)^{|S|-1}
subsets of
V(T)
of size
|S|-1
that can be matched with both
S\setminus\{u_{1}\}
and
S\setminus\{u_{2}\}
. Therefore, using Cramer’s rule and (
2.10
), we have

|((L^{-}\res S)^{-1})_{u_{2},u_{1}}|=\frac{|M_{u_{1},u_{2}}|}{\det((L^{-}\res S)^{-1})}\leq\frac{(k+1)^{|S|-1}d}{m(\emptyset,T)}\,.

Putting these two in (
2.13
) then back into (
2.12
) yields

\det(\id_{|I^{c}|}+C^{T}(L^{-}\res S)^{-1}C)\leq\exp\left(|S|^{2}\frac{(k+1)^{|S|-1}\delta d}{m(\emptyset,T)}\right)=1+o(1)\,,

since
\delta d\to 0
by (
2.1
) and all other quantities on the right hand side are fixed.
∎

2.3
Exclusion

Recall that given a valid finite rooted tree
(T,o)
the set
I(T)\subset V(T)
is the subset of even vertices (i.e., of
V(T))
of height
strictly
smaller than the height of
T
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(T,o)
be a valid rooted finite tree of height
r\geq 0
which is a subgraph of
G
and
o\in V
. Suppose also that
m(I(T),T)>0
. Then

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=(T,o)\mid U(T)\subset\mathcal{T})\leq(1+o(1))\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{m(\emptyset,T)}\,,

where the

depends on
|V(T)|
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Fix an even integer
r\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{T}\subset U(G)
and
o\in V(G)
be such that
\mathbb{P}^{H}(\mathcal{T})>0
and
B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)
is a valid rooted tree
(T,o)
with
|V(T)|<d
. Then the height of
T
is
r
and
m(I(T),T)>0
.

These two lemmas, together with
Lemma

2.7
give a us an explicit upper bound for the probability
\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=(T,o))
for any fixed valid finite rooted tree
(T,o)
that is a subgraph of
G
. This brings us very close to
Theorem

1.2
. For the proofs of the two lemmas we require some preparations.

2.3.1
Transversals in valid trees

Let
(T,o)
be a valid finite rooted tree that is a subgraph of
G
and write
D
for the corresponding
V(T)\times U(T)
submatrix of
B
. A subset
M\subset V(T)
with
|M|=|U(T)|
is called a
transversal
if there is a matching in
T
that saturates
M\cup U(T)
. Since
T
is a tree, every transversal
M
has a unique matching saturating it, and therefore, we may identify a matching saturating
U(T)
with its corresponding transversal. Hence,
\det(D\res M)=\pm 1
if
M
is a transversal and
0
otherwise, where as usual for
K\subset V(T)
we denote by
D\res K
the
K\times U(T)
submatrix of
D
. Cauchy-Binet formula now implies that the number of transversals in
T
is given by
\det(D^{T}D)
and that the random subset
\mathcal{M}
of
V(T)
sampled by the determinantal measure associated with the image of
D
is a
uniformly
drawn transversal.

Recall that for a subset
K\subset V(T)
we write
m(K,T)
for the number of matchings in
T
in which
K\cup U(T)
is saturated, or equivalently, the number of transversals containing
K
.

Claim 2.10
.

If
K\subset V(T)
such
m(K,T)>0
and
v_{0}\in V(T)\setminus K
, then

m(K\cup\{v_{0}\},T)<m(K,T)\,.

Proof.

If there is no matching saturating
K\cup\{v_{0}\}
, then the claim is trivial. Otherwise, given a matching
M
in which the vertices
K\cup\{v_{0}\}
are saturated, construct the following non-backtracking path in
T
denoted
v_{0},u_{0},....,v_{\ell},u_{\ell},v_{\ell+1}
for some
\ell\geq 0
as follows. Start with
v_{0}
and let
u_{0}\in U(T)
be its unique neighbor in
M
. Next choose
v_{1}
to be an arbitrary neighbor of
u_{0}
in
T
that is not
v_{0}
. If
v_{1}\not\in K
, terminate. Otherwise, let
u_{1}
be the unique neighbor of
v_{1}
in
M
. Continue similarly until terminating. Since no vertex of
U(T)
is a leaf of
T
and since
T
is a tree, the process must terminate at vertex
v_{\ell+1}\in V(T)\setminus(K\cup\{v_{0}\})
.

Now, for each
0\leq i\leq\ell
replace the edge
\{v_{i},u_{i}\}
of
M
with
\{v_{i+1},u_{i}\}
. This yields a matching that saturates
K
but not
v_{0}
and the claim follows.
∎

Claim 2.11
.

Let
V(T)=K\uplus J
be a partition of
V(T)
such that
m(K,T)>0
and let
v_{0}\in J
. Then there exists a vector
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
such that

(i)

\varphi^{T}\cdot D=0
, and,

(ii)

\varphi(v_{0})=1
, and,

(iii)

\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}=\frac{m(K,T)}{m(K,T)-m(K\cup\{v_{0}\},T)}
.

Proof.

We first claim that there exists a vector
\varphi
satisfying (i) and (ii). Indeed, otherwise the row of
v_{0}
in
D
is
n
ot spanned by the all the other rows. Since
\det(D\res M)=\pm 1
if
M
is a transversal and
0
otherwise, it follows the rows of
D
corresponding to vertices of
V(T)
saturated by
M
are a basis of the row space of
D
; hence
v_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
with probability
1
, contradicting
2.10
.

Next, denote by
P_{(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp}}
the orthogonal projection from
\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
onto
(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp}
, in other words, the operator zeroing the coordinates of
K
and leaving the coordinates of
J
unaltered. By definition it is clear that
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
satisfies
(i)
and
(ii)
if and only if

P_{(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp}}\varphi\in W:=(\ker D^{T}+\mathbb{R}^{K})\cap(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp}\quad\text{and}\quad(P_{(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp}}\varphi)(v_{0})=1\,,

and in this case,
\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}=\|P_{(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp}}\varphi)\|^{2}
. We will produce the desired vector
\varphi
by minimizing
\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}
over vectors satisfying
(i)
and
(ii)
.

The vector of the affine slice
W\cap\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}:w(v_{0})=1\}
minimizing the norm is given by
w=P_{W}e_{v_{0}}/\|P_{W}e_{v_{0}}\|^{2}
where as usual
P_{W}
is the orthogonal projection onto
W
and
e_{v_{0}}
is the vector taking
1
at
v_{0}
and
0
otherwise. Indeed, by letting
\alpha:=\|P_{W}e_{v_{0}}\|^{2}
we find that for
w
in the affine slice

1-\alpha=\|e_{v_{0}}-P_{W}e_{v_{0}}\|^{2}\leq\|e_{v_{0}}-\alpha w\|^{2}=1-2\alpha+\alpha^{2}\|w\|^{2}\,,

whence
\|w\|^{2}\geq 1/\alpha
. To compute the value of
\alpha
we observe that

W^{\perp}=(\mathrm{im}(D)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{K})^{\perp})+\mathbb{R}^{K}

is the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
that induces the determinantal probability measure of
\mathcal{M}
conditioned on the event
K\subset\mathcal{M}
, by (
1.8
). Therefore,

\alpha=1-\|P^{W^{\perp}}e_{v_{0}}\|^{2}=1-\mathbb{P}(v_{0}\in\mathcal{M}\mid K\subset\mathcal{M})=1-\frac{m(K\cup\{v_{0}\},T)}{m(K,T)}\,.

In conclusion, if
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
satisfies (i),(ii) and minimizes
\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}
then

\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha}=\frac{m(K,T)}{m(K,T)-m(K\cup\{v_{0}\},T)}\,,

as required.
∎

The following easy claim deals with the case that
m(K,T)=0
.

Claim 2.12
.

Suppose that
m(K,T)=0
. Then, there exists a non-zero vector
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
supported on
K
such that
\varphi^{T}D=0
.

Proof.

If
m(K,T)=0
, then the rows of
D
corresponding to
K
are linearly dependent since otherwise we could add more rows of
D
and obtain a basis which corresponds to a transversal that contains
K
.
∎

2.3.2
A determinantal version of the Nash-Williams inequality

We will need to lower bound the probability that an element is chosen in a determinantal probability measure. The following is the analogue of the well-known Nash-Williams inequality lower bounding the effective resistance in an electric network (see
Remark

2.15
below).

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{W}
be the determinantal probability measure associated with a subspace
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, denote by
\mathcal{W}\subseteq[m]
a sample of
\mathbb{P}^{W}
and fix
u\in[m]
. Suppose that
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
are linearly independent vectors in
W
such that
|\xi_{i}(u)|=1
for all
i\in[k]
and let
M
be their invertible Gram matrix. Then

\mathbb{P}^{W}(u\in\mathcal{W})\geq\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}(M^{-1})_{i,j}\,.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
\xi_{i}(u)=1
; otherwise replace
\xi_{i}
by
-\xi_{i}
. By (
1.5
), we have that
\mathbb{P}^{W}(u\in\mathcal{W})=\|P_{W}e_{u}\|^{2}
(as usual
e_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vector taking
1
at
u
and
0
elsewhere) and so it is bounded from below by the squared norm of the projection of
e_{u}
onto the linear span of
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
since these belong to
W
. Let
Q
be the
k\times m
matrix whose rows are
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
so that the projection matrix onto the linear span of
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
is
Q(QQ^{T})^{-1}Q^{T}=Q^{T}M^{-1}Q
. Our assumption that
\xi_{i}(u)=1
implies that
e_{u}Q^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the all-ones vector. Hence

\mathbb{P}^{W}(u\in\mathcal{W})\geq e_{u}Q^{T}M^{-1}Qe_{u}^{T}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}(M^{-1})_{i,j}\,.

∎

The following corollary will be useful.

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{W}
be the determinantal probability measure associated with a subspace
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\mathcal{W}\subseteq[m]
be a sample of
\mathbb{P}^{W}
. Let
u\in[m]
and
\gamma\in(0,{1\over k})
be fixed, where
k\in[m]
. Suppose that
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\in W
are vectors satisfying

(i)

|\xi_{i}(u)|=1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and,

(ii)

|\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle|\leq\gamma\min(\|\xi_{i}\|^{2},\|\xi_{j}\|^{2})
for all
i\neq j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Then

\mathbb{P}^{W}(u\in\mathcal{W})\geq\left(1-\frac{\gamma k}{1-\gamma k}\right)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}}\,.

Proof.

Let
M
be the Gram matrix of
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
, denote by
D
the diagonal matrix with entries
D_{i,i}=M_{i,i}=\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}
and put
R=I-D^{-1}M
. The second assumption implies that
|R_{i,j}|\leq\gamma
for all
i,j\in[k]
(since
R_{i,i}=0
). By induction it follows that for any integer
\ell\geq 1
we have
|(R^{\ell})_{i,j}|\leq\gamma^{\ell}k^{\ell-1}
since

|(R^{\ell})_{i,j}|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{k}|R_{i,t}||(R^{\ell-1})_{t,j}|\,.

Therefore
\tilde{R}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}R^{\ell}
converges in every coordinate and the upper bound above implies that
|\tilde{R}_{i,j}|\leq{\gamma\over 1-\gamma k}
. The matrix
I+\tilde{R}
is the inverse of
I-R=D^{-1}M
, hence
M
is invertible, so
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
are independent and
M^{-1}=(I+\tilde{R})D^{-1}
.
By
Lemma

2.13

\mathbb{P}^{W}(u\in\mathcal{W})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tilde{R}_{j,i}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{M_{i,i}}\,,

and the proof is concluded since

\tilde{R}_{i,j}\geq-{\gamma\over 1-\gamma k}
.
∎

Remark 2.15
.

Let us explain why
Lemma

2.13
extends the classical Nash-Williams inequality from electric networks theory (see
[
14
, Chapter 2]
) stating that if
x\neq y
are two vertices of a graph
G
and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
are disjoint edge sets each separating
x
from
y
, then the effective resistance from
x
to
y
is bounded below by
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{1\over|C_{i}|}
. Using Kirchoff’s theorem and the parallel law of electric networks (see
[
14
, Chapter 2]
and
[
14
, Chapter 4]
), it is easy to see that this is equivalent to the following statement about a uniform spanning tree
\mathcal{T}
of a finite connected graph
G
: if
e=(x,y)
is an edge of
G
and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
are edge sets each separating
x
from
y
so that
C_{i}\cap C_{j}=\{e\}
for all
i\neq j
, then,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(e\in\mathcal{T})\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{S}\right)^{-1}\quad\text{where}\quad S=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{|C_{i}|-1}\,,

where we interpret
1/S=0
in the case that
C_{i}=\{e\}
for some
i
(in this case
G\setminus\{e\}
is disconnected and
e\in\mathcal{T}
with probability
1
).

Let show how to prove (
2.14
) using the method of
Corollary

2.14
. In this case the corresponding subspace
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
is described in
Section

1.2.1
. Given the edge sets
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
above (
2.14
) we simply take
\xi_{i}
to be the indicator function of
C_{i}
. Then
\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}=|C_{i}|
and
\xi_{i}(e)=1
for all
i
. Furthermore,
\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle=\xi_{i}(e)\xi_{j}(e)=1
for every
i\neq j
since
C_{i}\cap C_{j}=\{e\}
. Let
M
be the Gram matrix of
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
and observe that
M=D+\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{T}
where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the all-ones vector and
D
is the diagonal matrix with entries
|C_{1}|-1,....,|C_{k}|-1
. By
Lemma

2.13
we obtain that

\mathbb{P}(e\in\mathcal{T})\geq\sum_{i,j=0}^{k}(M^{-1})_{i,j}=\mathbf{1}^{T}M^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{T}D^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\right)^{-1}\,,

where the last transition follows directly from the Sherman–Morrison formula (see
[
8
, Section 0.7.4]
). This recovers (
2.14
) since
\mathbf{1}^{T}D^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}1/(|C_{i}|-1)=S.

2.3.3
Proof of
Lemma

2.8

Denote by
N_{G}(v)
the neighbor set of a vertex
v
in the graph
G
and let
(T,o)
be a valid rooted tree.
Conditioned on
U(T)\subset\mathcal{T}
, the event that
B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=T
occurs if and only if for all
v\in I(T)
we have
(N_{G}(v)\setminus U(T))\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
.

Let
U_{0}=\{u\in U(G)\setminus U(T)\mid|N_{G}(u)\cap V(T)|>1\}
, and note that
|U_{0}|\leq|V(T)|^{2}
since every two vertices in
V(G)
have at most one common neighbor in
U(G)
(since
G
is
C_{4}
-free). We write
I(T)=\{v_{1},...,v_{|I(T)|}\}
and for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,|I(T)|\}
let
u_{i,1},....,u_{i,d_{i}}
be the set of vertices
N_{G}(v_{i})\setminus(U(T)\cup U_{0})
, so
d\geq d_{i}\geq d-|V(T)|
since
v_{i}
can share at most one neighbor with each vertex in
V(T)
.

Additionally, for every
1\leq i\leq|I(T)|
and
1\leq j\leq d_{i}
let

U_{i,j}=\bigcup_{i^{\prime}<i}\Big\{u_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}:i^{\prime}<i,j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,d_{i^{\prime}}\}\Big\}\bigcup\Big\{u_{i,j^{\prime}}:j^{\prime}<j\Big\}\,,

that is,
U_{i,j}
are the vertices which precede
u_{i,j}
in lexicographical order.
We have

(2.15)

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=T\mid U(T)\subset\mathcal{T})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{|I(T)|}\prod_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\mathbb{P}(u_{i,j}\notin\mathcal{T}\mid U(T)\subset\mathcal{T},~U_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset)\,.

We now upper bound the probabilities in the right-hand side of (
2.15
) using
Corollary

2.14
.
For every
1\leq i\leq|I(T)|
, we denote
K_{i}=\{v_{1},...,v_{i-1}\}\subset I(T)
and

\beta_{i}:=\frac{m(K_{i},T)}{m(K_{i},T)-m(K_{i+1},T)}\,.

Note that
\beta_{i}
is well defined by
2.10
and that
\beta_{i}>1
. The following lemma upper bounds each term in (
2.15
).

Lemma 2.16
.

For every
1\leq i\leq|I(T)|
and
1\leq j\leq d_{i}
we have

\mathbb{P}(u_{i,j}\in\mathcal{T}\mid U(T)\subset\mathcal{T},~U_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset)\geq\frac{1+O(d^{-1})}{d}\left(k+\left(\beta_{i}-\frac{j}{d}\right)^{-1}\right)\,.

Proof.

Denote by
P:\mathbb{R}^{U}(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{U\setminus U_{i,j}}
denote the orthogonal projection (i.e., coordinate restriction) onto the complement of
U_{i,j}
in
U(G)
. Recall that
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{U(G)}
denotes the row-space of the matrix
B
and consider its subspace

H^{\prime}=\{\psi\in H\mid\psi(u)=0\mbox{ for all $u\in U(T)$}\}\,.

By (
1.8
), the conditional probability measure given
U(T)\subset\mathcal{T},~U_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset

considered in this lemma is the determinantal probability measure associated with the subspace

W=\{P\psi\mid\psi\in H^{\prime}\}\,.

In words, to construct a vector
\xi\in W
, one starts with a vector
\psi\in H
that vanishes on
U(T)
(i.e.,
\psi\in H^{\prime}
) and restricts its coordinates to
U\setminus U_{i,j}
.

Let
N_{G}(u_{i,j})=\{v_{i},w_{1},...,w_{k}\}
be the neighbors of
u_{i,j}
in
G
. Since
u_{i,j}\not\in U_{0}
it follows that
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}\not\in V(T)
. We aim to use
Corollary

2.14
so we now construct
k+1
vectors
\xi_{0},...,\xi_{k}\in W
, where each vector is associated to a different neighbor of
u_{i,j}
.

Firstly, for every
1\leq t\leq k
, let
\psi_{t}
be the
w_{t}
-th row of
B
. If
w_{t}
has a neighbor in
U(T)
, then
w_{t}
must belong to
V(T)
since
T
is valid. Since
w_{t}\not\in V(T)
we deduce that
\psi_{t}\in H^{\prime}
.

Secondly, apply
2.11
with
K=K_{i}
and
J=V(T)\setminus K_{i}
and the vertex
v_{i}\in J
. Note that this partition satisfies the assumption in
2.11
since
m(K_{i},T)\geq m(I(T),T)>0
. Denote by
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(T)}
the vector obtained by
2.11
this way. Namely, we have that (i)
\varphi^{T}D=0
, (ii)
\varphi(v_{i})=1
, and (iii)
\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}=\beta_{i}.
Here
D
is the
V(T)\times U(T)
submatrix of
B
.
Extend
\varphi
to
\tilde{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
by padding zeroes in
V(G)\setminus V(T)
and define
\psi_{0}:=\tilde{\varphi}^{T}\cdot B
. Clearly,
\psi_{0}
is in the row-space
H
of
B
, and since
\varphi^{T}\cdot D=0
it follows that
\psi_{0}\in H^{\prime}
.

We define
\xi_{t}:=P\psi_{t}
for
t=0,...,k
and claim the following:

1.

|\xi_{t}(u_{i,j})|=1
for
0\leq t\leq k.

2.

\|\xi_{t}\|^{2}=(1+O(d^{-1}))d
for
1\leq t\leq k.

3.

\|\xi_{0}\|^{2}=(1+O(d^{-1}))(d\beta_{i}-j).

4.

|\langle\xi_{t},\xi_{s}\rangle|=O(1)
for every
0\leq t<s\leq k
.

By combining these with the facts that
\beta_{i}>1
and
j\leq d
, we find that the squared norms of
\xi_{0},...,\xi_{t}
are of order
d
while the inner-products are of order
1
. Therefore, by
Corollary

2.14
it suffices to prove the above four items.

Firstly, by definition for
1\leq t\leq k
we have
\xi_{t}(u_{i,j})=B(w_{t},u_{i,j})=\pm 1
. Also, for
t=0,

\xi_{0}(u_{i,j})=\psi_{0}(u_{i,j})=(\tilde{\varphi}^{T}B)(u_{i,j})=\varphi(v_{i})\cdot B(v_{i},u_{i,j})=\pm 1\,,

where we used that
\tilde{\varphi}(w_{t})=0
for
1\leq t\leq k
since
w_{t}\notin V(T)
and that
\tilde{\varphi}(v_{i})=\varphi(v_{i})=1
.

Secondly, for
1\leq t\leq k
, we have

\|\xi_{t}\|^{2}=\bigl|N_{G}(w_{t})\setminus U_{i,j}\bigr|,

which is in
[d-|V(T)|,d]
since
w_{t}
can share at most one neighbor with each vertex in
V(T)
(so since
U_{i,j}
are all neighbors of
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\}
the number of neighbors
w_{t}
has in
U_{i,j}
is at most
|V(T)|
).

Thirdly, let
u\in U(G)\setminus(U_{i,j}\cup U_{0})
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}
is supported on
V(T)
and
u
has no neighbors in
K_{i}
(since
u\not\in U_{i,j}
) it follows that if
u
has no neighbors in
J
, then
\xi_{0}(u)=\psi_{0}(u)=0
. Also, since
u\in U_{0}
it can only have more than one neighbor in
J
it only if it belongs to
U(T)
in which case

\xi_{0}(u)=(\tilde{\varphi}^{T}B)(u)=(\varphi^{T}D)(u)=0\,,

where the second equality follows since all the neighbors of
u
belong to
V(T)
, and the last equality by the construction of
\varphi
. Therefore
\xi_{0}(u)\neq 0
implies that it has one neighbor
v\in J
and hence
\xi_{0}(u)=\varphi(v)\cdot B(v,u)=\pm\varphi(v)
. This gives that

\|\xi_{0}\|^{2}=\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}\cdot|N_{G}(v)\setminus(U(T)\cup U_{i,j})|+\sum_{u\in U_{0}}\xi_{0}(u)^{2}\,.

Observe that the second sum bounded by
|U_{0}|(k+1)^{2}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}^{2}=O(1)
and that

|N_{G}(v)\setminus(U(T)\cup U_{i,j})|=\left\{\begin{matrix}d-j-O(1)&v=v_{i}\\
d-O(1)&v\in J\setminus\{v_{i}\}\,.\end{matrix}\right.

Indeed, the
O(1)
terms account for neighbors of
v
in
U_{0}\cup U(T)
. Besides these neighbors,
U_{i,j}
contains none of the neighbors of
v
if
v\neq v_{i}
and
j-1
of them if
v=v_{i}
. We derive that

\|\xi_{0}\|^{2}=\sum_{v\in J\setminus\{v_{i}\}}\varphi(v)^{2}(d-O(1))+\varphi(v_{i})^{2}\cdot(d-j-O(1))+O(1)=(1+O(d^{-1}))(d\beta_{i}-j)\,,

where the last equality follows from
\sum_{v\in J}\varphi(v)^{2}=\beta_{i}
and
\varphi(v_{i})=1
.

Lastly, if
1\leq t<s\leq k
then

|\langle\xi_{t},\xi_{s}\rangle|=1
since
w_{t}
and
w_{s}
share exactly one common neighbor, namely
u_{i,j}
. In addition, if
1\leq t\leq k
then

|\langle\xi_{0},\xi_{t}\rangle|\leq\sum_{u\in N_{G}(w_{t})\setminus U_{i,j}}|\xi_{0}(u)|\,,

since
\xi_{t}
is supported on
N_{G}(w_{t})\setminus U_{i,j}
and has sup norm at most
1
.
Recall that if
u\notin U_{i,j}\cup U_{0}
then
\xi_{0}(u)\neq 0
only if
u
has a unique neighbor
v\in J
, and then
|\xi_{0}(u)|=|\varphi(v)|.
Since
w_{t}
has at most one common neighbor with every
v\in J
, we find that

|\langle\xi_{0},\xi_{t}\rangle|\leq\sum_{v\in J}|\varphi(v)|+\sum_{u\in U_{0}}|\xi_{0}(u)|=O(1)\,,

since
|J|=O(1)
and
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=O(1)
and
\|\xi_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq(k+1)\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=O(1)
and
|U_{0}|=O(1)
.
∎

To conclude the proof of
Lemma

2.8
, we combine
Lemma

2.16
, (
2.15
) and
1-x\leq e^{-x}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=T\mid U(T)\subset\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(1+O(d^{-1}))\sum_{i=1}^{|I(T)|}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\left(k+\left(\beta_{i}-\frac{j}{d}\right)^{-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(1+O(d^{-1}))\sum_{i=1}^{|I(T)|}k+\log\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}\right)\right)\,,

where the last equality follows from the approximation

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\beta-j/d)^{-1}=(1+O(d^{-1}))\log\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}\right)

for every real
\beta>1
, and from
d_{i}=d-O(1)
, which implies that
any errors can be absorbed into the
O(d^{-1})
error term. Note that
\beta_{i}/(\beta_{i}-1)=m(K_{i},T)/m(K_{i+1},T)
whence

\sum_{i=1}^{|I(T)|}\log\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}\right)=\log\left(\frac{m(\emptyset,T)}{m(I(T),T)}\right)\,.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=T\mid U(T)\subset\mathcal{T})\leq(1+O(d^{-1}))\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{m(\emptyset,T)}

which concludes the proof of
Lemma

2.8
. ∎

2.4
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

Recall the parameters
\varepsilon,\delta
defined in (
2.1
) and the subset

defined in
Definition

2.1
. By combining
Lemma

2.7
and

2.8
we obtain the following.

Corollary 2.17
.

Let
(T,o)
be a valid rooted finite tree which is a subgraph of
G
and
o\in V
. Suppose that
U(T)\cap U_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\emptyset
and that

, then

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(o,r)=(T,o))\leq(1+o(1))e^{-k|I(T)|}m(I(T),T)d^{-|U(T)|}\,.

Therefore, to prove
Theorem

1.2
we need to count the number of finite rooted trees that are subgraphs of
G
and are isomorphic as rooted graphs to a given abstract finite rooted trees
(T,o)
. The following counting argument is due to Mészáros
[
15
, Lemma 3.10]
; we provide its proof in our setup here (in
[
15
]
the setup is for determinantal hypertrees
Section

1.2.2
of the complete complex only). Given a finite rooted tree
(T,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
and a vertex

of the underlying graph
(V,U,E)
we define

\mathcal{I}((T,o);v)=\Big\{T^{\prime}\mid(T^{\prime},v)\cong(T,o),\,(T^{\prime},v)\textrm{ is a subgraph of }G\Big\}\,.

Lemma 2.18
.

For any finite rooted tree
(T,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
and any

we have

{|\mathcal{I}((T,o);v)|\over d^{|U(T)|}}\leq{(k!)^{|U(T)|}\over|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\,.

Proof.

Consider a fixed arbitrary labeling of the vertices of
T
and an embedding

of
(T,o)
into
G
so that

; by embedding we mean that

preserves adjacency and non-adjacency. The number of such

’s is thus precisely
|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)||\mathcal{I}((T,o);v)|
. This number is can be easily upper bounded inductively. The root

must be mapped to
v
. Next, if the root
o
has degree
r
, there are at most

ways of embedding the neighbors of

into vertices

of

. Next, each of the
r
neighbors of

in

have precisely
k
children so the embedding

must embed these children into each of the
k
neighbors of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
that are not
v
(note that we do not rule out the invalid possibility that these
r
sets of
k
neighbors intersect; this is fine since we are only proving an upper bound) and due to the labeling we have
(k!)^{r}
such possibilities. We proceed iteratively and obtain an upper bound of precisely
d^{|U(T)|}(k!)^{|U(T)|}
.
∎

Let
v_{n},v_{n}^{\prime}
denote two independent uniform vertices from
V_{n}
which are independent from
\mathcal{T}
. Denote by
E
(resp.
E^{\prime}
) the event that
B_{\mathcal{T}}(v_{n},r)\cong(T,o)
(resp.
B_{\mathcal{T}}(v_{n}^{\prime},r)\cong(T,o)
). We have that

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}^{2}/|V_{n}|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{P}(E\cap E^{\prime}),

and we bound this probability by considering the following cases. First, by
Theorem

2.2
, the probability that one of the
r
-balls around
v_{n}
or
v_{n}^{\prime}
touch
U_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is negligible. Second, by (
2.8
) the probability that the balls of radii
2r
around
v_{n}
in
G[\mathcal{F}]
and
G[\mathcal{T}]
are different is also negligible. Therefore

\mathbb{P}(B_{\mathcal{T}}(v_{n},r)\cap B_{\mathcal{T}}(v_{n}^{\prime},r)\neq\emptyset)=\mathbb{P}(v_{n}^{\prime}\in B_{\mathcal{F}}(v_{n},2r))+o(1)=|V_{n}|^{-1}\mathbb{E}[|B_{\mathcal{F}}(v_{n},2r)|]+o(1)\,

which is negligible since the expectation of
|B_{\mathcal{F}}(v_{n},2r)|
is bounded as
n\to\infty
by (
2.6
). In conclusion, using
Lemma

2.18
and
Lemma

2.19
we find that, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}(E\cap E^{\prime})\leq\left(\frac{e^{-k|I(T)|}(k!)^{|U(T)|}m(I(T),T)}{|\mathrm{Aut}(T,o)|}\right)^{2}+o(1)\,,

whence
\mbox{Var}(Y_{n}/|V_{n}|)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and
Theorem

1.3
follows by Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎
