Debris flow disaster information representation and perception based on knowledge graphs and virtual geographic environments
Authors: Zhiyuan Zhang, Haohai Fu, Jiquan Zhang, Yichen Zhang, Zhongyuan Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, virtual geographic environments have played a crucial role in enhancing public risk perception through disaster simulations. Previous studies, such as Zhu et al.1, have proposed knowledge-driven visualization frameworks, offering valuable insights into public risk perception. Building on this foundation, this paper further focuses on the automated construction of knowledge organization and cross-platform adaptive presentation to better meet the public’s needs for understanding and participation in settings without professional guidance. This framework begins with a thorough analysis of the public’s specific needs for disaster visualization, using large language model (LLM) to extract triples and construct a detailed knowledge graph containing disaster geographic information. Under this knowledge framework, we realized precise modeling of virtual scenes and context-adaptive representation based on the theory of virtual geographic environments (VGEs). Then, we designed a cross-platform data organization and dynamic scheduling algorithm to enable content presentation across diverse devices. Finally, we conducted three groups of experiments using typical disaster cases, with user cognition assessed via eye-tracking. The experiment results indicate our method supports adaptive, smooth visualization on multiple platforms effectively. Compared to traditional approaches, it significantly improves debris flow disaster information dissemination efficiency by integrating scene context, user interest, demand response and spatial intelligence, offering advantages in standardized modeling, personalization and adaptive optimization, thereby enhancing public debris flow disaster information perception.Keywords: Information representation and perception, Knowledge graph, Large language model, Virtual geographic environmentsSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Natural hazards

Introduction

Global climate change has exacerbated the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events and natural disasters. According to data from the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR), there have been a number of major disaster events around the world in recent years, such as the 2010 Haiti earthquake, the 2011 Japan tsunami, and the 2017 Caribbean hurricanes2. These disasters have caused huge loss of lives and economic impacts, and brought a great influence to the society, economy and ecological environment. How to enhance public’s awareness of disasters, identify risk and build an effective social prevention and mitigation mechanism of disaster is one of the key problems in facing the challenges of disasters3.

With the rapid development of geographic information technology, especially the popularization and application of geographic information system (GIS), remote sensing (RS), global positioning system (GPS), the ability to obtain disaster information has been greatly improved. At the same time, with the development of 3D modeling technology, virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) technology, the ability to process and express information has also been improved4, further improved the visualization and interactivity methods of geographic information laid a solid technical basis for the construction of Virtual Geographic Environments (VGEs)5. At present, VGEs have been widely used in urban planning, environmental protection, disaster simulation and other fields, and have shown great potential in the field of disaster management.

VGEs can also assist the public and professionals to better understand the disaster process and possible risks through buildable realistic disaster situations, enhance the awareness of disaster prevention and emergency response ability. For instance, with the simulation of earthquake, flood, fire, etc., VGEs can combine the real-time disaster information and dynamically display the relevant relief information, such as the disaster evolution, rescue path, evacuation route, etc., which helps the people make correct decisions6. Besides, the VGEs system can optimize the allocation of rescue supplies, establish plans for the location of medical facilities and shelters, discover the potential risky areas and prepare for the response plans. Therefore, it can avoid the waste of resources and redundant construction. More importantly, VGEs provide a unified information platform for cross-departmental collaboration, offering functions such as integrating, processing, and analyzing disaster data7, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency of disaster response8.

Although VGEs have great potential for application in disaster management, they still encounter numerous challenges during their actual application. The existing systems still have many deficiencies in aspects such as public participation, personalized information presentation, and cross-platform adaptability, making it difficult to meet the diverse needs of different users. Therefore, in order to enhance the effectiveness of disaster risk communication and the public’s disaster awareness, it is urgent to build a new generation of VGE system that integrates disaster knowledge, user cognitive characteristics and interaction technology9,10.

This paper proposed and constructed a knowledge-driven 3D disaster scene visualization system aiming at overcoming the limitations of existing technologies in standardized modeling, personalized experiences and adaptive optimization. By developing the disaster information visualization system and combining it with eye-tracking technology to conduct user cognitive assessments, the methods show effectiveness and innovative value in improving public disaster awareness and behavioral responses. Related Works

Knowledge graph construction

In disaster scenario visualization, disaster information is characterized by multiple sources, heterogeneous data, and dynamic updates. Traditional visualization methods typically present information in unstructured formats, such as text, images, or videos, and lack a systematic organization of the intrinsic relationships among disaster elements. This approach makes it difficult for the public to quickly grasp key risk factors and their interrelationships when faced with complex disaster information, thereby affecting the efficiency and accuracy of risk perception. In the field of time-space data processing, continuous learning technology has made certain progress, but its application in disaster information visualization and public awareness enhancement is still relatively limited11. To address this issue, this study introduces knowledge graph technology, aiming to organize disaster geographic information in a structured manner, clarify the semantic relationships among disaster events, geographic entities, and environmental factors, and provide the public with a more intuitive, systematic, and easily understandable way of presenting information.

Based on the above considerations, this section outlines methods related to knowledge graph construction. Traditional knowledge graph construction methods primarily rely on manual rule design, structured data extraction, and supervised learning models (such as BiLSTM-CRF12, They are built incrementally through multi-module processes including entity recognition, relation extraction, and knowledge fusion. While this approach offers high controllability and accuracy, it also has significant limitations: it requires extensive manual annotation and expert involvement, resulting in long construction cycles and high costs, making it difficult to adapt to open domains and dynamic update requirements13,14.

In traditional disaster data processing and analysis, deep learning methods (such as CNN, RNN, etc.) have been widely applied to feature extraction and prediction tasks of spatiotemporal data to address complex spatiotemporal dependencies15. However, these methods mostly focus on the bottom-level numerical prediction and pattern recognition, often lacking high-level semantic understanding and interpretability, and thus are difficult to directly meet the public’s intuitive and logical cognitive needs for disaster knowledge. To address this issue, knowledge graph technology has attracted attention due to its structured knowledge organization and semantic expression capabilities. Early research focused on extracting knowledge from structured and semi-structured resources, relying on ontologies and manual templates to ensure semantic consistency16. With advancements in natural language processing and deep learning technologies, knowledge graph construction has gradually shifted from ontology-based and rule-driven frameworks to data-driven and automated approaches14. In recent years, neural networks, supervised learning, and open-domain information extraction techniques have enabled systems to directly discover entities and relationships from large-scale unstructured text17.

These methods significantly reduce manual effort and expand knowledge coverage, yet they still exhibit limitations in semantic coherence and contextual reasoning, restricting their application in dynamic or open environment18.

Disaster scenario modeling

Traditional modeling methods primarily based on historical statistical data and simplified physical models (such as Manning’s formula). These methods typically involve collecting multi-source data including historical disaster records, rainfall amounts, and river water levels, then integrating them with two-dimensional Geographic Information Systems (GIS) for spatial analysis to generate static disaster risk maps19. In practical applications, researchers have employed these methods to assess risks, analyze disaster intensity, and predict potential impact areas for various hazards including floods, debris flows, and landslides20. For instance, some researcher created flood inundation risk maps using historical hydrological data and topographic information to inform urban flood prevention planning21. Other research employed simplified physical models to analyze debris flow paths and impact zones, providing decision support for mountainous disaster prevention and mitigation22. Additionally, traditional modeling methods have yielded results in disaster grading, vulnerability assessment, and infrastructure layout optimization23.

However, these approaches also exhibit multiple limitations: First, they provide only static risk assessments, failing to capture the dynamic evolution of disasters over time. Second, lacking three-dimensional modeling capabilities, they struggle to accurately represent disaster propagation in complex terrains, limiting their ability to analyze interactions with factors like building structures and topographical variations. Third, traditional approaches primarily rely on data tables and two-dimensional maps, lacking modeling of semantic relationships between disaster elements. This hinders logical reasoning and intelligent decision support. Finally, these methods have limited capabilities for integrating multi-source heterogeneous data, making it difficult to incorporate real-time monitoring data or unstructured information like social media. This ultimately impacts the timeliness and accuracy of disaster emergency management.

Multi-source data fusion of VGEs

In recent years, with the rise of concepts such as digital twins and the metaverse, Virtual Geographic Environments (VGEs) have garnered significant attention as crucial platforms for simulating, analyzing, and supporting decision-making regarding complex geographic phenomena24. Previous research has primarily focused on high-precision 3D geometric modeling and visualization. By constructing photorealistic terrain, buildings, and disaster scenarios, these efforts have enabled intuitive displays of geospatial information and partial dynamic simulation. Additionally, some works have attempted to simulate disaster processes using physical models to support applications such as urban planning, emergency management, and environmental monitoring18.

However, existing VGEs still have numerous shortcomings. First, in terms of visualization and interaction, most systems lack cross-device adaptability, struggling to deliver consistent, smooth rendering experiences across high-performance desktop and lightweight mobile platforms. This hinders multi-device collaboration and results in fragmented user experiences. Meanwhile, some applications still fall short in areas such as visual realism, dynamic environment simulation, and natural interaction, limiting the immersive experience for users. Second, regarding data and cognition, existing technologies overly emphasize the detailed reconstruction of geometric forms while failing to deeply capture the semantic attributes and dynamic relationships of geographic objects8. Furthermore, due to inconsistent data formats and weak system integration, VGEs suffer from insufficient interoperability, hindering the effectiveness of true understanding of geographic phenomena.

In response to the core bottlenecks of dynamic simulation, semantic understanding, and cross-platform performance in current disaster VGEs, this paper proposes a systematic solution. By constructing knowledge graph, rich semantic attributes are assigned to geographical and disaster entities in the three-dimensional scene and their relationships are established, achieving semantic modeling of the scene. Advanced fluid physical simulation equations (such as shallow water equations) are integrated to replace the old simplified models, enabling high-fidelity numerical simulation of the dynamic evolution process of disasters, upgrading VGEs from static snapshots to dynamic simulation platforms. A cross-platform visualization engine that deeply integrates information classification and LOD technology is constructed, and strategies such as quadtree indexing and lazy loading are adopted for performance optimization to ensure efficient and smooth interaction experiences on various terminals. A visualization decision system that is easy to express disaster information is built, enabling intuitive disaster situation awareness and assisting scientific decision-making.

Methodologies

Framework overview

Figure 2 illustrates the framework for disaster information visualization, comprising four modules. Starting from user perception requirements, it utilizes large language models to construct disaster knowledge graphs. Guided by disaster knowledge, it integrates physical simulation theory to achieve debris flow fluid simulation. Simultaneously, leveraging virtual geographic environments technology, it merges disaster entity models with corresponding scene models of the surrounding environment to realize three-dimensional visualization of complex disaster scenarios. Furthermore, it enables adaptive expression and interaction tailored to different user groups and application scenarios.

Disaster scenario information visualization framework.

First, by identifying different user types and their requirements for disaster visualization, combined with basic disaster scenario data, an analysis is conducted. Next, based on the disaster ontology, a knowledge graph is constructed, including defining conceptual relationships, integrating multi-source data, and forming a triplet structure of “entity-semantic relationship-entity”. Subsequently, disaster scenario modeling is performed by combining multi-semantic constraints such as spatial topology, attribute information, and spatial direction. Dynamic simulation of the disaster is conducted using the shallow water equations and the Semi-Lagrangian method. Finally, disaster information is classified and organized, a quad-tree spatial index is established, and a diverse user interface is provided to ensure effective information transmission and application. Adaptive scheduling is performed for different terminal users, and the final results are output.

Public visualization requirement analysis

Different user groups have significantly different needs for visualization. Based on their roles and purposes, we categorize users into the following groups: disaster-affected persons, the general public, rescue personnel, and decision-makers. For the general public, the primary concern is the impact of the disaster on their lives, such as the safety of buildings, evacuation routes, and the locations of shelters. They hope to understand the scope and intensity of the disaster through visualization and learn how to protect themselves and their families. Disaster-affected individuals, on the other hand, are more concerned about their own safety, the location of rescue teams, and the availability of relief supplies. They need to know how to obtain assistance and the progress of rescue operations. Rescue personnel and decision-makers require detailed disaster information, such as the topography and buildings in the affected areas. They also need to know the locations and task assignments of other rescue teams to cooperative action. With decision-makers, it is necessary to develop scientific and comprehensive emergency response measures25.

In addition to analyzing the needs of different user groups, the following key visualization requirements should also be meted. (1) Multi-dimensional information display. Disaster information involves multiple dimensions such as time, geographical location, disaster type, and severity. Visualization tools should be able to integrate this information to comprehensively display the scope of the disaster’s impact and its evolving trends26. (2) Realism. The realism of the scenario to attract users’ attention and lower the user visualization’s accessibility. Realistic visual effects help users better understand and remember the information. (3) Timeliness. Disaster information should be timely released and the disaster scenarios could be quickly constructed when users viewed the information. Especially for emergency situations timeliness is very important27. (4) Understandability. To assist non-professional users to understand the disaster information, the information visualization should be intuitive and easy to understand. By using charts, maps and other easy to understand symbols, complex data and relationships can be displayed. (5) Interactivity. It refers to providing a variety of interactive tools that support communication and collaboration among users and promote the dissemination of disaster knowledge28. (6) Personalization. It refers to providing personalized visualization settings according to different users’ requirements. (7) Cross-platform compatibility. The system should run smoothly on multiple platforms, including desktop computers, mobile devices, and other commonly used devices, ensuring users can access visualization information at any time and from anywhere27.

Geographical knowledge graph construction

Data collection

Data collection serves as the foundational step in constructing a knowledge graph for debris flow disasters, with its quality and diversity directly influencing the accuracy of subsequent analysis and knowledge extraction. There are two main data sources for this study. One is literature data, primarily obtained through advanced search in academic databases such as Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com), using keyword combinations such as “debris flow disasters,” “disaster perception” and “disaster response” to retrieve a number of relevant literatures. The other is publicly reported data, primarily collected from the Weibo platform (https://www.weibo.com), by precisely setting keywords such as “debris flow visualization” and “desired mudslide data display,” to extract relevant posts, comments, and topic discussions.

Data cleansing

Before performing large language model extraction, we need to process the collected data. The first step is to remove irrelevant information. For Weibo data, we excluded advertising promotions and user chatter that are unrelated to the research topic. For literature data, we filtered out background descriptions and irrelevant cases that are unrelated to the core research direction. Additionally, regular expressions and specific algorithms are employed to comprehensively remove irrelevant characters mixed in the data, including special symbols, garbled characters, and unnecessary information such as HTML tags and formatting markers that may remain in literature, thereby achieving preliminary data purification. Furthermore, duplicate data is eliminated to ensure data uniqueness and validity29. Research indicates that deduplicating training data can significantly reduce the model’s “memory-based output,” improving accuracy and training efficiency. For example, in training data for language model, removing duplicate content can reduce the proportion of replicated training data in model output by up to 10 times30. Finally, the processed data is uniformly converted to PDF format for subsequent model processing and knowledge extraction tasks.

This method helps to clearly define the characteristics and functions of each type of object31.

Extraction of triples based on LLM

The construction of knowledge graphs is based on massive amounts of knowledge data, which include sources such as Weibo data and relevant literature data. Due to the heterogeneous, inconsistent, and incomplete nature of these data, previous work33 proposed a series of solutions to address this issue. Building on this, this paper proposes a method for constructing a Geographical Knowledge Graph from multi-source data.

To address public demand for disaster scenario visualization and domain-specific knowledge, we fine-tuned a large language model to enhance its knowledge graph completion and reasoning capabilities. Specifically, we selected Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as the base model and employed Lora (Low-Rank Adaptation) for efficient parameter fine-tuning34. The training was conducted over 3 epochs with a maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens. A micro-batch size of 2 combined with 8-step gradient accumulation yielded a global batch size of 16. The AdamW optimizer was used with Beta1 = 0.9, Beta2 = 0.999, and Epsilon=1e-8, without weight decay. The initial learning rate was set to 2e-5 with a cosine-smoothed annealing scheduler. Lora was applied to all linear layers, with rank 8, scaling factor 16, and a dropout rate of 0.1. The entire process ran at BF16 precision to balance computational efficiency and numerical stability.

Finally, we input the cleaned Weibo data and literature data into the fine-tuned large language model. The LLM automatically extracted entities and relationships by understanding the content of the text, inferred possible triples that may appear in the given text, and output the triples using a template as shown in Formula 1.

Building ontology-based knowledge graphs

Although LLMs are efficient and fast in triples extraction, the complexity of natural language, the specialized nature of domain knowledge, and limitations in model training can lead to issues such as inconsistent formats, duplicates, and semantic ambiguity in the extracted triples35. Furthermore, the direct use of large models may also encounter problems such as hallucinations, and there is a potential risk of generating content that does not match the facts or is unreliable36. Therefore, manual review is necessary to standardize the format of extracted triples, identify duplicates or synonymous information, resolve semantic ambiguities, and lay a solid foundation for the precise application of knowledge graphs37.

This paper uses the debris flow ontology as a conceptual template, extracting 32 entity nodes and 169 relationships. We chose the Neo4j graph database as the storage and management platform for the knowledge graph. During the data storage phase, based on the classes and relationships defined in the ontology model, the extracted triples are accurately mapped to the corresponding nodes and edges. These entities and their semantic relationships are stored in the database, thereby constructing a knowledge graph system that comprehensively reflects the characteristics of debris flow, as shown in Fig. 4. To meet the needs of different user groups, this study focuses on the public’s perception of disasters as its core objective, while also addressing the information needs of rescue personnel and decision-makers. We have optimized the system design (as shown in Fig. 5), to ensure the accuracy and completeness of the knowledge graph, intuitively illustrating the relationships between user types (such as decision-makers, researchers, and emergency responders) and the corresponding entities in the disaster scenario. We believe that enhancing public disaster awareness must rely on a closed-loop information flow with professional emergency response teams. Therefore, our knowledge graph and visualization platform primarily targets the general public, while also providing specialized information layers for rescue personnel and decision-makers as secondary beneficiaries. This enables each user group to quickly search for and obtain the information they need based on their specific requirements, thereby significantly improving the efficiency and effectiveness of disaster prevention, assessment, and response38.

Physical simulation representation

In the process of constructing disaster scenarios, it is often necessary to integrate scene object models from different data sources and fully consider their spatial relationships. To this end, the raw data must first be systematically cleaned to remove irrelevant, outdated, or redundant information. Based on this, the paper proposes semantic constraints, including spatial direction, attribute features, spatial topological structure and optimal scale to reasonably limit and optimize the key parameters of object position, size and direction of scene39.

Furthermore, leveraging knowledge graph applications, this paper can intelligently filter disaster-related object data based on users’ differentiated needs, providing high-quality, efficient data support for subsequent modeling. Simultaneously, it can rationally arrange the modeling sequence of various objects according to the associative relationships within the knowledge graph, effectively enhancing overall modeling efficiency and logical coherence40.

In fluid modeling, the industry commonly employs the shallow water equations combined with the semi-Lagrangian method to simulate and analyze disasters involving large-scale terrain displacement. The shallow water equations comprise the mass conservation equation and the momentum conservation equation. These equations describe the dynamic evolution of the fluid by determining flow velocities and water levels at different geographic locations, thereby revealing fluid behavior in complex terrain disasters41. This information provides a scientific basis for adjusting the position and orientation of the fluid’s three-dimensional model. The computed physical parameters can be directly input as attribute information into a 3D visualization system, enabling intuitive visual feedback.

In order to simulate the spatiotemporal evolution of the debris flow more accurately, we have also expended a lot of energy on the color design of virtual scenes. For instance, the brown color of the mudslide is consistent with people’s intuition; the damaged buildings are simplified into block models and marked with red, yellow, and green colors to represent the emergency warning levels of red (dangerous), yellow (moderate), and green (safe). This traffic light color representation method conforms to the intuition of visual perception45. In addition, the safety status of roads is also marked with colors: the roads in good condition are marked with green, with certain risks are marked with yellow, with serious damage are marked with red. This standardized color representation method also improves the recognizability of map symbols46. With this meticulous design, not only can the public understand the disaster situation quickly and easily according to their intuition, but it also enables comprehensive representation of the disaster site in cases of limited disaster data, which improves the applicability and reliability of the system47. Compared to other systems, our system not only enhances the efficiency of disaster information dissemination but also improves the scientific rigor of emergency decision-making.

Graded display and dynamic detail optimization strategy

Given the practical application of dealing with debris flow, in order to enhance the information transmission efficiency and system response ability, we use information importance grading and level of detail (LOD) technology to build an information visualization system with clear content hierarchy, efficient visual display, and smooth interaction experience48.

At the content level, we categorize disaster-related information into four types: scene information, simulation information, symbolic information, and textual information, as shown in the Fig. 8. Scene information serves as the foundational layer, encompassing basic environmental information about the disaster-affected area. While it does not directly reflect the severity of the disaster, it establishes the foundational framework for in-depth analysis of the disaster, serving as the cornerstone for the analysis and application of information at all subsequent levels. Building upon this foundation, we leverage scenario information and relevant data models to simulate the dynamic progression of disasters—such as their spread and intensity changes—thereby providing robust support for the dynamic presentation of disaster information. This enables response strategies can be timelier and more targeted. Symbolic information further enhances the efficiency of information transmission. It conveys specific disaster-related instructions or warnings in a highly condensed form, presenting complex disaster information succinctly. Compared to text, it can be quickly captured and understood by users, playing a critical role in guiding users swiftly during emergencies to meet the urgent needs of disaster response49. The textual information is at the highest level and can provide the most detailed and accurate core information about the disaster, including the specific nature and severity of the disaster, etc. The four types of information complement each other, providing in-depth and accurate basis for disaster management decisions50,51.

Information classification chart.

Additionally, we have introduced a LOD loading mechanism, which combines information display technology with data rendering strategies, achieving unified processing of the information hierarchy and data presentation strategies.

For lower-level information (such as scene information), low-resolution models are preloaded to ensure rapid system startup and reserve space for subsequent high-precision data loading. Along with the user’s focus shifts or the viewing distance decreases, higher-resolution terrain and building models are gradually loaded to meet the display requirements for disaster simulation information and symbolic information. When in-depth analysis or critical decision-making is required, the system automatically switches to the highest-level textual information and corresponding high-precision 3D models to ensure information integrity and visual accuracy52.

Furthermore, the system can efficiently manage large-scale geographic data through quad-tree spatial partitioning and dynamically loading disaster entity at different LOD levels based on the user’s perspective. For resources outside the field of view, a lazy-loading mechanism is implemented to effectively avoid main thread blocking and improve overall response speed53.

Finally, considering the diversity of rescue personnel’s devices, the system adopts a responsive design to ensure that information at all levels is clearly and accurately presented across different terminals, further enhancing the system’s accessibility and practicality54.

Experiments and analysis

Preparation of the experiments

To evaluate the effectiveness and innovation of the work presented in this paper, we designed two sets of experiments. One set was used to compare and validate the efficiency of the 3D visualization system in conveying and perceiving disaster information; the other set was used to validate the system’s flexibility and fluidity in expressing disaster information.

Disaster event selection and visualization

To validate the effectiveness of the framework we developed in enhancing public awareness of disasters, we selected Guzan Town in Kangding City, Sichuan Province, as the study area, as shown in Fig. 9. Located in the upper reaches of the Dadu River basin, the region is characterized by high mountains and deep valleys, resulting in complex topography. Annual precipitation in the area is primarily concentrated between June and September. On August 3, 2024, the region experienced a severe flash flood and debris flow disaster. Given the weak infrastructure and limited disaster prevention capabilities in some rural residential areas, this event holds significant research value. Based on this context, we first constructed a knowledge graph of the disaster event as shown in Fig. 4. Subsequently, leveraging physical simulation and virtual geographic environment technology, we developed a three-dimensional disaster information visualization system, with its representation results depicted in Fig. 6. First, the disaster report text is used as the raw input data. A fine-tuned large language model is employed to automatically extract entities, relationships, and attributes related to the disaster event from the report, generating structured triples (head entity – relationship – tail entity). That is, by leveraging its powerful reasoning ability, the unstructured text is transformed into a structured knowledge graph56. This step supports batch processing of multiple reports, significantly improving the efficiency of knowledge extraction.

Subsequently, the triples generated by the model undergo manual cleaning and verification. This process includes: removing duplicate or redundant triples, correcting misidentified entity types and relationship labels, supplementing missing key information, and standardizing entity naming conventions. This step aims to ensure the accuracy, consistency, and completeness of the triples, laying a solid foundation for subsequent knowledge fusion.

Finally, based on a pre-built disaster domain knowledge graph, the cleaned triples are aligned and fused with existing entities in the graph. Entity linking techniques are employed to determine whether newly extracted entities correspond to the same real-world objects as existing entities. Consequently, operations such as entity merging, relationship supplementation, or attribute updates are performed. The updated knowledge graph is shown in Fig. 11. This ultimately achieves the seamless integration of new knowledge into the knowledge graph, thereby enriching the graph’s coverage and reasoning capabilities.

Experimental process and perceptual materials

This study recruited a total of 120 participants from Changchun Institute of Technology, including 64 men (53.3%) and 56 women (46.7%). To enhance the diversity of the sample, we expanded the initial participant pool of 50 by adding another 70 participants, resulting in a final sample of 120 participants. The combined sample consisted of 72 students (42 undergraduates and 30 graduate students), 24 full-time faculty members, 14 administrative staff, and 10 other staff members. The age range of the participants was 18–55 years (M = 29.4, SD = 8.7). In terms of academic background, 58 participants (48.3%) had a background in science and engineering, while 62 (51.7%) had a background in non-science and engineering disciplines.

All participants were frequent daily media users, spending more than 2 h per day using media for entertainment purposes (M = 3.5 h, SD = 1.3). All participants viewed the same media content in a standardized laboratory setting, and their eye-tracking data during viewing was recorded using eye-tracking equipment. This method allows for the quantification of the effectiveness of different media formats in attracting and sustaining visual attention. Qualitative analysis of information perception

To ensure comparability among the animation group, image group, and text group in terms of information quantity and cognitive load, this study standardized the experimental materials during the design phase. Specifically, all three sets of materials were based on the same debris flow disaster event, covering the same time sequence, disaster evolution process, and core information elements. The content for the image group and text group was extracted from the animation group: the image group used key frames from the animation as static stimuli, while the text group converted the core information of the animation into written descriptions. The presentation time for all three sets of materials was uniformly set to 60 s, a duration determined based on a pilot study. The pilot study results showed that participants could fully view the animation, image, or text content within an average of 45–55 s. The 60-second setting ensured that participants had sufficient time to comprehend the information while avoiding potential fatigue effects caused by excessively long exposure. Through this design, the three sets of materials-maintained consistency in core information content, with differences only in presentation format, and the time condition was also kept uniform, thereby effectively ensuring the validity of between-group comparisons.

As shown in Fig. 13a of the experimental results, the animation group’s performance was particularly outstanding. This color chart clearly illustrates the dynamic shifts in participants’ attention during animation viewing: initial stages showed relatively dispersed attention, but as the animation progressed, attention rapidly concentrated on key information areas, revealing a distinct focusing trend. Particularly during the continuous layering of information, participants’ attention progressively converged on the core severe disaster zones, indicating that the animated format effectively guided visual focus and enhanced the efficiency of identifying critical information. In the animation segment, the heatmap shows attention gradually converging from multiple dispersed areas to the primary impact zone of the landslide disaster, with attention patterns evolving alongside the animation’s progression, enabling a more comprehensive acquisition of disaster-related information. This phenomenon indicates that animations not only effectively capture viewers’ attention but also guide them to focus on the most critical content, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of information transmission.

Experimental results. (a) Heatmap of animation group. (b) Heatmap of image group. (c) Heatmap of text group.

In contrast, the image group and text group exhibit distinct differences in attention allocation patterns. While both can capture audience attention to some extent, they are significantly less effective than the animation group in guiding and sustaining focus on key information. For instance, static images often increase cognitive load due to information overload, whereas animations facilitate better information recall and comprehension by presenting content progressively. In the image group as shown in Fig. 13(b), the heatmap shows that participants’ attention is somewhat concentrated in certain areas but overall distribution is scattered, lacking a sustained and clear focal point. Visual focus is relatively evenly distributed across different areas of the image, indicating that static images have certain limitations in guiding audience gaze and highlighting core information. In the text group as shown in Fig. 13(c), participants’ attention exhibited distinct phased changes: initially, attention was relatively concentrated due to the guidance of the title or opening information; particularly when specific data (such as casualty numbers or economic losses) were involved, attention levels increased. However, as reading progressed, attention gradually weakened and became more dispersed, failing to sustain focus on core content. This reflects the challenges faced by plain text formats in maintaining attention continuity and conveying varying amounts of information.

During the 60-second experimental period, eye-tracking data demonstrate that the animation group has a significant advantage in information transmission efficiency. Compared to the text group (pure text information presentation), the animation group had a shorter time to first fixation (1.2 s vs. 3.5 s), with users’ gaze quickly captured by the red high-risk area in the center of the map, indicating stronger visual saliency in this region. In terms of fixation distribution, users in the animation group generated approximately 165 fixations on average, with 45% of the total fixation time concentrated on the core area of disaster simulation. In contrast, the text group showed a more dispersed fixation distribution, with only 23% of fixations concentrated on the core area. Furthermore, users in the animation group made approximately 4 high-frequency returns to the legend on the right, indicating that they heavily relied on the legend to decode the danger levels represented by the 3D building models during in-depth risk assessment. In contrast, the text group lacked a structured legend and required repeated reading of text descriptions (approximately 6 returns on average). In terms of average fixation duration, the animation group recorded 320 milliseconds, which was significantly shorter than the text group’s 480 milliseconds (p < 0.05), further confirming that users in the animation group were not passively viewing but actively integrating spatial location and disaster attribute information.

Overall, compared to animations, images and text are less effective in guiding visual attention and reinforcing the identification of key information. Clearly, animation, with its dynamic presentation and sequential nature, offers significant advantages in enhancing information delivery efficiency and facilitating rapid absorption of key content.

Performance analysis and quality assessment of knowledge graphs

Performance analysis and quality assessment of knowledge graphs

As shown in Table 3, a comparison of the performance of the three models on the entity extraction task reveals significant differences among the architectures in terms of knowledge alignment and feature extraction capabilities. First, using the unfined-tuned base model (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) as a baseline, its low recall (0.3581) and precision (0.4505) reveal the limitations of general-purpose large models in a zero-shot setting. Due to the lack of instruction alignment with specific extraction formats and entity relationship patterns, its F1 score is only 0.3990, which strongly demonstrates the necessity of task-specific fine-tuning: the model’s built-in knowledge must be aligned with domain-specific data to be transformed into high-precision structured outputs. In terms of training convergence characteristics, the Fine-tuning LLM demonstrates extremely high sample utilization. Benefiting from the deep semantic priors accumulated during the pre-training phase, the model achieves a recall rate of 0.8421 and an F1 score of 0.7948 after fine-tuning—the best performance among the three model groups—validating the efficiency of domain alignment on a large-scale parameter foundation. In contrast, BiLSTM-CRF, as a typical non-pre-trained architecture, exhibits significant convergence lag due to the need to start fitting from a random initial state and the lack of semantic priors. Even after increasing the number of iterations, its F1 score (0.7081) still lags behind the fine-tuned LLM by approximately 8.67% points. The experimental results quantitatively demonstrate that large language models can achieve a superior balance between computational resource investment and knowledge capture precision through an extremely short fine-tuning cycle, making them a more advantageous technical approach for constructing high-quality domain knowledge graphs.

Limitations

The proposed framework has currently only been validated for debris flow disasters, and its applicability to other disaster types, such as earthquakes, floods, and typhoons, requires further investigation. We have added this as a core limitation in the revised manuscript and suggest that future work should conduct cross-type validation across different disaster categories.

Furthermore, when generalizing the findings of this study to the general public, attention must be paid to the following limitations regarding the sample and the experimental setting. With regard to the sample, although this study included participants from various roles on campus (students, faculty, administrative staff, and other staff) and with different educational backgrounds (low education: 10%, intermediate education: 55%, high education: 35%), all 120 participants were drawn from the same university campus. This sampling method may not fully reflect the diversity of the general public in terms of education, occupation, and age. The relatively small proportion of participants with low educational attainment (10%) limits the representativeness of this subgroup, while the absence of participants with no formal education or only elementary school education further limits the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, all participants were frequent daily media users (more than 2 h per day, with an average of 3.5 h), which may differ from low- or moderate-frequency media users in the general public; therefore, these conclusions may not be directly applicable to groups with less media exposure. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 55 years, covering young and middle-aged adults but excluding minors (under 18) and older adults (over 65). Given that visual attention patterns may vary significantly across different age groups, future research should encompass a broader age range, including both younger and older adults.

Regarding the experimental setting, while laboratory conditions ensure internal validity through strict control of variables, they lack the sense of urgency found in the real world. This may lead to discrepancies between participants’ performance in the lab and their actual behavior in real-life disaster scenarios. In real-world disaster scenarios, individuals often face immense physiological and psychological stress and are subject to various interfering factors, such as damaged infrastructure and chaotic environments. These real-world constraints are difficult to fully replicate in a laboratory setting, which may undermine the generalizability and practical applicability of the research findings in actual emergency situations.

Despite these limitations, this study provides reliable experimental evidence supporting the effectiveness of the proposed framework in debris flow disaster scenarios and among young and middle-aged adults. Future research should employ more diverse samples, encompassing different disaster types, age groups, educational backgrounds, and geographic regions, to further validate the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

By deeply understanding the public’s perception preferences regarding disaster visualization and integrating rich disaster geographic user preferences with virtual scene and context-adaptive display technologies, this study proposes and validates a knowledge-driven 3D disaster scene visualization system based on VGEs. This system not only supports multi-platform data organization and dynamic scheduling to ensure a seamless user experience across different computing devices, but also places particular emphasis on standardized modeling, personalized user experiences, and tiered display with dynamic detail optimization strategies. These features significantly enhance the efficiency of disaster information dissemination. By selecting representative disaster cases and employing eye-tracking analysis technology for evaluation, this method has been proven to offer significant advantages in enhancing public risk perception.

The results of this study indicate that by integrating scene context, user preferences, demand response, and spatial intelligence, it is possible to effectively bridge the cognitive gap between existing visualization technologies and public understanding and participation, providing robust technical support and practical guidance for future disaster education and risk management. Ultimately, our work not only promotes the enhancement of public disaster awareness but also lays a solid foundation for further research in related fields.
