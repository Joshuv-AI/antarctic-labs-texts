The left primary motor cortex and cerebellar vermis are critical hubs in bimanual sequential learning
Authors: Yuki H. Hamano, Sho K. Sugawara, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Masaki Fukunaga, Norihiro Sadato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We conducted an fMRI study to investigate the neural basis of bimanual coordination, which is fundamental to upper extremity control. Considering bimanual movement as a combination of bimanual chord formation and sequence control, we hypothesized that the areas with the learning effect of both chord formation and sequence learning are critical in bimanual coordination. We adopted the serial reaction time task (SRTT) to test this hypothesis. Thirty-five healthy right-handed volunteers practiced visually cued bimanual SRTT, including the “mirror” and more complex “parallel” modes of random movements or repeating fixed sequences to separately depict the neural substrates of bimanual posture control for chord formation and those of sequence. Random movements’ reaction time (RT) continuously declined, indicating learning of bimanual chord formation. The RT in the sequential condition declined more rapidly than in the random condition, confirming sequence learning. The parallel random conditions evoked a more prominent learning-related decrease of task-related activation in the left M1 and cerebellar vermis than the less difficult mirror random conditions. The left M1 showed learning-related enhancement of functional connectivity with the anterior cingulate cortex during the parallel random conditions compared with the mirror random conditions. Thus, the left M1, anterior cingulate cortex, and cerebellar vermis are related to learning bimanual chord formation. The left M1 and cerebellar vermis also showed sequence-specific learning-related activity increments more prominent in the parallel mode than in the mirror mode. Thus, the left M1 and cerebellar vermis are critical in the bimanual motor learning network.

Introduction

Bimanual sequential skills, such as typing, are essential in modern life and are characterized by temporally coordinated but independently controlled sequential finger movements. Successful bimanual coordination relies on a widespread neural network spanning cortical and subcortical areas (Swinnen 2002; Puttemans et al. 2005), including the cerebellum (van Dun et al. 2021). A recent systematic review of hand movements (Chettouf et al. 2020) indicated that the unimanual movement is a special case of bimanual coordination. They found three patterns of bi-hemispheric activation: (a) the increase in task complexity of the unimanual task requires more efficient communication between hemispheres, (b) the anatomical properties of transcallosal fiber tracts enable this interhemispheric information exchange, and (c) the left (pre) motor areas play a key role when performing more complex motor tasks, irrespective of whether the left or right hand is being used. They concluded that unimanual movements rely on a highly coordinated pattern of neural activation, consisting of both inhibition and excitation across the bilateral motor network. Thus, the understanding of neural substrates involved in learning bimanual coordination is critical for post-stroke recovery and rehabilitation (Chettouf et al. 2020).

Consistent with the idea that bi-hemispheric cortical activation is fundamental for upper extremity motor control (Chettouf et al. 2020), it has been found that the M1 codes for bimanual movements in non-human primates (Aizawa et al. 1990; Donchin et al. 1998; Gribova et al. 2002). Recent human studies using fMRI have demonstrated bimanual learning-related changes in the left M1 (Debaere et al. 2004; Karabanov et al. 2019). Finally, electrical stimulation with transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) of the left M1 of humans enhanced the bimanual coordination (Rizvi et al. 2023). Although the effect of tDCS on bimanual motor learning is variable across studies (Pixa and Pollok 2018), these studies suggest the importance of the M1 in bimanual learning. However, midline motor-related areas, such as the SMA and cingulate motor area, are also involved in bimanual motor learning (Stephan et al. 1999; Halsband and Lange 2006). Learning-related changes in the relationship between M1 and other bimanual movement-related areas, including the cingulate motor area, are largely unknown.

Manual movement learning has two components: sensorimotor learning, which includes learning novel kinematics and dynamics, and sequence learning (Hardwick et al. 2013), which is usually tested using a serial reaction time task (SRTT) (Robertson 2007). SRTT embeds a series of events in which the temporal organization of the behavior, or sequence, is implicitly learned by cued serial reactions. For example, repeatedly presented sequences of the tap are implicitly learned and measured by the reduction of the RT (Reed and Johnson 1994), compared with the random tap condition that does not contain sequence structure. A previous meta-analysis of manual motor learning (Hardwick et al. 2013) identified a consistent bilateral cortical-subcortical network underlying motor learning across different tasks, including the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd), supplementary motor cortex (SMA), primary motor cortex (M1), primary somatosensory cortex, superior parietal lobule, thalamus, putamen, and cerebellum. Sensorimotor tasks focus on learning novel movement kinematics and dynamics and demonstrate more vigorous activity in the basal ganglia and cerebellum. In contrast, SRTT variants focusing on sequence learning demonstrated more vigorous activity in the cortical structures and thalamus. In both tasks, the left PMd showed learning-related activities. The authors concluded that the left PMd was a critical node in the manual motor learning network. However, this meta-analysis did not distinguish between unimanual and bimanual learning. Whether this conclusion applies to bimanual sequential learning remains to be determined.

Like unimanual movements consisting of sensorimotor learning and sequence learning (Hardwick et al. 2013), bimanual movements should combine bimanual sensorimotor learning and sequence learning. In the following, we refer to sensorimotor learning as ‘chord formation’ in line with Verwey (2023). When investigating unimanual and bimanual chord learning, Verwey (2023) found that the skill to produce chords is based on learned postures of hands rather than on spatial representations. The author discussed that bimanual practice induces postures for each hand separately that can be simultaneously prepared and executed. In addition, the author found bimanual chord formation to be faster than unimanual chord formation, which is explained by the higher inter-finger interference than chords executed unimanually. Similarly, bimanual coordination is more stable in mirror mode, where homologous muscles are active simultaneously than in parallel mode, where they have engaged alternately (Swinnen et al. 1997; Sadato et al. 1997a; Walsh et al. 2008). Increasing movement frequency in parallel mode leads to a phase transition to mirror mode, but not vice versa (Kelso 1984). This phenomenon was first explained by dynamic systems theory at the behavioral level (Haken et al. 1985; Schöner and Kelso 1988). The phase transition reversal is mainly linked to the non-dominant hand (Byblow et al. 1994, 1998, 2000; Sherwood 1994; Semjen et al. 1995; Treffner and Turvey 1995; Garry and Franks 2000), suggesting left hemisphere dominance in bimanual movement, supported by neuroimaging studies (Walsh et al. 2008). Aramaki et al. (2006) found that cortical neural cross-talk occurs in networks upstream of the primary motor cortex through asymmetric interhemispheric interaction. Thus, we expect that both sequence and chord formation learning depend on mode, with differences in task-related activation and learning-related changes. We hypothesized that the M1 encodes the bimanual motor engram as a hub of the motor-control networks. Considering these factors characterizing bimanual movement learning, we designed the SRTT with a bimanual sequential tapping task. In this task, participants tap two buttons (one by left hand and another by right hand) simultaneously following the visually presented instructions (Fig. 1A), leading to a specific hand chord formation that has two modes: mirror and parallel. The visually guided bimanual chord formation is repeated every 1 s for 20 s per trial, forming movement order. The sequence order implicitly required continuous pressing of buttons in a predefined 10-element sequential order, whereas the random order required pressing buttons in random order. Thus, there are four conditions consisting of 2 × 2 combinations of mode and order that is mirror random (MR), mirror sequence (MS), parallel random (PR), and parallel sequence (PS) (Fig. 1B). Each condition was repeated four times per run repeated 5 times. We defined one trial as two 10-element taps during a task block. Therefore, each condition has 20 trials per participant. The Trials effect is considered a learning effect. With increased practice, decreased activation suggests that the task can be accomplished with fewer neuronal resources as quick learning progresses (Poldrack 2000), while increased activation indicates the formation of the internal model of the learned procedure (Imamizu 2000). Therefore, we measured increases and decreases in task-related activity to determine the neural substrates involved in learning the chord formation and sequence. Finally, we mapped seed-based connectivity to depict the M1-centered bimanual coordination network. According to Hardwick’s logic (Hardwick et al. 2013), we assumed regions with learning effects in both bimanual chord formation and sequence acquisition are critical for bimanual coordination.

Methods

Participants

Through the web-based advertisement, 35 healthy right-handed adult volunteers participated in the study (eighteen males and seventeen females; mean age ± standard deviation [M ± SD] = 21.87 ± 1.60 years). Handedness was assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield 1971). None of the participants had a history of neurological or psychiatric diseases. All participants provided written informed consent for participation in the experiment. The study was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the National Institute for Physiological Sciences, Japan (confirmation number: 19A003).

Experimental procedures

All participants performed the SRTT after a short practice session inside the 7T-MRI scanner (Magnetom 7T; Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany). Presentation 12.2 software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Albany, NY, USA) was implemented on a personal computer (dc7900; Hewlett-Packard, Palo Alto, CA, USA) for the stimulus presentation and response time measurements with two button boxes (Current Designs, Philadelphia, PA, USA). A digital light processing (DLP) projector (EH503, Optoma, New Taipei City, Taiwan), located outside and behind the scanner, projected stimuli through a waveguide onto a translucent screen that was viewed via a mirror attached to the head coil of the MRI scanner. The distance between the screen and each participant’s eyes was approximately 48 cm, and the angle was 5.2° (horizontal) × 3.9° (vertical).

First, we refer to one MRI imaging sequence as a “run.” During a run, this task began with a 15-s rest block because of stabilization of the magnetization, then the 21-s task and 21-s rest block were repeated alternately sixteen times. The last rest block lasted for 46 s because of the completion of the rest block. The total duration of a run was 712 s (= 11 min 52 s). Participants underwent 5 runs consecutively, in between the resting state fMRI (12 min 20 s twice) and anatomical MRI data acquisition (5 min 20 sec). The total time participants spent in the MRI scanner was approximately 100 min.

The task block consisted of a 1-s-long instruction phase and a 20-s-long execution phase. During the instruction phase, the message of the task condition was displayed above a white crosshair that appeared in the center of the screen. Eight filled white circles, corresponding to the four fingers of both hands, appeared in a horizontal arrangement. During the execution phase, only the two circles corresponding to the buttons that needed to be pressed were filled (with white color), while the other circles remained blank (not filled with color). During this phase, the participants were instructed to press the buttons corresponding to the filled circles as fast as possible. The positions of filled circles were then changed by 1 Hz. Like the task block, the rest block consisted of a 1-s instruction phase and a 20-s observational phase. During the instruction phase, the message “Rest” was displayed above a white crosshair that appeared in the center of the screen. Additionally, eight filled blue circles corresponding to the four fingers of both hands appeared in a horizontal arrangement. Only two circles were filled with blue during the observational phase, while the other circles remained blank. During this phase, the participants were instructed to watch the crosshair without making finger movements. The positions of filled circles were then changed by 1 Hz.

The task blocks included four conditions, designed using two factors. One factor is the coordination mode between the two hands. In the mirror mode, the participants were asked to press the response buttons simultaneously with homologous fingers on both hands. When one button was pressed with the index finger on the right hand, the other button was pressed with the index finger on the left. In contrast, in the parallel mode, participants were asked to press response buttons in spatially equivalent positions with both hands. When one button was pressed with the index finger on the right hand, the other button was pressed with the little finger on the left. The second factor was the order of movements. The sequence order required continuous pressing of buttons in a predefined 10-element sequential order. Two predetermined sequences were used: sequence A (index-ring-middle-little-index-ring-index-little-middle-ring fingers on right hand) and sequence B (little-index-ring-middle-little-middle-ring-index-little-middle fingers on right hand). For half of the participants, sequence A was trained with mirror coordination, while sequence B was trained with parallel coordination. Sequence B was trained with mirror coordination for the remaining half of the participants, whereas sequence A was trained with parallel coordination. Furthermore, the random order required continuous button pressing in random order. This task included four conditions: mirror sequence (MS), mirror random (MR), parallel sequence (PS), and parallel random (PR). In the task block, four conditions (MR, MS, PR, PS) were each repeated four times, and within the task block, there were two sets of 10-element taps (1 Hz). We refer to the set of 20 taps during the task block as a trial to maximize the neural responses while keeping the time resolution of the learning-related changes by reducing the trial duration. Since we conducted a total of 5 runs per participant, each condition was performed in 20 trials. The Trial effect is considered a learning effect. The order of task conditions was pseudo-randomized and identical across participants.

Two types of stimulus modes were used for the rest blocks: mirror and parallel. In the rest blocks that followed the task blocks with the MS and MR conditions, the circles at the location corresponding to the homologous fingers of both hands were filled. In contrast, in the rest blocks that followed the task blocks with the PS and PR conditions, circles at the location corresponding to the fingers in the spatially equivalent position were filled. In either coordination, the positions of the filled circles were changed by 1 Hz in random order.

Behavioral analysis

Performance was measured by response time, defined as the interval between the onset of the visual cue and the onset of pressing the button, and tapping error. For response time, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA) was conducted using three factors (hand, mode, and trials) for RT of random conditions (MR and PR) and the difference of RT between sequence and random conditions with the same mode in the trials ((MS-MR) and (PS-PR)). Thus, the former represents the performance of chord formation, and the latter represents that of sequence. The same procedure was applied to the tapping error. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 3.6.1; http://cran.us.r-project.org), and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

MRI data acquisition

Each participant’s head was immobilized within a 32-element phased array head coil and a 1-ch transmitter head coil. fMRI was performed using a multi-band Gradient-Echo Echo Planar Image (GE-EPI) sequence (Moeller et al. 2010) as follows: repetition time [TR] = 1,000 ms; echo time [TE] = 22.2 ms; field of view [FOV] = 208 × 208 mm2; flip angle = 45°; matrix size = 130 × 130; 85 slices; slice thickness = 1.6 mm; multiband factor = 5, GRAPPA = 2, Phase partial Fourier = 7/8, Bandwidth [BW] = 1924 Hz/Px. To measure the B0 field for EPI distortion correction, two spin echo EPI images with reversed phase encoding directions were acquired as follows: TR = 3,000 ms; TE = 60.4 ms; FOV = 208 × 208 mm2; FA = 90°/180°; matrix size = 130 × 130; 85 slices; slice thickness = 1.6 mm; GRAPPA = 2; Phase partial Fourier = 7/8; BW = 1924 Hz/Px.

7T-fMRI provides a better signal-to-noise ratio and higher spatial resolution than 3T-fMRI. However, geometric distortions become challenging because 7T-MRI is more susceptible to static magnetic field (B0) inhomogeneity resulting from differences in magnetic permeability of human brain tissues and gradient nonlinearity than 3T-MRI. To maximally and systematically correct the distortion, we constructed the preprocessing pipelines with 3T-fMRI data (Yamamoto et al. 2021), the modified version of the minimum preprocessing pipelines for the Human Connectome Project (Glasser et al. 2013). Thus, in addition to fMRI data acquisition using 7T-MRI, the HCP-style structural data of all participants were obtained using a 3T-MRI scanner (Magnetom Verio, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel receiving head coil at the National Institute for Physiological Sciences. As a 3T MRI structural study took 1.5 h to complete, we requested participants to join the study on a separate day. Two separate T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) images were acquired using a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid-acquisition gradient echo sequence (MPRAGE)(Mugler and Brookeman 1990) as follows: TR = 2,400 ms; TE = 2.24 ms; FOV = 256 mm ×240 mm; flip angle = 8°; matrix size = 320 × 320; slice thickness = 0.8 mm; 224 sagittal slices; GRAPPA = 2. The variable flip angle turbo spin-echo sequence (Siemens SPACE)(Mugler et al. 2000) was as follows: TR = 3,200 ms; TE = 560 ms; FOV = 256 × 240 mm2; matrix size = 320 × 320; slice thickness = 0.8 mm; 224 sagittal slices; GRAPPA = 2. Similar to the fMRI session, two spin-echo EPI images with reversed phase-encoding directions were acquired as follows: TR = 7,700; TE = 60 ms; FOV = 192 × 192 mm2; FA = 78°/160°; matrix size = 98 × 98; 72 slices; slice thickness = 2 mm; phase partial Fourier = 6/8.

MRI data preprocessing

To investigate group-averaged task-related activity, we normalized the functional images from the native space to the standard space. To correct the distortion of functional images measured by 7T-MRI, we adopted minimal preprocessing pipelines (Glasser et al. 2013) developed for the Human Connectome Project (HCP)(Glasser et al. 2016; Yamamoto et al. 2021). The HCP-style preprocessing consists of structural and functional pipelines. Although HCP-style preprocessing includes surface-based processing, we only used volume-based processing. We applied the structural pipeline to the T1w and T2w images. First, image distortions resulting from gradient nonlinearity were corrected. After extracting the brain regions, susceptibility-induced distortions were corrected using a field map generated from two spin-echo EPIs with opposite phase-encoding directions and the Topup toolbox(Andersson et al. 2003). Undistorted T1w and T2w images were registered using cross-modal boundary-based registration (BBR)(Greve and Fischl 2009). Because the intensities of the T1w and T2w images still had biases, a bias field correction was applied to the undistorted images. Finally, we estimated the nonlinear registration matrix from the native space to the MNI template space and applied this nonlinear registration to the T1w and T2w images.

The functional pipeline was also started by correcting the gradient nonlinearity and susceptibility-induced distortions. To correct head motion, EPIs were registered into a single-band reference (SBRef) EPI and scanned at the beginning of each session by estimating six rigid-body transformation parameters from each EPI to SBRef EPI. We conducted structural and fMRI sessions on different days. Thus, we calculated the session-specific field map from two spin echo (SE)-EPIs measured during the fMRI sessions. Using this field map, we applied a susceptibility-induced distortion correction to the motion-corrected EPIs using the Topup toolbox. The transformation matrix from the SBRef EPI to T1w images was estimated using the BBR cross-modal registration method. This BBR parameter was then applied to the undistorted EPIs to register all EPIs into the T1w image. The resulting EPIs were transformed into the MNI template space using T1w-to-MNI parameters estimated in the structural pipelines. Finally, the image intensities of the EPIs were normalized to a 4D whole-brain mean of 10,000 (arbitrary grayscale value). The normalized functional images were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian 5 mm full-width kernel at half maximum (FWHM) in the x-, y-, and z-axes.

We analyzed task-related activity using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging) in MATLAB 2020b. The first five volumes of each fMRI run were discarded for the unsteady signal. Statistical analysis of fMRI data was conducted at two levels. At the first level, a general linear model (GLM) was fitted to the fMRI data for each participant(Friston et al. 1994; Worsley and Friston 1995). Each run consisted of four MS, MR, PS, and PR epochs. Each task epoch was divided into an instruction event and an execution phase, a trial. In each run, all instruction events were modeled with a stick function, the onset of which was specified at the beginning of the instruction cue presentation with 0 duration and constituted in one regressor. Each trial was modeled with a boxcar function, the onset of which was specified at the beginning of the execution with a 20 s duration.

Because we conducted five runs, five instruction regressors and 80 execution regressors for four conditions by 20 trials were modeled in the GLM. All the regressors were convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function. In addition, six motion parameters and their first derivatives were contained in the GLM to consider the effects of head motions. The time series of each voxel was high-pass filtered at 1/128 Hz. For advanced rapid sampling techniques such as the multi-band GE-EPI used in this study, a first-order autoregressive model must sufficiently capture temporal correlations in time series with higher sampling rates (Bollmann et al. 2018). Thus, the “FAST” model implemented in SPM12 was applied to adequately address the temporal correlations in time series with higher sampling rates (Corbin et al. 2018) for data whitening. Then, to calculate the estimated parameters, a least-squares estimation was performed on the high-pass filtered and pre-whitened data. At this level, we estimated three types of linear contrasts (Fig. 2A). First, predefined contrasts were applied to depict the average execution-related activity for each task condition (i.e., MS, MR, PS, and PR). Furthermore, predefined linearly increasing and decreasing contrasts for each condition were estimated to investigate the changes in execution-related activities through repetitive practices. Increasing or decreasing contrast vectors were defined numerically as an increment or decrement of one per task epoch, keeping the mean equal to zero.

The contrast images obtained by the individual-level analysis were entered into the second-level group-level analysis (Fig. 2B). We created three design matrices for mean, increased, and decreased contrasts (Table 1).

For the mean contrast, to clarify the entire task-related network, we conducted a conjunction analysis of mean contrasts for four conditions (i.e., MS, MR, PS, and PR). To investigate activation related to bimanual chord formation within the task-relevant network, we estimated the subtractions between two random conditions (i.e., MR - PR or PR - MR).

Chord formation and sequence learning-related changes in execution-related activities were investigated by decreasing and increasing contrasts. Based on the difference between the MR and PR conditions, we evaluated the chord formation learning effect, which is attributed to the mode difference, as both parallel and mirror are bimanual chord formations. The sequence learning effect was estimated by subtracting the random-related contrast from the sequence-related contrast in each coordination mode (i.e., MS - MR or PS -PR). For all subtractions, to depict the effects on task-positive activity, we conducted a conjunction analysis of the subtraction and its base contrast (e.g., [MR– PR] and MR). The resulting set of voxel values for each contrast constituted the statistical parametric mapping of the t-value, SPM{t}. The statistical threshold for the spatial extent test on the clusters—defined by a height threshold of p < 0.005—was set at p < 0.05, corrected for family-wise errors (Friston et al. 1996), unless otherwise specified.

Seed-based functional connectivity analysis.

We conducted a seed-based functional connectivity analysis based on the residual time series obtained from GLM analysis. The seed regions were defined as the left M1 and the right cerebellar vermis, where the learning-related decrease in activity was more prominent in the parallel random condition than in the mirror random condition. Each seed volume of interest (VOI) was defined as a spherical region with a 5-mm radius. The center of each VOI was set as the peak coordinate resulting from the group-level GLM analysis: left M1 (-36, -30, 58) and right cerebellar vermis (7, -60, -29). We extracted the time series of the signals from each seed region. We then calculated the correlation coefficient of the residual time series between the seed VOI and all the voxels for each task epoch. Thus, 80 correlational maps were created for the left M1 and right cerebellar vermis. These correlation maps were normalized using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. We calculated the averaging, linearly increasing, and decreasing contrasts on the z-transformed correlation maps for each condition to investigate learning-related effects on seed-based connectivity. These contrasts were incorporated into a flexible factorial design at the group level. The estimated contrasts were identical to those of the task-related activation. The resulting set of voxel values for each contrast constituted the statistical parametric mapping of the t-value, SPM{t}. The statistical threshold for the spatial extent test on the clusters—defined by the height threshold of p < 0.005—was set at p < 0.05, corrected for family-wise error (Friston et al. 1996).

Neuroimaging

Bimanual chord formation

The motor control system exhibited mean activation throughout the random conditions common to mirror and parallel modes (Fig. 4A; Table 2). Within these regions, the right PMv and IPL showed more prominent activation during the parallel mode than the mirror mode, which was likely involved in the coordinate transformation per se (Fig. 4B; Table 2).

Task-related activity during bimanual chord formation

Both mirror and parallel random conditions decreased task-related activity in multiple motor control regions. In contrast, the visual cortex and thalamus, which were constantly activated by the task, did not show a decrease (Fig. 4C; Table 2). No regions showed an increase in task-related activation (Fig. 4E and F; Table 2). These findings indicate a learning-related decrease in prediction error at multiple levels of motor control, which is consistent with the hierarchical predictive coding hypothesis (Friston 2005). Random parallel conditions evoked a more prominent decrement than random mirror conditions in the left M1 and cerebellar vermis (Fig. 4D; Table 2), suggesting that these regions represent parallel mode-specific non-sequential chord formation learning, i.e., non-homologous coordination of both hands.

Psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analysis seeded at the left M1 showed learning-related enhancement of functional connectivity with the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) (MNI coordinates of 13, 16, 35) during parallel random conditions compared to random mirror conditions (Fig. 5A; Table 3). This finding indicates that the functional connectivity between the left M1 and ACC is related to bimanual chord formation learning during parallel conditions. The identical procedures with the right M1 seed showed no significant change in the functional connectivity across the whole brain (Fig. 5B; Table 3).

Discussion

Findings summary

Behavioral data showed that reaction time (RT) decreased during Random order tapping while the error rate remained unchanged, suggesting that sensorimotor postural control for chord formation was learned. In addition, the RT difference between Random and Sequence orders decreased, suggesting a more rapid decrease in RT during Sequence order tapping, representing sequence learning. The reduction in error rate further suggests that sequential spatial information was learned. RTs and error rates suggest that the parallel mode is more challenging than the mirror mode, consistent with previous literature (Swinnen et al. 1997; Sadato et al. 1997a; Walsh et al. 2008). Neuroimaging results revealed: (i) by directly comparing parallel and mirror modes during random conditions, the neural substrates of non-homologous bimanual posture control learning for chord formation were identified; (ii) with increasing practice, sequence-specific activation levels also increased, suggesting the use of additional cortical substrates; (iii) the left M1 and cerebellar vermis were involved in sensorimotor control learning of bimanual posture control during random conditions for chord formation, interacting with sequence learning, which is critical for bimanual coordination.

Chord formation learning specific decrement in random conditions

We found a shortening of the RT during random conditions, indicating the learning of bimanual chord formation. This finding suggests that learning shortens the duration of neural processing with more efficient informational transmission (Grill-Spector et al. 2006). Correspondingly, the activation of the cortical and subcortical motor-related areas showed a learning-related decrease in random conditions in the mirror and parallel modes.

Previous studies on bimanual sequence learning using the SRTT (Rauch et al. 1997; Willingham et al. 2002; Werheid et al. 2003; Zedkova et al. 2006; Purdon et al. 2011) adopted sequence learning with the index and middle fingers of the right and left hands. Because they focused on sequence learning, learning-related changes in the random control condition were not assessed. This is partly because random conditions required the formation of unimanual postures by each visual cue. In these studies, two boxes were visually presented on the screen’s left side, corresponding with two keys on the left-hand button box. The right-hand boxes corresponded with keys on the right-hand button box, and each press was performed with a different finger for each key (middle and index fingers of left and right hand).

In contrast, our study required that participants tap with their hands simultaneously in the mirror or parallel mode for bimanual chord formation. Thus, a decline in task-related activity reflected the visually-guided motor learning of bimanual chord formation. Notably, the visual cortex and thalamus were constantly activated during the chord formation without any learning-related changes (Fig. 4). Thus, the learning-related decline in task-related activation was motor-network specific. Within these regions, the left M1 and cerebellar vermis showed a more prominent decrease in task-related activity during the parallel random condition than during the mirror random condition. As the decrement of the task-related BOLD response represents the learning (Dayan and Cohen 2011), This finding indicates the learning of the bimanual chord formation. Because both parallel and mirror are bimanual chord formations, the difference between the parallel and mirror chords is attributed to the difference in bimanual coordination(Sadato et al. 1997a). The behavioral results showed that the parallel mode required longer RT than the mirror mode (Fig. 3), indicating additional processes, such as coordinate transformation, given that the mirror mode is the default (Cincotta and Ziemann 2008). This finding suggests that these areas represent the learning of non-homologous bimanual sensorimotor control, that is, chord formation (Verwey 2023).

Using the left M1 as a seed, functional connectivity analysis showed learning-related enhancement of functional connectivity with the ACC with the MNI coordinate of (13, 16, 35) during parallel random conditions compared to that during mirror random conditions. The identical analysis with the right M1 seed did not show any significant change in the functional connectivity. The functional role of the ACC in bimanual coordination was previously underscored by a kinematic analysis of bimanual in-phase and anti-phase movements in patients with cingulate lesions, which showed impairment in the establishment of unimanual independence and accurate temporal adjustments between the two hands(Stephan et al. 1999). Mars et al.(2005) found a learning-dependent shift in activation timing in the rostral cingulate zone of the ACC from external error feedback to internal error detection induced by an established internal model. As the M1 showed bimanual control-specific learning changes, whereas the ACC did not, enhancing functional connectivity between the left M1 and the ACC may represent a top-down signal transfer from the learned internal model for bimanual coordination in the left M1 to the ACC, where non-homologous bimanual control and temporal adjustment between the two hands are mediated. The present study indicates that the left M1 and ACC are related to learning sensorimotor control of bimanual posture.

However, the role of the cerebellar vermis in bimanual coordination remains to be elucidated. A recent meta-analysis (van Dun et al. 2021) showed a role for the anterior vermis in anti-phase and complex bimanual coordination and an exclusive role for the posterior vermis in complex bimanual movements. The results of the present study align with this notion, showing that the anterior vermis is a parallel mode-specific learning-related activator.

Sequence learning-specific increment during parallel mode

We found a sequence learning-specific increment in task-related activation in the bilateral PMd, M1, cerebellar hemispheres, vermis, and the left putamen in the parallel mode (Fig. 6H). Because the sequence condition includes sequence-specific learning, whereas the random condition does not, their subtraction depicts the formation of the internal model of the sequence. We also found that the left M1 and cerebellar vermis showed a learning-related increment in sequence-specific activities, as depicted by the contrast between the parallel and mirror sequence conditions controlled for each random condition (Fig. 6I). As these areas overlapped with bimanual chord formation learning (Fig. 4D), the left M1 and cerebellar vermis may represent mirror-to-parallel conversion by integrating bimanual posture control for chord formation with the sequence.

The spatial and temporal information of sequence movements is represented by multiple regions, such as the PMd, posterior parietal cortices, M1, putamen, and cerebellum (Hardwick et al. 2013), as shown in the present study. The bilateral PMd is known to encode spatial information and the ordering of the movements of different fingers in space during unimanual control. Movement sequences in extrinsic spatial coordinates are represented by the rostral PMd and the posterior parietal cortex, and in intrinsic spatial coordinates, they are represented by the caudal PMd (Wiestler et al. 2014). Sequence learning requires the integration of spatial and temporal features. The visually paced unimanual sequence learning task encoded the spatial and temporal information of learned finger sequences in the premotor cortex, which integrates information into M1 (Kornysheva and Diedrichsen 2014), where sequential digit movements are encoded as a motor engram (Hamano et al. 2020, 2021). These previous and present results indicated that bilateral PMd and M1 are involved in sequence learning.

The involvement of the lateral cerebellar hemispheres in motor control is well known (Imamizu 2000), presumably through the coding of the forward model. However, its involvement in bimanual coordination and learning remains unknown. The present study showed that, in addition to the sensorimotor control of bimanual postures for chord formation, the cerebellar vermis and cerebellar hemisphere are related to sequence-specific internal forward model formation.

A recent meta-analysis of sequence learning (Janacsek et al. 2020) showed consistent basal ganglia activation. The present study showed significant activation of the left striatum with bilateral M1-PMd in contrast to PS-PR (Fig. 6H), consistent with basal ganglia involvement in procedural learning (Purdon et al. 2011).

Previous functional connectivity analyses have revealed that M1, the premotor cortex, and the SMA have significantly greater inter- and intra-hemispheric coupling during early, compared to late, within-session explicit sequence learning (Sun et al. 2007). Thus, the present findings and previous literature indicate that the left M1 and cerebellar vermis likely function as hubs of the motor network, controlling and learning bimanual sequential coordination by combining spatiotemporal information of the sequence with bimanual postural coordination.

Mean activation

The right PMv and IPL showed more prominent activation during parallel than mirror mode during Random conditions. As activation was averaged across the whole trial, these areas likely represented the coordinate transformation from mirror to parallel mode. The default bimanual movement is the mirror mode (Meyer-Lindenberg et al. 2002), and the mirror-to-parallel transition may include an inhibition of the tendency to return to a more stable mirror mode. Electrophysiological analysis of human fine motor control using paired TMS pulses—one pulse delivered over an area of interest influencing M1 followed by a second pulse delivered over M1—showed that both the PMv and aIPL exert inhibitory control over M1 (Merchant et al. 2020). A previous TMS study showed that the single-pulse TMS with graded-intensity stimuli in the right premotor area evoked a phase transition from parallel to mirror mode (Meyer-Lindenberg et al. 2002). Thus, the right parietal-premotor areas may exert an inhibitory effect on the right M1 to maintain the parallel mode during bimanual chord formation.

Limitations

First, we found no learning-related increment of the task-related neural activation in random conditions but did find a learning-related decrement, reflecting a reduced need for additional cortical substrates than in sequence learning (Poldrack 2000). Second, we found no learning-related increments in sequence-specific activities during the mirror mode. This may be explained by the less prominent learning effect during mirror mode compared with that during parallel mode, as shown in the behavioral data (Fig. 3C). We utilized the fixed rate of 1 Hz to exclude the frequency effect on the neural activation (Sadato et al. 1997b). The relatively slow rate of the task movement may not elicit the learning effect in the cortical activation, particularly in easier mirror mode. Third, although we found a correlation between learning-related activity changes within the left M1 and cerebellar vermis, we could not establish any proof of a causal relationship. Although electrical stimulation with transcranial direct current stimulation of the left M1 enhanced the bimanual coordination (Rizvi et al. 2023), further interventional studies are warranted.

Conclusion

The left M1 and cerebellar vermis are involved in sequence-specific and chord formation learning of bimanual skills as a hub of the motor-control networks.
