Income dynamics and risk of early-onset dementia: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Byung-Joon Ko, Yong-Moon Mark Park, Kyu-Na Lee, Kyungdo Han, Ga Eun Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between income dynamics—including sustained income level, income changes, and income variability—and early-onset dementia (EOD) has not been well established. This study investigated the association between income dynamics and the risk of EOD.MethodsThis cohort study included 2,247,461 adults aged 40–60 years who underwent health examinations in 2012 through the Korean National Health Insurance Service, with a median follow-up duration of 5.4 years till 2018. Income status was assessed for 2012 and the 4 preceding years using monthly health insurance premiums, and income-related parameters were derived from this pre-baseline 5-year window. Income variability was defined as the intra-individual standard deviation of four consecutive annual percentage changes. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to evaluate the relationship between income dynamics and EOD risk, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsSustained low-income status over five years was associated with an increased risk of EOD (HR for 5 years vs. 0 years 1.63, 95% CI 1.49–1.78; P-trend < 0.001), whereas sustained high-income status was associated with a reduced EOD risk (HR for 5 years vs. 0 years 0.55, 95% CI 0.51–0.59; P-trend < 0.001). Notably, higher income variability was linked to a greater risk of EOD (HR for highest vs. lowest quartile 1.37, 95% CI 1.28–1.48; P-trend < 0.001). The decline in income–particularly to the lowest level (Medical Aid beneficiaries)–was associated with an elevated risk of EOD, irrespective of the initial income status.ConclusionsSustained low income, income decline, and greater income variability were associated with higher EOD risk. These findings may help identify socioeconomically vulnerable middle-aged adults for targeted dementia risk assessment and prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02067-8.

Introduction

Dementia, a syndrome characterized by a decline in cognitive function and changes in personality, renders independent daily living impossible and ultimately leads to mortality. Dementia represents a personal tragedy that imposes significant social costs. Early-onset dementia (EOD), which affects individuals aged < 65 years, prematurely diminishes social functionality, thereby exerting a profound effect on society [1, 2]. Thus, it is imperative to detect and mitigate the risk factors for EOD to prevent or delay its onset.

Low-income status is a risk factor for dementia [3]. Low income, along with health-related behaviors (such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical inactivity) and a high prevalence of cardiometabolic diseases (such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia), is correlated with an increased risk of dementia [3–5]. Several previous studies have explored the relationship between low-income status and the risk of dementia; however, the cross-sectional design [6] and small sample size [7–9] of most of these studies and the measurement of income at one timepoint [10, 11] have hindered the evaluation of the impact of income fluctuations over time. Furthermore, studies exploring the association between income or socioeconomic status (SES) and the risk of EOD are scarce [3, 4].

Therefore, the present study investigated the association between the changes in income status and the risk of EOD in a large cohort comprising over 2 million middle-aged individuals to gain insights into the effects of income dynamics on the likelihood of developing EOD. The findings of the present study will aid in the formulation of preventive strategies and interventions.

Methods

Data sources

The National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), a single insurance system that caters to residents of South Korea, provides coverage to 97% of the South Korean population (approximately 50 million people). The Medical Aid program, a medical assistance program targeting the lowest income class, provides coverage to the remaining 3% of the population [12]. Comprehensive claims data, such as demographic information; prescription history; and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes, are included in the NHIS database. Information regarding the anthropometric features, health-related behaviors, physical examination results, and mental health assessments were obtained through the standardized national health screening examination program. The Institutional Review Board of the Korea University Guro Hospital, Seoul, Korea, approved this study (No. 2022GR0325).

Study population

The dataset was constructed by applying a 40% random sampling to all adults aged ≥ 20 years who underwent the national health examination in 2012 (the baseline year). The analysis was then restricted to individuals aged 40–60 years, resulting in a total of 2,459,206 participants. EOD affects individuals aged < 65 years [1]. Given the rarity of EOD among individuals aged < 40 years [13], and the limited economic activity of individuals in their twenties owing to educational requirements or mandatory military service for men in South Korea, only participants aged 40–60 years were included in the present study. Participants with missing income (n = 160,761) or covariate (n = 44,682) data and those with a prior diagnosis of dementia between 2002 and the index date (2012), identified using ICD-10 codes (see the following Definition of EOD section) (n = 1417) were excluded. Furthermore, individuals who developed dementia within one year after the index date were also excluded to minimize bias related to undetected dementia at baseline (n = 4885). Thus, 2,247,461 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). After application of the one-year lag period, follow-up began one year after the index date. The earliest of the following four events was designated as the end of follow-up: death, reaching age 65 years, onset of dementia, or end of study (December 31, 2018).

Definition of income status

The NHIS does not record exact household income data. Consequently, the monthly health insurance premiums, which approximate household income, were used to define annual income status [14]. The health insurance premiums remained constant throughout the 1-year period unless there was a significant shift in income. The premium for self-employed individuals differs from that for employed individuals. The premiums for employed individuals are determined based on monthly wages, whereas the premiums for self-employed individuals are charged based on income and property (including land and rent) [15]. Income status was based on the NHIS-provided 20-level premium-based income rank. Income quartile cutoffs were based on the NHIS administrative income classification for the overall insured population and were not re-derived within the analytic cohort or within age- or sex-specific strata. For analysis, the 20 income ranks were collapsed into four categories, and Medical Aid beneficiaries were considered separately where indicated [16, 17]. The lowest income quartile and Medical Aid beneficiaries were classified as the low-income group, whereas the highest income quartile comprised the high-income group. Annual income status was assessed for the baseline year and the 4 preceding years. The income status four years prior to the baseline year (2012) was used as the initial pre-baseline income assessment.

Definition of income parameters

The cumulative number of years in the low- or high-income group was measured over the baseline year and the preceding four years. Income variability was calculated from the 20-level premium-based income rank rather than exact household income values to capture fluctuations over the study period. The 20 ordered income ranks were treated as consecutive integers. The intra-individual standard deviation (SD) of annual income changes was defined as income variability. For each individual, five annual income-rank values (baseline year and the 4 preceding years) were used to calculate four consecutive year-to-year percentage changes, and the within-person SD of these four percentage changes was defined as income variability. The SD of each person was divided into quartiles according to its distribution in the analytic cohort to create groups according to the income variability. The income of the individuals in the baseline year was categorized into quartiles. The difference in income status between the initial assessment (four years before the baseline year) and the final assessment (baseline year) was also determined. Baseline income status and the change in income status were analyzed separately and were not used in the calculation of income variability.

Definition of EOD

EOD, defined as the onset of dementia before the age of 65 years [1, 2], can be further categorized as Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia. This differentiation was performed by assessing the prescription (N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonists [memantine] or acetylcholinesterase inhibitors [donepezil hydrochloride, rivastigmine, or galantamine]) and the appropriate ICD-10 code (F00 or G30 for Alzheimer’s disease; F01 for vascular dementia; and F02, F03, G23.1, or G31 for other types of dementia) [19]. In accordance with the stipulation of the Ministry of Health and Welfare of Korea, insurance claims for the treatment of dementia are acknowledged only for individuals with a Mini-Mental State Examination score of ≤ 26, Clinical Dementia Rating of 1–3, or Global Deterioration Scale stage of 3–7. The total value of the medication was recovered from the prescribing physician after a subsequent evaluation if these criteria were not satisfied [19]. Thus, the dementia diagnosis code and a prescription for drugs used in the treatment of dementia lend credibility to the diagnoses.

Covariates

Self-reported questionnaires were used to obtain information regarding health-related behaviors. The participants were classified as non-smokers, former smokers, and current smokers according to smoking status. Similarly, the participants were classified as non-, mild-to-moderate (< 30 g/day), and heavy (≥ 30 g/day) drinkers according to alcohol consumption. Physical activity status was defined as regular exercise (moderate physical activity for at least 30 min a day for at least 5 days a week or vigorous physical activity for > 20 min a day for at least 3 days a week) or none. The body mass index (BMI) was determined by dividing the weight (kg) by the squared height (m2). Obesity was defined as a BMI of ≥ 25 kg/m2. Type 2 diabetes was defined as a history of claims for diabetes medication under ICD-10 codes E11–14 or fasting blood glucose levels of ≥ 126 mg/dL. Hypertension was defined as a history of claims for antihypertensive medication under ICD-10 codes I10–15 or a blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg. Dyslipidemia was defined as a history of claims for dyslipidemia medication under ICD-10 code E78 or total cholesterol levels of ≥ 240 mg/dL. Chronic kidney disease was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate of < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Atrial fibrillation was defined as ≥ 2 outpatient visits or hospitalization under ICD-10 code I48. Stroke was defined as hospitalization under ICD-10 code I63 or I64, accompanied by claims for magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography of the brain. Depression was defined as claims under ICD-10 code F32 or F33. Most covariates were measured at baseline, while atrial fibrillation, stroke, and depression were ascertained during both the pre-baseline and baseline periods, coinciding with the period over which income changes were assessed.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics are presented as numbers and percentages or means and SDs. A one-way analysis of variance was used to analyze and compare continuous variables. The chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables. The incidence rate of dementia per 1,000 person-years was determined by dividing the number of events by the total number of person-years of follow-up. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to determine the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between income dynamics and risk of dementia. The Schoenfeld residual test was used to examine the assumption of proportional hazards. No significant departure from the proportionality of the hazards over time was observed. Model 1 was not adjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and residential area. Model 3 was adjusted for obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and type of health insurance. Notably, lifestyle factors such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, and regular exercise were excluded from the main model owing to their potential mediation effects on the relationship between income and EOD risk [20]. Subgroup analysis was conducted using age, sex, type of health insurance, residential area, obesity, and type 2 diabetes to identify the potential effect modifiers that could affect the relationship between income variables and dementia risk. Subsequent sensitivity analyses were conducted as follows: (i) adjustment for family size (Model 4), depression (Model 5), and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (Model 6); (ii) exploratory adjustment for lifestyle factors (Model 7) to assess their influence on the observed associations; (iii) landmark analyses utilizing a 3- and 5-year time points to estimate the risk of EOD among individuals who remained event-free at each landmark, thereby evaluating long-term associations between income parameters and risk of EOD; (iv) an additional main analysis for EOD (all-cause dementia) with extended follow-up through December 31, 2022. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). A two-sided p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1 show the baseline characteristics according to the cumulative number of years in the low- and high-income categories and the income variability quartile. Compared with the participants who never experienced low income status, the participants with sustained low-income status were more likely to be older, females, and residing in a rural area. Individuals with sustained low-income status were less likely to be covered by self-employed insurance. Furthermore, participants with sustained low-income status were more likely to be non-smokers, non-drinkers, and physically inactive. Notably, the participants with sustained low-income status had higher odds of developing hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and depression. Except for the odds of developing atrial fibrillation, opposite patterns were observed among the participants with sustained high-income status. Supplementary Table 2 presents the characteristics of the baseline income quartiles. The participants with lower income variability (quartiles 1–2) were likely to experience a high income. In contrast, the participants with higher income variability (quartiles 3–4) were likely to experience a low income (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This nationwide cohort study involving over 2.2 million middle-aged adults revealed that sustained low-income status and greater income variability were associated with a significant increase in the risk of EOD, irrespective of the type of dementia, i.e., Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia. Participants with sustained low-income status over a five-year period were associated with a 63% higher risk of dementia than those without sustained low-income status. In contrast, participants with sustained high-income status over the same period were associated with a 45% lower risk of dementia than those without sustained high-income status. Participants with the highest income variability was associated with a 37% higher risk of dementia than those with the lowest income variability.

EOD, defined as dementia that develops before the age of 65 years [1], occurs during individuals’ economically productive years and can therefore impose substantial societal and economic burdens on patients and their families [2]. Identifying factors associated with the risk of EOD is therefore important for informing prevention strategies and reducing its impact. Previous studies have identified several factors associated with EOD, including genetic factors (apolipoprotein E), cardiovascular conditions (atrial fibrillation, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and stroke), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, and lack of physical activity), and psychiatric disorders (depression and anxiety) [4, 5].

Low SES and low-income status increase the risk of dementia [7, 10, 11, 21]. Poorer cognitive function was observed in individuals who experienced negative wealth shock or higher income volatility in previous studies; moreover, these individuals are more likely to develop dementia [8, 9]. However, most previous studies relied on one-time income assessments, and income dynamics over time were not estimated [10, 11]. SES across the life course at three stages, childhood, middle age, and old age, or multiple life stages, were assessed in some studies. However, the methodology used to quantify SES varied from stage to stage, and some methods were susceptible to recall bias as they were based on estimates from prior memories [7, 21]. In contrast, the present study utilized health insurance premiums mandated by the government as a reliable and objective measure of income [16, 17]. Tracking income over a five-year period facilitated the assessment of the relationship between income dynamics and the risk of EOD. Although the follow-up period of the present study was relatively short, the methodology used herein represents a novel approach [16].

Individuals with high income variability were associated with a higher risk of EOD. These individuals often had a low baseline income and were less likely to have a high income prior to baseline assessment. Income variability is associated with income uncertainty or fast and erratic fluctuations in income over time. It can potentially lead to increased vulnerability, particularly among low-income households [22]. Notably, income variability has been linked to metabolic dysregulation, chronic stress, and interrupted healthcare use [18, 22]. Participants exhibiting a decline in income to the lowest level (Medical Aid beneficiaries) were associated with the highest risk of dementia, irrespective of the initial income. These individuals tended to utilize medical services more frequently because they benefit from government support, which reduces their out-of-pocket expenses for outpatient care and hospitalization [23]. Notably, the living and working environments of these individuals were unhealthy, which can contribute to poor health outcomes such as dementia [24–26]. Thus, it is plausible that their lifestyle choices may have played a significant role in these results.

The present study revealed that even a single instance of low-income status within the five-year period was associated with an increased risk of EOD, which escalated further with sustained low-income status. Consistent with the findings of previous studies [16, 17], this finding indicates the presence of a linear dose-response relationship as the duration of low-income status increases. Factors such as non-adherence to medications for chronic diseases such as hypertension and type 2 diabetes, compounded by the lack of adequate access to healthcare services owing to sustained low-income status or adverse income changes, may have exacerbated the risk of EOD [27, 28]. Furthermore, the participants in the low-income groups often engaged in unhealthy behaviors, including excessive smoking and drinking and lower levels of physical activity, further increasing the risk of EOD [24–26].

Participants with a high-income status over the five-year period were associated with a reduced risk of EOD, with the lowest risk being observed in those who consistently maintained a high-income status. In addition, any rise in income status over this five-year period was linked to a lower incidence of dementia. Consistent with the findings of previous studies, higher income was correlated with a decreased risk of dementia [29]. This effect may be attributed to improved access to quality medical services and enhanced management of chronic diseases that increase the risk of dementia [28]. Moreover, individuals with higher incomes typically engaged in healthier lifestyle practices, which may have also mitigated the risk of dementia [24–26].

The association between the risk of EOD and income parameters exhibited no changes after subdividing the dementia group into the Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia groups. Few studies have examined the relationship between dementia and low SES by differentiating vascular dementia from Alzheimer’s disease in a single study. Notably, consistent with this finding, low SES was associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia in a UK Biobank study [30].

The strength of the present study lies in its longitudinal design, encompassing a large population of over 2 million participants, and the use of objective income data, which minimized exposure bias. Furthermore, the present study provides insights into income dynamics, rather than cross-sectional income levels. Few studies have explored the risk factors for EOD [4, 5]. The present study identified new variables that warrant further exploration. Nevertheless, there are some limitations to this study, such as the possibility of overlooking cases of dementia owing to the relatively short follow-up period and omission of key determinants of SES, such as education level and occupation. In addition, household members contributing to or reliant on household income and the total household income were not assessed. Although health insurance premiums serve as a proxy for income, they do not capture the actual individual income levels or changes within the same year. Because the NHIS income rank was not standardized by age or sex, age- and sex-related differences in earning patterns should be considered when interpreting these findings. We sought to minimize this concern by restricting the cohort to adults aged 40–60 years at baseline, censoring follow-up at age 65, adjusting for age and sex in all multivariable models, and conducting subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, some residual confounding related to life-stage differences in income patterns may remain. Other potential confounding factors, such as healthcare utilization patterns, dietary habits and a family history of dementia, were not considered. The effect size of the association between income fluctuations and the risk of EOD may have been overestimated by the global economic crisis of 2007–2008 and the subsequent economic recovery during the study period. Lastly, these findings may not be generalizable to other socioeconomic cultures or ethnic groups as the study focused on the Korean population.

In conclusion, the present study revealed that sustained low-income status, income decline, and higher income variability are associated with an increased risk of EOD. In contrast, sustained high-income status and income increase are linked to a reduced risk. Thus, new public health strategies aimed at mitigating or managing the incidence of EOD must be developed.
