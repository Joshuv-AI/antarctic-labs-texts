A comparative study on the clinical efficacy and complications of proximal femoral nail antirotation and femoral neck system in the treatment of basicervical femoral neck fracture (AO/OTA 31-B3)
Authors: Xiaole Jiang, Dongze Lin, Yixin Huang, Jiajie Liu, Fengfei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesBasicervical femoral neck fractures (FNFs) are prone to internal fixation failure owing to their inherent biomechanical instability. This study compares the efficacy of femoral neck system (FNS) and proximal femoral nail anti-rotation (PFNA) in treating basicervical FNF.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 99 patients with basicervical FNF admitted to fuzhou second general hospital from January 2020 to June 2024. Patients were divided into two groups based on the internal fixation method used: the FNS group (53 cases, average age 63.83 ± 18.80 years) and the PFNA group (46 cases, average age 69.78 ± 16.65 years). Main observation indicators included reduction quality and internal fixation-related complications, while secondary indicators included tip-apex distance, surgical time, occult blood loss, wound length, postoperative hospital stay, mortality, and Harris hip score.ResultsThe average follow-up time was 24.03 ± 9.4 months. Preoperative data were comparable between groups (P > 0.05). The PFNA group had a significantly lower overall incidence of implant-related complications than the FNS group (2.17% vs. 16.98%, P = 0.035), and the distribution of implant-related complication subtypes also differed significantly between groups (P = 0.042). No significant differences were observed in fracture reduction quality, tip-apex distance, postoperative hospital stay, or mortality (P > 0.05). The FNS group had significantly shorter incision length (4.60 ± 1.01 cm vs. 7.76 ± 1.32 cm, P < 0.001), less hidden blood loss (204.91 ± 61.66 ml vs. 391.27 ± 136.71 ml, P < 0.001), and shorter surgical time (0.62 ± 0.20 h vs. 0.70 ± 0.23 h, P = 0.048). In the full-cohort analysis including all patients with available HHS data, the Harris Hip Score was significantly higher in the PFNA group than in the FNS group (92.52 ± 4.15 vs. 89.28 ± 5.78, P = 0.002). After excluding patients with internal fixation failure, the result remained consistent, with the PFNA group showing a significantly higher HHS than the FNS group (92.78 ± 3.81 vs. 90.37 ± 4.35, P = 0.005).ConclusionsFor AO/OTA 31-B3 basicervical FNF, PFNA was associated with fewer implant-related complications and higher final follow-up HHS than FNS. The HHS result remained consistent after excluding patients with internal fixation failure. Considering the retrospective design and the small number of complication events, PFNA may be considered a favorable fixation option, but these findings should be validated in larger prospective studies.

Introduction

As a relatively rare type of hip fracture, basicervical femoral neck fracture accounts for 1.8% to 7.6% of all hip fractures [1]. In the present study, we defined basicervical femoral neck fracture as a fracture located at the base of the femoral neck, at the junction between the femoral neck and the intertrochanteric region, without extension into the greater or lesser trochanter. According to the AO/OTA classification, these fractures were classified as AO/OTA 31-B3 [2]. This fracture pattern is characterized by a fracture line at the junction of the femoral neck and the intertrochanteric crest. Due to sparse spongy bone structure, significant bone hardness gradient and diameter differences at the fracture ends, and weak surrounding soft tissue attachment, their biomechanical instability is much higher [2–5]. This inherent instability increases the risk of post-fixation complications like fixation failure, making treatment more complex [6]. A previous systematic review reported an overall fixation failure rate of approximately 13% for basicervical femoral neck fractures [7]. The high failure rate after treatment indirectly leads to a higher mortality and disability rate. Therefore, this type of fracture is increasingly drawing attention from the medical community. Research indicates that both intramedullary and extramedullary fixation can be used to treat this type of fracture. Hu et al. found that patients with basicervical FNF who received intramedullary fixation had good postoperative recovery through follow - up of 32 cases. This is contrary to the retrospective study of 11 cases of this type of fracture by Watson et al., who reported an intramedullary fixation failure rate as high as 55% and thus favored extramedullary fixation, noting that if intramedullary fixation was to be performed, a spiral blade intramedullary nail should be used [5, 8]. However, Lee et al. proposed the opposite view based on an analysis of risk factors for complications in 69 cases of basicervical femoral neck fracture, suggesting that for basicervical femoral neck fracture, the combination of intramedullary nails and spiral blades is superior to the combination of extramedullary plates and sliding hip screws and is more highly recommended [9].

With the continuous innovation of internal fixation technology, the Swiss Depuy Synthes company has developed a new type of extramedullary fixation device - FNS (Femoral Neck System) [10]. Clinical studies have found that the FNS (Femoral Neck System) demonstrates excellent clinical outcomes in the treatment of femoral neck fractures. It has lower rates of reoperation, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, and nonunion compared to traditional extramedullary internal fixation devices, and is gradually becoming the preferred treatment option for femoral neck fractures [11]. Some studies have suggested that the FNS may have mechanical deficiencies when fixing AO/OTA 31-B3 type basicervical femoral neck fracture [12]. However, there is currently a lack of large-sample clinical evidence on the use of FNS in treating patients with AO/OTA 31-B3 type basicervical femoral neck fracture. Proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA) is a commonly used cephalomedullary intramedullary device for proximal femoral fractures. Owing to its shorter lever arm, central load transfer, and enhanced rotational stability provided by the helical blade, PFNA is also considered a reasonable option for basicervical femoral neck fractures. Therefore, it was selected as the comparator implant in this study [13].

The clinical application of FNS remains relatively recent, and its efficacy and safety in the treatment of basicervical femoral neck fracture still need more clinical evidence to support. This study aims to compare the clinical outcomes of FNS and PFNA in the treatment of AO/OTA 31-B3 type basicervical femoral neck fracture, in order to provide a basis for the selection of a better internal fixation scheme in clinical practice.

Patients and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study compared the clinical efficacy of FNS and PFNA in the treatment of AO/OTA 31-B3 basicervical femoral neck fractures. The study followed the STROBE [14] reporting guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of fuzhou second general hospital (No: 2022,196). According to the revised Declaration of Helsinki in 2013, the data in this study was pseudonymized. Due to its retrospective nature and the absence of additional harm to participants, informed consent was not required. Because basicervical femoral neck fractures are uncommon and this was a retrospective study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Instead, all eligible patients treated during the predefined study period from January 2020 to June 2024 were included.

Definition of basicervical femoral neck fracture

In this study, a basicervical femoral neck fracture was defined as a fracture located at the junction between the base of the femoral neck and the intertrochanteric region, without extension into the greater or lesser trochanter. According to the AO/OTA classification system, this fracture type was classified as AO/OTA 31-B3 [2]. This definition was used consistently throughout patient screening, radiographic assessment, and data analysis (Fig. 1).

Inclusion criteria

(1) Patients aged ≥ 18 years; (2) Unilateral AO/OTA 31-B3 basicervical femoral neck fracture confirmed on preoperative imaging; (3) Treatment with either FNS or PFNA as the primary internal fixation device; (4) Follow-up time after surgery ≥ 12 months.

Exclusion criteria

(1) fracture line involving the femoral greater or lesser trochanter; (2) fractures with the fracture line extending through the lateral cortex of the greater trochanter; (3) concurrent distal femoral fractures; (4) suspected pathological fractures; (5)Treatment with internal fixation devices other than FNS or PFNA; specifically, all other intramedullary devices and all other extramedullary fixation systems were excluded; (6) patients unable to walk before injury; (7) Incomplete data.

Data source

The data for this study were derived from patients with hip fractures who underwent surgery at the Department of Orthopedic Trauma. We conducted a retrospective data extraction through the specialized clinical research database (HIPMED) constructed and maintained internally by the fuzhou second general hospital. The HIPMED database was a structured institution - specific database designed to standardize the collection and storage of the entire treatment process data for patients with orthopedic trauma. Its core data elements included patient demographic characteristics, injury mechanisms, imaging diagnoses, surgical details, internal fixation implant information, complication records, and postoperative follow - up outcomes, ensuring the integrity and traceability of the study data. We retrospectively searched for patient data from January 2020 to June 2024. The study subjects were patients with hip fractures, including femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures. Using the advanced search function of the database, we initially screened out 5,661 patients with hip fractures. After excluding 2,511 cases of intertrochanteric fractures, a total of 3,424 patients with femoral neck fractures were identified as potential candidates. According to the inclusion criteria, a total of 163 patients with basicervical femoral neck fracture were screened. Through strict screening of patient data according to the exclusion criteria, a total of 99 patients with basicervical femoral neck fracture were screened out and divided into two groups(FNS group :53 cases ; PFNA group :46 cases). This process ensured the accuracy of the study data and the reliability of the study results (Fig. 2).

Surgical procedures

By reviewing the surgical records, we ensured that all included patients underwent surgery performed by orthopedic surgeons with over 10 years of clinical experience at our hospital.

FNS group

General anesthesia combined with nerve block anesthesia was administered, and the patient was placed in the supine position. The affected limb was continuously tracted to reduce the fracture. Under fluoroscopy with a “C”-arm X-ray machine, once satisfactory fracture reduction was achieved, two Kirschner wires were inserted towards the femoral head for temporary fixation. An incision was made on the lateral side of the affected hip. A guide pin was placed in the center of the femoral neck. After measuring the depth and reaming the entry point, a dynamic nail and a bone plate were inserted along the guide pin. The bone plate was closely apposed to the lateral wall of the femoral trochanter. Under the protection of a sleeve, a suitable lateral femoral wall screw was inserted, followed by an appropriate anti-rotation screw through the sleeve. Fluoroscopy with the “C”-arm X-ray machine confirmed satisfactory fracture reduction and secure fixation. The incision was then irrigated, and thorough hemostasis was achieved with electrocautery. The wound was closed layer by layer to the skin, and a sterile dressing was applied.

Lateral plate screw selection: FNS used an angular - stable lateral locking plate available in 1 - or 2 - hole configurations for standard locking screws. Intraoperatively, the number of screws was chosen based on fracture stability, Pauwels angle, and bone quality. For fractures with high shear forces (e.g., Pauwels III) or when initial positioning was unstable, 2 locking screws were used to enhance rotational and shear stability. For relatively stable fractures or good bone quality, 1 locking screw could be selected to simplify the procedure and reduce implant volume.

PFNA group

General anesthesia combined with nerve block anesthesia was administered, and the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position. Closed reduction was first performed under continuous traction and fluoroscopic guidance. Reduction quality was assessed intraoperatively on both anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views, and fixation proceeded only after satisfactory reduction had been achieved and maintained. A longitudinal incision of approximately 6 cm was made proximal to the tip of the greater trochanter. After sequential dissection of the skin, subcutaneous tissue, fascia, and iliotibial band, the entry point was established at the tip of the greater trochanter under fluoroscopic guidance, and the medullary canal was prepared. A suitable PFNA nail was inserted, and a guidewire was advanced into the femoral head through the femoral neck using the aiming device. After confirming an appropriate position on both anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views, a helical blade of appropriate length was inserted, followed by distal locking and end cap placement. Final fluoroscopy confirmed satisfactory reduction and stable fixation. The wound was irrigated, hemostasis was achieved, and the incision was closed in layers. No cement augmentation of the PFNA blade was performed in any patient.

Perioperative management and follow-up

Patients were required to fast from midnight before surgery (a 12-hour fast). After surgery, patients were allowed to attempt drinking water once they regained consciousness. If no symptoms such as nausea or vomiting occurred, they could gradually resume their regular diet. To prevent infection, patients received prophylactic cefazolin sodium treatment from 30 min before surgery to 24 h after surgery. Tranexamic acid was routinely administered postoperatively for hemostasis. During hospitalization, to prevent the formation of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the lower limbs, patients were prescribed rivaroxaban orally. It should be noted that this medication was discontinued 12 h before surgery and could be resumed the day after surgery. Patients were also instructed to perform isometric contractions of the quadriceps and ankle pump exercises to prevent DVT in the lower limbs.

The postoperative rehabilitation protocol was divided into clearly defined phases. On postoperative day 1, both groups began early mobilization under the supervision of a physical therapist. This phase included sitting out of bed, transfer training, ankle-pump exercises, and protected partial weight-bearing ambulation with a walker. Weight bearing at this stage was pain-guided and limited to forefoot-contact or toe-touch partial weight bearing; full weight bearing was not permitted. From postoperative days 2 to 5, protected partial weight-bearing training was continued with assistance, and the walking distance was gradually increased according to pain tolerance, muscle control, and the patient’s general medical condition. During inpatient rehabilitation, patients performed approximately 30 min of protected partial weight-bearing walking training daily until discharge, with walking distance generally limited to 6–10 m. At approximately 1 week after surgery, patients were allowed to practice stair negotiation with crutches only under full supervision and fall-prevention protection. From 6 to 12 weeks postoperatively, patients continued tolerable partial weight-bearing training if follow-up radiographs showed maintained reduction, no implant migration, and progressive fracture healing. The degree of loading was gradually increased but remained below full weight bearing unless radiographic healing was sufficient. Between 12 and 16 weeks postoperatively, transition to full weight bearing was considered only when radiographs, and CT when clinically necessary, demonstrated continuous callus bridging or stable fracture healing without signs of fixation failure. Therefore, pain tolerance guided the intensity of protected partial weight bearing during early rehabilitation, whereas radiological evidence of fracture healing was required before progression to full weight bearing.This rehabilitation protocol was formulated based on clinical evidence and biomechanical principles, with particular consideration of the anatomical and biomechanical characteristics of basicervical femoral neck fractures. At discharge, family members were informed of the importance of assisting patients with continued rehabilitation training and fall prevention. The physical therapist also conducted regular follow-up calls to guide and supervise subsequent functional training. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 18 months postoperatively. At each visit, two independent orthopedic surgeons conducted clinical and radiographic assessments simultaneously. Functional status was quantified using the Harris Hip Score (HHS). All data were entered into the HipMed database in real-time to ensure long-term traceability.

Data collection and outcome measures

The initial information collected included demographic details such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI), Smoking and alcohol use history, Garden classification, Pauwels classification, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, and classification of internal fixation devices.

This study treated alcohol use as a binary variable:

No: Referred to non-drinkers or those who only occasionally consumed a very small amount of alcohol in social settings.

Yes: Defined as meeting any of the following criteria: ① Medical records indicated a history of long-term alcohol use; ② Admission diagnosis included “alcohol use disorder” or “alcohol-related liver disease”; ③ Self-reported daily alcohol intake > 28 g of pure alcohol.

Primary outcomes

Reduction quality

The quality of fracture reduction was evaluated using the Gotfried reduction. In the Gotfried system, reduction is classified into neutral buttress, positive buttress, and negative buttress [15].

Implant-related complications

Peri-implant fracture: Fractures around the implant caused by trauma or other reasons, confirmed by X - ray or CT examination.

Internal fixation failure: This includes implant cutout and failure, detected by imaging studies showing displacement, loosening, or fracture of the fixation device.

Secondary outcomes

TAD (tip-apex distance)

The TAD is determined by measuring the distance from the tip of the screw (Tip) to the apex (Apex), which is the intersection extended from the central axis of the femoral head-neck and the articular surface of the femoral head. This measurement is taken in the anteroposterior (Xap) and lateral (Xlat) views. The magnification factor between the actual bolt width (Dtrue) and the bolt width measured on the X-ray (Dap and Dlat) needs to be corrected. The formula for calculating the TAD value is: TAD = Xap × (Dtrue/Dap) + Xlat × (Dtrue/Dlat) [16].

Immediate postoperative neck-shaft angle

On the first postoperative day, the Neck-Shaft Angle of the femur was measured via follow-up X-ray to assess the surgical correction effect and early postoperative recovery.

Hidden bleeding

Perioperative hidden blood loss was estimated as the difference between total blood loss and visible blood loss. Visible blood loss was derived primarily from intraoperative anesthesia and surgical records. In our institution, intraoperative blood loss was routinely estimated based on the volume collected in suction bottles together with the recorded use of gauze and dressings; when available, the volume of irrigation fluid was deducted from the total suction volume to reduce overestimation. Hidden blood loss, mainly resulting from postoperative oozing into the surrounding soft tissues, cannot be measured directly and was therefore calculated indirectly as total blood loss minus visible blood loss. Total red blood cell loss was estimated as preoperative blood volume (PBV) × (preoperative hematocrit − postoperative hematocrit). The preoperative hematocrit value used was the one closest to surgery, and the postoperative hematocrit value was the earliest available postoperative measurement. PBV was calculated using Nadler’s formula: PBV = k1 × height³ + k2 × weight + k3. For male patients, k1 = 0.3669, k2 = 0.03219, and k3 = 0.6041; for female patients, k1 = 0.3561, k2 = 0.03308, and k3 = 0.1833. Total blood loss was then estimated as total red blood cell loss divided by the preoperative hematocrit [17, 18].When available, blood transfusion and fluid infusion records from anesthesia and nursing charts were also incorporated into the calculation to improve the estimation of perioperative blood loss. Because this was a retrospective study, the accuracy of blood loss estimation depended on the completeness and consistency of routine clinical documentation throughout the study period. Therefore, these values should be interpreted as estimates rather than exact measurements.

Remaining indicators

Wound length, postoperative hospital stay, surgical time, and mortality were extracted from the HipMed database.

Harris hip score

Patients were evaluated at 2 months, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and during the last follow-up after surgery using the Harris Hip Score (HHS), which evaluates hip function in four domains: pain, function, range of motion, and deformity. The maximum score is 100 points. Scores from 90 to 100 are considered excellent, 80 to 89 good, 70 to 79 fair, and below 70 poor [19]. The primary HHS analysis included all patients with available follow-up HHS data, including patients who experienced internal fixation failure. Because internal fixation failure and subsequent revision surgery may influence final functional assessment, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed after excluding patients with internal fixation failure. The research team collected patient outcome data through regular telephone and outpatient questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used for continuous data to determine whether the data were normally distributed. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median (P25, P75). The Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical data were presented as frequency (%). Pearson’s chi-square test was used for categorical variables when the assumptions for chi-square testing were met, and Fisher’s exact test was used for sparse data or small event numbers. The significance level α was set at 0.05 (two-sided). P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary outcome of implant-related complications using the observed complication rates in the two groups and a two-sided α level of 0.05. Because the power analysis was performed after data collection, it was considered exploratory and was used only to evaluate the statistical interpretability of the findings. For HHS, the primary analysis was performed in the full cohort with available HHS data. A sensitivity analysis was then performed after excluding patients with internal fixation failure to assess whether the functional comparison was affected by these cases.

Results

After screening 5,661 patients with hip fractures, 99 patients with basicervical femoral neck fracture were included in this study. The incidence rate of basicervical femoral neck fracture was 1.75%, consistent with the epidemiological characteristics. Among them, there were 53 patients in the FNS group and 46 patients in the PFNA group. The average (SD) follow-up time for the 99 patients was 24.03 ± 9.4 months (range 12–48 months).

Surgical-related data

On the first postoperative day, TAD, immediate postoperative neck-shaft angle, and reduction quality were measured using postoperative radiographs. No statistically significant differences were observed between the FNS group and the PFNA group in TAD, immediate postoperative neck-shaft angle, or reduction quality (all P > 0.05). Compared with the PFNA group, the FNS group had a significantly shorter incision length (4.60 ± 1.01 cm vs. 7.76 ± 1.32 cm, P < 0.001), lower intraoperative blood loss (40.57 ± 18.98 ml vs. 56.09 ± 34.22 ml, P = 0.008), lower total blood loss (238.49 ± 72.36 ml vs. 441.49 ± 138.03 ml, P < 0.001), and lower hidden blood loss (204.91 ± 61.66 ml vs. 391.27 ± 136.71 ml, P < 0.001). Operation time was also shorter in the FNS group than in the PFNA group (0.62 ± 0.20 h vs. 0.70 ± 0.23 h, P = 0.048) (Table 2).

3.Postoperative follow-up and prognosis

Postoperative follow-up and prognosis

The incidence of implant-related complications was compared between the FNS and PFNA groups. Overall, implant-related complications occurred in 10.1% of patients (10/99). In the binary comparison, the overall incidence of implant-related complications was significantly higher in the FNS group than in the PFNA group (16.98% [9/53] vs. 2.17% [1/46], χ²=4.43, P = 0.035). A post hoc power analysis based on these observed rates showed an achieved power of 78.4% at a two-sided α level of 0.05; approximately 103 patients would be required to reach 80% power under the same assumptions. A separate analysis of complication subtypes also showed a significant difference between groups (χ²=6.35, P = 0.042). In the FNS group, peri-implant fracture occurred in 5 patients (9.4%) and internal fixation failure occurred in 4 patients (7.5%). In contrast, only 1 patient (2.2%) in the PFNA group developed internal fixation failure, and no peri-implant fracture was observed. In the FNS group, the four cases of internal fixation failure underwent total hip arthroplasty, and the five cases of peri-implant fracture were treated with plate fixation (Fig. 4). In the PFNA group, the single case of internal fixation failure was treated with InterTan intramedullary nail fixation (Table 3). At the final follow-up, the full-cohort analysis including all patients with available HHS data showed that the HHS was significantly higher in the PFNA group than in the FNS group (92.52 ± 4.15 vs. 89.28 ± 5.78, P = 0.002). To further assess whether internal fixation failure influenced the functional comparison, a sensitivity analysis was performed after excluding patients with internal fixation failure, including 4 patients in the FNS group and 1 patient in the PFNA group. The result remained consistent, with the PFNA group showing a significantly higher HHS than the FNS group (92.78 ± 3.81 vs. 90.37 ± 4.35, P = 0.005) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study comparatively analyzed the clinical outcomes of the femoral neck system (FNS) and the proximal femoral anti-rotation nail (PFNA) in treating basicervical femoral neck fracture. The results showed that although blood loss, operation time, and incision length were greater in the PFNA group than in the FNS group (P < 0.05), the PFNA group had a lower incidence of implant-related complications. PFNA was also associated with a higher final follow-up HHS in the full-cohort analysis including all patients with available HHS data. After excluding patients with internal fixation failure, the functional comparison remained consistent, indicating that the HHS result was not driven solely by the inclusion or exclusion of fixation failure cases.

Proximal femoral nail antirotation: fewer complications, better functional recovery

In the treatment of basicervical femoral neck fracture, over 90% of cases can achieve healing. Intramedullary fixation and extramedullary fixation are the two main surgical methods for treating basicervical femoral neck fracture [1, 2, 7, 9, 20]. Due to the rich blood supply of the femoral medullary cavity, the reaming step during intramedullary fixation can lead to increased occult bleeding. In contrast, extramedullary fixation does not require reaming, and the occult bleeding during the perioperative period can be significantly reduced [21]. Our findings are consistent with the above biomechanical considerations. Specifically, the incision length was greater in the PFNA group than in the FNS group. However, incision length alone is insufficient to infer a clinically meaningful increase in infection risk. Therefore, the observed difference in incision length in this study has limited impact on the overall prognosis of patients with hip fractures and is not clinically significant [22]. In this study, the failure rate of internal fixation in the FNS group was much higher than that in the PFNA group. This result may be related to the biomechanical advantages of PFNA: intramedullary fixation results in a shorter lever arm and a fulcrum closer to the medial anatomical position, which can effectively support the proximal bone fragment and prevent displacement [23]. Meanwhile, the helical blade of PFNA enhances the anti-rotation stability significantly by rotating and compressing the cancellous bone [13, 24].

In terms of functional recovery, Guo et al. retrospectively reviewed patients with basicervical femoral neck fractures treated with proximal femoral nail antirotation and reported satisfactory follow-up outcomes and postoperative hip function [1]. This finding is in line with our results. Although PFNA may be associated with slightly more occult blood loss and a longer incision during the procedure, its significantly lower rate of internal fixation failure and reliable functional prognosis indicate that PFNA may be a better choice in the treatment of basicervical femoral neck fractures.

Postoperative rehabilitation strategies and the timing of weight-bearing

In 2016, Watson et al. [5] reported that among 11 patients with basicervical femoral neck fractures treated with intramedullary nails, the failure rate was as high as 55%, and they concluded that DHS is superior to intramedullary nails in the treatment of this type of fracture.However, this conclusion contradicts the findings of Hu et al. [25] and Kim et al. [26]. Hu et al. found through postoperative follow-up that patients treated with CMN had good recovery after surgery. Kim et al. found through comparison that the sliding hip screw has a higher tendency for excessive displacement when used to treat basicervical femoral neck fractures, indicating that DHS is less stable than intramedullary nails in treating basicervical femoral neck fractures. Based on the existing evidence, the academic community believes that the conclusion drawn by Watson et al. may be due to Premature full weight-bearing after intramedullary nail fixation for this type of fracture, rather than a design flaw of the intramedullary nail itself.

Basicervical femoral neck fractures present unique biomechanical challenges in postoperative rehabilitation due to their anatomical location at the junction between the neck and the trochanter. The fracture line in these cases is typically oblique and located at the intersection of the femoral neck and the trochanter. The medial cortex often lacks effective support. Under axial loading, this area is prone to significant varus moments, which increase the risk of redisplacement after reduction and may further lead to fatigue fractures of the internal fixation devices [27]. In addition, this area is a transitional zone from cortical bone to cancellous bone. The proximal bone is relatively dense, while the distal bone is more porous, forming a mechanically asymmetric structure that is “hard on top and soft on the bottom.” Premature full weight-bearing can lead to changes such as shortening of the femoral neck and reduction of the abductor lever arm. These changes not only disrupt gait and joint function but also increase the risk of recurrent falls due to muscle imbalance and significantly raise the failure rate of internal fixation [28]. This inherent instability has traditionally formed the biomechanical basis for strict weight-bearing restrictions. However, increasing clinical evidence questions the effectiveness and safety of strict weight-bearing restrictions, especially in the elderly patient population. Studies have found that postoperative weight-bearing restrictions are independently associated with an increased 30-day mortality rate, as well as higher rates of major adverse events and surgical site infections [29].

The core of modern rehabilitation concepts lies in adopting an individualized balanced strategy that takes into account both mechanical stability and early mobilization. The primary foundation is to achieve sufficient stability through internal fixation, and then shift the rehabilitation focus to encouraging patients to engage in early tolerance-based weight-bearing under close monitoring — that is, a gradual process starting with partial weight-bearing, guided by pain, and progressively increasing, aiming to promote bone healing while minimizing mechanical complications [30]. To ensure the safe implementation of this strategy, the following key safeguards are essential: regular imaging follow-ups to objectively assess fracture healing and the status of internal fixation; systematic training under the guidance of professional therapists to ensure proper movement, prevent falls, and monitor patient responses in real-time. The entire process should emphasize “progression” rather than “prohibition,” always keeping weight-bearing within the tolerable range of the patient, thereby maximizing rehabilitation outcomes while ensuring safety [29, 31].

Therefore, for basicervical femoral neck fracture, it is necessary to carefully and appropriately select internal fixation devices and formulate correct rehabilitation plans.

The radiographic manifestations of FNS internal fixation failure

Although the difference between 1-hole and 2-hole FNS constructs may be clinically relevant, no formal subgroup analysis was performed in this study because of the limited sample size and small number of failure events. Therefore, the following observations should be interpreted as exploratory rather than conclusive. This study analyzed internal fixation failure into two categories: Internal fixation failure and peri-implant fracture. In this study, the incidence of internal fixation failure with FNS was 7.5%, and the incidence of peri-implant fracture was 9.4%. Finite element studies have found that in the basicervical femoral neck fracture model, the stress in FNS fixation with a single-hole lateral plate is mainly concentrated in the contact area between the bolt and the femoral shaft, while the stress in FNS fixation with a double-hole lateral plate is mainly concentrated at the distal screw - bone interface of the lateral plate. This is highly consistent with the locations of internal fixation failure in this study [12]. In the cases of internal fixation failure in this study, FNS with a single-hole lateral plate exhibited fractures around the cortical bone at the distal end of the locking hole (Fig. 4). This can be attributed to the fact that the single-hole lateral plate of FNS results in an insufficient working length of the locking screw, preventing the load from being adequately distributed to the distal bone. Consequently, a sudden change in cross-sectional area occurs at the end of the locking hole, leading to a concentration of local stress. This stress concentration causes microcracks to first appear in the cortical bone surrounding the screw, which then propagate into splitting fractures. Unlike the former, the FNS case using a double - hole lateral plate had a fracture at the distal plate - screw junction (Fig. 5). Fan et al. ‘s [32] study showed that the two distal locking screws can enhance the shear resistance of FNS and are more suitable for unstable fractures. However, they also lead to excessive stress at the distal plate - screw junction, resulting in distal plate - screw junction fracture.

Therefore, during the clinical treatment phase, when selecting internal fixation devices, if the fracture is unstable and requires enhanced shear resistance, the double - hole lateral plate FNS can provide stronger support. However, early weight - bearing should be avoided to prevent excessive stress at the distal connection. For more stable fractures, the single - hole lateral plate FNS may be more suitable, but stress concentration due to insufficient working length should be avoided.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, as patients were not randomly assigned to treatment groups. Second, several potential confounders, such as bone mineral density, fracture complexity, and posterior or lateral cortical comminution, were not available and may have affected fixation stability and prognosis. Third, the lack of age restriction may have increased population heterogeneity. Fourth, although the primary HHS analysis included patients with internal fixation failure, their final functional scores may have been influenced by revision surgery and recovery after secondary procedures; therefore, the sensitivity analysis excluding these cases should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of failure events. Fifth, no a priori sample size calculation was performed, and only 10 implant-related complications occurred among 99 patients. Although the post hoc power for the primary comparison was 78.4%, the limited number of events may have reduced statistical precision and increased the risk of type II error. In addition, the actual timing of full weight bearing and return to daily or recreational activities was not systematically recorded, and perioperative blood loss was estimated retrospectively rather than measured prospectively using a standardized protocol. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger prospective studies.

Future research

Future research should focus on large-sample long-term follow-up studies to evaluate the clinical efficacy of FNS and PFNA in treating basicervical femoral neck fracture. In addition, larger-sample randomized controlled trials are necessary to provide stronger evidence for the clinical application of PFNA in fixing basicervical femoral neck fracture. Further research can explore the biomechanical properties of FNS and PFNA in repairing different types of basicervical femoral neck fracture and their impact on fracture healing.

Conclusion

In conclusion, compared with FNS, PFNA was associated with a lower rate of implant-related complications and higher Harris Hip Score at final follow-up in patients with AO/OTA 31-B3 basicervical femoral neck fractures. The functional advantage of PFNA remained after excluding patients with internal fixation failure in the sensitivity analysis. These findings suggest that PFNA may be a more favorable fixation option for this fracture type, although the retrospective design, small number of complication events, and potential influence of revision surgery on HHS require cautious interpretation and further validation in larger prospective studies.
