Effects of insect-derived protein form and replacement level on growth performance and skeletal traits in broiler pheasants (Phasianus colchicus)
Authors: Cezary Osiak-Wicha, Katarzyna Kras, Ewa Tomaszewska, Grzegorz Rytlewski, Marian Flis, Iwona Puzio, Michał Świetlicki, Piotr Czyżowski, Sławomir Beeger, Marcin B. Arciszewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) products as alternatives to soybean meal protein in broiler pheasants, focusing on growth performance and skeletal traits in both sexes. Birds were assigned to five dietary treatments: a soybean meal control and diets in which 50% or 100% of soybean protein was replaced with either insect meal or dried larvae.Growth performance was recorded, and bone quality was assessed using densitometry, mechanical testing, and geometrical analysis of the femur and tibia. In males, insect-based diets increased body weight and thigh muscle mass, whereas females showed only minor or non-significant changes in performance traits. Skeletal responses were sex- and bone-specific. In males, higher replacement levels were associated with improved bone mineralization and enhanced structural mechanical properties, particularly in the tibia. In contrast, females exhibited more pronounced changes in apparent material properties of bone tissue, with relatively minor effects on bone geometry and whole-bone strength.Differences between insect meal and dried larvae were evident in several skeletal parameters, particularly in tibial apparent material properties, indicating that the physical form of the insect-derived ingredient influenced bone tissue behavior rather than gross bone size alone. Overall, skeletal responses reflected combined effects of dietary composition and growth-related mechanical loading.These findings indicate that skeletal responses reflect adaptive adjustments of bone to combined nutritional and mechanical stimuli, rather than uniform changes in bone mass or size.

Introduction

Soybean meal remains the primary protein source in poultry diets due to its high crude protein content, favorable amino acid profile, and consistent availability (Ravindran, 2013). Nevertheless, concerns related to sustainability, market volatility, and supply security have intensified interest in alternative feed ingredients capable of sustaining growth performance while maintaining skeletal integrity under intensive rearing conditions (Rath et al., 2000). Among these, insect-derived proteins have emerged as promising candidates, with black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) receiving considerable attention (Makkar et al., 2014; Veldkamp and Bosch, 2015).

Black soldier fly larvae provide highly digestible protein and lipid fractions and can be incorporated into poultry diets in different physical forms, including dried larvae and processed meals (Bellezza Oddon et al., 2021; Schäfer et al., 2023). Studies in chickens indicate that Hermetia-based ingredients can be included in broiler and layer diets without detrimental effects on growth or production, although responses depend on inclusion level, processing method, and diet formulation (Khan, 2018). Importantly, the physical form and structural characteristics of insect-derived ingredients may influence feeding behavior and nutrient utilization beyond their chemical composition alone. Feeding live black soldier fly larvae to laying hens has been shown to modify feeding patterns and activity without impairing egg quality or health parameters, suggesting that ingredient form represents a biologically relevant factor in poultry nutrition (Rytlewski et al., 2025b).

Rapid growth in poultry places substantial demands on the skeletal system, making bone integrity a critical indicator of nutritional adequacy (Rath et al., 2000). Bone strength reflects not only mineralization but also geometry and material properties, which together determine resistance to mechanical loading (Whitehead, 2004). In broiler chickens, tibial morphology and strength-related traits are closely associated with growth rate and body weight (Kim et al., 2012), and nutritional interventions can modify bone mineral density and mechanical competence even in the absence of changes in body weight (Dijkslag et al., 2021).

In contrast to chickens, information on insect-based diets in gamebirds remains limited despite increasing intensification of pheasant production systems (Whiteside et al., 2015). Pheasant production depends not only on growth performance but also on skeletal development, which may influence locomotion, welfare, and overall fitness. Moreover, responses to nutritional interventions may differ between sexes due to differences in growth dynamics, endocrine regulation, and the multifactorial control of musculoskeletal development, including hormonal, metabolic, and mechanical factors. Recent work has shown that replacing soybean meal with black soldier fly products in pheasant breeders alters egg composition and production traits, consistent with biologically relevant effects of insect-derived proteins in this species (Rytlewski et al., 2025b). However, it remains unclear whether continued exposure to insect-based diets influences post-hatch growth and skeletal development.

We hypothesized that partial replacement of soybean meal with insect-derived protein would maintain growth performance and skeletal integrity, whereas complete replacement and differences in ingredient form would differentially affect bone characteristics in a sex-dependent manner. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the effects of partial and complete replacement of soybean meal with black soldier fly products provided in different physical forms on growth performance and bone characteristics in pheasants, with bone traits serving as indicators of nutritional adequacy under alternative protein feeding strategies.

Materials and methods

Birds, housing, and experimental design

The study was conducted on common pheasants (Phasianus colchicus) originating from breeders previously involved in a feeding trial in which soybean meal was partially or completely replaced with black soldier fly products (Rytlewski et al., 2025b). The birds used in the present experiment therefore represented a subsequent generation exposed to comparable dietary protein sources at the parental level.

After hatching, birds were reared under standardized management conditions and allocated to five dietary treatments. Each dietary treatment consisted of separate pens for males and females. Within each sex and dietary group, birds were distributed among eight pens, with eight birds per pen. This arrangement allowed the pen to be treated as the experimental unit for allocation while minimizing potential effects of social hierarchy and stocking density. Each pen was equipped with one feeder and one drinker per eight birds, ensuring adequate access to feed and water throughout the experimental period. Birds were housed in floor pens under controlled environmental conditions appropriate for pheasant rearing. Housing conditions included environmental enrichment (shelter roofing, sand substrate, branches, and protective netting), providing opportunities for locomotion, foraging, and expression of species-specific behaviours.

The experimental diets differed in the level and form of soybean meal replacement with black soldier fly products. Diet 1 served as the control and contained soybean meal as the sole protein source, with no inclusion of insect-derived ingredients. In Diet 2, 50% of soybean meal protein was replaced with larval meal (IM50), whereas Diet 3 involved complete replacement of soybean meal protein with larval meal (IM100). In Diet 4, 50% of soybean meal protein was replaced with dried larvae (DL50), and in Diet 5, soybean meal protein was completely replaced with dried larvae (DL100). All diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of growing pheasants and were provided ad libitum, with unrestricted access to water.

Birds were slaughtered at 112 days of age (16 weeks). A total of sixteen birds per dietary treatment were selected for detailed analyses, including eight males and eight females (n = 8). To avoid pseudo-replication, one bird was randomly selected from each pen, ensuring that all sampled individuals originated from different pens. Selected birds were weighed prior to sampling and subsequently used for analyses of body weight and skeletal characteristics. Throughout the experimental period, birds were monitored daily for health status and general behavior.

Diets and feeding

From 5 weeks of age, pheasants were offered Grower and Finisher complete compound feeds in five dietary treatments (Table 1). The control diet was based on standard plant-derived ingredients used in pheasant nutrition and was formulated in line with established recommendations (EU Commission Regulation 152/2009) and feed composition references (Wise and Ewins, 1980; Whiteside et al., 2015). Soybean meal served as the primary supplemental protein ingredient in the control formulation. Diets were formulated to provide comparable calcium and total phosphorus levels across treatments according to established recommendations. No additional phytase was included, and mineral digestibility was not directly assessed. Chitin content of insect products was not determined; therefore, potential effects of chitin on nutrient and mineral utilization were not directly assessed. In the experimental diets, soybean meal was partially or completely substituted with black soldier fly products supplied by HiProMine S.A. (Robakowo, Poland). Two insect product forms were evaluated: full-fat larval meal (Insect meal) and whole dried larvae. Black soldier fly larvae were reared on a mixture of plant by-products. Whole larvae intended for dietary inclusion were dried and incorporated into the experimental diets as a complete ingredient. For production of full-fat insect meal, the larval biomass was frozen at −20 °C and subsequently dried at 50 °C for 24 h. The dried material was then ground using a hammer mill to obtain particles below 0.1 mm. To ensure hygienic quality, the raw insect material was heat-treated at 100 °C for 95 min, in accordance with Annex IV of Commission Regulation No 141/2011 establishing health rules for animal by-products. Experimental feeds were produced by weighing all ingredients, applying the intended soybean meal replacement with either insect meal or whole dried larvae, and mixing the components thoroughly prior to pelleting. All diets were granulated at 60 °C under the same manufacturing conditions to minimize processing-related differences among treatments.

Birds had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experimental period. Feed intake was monitored daily by recording the amount of feed provided and the quantity remaining in the feeders, allowing calculation of consumption on a pen basis. The chemical composition of the control and experimental feeds was determined using standardized methods. Proximate analyses of dry matter, crude protein, and ash were conducted according to Commission Regulation (EC) No 152/2009, Annex III (points A, C, H, I, and M, respectively), and AOAC procedures (Table 2). Total phosphorus was assessed according to Commission Regulation (EC) No 152/2009, Annex III, point P, and calcium concentration was measured by flame atomic absorption spectrometry in accordance with ISO 6869:2000. Nitrogen content was determined by the Kjeldahl method and used to calculate crude protein. A general nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25 was applied for conventional protein sources, while diets containing Hermetia illucens meal were additionally recalculated using a species-specific conversion factor of 4.67 to account for non-protein nitrogen associated with chitin (Janssen et al., 2017). Bone analysis

The right femur and tibia from each bird were collected for skeletal assessments, including bone length, bone weight, and relative bone weight (RBW = bone weight / body weight × 100). Immediately after collection, bones were cleaned of adherent soft tissues and stored frozen at −20 °C. Before morphometric measurements and densitometric analysis, bones were thawed overnight at 4 °C. After completion of these procedures, the bones were refrozen at −20 °C and subsequently thawed again overnight at 4 °C prior to three-point bending testing. Thus, each bone underwent 2 freeze-thaw cycles in total.

Whole-bone mineral density was determined by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) using a Lunar densitometer (GE, Madison, WI, USA). Each bone was scanned along its entire length, and bone mineral density (BMD) values for the whole bone region were recorded for subsequent analyses.

Immediately after densitometry, bones were subjected to a three-point bending test to characterize structural mechanical performance. Testing was performed using a universal testing machine Zwick Z010 (Zwick-Roell GmbH & Co., Ulm, Germany). Bones were positioned horizontally on two supports and loaded at the mid-diaphysis. The support span was set to 44 mm for tibiae and 32 mm for femurs, corresponding to approximately 40% of the mean bone length, and loading was applied at a constant crosshead speed of 10 mm/min until failure. Load-displacement curves were recorded and used to derive yield load (Fyield), maximum load (Fmax), stiffness, elastic work (Wyield), and work to maximum load (Wmax). Mechanical data processing was performed in Origin ver. 2022 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA).

Following mechanical testing, bones were sectioned transversely at the mid-diaphysis using a diamond bandsaw (MBS 240/E; Proxxon GmbH, Foehren, Germany). Midshaft external and internal diameters were measured in two orthogonal planes: transversal outer diameter (TRout), transversal inner diameter (TRinn), anteroposterior outer diameter (APout), and anteroposterior inner diameter (APinn). These geometric measurements were used to calculate cross-sectional properties required for bending analysis. Cross-sectional area (CSA), mean relative wall thickness (MRWT), cortical index (Cl), and second moment of inertia (Ix) were calculated according to standard equations for elliptical cross-sections (Demes et al., 1991; Muszyński et al., 2017)

Apparent σ, Ɛ, and E variables were subsequently estimated from whole-bone three-point bending using standard beam-theory equations combined with the measured mid-diaphyseal geometry. Specifically, yield strain (Ɛyield), maximum strain (Ɛmax), Young’s modulus (E), yield stress (σyield), and maximum stress (σmax) were calculated. Because the femur and tibia are not a uniform prismatic beam, these values represent geometry-dependent, structurally derived estimates rather than true material properties. Accordingly, they are interpreted as apparent material properties.

Sternum morphometry, after dissection and cleaning of adherent soft tissues, was quantified using two linear measures, sternum length (Sl) and sternal crest height (Ch), obtained with digital calipers from clearly defined bony landmarks. These measurements were selected because sternum length and crest height are major determinants of keel surface area and, by extension, the skeletal framework available for pectoral muscle attachment (Feneck et al., 2021). To summarize sternal development in a way that reflects attachment capacity rather than a single linear dimension, an estimated keel area (EKA, mm²) was calculated using a right-triangle approximation: EKA = 0.5 × Sl × Ch, where 0.5 represents the geometric constant for the area of a triangle (Casey-Trott et al., 2017). Because sternum dimensions are expected to covary with overall bird size, keel morphometric outcomes were additionally expressed as a body size-adjusted index. Specifically, a relative keel attachment index (KAI) was calculated as KAI = EKA / body weight (g) and reported in mm²/g. This normalization was used to provide a size-corrected descriptor of sternal development, reflecting estimated keel surface area per unit body mass, and to facilitate comparison among birds differing in body weight. Because EKA and KAI are derived from a simplified geometric approximation, they are interpreted as practical morphometric proxies of sternal development that may relate to the potential capacity of the pectoral muscle attachment framework, rather than as direct measurements of pectoral muscle mass, volume, or true attachment surface area. Accordingly, KAI was used as a body size-adjusted index for standardized between-group comparisons under an identical measurement protocol.

Statistical analysis

Growth performance data were analyzed using pen as the experimental unit. For skeletal traits, the individual bird was considered the observational unit; however, because one bird per pen was sampled, each observation represented an independent pen replicate and no pseudo-replication occurred. All analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed within sex. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and the Levene test, respectively. When required, data were transformed to better meet model assumptions. For each response variable, dietary effects were first evaluated using one-way ANOVA with diet (Control, IM50, IM100, DL50, DL100) as the fixed factor. A priori comparisons included a contrast of the control group versus the mean of all insect-based treatments and Dunnett’s test to evaluate each insect-based group against the control (IM50, IM100, DL50, DL100).

To quantify treatment structure among insect-based diets only, the control group was excluded and data were additionally analyzed using a two factorial ANOVA with insect product form (insect meal, IM; whole dried larvae, DL) and replacement level (50% vs 100%) as fixed factors, including their interaction. When a significant form × level interaction was detected, differences among the four insect-fed groups were examined using the Student-Newman-Keuls multiple comparison procedure and denoted by different superscript letters in tables. Results are presented as means ± SEM, and differences were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Growth performance

In male pheasants, final body weight was greater in birds fed insect-based diets than in the control group (P = 0.007; Table 1). Neither replacement level nor insect form affected this trait, and no interaction between these factors was detected (P = 0.187). Thigh muscle mass showed a similar response, being higher in insect-fed birds than in control group (P = 0.006), whereas replacement level, insect form, and their interaction were not significant (P = 0.580). Lower leg muscle mass was not affected by the overall control contrast (P = 0.189), but it differed markedly according to insect form (P < 0.001). Birds fed IM had greater lower leg muscle mass than those fed DL, and the individual treatment means indicated that both insect meal groups exceeded the control, whereas both dried-larvae groups were lower than the control. No effect of replacement level or meal × dose interaction was observed (P = 0.556). Pectoral muscle mass did not differ among treatments (P = 0.183). In female pheasants, final body weight, thigh muscle mass, pectoral muscle mass, and the relative keel attachment index were not affected by dietary treatment (P = 0.999; P = 0.860; P = 0.422 and P = 0.776 respectively). Lower leg muscle mass, however, differed significantly between the control and insect-fed birds (P = 0.002) and was influenced by replacement level, insect form, and their interaction (P < 0.001 for all). Discussion

The present study indicates that the effects of insect-based diets on skeletal traits in pheasants are not uniform, but vary with sex, bone type, and the physical form of the insect-derived ingredient. Overall, insect inclusion increased body weight and selected muscle traits in males, whereas females showed more limited changes in production traits but exhibited shifts in several apparent material properties. Across bones, diet-associated responses were more evident for tibial traits, particularly in females, consistent with the functional role of the tibia as a primary weight-bearing element of the pelvic limb. Collectively, these findings reflect the integrated interaction among nutrient supply, musculoskeletal loading, and sex-dependent physiological regulation (Hughes et al., 2020). Given the large number of measured traits, statistically significant effects were interpreted with caution and considered biologically meaningful primarily when consistent across related parameters or supported by functional interpretation. Isolated significant effects not supported by consistent patterns were interpreted conservatively.

Growth performance and functional loading of the skeleton

Changes in growth performance observed in the present study are essential for interpreting skeletal outcomes. Bone does not respond to nutrition in isolation but integrates dietary effects through their influence on growth rate, muscle development, and mechanical loading (Rath et al., 2000; Santos et al., 2022). In poultry, increases in body mass are accompanied by greater forces transmitted through the appendicular skeleton during standing and locomotion, with a substantial proportion of the load borne by the pelvic limbs (Rath et al., 2000; Knowles et al., 2008; Santos et al., 2022).

In the present experiment, males fed insect-based diets exhibited higher final body weight and greater thigh muscle mass. These changes likely contributed mechanical loading of the hindlimb skeleton, particularly affecting the tibia, which functions as a primary weight-bearing element during standing and locomotion. In broiler chickens, tibial morphology and mechanical competence are closely associated with growth rate and body mass development, and the tibia is frequently identified as a site where rapid growth may challenge structural capacity and contribute to leg health problems (Corr et al., 2003; Santos et al., 2022). Differences among poultry strains further support that tibial geometry and strength-related traits track body mass development and locomotor demands.

In contrast, females showed relatively stable body weight and most muscle traits across dietary treatments, although lower leg muscle mass responded markedly to the level and form of insect inclusion. Because distal hindlimb muscles, including the gastrocnemius complex, are primary generators of ankle moments and contribute directly to forces transmitted through the tibiotarsus, diet-associated changes in lower leg muscle mass may have modified the mechanical environment experienced by the tibia (Robling et al., 2006).

Bone-specific responses: tibia versus femur

The present results indicate that the femur and tibia differed in their sensitivity to dietary treatments, and that this pattern was sex-dependent. Femoral responses were relatively selective, with changes limited to specific geometric and mechanical traits in males, whereas females showed largely stable femoral geometry with only minor alterations in mechanical or apparent material properties. In contrast, the tibia exhibited a broader response profile, including changes in mechanical and apparent material traits and, in females, structural features consistent with altered cortical organization.

These differences are consistent with the distinct functional roles of these bones. The tibia represents the primary load-bearing element of the pelvic limb and is exposed to substantial forces during standing and locomotion, which may promote stronger adaptive responses when body mass or muscle-driven loading increases (Corr et al., 2003). Previous studies in broiler chickens similarly demonstrate that tibial morphology and mechanical properties closely track growth rate and body mass development and are strongly associated with locomotor loading (Santos et al., 2022). In contrast, the femur appears to respond more selectively and in a sex-dependent manner, suggesting lower sensitivity to changes in external loading conditions. This divergence likely reflects differences in functional loading patterns and biomechanical demands between the femur and tibia within the avian pelvic limb. Accordingly, functional loading is a key determinant of how individual bones respond to nutritional interventions (Shim et al., 2012; Paxton et al., 2014).

Sex-dependent skeletal responses

One of the most consistent findings of the present study was the sex-dependent nature of skeletal responses. In males, dietary treatments more frequently affected traits reflecting whole-bone structure and mechanical performance, whereas in females responses were more often expressed in apparent material properties and, in the tibia, in indices consistent with altered cortical organization.

This divergence likely reflects differences in growth dynamics and endocrine regulation between sexes. Sex-related differences in long-bone development have been documented in broiler chickens, with dimorphism in tibial morphology and strength emerging during growth (Rose et al., 1996; Santos et al., 2022). Males typically exhibit greater variability in body mass development and musculoskeletal loading, which may stimulate structural adaptation through mechanobiological pathways (Frost, 2003). In contrast, under more stable body mass conditions, skeletal responses may be expressed primarily at the tissue level, influencing apparent material behavior rather than bone geometry.

More broadly, poultry studies indicate that growth rate and locomotor loading are closely linked to tibial mineralization and mechanical competence, and rapid body mass accretion may increase the risk that skeletal development does not keep pace with production demands (Qin et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2022). Differences in hormonal milieu between sexes may therefore shift the balance between structural modeling and tissue-level adaptation. Sexual dimorphism in bone physiology is well documented and has been associated with differences in sex-steroid signaling, remodeling dynamics, and cellular responsiveness to mechanical stimuli (Seeman, 2001; Zhang et al., 2024). At the cellular level, osteocytes play a central role in integrating mechanical and metabolic signals and coordinating adaptive responses in bone tissue (Bonewald, 2011; Dallas et al., 2013; Qin et al., 2020).

Interpretation within the mechanostat framework

The observed responses can be interpreted within the framework of the mechanostat theory of bone adaptation, which proposes that bone adjusts its structure and material properties to maintain mechanical competence under changing loading conditions. Adaptation may occur through modifications in geometry, mineralization, or intrinsic tissue properties, depending on the magnitude and distribution of mechanical load (Hughes et al., 2020).

In the present study, some responses were consistent with structural adaptation, involving traits such as Fmax, stiffness, and cross-sectional geometry, whereas others were more evident at the level of apparent material behavior. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that bones employ different adaptive strategies to preserve mechanical safety under varying physiological and mechanical conditions (Hart et al., 2017).

Importantly, the dominant mode of response appeared to differ between sexes. In males, dietary effects were more frequently reflected in whole-bone structural and performance traits, whereas in females responses were more consistently expressed in apparent material behavior and, in the tibia, in indices consistent with cortical reorganization. These differences are likely associated with variation in growth dynamics and musculoskeletal loading between sexes.

At the cellular level, osteocytes are thought to mediate these processes by sensing mechanical strain and coordinating adaptive responses, thereby integrating mechanical loading with metabolic and nutritional signals (Robling et al., 2006; Dallas et al., 2013).

Nutritional mechanisms associated with insect-based diets

Replacement of soybean meal with insect-derived ingredients may influence skeletal development through several nutritional mechanisms (Rath et al., 2000). Insect meals differ from conventional plant protein sources not only in amino acid composition but also in lipid profile, mineral availability, and structural components such as chitin (Makkar et al., 2014; De Marco et al., 2015). Black soldier fly larvae meals are particularly characterized by high lipid content, including lauric acid, and the presence of chitin in the exoskeleton (Spranghers et al., 2017). Particularly important may be the availability of phosphorus and its interaction with calcium metabolism. Studies evaluating black soldier fly larvae products have shown that although intestinal digestibility of phosphorus may be relatively high, its bioavailability at the level of bone mineralization may differ substantially (Matin et al., 2021). Consequently, bone mineral density assessed by densitometry does not always correspond directly with mechanical indicators of bone strength (Seeman and Delmas, 2006). This may help explain why densitometric and biomechanical outcomes did not always change in parallel across treatments. Bone strength reflects not only mineral quantity but also the spatial distribution of mineral, collagen-mineral organization, and features of microstructure that contribute to overall bone quality. Although these microstructural characteristics were not directly assessed in the present study, they can substantially influence mechanical competence at a given BMD and may therefore contribute to discordance between densitometric and mechanical indicators of bone quality (Hernandez and Keaveny, 2006; Hart et al., 2017). In the present experiment, this framework may be particularly relevant for the male tibia, where the form of the insect ingredient was associated with shifts in several apparent material indices. Such a pattern is consistent with potential effects of dietary treatment on tissue-level organization. Moreover, tissue-level indicators derived from bending (σ, ε, and E) are less directly governed by whole-bone dimensions than structural outcomes such as Fmax, although they remain geometry-dependent estimates when calculated from whole-bone tests using beam assumptions (Wang et al., 2022). A key limitation of the present study is that chitin content, mineral digestibility, and phosphorus bioavailability were not directly measured, which limits the ability to identify the mechanisms underlying the observed skeletal responses and precludes causal interpretation. Therefore, the proposed mechanisms should be interpreted as biologically plausible explanations supported by existing literature rather than direct evidence from the present study and should be interpreted with caution.

Influence of insect product form and dose

An additional finding of the present study was that the physical form of the insect ingredient influenced skeletal outcomes. Differences between insect meal and dried larvae were most apparent in tibial traits, including several mechanical parameters and apparent material indices. This form dependence is biologically plausible, as processing of larvae into meal and differences in lipid fraction, particle size, and chitin structure may alter digestive kinetics and nutrient availability (Makkar et al., 2014; Matin et al., 2021; Zulkifli et al., 2022). In males, form-related differences in tibial traits were accompanied by divergence in distal hindlimb musculature. Diets containing dried larvae were associated with reduced lower leg muscle mass relative to the control, whereas insect meal tended to increase this trait. These changes may have modified tibial loading not only through body mass but also through variation in muscle forces transmitted across the distal limb, providing a functional explanation for form-dependent skeletal responses.

Differences between IM and DL were also evident in apparent material indices, suggesting that dietary form influenced tissue-level properties rather than gross bone size alone. In this context, material-level parameters derived from bending tests can provide additional insight when changes are not fully reflected in densitometric or structural measures (Turner and Burr, 1993; Hart et al., 2017).

In addition to ingredient form, the level of soybean meal replacement contributed to several skeletal responses, suggesting dose-dependent effects. In males, higher replacement levels were in some cases associated with enhanced mineralization and mechanical performance, particularly in the tibia, although responses depended on ingredient form and were not consistent across all traits. In females, dose-related effects were expressed primarily in apparent material behavior, with limited influence on bone geometry or whole-bone strength.

Such patterns are biologically plausible because progressive substitution modifies not only amino acid supply but also the balance of minerals and structural components associated with insect biomass, including phosphorus availability and its interaction with calcium homeostasis (Makkar et al., 2014; Matin et al., 2021).

The present findings indicate that skeletal responses to insect-based diets in pheasants reflect an integrated interaction among growth performance, musculoskeletal loading, nutrient supply, and sex-dependent physiology. The physical form of the insect-derived ingredient further modulated these responses, suggesting that processing and ingredient structure are biologically relevant beyond simple inclusion level.

Overall, the results do not indicate a uniform deterioration of skeletal integrity following replacement of soybean meal with black soldier fly products under the conditions of this experiment. However, the occurrence of sex- and bone-specific responses, including changes in selected mechanical and apparent material indices, indicates that skeletal outcomes are context dependent. These findings support the use of black soldier fly products as alternative protein sources in pheasant diets, while highlighting the importance of considering sex, bone type, and ingredient form when evaluating skeletal safety and performance in poultry feeding strategies.
