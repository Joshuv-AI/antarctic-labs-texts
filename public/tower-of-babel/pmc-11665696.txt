Fish oil attenuates the expression of the CCL2 chemokine and histone-modifying enzymes in LPS-stimulated human preadipocytes
Authors: Jussara de Jesus Simão, Andressa França de Sousa Bispo, Victor Tadeu Gonçalves Plata, Ana Beatriz Marques Abel, Monica Marques Telles, Lucia Maria Armelin-Correa, Maria Isabel Cardoso Alonso-Vale
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In obesity, C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) plays a critical role in recruiting macrophages to white adipose tissue (WAT), contributing to chronic inflammation. In this study, we sought to explore the effects of fish oil (FO) on CCL2 expression and histone (H3K27)-modifying enzymes in both human model of preadipocytes and primary adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs). Present findings in preadipocytes lineage evidenced that lipopolysaccharide (LPS) increased TNF-alpha (∼5.8-fold) and CCL2 (∼3.8-fold) expression, modulating H3K27 modifying enzymes expression, including KDM6B and EP300. FO, in turn, significantly attenuated LPS-induced CCL2 expression and secretion and downregulated KDM6B and EP300, elucidating an important mechanism of action involved in the anti-inflammatory role of FO. We next isolated mature hypertrophied adipocytes from patient with overweight and exposed to LPS, resulting in increased CCL2/MCP-1 (∼3.8-fold) and TNF-alpha (∼4.5-fold) expression, effects significantly attenuated by FO. We also generated adipocyte-conditioned medium (ACM) and exposed ASCs to LPS or ACM for up to 72 h to assess CCL2/MCP-1 secretion. ACM from hypertrophied adipocytes stimulated increased CCL2/MCP-1 expression, which was partially reduced by FO. LPS treatment of primary ASCs led to a marked increase in CCL2 secretion, which was completely abolished by FO after 6 h, highlighting its potent anti-inflammatory effect. After 72 h, FO consistently maintained lower levels of CCL2, even during sustained inflammatory stimulation, underscoring its ability to modulate chronic inflammation. Additionally, the inhibition of NF-κB with JSH-23 mimicked the effects of FO on CCL2 expression, further suggesting that the anti-inflammatory actions of FO may involve NF-κB signaling. In conclusion, FO attenuates CCL2 expression and secretion in both preadipocytes and ASCs, providing evidence of its potential in modulating inflammation in WAT progenitor cells by modulating histone-modifying enzymes and inflammatory pathways.

Introduction

Studies have demonstrated that the increase in macrophages within the white adipose tissue (WAT) of mice and humans with obesity is a major contributor to the induction of local and systemic low-grade chronic inflammation, which is central to the development of insulin resistance and related metabolic disorders [1,2]. The C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) also known as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), promotes the recruitment of monocytes, the precursors of macrophages that accumulate in WAT during obesity. CCL2 is secreted by adipocytes along with other adipokines and appears to play a more significant role in obesity-related risks compared to other adipokines [3]. Moreover, it has been suggested that preadipocytes could be a major source of proinflammatory mediators, including CCL2 [4].

While lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has often been found elevated in individuals with obesity and associated with high levels of CCL2 as an exacerbated response from macrophages and adipocytes, it remains unclear if in preadipocytes, LPS interferes with CCL2 expression, and furthermore, the effects of fish oil (FO) on this regulation are not understood. Regarding FO, among the significant beneficial effects already described in the context of obesity, it has been shown that omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), attenuate CCL2 levels secreted by both primary adipocytes (in response to LPS) and WAT explants from obese subjects [5] or from mice with high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity [[6], [7], [8]]. However, it is still unknown whether the anti-inflammatory effects of FO extend to the regulation of CCL2 expression by adipose progenitor (or pre-) adipocytes within the stromal vascular fraction of WAT, which coexist as adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASCs).

It was recently showed that the acetylation of lysine 27 on histone 3 (H3K27) exerts an important role in upregulating CCL2 expression by monocytic cells in response to LPS [9]. Using the Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV) software, we found enrichment of acetylated H3K27 (H3K27ac) in the promoter region of CCL2 in human ASCs/preadipocytes [10]. Therefore, herein we explored in human preadipocytes and primary ASCs: 1) the action of FO, rich in ω-3 PUFAs (EPA:DHA, 5:1), in countering LPS-induced pro-inflammatory CCL2 expression and secretion, and 2) the expression of genes encoding histone (H3K27)-modifying enzymes in these cells in response to exposure to these agents.

Methods

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Unifesp (CEP/UNIFESP Project No.: 0268/2022).

Human cell line hWA A41

Cell culture

The hWA A41 preadipocytes lineage was kindly provided by Prof. Marcelo Mori (UNICAMP, Campinas, Brazil) with approval from Dr Yu-Hua Tseng (Joslin Diabetes Center, Boston, USA). Cells were grown in 100 mm culture dishes (Corning, Oneonta, NY, USA) and maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium – D'MEM/High Glucose (4.5 g/l - LGC, Brazil) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS - LGC, Brazil) and antibiotics [1 % penicillin/streptomycin (LGC, Brazil)] in an incubator at 5 % CO2 and 37°C. On the confluence, cells were resuspended, counted and re-plated (1 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates 35 mm).

Cell treatments

The FO used is rich in EPA (EPA/DHA ratio 540/100 mg/g, or 5:1, HiOmega-3, Naturalis, São Paulo, Brazil). FO was added to the cultures at concentrations of 50 μM, dissolved in ethanol (vehicle, not exceeding 0.05 %), and incubated for 6, 12, 24, and 72 h, as indicated in the text. The same vehicle was added to the control group. Cells were also stimulated with LPS (E. coli 05:88; Sigma) at concentrations ranging from 10 ng/ml to 10 μg/ml in culture medium, and toxicity was assessed using the MTT assay. In cell culture from ASCs, the NFκB inhibitor JSH23 [4-methyl-N1-(3-phenylpropyl)benzene-1,2-diamine, SigmaAldrich, St Louis, MO, USA] was used at a concentration of 10 μM as described in previous studies [11].

MTT toxicity assay

Cell toxicity was evaluated by the 3- [4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-diphenyltetrazolio] formazan bromide reduction method by the MTT cell proliferation Kit (Cat. No. 11465007001, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Results were expressed as percentage values concerning the control.

Human subjects

Patients were recruited through the bariatric surgery and visceral WAT from abdominal region collected at the Municipal Hospital of Paulínia-SP. All male patients were ≥30 years of age, overweight (BMI >25 < 30) and provided consent prior to participation in the study.

Samples from visceral adipose tissue were processed in the Adipose Tissue Physiology and Epigenetics Laboratory of UNIFESP as the protocol described [12]. The stromal vascular fraction (cell pellet containing the ASCs – Adipose tissue-derived stromal cells) and adipocytes cells were cultured in D’MEM Han's F12 (LGC, Brazil), supplemented with 10 % Fetal bovine serum (FBS - LGC, Brazil) and antibiotics [1 % penicillin/streptomycin (LGC, Brazil)] in an incubator at 5 % CO2 and 37°C. In the end of 24 h, culture medium from adipocytes was collected and obtained adipocyte-conditioned medium (ACM) this supernatant medium was frozen and used in the experiments. The ASCs were maintained in culture and medium renewed every two days. On the confluence at 70 %, cells were resuspended, counted and re-plated in 6wells plate (1 × 105 cells/well).

Adipocyte-conditioned medium

To prepare the ACM, we used mature adipose cells (after isolation) from an obese patient. Adipocytes were cultured in culture bottles with D’MEM Han's F12 medium containing fetal bovine serum - FBS (10 %), 100U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin at 37°C and 5 % CO2 for 24 h. For each 1 ml of mature cells, we added 10 ml of culture medium. After this period, the culture medium was collected and frozen. When used, the ACM was thawed, centrifuged, concentrated to one-third of the initial volume, filtered, completed with the same amount of culture medium and distributed in the ASCs.

Morphometric analysis

For morphometric analysis, aliquots of mature adipose cell suspension were evaluated under an optical microscope with a graduated eyepiece to measure the mean cell diameter (resulting from the measurement of 100 cells). From the mean cell diameter and assuming that the isolated adipocyte is spherical, the cell volumes were calculated according to the following formula: V = (π/6) x D3/103, where, D is the average diameter of 50 adipocytes in micrometer (μm) and V is the average volume [13]. Dividing by 1000 in the first equation is used to express the volume in picoliters (pL).

RNA extraction and real time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (TRIzol, Invitrogen), according to the instructions by the provider. Gene expression was evaluated by RT-PCR and the analysis of the results obtained was carried out using the -ΔΔCt method. Data is expressed as the ratio between the expression of the target gene and housekeeping gene (GAPDH). Primers used: CCL2 (5’-3'sense: AGAATCACCAGCAGCAAGTGTCC; 5′-3′antisense: TCCTGAACCCACTTCTGCTTGG); EP300 (5’-3'sense: TGCAGGCATGGTTCCAGTTT; 5′3′antisense: AGGTAGAGGGCCATTAGA AGTCA); EZH2 (5’- 3'sense: GCTGGAATCAAAGGATACAGACA; 5′-3′antisense: GACACCGAGAATTTGCTTCAG); GAPDH (5’-3'sense:GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGC; 5′-3′antisense: ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA); KDM6B (5’-3'sense: CTCAACTTGGGCCTCTTCTC; 5′-3′antisense: GCCTGTCAGATCCCAGTTCT); TNFA (5’- 3'sense: CTCTTCTGCCTGCTGCACTTT; 5′- 3′antisense: ATGGGCTACAGGCTTGTCACTC);.

Adipokines measurements

The concentrations of adipokines from culture supernatant were determined using specific ELISA kits (Quantikine M; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A; Catalog numbers DY279-05 (MCP1/CCL2), DY210-05 (TNFA) and DY398-05 (Leptin), respectively).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups, and one-way (or two-way) ANOVA for experiments involving more than two groups, as indicated in each figure. The program used was GraphPad Prisma version 9.1. Differences were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

LPS alters the expression of inflammatory mediators and histone-modifying enzymes in human preadipocytes

To evaluate the effects of FO, we added FO (50 μM, a dose previously established in in vitro studies by our group [15]) to the LPS-stimulated preadipocyte cultures for 12 h. After this stimulation period, a remarkable increase in CCL2 gene expression (∼50-fold, Fig. 2C) and secretion (∼5.2-fold, Fig. 2B) was observed in the LPS-treated group compared to the control. When FO was combined with LPS, it completely inhibited gene expression (Fig. 2C) and reduced secretion by 25 % (Fig. 2B), thereby attenuating the LPS-induced effect on CCL2. Furthermore, we assessed the influence of FO on the expression of histone-modifying enzymes in these LPS-stimulated preadipocytes. While EZH2 showed no significant changes in mRNA levels (Fig. 2D), the demethylase KDM6B (Fig. 2E) and acetylase EP300 (Fig. 2F), which exhibited notable increases in expression upon LPS stimulation, were downregulated by FO treatment.

At the end of the 24 h of cell culture assay, we obtained ACM from control (CO) cells (not exposed to LPS nor LPS + FO) to stimulate the ASCs. The primary human ASCs were then exposed to different types of stimuli (ACM and LPS) and at different time points (6 and 72 h) to assess CCL2/MCP-1 expression (Fig. 3D–G). These time points were chosen because human preadipocytes showed a significant increase in CCL2 secretion after 6 h of LPS stimulation compared to control cells, and this increase reached approximately 6-fold after 72 h (Fig. 2A). Thus, we investigated both the acute (6 h) and chronic (72 h) phases of the inflammatory response, with a focus on CCL2 expression as a downstream target of NF-κB activation. We recently showed that after 72 h of chronic treatment, LPS-induced NF-κB activation was associated with persistent changes in adipocyte-related gene expression [12].

ASCs exposed to ACM (from hypertrophied adipocytes) for 6 h showed increased CCL2/MCP-1 expression, which was also attenuated by FO (∼10 %, Fig. 3D). It is important to highlight that although the ACM already contained “background noise” of CCL2 secreted by the adipocytes, the additional contribution of CCL2 secreted by the ASCs was diminished in the presence of FO.

Primary ASCs were next exposed to directly to LPS for 6 and 72 h (Fig. 3D–F). Notably, after 6 h of culture in DMEM, detectable levels of CCL2/MCP-1 were only present in cells stimulated with LPS, and very interestingly, FO entirely abolished the secretion of this chemokine in these mesenchymal cells (Fig. 3E). This observation highlights the strong anti-inflammatory effect of FO, as it was able to completely block the LPS-induced secretion of CCL2/MCP-1 by ASCs. Furthermore, when evaluating the response of ASCs to LPS after 72 h of exposure, the secretion of CCL2/MCP-1 was notably exacerbated. However, FO treatment consistently maintained lower CCL2 levels in the secreted medium (Fig. 3F), reinforcing the idea that FO exerts a sustained inhibitory effect on pro-inflammatory chemokine secretion even during prolonged inflammatory stimulation. This robust attenuation of cytokine secretion by FO underscores its potential role in modulating chronic inflammation in mesenchymal cells.

Finally, the addition of the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23 reduced CCL2 expression, similar to FO treatment, with no additive effect observed when combined (Fig. 3G).

Discussion

Present data show that LPS triggers a robust inflammatory response in human preadipocytes, as evidenced by the significant increases in TNFA and CCL2 gene expression, both of which are well-documented players in inflammatory processes. The increase of CCL2, in particular, was attenuated by FO treatment, suggesting that omega-3 fatty acids exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the response to LPS stimuli. This anti-inflammatory effect has been previously observed in mature adipocytes and WAT explants [[5], [6], [7], [8]], and present data expand these findings to adipocyte progenitor cells.

We also demonstrate that LPS increases the expression of the histone modifying enzymes KDM6B and EP300, both of which are associated with H3K27 demethylation and acetylation, respectively. These results are consistent with findings showing that LPS stimulates KDM6B expression in macrophages by stimulating p65 binding to the KDM6B regulatory region [17] and with bioinformatic analysis showing that there are putative p65 binding sites in the EP300 regulatory region (data not shown). LPS induces p65 phosphorylation [18], which promotes NF-κB p65 interaction with the coactivator CBP/EP300, thereby enhancing NF-κB transcriptional activity [[19], [20], [21]]. It has also been shown that KDM6B is required for increased gene activation by p65 in a mouse skin wound healing model [22]. Thus, LPS mediates epigenetic reprogramming of cells by activating NF-κB signaling pathway which recruits p65 and both histone H3K27 modifiers to the regulatory regions of specific genes inducing gene expression by increasing histone acetylation.

FO was able to reduce these LPS-induced epigenetic changes. It has been shown that LPS and IFN-γ induce synergistic expression of CCL2 in monocytic cells via H3K27 acetylation [9]. Moreover, histone acetylation has been shown to play important roles in upregulating CCL2 expression in inflammatory cells [23]. KDM6B demethylase acts on H3K27, demethylating it to facilitate subsequent acetylation by the H3K27 acetylase EP300, thereby activating gene transcription [24,25]. This suggests an additional mechanism by which FO exerts protective effects in attenuating LPS-related inflammatory genes expression, as described here for CCL2 (Fig. 4). Corroborating these, we previously demonstrated in human primary ASCs, that FO attenuated H3K27 acetylation induced by LPS through mechanisms involving NF-κB [12]. These findings support the idea that FO would alter the new epigenetic landscape established by NF-κB signaling pathway in these cells. Omega-3 fatty acids have previously been shown to inhibit the NF-kB pathway in a model of neuroinflammation [26]. Therefore, we will further investigate the distribution of H3K27ac and p65 in the nuclei of LPS-stimulated preadipocytes and FO treated cells using Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay. Additionally, we also described that FO supplementation demonstrated efficacy in mitigating HFD-induced obesity, influencing epigenetic and molecular pathways, which included the levels of H3K27ac and H3K27me3 in mice WAT [27,28]. We then subsequently sought to validate our results concerning CCL2 within the preadipocyte lineage using a primary culture of ASCs. For this, both ASCs and mature adipocytes were isolated from a patient with overweight, and the mature adipocytes were subjected to morphometric analysis to determine the average diameter. It has been described that the average diameter of adipocytes in lean individuals ranges from 50 to 100 μm, whereas in individuals with obesity, it ranges from 100 to 150 μm or even larger [12]. The values obtained are consistent with those reported for hypertrophied adipocytes, which are associated with impaired cellular function and various pathophysiological conditions [16]. It is well established that hypertrophied adipocytes exhibit increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating inflammatory processes [24,25,29], with effects like those triggered by LPS and confirmed herein.

Compared to LPS, the conditioned medium (rich in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, leptin, and CCL2) secreted by hypertrophied adipocytes, was equally effective in stimulating ASCs to express CCL2, an effect that was inhibited by the presence of FO. Thus, the nature of the observed effect on ASCs, whether it results directly from LPS action on the cells, indirectly through a positive feedback loop via elevated MCP-1 and TNF-α (also secreted by ASCs), or through a synergistic effect, remains to be further investigated. Anyway, the experiments using isolated mature adipocytes confirm that FO exerts a protective effect against the exaggerated secretion of inflammatory cytokines, such as CCL2 and TNFA, induced by LPS. This finding is particularly relevant, given that hypertrophied adipocytes, as seen in the patient with overweight, are associated with a pro-inflammatory environment that exacerbates the secretion these cytokines [30,31]. Indeed, the ability of FO to mitigate these inflammatory responses, in both preadipocytes and mature adipocytes, highlights its potential as a therapeutic agent in preventing or treating inflammation-related obesity complications.

Finally, the ASCs were exposed to the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23 to assess CCL2 expression in a primary ASC culture model. The presence of the inhibitor attenuated the LPS-induced effect on CCL2 expression, bringing it to a level similar to that observed in the control, suggesting that the mechanism of action likely involves transcriptional modulation mediated by NF-κB. The reduction of CCL2 expression in ASCs treated with FO in an inflammatory cytokine-rich environment, along with the effects observed with the NF-κB inhibitor, reinforces the notion that FO primarily acts by inhibiting NF-κB activation. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that NF-κB is a key regulator of inflammation in adipose-derived stem cells, responsible for modulating the expression of cytokines, such as MCP-1 and TNFA, in response to inflammatory stimuli [32]. Moreover, the expression of CCL2/MCP-1 was lower in cells exposed to ACM in the presence of FO, indicating that the secretion product of adipocytes influenced the secretion of ASCs, that is, the environment rich in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFA, leptin and MCP-1) secreted by hypertrophied adipocytes, paracrinely affects ASCs, and FO has potential in modulating chronic inflammation in mesenchymal cells.

Although the present study primarily focuses on the molecular mechanisms by which FO modulates inflammation in preadipocytes and adipocyte progenitor cells, the translational implications of these findings are indeed significant. Given the increasing evidence linking chronic inflammation in adipose tissue to metabolic disorders, particularly obesity, FO's potential to modulate CCL2 expression and histone-modifying enzymes such as KDM6B and EP300 suggests it could serve as a promising therapeutic agent for mitigating obesity-related inflammation. Clinical studies exploring FO supplementation in individuals with obesity or metabolic syndrome could further elucidate its impact on inflammatory pathways in adipose tissue, particularly in preadipocytes and macrophage-enriched environments. Additionally, dietary interventions incorporating omega-3 PUFAs may help attenuate the inflammatory milieu in adipose tissue, thus contributing to the prevention or management of obesity-associated complications, including insulin resistance and cardiovascular diseases. These potential applications of FO underscore its value as an adjunctive treatment in reducing the systemic effects of chronic inflammation.

While there are some limitations to this study, including the use of monolayer cell cultures that may not fully represent the complexity of cellular responses in native tissues, the physiological relevance of our findings remains strong. Preadipocytes comprise approximately 30 % of the cell population in WAT, underscoring the potential significance of our results. Specifically, our data suggest that FO may modulate inflammation in WAT by reducing CCL2 expression in LPS-stimulated preadipocytes, providing further support for the anti-inflammatory properties of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). Although multiple mechanisms could contribute to these effects, our findings point to the involvement of NF-κB signaling, potentially leading to epigenetic modifications as demonstrated in this study. Further investigation into these mechanisms is critical and is currently being explored in our ongoing research.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the effectiveness of FO in attenuating CCL2 expression in preadipocytes and primary human ASCs, suggesting FO's potential as a therapeutic agent in combating inflammation by acting on WAT progenitor cells. Specifically, we demonstrate that FO may reduce inflammation by inhibiting LPS-induced expression of histone-modifying enzymes KDM6B and EP300, which are involved in CCL2 regulation. These results contribute to the growing understanding of how FO modulates inflammatory pathways, particularly in the context of WAT. Furthermore, our study lays the groundwork for future clinical trials exploring the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying FO's effects, including its influence on NF-κB signaling and H3K27ac modifications in both preadipocytes and adipocytes. Such investigations are essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to address metabolic disorders linked to chronic inflammation, especially obesity.
