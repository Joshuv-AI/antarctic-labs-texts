Evaluation of the first standardized registry of approved research studies by the research ethics committees in Ecuador
Authors: Katherine Simbaña-Rivera, Damary S Jaramillo-Aguilar, Víctor Hugo Almeida Arteaga, José Ruales Estupiñán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEthical regulation of biomedical research is essential to safeguard the rights and well-being of participants. In Ecuador, Research Ethics Committees (RECs) have undergone progressive growth. This study describes and evaluates the performance of the RECs of Ecuador using the first standardized registry of approved research studies implemented by the Ministry of Public Health.MethodsAn observational, descriptive, and retrospective study was conducted using administrative records corresponding to the first year of implementation of the First Standardized Registry of Approved Research Studies (September 2021–October 2022). All functional RECs and approved research projects were included, characterizing variables related to geographical distribution, institutional affiliation, researcher profiles, study type, review modality, response times, and protocol follow-up.ResultsA total of 957 projects approved by 21 RECs were evaluated. Of these, 52% were concentrated in Quito, and 62% were affiliated with higher education institutions. Most studies were observational (96%), with a predominance in Medicine and Nursing (63%), followed by Psychology (15%) and Sociology (10%). Geographical distribution revealed a high concentration in Azuay (52%) and Pichincha (43%). Only 3% of the projects had a unique identification code, 40% involved vulnerable populations, and 9% used biological samples. The average waiting time to obtain a resolution was 31 days (95% CI: 28.5–34.2).ConclusionsDespite regulatory advancements and the implementation of the First Standardized Registry of Approved Research Studies, challenges persist in terms of decentralization, operational efficiency, and follow-up. Optimizing the regulatory framework, strengthening RECs training, and fostering public-private collaboration are crucial to consolidating a robust and adaptable ethical system capable of addressing emerging challenges.Keywords: Research ethics committees, Biomedical research, Legislation, Ecuador

Background

Over the past decades, biomedical research has undergone significant advancements, transforming the approach to health challenges and the development of novel therapies [1]. However, this scientific progress has introduced ethical dilemmas that require rigorous scrutiny. To safeguard the dignity, rights, and well-being of participants in scientific studies, research ethics committees (RECs) have been established [2].

The Nuremberg Code (1947) established the foundational concepts of informed consent and voluntariness in medical research, yet it did not explicitly propose the creation of ethics committees [3]. The first Declaration of Helsinki (1964) reinforced the ethical obligations of physicians in research but also made no reference to RECs; this recognition only appeared in the 1975 Tokyo revision, which introduced the requirement for independent ethical review [4]. Later, the Belmont Report (1979) consolidated the principles of respect for people, beneficence, and justice, shaping modern regulatory frameworks [5]. Over time, and particularly with technological advancement and research globalization, RECs have expanded their scope to address emerging ethical challenges, including data protection, equity in research participation, the application of artificial intelligence in clinical contexts, decentralized trials, and the inclusion of culturally diverse perspectives. This continuous evolution remains essential to safeguarding the integrity and transparency of human subjects research and to ensuring the protection of fundamental human rights [6].

In Latin America, the establishment of RECs has been heterogeneous, reflecting the diverse legislative frameworks and socioeconomic contexts of each nation. Ecuador exemplifies this variability, as it lacked specific legislation until 2006 [7, 8]. Nonetheless, the first REC in the country was established in 1996, demonstrating a proactive approach to addressing ethical research requirements even before the enactment of formal legal frameworks [7, 9].

A pivotal regulatory milestone occurred in 2006 with the enactment of Ministerial Agreement No. 00099, which delineated the initial guidelines regarding the objectives, purposes, organization, and structure of bioethics committees affiliated with the Ministry of Public Health (MPH), focusing on health research regulation [8].

Subsequently, in 2014, Ministerial Agreement No. 4889 introduced the “Regulation for the Approval and Monitoring of Research Ethics Committees and Healthcare Ethics Committees,” clearly distinguishing between committees dedicated to ethical research evaluation and those addressing hospital ethics, with defined objectives and functions for each category [10]. However, this regulatory framework was limited to clinical trials and lacked effective monitoring mechanisms or standardized review and approval procedures.

Recognizing the need for a more robust strategy to oversee RECs operations and consolidate information on approved research and related activities, the National Health Authority (NHA) announced the standardization of the registration and monitoring system in August 2021 through the implementation of the First Standardized Registry of Approved Research Studies, while concurrently amending the legislation. Subsequently, in August 2022, Ministerial Agreement No. 0005 was issued to replace Ministerial Agreement No. 4889 [10, 11]. This new regulatory framework introduced substantial improvements, including risk classification based on research type, defined response timelines, and the official standardization of national registration requirements and formats. In this regard, this framework encompasses the ethical review process, which is independently conducted by RECs distributed across higher education institutions and healthcare establishments nationwide, followed by the mandatory registration of the approved protocol in the Standardized National Registry of Approved Research Studies. In the case of multicenter studies, prior to their implementation in the country, they must be evaluated and approved by at least one REC. These enhancements have strengthened Ecuador’s regulatory landscape for biomedical research and improved the transparency and traceability of the ethical evaluation of scientific studies.

As this is the first registry, no prior literature exists on the subject that contains such specific information. In this context, this study aimed to describe and evaluate the performance of the RECs of Ecuador using the first standardized registry of approved research studies during its inaugural year.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective, observational, and descriptive study was conducted based on the first national registry of research studies that have been reviewed and approved by the RECs in Ecuador. The analysis period spanned from September 2021 to October 2022, with the objective of evaluating the performance of the RECs in overseeing scientific research.

Study setting

Ecuador, a middle-income country located in the northwestern region of South America, covers an area of 283,000 km² and is politically and administratively divided into 24 provinces, distributed across four regions: Coast (seven), Highlands (ten), Amazon (six), and Insular (one). In terms of population density and economic contribution, the main cities are Guayaquil (Guayas), Quito (Pichincha), and Cuenca (Azuay). Together, these cities host 50% of the country’s higher education institutions and approximately 90% of the most important public tertiary healthcare facilities affiliated with the Ministry of Public Health [12, 13].

Data sources, sample and inclusion criteria

Data for this study were formally requested from the National Directorate of Health Research (NDHR) at the MPH, the institution responsible for managing and coordinating the Standardized National Registry of Approved Research Studies. The request was submitted through the official document management system in accordance with national administrative procedures.

The obligatory registry consolidates information submitted by all RECs. Data are compiled in an Excel-based database, where each spreadsheet corresponds to an individual research protocol. The database is structured into three main sections: (i) identification of the investigators and their affiliated institutions; (ii) study details and methodological characteristics; and (iii) the current status of ethical review. All RECs are required to submit these reports on a monthly basis, ensuring the continuous updating and traceability of research oversight processes nationwide.

The study encompassed all RECs approved by the NHA during the study period. Research protocols reported by these RECs were included in the analysis, provided that at least one study had been formally registered, regardless of its review status at the time of data collection. This inclusive approach allowed for a more comprehensive representation of the ethical review landscape across the country.

Variables

The analysis considered variables related both to the RECs and to the research protocols they approved.

RECs were characterized according to their geographical distribution (city; province) and their institutional affiliation (higher education institution; hospital).

Approved research protocols were analyzed across several dimensions, including the assignment of unique identification codes to approved studies (no; yes), field of knowledge (Dentistry; Education; Medicine and Nursing; Nutrition; Pedagogy; Psychology; Sociology), review modality (exemption; expedited; full-committee), review status (amendments; initial evaluations; approval renewals), type of researcher (graduate or doctoral student; professional; undergraduate student), type of study (clinical trials; interventional studies; observational studies), use of biological samples (no; yes), and vulnerable population (no; yes).

According to Ecuadorian legislation, type of study were categorized in [14–16]: (i) clinical trials: experimental investigations involving human participants conducted to assess the efficacy or safety of drugs, medical devices, biological products, natural medicinal products, or other products subject to registration and sanitary control; (ii) interventional studies: investigations introducing a specific intervention to evaluate its effects, not necessarily involving drugs or devices, and included quasi-experimental studies, field trials, and randomized controlled trials without pharmacological or device components; and, (iii) observational studies: investigations that observe and analyze phenomena without direct intervention, encompassing cross-sectional, ecological, case reports, case series, case-control, and cohort designs.

Vulnerable populations were defined in accordance with Article 35 of the Constitution of the Republic of Ecuador, which recognizes priority and specialized care groups [17, 18]. These include older adults, children and adolescents, pregnant women, persons with disabilities, persons deprived of liberty, and individuals affected by catastrophic or high-complexity diseases.

Additionally, data were collected on response times and follow-up procedures. Response time referred to the period between the submission of a research protocol and the final decision issued by a REC—whether approval, approval with observations, or rejection—and was categorized as less than 15 days, 16–45 days, 46–60 days, over 61 days, or not reported. Follow-up intervals were also recorded based on RECs practices, including monthly, quarterly, semi-annual, annual, not applicable, or not reported schedules.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed, including the calculation of absolute frequencies, percentages, measures of central tendency, and dispersion. Microsoft Excel 2016 was used for data processing and tabulation, while figures were created using BioRender.com (www.biorender.com).

Characteristics of studies

Regarding methodological design, the majority of approved studies were observational (96%; n = 918), with 64% (n = 588) belonging to the fields of Medicine and Nursing. In contrast, interventional studies accounted for 4% (n = 33), primarily conducted in Medicine and Nursing (36%; n = 12), followed by Dentistry (15%; n = 5) and Nutrition (12%; n = 4).

In terms of sample usage, 9% (n = 83) of the studies employed biological samples. Additionally, 40% (n = 379) included vulnerable populations, with the highest proportions observed in Sociology (59%; n = 55) and Psychology (46%; n = 68).

Investigator profiles and review process

Most studies were submitted by undergraduate students (38%; n = 367), followed by graduate and doctoral students (33%; n = 317). Expedited review was the most common modality (62%; n = 597), and in 82% (n = 784) of cases, the evaluation corresponded to a first review.

Evaluation timelines

The average time to receive an REC decision was 31 working days (95% CI: 28.5–34.2) (Fig. 2). In 48% (n = 463) of cases, approval was granted in fewer than 15 working days, whereas in 22% (n = 212) the review process exceeded 45 days. Notably, follow-up requirements were not reported in 19% (n = 185) of cases, while 37% (n = 350) of studies had a quarterly follow-up schedule.

Discussion

The establishment of the First Standardized Registry of Approved Research Studies represents a pivotal advancement in Ecuador’s research ethics infrastructure, providing the first national overview of RECs activity and study characteristics. However, this achievement also highlights critical gaps in equity, efficiency, and standardization that must be strategically addressed to ensure ethical robustness and national inclusivity.

The concentration of 52% of RECs in Quito and 62% within higher education institutions reflects both the centralization of ethical oversight and the academic dominance in research governance. Similar patterns in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) show RECs clustered in universities and tertiary hospitals in capital cities. This trend mirrors broader global dynamics, as seen in China, Egypt, India, and Japan, where urban-based institutional RECs prevail, and in high-income countries such as the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia, which have transitioned toward nationally coordinated or hybrid systems to mitigate geographic inequities [19, 20]. While centralization facilitates quality control and adherence to national norms, it also limits equitable access to ethical review in peripheral regions, particularly in the Amazon and Insular provinces, where research on endemic and environmental health issues is increasingly needed. In these regions, such as among Amazonian indigenous populations including the Waorani, serious ethical breaches have been documented in both national and international studies, involving lack of informed consent, coercion, and failure to return results [21]. These cases underscore the urgent need not only for the establishment of RECs in remote areas but also for more robust evaluation criteria, continuous monitoring, and operational capacity to ensure compliance with ethical standards [22]. Despite a gradual increase in scientific output from these regions, their contribution to national biomedical research remains limited [23]. Addressing this imbalance requires targeted investments, interinstitutional and community-based RECs, and the establishment of regional ethics hubs, as exemplified by Brazil, where RECs have been strategically expanded across the Amazon to protect indigenous populations and traditional knowledge [22], and similar approaches in the Pacific Islands highlight the value of building local ethics committees to strengthen culturally appropriate oversight and sustainable research capacity in under-resourced regions [24]. Accreditation frameworks recognizing ethical governance as a quality indicator could further incentivize compliance and accountability. Ultimately, decentralization should pursue not only administrative efficiency but also ethical justice, ensuring that all regions possess the autonomy, technical capacity, and cultural grounding to govern research responsibly within a transparent, nationally integrated ethics system.

During the observation period, 957 studies were approved, most within Medicine and Nursing, consistent with national biomedical trends [23]. The predominance of observational designs (96%) and the scarcity of interventional studies (4%) mirror structural limitations typical of LMICs, including insufficient funding, infrastructure, and regulatory capacity for clinical trials [25]. Beyond these operational constraints, ethical bottlenecks in biobank research and biospecimen governance further limit scientific advancement. Only 9% of REC-approved studies involved biological samples, reflecting persistent barriers to the use, storage, and secondary utilization of human materials. This limited engagement parallels findings from Mexico, Colombia, Guatemala, and Costa Rica, where over 40% of REC members were unaware of appropriate procedures for including residual samples, and broad consent was rarely applied [26]. Such gaps reveal the need to strengthen RECs’ capacity in biobank ethics, harmonize consent frameworks, and establish traceability systems to align national practices with international standards and safeguard participant autonomy.

Ecuador exhibits a strikingly low output of domestic clinical trials, with only six REC-approved studies between 2021 and 2022. Considering its population of approximately 18 million and its existing research infrastructure, this number is markedly lower than expected for countries with similar socioeconomic and demographic profiles. In comparison, neighboring nations such as Paraguay and Uruguay approved 25–40 trials, and Peru, Colombia, and Chile exceeded 100 during the same period [27]. This gap reflects not only infrastructural limitations but also the absence of mandatory cross-referencing between REC approvals and international trial registries, resulting in underreporting and diminished global visibility [28]. Furthermore, Ecuador’s research output is poorly aligned with the nation’s most prevalent and high-burden diseases, including diabetes, renal disorders, and chronic non-communicable conditions, which together account for substantial morbidity and mortality nationwide [29]. Addressing these gaps requires targeted strategies that extend beyond increasing trial numbers. Strengthening collaboration among RECs, academic institutions, and public health agencies could facilitate data sharing, coordinate multicenter studies, and prioritize research addressing the country’s most pressing health needs. The experience of Brazil illustrates the potential impact of integrated systems. The Rebec@ platform within the Brazilian Registry of Clinical Trials (ReBEC) standardizes trial registration under ICTRP guidelines, provides clear guidance on documentation, deadlines, and study types, and implements priority fast-tracks that allow urgent trials, including those involving indigenous or vulnerable populations, to receive express approval within 48 h [30]. Adopting similar harmonized reporting frameworks, automated linkage systems, and risk-adjusted approval matrices in Ecuador could reduce delays, enhance compliance, and ensure that clinical trials are responsive to high-priority public health challenges.

From a bioethical standpoint, the inclusion of vulnerable populations in 37% of approved studies demands particular attention. Ecuador’s legal framework defines vulnerability broadly, encompassing children, older adults, and individuals with disabilities or catastrophic illnesses [17, 18]. However, international standards emphasize a continuum model of vulnerability, contextualized by autonomy, social determinants, and exposure to risk [4, 31]. To operationalize this, RECs should adopt structured vulnerability assessment frameworks and context-sensitive guidelines—particularly for community-based, intercultural, and environmental research—ensuring protection without exclusion. Embedding One Health and Buen Vivir principles within national ethics guidelines would also foster equitable and culturally attuned research governance.

A significant proportion of studies (72%) were led by undergraduate, graduate, or doctoral students, particularly in Medicine and Nursing, highlighting the central role of academic research in driving ethical review activity. This finding aligns with experiences in Egypt and Ghana, where student-led projects constitute the majority of submissions to RECs [32, 33]. While student-led submissions enhance institutional accountability and contribute to training, they also increase REC workload and demand stronger mentorship [34]. Embedding mandatory modules in bioethics and research methodology across undergraduate and postgraduate curricula, paired with structured mentorship linking senior and junior investigators, would promote methodological and ethical rigor. Additionally, accreditation incentives for research-integrity education may reinforce RECs’ educational mandate and align academic performance metrics with ethical compliance, fostering the development of competent, ethically aware health professionals [35].

Procedural efficiency remains a mixed outcome. The mean decision time was 31 working days, yet 22% of cases exceeded 45 days and 19% lacked documented follow-up. These inconsistencies mirror structural factors observed internationally. For example, RECs with smaller membership and centralized authority, such as the Helsinki Committee in Gaza (3 active reviewers, one-week average turnaround), achieve faster decisions, whereas larger, multidisciplinary committees like Cayetano Heredia in Peru (29 members, four-week average) or the Democratic Republic of the Congo (15 members, six-week average) experience longer review times and limited ability to convene all members [36]. Variability in dossier requirements and processing fees further complicates standardization, with continuing review and compliance reporting remaining underdeveloped. In Tanzania, for instance, investigators may pay an additional fee for expedited review, highlighting institutional differences in access and prioritization of protocol assessment [37]. Moreover, bureaucratic hurdles have contributed to significant delays in ethical reviews, adversely affecting scientific productivity in Ecuador [38–40], a problem that became particularly evident and was further exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic [41–43] REF. Therefore, Ecuador should adopt national digital monitoring systems to harmonize REC procedures, reduce delays, and facilitate multi-site research. Evidence from Argentina’s PRIISA.BA system shows transient approval delays initially, followed by faster first reviews [44]. Similar platforms in India and WHO South-East Asia demonstrate feasibility [45]. Complemented with AI tools, these measures would enhance traceability, compliance, and public trust [46].

A particularly concerning finding in Ecuador is that 19.3% of REC-approved studies and up to 81% of Nutrition projects lacked follow-up documentation, revealing a systemic deficit in post-approval monitoring. Although 37% implemented quarterly reviews and 25% semi-annual ones, the absence of structured oversight undermines participant protection. This gap is especially critical for clinical trials, where continuous monitoring of protocol deviations, adverse events, and participant safety is a cornerstone of ethical compliance and data integrity [47]. Across Latin America, similar asymmetries are observed, where legal frameworks for post-approval monitoring exist but training, adverse event reporting, and continuing review are inconsistently applied [48]. In Australia, China, and India, most ethics committees conduct continuing review of protocols, site monitoring, serious adverse event evaluation, and review of protocol deviations [39, 49], with more than two-thirds of member secretaries actively engaged in these post-approval activities [49]. Nevertheless, challenges such as incomplete submissions, reviewer unavailability, delays, and excessive paperwork continue to impede timely follow-up. European countries display comparable trends, with monitoring frequently restricted to protocol amendments due to resource and legislative limitations [50]. These international comparisons highlight the need for Ecuador to implement legally mandated, risk-adjusted follow-up mechanisms, automated monitoring systems, and digital audit trails. Structured standard operating procedures, trained personnel, and dedicated digital platforms, as seen in India, could enhance transparency, accountability, and the protection of study participants [49].

Strengths and limitations

Despite being the first study to systematically evaluate the performance of Research Ethics Committees (RECs) in Ecuador using the First Standardized Registry of Approved Research Studies following Ministerial Agreement No. 0005, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a retrospective analysis relying on administrative records, the accuracy and completeness of data are contingent on the quality and consistency of reports submitted by each REC. Missing, incomplete, or inconsistent information may have affected the representativeness of specific variables. Furthermore, the study lacks a qualitative assessment of ethical review processes, limiting insights into decision-making rigor, interpretation of regulatory standards, and the nuances of approval deliberations. Given that the analysis covers only the first year of registry implementation, it does not capture longitudinal trends or potential improvements in REC performance over time. Additionally, while the registry standardizes key administrative and procedural data, it does not evaluate the methodological quality, scientific impact, or societal relevance of approved studies.

The recent incorporation of the term “interventional studies” in Ministerial Agreement No. 0015-2021 was intended to formally recognize and categorize research designs in which a specific intervention is introduced to evaluate its effects, even when no drugs or medical devices are involved, such as quasi-experimental studies, field trials, and randomized controlled trials without medicinal or device interventions [14]. This regulatory adjustment addressed gaps left by Ministerial Agreement No. 0075-2017, which had not explicitly recognized these types of studies, thereby limiting clarity in classification and oversight [16]. While this inclusion is expected to increase the number of studies formally classified under this category, the latest 2024 update (Ministerial Agreement No. 0069-2024) still lacks specific guidance for clinical trials involving medical devices, underscoring the need for complementary regulations to ensure comprehensive oversight and alignment with international ethical standards [15].”

Further research and knowledge perspectives

Future research should explore the qualitative dimensions of ethical review performance in Ecuador, focusing on REC decision-making processes, investigator perceptions, and compliance behavior across institutions and disciplines. Particular attention should be given to evaluating post-approval monitoring practices in clinical trials, as these studies involve higher risk and require sustained ethical vigilance. Comparative regional analyses could elucidate how governance models, resource allocation, and digitalization influence efficiency and ethical rigor in Latin American and Caribbean contexts. Moreover, integrating longitudinal monitoring and qualitative methodologies alongside quantitative data would provide a more comprehensive understanding of REC operations and inform strategies for governance and capacity building. Linking REC data with national research and health outcome indicators could further clarify how ethics oversight translates into societal benefit and scientific quality. Finally, studies incorporating social, environmental, and intercultural variables would enrich understanding of ethics within the One Health framework, particularly in rural and Amazonian territories where research participation remains limited, fostering a more equitable and contextually grounded ethics ecosystem.

Conclusions

This study highlights both the progress and challenges in the performance of RECs in Ecuador. While significant advancements have been made through digitalization and regulatory updates, persistent challenges remain concerning centralization, operational efficiency, and study oversight. The integration of optimization strategies—focused on decentralization, infrastructure improvements, capacity building, and collaboration—will be essential to consolidate a robust, transparent, and ethically sound research system adapted to emerging challenges.
