Characteristic polynomials of { ± 1 } \{\pm 1\} -matrices modulo a power of 2 2
Gary Greaves, Huu An Phan

Abstract
For a fixed integer e ⩾ 3 e\geqslant 3 and n n large enough, we show that the number of congruence classes modulo 2 e 2^{e} of characteristic polynomials of n × n n\times n symmetric { ± 1 } \{\pm 1\} -matrices with constant diagonal is equal to { 2 ( e − 2 2 ) , if n is even ; 2 ( e − 2 2 ) + 1 , if n is odd , \begin{cases}2^{\binom{e-2}{2}},&\text{ if $n$ is even};\\ 2^{\binom{e-2}{2}+1},&\text{ if $n$ is odd},\end{cases} thereby solving a conjecture of Greaves and Yatsyna from 2019. We also show that, for n n large enough, the number of congruence classes modulo 2 e 2^{e} of characteristic polynomials of n × n n\times n skew-symmetric { ± 1 } \{\pm 1\} -matrices with constant diagonal is equal to { 2 ⌊ e − 1 2 ⌋ ​ ⌊ e − 2 2 ⌋ , if n is even ; 2 ⌊ e − 2 2 ⌋ ​ ⌊ e − 3 2 ⌋ , if n is odd . \begin{cases}2^{\lfloor\frac{e-1}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-2}{2}\rfloor},&\text{ if $n$ is even};\\ 2^{\lfloor\frac{e-2}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-3}{2}\rfloor},&\text{ if $n$ is odd}.\end{cases} We introduce the concept of a lift graph/tournament , which serves as our main tool. We also introduce the notion of the walk polynomial of a graph, which enables us to show the existence of the requisite lift tournaments.

1.2
Outline of the proofs

Our arguments are elementary, relying on relations between elementary-symmetric and power-sum polynomials together with combinatorial constructions involving paths, cycles, and almost-transitive tournaments.
A central tool is the notion of a
lift graph
, introduced in Section
3.2
.
For the symmetric case, the relevant lift graphs can be constructed explicitly from unions of paths and cycles.
The skew-symmetric case requires a non-explicit construction based on joins of
1
-almost transitive tournaments in the sense of
[
4
]
.
We introduce the
walk polynomial
in Section
6.3
, which facilitates the verification of the required congruence conditions for the existence of the relevant lift tournaments.

Our results determine, for each fixed
e
, the precise number of distinct congruence classes of characteristic polynomials of natural families of
\{\pm 1\}
-matrices for sufficiently large
n
.
Our bounds on the minimal such
n
are recursive and, based on empirical evidence, far from optimal.
We leave the problem of optimising these bounds as an avenue for further research.

Organisation

Let
p(x)=c_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\theta_{i})\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be a polynomial.
Denote by

For

It follows from these definitions and from Newton’s identities that

\mathsf{e}_{k}(p)=(-1)^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k}(p)/\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)
and

\mathsf{p}_{k}(p)=-\frac{1}{\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathsf{c}_{i}(p)\mathsf{p}_{k-i}(p)+k\mathsf{c}_{k}(p)\right).

(1)

We further remark here that, for polynomials

and

we have

\mathsf{p}_{k}(p\cdot q)=\mathsf{p}_{k}(p)+\mathsf{p}_{k}(q).

(2)

For a nonzero integer

2
-adic valuation

of

is the multiplicity of

in the prime factorisation of

.
The 2-adic valuation of

is defined to be

.
For an integer

, the

-adic valuation

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
m>n
.
Suppose that

\mathsf{p}_{k}(p)\equiv 0\mod{2^{m}}\text{ for each }k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\cup\{n+1\}.

Then
\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)\mathsf{p}_{n}(p)\equiv-n\mathsf{c}_{n}(p)\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}((n-1)!)}}
and

\mathsf{c}_{k}(p)\equiv 0\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}(k!)}}\text{ for each }k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\cup\{n+1\}.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k
that

2^{m-\nu_{2}(k!)}
divides
\mathsf{c}_{k}(p)
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
The base case
k=1
follows immediately since
\mathsf{c}_{1}(p)=-\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)\mathsf{p}_{1}(p)
.
Assume that

2^{m-\nu_{2}(j!)}
divides
\mathsf{c}_{k}(p)
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,j\}
.
Using (
1
), we have

\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)\mathsf{p}_{j+1}(p)+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathsf{c}_{i}(p)\mathsf{p}_{j+1-i}(p)+(j+1)\mathsf{c}_{j+1}(p)\equiv 0\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}(j!)}}.

Hence
\mathsf{c}_{j+1}(p)\equiv 0\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}((j+1)!)}}
, as required.

It further follows that
\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)\mathsf{p}_{n}(p)\equiv-n\mathsf{c}_{n}(p)\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}((n-1)!)}}
.
Lastly,
again using (
1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{0}(p)\mathsf{p}_{n+1}(p)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{c}_{i}(p)\mathsf{p}_{n+1-i}(p)-(n+1)\mathsf{c}_{n+1}(p)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{c}_{1}(p)\mathsf{c}_{n}(p)-(n+1)\mathsf{c}_{n+1}(p)\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}((n-1)!)}}.

Hence,
\mathsf{c}_{n+1}(p)\equiv 0\mod{2^{m-\nu_{2}((n-1)!)-\nu_{2}(n+1)}}
, as required.
∎

k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{f})^{k}\mathbf{1}\equiv\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\vec{P}_{f-1+2^{e}})^{k}\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\vec{P}_{f-1})^{k}\mathbf{1}\mod{2^{e}}.

Observe that

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\vec{P}_{n})^{k}\mathbf{1}=\begin{cases}n-k,&\text{ if $k\leqslant n$};\\
0,&\text{ if $k\geqslant n$}.\end{cases}

Hence, for each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,e\}
,

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{f})^{k}\mathbf{1}\equiv\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }k<f;\\
f-k-1&\text{ if }k\geqslant f\end{cases}\mod{2^{e}}.

(5)

We choose
N_{e}=2^{2e}\cdot\binom{e+1}{2}
.
It suffices to show that for
n>N_{e}
and
(c_{2},c_{3},\dots,c_{e})\in\{1,\dots,2^{e}\}^{e-1}
, there exists an
n
-vertex digraph
\Gamma
such that, for each
k\in\{2,3,\dots,e\}
, we have
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})\equiv 2^{k-1}c_{k}\mod{2^{e}}
.

Take
(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{e-1})\in\{1,\dots,2^{e}\}^{e-1}
such that, for each
i\in\{2,\dots,e\}
, we have

d_{i-1}+2d_{i-2}+3d_{i-3}+\dots+(i-1)d_{1}\equiv(-1)^{i-1}c_{i}\mod{2^{e}}.

Let

\Gamma=d_{1}\Gamma_{1}\cup d_{2}\Gamma_{2}\cup\dots\cup d_{e-1}\Gamma_{e-1}\cup(n-2^{e}\sum_{f=1}^{e-1}f\cdot d_{f})\vec{P}_{1}.

Clearly, we have
\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)}(x)=x^{n}
.
By Lemma
2.2
, for each
k\in\{2,\dots,e\}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})

\displaystyle=2^{k-1}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{e-1}d_{i}\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{i})^{k-1}\mathbf{1}.

(6)

Using (
5
), (
6
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})

\displaystyle\equiv 2^{k-1}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_{i}(i-k)\equiv 2^{k-1}c_{k}\mod{2^{e}},

as required.
∎

Note that, in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, for each tuple
\left(\overline{a_{2}}^{(e)},\overline{a_{3}}^{(e)},\dots,\overline{a_{e}}^{(e)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{U}_{n})
we were able to directly find a digraph
\Gamma
such that
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})\equiv a_{k}
for each
k\in\{2,\dots,e\}
.
This worked since we can find digraphs with at least one arc whose characteristic polynomial is a monomial.
For the cases of
\mathsf{S}_{n}
and
\mathsf{T}_{n}
, however, this does not work.
Instead, our approach is to take a tuple in
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{M}_{n})
and “lift” it to tuples in
\mathcal{C}_{e+1}(\mathsf{M}_{n^{\prime}})
for some
n^{\prime}>n
.

3.2
Summary of our approach

In this section, we give an overview of our approach for establishing the requisite lower bounds.
The techniques required to establish the upper bounds have been covered in
[
8
]
.

To illustrate the idea, we provide a simple example.

Example 3.2
.

Suppose
e,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
e\geqslant 3
,
n
odd, and
\left(\overline{a_{2}}^{(e)},\overline{a_{3}}^{(e)},\dots,\overline{a_{e}}^{(e)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n})
.
Let
\Gamma
be an
n
-vertex graph such that
c_{k}=\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})\equiv a_{k}\mod 2^{e}
for each
k\in\{2,\dots,e\}
.
It is straightforward to show that, for some integer
c_{e+1}
,

\left(\overline{0}^{(e+1)},\overline{c_{3}}^{(e+1)},\dots,\overline{c_{e}}^{(e+1)},\overline{c_{e+1}}^{(e+1)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e+1}(\mathsf{S}_{n+2^{e}}).

Indeed, one can use the matrix
J-2A(\Gamma\cup 2^{e}\vec{P}_{1})
.

Now, consider the graph
\Lambda=2^{e-2}P_{2}
where
P_{2}=(\{1,2\},\{(1,2),(2,1)\})
.
Observe that

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Lambda)^{k}\mathbf{1}=2^{e-1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Furthermore, since
\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Lambda)}(x)=(x^{2}-1)^{2^{e-2}}
, it follows that, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e\}
, we have

\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Lambda)})\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }k\neq 2;\\
2^{e-2},&\text{ if }k=2\end{cases}\mod{2^{e+1-k}}.

(7)

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup\Lambda
.
Then clearly, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we have
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{k}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}+2^{e-1}
.
Furthermore,
\operatorname{Char}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(x)=\operatorname{Char}_{\Gamma}(x)\operatorname{Char}_{\Lambda}(x)
, whence, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e\}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{j}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Lambda)})\mathsf{c}_{k-j}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)}).

Combining with (
7
), we find that, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e\}
,

(-2)^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})\equiv\begin{cases}(-2)^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)}),&\text{if }k\neq 2;\\
(-2)^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})+2^{e},&\text{if }k=2\end{cases}\mod{2^{e+1}}.

Now, using Lemma
2.2
and Proposition
2.6
, for each
k\in\{3,\dots,e\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=(-2)^{k}\left(\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv(-2)^{k}\left(\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\right)+2^{e}\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k-3}\mathbf{1}\mod{2^{e+1}}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)}),&\text{ if }k\neq 3;\\
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})+2^{e},&\text{ if }k=3\end{cases}\mod{2^{e+1}}.

As a consequence, we see that for some integer
c_{e+1}^{\prime}
,

\left(\overline{0}^{(e+1)},\overline{c_{3}+2^{e}}^{(e+1)},\dots,\overline{c_{e}}^{(e+1)},\overline{c_{e+1}^{\prime}}^{(e+1)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e+1}(\mathsf{S}_{n+2^{e}}).

Example
3.2
demonstrates that there exist certain graphs that one can use to lift tuples from
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{M}_{n})
to
\mathcal{C}_{e+1}(\mathsf{M}_{n+2^{e}})
in a controlled manner in the sense that, after lifting to modulo
2^{e+1}
, only the congruence class of the third coefficient was shifted by
2^{e}
.
One consequence of Example
3.2
is that the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}_{e+1}(\mathsf{M}_{n+2^{e}})
is at least twice that of
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{M}_{n})
.
Below, we will develop this approach to show that, for some
n^{\prime}>n
, the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}_{e+1}(\mathsf{M}_{n^{\prime}})
must be a larger multiple that of
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{M}_{n})

The graph
\Lambda
in Example
3.2
motivates the definition of a “lift graph”, which we define below.

Our approach is inductive.
Consider a subset
\mathsf{M}_{n}\subset\mathsf{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.
For each tuple
\left(\overline{a_{2}}^{(e)},\overline{a_{3}}^{(e)},\dots,\overline{a_{e}}^{(e)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{M}_{n})
, there must exist
M\in\mathsf{M}_{n}
such that
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{M})\equiv a_{k}\mod{2^{e}}
for all
k\in\{2,3,\dots,e\}
.
Our strategy is to “lift” the matrix
M
to a larger corresponding matrix
M^{\prime}
where
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{M^{\prime}})\equiv a_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}2^{e}\mod{2^{e+1}}
for each
k\in\{2,3,\dots,e\}
where
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{0,1\}
.
The possible values for
\varepsilon_{k}
will depend on the set of matrices
\mathsf{M}_{n}
that we are considering, i.e., for some
k\in\{2,3,\dots,e\}
the corresponding value of
\varepsilon_{k}
may always equal
0
.

In order to find this “lifting” matrix
M^{\prime}
, we introduce the notion of a lift graph (resp. lift tournament).
We will demonstrate that lift graphs and lift tournaments exhibit the desired lifting effect on the coefficients of the resulting characteristic polynomials, and we will also need to show that such digraphs exist.

4
Lift graphs

In this section, we define two types of lift graphs and provide constructions of families of these graphs.
First, we define lift graphs of type I and type II.

4.1
Definitions

For positive integers
e
and
f
, we call a graph
\Gamma
an
(e,f)
-
lift graph of type I
if

(L1a)

2^{e-2}
divides
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
;

(L1b)

for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e-1\}
,

\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }k\neq f;\\
2^{e-f-1},&\text{ if }k=f\end{cases}\mod{2^{e-k}}.

Remark 4.1
.

We remark that the graph
2P_{2}
(as in Example
3.2
) is a
(4,2)
-lift graph of type I.
Indeed,
\operatorname{Char}_{A(2P_{2})}=x^{4}-2x^{2}+1
and
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(2P_{2})^{k}\mathbf{1}=2\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(P_{2})^{k}\mathbf{1}=4
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

For
e\in\mathbb{N}
, we call a graph
\Gamma
an
e
-
lift graph of type II
if

(L2a)

for each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,e\}
,

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }k\neq e;\\
2,&\text{ if }k=e\end{cases}\mod{2^{e+2-k}};

(L2b)

2^{e+2-k}
divides
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})
for each
k\in\{1,2,\dots,e+1\}
.

4.2
Lift graphs of type I

Remark
4.1
shows that there exist
(4,2)
-lift graphs of type I.
Before we can provide a construction of
(f+2,f)
-lift graphs of type I for
f\geqslant 3
, we establish a formula for the trace of powers of the adjacency matrix of a cycle graph
C_{n}
, which is defined as

C_{n}=\left(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\{(i,i+1)\;:\;i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\{(i,i-1)\;:\;i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}\right)
.

Proposition 4.2

(cf.
[
2
, Proposition 2.3.]
)
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
be an integer.
Then, for each
k\in\{1,2,\dots,n+1\}
, we have

\operatorname{tr}(A(C_{n})^{k})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }n\neq k\text{ is odd};\\
n{\binom{k}{\frac{k}{2}}},&\text{if }n\neq k\text{ is even};\\
2n,&\text{if }n=k\text{ is odd};\\
n{\binom{n}{\frac{n}{2}}}+2n,&\text{if }n=k\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We count the number of closed walks
\mathbf{x}=x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{k-1}
.
For
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,
x_{i+1}=x_{i}+1
or
x_{i+1}=x_{i}-1
. Let
m
be the number of indices
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
such that
x_{i+1}=x_{i}+1
, then
k-m
is the number of indices
i
such that
x_{i+1}=x_{i}-1
. Since
\mathbf{x}
is a closed walk, it follows that
n
divides
m-(k-m)
.
Furthermore, if
k\leqslant n
then
|2m-k|\leqslant n
, which implies
2m=k
or
n=k
and
m\in\{0,n\}
.
Otherwise, if
k=n+1
then
|2m-k|\leqslant n+1
.
But, since
2m-k\equiv n+1\mod 2
, we must have
2m=k
.

For the case
m=\frac{k}{2}
, the number of closed walks is
n\cdot{\binom{k}{\frac{k}{2}}}
, that is,
n
ways to choose
x_{0}
and
{\binom{k}{\frac{k}{2}}}
ways to choose
m
indices from
\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
.
∎

Now we provide a construction of
(f+2,f)
-lift graphs of type I for each integer
f\geqslant 3
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f\geqslant 3
be an integer.
Then
C_{f}\cup C_{2^{f+1}(f+1)!-f}
is an
(f+2,f)
-lift graph of type I.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=C_{f}\cup C_{2^{f+1}(f+1)!-f}
,
n=2^{f+1}(f+1)!
, and
A=A(\Gamma)
.
Since
\Gamma
is
2
-regular, we have

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A^{k}\mathbf{1}=n2^{k}.

Thus,
\Gamma
satisfies (L1a).
Since
n-f>f+1
, using Proposition
4.2
, it follows that, for
k\leqslant f+1
, we have

\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})=\operatorname{tr}(A(C_{f})^{k})+\operatorname{tr}(A(C_{n-f})^{k})\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }k\neq f;\\
2f,&\text{if }k=f\end{cases}\mod n.

Applying Lemma
2.1
, we obtain that, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,f+1\}
,

\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A})\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }k\neq f;\\
-2,&\text{if }k=f\end{cases}\mod 2^{f+1}.

Hence,
\Gamma
satisfies (L1b).
∎

The next lemma allows us to use the two constructions (Remark
4.1
and Lemma
4.3
) above to generate
(e,f)
-lift graphs of type I for all integers
e
and
f
such that
e\geqslant f+2\geqslant 4
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is an
(e,f)
-lift graph of type I.
Then
2\Gamma
is an
(e+1,f)
-lift graph of type I.

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(2\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}=2\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}\equiv 0\mod{2^{e-1}}
.
Hence, we obtain (L1a).
Since
\operatorname{Char}_{2\Gamma}(x)=\operatorname{Char}_{\Gamma}^{2}(x)
, we can deduce that
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{2\Gamma})\equiv 2\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{\Gamma})\mod{2^{e+1-k}}
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e-1\}
, from which (L1b) follows.
∎

4.3
Lift graphs of type II

In this section, we provide a construction of
e
-lift graphs on type II.
For this, we will use unions of path graphs.
The path graph
P_{n}
is defined as

P_{n}=\left(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\},\{(i,i+1)\;:\;i\in\{0,\dots,n-2\}\}\cup\{(i,i-1)\;:\;i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\}\right).

For a nonnegative integer
c
, a vertex
v\in V(P_{n})
is called
c
-central
if the minimum distance from
v
to a leaf vertex is
c
.
The two leaf vertices of
P_{n}
are precisely its
0
-central vertices.

Denote by
W_{n}(v,k)
the set of walks
\textbf{x}=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
in
P_{n}
where
x_{0}=v
.
Each walk in
W_{n}(v,k)
can be identified with a unique binary string
(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, with
b_{i}=1
if
x_{i}=x_{i-1}+1
and
b_{i}=0
otherwise.
Denote by
\mathsf{w}_{n,v,k}:W_{n}(v,k)\to\{0,1\}^{k}
be the corresponding injective map.

We will require a few results that count the number of certain walks in path graphs.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
be an integer.
Suppose
v\in V(P_{n})
is
c
-central.
Then

(i)

for each nonnegative integer
k\leqslant c
,

\left[A(P_{n})^{k}\mathbf{1}\right]_{v}=2^{k};

(ii)

for each nonnegative integer
k
satisfying
k\leqslant 2c
,

\left[A(P_{n})^{k}\right]_{v,v}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }k\text{ odd},\\
{\binom{k}{\frac{k}{2}}},&\text{ if }k\text{ even.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
k\leqslant c
, we have
\mathsf{w}_{n,v,k}(W_{n}(v,k))=\{0,1\}^{k}
.
Whence, we obtain (i).

Now consider the subset
W^{\prime}(v,k)\subset W(v,k)
of closed walks.
Since
x_{k}=x_{0}
, the image
\mathsf{w}_{n,v,k}(\mathbf{x})
must have the same number of 1s as 0s for each
\mathbf{x}\in W^{\prime}(v,k)
.
Thus, if
k
is odd then
|W^{\prime}(v,k)|=0
.
Otherwise, since
k\leqslant 2c
, we find that
|W^{\prime}(v,k)|=\binom{k}{k/2}
, which establishes (ii).
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geqslant 2
, let
v\in V(P_{2n-1})
and
w\in V(P_{2n-1+2^{m}})
both be
c
-central vertices.

(i)

If
c=n-1
then

\begin{aligned} \left[A(P_{2n-1})^{n}\mathbf{1}\right]_{v}&=2^{n}-2\\
\left[A(P_{2n-1})^{2n}\right]_{v,v}&=\binom{2n}{n}-2\end{aligned}\quad\text{and }\quad\begin{aligned} \left[A(P_{2n-1+2^{m}})^{n}\mathbf{1}\right]_{w}&=2^{n}-1;\\
\left[A(P_{2n-1+2^{m}})^{2n}\right]_{w,w}&=\binom{2n}{n}-1.\end{aligned}

(ii)

If
c<n-1
and
k\leqslant n
then

\left[A(P_{2n-1})^{k}\mathbf{1}\right]_{v}=\left[A(P_{2n-1+2^{m}})^{k}\mathbf{1}\right]_{w}.

(iii)

If
c\leqslant n-1
and
k\leqslant 2n-1
then

\left[A(P_{2n-1})^{k}\right]_{v,v}=\left[A(P_{2n-1+2^{m}})^{k}\right]_{w,w}.

(iv)

If
c<n-1
then

\left[A(P_{2n-1})^{2n}\right]_{v,v}=\left[A(P_{2n-1+2^{m}})^{2n}\right]_{w,w}.

Proof.

Clearly, we have
\mathsf{w}_{2n-1,v,n}(W_{2n-1}(v,n))=\{0,1\}^{n}\backslash\{(0,\dots,0),(1,\dots,1)\}
.
In a similar manner, one observes
\mathsf{w}_{2n-1+2^{m},w,n}(W_{2n-1+2^{m}}(w,n))=\{0,1\}^{n}\backslash\{(0,\dots,0)\}
or
\{0,1\}^{n}\backslash\{(1,\dots,1)\}
.
Let
W_{2n-1}^{\prime}(v,k)\subset W_{2n-1}(v,k)
and
W^{\prime}_{2n-1+2^{m}}(w,k)\subset W_{2n-1+2^{m}}(w,k)
be the subsets of closed walks.
Denote by
B(n)
the subset of
\{0,1\}^{2n}
consisting of elements that have precisely
n
entries equal to
1
.
Since we are considering closed walks,
\mathsf{w}_{2n-1,v,2n}(W^{\prime}_{2n-1}(v,2n))
and

\displaystyle=(-2)^{k}\left(\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})+2^{e-1}(d_{e-1}+\dots+d_{k+1})+2^{e-1}(d_{e-1}+\dots+d_{k})\mod{2^{e}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})+d_{k}2^{e-1}\mod{2^{e}}

\displaystyle\equiv c_{k}\mod{2^{e}}.

Therefore, for some
c_{e}
, we have
\left(\overline{0}^{(e)},\overline{c_{3}}^{(e)},\overline{c_{4}}^{(e)},\dots,\overline{c_{e-1}}^{(e)},\overline{c_{e}}^{(e)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n^{\prime}})
, which is a subset of
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{N})
by Proposition
5.1
together with the fact that
N\equiv n^{\prime}\equiv n\mod{2^{e-2}}
.
∎

Using Lemma

, we can prove the odd case of Theorem

Theorem 5.4
.

For each integer

,
there exists an integer

By Theorem

, it suffices to show that
|\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n})|\geqslant 2^{\binom{e-2}{2}+1}
.
We proceed by induction on

For

The main utility of lift graphs of type II is the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
e
be a positive integer,

\Gamma
be a graph,
and
\Lambda
be an
e

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Gamma\cup\Lambda)^{k}}\mathbf{1}

Since

Lastly, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Gamma\cup\Lambda)^{k}}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Gamma)^{k}}\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Lambda)^{k}}\mathbf{1}
.
The rest of the lemma follows from (L2a).
∎

Now we can prove that the cardinality of

Lemma 5.6
.

Let

be an integer.
Suppose

, where

-lift graph of type I and

-lift graph of type II.

\left|\mathcal{C}_{e}\left(\mathsf{S}_{N}\right)\right|\geqslant 2^{e-3}|\mathcal{C}_{e-1}(\mathsf{S}_{n})|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
, for each
f\in\{3,\dots,e-2\}
, there exists an
(e,f)
-lift graph
\Lambda_{f}
of type I.
By Lemma
4.8
, there exists an
(e-2)
-lift graph
\Lambda_{e-1}

Denote by

the order of each graph
\Lambda_{f}
and by

the order of
\Lambda_{e-1}

Let
(c_{3},\dots,c_{e-1})\in\{1,\dots,2^{e}\}^{e-3}
such that

\left(\overline{0}^{(e-1)},\overline{c_{3}}^{(e-1)},\dots,\overline{c_{e-1}}^{(e-1)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e-1}(\mathsf{S}_{n}).

Let
\Gamma
be an
n
-vertex graph such that

\mathsf{c}_{i}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})\equiv c_{i}\mod{2^{e-1}}
for each

.
Hence, there must exist

such that

.

Let

\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup d_{3}\Lambda_{3}\cup d_{4}\Lambda_{4}\cup\dots\cup d_{e-2}\Lambda_{e-2}\cup d_{e-1}\Lambda_{e-1}

be the order of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.
Then

.
By Lemma
5.2
and Lemma

, for each

\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})\equiv\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})+d_{k}2^{e-1-k},&\text{if }k\leqslant e-2;\\
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)}),&\text{if }k=e-1\end{cases}\mod{2^{e-k}}.

Furthermore, for each

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{k}}\mathbf{1}\equiv\begin{cases}\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Gamma)^{k}}\mathbf{1},&\text{if }k\leqslant e-3;\\
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}{A(\Gamma)^{k}}\mathbf{1}+2d_{e-1},&\text{if }k=e-2\end{cases}\mod{2^{e-k}}.

Hence, using Lemma
2.2
and Proposition
2.6
, when

, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=(-2)^{k}(\mathsf{c}_{i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{i-1}\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})+d_{k}2^{e-1}\equiv c_{k}\mod{2^{e}}

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{e-1}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma^{\prime})})

\displaystyle=(-2)^{e-1}(\mathsf{c}_{e-1}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{e-1}\mathsf{c}_{e-1-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{i-1}\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle\equiv(-2)^{e-1}\mathsf{c}_{e-1}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})-\sum_{i=1}^{e-1}\mathsf{c}_{e-1-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma^{\prime})})(-2)^{e-2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma^{\prime})^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\mod{2^{e}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{c}_{e-1}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})+d_{e-1}2^{e-1}\equiv c_{e-1}\mod{2^{e}}.

Therefore, for some
c_{e}
, we have
\left(\overline{0}^{(e)},\overline{c_{3}}^{(e)},\overline{c_{4}}^{(e)},\dots,\overline{c_{e-1}}^{(e)},\overline{c_{e}}^{(e)}\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n^{\prime}})
which is a subset of
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{N})

5.1
together with the fact that
N\equiv n^{\prime}\equiv n\mod{2^{e-2}}
.
∎

Using Lemma
5.6
, we can prove the even counterpart of Theorem

.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
e\geqslant 3
be an integer.
Then there exists
N_{e}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

|\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n})|=2^{\binom{e-2}{2}}
for each even integer

.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to show that
|\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n})|\geqslant 2^{\binom{e-2}{2}}
.
We proceed by induction on
e
.
For
e=3
, the bound is trivial.
For
e=4
, we can take
N_{4}=4
.
Then
\{([0]_{4},[0]_{4},[0]_{4}),([0]_{4},[8]_{4},[0]_{4})\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{4}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
for each even

.
Indeed, we have the graph
\Gamma_{1}=nP_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}=P_{3}\cup(n-3)P_{1}
.
For the former, one finds that
\mathsf{c}_{3}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma_{1})})\equiv 0\mod{16}
and for the latter,
\mathsf{c}_{3}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma_{2})})\equiv 8\mod{16}
.

Now we can assume
e\geqslant 5
for the inductive step.
Take
N_{e}=N_{e-1}+\sum_{f=3}^{e-2}(e-f-1)n_{f}+m
where

is the order of an
(e,f)
-lift graph of type I and
m
is the order of an
(e-2)
-lift graph of type II.

Suppose
n=N_{e}+2k
for some nonnegative integer

.
Then, by the implicit inductive hypothesis, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{e-1}(\mathsf{S}_{N_{e-1}+2k})|\geqslant 2^{\binom{e-3}{2}}
.
By Lemma
5.6
, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n})|\geqslant 2^{e-3}2^{\binom{e-3}{2}}=2^{\binom{e-2}{2}}
.

When
n
is even, Lemma
2.3
can be extended to the statement that says

divides

for each

.
Combining with Theorem

reveals that, for even

large enough, we must have

\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{S}_{n})=\left\{\left(\overline{0}^{(e)},\overline{a_{3}}^{(e)},\overline{a_{4}}^{(e)},\dots,\overline{a_{e-1}}^{(e)},\overline{0}^{(e)}\right)\;:\;2^{i}\text{ divides }a_{i}\text{ for each $i\in\{3,\dots,e-1\}$}\right\}.

).

6
Lift tournaments

In this section, we define two types of lift tournaments and provide constructions of families of these tournaments.
First, we give the main definitions.

6.1
Definitions

For
e,f\in\mathbb{N}
, we call a tournament
\Gamma
an
(e,f)
-
lift tournament of type I
if

(LT1a)

2^{e-k}
divides
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}
for each
k\in\{0,\dots,e-1\}
;

(LT1b)

for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e-1\}
,

\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})\equiv\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }k\neq f;\\
2^{e-f-1},&\text{ if $k=f$}\end{cases}\mod{2^{e-k}}.

Our constructions of lift tournaments of type I are obtained from Lemma
6.9
and Lemma
6.10
.

For an integer
e\geqslant 2
, we call a tournament
\Gamma
an
e
-
lift tournament of type II
if

(LT2a)

2^{e}
divides
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k}\mathbf{1}
for each
k\in\{0,\dots,e-3\}
;

(LT2b)

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{e-2}\mathbf{1}\equiv 2\mod 4
and
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{e-1}\mathbf{1}\equiv 1\mod 2
;

(LT2c)

2^{e}
divides
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,e-2\}
.

Lemma
6.11
provides constructions of lift tournaments of type II.

6.2
The join of two tournaments

For tournaments
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, we will refer
\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
as the tournament that has adjacency matrix

A(\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}A(\Gamma_{1})&O\\
J&A(\Gamma_{2})\end{pmatrix}.

This means that the tournament
\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
has two components
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
and edges from each vertex of
\Gamma_{2}
to
\Gamma_{1}
.
For a positive integer
d
and tournament
\Gamma
, we use
d\Gamma
to denote
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\Gamma
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be tournaments.
Then, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2})^{k}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{1})^{k}\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{2})^{k}\mathbf{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{1})^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{2})^{k-i}\mathbf{1}.

Proof.

The number of walks
\mathfrak{w}_{k}
consisting of
k
arcs is equal to
\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2})^{k}\mathbf{1}
.
On the other hand,
\mathfrak{w}_{k}
is the number of walks with
k
arcs, each consisting of
k+1
vertices.
Since there are no arcs from
\Gamma_{1}
to
\Gamma_{2}
, each walk has the form
(v_{1},\dots,v_{r},w_{1},\dots,w_{k+1-r})
for some
r\in\{0,1,\dots,k+1\}
, where
w_{j}\in V(\Gamma_{1})
and
v_{i}\in V(\Gamma_{2})
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,k+1-r\}
.
Therefore, we must have

\mathfrak{w}_{k}=\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{1})^{k}\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{2})^{r}\mathbf{1}+\sum_{r=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{1})^{r-1}\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma_{2})^{k-r}\mathbf{1},

as required.
∎

6.3
The walk polynomial

Given a tournament
\Gamma
and a positive integer
d
, we define the
walk polynomial

\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d}(x)
by

\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d}(x):=x^{d}+\sum^{d}_{i=1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{i-1}\mathbf{1}x^{d-i}.

Note that the coefficient of
x^{d-k}
in
\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d}(x)
is equal to the number of walks in
\Gamma
on
k
vertices, that is,
\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d})=\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{k-1}\mathbf{1}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
and
\mathsf{c}_{0}(\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d})=1
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
d
be a positive integer, and let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be tournaments.
Suppose that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\oplus\Gamma_{2}
.
Then
\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d}(x)=\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma_{1},d}(x)\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma_{2},d}(x)
.
Furthermore, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, we have

\mathsf{p}_{k}(\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d})=\mathsf{p}_{k}(\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma_{1},d})+\mathsf{p}_{k}(\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma_{2},d}).

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, we have
\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma,d}(x)=\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma_{1},d}(x)\operatorname{Walk}_{\Gamma_{2},d}(x)
, from which the lemma immediately follows.
∎

6.4
Almost transitive tournaments

We define the tournament
T_{t}
as the tournament having
t+2
vertices and, for each
i
and
j
satisfying
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant t+1
,

Lemma 6.3

Let

Similarly, we must have

If

Now we can give our first construction of lift tournaments.

, the coefficients

and
\mathsf{c}_{2}(\operatorname{Char}_{M})

\mathsf{c}_{j}(\operatorname{Char}_{M})
for each

The lemma follows since
\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{T}_{n^{\prime}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{T}_{N})
by Proposition
7.2
and the fact that
N\equiv n^{\prime}\equiv n\mod{2^{e}}
.
∎

Using Lemma
7.4
, we can prove the odd case of Theorem
1.4
with a simple induction.

Theorem 7.5
.

For each integer
e\geqslant 2
,
there exists an integer
N_{e}
such that
\left|\mathcal{C}_{e}\left(\mathsf{T}_{n}\right)\right|=2^{\lfloor\frac{e-2}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-3}{2}\rfloor}
for each odd integer

.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.1
, it suffices to show that
|\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{T}_{n})|\geqslant 2^{\lfloor\frac{e-2}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-3}{2}\rfloor}
.
We proceed by induction on
e
.
For
e\geqslant 4
, the claim is vacuous: one can take
N_{e}=1
.

Now we can assume
e\geqslant 5
for the inductive step.
Take
N_{e}=N_{e-1}+\sum_{f=2}^{\lceil e/2\rceil-1}n_{2f}
where
n_{f}
is the order of an
(e,f)
-lift tournament of type I.
Suppose
n=N_{e}+2k
for some nonnegative integer

.
Then, by the implicit inductive hypothesis,
|\mathcal{C}_{e-1}(\mathsf{T}_{N_{e-1}+2k})|=2^{\lfloor\frac{e-3}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-4}{2}\rfloor}
.
By Lemma
7.4
, we have

|\mathcal{C}_{e}(\mathsf{T}_{n})|\geqslant 2^{\lfloor\frac{e-3}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-4}{2}\rfloor}2^{\lceil e/2\rceil-2}=2^{\lfloor\frac{e-2}{2}\rfloor\lfloor\frac{e-3}{2}\rfloor}.\qed

The main utility of lift tournaments of type II is the following lemma.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
e
be an even integer,
\Gamma
be a tournament of even order, and
\Lambda
be an
e
-lift tournament of type II.
Then, for each even

\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)})\equiv\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)}),&\text{ if }k\leqslant e-2\\
\mathsf{c}_{e}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma)})+2^{e-1},&\text{ if $k=e$}\end{cases}\mod{2^{e}}.

Using (LT2c) with the identity
\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)}(x)=\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)}(x)\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Lambda)}(x)
, it follows that, for each

,

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Lambda)})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})\mod{2^{e}}

(23)

By Lemma
6.1
together with (LT2a), it follows that, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,e-2\}
, we have

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\equiv\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\mod{2^{e}}.

(24)

By Lemma
2.2
, (
23
),
and (
24
), for even
k\in\{2,\dots,e-2\}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{J-2A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)})

\displaystyle=2^{k}\left(\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)})\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma\oplus\Lambda)^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv 2^{k}\left(\mathsf{c}_{k}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{c}_{k-i}(\operatorname{Char}_{A(\Gamma)})\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{i-1}\mathbf{1}\right)\mod{2^{e}}

\mathbf{1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}A(\Gamma)^{0}\mathbf{1}

, for each

(c_{4},c_{6},\dots,c_{e-2})\in\{1,\dots,2^{e}\}^{e/2-2}

Theorem 7.8

, it suffices to show that

, and
