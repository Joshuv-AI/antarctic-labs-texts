Psychometric Properties of the Turkish Version of the Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen–2 (CATS-2) and Its Associations With Posttraumatic Stress, Depression, and Anxiety Symptoms
Authors: Fatmanur Çimen, İsmail Seçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the aim was to adapt the Child and Adolescent Trauma Screening-2 (CATS-2) to Turkish and evaluate its psychometric properties. The study also examined how posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, depression, and anxiety are associated with each other. The results showed that the Turkish CATS-2 is a valid and reliable tool for assessing trauma-related symptoms in children and adolescents. Network analysis revealed that emotion regulation occupies a central position in the symptom network and has strong connections with re-experiencing and hyperarousal symptoms. Further analysis indicated that anxiety and relational difficulties are strongly connected to other symptoms within the network. Overall, the findings suggest that emotion regulation difficulties, anxiety, and relational problems are important aspects of trauma-related symptom patterns in children and adolescents. The Turkish CATS-2 can be used to help understand these symptom patterns, supporting research and practice in evaluating trauma responses in youth.Keywords: posttraumatic stress disorder, complex PTSD, depression, anxiety

Introduction

Events that cause intense stress or pose a threat to life—such as the loss of a loved one, traffic accidents, illness, injury, or death—are defined as traumatic. Studies have shown that approximately 70% of individuals worldwide experience at least one traumatic event during their lifetime, and 31% experience four or more traumatic events (Benjet et al., 2016). Therefore, traumatic events, which are highly common, are also associated with serious mental health problems such as depression, stress, and anxiety. PTSD is a severe and highly persistent consequence of exposure to traumatic events (Du et al., 2022).

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder was officially recognized in 1980 with the publication of DSM-III (Condiotte, 2021; Du et al., 2022). According to the DSM-5, one of the world’s leading diagnostic systems, PTSD is categorized into four symptom clusters that emerge following direct or indirect exposure to a traumatic event: intrusion, avoidance, negative changes in cognition and mood, and heightened arousal (APA, 2013). In the ICD-11, another major diagnostic system, a revision in 2018 proposed a new diagnosis in addition to PTSD, known as complex posttraumatic stress disorder (CPTSD). CPTSD is defined to encompass two diagnostic categories: PTSD and disturbances in self-organization (DSO). PTSD is characterized by symptoms of re-experiencing traumatic events, avoidance, and hyperarousal, whereas DSO includes symptoms of emotional dysregulation, negative self-concept, and interpersonal disturbances (Clifford et al., 2018; WHO, 2018).

The literature indicates that PTSD is a highly prevalent mental health disorder. In a meta-analysis conducted by Schincariol et al. (2024) aiming to determine the prevalence of PTSD, the overall prevalence was reported as 23.95%. The prevalence of PTSD is notably high among children and adolescents, who are the most vulnerable group to the effects of traumatic events (Tamir et al., 2025). In a meta-analysis by Rezayat et al. (2020) examining the prevalence of PTSD in children and adolescents following earthquakes and floods, the prevalence rates during the first, second, third, and fourth six-month periods were reported as 19.2%, 30.0%, 24.4%, and 20.4%, respectively. In the study conducted by Çimen, Seçer, Alberici, and Meiser-Stedman (2025) on children and adolescents exposed to the earthquake, it was reported that more than half of the participants exhibited moderate to severe PTSD symptoms even two years after the earthquake.

PTSD, which has been reported to have a high prevalence, exerts profound effects on the development and well-being of children and adolescents, making it a significant mental health concern for this population (Li et al., 2020). In a study conducted by Skrypnyk et al. (2024) on children affected by war, PTSD was identified as a risk factor for the development of depression, anxiety, behavioral disorders, as well as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. A study by Xu & Wei (2013) involving children and adolescents exposed to earthquakes reported a causal relationship between PTSD and depression. Furthermore, Lofthouse et al. (2023) investigated adolescents exposed to multiple traumatic events and found that a large proportion of these youths met the criteria for CPTSD. Among adolescents with CPTSD, the severity of comorbid depression and panic symptoms was higher, and more negative posttraumatic cognitions were observed.

In the literature, considering the DSO dimension of CPTSD, which includes negative self-concept and disturbances in relationships, it has been reported that symptoms of depression and CPTSD significantly overlap (Fung et al., 2025; Vang et al., 2021). Recent studies have particularly examined the co-occurrence rates of CPTSD and depression (Hyland et al., 2018; Karatzias et al., 2019). In their study screening depressive patients, Fung et al. (2025) found that 57% of individuals with depression might also meet criteria for CPTSD. Furthermore, other studies reported that 67% to 89% of individuals with CPTSD symptoms also exhibited depressive symptoms (Hyland et al., 2021; Karatzias et al., 2019). These findings indicate that while depression and CPTSD share common features, their distinct symptomatology highlights the complexity of their relationship. In a study conducted by Hyland et al. (2017), it was found that the DSO dimension, which constitutes CPTSD, is closely associated with depressive symptoms, whereas the PTSD structure is linked to generalized anxiety disorder. In this context, a diagnosis of CPTSD was differentiated from PTSD by higher levels of depression. Considering the relationships among PTSD, anxiety, and depression, it has been suggested that there is no clear distinction between these disorders (Grant et al., 2008). Consequently, the strong associations among these conditions raise questions regarding the onset and maintenance of these disorders. In this regard, it remains unclear whether PTSD/CPTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms form distinct clusters and how similar symptoms are interconnected (Gilbar, 2020). The co-occurrence of these disorders may further compromise an individual’s mental health and lead to the emergence of specific comorbid conditions. Therefore, addressing the complex relationships among symptoms is crucial for developing and implementing interventions both in research and clinical practice (Lin et al., 2025). However, conducting such studies requires valid and reliable measurement tools. While numerous assessment instruments have been developed for diagnosing PTSD, the use of tools that have been updated or newly designed in accordance with recent revisions in diagnostic categories is particularly important (Cohen, 2010; Dowdy-Hazlett et al., 2021).

While numerous assessment instruments have been developed for diagnosing PTSD, the use of tools that have been updated or newly designed in accordance with recent revisions in diagnostic categories is particularly important (Cohen, 2010; Dowdy-Hazlett et al., 2021). Various internationally used assessment tools are available for diagnosing and evaluating PTSD symptoms in children and adolescents. One of these, the child and adolescent version of the ITQ, was developed for children aged 7–17. This 12-item measure has been adapted into Turkish by Çimen and Seçer (2025) and assesses PTSD solely based on the ICD-11 framework (Cloitre et al., 2018). The CATS-2, developed by Sachser et al. (2021), evaluates PTSD in children and adolescents according to both the DSM-5 and ICD-11 frameworks.

Accordingly, the primary aim of this study is to adapt the CATS-2 for the Turkish culture and examine its validity and reliability. Additionally, the study aims to investigate the direct and indirect relationships among trauma symptoms, anxiety, and depression in children and adolescents.

Method

Participants

Data were collected from children and adolescents aged 7–17 who experienced the February 6, 2023 earthquakes centered in Kahramanmaraş. It should be noted that participants were not diagnosed with PTSD; rather, they were included based on exposure to this potentially traumatic event. Prior to data collection, the study was introduced to and explained to local authorities and school principals. Following approval from the local authorities and school principals, contact was made with the children and their parents. Participants were provided with information about the purpose and scope of the study, emphasizing confidentiality and voluntary participation. In addition, the parents of the participating children and adolescents were fully informed about the study’s objectives and procedures. Informed consent was obtained from parents before administering the assessment tools; children whose parents did not provide consent were not included in the study. Data were collected online via a link created using Google Forms. Detailed information about the study sample is presented in Table 1.

Measures

Child and Adolescent Trauma Screening-2 (CATS-2)

The scale developed by Sachser et al. (2021) to screen for posttraumatic stress symptoms can assess children and adolescents aged 7–17 in accordance with both DSM-5 and ICD-11 diagnostic criteria. The scale consists of three sections. The first section includes a 15-item checklist (yes/no) evaluating potential traumatic events experienced by the participants. The second section consists of a 25-item frequency scale using a 5-point Likert format [0 = Never, 3 = Almost Always] to assess trauma symptom severity. The final section contains a checklist (yes/no) assessing the impact of posttraumatic stress symptoms on psychosocial functioning across five key domains: getting along with others, school/work, hobbies, family relationships, and overall well-being.

Revised Children’s Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS-25)

The scale developed to assess depression and anxiety in children and adolescents was adapted to Turkish culture by Seçer and Şimşek (2015) (Ebesutani et al., 2012). The scale consists of 25 items rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale and includes two subscales: depression and anxiety. The adaptation study reported that the scale demonstrated adequate validity and reliability for use within the Turkish cultural context.

Procedure

The adaptation process of the scale began with obtaining permission from the original developers. Once permission was granted, the translation process commenced. To ensure linguistic equivalence, a forward-backward translation procedure was followed. Accordingly, the scale was translated from English to Turkish. In the initial stage, two researchers with a strong command of English and expertise in guidance and psychological counseling independently translated the scale items. The translated items were then reviewed by an English language expert, and feedback was obtained. Subsequently, the authors compiled and compared the translated items and finalized the Turkish version of the scale. To evaluate the appropriateness of the Turkish version, it was back-translated into English by an English language expert. The original and back-translated versions were compared, and the items were found to be consistent with the original, indicating that the scale possessed linguistic validity.

Data Analysis

Construct Validity

The structural validity of the CATS-2 was examined using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Model fit was evaluated by examining the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values within the CFA framework. In CFA, a χ2/df value of ≤5 is considered acceptable, while values ≤3 indicate good model fit. For CFI and TLI, values ≥.90 are acceptable, and values ≥.95 indicate good fit. RMSEA and SRMR values ≤.08 are considered acceptable, whereas values ≤.06 reflect good fit (Collier, 2020). To identify the best-fitting model, AIC values were examined. In model comparisons, the model with the lowest AIC and BIC values is considered the most likely to be replicated and provides better fit compared to competing models with fewer free parameters (Kline, 2016).

To assess the reliability of the scale, the Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficient was examined, with values of α ≥ .70 considered indicative of high reliability (Karagöz, 2017). Parallel form evaluation was used to assess the structural validity of the scale. For this purpose, the RCADS-25, a measure used to assess depression and anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents, was employed as a reference. The relationship between the scale and the RCADS-25 was evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Accordingly, values between .70 and .90 indicate a strong relationship, values between .40 and .60 indicate a moderate relationship, and values between .10 and .30 indicate a weak relationship (Dancey & Reidy, 2011).

Network Analysis

Network analysis was conducted to examine the complex interrelationships among PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms at the symptom level. Unlike latent variable approaches, network analysis conceptualizes psychopathology as a system of directly interacting symptoms, allowing the identification of central symptoms that may play a key role in maintaining the symptom network (Borsboom et al., 2021; Briganti et al., 2021). This approach was particularly suitable for the present study, as it enabled the simultaneous evaluation of direct and indirect associations among symptom clusters and facilitated the identification of bridge symptoms linking PTSD, depression, and anxiety.

The network was estimated using graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC). A hyperparameter setting of 0.5 was applied, which enabled the construction of a more conservative model (Burger et al., 2023). Relationships between all node pairs were estimated using partial correlations (Friedman et al., 2008). The R packages qgraph and bootnet were used to visualize the network model (Epskamp & Fried, 2018), while the mgm package in R was employed to compute the predictability of network nodes.

To determine the importance of each node in the network, the Expected Influence (EI) centrality index, which has been reported to provide more reliable results in psychopathological networks, was calculated (Bringmann et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019). In addition, Bridge Expected Influence (Bridge EI) was computed to identify bridge symptoms with the strongest connections to symptom clusters. The magnitude of the obtained values indicates the centrality of the respective node within the network. Network stability was evaluated by calculating the Correlation Stability (CS) coefficients, with values above 0.5 indicating higher stability. Network accuracy and stability were assessed using the bootnet package in R (Epskamp & Fried, 2018).

Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) Analysis

Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) analysis was conducted to explore potential directional relationships among trauma symptoms, depression, and anxiety. While standard network models estimate undirected associations, DAG analysis allows the estimation of probabilistic directional pathways based on conditional dependencies among variables. Therefore, DAG was employed to complement the network analysis by providing insight into potential hierarchical or causal structures among symptom clusters.

To construct the DAG, a score-based hill-climbing algorithm was employed. This algorithm evaluates different combinations of edges among variables, adds or removes edges, reverses their directions, and identifies the optimal model based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Network stability was assessed using 10,000 bootstrap samples, with the parameters set to 5 random restarts and 10 perturbations (Bernstein et al., 2019). The DAG analysis was conducted using the bnlearn package in R.

Discussion

The results of the study indicated that the Turkish-adapted version of the CATS-2 is a valid and reliable instrument for use with children and adolescents in Türkiye. This finding is consistent with previous validation studies conducted in different linguistic contexts. For example, Kooij et al. (2024) reported that the Dutch version of the CATS-2 demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties, and Karagiorga et al. (2024) similarly found that the Greek version was valid and reliable for traumatized children and adolescents. Taken together, these findings suggest that the CATS-2 demonstrates stable psychometric performance across different language adaptations and may serve as a useful instrument for assessing PTSD symptoms in diverse populations.

The network analysis examining the relationships among PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms indicated that emotion regulation was positioned as a relatively central node within the symptom network and showed strong associations with re-experiencing and hyperarousal symptoms. These findings highlight the prominence of emotion regulation within the overall configuration of symptoms; however, they do not imply directional or causal relationships. In the broader literature, emotion regulation has been conceptualized as a transdiagnostic construct across various mental disorders, including PTSD (Fitzgerald et al., 2018; Muñoz-Rivas et al., 2021).

Previous research has reported associations between emotion regulation difficulties and the severity or maintenance of PTSD-related symptoms (Conti et al., 2023; Knefel et al., 2018). Similarly, emotional suppression and experiential avoidance have been linked to PTSD symptom clusters (Henschel et al., 2021), and impulse control–related emotion regulation difficulties have been found to co-occur with PTSD symptoms in trauma-exposed individuals (Short et al., 2018). Deficits in emotion regulation strategies have also been reported to be associated with re-experiencing and avoidance symptoms (Short et al., 2016; Steil et al., 2011). Within the CPTSD framework, impairments in emotional functioning have been described as potentially linking DSO and PTSD symptom components (Jaworska-Andryszewska & Rybakowski, 2019; Liang & Yang, 2023; Ono et al., 2016). In line with these findings, Yousefi and Abdoli (2024) observed that individuals with elevated PTSD and DSO scores also reported higher levels of emotion regulation difficulties, anxiety, and depression. Overall, the present findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that emotion regulation difficulties are closely associated with PTSD symptom networks.

In addition, the DAG analysis conducted to explore the pattern of associations among depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms suggested that Disturbed Relationships and anxiety occupied relatively influential positions within the estimated network structure. These findings should be interpreted as reflecting statistical dependencies rather than directional or causal pathways. The prominence of anxiety within the network is consistent with previous literature reporting high levels of anxiety and related disorders following exposure to natural disasters such as earthquakes, droughts, floods, storms, and tsunamis (Ilhan et al., 2023; Keya et al., 2023; Çimen, Seçer, & Pakiş, 2025). A systematic review and meta-analysis by Keya et al. (2023) identified anxiety-related conditions and PTSD among the most prevalent mental health concerns in disaster-exposed populations. Furthermore, prior research has documented substantial overlap between anxiety symptoms and PTSD among earthquake survivors (Yıldırım et al., 2025).

Consistent with the positioning of Disturbed Relationships within the network, previous research has highlighted the relevance of social factors in post-disaster psychological adjustment. Changes in social relationships and perceived social support have been associated with variations in anxiety and trauma-related symptoms (Nickerson et al., 2017; Xu & Wang, 2012). Although the present network findings do not establish causal influence, they suggest that social-interpersonal difficulties are closely interconnected with anxiety and PTSD symptoms within the overall symptom configuration. These associations underscore the potential importance of considering social-contextual variables when evaluating trauma-related symptom presentations (Xi et al., 2020).

Although exploratory edge-level analyses could provide additional insight into the relationships among anxiety, depression, and PTSD symptom clusters, such analyses were not conducted in the present study. Future research with larger or clinically diverse samples may consider this approach to further examine fine-grained associations between symptom clusters.

Strengths and Limitations

This study contributes to the literature by providing evidence regarding the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the CATS-2 in a sample of children and adolescents exposed to a natural disaster. Additionally, the use of network and DAG approaches allowed for the examination of complex statistical associations among PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms at the symptom-cluster level.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, participants were included based on exposure to a potentially traumatic event and were not clinically diagnosed with PTSD. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to clinical populations. Second, the sample consisted exclusively of children and adolescents who experienced an earthquake; future research should examine the psychometric properties of the scale in samples exposed to other types of traumatic events, such as abuse, neglect, interpersonal violence, or bereavement. Third, although associations among symptom clusters were examined using network and DAG analyses, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding temporal or causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how these symptom domains interact over time.
