On Minkowski–Weyl theorem and Gordan’s lemma up to symmetry
Dinh Van Le

Abstract
. We investigate equivariant analogues of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem and Gordan’s lemma in an infinite-dimensional setting, where cones and monoids are invariant under the action of the infinite symmetric group. Building upon the framework developed in [ 15 , 18 ] , we extend the theory beyond the nonnegative case. Our main contributions include a local equivariant Minkowski–Weyl theorem, local-global principles for equivariant finite generation and stabilization of symmetric cones, and a full proof of the equivariant Gordan’s lemma conjectured in [ 18 ] . We also classify non-pointed symmetric cones and non-positive symmetric normal monoids, addressing new challenges in the general setting.

Primary: 05E18; Secondary: 52B99, 20M30, 90C05

1.
Introduction

The Minkowski–Weyl theorem and Gordan’s lemma are foundational results in convex geometry. The former establishes the duality between two representations of a polyhedral cone: as the conical hull of finitely many vectors, or as the intersection of finitely many linear halfspaces. The latter states that the lattice points within a rational polyhedral cone form an affine monoid. These results are not only cornerstones of polyhedral theory, but also underlie significant developments in areas such as linear and integer programming, invariant theory, toric geometry, algebraic statistics, and the theory of lattice polytopes; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
8
,
9
,
26
]
for details.

In this paper, we investigate equivariant analogues of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem and Gordan’s lemma in an infinite-dimensional setting, where cones and monoids are invariant under the action of the infinite symmetric group
\Sym
(see
Section

2
for precise definitions). Such symmetry arises naturally in various fields, including algebraic statistics
[
1
,
2
,
14
]
, commutative algebra
[
10
,
11
,
16
,
17
,
22
,
23
]
, convex optimization
[
20
]
, group theory
[
7
]
, machine learning
[
21
,
24
]
, and representation theory
[
5
,
6
,
25
]
.

A foundational framework for the study of
\Sym
-invariant cones and monoids was laid out in
[
15
]
, with a primary focus on the nonnegative case. There, the authors considered
global
cones
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}_{\geq 0}
and associated to them chains of
local
cones
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
C_{n}=C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A key result in that setting was a
local-global principle
which characterizes when a global cone is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated in terms of its local truncations. An analogous principle was established for
\Sym
-invariant monoids in
\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}_{\geq 0}
.

This line of work was extended in
[
18
]
, where the authors pursued equivariant extensions of classical results in convex geometry (see
[
18
, Problem 1.1]
). In particular, they proposed the following conjectural analogue of Gordan’s lemma (see
[
18
, Conjecture 1.4]
):

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be a
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated rational cone. Then
M=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is a
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated normal monoid.

This conjecture was verified for nonnegative cones in
[
18
, Theorem 6.1]
. Moreover, it was also shown that the dual cone of a
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated cone need not be
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated
[
18
, Theorem 4.9]
, indicating that a global equivariant analogue of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem does not hold in general. Nevertheless, a local version of this theorem was established for chains of nonnegative cones
[
18
, Theorem 4.4]
, providing an explicit description of the corresponding dual chains.

The goal of the present paper is to generalize the results of
[
15
,
18
]
to arbitrary
\Sym
-invariant cones, thus removing the nonnegativity assumption and substantially broadening the scope of the theory. More precisely, we prove:

•

a local equivariant analogue of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem for any stabilizing
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones (see
Theorem

3.3
);

•

local-global principles characterizing equivariant finite generation and stabilization of general
\Sym
-invariant cones (see
Theorems

4.1
and
4.3
);

•

Conjecture

1.1
in its full generality (see
Theorem

6.1
).

Several new challenges arise in this more general setting. For instance, we do not yet know whether a local-global principle holds for all
\Sym
-invariant monoids (see
Problem

4.7
). However, we are able to establish such a principle under the additional assumption that the monoid is both positive and normal (see
Corollary

4.5
). Moreover, in contrast to the nonnegative case, a cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is not necessarily pointed, and thus the associated monoid
C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
may fail to be positive. This prevents a direct application of the aforementioned principle for positive normal monoids. To address this, we classify all non-pointed
\Sym
-invariant cones (see
Theorem

5.1
) and all non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoids (see
Corollary

5.9
).

The results obtained in this paper contribute to a broader program aimed at understanding algebraic and geometric structures in infinite-dimensional settings up to symmetry. They provide new techniques for analyzing symmetric cones and monoids, and offer a foundation for further developments in this direction.

2.
Symmetric cones and monoids

We begin by introducing the infinite-dimensional ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, equipped with the natural action of the infinite symmetric group
\Sym
via coordinate permutations. Our focus is on cones and monoids in this space that are invariant under the
\Sym
-action. These symmetric structures form the main objects of study in this paper.

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of positive integers. Throughout the paper, we consider the infinite dimensional real vector space

\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\bigoplus_{\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{R},

which has a basis indexed by
\mathbb{N}
. Any element
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
can be expressed as
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where each coordinate
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and only finitely many of them are nonzero. We call

\supp({\bm{u}})=\{i\in\mathbb{N}\colon u_{i}\neq 0\}

the
support
of
{\bm{u}}
. The number
|\supp({\bm{u}})|
is referred to as the
support size
of
{\bm{u}}.

Let
\{{\bm{e}}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, where
{\bm{e}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is the vector consisting of all zeroes except in the
i
-th coordinate, which is 1.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we regard
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
spanned by
{\bm{e}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{e}}_{n}
. That is, each element
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is identified with the vector

(u_{1},\dots,u_{n},0,0,\dots)\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, where all coordinates beyond the
n
-th position are zero.
Via this identification,
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is realized as the direct limit of the following ascending chain of finite dimensional subspaces:

\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\cdots.

2.1.
Symmetric groups and their actions

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Sym(n)
denote the symmetric group on
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
. By identifying
\Sym(n)
with the stabilizer subgroup of
n+1
in
\Sym(n+1)
, we obtain an ascending chain of finite symmetric groups:

\Sym(1)\subset\Sym(2)\subset\cdots\subset\Sym(n)\subset\cdots.

The direct limit of this chain is the infinite symmetric group

\Sym(\infty)=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\Sym(n),

which consists of all permutations of
\mathbb{N}
that fix all but finitely many elements. For brevity, we will henceforth refer to
\Sym(\infty)
simply as
\Sym
.

The group
\Sym
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
by permuting coordinates. More specifically, for
\sigma\in\Sym
and
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}{\bm{e}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, the action is given by

\sigma({\bm{u}})=(u_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},u_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}{\bm{e}}_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}{\bm{e}}_{\sigma(i)}.

In particular,
\sigma({\bm{e}}_{i})={\bm{e}}_{\sigma(i)}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Evidently, this action induces an action of
\Sym(n)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for each
n\geq 1
.

For a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
and a subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\Sym
denote

\Gamma(A)=\{\sigma({\bm{u}})\mid\sigma\in\Gamma,\ {\bm{u}}\in A\}.

We say that
A
is
\Gamma
-invariant
if
\Gamma(A)\subseteq A
.
In this paper, we mainly focus on the case
\Gamma=\Sym
or
\Gamma=\Sym(n)
for some
n\geq 1
. An ascending chain

A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots\subset A_{n}\subset\cdots,

where
A_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
, is called
\Sym
-invariant
if each
A_{n}
is
\Sym(n)
-invariant. For such a chain, one checks readily that its direct limit
A=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}A_{n}
is
\Sym
-invariant. Conversely, given a
\Sym
-invariant subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, the ascending chain of its truncations:

A\cap\mathbb{R}\subset A\cap\mathbb{R}^{2}\subset\cdots\subset A\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\cdots

is
\Sym
-invariant. In what follows, we typically use the term
global
to refer to a set, cone, or monoid

A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, while a set, cone, or monoid
A_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is referred to as
local
.

2.2.
Symmetric cones

Given a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, the
cone generated by
A
is defined as

\cone(A)=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}A\coloneqq\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}\mid{\bm{a}}_{i}\in A,\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

This cone is said to be
finitely generated
if
A
is finite, and
rational
if
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Observe that any finitely generated cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
must be contained in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The classical Minkowski–Weyl theorem states that every finitely generated cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
can equivalently be represented as the intersection of finitely many linear halfspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see, for example,
[
3
, Theorem 1.15]
). The proof of this result, which utilizes Fourier–Motzkin elimination, further shows that when
C
is rational, it is the intersection of linear
rational
halfspaces, i.e., halfspaces defined by linear forms with integer coefficients.

Now suppose that
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is a
\Gamma
-invariant cone, where
\Gamma=\Sym
or
\Gamma=\Sym(n)
for some
n\geq 1
. Then
C
always has a
\Gamma
-equivariant generating set
, meaning a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
such that
\Gamma(A)
generates
C
:

C=\cone(\Gamma(A))=\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}({\bm{a}}_{i})\mid{\bm{a}}_{i}\in A,\ \sigma_{i}\in\Gamma,\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

For example, the cone
C
itself forms a (possibly infinite)
\Gamma
-equivariant generating set for
C
.
We say that
C
is
\Gamma
-equivariantly finitely generated
if it admits a finite
\Gamma
-equivariant generating set. As we will see in
Section

4
, the
\Sym
-equivariant finite generation of a
\Sym
-invariant global cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
can be characterized in terms of any
\Sym
-invariant chain of local cones whose direct limit is
C
. To prepare for this, we introduce here the relevant terminology. Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones
. This means that
\mathcal{C}
is an ascending chain and each
C_{n}
is a
\Sym(n)
-invariant cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consequently, we have the containment

\cone(\Sym(n)(C_{m}))\subseteq C_{n}\quad\text{for all }\ n\geq m\geq 1.

The chain
\mathcal{C}
is said to
stabilize
if there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that

C_{n}=\cone(\Sym(n)(C_{m}))\quad\text{for all }\ n\geq m\geq r.

The
stability index
of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted
\ind(\mathcal{C})
, is the smallest such
r
.

2.3.
Symmetric monoids

Monoids are discrete analogues of cones. A monoid
M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is
generated
by a set
A
, denoted
M=\mn(A)
, if

M=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}A\coloneqq\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}\mid{\bm{a}}_{i}\in A,\ m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\ k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

This monoid is called
affine
or
finitely generated
if it has a finite generating set.
The
subgroup of units
of
M
is given by

\U(M)=\{{\bm{u}}\in M\mid-{\bm{u}}\in M\}.

We say that
M
is
positive
if
\U(M)=\{0\}
. This condition holds, for instance, when
M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 0}
. If
M
is a positive affine monoid, then it has a unique minimal generating set known as the
Hilbert basis
of
M
, denoted
\mathcal{H}(M)
(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Definition 2.15]
). Moreover, in this case,
\mathcal{H}(M)
consists precisely of the
irreducible
elements of
M
, i.e., those elements
{\bm{u}}\in M\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
with the property that if
{\bm{u}}={\bm{v}}+{\bm{w}}
for
{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}\in M
, then either
{\bm{v}}={\bm{0}}
or
{\bm{w}}={\bm{0}}
.

The
normalization
of
M
is defined as

\widehat{M}=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}\colon k{\bm{u}}\in M\ \text{ for some }\ k\in\mathbb{N}\}.

We say that
M
is
normal
if
M=\widehat{M}.
Normal monoids are closely related to cones. In fact, it is straightforward to see that
\widehat{M}=\cone(M)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, where
\cone(M)
is the cone generated by
M
. Thus, if
M
is normal, then it can be expressed as
M=\cone(M)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Conversely, for any cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, the intersection
C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
defines a normal monoid. In this setting, the classical Gordan’s lemma asserts that if
C
is a finitely generated rational cone, then the normal monoid
C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is affine (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Lemma 2.9]
).

As with cones, one can define
equivariant generating sets
for
\Sym(n)
- and
\Sym
-invariant monoids in
\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, along with the notion of
stabilization
for
\Sym
-invariant chains of local monoids. Similarly,
equivariant Hilbert bases
can be defined for
\Sym(n)
- and
\Sym
-invariant positive monoids. We omit the analogous details here. In
Section

4
, we will provide a characterization of equivariant finite generation of
\Sym
-invariant normal monoids in terms of their associated
\Sym
-invariant chains of local monoids.

3.
Equivariant Minkowski–Weyl theorem

An alternative formulation of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem states that the dual cone of any finitely generated cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is also finitely generated. This section aims to extend this classical result to the equivariant setting. While a
global
extension to arbitrary
\Sym
-invariant cones
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
does not hold, we establish a
local
extension that describes the dual cones of any stabilizing
\Sym
-invariant chain of local cones.

3.1.
Dual cones

Let
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
denote the Cartersian power of
\mathbb{R}
indexed by
\mathbb{N}
. This vector space contains
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
as a subspace, and it elements take the form
(v_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We identify
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the dual vector space of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
via the dual pairing

\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

given by

\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}v_{i}\ \text{ for any ${\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}$ and ${\bm{v}}=(v_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$. }

When restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, this pairing yields the standard identification of the dual space of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with itself for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Given a cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, its
dual cone
is defined as

C^{*}=\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in C\}.

Similarly, for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
dual cone
of a cone
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

C_{n}^{*}=\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in C_{n}\}.

Note that if
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then it is also a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for any
m\geq n
. Consequently, the definition of the dual cone of
C_{n}

depends
on the ambient space in which it is considered. Nevertheless, this dependence causes no confusion in our later discussion because, for a chain of cones
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we always consider the dual cone of
C_{n}
with respect to the embedding
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

If a cone
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the intersection of finitely many halfspaces, then its dual cone
C_{n}^{*}
is generated by the linear forms defining these halfspaces (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 1.16]
). This duality leads to the alternative formulation of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem mentioned at the begining of this section. We now turn to equivariant extensions of this result. Note that the dual of a
\Sym
-invariant (or
\Sym(n)
-invariant) cone is again
\Sym
-invariant (respectively,
\Sym(n)
-invariant); see
[
18
, Lemma 4.3]
.

3.2.
Equivariant Minkowski–Weyl theorem: global extension

Given a
\Sym
-invariant cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, it is shown in
[
18
, Theorem 4.9]
that its dual cone
C^{*}
need not be
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated, even when
C
itself is. This demonstrates that the classical Minkowski–Weyl theorem does not have a direct global extension to the equivariant setting. However, when restricting the dual cone to the subspace
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be any
\Sym
-invariant cone. Then the restricted dual cone
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is one of the following:

\{{\bm{0}}\},\quad\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})},\quad\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})},\quad\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}.

In particular,
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is always
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated.

The proof of this result relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
be such that

\langle\sigma({\bm{u}}),\tau({\bm{v}})\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\sigma,\tau\in\Sym.

Then both
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{v}}
must belong to either
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
or
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

Proof.

First, we show that
{\bm{u}}
belongs to either
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
or
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Assume, for contradiction, that this is not the case. Then there exists
\sigma\in\Sym
such that

\sigma({\bm{u}})=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n},0,0,\dots)

for some
n\geq 2
, where
a_{1}>0
,
a_{2}<0
, and
a_{i}=0
for all
i\geq n+1
. Since
{\bm{v}}\neq{\bm{0}}
, we can find
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\Sym
such that

\tau_{1}({\bm{v}})=(b_{1},0,\dots,0,b_{n+1},b_{n+2},\dots),\quad\tau_{2}({\bm{v}})=(0,b_{1},0,\dots,0,b_{n+1},b_{n+2},\dots),

where
b_{1}\neq 0
and
b_{i}=0
for
i=3,\dots,n
. We then have

\langle\sigma({\bm{u}}),\tau_{1}({\bm{v}})\rangle=a_{1}b_{1}\ \text{ and }\ \langle\sigma({\bm{u}}),\tau_{2}({\bm{v}})\rangle=a_{2}b_{1}.

Since
b_{1}\neq 0
and
a_{1},a_{2}
have opposite signs, one of these inner products must be negative, contradicting the assumption that all such inner products are nonnegative. This contradiction confirms that
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}\cup\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Applying the same argument to
{\bm{v}}
yields
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}\cup\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Finally, since
\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0
, both
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{v}}
must belong to the same orthant.
∎

We are ready to prove
Proposition

3.1
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

It suffices to prove the first assertion since the cones
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})},

\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, and
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
are
\Sym
-equivariantly generated by
{\bm{e}}_{1}
,
-{\bm{e}}_{1}
, and
\pm{\bm{e}}_{1}
, respectively. If
C=\{{\bm{0}}\}
, then
C^{*}=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and hence
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Now assume that
C\neq\{{\bm{0}}\}
. We consider the following cases:

Case 1
:
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Then
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C^{*}
, so
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}\subseteq C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. On the other hand, it follows easily from
Lemma

3.2
that
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Therefore,
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

Case 2
:
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Arguing similarly to Case 1, we obtain
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

Case 3
:
C\nsubseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}\cup\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. In this case, there exists
{\bm{u}}\in C
with both positive and negative coordinates. Take any
{\bm{v}}\in C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Since
\langle\sigma({\bm{u}}),\tau({\bm{v}})\rangle\geq 0
for all
\sigma,\tau\in\Sym
,
Lemma

3.2
implies that
{\bm{v}}={\bm{0}}
. Hence,
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\{{\bm{0}}\}
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Equivariant Minkowski–Weyl theorem: local version

In view of
Proposition

3.1
, although the restricted dual cone
C^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated, it offers limited insight into the full dual cone
C^{*}
(as well as the original cone
C
). To gain a better understanding of
C^{*}
, we consider the chain
\mathcal{C}^{*}=(C^{*}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of dual cones associated with a chain
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of local cones derived from
C
. As will be shown in
Theorem

4.1
, if the global cone
C
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated, then the chain
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes and eventually becomes finitely generated. In this case, we are able to explicitly describe a generating set for
C^{*}_{n}
when
n
is large enough, thereby providing further information about the global dual cone
C^{*}
. This result may be viewed as a local equivariant extension of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem. To state it precisely, we first introduce some notation.

For
n\geq 1
, denote by
\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
consisting of non-decreasing and non-increasing vectors, respectively. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid u_{1}\leq\dots\leq u_{n}\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle=\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid v_{1}\geq\dots\geq v_{n}\}.

More generally, for any subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\mathbb{O}(D)\coloneqq\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap D.
In particular, if
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\leq b
, then
\mathbb{O}([a,b]^{n})
represents the set of non-decreasing vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where each entry lies between
a
and
b
(inclusive). Let
I_{n}(a,b)
be the subset of
\mathbb{O}([a,b]^{n})
consisting of vectors whose entries are either
a
or
b
. For instance,

\displaystyle I_{1}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\{a,b\},

\displaystyle I_{2}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\{(a,a),(a,b),(b,b)\},

\displaystyle I_{3}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\{(a,a,a),(a,a,b),(a,b,b),(b,b,b)\}.

It is important to note that if
a<b
, then the set
I_{n}(a,b)
consists of exactly
n+1
affinely independent elements.

Now, let
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and fix an index
i\in[n-1]
. For
m>n
, define
F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})
as the subset of
\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
consisting of vectors obtained from
{\bm{u}}
by inserting elements of
I_{m-n}(u_{i},u_{i+1})
between
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
:

F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{i})\}\times I_{m-n}(u_{i},u_{i+1})\times\{(u_{i+1},\dots,u_{n})\}.

For example, if
{\bm{u}}=(1,2,3,4)\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
and
i=2
, then

\displaystyle F_{2,5}({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\{(1,2,2,3,4),(1,2,3,3,4)\},

\displaystyle F_{2,6}({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\{(1,2,2,2,3,4),(1,2,2,3,3,4),(1,2,3,3,3,4)\},

\displaystyle F_{2,7}({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\{(1,2,2,2,2,3,4),(1,2,2,2,3,3,4),(1,2,2,3,3,3,4),(1,2,3,3,3,3,4)\}.

We are prepared to state a local equivariant extension of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem. The following result extends the case of chains of nonnegative cones established in
[
18
, Theorem 4.4]
. Recall that for a chain of cones
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, the dual cone
C_{n}^{*}
is always considered with respect to the embedding
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a stabilizing
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones with stability index
r=\ind(\mathcal{C}).
Denote by
\mathcal{C}^{*}=(C_{n}^{*})_{n\geq 1}
the corresponding chain of dual cones. Suppose
F_{2r}\subseteq\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{2r})
is a generating set for
\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})
. Then for all
n>2r
, the set

F_{n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{{\bm{u}}\in F_{2r}}F_{r,n}({\bm{u}})

is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}^{*}
.

We illustrate this result with the following example.

Example 3.4
.

Consider a
\Sym
-invariant chain
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with stability index
\ind(\mathcal{C})=3
, where the cone
C_{3}
is
\Sym(3)
-equivariantly generated by the two vectors:

(-2,-1,4)\quad\text{and}\quad(-3,1,3).

Using Normaliz
[
4
]
and Macaulay2
[
12
]
, we find the following generating set for
\mathbb{O}(C_{6}^{*})
:

F_{6}=\{(1^{(6)}),\ (5,6^{(4)},7),\ (3^{(5)},4),\ (3^{(4)},4^{(2)}),\ (3^{(3)},4^{(3)}),\ (3^{(2)},4^{(4)}),\ (3,4^{(5)})\},

where the notation
a^{(k)}
indicates that the entry
a
is repeated
k
times. By
Theorem

3.3
, for all
n\geq 7
, the cone
C_{n}^{*}
is
\Sym(n)
-equivariantly generated by the set

F_{n}=\bigcup_{{\bm{u}}\in F_{6}}F_{3,n}({\bm{u}})=\{(1^{(n)}),\ (5,6^{(n-2)},7)\}\cup\{(3^{(i)},4^{(n-i)})\mid i\in[n-1]\}.

It is not hard to show that
(1^{(n)})
is a redundant generator of
C_{n}^{*}
(see
Claim

5.3.1
). Therefore, for all
n\geq 7
,
C_{n}^{*}
admits the following
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set:

F_{n}^{\prime}=\{(5,6^{(n-2)},7)\}\cup\{(3^{(i)},4^{(n-i)})\mid i\in[n-1]\}.

Remark 3.5
.

Keep the notation of
Theorem

3.3
.

(i)

For any
\Sym(n)
-invariant cone
D_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
D_{n}=\Sym(n)(\mathbb{O}(D_{n}))
. This implies that any generating set for
\mathbb{O}(D_{n})
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
D_{n}
. Thus, in particular,
F_{2r}
is a
\Sym(2r)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{2r}^{*}
. On the other hand, a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
D_{n}
contained in
\mathbb{O}(D_{n})
need not generate
\mathbb{O}(D_{n})
. For instance, consider the cone
D_{3}=\cone(\Sym(3)({\bm{u}}))
with
{\bm{u}}=(1,2,3)
. Then
\{{\bm{u}}\}
is a
\Sym(3)
-equivariant generating set for
D_{3}
, but it is not a generating set for
\mathbb{O}(D_{3})
, since

(1,1,1)=\frac{1}{4}\big((1,2,3)+(3,2,1)\big)\in\mathbb{O}(D_{3})\setminus\cone({\bm{u}}).

(ii)

If the chain
\mathcal{C}
consists of finitely generated cones, then by the Minkowski–Weyl theorem, the dual chain
\mathcal{C}^{*}
also consists of finitely generated cones. In particular, it follows from
[
18
, Lemma 4.6]
that the cone
\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})
is finitely generated. Thus,
F_{2r}
can be chosen to be finite, and
Theorem

3.3
provides a finite
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}^{*}
for all
n>2r
.

(iii)

Theorem

3.3
does not fully generalize
[
18
, Theorem 4.4]
. In the case of nonnegative cones, the latter yields a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}^{*}
for all
n>r
, whereas the former only guarantees such a set for
n>2r
. While a full generalization of
[
18
, Theorem 4.4]
is indeed possible (see
Remark

3.11
below), we have opted for the current version of
Theorem

3.3
for the sake of simplicity and conciseness.

The proof of
Theorem

3.3
requires some preparations. Recall that the
convex hull
of a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is given by

\conv(A)=\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}\mid{\bm{a}}_{i}\in A,\ \lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1,\ k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

Clearly,
\conv(A)\subseteq\cone(A).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\leq b
. Then
\mathbb{O}([a,b]^{n})=\conv(I_{n}(a,b))
.

Proof.

Let
P=\conv(I_{n}(a,b))
. It suffices to show that
\mathbb{O}([a,b]^{n})\subseteq P
, as the reverse inclusion is straightforward. The statement is trivially true when
a=b
, since both sets reduce to the singleton
\{(a,\dots,a)\}
. Now, suppose that
a<b
. Then
I_{n}(a,b)
is an affinely independent set with
n+1
elements, so
P
is an
n
-dimensional simplex. It follows that
P
is the intersection of
n+1
half-spaces, each bounded by a supporting hyperplane that passes through exactly
n
elements of
I_{n}(a,b)
. In other words,
P
is defined by the following inequalities:

a\leq x_{1},\ x_{1}\leq x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1}\leq x_{n},\ x_{n}\leq b.

Evidently, any element of
\mathbb{O}([a,b]^{n})
satisfies all these inequalities. Therefore,
\mathbb{O}([a,b]^{n})\subseteq P
, as required.
∎

Let
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
i\in[n-1]
and
m>n
, denote by
\mathbb{O}_{i,m}({\bm{u}})
the subset of
\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
obtained from
{\bm{u}}
by inserting elements of
\mathbb{O}([u_{i},u_{i+1}]^{m-n})
between
u_{i}
and
u_{i+1}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}_{i,m}({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{i})\}\times\mathbb{O}([u_{i},u_{i+1}]^{m-n})\times\{(u_{i+1},\dots,u_{n})\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then for any
i\in[n-1]
and
m>n
, we have

\mathbb{O}_{i,m}({\bm{u}})=\conv(F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})).

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.6
, we know that
\mathbb{O}([u_{i},u_{i+1}]^{m-n})=\conv(I_{m-n}(u_{i},u_{i+1}))
. Hence,

\mathbb{O}_{i,m}({\bm{u}})=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{i})\}\times\conv(I_{m-n}(u_{i},u_{i+1}))\times\{(u_{i+1},\dots,u_{n})\}=\conv(F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})),

as claimed.
∎

Recall that the
Minkowski sum
of subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{s}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
with weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}A_{j}=\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}\mid{\bm{a}}_{j}\in A_{j}\text{ for }j=1,\dots,s\Big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}.

Obviously, if
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
for all
j\in[s]
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}A_{j}\subseteq\cone\big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}A_{j}\big)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose
{\bm{u}}\in\cone(A)
for some
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any
i\in[n-1]
and
m>n
, it holds that

F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})\subseteq\cone\Big(\bigcup_{{\bm{a}}\in A}F_{i,m}({\bm{a}})\Big).

Proof.

We can write
{\bm{u}}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}{\bm{a}}_{j}
with
{\bm{a}}_{j}\in A
and
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
for all
j\in[s]
. From the definition of
F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})
, it is easy to check that

F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})\subseteq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}F_{i,m}({\bm{a}}_{j}).

Hence,

F_{i,m}({\bm{u}})\subseteq\cone\Big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}F_{i,m}({\bm{a}}_{j})\Big)\subseteq\cone\Big(\bigcup_{{\bm{a}}\in A}F_{i,m}({\bm{a}})\Big).\hfil\qed

For the proof of the next lemma, we will employ the classical
rearrangement inequality

[
13
, Theorem 368]
, which states that for any two non-decreasing sequences of real numbers
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
,
b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{n}
, and for any permutation
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
, the following inequality holds:

a_{\sigma(1)}b_{1}+\cdots+a_{\sigma(n)}b_{n}\geq a_{n}b_{1}+\cdots+a_{1}b_{n}.

In other words, if
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then for any
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
, we have

\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{b}}\rangle\geq\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\rangle.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\rangle\geq 0
. Suppose there exists an index
i\in[n-1]
such that
a_{i}\geq 0\geq a_{i+1}
. Then for any
m>n
, we have

\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{d}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all ${\bm{d}}\in\mathbb{O}_{i,m}({\bm{b}})$ and $\sigma\in\Sym(m)$.}

Proof.

Let
{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be the vector obtained from
{\bm{a}}
by inserting
m-n
zeros between
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
, i.e.,

{\bm{c}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i},0,\dots,0,a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n}).

Then
{\bm{c}}=\tau({\bm{a}})
for some
\tau\in\Sym(m)
. Moreover, for any
{\bm{d}}\in\mathbb{O}_{i,m}({\bm{b}})
, it is easily seen that
\langle{\bm{c}},{\bm{d}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\rangle
. Since
{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
{\bm{d}}\in\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, the rearrangement inequality yields

\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{d}}\rangle=\langle\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}({\bm{c}}),{\bm{d}}\rangle\geq\langle{\bm{c}},{\bm{d}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}\rangle\geq 0

for all
\sigma\in\Sym(m)
.
∎

We also need the following basic yet crucial relationship between successive dual cones.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones, and let
\mathcal{C}^{*}=(C_{n}^{*})_{n\geq 1}
denote the corresponding chain of dual cones. If
{\bm{v}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n+1})\in C_{n+1}^{*}
, then for any
i\in[n+1]
, the vector
\hat{\bm{v}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n+1})
belongs to
C_{n}^{*}.

Proof.

For any
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in C_{n}
, define
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1},0,u_{i},\dots,u_{n}).

Then it is clear that
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}\in C_{n+1}
, and that
\langle{\bm{u}},\hat{{\bm{v}}}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0
, where the inequality holds because
{\bm{v}}\in C_{n+1}^{*}
. Hence,
\hat{\bm{v}}\in C_{n}^{*}
, as claimed.
∎

With these preparations in place, we are now ready to prove
Theorem

3.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

We first show that
F_{n}\subseteq C_{n}^{*}
for all
n>2r.
Let
G_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{O}^{-}(C_{r})
be a
\Sym(r)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{r}
. For instance, one may simply take
G_{r}=\mathbb{O}^{-}(C_{r})
, since
C_{r}=\Sym(r)(\mathbb{O}^{-}(C_{r}))
. Given that
\ind(\mathcal{C})=r
, it is easy to see that
G_{r}
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}
for all
n\geq r
. Thus, in order to prove that
F_{n}\subseteq C_{n}^{*}
for all
n>2r
, it suffices to verify that

\langle\sigma({\bm{u}}),{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in G_{r},\ {\bm{v}}\in F_{n},\text{ and }\sigma\in\Sym(n).

Fix
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in G_{r}
,
{\bm{v}}\in F_{n}
, and
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
. Adopting the convention that
u_{0}=+\infty
and
u_{r+1}=-\infty
, we can find an index
j\in\{0,1,\dots,r\}
such that
u_{j}\geq 0\geq u_{j+1}
. Define
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}\in\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{2r})
to be the vector obtained from
{\bm{u}}
by inserting
r
zeros between
u_{j}
and
u_{j+1}
:

\tilde{{\bm{u}}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{j},0,\dots,0,u_{j+1},\dots,u_{r})\in\mathbb{O}^{-}(\mathbb{R}^{2r}).

Then
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}=\tau({\bm{u}})
for some
\tau\in\Sym(2r)
, and so
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}\in C_{2r}
. Moreover, by construction, the
r
-th and
(r+1)
-th entries of
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}
satisfy
\tilde{u}_{r}\geq 0\geq\tilde{u}_{r+1}
. Since
{\bm{v}}\in F_{n}
, there exists
{\bm{w}}\in F_{2r}
such that
{\bm{v}}\in F_{r,n}({\bm{w}})
. We have
\langle\tilde{{\bm{u}}},{\bm{w}}\rangle\geq 0
because
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}\in C_{2r}
and
{\bm{w}}\in F_{2r}\subset C_{2r}^{*}
. Applying
Lemma

3.9
to
\tilde{{\bm{u}}}
and
{\bm{w}}
, noting that
\tilde{u}_{r}\geq 0\geq\tilde{u}_{r+1}
and that
{\bm{v}}\in F_{r,n}({\bm{w}})\subseteq\mathbb{O}_{r,n}({\bm{w}})
, we obtain

\langle\sigma({\bm{u}}),{\bm{v}}\rangle=\langle\sigma\circ\tau^{-1}(\tilde{{\bm{u}}}),{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0,

as desired.

To complete the proof, it remains to show that
C_{n}^{*}\subseteq\cone(\Sym(n)(F_{n}))
for all
n>2r
. Since
C_{n}^{*}=\Sym(n)(\mathbb{O}(C_{n}^{*}))
, it is enough to prove the inclusion
\mathbb{O}(C_{n}^{*})\subseteq\cone(F_{n})
.
Take any
{\bm{b}}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{O}(C_{n}^{*})
. Then repeated applications of
Lemma

3.10
yield

{\bm{c}}\coloneqq(b_{1},\dots,b_{r},b_{n-r+1},\dots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})=\cone(F_{2r}),

where the last equality follows from the assumption that
F_{2r}
generates
\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})
.
Evidently,
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{O}_{r,n}({\bm{c}})
, and thus
{\bm{b}}\in\conv(F_{r,n}({\bm{c}}))
by
Lemma

3.7
. According to
Lemma

3.8
, we have

F_{r,n}({\bm{c}})\subseteq\cone\Big(\bigcup_{{\bm{a}}\in F_{2r}}F_{r,n}({\bm{a}})\Big)=\cone(F_{n}).

Consequently,

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}\in\conv(F_{r,n}({\bm{c}}))\subseteq\conv(\cone(F_{n}))=\cone(F_{n}).

Therefore,
\mathbb{O}(C_{n}^{*})\subseteq\cone(F_{n})
, as required.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Theorem

3.3
can be slightly refined to encompass the case of nonnegative cones treated in
[
18
, Theorem 4.4]
. Adopting the notation from the proof of
Theorem

3.3
, we define

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\max\{i\in[r]\mid u_{i}>0\ \text{ for some }\ (u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in G_{r}\},

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\max\{j\in[r]\mid u_{r-j}<0\ \text{ for some }\ (u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in G_{r}\},

with the convention that
s=0
if
G_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{r}
and
t=0
if
G_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{r}
.
Let
p=\max\{s+t,r+1\}
. Then
r+1\leq p\leq 2r
, and
p=r+1
if
G_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{r}
or
G_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{r}
. Suppose
F_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
is a generating set for
\mathbb{O}(C_{p}^{*})
. Then by adapting the argument used in the proof of
Theorem

3.3
, one can show that for all
n>p
, the set

F_{n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{{\bm{u}}\in F_{p}}F_{s,n}({\bm{u}})

is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}^{*}
. This result generalizes
[
18
, Theorem 4.4]
, because in the context of nonnegative cones, it can be verified that given a generating set
F_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{r})
for
\mathbb{O}(C_{r}^{*})
, the set

F_{r+1}=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{r},u_{r})\mid{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})\in F_{r},\ |\supp({\bm{u}})|\geq 2\}\cup\{{\bm{e}}_{r+1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{O}(\mathbb{R}^{r+1})

is a generating set for
\mathbb{O}(C_{r+1}^{*})
.

Given a
\Sym
-invariant cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
,
Theorem

3.3
has implications for the global dual cone
C^{*}
. When
C\neq\{{\bm{0}}\}
, identifying an explicit nonzero element of
C^{*}
is generally nontrivial. However, as a consequence of
Theorem

3.3
, we obtain the following result. For
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\bm{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, let
({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
denote the element of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
formed by concatenating
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{v}}
:

({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n},v_{1},v_{2},\dots).

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be a
\Sym
-invariant cone and let
C^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
denote its dual cone. Suppose that
C
is
\Sym
-equivariantly generated by a subset
G_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
for some
r\geq 1
. Let
C_{2r}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2r}
be the dual cone of the cone
C_{2r}=\cone(\Sym(2r)(G_{r}))
. Then the set

F=\{({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})\mid{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{2r})\in\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*}),\ {\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{O}([u_{r},u_{r+1}]^{\mathbb{N}})\}

is contained
C^{*}
.

Proof.

Consider the chain of cones
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
defined by

C_{n}=\begin{cases}\{{\bm{0}}\}&\text{if }n<r,\\
\cone(\Sym(n)(G_{r}))&\text{if }n\geq r.\end{cases}

Evidently,
\mathcal{C}
is a stabilizing
\Sym
-invariant chain with stability index
r.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{*}=(C_{n}^{*})_{n\geq 1}
denote the corresponding chain of dual cones. Since
\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})
is trivially a generating set for
\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})
, it follows from
Theorem

3.3
that

F_{n}=\bigcup_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})}F_{r,n}({\bm{u}})

is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}^{*}
for all
n>2r
.

In order to show that
F\subseteq C^{*}
, it suffices to verify that

\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{w}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{a}}\in G_{r},\ {\bm{w}}\in F,\ \sigma\in\Sym.

Choose
n>2r
such that
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
. Then
\sigma({\bm{a}})\in C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and thus

\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{w}}\rangle=\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{w}}_{n}\rangle,

where
{\bm{w}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the truncation of
{\bm{w}}
to its first
n
coordinates. By the definition of
F
, we have
{\bm{w}}_{n}=({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}_{n-2r})
for some
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{O}(C_{2r}^{*})
and
{\bm{v}}_{n-2r}\in\mathbb{O}([u_{r},u_{r+1}]^{n-2r})
. Consequently, there exists
\tau\in\Sym(n)
such that

\tau({\bm{w}}_{n})=(u_{1},\dots,u_{r},{\bm{v}}_{n-2r},u_{r+1},\dots,u_{2r})\in\mathbb{O}_{r,n}({\bm{u}}).

It thus follows from
Lemma

3.7
that

\tau({\bm{w}}_{n})\in\conv(F_{r,n}({\bm{u}}))\subseteq\conv(F_{n})\subseteq\cone(F_{n})\subseteq C_{n}^{*}.

This implies
{\bm{w}}_{n}\in C_{n}^{*}
since
C_{n}^{*}
is
\Sym(n)
-invariant. Therefore,

\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{w}}\rangle=\langle\sigma({\bm{a}}),{\bm{w}}_{n}\rangle\geq 0,

as desired.
∎

Example 3.13
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be the cone
\Sym
-equivariantly generated by the two vectors:

(-2,-1,4)\quad\text{and}\quad(-3,1,3).

Then
C
is the direct limit of the chain
\mathcal{C}
considered in

3.4
. From this example and
Corollary

3.12
, we obtain the following elements of the dual cone
C^{*}
:

(1,1,1,\dots),\ (5,6^{(4)},7,6,6,6,\dots),\ (3^{(i)},4^{(6-i)},{\bm{v}})

for all
i\in[5]
and
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{O}([3,4]^{\mathbb{N}})
.

Theorem

3.3
establishes a necessary condition for the stabilization of a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones
\mathcal{C}
in terms of its dual chain
\mathcal{C}^{*}
. It remains an open question whether this condition is also sufficient. More broadly, the following problem merits further investigation.

Problem 3.14
.

Characterize the stabilization, and more generally, other properties of interest (such as equivariant finite generation), of a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones
\mathcal{C}
in terms of its dual chain
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.

4.
Local-global principles

In this section, we characterize equivariant finite generation of
\Sym
-invariant cones in terms of their associated chains of local cones. We also present a generalization of this result that characterizes the stabilization of such chains. As a consequence, we derive a characterization of equivariant finite generation for
\Sym
-invariant normal monoids. These results, which are of independent interest, play a crucial role in our proof of the equivariant Gordan’s lemma in
Section

6
.

We first establish the following characterization of equivariant finite generation for global cones, extending the case of nonnegative cones considered in
[
15
, Corollary 5.5]
and
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
. An analogous result for lattices can be found in
[
19
, Theorem 3.6]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones with limit
C=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}C_{n}.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{C}
stabilizes, and
C_{n}
is eventually finitely generated;

(ii)

There exist
p\in\mathbb{N}
and a finite subset
G_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
G_{p}
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}
for all
n\geq p
;

(iii)

There exists
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq q
, the following conditions hold:

(a)

C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
,

(b)

C_{n}
is finitely generated by elements of support size at most
q
;

(iv)

C
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Assume that
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes with stability index
r
. Choose
p\geq r
such that
C_{p}
has a finite generating set
G_{p}
. Since the chain stabilizes at
r
, we have

C_{n}=\cone(\Sym(n)(C_{p}))=\cone(\Sym(n)(G_{p}))\quad\text{for all }\ n\geq p.

Hence,
G_{p}
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}
for all
n\geq p
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): We show that the two conditions in (iii) hold with
q=2p
. By assumption,
C_{n}
is generated by
G_{n}\coloneqq\Sym(n)(G_{p})
for all
n\geq p
. Since
G_{p}
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it is evident that
G_{n}
is also finite and its elements have support size at most
p
for all
n\geq p
. In particular, every element of
G_{n}
has support size at most
q
for all
n\geq q
, establishing (iii)(b).

To verify (iii)(a), it suffices to show that
C_{n+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
for all
n\geq q
. Indeed, this implies

C_{m}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=(C_{m}\cap\mathbb{R}^{m-1})\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{m-1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=\cdots=C_{n+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}

for all
m>n\geq q
, and hence

C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigcup_{m\geq 1}C_{m}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigcup_{m\geq n+1}(C_{m}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n})=C_{n}

for all
n\geq q
, as desired.

To prove that
C_{n+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
for all
n\geq q
, we use two key observations:

•

Since
C_{n}
is finitely generated, it satisfies
C_{n}=C_{n}^{**}
, where
C_{n}^{**}
denotes the double dual cone of
C_{n}
(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 1.16(b)]
).

•

The chain
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes with stability index
r\leq p=q/2
, and thus, by
Theorem

3.3
and
Remark

3.5
(i), there exists a
\Sym(2r)
-equivariant generating set
F_{2r}
for
C_{2r}^{*}
that determines a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set
F_{n}
for
C_{n}^{*}
for all
n\geq q
.

Now let
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in C_{n+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\geq q
.
To show that
{\bm{u}}\in C_{n}=C_{n}^{**}
, we need to verify that
\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0
for all
{\bm{v}}\in C_{n}^{*}
. Since
C_{n}^{*}
is
\Sym(n)
-equivariantly generated by
F_{n}
, it is enough to check that
\langle{\bm{u}},\sigma({\bm{w}})\rangle\geq 0
for all
{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in F_{n}
and all
\sigma\in\Sym(n).

From the construction of
F_{n}
and
F_{n+1}
, it follows that

\widetilde{{\bm{w}}}\coloneqq(w_{1},\dots,w_{r},w_{r},w_{r+1},\dots,w_{n})\in F_{n+1}.

Choose
\tau\in\Sym(n+1)
such that
\tau(\widetilde{{\bm{w}}})=(\sigma({\bm{w}}),w_{r}).

We have
\langle{\bm{u}},\tau(\widetilde{{\bm{w}}})\rangle\geq 0
because
{\bm{u}}\in C_{n+1}
and
\tau(\widetilde{{\bm{w}}})\in\Sym(n+1)(F_{n+1})\subseteq C_{n+1}^{*}.

Since

0\leq\langle{\bm{u}},\tau(\widetilde{{\bm{w}}})\rangle=\langle{\bm{u}},\sigma({\bm{w}})\rangle+0\cdot w_{s}=\langle{\bm{u}},\sigma({\bm{w}})\rangle,

we are done.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i): It remains to show that
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes. Let
n\geq q
and let
G_{n}
be a generating set for
C_{n}
whose elements have support size at most
q
. Then for each
{\bm{u}}\in G_{n}
, there exists a
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
such that
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}=\sigma({\bm{u}})\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Since
{\bm{u}}=\sigma^{-1}({\bm{u}}^{\prime})
and

{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\in C\cap\mathbb{R}^{q}=C_{q},

we deduce that
G_{n}\subseteq\Sym(n)(C_{q}).
Therefore,

C_{n}=\cone(G_{n})\subseteq\cone(\Sym(n)(C_{q}))\subseteq C_{n},

and consequently,
C_{n}=\cone(\Sym(n)(C_{q}))
for all
n\geq q
, as required.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iv): The set
G_{p}
clearly forms a
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for
C=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}C_{n}.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Let
G
be a finite
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for
C
. Since
C=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}C_{n}
, there exists
p\geq 1
such that
G\subseteq C_{p}
. Define a new chain
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(C_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 1}
with

C_{n}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\{{\bm{0}}\}&\text{if }1\leq n<p,\\
\cone(\Sym(n)(G))&\text{if }n\geq p.\end{cases}

Evidently,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is a
\Sym
-invariant chain whose limit is
C=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}C_{n}^{\prime}
, and
G
serves as a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}^{\prime}
for all
n\geq p
. By the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) already proved, we have
C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}^{\prime}
for all
n\geq 2p
. Furthermore, since

C_{n}^{\prime}=\cone(\Sym(n)(G))\subseteq\cone(\Sym(n)(C_{p}))\subseteq C_{n}\subseteq C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}

for all
n\geq p
, we conclude that

C_{n}=\cone(\Sym(n)(G))=C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \text{ for all }n\geq 2p.

This proves (iii) and thus completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

As we have seen in the proof of the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) of
Theorem

4.1
, the property of the chain of dual cones established in
Theorem

3.3
is instrumental in proving the equality
C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. In the case of nonnegative cones, however, the argument simplifies significantly and relies essentially on the following elementary fact: if
{\bm{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}{\bm{a}}_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}>0
and
{\bm{a}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}_{\geq 0}
, then
\supp({\bm{a}}_{i})\subseteq\supp({\bm{u}})
for all
i\in[s]
. For further details, see the proof of
[
18
, Corollary 2.2]
.

The next result provides several characterizations of the stabilization of
\Sym
-invariant chains of local cones. One characterization generalizes and refines the case of nonnegative cones treated in
[
18
, Lemma 6.2]
, while the remaining characterizations are obtained by removing the finite generation conditions from
Theorem

4.1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones with limit
C=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}C_{n}.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{C}
stabilizes;

(ii)

There exist
p\in\mathbb{N}
and a subset
G_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
G_{p}
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}
for all
n\geq p
;

(iii)

There exists
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq q
, the following conditions hold:

(a)

C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
,

(b)

For any
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n+1})\in C_{n+1}
, there exists
\sigma\in\Sym(n+1)
such that

u_{\sigma(n)}u_{\sigma(n+1)}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{\sigma(1)},\dots,u_{\sigma(n-1)},u_{\sigma(n)}+u_{\sigma(n+1)})\in C_{n};

(iv)

There exists
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq q
, the following conditions hold:

(a)

C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
,

(b)

C_{n}
is generated by elements of support size at most
q
.

(v)

C
is
\Sym
-equivariantly generated by a subset of
C_{r}
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

Given
Theorem

4.1
, the main task in the proof of the theorem above is to establish the equivalence between statement (iii) and the others. To this end, the following preparatory result is needed.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
p\geq 1
and let
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\Sym(n)
-invariant cone generated by elements of support size at most
p
. If
n\geq 3p^{2}
, then for any
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in C_{n}
, there exists
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
such that

u_{\sigma(n-1)}u_{\sigma(n)}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{\sigma(1)},\dots,u_{\sigma(n-2)},u_{\sigma(n-1)}+u_{\sigma(n)})\in C_{n}.

Proof.

By Carathéodory’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Theorem 1.55]
), we can write
{\bm{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
,
{\bm{v}}_{i}\in C_{n}
, and moreover,
|\supp({\bm{v}}_{i})|\leq p
for all
i\in[n]
by assumption. For each
j\in[n]
, set

T_{j}=\{i\in[n]\mid j\in\supp({\bm{v}}_{i})\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{j}=\bigcup_{i\in T_{j}}\supp({\bm{v}}_{i}).

Then

(1)

|S_{j}|\leq\sum_{i\in T_{j}}|\supp({\bm{v}}_{i})|\leq p|T_{j}|\ \text{ for all }j\in[n].

Let us first prove the following:

Claim 4.4.1
.

Up to a permutation of the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, we have

n-1\notin S_{n}\ \text{ and }\ n-2\notin S_{n-1}\cup S_{n}.

Proof of
Claim

4.4.1
.

By double counting, we obtain

(2)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|T_{j}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\supp({\bm{v}}_{i})|\leq np.

Permuting the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, if necessary, we may assume that
T_{n}
has minimal cardinality among the sets
T_{j}
for
j\in[n]
. Then (
2
) yields
|T_{n}|\leq p.
It thus follows from (
1
) that

|S_{n}|\leq p^{2}.

Hence,
[n]\setminus S_{n}\neq\emptyset
since
n\geq 3p^{2}
. Permuting coordinates again if needed, we may assume that
n-1\in[n]\setminus S_{n}
and that
T_{n-1}
has minimal cardinality among the sets
T_{j}
with
j\in[n]\setminus S_{n}
. Then

(n-p^{2})|T_{n-1}|\leq(n-|S_{n}|)|T_{n-1}|\leq\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus S_{n}}|T_{j}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}|T_{j}|\leq np.

Since
n\geq 3p^{2}
, it is easy to check that

|T_{n-1}|\leq\frac{np}{n-p^{2}}<2p.

This together with (
1
) implies that

|S_{n-1}|<2p^{2}.

Therefore,

|S_{n-1}\cup S_{n}|\leq|S_{n-1}|+|S_{n}|<2p^{2}+p^{2}=3p^{2}\leq n.

Thus, there exists
k\in[n]\setminus(S_{n-1}\cup S_{n})
, and by permuting coordinates once more, we may assume that
k=n-2
. This completes the proof of the claim.
∎

Claim 4.4.2
.

Up to a permutation of the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, it holds that
n-1\notin S_{n}
and
u_{n-1}u_{n}\geq 0
.

Proof of
Claim

4.4.2
.

By
Claim

4.4.1
, we may assume that
n-1\notin S_{n}
and
n-2\notin S_{n-1}\cup S_{n}
. If
u_{n-1}u_{n}\geq 0
, we are done. Otherwise, either
u_{n-2}u_{n}\geq 0
or
u_{n-2}u_{n-1}\geq 0
must hold. Since
n-2\notin S_{n}
and
n-2\notin S_{n-1}
, the desired conclusion results from transposing
u_{n-2}
with either
u_{n-1}
or
u_{n}
.
∎

Let us resume the proof of the lemma. By
Claim

4.4.2
, we may assume that
n-1\notin S_{n}
and
u_{n-1}u_{n}\geq 0
. So it remains to show that

(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-2},u_{n-1}+u_{n})\in C_{n}.

Recall that

{\bm{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}=\sum_{i\in T_{n}}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}+\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus T_{n}}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}.

Since
n\notin\supp({\bm{v}}_{i})
for all
i\in[n]\setminus T_{n}
and
n-1\notin\supp({\bm{v}}_{i})
for all
i\in T_{n}
, we can write

\sum_{i\in T_{n}}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}=(u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n-2}^{\prime},0,u_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus T_{n}}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}=(u_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,u_{n-2}^{\prime\prime},u_{n-1},0),

where
u_{j}=u_{j}^{\prime}+u_{j}^{\prime\prime}
for all
j\in[n-2]
.
Let
\pi\in\Sym(n)
be the transposition that swaps
n-1
and
n
. Then

(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-2},u_{n-1}+u_{n},0)=\sum_{i\in T_{n}}\lambda_{i}\pi({\bm{v}}_{i})+\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus T_{n}}\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}.

Since
C_{n}
is
\Sym(n)
-invariant, it follows that

(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-2},u_{n-1}+u_{n})\in C_{n},

as required.
∎

Let us now prove
Theorem

4.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.3
.

We only prove the implications (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv).
The implications (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(v)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) can be established similarly to the proof of
Theorem

4.1
; the details are left to the reader.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Suppose
G_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}
for all
n\geq p
. We show that the two conditions in (iii) hold with
q=3p^{2}
. To prove (iii)(a), let
n\geq 3p^{2}
and take
{\bm{u}}\in C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
{\bm{u}}\in C_{m}
for some
m\geq n
, and we can write

{\bm{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}({\bm{v}}_{i}),\quad\text{where $\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ {\bm{v}}_{i}\in G_{p}$, and $\sigma_{i}\in\Sym(m)$ for all $i\in[t]$.}

Set
G_{p}^{\prime}=\{{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{t}\}
. Consider the
\Sym
-invariant chain
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(C_{k}^{\prime})_{k\geq 1}
defined by

C_{k}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\{{\bm{0}}\}&\text{if }1\leq k<p,\\
\cone(\Sym(k)(G_{p}^{\prime}))&\text{if }k\geq p,\end{cases}

and denote
C^{\prime}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}C_{k}^{\prime}
.
Then
G_{p}^{\prime}
is a finite
\Sym(k)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{k}^{\prime}
for all
k\geq p
. From the proof of the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) in
Theorem

4.1
, it follows that
C^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}=C_{k}^{\prime}
for all
k\geq 2p
. In particular, since
n\geq 3p^{2}>2p
, we have
C^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}^{\prime}
. Since
{\bm{u}}\in C^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
C_{n}^{\prime}\subseteq C_{n}
, we deduce that
{\bm{u}}\in C_{n}
. Therefore,
C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
, as required.

To prove (iii)(b), we first note that
C_{n+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
for all
n\geq 3p^{2}
. Indeed, this follows from (iii)(a) and the obvious inclusions
C_{n}\subseteq C_{n+1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}\subseteq C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Now
Lemma

4.4
yields the desired conclusion.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv): We need to show that any element
{\bm{u}}\in C_{n}
with support size
s>q
can be generated by elements of
C_{n}
with smaller support sizes. Permuting the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, if necessary, we may assume that
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{s})
, where
{\bm{u}}_{i}\neq 0
for all
i\in[s].
Then we have
{\bm{u}}\in C\cap\mathbb{R}^{s}=C_{s}
since
s>q
. By (iii)(b), up to a permutation of the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, it can be assumed that
u_{s-1}u_{s}>0
and
{\bm{v}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{s-2},u_{s-1}+u_{s})\in C_{s-1}
. The latter implies

{\bm{w}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{s-2},0,u_{s-1}+u_{s})\in C_{s}.

Observe that
{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}\in C_{n}
(since
s=|\supp({\bm{u}})|\leq n
) and both of them have support size
s-1
.
Evidently,
{\bm{u}}=\lambda{\bm{v}}+\mu{\bm{w}}
, where
\lambda=u_{s-1}/(u_{s-1}+u_{s})
and
\mu=u_{s}/(u_{s-1}+u_{s})
are positive numbers, because
u_{s-1}u_{s}>0
. This shows that
{\bm{u}}
is generated by elements with smaller support size, completing the proof.
∎

Theorems

4.1
and
4.3
yield the following characterization of equivariant finite generation of positive normal
\Sym
-invariant monoids. In the case of nonnegative monoids, similar characterizations have been established in
[
15
, Corollary 5.13]
,
[
18
, Lemmas 5.1 and 6.5]
, and
[
19
, Theorem 3.14]
. For any element
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{{\bm{i}}\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
and any subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, we define

\|{\bm{u}}\|=\sum_{{\bm{i}}\in\mathbb{N}}|u_{i}|\quad\text{and}\quad\|A\|=\sup\{\|{\bm{u}}\|:{\bm{u}}\in A\}.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of positive normal affine monoids with limit
M=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}M_{n}.
For each
n\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{H}_{n}
denote the Hilbert basis of
M_{n}
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{M}
stabilizes;

(ii)

There exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{r}
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant Hilbert basis for
M_{n}
for all
n\geq r
;

(iii)

There exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq p
, the following conditions hold:

(a)

M\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=M_{n}
,

(b)

\|\mathcal{H}_{m}\|\leq\|\mathcal{H}_{n}\|
for all
m\geq n\geq p
;

(iv)

There exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq p
, the following conditions hold:

(a)

M\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=M_{n}
,

(b)

For any
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n+1})\in M_{n+1}
, there exists
\sigma\in\Sym(n+1)
such that

u_{\sigma(n)}u_{\sigma(n+1)}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{\sigma(1)},\dots,u_{\sigma(n-1)},u_{\sigma(n)}+u_{\sigma(n+1)})\in M_{n};

(v)

There exists
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq q
, the following conditions hold:

(a)

M\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=M_{n}
,

(b)

M_{n}
is generated by elements of support size at most
q
;

(vi)

M
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated.

To prove this corollary, we recall the following auxiliary result from
[
18
, Corollary 6.4]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
M_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\Sym(n)
-invariant normal monoid. If
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in M_{n}
and
v_{n-1},v_{n}
lie in the interval with the endpoints
u_{n-1}
and
u_{n}
such that
v_{n-1}+v_{n}=u_{n-1}+u_{n}
, then
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-2},v_{n-1},v_{n})\in M_{n}.

Proof of
Corollary

4.5
.

Let
C=\cone(M)
and
C_{n}=\cone(M_{n})
for
n\geq 1.
Then
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
forms a
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones whose limit is
C
. Moreover, since
M_{n}
is a normal monoid, we have
M_{n}=C_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
. It follows that
M=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

(vi)
\Rightarrow
(iv): Suppose
M
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated. Then the cone
C=\cone(M)
is also
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated. By the equivalence of statements (iii) and (v) in
Theorem

4.3
, it follows that
C\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}=C_{n}
for all
n\gg 0
. Therefore,

M\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=C_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=M_{n}

for all
n\gg 0
, which establishes part (iv)(a). Part (iv)(b) follows readily from statement (iii)(b) of
Theorem

4.3
, together with the fact that
M_{n}=C_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii): It is enough to show that
\|\mathcal{H}_{n+1}\|\leq\|\mathcal{H}_{n}\|
for all
n\geq p
. Since
M_{n+1}
is
\Sym(n+1)
-invariant, so is its Hilbert basis
\mathcal{H}_{n+1}
. Let
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n+1})\in\mathcal{H}_{n+1}
be such that
\|{\bm{u}}\|=\|\mathcal{H}_{n+1}\|.
By permuting the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, we may assume that
u_{n}u_{n+1}\geq 0
and

{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\coloneqq(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-1},u_{n}+u_{n+1})\in M_{n}.

Note that
\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\|=\|{\bm{u}}\|
since
u_{n}u_{n+1}\geq 0
. So to conclude the implication, it suffices to show that
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}^{\prime}+{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
for some
{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in M_{n}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
. Write
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
and
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
. Then
u_{n}+u_{n+1}=v_{n}+w_{n}
, and the condition
u_{n}u_{n+1}\geq 0
implies

|u_{n}|+|u_{n+1}|=|u_{n}+u_{n+1}|=|v_{n}+w_{n}|\leq|v_{n}|+|w_{n}|.

Without loss of generality, suppose that
|u_{n}|\leq|u_{n+1}|
and
|w_{n}|\leq|v_{n}|
. Then
|u_{n}|\leq|v_{n}|
. It must also hold that
u_{n}v_{n}\geq 0
, because

u_{n}v_{n}+u_{n}w_{n}=u_{n}(u_{n}+u_{n+1})\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad|u_{n}w_{n}|\leq|u_{n}v_{n}|.

Thus, both
u_{n}
and
v_{n}-u_{n}
lie between
0
and
v_{n}
. Since
{\bm{v}}^{\prime}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n},0)\in M_{n+1}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
, it follows from
Lemma

4.6
that
{\bm{v}}\coloneqq(v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1},u_{n},v_{n}-u_{n})\in M_{n+1}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}.
Moreover, we have
{\bm{w}}\coloneqq(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1},0,w_{n})\in M_{n+1}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}
because
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n},0)\in M_{n+1}\setminus\{{\bm{0}}\}.
Finally, since
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}={\bm{v}}^{\prime}+{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
, we obtain
{\bm{u}}={\bm{v}}+{\bm{w}}
, contradicting the fact that
{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{H}_{n+1}.
Therefore,
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
, and the desired inequality follows.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(v): Let
q=\max\{p,\|\mathcal{H}_{p}\|\}
. Then for any
{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
with
n\geq q
, we have

|\supp({\bm{u}})|\leq\|{\bm{u}}\|\leq\|\mathcal{H}_{n}\|\leq\|\mathcal{H}_{p}\|\leq q.

Therefore,
M_{n}
is generated by elements of support size at most
q
for all
n\geq q
.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(i): This implication follows by an argument analogous to that used in the proof of (iii)
\Rightarrow
(i) in
Theorem

4.1
, and we omit the details here.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): This is immediate from the definition of stabilization.
∎

To conclude this section, we propose the following problem, which points to a promising direction for future research.

Problem 4.7
.

Investigate the extent to which
Corollary

4.5
can be generalized to
\Sym
-invariant chains of monoids that are not necessarily positive or normal.

5.
Non-pointed symmetric cones and non-positive symmetric monoids

In this section, we continue the preparatory work for the proof of the equivariant Gordan’s lemma by classifying non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoids, thereby complementing the treatment of positive normal monoids given in
Corollary

4.5
. This result is derived from a classification of non-pointed
\Sym
-invariant cones, which is also of independent interest. Local versions of both classification results are included, providing further instances where local and global settings exhibit distinct behavior.

5.1.
Non-pointed symmetric cones

The
lineality space
of a cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is defined as

\lin(C)=\{{\bm{u}}\in C\mid-{\bm{u}}\in C\}.

This is the largest vector subspace contained in
C
. We say that
C
is
pointed
if
\lin(C)=\{0\}.

Our goal here is to classify all non-pointed
\Sym
-invariant cones in
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. To state the result, we introduce the following notation:
for any
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, let

s({\bm{u}})=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}
denote the sum of its entries. Note that
s({\bm{u}})
is well-defined since
{\bm{u}}
has finite support.

Theorem 5.1
.

There are precisely four non-pointed
\Sym
-invariant cones in
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}\mid s({\bm{u}})=0\}=\mathbb{R}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}\mid s({\bm{u}})\geq 0\}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}+\mathbb{R}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{4}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}\mid s({\bm{u}})\leq 0\}=\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}+\mathbb{R}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}).

In particular, every non-pointed
\Sym
-invariant cone in
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated.

Before proceeding to the proof of this result, let us verify the equivalence of the two descriptions given for each of the cones
\mathfrak{C}_{2},\mathfrak{C}_{3},\mathfrak{C}_{4}
. Observe that for all
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
,
\sigma\in\Sym
, and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, the following identities hold:

s(\sigma({\bm{u}}))=s({\bm{u}})\quad\text{and}\quad s(\lambda{\bm{u}}+\mu{\bm{v}})=\lambda s({\bm{u}})+\mu s({\bm{v}}).

It follows that the second description of each cone is contained in the first. For the reverse inclusion, one only needs to check that

{\bm{u}}=s({\bm{u}}){\bm{e}}_{1}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}({\bm{e}}_{i}-{\bm{e}}_{1})

for any
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

Theorem

5.1
yields the following classification of
\Sym
-invariant vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

With notation as in
Theorem

5.1
, the only nonzero
\Sym
-invariant vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
are
\mathfrak{C}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{C}_{2}
.

In fact, the proof of
Theorem

5.1
will rely on
Proposition

5.2
. Therefore, we first establish the latter. To this end, we require the following characterization of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
as a
\Sym
-invariant cone.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be a
\Sym
-invariant cone. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

C=\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
;

(ii)

There exist
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in C
such that
s({\bm{u}})>0

and
s({\bm{v}})<0
.

Proof.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}={\bm{e}}_{1}+\cdots+{\bm{e}}_{n}
.
We first prove the following claim.

Claim 5.3.1
.

Let
{\bm{w}}\in C
with
s({\bm{w}})\neq 0
, and denote by
\omega
the sign of
s({\bm{w}})
. Then

\omega{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in C\quad\text{for all }\ n\geq|\supp({\bm{w}})|.

Proof of
Claim

5.3.1
.

By permuting the coordinates of
{\bm{w}}
, if necessary, we may assume that
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m=|\supp({\bm{w}})|
. For any
n\geq m
, we compute

\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sym(n)}\sigma({\bm{w}})=s({\bm{w}})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{e}}_{i}=s({\bm{w}}){\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}.

Since the cone
C
is
\Sym
-invariant, it follows that
s({\bm{w}}){\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in C
, and consequently,
\omega{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in C
for all
n\geq m
.
∎

Let us now proceed with the proof of the lemma. Evidently, only the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) requires justification. Set
l=\max\{|\supp({\bm{u}})|,|\supp({\bm{v}})|\}
. Then it follows from
Claim

5.3.1
that
\pm{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in C
for all
n\geq l
. Hence, for all
n\geq l+1
, we have

{\bm{e}}_{n}={\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}+(-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n-1})\in C\quad\text{and}\quad-{\bm{e}}_{n}={\bm{\epsilon}}_{n-1}+(-{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n})\in C.

Since
C
is
\Sym
-invariant, we deduce that
\pm{\bm{e}}_{i}\in C
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,
C=\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.
∎

We also need the following simple property of
\Sym
-invariant vector spaces.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be a
\Sym
-invariant vector space. If there exists
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
u_{j}\neq u_{k}
for some
k\neq j
, then
\mathfrak{C}_{2}\subseteq L
.

Proof.

By permuting the coordinates of
{\bm{u}}
, we may assume that
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
. Let
\sigma\in\Sym
be the transposition that swaps
1
and
2
. Then

\sigma({\bm{u}})=(u_{2},u_{1},u_{3},\dots)\in L.

It follows that

(u_{1}-u_{2})({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})={\bm{u}}-\sigma({\bm{u}})\in L,

and consequently,
{\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}\in L
. This yields
\mathfrak{C}_{2}=\mathbb{R}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})\subseteq L
, as claimed.
∎

We are ready for the proof of
Proposition

5.2
.

Proof of
Proposition

5.2
.

Let
L\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be any nonzero
\Sym
-invariant vector subspace. We aim to show that
L=\mathfrak{C}_{2}
. Take any
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in L\setminus\{0\}
. Then
u_{i}\neq 0
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
u_{n}=0
for
n\gg 0
, it follows from
Lemma

5.4
that
\mathfrak{C}_{2}\subseteq L
. Conversely, for any
{\bm{v}}\in L
, we have
-{\bm{v}}\in L
and
s(-{\bm{v}})=-s({\bm{v}})
. Since
L\neq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
,
Lemma

5.3
implies that
s({\bm{v}})=0
. Hence,

L\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{2}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Let us now prove
Theorem

5.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

It suffices to prove the first assertion, as the following finite sets are
\Sym
-equivariant generating sets for the cones
\mathfrak{C}_{1},\mathfrak{C}_{2},\mathfrak{C}_{3},\mathfrak{C}_{4}
, respectively:

\mathfrak{G}_{1}=\{\pm{\bm{e}}_{1}\},\ \ \mathfrak{G}_{2}=\{\pm({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})\},\ \ \mathfrak{G}_{3}=\{{\bm{e}}_{1},\pm({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})\},\ \ \mathfrak{G}_{4}=\{-{\bm{e}}_{1},\pm({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})\}.

Let
C\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be any non-pointed
\Sym
-invariant cone. Then
\lin(C)
is a nonzero proper
\Sym
-invariant vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. So
\lin(C)=\mathfrak{C}_{2}
by
Proposition

5.2
. It is thus enough to show that if
\lin(C)\subsetneq C
, then either
C=\mathfrak{C}_{3}
or
C=\mathfrak{C}_{4}
. Indeed, since
\mathfrak{C}_{2}\subsetneq C
, there exists
{\bm{u}}=(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in C
such that
s({\bm{u}})\neq 0
. Consider first the case
s({\bm{u}})>0
. Then from
Lemma

5.3
it follows that
s({\bm{v}})\geq 0
for all
{\bm{v}}\in C
, i.e.,
C\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{3}
. On the other hand, we have

s({\bm{u}}){\bm{e}}_{1}={\bm{u}}+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{i})\in C,

since
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}u_{i}({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{i})\in\mathfrak{C}_{2}\subseteq C
. This implies
{\bm{e}}_{1}\in C
, and therefore,

\mathfrak{C}_{3}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}+\mathfrak{C}_{2}=\cone(\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}))+\mathfrak{C}_{2}\subseteq C.

We have thus shown that
C=\mathfrak{C}_{3}
if
s({\bm{u}})>0
. When
s({\bm{u}})<0
, an analogous argument yields
C=\mathfrak{C}_{4}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Motivated by
Theorem

5.1
, it is natural to investigate the classification of non-pointed
\Sym(n)
-invariant cones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
. In the case
n=1
, the only non-pointed cone is trivially the entire space
\mathbb{R}
. For
n\geq 2
, one might expect that the restrictions of the four global cones from
Theorem

5.1
would exhaust all possibilities. However, somewhat surprisingly, a fifth cone emerges. The next result may be viewed as a local counterpart of
Theorem

5.1
.

Proposition 5.5
.

For
n\geq 2
, there are exactly five non-pointed
\Sym(n)
-invariant cones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid s({\bm{u}})=0\}=\mathbb{R}\Sym(n)({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid s({\bm{u}})\geq 0\}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}+\mathbb{R}\Sym(n)({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{4}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid s({\bm{u}})\leq 0\}=\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}^{n}+\mathbb{R}\Sym(n)({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(a,\dots,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid a\in\mathbb{R}\}=\mathbb{R}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n},

where
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}={\bm{e}}_{1}+\cdots+{\bm{e}}_{n}.

The proof of this proposition closely follows the argument used for
Theorem

5.1
, with some minor modifications. Therefore, we will outline only the main steps, highlighting the necessary adjustments required for the local setting. Let us begin with a local counterpart of
Lemma

5.3
, which characterizes when a
\Sym(n)
-invariant cone equals the whole space.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\Sym(n)
-invariant cone for some
n\geq 2
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

C_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(ii)

There exist
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in C_{n}
with
|\supp({\bm{u}})|,|\supp({\bm{v}})|<n
such that
s({\bm{u}})>0

and
s({\bm{v}})<0
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Define
l=\max\{|\supp({\bm{u}})|,|\supp({\bm{v}})|\}
. Following the argument in the proof of
Claim

5.3.1
, we obtain
\pm{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}\in C_{n}
for all
l\leq k\leq n
. Thus, in particular,
\pm{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n},\pm{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n-1}\in C_{n}
since
l<n
. A similar reasoning as in the proof of
Lemma

5.3
then implies that
\pm{\bm{e}}_{i}\in C_{n}
for all
i\in[n]
. This yields
C_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as desired.
∎

The next result identifies all
\Sym(n)
-invariant vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, showing how the local scenario differs from the global one established in
Proposition

5.2
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Keep the notation from
Proposition

5.5
. Then for
n\geq 2
, the only nonzero
\Sym(n)
-invariant vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{D}_{5}
.

Proof.

Let
L_{n}
be a nonzero
\Sym(n)
-invariant vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that
L_{n}\neq\mathfrak{D}_{5}
, i.e., there exists
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in L_{n}
with
u_{i}\neq u_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. We claim that
L_{n}
must be either
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
. Indeed, following the argument in the proof of
Lemma

5.4
, we obtain
{\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}\in L_{n}
, which yields
\mathfrak{D}_{2}\subseteq L_{n}
. If
s({\bm{v}})=0
for all
{\bm{v}}\in L_{n}
, then
L_{n}=\mathfrak{D}_{2}
. Otherwise, if
s({\bm{v}})\neq 0
for some
{\bm{v}}\in L_{n}
, we must show that
L_{n}=\mathfrak{D}_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
Lemma

5.6
, it suffices to find an element
{\bm{w}}\in L_{n}
satisfying
|\supp({\bm{w}})|<n
and
s({\bm{w}})>0
, as this would imply
-{\bm{w}}\in L_{n}
with
|\supp(-{\bm{w}})|<n
and
s(-{\bm{w}})<0
. Since
\pm{\bm{v}}\in L_{n}
and
s({\bm{v}})\neq 0
, it follows from the proof of
Claim

5.3.1
that
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in L_{n}
. Therefore,

{\bm{w}}\coloneqq({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})+{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}=2{\bm{e}}_{1}+{\bm{e}}_{3}+\cdots+{\bm{e}}_{n}\in L_{n}.

As
|\supp({\bm{w}})|=n-1
and
s({\bm{w}})=n
, we are done.
∎

We are now prepared to prove
Proposition

5.5
.

Proof of
Proposition

5.5
.

Suppose that
C_{n}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a non-pointed
\Sym(n)
-invariant cone. Then
\lin(C_{n})
is either
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{5}
by
Lemma

5.7
.

Case 1
:
\lin(C_{n})=\mathfrak{D}_{2}
. We claim that
C_{n}
must be one of
\mathfrak{D}_{2},\mathfrak{D}_{3}
, or
\mathfrak{D}_{4}
. As in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
, it suffices to show that
C_{n}
is contained in either
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{4}
. If this were not the case, there would exist
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in C_{n}
such that
s({\bm{u}})>0
and
s({\bm{v}})<0
. It then follows from the proof of
Claim

5.3.1
that
\pm{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in C_{n}
. Since
\pm({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2})\in\mathfrak{D}_{2}\subseteq C_{n}
, we deduce that
\pm({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}+{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n})\in C_{n}
. This together with
Lemma

5.6
implies that
C_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a contradiction.

Case 2
:
\lin(C_{n})=\mathfrak{D}_{5}
. We show that
C_{n}=\mathfrak{D}_{5}
. Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in C_{n}
with
u_{i}\neq u_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. By permuting the coordinates, we may assume that
u_{1}\leq\cdots\leq u_{n}
. Set
{\bm{v}}={\bm{u}}-u_{1}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}
and
{\bm{w}}={\bm{u}}-u_{n}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}
. Then
{\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}\in C_{n}
since
\pm{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}\in\mathfrak{D}_{5}\subseteq C_{n}
. It is clear that
|\supp({\bm{v}})|,|\supp({\bm{w}})|<n
and
s({\bm{v}})>0,s({\bm{w}})<0
. Applying
Lemma

5.6
, we obtain
C_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

5.2.
Non-positive symmetric normal monoids

Building on the classifications of non-pointed symmetric cones established above, we now derive classifications for non-positive symmetric normal monoids. Let us begin with a characterization of positivity for monoids in terms of their associated cones. This slightly generalizes a classical result for affine monoids (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Proposition 2.16]
). Recall that a monoid
M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is said to be positive if its group of units
\U(M)
is trivial.

Lemma 5.8
.

For any monoid
M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, the following equality holds:

\lin(\cone(M))=\mathbb{R}\U(M).

In particular,
M
is positive if and only if
\cone(M)
is pointed.

Proof.

It suffices to establish the first assertion. The inclusion
\mathbb{R}\U(M)\subseteq\lin(\cone(M))
is immediate. Conversely, take any
{\bm{u}}\in\lin(\cone(M)).
Since both
{\bm{u}}
and
-{\bm{u}}
lie in
\cone(M)
, they can be written as nonnegative linear combinations of finitely many elements of
M
. Thus, there exists an affine submonoid
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
such that
\pm{\bm{u}}\in\cone(M^{\prime})
, which implies
{\bm{u}}\in\lin(\cone(M^{\prime})).
Because
\cone(M^{\prime})
is finitely generated, it lies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\geq 1
. Furthermore, by the Minkowski–Weyl theorem, this rational cone can be expressed as the intersection of finitely many linear rational halfspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Evidently,
\lin(\cone(M^{\prime}))
is the intersection of the hyperplanes defining these halfspaces, i.e., it is the solution space of a system of linear equations with integer coefficients. It follows that
\lin(\cone(M^{\prime}))
is spanned by
N\coloneqq\lin(\cone(M^{\prime}))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. For any
{\bm{v}}\in N
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\pm k{\bm{v}}\in M^{\prime}
, which implies
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}\U(M^{\prime})
. Hence,

N\subseteq\mathbb{R}\U(M^{\prime}).

Therefore,

{\bm{u}}\in\lin(\cone(M^{\prime}))=\mathbb{R}N\subseteq\mathbb{R}\U(M^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbb{R}\U(M).

This shows that
\lin(\cone(M))\subseteq\mathbb{R}\U(M)
, completing the proof.
∎

Combining the preceding lemma with
Theorem

5.1
, we obtain the following classification of all non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoids.

Corollary 5.9
.

There are exactly four non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoids in
\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}\mid s({\bm{u}})=0\}=\mathbb{Z}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}\mid s({\bm{u}})\geq 0\}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}+\mathbb{Z}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{4}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}\mid s({\bm{u}})\leq 0\}=\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}^{(\mathbb{N})}+\mathbb{Z}\Sym({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}).

In particular, every non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoid in
\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
, it suffices to establish the first assertion.
Using the notation from
Theorem

5.1
, we note that
\mathfrak{C}_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\mathfrak{M}_{i}
for all
i\in[4]
. Hence,
\mathfrak{M}_{i}
is a non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoid for all
i\in[4]
. Conversely, let
M
be any non-positive
\Sym
-invariant normal monoid in
\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Then the normality of
M
implies that
M=\cone(M)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. According to
Lemma

5.8
,
\cone(M)
is a non-pointed cone. Therefore, by
Theorem

5.1
,
\cone(M)=\mathfrak{C}_{i}
for some
i\in[4]
. Since
\mathfrak{C}_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}=\mathfrak{M}_{i}
, we conclude that
M=\mathfrak{M}_{i}
for some
i\in[4]
, as required.
∎

As a discrete analogue of
Proposition

5.5
, the following result classifies all non-positive
\Sym(n)
-invariant normal monoids in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Since its proof parallels that of
Corollary

5.9
, we omit the details.

Corollary 5.10
.

For
n\geq 2
, there are exactly five non-positive
\Sym(n)
-invariant normal monoids in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid s({\bm{u}})=0\}=\mathbb{Z}\Sym(n)({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid s({\bm{u}})\geq 0\}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}+\mathbb{Z}\Sym(n)({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{4}

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid s({\bm{u}})\leq 0\}=\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}^{n}+\mathbb{Z}\Sym(n)({\bm{e}}_{1}-{\bm{e}}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{5}

\displaystyle=\{(a,\dots,a)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid a\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\mathbb{Z}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{n}.

We conclude this section with the following problem, which arises naturally from the preceding results.

Problem 5.11
.

Extend
Corollaries

5.9
and
5.10
to monoids that are not necessarily normal.

6.
Equivariant Gordan’s lemma

Having established the necessary groundwork in the preceding sections, we are now in a position to prove the following equivariant analogue of Gordan’s lemma. This result, which generalizes
[
18
, Theorem 6.1]
, completely resolves
Conjecture

1.1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be a
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated rational cone. Then
M=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is a
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated normal monoid.

Proof.

If
M
is non-positive, then it is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated by
Corollary

5.9
. Assume now that
M
is positive. Let
G\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for some
r\geq 1
be a finite
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for
C
. Consider the
\Sym
-invariant chain of cones
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
defined by

C_{n}=\begin{cases}\{{\bm{0}}\}&\text{if }n<r,\\
\cone(\Sym(n)(G))&\text{if }n\geq r.\end{cases}

It is evident that
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes with direct limit
C
, and each
C_{n}
is a finitely generated rational cone. Now consider the associated
\Sym
-invariant chain of monoids
\mathcal{M}=(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
M_{n}=C_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. Clearly,
M
is the direct limit of this chain. Since
M
is positive, each
M_{n}
is also a positive monoid. Furthermore, by the classical Gordan’s lemma, each
M_{n}
is an affine normal monoid. Because the chain
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes,
Theorem

4.3
ensures that statement (iii) of that theorem holds for
C
and
\mathcal{C}
. In turn, this implies that statement (iv) of
Corollary

4.5
holds for
M
and
\mathcal{M}
. Therefore, by
Corollary

4.5
,
M
is
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated.
∎

Given a
\Sym
-equivariantly finitely generated rational cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
, it is natural to ask how to find a finite
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for the monoid
M=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. If
M
is non-positive, then
Corollary

5.9
provides an explicit description of
M
, making it straightforward to produce such a generating set. When
M
is positive, a finite
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for
M
is implicitly given in the proofs of
Theorems

4.3
and
4.5
. We now make this set explicit in the following result.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be a pointed
\Sym
-invariant rational cone that admits a finite
\Sym
-equivariant generating set
G\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for some
r\geq 1
. Consider the chain
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
C_{n}=\{{\bm{0}}\}
if
n<r
and

C_{n}=\cone(\Sym(n)(G))\quad\text{if }\ n\geq r.

Let
M_{n}=C_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
, and denote the Hilbert basis of
M_{n}
by
\mathcal{H}_{n}
. Set

q=\max\{3r^{2},\|\mathcal{H}_{3r^{2}}\|\}.

Then
\mathcal{H}_{q}
is a
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for the monoid
M=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.

Proof.

Since
G\subseteq M
, it is clear that
C=\cone(M)
. Thus, by
Lemma

5.8
, the monoid
M
is positive. Arguing as in the proof of
Theorem

6.1
, we deduce that
\mathcal{M}=(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a
\Sym
-invariant chain of positive affine normal monoids whose direct limit is
M
.
By construction,
G
is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant generating set for
C_{n}
for all
n\geq r
. Hence, from the proof of the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) in
Theorem

4.3
, it follows that both conditions in statement (iii) of that theorem hold for
C
and
\mathcal{C}
when
n\geq p\coloneqq 3r^{2}
. Consequently, both conditions in statement (iv) of
Corollary

4.5
are satisfied for
M
and
\mathcal{M}
when
n\geq p
. Now, invoking the proof of the implications (iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(v) in
Theorem

4.3
, we conclude that for all
n\geq q=\max\{p,\|\mathcal{H}_{p}\|\}
, every element
{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
has support size at most
q
. In particular, there exists
\sigma\in\Sym(n)
such that

\sigma({\bm{u}})\in M\cap\mathbb{R}^{q}=M_{q}.

Since
{\bm{u}}
is irreducible in
M_{n}
, it follows that
\sigma({\bm{u}})
is irreducible in
M_{q}
, i.e.,
\sigma({\bm{u}})\in\mathcal{H}_{q}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq\Sym(n)(\mathcal{H}_{q})
for all
n\geq q
, which implies that
\mathcal{H}_{q}
is a
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for
M
.
∎

The following local counterpart of
Proposition

6.2
follows directly from that proposition and its proof.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a stabilizing
\Sym
-invariant chain of pointed, finitely generated rational cones with stability index
r
. Let
\mathcal{M}=(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the associated chain of monoids, where
M_{n}=C_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
. Denote the Hilbert basis of each
M_{n}
by
\mathcal{H}_{n}
. Then the chain
\mathcal{M}
stabilizes with stability index at most
q\coloneqq\max\{3r^{2},\|\mathcal{H}_{3r^{2}}\|\}
.

As the next example demonstrates, the bound for the stabilization of equivariant Hilbert bases given in
Propositions

6.2
and
6.3
, while seemingly loose, is in fact sharp.

Example 6.4
.

Consider the
\Sym
-invariant cone
C
that is
\Sym
-equivariantly generated by
G=\{(-1,a)\}
, where
a\geq 2
is an integer. Let
\mathcal{C}=(C_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
\mathcal{M}=(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
denote the associated chains of cones and monoids, respectively, as defined in
Proposition

6.2
. Then the chain
\mathcal{C}
stabilizes with stability index
r=2
. For
n\geq 2
, it can be shown that the set

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\{{\bm{e}}_{1}\}\cup\Big\{(-1,b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1})\mid b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}=a\Big\}

is a
\Sym(n)
-equivariant Hilbert basis for
M_{n}
. In particular,
{\bm{u}}\coloneqq(-1,1^{(a)})\in\mathcal{H}_{a+1}
. Since
{\bm{u}}
is irreducible in
M_{a+1}
and has support size
a+1
, it is easily seen that

{\bm{u}}\in M_{a+1}\setminus\mn(\Sym(a+1)(M_{a})).

Thus,
\ind(\mathcal{M})\geq a+1
, where
\ind(\mathcal{M})
denotes the stability index of
\mathcal{M}
. On the other hand, since every element of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
has support size at most
a+1
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subseteq\Sym(n)(\mathcal{H}_{a+1})
for all
n\geq a+1
. Hence,
\ind(\mathcal{M})=a+1
, and
\mathcal{H}_{a+1}
is a
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for the monoid
M=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
.
Now, since
\|\mathcal{H}_{n}\|=a+1
for all
n\geq 2
,
Corollary

6.3
yields

\ind(\mathcal{M})\leq q\coloneqq\max\{3r^{2},\|\mathcal{H}_{3r^{2}}\|\}=\max\{12,a+1\}.

Therefore, if
a\geq 11
, then
\ind(\mathcal{M})=a+1=q
, and
q
is the minimal index for which
\mathcal{H}_{q}
is a
\Sym
-equivariant generating set for
M
.

7.
Further open problems

We propose here two more open problems concerning dual monoids and dual lattices that are inspired by the equivariant analogues of the Minkowski–Weyl theorem established in
Section

3
.

In analogy with cones, the
dual monoid
of a monoid
M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is defined as

M^{*}=\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in M\},

and for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the dual of a monoid
M_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is similarly given by

M_{n}^{*}=\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in M_{n}\}.

In light of
Theorems

3.3
and
3.12
, the following problem naturally arises.

Problem 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of monoids with direct limit
M=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}M_{n}.
Describe the global dual monoid
M^{*}
and the chain
\mathcal{M}^{*}=(M_{n}^{*})_{n\geq 1}
of local dual monoids.

A solution to this problem may provide valuable insight into the local-global behavior of monoids (see
Problem

4.7
), given the critical role played by
Theorem

3.3
in the proofs of
Theorems

4.1
and
4.3
.

Recall that a
lattice

L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
is a free abelian subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
. Its
dual lattice
is defined as

L^{*}=\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in L\}.

Similarly, for a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the dual of a lattice
L_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

L_{n}^{*}=\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\ \text{ for all }\ {\bm{u}}\in L_{n}\}.

Motivated by finiteness questions concerning Markov bases in algebraic statistics, a general framework for analyzing symmetric lattices has been developed in
[
19
]
, with a particular focus on their equivariant generating sets, equivariant Markov bases, equivariant Gröbner bases, and equivariant Graver bases. Within this context,
Theorems

3.3
and
3.12
suggest the following problem.

Problem 7.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{L}=(L_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a
\Sym
-invariant chain of lattices with limit
L=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}L_{n}.
Describe the global dual lattice
L^{*}
and the chain of local dual lattices
\mathfrak{L}^{*}=(L_{n}^{*})_{n\geq 1}
. In particular, determine whether
L^{*}
and the
L_{n}^{*}
admit finite equivariant generating sets, equivariant Markov bases, equivariant Gröbner bases, and equivariant Graver bases, when they exist.
