Fault correcting adder design for low power applications
Authors: Pritty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractField Programmable Gate Arrays are extensively used in space, military, and commercial sectors due to their reprogrammable nature. In high-safety environments, ensuring fault tolerance is crucial to improving the performance of electronic and computational systems. Common fault-tolerant methods include time redundancy, double modular redundancy, triple modular redundancy, hardware redundancy, self-checking, self-repairing, and Operand Width Aware Hardware Reuse. This paper introduces a novel approach based on error correction and detection techniques, aimed at reducing hardware complexity while optimizing for low power consumption, high speed, and minimal transistor count. The proposed technique is applicable to various arithmetic circuits. Fault-correcting and fault-detecting adders were designed using a combination of components, including a full adder (comprising one XOR gate, one NOT gate, and two 2:1 multiplexers), three XOR gates, three XNOR gates, one functional unit, two inverters, and two multiplexers (2:1 MUX). To assess the fault tolerance of the design, the technique was applied to an adder circuit, with its performance in terms of power consumption, hardware usage, energy efficiency, and delay simulated using Cadence Virtuoso @90 nm technology. Pre-layout and post-layout simulation results showed a 98% reduction in power consumption, 82% energy savings, 36.5 and 55.95% transistor savings, and a 215% reduction in area overhead compared to existing fault-tolerant adders. Additionally, multiplier designs were tested to validate the fault-correcting adder design.Keywords: Full adder, XOR gate, XNOR gate, Fault detecting adder, Fault correcting adder, Transistor overhead, Multiplexer (MUX)Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Other nanotechnology, Techniques and instrumentation, Engineering

Introduction

Today, advancements in integrated circuit (IC) technology have led to a rapid reduction in the number and sizes of devices, along with an increase in the density of MOSFETs. VLSI circuits that consume a significant amount of power can result in chip failures in both digital and analog systems. High-speed and low-power operations are essential features in the latest designs of various microelectronic components. The adder is a key element in this context, extensively used in compact devices such as mobile phones, remote sensors, virtual reality systems, and other applications that require minimal power dissipation. Adders are integral to numerous computational circuits designed for energy efficiency and play a vital role in the arithmetic units of devices. They are commonly employed in various multiplier designs and numerous circuits and systems1. Additionally, adders are utilized in digital signal processors, microprocessors, and mobile and portable devices for performing arithmetic operations. An adder circuit optimized for high-speed using 90 nm CMOS technology has been documented in2. However, as the transistors or components are enhanced, the likelihood of failures also increases3. Fault tolerance refers to the ability of an electronic system or network to maintain continuous operation even when one or more components fail. Fault-tolerant circuitry addresses interruptions caused by logic errors and other types of failures. The likelihood of faults occurring in specific applications, such as medical monitoring systems, defense surveillance, and space applications, can severely disrupt system functionality4.

Faults are identified using the self-checking technique within the circuit. Most of the approaches reported require repeated execution of instructions for fault detection5. The self-checking technique enables high accuracy through parallel checking, utilizing additional circuitry that is more cost-effective than duplicating circuits for monitoring and allowing precise error detection6. A device achieves total self-checking (TSC) when it is self-testing and operates without faults7. This technique has been applied in various applications, including self-testing arithmetic logic units (ALUs) and self-healing networks8. Circuits and systems employing self-checking have been designed to identify different types of faults, such as transient (temporary) and permanent faults9. Currently, numerous methods have been developed for fault removal in various adder designs10–16. Other techniques for fault detection and repair include the OWHR self-repairing method and self-checking methods17. State-of-the-art research indicates that various works have been published to achieve energy-delay-product (EDP) efficient adders and multipliers18. Fault-tolerant adders may also incorporate specialized types of FETs19, quantum-dot cellular automata20,21, and reversible logic methods22. Additionally, they can be designed using parallel prefix adders23, carry-select adders24,25, and other techniques26–33.

In reference34, an adder was created using either a fault-tolerant full adder (FTFA) or an exact full adder (EFA) employing 90 nm technology. This design achieved a low count of Look-Up Tables (LUTs), but power consumption still fell within the milliwatt (mW) range. It also attained minimal power consumption in the nanowatt (nW) range and occupied an area of 13 µm2; however, the reduction in power-delay product (PDP) compared to other adders was minimal. In reference35, the Optimized Fault-Tolerant Adder (OFTA), Selector-Based Fault-Tolerant Adder-II (SBFTA-II), and Selector-Based Fault-Tolerant Adder-I (SBFTA-I) were developed, resulting in reduced power and delay, though they required a larger area. Self-checking adders have been utilized to create fault-tolerant adders designed for applications that demand minimal area and operate at low voltage using nano meter technology36–38.

This paper presents a high-speed, fault-tolerant full adder designed with a straightforward architecture using 90 nm CMOS technology, which can be further scaled. The adder is initially constructed using a multiplexer and an inverter. Subsequently, the proposed fault-detecting and fault-correcting adders are designed to detect and repair errors. The proposed adder employs a 2:1 MUX and a NOT gate, focusing on minimizing the transistor count. Fault detection in the adder is managed by a fault-detecting block, while corrections are made by a fault-correcting block. The fault-detecting block features XNOR gates, a functional unit, and XOR gates, utilizing fewer transistors and operating at low power and voltage. The paper is organized into several sections: Section “State-of-art techniques” elaborates on state-of-the-art fault-detecting and fault-correcting techniques, Section “Proposed fault-tolerant adder design” discusses the proposed fault-detecting and fault-correcting adder designs, Section “RC model for full adder” analyzes the output of the proposed design along with tables, graphs, and comparisons to relevant state-of-the-art adder designs, and Section “Result and discussion” concludes the discussion on the proposed fault-correcting and fault-detecting adder designs.

State-of-art techniques

State-of-art fault-tolerant adder designs are reported in4,5,17. They have been designed using several techniques. Most designs have used some type of redundancy: (a) Time redundancy, (b) Double modular redundancy, (c) triple-modular redundancy, (d) hardware redundancy, (e) Operand width aware hardware reuse (OWHR).

State-of-art and adder design techniques are been based on these types of redundancy. Some adders are able to detect faults and some have ability to correct the fault, while others can do both. Four fault correction and detection adder techniques are explained below.

Fault detection techniques

Self-checking adder using XNOR, full adder and Boolean expression

The self-checking adder was developed by Vasudevan17. This design distinguishes between fault-free conditions and stuck-at faults in the adder circuit. The fault-checking system includes XOR gates, a fault-repairing multiplexer, two adders, and two rail checkers. Figure 1a illustrates the self-checking adder from17. Z1 and Z2 are the checker outputs, and if they are the same, the circuit is fault-free. For a fault-free case, both Z1 and Z2 are either zero or both are one. If Z1 is low and Z2 is high, or vice versa, the circuit is also considered fault-free. While this design can detect single-net faults, it cannot pinpoint the fault location in the adder. Fault repair is also not feasible, as the entire unit must be replaced. Additional limitations include its inability to detect double faults or faulty carry propagation, and the design is hardware-expensive.

The self-checking full adder circuit is designed to detect single or multiple faults and accurately identify their location. Both the carry select adder (CSA) and ripple carry adder (RCA) have been implemented. The CSA is the fastest adder and requires fewer transistors compared to other designs, while the RCA is simpler to implement but requires more time for computation. In this design, the carry and sum outputs are repeatedly measured and checked to detect any faults in the outputs. The detailed diagram of the self-checking (SC) adder design is shown in Fig. 1b. Fault correction techniques

Self-repairing (SR) adder using full adder and multiplexers

The SR adder, reported by Muhammad Ali Akbar5, detects faults and identifies the location of single and multiple faults. Some of the limitations from a previous adder design17 have been addressed. This design includes a rail checker, a full adder, and two XNOR gates for fault detection. It uses two circuits of adders to correct faults in the SR adder. The XNOR gate (X1) compares the outputs of the carry and sum of the full adder. When all the inputs are the same, the carry and sum outputs match. If one input differs from the others, the carry and sum outputs will be different. The second XNOR gate (X2) ensures fault-free operation. The fault in the adder design is represented by Ef, as shown in Fig. 1a. The fault-detecting adder can detect two or more errors, while the fault-correcting adder identifies these errors and requires replacing the faulty full adder circuit with other full adders, as shown in Fig. 1c. This design requires additional full adders to achieve fault-free operation. However, it cannot detect faults when two faults occur simultaneously, leading the fault-correcting adder to show a fault-free output (Ef) despite the presence of errors. The SR adder5 is insufficient in eliminating these faults, making the design less reliable. The occurrence of double faults is a key limitation of this design.

Proposed fault-tolerant adder design

Adder design

Adder is a fundamental portion of electronic systems. Its primary aim is to add two n-bit numbers and input carry. For e.g. An … A3 A2 A1 + Bn…B3 B2 B1 = C0 Sn … S3 S2 S1.

For example, a 1-bit adder has inputs A, B, and a carry input C, with SUM and CARRY as outputs. Typically, adder circuits use XOR gates for the sum and AND-OR gates for the carry. In this adder design, one 2:1 MUX takes inputs B and B′ (NOT of B), while another 2:1 MUX for the SUM takes inputs A and A′' (NOT of A). The 2:1 MUX for the carry takes inputs A and B. The output from one 2:1 MUX is used as the select line input for both the SUM and CARRY 2:1 MUXes. The result from the SUM 2:1 MUX is the final sum. For instance, if A = 1, B = 1, and C = 0, the output of the SUM 2:1 MUX is B or 1. The select line input for the CARRY 2:1 MUX is high (1). When the output of the SUM 2:1 MUX is low (0), the output of the CARRY 2:1 MUX is high (1). This process is how all the outputs of the adder are obtained. The adder schematic is shown in Fig. 2a. The W/L ratios for the N-MOSFETs and P-MOSFETs are 0.180/0.09 µm and 0.360/0.09 µm, respectively.

Proposed fault-correcting (FC) adder

Additional circuitry is used in the fault-detecting adder to correct faults occurring in the circuit. The fault-tolerant adder reported in17 was designed solely with XNOR gates, while the proposed FC/FD adder is built using a combination of XOR and XNOR gates. The functional unit in this design utilizes fewer transistors than4, and the functional unit produces different outputs as proposed in this paper. The red line illustrates the differences between the FC adder designs from4,17. Two 2:1 MUXs are employed in the fault-correcting adder block—one for the sum and another for the carry. The output from the fault-detecting adder serves as the select line input for the fault-correcting adder. In a fault-free condition (FC = 0), the MUX selects the carry output; if a fault occurs (FC = 1), the MUX selects the inverted carry. Similarly, for the sum, FS = 0 (fault-free) selects the sum, while FS = 1 (faulty) selects the inverted sum. The outputs, SumF and CarryF, show the auto-corrected values even after faults affect the sum and carry terminals of the adder. RC model for full adder

The full adder design is asymmetrical, with the gate capacitance being directly proportional to the transistor’s width. The adder is constructed using basic digital logic components like XNOR, NOT, and MUX gates. Capacitance within the adder can be calculated using the internal nodes (X and Y). By reducing the width-to-length ratio (W/L) of the transistors, the capacitance load at these nodes is minimized, which in turn decreases the delay for various input values.

In this design, inverter MOSFETs and the body terminals of MOSFETs are connected to VDD or ground (GND) terminals. The proposed FC and FD adder designs have fewer connections to VDD and GND compared to4,17, making them less vulnerable to voltage fluctuations. As a result, the proposed FC and FD adders are highly reliable. The full adder is implemented with a reduced number of transistors (57 transistors in the proposed design) compared to the 82-transistor design reported in4, as shown in Tables 4 and 5. This reduction in transistor count contributes to power savings in the proposed FC adder by eliminating unnecessary switching, unlike state-of-the-art designs. The design in4, implemented at 55 nm, experiences significant leakage power, while the proposed design at 90 nm does not suffer from the same level of leakage. The reduction in leakage power dissipation and transistor counts together result in substantial power savings in the proposed FC and FD adders.

Adder and multiplier designs from4 were re-evaluated at 90 nm in the current evaluation environment, and the results were compared with the proposed adder and multiplier, as shown in Figs. 7 and 8. The MUX-based adder in the proposed design is least affected by voltage fluctuations, as all connections to VDD/GND are routed through inverters. These inverters are designed to provide noise margin and other characteristics that make the circuit nearly immune to voltage fluctuations, whereas state-of-the-art designs, which directly connect to VDD/GND, are more susceptible to voltage variations.

Conclusion

This work presents a highly reliable, fault-tolerant, and power-efficient design for a full adder. The full adder and fault-correcting block are designed using a 2 × 1 multiplexer (MUX) and a NOT gate with a minimal number of transistors. The fault-detection block incorporates XNOR gates, a functional unit, and XOR gates to ensure low power dissipation. The proposed fault-detecting and fault-correcting adder minimizes the transistor count, enabling the adder circuit to detect and correct single and multiple faults, effectively making it fault-free. This design significantly reduces area overhead, transistor overhead, and other parameters. The proposed fault-detecting adder shows a notable decrease in the number of transistors. The fault-correcting (FC) adder was also evaluated and demonstrated a substantial reduction in transistor count compared to state-of-the-art designs. In terms of transistor overhead, an approximate reduction of 50% was achieved. When comparing area overhead, the proposed design offers double the area savings compared to other adder designs. Additionally, massive power savings were observed, with the proposed design showing a 100% reduction in power consumption and a significant decrease in energy consumption compared to existing fault-tolerant designs.
