Time-to-event risk prediction of dual sensory loss in middle-aged patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis: model development, preliminary external validation, and explainability analysis
Authors: Yinxian Li, Xuejiao Cao, Juzi Wang, Limei Qi, Jing Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a highly prevalent chronic condition that substantially impairs functional capacity and quality of life among middle-aged and older adults. Sensory loss, including hearing and vision loss, is another major health concern in aging populations. Dual sensory loss (DSL), the coexistence of visual and auditory impairment, leads to more severe clinical consequences than single sensory deficits, largely due to disrupted sensory integration and diminished neural compensatory mechanisms. Emerging evidence indicates that osteoarthritis is linked to progressive deterioration of auditory and visual function, highlighting the need for early identification of individuals at risk. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a machine learning-based time-to-event prediction model for DSL among middle-aged patients with symptomatic KOA and to externally validate its performance in an independent hospital-based cohort, then identify its risk factors through interpretable analysis, providing essential evidence to support early preventive interventions.MethodsData from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (N = 605) were utilized in model development phase. After data preprocessing steps, we trained and tested four time-to-event ML algorithms. Model performance was evaluated in 10-fold cross-validation by using the concordance index (C-index), Brier scores and calibration plots. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by redefining DSL using a broader cutoff and re-training all models under the same cross-validation framework to assess the robustness and stability of the finding. An independent hospital-based cohort (N = 195) was used for preliminary external validation. The optimal model was further evaluated by the decision curve analysis (DCA) to assess its clinical utility and interpreted with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to quantify feature contributions and directional effects.Results15 variables with the highest predictive capacity were retained. The DeepSurv model demonstrated superior performance in both the construction and validation phase, achieving C-index exceeding 0.8, with a Brier score below 0.1. The sensitivity analyses results were largely consistent with our primary findings, supporting the robustness of the associations. SHAP analysis revealed self-rated health and sleep duration as the most important predictors, both negatively influencing DSL risk.ConclusionsThe DeepSurv model effectively predicts time-to-event risk of DSL in KOA patients, highlighting subjective health perception and sleep duration as critical modifiable factors. These findings support the development of targeted early preventive strategies in clinical practice to preserve sensory function and reduce the long-term disease burden associated with KOA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03485-2.

Background

Arthritis is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide. The reported prevalence is 31.4% in the Chinese population [1] and as high as 41.8% in the United States [2]. Osteoarthritis (OA), the most common subtype, is a degenerative joint disease characterized by pain, stiffness, and synovial swelling, which severely impairs patients’ quality of life [3]. Among various affected sites, the knee joint is the most commonly involved [4]. Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) not only markedly restricts the daily activities and self-care ability of middle-aged and older adults but may also increase their risk of mortality. Previous researches have shown that the number of individuals with symptomatic KOA in China has reached 110 million [5]. With the accelerating global aging process, the social and familial burden associated with KOA is expected to increase further.

Sensory loss (SL), including subtypes such as hearing loss (HL) and vision loss (VL), is also a major health concern among middle-aged and older adults. Various forms of SL have been shown to be associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including social isolation [6], depression [7, 8], cognitive decline and dementia [7], drop attack [9], and increased risk of cardiovascular disease [10], particularly when coexisting with other chronic conditions [11]. A recent study reported that 58.96% of older adults in China experience dual sensory loss (DSL), characterized by the coexistence of HL and VL [12]. Compared with single sensory impairment, DSL has more severe clinical consequences, as it not only disrupts the integration and complementarity of visual and auditory information in the cortical association areas but also limits sensory reorganization and neural plasticity compensation pathways. This leads to excessive consumption of cognitive resources and reduced efficiency in attention allocation [13]. Moreover, DSL contributes to a vicious cycle by decreasing social participation and impairing the coordination of the visual-vestibular-proprioceptive systems, thereby significantly increasing the risk of cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease [14].

Emerging evidence suggests that OA is closely linked to progressive declines in auditory and visual function [15, 16]. Given the severe clinical consequences of DSL, it is essential to enhance early risk prediction and identify related risk factors. Traditional statistical models mainly rely on linear assumptions and may be limited in capturing complex, nonlinear relationships and high-dimensional interactions among predictors. In recent years, advances in computational methods have increasingly enabled the use of machine learning (ML) techniques to predict clinical risks in complex patient populations. ML-based survival (time-to-event) analysis methods can accommodate nonlinear effects, handle high-dimensional data, and improve predictive accuracy and generalizability, making it particularly suitable for modeling multifactorial conditions. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a machine learning-based time-to-event prediction model for DSL among middle-aged patients with symptomatic KOA and to externally validate its performance in an independent hospital-based cohort. In addition, we applied interpretable modeling techniques to identify and quantify key risk factors. The findings are expected to provide valuable insights for healthcare professionals to implement early preventive interventions against sensory function decline, reducing the long-term disease burden and improving the overall quality of life and health outcomes of KOA patients.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective longitudinal observational cohort study.

Data source and participants

The data used in the model development phase were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [17], a high-quality cohort study targeting middle-aged and older adults in China. CHARLS provides comprehensive information on participants’ social background, daily behaviors, health status, and household economic conditions. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011, enrolling 17,708 participants, with follow-up surveys carried out in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. All data are publicly available at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en. Since information related to hearing and vision assessment was not collected in the 2020 wave, this study utilized data from the first (2011) to the fourth (2018) waves of the CHARLS cohort. Participants included in this study were middle-aged individuals (45–59 years) who self-reported KOA at baseline but did not have DSL. After excluding participants with missing data, a total of 605 individuals were included in the sample, of whom 124 reported DSL during the three waves (2, 4, and 7 years) follow-up.

For preliminary external validation, we established an independent hospital-based cohort. This prospective cohort consisted of 195 patients from the department of rheumatism and immunology of a tertiary hospital in Northern China, with inclusion criteria and baseline assessments consistent with those of the CHARLS participants used in the model development phase. All participants were free of DSL at baseline (2022) and were followed for two years, during which 15 participants reported DSL at follow-up. Although long-term validation was not feasible at this cohort, it allows for an initial evaluation of early predictive performance and model robustness in an independent population.

Variables

Assessment of symptomatic knee osteoarthritis

In this study, the assessment of symptomatic KOA was based on participants’ self-reports, which was consistent with previous literature [18]. Participants were considered to have symptomatic KOA if they answered “yes” to both of the following questions: “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have arthritis or rheumatism?” and “Do you often experience pain?” and also indicated “knees” in response to “Which parts of your body are painful?”

Assessment of dual sensory loss

DSL was assessed based on participants’ hearing and vision scores. In the CHARLS survey, participants rated their hearing as excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor, corresponding to scores from 1 (excellent) to 5 (poor). Participants who scored 5 or who reported frequently using a hearing aid were classified as having hearing loss. Vision was assessed based on participants’ ability to see objects at a distance and up close (1 = excellent, 5 = poor). Participants were classified as having vision loss if they scored “poor” on either distance or near vision. If participants met the criteria for both hearing and vision loss, they were considered to have DSL in this study. The hearing and vision questions were identical across CHARLS waves included in this study. The same assessment criteria for hearing and vision were applied in the prospective external validation cohort.

In the primary analysis, this strictest response category (“poor”) was used to define sensory impairment in order to enhance classification specificity and minimize the risk of false-positive misclassification. This approach prioritized the identification of functionally significant impairment and was consistent with previous literature [9]. Although this definition may underestimate mild impairment, it increased the probability that identified cases represented clinically meaningful sensory loss. To assess robustness, we also conducted sensitivity analyses using a broader definition of DSL (fair/poor).

Assessment of characteristic factors

The major characteristics encompassed demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and physical conditions in this study. Demographic characteristics included gender (1 = male, 0 = female), age, education level (1 = high school or above, 0 = middle school or below), marital status (1 = married, 0 = others), retirement (1 = yes, 0 = no), residence (1 = rural, 0 = urban), total household income (in thousands of RMB), and per capita household consumption (in thousands of RMB). Lifestyle factors included smoking and drinking status (1 = yes, 0 = no), social activities (1 = yes, 0 = no) and physical exercise participation (1 = yes, 0 = no), sleep duration (1 = more than 6 h, 0 = 6 h or less), and midday rest (1 = yes, 0 = no). Physical conditions included the number of hospitalizations in the past year, the number of outpatient visits in the past month, physical disability (1 = yes, 0 = no), self-rated health status (1–5, with higher scores indicating poorer health), functional independence, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and other coexisting chronic diseases. Functional independence encompassed both activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs). ADLs included six items: eating, bathing, dressing, toileting, controlling urination and defecation, and indoor mobility. Participants were considered unable to perform an item independently if they reported “having difficulty and needing help” or “unable to perform,” which was scored as 1 point (total score 0–6, with higher scores indicating poorer functioning). IADLs included five items: housework, cooking, shopping, taking medication, and managing finances, evaluated using the same criteria (total score 0–5). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), with scores ranging from 0 to 30, where higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. Cognitive function was assessed in two domains (episodic memory and mental status), with a total score ranging from 0 to 21, where higher scores indicate better cognitive performance. Other chronic diseases besides arthritis were also included as predictor variables. Participants were considered to have a specific chronic condition if they answered “yes” to the question, “Has a doctor ever told you that you have any of the following conditions: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive system disease, mental illness, memory-related disease, or asthma?” The corresponding variable was coded as 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no”.

To minimize bias and enhance objectivity, all variables in this study were blinded and assigned coded labels to replace the actual variable names and data types [19], as detailed in Table 1 (Y representing the outcome variable, X1 representing continuous predictor variables, and X2 representing binary predictor variables). The outcome variable Y was further divided into two components. Y-time was defined as the interval from baseline to the wave in which DSL was first identified (model construction phase: 2, 4, or 7 years; external validation phase: 2 years). Remaining participants who did not experience DSL by the end of follow-up were treated as right-censored, with Y-time corresponding to the maximum observed follow-up duration. Y-event indicated whether the DSL event actually occurred during the follow-up period (coded as 1 for event occurrence and 0 for censoring). This distinction allows for accurate time-to-event modeling, ensuring that both the timing and the occurrence of events are properly accounted for in the statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we used Python 3.11 to preprocess data and develop models. The complete codes and intermediate results were documented in Jupyter Notebook Markdown to ensure the traceability of the research process.

After conducting descriptive statistical analysis of baseline characteristics, we calculated the correlation coefficients of the feature matrix. Highly correlated features (correlation coefficient > 0.9) were removed to reduce the impact of multicollinearity on model performance. To preliminarily evaluate the model’s generalizability, we divided the dataset into a training set (Training set, N = 484) and a test set (Test set, N = 121) at a 4:1 ratio using stratified random sampling based on event status to ensure comparable proportions of events and censored observations in both subsets. Feature selection was then performed using recursive feature elimination (RFE) with the Cox proportional hazards model as the base learner. At each iteration, a Cox model was fitted to the training data, and variables were ranked according to the absolute magnitude of their regression coefficients. The least important variable was removed sequentially, and model performance was evaluated using cross-validated C-index to determine the optimal subset of predictors. Considering the potential impact of class imbalance on model performance, we applied the SMOTE-Tomek hybrid sampling technique to the training set, performing combined oversampling of minority events and undersampling of majority cases to achieve more balanced categories. SMOTE-Tomek was chosen because it balances categories effectively without altering time-to-event information, and is considered appropriate for small or imbalanced clinical datasets where preserving multivariate structure is important [20]. Specifically, resampling in this study was conducted solely based on the event indicator, while each individual’s observed survival time and censoring status were preserved during the generation of synthetic minority samples. Subsequently, we use Z-score method (z=(X-µ)/σ) to standardize the continuous variables, which could improve model convergence speed and optimize the contribution of different features in the classification model. The complete data preprocessing workflow is shown in supplementary materials (Figure S1).

In the model development stage, we selected four time-to-event ML algorithms for prediction, including Random Survival Forest (RSF), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), LASSO-penalized Cox proportional hazards model (Cox-LASSO), and DeepSurv. DSL onset in this study was identified at scheduled survey waves (2, 4, and 7 years). The exact timing of event occurrence was not directly observed but was known to have occurred within the interval between two consecutive visits. For modeling purposes, event time was approximated by the wave at which DSL was first detected, which was consistent with the prior longitudinal cohort studies. All models were trained and evaluated using a ten-fold cross-validation framework, with a fixed random seed (random_state = 42) applied to ensure reproducibility of data splits, fold assignments, and model initialization. Each fold used nine subsets of the Training set (N = 484) to train the model, with the remaining subset used for calculating the Training concordance index (C-index). The trained model from each fold was then applied to the Test set (N = 121) to compute fold-specific Test C-index, which were used to assess model stability and generalizability across different training splits. For hyperparameter tuning, a predefined set of candidate hyperparameters was evaluated within the training set using this cross-validation framework. For each hyperparameter combination, models were trained on nine folds and validated on the remaining fold, and the mean C-index across the ten folds was computed. The hyperparameters achieving the highest mean C-index were selected for the final model. Finally, a single final model was trained on the entire Training set using these selected hyperparameters. Its performance was then evaluated once on the full Training set and once on the independent Test set, including C-index, Brier score, and calibration curves. Time-dependent Brier scores at predefined time points were computed using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW), with the censoring distribution estimated via the Kaplan–Meier method, as implemented in the sksurv.metrics module of scikit-survival. Calibration curves were constructed by grouping predicted risks into deciles based on quantiles of predicted probabilities. Observed event probabilities within each group were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. To reduce overfitting bias, bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was performed for optimism correction. This approach allows reporting both the stability of predictions across folds and the generalization ability of the final model on unseen data. This procedure was applied to all four time-to-event machine learning models evaluated in the study. A schematic of the complete ten-fold cross-validation pipeline is shown in supplementary material (Figure S2).

To assess the robustness of the DSL definition in this study, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a broader cutoff, in which participants reporting “fair” or “poor” hearing and/or vision were classified as having sensory impairment. DSL was redefined accordingly, and all models were re-trained and re-evaluated under the identical cross-validation scheme. Model performance under the alternative definition was compared with that of the primary analysis to evaluate the stability and generalizability of the findings.

An independent hospital-based cohort with baseline and two-year follow-up data (n = 195) was used for preliminary external validation to evaluate model performance in a real-world clinical setting. The pre-trained models were applied directly to this external cohort without refitting. Due to the limited number of events, constructing a reliable calibration curve in this dataset was considered inappropriate, as dividing the cohort into risk strata would result in very few or zero events per group, making the observed probabilities unstable. Moreover, with identical follow-up times (Y-time = 2) for all 195 participants, reliable estimation of cumulative event probabilities for survival models such as RSF is not possible. Therefore, model performance in this external cohort was evaluated at the two-year time point using the C-index and Brier score. The C-index calculated in this cohort reflects the concordance between predicted risk scores from the time-to-event models and the observed 2-year event outcomes, rather than the full survival time ranking. It was computed using all comparable pairs of participants, where one participant experienced the event within 2 years and the other did not. To complement this evaluation, we additionally assessed the model’s discrimination at 2 years using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC). Predicted 2-year risk probabilities were derived from the model’s predicted survival probabilities at 2 years, and the AUC was calculated by comparing these predicted risks with the observed 2-year event status. To quantify statistical uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the C-index, Brier score, and ROC AUC were estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples of the external cohort. In each bootstrap iteration, participants were sampled with replacement, and performance metrics were recalculated. Percentile-based 95% CIs were obtained from the empirical distribution of the bootstrap estimates.

Additionally, Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed for the optimal model, which was selected based on previously reported performance metrics, to assess its clinical utility at the two-year time point. Net benefit was calculated across a range of threshold probabilities (0.05–0.4) to compare the model-based strategy with default strategies of treating all or no patients. This provides an estimate of the potential improvement in clinical decision-making offered by the model within the external validation cohort.

Finally, we used the game theory-based SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework to conduct interpretability analysis of the optimal model. By calculating Shapley values, we constructed a feature importance ranking, and generated SHAP summary plots to visualize the marginal contributions and directional effects of each feature on the model output. This method not only followed the technical standards of explainable artificial intelligence, but also provided feature-level causal inference insights to support clinical decision-making.

Results

Descriptive statistical analysis of baseline characteristics

A total of 605 middle-aged patients with symptomatic KOA but without DSL were included in the model development and internal validation phase, among whom 124 reported the onset of DSL during the seven-year follow-up period. Baseline demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and physical conditions of the study sample were compared between participants who developed DSL and those who did not. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and binary variables were presented as frequency (percentage) for value = 0, as shown in Table 2.

Overall, patients in the DSL group were older on average and had lower level of educational, household income, and proportions of being married or residing in urban areas. They were less likely to engage in physical exercise but more likely to participate in social activities, exhibited more severe depressive symptoms, had poorer cognitive and self-care abilities, shorter nighttime sleep duration, and a lower likelihood of taking midday rests. In addition, the DSL group had higher prevalences of asthma and heart disease but a lower prevalence of dyslipidemia compared with the non-DSL group.

Feature selection

The correlation analysis of the feature matrix revealed the highest collinearity between X2-1 and X2-7, with a correlation coefficient of 0.77, followed by X1-9 and X1-10, with a correlation coefficient of 0.74. No highly correlated feature pairs (correlation coefficient > 0.9) were identified. The correlation matrix is presented in Fig. 1.

Subsequently, RFE was performed using the Cox proportional hazards model as the base learner. Within the training set, variables were ranked by the absolute magnitude of their regression coefficients, and the least important variables were iteratively removed. Using cross-validated C-index to determine the optimal subset, a total of 15 variables with the highest predictive capacity were retained: X1-1, X1-2, X1-3, X1-4, X1-5, X1-6, X1-7, X1-8, X1-9, X1-10, X2-1, X2-9, X2-11, X2-13, and X2-22.

Model construction and internal validation

The variability of the C-index across 10-fold cross-validation is shown in Fig. 2, demonstrating consistent and stable model performance across all folds for each algorithm. Following cross-validation and hyperparameter selection, a single final model for each algorithm was trained on the entire Training set and evaluated once on the independent Test set. The corresponding performance metrics are summarized in Table 3. All algorithms exhibited acceptable discriminatory performance, with C-index exceeding 0.85 in the Training set and remaining above 0.70 in the Test set. Among them, DeepSurv achieved the highest predictive accuracy across all three predefined follow-up time points, with Brier scores of 0.06, 0.03 and 0.07 in the Test set, respectively. The calibration performance of the final models is shown in Fig. 3. DeepSurv demonstrated superior calibration performance across all time horizons, particularly at 4 and 7 years. Cox-LASSO showed stable and well-aligned calibration curves at the mid-term follow-up. In contrast, RSF and GBM exhibited noticeable deviations from the ideal line, suggesting limited calibration reliability.

Sensitivity analysis

Under the broader definition of DSL, 129 participants reported DSL during the seven-year follow-up period, which was five more than the 124 identified in the primary analysis. The distribution of baseline characteristics between the DSL and non-DSL groups remained generally comparable to that observed in the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S1). After the same data preprocess procedure, the C-index and Brier scores in both the Training and internal validation (Test) sets were largely consistent with those obtained under the primary DSL definition (Supplementary Table S2). These findings indicate that the models’ predictive performance was not materially influenced by the alternative classification. Consistent internal validation results suggest that the model structure is robust to exposure reclassification, supporting the stability of both the modeling framework and its external evaluation.

Model external validation

To further assess the generalizability of the models, a preliminary external validation was conducted in an independent hospital-based cohort (N = 195) with a 2-year follow-up, during which 15 patients self-reported DSL at the 2-year time point. The baseline characteristics of this cohort are summarized, with detailed information provided in the supplementary materials (Table S3). Overall, all models maintained acceptable discriminatory ability in the external dataset, as shown in Table 4. Specifically, the C-index values ranged from 0.79 to 0.92 across the different time-to-event models, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals estimated via 1,000 bootstrap resamples. These C-indices were calculated based on 2700 comparable pairs, defined as pairs in which one participant experienced the event within 2 years and the other did not. In addition, predicted 2-year risks derived from the models’ survival probabilities at 2 years yielded ROC AUC values ranging from 0.82 to 0.90. However, given the limited number of events in the external cohort, the confidence intervals were wide, indicating statistical uncertainty. Among the evaluated models, DeepSurv continued to demonstrate superior predictive performance, achieving the highest C-index and lowest Brier score at 2 years. Based on the overall performance across evaluation metrics, DeepSurv was selected as the optimal predictive model in this study. For full details of the DeepSurv model architecture and training parameters, please refer to supplementary material (Table S4).

Decision curve analysis for the DeepSurv model is shown in Fig. 4. The DCA demonstrates that, across threshold probabilities of 0.05–0.4, using the model to guide clinical decisions provides a higher net benefit compared with default strategies of treating all or no patients. This indicates that applying the model could improve decision-making and identify patients who may benefit most from interventions at the two-year horizon.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that machine learning-based time-to-event models can effectively predict the risk of DSL in middle-aged patients with symptomatic KOA. The identification of key predictors related to subjective health status and sleep underscores the importance of patient-reported lifestyle factors in shaping DSL risk. These findings provide valuable insights to inform early risk stratification and the development of targeted prevention and intervention strategies for sensory function decline in patients with KOA.

The superior performance of the DeepSurv model indicates its particular suitability for capturing the complex, time-dependent patterns underlying DSL development. This may be attributed to its capacity to model nonlinear and high-dimensional relationships among predictors, which may interact in ways that traditional tree-based models cannot fully capture [21]. Unlike RSF or GBM, which rely on ensemble decision trees and may be limited in representing smooth hazard functions, DeepSurv leverages a neural network framework to flexibly approximate the underlying hazard function and learn subtle, patient-specific risk trajectories over time [22]. Its ability to efficiently incorporate censored survival data allows for more accurate individualized predictions, reflecting the dynamic and multifactorial nature of DSL progression in KOA patients. Moreover, the DeepSurv algorithm’s optimization through stochastic gradient descent, combined with regularization techniques such as dropout or weight decay can help mitigate overfitting even in relatively small samples or when the number of events is limited [23]. This enables stable learning of complex risk patterns where other models may struggle, improving both generalization performance and clinical interpretability of the model. However, because the current external validation was restricted to a 2-year follow-up period, the observed results should be interpreted as evidence of short-term predictive capability only, while its accuracy and stability over longer time horizons remain to be established. Notably, the current external validation yielded even lower Brier scores compared with the Training set, indicating potentially strong predictive performance of the model. However, this result should be interpreted with caution, as the small sample size (N = 195) and limited number of DSL events (N = 15) may have led to an underestimation of the true prediction error. Sparse events can reduce variability in the estimated probabilities and artificially lower the Brier score [24]. Consequently, although these findings are promising, further validation in larger cohorts with a higher number of events is warranted to confirm the model’s long-term reliability and clinical utility.

SHAP analysis indicated that self-rated health was the most important feature in the DeepSurv model. However, group comparisons revealed no significant difference in self-rated health between the DSL and non-DSL groups (P > 0.05). This discrepancy may be because these two analysis methods have inherent differences. Specifically, traditional t-test mainly evaluate overall mean differences between groups, which may ignore the influence of individual effects, nonlinear relationships, or feature combinations on risk. In contrast, machine learning models evaluate the contribution of each feature to the prediction result for each individual, which could enable the detection of signals that may be invisible on the average level of group but are critical for individual DSL risk. As a subjective comprehensive indicator, self-rated health status may reflect early functional decline or psychological fragility. These conditions may not be reflected in objective health indicators or the number of chronic diseases, but they have affected the risk of DSL. Therefore, self-rated health status shows high sensitivity and importance in the model, highlighting its unique value in early risk identification.

SHAP analysis results also indicated that poorer self-rated health was associated with a lower predicted risk of DSL, which is a somewhat surprising discovery. A possible explanation is that, within a chronic disease cohort, individuals with poorer self-rated health are often with more aware of the severity and complexity of their diseases. This perception of high risk, rather than the disease itself, may motivate them to maintain higher levels of treatment adherence, engage more frequently in medical monitoring, and actively adopt healthy lifestyle throughout the follow-up period [25], which could provide more opportunities for early detection of potential complications and timely intervention. Therefore, poorer self-rated health may be associated with a more proactive approach to health risk management, which is shown in the model as a negative contribution to DSL risk. However, this interpretation remains speculative, and the observed negative association should be interpreted with more caution. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and to determine whether this relationship is reproducible in other populations and study settings.

In addition, sleep duration was also identified as an important factor, with longer sleep associated with a reduced risk of DSL, which was consistent with previous studies [26, 27]. Adequate sleep can promote physiological self-repair and strengthen immunity, which could help to slow down functional decline. Researches have shown that slow-wave sleep can improve the efficiency of cerebrospinal and interstitial fluid exchange, accelerate the cleaning of metabolic wastes in the body and promote cell repair [28], which may be beneficial to the regeneration of retinal photoreceptor cells and cochlear hair cells. Sufficient sleep has also been proved to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and enhance anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10), which can inhibit sensory organ degeneration caused by oxidative stress [29]. Furthermore, longer sleep duration could significantly improve functional connection between the parietal and prefrontal cortices. Such optimization of neural networks may enhance compensatory adaptation [30, 31] and alleviate functional impairments caused by sensory decline. Overall, this study supports us to adopt a multi-faceted health management method to prevent DSL, including interventions targeting subjective health perception, optimizing sleep, and other personalized health promotion measures. Future research can further explore the precise principle of sleep cycle, and combine other changeable factors, such as nutrition, physical activity and cognitive training, to formulate more comprehensive DSL early prevention strategies.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop a predictive model for future sensory loss in KOA patients. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both hearing and vision scores were based on self-reports, which may introduce subjective bias and affect measurement accuracy. Participants’ perceptions of their sensory abilities can be influenced by factors such as cognitive status or social desirability, potentially leading to overestimation or underestimation of their true sensory function. Second, DSL occurrence was determined at discrete survey waves, and the event time was approximated by the wave at which DSL was first detected. Such approximation may affect hazard ratio estimation and the precision of time-to-event inference. Furthermore, although preliminary external validation was conducted, it was limited to short-term risk stratification, and the model’s long-term predictive performance has yet to be validated. Our future studies will further extend the follow-up period of prospective hospital-based cohorts to conduct long-term external validation, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s predictive performance and enhancing its generalizability and clinical utility.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed four machine learning-based time-to-event prediction model for DSL in middle-aged patients with symptomatic KOA and conducted preliminary evaluations of their external generalizability. Among the models assessed, DeepSurv demonstrated the best overall performance. SHAP analysis identified subjective health perception and sleep duration as the most important predictors of DSL, providing valuable guidance for healthcare providers in implementing early preventive interventions targeting sensory function decline. Future research will focus on further validating the model’s long-term predictive performance in hospital cohorts with extended follow-up periods, as well as developing multidisciplinary intervention programs to predict and slow the progression of sensory loss, thereby improving KOA patients’ quality of life and long-term health outcomes.
