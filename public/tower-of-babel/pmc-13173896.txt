Views vs. reliability : a cross-sectional analysis of reliability and risk discussion in YouTube anesthesia videos
Authors: Agah Abdullah Kahramanlar, Kubra Yazici, Ozlem Dilara Erguney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the quality and reliability of anesthesia-related videos on YouTube, a widely used online platform for patient education.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted on June 15, 2025. Using the keywords “anesthesia,” “general anesthesia,” “regional anesthesia,” “spinal anesthesia,” and “epidural anesthesia,” the first 50 English-language videos ranked by relevance were selected. Inclusion criteria were relevance to anesthesia, adequate audiovisual quality, and narration or subtitles. Data extracted included video characteristics (upload date, duration, views, likes, comments, and source). Two anesthesiologists independently assessed video quality using the Global Quality Scale (GQS, 1–5) and reliability using the modified DISCERN tool (1–5). Interobserver agreement was measured by intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Statistical analysis was performed with independent t-tests and chi-square tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe 50 videos had a cumulative 760,000 views, with a mean duration of 480 ± 360 s. Most were uploaded by anesthesiologists (70%), but only 40% originated from academic institutions. The mean GQS score was 3.70 ± 0.95, while the mean DISCERN score was 2.62 ± 1.22; only 18% of videos were classified as highly reliable. Interobserver agreement was excellent (ICC = 0.921 for GQS, ICC = 0.934 for DISCERN). Academic sources demonstrated significantly higher reliability scores compared to non-academic sources (p = 0.03). Risk and complication discussions were present in only 40% of videos, and long-term outcomes in 18%.ConclusionAlthough YouTube provides accessible content on anesthesia, overall video quality and reliability remain limited, with only 18% of videos classified as highly reliable. The majority of videos did not adequately address risks, complications, or long-term outcomes (40% and 18%, respectively). Academic and professional involvement is needed to enhance the reliability of online patient education materials, as current determinants of quality appear to be source credibility rather than viewing metrics.

Introduction

Anesthesia is one of the most important advancements in modern medicine, enabling the safe and comfortable performance of complex surgical procedures [1]. General, local, and regional anesthesia techniques are available and can be tailored according to the type of surgery and the patient’s comorbidities [2]. Although anesthesia presents a generally safe profile and has advanced significantly, it can still lead to misunderstandings for some patients. For this reason, patients often wish to learn about the potential complications of anesthesia and improve their level of knowledge [3].

In the digital age, patients can obtain information not only from physicians or healthcare professionals but also increasingly turn to the internet and various social media platforms [4]. With over two billion users, YouTube has become one of the platforms with the largest amount of health-related content [5]. The audiovisual video format makes YouTube particularly appealing for understanding medical procedures, including anesthesia [6]. However, despite all these advantages, the lack of expert evaluation or quality control mechanisms raises concerns within the academic medical community regarding the accuracy and reliability of the information provided [7]. Several studies in the field of medicine have pointed out this deficiency and investigated the quality of YouTube videos [8–10]. On the other hand, analyses of videos related to kidney stone treatment, lumbar puncture and neuraxial block techniques, and breast self-examination have shown that while many videos appear visually appealing and superficially informative, they often lack references, evidence-based recommendations, or balanced discussions on risks and alternatives, and therefore contain scientifically inadequate content [11–13]. These findings suggest that there are potential risks when patients rely on such video contents.

Importantly, there is currently no standardized framework, regulatory oversight, or certification system to ensure the accuracy, completeness, or clinical appropriateness of anesthesia-related content shared on online platforms such as YouTube. As a result, the quality of information largely depends on the content creator rather than adherence to established clinical guidelines or peer-reviewed standards.

Poor-quality or misleading anesthesia-related content may not only increase general anxiety but also negatively affect patients’ clinical understanding of perioperative risks, safety measures, and expected outcomes. Misinterpretation of such information may lead to unrealistic expectations, inappropriate risk perception, reduced trust in healthcare professionals, or delayed acceptance of necessary procedures. Therefore, the quality and reliability of online anesthesia-related educational content have direct implications for clinical communication and informed decision-making [3, 11, 12]. To the best of our knowledge, there are only a limited number of studies that have systematically evaluated the quality and reliability of anesthesia-related videos on YouTube [14].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the quality and reliability of YouTube videos on anesthesia in order to assess the current state of online patient education.

Methods

This study did not require ethical approval, as all YouTube videos are publicly accessible. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki regarding research using publicly available data.

On June 15, 2025, a systematic search was conducted on YouTube using the keywords “anesthesia,” “general anesthesia,” “regional anesthesia,” “spinal anesthesia,” and “epidural anesthesia.” To minimize personalization, the search was performed in English using incognito mode, without user login, and with a cleared browser history and cookies.

The videos were ranked by relevance, and the first 50 videos meeting the inclusion criteria were selected. The primary rationale for selecting the first 50 videos was to reflect real-world patient exposure, as users typically engage with only the first few pages of search results. Additionally, this approach is consistent with previous studies in other medical fields, which commonly analyze the top 50 videos to ensure methodological comparability [15, 16]. Individual video titles and URLs were not reported, as videos were anonymized to avoid potential ethical concerns related to the identification of content creators, despite the public availability of the material.

Inclusion and exclusion of videos

Inclusion criteria were English language, relevance to anesthesia, audio narration or subtitles, and adequate technical quality. Exclusion criteria were irrelevant content, non-English videos, videos without sound or with poor quality, and duplicates.

Evaluation process and data collection

The videos were evaluated by two independent anesthesiologists with more than 5 years of clinical experience (AAK, KY). Interobserver agreement between the two primary reviewers was excellent, and therefore, the involvement of a third reviewer was not routinely required. In rare cases of disagreement, discrepancies were resolved by consensus discussion. A third senior anesthesiologist (ODE) was available for adjudication if necessary but did not independently score the videos. The following data were extracted: upload date, video duration, number of views, likes, comments, and source of upload. The sources were categorized as academic healthcare professionals, non-academic healthcare professionals, patients, or commercial organizations. Academic healthcare professionals were defined as content creators affiliated with a university or academic institution, holding an academic title (professor, associate professor, or assistant professor/doctoral faculty member), or formally associated with a recognized medical society or academic organization.

Assessment of video quality and reliability

Video quality was assessed using the Global Quality Scale (GQS), a five-point rating system in which 1 indicated poor quality and 5 represented excellent quality suitable for patient education [17]. Reliability was evaluated with the modified DISCERN tool, which consists of five yes/no questions addressing clarity of aims, source reliability, balance, provision of references, and coverage of uncertainties. Scores of 4–5 were considered high reliability, 3–4 moderate reliability, and 1–2 low reliability. We used the modified 5-item DISCERN tool, which is a simplified version adapted from the original 16-item DISCERN instrument developed by Charnock et al. These cut-off values were selected in accordance with previously published studies using the modified DISCERN tool and reflect commonly adopted thresholds in the literature [18]. In addition to numerical scoring, raters followed a predefined qualitative framework based on the original definitions of the GQS and modified DISCERN tools. For GQS assessment, videos were evaluated according to overall educational value, clarity of explanations, logical flow, completeness of information, and suitability for patient education. For the modified DISCERN tool, “yes/no” decisions were based on the presence of clearly stated aims, balanced and unbiased information, identification of reliable sources, citation of references when available, and discussion of uncertainties or risks related to anesthesia. This structured approach ensured consistency and transparency in the raters’ assessments. Although DISCERN was originally developed for written health information, the modified 5-item DISCERN tool has been widely used in the evaluation of audiovisual medical content on YouTube [15, 16]. As it assesses content-based domains applicable to both written and video formats, it was considered appropriate for the present study.

General complications were defined as short-term or perioperative risks related to anesthesia, whereas long-term complications and outcomes were evaluated as a separate category.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. Normality was tested with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and range, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Interobserver agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values greater than 0.90 considered excellent. Group comparisons (academic vs. non-academic sources) were conducted using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. GQS descriptive statistics were recalculated based on individual video scores (1–5) after separating videos initially grouped as GQS 1–2. Correlation analyses were performed to assess the relationship between video popularity metrics (views and likes) and quality and reliability scores (GQS and DISCERN) using Spearman’s rank correlation test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 50 YouTube videos on anesthesia were analyzed. The mean number of views per video was 15,200 ± 19,300, with a cumulative total of 760,000 views. Video durations ranged from 60 to 1,500 s, with a mean duration of 480 ± 360 s. The average number of likes per video was 42 (range 0–280), and the average number of comments was 15 (range 0–120). Most videos (70%, 35/50) were uploaded by anesthesiologists, of whom 40% (20/50) were affiliated with academic institutions. Non-academic healthcare professionals accounted for 20% (10/50), while patients and commercial entities contributed 10% (5/50). Advertisements promoting clinics or products were identified in 6% (3/50) of videos. The most frequently discussed topics were anesthesia types (68%), preoperative preparation (62%), intraoperative monitoring (60%), and postoperative recovery (58%). Risks and short-term complications were discussed in 40% of videos, whereas long-term complications and outcomes were addressed in only 18%. The technical explanation of anesthesia induction and maintenance appeared in 52% of videos, while patient-focused reassurance content appeared in 36%. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between video popularity metrics and content quality and reliability. A weak positive correlation was observed between the number of views and GQS scores (r = 0.29, p = 0.041), as well as between likes and GQS scores (r = 0.33, p = 0.021).

In contrast, the correlations between views and DISCERN scores (r = 0.12, p = 0.38) and between likes and DISCERN scores (r = 0.15, p = 0.29) were weak and not statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study systematically evaluated the quality and reliability of YouTube videos related to anesthesia, which represents one of the most essential domains of modern medical practice. Our analysis of 50 videos revealed that although the platform provides widely accessible content with high viewing numbers, the overall scientific rigor and reliability of these materials remain limited. The mean Global Quality Scale (GQS) score was moderate 3.70 ± 0.95, with only about one-fifth of the videos reaching excellent quality standards. Similarly, the mean DISCERN score was 2.62 ± 1.22, indicating low overall reliability, with fewer than one in five videos considered highly reliable. Despite the majority of videos being uploaded by anesthesiologists (70%), only 40% were affiliated with academic institutions, suggesting that institutional oversight and evidence-based structuring of online educational content is still inadequate. The most frequently discussed themes included anesthesia types, preoperative preparation, intraoperative monitoring, and postoperative recovery, yet important issues such as risks, complications, and long-term outcomes were often underrepresented. Although our study did not directly assess patient demand, the high viewing numbers suggest public interest in accessible anesthesia-related information. Previous studies have shown that patients frequently use the Internet before anesthesia consultation, highlighting the need for patient-friendly educational resources [19, 20]. However, the quality of freely accessible content on social media remains insufficient.

In contrast, structured, peer-reviewed, and academically supervised educational approaches have been shown to provide significantly higher-quality and more reliable medical information. For instance, a recent study demonstrated that anesthesia education delivered through academically supervised and peer-reviewed frameworks offers clearer, more balanced, and evidence-based content compared with freely accessible online materials. This contrast further supports our findings and highlights the gap between the educational standards promoted in scientific journals and the insufficient quality observed in many anesthesia-related YouTube videos [21].

Similar findings have been reported in other medical fields evaluating YouTube as a source of health information. Studies in hypertension and surgical education have demonstrated that although videos are widely accessible and frequently viewed, their educational quality and reliability remain variable or limited, suggesting that the discrepancy between accessibility and informational quality represents a broader issue in online medical education rather than a problem specific to anesthesia alone [22, 23].

In their study on kidney stone treatment–related videos, the authors found that although many materials were visually appealing and frequently viewed, the majority lacked accurate referencing, balanced discussion of risks and alternatives, and evidence-based recommendations [11]. In their analysis of videos on lumbar puncture and neuraxial block techniques, investigators similarly reported that while procedural demonstrations were often present, the educational quality and reliability varied widely, with significant deficiencies in risk disclosure [12]. In their study evaluating breast self-examination videos, the authors found that although these videos generated high engagement, they frequently delivered oversimplified or even misleading information [13]. Taken together, these previous findings align with our current results, confirming that despite YouTube’s potential as a global educational tool, substantial limitations remain in the quality and reliability of content.

Specific to anesthesia, the lack of systematic evaluations in the literature makes our study particularly valuable. A limited number of studies have addressed anesthesia-related content [14]. In their analysis of regional anesthesia and pain management videos, the authors found that a considerable proportion of the content was inaccurate or incomplete, reinforcing concerns about potential patient misinterpretation [24]. Similarly, in their study of general perioperative educational videos, researchers reported that while some high-quality academic content existed, it was overwhelmed by videos of questionable reliability, often uploaded by non-academic professionals or commercial sources [14]. Our findings echo these observations, particularly regarding the influence of non-academic creators and commercial entities, which together accounted for approximately 16% of all analyzed videos.

A closer look at the thematic content of the videos in our study reveals several notable points. While anesthesia types, preoperative preparation, intraoperative monitoring, and postoperative recovery were discussed in the majority of videos, critical issues such as risks, complications, and long-term outcomes received much less attention. Although academic sources demonstrated higher reliability scores, the limited discussion of long-term complications was observed across both academic and non-academic videos, suggesting that this deficiency reflects a broader content-related limitation rather than being restricted to a specific source type. This imbalance reflects a broader trend described in previous analyses across other medical fields, where the authors found that creators tend to emphasize procedural steps or general information rather than nuanced discussion of potential complications or long-term prognosis. The underrepresentation of risk-related content raises a specific concern for anesthesia, where patient apprehension and misunderstanding are common. For example, in their study of patient perceptions regarding anesthesia, the authors found that anxiety about complications such as intraoperative awareness, postoperative nausea and vomiting, or cardiovascular instability was prevalent, underscoring the need for balanced and evidence-based online education [25]. Our study suggests that YouTube currently fails to meet this need adequately.

The strengths of the present study should be emphasized. First, we conducted a systematic search strategy with predefined keywords, minimizing selection bias and ensuring that the dataset was comparable to prior studies in other specialties that also restricted their analyses to the first 50 videos. Second, the evaluation was performed independently by two anesthesiologists with more than five years of clinical experience, and interobserver agreement was excellent, with ICC values above 0.90 for both GQS and DISCERN scores. This high level of reliability reinforces the robustness of our findings. Third, our study focused specifically on anesthesia, an area where systematic evaluations are scarce, thereby filling an important gap in the literature and providing a foundation for future research.

Our study had some limitations. Our analysis was restricted to the first 50 videos, a methodological choice that improves comparability but might not capture less prominent content that could still hold educational value. Moreover, only English-language videos were included, limiting the generalizability of our findings to non-English-speaking populations, which represent a significant proportion of YouTube’s global audience. Additionally, YouTube’s search algorithm is dynamic and subject to personalization, meaning that video rankings may change over time or differ between users. Although we attempted to minimize this by clearing browsing history and cookies, absolute standardization cannot be guaranteed. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design of the study, which provides a snapshot of content available at a single point in time but does not capture longitudinal changes, updates, or removal of videos. Additionally, although content sources were categorized as academic and non-academic healthcare professionals, no direct comparative analysis between these groups was performed, which may have limited the depth of subgroup-specific interpretations. While GQS and DISCERN are widely validated tools, they are inherently subjective and may not capture all dimensions of video quality, such as cultural appropriateness or accessibility for patients with low health literacy. Finally, although GQS and DISCERN are widely validated tools, their reliance on expert judgment may introduce a degree of subjectivity and may not fully capture aspects such as cultural appropriateness or accessibility for individuals with low health literacy.

From a clinical perspective, our findings have important implications. Patient education in anesthesia is crucial, as anxiety and misunderstanding are common and can significantly affect perioperative experiences and outcomes. High-quality online resources could serve as valuable adjuncts to traditional counseling, especially in settings where consultation time is limited. However, our results suggest that current YouTube content is insufficiently reliable to serve this purpose. The low reliability scores observed in our study highlight the need for greater involvement of academic institutions, professional societies, and healthcare providers in producing and disseminating accurate, evidence-based online educational materials.

Future research should aim to expand upon our findings in several directions. First, analyses should be conducted across multiple languages and cultural contexts to assess the global applicability of current online educational content. Second, longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate how video quality evolves over time and whether interventions, such as targeted uploads from academic societies, can improve overall standards. Finally, collaborations between medical professionals, anesthesiology associations, communication experts, and technology companies could help establish quality control mechanisms or certification labels for trustworthy health-related content.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that although anesthesia-related videos on YouTube are widely accessible and frequently viewed, their overall educational quality and reliability are generally moderate to low. Consistent with the observed Global Quality Scale and DISCERN scores, only a small proportion of videos met high-quality or high-reliability criteria, and essential topics such as risks, complications, and long-term outcomes were insufficiently addressed in the majority of content.

Furthermore, the weak correlations identified between engagement metrics and quality scores indicate that video popularity does not reliably reflect informational quality or reliability. These findings suggest that viewers may be exposed to content with substantial educational limitations despite high visibility.

While the present results do not allow causal inferences, they highlight an important opportunity for improvement. Greater involvement of academic and professional healthcare stakeholders in content development may help enhance the accuracy and educational value of anesthesia-related information available online. Addressing these gaps may support more informed patient expectations and improve the overall quality of perioperative information accessed through digital platforms.
