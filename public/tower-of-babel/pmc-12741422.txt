Graves’ disease with autoimmune hepatitis and primary Sclerosing cholangitis: an overlap syndrome
Authors: L K Pragathi, Dhruv Gandhi, Shivangi Tetarbe, Sudha Rao Chandrashekhar, Ira Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic inflammatory disease that results from autoantibody mediated hepatocyte injury. Given its immune-mediated mechanism, it is more likely to present with other autoimmune conditions, particularly involving thyroid gland. In patients presenting with both Graves’ disease and hepatitis, the diagnostic challenge is to determine the cause of elevated liver enzymes. We present an 11-year-old girl with acute liver cell failure and cholestasis in a setting of untreated hyperthyroidism. She was diagnosed to have Graves’ disease with AIH and primary sclerosing cholangitis. She underwent radioactive iodine ablation of thyroid gland and is currently responding well on steroid, azathioprine, and thyroid replacement therapy.

Introduction

Graves’ disease is an autoimmune disorder characterized by autoantibodies against the thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)-receptor. Hyperthyroidism from Graves’ disease is a high turnover state which can result in elevated liver enzymes [1]. Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic liver disease with elevated liver enzymes, hypergammaglobulinemia, and unique histological features [2]. Although the exact relationship between the two is unclear, AIH and Graves’ disease are both immune-mediated. The liver requires adequate amounts of thyroid hormones to execute its metabolic functions optimally [3]. A recent meta-analysis by Scappaticcio et al. [4] found that 55–60% of patients with untreated hyperthyroidism had at least one abnormal liver function value. The diagnosis of AIH may be delayed in cases where the initial liver function abnormalities are attributed to hyperthyroidism. Hence, a high index of suspicion is required to diagnose concomitant AIH. Patients with Graves’ disease, after initiating treatment, will show improvements in liver enzyme levels. Thus, if the liver profile worsens despite stable thyroid function, an alternative cause of elevated liver enzymes should be sought [1, 5]. We present an 11-year-old girl with liver dysfunction in concomitant Graves’ disease and AIH-primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) overlap and highlight the diagnostic challenges in such cases.

Case

An 11-year-old girl presented in April 2023 with fever, malaise, and jaundice for 15 days along with vomiting and diarrhea for 3 days, and clay stools with itching for 1 day. The child was 2nd in birth order and born of a non-consanguineously marriage. She initially developed irritability, palpitations, anxiety, and weight loss in March 2022 and was evaluated for the same at another centre in December 2022. Her free T3, free T4 and TSH levels were 16.9 pg/mL, 7.6 ng/dL and < 0.005mIU/L, respectively. She was diagnosed with hyperthyroidism but received no treatment. On presentation in April 2023, she had tachycardia (150/min), respiratory rate of 20/min, and blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg. On examination, she had jaundice, pallor, palmar erythema, hepatosplenomegaly, and ascites. There was no goitre, exophthalmos or tremors. Other general and systemic examinations were normal. Investigations are shown in Table 1. Hepatitis-A IgM, Hepatitis-E IgM, Hepatitis-B surface antigen, and anti-Hepatitis-C ELISA were negative. Blood culture did not grow any organism. Slit lamp examination did not show Kayser-Fleischer ring. Abdominal ultrasound showed hepatomegaly with coarse echotexture, splenomegaly, and the absence of porto-systemic collaterals. Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) by immunofluorescence was positive (1:320). Anti-liver-kidney-microsomal, anti-smooth muscle antibody, anti-soluble liver antigen, and anti-microsomal antibody was negative. Serum IgG level was 1308 mg/dl (Normal:595-1300 mg/dl). Serum IgA was 229 mg/dl (Normal:58-358 mg/dl) and tissue transglutaminase IgA was 0.235 IU/L (Normal:<8 IU/L). Trans-jugular liver biopsy showed interface hepatitis with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate in portal and periportal tracts with Metavir-F3 fibrosis, suggestive of AIH(Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) showed slit-like common bile duct (CBD) with proximal and mid-CBD narrowing. Narrowing was also seen at confluence and proximal right and left common hepatic ducts. Irregularity and focal short-segment strictures in segmental ducts without intrahepatic biliary duct dilation were seen(Fig. 2). Thyroid ultrasound showed a bilaterally enlarged thyroid gland with coarse echotexture and increased vascularity. TSH-receptor antibody was 3.50 IU/L (Normal:<1.75 IU/L), anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody was 345.77 IU/mL (Normal:<5.61 IU/mL), and anti-thyroglobulin antibody was 76.05 IU/mL (Normal:<4.11 IU/mL). Radioiodine uptake scan showed an increased uptake in thyroid gland, with a 2-hour uptake of 18.4% and a 24-hour uptake of 65.30%. Echocardiogram was normal. The child was thus diagnosed with AIH-PSC overlap syndrome with Graves’ disease. She was started on oral propranolol (1 mg/kg/day), cholestyramine (8 g/day), prednisolone (60 mg/day), azathioprine (1 mg/kg/day) and iodine-deficient diet. She underwent radiofrequency ablation with I131 at 9.1 mCi. At the 6-week follow-up, she was anicteric, clinically improving and liver function was recovering. Prednisolone was tapered and continued at 5 mg/day and azathioprine was optimised to 2.5 mg/kg/day. She was hypothyroid by 10 weeks of radiofrequency ablation and is currently on thyroid replacement therapy.

Discussion

Hepatic dysfunction in Graves’ hyperthyroidism is common, with the possible causes being elevated thyroid hormones, side-effects of the anti-thyroid drugs or associated autoimmune liver diseases [6]. Several direct and indirect mechanisms can cause liver dysfunction in hyperthyroidism. The hypermetabolic state in hyperthyroidism can cause hepatocyte anoxia and liver cell degeneration due to protein and glycogen decomposition, free radicle injury or congestive hepatopathy. Other causes include underlying liver disease, thyrotoxic heart failure or antithyroid medication related liver injury [3]. Our patient presented with cholestasis and synthetic dysfunction in the setting of untreated hyperthyroidism. The use of the antithyroid medications, especially thioamide group of drugs (propylthiouracil, methimazole, and carbimazole), in a hyperthyroid patient has been reported to cause hepatic injury [6]. Drug-induced liver injury was unlikely as our patient was never treated for hyperthyroidism. Though our patient had tachycardia at presentation, echocardiogram was normal, thus, thyrotoxic heart failure worsening the liver function was unlikely.

Hyperthyroidism-induced liver injury causes necrosis and inflammation in the centrilobular area [2]. However, in our patient, the liver biopsy showed portal inflammation with interface hepatitis, characteristic of AIH. Hence, liver biopsy is useful to diagnose AIH and rule out alternate causes [7]. Concomitant AIH was diagnosed in our patient based on high titres of ANA(1:320), high serum IgG, interface hepatitis with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates on liver histology, absence of viral hepatitis with a score of 7 as per international autoimmune hepatitis group [8]. In view of disproportionate increase in cholestasis on day 4 and histology suggestive of occasional foci of intrahepatic duct injury, MRCP was done which was suggestive of PSC. Hence, diagnosis of AIH-PSC overlap syndrome was concluded.

Grave’s disease with AIH is known to have a variable clinical course ranging from attaining remission with normalization of both thyroid and liver function to recurring episodes of thyrotoxicosis with hepatitis in remission [5, 9]. In addition, despite the remission of Graves’ disease, liver dysfunction may progress to the stage of requiring transplant [1]. Our patient was not started on thioamides in view of severe liver dysfunction and was subjected to I131 radiofrequency ablation. Immunosuppression with steroids and azathioprine were started simultaneously as she had AIH-PSC overlap. At 6-week and 12-week follow-up, liver enzymes and bilirubin levels improved. She was hypothyroid at 10-week follow-up and is currently on levothyroxine (25mcg/day). This case highlights the diagnostic challenges in approaching hepatic dysfunction and cholestasis in Graves’ disease.

The association between Grave’s disease and PSC has been documented by just a handful of case reports in current literature [10, 11]. While the exact mechanism behind this association remains unknown, it has been speculated that the involvement of certain common genes such as HLA-B8 and HLA-DR3 may be responsible for the development of both diseases in a genetically predisposed individual [11].
