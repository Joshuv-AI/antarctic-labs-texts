Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis complicating typhoid fever: A case report from Senegal
Authors: Mourno Tarsa Nousradine, Omar Boun Khatab Diouf, Michel Assane Ndour, Abou Koundio, Mouhamed Dieng, Melissa D’Erneville, Stive Denilson Padounendji, Abel Ndongar, Khadim Mbow, Abilhabibi Ahamadi, Anna Sarr, Maimouna Ndour Mbaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) triggered by confirmed typhoid fever (Salmonella Typhi) in a 23-year-old Senegalese man. The initial radiological presentation suggested ileo-caecal tuberculosis. Thus, persistent fever, marked hyperferritinaemia and systemic inflammation raised suspicion of HLH, confirmed by bone marrow examination demonstrating hemophagocytosis. The H-Score was 206, corresponding to 88–93% probability. Blood cultures isolated ciprofloxacin-susceptible Salmonella Typhi. Fluoroquinolone monotherapy led to complete clinical and biological remission without the need for immunosuppression. This case underscores the importance of including typhoid fever in the differential aetiology of HLH in endemic settings, and of early H-Score calculation to expedite diagnosis.

Introduction

Haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome driven by uncontrolled activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and macrophages, culminating in a cytokine storm and potential multi-organ failure [1], [2]. Secondary HLH most commonly arises in the context of viral infections, principally Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), but bacterial, parasitic, and fungal triggers are well recognised [1]. Enteric fever, caused by Salmonella Typhi or S. Paratyphi, remains a major public health burden in low- and middle-income countries, with complications occurring in 10–15% of cases [3]. The co-occurrence of HLH and typhoid fever is exceptionally rare: a 2024 systematic review identified only 53 reported cases worldwide, with a single case documented in Africa [4]. We present a case from Senegal in which this association was initially obscured by an imaging phenotype mimicking ileo-caecal tuberculosis, and in which aetiological antibiotic therapy alone achieved full remission.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old Senegalese man, employed as a minibus driver's apprentice, was admitted with a two-week history of high-grade fever with rigors, profuse night sweats, non-bloody watery diarrhoea with mucus, post-prandial vomiting, headache, insomnia, marked asthenia, anorexia, and a 10 kg weight loss. He had no history of tuberculosis contact, alcohol or tobacco use, or herbal remedy intake. On examination, oral temperature was 40°C, all other vital signs were within normal limits. Abdominal palpation revealed hepatomegaly and diffuse guarding with point tenderness in the right iliac fossa. There were no lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, jaundice, skin lesions, or overt haemorrhage.

Given the persistence of fever despite paracetamol, metamizole, along with empirical ceftriaxone treatment, therapy, secondary HLH was suspected. Ferritin, LDH, and fibrinogen levels confirmed a state of systemic hyperinflammation. Bone marrow aspirate revealed a normocellular marrow (grade III) with a predominant granulocytic lineage, near-absent erythropoiesis, rare megakaryocytes, and multiple macrophages displaying active haemophagocytosis of red cells, leucocytes, and platelets (Fig. 2). Aetiological workup was systematic. Thick blood film, Quantitative Buffy Coat (QBC) for haemoparasites and Borrelia spp., HIV serology, HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HCV, anti-HAV IgM, stool microscopy (KAOP method), and stool cultures were all negative. Three sets of blood cultures isolated Salmonella Typhi, susceptible to ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone (MIC not reported).

The patient was treated with oral ciprofloxacin for seven days. Clinical response was rapid: fever resolved within 72 h, gastrointestinal symptoms abated, and repeat blood tests at day 5 demonstrated normalisation of inflammatory markers, transaminases, and blood count (Table 1). No immunosuppressive therapy was required.

Discussion

Enteric fevers (typhoid and paratyphoid fever) are common febrile illnesses in low-resource settings. Their incidence remains high in Africa, with substantial associated morbidity and mortality [3], [5], [6]. Salmonella Typhi initially invades the gastrointestinal tract before progressing to systemic infection [7]. Severe forms occur in 10–15% of cases and include complications such as myocarditis, intestinal perforation, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and typhoid encephalopathy [8]. Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by uncontrolled cytokine release leading to multiorgan failure [1], [9]. Infectious triggers are most commonly associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection [9]. Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical and laboratory criteria. The HLH-2004 score, initially developed in pediatrics, is often difficult to apply in resource-limited settings due to the unavailability of key parameters such as soluble IL-2 receptor levels and natural killer (NK) cell activity. The H-score, a more recent tool, provides a probabilistic diagnostic approach with a threshold of 169 points [10], [11], [12].

The association between typhoid fever and HLH remains rare. A 2024 systematic review identified 53 reported cases in the literature, with only one case from Africa [4]. The mean patient age was 18 years, and diagnosis was most frequently established during the third week of illness. Ceftriaxone was the most commonly used antibiotic. Given the rarity of this association, no specific therapeutic guidelines currently exist.

In certain infectious contexts such as tuberculosis, leishmaniasis, and rickettsial diseases, etiological treatment alone may be sufficient [13]. However, in the aforementioned review, nearly 50% of patients also received immunosuppressive therapy [4]. The overall mortality rate was 9.4%, with most deaths occurring among patients who required immunosuppression. Our patient achieved full remission with a seven-day course of ciprofloxacin, without recourse to immunosuppression, consistent with the favourable outcomes reported when aetiological therapy is initiated promptly.

In the absence of evidence supporting tuberculosis and with confirmed Salmonella Typhi infection, imaging findings were considered likely related to typhoid ileo-caecal involvement. Typhoid fever can involve the ileocaecal region due to lymphoid tissue involvement and may mimic intestinal tuberculosis, especially in endemic areas [14], [15], [16], [17]. Follow-up imaging was not performed due to clinical and biological improvement.

Conclusion

This case underscores that typhoid fever, although a rare trigger, should be considered in the aetiological workup of HLH in endemic settings particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where both infections are prevalent. Early H-Score calculation enables timely diagnosis without requiring unavailable assays. Prompt initiation of fluoroquinolone therapy, guided by antibiogram, may achieve complete remission without immunosuppression when HLH is recognised early. Clinicians should also be aware of the potential imaging overlap between typhoid ileocolitis and ileo-caecal tuberculosis, which may delay the correct diagnostic pathway.
