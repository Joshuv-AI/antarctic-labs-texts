Histopathologic clues to mismatch repair deficiency in colon adenocarcinoma: the role of mucinous component and intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response
Authors: Ömer Atmış, Sema Ocak, Fatma Seher Pehli̇van, Hanife Seda Mavi̇li̇, Ayça Tan, Semin Ayhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeficient mismatch repair (dMMR) identifies a biologically distinct subset of colon adenocarcinomas with important diagnostic, hereditary, prognostic, and therapeutic implications. This study investigated whether mucinous component and immune response pattern may serve as practical histopathologic clues to dMMR in routine pathology practice.MethodsThis retrospective study included 222 consecutive colon adenocarcinoma resection cases with available mismatch repair (MMR) immunohistochemistry from 2022 to 2025. Clinicopathologic, histomorphologic, and immune response features were evaluated and compared according to MMR status. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with dMMR.ResultsOf 222 tumors, 29 (13.1%) were dMMR and 193 (86.9%) were proficient MMR. dMMR was significantly associated with age < 50 years (40.9% vs. 10.0%, p < 0.001), high grade (25.8% vs. 8.5%, p = 0.010), absence of lymph node metastasis (18.8% vs. 6.7%, p = 0.007), absence of perineural invasion (21.6% vs. 6.4%, p = 0.001), larger tumor size (p < 0.001), fewer metastatic lymph nodes (p = 0.038), and higher lymph node yield (p = 0.019). Intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response was strongly associated with dMMR (33.9% vs. 6.1%, p < 0.001). Tumors with a mucinous component showed a higher dMMR rate than those without (26.2% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.005). All associations that were significant in the unadjusted analyses remained significant after false discovery rate correction (q < 0.05). In the combined histopathologic clue analysis, the dMMR rate increased from 2.3% in tumors with no clue to 28.2% with one clue and 40.0% with both clues. In multivariable analysis, age < 50 years (OR 7.212, p = 0.002), intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response (OR 7.089, p < 0.001), and mucinous component (OR 6.067, p = 0.001) remained independently associated with dMMR.ConclusionsColon adenocarcinomas with a mucinous component and an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response are enriched for dMMR, whereas tumors lacking both features show a low dMMR rate. These findings provide a detailed histopathologic characterization of a dMMR-enriched phenotype but should not be used to select cases for MMR testing or replace universal testing.Keywords: Colon adenocarcinoma, Mismatch repair deficiency, DNA mismatch repair, Mucinous component, Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, Crohn-like lymphoid reaction, Histopathologic clues, Immunohistochemistry

Background

Colorectal cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide, and colon adenocarcinoma constitutes the predominant histologic type. Among its molecular subgroups, mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) represents a distinct biologic subset with important diagnostic, prognostic, hereditary, and therapeutic implications [1, 2]. dMMR/microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) colorectal carcinomas account for approximately 15% of colorectal cancers, and while most are sporadic, a clinically important proportion is associated with Lynch syndrome [1, 2]. In current practice, the assessment of mismatch repair (MMR) status has gained even greater relevance because universal tumor screening facilitates the identification of patients at risk for Lynch syndrome, while dMMR status also predicts sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade [3].

Mismatch repair-deficient colorectal carcinomas have long been recognized to display characteristic clinicopathologic features. These tumors are often associated with mucinous or medullary morphology, prominent tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and Crohn-like lymphoid reaction, reflecting their immunogenic tumor microenvironment [4–6]. The intense immune infiltration seen in dMMR tumors is thought to be driven by high mutational burden and neoantigen load, which may also underlie their distinct response to immunotherapy [7, 8]. In routine surgical pathology practice, these findings are recognized components of the histopathologic phenotype associated with dMMR [4, 9].

Despite these recognized associations, their distribution and strength remain incompletely characterized in routine colon adenocarcinoma cohorts, particularly when subtler findings such as a partial mucinous component and distinct immune-response patterns are evaluated together. Recent work has suggested that not only classic mucinous adenocarcinoma but also tumors with lower proportions of mucin may show a distinct biologic and immune profile in relation to MMR status [10, 11]. In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the relationship between MMR status and clinicopathologic, histomorphologic, and immune response features in colon adenocarcinoma, with particular emphasis on whether mucinous component and immune response pattern may provide histopathologic clues to mismatch repair deficiency.

Methods

Study design and case selection

This retrospective study included consecutive resection cases diagnosed as colon adenocarcinoma in the Department of Pathology, Manisa Celal Bayar University Faculty of Medicine, between 2022 and 2025. During the study period, mismatch repair (MMR) immunohistochemistry was performed routinely in all colon adenocarcinoma cases in our institution. Accordingly, all eligible cases within the study period were included without selective case sampling. Patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy were excluded. Rectal tumors, cases with multiple synchronous tumors, and cases lacking adequate archival material for review were not included in the study. A total of 222 cases with available MMR immunohistochemical results were included.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Manisa Celal Bayar University Faculty of Medicine Health Sciences Ethics Committee (decision date: 13.05.2026, decision no: 20.478.486/4129).

Histopathologic evaluation and data collection

Clinicopathologic data were obtained from pathology reports, archival records, and review of archived slides. The original cohort-level histopathologic assessments, including immune-response pattern and the presence and extent of mucinous differentiation, were performed during routine diagnostic evaluation by one of two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists (Ö.A. or S.A.) before MMR immunohistochemistry was performed and before MMR status was available. Therefore, these histopathologic assessments were conducted blinded to MMR status. Diagnostically challenging cases were jointly reviewed by the two pathologists, and the final classification was reached by consensus during routine reporting. The variables evaluated included age, sex, tumor location, histologic type, tumor grade, immune response pattern, tumor budding, lymph node metastasis, tumor deposit, extramural venous invasion (EMVI), intramural venous invasion (IMVI), lymphovascular invasion (LVI), perineural invasion (PNI), pT stage, pN stage, serosal involvement, tumor size, number of metastatic lymph nodes, and total number of retrieved lymph nodes.

Tumor location was grouped as right colon or left colon. Histologic tumor types included adenocarcinoma, not otherwise specified (NOS), mucinous adenocarcinoma, medullary carcinoma, serrated adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, micropapillary adenocarcinoma, and goblet cell adenocarcinoma. Tumor grade was categorized as low-grade or high-grade.

During routine histopathologic assessment, all available tumor-bearing hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections were evaluated. The number of tumor-bearing sections varied among cases according to tumor size and routine sampling and was not recorded as a separate study variable; however, all available tumor-bearing sections were evaluated in every case. The slides were initially surveyed at low magnification, and areas showing the densest lymphocytic infiltration were selected for high-power assessment. The immune response was classified into four mutually exclusive categories according to the predominant pattern across the available tumor sections. The predominant pattern was defined as the immune-response pattern showing the greatest overall extent across all available tumor-bearing sections; diagnostically equivocal cases were resolved by consensus during routine reporting. Cases with no appreciable lymphocytic reaction were classified as absent. Peritumoral immune response was defined as lymphocytic inflammation mainly confined to the invasive front or peritumoral stroma without prominent infiltration of tumor glands. Intratumoral immune response was defined as lymphocytes infiltrating the tumor epithelium or closely permeating tumor glands and was considered present when more than 50 lymphocytes were identified within a single high-power field (×400) in the most densely infiltrated intratumoral area. Crohn-like immune response was defined as an intense nodular lymphoid reaction composed of multiple conspicuous lymphoid aggregates or follicles located at or beyond the advancing tumor front, predominantly within the muscularis propria or pericolic adipose tissue. Germinal-center formation was frequently present but was not required. A single small isolated lymphoid aggregate or diffuse/band-like peritumoral lymphocytic inflammation without a prominent nodular or follicular pattern was not considered sufficient. The assessment was pattern-based rather than count-based, and no formal aggregate-number, diameter, or density threshold was applied. When this characteristic pattern was identified, even focally, in any of the available tumor-bearing sections, Crohn-like reaction was recorded as present in the separate non-mutually exclusive binary assessment [6, 12]. CD3 or CD8 immunohistochemistry was not performed, as the assessment was based on routinely available hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections. For the primary analysis, cases showing both intratumoral and Crohn-like responses were categorized according to the predominant pattern. Immune response was additionally dichotomized as an immune-active pattern, including intratumoral and Crohn-like responses, versus a non-immune-active pattern, including absent and peritumoral responses. For sensitivity analyses, intratumoral and Crohn-like immune responses were also recorded as separate, non-mutually exclusive binary variables. Consequently, tumors showing both patterns were included in both corresponding categories in these analyses.

Mucinous differentiation was evaluated on all available tumor-bearing hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections. Any unequivocal extracellular mucinous component within the tumor was recorded as present, without applying a minimum percentage threshold. The extent of the mucinous component was estimated semiquantitatively as a percentage of the total tumor area. Tumors with more than 50% extracellular mucin were classified as mucinous adenocarcinoma, whereas tumors with a mucinous component below this threshold were recorded as adenocarcinoma with a mucinous component. For the main analysis, mucinous differentiation was assessed as present or absent. In the subgroup analysis restricted to adenocarcinoma, NOS and mucinous adenocarcinoma cases, tumors were categorized as having no mucinous component (0%), a partial mucinous component below the threshold for mucinous adenocarcinoma (operationally reported as the 1%–49% category), or mucinous adenocarcinoma (> 50%). The lower boundary of 1% was used to distinguish a non-zero mucinous component from complete absence and was not evaluated as a separate biological or diagnostic cutoff.

Tumor budding was classified as low (0–4 buds) or high (≥ 5 buds). However, tumor budding was not assessed in mucinous and signet ring cell carcinomas, in keeping with current recommendations; therefore, budding data were unavailable for these cases.

Interobserver reproducibility assessment

To assess interobserver reproducibility, 50 cases with available archival slides were selected, giving preference to recent cases while ensuring representation of the predefined mucinous-component and immune-response categories. MMR status was not considered during case selection. All available tumor-bearing hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections from each selected case were independently re-evaluated by two pathologists (Ö.A. and S.O.). Both observers were blinded to MMR status, the original histopathologic classifications, and each other’s assessments. Using the predefined criteria described above, each observer independently estimated the percentage of extracellular mucin and recorded the predominant immune-response pattern, the presence or absence of intratumoral immune response, and the presence or absence of Crohn-like lymphoid reaction. Agreement was assessed using the independently recorded evaluations. Because the reassessment was performed solely to quantify interobserver reproducibility and did not replace the original cohort-level data, discrepant observer ratings were not adjudicated into a new consensus classification.

Immunohistochemical assessment of mismatch repair status

Mismatch repair status was assessed by immunohistochemistry for MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6, as performed during routine diagnostic work-up at the time of the original diagnosis. All sections were stained on a fully automated Ventana Benchmark XT IHC/ISH platform. The antibodies used were anti-human MLH1 (clone M1, Ventana), anti-human PMS2 (clone A16-4, Ventana), anti-human MSH2 (clone G219-1129, Ventana), and anti-human MSH6 (clone SP93, Ventana), either in concentrated or prediluted forms. Stromal and inflammatory cells were accepted as internal positive controls, while normal colon tissue served as an external control. Nuclear staining in tumor cells was evaluated only when appropriate internal control staining was present. Complete loss of nuclear expression of one or more MMR proteins in tumor cells, with preserved staining in internal control cells, was interpreted as deficient MMR (dMMR). Retained nuclear expression of all four proteins was interpreted as proficient MMR (pMMR). The MMR immunohistochemical results had been evaluated during routine diagnostic reporting by one or both of two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists (Ö.A. and S.A.); when both pathologists participated, the final interpretation was reached by consensus. These results were retrieved from archival pathology records for the present study. For dMMR tumors, the expression status of each individual MMR protein was additionally retrieved from the original diagnostic reports. MMR protein-loss patterns were categorized as concurrent MLH1/PMS2 loss, concurrent MSH2/MSH6 loss, isolated PMS2 loss, isolated MSH6 loss, or an atypical loss pattern. Electronic medical records were also reviewed for available BRAF V600E and MLH1 promoter methylation results in tumors showing MLH1/PMS2 loss.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed by visual and analytical methods. Variables showing near-normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as number and percentage. For categorical analyses, age was dichotomized at 50 years as a clinically relevant threshold for younger-onset colorectal cancer.

Comparisons between the pMMR and dMMR groups were made using appropriate statistical tests according to the type and distribution of the variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. As a sensitivity analysis, intratumoral and Crohn-like immune responses were examined separately as non-mutually exclusive binary variables. Their associations with MMR status were evaluated using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests because of small expected cell counts. Within the dMMR subgroup, selected clinicopathologic characteristics were compared between tumors with MLH1/PMS2 loss and those with other MMR protein-loss patterns. Because of the small subgroup sizes, Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical comparisons, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for patient age. These subgroup analyses were considered exploratory. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on distributional characteristics. A combined histopathologic clue analysis was also performed to descriptively examine how dMMR frequency varied according to the presence of the two main morphologic findings. For this analysis, one point was assigned for the presence of a mucinous component and one point for the presence of an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune-active pattern. Cases were then grouped according to the number of histopathologic clues present as 0, 1, or 2. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated for individual and combined histopathologic clues. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals for dMMR proportions and performance measures were calculated using the Wilson score method. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify factors independently associated with dMMR status. Variables entered into the model were age group, tumor grade, immune response group, and presence of mucinous component. Model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke R² and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Interobserver reproducibility was evaluated using observed percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Unweighted Cohen’s kappa was used for binary variables and the nominal four-category predominant immune-response pattern. Linearly weighted Cohen’s kappa was used for the ordered three-category mucin classification (0%, 1%–49%, and > 50%). The combined immune-active classification was derived separately for each observer from the presence of either intratumoral or Crohn-like immune response.

To account for multiple testing in the principal univariable comparisons between pMMR and dMMR tumors, the 13 p values presented in Table 1 were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Both unadjusted p values and FDR-adjusted p values (q values) are reported, and q < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The correction was limited to the principal comparisons presented in Table 1; the focused subgroup and multivariable analyses were evaluated separately.

Results

Clinicopathologic characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 222 colon adenocarcinoma cases were included in the study. Of these, 193 (86.9%) were classified as pMMR and 29 (13.1%) as dMMR. The cohort comprised 131 men (59.0%) and 91 women (41.0%), with a mean age of 66.14 ± 12.66 years. Overall, 22 patients (9.9%) were younger than 50 years, whereas 200 (90.1%) were aged 50 years or older. The most common histologic subtype was adenocarcinoma, NOS (84.2%), followed by mucinous adenocarcinoma (7.7%). Additional clinicopathologic characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 2.

MMR protein-loss patterns

Among the 29 dMMR tumors, concurrent MLH1/PMS2 loss was identified in 21 cases (72.4%), concurrent MSH2/MSH6 loss in 2 cases (6.9%), and isolated PMS2 loss in 4 cases (13.8%). Two tumors (6.9%) demonstrated an atypical pattern consisting of isolated MSH2 loss with retained MSH6 expression. No isolated MSH6 loss or heterogeneous loss pattern was identified. BRAF V600E and MLH1 promoter methylation results were not available in the institutional medical records. In the exploratory analysis restricted to dMMR tumors, MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumors were more frequently located in the right colon than tumors with other loss patterns (71.4% vs. 25.0%; Fisher’s exact p = 0.038). Median age was 65 years (IQR, 27) in the MLH1/PMS2-loss group and 53 years (IQR, 31) in the other dMMR group, with no statistically significant difference (Mann–Whitney U = 73.0, p = 0.591). No significant differences were identified in sex (p = 0.697), age group (p = 0.209), tumor grade (p = 0.176), or the presence of a mucinous component (33.3% vs. 50.0%; p = 0.433). An intratumoral/Crohn-like immune-active pattern was numerically more frequent in MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumors than in tumors with other loss patterns (76.2% vs. 37.5%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.083). These comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of the small subgroup sizes. The findings are summarized in Table 3.

Comparison of clinicopathologic features according to MMR status

When the pMMR and dMMR groups were compared, several significant differences emerged. The proportion of dMMR tumors was higher in patients younger than 50 years than in those aged 50 years or older (40.9% vs. 10.0%, p < 0.001). dMMR was also more frequent in poorly differentiated tumors than in low-grade tumors (25.8% vs. 8.5%, p = 0.010), in cases without lymph node metastasis compared with those with nodal metastasis (18.8% vs. 6.7%, p = 0.007), and in tumors without perineural invasion compared with those showing perineural invasion (21.6% vs. 6.4%, p = 0.001). In addition, pN stage was significantly associated with MMR status (p = 0.016). The clinicopathologic features compared according to MMR status are summarized in Table 1.

By contrast, tumor location was not significantly associated with MMR status, although dMMR tumors were numerically more frequent in the right colon than in the left colon (17.5% vs. 9.8%, p = 0.091). No significant differences were identified between pMMR and dMMR tumors with respect to tumor deposit, extramural venous invasion, intramural venous invasion, lymphovascular invasion, tumor budding group, serosal involvement, or pT stage.

A marked difference was observed in immune response patterns between the two groups (p < 0.001). Intratumoral immune response was particularly enriched in the dMMR group: 11 of 20 tumors with an intratumoral immune response (55.0%) were dMMR, compared with only 8.3% of tumors with no immune response and 5.7% of those with a peritumoral response. Among tumors with a Crohn-like response, 8 of 36 cases (22.2%) were dMMR. When immune response was dichotomized, dMMR was significantly more common in tumors showing an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune-active pattern than in those with an absent/peritumoral pattern (33.9% vs. 6.1%, p < 0.001). In the non-mutually exclusive sensitivity analysis, intratumoral immune response was present in 24 of 220 tumors (10.9%), Crohn-like lymphoid reaction in 36 (16.4%), and both patterns in four tumors (1.8%). The proportion of dMMR tumors was significantly higher among tumors with an intratumoral immune response than among those without this response (13/24, 54.2% [95% CI: 35.1–72.1%] vs. 16/196, 8.2% [95% CI: 5.1–12.8%]; two-sided Fisher’s exact p < 0.001). Crohn-like lymphoid reaction was associated with a numerically higher dMMR rate, although the difference was not statistically significant (8/36, 22.2% [95% CI: 11.7–38.1%] vs. 21/184, 11.4% [95% CI: 7.6–16.8%]; two-sided Fisher’s exact p = 0.103).

Mucinous differentiation was also associated with MMR status. Tumors containing a mucinous component showed a significantly higher rate of dMMR than tumors without a mucinous component (26.2% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.005).

Continuous variables according to MMR status

Among continuous variables, patient age did not differ significantly between the pMMR and dMMR groups when analyzed as a continuous measure (median 68 years [IQR: 17] vs. 63 years [IQR: 29], p = 0.076). However, dMMR tumors were significantly larger than pMMR tumors (median 6.5 cm [IQR: 3.8] vs. 4.5 cm [IQR: 2.2], p < 0.001). The number of metastatic lymph nodes was lower in the dMMR group (median 0 [IQR: 0]) than in the pMMR group (median 1 [IQR: 3], p = 0.038), whereas the total number of retrieved lymph nodes was higher in dMMR cases (median 22 [IQR: 13] vs. 16 [IQR: 10], p = 0.019). Tumor budding count did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.137). After Benjamini–Hochberg correction for the 13 comparisons presented in Table 1, all associations that were significant in the unadjusted analyses remained statistically significant at an FDR threshold of q < 0.05. The highest adjusted value among these associations was observed for the number of metastatic lymph nodes (q = 0.049).

Subgroup analysis of mucinous component

To reduce the potential confounding effect of histologic subtype, a subgroup analysis was performed including only adenocarcinoma, NOS and mucinous adenocarcinoma cases. In this analysis, the presence of a mucinous component remained significantly associated with dMMR status. dMMR was observed in 26.8% of tumors with a mucinous component, compared with 7.4% of tumors without mucinous differentiation (p = 0.001). Moreover, the distribution of MMR status differed significantly according to the extent of mucinous component (p = 0.002). The proportion of dMMR was 7.4% (95% CI: 4.3–12.4%) in tumors with no mucinous component, 25.0% (95% CI: 12.0–44.9%) in tumors with a 1%–49% mucinous component, and 29.4% (95% CI: 13.3–53.1%) in tumors with a > 50% mucinous component. Notably, the dMMR rate was increased not only in mucinous adenocarcinomas with > 50% mucinous component, but also in tumors with a partial mucinous component of 1%–49%. Interobserver reproducibility

In the 50-case reproducibility subset, agreement for the presence or absence of mucinous differentiation was 96.0% (48/50; κ = 0.918). Agreement for the ordered mucin categories was 94.0% (47/50; linearly weighted κ = 0.919). Agreement for the four-category predominant immune-response pattern was 84.0% (42/50; κ = 0.771). For the separately recorded binary immune-response variables, agreement was 86.0% for intratumoral immune response (43/50; κ = 0.720) and 92.0% for Crohn-like lymphoid reaction (46/50; κ = 0.834). Agreement for the combined immune-active classification was 92.0% (46/50; κ = 0.811).

Combined histopathologic clue analysis

A combined histopathologic clue analysis was performed using the presence of a mucinous component and an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune-active pattern as the two main histopathologic clues. Among 220 cases with available data for both parameters, the dMMR rate increased progressively according to the number of clues present. dMMR was identified in 3 of 132 tumors with no clue (2.3%; 95% CI: 0.8–6.5%), 22 of 78 tumors with one clue (28.2%; 95% CI: 19.4–39.0%), and 4 of 10 tumors with both clues (40.0%; 95% CI: 16.8–68.7%) (p < 0.001). The linear-by-linear association was also significant (p < 0.001), supporting a stepwise increase in dMMR frequency with increasing number of histopathologic clues. In the exploratory descriptive analysis of performance characteristics, the presence of a mucinous component showed a sensitivity of 37.9%, specificity of 83.9%, positive predictive value of 26.2%, and negative predictive value of 90.0% for dMMR. The intratumoral/Crohn-like immune-active pattern showed a sensitivity of 65.5%, specificity of 80.6%, positive predictive value of 33.9%, and negative predictive value of 93.9%. The presence of at least one of these two clues showed high sensitivity (89.7%) and negative predictive value (97.7%), whereas the combined presence of both clues showed high specificity (96.9%) but low sensitivity (13.8%). Multivariable analysis

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, age younger than 50 years, presence of a mucinous component, and an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response pattern were independently associated with dMMR status. Patients younger than 50 years had a higher likelihood of dMMR than those aged 50 years or older (OR: 7.212, 95% CI: 2.076–25.058, p = 0.002). Likewise, the presence of a mucinous component was associated with an approximately 6.1-fold increase in the likelihood of dMMR (OR: 6.067, 95% CI: 2.126–17.316, p = 0.001), while an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response pattern was associated with an approximately 7.1-fold increase (OR: 7.089, 95% CI: 2.505–20.059, p < 0.001). Tumor grade did not retain independent significance in the multivariable model (OR: 2.780, 95% CI: 0.925–8.358, p = 0.069). Discussion

In the present study, dMMR was identified in 13.1% of colon adenocarcinomas, and this frequency is broadly consistent with the rate generally reported for localized colorectal cancer in contemporary reviews and clinicopathologic series [13, 14]. More importantly, our results suggest that dMMR does not occur randomly across morphologic patterns; instead, it tends to cluster with a recognizable histopathologic phenotype that includes mucinous differentiation and a conspicuous intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response [6, 14, 15]. The associations of mucinous morphology and lymphocytic or Crohn-like inflammatory reactions with dMMR are well established. Accordingly, the contribution of the present study is not the initial identification of these relationships, but their detailed quantification in a consecutive, universally tested cohort, with particular emphasis on a partial mucinous component below the conventional 50% threshold, distinct immune-response patterns, and individual MMR protein-loss profiles.

One of the strongest findings in our cohort was the association between dMMR and the immune response pattern. Tumors showing intratumoral or Crohn-like inflammation were markedly enriched for dMMR, and this relationship remained independently significant in multivariable analysis. This finding is biologically plausible because dMMR tumors accumulate numerous insertion-deletion mutations and neoantigens, which promote an immune-reactive microenvironment and help explain the dense lymphocytic infiltration that is so often seen histologically [8, 13]. Tertiary lymphoid structures and Crohn-like lymphoid reaction are now increasingly viewed not simply as descriptive histologic curiosities, but as meaningful components of the host antitumor immune response in colorectal cancer [6, 16]. Earlier prognostic work also showed that tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and Crohn-like lymphoid reaction are independently associated with colorectal cancer outcome, supporting the idea that these patterns reflect real tumor biology rather than incidental inflammation [17, 18].

When the two immune patterns were examined separately as non-mutually exclusive variables, the association with dMMR was primarily driven by the intratumoral immune response. Crohn-like lymphoid reaction was numerically more frequent among dMMR tumors, although this difference did not reach statistical significance in the separate sensitivity analysis. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence that Crohn-like reaction lacks biological relevance, particularly given the modest number of dMMR tumors and the limited number of tumors showing each immune pattern.

The reproducibility analysis supported the applicability of the predefined histopathologic criteria. Agreement was almost perfect for the presence and extent categories of mucinous differentiation. Agreement was also almost perfect for Crohn-like lymphoid reaction and the combined immune-active classification, and substantial for intratumoral immune response and the four-category predominant immune-response pattern. The somewhat lower agreement for the latter two assessments likely reflects the additional judgment required to identify the most densely infiltrated intratumoral area and to assign a single predominant pattern, particularly when different immune-response patterns coexist or when the distinction between absent and mild peritumoral inflammation is subtle. These findings suggest that explicitly defined criteria can provide reproducible assessments between independent observers, while also supporting the separate recording of intratumoral and Crohn-like responses in addition to assignment of an overall predominant pattern. Previous studies have similarly shown that reproducibility of Crohn-like lymphoid reaction improves when explicit and standardized assessment criteria are used [12].

From a diagnostic pathology perspective, this matters because not all inflammatory reactions carry the same implication. In routine sections, a predominantly peritumoral inflammatory cuff may be much less informative than inflammation that permeates the tumor or forms Crohn-like aggregates at the advancing edge. Our results support that distinction and suggest that the pattern of immune response deserves attention, not merely its presence or absence. This interpretation is also in line with recent clinicopathologic studies showing that dMMR status correlates particularly well with tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, Crohn-like reaction, and other “MSI-type” histologic patterns [14, 15, 19].

The second major finding of our study was the association between mucinous differentiation and dMMR. Tumors containing a mucinous component were significantly more likely to be dMMR, and this association remained independent after adjustment for age, grade, and immune response pattern. This observation is consistent with long-standing literature describing mucinous morphology as one of the classic histopathologic correlates of dMMR/MSI-H colorectal carcinoma [5]. However, our subgroup analysis adds a useful nuance: the relationship was not restricted to tumors meeting the traditional threshold for mucinous adenocarcinoma, but was also seen in tumors with a partial mucinous component. In that respect, our findings are in keeping with recent studies suggesting that mucinous differentiation may carry biologic and immune relevance beyond a purely categorical classification of mucinous adenocarcinoma [20, 21]. MLH1/PMS2 loss constituted the predominant dMMR pattern in our cohort. Nevertheless, a mucinous component was identified in both MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumors and tumors with other loss patterns, with no statistically significant difference between these groups. Although limited by the small number of non-MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumors, this finding suggests that the observed association between mucinous differentiation and dMMR was not exclusively attributable to the predominance of MLH1/PMS2 loss.

This point is worth emphasizing because pathologists often think in categorical terms: either a tumor is “mucinous adenocarcinoma” or it is not. In practice, however, smaller amounts of extracellular mucin may also carry biologic information. Our results indicate that the presence of a mucinous component below the conventional threshold for mucinous adenocarcinoma may form part of the broader histopathologic phenotype associated with dMMR, particularly when accompanied by intratumoral lymphocytes or Crohn-like inflammation. Similar reasoning is supported by recent work linking mucinous differentiation with distinct immune cell infiltration patterns in colorectal adenocarcinoma [20].

The combined histopathologic clue analysis provided a descriptive assessment of how dMMR frequency varied according to the coexistence of these two features. Tumors lacking both a mucinous component and an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune-active pattern showed a low dMMR rate, whereas the frequency of dMMR increased stepwise when one or both features were present. This analysis was exploratory and was not intended to establish a clinical prediction rule or determine which tumors should undergo MMR testing. Rather, it illustrates the histologic phenotype in which dMMR was enriched within this universally tested cohort. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that MSI/dMMR colorectal carcinomas can be enriched by pattern-based assessment of morphologic features such as mucinous differentiation, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and Crohn-like lymphoid reaction [4, 9, 15, 19, 22, 23].

Another notable result was the association between younger age and dMMR. In our series, dMMR was more common in patients younger than 50 years, and age below 50 remained independently associated with dMMR in the regression model. This finding should be interpreted carefully. Sporadic dMMR colorectal carcinomas, especially those related to MLH1 promoter hypermethylation, are often encountered in older adults, but younger age remains clinically important because it raises the possibility of Lynch syndrome and is also relevant in the broader context of early-onset colorectal cancer [8, 13, 24]. Recent comparative cohort data have likewise reported a higher relative proportion of MSI-H/dMMR tumors in younger patient groups, although the exact age effect varies across populations [24, 25]. In our study, the use of a 50-year threshold provided a clinically recognizable cut-off for younger-onset disease and identified a subgroup with a markedly increased dMMR rate. Within the dMMR subgroup, patients with MLH1/PMS2 loss were numerically older and less frequently younger than 50 years than those with other loss patterns, although these differences were not statistically significant. MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumors were more frequently right-sided; however, the absence of BRAF V600E and MLH1 promoter methylation data precluded further etiologic classification of these tumors. Therefore, the observed loss patterns cannot be used to distinguish sporadic from Lynch syndrome-associated dMMR in this cohort.

Several of our additional findings also fit the broader dMMR phenotype described in the literature. dMMR tumors in our cohort were larger, had fewer metastatic lymph nodes, were more often node-negative, and were associated with a higher total lymph node yield. Reviews and retrospective studies continue to show that dMMR colorectal cancers may appear morphologically aggressive in some respects, yet still display lower nodal burden in localized disease [8, 13, 15]. The increased lymph node yield seen in our dMMR cases is also biologically plausible, since recent work has linked lymph node yield to an immune-high tumor microenvironment and suggested that host immune activation may influence nodal retrieval [26]. This may help explain why tumors with pronounced immune activity, including many dMMR cancers, yield higher nodal counts at resection.

We also found that perineural invasion was less frequent in dMMR tumors. Although perineural invasion is not among the most classically discussed features of dMMR colorectal cancer, recent clinicopathologic series suggest that dMMR tumors often diverge from conventional pMMR tumors across several invasion-related parameters, including nodal spread and certain aggressive growth features [14, 15, 19]. Our data fit that broader pattern, even if this particular association deserves confirmation in larger cohorts.

Tumor location did not reach statistical significance in our study, although dMMR tumors were numerically more frequent in the right colon. This is not entirely unexpected. Right-sided predominance remains one of the most familiar clinicopathologic associations of dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer, but it is not reproduced with equal strength in every cohort [8, 13, 15]. In our series, the relatively small number of dMMR cases and the restriction to colon tumors may have limited our power to detect this difference. For daily practice, the more useful takeaway is that absence of a right-sided location should not discourage suspicion for dMMR when stronger morphologic clues are present, especially mucinous differentiation and an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response [14, 19].

An additional point worth noting is that tumor grade lost significance in the multivariable model. Poor differentiation was associated with dMMR in univariate analysis, but this relationship weakened after adjustment for mucinous differentiation and immune response pattern. This suggests that grade alone may be a relatively nonspecific clue, whereas a broader pattern-based assessment may be more informative. A similar message emerges from prior clinicopathologic studies, in which poor differentiation is associated with the dMMR/MSI phenotype but does not always remain independently associated after accounting for other histologic features [15].

From a practical standpoint, our findings provide histologic context for dMMR in a setting where MMR immunohistochemistry was routinely performed in all eligible colon adenocarcinomas. The presence or absence of the evaluated morphologic features should neither determine whether MMR testing is performed nor alter the degree of care applied to immunohistochemical interpretation. All MMR immunohistochemistry results should be assessed according to the same technical criteria and internal-control standards. The observed features should therefore be regarded as components of the histopathologic phenotype of dMMR tumors rather than as criteria for selective testing. That remains relevant not only for Lynch syndrome screening but also for therapeutic stratification, given the continuing central role of dMMR/MSI-H status in colorectal cancer management [8, 13].

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective and single-center in design, and the number of dMMR cases was relatively modest. Tumor budding data were unavailable in a subset of mucinous and signet ring cell carcinomas because budding is not routinely recommended in those subtypes. We relied on immunohistochemical MMR status and did not incorporate parallel molecular MSI testing. In addition, BRAF V600E and MLH1 promoter methylation results were unavailable; therefore, MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumors could not be further classified as sporadic MLH1-hypermethylated tumors or potentially Lynch syndrome-associated tumors. The absence of these molecular data restricted the etiologic interpretation of the observed associations with age, tumor location, and mucinous differentiation. Although the original cohort-level histopathologic assessments were performed before MMR status was available and were therefore blinded to MMR status, they were conducted by one of two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists rather than through an independent dual-observer review of all 222 cases. Interobserver reproducibility was therefore evaluated in a separately selected 50-case subset by two independent pathologists. Although this subset included representation of the relevant mucinous-component and immune-response categories, it was not a random sample of the entire cohort; consequently, the agreement estimates may not fully reflect reproducibility across all cases. The combined analysis used two established histopathologic features evaluated in predefined categories and did not involve data-driven feature selection, coefficient-based scoring, or cutoff optimization. It should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory descriptive analysis rather than as a newly developed clinical prediction model. Because these performance characteristics were estimated in a single cohort, their applicability to other populations requires independent confirmation. At the same time, the study has important strengths: it was based on consecutive resection cases, avoided selective enrichment of the main study cohort, used explicitly defined histopathologic criteria, demonstrated substantial-to-almost-perfect interobserver agreement for the principal morphologic variables, and focused on parameters directly relevant to routine practice.

Conclusions

Our findings provide a detailed characterization of the histopathologic phenotype associated with dMMR in colon adenocarcinoma. Mucinous differentiation, including a partial mucinous component below the conventional 50% threshold, and an intratumoral/Crohn-like immune response were the most prominent associated features, while younger age also contributed independently. These findings should not be used to select cases for testing or to modify the level of scrutiny applied to MMR immunohistochemistry. Universal MMR testing remains essential, and the observed morphologic features should be interpreted as descriptive correlates of dMMR that require confirmation in independent cohorts.
