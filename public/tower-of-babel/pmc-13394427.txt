Gut‐microbiota‐brain Axis and post‐traumatic epilepsy
Authors: Andrey Mazarati
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere has been growing evidence that perturbations in gut‐microbiota‐brain axis (GMBA) are involved in mechanisms of chronic sequelae of traumatic brain injury (TBI). This review discusses the connection between GMBA and post‐traumatic epilepsy (PTE), the latter being a common outcome of TBI. The focus is on two aspects of post‐TBI GMBA dysfunction that are relevant to epilepsy. First are impairments in intestinal permeability with subsequent translocation of gut bacteria into the bloodstream. Specifically, endotoxemia following TBI may have a serendipitous protective effect against PTE through lipopolysaccharide conditioning, which may be leveraged for the development of therapeutic interventions. Second are changes in microbial composition (i.e., dysbiosis). Here, the GMBA‐PTE connection is explored from predictive biomarker perspective, whereby the risk of PTE can be stratified based on specific microbial profiles. Finally, microbiota transplantation is discussed both as a tool to examine the role of gut microbiota in PTE and as a prelude to novel approaches for PTE therapy and prevention.Keywords: endotoxemia, intestinal barrier, kindling, microbiome, microbiota transplant, traumatic brain injury

POST‐TRAUMATIC EPILEPSY

Epilepsy is a common outcome of traumatic brain injury (TBI). After severe TBI, between 16% and 50% of patients develop epilepsy 1 ; at the same time, between 10% and 20% of all symptomatic epilepsies are etiologically post‐traumatic. 2

Post‐TBI epileptogenesis is characterized by a “silent” period, which can span between weeks and years. 2 , 3 This period is characterized by a multitude of maladaptive events, which include acute neuronal injury, neurodegeneration, inflammation, aberrant plasticity, and which culminate in epilepsy. The first several days after TBI provide two opportunities to improve patient outcomes. 2 The first is the identification of patients with high risk of post‐traumatic epilepsy (PTE) through the development of early predictive biomarkers. The second is the introduction of preventive measures that would reduce this risk. On both accounts the progress remains modest, which calls for the search of novel biomarkers that are prospectively and reliably associated with PTE, as well as for novel approaches for the effective PTE prevention in vulnerable individuals. Among such modalities, gut‐microbiota‐brain axis (GMBA) has recently emerged as the one potentially associated with the PTE risk and with its mechanisms. Here, I discuss studies that are being performed in our laboratory to examine the involvement of GMBA in PTE, presented in a broader context of the role that GMBA may play in epilepsy and in regulating neuronal excitability in general.

GUT‐MICROBIOTA‐BRAIN AXIS

GMBA is a term used to describe structural and functional bidirectional connection between the brain and the gut. 4 , 5 , 6 Top‐down, parasympathetic nervous system (i.e., vagal efferents), hypothalamo‐pituitary‐adrenocortical axis (HPA‐A), neuromodulators such as neuropeptides, are involved in the regulation of intestinal motility and secretion, in maintaining the integrity of intestinal barrier, and in the composition of intestinal flora (i.e., microbiome). Bottom‐up, vagal afferents, gastro‐intestinal peptides, pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory mediators produced by intestinal macrophages, and products of the gut microbiota (e.g., neurotransmitters, vitamins, and short‐chain fatty acids [SCFA]) are all involved in maintaining brain homeostasis (Figure 1A). Any disruption to the proper gut structure and function is likely to have detrimental effects on the brain, which in turn may translate in chronic TBI sequelae including, but not limited to, PTE (Figure 1B).

GUT‐MICROBIOTA‐BRAIN AXIS AND TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY

It has been well established that TBI affects the structure and the function of the gut. 7 , 8 Many maladaptive events occurring in and around the site of the injury, and even remote to the injury, (e.g., neurodegeneration, inflammation, blood–brain barrier disruption, dysregulation of HPA‐A and of parasympathetic innervation) profoundly affect the core function of the gut. 9 , 10 , 11 Perturbations in GMBA that are particularly relevant to PTE, occur in two distinct, albeit overlapping, domains‐intestinal barrier and gut microbiome (Figure 1B).

DISRUPTION OF INTESTINAL BARRIER

A key element of the intestinal barrier (i.e., a barrier that separates gut lumen from the bloodstream) is the tight junction. Tight junction is a polyprotein complex (including occludin, claudins, zonula occludens proteins), whose primary function is to seal intercellular spaces between adjacent epithelial cells, thus preventing a paracellular flow of the components of the gut content into the bloodstream. 12 , 13 This ensures that under normal conditions all intended molecules (i.e., nutrients and gut microbial metabolites) reach the bloodstream via intracellular transport. Structural and/or functional damage to the tight junctions leads to the breakdown of intestinal barrier, producing a condition sometimes referred to as is “leaky gut,” whereby components of the gut lumen reach the bloodstream via paracellular transport. 14 These components include both those that are normally transported trans‐cellularly, for example nutrients, and those from which the bloodstream is normally insulated, for example gut bacteria. 15 , 16

Leaky gut is a hallmark of several intestinal disorders, such as celiac disease, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. 17 , 18 , 19 However, it has been also well documented after TBI, both in patients and in animal models. 20 , 21 , 22 Inflammation appears to be a major factor, with specific impairments attributed to the action of certain cytokines. For example, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) appears to selectively affect zonula occludens‐1 protein, while Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) selectively affects occludin synthesis. 12 Other factors include hyperactive HPA‐A (i.e. chronic stress) 16 and suppressed parasympathetic innervation, 23 although even here inflammation appears to be a common final pathway. 24 , 25

DISRUPTION OF INTESTINAL BARRIER, ENDOTOXEMIA, AND POST‐TRAUMATIC EPILEPSY

As it has been mentioned earlier, disruption of intestinal barrier enables translocation of gut bacteria into the blood. 15 , 16 Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, a.k.a. endotoxin) is a component of the outer membrane of gut Gram‐negative bacteria, including those that normally reside in the gut (e.g., Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Fusobacteria). 26 LPS is a prototypical pathogen‐associated molecular pattern (PAMP) acting at the toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4). 27 , 28 LPS exerts broad pro‐convulsant effect, including the one after its systemic administration, which mimics endotoxemia. 29 , 30 , 31

TBI induced in rats by lateral fluid percussion injury (LFPI), resulted in appearance of LPS in blood, which was observed as early as 7 days (i.e., at the time considered as critical for epileptogenesis) and as late as 7 months (i.e., at the time when PTE is apparent) after trauma. 32 Concurrently with endotoxemia, the animals had increased permeability of intestinal barrier, with strong positive correlation between the two perturbations, which suggested that LPS detected in plasma was of the intestinal origin. (Early post‐TBI intestinal barrier disruption and concurrent endotoxemia were also reported in the rat weight drop model 33 ). Furthermore, those animals that had elevated plasma LPS levels 7 months after LFPI were more likely to have PTE than those with no endotoxemia, an observation that was in line with the accepted pro‐epileptic role of LPS. Surprisingly however, those rats that had elevated LPS levels 1 week after TBI, were less likely to develop PTE than those that had no signs of endotoxemia at that time point. 32 It thus appeared that following LFPI, endotoxemia first peaked within days, and was apparently a result of TBI‐induced injury to intestinal barrier, and then months later, when it coincided with the presence of PTE and was conceivably induced by recurrent seizures (Figure 2), ostensibly through the intestinal barrier disruption. 34 Furthermore, early post‐TBI endotoxemia appeared to protect against PTE. The seemingly counterintuitive anti‐epileptogenic effect of early post‐TBI endotoxemia may be explained by a phenotypic diversity of microglia and in the role that LPS plays in driving this diversity.

PHENOTYPIC DIVERSITY OF MICROGLIA AND LIPOPOLYSACCHARIDE CONDITIONING

Each microglial cell contains a large collection of factors of innate immunity and other molecules involved in regulating neuronal homeostasis. Many of these factors are pro‐inflammatory (e.g., TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐6, inducible nitric oxide synthase [iNOS], High mobility group box 1 [HMGB1] etc.) and have been widely proven to be epileptogenic. The same cell, however, contains anti‐inflammatory factors (e.g., IL‐4, IL‐10, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 [TREM‐2], transforming growth factor β [TGF‐β], arginase‐1, etc.), that may have neuroprotective and anti‐epileptic properties. 35 , 36 , 37 Depending on the prevalence of one or another type of factors, the cell can be broadly classified as either pro‐ or anti‐inflammatory (formerly, these cells were described as either M1 or M2 respectively). 37 There is an evidence that LPS, at very low levels (read: low‐grade endotoxemia) selectively activates anti‐inflammatory factors. This phenomenon known as LPS (pre)conditioning boosts defensive effects of microglia against detrimental events, 38 , 39 which (events) may include TBI and its sequalae. LPS conditioning has not been receiving as much attention as detrimental effects of this PAMP; nevertheless, it has been shown to occur after TBI 40 and seizures. 41

We proposed that, via the LPS conditioning, low‐grade endotoxemia occurring during the first several days after TBI might have had long‐lasting protective effect, including the reduction of risk of PTE.

In order to test this possibility directly, we examined effects of exogenously administered LPS on post‐TBI epileptogenesis. LPS from either E‐Coli, or from Akkermansia (both are commensal gut microbes) or saline (control) was continuously delivered through subcutaneously placed osmotic minipumps between days 0 and 7 after LFPI at 100 μg/kg/24 h. 42 Six‐seven months after LFPI, when the animals were examined for the presence of PTE, we found no effects of LPS treatment on the incidence of spontaneous seizures. 42 In order to reveal more subtle effects of LPS, we further subjected rats to kindling of basolateral amygdala. Before proceeding to the description of results of these experiments, a brief discussion of the kindling phenomenon.

OFF‐NOTE: KINDLING AS MODEL OF LIMBIC EPILEPSY AND AS A HEURISTIC APPROACH FOR GAUGING THE SUSCEPTIBILITY TO (POST‐TRAUMATIC) EPILEPSY

Kindling is an animal (most commonly a rodent) model of limbic epilepsy, whereby repeated electrical stimulations of a limbic structure (most commonly amygdala) lead to the occurrence and progressive development of seizures. Seizures are initially focal (an analog of focal impaired awareness seizures 43 ), but with repeated stimulations they become secondary generalized and behaviorally tonic–clonic. 44 Kindling used to be a major model of limbic epilepsy, with “kindling era” lasting between 1967 (the first description of the phenomenon by Goddard 45 ) and circa 2005 (when Kindling 6, the last book in the series, was published 46 ). The employment of kindling in epilepsy research led to significant progress in our understanding of mechanisms of epileptogenesis, and to the discovery of anti‐epileptic effects of levetiracetam. 47 Here, I would be remiss not to mention that Dr. Moshé has been one of pioneers and a major champion of kindling and was the first to introduce kindling for studying epileptogenesis during early stages of ontogenetic development. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56

Post‐LFPI epileptogenesis is characterized by a relatively low seizure incidence: in various studies, the proportion of rats presenting with spontaneous seizures varies between 25% and 50%. Furthermore, even in the animals which do develop seizures, their frequency is often low, with as few as a single seizure detected during the 4 weeks of continuous video‐EEG monitoring. While this feature of post‐LFPI epilepsy is useful for the pursuit of the biomarker development (as it allows subject stratification based on the PTE risk), it represents a major challenge when it comes to preclinical trials of antiseizure medications.

There have been attempts to develop surrogate markers of post‐LFPI epileptogenesis (for example pentylenetetrazol titration 57 ), with varying success. We decided to apply kindling for gauging post‐LFPI epileptogenesis, specifically to identify those animals which do not present with spontaneous seizures proper but may have increased seizure susceptibility. The idea then was to use kindling not as an epilepsy model, but rather as a measuring tool, whereby seizure susceptibility could be gauged by counting the number of stimulations required to reach full kindling state (i.e., secondary generalized tonic–clonic seizures). When rats were kindled 6 or more months after LFPI, three kindling patterns were identified. 58 Approximately one third of the animals kindled faster than control (i.e., sham‐LFPI) rats, thus displaying increased susceptibility to epilepsy. Another third of the animals kindled at a rate similar to controls. Lastly, one third of the rats kindled slower than controls, or failed to kindle at all (Figure 3A). There was also a good statistical correlation between the kindling progression and the frequency of spontaneous seizures (Figure 3B), confirming that kindling indeed might be a suitable heuristic tool for analyzing post‐LFPI epileptogenesis. 58

The delayed/failed kindling in a subset of LFPI rats was unexpected; indeed, if TBI is universally considered to be epileptogenic, how does it create a state of resistance to epilepsy in some rats? It is agreed that maladaptive epileptogenic events (such as TBI, status epilepticus, stroke, etc.) always trigger a compensatory response‐ the engagement of adaptive, endogenous anti‐epileptogenic mechanisms. Clearly, in those animals which develop epilepsy, compensation is not sufficient to effectively counteract the maladaptive process. But it is conceivable that in some subjects, compensatory mechanisms are sufficiently strong and in some cases may even overcompensate, and this overcompensation would manifest itself as the increased resistance to seizures.

EFFECTS OF LIPOPOLYSACCHARIDE CONDITIONING ON KINDLING AFTER TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY

As it has been mentioned before the preceding section, after completing video‐EEG monitoring for identifying animals with spontaneous seizures, 7 months after LFPI, the animals underwent kindling of basolateral amygdala. We found that 1‐week‐long treatment of LFPI rats immediately after trauma with LPS from both E. Coli and from Akkermansia, significantly slowed kindling as compared with saline. Effectively, both treatments significantly reduced the number of animals with fast kindling; instead kindling progressed at either normal or slow rates as compared to sham‐LFPI subjects. The effect was more pronounced in the Akkermansia LPS‐treated animals. 42 Furthermore, at the applied dose, LPS had no effects on kindling in sham‐LFPI animals (data not shown). A possible explanation is that there was an additive effect between LPS from translocated commensal microbes and LPS administered exogenously. Stronger effects of Akkermansia‐derived LPS may be attributed to the suggestion that LPS from some microbes, such as Verrucomicrobiota (to which Akkermansia belongs) has subtle structural differences vs. LPS from other Gram‐negative bacteria (e.g., from E. Coli), which (differences) result in the reduction of toxicity without affecting the affinity to TLR‐4, where it competes with LPS from other microbes. 59 In such case, Akkermansia‐derived LPS may possess a dual protective mechanism, one through LPS conditioning and another through competition with LPS from other bacteria.

The apparent protection of TBI‐induced low‐grade endotoxemia against PTE was a serendipitous discovery. Indeed, both TBI‐induced intestinal barrier disruption and subsequent endotoxemia are likely to have multiple detrimental effects and contribute to both central and peripheral chronic TBI sequelae. However, the finding may be potentially leveraged. For example Monophosphoryl lipid A—an LPS derivative that is void of toxic effects, 60 which has clinical use as a vaccine adjuvant, exhibited anticonvulsant effects after TBI in rats. 61

GUT MICROBIOME AND POST‐TRAUMATIC EPILEPSY

Gut contains billions of microbes that play the essential role in homeostasis, including proper brain function. Functions of gut microbiota that are of particular relevance to epilepsy and briefly discussed below.

Gut microbiota interacts with macrophages; 62 thus, it is involved in regulating innate immunity (e.g., cytokine production); it interacts with enteroendocrine cells 63 that produce gastro‐intestinal peptides, including those that have either anti‐ or pro‐epileptic properties (e.g., somatostatin, neuropeptide Y, substance P, galanin) 64 ; gut microbes synthetize neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin, dopamine, γ‐amino‐butyric acid) 65 and biologically active metabolites (e.g., vitamins 66 and short‐chain fatty acids [SCFAs]). 67

Both clinical and experimental studies have established that TBI produces dysbiosis, which may be detectable years after the injury. 8 , 20 , 68 , 69 Perturbations in gut microbial metabolism, particularly changes in levels and composition of SCFAs in fecal samples have been detected after TBI. 70 Dysbiosis associated with epilepsy has also been established, 71 , 72 with most compelling evidence coming from experimental studies; for example, changes in gut microbial composition have been shown in the kainic acid model of temporal lobe epilepsy, 34 and in genetic absence model in Wag‐Rij rats. 73

We examined whether epilepsy developing after LFPI in rats was associated with dysbiosis. 74 We confirmed that both severe and moderate LFPI produced long‐lasting changes in microbial composition in fecal samples, mostly evident as differences in beta‐diversity and in selected microbial abundances. At the same time, we failed to find any connection between the LFPI‐induced dysbiosis and PTE. However, further analysis led to a surprising finding that there existed a statistically significant association between pre‐TBI microbial profiles and the risk of PTE. Based on this association, we developed a classifier, whereby the risk of PTE could be predicted with a moderate accuracy (receiver operating characteristic [ROC] area under the curve [AUC] 0.73) from the abundances of selected microbes in fecal samples of naïve (i.e., prior to LFPI) rats 74 (Figure 4A,B).

Furthermore, we found a similar association between fecal content of SCFAs in naïve rats and the risk of PTE (AUC 0.72, Figure 4C,D). Finally, by combining microbes and SCFAs, we developed a refined predictive classifier, with the improved AUC of 0.78 (Figure 4E,F).

This finding points to a potential role of envirome (and microbiome is strongly influenced by envirome through diet, lifestyle etc.) in determining the risk of PTE. It has been suggested that the risk of PTE may be associated with endophenotype, specifically, with single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) rs1143634 of the IL‐1β gene, and rs10920573 of the adenosine A1 receptor (A1AR) gene. 75 Hence, as it has been pointed out, there may exist “genetic variability that is not pathological under normal circumstances but predisposes individuals to developing PTE”. 76 This is an important conclusion, which means that in order to identify PTE‐vulnerable individuals, we may need to look for biomarkers not produced by TBI itself, but rather for a variety of predisposing factors including those that predate trauma. Our finding of the association of pre‐TBI microbial profiles and the risk of PTE expands the concept of such pre‐existing predisposing factors by adding envirome/microbiome to endophenotypic determinants of PTE risk. It should be noted, however, that while SNPs are hardly modifiable, gut microbiota can be effectively modified (e.g., with diet, prebiotics or probiotics) to reduce this risk in vulnerable individuals (e.g., in those at a high risk of TBI by the virtue of their occupation, including military and athletes). On a more practical note, since the microbiome‐PTE association was stable and persisted after LFPI, 74 microbiome analysis shortly after trauma would be as informative for the PTE risk stratification after the occurrence of TBI as well.

MICROBIOTA TRANSPLANTATION MAY OFFER FURTHER INSIGHT IN THE ROLE OF MICROBIOME IN EPILEPSY

Microbiota transplantation, specifically, fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) has been used for studying effects of gut microbes on brain function and their possible involvement in mechanisms of several neurobehavioral disorders (e.g., autism 77 and depression 78 ). Several studies employed FMT in TBI and epilepsy research. 79 For instance, FMT from naïve donors restored functional deficits induced by TBI in recipients, 80 and exerted anti‐inflammatory effects. 81 FMT from animals with epilepsy to naïve subjects increased recipients' susceptibility to seizures and conversely, FMT from naïve donors to animals with epilepsy, exerted quasi‐therapeutic effects. 73 , 82 , 83 In the context of this review, FMT may help answering a question as to whether the association between gut microbial composition and PTE is of a merely surrogate nature (thus having only biomarker potential) or does gut microbiome effectively regulate susceptibility to epilepsy (thus having mechanistic role with possible therapeutic implications). In our studies involving the LFPI‐PTE model, we found that FMT from naïve rat donors that proceeded to develop epilepsy after TBI to recipient rats 1 week after trauma, tended to increase the incidence of PTE, and significantly accelerated kindling in recipients. 84 This contrasted with FMT from naïve donors that did not develop PTE, when FMT affected neither spontaneous seizures, nor kindling in recipients (Figure 5). This finding pointed to the direct regulation of susceptibility to PTE by gut microbiome (or rather its products, which are yet to be discovered).

CONCLUSIONS

While studies of GMBA dysfunction in neurobehavioral disorders (e.g., autism, 85 depression, 86 Parkinson's disease, 87 Alzheimer's disease, 88 post‐traumatic stress disorder 89 ) have a relatively strong record, the examination of the GMBA‐epilepsy connection (and especially GMBA‐PTE connection) is still at nascent stages. Still, even presently there is no doubt that such a connection does exist and that it can be leveraged for the pursuit of both biomarkers and therapies. A major challenge and an overarching goal are the identification of products of bacterial metabolism that are ultimately involved in determining the risk of and regulating the susceptibility to epilepsy. This conceivably may lead to the development of both novel non‐invasive predictive biomarkers, and new preventive measures that would reduce the PTE risk in vulnerable individuals. The employment of global metabolomics, metagenomics and meta‐transcriptomics, which would involve concerted efforts between epilepsy and microbiome researchers should be able to achieve this goal.

Another aspect worthy of investigation is a potential role of dysbiosis in PTE comorbidities. As it has been mentioned above, perturbations in the gut microbiota have been established for a number of neurobehavioral disorders, including those that may represent chronic sequelae of TBI. Identification of gut microbes and attending metabolic pathways that are specifically associated with PTE comorbidities may advance both a more effective diagnosis and prevention of epilepsy in TBI patients.
