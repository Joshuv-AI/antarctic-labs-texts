METTL14-mediated m6A methylation orchestrates early NK cell development by maintaining metabolic fitness
Authors: Huan Ma, Zhenzhen Tu, Surong Deng, Yirong Sun, Shuxin Wang, Hongxia Wang, Chen Cheng, Xiang Li, Zhongjun Dong, Shasha Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N6-methyladenosine (m6A) represents the most abundant mRNA modification, yet its role in natural killer (NK) cell development remains incompletely understood. Here we demonstrate that the m6A methyltransferase METTL14 plays an indispensable, stage-specific role in early NK cell development. Mettl14 deficiency at the progenitor stage caused severe NK cell lymphopenia by disrupting the NK progenitor to immature NK cell transition. Paradoxically, residual Mettl14-null NK cells exhibited a hypermetabolic state characterized by mTORC1 hyperactivation and enhanced mitochondrial function, which drove both hyperproliferation and activation-induced cell death via p53 and apoptotic pathway activation. Despite developmental defects, these cells demonstrated superior capacity to control melanoma metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, METTL14 fine-tuned IL-15 responsiveness likely by sustaining SOCS3 expression to restrain JAK-STAT5 signaling. Terminal deletion of Mettl14 produced no phenotype, underscoring its specific requirement during early development. Our findings establish METTL14 as a crucial checkpoint coordinating transcriptional and metabolic programs to ensure NK cell homeostasis.

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells are innate lymphocytes critical for the first-line defense against infected or malignant cells. They directly eliminate targets via cytotoxic mediators (granzyme B, perforin) or death receptors (FASL, TRAIL) and secrete potent antiviral/antitumor cytokines like IFN-γ and TNF-α [1–3].

NK cells develop from common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) in the bone marrow, progressing through NK progenitor (NKP) stages marked by CD122 (IL-2/15Rβ) expression [4]. CD122 is pivotal for NK cell lineage commitment by enabling responsiveness to IL-15. IL-15/IL15R signaling induces key transcription factors (E4BP4, Eomes, ID2, T-bet, ETS1) essential for development, maturation, survival, and function through JAK-STAT5, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, and MAPK pathways [5–12]. While transcriptional regulation is well-characterized, post-transcriptional control of NK cell development and homeostasis remains poorly understood.

Resting NK cells constitutively transcribe effector genes (Gzmb, Prf1, Ifng) but exhibit minimal protein expression [13, 14], indicating stringent post-transcriptional restraint (e.g., mRNA stability/ translation control) to prevent autoreactivity and maintain a poised state until activation [15]. The mechanisms underlying this regulation are unclear.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the predominant mRNA modification, is dynamically regulated by writers, erasers, and readers. The METTL3/METTL14 complex is the primary writer, with METTL14 crucial for substrate recognition [16–18]. m6A influences mRNA splicing, translation, stability, and degradation [16], and its dysregulation disrupts hematopoiesis and immune cell development/function [19–30]. Studies using Mettl3 (writer)-, Ythdf2 (eraser)-, or Fto (eraser)-deficient mice implicate m6A in NK cell homeostasis/function [25, 31, 32]. However, stage-specific role of m6A modification, particularly within early NK cells, remain poorly defined.

In this study, we observed peak expression of m6A machinery components in NKPs, suggesting a key role in early ontogeny. To test this, we conditionally deleted Mettl14 (a core methyltransferase component) in early NK cells using CD122-Cre. This study demonstrates that METTL14-mediated m6A methylation is essential for NK cell homeostasis and maturation. Mettl14 deficiency caused severe lymphopenia and impaired maturation due to disrupted gene programs critical for the NKP-to-immature NK (iNK) transition. Residual Mettl14-deficient NK cells displayed a hyperactivated phenotype. These findings establish a vital role for m6A in maintaining metabolic fitness and regulating early NK cell developmental checkpoints.

Results

Dynamic changes in m6A-modified transcripts across NK cell development

To investigate the potential roles of m6A methylation in NK cell biology, we mapped the global m6A landscape in murine NK cells using Methylated RNA Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (MeRIP-seq). Quality control (QC) analysis using deepTools indicated high concordance among biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B). Picard-based QC analysis of the RNA-Seq coverage profile across transcript positions showed high reproducibility among biological replicates, with no obvious degradation or bias, indicating optimal library construction (Supplementary Fig. S1C). The genomic region distribution further validated the specificity of m⁶A enrichment in the UTRs, consistent with the known preference of m⁶A for UTRs in mammalian mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S1D). MACS2 peak calling identified ~5918 high-confidence m6A-modified transcripts across three biological replicates (Fig. 1A). Consistent with established patterns observed in other cell types, m6A peaks were significantly enriched with the consensus motif “GGACU”across all replicates (Fig. 1B). Metagene analysis further delineated the positional enrichment of m6A peaks. These peaks were predominantly localized within coding sequences (CDS) and 3’ untranslated regions (3’ UTRs), with the highest density clustered proximally to stop codons (Fig. 1C).

To further investigate m6A functions in NK cells, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on three distinct wild-type (WT) NK cell developmental populations: NKP, iNK, and mature NK cells (mNK). Integrating these transcriptomic profiles with our global m6A map revealed that developmental transitions, particularly during NKP-to-iNK stage, coincided with widespread gene expression changes for m6A-modified transcripts. Comparative analysis indicated significantly more pronounced transcriptomic alterations (both upregulation and downregulation) occurred between the NKP and iNK stages than between the iNK and mNK stages (Fig. 1D). Pearson correlation analysis confirmed a closer transcriptional relationship between iNK and mNK cells than between either population and NKP cells (Fig. 1E), reinforcing the significance of the NKP-to-iNK transition.

Focusing on this key developmental transition (NKP vs. iNK), differential expression analysis (DESeq2/DEG analysis) identified 2443 upregulated and 2098 downregulated transcripts. Among these developmentally regulated genes, a substantial subset (1136 upregulated; 849 downregulated) were also m6A-modified (Fig. 1F), suggesting potential post-transcriptional regulation by m6A during early NK cell commitment and maturation. In contrast, the transcriptional shift between iNK and mNK stages was less extensive, yielding 490 upregulated and 814 downregulated transcripts. Of these, 282 upregulated and 337 downregulated genes exhibited m6A modification (Fig. 1G).

To delineate functional pathways governed by m6A-modified mRNAs during NK cell development, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis on differentially expressed m6A-marked transcripts across key developmental transitions. Focusing on the critical NKP-to-iNK maturation stage, upregulated m6A-modified transcripts were significantly enriched in pathways essential for effector function acquisition, including Natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, Apoptosis, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, and JAK-STAT signaling. Conversely, downregulated m6A-modified transcripts in iNK versus NKP cells exhibited enrichment in pathways associated with early lineage commitment and progenitor activity, including Hematopoietic cell lineage, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, MAPK signaling, and NF-κB signaling (Fig. 1H).

Despite minimal transcriptional differences between mNK and iNK cells, upregulated m6A-modified transcripts in mNK cells exhibited significant enrichment in pathways critical for effector function refinement, including lysosomal function, apoptosis, and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Conversely, downregulated m6A-modified transcripts were linked to pathways modulating developmental signaling and inflammatory priming: NF-κB signaling, Notch signaling, JAK-STAT signaling, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions. Collectively, these data demonstrate that m6A modification likely fine-tunes early-stage NKP-to-iNK differentiation and terminal NK cell competence (Fig. 1I).

Mettl14 genetic ablation at the NKP stage causes severe NK cell lymphopenia

Integrated analysis of MeRIP-seq and RNA-seq data across three NK cell developmental populations (NKP, iNK, mNK) revealed pronounced expression changes in m6A-modified transcripts specifically during the NKP-to-iNK transition (Fig. 1D). This finding suggests that m6A RNA modification critically regulates early NK cell development. To validate this hypothesis, we quantified the expression of m6A regulatory machinery components (writers, readers, erasers) in murine NK cell subsets using transcriptomic data. Notably, m6A machinery genes, including writers (Mettl3, Mettl14), readers (Ythdf1, Ythdf2, Ythdc1, Ythdc2), and the eraser Fto, exhibited peak expression in NKP cells compared to iNK and mNK populations (Fig. 2A). The METTL3-METTL14 complex constitutes the core catalytic module for RNA m6A methylation. Prior studies established that ablation of METTL14, a scaffold component essential for methyltransferase complex integrity, completely abolishes global m6A modification [17]. To dissect the role of m6A in early NK cell development, we generated conditional knockout mice (Mettl14fl/flCD122-Cre+, hereafter Mettl14CD122-Cre) by crossing Mettl14-floxed mice (Mettl14fl/fl) with CD122-Cre knock-in mice. CD122-Cre drives NK lineage-specific deletion from the NK progenitor (NKP) stage onward [33], achieving dramatic reduction of Mettl14 mRNA in sorted NK cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Compared to Mettl14WT littermate controls (Mettl14fl/flCD122-Cre−), conditional Mettl14 deletion caused severe NK cell lymphopenia, with 80–85% reductions in NK cell frequency and absolute numbers across multiple organs, including spleen, bone marrow (BM), liver, and lung (Fig. 2C–E). To determine whether this defect stemmed from cell-intrinsic mechanisms, we performed competitive bone marrow reconstitution assays. Lethally irradiated Rag1−/−γc−/Y immunodeficient mice (lacking mature T, B, and NK cells) were reconstituted with mixed BM from WT (CD45.1+) and Mettl14CD122-Cre (CD45.2+) donors. Despite equivalent reconstitution efficiency of Mettl14-deficient hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in spleen and BM, NK cells derived from Mettl14CD122-Cre BM were markedly reduced (Fig. 2F, G). This selective NK cell deficiency conclusively demonstrates that METTL14 regulates NK cell homeostasis through cell-intrinsic mechanisms.

We quantified other major immune cell populations in the spleen, bone marrow, and liver of Mettl14WT and Mettl14CD122-Cre mice. CD122-Cre-mediated Mettl14 deletion minimally affected conventional lymphocyte development, and caused only slight reductions in the frequencies and absolute numbers of T and B cells across all tissues (Supplementary Fig. S2B, C). However, IL-15 dependent innate lymphoid populations were significantly reduced in Mettl14-knockout mice. NKT cell frequencies and absolute numbers were markedly decreased in the thymus and spleen, with only a reduction in frequency observed in the liver (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E). Similarly, hepatic conventional NK (cNK) cells showed decreases in both frequency and absolute number, whereas hepatic ILC1s exhibited a significant reduction only in absolute counts (Supplementary Fig. S2F–H).

METTL14 deficiency disrupts the transition of NKP/iNK to mNK

NK cell development proceeds from common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) through pre-NKPs, refined NKPs (rNKPs), NKPs, iNK cells, to mNK cells [4]. To define the impact of METTL14 ablation on this pathway, we analyzed developmental stages in Mettl14CD122-Cre mice. Evaluation of bone marrow progenitors revealed no significant alterations in CLP, pre-NKP, or rNKP populations compared to wild-type controls (Fig. 3A, B). However, analysis of later developmental stages uncovered a profound defect. In spleen and bone marrow, Mettl14CD122-Cre mice exhibited significantly increased frequencies of NKPs and iNK cells, but decreased frequencies of mNK cells (Fig. 3C, D). Despite the elevated frequency of NKPs, their absolute numbers remained normal. Conversely, absolute numbers of iNK and mNK cells were substantially reduced in both tissues (Fig. 3E). This differentiation defect was cell-intrinsic, as evidenced by bone marrow chimera experiments: The CD45.2⁺ compartment derived from Mettl14CD122-Cre mice recapitulated the phenotype of increased NKP frequency and decreased mNK frequency in both the spleen and bone marrow. However, the reduction in iNK frequency was observed only in the bone marrow when compared with CD45.1⁺ WT-derived cells (Fig. 3F, G). We next assessed NK cell maturation using the canonical CD27 and CD11b-defined stages: DN (CD27−CD11b−), CD27 SP (CD27+CD11b−), DP (CD27+CD11b+), and CD11b SP (CD27−CD11b+) [34]. Mettl14 deficiency severely disrupted this maturation program. In the spleen of Mettl14CD122-Cre mice, we observed a significant accumulation of immature DN and CD27 SP subsets and a concomitant reduction in the mature CD11b SP population in the spleen (Fig. 3H, I). A parallel skewing toward immature subsets was confirmed in the CD45.2+ compartment of competitive bone marrow chimeras (Supplementary Fig. S2I), establishing the cell-intrinsic nature of this defect. In the bone marrow, Mettl14-deficient NK cells similarly exhibited an increase in the immature DN subset and a decrease in the DP population (Fig. 3H, I). Critically, despite the relative accumulation of certain immature subsets by frequency, the absolute numbers of NK cells at all maturation stages were markedly reduced in both spleen and bone marrow of knockout mice (Fig. 3J). This global reduction underscores a profound deficit in NK cell development.

Further surface phenotyping revealed the molecular underpinnings of this maturation arrest. Splenic Mettl14-deficient NK cells displayed retained expression of early developmental markers, with increased proportions of CD117+ and CD127+ cells (Fig. 3K–M). Conversely, the acquisition of late-stage maturation markers was severely impaired, as evidenced by a diminished proportion of CD49b+ NK cells and a stark reduction in the expression of individual Ly49 receptors (Fig. 3K–M). Collectively, these data demonstrate that METTL14 is essential for coordinating the transition from immature to functionally mature NK cells, with its loss enforcing a blockade at the late stages of maturation.

METTL14 coordinates the transcriptional program for NKP to iNK transition

Although Mettl14-deficient mice maintained normal numbers of NKPs (Fig. 3E), the severe reduction in iNK and mNK populations pointed to a potential developmental blockade at the NKP-to-iNK transition. To investigate the underlying molecular mechanism, we performed RNA-seq on sorted NKPs from Mettl14CD122-Cre and wild-type mice.

We identified 1428 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Mettl14-deficient NKPs, with a strong overrepresentation of m6A-modified transcripts (420 upregulated and 211 downregulated; Fig. 4A). Strikingly, ~65% (934/1428) of these DEGs are normally dynamically regulated during the NKP-to-iNK transition in wild-type cells (Fig. 4B). The transcriptional landscape of Mettl14-deficient NKPs exhibited a specific pattern of precocious activation and silencing. Most genes (674/934) that are normally upregulated during the NKP-to-iNK transition, including 321 m6A-modified genes, were highly expressed in Mettl14-deficient NKPs. Conversely, most genes (196/934) that are normally downregulated during this transition, including 121 m6A-modified transcripts, showed low expression in the knockout NKPs (Fig. 4D, E).

This coordinated dysregulation extended to 57 transcription factors (TFs) critical for NK cell differentiation, 43 of which were m6A-modified (Fig. 4F). Notably, key differentiation regulators, including Nfil3 (E4BP4), Tbx21 (T-bet), Eomes, Id2, and Tcf7 (TCF-1), were all m6A-modified and exhibited significantly altered expression in Mettl14-deficient NKPs (Fig. 4F, G). Flow cytometry confirmed elevated protein levels of E4BP4, T-bet, and Eomes in Mettl14-deficient NKPs, with E4BP4 and Eomes also increased in iNK and mNK cells, whereas TCF-1 was downregulated in later stages (Fig. 4H, I).

These results demonstrate that METTL14-mediated m6A methylation is essential for orchestrating the precise transcriptional timing required for the NKP to iNK transition, and its loss disrupts the coordinated activation and repression of a core differentiation program.

Consistent with the transcriptomic data, cell-cycle analysis revealed a higher percentage of Mettl14-deficient NK cells in G1 and S phases, and a corresponding decrease in G0 (Fig. 5E), indicating accelerated cell cycle entry and progression. This was further supported by the substantial upregulation of key cell cycle promoters, e.g., Cdk1, Ccna2, Ccnb1, Ccnb2, Ccne1, Ccne2 and Cdc45, at the mRNA level, although Cdk2 expression was notably reduced (Fig. 5F).

This hyperproliferative state was coupled with clear signs of cellular stress. We observed significant enrichment of the p53 signaling pathway (Fig. 5C, D; Supplementary Fig. S3C, D), accompanied by increased p53 protein and its transcriptional target Cdkn1a (p21) mRNA (Fig. 5G, H), suggesting activation of a DNA damage response. GSEA also indicated enhanced activity in DNA repair pathways, including homologous recombination, base excision repair and mismatch repair (Supplementary Fig. S3C, D). In line with this, our RNA-seq data revealed significant upregulation of genes encoding key machinery involved in base excision repair (Pcna, Pole, Lig1, Fen1, Tdg, Neil3, Apex1, Ung) and mismatch repair (Msh6, Exo1, Rfc5, Rfc3) in Mettl14-deficient NK cells (data not shown). As a potential source of this genomic stress, we measured reactive oxygen species (ROS) and found that Mettl14-deficient NK cells exhibited significant accumulation of mitochondrial superoxide (Fig. 5J), despite normal levels of cytosolic ROS (Fig. 5I). The expression of genes encoding the antioxidant enzymes GPX4 and GPX8 was significantly downregulated in the absence of Mettl14 (data not shown), which may render the cells more susceptible to ROS-induced cell death.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that METTL14 deficiency induces a hyperproliferative phenotype in NK cells, which is paradoxically linked to metabolic oxidative stress and the activation of p53-mediated checkpoint signaling, likely contributing to the overall homeostatic defect.

METTL14 deficiency triggers apoptosis through convergent extrinsic and intrinsic pathways

Given the observed mitochondrial ROS accumulation and p53 pathway activation, we hypothesized that Mettl14-deficient NK cells undergo enhanced apoptosis. Indeed, freshly isolated Mettl14CD122-Cre NK cells exhibited an approximately two-fold increase in Annexin V⁺ cells and significantly elevated caspase activity (Fig. 5K, M). This pro-apoptotic phenotype was most pronounced within the iNK and mNK subsets (Fig. 5L, N), corroborated by significant enrichment of apoptosis-related KEGG pathways in these populations (Fig. 5C, D).

Transcriptomic analysis revealed broad upregulation of apoptotic genes in Mettl14-deficient iNK and mNK cells (Fig. 5O). This included classical p53 transcriptional targets, such as the BH3-only proteins Pmaip1 (Noxa), Bax, and Bak1, death receptors Fas and Tnfrsf10b (DR5), and the adapter Pidd1. Furthermore, death ligands Tnf (TNF-α) and Tnfsf10 (TRAIL) were upregulated alongside their receptors. Additional pro-apoptotic genes not directly regulated by p53, including Bcl2l11 (Bim), Tradd, and Casp3, were also elevated, as were transcripts for the lysosomal enzymes Ctss and Ctsz (Fig. 5O), which can amplify caspase activation via mitochondrial damage.

This transcriptional profile was validated at the protein level. Flow cytometry confirmed increased surface expression of FAS, DR5, and TRAIL on total, iNK, and mNK cells from Mettl14CD122-Cre mice, alongside elevated FASL, particularly on NKP and mNK subsets (Fig. 5P–S). Concurrently, while BIM protein levels were unchanged, the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5T, U).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that METTL14 deficiency induces concurrent activation of both the extrinsic (death receptor/ligand) and intrinsic (mitochondrial/BCL-2 family) apoptotic pathways, culminating in the preferential loss of iNK and mNK cells probably through FAS-FASL, TRAIL-DR5, and TNF-α-TNFR1 mediated mechanisms.

Mitochondria are essential hubs that orchestrate cellular metabolism and have recently been shown to be regulated by mTOR signaling [35, 36]. We next investigated whether the metabolic shift was linked to mTOR signaling, a central regulator of cellular metabolism. GSEA indicated strong positive enrichment of mTORC1-related gene sets in both Mettl14-deficient iNK and mNK cells (Fig. 6C, D). Accordingly, phosphorylation of the mTORC1 substrate S6 (p-S6) was significantly elevated, particularly in mNK subsets (Fig. 6E, F). While AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 and Thr308 remained unchanged (Fig. 6G, H), we observed a marked downregulation of Tsc2, a key negative regulator of mTORC1 (Fig. 6I), suggesting a mechanism for mTORC1 hyperactivation independent of canonical AKT signaling.

This metabolic rewiring was functionally evident in several readouts: Surface expression of the transferrin receptor CD71 and the amino acid transporter CD98 was significantly upregulated in Mettl14-deficient splenic NK cells (Fig. 6J, K). Glucose uptake, measured by 2-NBDG incorporation, was enhanced (Fig. 6L). Endoplasmic reticulum abundance, visualized by ER-Tracker Green, was increased (Fig. 6M), indicating elevated biosynthetic capacity. Mitochondrial analysis further revealed that Mettl14-deficient NK cells possessed higher mitochondrial mass (MitoTracker Green) and membrane potential (TMRM) than wild-type cells (Fig. 6N, O). A substantial fraction of knockout cells maintained both high mitochondrial content and elevated membrane potential at steady state (Fig. 6P), indicative of enhanced mitochondrial fitness.

In summary, METTL14 deficiency drives NK cells into a hypermetabolic state characterized by mTORC1 activation, increased nutrient uptake, and augmented mitochondrial function. While supporting effector potential, this chronically activated metabolic profile ultimately disrupts NK cell development and survival in vivo, underscoring the role of METTL14 in calibrating metabolic fitness to ensure homeostatic balance.

Functionally, METTL14-null NK cells exhibited heightened activation even without stimulation, as evidenced by increased frequencies of TNF-α⁺ and CD107a⁺ cells, along with elevated per-cell expression of IFN-γ, TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin (Fig. 7E–H). Upon stimulation with RMA-S or YAC-1 tumor cells or plate-bound anti-NK1.1, Mettl14-deficient NK cells consistently showed enhanced cytokine production, degranulation, and cytotoxic molecule release (Fig. 7E–H). In contrast, PMA/ionomycin stimulation led to reduced proportions of IFN-γ⁺ and CD107a⁺ cells despite increased TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin (Fig. 7E, G), a phenotype associated with impaired PKC phosphorylation and diminished cytotoxic capacity in this specific context (Fig. 7I, J).

To assess the physiological relevance of this hyperactivation, we employed a B16-F10 melanoma metastasis model. Although Mettl14-deficient mice exhibited reduced percentages and absolute numbers of tumor-infiltrating NK cells, they developed significantly fewer lung metastatic nodules than wild-type controls (Fig. 7K, L). PK136-mediated depletion of NK cells resulted in a substantial reduction in the number of infiltrating NK cells in the lung and a corresponding increase in metastasis in both genotypes, thereby confirming the protective role of NK cells (Fig. 7K, L). The enhanced tumor control in Mettl14-deficient mice correlated with increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in the lungs, which was abrogated upon NK cell depletion (Fig. 7M). Furthermore, lung-infiltrating NK cells from knockout mice maintained elevated CD69 expression (Fig. 7N) and showed enhanced IFN-γ/TNF-α production, CD107a exposure, and granzyme B/perforin release compared to wild-type NK cells (Fig. 7O, P).

Based on GSEA of our RNA-seq data, pathways associated with cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions were significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. S3C, D). In particular, expression of Xcl1 was markedly increased in both iNK and mNK subsets, and that of Ccl5 was elevated specifically in mNK cells (data not shown). Given that CCL5 and XCL1 are potent chemoattractants for cDC1s, and that recruitment of cDC1s into the tumor microenvironment (TME) is critical for initiating antitumor immunity, we hypothesized that enhanced secretion of these chemokines by Mettl14‑deficient NK cells would promote cDC1 recruitment. This in turn could improve antigen presentation and T cell activation, ultimately suppressing tumor growth.

These results collectively demonstrate that METTL14 deficiency drives NK cells into a hyperactive state, which, despite homeostatic defects, confers superior antitumor immunity through enhanced effector function and potential coordination with adaptive immune responses.

Notably, however, this sustained hyperactivation may also incur immunological costs. During long-term monitoring of Mettl14‑deficient mice for autoimmune phenotypes, we observed spontaneous autoimmune-like manifestations in 16‑week‑old animals, including splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy, accompanied by an increased splenocyte count (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). These findings suggest that chronic hyperactivation of NK cells in the absence of METTL14 may contribute to immunopathology.

Given the central role of IL-15 in NK cell biology and the observed upregulation of IL-15–responsive genes (e.g., Nfil3, Eomes), we asked whether m6A methylation negatively regulates IL-15 signaling. Indeed, freshly isolated Mettl14-deficient NK cells exhibited elevated phosphorylation of STAT5 under steady-state conditions, despite unchanged surface expression of the IL-15 receptor subunit CD122 (Fig. 8B–D). We therefore examined key endogenous suppressors of JAK-STAT signaling and found that transcripts encoding Cish, Socs3, and Ptpn11 all carried m6A modifications in NK cells (Fig. 8E). Among these, Socs3 mRNA was significantly reduced in Mettl14-null NK cells, while Cish and Ptpn11 levels were unaffected (Fig. 8F). Consistent with this, SOCS3 protein expression was markedly decreased in splenic NK cells from Mettl14CD122-Cre mice (Fig. 8G). A direct link between SOCS3 depletion and heightened IL-15/JAK-STAT5 signaling was reported by Wang et al, who demonstrated that SOCS3 acts as a negative regulator of IL-15-driven NK cell maturation by promoting dephosphorylation of STAT5 [37].

TNF-α is one of the pleiotropic cytokines that play a critical role in NK cell proliferation and enhance NK cell function [38, 39]. TNFR engagement triggering p38 MAPK, JNK, and NF-κB signaling cascades implicated in cytokine production, survival, proliferation, and activation [40]. We also assessed TNF signaling, which influences NK cell proliferation, survival, and effector function. Phospho-flow analysis revealed significant increases in phosphorylated p65 (NF-κB), p38, and JNK in Mettl14-deficient NK cells (Fig. 8H–J), indicating hyperactivation of downstream inflammatory cascades. This dysregulation likely contributes to the observed proinflammatory phenotype, spontaneous cytokine release, and aberrant proliferation.

Together, these results establish METTL14 as a key post-transcriptional brake on IL-15 and TNF signaling, mediated in part through m6A-dependent suppression of SOCS3, thereby preserving NK cell quiescence and metabolic homeostasis.

METTL14 is dispensable for late-stage NK cell maturation and function

Given the minimal transcriptomic changes and limited m6A modification observed during the iNK to mNK transition (Fig. 1D, E), we asked whether METTL14 is required for terminal NK cell maturation and homeostasis. To address this, we induced Mettl14 deletion after the NKP-iNK stages using Ncr1-Cre mice (Fig. S5A). In contrast to the severe defects seen with early deletion, terminal ablation of Mettl14 did not significantly alter the percentages or absolute numbers of NK cells in the spleen or bone marrow (Supplementary Fig. S5B–D). Moreover, Mettl14Ncr1-Cre mice exhibited normal distribution of CD27 and CD11b-defined maturation subsets and showed no significant alterations in the NK cell receptor repertoire compared to littermate controls (Supplementary Fig. S5E–G).

Functional assessment further confirmed that late deletion of METTL14 did not impair NK cell effector responses. Upon ex vivo stimulation with RMA-S, YAC-1, or PMA/ ionomycin, Mettl14Ncr1-Cre NK cells exhibited cytokine production and degranulation capacity comparable to wild-type controls (Supplementary Fig. S5H). In vivo functional assays revealed intact clearance of MHC class I-deficient splenocytes (Supplementary Fig. S5I) and control of melanoma metastasis (Supplementary Fig. S5J) in Mettl14Ncr1-Cre mice.

These results demonstrate that METTL14 is dispensable for terminal NK cell maturation, homeostasis, and effector function, highlighting its stage-specific requirement during the early phases of NK cell development.

Discussion

This study establishes the RNA methyltransferase METTL14 as a critical post-transcriptional regulator of early natural killer (NK) cell development and metabolic homeostasis. By employing a conditional knockout model that ablates Mettl14 from the NK progenitor (NKP) stage, we demonstrate that m6A methylation safeguards NK cell ontogeny through two principal mechanisms: ensuring the fidelity of the NKP-to-iNK transcriptional transition and restraining aberrant metabolic and signaling activation to prevent activation-induced cell death (AICD).

Our findings reveal a stage-specific function for METTL14, with its most profound impact exerted during the early commitment and maturation phases. This is evidenced by the severe developmental blockade and lymphopenia in Mettl14CD122-Cre mice, contrasting sharply with the minimal phenotypic consequences of Ncr1-Cre-mediated deletion of Mettl14 in mature NK cells. This temporal requirement is further underscored by the peak expression of m6A machinery components in NKPs and the massive dysregulation of m6A-modified transcripts during the NKP-to-iNK transition. The transcriptional landscape of Mettl14-deficient NKPs was characterized by a premature and disorganized execution of the differentiation program, with key transcription factors like Nfil3, Tbx21, and Eomes being aberrantly expressed. This suggests that m6A methylation serves as a crucial timer, ensuring the sequential and coordinated activation of gene networks that guide early lineage progression.

A central and paradoxical phenotype in Mettl14CD122-Cre mice was the coexistence of NK cell lymphopenia with a hyperproliferative and hypermetabolic state in the residual iNK and mNK populations. This was driven by the hyperactivation of the mTORC1 signaling axis, likely due to the loss of the negative regulator TSC2, and was accompanied by increased nutrient uptake and mitochondrial biogenesis. However, this heightened metabolic and proliferative activity was unsustainable, leading to genomic stress, as indicated by mitochondrial ROS accumulation and DNA damage response pathway activation. Consequently, it triggered a p53-dependent apoptotic program, which was amplified by the concurrent upregulation of both intrinsic (e.g., BCL-2 family) and extrinsic (e.g., FAS/FASL, TRAIL/DR5) death pathways. This confluence of hyperactivation and apoptosis exemplifies a classic AICD scenario, ultimately accounting for the specific loss of iNK and mNK cells.

The spontaneous hyperactivation of Mettl14-deficient NK cells, characterized by elevated CD69/CD25 expression and enhanced cytokine production at steady state, points to a breakdown in signaling quiescence. We identified a key mechanism underlying this phenomenon: the m6A-mediated suppression of Socs3. The downregulation of SOCS3 protein in knockout cells led to heightened IL-15/JAK-STAT5 signaling, a master pathway for NK cell homeostasis and metabolism. This, combined with concurrent hyperactivation of NF-κB and p38/JNK pathways, creates a signaling environment that primes NK cells for effector responses even in the absence of infection or transformation. Notably, this hyperactive state, while detrimental to homeostasis, translated into superior control of melanoma metastasis in vivo, highlighting a potential trade-off between steady-state population maintenance and acute effector potential.

Although Mettl14-deficient NK cells in Mettl14CD122-Cre mice exhibited superior antitumor responses, this effect was model-specific. Deletion of Mettl14 in mature NK cells using Ncr1-Cre did not reproduce the same benefit. Reanalysis of published data by Song et al. showed that METTL14 expression is significantly downregulated (~49%) in tumor-infiltrating NK cells from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients relative to non-tumor controls [25]. This finding further suggests that METTL14 downregulation in human tumor-infiltrating NK cells is not consistently associated with increased antitumor function, underscoring the context-dependent nature of its role. Thus, METTL14 serves as a key regulator of both NK cell homeostasis and functional output. Moreover, given its ubiquitous expression, METTL14 is unlikely to be a viable therapeutic target for NK cell-based immunotherapies.

Our research clarifies the non-redundant role of METTL14 compared to its catalytic partner METTL3. The severe phenotype observed in Mettl14CD122-Cre mice, which was absent in Mettl3Ncr1-Cre models from prior studies, suggests METTL14 possesses unique functions beyond its role in the canonical methyltransferase complex. These may include METTL3/m6A-independent roles in chromatin regulation or a unique specificity for transcripts that are critical for early lymphoid development.

In conclusion, our work delineates a critical role for METTL14-mediated m6A methylation in calibrating the metabolic and signaling fitness of developing NK cells. It acts as a dual-purpose safeguard: first, by enforcing the precise transcriptional timing required for early development, and second, by erecting post-transcriptional checkpoints that prevent metabolic over-activation and AICD, thereby ensuring the generation of a stable and functional NK cell pool.

Materials and methods

Animals

CD45.1+ congenic mice and β2 m−/− mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). Rag1−/−γc−/Y mice have been described previously [7]. Mettl14fl/fl mice were generously provided by Dr. Meng Xu (Tsinghua University, Beijing, China). Mettl14CD122-Cre and Mettl14Ncr1-Cre mice were generated by crossing Mettl14fl/fl mice with CD122-Cre or Ncr1-Cre mice produced in our laboratory [7, 33]. All mice were on the C57BL/6 background and housed in specific pathogen-free facilities at Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China) or Tsinghua University (Beijing, China). Sex- and age-matched littermate mice (6–12 weeks old) were sacrificed for experiments, while 16-week-old Mettl14fl/fl and Mettl14CD122-Cre mice were employed for long-term monitoring of autoimmune phenotypes. Sample sizes were determined based on prior experience and common practices in the field, and were deemed sufficient to detect biologically meaningful and reproducible effects. The number of animals was minimized in accordance with the reduction principle, while still ensuring reliable results and statistical validity. No blinding was performed for data collection, or data analysis. No mice were excluded from the study, and no randomization was used to assign animals to experimental groups. The animal experiments in this study were complied with ARRIVE guidelines and performed in accordance with protocols approved by the by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval Number: LLSC20230332).

Cell lines

The RMA-S cell line (an MHC-I-deficient mouse lymphoma) was originally developed by Klas Kärre at Karolinska Institute (Sweden). YAC-1 and B16-F10 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium or DMEM medium (WISENT, QC, Canada), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol™ Reagent (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) and used for cDNA synthesis with the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; #R211-01). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q311-02) on a CFX Opus™ Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1). Fold changes in mRNA expression were calculated using the ΔΔCT method, with Actb as the endogenous control. Results are expressed as fold change relative to controls.

meRIP-seq and data analysis

For meRIP-seq, splenic NK cells (CD3⁻NK1.1⁺) were sorted from wild-type C57BL/6 mice using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer. Total RNA was isolated from ~5 × 10⁷ NK cells and poly(A)+ RNA was purified using the Dynabeads™ mRNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Purified mRNA (100–150 ng) was fragmented into 100–200-nucleotide fragments using RNA Fragmentation Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Five percent of the fragmented RNA was reserved as input, while the remainder was subjected to m⁶A enrichment using the EpiMark™ N⁶-Methyladenosine Enrichment Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA ; #E1610S). Enriched RNA was eluted in 500 µL TRIzol™ and concentrated using the RNA Clean & Concentrator™-5 kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Indexed cDNA libraries were prepared from both input and m⁶A-IP samples using the SMARTer® Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 (Takara Bio) for Illumina sequencing. After adapter trimming and quality filtering, sequencing reads were aligned to the mouse genome (mm10 assembly) using HISAT2. m⁶A peaks enriched in IP versus input samples were called using MACS2 and annotated using the ChIPseeker R package. Motif analysis was performed using HOMER. KEGG pathway enrichment was analyzed using the MetaScape online platform.

RNA-seq and data processing

For RNA-seq analysis of NK cell subsets, splenic NKP (CD3−CD122+NK1.1−CD11b−), iNK (CD3−CD122+NK1.1+ CD11b−), and mNK (CD3−CD122+NK1.1+CD11b+) cells were isolated from both Mettl14WT and Mettl14CD122-Cre mice using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer. Total RNA was extracted from all sorted cells using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Subsequently, RNA libraries were constructed using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Constructed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. RNA-seq reads were trimmed to remove Illumina adapter sequences using Trim Galore. Ribosomal RNA was then depleted using Bowtie2. The remaining reads were aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) with HISAT2. After removing low-quality reads using SAMtools, uniquely mapped reads were selected and quantified at the gene level using featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (Bioconductor) to identify differentially expressed genes between sample groups. Enriched KEGG pathways were identified using the online bioinformatics platform MetaScape.

Generation of mixed bone marrow chimeras

To generate competitive bone marrow chimeras, bone marrow (BM) cells from 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-treated WT (CD45.1⁺) mice and Mettl14CD122-Cre (CD45.2⁺) mice were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and intravenously (i.v.) transferred into lethally irradiated Rag1−/−γc−/Y (CD45.2⁺) mice. Chimeric recipients were assessed by flow cytometry six weeks post-transplantation.

Ex vivo NK cell functional assay

NK cell function (cytokine production and degranulation) was assessed using splenocytes from naïve mice or lung-infiltrating lymphocytes from mice with metastatic melanoma. Cells were co-cultured for 5 h with target cells (RMA-S or YAC-1) at a 1:1 effector-to-target (E: T) ratio or stimulated on anti-NK1.1-coated plates (2 µg/ml). Negative controls received RPMI 1640 medium alone; positive controls were stimulated with PMA (50 ng/ml) plus ionomycin (1 µM). Throughout the incubation, Brefeldin A, monensin, and anti-CD107a antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were present. Cells were then harvested, surface-stained for CD3 and NKp46, followed by fixation and permeabilization using Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer (BD Biosciences). Intracellular IFN-γ, TNF-α, perforin, and granzyme B were detected using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies.

In vivo NK cell-mediated rejection assay

To assess NK cell-mediated rejection in vivo, splenocytes from WT and β2m−/− mice were labeled with CFSE at 0.5 mM and 5 mM, respectively. Equal numbers (1 × 106) of labeled cells were co-injected intravenously into recipient mice pre-treated 18 h earlier with 200 μg Poly(I:C) (intraperitoneal). Splenic and lymph node cells were harvested 6 h post-transfer, and the proportion of CFSE+ cells was analyzed by flow cytometry. Rejection was quantified as the ratio of β2m−/− splenocytes remaining relative to co-injected WT splenocytes.

Metastatic melanoma model

Mice were intravenously (i.v.) injected with 2 × 105 B16-F10 cells. After 14 days, lungs were harvested and metastatic nodules counted macroscopically. To confirm NK cell involvement, mice were depleted of NK cells via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of anti-NK1.1 antibody (clone PK136; 200 µg) on days 4 and day 2 prior to tumor inoculation and then every 3 days post-inoculation.

Measurement of mitochondrial activity

Splenic cells were suspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with specific fluorescent dyes: 1 μM ER Tracker (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), 75 nM Mitotracker Green (Beyotime), 20 nM TMRE (Beyotime), 10 μM DCFH-DA (Thermo Fisher Scientific), or 1 mM MitoSOX (MitoSOX). The cells were incubated with the respective dyes for 20–30 min at 37 °C in darkness. After washing three times with FBS-free RPMI-1640 medium, surface markers were stained for flow cytometric analysis.

Assessment of glucose uptake

Freshly isolated splenocytes were suspended in glucose-free RPMI-1640 medium containing 50 μM 2-NBDG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were stained for surface markers and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Western blot

Splenic NK cells (CD3⁻NK1.1⁺) were sorted from Mettl14WT and Mettl14Ncr1-Cre mice using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer. Protein samples from the lysed NK cells were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore) via wet transfer (Bio-Rad, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the GAPDH-specific antibody (CST, 2118) at a 1:1000 dilution, followed by washing and incubation with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Bio-Rad, USA), and visualized using Amersham ImageQuant software (Cytiva, USA). To detect METTL14, membrane stripping was performed to remove antibodies bound to GAPDH, followed by reprobing with the METTL14-specific antibody (CST, 48699).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). For comparisons between two independent groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests were used; the F test was applied to assess variance equality between the groups. When comparing three or more groups or multiple levels of a single factor, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, with the Brown–Forsythe test used to evaluate homogeneity of variances. Data were checked for conformity with the assumptions of the statistical tests employed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance is denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Only statistically significant comparisons relative to controls are displayed.
