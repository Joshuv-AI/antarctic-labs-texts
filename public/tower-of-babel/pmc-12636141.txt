Effects of stance width and foot placement angle on knee joint loading of partial squats in healthy female university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Weilan Jiang, Liang Chen, Liao Wang, Yi Gong, Yuxuan Liu, Wenchao Jin, Yongjun Zheng, Bingchen An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates how stance width and foot placement angles influence knee joint loading during partial squats, aiming to optimize exercise performance while reducing injury risks.MethodsEight healthy female college students performed partial squats with varying stance widths (1x and 1.5x shoulder width) and foot placement angles (0°, -20°, and + 20°). Kinematic and kinetic data were collected using a 12-camera motion capture system and force platforms. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS, including correlation and multiple linear regression analyses to examine the effects of stance width and foot placement angle on knee joint moments.ResultsStance width significantly affected knee adduction, abduction, and external rotation moments. Increased stance width reduced knee adduction moments, suggesting benefits for alleviating medial compartment stress in knee osteoarthritis. Foot placement angles primarily influenced knee extension, internal rotation, and external rotation moments, with certain angles enhancing knee extension moments, beneficial for quadriceps strength.ConclusionsAdjusting stance width and foot placement angles in partial squats is important for injury prevention and rehabilitation. These findings offer practical recommendations for optimizing squat performance in both clinical and athletic context. Future studies should consider exploring a wider range of stance widths and foot angles, as well as diverse populations, including different age groups, sexes, and individuals with specific knee conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01386-x.

Introduction

A partial squat, also known as a mini-squat, is an exercise involving femoral-on-tibial extension between 45 and 0 degrees of knee flexion, designed to strengthen the quadriceps muscles [1]. Increasingly recognized for its benefits in both rehabilitation and general fitness, the partial squat offers a balanced approach to joint loading compared with straight-leg raises (SLR) and deep squats. SLR primarily target the quadriceps with minimal knee joint loading, making them suitable for early rehabilitation but less effective for functional strength gains [2].In contrast, deep squats generate significantly higher knee joint moments, increasing stress on the knee, ankle, hip, and lumbar regions, which may elevate injury risk in individuals with joint concerns [3]. Hartmann et al. advocated for the use of partial squats in the rehabilitation of those with knee injuries due to the suitable load they place on the knee joint [4].

When performed with appropriate techniques, partial squats are a safe and effective exercise. However, during partial squats, some participants may experience discomfort or pain. This can be attributed not only to a reduction in quadriceps and hip girdle muscular strength but also to the strain on internal knee structures such as the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments (ACL and PCL, respectively). The ACL and PCL are integral to knee stability, particularly in the prevention of excessive rotational and forward/backward movement of the tibia relative to the femur. The ACL is especially vulnerable to injuries caused by sudden changes in direction and speed or when the knee joint is subjected to extreme rotation and lateral impacts [5]. The pressure on these structures may be caused by improper execution, muscle imbalance, or pre-existing diseases, rather than risks associated with the exercise itself. Therefore, it is meaningful to find methods to minimize the potential damage to the knee joints structure in partial squats.

The varying directional moments exerted on the knee joint are critical indicators of knee loading conditions and potential injury risks in different directions [6]. The peak knee adduction moment (KAM) has been identified as a key biomarker in biomechanics research, representing the medial-lateral force distribution across the medial compartment of the knee during weight-bearing activities. This biomarker is particularly implicated in the pathogenesis of medial compartment knee osteoarthritis [7, 8]. Knee abduction moment, though less commonly studied, often observed in valgus collapse scenarios during dynamic movements, may lead to lateral compartment overload and increase the risk of patellofemoral pain syndrome and ACL injuries [9]. During knee flexion and extension, moments are exerted on the knee joint, and the quadriceps muscles play a pivotal role in controlling these movements. High flexion and extension moment, as seen in activities such as jumping, landing, or sudden changes in direction, can cause excessive strain on the ACL [10]. Axial moment, including internal and external rotation moment, often manifested in activities involving rotation of the knee joint, is frequently associated with rotational injuries, particularly meniscal and ligamentous tears [11].

Previous studies have found that altering movement patterns, such as changing stance width or foot placement angles, can effectively reduce knee joint loading [12]. For instance, Quenten L Hooker et al. [13] discovered that an externally rotated foot position and wider stance width decreased the lead limb peak external KAM during a golf swing. Similarly, Omar W. Althomali et al. [14] reported that the first peak of external KAM was significantly smaller at a 20° toe-out angle compared to a 20° toe-in angle when studying the effects of foot position on knee kinematics and kinetics during walking. Despite these insights, little is known about how stance width and foot placement angle specifically influence knee joint loading during partial squats—a widely prescribed exercise in both sports and rehabilitation contexts. Most existing studies have focused on walking, running, or deep squat variations, leaving the biomechanics of partial squats underexplored. To address this gap, we conducted a controlled laboratory study to examine the effects of stance width and foot placement angle on knee joint kinetics during partial squats. By identifying movement patterns that reduce harmful knee loading while maintaining quadriceps activation, our study provides evidence-based recommendations for optimizing squat techniques. These findings have practical implications for rehabilitation specialists aiming to protect vulnerable knee structures, as well as for coaches and athletes seeking safe and effective training strategies.

Methods and participants

Subjects

Eight healthy female college student volunteers were recruited. The study inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–25 years, (2) body mass index (BMI) < 30 kg/m², (3) no history of lower limb fractures or trauma, and no abnormalities in the knee, hip, or lumbar spine. Participants did not have prior experience with some partial squat exercises, but received standardized training prior to testing to ensure consistency and correctness of the exercise. The sample size was determined based on an a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-factors design) to detect differences in knee joint moments across stance widths and foot placement angles. Assuming a moderate effect size (f = 0.25, based on prior studies of KAM in squats [12]), α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.80, and a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, a minimum of eight participants was required to achieve adequate power for detecting significant differences in the primary outcome (peak KAM). This work was conceived as a preliminary laboratory study. Based on an a priori sample-size estimation for the primary outcome, we targeted eight participants. The procedures were performed in accordance with our institutional ethics committee clearance (approval No. 20140049). All subjects provided written informed consent.

Testing procedure

Our experiment employed the partial squat movement as described in Form 3 of the Baduanjin exercise, a traditional Chinese qigong practice involving slow, controlled movements to enhance physical health [15]. In Form 3 of Baduanjin, known as “Draw a bow on both sides like shooting a vulture”, participants perform a partial squat characterized by controlled knee flexion (Fig. 1). To avoid the influence of upper limb movements on lower limb biomechanical data, we only had participants perform the partial squat movement in Form 3, with the upper limbs in a relaxed and drooping state. Each participant was instructed to perform the partial squat with two different stance widths and three foot placement angles, resulting in six distinct squat patterns. Stance width was defined as the distance between the centers of the heels [13], with two variations tested: (1) feet apart at 1 times shoulder width (1x), and (2) feet apart at 1.5 times shoulder width (1.5x) (Fig. 2a). 1x shoulder width refers to the distance between the outer edges of the shoulders when the arms are relaxed at the sides, measured horizontally. 1.5x shoulder width refers to a stance in which the distance between the feet is 1.5 times that measurement, which increases the base of support. Foot placement angles were defined as the angle formed by the intersection of the long axis of the foot and the direction of forward progression [16], while three different foot placement angles were examined in the test: 0°, toe-in 20 degrees (−20°), and toe-out 20 degrees (20°) (Fig. 2b-d). Each participant performed a partial squat under the six conditions twice. The experiment count was selected based on preliminary intra session retest priors, which showed high consistency in peak knee joint torque using the same protocol and low task intensity, indicating that two repetitions were sufficient to capture stable kinematic and dynamic estimates while avoiding unnecessary burden and fatigue [17]. To further alleviate fatigue and sequential effects, we took a 30 s break between repetitions, a 2-minute break between the two scenarios, and limited the total testing time for each participant to approximately 5 min. These safeguard measures are consistent with previous evidence that similar low-intensity tasks will not cause meaningful fatigue during this period.

Kinematics and kinetics records

Kinematic data were collected using a 12-camera Vicon 3D infrared motion capture and analysis system (200 Hz, Vicon Motion Analysis Inc., Oxford, UK). Twenty-seven reflective marker spheres were affixed to key bony landmarks, including the highest point of the superior iliac spine, anterior superior iliac spine, posterior superior iliac crest, greater trochanter, medial and lateral femoral condyles, inner and outer ankle, heel, first and fifth metatarsals, mid-thigh, and mid-calf, in accordance with the point cluster technique (PCT) protocol. Kinetic data were recorded synchronously with the kinematic data using four Kistler force platforms (Kistler Instruments AG, Winterthur, Switzerland) operating at 1200 Hz.

Data processing

Kinematic and dynamic data were analyzed during the eccentric phase of partial squatting (descending from standing to maximum knee flexion of approximately 45°) and the brief duration of maximum flexion (1–2 s), as these phases captured peak knee joint moments relevant to the research objectives. The collected data were pre-processed in Vicon Nexus software and saved in C3D format before being imported into Visual 3D software (C-Motion Inc., USA). External knee moments were calculated using an inverse dynamics approach based on the Newton-Euler Eq [1]. The joint moments were resolved in the proximal segment’s coordinate system using an X-Y-Z Cardan sequence.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp, USA), with graphs plotted using Origin 9.0 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. For Table 1, which reports p-values for comparisons of peak knee joint moments across stance widths (1x vs. 1.5x shoulder width) and foot placement angles (toe-in 20°, 0°, toe-out 20°), two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the main effects of stance width and foot placement angle, as well as their interaction, for each knee joint moment (adduction, abduction, flexion, extension, internal rotation, external rotation). The within-subject design accounted for dependence between repeated trials by modeling each participant’s measurements across the six conditions (2 stance widths × 3 angles). Where significant main effects or interactions were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests with Bonferroni corrections to control the family-wise error rate, adjusting the significance threshold to α = 0.05/k, where k is the number of comparisons per moment (e.g., k = 3 for stance width comparisons at each angle, k = 3 for angle comparisons at each stance width). For Table 2, which presents mean ± standard deviation (SD) of knee joint moments and correlation analyses, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships between stance width, foot placement angles, and knee joint moments. Multiple linear regression was used to establish equations for significant correlations, with stance width and foot placement angles as independent variables and knee joint moments as dependent variables. To address dependence in regression analyses, participant-specific effects were modeled using a mixed-effects approach, accounting for repeated measures within individuals. Results

Comparison of the peak knee joint moments across different stance widths and foot placement angles in different directions

Significant differences were observed in several knee joint moments across stance widths and foot placement angles. A wider stance (1.5× shoulder width) consistently reduced knee adduction moments compared to a narrower stance (1×), across toe-in, neutral, and toe-out positions (P < 0.05, Fig. 3a). Conversely, knee abduction moments were significantly higher with a wider stance, particularly at 0° and toe-out 20° (P < 0.01, Fig. 3b). Regarding foot placement, knee extension moments were significantly greater in the toe-in (− 20°) condition at 1× stance width (P < 0.01, Fig. 3d). Foot placement angles also strongly influenced rotation moments: internal rotation moments increased with toe-in angles, while external rotation moments increased with toe-out angles at both stance widths (P < 0.001, Fig. 3e–f).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that variations in stance width and foot placement angles significantly influence the peak moments of the knee joint in different directions during partial squats. However, there was no interaction between stance width and foot placement angles. We observed significant negative correlations between stance width and peak knee joint adduction and external rotation moments, and positive correlations with peak abduction moments. Foot placement angles were negatively correlated with peak knee extension moment and internal rotation moment, while positively correlated with peak external rotation moment.

Partial squats, as a very effective fitness method and rehabilitation therapy, can alleviate knee osteoarthritis symptoms by improving the strength of the quadriceps muscles [18], which plays a key role in knee joint moments [19]. The orientation of the limbs and the vector of external forces during exercise generate an adduction moment that influences the knee joint. This biomechanical parameter is widely recognized as a proxy for assessing medial compression within the knee [20]. The knee adduction moment (KAM) has been acknowledged as an important predictor of the presence, severity and progression of knee OA [21], serving as a focal point for non-invasive biomechanical treatment approaches aimed at alleviating stress on the knee’s medial aspect [22]. Our findings indicated a negative correlation between stance width and KAM, consistent with previous literature that found increased stance width results in small reductions in KAM [23, 24]. This may be due to the shifting of the center of gravity with increased stance width, reducing the KAM lever arm [25]. A wider stance shifts the feet laterally, repositioning the GRF vector closer to the knee joint center in the frontal plane, thereby reducing the lever arm for the adduction moment. This aligns with previous studies, such as Mina Baniasad et al. [26], who noted that lateral foot placement decreases the external KAM by minimizing the medial-lateral distance between the GRF and the knee. Additionally, a wider stance may enhance the activation of hip abductors (e.g., gluteus medius), which counteract medial knee loading by stabilizing the pelvis and reducing dynamic knee valgus [27]. As illustrated in Table 2, KAM decreased from 7.58 ± 3.80 Nm/kg at 1x stance width to 4.24 ± 3.62 Nm/kg at 1.5x stance width with a 0°foot placement angle, representing a mean reduction of approximately 3.34 Nm/kg. Prior research by R. E. Richards et al. [28] suggests that even modest reductions in KAM (as little as 0.1–0.5 Nm/kg) can lower medial compartment contact forces by 5–10%, potentially slowing the progression of cartilage degeneration. Notably, our results demonstrated consistent reductions in KAM across different foot placement angles, indicating that stance width is the dominant factor in modulating medial knee loading. This effect may stem from a more vertical orientation of the GRF, which distributes compressive forces more evenly across a broader base of support. For patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis, an appropriate increase in stance width during partial squats may provide symptomatic relief.

The knee abduction moment is also a key factor influencing the risk and progression of knee injuries, reflecting imbalanced loading on the knee joint in the frontal plane [22]. A meta-analysis examining the effect of dynamic knee valgus (DKV) on patellofemoral pain (PFP) showed that PFP patients, compared to healthy individuals, had an increased knee abduction moment. This increased moment can lead to a decrease in the patellofemoral joint (PFJ) contact area, concentrating the load on a smaller area of the patella, which may contribute to the development of PFP and potentially osteoarthritis [29]. The knee abduction moments during dynamic movements can also cause a higher risk for ACL injuries [30]. Hewett et al.‘s study [31] revealed that female athletes who later experienced ACL injuries exhibited notably higher knee abduction angles and moments during jump-landing tasks compared to those who remained injury-free. This may be related to muscle strength imbalances, altered neuromuscular control, or reduced core stability and proprioception [32, 33]. Our findings showed a positive correlation between stance width and knee abduction moment, which indicates that an appropriate stance width may help reduce the risk of knee valgus, while excessive narrowing of the stance width should be avoided as it may exacerbate knee stress.

The knee extension moment is closely related to quadriceps muscle strength [34]. According to a systematic review, patients with knee osteoarthritis have reduced knee extension moments, potentially as a compensatory strategy for pain relief [35]. Knee extension moments are also significant in the context of ACL injuries, as the strength and control of knee extension driven by the quadriceps play a crucial role in preventing excessive anterior tibial translation-a common mechanism of ACL injuries [36]. By appropriately increasing knee extension moments through targeted training, it is possible to enhance quadriceps strength, boost knee joint stability, and reduce the risk of ACL injuries [37]. The observation that the external knee extension moment is greater than the external flexion moment during part of a squat is worth clarifying because squatting typically involves resisting the external flexion moment of internal extension (via the femoral quadriceps). Our study used inverse dynamics to calculate external knee moments, reflecting net torques from GRF and body weight. During the eccentric phase of the partial squat (~ 0–45° knee flexion), the quadriceps generate an internal extension moment to resist an external flexion moment from gravity. Internal moments oppose external moments of the opposite sign (internal extension vs. external flexion). The reported “external extension moments” (e.g., up to 42.042 ± 9.932 Nm/kg at toe-in −20° and 1x stance width; Table 2) may result from shallow flexion and GRF alignment posterior to the knee, differing from deeper squats. In addition, variations in stance width and foot placement angle, especially toe-in angle, may enhance quadriceps activation and further amplify the external extension moment [34]. For example, toe-in stance (−20°) increases knee extension moments (Table 2), which may be due to greater engagement of the lateral femoral muscles. This contrasts with the deep squat, where a greater knee flexion angle increases the external flexion moment. The controlled shallow movement and upright trunk posture of the partial deep squat may minimize the external flexion moment and emphasize the extension moment [38]. Our study demonstrated a negative correlation between foot placement angle and knee extension moment, suggesting that internal rotation of the foot placement angle could potentially increase knee extension moment, thereby enhancing quadriceps strength and alleviating knee osteoarthritis symptoms. To better contextualize the practical significance of our results, we applied the regression equation derived from our data to estimate how changes in stance width could impact the knee adduction moment (KAM). Based on our regression (KAM = 13.619 − 6.456 × stance width [m]), a 10-cm increase in stance width is predicted to reduce KAM by ≈ 0.65 Nm/kg. This could have potential clinical applications for individuals with knee osteoarthritis or those at risk for medial knee joint stress, as reducing KAM is known to alleviate load on the medial compartment and potentially reduce pain or progression of the condition. This analysis helps solidify the connection between our findings and their clinical relevance, offering valuable insights for interventions aimed at mitigating knee stress through stance adjustments.

The knee internal and external rotation moments mainly affect the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) [39], with injury to this ligament leading to altered joint kinematics and dynamics, which may change the position and size of the load-bearing zone of the articular cartilage, triggering degenerative changes in the joint [40, 41]. These changes may also influence the biomechanics of the menisci, particularly the medial meniscus, which is often injured concurrently with the ACL [42, 43]. Fleming et al. [44] found that tibial external loading significantly increases ACL strain, while external rotation moments had little effect on ACL strain under weight-bearing conditions. Placing the toes outward on the foot causes the tibia to rotate outward relative to the femur, increasing the knee’s external rotation moment by aligning the long axis of the foot with the direction of the GRF vector, thereby generating a greater force arm for the rotational force. As reported by Sabashi K et al. [38], this kinematic transformation is evident in the tibiofemoral joint, where external toe positioning reduces tibial internal rotation. The muscle activation mode further amplifies the external rotation moment, especially in the toe eversion posture, where the involvement of the lateral quadriceps (lateral quadriceps) and gluteus (gluteus medius and gluteus maximus) muscles increases [45]. These muscles are more active during lower limb external rotation, producing greater moment to stabilize the knee and pelvis, resulting in higher external rotation moment. Our study illustrated a negative correlation between foot placement angle and knee internal rotation moment, as well as a positive correlation between foot placement angle and knee external rotation moment. This indicates that to avoid ACL damage and reduce tibial internal rotation, the foot placement angle should be appropriately externally rotated.

Our study, while providing valuable insights, has limitations that warrant attention in future research. The exclusive focus on female participants and the small sample size limit the generalizability of our findings to all genders. The gender differences in musculoskeletal biomechanics, such as the risk of ACL injury and the progression of knee osteoarthritis, may affect the response to squats. Therefore, it is essential for future studies to include male participants and increase the sample size to ensure more broadly applicable results. Our study only investigated two stance widths and three foot placement angles, which might have constrained our analyses. Future research should explore a wider range of stance widths and foot placement angles to gain a more comprehensive understanding of knee joint mechanics, particularly in the context of KOA development and prevention. In addition, our knee moment based findings can be placed in a broader biomechanical context. A previous study used OpenSim to quantify the internal load on the knee joint during squats and summarized standard techniques (mathematical modeling, in vivo telemetry, cadaver/in vitro) and typical ranges for determining knee joint contact forces [46]. Future research should include larger mixed gender cohorts to track long-term biomechanical changes during repeated squats, and modeling work can also be used to deeply analyze changes in biomechanical indicators during squats. In summary, while our study offers valuable biomechanical data and insights, future research must expand the demographic and biomechanical scope to further advance our knowledge and improve prevention strategies for KOA and ACL injuries.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that stance width predominantly influences the adduction, abduction, and external rotation moments of the knee joint during partial squats, whereas foot placement angles primarily affect the extension, internal rotation, and external rotation moments. This suggests that in clinical applications of partial squat training, selecting an appropriate stance width and foot placement angle based on the specific direction of knee joint force imbalances is crucial. For example, when there is concentrated stress in the medial compartment of the knee joint, increasing the stance width and moderately externally rotating the feet can reduce the load on the medial knee, thereby protecting the joint and enhancing the therapeutic effects during training. In addition, emerging technologies may enhance the practical application of our findings. For example, 3D-printed insoles and footwear can be designed to implement specific stance width or foot placement adjustments that our results show to reduce medial loading or modify quadriceps demand [47]. Similarly, machine-learning based gait analysis could be used to predict individual responses to these technique variations and guide personalized rehabilitation or training programs [48]. Future research may consider integrating these innovative interventions, such as 3D printed insoles and footwear, into training and rehabilitation programs to explore how they improve foot posture, prevent injuries, and enhance functional outcomes. These intervention measures, as well as advances in biomechanical modeling and real-time feedback systems, may lead to more targeted and effective rehabilitation and performance improvement strategies.
