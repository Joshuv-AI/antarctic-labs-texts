“Intraoperative pillar corticosteroid injection”: does it improve clinical outcomes after carpal tunnel release surgery? A double-blind, randomized controlled study
Authors: Panithan Tuntiyatorn, Chanakarn Rojpitipongsakorn, Siraphat Ponghunsa, Thepparat Kanchanathepsak, Tulyapruek Tawonsawatruk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study was to assess the therapeutic effects of intraoperative direct pillar corticosteroid injection in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome(CTS) who underwent open carpal tunnel release (OCTR) surgery. A prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted on 62 patients with carpal tunnel syndrome. Two intraoperative pillar injection solutions were compared: the lidocaine with triamcinolone acetonide and the lidocaine alone. Injections were administered at two points each at the scaphoid and trapezium bones in the radial pillar area, and at the hamate and pisiform bones in the ulna pillar area. Post-operatively, there were no significant differences in patient satisfaction, functional outcomes and pillar pain between the groups. Notably, the resolution time for pillar pain was prolonged in the steroid group. Conclusion Intraoperative corticosteroid pillar injection is not an effective option for preventing pillar pain symptoms. Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome(CTS) is a common nerve entrapment disease in both working age and elderly [1]. In cases of severe compressive symptoms or failed conservative management, the standard open carpal tunnel release(OCTR) is suggested for treatment to this condition [2]. The main feature of the procedure is that the transverse carpal ligament will be divided to reduce the intra-carpal tunnel pressure, allowing the entrapped median nerve to gradually recover.

However, post-operative pillar pain is a frequent complication for this type of procedure with the incidence of 32.5–49% [3, 4]. Patients experience deep-seated pain around the pillar bones, which include the trapezium, scaphoid, hamate, and pisiform bone which is the attachment of the transverse carpal ligament [5]. This symptom significantly affects a patient’s wrist function in daily life activities and decreases patient’s satisfaction in post-operative period. Typically, pillar pain can spontaneously recover within 3–6 months [6]. However, it may limit functional activities and may incur a long period of disturbance for some patients.

Current hypotheses regarding the etiology of pillar pain consist of: (1) Biomechanical widening of the carpal arch after incise the transverse carpal ligament, which stabilizes the pillar bones [7–9]. (2) Neurogenic inflammation, thought to result from microvascular disturbances affecting free nerve endings at the surgical site [10]. (3) Soft tissue edema localized in the operative field [5, 11].

Prolonged pillar pain is generally managed symptomatically. Common approaches include splinting, warm baths, local massage around the pillar area, corticosteroid injection(CSI) at the painful site [11] and extracorporeal shockwave therapy, [12, 13] which has demonstrated efficacy in symptom reduction. To prevent pillar pain, recent literature emphasizes minimally invasive surgical strategies that reduce trauma to the pillar region. These include endoscopic carpal tunnel release, split skin incisions, limited open or mini incisions or modified transverse carpal ligament release [4, 14–17]. These techniques have been associated with a reduced incidence of pillar pain and shorter time to return to work. Recent studies have investigated the impact of different corticosteroid administration techniques on postoperative outcomes following standard OCTR [18, 19]. Evidence suggests that intraoperative CSI may reduce postoperative pain and facilitate earlier functional recovery [20]. However, evidence remains limited regarding the efficacy of direct intraoperative CSI targeted at the pillar region. Within the scope of our study, the intraoperative pillar CSI was introduced immediately before the onset of pillar pain symptoms. We hypothesized that corticosteroids may attenuate post-operative neurogenic inflammation and soft tissue edema, thereby alleviating pillar pain and improving clinical outcomes.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to compare clinical and functional outcomes in CTS patients undergoing OCTR who received intraoperative pillar injections with either lidocaine plus triamcinolone acetonide or lidocaine alone.

Methods

Following approval from the Institutional Review Board of Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (COA.MURA2021/920), this study was conducted at the orthopedic operative theatre and outpatient clinic of Ramathibodi-Chakrinaruebodindra Hospital from January 2022 to June 2024. This was a prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial, and the study adhered to the CONSORT guidelines. The inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged 35–65 years with a diagnosis of CTS who had failed conservative treatment such as medication, splinting, warm baths, and activity modification, for at least three months, and who were indicated for surgery due to severe nerve compression symptoms, including painful or constant numbness, sleep disturbance due to numbness, and weakness or atrophy of the abductor pollicis brevis [11]. Exclusion criteria included patients with neuromuscular disorders, pregnancy, allergies to anesthetic or corticosteroids, chronic kidney disease, uncontrolled diabetes, signs of infection or inflammation around wrist, wrist osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, numbness caused by ulnar nerve or radial nerve lesions or cervical spine disorders, as well as patients with prior history of CSI into the carpal tunnel, prior wrist injuries, or those who declined or withdrew from participation.

The diagnosis of CTS in all participant was based on clinical examination by a single experienced hand surgeon. Diagnostic tools included sensory testing of the hand, evaluation of abductor pollicis brevis atrophy and motor power, and performance of Durkan’s test, Phalen’s test and Tinnel’s test [21, 22]. Clinical assessments were also conducted to rule out other pathologies such as ulnar or radial neuropathy, cervical spondylosis with nerve compression symptoms. Electrodiagnostic studies were performed only when the diagnosis was unclear. Demographic data, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), severity of CTS, dominant hand, affected wrist, symptom duration and tourniquet time were collected. In this study, CTS severity was categorized into three clinical levels based on the frequency of symptoms, numbness, pain, APB motor power and presence of APB muscle atrophy. Mild: Intermittent symptoms occurring during certain activities or at night. Pain, numbness, and tingling are present but not constant. Moderate: Frequent and persistent symptoms that affect daily activities or sleep. Severe pain may be present, but there is no APB weakness or muscle atrophy. Severe: Constant symptoms with significant weakness of APB motor power (≤ grade IV) or the presence of APB muscle atrophy [23–25].

All patients underwent standard OCTR [26] performed by a single experienced hand surgeon. Regarding surgical technique, after administration of adequate local anesthesia, a tourniquet was applied at the proximal arm (250 mmHg). Surface landmarks included the distal wrist crease and the intersection point of two lines: the first line drawn from the ulnar border of the abducted thumb, and the second line drawn from the center, specifically over the ring finger. A skin incision was made, followed by sharp dissection through the subcutaneous fat and palmar fascia to reach the transverse carpal ligament. Care was taken to preserve the recurrent motor branch of the median nerve if it was present in the surgical field, due to its potential variation. Neurolysis of the median nerve was performed. Successful decompression was confirmed by observing perineural blood flow. Subsequently, the intraoperative pillar injection was administered. The wound was then closed using 4−0 Nylon simple sutures and a standard compressive dressing was applied. Routine postoperative antibiotics were not prescribed for surgical site infection prophylaxis, as the procedure was classified as standard clean hand surgery. Post-operative follow-ups were scheduled at 1, 2, 6, 12 and 24 weeks.

As for the pillar injection protocol, in the treatment group, a mixture of 1 mL of 1% lidocaine without adrenaline and 1 mL of triamcinolone acetonide (TA) (10 mg/mL; SHINCORT, Yung Shin, Taiwan) was prepared, yielding a total volume of 2 mL. Using a 25-gauge needle, skin incision edges were measured and marked equally, with half on each side. A needle was then inserted from the volar aspect toward the remnant of the transverse carpal ligament in each half. A total of 0.5 mL of the prepared solution was injected at two points over the trapezium and scaphoid bones (radial pillar; Fig. 1A) and two points over the hamate and pisiform bones (ulnar pillar; Fig. 1B). During injection, the needle was advanced through the thenar and hypothenar fat pads until bone contact was felt, then withdrawn approximately 1 mm before administering the injection into the surrounding soft tissue. Care was taken to avoid injury to the median nerve within the carpal tunnel. The control group received 2 mL of 1% lidocaine (without adrenaline), with 0.5 mL injected at each of the same four anatomical points using the same technique as in the treatment group (2 points at radial pillar and 2 points at ulnar pillar).

Based on previous reports evaluating pillar pain treatment following CTR [12], a sample size of sixty patients was calculated to achieve > 80% power to detect a minimum difference of 1 point in the VAS score between injection types, assuming a two- sided significance level of 0.05, and a standard deviation of 2. With an expected dropout rate of 20%, a total of 60 patients (30 per group) was considered adequate.

Randomization of each type of injection was conducted using computerized software. Treatment allocation was sealed in envelopes prepared in participant order and opened only after surgery to preserve blinding. Outcome assessors were also blinded during follow-up evaluations.

At the two-week follow-up, the surgical wound was examined, and any complications potentially related to corticosteroid injection such as wound infection or dehiscence, were assessed before suture removal.

Post-operative outcomes including time to return to work, time return to the same type of work, and time until resolution of pillar pain, were recorded. Resolution of pillar pain was adjudicated as the absence of deep-seated wrist pain unrelated to activity. The incidence of pillar pain [5], defined as a deep-seated, non-activity-related wrist pain, was evaluated at 12 weeks. Patient satisfaction (PS) scores were collected at 24 weeks using the following scale: Very satisfied = 5, Somewhat satisfied = 4, Neutral = 3, Somewhat dissatisfied = 2, Very dissatisfied = 1.

At each follow-up, patients completed a Brief Michigan Hand Questionnaire (BriefMHQ) [27, 28], which assessed overall hand function, activities of daily living, work performance, pain, aesthetics, and satisfaction. Clinical examination involved evaluating tenderness in the radial and ulnar pillar areas by applying thumb pressure to identify one point of maximal pain on each side. Tenderness of each pillar was recorded separately using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS pillar). Additionally, the thenar and hypothenar subcutaneous fat and skin condition were assessed, and any post-operative complications were documented.

In cases of pillar pain, patients were permitted to take acetaminophen for pain control. NSAIDs were prohibited. Supportive measures such as warm baths, warm compresses, and gentle massage of the pillar region were advised.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Continuous variables were expressed as means, while categorical variables were presented as percentages. For demographic data, continuous variables were compared between groups. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. An independent t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. For binary or categorical variables, a chi-square test was used. Statistical tests were chosen based on the distribution of the data. For treatment comparisons, a two-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A statistically significant difference was observed in the time to resolution of pillar pain: 7.1 weeks in the lidocaine group versus 11.1 weeks in the lidocaine with TA group (p = 0.038). However, the incidence of pillar pain at 12 weeks post-surgery did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.145), with an overall incidence of 64.4%. Similarly, no statistically significant difference was observed in patient satisfaction scores at 24 weeks (lidocaine: 4.8; lidocaine with TA: 4.5; p = 0.068) (Table 2).

Regarding VAS for pillar tenderness, assessed at both radial and ulnar sides, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups at any time point (1, 2, 6, 12, and 24 weeks postoperatively; p > 0.05). However, VAS scores decreased over time in both groups (p < 0.001) (Tables 3 and 4, Fig. 3). The multiple values outcomes, as measured using briefMHQ, indicated no significant differences between groups at any time point (day of surgery and at 1, 2, 6, 12 and 24 weeks post-surgery). Nevertheless, functional scores improved over time in both groups (p < 0.0001) (Table 5 and Fig. 4).

Discussion

In the context of contemporary management to improve post-operative pain and patient satisfaction in orthopedic surgery, intraoperative corticosteroid administration has shown clinical benefits in major procedures involving the hip, knee, and spine [29, 30]. However, intra-operative CSI for wrist surgery has not gained popularity, primarily due to concerns about fat pad atrophy, wound infection, and wound dehiscence, all of which may negatively impact patient satisfaction [20]. In wrist surgery, the wrist differs structurally from larger joints such as the hip, knee, and shoulder, as the articulation spaces between the carpal bones, radius, and ulna are smaller. Additionally, soft tissue coverage around the wrist is limited and primarily consists of tendons, ligaments, and a thin layer of subcutaneous fat, which may not prevent steroid leakage to wound edges. Accordingly, the CSI technique employed in this study was developed with a strong ethical emphasis, ensuring the injection was administered deeply into the pillar bone region to minimize steroid dispersion. A low dosage was also used to reduce the risk of potential complications.

Recent literature has examined the influence of CSI on postoperative outcomes as an adjunct to standard OCTR. Kumaş et al. systematically reviewed various corticosteroid formulations used intraoperatively during CTR such as dexamethasone, betamethasone, methylprednisolone and triamcinolone [20]. In addition, different administration techniques are also examined including instillation into the carpal canal, wound irrigation, nerve irrigation, and perineural injection [18, 19, 31, 32]. The results suggested that intraoperative CSI may help reduce postoperative pain and promote earlier recovery of hand function. However, no clear benefit was observed in terms of long-term functional outcomes, symptom relief, or electrophysiological improvements [20]. Mottaghi et al. explored the effectiveness of CSI injected into the flexor tendon sheath and within the borders of the transverse carpal ligament incision, as well as around the median nerve prior to skin closure, comparing it to CTR alone. Their results indicated that CSI did not significantly affect electrophysiological outcomes or Boston CTS Index scores [31]. In a seperate study of 103 patients by Hanssen et al. reported a postoperative deep infection rate of 22% in the intraoperative CSI group, compared to only 4.5% in the control group—highlighting a significantly increased risk of infection associated with CSI [33].

In this study, which focused specifically on pillar injection methods, no statistically significant differences were found in primary outcomes such as patient satisfaction, time to return to work, or time to return to the same work. The incidence rate of pillar pain were 73.3% in Lidocaine group vs. 55.2% in Lidocaine with TA group, which is consistent with previous reports [3], with no significant difference between groups. Both groups showed improvement over time. Although numerical differences were observed, no statistically significant differences were found [34, 35]. Analysis of VAS scores and functional recovery(BriefMHQ) indices revealed no significant differences between groups at either short- or long-term follow-up. The incomplete CTR case, which was recognized as a major complication in the corticosteroid group, was not considered to result from steroid-related adverse tissue reactions [36], but rather from the clearly identifiable uncut portion of the transverse carpal ligament that continued to compress the median nerve [37]. Notably, the corticosteroid group experienced a significantly longer time to resolution of pillar pain and exhibited minor complications, including a superficial wound infection. Skin hypopigmentation, a side effect observed exclusively in the steroid group, was also present. These results are consistent with previous clinical trials [31, 33]. Based on these findings, we propose that intraoperative CSI has no preventive role in the development of pillar pain. The results do not support the hypothesis that corticosteroids reduce neurogenic inflammation or soft tissue edema in this context. Our data suggest that pillar pain is a self-limiting condition, with natural resolution occurring over time. Further research should explore the biomechanical theory that dynamic adaptations of the pillar bones following carpal arch changes may underlie pillar pain symptoms.

A major strength of this study is its comprehensive evaluation of pillar pain, including both subjective patient-reported symptoms and objective clinical assessments of pressure sensitivity, as well as functional outcomes. The randomized, double-blind, controlled trial design ensured rigorous data collection and minimized bias. Both groups were comparable in baseline characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, and CTS severity.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. For the major limitation, the diagnosis and grading of CTS severity were based primarily on clinical evaluation [24, 25] since nerve conduction studies, which can strengthen diagnostic accuracy [38], was not performed in all cases due to research funding constraints. Consequently, the heterogeneity of CTS diagnosis, based solely on clinical assessment without supporting laboratory or electrodiagnostic studies, may confound functional outcomes. The standardized carpal tunnel severity should be determined using either nerve conduction studies or the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire [39, 40]. However, we attempted to define a clear, objective, and detailed clinical severity grading system to minimize potential bias. This study was conducted at a single center and had a relatively short follow-up period, which may limit the generalizability of the results and the ability to assess long-term outcomes. The sample size was relatively small, but despite this, statistical significance was achieved in terms of prolong resolution time for pillar pain in CSI group. This study evaluated only one type of corticosteroid, triamcinolone acetonide (TA). The use of other corticosteroids could potentially yield different therapeutic outcomes, reflecting variations in their solubility, potency, particle size, tissue retention, and pharmacokinetic profiles. Future studies comparing different corticosteroid formulations are warranted to determine whether these pharmacologic differences translate into meaningful variations in clinical efficacy and safety. Another limitation was the assessment of pillar tenderness, which relied on subjective clinical pressure testing and may vary between assessors. Future studies could improve precision by incorporating objective pressure measurement tools, such as a calibrated dynamometer. Imaging methods like ultrasound or fluoroscopy could also be considered for more detailed analysis of the pillar bone and surrounding structures.

Conclusion

Intraoperative corticosteroid pillar injection does not improve clinical or functional outcomes in CTS patients undergoing OCTR and is not recommended for the prevention or treatment of pillar pain. Rather than shortening recovery time, CSI significantly prolonged the time to pillar pain resolution and increased the risk of minor complications such as skin hypopigmentation and superficial wound infections.
