Sprayable hydrogel loaded with engineered fusion cellular vesicles for cancer recurrence suppression
Authors: Yumiao Yang, Yuyue Zhao, Guowang Cheng, Zhou Lan, Hao Cui, Wei Liu, Bowen Li, Jiaxian Yu, Zhen Tian, Zhiyang Zhu, Jiaxin Wu, Qibu Luo, Yingluo Chen, Zhenzi Lin, Guangtao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical resection often leaves residual lesions that lead to tumor recurrence and high mortality across various cancer types, a challenge that is particularly pronounced in oral squamous cell carcinoma. Herein, we developed a sprayable hydrogel loaded with fusion cellular vesicles (Fus-CVs) and characterized its structural and mechanical properties for the prevention of postoperative tumor recurrence. Fus-CVs co‑express PD‑1 to engage PD‑L1 on tumor cells and CD36 to competitively bind oxidized lipids in the tumor microenvironment, thereby effectively reversing T cell exhaustion and restoring T cell effector function. Additionally, the sprayable hydrogel, formed by crosslinking sodium alginate (SA) with the ionic solution (calcium chloride, CaCl2; manganese chloride, MnCl2), enables sustained local delivery of Fus‑CVs to potentiate T cell‑mediated killing of residual tumor cells.ResultsIn vitro experiments and primary tumor model confirmed that Fus‑CVs specifically achieve dual targeting of PD-L1 on tumor cells and oxidized lipids in the tumor microenvironment, reverse T cell exhaustion and achieve combination therapy. In an established postoperative recurrence mouse model, the SA solution containing Fus-CVs and the ionic solution could be co-sprayed to rapidly form a conformal, uniform and stable layer on irregular tissue surfaces, enabling sustained local release of Fus-CVs and achieve effective tumor recurrence suppression.ConclusionFus‑CVs-loaded sprayable hydrogel not only achieves dual functionality in immune checkpoint blockade and lipid metabolic regulation but also offers localized controlled release, and reduced systemic toxicity, thereby presenting a clinically translatable strategy for postoperative recurrence prevention.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-05062-1.Keywords: Fusion cellular vesicles, Lipid metabolism reprogramming, Immunotherapy, T cell exhaustion, Cancer recurrence, Sprayable hydrogel

Introduction

Globally, postoperative recurrence remains a formidable challenge compromising the long-term survival of patients with various solid tumors [1–3]. Despite extensive surgical resection, oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), a locally aggressive malignancy, frequently leaves residual microscopic lesions within the peritumoral tissue, which serve as the primary reservoir for local recurrence and metastasis [4, 5]. While immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative modality to eliminate these residual cancer cells, its efficacy is often limited by immune‑related adverse events (irAEs) and a localized immunosuppressive microenvironment, with consequent T cell exhaustion [6–8]. Specifically, T cell dysfunction impairs the immune-mediated elimination of residual tumor deposits, ultimately predisposing to local recurrence [9]. Therefore, developing innovative immunotherapeutic strategies to reinvigorate exhausted T cells and effectively clear postoperative residual lesions is of paramount importance for preventing OSCC recurrence.

Currently, T-cell-based immune checkpoint blockade therapy has become a core therapy in the field of cancer treatment [10, 11]. Nevertheless, its clinical efficacy is profoundly attenuated by T cell exhaustion. As core molecules of the immune checkpoint pathway, the PD‑1/PD‑L1 axis induces T cell exhaustion and dampens cytokine secretion, thereby resulting in tumor immune evasion [12, 13]. Meanwhile, emerging evidence underscores that T cell exhaustion is also intricately orchestrated by the metabolic milieu of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [14]. Notably, lipid metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of cancer, contributes to the immunosuppressive TME and drives T cell exhaustion, characterized by enhanced lipid uptake and synthesis, increased fatty acid oxidation, and lipid accumulation [15, 16]. CD36, a transmembrane lipid transporter also known as a scavenger receptor, is widely expressed on various immune cells and serves as a critical bridge linking lipid metabolism and immune function [17, 18]. Mechanistically, the lipid-rich TME triggers the pathological upregulation of CD36 on CD8⁺ T cells, which subsequently catalyzes excessive lipid uptake (e.g., oxidized low-density lipoprotein, OxLDL), exacerbates lipid peroxidation, and ultimately precipitates terminal T cell exhaustion [18, 19]. Consequently, the convergence of PD-1-mediated signaling and CD36-driven lipid dysmetabolism presents a synergistic regulatory network, highlighting dual-targeting strategies as a promising frontier for restoring potent antitumor immunity in OSCC.

Owing to their homotypic targeting, low immunogenicity, longer internal circulation time and in situ protein display, engineered cell membranes offer an ideal platform for immunotherapy intervention [20–22]. These features enable tumor-selective accumulation and reduced off-target distribution, thereby potentially mitigating irAEs while enhancing antitumor efficacy. For example, the expression of SIRPα on engineered membranes enables targeting of CD47 on colorectal cancer cells, thereby providing a new perspective for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and safety [23]. More importantly, in contrast to nongenetic modification methods such as click chemistry, hydrophobic insertion, and electrostatic interactions, genetic engineering exhibits unique advantages, notably maintaining cell activity and preserving the original bioactivity of proteins on cell membrane nanovesicles [24]. Accordingly, fusion cellular vesicles (Fus-CVs), generated by fusing engineered cell membranes that natively display PD-1 and CD36 respectively, representing a promising strategy to synergistically alleviate CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and bolster effector function, ultimately leading to improved clinical outcomes.

The anatomical structure of the oral and maxillofacial region facilitates direct concentration of therapeutic agents in the tumor cavity, thereby enabling direct targeting of residual lesions while reducing systemic toxicity, representing a promising intervention for the treatment of postoperative recurrence [5]. Owing to their inherent biocompatibility and sustained release capacity, hydrogels, especially sprayable formulations, have emerged as compelling platforms for locoregional therapy, attributable to their rapid gelation kinetics, conformal coverage of irregular surfaces, and operational simplicity [25, 26]. Despite the demonstrated utility of sprayable hydrogels in other malignancies, exemplified by a thermoresponsive ozone‑enriched formulation for hepatocellular carcinoma recurrence [27], investigations into postoperative recurrence management in OSCC remain comparatively scarce. Here, we constructed a sprayable hydrogel loaded with Fus‑CVs for recurrence suppression. Fus-CVs were assembled from PD-1-cellular vesicles (PD-1‑CVs) and CD36-cellular vesicles (CD36‑CVs) via ultrasonication and microextrusion, thus conferring dual functionality: PD-1 enables immune checkpoint blockade while CD36 scavenges excess lipids accumulated due to metabolic reprogramming, thereby reversing T cell exhaustion and promoting cytokine (i.e., tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α] and interferon-γ [IFN-γ]) production and granzyme B (GZMB) secretion to enhance T cell effector function. Administered via direct spraying onto postoperative wound beds, the hydrogel wherein a sodium alginate (SA) solution containing Fus-CVs and the ionic solution containing Ca2+ and Mn2+ can be co-sprayed to form an even and stable layer in the tumor cavity, enabling sustained local release of Fus-CVs. This delivery system maximizes the specific tumor-targeting capability and microenvironment-modulating functions with the goal of effectively suppressing postoperative recurrence of OSCC while minimizing systemic adverse effects (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Reagents and machine

Cell lysis buffer for Western Blotting (WB) analysis was acquired from Ranjeck Technology Co., Ltd, (Beiiing, China). Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) supplied the protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails and the Chemiluminescence detection kit for WB, 1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine perchlorate (DiD), 3,3’-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO) and 1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR) were obtained from Biosharp (Hefei, China). Live/Dead staining kit (Calcein acetoxymethyl ester and propidium iodide probes), 2’,7’-Dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) probes, Hoechst 33,342 stain solution, DAPI (Beyotime, C1006) and mouse peripheral blood lymphocyte isolation kit were purchased from solarbio (Beijing, China). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit was supplied from BKMAM (Changde China). Monoclonal antibodies of anti-PD-L1 were obtained from Bio-X cell and RBC Lysis Buffer was obtained from CWBIO. The ultrasonic cell disruptor was sourced from Xiaomei Ultrasonic Instrument (Kunshan) Co., Ltd. Alexa Flour488/594 AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) was acquired from Beijing Emarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. 5X SDS-PAGE loading buffer (reduced) obtained from Affinibody (Wuhan, China).

Cell lines and animals

The murine OSCC cell line MOC2 (RRID: CVCL_ZD33) was sourced from AnWei-sci (Shanghai, China). MOC2 cells stably expressing PD-1and CD36 generated by electroporation by Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, China). All of the above cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The culture medium of MOC2-PD-1 and MOC2-CD36 cells were additionally supplemented with 4 µg/ml puromycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C under a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere for optimal growth. All cell lines involved in the experiments were routinely tested and found to be negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks of age) purchased from Guangzhou Zhiyuan Life Science company (Guangzhou, China) were used in this research. A specific pathogen‑free (SPF) environment was maintained for the mice, with a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animals received sterilized drinking water and standard rodent chow ad libitum. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of Southern Medical University and were conducted in accordance with its relevant guidelines. The maximum allowable tumor volume was 2000 mm³. When tumors reached approximately 1500 mm3, the mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation in accordance with institutional policies.

Isolation and harvest of PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs

The expression of PD-1 and CD36 in transfected MOC2 cells were confirmed by IF staining and flow cytometry. Subsequently, cellular vesicles (CVs) were prepared from the two cell populations. Briefly, cells were subjected to hypotonic lysis and sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 30 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was collected and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 35 min at 4 ℃ to obtain a pellet of membrane vesicles. The pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing protease inhibitors, and the resulting suspension was passed through a micro-extruder to obtain nano‑sized CVs [28].

Preparation and characterization of Fus-CVs

After generating Original-CVs, PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs, the Fus-CVs were fabricated according to a protocol [29]. For storage stability evaluation, Fus-CVs were aliquoted and stored at -20 °C or -80 °C, with each aliquot thawed only once prior to analysis. The PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs were mixed (protein weight ratio of 1:1, 1:2, 2:1,1:4), after 5 min of sonication, the samples were passed through 100‑nm pores using a mini extruder. The preparation of Fus-CVs was monitored by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The morphologies of Fus-CVs were visualized by a transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai F20, the United States FEI). The TEM samples were negatively stained with uranyl acetate. Co‑localization analysis was also performed to determine whether PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs were fused. Before the extruding, PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs were labeled with DiO and DiD, respectively. Following fusion, the resulting Fus-CVs were mounted in glycerol and imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica). For quantitative colocalization analysis at the single‑vesicle level, the Coloc2 plugin in Fiji was used to calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient between DiO (PD‑1) and DiD (CD36) signals. A total of 20 individual vesicles were randomly selected for analysis. The DLS, and zeta potential experiments were determined by Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern Instruments).

SDS-PAGE and WB analysis of CVs

As for sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Original-CVs, PD-1-CVs, CD36-CVs and Fus-CVs were added to the protein extraction buffer. The protein concentration was measured using the bicinchoninic acid kit (Epizyme, Shanghai, China). All samples were heated at 95 ℃ for 10 min, and 20 µg of each sample was applied to a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. The protein samples were electrophoresed at 60 V for 30 min and then at 120 V for 1 h. Finally, Coomassie blue staining was performed on the gel for 4 h and destained overnight before the observation. In WB experiments, denatured protein samples were resolved on a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. The segregated proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA), blocked with 5% (w/v) skimmed milk at 25 ℃ for 1 h, and incubated with PD-1 and CD36 primary antibodies, respectively (PD-1 from Proteintech, Catalogue number: 66220-1-Ig, WB: 1:5000 dilution; CD36 from Cell Signaling Technology, Catalogue number: 28109, WB: 1:1000 dilution) at 4 ℃ overnight, and further incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beijing Emarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). After incubation, membranes were then visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Biosharp, Chongqing, China).

Cell uptake

Uptake of PD-1-CVs, CD36-CVs and Fus-CVs by MOC2 cells, MOC2 cells were planted in 12-well plates overnight, and DiO-labeled CVs were added cells for 2 h and then were digested and the fluorescence intensity of DiO was measured by flow cytometry.

Cell targeting and binding assay of PD-1-CVs

Following established protocols [30]. To assess the targeting capacity of PD-1-CVs, MOC2 cells were seeded in confocal dishes to culture for 24 h. DiD was used to label PD-1-CVs or free-CVs derived from wild-type MOC2 cells (100 µg, protein weight) were added to a medium and co-incubated for 2 h. Alternatively, cells were incubated with anti-PD-L1 antibody (20 µg/mL) for 4 h before PD-1-CVs were introduced to the culture medium. Next, cells were fixed using paraformaldehyde for 15 min and incubated with DAPI at 25 °C for 10 min. Afterwards, cells were cleaned using PBS three times and monitored under the confocal microscope (Leica). Additionally, flow cytometry was also utilized to assess the targeting of PD-1-CVs to MOC2 cells. First, MOC2 cells were cultured in a 12-well plate overnight. Then, cells were co-cultured with DiO-labeled PD-1-CVs (200 µg, protein weight) for 2 h. Or anti-PD-L1 antibody (20 µg/mL) was introduced to the culture medium for 4 h before adding CVs to the culture medium, the fluorescence intensity of DiO was measured by flow cytometry. For the binding assay of PD-1-CVs and Fus-CVs, MOC2 cells were planted in a 12-well plate overnight. DiO-labeled PD-1-CVs or Fus-CVs (100 µg, protein weight or 133 µg, protein weight) was added to the cell culture medium, respectively and co-incubated for 2 h. Next, cells were fixed using paraformaldehyde for 15 min and blocked with goat serum for 30 min. After that, cells were incubated with an anti-PD-L1 antibody (0.4 µg/mL) overnight at 4 °C, and Alexa Flour 594 AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) was incubated for 20 min. Finally, Cells were DAPI-stained (10 min, 25 °C) and imaged by confocal microscopy (Leica).

Cell toxicity assay

MOC2 and human oral keratinocytes (HOK) cells were seeded in 6‑well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then treated with PBS, PD‑1‑CVs, CD36‑CVs, or Fus‑CVs (all at 50 µg/mL) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using Calcein‑AM/PI live/dead staining and visualized by confocal microscopy (Leica).

Binding assay of Fus‑CVs to OxLDL

To evaluate the binding capacity of Fus‑CVs to OxLDL, Fus‑CVs and Original‑CVs were labeled with DiO prior to the assay. DiI‑labeled OxLDL (DiI-OxLDL) was incubated with DiO‑labeled Fus‑CVs or Original‑CVs in PBS at 37 °C for 4 h with gentle shaking. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove unbound DiI-OxLDL, and the pellets were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in PBS. The samples were then mounted onto glass slides and imaged under a confocal laser scanning microscope. Colocalization of DiO (green) and DiI (red) signals was analyzed to assess the binding affinity of Fus‑CVs to OxLDL. Original‑CVs incubated with DiI‑OxLDL under the same conditions served as a negative control.

In vitro T cell activation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement and cytokine secretion

MOC2 cells (2 × 10⁶ per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the backs of C57BL/6 mice. Seven days later, mice were euthanized, and spleens or draining lymph nodes were collected. Tissues were homogenized and filtered through a 70‑µm cell strainer (Becton & Dickinson) to obtain single‑cell suspensions. To obtain T cells, splenocytes were isolated using mouse peripheral blood lymphocyte isolation kit, and red blood cells were removed with red blood cell lysis buffer [29]. The T cells were seeded into 12‑well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and activated in vitro with anti‑CD3 (1.5 µg/mL, BD Biosciences) and anti‑CD28 (1 µg/mL, BD Biosciences) for 48 h [31]. Activated T cells were then co‑cultured with MOC2 cells at a T cell‑to‑tumor cell ratio of 2:1 in the presence of OxLDL (25 µg/mL, Bioss) and treated with Original‑CVs, PD‑1‑CVs, CD36‑CVs, or Fus‑CVs at various concentrations (0, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL) to determine the optimal working concentration; 50 µg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments based on the dose‑response results for 24 h. After co-incubation, only T cells were collected for intracellular ROS measurement, which was measured using DCFH‑DA probes and analyzed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. Moreover, cytokine secretion was measured by flow cytometry.

T cell functional assessment

After T cell activation and treatment with the indicated CVs as described above, T cells and tumor cells were harvested for flow cytometric analysis.

Ex vivo imaging

A subcutaneous tumor model was established in female C57BL/6 mice by injecting 1.4 × 10⁶ MOC2 cells resuspended in serum‑free medium into the dorsal region. 14 days later, the mice were randomly divided into two groups and intravenously injected via caudal intravenous injection with either free DiR or DiR‑labeled Fus‑CVs (100 µg Fus‑CVs in 100 µL PBS per mouse) [29]. After 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney), together with tumor tissues were collected for ex vivo imaging using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Lumina I, Revvity).

Preparation, characterization, sprayability and adhesion of the sprayable hydrogel

The preparation and characterization of the sprayable hydrogel were performed as follows. First, 400 mg of SA was dissolved in 15 mL of deionized water. After sonication for 15 min, the solution was continuously stirred using a magnetic stirrer with heating for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous SA solution. Methylene blue was added and mixed thoroughly, and the solution was loaded into a spray bottle. Separately, 60 mg of manganese chloride (MnCl₂) and 100 mg of calcium chloride (CaCl₂) were prepared in 15 mL of deionized water, sonicated, and transferred to another spray bottle for use as the ionic solution. The hydrogel was formed by co‑spraying the Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linking agent and the SA solution. Gel formation was observed within 15 s. To evaluate flowability and stability, the hydrogel was sprayed into different molds to generate various shapes. The hydrogel alone and hydrogel loaded with Fus-CVs were further characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS, The Czech Republic) and rheological analysis (Rheometer, Thermo Scientific Haake Mars40, France).

The sprayability and nozzle compatibility of the hydrogel precursor solutions were assessed using a commercially available spray bottle. The SA solution and the Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linking agent were loaded into separate spray bottles and sprayed onto a target surface; no clogging or obstruction was observed during or after spraying. To evaluate adhesion, the two solutions were co‑sprayed onto the inner surface of a Petri dish. After gelation, the dish was inverted.

Encapsulation efficiency of Fus‑CVs in the hydrogel

Fus‑CVs were labeled with DiD prior to hydrogel preparation. The labeled Fus‑CVs were mixed with SA solution at a known concentration, followed by co‑spraying with Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linking agent to form the gel. Immediately after gelation, the supernatant was collected, and its fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader to quantify the unencapsulated (free) Fus‑CVs. The fluorescence intensity was compared with a standard curve generated from known concentrations of DiD‑labeled Fus‑CVs. Confocal imaging of Fus‑CVs distribution in the hydrogel

To visualize the distribution of Fus‑CVs within the hydrogel, Fus‑CVs were labeled with DiO prior to hydrogel preparation. The labeled Fus‑CVs were mixed with SA solution and then co‑sprayed with Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linking agent to form the gel. After gelation, the hydrogel was immersed in 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C overnight, then embedded in OCT compound, frozen at -20 °C, and sectioned into 50‑µm‑thick slices using a cryostat. The sections were mounted onto glass slides and imaged under a confocal laser scanning microscope to examine the distribution of DiO‑labeled Fus‑CVs within the hydrogel matrix.

In vitro release of Fus‑CVs from the hydrogel

The in vitro release profile of Fus‑CVs from the hydrogel was evaluated by incubating the hydrogel (containing 600 µL of gel with approximately 120 µg of Fus‑CVs) in 900 µL of PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C with gentle shaking at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h), 300 µL aliquots of the release medium were collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The amount of Fus‑CVs released at each time point was quantified by measuring the fluorescence intensity of DiO‑labeled Fus‑CVs in the collected medium using a fluorescence microplate reader, and compared with a standard curve. For comparison, free Fus‑CVs (equivalent to 120 µg) in 900 µL of PBS were also evaluated under the same conditions. Cumulative release was calculated and expressed as a percentage of the total loaded amount. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± SD.

In vivo degradation of the hydrogel

The in vivo degradation of the sprayable hydrogel was evaluated in C57 mice bearing subcutaneous tumors, using the same model as described in the in vivo recurrence study. Briefly, the hydrogel (prepared as described above) was sprayed onto the tumor resection site of anesthetized mice using the spray system, followed by wound closure. At predetermined time points (days 0, 1, 3, and 5), mice were euthanized, and the residual hydrogel at the tumor resection site was visually examined for its macroscopic appearance and integrity. The degradation behavior was recorded by photography.

In vivo biosafety evaluation of Fus‑CVs and Fus‑CVs‑Loaded sprayable hydrogel

To evaluate the biosafety of Fus‑CVs, female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, n = 4 per group) were intravenously injected with PBS or Fus‑CVs twice weekly. Body weight was monitored every two days. After 28 days, mice were euthanized, and blood samples were collected for hematological and serum biochemical analysis. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and followed by H&E staining. Scanning of the histological sections was performed with an Aperio VERSA slide scanner (Leica, Germany).

To evaluate the biosafety of the Fus‑CVs‑loaded hydrogel, female C57BL/6 mice (as described above, n = 4 per group) were used. Following a skin incision on the back, the Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linking agent and SA aqueous (with or without Fus‑CVs) were co‑sprayed onto the wound bed. The incisions were then closed with sutures. Body weight was recorded every two days throughout the 14‑day observation period. Mice were subsequently euthanized, and blood samples as well as major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for hematological analysis and H&E staining.

Immunogenicity evaluation

To assess the immunogenicity of the Fus‑CVs‑loaded hydrogel, healthy C57 mice were treated with either the blank hydrogel (hydrogel alone) or the Fus‑CVs‑loaded hydrogel via local spraying onto the subcutaneous space using the spray system, followed by wound closure. Blood samples were collected, and serum was separated by centrifugation. The levels of immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, and IgA) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the institutional guidelines for animal care and use.

In vivo antitumor immunotherapy efficacy of Fus-CVs

For the primary tumor model, 1 × 10⁶ MOC2 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the dorsal region of female C57BL/6 mice [32]. On day 8 post‑inoculation, tumor‑bearing mice were randomly assigned to five groups: G1 (PBS), G2 (PD‑1‑CVs), G3 (CD36‑CVs), G4 (PD‑1‑CVs + CD36‑CVs) and G5 (Fus‑CVs). Each group received a total of three doses of the corresponding treatments via caudal intravenous injection over one week: PBS, PD‑1‑CVs (75 µg per mouse), CD36‑CVs (75 µg per mouse), an equivalent mixture of PD‑1‑CVs and CD36‑CVs, or Fus‑CVs (150 µg per mouse). Tumor volumes were measured every other day using a caliper, and body weights were recorded accordingly.

A postoperative recurrence model of OSCC was established in female C57BL/6 mice. Briefly, a subcutaneous tumor model was first generated by injecting 1 × 10⁶ MOC2 cells. On day 8 post‑inoculation, mice were randomly assigned into six groups. As follows: G1 (untreated control), G2 (hydrogel alone), G3 (hydrogel loaded with PD‑1‑CVs), G4 (hydrogel loaded with CD36‑CVs), G5 (hydrogel loaded with PD-1-CVs + CD36-CVs) and G6 (hydrogel loaded with Fus‑CVs). Under anesthesia, the primary tumors were incompletely resected, leaving behind approximately 1% of the original tumor tissue to mimic recurrence [33]. The Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linking agent and SA aqueous were applied simultaneously onto the wound bed via spraying. The SA aqueous was loaded with PBS, PD‑1‑CVs (75 µg per mouse), CD36‑CVs (75 µg per mouse), an equivalent mixture of PD‑1‑CVs and CD36‑CVs, or Fus‑CVs (150 µg per mouse), depending on the group assignment. The incisions were then closed with sutures. Tumor volumes were measured every other day using a caliper, and body weights were recorded accordingly.

Mice from the primary or recurrence tumor models were euthanized on day 16 or day 17, respectively. Tumor tissues were collected for flow cytometry, IF, immunohistochemistry. Besides, tumor tissues from the primary model were subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA‑seq) and WB analysis.

RNA‑seq

Total RNA was extracted from three tumor tissue samples from each of the G1 and G5 groups by NovoTech. After quality assessment, qualified RNA samples were then subjected to library preparation and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Differentially expressed genes were identified based on a threshold of |fold change| ≥ 2 and adjusted P < 0.05, followed by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis to identify significantly enriched signaling pathways. The results were further validated by WB.

WB for primary tumor model

Ex vivo tumor tissues were weighed, homogenized, and filtered through 70 μm and 40 μm cell strainers. The resulting samples were lysed in a strong lysis buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors. After lysis, 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (reduced) was added, the samples were denatured by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Protein samples containing 10% 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer were separated by electrophoresis, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes in an ice‑bath. Membranes were blocked with protein free rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme) for 10 min at 37 °C with shaking. Overnight primary antibody incubation (4 °C) was then performed, followed by TBST washing and secondary antibody incubation. Membranes were then visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit. The following antibodies were employed in this study: Beta Actin Monoclonal Antibody (Proteintech, Catalogue number: 66009-1-Ig, 1:20000 dilution); IFN-γ (Affinity, Catalogue number: DF6045, 1:1000 dilution); STAT1 (Abmart, Catalogue number: T55227, 1:1000 dilution); pSTAT1(Zenbio, Catalogue number: 340797, 1:1000 dilution); STAT3 (Proteintech, Catalogue number: 60199-1-Ig, 1:500 dilution); pSATA3 (Abmart, Catalogue number: T56566, 1:2000 dilution); PD-L1 (Abmart, Catalogue number: M033179, 1:1000 dilution).

Immunofluorescence (IF) and Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Tumor tissues were fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Deparaffinization and antigen retrieval procedures were per-formed on paraffin-embedded histological specimens. Primary antibodies included CD4 (Servicebio, Catalogue number: GB15064, IF: 1:400 dilution); CD8 (Servicebio, Catalogue number: GB15068, IF: 1:400 dilution). TIM-3 (Proteintech, Catalogue number:11872-1-AP; IF: 1:200 dilution); IFN-γ (Affinity, Catalogue number: DF6045, IF/IHC: 1:50 dilution), TNF-α (Affinity, Catalogue number: AF7014, IF/IHC:1:100 dilution); PCNA (Cell signaling technology, IHC: Catalogue number: 13,110, IHC: 1:8000 dilution); GZMB (Affinity, Catalogue number: AF0175, IHC:1:100 dilution). IF images were acquired by a confocal microscope (Leica), and a digital pathology scanner (Leica) was used to capture IHC images.

Flow cytometric analysis

For in vitro flow cytometry, following co-incubation, T cells and tumor cells were collected for flow cytometric analysis, as described above. Cells were first stained for surface markers, then cells were restimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (MultiScience, Hangzhou, China) for 6 h, followed by fixation, permeabilization with Fixation & Permeabilization Kit (MultiScience, Hangzhou China), and intracellular cytokine staining. Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Fus-CVs

Fus-CVs were prepared through two steps: (1) generation of engineered MOC2 murine OSCC cells stably expressing EGFP-tagged PD-1 and CD36 via electroporation, followed by isolation of CVs (PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs); and (2) fusion of the two vesicle populations. Expression of PD-1 and CD36 on MOC2 cells was confirmed by IF staining and flow cytometry (Fig. 1A-B). Subsequently, the above two types of cells were collected. The intracellular contents were removed through hypotonic lysis, repeated freezing and thawing, ultrasonic disruption, and gradient centrifugation. The CVs were obtained by squeezing with a microextruder [30]. In the preparation of Fus-CVs, the two extracted CVs were pre-mixed, then mechanically disrupted by an ultrasonic disintegrator and repeatedly squeezed by a micro-extractor, ultimately forming Fus-CVs. The main preparation processes of PD-1-CVs, CD36-CVs and Fus-CVs are shown in Fig. 1C.

To ensure the successful preparation of the Fus-CVs, we labeled PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs with DiO and DiD respectively, and mixed them in different proportions. The results of confocal microscopy imaging showed that in each proportion group, both CVs presented varying degrees of fusion (Fig. S1). When the protein mass ratio was 1:1, the fluorescence overlap was the highest (Fig. 1D, Fig. S1). Additionally, the SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis results indicated that Fus-CVs are similar protein bands to PD-1-CVs and CD36-CVs (Fig. S2). Further analysis by WB revealed that Fus-CVs could detect PD-1 and CD36 protein bands, successfully inheriting the protein markers of the above two types of CVs, thereby confirming the successful preparation of Fus-CVs (Fig. 1E). Single‑vesicle colocalization analysis further revealed a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.92 ± 0.01 between PD‑1 and CD36 signals, confirming their coexistence on the same individual vesicles (Fig. S3A-C). TEM and DLS analysis showed that Fus-CVs presented a typical double-layer membrane structure, with an average particle size of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 1F-G). The particle sizes and zeta potential of the three types of CVs were also measured (Fig. 1H-I, S4A-B). Notably, These CVs remained stable in the buffer for 14 days, demonstrating favorable stability (Fig. 1J, S4B). Collectively, these results confirm the successful preparation and comprehensive characterization of Fus-CVs.

Targeting and binding of Fus-CVs in vitro and in vivo

CD8⁺ T cells serve as the primary effector cells within the tumor immune microenvironment. During activation, they upregulate inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 to prevent excessive immune responses [34]. Engagement of PD-1 with PD-L1, which is highly expressed on tumor cells, suppresses T cell function and facilitates immune evasion [35]. In this study, flow cytometry first confirmed that tumor cells can efficiently internalize CVs via homotypic targeting (Fig. S5). Figure 2A shows a schematic illustration of PD-1-CVs and Fus-CVs targeting and specifically binding to PD‑L1 via PD-1. To assess the targeting capability of the engineered PD-1-CVs toward PD-L1, DiD-labeled PD-1-CVs were incubated with MOC2 cells for 2 h. Confocal microscopy revealed that, compared with free CVs derived from wild-type MOC2 cells, PD-1-CVs exhibited significantly enhanced targeting efficiency and binding affinity to MOC2 cells (Fig. 2B). To assess binding specificity, MOC2 cells were pretreated with a PD-L1 blocker for 4 h prior to incubation with PD-1-CVs. Fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry revealed that PD-L1 blockade markedly reduced PD-1-CVs binding to MOC2 cells, whereas the blocking effect was barely detectable in PBS pretreatment group (Fig. 2C-D, S6), indicating that PD-1-CVs specifically target and bind PD-L1 on MOC2 cells. To further visualize this interaction and confirm that Fus-CVs retain the PD-1 protein from PD-1-CVs, DiO-labeled Fus-CVs were added to co-incubation with MOC2 cells, after cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, incubated with PD‑L1 antibody overnight at 4 °C, and then processed for IF. Co-localization of Fus-CVs with PD-L1 was observed (Fig. 2E), confirming that PD-1 on Fus-CVs specifically binds to PD-L1 on tumor cell membranes.

Furthermore, to evaluate the targeting ability of Fus-CVs in tumor tissues in vivo and distribution in major organs, this study used Fus-CVs prepared by DiR pre-labeling and free DiR, and injected them into MOC2 tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice via the caudal intravenous injection. The mice were euthanized at 24 h post‑injection, excised tumor tissues and major organs were subjected to ex vivo imaging using in vivo imaging system (Fig. 2F). The fluorescence enrichment of Fus-CVs in tumor tissues and quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis suggested that compared with the free DiR group, DiR-Fus-CVs group was more significant, and in terms of major organs, distribution characteristics of Fus-CVs were similar to those of the free DiR group, predominantly accumulated in the liver, spleen, and lungs (Fig. 2G-H). In conclusion, the above results indicate that the Fus-CVs have an effective tumor-targeting ability in vivo.

Biocompatibility of Fus-CVs in vitro and in vivo

Although engineered membranes have been reported to exhibit negligible toxicity [36], their toxicity and biosafety profiles must be thoroughly evaluated as a new type of antitumor nanomaterial. Therefore, MOC2 and HOK cells in vitro were incubated with PD-1-CVs, CD36-CVs, or Fus-CVs, followed by live/dead cell staining for cytotoxicity. None of CVs exhibited direct cytotoxic effect towards either cell type, indicating favorable cell viability (Fig. S7). Biosafety of Fus-CVs in vivo was assessed in healthy C57 mice that received two intravenous injections of either Fus-CVs or PBS. Body weights were recorded every two days over a 4-week observation period, with no significant intergroup differences observed (Fig. S8). On day 28, peripheral blood was collected for complete blood count and serum biochemistry, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested for H&E staining. No notable abnormalities were detected in hematological or biochemical parameters (Fig. S9), nor were any pathological changes noted in organs (Fig. S10). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Fus-CVs possess favorable biosafety profiles both in vitro and in vivo.

Fus-CVs enhance T cell antitumor effect in vitro

CD8⁺ T cells in the TME undergo adaptive metabolic changes driven by deregulated lipid metabolism, characterized by upregulation of CD36 to facilitate lipid uptake, which in turn elevates intracellular ROS levels and enhances CD8⁺ T cell lipid peroxidation, thereby compromising T cell function [19, 37]. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated the impact of Fus-CVs on T cell antitumor effect in vitro after supplementation with exogenous oxidized lipids (Fig. 3A). T cells isolated from draining lymph nodes of tumor-bearing C57 mice were activated for 48 h and co-cultured with MOC2 cells in the medium containing OxLDL. The co-cultures were then treated with PBS, PD-1-CVs, CD36-CVs, or Fus-CVs (at 50 µg/mL based on a concentration‑dependent study; Fig. S11). After 24 h, T cells were collected for flow cytometric analysis of ROS levels. The results showed that treatment with CD36-CVs or Fus-CVs significantly downregulated ROS levels compared with the PBS group (Fig. 3B), a finding further supported by fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3C). To explore the underlying mechanism, we performed a fluorescence binding assay, which confirmed that Fus‑CVs directly bind OxLDL via CD36, whereas Original‑CVs exhibited negligible binding (Fig. S12). Consistently, flow cytometry revealed that Fus-CVs treatment enhanced the secretion of the antitumor cytokine IFN-γ and TNF in CD8⁺ T cells, indicating alleviation of CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction caused by lipid metabolic abnormalities (Fig. 3D-E). These results align with the notion that reversing lipid‑induced metabolic impairment restores T cell effector functions [38]. To further assess the in vitro cytotoxic activity of CD8⁺ T cells, GFP-labeled MOC2 cells were co-cultured with activated T cells and treated with the indicated original CVs, PD-1-CVs, CD36-CVs, or Fus-CV under OxLDL presence. Flow cytometry revealed that Fus-CVs enhanced the killing capacity of CD8⁺ T cells against tumor cells, concomitantly elevating the expression of IFN-γ (Fig. 3F-G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Fus-CVs reverse T cell exhaustion, thereby potentiating CD8⁺ T cell-mediated tumor killing and effectively enhancing antitumor immune responses in vitro.

In vivo antitumor immunotherapy efficacy of Fus-CVs

Building on the in vitro evidence that Fus-CVs enhance T cell effector function, the in vivo antitumor efficacy of Fus-CVs was next evaluated in a subcutaneous MOC2 tumor model established in C57 mice. On day 8 post-inoculation, tumor-bearing mice were randomized into five groups: G1 (PBS), G2 (PD-1-CVs), G3 (CD36-CVs), G4 (PD-1-CVs + CD36-CVs), and G5 (Fus-CVs). Each group received a total of three doses of the corresponding formulation via caudal intravenous injection over one week. Tumor growth was monitored by measuring tumor volume and body weight. Mice were euthanized on day 16, and tumor tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis (Fig. 4A-B). Ex vivo tumor image and showed that Fus-CVs group exhibited the smallest tumor volume, followed by G4, whereas G2 and G3 showed modest antitumor effects, suggesting that Fus-CVs may exert their therapeutic activity through specific targeting and modulation of lipid metabolic reprogramming in the TME (Fig. S13). The tendency of the volume of the isolated tumor tissues was consistent with the in vivo tumor growth curves (Fig. S14, 4 C), confirming the significant tumor growth inhibition by Fus-CVs. Body weight measurements revealed no significant differences among groups during the 16‑day observation period (Fig. S15), further supporting the favorable biosafety profile of Fus-CVs.

Subsequently, tumor tissues were dissociated and processed for flow cytometric analysis of immune cell populations (Fig. 4D), the gating strategy is depicted in Figure S16. Compared with the PBS group, all treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of increased T cell infiltration within the TME (Fig. 4E). Specifically, CD4⁺ T cells were significantly elevated across treatment groups, whereas CD8⁺ T cell infiltration was highest in the G5 group relative to PBS and the other vesicle‑treated groups (Fig. 4F). Conversely, the expression of PD‑1 was decreased in the G5 group, with mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) substantially higher in the PBS group (Fig. 4G), suggesting that Fus‑CVs mitigate CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion. In addition, myeloid‑derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which play a critical immunosuppressive role in the TME [39], were downregulated in the G5 group relative to the other groups, indicating disruption of the immune-suppressive microenvironment (Fig. 4H). Increased proportions of splenic CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells were also observed in the Fus‑CVs group compared to the PBS control (Fig. S17).

Additionally, tumor tissue sections underwent IHC and IF staining. The immunohistochemistry results revealed that the proliferative marker PCNA was most highly expressed in the G1 group and lowest in the G5 group, further confirming that tumor growth was significantly inhibited, the cytotoxic factors IFN-γ, TNF-α, together with GZMB, which are predominantly secreted by effector T cell subsets, were most abundant in the G5 group, scarcely detectable in the G1 group, and were present at lower levels in the other vesicle-treated groups compared with G5 group (Fig. 4I). IF staining revealed co-localization of IFN-γ and TNF-α with CD8⁺ T cells, with the most pronounced co-localization signals observed in the G5 group (Fig. 4J), further validating the infiltration of effector T cell subsets within the TME. Additionally, co-localization analysis of the exhaustion marker TIM-3 with CD8⁺ T cells showed the merged signal was the least in the G5 group, intermediate levels in other groups, and the highest signals in the G1 group (Fig. 4K). Thus, the high TIM‑3 co‑localization in the PBS group indicates that the observable CD8⁺ T cells were predominantly exhausted. In summary, these results demonstrate that Fus-CVs promote effective CD8⁺ T cell infiltration into the TME, enhance their secretion of cytotoxic factors and GZMB, thereby reversing T cell exhaustion and potentiating antitumor immunity in vivo.

Fus-CVs counteract tumor immune evasion via the IFN-γ–STAT pathway

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism responsible for the Fus-CVs-mediated enhancement of T cell function, RNA sequencing was performed on tumor tissues from the G1 and G5 groups. Correlation analysis was first conducted on three samples per group (Fig. 5A). Differential expression analysis, visualized by volcano plot and heatmap, revealed 146 upregulated and 117 downregulated genes in the G5 group compared with the G1 group (Fig. 5B-C). Subsequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes identified significant enrichment in pathways including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, antigen processing and presentation, PD-L1 expression and the PD-1 checkpoint pathway (Fig. 5D). Notably, expression of IFN-γ and STAT family members differed markedly between the two groups. Previous studies have established that IFN-γ exerts dual effects in the TME, which enhances CD8⁺ T cell effector function and promotes tumor killing, yet excessive IFN-γ can bind to IFNGR on tumor cells, inducing STAT1 phosphorylation (pSTAT1) and nuclear translocation, thereby upregulating PD-L1 expression and facilitating immune evasion [40, 41]. In the sequencing data of this study, however, differentially expressed genes were not enriched for PD‑L1, suggesting that PD‑L1 expression may be regulated by other genes to maintain relative stability. Our previous studies have shown that other STAT family members, such as STAT3, can regulate TME in OSCC [42], while STAT3 a key regulator of PD‑L1 in the TME [43]. Consequently, we hypothesized that upon Fus‑CVs treatment, reduced phosphorylation of STAT3 leads to decreased nuclear translocation of pSTAT3, thereby counteracting the potential upregulation of PD‑L1 induced by excessive IFN‑γ secretion and resulting in stable PD‑L1 expression before and after treatment (Fig. 5E). This hypothesis was subsequently validated by WB analysis (Fig. 5F). Compared with the G1 group, the G5 group exhibited increased IFN‑γ expression, along with elevated levels of its downstream effectors STAT1 and pSTAT1. In contrast, STAT3 and pSTAT3 levels were reduced in G5, with PD‑L1 expression remaining comparable to that in G1. In the G1 group, although the decreased expression of IFN-γ might reduce PD-L1 expression, the sustained expression of STAT3 and pSTAT3 maintained PD‑L1 levels. Conversely, the reduced IFN‑γ secretion in G1 led to decreased caspase 3 levels and reduced tumor cell apoptosis compared with the G5 group (Fig. S18), resulting in markedly diminished T cell‑mediated tumor killing. Collectively, these findings indicate that Fus‑CVs enhance effector T cell‑mediated antitumor immune response in vivo, at least in part by modulating the IFN‑γ–STAT signaling axis.

Preparation, characterization and performance evaluation of Fus‑CVs‑loaded sprayable hydrogel

Having demonstrated the antitumor efficacy and safety of systemically administered Fus‑CVs in the subcutaneous tumor model, we next sought to develop a locally deliverable formulation to address postoperative recurrence. Accordingly, a Fus‑CVs‑loaded sprayable hydrogel was designed for sustained local release at the tumor resection site. The sprayability of this system was first validated: the formulation could be successfully sprayed through a commercial nozzle without clogging, and the sprayed gel adhered firmly to an inverted Petri dish, confirming strong adhesion (Fig. S19A-B). The preparation of the sprayable hydrogel involved two main steps: (1) material pretreatment, including the preparation of SA and the ionic solution (Ca2+, Mn2+), and (2) hydrogel dispensing, in which Ca2+/Mn2+ cross-linking agent and SA solutions were loaded into separate spray bottles and subsequently sprayed to form the gel (Fig. 6A). To enable visualization, methylene blue was premixed with the SA solution prior to loading into the spray bottle containing the Mn2+ aqueous, as both solutions are colorless. Upon spraying, although gelation was achieved, the process suffered from slow kinetics and poor mechanical integrity (Fig. 6B-C). Therefore, introducing Ca2+ to form a Ca2+/Mn2+ cross-linking agent, followed by reaction with the SA solution, promoted rapid gelation and superior mechanical integrity, as evidenced by the ability to lift the gel intact with forceps without breakage (Fig. 6D). The Ca²⁺: Mn²⁺ ratio was selected to achieve optimal gelation performance, also considering the reported immune‑activating property of Mn²⁺ as a cGAS‑STING agonist [44]. After that, the two solutions were sprayed onto various molds to evaluate the flowability and stability of the resulting hydrogels. The gel could be fully retrieved from all molds, maintaining a well-defined and intact structure (Fig. 6E). Rheological analysis was employed to further characterize the mechanical properties of the Fus-CVs-loaded hydrogel system and hydrogel alone, revealing excellent elasticity, stable gel-state maintenance, and shape recovery after stress testing (Fig. 6F, S20A), and SEM revealed well-defined three-dimensional reticular structures (Fig. 6G, S20B). These results collectively confirm that the optimized Ca²⁺/Mn²⁺ cross‑linked SA hydrogel possesses favorable physicochemical and mechanical properties for localized application.

Having established the robust gel performance, we next evaluated its capacity for Fus‑CVs encapsulation and sustained release. The encapsulation efficiency of Fus‑CVs in the hydrogel reached 99.91% ± 0.56% (Fig. S21), indicating efficient vesicle entrapment. The loading amount of Fus‑CVs in the hydrogel was approximately 120 µg per 600 µL gel (equivalent to 200 µg/mL). Confocal imaging of the hydrogel sections revealed homogeneous distribution of Fus‑CVs throughout the gel matrix without obvious aggregation (Fig. S22). In vitro release study showed that free Fus‑CVs released rapidly, reaching nearly 90% within 0.5 h under the same conditions (Fig. S23A). In contrast, when encapsulated in the hydrogel, Fus‑CVs were liberated in a sustained manner over 5 days, with a cumulative release of approximately 30% at day 5 (Fig. S23B), confirming the sustained release capability of the hydrogel. In vivo macroscopic observation at the tumor resection site further revealed that the hydrogel remained visibly intact at days 0, 1, and 3, whereas degradation was observed at day 5 (Fig. S24), demonstrating its biodegradable nature. Together, these data indicate that the Fus‑CVs‑loaded sprayable hydrogel not only achieves high payload retention but also affords a sustained and degradable release platform.

Having confirmed the sustained release and biodegradability, we next investigated the biosafety of the Fus‑CVs‑loaded hydrogel in healthy C57 mice. SA solution premixed with either Fus‑CVs or PBS was co‑sprayed with Ca2+/Mn2+ cross-linking agent into the subcutaneous space of anesthetized mice, followed by wound closure. Body weight was monitored every two days over the 14‑day observation period. Two weeks post‑administration, both the hydrogel alone and Fus-CVs-loaded hydrogel groups showed no significant differences in body weight (Fig. S25). Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for H&E staining, which revealed no obvious pathological changes (Fig. 6H). Peripheral blood analysis showed that all hematological and biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges (Fig. S26). In addition, serum immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgM, and IgA) were measured to evaluate immunogenicity, showing no significant elevation compared with the control group (Fig. S27). Together, these results confirm that the hydrogel alone, as well as the Fus‑CVs‑loaded hydrogel, exhibits favorable biosafety profiles and low immunogenicity, supporting their translational potential for postoperative recurrence prevention.

Potent anti-recurrence efficacy of the Fus-CVs-loaded sprayable hydrogel in vivo

Sprayable hydrogels offer distinct advantages for inhibition of postoperative tumor recurrence due to their rapid gelation, favorable stability, portability and presence of antitumor components [26]. Thus, in this study, we leveraged such a hydrogel as a local delivery platform for Fus-CVs to inhibit postoperative recurrence of OSCC. We established postoperative OSCC recurrence model in C57 mice using MOC2 cells. On day 8 post‑inoculation, tumor‑bearing mice were randomly divided into six groups and underwent incomplete tumor resection, followed by local spray application of untreated control (G1), hydrogel alone (G2), hydrogel loaded with PD‑1‑CVs (G3), hydrogel loaded with CD36‑CVs (G4), hydrogel loaded with a PD‑1‑CVs + CD36‑CVs (G5), or hydrogel loaded with Fus‑CVs (G6) (Fig. 7A). After treatment, recurrent tumor growth and body weight changes were recorded at regular intervals. Mice were euthanized on day 17, and tumor tissues, draining lymph nodes, and spleens were collected for subsequent analysis. Ex vivo tumor image showed that G2 tumors were slightly smaller than those in G1, likely attributable to the potent antitumor activity of Mn2+ [45], though a Mn²⁺‑free control would be required to fully deconvolute its contribution. The G3 to G5 groups exhibited more significant tumor suppression effects compared to the G1 group, among which the G6 group (hydrogel loaded with Fus‑CVs) had the most significant effect in inhibiting tumor recurrence, with effective inhibition of tumor growth (Fig. 7B). Tumor growth curves further confirmed a significant difference between G6 and G1 (Fig. 7C). Flow cytometry revealed markedly increased proportions of CD4⁺ T and CD8⁺ T cells in draining lymph nodes of G6 mice (Fig. 7D), as well as enhanced T cell infiltration in the spleens compared with G1 (Fig. 7E), indicating systemic immune activation. IF staining demonstrated increased CD4⁺ T cell infiltration in TME and the most pronounced co‑localization of IFN‑γ and TNF‑α with CD8⁺ T cells in G6, whereas overlap signals were minimal in G1 (Fig. 7F). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed elevated expression of IFN‑γ and TNF‑α in G6, along with increased GZMB, indicating bolstered cytotoxic capacity of CD8⁺ T cells within the TME. Concurrently, the expression of PCNA was markedly reduced in G6, suggesting effective suppression of tumor proliferation (Fig. 7G). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that hydrogel‑delivered Fus‑CVs effectively inhibit tumor recurrence by promoting T cell infiltration and enhancing effector function.

Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a postoperative cancer immunotherapy using a sprayable hydrogel loaded with Fus-CVs that forms in situ at the tumor resection site, achieving dual‑target synergistic therapy that directly eliminates residual tumor cells, thereby effectively inhibiting tumor recurrence. Using engineered cell membranes as carriers, Fus-CVs preferentially accumulate at tumor sites, where PD-1 specifically engages PD-L1 on tumor cells, and CD36 enables competitive uptake of oxidized lipids within the TME, thereby alleviating CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and ultimately enhancing antitumor immunity. Meanwhile, the sprayable hydrogel enables direct enrichment of Fus‑CVs into the recurrent tumor cavity, providing sustained in situ release, thereby elevating the local concentration of Fus‑CVs while minimizing systemic adverse effects. Collectively, this Fus-CVs-loaded sprayable hydrogel system represents a promising strategy with strong potential for clinical application after tumor resection.
