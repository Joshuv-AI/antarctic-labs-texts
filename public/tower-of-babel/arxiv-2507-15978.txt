A note on the class of sober rings
Saeid Jafari, Ernesto Lax

Abstract
. We introduce the class of sober rings and investigate it through several key results, highlighting connections to some other known classes of rings. We analyze sufficient conditions for a ring to be sober, as well as necessary conditions. We also provide examples to illustrate the behavior of this property.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 13A15, Secondary 13F05, 13F10

1.
Introduction

The foundations of ring theory date back to the late 19th century, arising from the work of Richard Dedekind who first introduced the concept of ring of integers of a number field. In 1897, David Hilbert further advanced this line by introducing the term “Zahlring”, literally
number ring
, which appears for the first time in his seminal work
[
2
]
. This terminology reflected an effort to generalize arithmetic in algebraic number fields and a shift toward a more systematic classification of algebraic structures. However, the true formalization of ring theory emerged in the early 20th century, from the work of Fraenkel
[
1
]
and Emmy Noether
[
3
]
, who played decisive roles in axiomatizing the concept of
ring
. Over nearly two centuries, the notion of ring has evolved into one of the most central structures in algebra. Its development has led to a vast theory, connecting fields as diverse as number theory, algebraic geometry, and functional analysis. Within this framework, the concept of
ideal
remains crucial, allowing to define key properties and distinguish between various classes of rings.

In this note, we introduce the novel class of sober rings. The notion of soberness originates from topology, where a topological space is called
sober
if every non-empty irreducible closed subset is the closure of a unique point. This property has become a central idea in many fields apart from topology, such as algebraic geometry and category theory. Moreover, such notion has an algebraic relevance, in fact the spectrum of a commutative ring with the Zariski topology is an example of sober space. A ring
R
is called a
sober ring
if every prime non-maximal ideal of
R
is not equal to the intersection of the prime ideals properly containing it.

This work is structured as follows. In Section
2

we introduce the notion of sober ring and illustrate some preliminary results (Proposition
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
). Subsequently, sufficient conditions for a ring to be sober are also established, giving connections with other classes of rings, including semilocal rings (Proposition
2.4
). With these investigations, it is possible to find rings that are not sober, such as Jacobson rings (Proposition
2.9
). Moreover, the behavior of soberness under ring constructions, such as polynomial rings, is described. The obtained results allow us to formulate some questions, which motivate a further and in-depth study of this class of rings.

2.
Base properties of sober rings

In what follows, we will assume that
R
is a commutative ring with identity. A
prime ideal
of
R
is a proper ideal
P\subset R
such that whenever
ab\in P
, one has
a\in P
or
b\in P
. The set of all prime ideals of
R
is called the
spectrum
of
R
and denoted by
\Spec(R)
. A
maximal ideal
of
R
is a proper ideal
M\subset R
such that there not exists a proper ideal
J\subset R
such that
M\subset J\subset R
. The set of all maximal ideal of
R
will be denoted by
\Max(R)
. The intersection of all maximal ideal of
R
is called the
Jacobson radical
of
R
and denoted by
J(R)
. It is clear that every maximal ideal is also prime, hence
\Max(R)\subseteq\Spec(R)
, but in general these two sets are not equal.

Definition 2.1
.

A ring
R
is called a
sober ring
if for every ideal
P\in\Spec(R)
, with
P\notin\Max(R)
, one has

P\neq\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\Spec(R)\\
P\subsetneq Q\end{subarray}}Q.

First recall that the
Krull dimension
of a ring
R
, denoted by
\dim(R)
, is the supremum of the lengths of the finite ascending chains

P_{0}\subset P_{1}\subset\ldots\subset P_{k-1}\subset P_{k},

of prime ideals of
R
. A ring is called
zero-dimensional
if it has Krull dimension zero. In a zero-dimensional ring every prime ideal is also maximal, hence the next result follows.

Proposition 2.2
.

Every zero-dimensional ring is a sober ring.

Recall that an
Artinian ring

R
is a ring satisfying the
descending chain condition
on ideals, that is every decreasing sequence of ideals of
R
,

I_{1}\supseteq I_{2}\supseteq I_{3}\supseteq\cdots

eventually stabilizes,
i.e.
there exist an integer
m>0
such that
I_{j}=I_{m}
for all
j\geq m
. One can prove that any Artinian ring is also a zero-dimensional ring. As a consequence of Proposition
2.2
one has the following corollary.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
R
be an Artinian ring. Then
R
is a sober ring.

An Artinian ring has only finitely many maximal ideals. More in general, a ring with such a property is called a
semilocal ring
. It is natural to ask, inspired by Corollary
2.3
, whenever the semilocality of the ring guarantees the soberness. The next result gives a partial answer to this problem. In the following, unless specified, every ring will have at least Krull dimension
1
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
R
be a semilocal ring with
\dim(R)=1
. Then
R
is a sober ring.

Proof.

Since
\dim(R)=1
, every non-zero prime ideal is maximal. Hence, it suffices to show that the zero ideal is different to the intersection of the maximal ideals of
R
. Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}
be the maximal ideals of
R
and let
I=\displaystyle{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}}M_{i}
. We want to show that
I\neq(0)
. Observe that for
i=1,\ldots,n
, since
M_{i}\neq(0)
, we can choose
x_{i}\in M_{i}
such that
x_{i}\neq 0
. Therefore, set
x=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, one has
x\in I
, but
x\neq 0
. Hence,
I\neq(0)
and
R
is sober.

∎

Remark 2.5
.

Observe that the semilocality of the ring in Proposition
2.4
is a fundamental assumption. In fact, a
1
-dimensional ring with an infinite number of maximal ideals may not be sober in general.

Example 2.6
.

The ring
\mathbb{Z}
of integers is not a sober ring. In fact, in
\mathbb{Z}
every non-zero prime ideal is maximal and has the form
(p)
, with
p
a prime number. Hence, the only prime non-maximal ideal is
(0)
. Clearly

(0)=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\text{$p$ prime}\end{subarray}}(p),

hence
\mathbb{Z}
is not sober.

Recall that a Dedekind domain is an integral domain which is not a field, such that every non-zero proper ideal factors into the product of prime ideals. For a Dedekind domain
R
one has
\dim R\leq 1
.

The next result states a more general property then the one illustrated in Example
2.6
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
R
be a Dedekind domain that is not a field and suppose
J(R)=(0)
. Then
R
is not a sober ring.

Proof.

Recall that a Dedekind domain has zero Jacobson radical if and only if there are infinitely many maximal ideals in
R
. Moreover, in such a ring every non-zero prime ideal is maximal. Hence, the only prime non-maximal ideal is
(0)
, which is the intersection of all (maximal) ideals of
R
(since
J(R)
vanishes). It follows that
R
is not sober.

∎

As a striking consequence of Proposition
2.7
, we have that principal ideal domains with zero Jacobson ideal are not sober rings.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
R
be a principal ideal domain that is not a field and suppose
J(R)=(0)
. Then
R
is not a sober ring.

Proof.

It is clear that every principal ideal domain that is not a field is a Dedekind domain, then the assertion follows.

∎

By Proposition
2.4
, we have that
1
-dimensional semilocal rings are sober. Whereas, there are class of rings of dimension
1
, with infinitely many maximal ideals that are not sober (as seen in Proposition
2.7
and
2.8
). Hence, the following question arises naturally.

Question 1
.

Let
R
be a ring, with
\dim(R)=1
and infinitely many maximal ideals. Is it always true that
R
is not a sober ring?

A commutative ring
R
is called a
Jacobson ring
if every prime ideal of
R
is the intersection of maximal ideals of
R
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
R
be a commutative, Jacobson ring. Then
R
is not a sober ring.

Proof.

Observe that, since
\dim(R)\geq 1
, one has
\Spec(R)\setminus\Max(R)\neq\emptyset
.Let
P\in\Spec(R)\setminus\Max(R)
. Since
R
is a Jacobson ring,
P
is the intersection of the maximal ideals properly containing it. Hence, let
\mathcal{M}=\{M\in\Max(R)\ :\ M\supset P\}
, we have

P=\bigcap\mathcal{M}.

Let now
\mathcal{P}=\{Q\in\Spec(R)\ :\ Q\supset P\}
, the family of prime ideals properly containing
P
. It is clear that
P\subseteq\bigcap\mathcal{P}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}
, one has
\displaystyle{\bigcap\mathcal{P}\subseteq\bigcap\mathcal{M}}
.

Hence,

P\subseteq{\bigcap\mathcal{P}}\subseteq{\bigcap\mathcal{M}}=P.

Therefore,
P=\displaystyle\bigcap\mathcal{P}
and
R
is not sober.

∎

Let us now focus on polynomial rings. One may ask.

Question 2
.

Let
R
be a sober ring. Is
R[x]
also sober?

Unfortunately, the above question is not true in general, as illustrated in the next Example.

Example 2.10
.

Let
K
be a field and let
R=K[x]
.

K
is sober, since
\dim(K)=0
. But
R
is not sober, since
R
is a principal ideal domain with
J(R)=0
, by Corollary
2.8
it follows that
R
is not sober.
