Dextrocardia with complete atrioventricular block—the right clinical approach to pace a heart on the right hemithorax: a case report
Authors: Ralph Abi Nader, Mikhael Kossaify, Antoine Kossaify
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA heart positioned on the right side of the thorax can be more a complex situation than it seems, also the potentially associated congenital cardiopathies are variable. In this regard, patients with dextrocardia presenting with complete atrioventricular block require a thorough anatomical investigation to map the veno-arterial system and potential cardiovascular defects before proceeding with a permanent pacing procedure.Case presentationA 73-year-old Caucasian woman with no significant medical history presented with syncope. Physical examination showed regular heart sounds with an average heart rate of 35–40 beats per minute and no other relevant physical findings. Chest X-rays revealed a heart positioned in the right hemithorax. A standard electrocardiogram revealed complete atrioventricular block with junctional escape rhythm at nearly 35–40 beats per minute. A transthoracic echocardiogram showed a mirror image dextrocardia without any other remarkable abnormalities. Venogram performed using the left peripheral cephalic vein showed normal venous return into the right atrium positioned on the left side. Surgical intervention consisted of permanent dual chamber pacemaker implantation using the left subclavian vein. Introduction

A right positioning of the heart in the thorax can be a more complex situation than it seems, and it can be related to dextrocardia or dextroversion [1]. While the terminology might be confusing, it is important to make the difference especially when a cardiac intervention is intended. In dextrocardia, the heart apex is pointing to the right while in dextroversion, the heart apex is pointing to the left [1]. Dextroversion is often associated with extracardiac anatomical conditions that cause the shift of the heart to the midline (mesocardiac) or even to the right hemithorax; these conditions encompass right pulmonary hypoplasia, left-sided chest lesions such as tumors or diaphragmatic hernia, and so on [2].

Dextrocardia prevalence is around 1/12,000, it can be isolated (situs solitus) or associated with displacement/inversion of other organs (situs inversus) [1]. In dextrocardia with situs solitus, the heart is on the right side, while other organs are in normal positions; however it is a condition often associated with congenital heart defects. In dextrocardia with situs inversus, the heart and other organs are mirrored, and this condition is associated with fewer congenital heart defects [1]. Congenital heart defects, mostly encountered in dextrocardia with situs solitus, encompass ventricular septal defect, atrial septal defect, tricuspid and pulmonic atresia, anomalous systemic or pulmonary venous return, and transposition of the great arteries, and so on.

In patients presenting with heart rhythm disorder requiring electrophysiological study or pacing, it is essential to outline and map cardiac and great vessel anatomy to facilitate the performance of such invasive or interventional procedure given the potential anatomical variations in the main vessels (for example, transposition, anomalous venous return) and/or associated congenital defects (septal defect, tricuspid atresia, and so on) [3]. We report on a patient with dextrocardia situs inversus who presented with complete atrioventricular block and for whom a dual-chamber transvenous pacemaker was implanted successfully.

Case presentation

A 73-year-old Caucasian woman presented to the emergency room for fainting and presyncope. The patient had no previous cardiovascular condition noticeable, her main risk factors were tobacco smoking, age, and a sedentary lifestyle; also, there was no relevant medical family history. She was not on any long-term medications, except occasional analgesics for headaches. Physical examination in the emergency room revealed muffled heart sounds (left-sided auscultation), bradycardia at nearly 35 beats per minute (bpm) and blood pressure at 90/50 mmHg. Electrocardiogram (EKG; recording electrodes were placed following the routine left sided positioning) showed complete atrioventricular block with escape junctional rhythm at nearly 35 bpm (Fig. 1). Chest X-rays showed normal lung parenchyma, and right-sided heart with the apex pointing to the right evoking dextrocardia. Laboratory results were not remarkable.

Preoperative EKG (standard leads position) showing complete atrioventricular block

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) using the Philips iE33 ultrasound system (right-sided approach, basically right parasternal long axis and apical) with image acquirement while in dorsal supine or right decubitus positions revealed a mirror image dextrocardia with right cavities on the left side and left cavities on the right side, normal tricuspid valve, normal cavities dimensions, and normal left ventricular function; importantly, there were no congenital abnormalities noticeable. There were difficulties in assessing pulmonary artery pressure and systemic/pulmonary venous return, and three-dimensional echo imaging was unavailable. Further imaging (cardiac tomography, abdominal ultrasound, chest angio-MRI) were planned; however, these tests were refused by the patient, also she declined transesophageal echo.

The patient’s hemodynamic condition was still critical, and after discussing the case within the cardiac team, also after explaining to the patient and family the medical context and therapeutic options, and after acquiring their consent, a dual-chamber pacemaker implantation was planned and performed within hours after admission. This was performed using left subclavian approach, and a contrast injection in the beginning of the procedure confirmed a normal systemic venous return into the right atrium; the procedure was smooth with no peroperative remarkable incident. Figure 2 shows chest X-rays and leads positioning postimplantation; also, Fig. 3 showed EKG, postimplantation. Following the procedure, the patient’s hemodynamic condition improved and became asymptomatic, she was discharged within 24 hours after admission.

Discussion

This report highlights an exceptional case of complete atrioventricular block in a patient with mirror image dextrocardia situs inversus, for whom a successful transvenous dual-chamber pacemaker was implanted. Although rare, successful endocavitary pacing for patients with dextrocardia situs inversus has been reported [4, 5].

A clinical approach and imaging are crucial for accurate diagnosis, for providing essential anatomical data for the operator. The “muffled” cardiac sound reported in the emergency room was a consequence of left-sided auscultation in a patient with dextrocardia, so far undiagnosed as such. The main concern to address before engaging in implantation procedure is the potential cardiac or major vascular congenital abnormalities, which can make the implantation process challenging or impossible.

In cases where anatomical abnormalities make it challenging to implant a permanent pacing device via classical leads/venous route, leadless pacemaker or even epicardial approach might constitute an alternative [3, 6]. In all cases, a step-wise approach along with multimodality imaging (transesophageal echocardiography cardiac computed tomography, cardiac magnetic resonance) can provide more safety and anatomical details for the pacing procedure [7].

In this patient, chest X-rays showed the dextrocardia along with the gastric air bubble on the right side, making the diagnosis of dextrocardia situs inversus certain, also the transthoracic echocardiography did not show any noticeable cardiac abnormalities. In addition, the venogram allowed a normal systemic venous return. However, if the patient were more compliant, more imaging modalities could have provided better insights into the special patient anatomy and potential abnormalities to address potential challenges during the implantation procedure.

Limitations

An EKG was not performed with electrodes on the right side of the chest given that the diagnosis of dextrocardia was initially unconfirmed. In addition, the rapidly deteriorating condition of the patient, with the need for immediate intervention, along with the patient non-compliance, made the caring team miss this “right-sided” EKG recording. The post-operative EKG was a paced rhythm, and we judged unnecessary to show atypical QRS axis consecutive to dextrocardia, whether the recording is made from the standard leads or from the right sided leads.

Conclusion

A heart on the right side could indicate either dextrocardia or dextroversion. Dextrocardia can be either situs solitus, which is associated with high cardiac structural abnormalities, or situs inversus, which has a low rate of associated cardiac abnormalities. Cardiac pacing in dextrocardia can be challenging, and clearance for the pacing procedure should be granted after thorough identification of the cardiac and major vascular anatomy. We report on a patient with dextrocardia and situs inversus presenting with complete atrioventricular block, for whom a dual-chamber pacemaker was successfully implanted via the left subclavian access.
