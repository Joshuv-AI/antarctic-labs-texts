The relationship between professional commitment and anxiety with human error in the operating room nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jamshid Eeslami, Shiva Sadeghi Meymand, Mehdi Hasanshahi, Fereshte Parvizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe operating room is a stressful environment where stress and anxiety can lead to human errors, jeopardizing patient safety. Professional commitment is believed to play a significant role in the quality of care provided and in reducing errors. Therefore, this study aims to explore the connection between professional commitment, anxiety, and human errors among operating room nurses.MethodsIn this descriptive cross-sectional study, the research population consisted of all operating room nurses (both scrub and circulating) working in hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The sample was selected based on convenience. The instruments used included a demographic information questionnaire, the Spielberger Trait Anxiety Questionnaire, a professional commitment questionnaire, and a researcher-made questionnaire on human medical errors in the operating room. Statistical data analysis was conducted using SPSS software and descriptive statistics methods (mean and standard deviation), as well as the Spearman correlation coefficient test.ResultsSignificant associations were observed between professional commitment and human errors, as well as between anxiety and human errors among the participating operating room nurses (r = − 0.155, p = 0.011 and r = 0.168, p = 0.008, respectively). Additionally, professional commitment and anxiety appeared to be modest predictors of human errors (p < 0.001 and p = 0.013, respectively), suggesting that higher professional commitment may be related to lower levels of human error, while higher anxiety may be associated with increased errors.ConclusionThe findings of the present study indicate significant associations between professional commitment and human errors, as well as between anxiety and human errors among the participating nurses. These results suggest that interventions aimed at reducing anxiety and enhancing professional commitment may help to mitigate the risk of human errors in the operating room.

Introduction

Operating rooms are tense and stressful environments where it is essential for the surgical team members to cooperate with each other to provide safe care. However, this coordination is difficult to achieve [1–3]. Inappropriate emotional reactions, such as anxiety, have a great impact on people’s lives, leading to accidents and work mistakes [4, 5]. Stress and anxiety are factors that everyone experiences throughout their lives. One of the main sources that contribute to these feelings is work environments that may be tolerable in the short term but can lead to tension and conflict in the long run [6, 7]. Due to the presence of stressors in the hospital environment, particularly in sensitive areas like the operating room, medical staff are exposed to constant stress more than other sectors of society. Additionally, anxiety is a factor that can reduce productivity in organizations [6, 8, 9].

Anxiety is defined as a diffuse, unpleasant, and vague feeling of worry and a sense of inability to interpret important events [10]. Among the causes of anxiety, biological and physical factors, environmental changes, and genetic factors can be mentioned [11]. Research has shown that the decrease in public health levels and the increase in anxiety scores caused by the COVID-19 pandemic have led to an increase in medication errors [12]. However, patient safety and the provision of safe care services should be top priorities for service providers [13–15]. Patient safety is defined as the prevention of losses arising from mistakes during service provision, errors due to carelessness, and planned requirements to prevent the consequences of medical errors [16, 17]. Research in the field of health and treatment shows that 87% of the reasons endangering patient safety originate from human errors [18].

Human error in the medical profession refers to the failure to perform a task correctly or a mistake in planning an action that results in inactivity or an unwanted accident. This can ultimately lead to longer hospitalization durations, discharge disabilities, and even death [19, 20]. The main cause of these errors can be attributed to the high stress and complexity of nurses’ duties [21]. Research indicates that 210,000 preventable deaths have been caused by medical errors [22, 23]. Balas’s study shows that 31% of nurses are involved in at least one error within a 28-day period [24]. When these errors occur, they can result in injuries to patients [18] and medical personnel, ultimately reducing patients’ trust in healthcare facilities [22, 23].

In this regard, professional commitment is one of the important factors that determine the qualifications of service providers in the medical field [25, 26]. Professional commitment is a mental state that expresses a desire and commitment to be engaged in a specific profession [27, 28]. Studies show that nurses with higher professional commitment tend to have a greater sense of responsibility towards their duties [29, 30].

Recognizing the significance of addressing human errors and identifying the factors influencing them, and noting the absence of prior research on this topic, the current study aimed to investigate the correlation between professional commitment and anxiety levels with human errors among operating room nurses.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The present study was a cross-sectional study conducted in 2023 and 2024 at hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences in Iran. The research population consisted of all operating room nurses (scrub and circulating) employed at these hospitals. The research sample was chosen using a convenience sampling method.

To determine the sample size, a predetermined sample size calculator for multiple regression was utilized, available at https://www.danielsoper.com/statcalc/calculator.aspx?id=1. Considering a significance level of 0.05, a power of 90%, 11 independent variables, an effect size of 0.1, and the absence of similar articles for reference, a sample size of 223 was calculated. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, data from at least 268 individuals were collected.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) at least six months of experience as an operating room nurse, and (2) willingness to participate. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis.

Data collection

Following ethical approval and administrative authorization, data were collected by a trained researcher who attended operating rooms in different hospitals. After introducing himself, explaining the study objectives, and screening participants based on the inclusion criteria, eligible nurses were asked to provide written informed consent.

The researcher then provided the participants with a 4-part questionnaire that included a demographic questionnaire, a professional commitment questionnaire, a Spielberger anxiety questionnaire, and a human error questionnaire. To maximize the use of operating room capacity, sampling was conducted during different work shifts (morning, afternoon, and night). Additionally, to increase the accuracy of the study, sampling was conducted during breaks between surgeries so that the nurses could concentrate enough to respond.

Measurement tools

Demographic questionnaire

This form included demographic information including age, gender, education level, marital status, and work experience.

Professional commitment questionnaire

The Mayer, Allen, and Smith (1993) Professional Commitment Questionnaire comprises 18 questions and 3 components: affective professional commitment, normative professional commitment, and continuum professional commitment. Responses to the questionnaire items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree). The total possible scores range from 18 to 90. A score below 30 indicates low professional commitment, a score between 31 and 60 indicates moderate professional commitment, and a score above 61 indicates high professional commitment [31].In the study conducted by Poorkarimi et al., face and content validity were confirmed through expert opinions, and the questionnaire’s reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 [22].In the current study, the questionnaire’s face and content validity were confirmed, and reliability was determined using Cronbach’s alpha method: affective commitment 0.52, continuance commitment 0.72, normative commitment 0.70, and total instrument 0.81.

Trait anxiety inventory

This 20-item questionnaire was developed by Spielberger. All questions have a 4-point Likert scale (1 = very little, 2 = little, 3 = a lot, and 4 = very much) [32]. The scores for questions 21, 23, 26, 27, 30, 33, 34, 36, and 39 should be reversed during data analysis. The anxiety score ranges from 20 to 80. A score of 20 to 30 indicates no anxiety, 31 to 42 indicates mild anxiety, 43 to 53 indicates moderate anxiety, and 54 to 80 indicates severe anxiety [33, 34]. Its internal consistency has been reported as 0.90 based on Cronbach’s alpha coefficient [35]. This questionnaire has been translated and validated in the Iranian population with satisfactory psychometric properties [36, 37].

Human errors in the operating room questionnaire

This researcher-developed questionnaire was created for use in this study. Interviews with operating room nurses and a literature review were utilized to develop the initial items of the questionnaire. Subsequently, to assess the psychometric properties of the questionnaire, validity (face and content) and reliability (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient) were examined. In the qualitative face validity study, an interview was conducted with 15 operating room nurses to evaluate the level of difficulty, presence of ambiguity, and appropriateness of the items. In the quantitative face validity study, the impact score (IS) values of each item were calculated based on the ratings given by 15 operating room nurses. For qualitative content validity, 15 instrument development experts and members of the operating room faculty were consulted to provide feedback on the appropriateness of the instrument’s content in terms of grammar, word choice, item placement, and scoring. The items were then adjusted based on their input. In quantitative content validity, the content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) of the instrument were calculated using the opinions of 15 experts. Finally, the reliability of the questionnaire was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The results indicated that the IS score of all items was above 1.5, which was deemed acceptable [38]. Additionally, the CVR score of the items exceeded 0.49, meeting acceptability criteria according to the Lawshe Table [39]. The CVI score of the items was also above 0.79, indicating acceptability [40]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the entire instrument, tested on a sample of 30 individuals, was calculated to be 0.87, confirming the questionnaire’s reliability.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. The normality of continuous variables (professional commitment, trait anxiety, and human error scores) was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

To compare mean scores between dichotomous demographic groups (e.g., gender), the independent-samples t-test was applied. For demographic variables with more than two categories (e.g., education level), mean score differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

For the main objective of the study, the associations among quantitative variables (professional commitment, trait anxiety, and human error) were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Correlations were reported along with the correlation value (r) and p-value.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22, with a significance level set at 0.05 for all tests.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Number: IR.SUMS.NUMIMG.REC.1402.144). All participants were informed of the study objectives and signed an informed consent form. They were assured that their information would remain confidential and that they had the right to withdraw at any time. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The analysis also showed a significant association between education level and the mean anxiety score (p = 0.008), with nurses holding an associate’s degree displaying higher anxiety levels. Moreover, a significant direct association was found between anxiety and human error (r = 0.168, p = 0.008), indicating that higher anxiety levels may be linked to an increased likelihood of human error among the nurses. Discussion

The aim of the present study was to investigate the relationship between anxiety and professional commitment and the rate of common human errors in the operating room affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The study involved 268 operating room nurses (both scrub and circular) with an average age between 20 and 40 years.

The findings of this study indicated that the average level of anxiety among the participating operating room nurses was moderate. In a study by Soltanipour et al. (2018) on anxiety and depression in radiologists and operating room technicians, similar results were found, with most participants experiencing moderate anxiety [41]. The present study also found that the average score of professional commitment was moderate among the participants. Normative commitment was the dimension with the highest average score in the sample studied. Duran et al. (2021) conducted a cross-sectional study of 384 nurses in Turkey, revealing that the level of professional commitment among the nurses was high [42]. In a similar study, Gizaw et al. (2016) examined the level of professional commitment and its influencing factors among nurses in public hospitals in the Jimma region of southwestern Ethiopia. The findings indicated that 115 nurses (36.3%) had low professional commitment, 88 nurses (27.8%) had medium commitment, and only one third of 114 nurses (36%) displayed high commitment to their profession [43]. These results contrast with the findings of the current study, possibly due to variations in economic conditions, workload, and available resources across the three countries.

In addition, the results of the present study indicated that the average level of human errors among the operating room nurses studied was moderate. Participants identified errors such as “not communicating correctly with the patient during admission” and “not checking preoperative medication on the day of surgery and while the patient is fasting” as the most common in the operating room. Jezini et al. (2019) reported that the average percentage of errors during admission to the operating room was 29.4%, which was related to checking preoperative medication and the duration of the patient’s fasting, respectively. Additionally, correct communication with the patient (39.7%) had the highest percentage of errors [44]. These results align with current research. Abolghasem Gorji et al. also showed in their research that out of 187 error cases identified in 21 surgeries, 32% were related to preoperative procedures. They also stated that the most dangerous error identified was related to the admission of the patient to the operating room [45]. In the present study, the most common human errors were reported during admission. Therefore, based on the results of the present study and studies conducted in this field, managing the patient admission section in the operating room can significantly reduce medical errors and complaints.

In the present study, a significant association was found between education level and anxiety among operating room nurses. Nurses with an associate’s degree appeared to show higher anxiety scores compared with those holding higher academic qualifications. This finding contradicts a study by Salehinia et al. (2022), which focused on job stress and related factors among operating room personnel during the COVID-19 pandemic in hospitals in Guilan province from 2021 to 2022. Their study reported that individuals with higher degrees experienced higher levels of anxiety [46]. The discrepancy between these results may be due to the unique psychological pressures induced by the COVID-19 pandemic and its potential confounding effects.

The analysis of demographic characteristics showed a significant association between age and education level with the mean professional commitment score among operating room nurses. Latifi et al. (2023) examined the attitudes of general medical doctoral students towards professional commitment and found that the students’ age and academic semester had a significant impact on their attitudes towards professional commitment [47]. This finding aligns with the results of the present study. In Gronowski’s study, no significant relationship was discovered between the professional attitudes of professors and pathology residents in three major academic centers in the United States when considering age and service experience [48]. These results differed from the findings of the present study. Potential explanations for this inconsistency may include cultural and social variations among different societies.

An analysis of the relationship between demographic characteristics of operating room nurses and the average human error score indicated a notable association between age and human error. Nurses aged 20 to 40 years tended to exhibit higher human error scores, which may be related to the demanding nature of the work and potential physical fatigue; however, further research is needed to confirm these contributing factors. A study conducted by Shams et al. in 2013 discovered a direct and significant connection between errors and age [49]. Conversely, in a study by Tang et al. involving 72 nurses, it was demonstrated that the error rate decreases with age, which contradicts the findings of this study [50]. It is evident that, in addition to age, there are various factors that could elucidate the impact of age on the reduction or escalation of errors.

Based on the results of the current study, a direct and significant association was found between professional commitment scores and human errors among the participating nurses. This suggests that higher professional commitment may be linked to lower levels of human error. Baghaei et al. (2012) did not find a significant relationship between professional commitment and medication errors in their study [51]. Joulai et al. (2014) also reported in their study that there was no significant relationship between medication errors and the professional commitment of nurses [52]. These two studies differ from the present study, with one possible reason being the different types of errors studied. In contrast, Wu et al. (2020) examined professional commitment and the “knowledge-attitude-practice” of patient safety among undergraduate nursing students at the patient’s bedside. Their results showed that improving professional commitment and the knowledge, attitude, and performance of nursing students in patient safety can enhance their ability to provide high-quality nursing care to patients [53]. These findings align with the results of the current study.

An examination of the relationship between mean anxiety scores and human errors among the participating nurses indicated a significant association, with human errors tending to be higher in nurses with elevated anxiety levels. This suggests that higher anxiety may be related to an increased likelihood of human error. Raeisi et al. (2017) also noted in their study that the occurrence of clinical errors is influenced by job stress [54], which aligns with the findings of this study. Furthermore, other research has shown that medical errors, particularly medication errors, can be exacerbated by physical fatigue, anxiety, and stress [55, 56].

In the present study, the regression model showed that although professional commitment and anxiety of operating room nurses were related to human errors, they were only a small part of the factors affecting human errors. This suggests that several important determinants were not measured in this study. Future research should include a broader range of psychosocial, organizational, and environmental variables to develop more comprehensive models that better explain the multifactorial nature of human error in operating room settings.

Limitations

The present study was conducted in one of the largest urban areas in southern Iran. However, it is recommended that future studies be conducted in a larger area and with a larger population for greater comprehensiveness. In this study, a self-report instrument was used to collect data, which may introduce bias in responses. It is suggested that in future studies, researchers utilize objective methods and valid observational checklists. Additionally, fatigue and work pressure may have influenced the accuracy of participants’ responses. Therefore, the researchers made an effort to sample during quieter work shifts and nurses’ breaks. Another important limitation of the present study is the use of convenience sampling. Although this sampling approach is practical in clinical settings, it restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of operating room nurses. Nurses who were more accessible or willing to participate may differ from others in levels of professional commitment, anxiety, or susceptibility to human error. Consequently, the associations observed in this study should be interpreted with caution. Future research is recommended to employ probabilistic sampling techniques in order to enhance external validity and provide more representative evidence regarding factors associated with human error in operating room environments. Another limitation of this study concerns the reliability of the affective commitment subscale, which showed a relatively low Cronbach’s alpha (0.52). This issue may partly be due to the small number of items within this subscale, as Cronbach’s alpha is known to be sensitive to the number of items and tends to underestimate internal consistency when item count is low. Additionally, the sample size available for reliability estimation may not have been sufficient to ensure stable reliability coefficients. Therefore, the findings related to affective commitment should be interpreted with caution, and future studies are encouraged to examine the psychometric properties of this subscale using larger samples or consider alternative measures with more items and established reliability.

Conclusion

Based on the results of the present study, significant associations were found between professional commitment and human errors, as well as between anxiety and human errors among the participating nurses. In addition, professional commitment and anxiety appeared to be modest predictors of human errors in the operating room, explaining a small proportion of the variance. These findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing anxiety and enhancing professional commitment may help to decrease the likelihood of human errors, although further research is needed to confirm the effectiveness of such strategies in clinical practice.
