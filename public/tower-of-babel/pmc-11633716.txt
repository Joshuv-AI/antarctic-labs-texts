Evaluation of self‐stigma in patients with epilepsy: Validation of the self‐stigma scale to Spanish (ESSS‐S)
Authors: Isabel Manzanares, Izumi Kuramochi, Marta Olivera, María Centeno, Mariam Khawaja, Luis Pintor, Antonio Donaire, María del Mar Carreño, Estefanía Conde‐Blanco
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Epilepsy Self‐Stigma Scale (ESSS) has been developed in Japan for patients with epilepsy (PWE). We aimed to validate the scale in Spanish and examine its validity and reliability.MethodsThe transcultural adaptation of the ESSS was conducted using translation and back‐translation, along with pilot testing and an expert panel review. An online questionnaire was used to assess construct validity, criterion validity, and internal consistency.ResultsA total of 260 out of 628 participants completed the questionnaires. We found a two‐factor structure that explained 61.3% of the total variance: factor 1 “self‐perceived discrimination” with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.80, and factor 2 “social isolation” with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.75. The reliability of overall ESSS‐S 8‐item scores was high (Cronbach's alpha = 0.74). Convergent validity was tested and demonstrated a significant correlation. P.Self‐perceived discrimination scores were associated with higher anxiety and depression scores (r = 0.53/0.54; p < 0.001), lower self‐esteem (−0.53; p < 0.001), and lower quality of life (r = −0.66; p < 0.001). Social isolation scores were associated with higher anxiety and depression scores (r = 0.41/0.33; p < 0.001), lower self‐esteem (−0.34; p < 0.001), and lower quality of life (r = −0.35; p < 0.001). INTRODUCTION

Stigma is socially related to a negative stereotype toward the person who suffers it and is associated with psychological stress. In epilepsy, stigma can be divided into self‐stigma and perceived stigma, inflicted by individuals with whom people with epilepsy (PWE) interact, including health professionals, educators, and the general population. 1 Also, stigma in epilepsy can be conceptualized as both “felt” and “enacted.” PWE and those who are close to them also endure felt stigma because they worry about being the target of courtesy stigma. Felt stigma is associated with anticipating the negative views of others, internalizing these views, and becoming self‐stigmatized. Enacted stigma describes episodes of discrimination against PWE only based on their condition. Felt stigma might cause a greater worsening of quality of life than enacted stigma. 2

PWE have experienced social exclusion, negative attitudes, and discrimination, which can be internalized as a feeling of guilt and fear. 3 , 4 Stigma is correlated with clinical aspects of the disease, such as the presence of seizures, seizure frequency, longer duration of the illness, and anti‐seizure medication (ASM) along with their side effects. 4 , 5 Also, lower economic and social status may have an effect. 2

The impact of stigma goes beyond a mere feeling. It is associated with a lower quality of life 5 and higher rates of depressive symptoms and anxiety. Aspects such as having social support, disease understanding, or sharing act as protective factors. PWE are vulnerable to endorsing stereotypes about themselves, leading to self‐stigma, and resulting in self‐discrimination and isolation. 6 Specific health interventions could promote the well‐being of PWE, although they are rather scarce in the current literature.

Stigma linked to appearance, behavior, or group may be difficult to measure and control. Additionally, stigma is relationship‐ and social context‐specific. 7 In epilepsy, it is intertwined with how families communicate about the disease. Increased stigma perceptions between parents and children correlate with a greater hiding of epilepsy. 8 Health professionals should assist families in enhancing communication about epilepsy.

Cultural diversity must be considered when researching stigma in epilepsy, and therefore, we face the need to validate tools or surveys. Researchers need valid and reliable instruments of interest adapted to their language to carry out high‐quality scientific and methodological studies.

So far, there is a notable absence of specifically designed scales in Spanish to accurately measure stigma associated with epilepsy. Prior studies of stigma in Spanish were not validated and focused on social stigma. 7 The lack of standardized instruments for assessing the social and emotional impact of stigma on PWE may compromise a correct understanding of their experiences. There is a clear need to assess instruments tailored to the cultural and social context of the country, as stigma can adversely affect the quality of life and social integration of those living with this condition. The creation and validation of specific scales to measure stigma in the Spanish context emerge as a crucial challenge to move forward in the comprehensive understanding and addressing of the psychosocial repercussions of epilepsy. 8

The Epilepsy Self‐Stigma Scale (ESSS) is an 8‐item, self‐administered questionnaire developed in Japan 9 and translated to other languages, 10 , 11 as encouraged by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), 12 which measures the self‐stigma suffered by PWE. It is rated on a four‐point Likert‐type scale: 1: Strongly Disagree, 2: Slightly agree, 3: Agree, 4: Strongly Agree, with a total score range from 8 to 32. Higher scores represent greater self‐stigma. 9

We aimed to adapt the ESSS to the Spanish version (ESSS‐S) and examine its reliability and validity. The secondary aim was to measure stigma in PWE who attended the outpatient clinic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participant and study characteristics

A cross‐sectional survey designed to evaluate cultural adaptation of the ESSS to the ESSS‐S and to measure reliability and validity. We sent the survey to patients who were attended at the outpatient clinic of the epilepsy unit of Hospital Clinic de Barcelona. Data were collected in September 2023, from the 13th to the 25th. Participants met the inclusion criteria of being ≥18 years of age, Spanish speakers, and voluntarily complete the questionnaire. People with epilepsy with severe cognitive or psychiatric disabilities that cause inability to complete the questionnaires were excluded. This study was approved by the Hospital Clinic Barcelona Ethics Committee (reference HCB/2022/0743). Voluntary participants received information about the aims of the study and agreed to the terms of the informed consent. Confidentiality of data and anonymity were maintained throughout the data collection.

ESSS‐S. Translation and validation processes

Following the Principles of Good Practice Translation and Cultural Adaptation of Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measures, 13 we translated the 18‐item prototype of the ESSS from Japanese to two versions of the scale in Spanish. The two Spanish versions were merged by an expert committee. This version of the questionnaire was retranslated to English by two native translators to work out ambiguities and disagreements. Both versions were compared with the original one by a multidisciplinary team, solving discrepancies. The original author was consulted during the process, as well as an independent Japanese translator to preserve the meaning of the text.

Preliminary pilot testing and expert panel

An expert panel was formed by three neurologists, a social worker, an advanced practice nurse in epilepsy, a generalist nurse, a technician of the epilepsy monitoring unit, and two advanced practice nurses in mental health. The items of the scale were rated by evaluating the clarity (confusing or clear) of the questions and the relevance using a Likert scale from 1 to 4 (not relevant—relevant and concise). The panel met in person to discuss the results of the pilot study, evaluate the answers and comments of the panel, and assess every item and the differences between the original ESSS 8‐items, the adaptation, and the adapted first version of the ESSS‐S 8‐items.

Psychometric testing

The questionnaire was sent using an in‐house survey software, the Lime Survey program, which guaranteed a secure and confidential application. Participants from the epilepsy outpatient clinic were reached by email, where information about the study, such as informed consent, demographic and clinical questionnaires, the ESSS‐S, the Rosenberg self‐esteem scale (RSES), the Quality Of Life In Epilepsy (QOLIE‐10), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was presented. To evaluate temporal stability the questionnaire was readministered after 10 days.

Measures and variables

Rosenberg self‐esteem scale (RSES)

We measured participants' self‐esteem using the RSES, which rates 10 items on a four‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). It is unidimensional and has high internal consistency, with Cronbach's α above 0.80. Higher scores on the scale indicate a higher level of self‐esteem. 14

The hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

We measured depression and anxiety using the HADS questionnaire, which comprises seven items for each subscale of depression and anxiety. Scoring for each item ranges from zero to three. Higher scores on the scale, with a total subscale score of >8 points out of 21, indicate a higher level of anxiety or depression. 15

The quality of life in epilepsy (QOLIE‐10)

We measured quality of life in epilepsy using the QOLIE‐10. The 10‐item questionnaire score ranges from worst to best quality of life, from 10 to 50 points. 16 The QOLIE‐10 covers various aspects of daily functioning, emotional well‐being, and overall satisfaction with life. It is often used in clinical research and practice to evaluate the impact of epilepsy on a person's quality of life.

Statistical analysis

We used STATA 18 for the analysis. The type I error was set at the usual 0.05 value. Pearson correlation was calculated to evaluate the raw association between the items of the Spanish‐adapted version of the ESSS. A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and an exploratory factor analysis with oblique rotation were estimated to assess the content validity. 17 Evaluation of the fit of the CFA models was done using the parameters recommended in the literature 18 , 19 : the chi‐squared statistic, the Root Mean Square Error Approximation (RMSEA), the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), the Comparative Fit Index and the Tucker‐Lewis Index.

Invariance across gender and age group, which establishes if the selected factorial structure is shared between sexes and groups of age, was assessed.

To assess the internal consistency of the questionnaires, Cronbach's alpha coefficients were calculated. The test–retest reliability was analyzed with Pearson correlation and with the intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) and interpreted according to Fleiss, 20 who suggested that ICC values above 0.70 indicated high test–retest reliability. To study the external construct validity, Pearson correlations between stigma and anxiety, depression, self‐esteem, and quality of life were calculated. Construct validity analysis compared the means of the stigma factors between groups of participants using independent samples t‐test and post‐hoc comparison with Sidák's correction. 21

Content validity and expert panel

Table S1 summarizes the item adaptation process carried out by the expert committee. The items were assessed in clarity and relevance according to the expert panel criteria, then collectively analyzed and discussed. Based on cultural considerations, 3 items were modified from the original scale.

Item and factor analysis, and reliability

Univariate descriptive analysis and Pearson correlations of the set of adapted items were performed. Values ranged from 0.07 to 0.67. Adapted items can be reviewed at (Table S1). Pilot testing results were described at Table S2.

To study the 3‐factor structure of the questionnaire, a CFA with the set of stigma items was done. The obtained goodness of fit indices (RMSEA = 0.145, CFI = 0.791, TLI = 0.706, SRMR = 0.079) suggested that empirical data did not support the 3‐factor structure (Tables S3 and S4).

The Barlett's test of sphericity applied to the set of 8 items (1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10) was statistically significant (p < 0.001), reflecting that items were intercorrelated. Results from the PCA applied to the 8 items showed a 2‐factor structure with two factors with eigenvalues above the value 1, explaining 61.31% of the total variance (Table S5).

Table S5 shows the nonorthogonal oblique rotated (oblimin) factor loadings and the communalities of the 8 items. Items have been ordered to be sequentially grouped in the two factors. The items 3, 5, 7, 9, and 10 defined the first factor that was identified as “Self‐perceived discrimination.” The second factor grouped items 1, 6, and 8 and represented “Social isolation.” Communalities were homogeneous, with a range from 0.46 (item 3) to 0.81 (item 8). The correlation between the 2 factors was r = 0.44. The mean of the residuals (observed less fitted correlations between items) was 0.08 (SD = 0.05), with 60.71% of them above 0.05.

Cronbach's alpha for factor 1 “self‐perceived discrimination” was 0.80, for factor 2 “Social isolation” was 0.75, and for the global 8‐items was 0.74. Internal consistency showed appropriate values for every factor and the global value without the need to eliminate any items.

Construct validity

Table 5 shows the correlation between each factor of stigma and the 4 measures of validity, together with its mean and standard deviation. We found a weak positive correlation of both self‐perceived discrimination and social isolation factors (r = 0.53, p < 0.001; r = 0.41, p < 0.001) with HADS anxiety and (r = 0.54, p < 0.001; r = 0.33, p < 0.001) with HADS depression. Also, a week to moderate negative correlation with RSES (r = −0.53, p < 0.001; r = −0.34, p < 0.001) and QOLIE‐10 (r = −0.34, p < 0.001; r = −0.35, p < 0.001). Thus, higher self‐perceived discrimination and social isolation scores were associated with higher anxiety, higher depression, lower self‐esteem, and lower quality of life scores.

Temporal stability

To evaluate temporal stability, the questionnaire was re‐administered, and we collected a total of 123 surveys. The mean time interval between the test–retest was 23 days, with a minimum of 10 days and a maximum of 34 days. The Pearson correlation and ICC between test–retest for self‐perceived discrimination were r = 0.77 and ICC = 0.87, and for social isolation, they were also r = 0.77 and ICC = 0.87.

DISCUSSION

Stigma is a prevalent perception among PWE, influencing aspects such as quality of life, mood, and feelings of isolation. A validated scale is required to measure the intensity of stigma and to facilitate the development of educational interventions. To our knowledge, this is the first study that validates a self‐stigma scale in a Spanish‐speaking cultural context.

The perception of epilepsy‐related stigma varies according to the cultural features and language of the country. The ESSS was based on the Japanese population, thus, the Spanish validation required sociocultural adaptation. Etymologically, the word epilepsy is derived from the ancient Greek, meaning “to seize” and has been interpreted as a form of divine punishment or “to possess,” associated in the Middle Ages with demon or spirit possession. In ancient cultures, spirit possession was held accountable for the disease. In Spanish, the term is related to an action exerted by another person or force; thus, the person experiencing it has no control over it, which partially explains the concept of supernatural forces of epilepsy in Western cultures. In Japanese, “ten‐kan” attributes “madness” and “irritability” to the person affected and is associated with violent behaviors or seizures. 22 There are cultural differences regarding stigma in both populations, so we adapted the 18‐item Japanese prototype, resulting in an 8‐item ESSS‐S. An example of this adaptation need is the item about traffic accidents related to seizures. In Japan, there was a lot of media uproar due to traffic accidents resulting in fatalities caused by individuals with epilepsy. The public exposure was very intense, leading to an increased stigma toward the condition, as evidenced by some conducted studies. 23 Legislative changes were also implemented to enhance controls for issuing licenses to individuals with epilepsy. Given the difficulty in obtaining a driver's license legally, some PWE chose to drive without a license, and some of them caused accidents, reigniting the controversy. In Spain, there has been no public and social repercussion in this regard, reflecting that this issue was irrelevant for patients and experts in our study to measure the impact of stigma in Spanish PWE.

On the other hand, on the Japanese version, a 3‐factor analysis was obtained: “Internalized stigma,” “Social incomprehension,” and “Confidentiality.” In ESSS‐S, items were grouped into a two‐factor structure, “Self‐perceived discrimination” and “Social isolation,” with a good internal consistency like the original Japanese version. The ESSS‐S demonstrated good reliability and good temporal stability for “Self‐perceived discrimination” and “Social isolation.”

This result correlates with the theoretical foundation of stigma previously proposed, 24 which explained stigma perception through experiences like employment discrimination or internalized stigma and practices like social rejection and distancing and stereotypes about PWE and their ability to be productive members of society. Item correlation was good and reliability, as measured with Cronbach's α, was high; thus, the ESSS‐S is considered to have adequate reliability.

ESSS‐S overall score in our study was lower than collected in other countries such as Turkey and Japan; variations of social aspects of the population and culture in Spain were associated with stigma score. A previous study that aimed to study the cross‐cultural factors likely to be influential in the stigma of PWE concluded that the country of origin was an important factor associated with stigma and that Spanish participants reported the lowest levels of stigma and French respondents the highest. 4 Different hypotheses had been mentioned to explain these cultural differences, including sociocultural bias against epilepsy, the organization of the national healthcare system in each country, the existence of role models with epilepsy, educational differences, and cultural norms for concealment, among others. Also, family and community values are highly appreciated in Spanish‐speaking nations related to their religious roots; this has been linked to more sympathetic and caring views toward those who suffer from mental illnesses, and it has an impact on the beliefs of the general population and the patient independently of the social group. 25 , 26 Religious values have an impact on the importance of family and are relevant to understanding stigma in PWE from Spanish‐speaking countries. In the context of self‐stigma, religion might represent a potential way of coping. The stigma factors showed no differences across sex and age groups. This enables relational analysis of stigma factors with other constructs for cross‐sectional comparisons and allows for comparisons within the same group over time with longitudinal data. 27

Despite intercultural differences, stigma in PWE is common, and we found that the ESSS‐S showed a negative correlation with QOLIE‐10. Generalized seizures, disease progression, and stigma have been associated with a poorer quality of life, 26 , 28 contributing to physical and mental health decline, lower educational and employment access, and social isolation and exclusion. The ESSS‐S has shown a positive correlation with the Rosenberg Self‐Esteem Scale and HADS anxiety and depression scales for both self‐perceived discrimination and social isolation. All the correlations were statistically significant, with values from medium to high, according to Cohen. 29

Overall, there were no significant differences in stigma scores by gender. However, female patients with earlier seizure onset, with first seizure between ages 0 and 10, showed a significant higher self‐perceived discrimination. Social isolation score is also higher in females with earlier seizure onset and with 1 or more seizure per week. Epilepsy in females has an impact on sexuality, hormonal aspects, contraception, and pregnancy. 30 Also, social stigma may also have a greater negative impact on females with severe epilepsy, leading to higher self‐perceived discrimination and isolation. Specific social aspects that could affect stigma in women should be studied to improve interventions and implement social strategies. Research with a gender perspective in epilepsy studies is scarce, and more studies are required.

Limitations

This survey was conducted at a referral university hospital in Barcelona. Participants in the study might have suffered refractory epilepsy with a higher frequency than expected in the general population. This might have had an impact on stigma level in these patients but should not be an issue in this validation study. Multicenter studies should be planned to accurately measure stigma in a broader population of PWE. Psychiatric comorbidities and their relationship should also be addressed specifically with future studies. Stratification might lower statistical power, and future studies might be required to properly address gender impact on stigma.

CONCLUSION

This is the first assessment of self‐stigma in the Spanish community with epilepsy. We demonstrate the validity and reliability of the ESSS‐S as a tool for assessing self‐stigma in the Spanish‐speaking community. Accurate adaptation has confirmed its value in measuring stigma while taking cultural factors into account. The scale adjustment has ensured content validity, thereby validating the power and future comparability of self‐stigma in other countries. Future research on how epilepsy affects patients' perceptions of social exclusion should be explored.
