Association of social determinants of health with opioid misuse among individuals with common psychiatric conditions
Authors: Angélica K. Zayas-Ortiz, Samálix E. Torres-Rivera, Amanda Acosta-Mirabal, Jonathan Hernández-Agosto, Bianca A. Torres-Hernández, Kyle Melín
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Describe the prevalence of opioid misuse among patients with psychiatric conditions in the All of Us (AOU) Research Program, its association with social determinants of health (SDOH), and psychiatric comorbidities.Methods:This observational, retrospective analysis of participants with common psychiatric conditions used survey and medical record data from AOU. Pearson correlation and ordinal logistic regression were used to examine associations between SDOHs and opioid misuse.Results:The study included a total of 49,964 participants who were predominantly white (79.6%), female (71.2%), and with an average age of 57.5 years; most had depression (77.8%). Final logistic regression identified significant predictors of opioid misuse: bipolar disorder (OR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.57–1.86), PTSD (OR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.38–1.57), depression (OR = 1.33, 95% CI 1.23–1.44), anxiety (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.12–1.27), white (OR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.34–1.61), food insecurity (OR = 1.45, 95% CI 1.35–1.57), perceived neighborhood disorder (PND) (OR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.02), and male (OR = 1.43, 95% CI 1.34–1.52)(p < 0.001 for all). Higher education (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.82–0.87, p < 0.001) and income (OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.98, p < 0.001) were associated with lower opioid misuse.Conclusion:In addition to common psychiatric conditions, food insecurity and PND were associated with opioid misuse, highlighting the need for integrated prevention strategies, early intervention, and targeted opioid misuse screening.

Introduction

Opioid use disorder (OUD) and opioid abuse have reached epidemic levels in the United States, impacting over three million Americans (Azadfard et al., 2025). This has prompted greater efforts to investigate the factors that may contribute to opioid misuse, taking into consideration the possible consequences they could have on public health. Researchers have explored the connection between opioid use and mental health conditions among these factors. Several studies have found significantly higher rates of prescribed opioids among American adults with mental health disorders, with some estimating that these patients account for over half of all annual opioid prescriptions (Davis et al., 2017; Edlund et al., 2010; Halbert et al., 2016; Swartz et al., 2023). These findings are of great concern, considering that in recent years there has also been a substantial increase in the prevalence of people suffering from mental health disorders. The literature has shown that more than 1 in 5 adults are currently affected by mental health disorders, and over 1 in 5 adolescents will experience severe symptoms at some point in their lives (CDC, 2024a). In addition, individuals with mental health disorders may be at greater risk for opioid misuse and nonmedical prescription opioids, which can lead to overdose (Cho et al., 2021).

Comorbidity between mental health disorders and substance use disorders (SUDs) may have significant implications for the treatment and outcomes of affected individuals (Blanco et al., 2012; Novak et al., 2019). Individuals with co-occurring mental illness and SUDs face barriers to treatment and experience poorer treatment outcomes compared to those with no mental illness (Novak et al., 2019). Patients with comorbid Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and SUD are especially vulnerable to a range of adverse outcomes. They are more susceptible to the development of additional mental health issues, experience increased frequency and severity of depressive episodes, and have a higher risk of suicide attempts (Blanco et al., 2012). As a result, comorbidity between mental health disorders and SUDs has become a common and concerning phenomenon, considering that approximately 1 in 4 individuals with serious mental illnesses, such as MDD, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder, also have a SUD (National Institutes on Drug Abuse, 2020).

Understanding the relationship between mental illness and opioid use is critical. Studies suggest that common risk factors, bidirectional causation, and societal norms can influence the comorbidity between these conditions (National Institutes on Drug Abuse, 2020). Blair and Siddiqi, 2022 suggest that variations in substance use may stem from social, cultural, religious, and gender-related norms that shape its perceived acceptability (Blair and Siddiqi, 2022). Moreover, a high prevalence of comorbidity between MDD and SUDs has been reported, emphasizing the need for further research to develop effective prevention strategies for reducing the prevalence of SUD in individuals with depression, particularly considering the increasing use of opioids (Hunt et al., 2020).

SDOH are the environmental conditions (economic stability, education, healthcare access, among others) that affect a wide range of health and quality of life outcomes and risks (“Social Determinants of Health - Healthy People, 2030,” 2010). These factors may be crucial in determining how people with mental health disorders abuse substances, especially opioids. A study in rural Alabama found a significant association between education, housing, and employment status and opioid use frequency, suggesting that targeting these factors may allow for further predictive interventions to mitigate opioid misuse and potential mortality (Albright et al., 2021). More recently, Do et al. (2025) examined associations between SDOH and OUD-related diagnoses among chronic pain patients in the All of Us Research Program, further supporting the relevance of social and structural factors in opioid-related outcomes. However, less is known about the specific SDOH that influence opioid misuse among individuals with psychiatric disorders. Social-context factors may also be relevant, as prior evidence has linked opioid misuse with social relationship factors, including social support and social isolation (Qeadan et al., 2021).

This research aimed to describe the prevalence of reported opioid misuse among patients with common psychiatric illnesses in the AOU cohort and to explore how sociodemographic status, educational level, health insurance, income status, perceived neighborhood disorder (PND), social support, food insecurity, and healthcare access are associated with opioid misuse in individuals with common psychiatric conditions.

Methods

Data source and measures

Data collection and study population

The study used an observational, retrospective analysis of linked survey and electronic health record (EHR) data from the All of Us Research Program. Because SDOH measures were obtained from participant surveys and opioid misuse was ascertained using both EHR diagnostic data and survey responses, the analysis was designed to estimate cross-sectional associations rather than temporal or causal relationships. The data for the cohort incorporated the surveys and electronic health record (EHR) data from the AOU Research Program (All of Us Controlled Tier Dataset v8), which includes data from 1939 to the present. Participants provided consent before enrollment, and the program ensures participant confidentiality by de-identifying data sets before they are made available for research (“All of Us Research Program | National Institutes of Health,” 2020). The research protocol was submitted to the University of Puerto Rico Medical Sciences Campus Institutional Review Board and determined to be exempt as secondary research for which broad consent is required (Protocol Number: 2402190861).

The study included individuals aged 18 years or older, representing diverse backgrounds and geographic regions across the United States. Eligible participants were those with common psychiatric conditions (anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and/or schizophrenia) reported in their EHR or identified through the Personal and Family Health History Survey (Appendix Table 1). To be eligible for inclusion, participants must also complete the following four surveys: The Basics, SDOH, Healthcare Access and Utilization, and Lifestyle Survey. Aside from not meeting these inclusion criteria, the study did not apply any specific exclusion criteria to the cohort builder in the AOU workbench. Because the five psychiatric conditions were operationalized as composite indicators (self-reported diagnosis on the Personal and Family Health History Survey and EHR diagnoses mapped to each category), which apply a more specific definition than the broader cohort-builder eligibility criteria, a small proportion of otherwise-eligible participants did not meet the threshold for any of the five conditions; these participants constitute the zero-condition reference group.

The study focused on psychiatric comorbidities associated with opioid misuse, as described in the NIH Research Report on Common Comorbidities with SUD (National Institutes on Drug Abuse, 2020). The World Health Organization identifies the targeted conditions as the most prevalent globally (World Health Organization, 2025). Researchers standardized data from EHRs using the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership Common Data Model (OMOP CDM) (“All of Us Research Program | National Institutes of Health,” 2020).

Study variables

The study explored SDOH as the independent variable using demographic data and answers to selected questions in the following surveys: The Basics, SDOH, and Healthcare Access and Utilization. Demographic data included age, ethnicity, race, gender identity, sex assigned at birth, and sexual orientation. The Basics survey provided variables of participants’ educational level, marital status, health insurance, and income status. The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) supplied the survey questions on educational level, marital status, and income, while the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) contributed questions on health insurance (All of Us Research Program, 2020). The SDOH survey provides PND, social support, and food insecurity variables. The study obtained questions of the above variables from previously validated scales, including the Ross-Mirowsky PND scale (Ross and Mirowsky, 1999), the Rand-Mos Social Support Survey Instrument (Sherbourne and Stewart, 1991), and the Hunger vital sign (Hager et al., 2010), respectively. Also, selected questions of the Healthcare Access and Utilization Survey were used to determine the variable of healthcare access, obtained and validated from the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS)(All of Us Research Program, 2020). Appendix Table 2 contains the survey questions used to define each variable.

The study’s primary dependent variable was opioid misuse, defined as the use of illegal drugs and/or the use of prescription drugs in a manner other than as directed by a doctor, such as use in greater amounts, more often, or longer than prescribed or using someone else’s prescription (CDC, 2024b). For this study, we constructed an ordinal composite endpoint (range: 0–3) representing the total number of reported types of opioid misuse among AOU participants. These included: 1.) a diagnostic term documented in their EHR that would indicate current or previous opioid use (i.e. Opioid dependence, Opioid abuse, Opioid dependence in remission, Nondependent opioid abuse, and Nondependent opioid abuse in remission), 2.) reported street opioid use in the Lifestyle survey, and 3.) reported non-medical use of prescription opioids in the Lifestyle survey (Appendix Table 3).

No uniform index date was assigned because the opioid misuse composite incorporated both EHR-derived diagnostic terms and self-reported survey indicators, while SDOH variables were derived from survey responses. Therefore, the temporal sequence between SDOH measurement and opioid misuse ascertainment could not be established consistently across participants. All regression estimates should be interpreted as adjusted associations with reported opioid misuse, not as evidence that SDOH factors preceded or caused opioid misuse.

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and preprocessing

All data manipulation and statistical analyses were conducted using Python within the All of Us Researcher Workbench’s integrated, cloud-based Jupyter Notebook environment. The study applied imputation methods where feasible for predictors with missing values; otherwise, it excluded records with missing data. It also encoded categorical variables into binary or dummy variables to ensure compatibility with logistic regression analyses. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was calculated for all predictors to address potential multicollinearity, retaining only those with VIF values below five to maintain model stability.

Data analysis

Chi-squared tests were performed to evaluate categorical variables such as race, sex at birth, sexual orientation, instances of opioid use, marital status, health insurance, food insecurity, mental disorder, income, highest grade achieved, and health care access. The study employed one-way ANOVA to measure continuous variables such as age, PND, and social support.

For the primary analysis, bivariate associations between opioid misuse and the candidate predictors were first examined using correlation analysis with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure (False Discovery Rate [FDR] correction) (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) as an exploratory screening step. The multivariable model was then specified to include an a priori set of conceptually relevant predictors — the five psychiatric conditions of interest together with established demographic and social-determinant covariates (race, sex at birth, income, educational attainment, perceived neighborhood disorder [PND], and food insecurity) — regardless of their bivariate significance. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) after one-hot encoding of categorical variables, with each indicator evaluated separately; predictors with VIF ≥ 5 (limited to mutually exclusive marital-status indicators, an artifact of dummy coding) were not retained. Because the outcome was an ordinal count (0–3) of the number of opioid-misuse indicator types reported, the primary analysis used an ordinal logistic regression (proportional-odds) model estimated by maximum likelihood. The proportional-odds assumption was assessed by fitting separate binary logistic regressions at each cumulative cut point (≥1, ≥2, ≥3) and comparing coefficients across cut points. As a sensitivity analysis, a binary logistic regression contrasting any reported opioid misuse (score ≥1) with none (score 0) was also fit. Finally, a comorbidity network analysis (Cramer et al., 2010; Borsboom and Cramer, 2013) was performed for the five mental health conditions of interest and OUD from EHR data to illustrate the interrelationships between these conditions. For each pair of nodes (the five psychiatric conditions and OUD), participants were cross-classified in a 2×2 table according to the presence or absence of each condition, with cells a (both present), b (first present, second absent), c (first absent, second present), and d (both absent). From this table we calculated the odds ratio [(a×d)/(b×c)] and the relative risk {[a/(a+b)]/[c/(c + d)]} of co-occurrence. The statistical significance of each pairwise association was tested with Fisher’s exact test, and the resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. In the resulting network, nodes represent conditions (size proportional to their prevalence in the cohort) and edges connect pairs whose association remained significant after adjustment (adjusted p < 0.05), with edge width proportional to the relative risk.

Results

A complete description of all study variables, disaggregated by ethnicity, is included in Table 1. A total of 43,924 participants were identified as Not Hispanic or Latino, and the average age of participants was 57.5 ± 16.3 years, with the majority being female at birth (35,593 participants; 71.2%), white (39,788; 79.6%), and identified as Heterosexual (47,602; 95.3%). Regarding SDOH, the average PND score was classified as 21.1 (range 13–52), indicating a moderate level of PND. Social support, calculated as the average of eight items, scored 3.8 ± 1.1, reflecting moderate social support. Food insecurity was prevalent in 9187 (18.4%) of participants. In terms of health, depression (77.8%) was the most common mental disorder, and 47,290 participants (94.6%) had health insurance. To characterize psychiatric multimorbidity, we examined the number of co-occurring conditions per participant: 1067 participants (2.1%) had none of the five psychiatric conditions, 21,984 (44.0%) had exactly one, and 26,913 (53.9%) had more than one. The most common combinations were anxiety with depression (30.7%), depression alone (26.1%), anxiety alone (15.1%), and anxiety with depression and PTSD (9.7%). Because most participants had more than one condition, the comparison group for each condition in the regression reflects participants with versus without that specific condition while holding the other conditions and social determinants constant, rather than a psychiatrically unaffected group.

For the inferential analysis, participants with missing values for independent variables were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 43,909 valid participants. Table 2 displays the results from the Pearson correlation analysis. Positive Pearson correlations between participant characteristics and reported opioid misuse were observed with income (0.116, p < 0.001), depression (0.107, p < 0.001), schizophrenia (0.097, p < 0.001), and social support (0.084, p < 0.001). Other significant positive correlations included PTSD (0.048, p < 0.001) and bipolar disorder (0.042, p < 0.001). In contrast, the analysis found negative correlations with healthcare access (−0.104, p < 0.001), anxiety (−0.064, p < 0.001), food insecurity (−0.041, p < 0.001), and education level (−0.102, p < 0.001). The variance inflation factor (VIF) high-lighted multicollinearity concerns, particularly for variables like marital status.

The ordinal logistic regression model (Table 3) successfully converged. The likelihood-ratio test indicated that the predictors significantly improved model fit compared with the null model (p < 0.001); however, the McFadden pseudo R-squared value of 0.040 indicates limited explanatory power. When analyzed together, significant predictors of opioid misuse included bipolar disorder (Odds Ratio [OR] = 1.71, 95% Confidence Interval [CI] 1.57–1.86, p < 0.001), PTSD (OR = 1.47, CI 1.38–1.57, p < 0.001), white race (OR = 1.47, CI 1.34–1.61, p < 0.001), food insecurity (OR = 1.45, CI 1.35–1.57, p < 0.001), male sex at birth (OR = 1.43, CI 1.34–1.52, p < 0.001), other race (OR = 1.40, CI 1.19–1.66, p < 0.001), depression (OR = 1.33, CI 1.23–1.44, p < 0.001), anxiety (OR = 1.19, CI 1.12–1.27, p < 0.001), and PND score (OR = 1.02, CI 1.01–1.02, p < 0.001). Schizophrenia was not significantly associated with opioid misuse in the adjusted model (OR = 1.13, CI 0.92–1.38, p = 0.26). Conversely, higher educational attainment (OR = 0.84, CI 0.82–0.87, p < 0.001) and higher income (OR = 0.97, CI 0.95–0.98, p < 0.001) were associated with decreased odds of opioid misuse. A binary logistic regression sensitivity analysis (any misuse vs. none) produced nearly identical estimates, confirming that the findings were robust to the outcome specification.

When analyzed together, we found that some variables were not independently associated with opioid misuse in this population. These included sexual minority status, marital status, health insurance, healthcare access, and ethnicity.

Comorbidity network analysis for mental health conditions and OUD from EHR

The comorbidity network analysis for mental health conditions and OUD from EHR (Table 4 and Fig. 1) reveals complex interrelationships. Among the psychiatric conditions, bipolar disorder showed the strongest association with OUD (relative risk [RR] = 1.82; OR = 1.99), followed by schizophrenia (RR = 1.63; OR = 1.65), PTSD (RR = 1.51; OR = 1.70), and depression (RR = 1.39; OR = 1.43), while anxiety showed the weakest association (RR = 1.22; OR = 1.25). Among the psychiatric conditions themselves, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia demonstrated the strongest co-occurrence (RR = 11.90; OR = 12.89), and bipolar disorder was also strongly associated with PTSD (RR = 2.35; OR = 3.33). We report both relative risk and odds ratio to allow comparison across the network and regression analyses; the network estimates describe unadjusted pairwise co-occurrence, whereas the regression estimates are adjusted for all other conditions and social determinants. Notably, although schizophrenia co-occurs strongly with OUD and with bipolar disorder in the network analysis, it was not independently associated with opioid misuse in the adjusted regression, indicating that its apparent association may be largely explained by co-occurring conditions.

Discussion

This study examined the association between SDOH and opioid misuse among individuals with common psychiatric conditions. Our findings identified multiple factors associated with opioid misuse, reinforcing the complex interplay between psychiatric disorders, SDOHs, and demographic characteristics.

Among social and demographic characteristics, being white and male at birth was significantly associated with opioid misuse. This finding is consistent with prior research, likely reflecting historical prescribing patterns and greater access to prescription opioids (Davis et al., 2017; Jennings et al., 2022). Additionally, income was found to be significantly associated with opioid misuse, with lower-income individuals exhibiting higher rates of opioid misuse. The National Survey on Drug Use and Health study also reported that opioid misuse was most prevalent among individuals earning less than $50,000 annually (Han et al., 2017). Neighborhood-level economic factors, such as poverty, have been linked to opioid-related mortality (Flores et al., 2020). On the other hand, educational level demonstrated an inverse relationship with opioid misuse, meaning that higher education levels could serve as a protective factor against opioid use. These findings align with previous reports that individuals without a high school diploma faced significantly higher overdose death rates, particularly due to synthetic opioid use (Powell, 2023).

Sexual minority status and identifying as Not Hispanic or Latino initially showed a positive correlation with opioid misuse. However, this association lost significance in the final model. Previous studies have attributed the elevated risk among sexual minorities to discrimination, stigma, and social exclusion (Duncan et al., 2018), which can lead to higher rates of mental health disorders and self-medicating with opioids (Schuler et al., 2019). In the present study, it is possible that by including the effects of other potentially correlated SDOH, the significance of these identities diminished (Schuler et al., 2019).

Other SDOH, such as health insurance, healthcare access, and social support, were not significantly associated with opioid misuse in the final model. Social relationship factors have been associated with opioid misuse in prior work, including evidence linking opioid misuse with social support and social isolation (Qeadan et al., 2021). In the present study, social support showed a bivariate association with opioid misuse but was not independently associated after adjustment for psychiatric comorbidities and other SDOH. This suggests that the relationship between social support and opioid misuse may overlap with other clinical and social risk factors captured in the model. Similarly, health insurance status showed a bivariate association with opioid misuse but was not significant in the adjusted model. Other factors, such as education, occupation, and other demographic factors, may influence this association (Vootukuri et al., 2024).

Conversely, the final model identified PND and food insecurity as significantly associated with opioid misuse. These findings align with previous research demonstrating strong correlations between neighborhood environmental factors, crime, and opioid risk in Delaware communities (Gavali et al., 2021). A different study in Massachusetts found that neighborhood-level economic factors, such as food insecurity, were also linked to opioid-related mortality (Flores et al., 2020). Notably, although anxiety and food insecurity showed negative bivariate correlations with opioid misuse, both were positively associated with opioid misuse in the adjusted model (anxiety OR = 1.19; food insecurity OR = 1.45). This reversal in direction reflects confounding by co-occurring psychiatric conditions and social determinants that are accounted for only in the multivariable model, rather than a difference in variable coding, and underscores the importance of adjusted estimates when interpreting these associations.

Our findings are also consistent with Do et al. (2025), who used All of Us data to examine associations between SDOH and OUD-related diagnoses among chronic pain patients. However, the studies differ in important ways: Do et al. focused on chronic pain patients and OUD-related diagnoses, whereas the present study focuses on adults with common psychiatric conditions and uses a broader opioid misuse composite incorporating EHR diagnostic terms, reported street opioid use, and reported nonmedical prescription opioid use. In addition, our analysis emphasizes individual-level survey-derived SDOH measures, including perceived neighborhood disorder, food insecurity, social support, healthcare access, income, and education.

In terms of the multifaceted aspects of comorbidity involving common psychiatric disorders and opioid misuse, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and depression showed all positive Pearson correlation and were significantly associated with opioid misuse in the final model. These results are in line with previous studies associating mood and SUDs. People who have bipolar disorder may use opioids as a form of self-medication, especially for mood shifts, depression, and chronic pain (Goldner et al., 2014). Sullivan et al. pointed out that depression is a key factor in prolonged use of opioid medication and may enhance the chances of becoming dependent on those drugs in the future (Sullivan et al., 2006). Another study demonstrated that depression mediates the relationship between PTSD and opioid misuse, suggesting that depressive symptoms may represent one pathway through which PTSD is associated with opioid misuse (Hruschak et al., 2020). Although anxiety initially showed a negative correlation with opioid misuse, the final logistic regression analysis revealed it as significantly associated with opioid misuse. Previous work has found that individuals with anxiety may misuse opioids due to emotional dysregulation, making it a relevant but indirect risk factor (Rogers et al., 2021). Similarly, although a relationship between schizophrenia and opioid misuse was observed in the initial model, it was not significant in the final logistic regression model. Further research is needed to explore potential mediating or confounding factors that may influence this relationship. One contributing factor could be that individuals with schizophrenia often receive reduced medical care and limited access to treatment, which may exacerbate their vulnerability to opioid misuse (Danilewitz and Bahji, 2021).

The comorbidity network analysis using only EHR data demonstrated similar relationships between the psychiatric comorbidities and OUD as were observed with opioid misuse in the final regression model. In the comorbidity network analysis, bipolar disorder had the highest association with OUD, aligning with previous work that identified an increased association of lifetime opioid disorder among those with mood disorders such as bipolar disorder and MDD (Martins et al., 2012). Likewise, the observed association between PTSD and opioid misuse is consistent with prior work by Hruschak et al. (2020) and with findings from a 12-month cohort study showing that PTSD is prevalent among patients receiving treatment for opioid use disorder (Rosic et al., 2021). However, certain socioeconomic factors and a lack of treatment options may also be confounding variables (Jiménez-Castro et al., 2011; Levis et al., 2021). In this comorbidity network analysis, the highest co-occurrence of diagnoses was between bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. Interestingly, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia share overlapping symptoms, particularly manic episodes with psychotic features, which may lead to misdiagnosis between the two disorders (Paul et al., 2021). Similarly, the strong relationship between bipolar disorder and PTSD supports prior evidence that trauma-related disorders frequently co-occur with mood dysregulation, making affected individuals more susceptible to opioid misuse (Hruschak et al., 2020).

The large, diverse sample from the AOU Research Program, robust statistical methods, and validated survey instruments are major strengths of this study. Multiple regression models controlled for confounders, while the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure minimized false discovery rates, and VIF analysis ensured minimal multicollinearity. However, some limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The observational design prevents causal inference, and reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional associations rather than evidence of temporal or causal relationships. In addition, although the ordinal logistic regression model significantly improved fit compared with the null model, the McFadden pseudo R-squared value was low, indicating limited explanatory power. This suggests that opioid misuse is likely influenced by additional clinical, social, structural, and temporal factors not fully captured in the available All of Us variables. Further, EHRs may not capture all opioid misuse cases, particularly among those without healthcare access, leading to possible selection bias. We have tried to mitigate these biases by including both self-reported opioid misuse and EHR data and creating an ordinal composite endpoint for opioid misuse.

Despite these limitations, the findings are significant as they reinforce the strong link between psychiatric conditions and opioid misuse, emphasizing the need for integrated mental health and SUDs prevention and treatment approaches. By identifying anxiety, bipolar disorder, PTSD, and depression as factors independently associated with opioid misuse, this study highlights the importance of early screening and targeted interventions among patients with psychiatric multimorbidity. These findings support integrated mental health and substance-use screening, particularly for patients with overlapping psychiatric diagnoses and concurrent social risks such as food insecurity or perceived neighborhood disorder. The role of SDOH, particularly food insecurity and PND, in opioid misuse underscores the necessity of addressing these factors in prevention strategies. These insights contribute to the growing body of research on mental health and opioid misuse, informing public health policies and clinical interventions aimed at reducing opioid-related harm. They also emphasize the need for integrated, multidisciplinary approaches to opioid misuse prevention, including targeted opioid risk assessments and socioeconomic interventions such as expanded food assistance programs. Future research should incorporate longitudinal and intervention-based studies to establish causal relationships and refine targeted prevention efforts.

Conclusion

This observational retrospective study found that bipolar disorder, depression, PTSD, and a variety of SDOHs were associated with opioid misuse. Food insecurity, PND, education, and income level were associated with opioid misuse, highlighting the importance of integrated prevention. The results underscore the importance of early identification and treatment of mental health issues, as well as the need for more social strategies to help prevent the risk of opioid misuse. Despite some psychiatric screening measures already having SUD assessment, explicit inclusion of opioid misuse screening in the guidelines for PTSD, bipolar disorder, anxiety, and depression may enable greater early intervention, improve patient outcomes, and curb opioid abuse in at-risk groups. Longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to help optimize prevention efforts in the future.
