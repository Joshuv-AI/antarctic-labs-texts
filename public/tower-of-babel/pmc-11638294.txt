Endoscopic-assisted anterior pelvic ring osteosynthesis for pelvic ring injuries: a technical report
Authors: Chih-Yang Lai, I-Jung Chen, Po‑Ju Lai, Yung-Heng Hsu, Ying-Chao Chou, Steve W N Ueng, Yi-Hsun Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the surgical treatment of pelvic ring injuries (PRIs), there is an increasing adoption of minimally invasive techniques to improve surgical outcomes. Since the introduction of endoscopic-assisted osteosynthesis for PRIs in 2019, various surgical challenges have been identified. To improve surgical and clinical outcomes, we modified the existing procedures and aimed to present the surgical outcomes of patients with pelvic fractures who underwent endoscopic-assisted surgery.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with PRIs treated with endoscopic technique over a 15-month period. All patients were diagnosed with diastatic symphysis, superior pubic ramus fracture, or both. The postoperative radiological and functional outcomes were assessed and reported. Details of the preoperative planning and surgical procedures were reviewed.ResultsThirteen patients with PRIs were enrolled: 10 patients for diastatic pubic symphysis (three of whom received superior ramus screw fixation) and three for isolated superior pubic ramus fractures. All surgeries were completed with endoscopic assistance, without the need for conversion to open surgery. The average surgical duration was 204.1 min, the endoscopy time was 103.7 min, and the volume of blood loss was 185.0 ml. Excellent reduction was achieved in all patients according to the Matta/Tornetta criteria and in nine patients according to the Lefaivre criteria. The mean Majeed scores were 71 and 82 and the mean Merle d’Aubigné scores 11 and 15 at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, respectively.ConclusionEndoscopic-assisted pelvic surgery is a safe and effective treatment alternative for PRIs, particularly when focusing on the anterior pelvic ring. The advantages of this approach, including small incisions and preservation of abdominal and pelvic floor muscle integrity, contribute to improved patient recovery. Future research should focus on comparative studies of this technique with other minimally invasive techniques and open surgery.Keywords: Pelvic ring injury, Endoscopy, Minimally invasive osteosynthesis, Diastatic symphysis, Superior pubic ramus fracture

Introduction

Pelvic ring injuries (PRIs) present a significant clinical challenge owing to the complexity of the pelvic anatomy and the high-energy mechanisms often associated with these injuries [1]. Managing PRIs presents ongoing challenges for orthopedic surgeons. Traditional surgical methods prioritize clear visualization of vital organs to ensure safe and effective fracture reduction through open approaches [2]. In recent years, minimally invasive approaches have gained prominence in the management of pelvic fractures because of improvements in the understanding of surgical anatomy and modifications in surgical techniques [3, 4].

Among the fracture patterns of a PRI, a diastatic pubic symphysis accounts for 15–20%; moreover, a PRI that involves a pubic rami fracture is common [5, 6]. Historically, the stabilization of PRIs with pubic symphysis diastasis required extensive surgical approaches owing to the deep and intricate nature of the anterior pelvic ring [7]. However, surgical fixation of the superior pubic ramus reduces chronic pain and improves the quality of life in these patients [8, 9]. The Pfannenstiel incision, which has become the gold standard for managing PRI with pubic symphysis diastasis, provides access to the symphysis and superior pubic ramus and leads to satisfactory surgical outcomes [7, 10]. However, this open approach is associated with significant complications, including bleeding from the inferior epigastric vessels, iatrogenic nerve injuries, incisional hernias owing to abdominal muscle detachment, and potential urinary bladder injuries [7, 11, 12].

Kuper et al. introduced the endoscopic approach to the symphysis (EASY) technique, which is a minimally invasive method that uses laparoscopic instruments to stabilize the symphysis with plate fixation [13]. This approach has multiple advantages over traditional open surgery methods. The initial cadaver study and early clinical results of the EASY technique showed promising results in terms of reducing postoperative morbidity and improving patient outcomes [11]. Subsequent studies extended the indications for endoscopic-assisted osteosynthesis (EAO) to the quadrilateral plate (EAQUAL), acetabulum (LASY), anterior pelvic ring (APACHE), and full endoscopic anterior intrapelvic plate osteosynthesis emphasizing the potential for minimally invasive pelvic surgery [14–17].

Despite the potential benefits, the literature on endoscopic-assisted approaches for anterior pelvic ring fixation remains limited, with only a few studies detailing techniques and outcomes of this approach. Therefore, this report presents a series of cases utilizing endoscopic-assisted anterior pelvic ring osteosynthesis, emphasizing technical nuances and patient outcomes. By detailing individual patient presentations, operative approaches, and clinical results, this case series aims to contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting minimally invasive pelvic fixation techniques, specifically highlighting the surgical technique modifications associated with endoscopic-assisted pelvic surgery.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with PRIs who underwent EAO between January 2021 and January 2024. Thirteen patients who met the surgical indications were enrolled in this study. This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 202401217B0).

The indications for EAO included diastatic pubic symphysis and a displaced superior ramus fracture with a fracture gap > 10 mm in the axial, coronal, or sagittal plane of the computed tomography (CT) scan. This technique was contraindicated in patients with concomitant blunt abdominal trauma. The patient demographic profiles, injury severity scores (ISS), new injury severity scores (NISS), fracture pattern-associated injuries, quality of reduction, and functional outcomes were recorded. All medical documents, pre-, intra-, and post-operative radiological assessments, and functional evaluations were recorded at least 6 months postoperatively.

Classification of PRI and parameters of radiological evaluations

In the present study, we adapted two classifications, the Young-Burgess and Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO) classifications, to classify the injury patterns of PRIs. The Young-Burgess classification system [18] describes the force vector responsible for pelvic ring injury, which allows surgeons to easily understand the causative mechanisms of PRIs, whereas the AO classification provides a more comprehensive description of the injury patterns of PRIs [19].

Radiological outcomes were determined through plain postoperative radiographs using three standard pelvic radiographic views: anteroposterior, inlet, and outlet views. Regarding the quality of reduction, we adapted the Matta/Tornetta and Lefaivre criteria to evaluate the residual vertical displacement and pelvic symmetry, respectively [20–22].

Functional evaluations

Functional outcomes were assessed using the Majeed and Merle d’Aubigné scores at 3 and 6 months after injury in all patients. The Majeed score is a pelvic injury-specific functional assessment comprising seven items, with a total score range of 0–100 points [23]. The Merle d’Aubigné score includes parameters for pain, mobility, and walking ability, with each parameter rated from 0 (worst condition) to 6 points (best condition) [24].

Preoperative planning

The major modification of EAO from the published techniques was optimizing working portal locations through the preoperative CT scan: “Individualize working portals” (Fig. 1). Owing to individual anatomical variations, the inclination of the pubis in the sagittal view differs. Our focus was optimizing the sagittal angle of the superior pubic ramus within the Nakatani zone I [25]. This emphasis stems from the fact that aligning the longest screw application parallel to the pubis in the sagittal view enhances stability to its maximum potential. The drill angle is crucial for achieving a more appropriate trajectory for screw fixation. Therefore, a suitable portal position for each patient was planned by utilizing the sagittal view of the CT scan and delineating the pubis inclination line to represent the appropriate trajectory (yellow line in Fig. 1a). This line intersects the skin at point A and serves as the planned portal position. The intersection of the pubis horizontal line (blue line in Fig. 1a) with the skin is point P, and the position of the umbilicus is point U. By calculating the ratio of the lengths from point P to point A and from point A to point U, we can determine the personalized location of point A preoperatively (Fig. 1a). Using this method, Point A was identified as the first working portal. The portal was created under direct visualization using an endoscope camera. Following the first portal, the other two working portals (A1 and A2 in Fig. 1b), which are anatomically medial to the inferior epigastric vessel and identified under the endoscope (Fig. 1c), were created at the same horizontal level as point A. Surgical technique

The patient was placed in the supine position on a radiolucent table (Modular Table System; Mizuho OSI, California, USA) for easy imaging, with both arms located away from the body. The endoscopic and osteosynthesis surgeons stood on opposite sides of the patient, switching roles as needed. Prior to surgery, evaluation under anesthesia with real-time fluoroscopy was routinely performed to assess pelvic ring stability, especially for posterior pelvic lesions [26]. Whenever a posterior pelvic lesion was identified, a closed reduction with percutaneous iliosacral or trans-iliac-trans-sacral screw fixation was preferred.

The endoscopy monitor was positioned caudally for clear viewing by all staff (Fig. 2a), with fluoroscopy on the side of the endoscopist. The procedure began with closed pelvic ring reduction via internal rotation of the lower extremity, stabilized with supraacetabular external fixation, and confirmed with intraoperative fluoroscopy (Fig. 2b). The external fixator’s connecting rod was placed caudal to the pubic symphysis to avoid interference with the endoscopic procedure and allow optimal fluoroscopic assessment.

A longitudinal skin incision was made 15–20 mm caudal to the belly button, followed by blunt dissection through the subcutaneous tissue and Scarpa’s fascia. he anterior rectal sheath was then incised, and the rectus muscle was retracted laterally to insert a balloon trocar (Spacemaker PDB™, Covidien) along the posterior rectus sheath to the symphysis, inflating it to dissect the preperitoneal space. Three working portals were placed under endoscopic guidance to avoid the inferior epigastric vessels, with positions determined using preoperative CT planning. Debridement, evacuation of the hematoma, and identification of vital tissues were performed before the osteosynthesis procedure. A 3.5 mm pubis symphyseal plate (DePuy Synthes, Paoli, PA, USA) or a 3.5 mm reconstruction plate (Syntec Technology Co., Hsinchu, Taiwan) was used for diastatic pubic symphysis. The plate was lassoed with an absorbable suture (Fig. 2c), positioned via the first working portal, and adjusted using endoscopic forceps under direct vision (Fig. 2d). Drilling and screw placement followed within a protective sleeve, with trajectories planned preoperatively. Fluoroscopy confirmed final reduction and implant positioning.

To measure screw length accurately, a K-wire of equal length to the drill bit was prepared prior to drilling (Fig. 2e). During drilling, as the bit passed the pubic second cortex, the K-wire was placed in the protective sleeve near the cortex (Fig. 2f). The length difference between the drill bit and K-wire indicated the screw length, enhancing precision and reducing screw exchanges. Bone wax applied to the screw head before insertion improved control and prevented dislodgment. If a screw required replacement, a grasper was used for extraction (Fig. 2g). This modified EAO technique is effective for symphyseal plate, superior ramus screw, and suprapectineal plate fixation (Fig. 3a–c).

Retrograde intramedullary screw fixation of the fractured superior pubic ramus was achieved endoscopically (Fig. 4). After visualizing the fracture with an endoscopic camera, hematoma or pectineal ligament debris was cleared. A 2.0 mm K-wire, inserted at a palpable point medial and caudal to the pubic tubercle, guided the intramedullary screw. The K-wire was advanced into the intramedullary canal and extended slightly past the fracture into the preperitoneal space for confirmation, then retracted. Using a ball-spike pusher (DePuy Synthes), the fracture was reduced, and the K-wire advanced through the iliac cortex. Intramedullary screw fixation followed standard procedure, with fluoroscopic verification to prevent hip joint penetration.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize cohort characteristics, with frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. For between-group comparisons, categorical data were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or the Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables. The study variables included age, ISS, NISS, PRI classification, radiological outcomes, and functional outcomes. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed p-value ˂ 0.05.

Results

In the present study, 13 patients underwent EAO for the treatment of PRIs: 10 for diastatic pubic symphysis (three underwent additional superior ramus screw fixation) and three for isolated superior pubic ramus fracture. A detailed description of the 10 patients who underwent EAO for diastatic pubic symphysis is presented in Table 1. These patients exhibited a range of injury severities, and the medians for ISS and NISS were 17 (IQR 10.75–23.75) and 23 (IQR 18–25), respectively. The average time for the endoscopic procedure was 103.7 ± 41.1 min. All surgeries were performed with endoscopic assistance without any complications.

Discussion

The development of surgical techniques for the management of anterior PRIs has markedly advanced with the introduction of minimally invasive methods, particularly EAO [3, 13]. We modified the previously described endoscopic procedures to facilitate its clinical availability and reduce the learning curve for future users. The modifications included individualized working portals for accurate screw trajectory, adjusting the connecting bar of the external fixator, using sutures on the plate, and accurately measuring the screw length using a K-wire. These modifications were aimed at facilitating the surgical process.

Traditional open surgery approaches, such as the ilioinguinal and modified Stoppa approaches, are widely used for the treatment of anterior PRIs [27]. However, some drawbacks of open approaches have been reported, including blood vessel injury, spermatic cord damage, and incisional hernia of the abdominal wall [28, 29]. Theoretically, these complications can be reduced by using endoscopic-assisted surgery [3, 13]. For instance, to avoid vessel injury, we created working portals away from the inferior epigastric vessels under endoscopic visualization. Minimizing trauma to the abdominal and pelvic floor muscles and decreasing the incidence of incisional hernias are other potential advantages.

There are several studies on the treatment of PRIs using endoscopic-assisted surgery. Kuper et al. first described the surgical technique (EASY) for PRIs [13]. Following this study, Kabir et al. published a modified technique for treating similar injury patterns [3]. The mean operative times were 197 min and 169.8 min in the studies by Kuper et al. and Kabir et al., respectively. However, some studies reported that a longer duration of endoscopic surgery may be associated with increased complications and negative outcomes such as wound infection, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, and deep vein thrombosis [30, 31]. The surgical and endoscopy times in this study were shorter than those in previous studies. We postulate that the reduction in surgical time might be attributable to our modifications of the osteosynthesis procedure. Although the reduction in surgical time might not be positively correlated with satisfactory surgical outcomes, it correlated with patient safety during anesthesia and surgery.

Functional improvement assessments are among the evaluation factors that determine the successful treatment of PRIs [32–34]. Theoretically, EAO has the potential to achieve greater functional improvements than open surgery if anatomical reduction of the pelvis is attained. However, functional evaluation of patients who have undergone EAO has not been reported in previous studies. In our study, the functional outcomes, as measured by the Majeed and Merle d’Aubigné scores, demonstrated progressive improvement over time, suggesting not only the restoration of anatomical integrity but also improvements in patient’s quality of life postoperatively.

At present, radiation hazards are a perpetual concern for both patients and surgeons [35, 36]. Intraoperative fluoroscopy is a common practice that ensures the precise reduction of fractures and accurate positioning of implants, particularly in orthopedic surgeries [37, 38]. Overexposure to radiation may occur during osteosynthesis, especially in the treatment of PRIs. As mentioned in the surgical technique section, intraoperative fluoroscopy was applied only at the end of osteosynthesis. Moreover, the application of a retrograde intramedullary screw for superior pubic fractures, which is routinely performed under fluoroscopic guidance, can be performed without the assistance of fluoroscopy. Therefore, theoretically, radiation exposure during endoscopic surgery can be minimized.

Our study had several limitations. First, the study has a small sample size owing to the single-institution retrospective design and the strict indication selection. Thus, the actual outcomes might have been over-emphasized, and potential complications may have been underestimated. Second, we aimed to reduce intraoperative radiation exposure; however, a comparative group was lacking in this study. Lastly, the enrolled patients were relatively young, and functional performance assessments were only conducted at 6 months post-operation. This limited timeframe and demographic make it challenging to compare our data with other patient groups, such as those who underwent open reduction internal fixation or were included in other studies.

Conclusion

Endoscopic-assisted pelvic surgery is a safe and effective alternative for managing anterior PRIs. This approach minimized tissue disruption and yielded satisfactory functional and radiological outcomes. Further studies should focus on expanding the surgical indications, such as acetabular anterior column or quadrilateral plate fractures, and enrolling more patients.
