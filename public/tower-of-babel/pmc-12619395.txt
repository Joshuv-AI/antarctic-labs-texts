PBM alleviates depression and anxiety like behaviors in mice: insight from local field potential
Authors: Yifei Tan, Jinwei Liu, Yuanrong Qiu, Huaqiang Ruan, Qiaozhen Qin, Xiaotong Li, Zhenhua Xu, Xin Qiao, Xiaoxia Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression involves abnormal neural oscillations. Photobiomodulation (PBM) modulates such oscillations but lacks behavioral electrophysiological mechanistic studies. We explored PBM’s effects on hippocampal CA1 oscillations and phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) in a depression model.MethodsMale C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into saline, LPS (2 mg/kg i.p.), and LPS + PBM groups (n = 10/group for behavioral tests, n = 8/group for electrophysiology). LPS groups received lipopolysaccharide to induce neuroinflammation. The LPS + PBM group underwent 810 nm PBM (20 mW/cm², 12 min/day × 4 days) starting day 4 post-injection. Anxiety- and depression-like behaviors were assessed via open field, elevated plus-maze, and tail suspension tests. Wireless electrophysiology recorded CA1 local field potentials (LFP) during rest and behaviors. Oscillations and PAC were analyzed. Data are presented as mean ± SD; group differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction and ɳ² effect sizes, with two-tailed p < 0.05 taken as statistically significant.ResultsPBM (20 mW/cm2) alleviated LPS-induced anxiety and depressive behaviors. Electrophysiologically, PBM restored resting-state δ power (LPS + PBM: 0.0499 ± 0.0282, LPS: 0.1491 ± 0.0887; p < 0.01) and enhanced δ-γ coupling (LPS + PBM: 2.049 ± 0.447, LPS: 0.230 ± 0.298; p < 0.05). During anxiety tasks, PBM suppressed γ power (LPS + PBM: 0.3709 ± 0.1569, LPS: 0.5165 ± 0.06896; p < 0.05) and strengthened δ-γ PAC (LPS + PBM: 0.741 ± 0.508 vs. LPS: 0.217 ± 0.218, p < 0.05). In depression tests, PBM normalized δ power (LPS + PBM: 0.0261 ± 0.0182, LPS: 0.1315 ± 0.0619; p < 0.001) and reduced γ power (LPS + PBM: 0.2848 ± 0.0921, LPS: 0.4067 ± 0.0892; p < 0.05). No significant PAC changes was observed during depression tasks.ConclusionPBM therapy ameliorates LPS-induced depression and anxiety behaviors while normalizing hippocampal CA1 oscillations and cross-frequency coupling. Its effects are state-dependent, modulating distinct frequency bands and PAC across rest and behavioral contexts, revealing potential electrophysiological therapeutic mechanisms.

Introduction

Depression, one of the most significant global health burdens today, is characterized by impaired synaptic plasticity, neurotransmitter dysregulation, neuroinflammation, defective neurogenesis, and-critically-disruption of coordinated large-scale circuits such as limbic-cortical dysregulation and default-mode network hyper-connectivity. Oxidative stress, chronic low-grade inflammation, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis hyperactivity are now recognized as core pathophysiological drivers that link these cellular alterations to clinical phenotype [1–3]. Among these disrupted processes, neuronal oscillations stand out as a crucial aspect that underpins central nervous system information processing [4].

Among the measurable consequences of these disturbances, aberrant neuronal oscillations have emerged as a key proxy for faulty inter-regional communication. Rhythmic electrical activity in the 0.5–100 Hz range integrates distributed networks through phase synchronization and cross-frequency coupling (CFC)- for example, θ-phase-γ-amplitude coupling (θ-γ CFC) [5, 6]. However, clinical studies have revealed distinct abnormalities in these oscillatory patterns in individuals with depression.

For instance, there is increased frontoparietal beta-band power observed on EEG, reduced hippocampal-prefrontal θ-γ coupling [7], and elevated left-frontal alpha activity, which is associated with a negative emotional bias [8]. Importantly, these aberrations in neuronal oscillations correlate with the core symptoms of depression. Hippocampal CA1 atrophy [9, 10], linked to depression severity, has shown specific connections between γ-wave dysfunction and anhedonia [11].

PBM employs low-intensity red-to-near-infrared light (600–1100 nm) to non-thermally modulate mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, up-regulating ATP, ROS and Ca2+ signalling to enhance cellular repair and neuroprotection [12]. Emerging evidences have showed that PBM offers an effective therapeutic approach.

In the clinic, 2–8 weeks of near-infrared stimulation (810–823 nm, 36–60 J/cm2, bilateral or unilateral transcranial delivery) consistently produces moderate-to-large reductions in depression, anxiety and craving scores that persist for at least one month [13–16].Pre-clinically, single or repeated exposures in the 808–830 nm range (8–41 J/cm2) replicate these antidepressant and anxiolytic outcomes and match citalopram-like antioxidant effects [17–19].Converging electrophysiological data show that PBM and standard pharmacotherapy both remodel cortical oscillations: conventional agents suppress frontal/temporal δ/θ within hours and raise right-frontal α after one week [20], whereas tPBM acutely increases θ, β and γ power in the same regions [21, 22].

Notwithstanding these encouraging outcomes, research into PBM’s effects on neuronal oscillations within models of depression is still scarce. To date, prior studies have primarily examined the link between PBM’s effects on neuronal oscillations in specific brain regions and cognitive functions. While some studies have been made in exploring the connection between PBM and hippocampal CA1 oscillation abnormalities, the specific relationship between PBM and the anxiety and depressive behaviors caused by these oscillation abnormalities still requires further investigation.

Given that hippocampal CA1 oscillatory deficits and θ-γ phase-amplitude coupling have been causally linked to LPS-induced depressive-like behaviour [23], and that PBM is known to entrain network oscillations, we tested the hypothesis that PBM rescues LPS-induced affective dysfunction by selectively restoring CA1 rhythmic activity and CFC. We have investigated the therapeutic effects of PBM in a mouse model of LPS-induced depression. Using wireless multichannel electrophysiological recordings targeting the hippocampal CA1 region, we have systematically evaluated the dynamic effects of PBM on local field potentials, including neuronal oscillations and phase-amplitude coupling. This approach seeks to provide novel mechanistic insights into the anxiolytic and antidepressant effects of PBM from an electrophysiological perspective.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and ethical approval

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Gempharmatech and randomly assigned to Saline, LPS, or LPS + PBM groups. They were housed in standard cages with free access to food and water, under a 12 h light/dark cycle and constant temperature and humidity. The animal ethics committee approved the experimental protocol (No.: IACUC-DWZX-2024-530), aligning with laboratory animal management and usage guidelines. Upon completion of the experiment, mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation; all measures were taken to alleviate animal distress, in compliance with the laboratory animal care and use guidelines. n = 10 per group for all behavioral tests; n = 8 per group for all electrophysiological recordings.

Intraperitoneal LPS injection

After disinfecting the left lower abdomen with a 75% alcohol swab (avoiding the midline and bladder area), a needle was inserted into the abdominal cavity at a 30–45° angle (0.5-cm depth). After confirming no blood or urine withdrawal, LPS (L2880, Sigma-Aldrich) solution (2 mg/kg) was slowly administered. Post-injection, the site was pressed for 10 s to prevent leakage.

Electrode implantation surgery in the hippocampal CA1 region

Three days before LPS injection, mice were anesthetized with 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (350 mg/kg). A cranial window was opened (coordinates: 2 mm posterior to bregma, 1.5 mm lateral to midline), and micro-wire array electrodes (KD-DZ, Shanghai Quanlan) were implanted into the hippocampal CA1 region (dorsoventral depth: -1.5 mm). Reference and ground electrodes were fixed with dental cement.

PBM treatment protocol

From days 4–7 post-LPS injection, the scalp of each mouse in the PBM group was shaved to expose the area bordered by a line connecting the two eyes anteriorly and a line connecting the two ears posteriorly, and then received daily LED-array (Shenzhen Fangpu) irradiation (wavelength 810 ± 10 nm) at Zeitgeber time (ZT2). The power density was 20 mW/cm² for 12 min/session. Control mice were shaved and placed under identical environmental conditions with the light source off.

Open field test (OFT)

In the open field test, mice were placed in a 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm box with opaque walls (central area: 25 cm × 25 cm square) after acclimation for 30 min. They were then positioned in the center, and the total distance moved, central distance moved, and time spent in the center were recorded for 5 min using the Labmaze Behavioral Analysis System (Beijing Zhongshidi Chuang). Post-test, feces and urine were removed, and the box bottom was disinfected with 75% alcohol and dried with clean gauze to eliminate residual odors. A reduction in central time and distance is indicative of heightened anxiety. Testing was initiated at the end of the light phase, corresponding to approximately ZT10.

Elevated plus-maze (EPM)

In the elevated plus-maze test, the EPM consisted of two open arms (30 cm × 6 cm) and two closed arms (30 cm × 6 cm × 15 cm), elevated 50 cm above the ground. After a 30-min acclimation, mice were placed in the maze center. The percentage of entries into open arms and time spent there were recorded for 5 min using the Labmaze Behavioral Analysis System. Post-trial, the maze was cleaned with 75% alcohol to remove residues. A decrease in open arm time and distance traveled signifies increased anxiety in mice.Testing began at ~ ZT10.

Tail suspension test (TST)

For the tail suspension test, mice were acclimated in the test room for 30 min before the experiment. Their tails (1–1.5 cm from the tip) were fixed with medical tape and suspended in an opaque box, with the head 30 cm above the ground. The Labmaze Behavioral Analysis System recorded immobility time for 6 min, excluding the first minute. Immobility was defined as passive hanging without active movement. Increased immobility time indicates a more severe depressive-like state.Testing began at ~ ZT10.

Data acquisition

During the test phase, electrophysiological signals from the mice’s brains were recorded utilizing a wireless in vivo electrophysiological recording system (AR4-M, Shanghai Quanlan). The electrodes implanted in the brain transmitted signals via a wireless preamplifier. Subsequently, after undergoing analog-to-digital conversion, the signals were conveyed to the system’s receiver through Bluetooth. Notably, the sampling rate for local field potential signals was set at 500 Hz.

Power spectral analysis and relative power

The LFP signal analysis for power spectral density (PSD) was performed using the QLanalyser software (version 1.2.5). Welch algorithm-based spectrum estimation was applied to the 0–200 Hz frequency band, with Hanning windowing, 5-second analysis windows, and 50% overlap.

Phase-amplitude coupling

To quantify the modulation of high-frequency amplitude by low-frequency phase, we performed a PAC analysis. The phase frequency was defined as 1–12 Hz, and the amplitude frequency was defined as 30–120 Hz (gamma band).

For the analysis, the raw signal was first filtered to extract the phase and amplitude components using a 4th-order Butterworth filter, applied bidirectionally (zero-phase filtering) to prevent phase distortion. The PAC strength was then calculated for each channel and averaged across animals; each comodulogram thus represents the mean PAC values per channel. To enable comparison across animals, PAC values were normalized between animals before plotting. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (San Diego GraphPad). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.Comparisons among groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software Inc). A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PBM alleviates anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in LPS-treated mice

Previous investigations have demonstrated that a single intraperitoneal administration of LPS at 2 mg/kg reliably elicits anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in mice. Therefore, we adopted this dosage to establish a murine model of depression [24].Having thus established the murine model of depression, we next sought to objectively quantify the resultant affective alterations. OFT, EPM, and TST are the three most widely validated paradigms for indexing anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in rodents [25]. Accordingly, we employed this behavioral battery to evaluate the anxiogenic and depressogenic effects of the 2 mg/kg LPS challenge.The experimental time schedules are shown in Fig. 1a, including LPS injection, PBM treatment protocol (20 mW/cm², 12 min/day for 4 days), and sequential behavioral assessments. Representative movement trajectories in the OFT (Fig. 1b) illustrated significantly reduced locomotor activity in LPS mice. Quantitative analysis showed that LPS mice exhibited significantly less total locomotor activity (Saline: 2639 ± 372.1 cm, LPS: 1181 ± 465.2 cm; p < 0.001; Fig. 1c) and less central zone exploration distances (Saline: 403.7 ± 92.86 cm, LPS: 64.15 ± 53.47 cm; p < 0.001; Fig. 1d) than those in Saline mice. In addition, LPS mice spent less time in the center compared with Saline mice (Saline: 24.56 ± 5.731 s, LPS: 5.244 ± 3.722 s; p < 0.001; Fig. 1e). PBM intervention reversed these effects, with LPS + PBM mice achieving significant improvements in total movement distance (LPS + PBM: 2576 ± 396.9 cm, LPS: 1181 ± 465.2 cm; p < 0.001), central exploration distance (LPS + PBM:281.7 ± 61.55 cm, LPS: 64.15 ± 53.47 cm; p < 0.001) and center duration (LPS + PBM: 19.16 ± 2.748 s, LPS: 5.244 ± 3.722 s; p < 0.001) compared to LPS mice.

In the EPM (Fig. 1f), LPS mice demonstrated lower open arm exploration than Saline mice, evident in both entry frequency (Saline: 40.13 ± 8.71%, LPS: 31.00 ± 7.34%; p < 0.05; Fig. 1g) and time spent (Saline: 19.11 ± 9.25%, LPS: 4.38 ± 2.59%; p < 0.001; Fig. 1h). PBM treatment restored open arm preference in LPS mice for both entry frequency (LPS + PBM: 42.79 ± 4.78%, LPS: 31.00 ± 7.34%; p < 0.01) and duration (LPS + PBM: 18.95 ± 5.20%, LPS: 4.38 ± 2.59%; p < 0.001).

Consistent with these behavioral changes, the TST (Fig. 1i) showed that LPS mice exhibited prolonged immobility time compared to Saline (Saline: 169.5 ± 54.37 s, LPS: 244.2 ± 20.34 s; p < 0.001). This effect was significantly attenuated by PBM treatment (LPS + PBM: 187.9 ± 19.4 s, LPS: 244.2 ± 20.34 s; p < 0.01).

Overall, 810 nm PBM significantly improved LPS-induced anxiety and depressive-like behaviors, highlighting its potential for treating emotional disorders.

PBM reverses LPS-Induced suppression of δ, θ and γ oscillations and restores δ/θ-γ phase-amplitude coupling in resting state

To investigate the effect of PBM on neural oscillations, LFP recordings in the CA1 hippocampal region were obtained only after each mouse had remained in a strictly defined resting state for ≧ 10 min (no locomotion, eyes open but quiet). Recordings were performed inside a sound-attenuated behavioral testing chamber maintained at 23 ± 1 ℃ and < 40 dB background noise, beginning at ZT10 to control for circadian variation. Under these conditions, resting-state LFP revealed significant spectral power alterations across experimental groups (Fig. 2a).Quantitative spectral analysis demonstrated LPS-induced dysregulation across three frequency bands (Fig. 2b-d). Compared with Saline mice, LPS treatment produced a marked suppression of δ-band relative power (Saline: 0.0299 ± 0.0121, LPS: 0.1491 ± 0.0887; p < 0.001), θ-band relative power (Saline: 0.0238 ± 0.0059, LPS: 0.1186 ± 0.0482; p < 0.001), and γ-band activity (Saline: 0.2040 ± 0.01402, LPS: 0.4758 ± 0.1555; p < 0.001). Interestingly, PBM administration reversed these suppressive effects: it partially restored δ-band (LPS + PBM: 0.0499 ± 0.0282, LPS: 0.1491 ± 0.0887; p < 0.01) and θ-band relative power (LPS + PBM: 0.0566 ± 0.0254, LPS: 0.1186 ± 0.0482; p < 0.01), and rescued γ-band suppression (LPS + PBM: 0.2737 ± 0.1362, LPS: 0.4758 ± 0.1555; p < 0.01).

Functional network integration analysis revealed severe LPS-induced disruption of PAC between low-frequency rhythms and γ oscillations (Fig. 2e-g). LPS mice demonstrated lower δ-γ coupling (Saline: 3.499 ± 2.399, LPS: 0.230 ± 0.298; p < 0.001) and θ-γ coupling (Saline: 5.762 ± 3.102, LPS: 1.516 ± 0.993; p < 0.001) than Saline mice. PBM intervention significantly restored these interactions, enhancing δ-γ coupling (LPS + PBM: 2.049 ± 0.447, LPS: 0.230 ± 0.298; p < 0.05) and θ-γ coupling (LPS + PBM: 4.036 ± 0.908, LPS: 1.516 ± 0.993; p < 0.05).

PBM attenuates δ, θ, and γ power during TST in LPS mice

During the TST in mice, LFP signals in the hippocampal CA1 region were recorded. Figure 5a illustrates the power spectra of the three groups of mice at rest.

Power spectral analysis during the TST (Fig. 5b-d) revealed distinct LPS-induced oscillatory abnormalities. Compared with Saline mice, significantly higher δ-band relative power (Saline: 0.0267 ± 0.0123, LPS: 0.1315 ± 0.0619; p < 0.001) and θ-band relative power (Saline: 0.0474 ± 0.0132, LPS: 0.1548 ± 0.0707; p < 0.001) were found in LPS mice. PBM intervention fully normalized δ-band relative power (LPS + PBM: 0.0261 ± 0.0182, LPS: 0.1315 ± 0.0619; p < 0.001) and θ-band relative power (LPS + PBM: 0.0491 ± 0.0237, LPS: 0.1548 ± 0.0707; p < 0.001). Conversely, γ-band activity showed LPS-induced hyperactivation compared to Saline (Saline: 0.2171 ± 0.0687, LPS: 0.4067 ± 0.0892; p < 0.001), which was significantly attenuated by PBM treatment (LPS + PBM: 0.2848 ± 0.0921, LPS: 0.4067 ± 0.0892; p < 0.05).

PAC analysis (Fig. 5e-g) demonstrated impaired δ-γ coordination. LPS mice showed significantly lower δ-γ coupling than Saline mice (Saline: 1.751 ± 0.6530, LPS: 0.5732 ± 0.7561; p < 0.01). PBM intervention failed to restore δ-γ coupling between LPS + PBM and LPS mice (LPS + PBM: 0.5894 ± 0.3536, LPS: 0.5732 ± 0.7561;ns). θ-γ coupling showed no significant differences between Saline and LPS mice (Saline: 3.389 ± 1.046, LPS: 2.869 ± 1.007; ns) or between LPS + PBM and LPS mice (LPS + PBM: 2.761 ± 0.8333, LPS: 2.869 ± 1.007;ns).

Discussion

In this study, we explored the effects of PBM therapy with a wavelength of 810 nm and a power density of 20 mW/cm² on LPS-induced mood disorder model and its underlying mechanisms. After four days of PBM treatment, we observed significant alleviation of anxiety-and depression-like behaviors, which were accompanied by the improvement of LPS-induced abnormal neural oscillations and PAC in the hippocampal CA1 region (Fig. 6).

Our findings revealed that the amelioration of neural oscillation dysregulation is correlated with the therapeutic effects of PBM on depression. However, exact causal evidence requires further investigation. Previous studies have demonstrated that in LPS-induced neuroinflammatory models, θ (4–12 Hz) and γ (30–100 Hz) oscillations in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and hippocampus are impaired [26, 27]. For instance, Liu et al. reported that the combination of LPS and chronic stress significantly reduced the activity of parvalbumin-containing interneurons in the hippocampus, which was associated with a decrease in θ/γ oscillation power and accompanied by anhedonia and cognitive deficits [28]. These behavioral manifestations are highly similar to those observed in our LPS-induced animal model. Notably, γ oscillations serve as a fundamental indicator of neural network synchrony, and their dysregulation is mechanistically linked to impaired synaptic plasticity and imbalance of glutamate/γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [29]. Supporting this notion, Our data demonstrated that compared with the LPS group, PBM treatment exhibited improved neurophysiological changes, restoring them to normal levels. At rest, PBM treatment decreased relative δ (0.5–4 Hz), θ (4–12 Hz) and γ (30–100 Hz) power. Furthermore, δ/θ-γ CFC was enhanced. In anxiety-related tasks, the PBM group displayed enhanced δ-γ PAC, but γ activity was inhibited and δ/θ power was elevated compared with the LPS group. This paradoxical finding-γ suppression concurrent with enhanced δ-γ PAC suggests a complex, region-specific modulation of oscillatory dynamics that cannot be fully explained by current models and warrants further mechanistic investigation.In depression-related tasks, PBM treatment decreased relative δ, θ, and γ power compared with the LPS group, but the difference in PAC between the two groups was not significant. The absence of PAC modulation during depression-related behavioral recovery implies that PBM may exert its therapeutic effects through alternative neural mechanisms, such as power spectral reorganization or inter-regional coherence, rather than phase-amplitude coupling per se. Overall, these findings align with the improvement of anxiety-and depression-like behaviors in mice and suggest that PBM alleviates LPS-induced emotional symptoms by improving these neurophysiological characteristics, though whether these oscillatory changes are causally linked to behavioral recovery remains to be determined.

PBM therapy, as a non-invasive neuromodulatory approach targeting depression based on neural oscillations, has demonstrated remarkable potential. When compared with other therapeutic modalities, it shares certain mechanistic commonalities while also exhibiting unique differences. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is an established technique. 10-Hz high-frequency stimulation can enhance orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)-hippocampal (HPC) circuit function by entraining low-frequency neural oscillations (e.g., delta, theta oscillations), thereby exerting antidepressant effects [30]. This mechanism bears a striking resemblance to the restoration of abnormal oscillations induced by PBM in our study. Similar to how PBM requires optimization of wavelength and power density, the efficacy of rTMS is highly contingent upon the precision of stimulation frequency and target localization, underscoring the concept of precise modulation shared between the two therapies.

However, PBM and rTMS also differ in several key aspects. Unlike rTMS, which primarily modulates cortical neurons through induced electric currents, PBM is thought to act via photonic energy absorption by chromophores in neurons and glial cells, triggering intracellular signaling cascades and metabolic changes [31]. This fundamental difference in the biophysical interaction mechanisms may lead to divergent effects on neural plasticity, neurogenesis, and neuroinflammation, which warrant further comparative studies. Additionally, the depth of brain tissue penetration and the spatial distribution of the therapeutic effects may vary between PBM and rTMS, influencing their clinical applications and treatment protocols [32].

Previous studies have shown that 60 days of PBM light irradiation with a wavelength of 1070 ± 50 nm, lasting 6 minutes, and a power density of 25 mW/cm² significantly improved the performance of Alzheimer’ s disease (AD) mice in the novel object recognition (NOR) test. In addition, θ and γ relative power in the hippocampal CA1 region and δ/θ-γ CFC were significantly enhanced [33]. These findings are consistent with our results, which showed that compared with LPS mice, PBM-treated mice exhibited increased relative γ power and enhanced δ/θ-γ CFC at rest. This suggests that γ and δ/θ-γ CFC may play a role in regulating both emotion and cognition in mice. However, PBM treatment reduced relative θ power, which may indicate wavelength specificity. Future studies are needed to optimize parameters to achieve the best therapeutic effects. Moreover, the response to photobiomodulation may vary across different disease models. Our experiments were only conducted in the LPS model, and further studies are required in other models such as chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS), chronic restrained stress (CRS). The task-specific differences in neurophysiological responses to PBM warrant further investigation. The distinct patterns of neural oscillation changes observed in anxiety-versus depression-related tasks may reflect the involvement of different neural circuits or the preferential modulation of specific aspects of neural network dynamics by PBM. Long-term electrophysiological recordings and advanced imaging techniques could help delineate the spatiotemporal profiles of PBM-induced neurophysiological changes across different brain regions and their correlation with specific behavioral outcomes.

It is important to consider the distinct pathological features of different depression models when interpreting the therapeutic effects of PBM. The LPS-induced model primarily focuses on neuroinflammation [34], while the CUMS model emphasizes the stress-related aspects of depression [35]. These differences may lead to variations in the neurophysiological and behavioral responses to PBM. For instance, the prominent neuroinflammatory signature of the LPS paradigm may render defined cortico-limbic microcircuits particularly sensitive to PBM-driven entrainment of neural oscillations. Conversely, the maladaptive neuroplastic reorganization characteristic of the chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) paradigm could necessitate protocol refinements—such as altered irradiance, wavelength, or protracted treatment duration—to achieve comparable therapeutic efficacy. It should be emphasized that our findings are derived exclusively from male mice. Given the known sex differences in both depression pathophysiology and neural oscillatory profiles-potentially influenced by hormonal fluctuations-future studies should include female cohorts to assess the generalizability and potential sex-specific responsiveness of PBM therapy.

Furthermore, recent studies have shown that 5 days of treatment with a wavelength of 808 nm, a power density of 6 mW/cm², and lasting 5 min significantly improved the learning and memory performance of mice in the Barnes maze and Y-maze tests. θ, high-frequency γ, and θ-γ PAC in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) region were enhanced [22]. This indicates that PBM not only regulates neural oscillations in the hippocampal CA1 region but also exerts modulatory effects on multiple brain regions. The significant enhancement of δ-γ PAC in anxiety-related tasks by PBM but the lack of significant PAC differences in depression-related tasks may imply that the hippocampus CA1 is not the target region for PBM-induced improvement in depression-related tasks. Future research should explore other brain regions.

The lack of significant PAC differences in depression-related tasks despite improvements in other neurophysiological parameters suggests that the therapeutic effects of PBM may not be solely dependent on PAC modulation. Alternative neurophysiological mechanisms, such as changes in power spectra or coherence between distant brain regions, might contribute to the antidepressant effects. Moreover, the interactions between the hippocampus and other limbic or cortical regions implicated in depression pathophysiology, such as the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex, should be investigated to identify the full network-level changes induced by PBM. Advanced neuroimaging techniques and multielectrode array recordings could facilitate the mapping of these complex neural circuits and their dynamic reorganization following PBM treatment.

Conclusion

Our study revealed that a 4-day course of 810 nm, 20 mW/cm² PBM treatment effectively mitigated anxiety- and depression-like behaviors induced by LPS in mice. Moreover, PBM also improved the LPS-induced abnormal neural oscillations. These results suggest that assessing neural oscillations may serve as a valuable method for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of PBM.
