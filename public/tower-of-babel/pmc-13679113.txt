Malocclusion and orthodontic treatment needs among individuals with down syndrome in Egypt: a multicentre cross-sectional study

Introduction

Individuals who require additional care and attention in healthcare settings are commonly referred to as individuals with special healthcare needs [1, 2]. This group includes both children and adults who present with medical, physical, or intellectual conditions that may limit their ability to receive routine dental care. Such limitations often involve difficulties in communication, reduced cooperation, or physical constraints that complicate standard clinical procedures [3, 4].

Among these conditions, Down syndrome (DS), also known as trisomy 21, is one of the most prevalent genetic disorders. It results from the presence of a complete or partial extra copy of chromosome 21 [5]. This condition is associated with characteristic craniofacial features, delayed physical growth, varying degrees of intellectual disability, and an increased susceptibility to several systemic and oral health conditions. Early intervention and appropriate multidisciplinary care can significantly improve the quality of life and functional outcomes of affected individuals.

From a dental perspective, individuals with Down syndrome commonly exhibit a range of oral and craniofacial anomalies. These include malocclusion, delayed eruption of teeth, congenitally missing teeth, discrepancies in tooth size, impacted or transposed teeth, tongue thrusting habits, and a protrusive tongue posture. Additionally, skeletal discrepancies such as maxillary hypoplasia in both anteroposterior and transverse dimensions are frequently observed [6, 7]. These factors collectively contribute to a higher prevalence of orthodontic problems within this population.

A recent meta-analysis by Vicente et al. highlighted that reduced maxillary dimensions in individuals with Down syndrome restrict upper arch development, directly predisposing patients to a severe skeletal Class III malocclusion and posterior crossbite. This deficient maxillary growth, compounded by a relatively large tongue and habit patterns like tongue thrusting, frequently causes an anterior open bite where the opposing front teeth fail to meet. Furthermore, underlying T-cell deficiencies and impaired neutrophil function drastically reduce the body’s defense mechanisms against oral bacteria, leading to an exceptionally high prevalence of early-onset, aggressive periodontal disease even in the absence of heavy plaque accumulation [8].

Individuals with Down syndrome exhibit a high prevalence of severe skeletal malocclusions and an urgent need for orthodontic intervention compared to non-syndromic populations [9, 10]. Angle Class III malocclusion is the most dominant sagittal trait, affecting approximately 59% of individuals across both pediatric and cross-sectional global cohorts [11]. Affected individuals also present with distinct morphological differences, including significantly elevated rates of missing teeth (56.2%), incisal spacing (73.0%), and anterior maxillary irregularity (95.5%) compared to healthy controls [9]. Consequently, objective clinical indices reveal that between 81.9% and 85.3% of these patients present with an absolute, mandatory need for orthodontic care [10, 11].

However, significant barriers to successful treatment persist, including a widespread 30.4% lack of specialist referrals and a high 45% premature treatment termination rate due to patient compliance issues and severe hypodontia [11, 12].

Previous studies have highlighted the significant need for orthodontic intervention among individuals with Down syndrome. For instance, a cross-sectional study conducted by Alkawari [13] assessed orthodontic treatment needs using the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) and reported that 81.9% of participants required treatment. Among these, Class III malocclusion was the most prevalent (59.1%), followed by Class I occlusion (36.4%). No statistically significant differences were observed between genders in relation to treatment need.

Despite the high prevalence of dental anomalies, routine dental examination and management of individuals with Down syndrome can be challenging for both caregivers and dental professionals due to behavioral and medical considerations. Additionally, there is a lack of research comprehensively linking malocclusion patterns, treatment needs, oral health, and craniofacial features across varied Down syndrome cohorts. Furthermore, there is a distinct lack of comprehensive research assessing malocclusion patterns and orthodontic intervention priorities specifically tailored to Down syndrome populations in Egypt and the broader Middle East. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of their specific needs is essential to provide appropriate and effective dental care. Consequently, there is a compelling need to investigate these specific regional manifestations, serving as a vital clinical foundation to optimize orthodontic care delivery for this underserved population.

Aim of the study

The aim of this cross-sectional observational study was to assess the prevalence, patterns, and severity of malocclusion, oral health status, and craniofacial features and orthodontic treatment needs among individuals with Down syndrome in Egypt.

This aim was achieved through the following approaches:

To clinically characterize malocclusion in patients with Down syndrome using Angle’s classification and the Dental Health Component (DHC) of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN).To clinically assess occlusal relationships and dental arch morphology utilizing a non-invasive approach for such special needs group. Materials and methods

Study design

This investigation was designed as an observational, cross-sectional study conducted over a three-month period from January 2026 to April 2026. The study aimed to evaluate the prevalence, patterns, and severity of malocclusion and orthodontic treatment needs among individuals with Down syndrome attending specialized care institutions.

The study was carried out across multiple specialized institutions in Alexandria and Beheira governorates, Egypt, including Gam’iyat Al-Hayah School, El Tarbeya El Fekreya School and Fairhaven School. Research was conducted at such centers based on accessibility and availability of eligible participants.

To ensure methodological rigor, reliability, and consistency of clinical assessments, all examinations were conducted by four well trained calibrated examiners, orthodontist and community dentist with experience in special needs populations. Comprehensive calibration sessions were conducted on 20 participants prior to the initiation of data collection to standardize diagnostic criteria, particularly concerning the application of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) and Angle’s classification system. Calibration participants were included in the final sample. Inter-examiner reliability was assessed using kappa statistics, with values exceeding 0.85 indicating excellent agreement.

Target population/sample size

Based on a previous study by Alkawari H [13] showing expected prevalence of 81.9%, and using margin of error of 7% and confidence level of 95%, the estimated minimum required sample size was 117 participants. The sample size was calculated using Epi-info software (Epi-info software for Windows, Version 7.2.6, StatCalc Module, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, Georgia).

Study participants

An initial sample of 200 individual with Down syndrome was assessed for eligibility. Following the application of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 117 individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of Down syndrome were included in the final analysis. Three participants were excluded from the study; two participants were diagnosed with relatively unstable systemic condition and one participant was diagnosed with acute oral infection. Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling technique from the selected institutions. Enrollment was conducted following coordination with institutional administrators, healthcare providers, and primary caregivers.

Clinical photography protocol

Standardized intraoral and extraoral photographs were obtained for all cooperative participants. Intraoral photographs were captured using a [Nikon] [model, D5600] digital camera with a [Sigma 105 mm f2.8 macro] macro lens and [Godox mf 12 twinflash K&F, ringflash, Sbarkdent octa v8 difuser], and included the following views: frontal in occlusion, right and left lateral, and maxillary and mandibular occlusal. Cheek retractors and intraoral mirrors were used where tolerated. Extraoral photographs comprised frontal (at rest and smiling), right and left profile views, taken against a neutral background under consistent ambient lighting.

All Photographs were taken in standard settings in manual mode, employing aperture (f/22), shutter speed (1/100s), and low ISO (100) for consistent, deep-focus images. Imaging relies on fixed magnification ratios (1:2.5), and patient positioning in Natural Head Position to eliminate distortion across records.

To safeguard participant anonymity, the ocular region was digitally blurred in all facial photographs. Explicit informed consent for publication of identifiable facial images was obtained. Photographs were acquired only after separate written consent for photography and publication was obtained from parents or legal guardians. The IOTN-DHC isolates a patient’s single worst occlusal defect by scanning the dentition sequentially through the MOCDO hierarchy (Missing teeth, Overjet, Crossbite, Displacement, Overbite) and recording only the anomaly that yields the highest numerical grade. Any doubtful or borderline cases falling precisely between two categories are systematically resolved by awarding the lower, more conservative treatment grade to prevent over-diagnosis and prioritize public health resources.

Clinical examination and data collection were performed by the four calibrated examiners, both formally trained in the application of the IOTN to ensure consistency, accuracy, and inter/intra examiner reliability.

Clinical procedures and infection control

A digital intraoral scanner (Runyes H10, Runyes Medical Instrument Co., Ltd, Ningbo, China) was employed to obtain precise, high-resolution digital impressions of participants’ dentition. Digital scanning offered several advantages in this population, including reduced examination time, elimination of traditional impression material discomfort, and immediate visualization for patient and caregiver education. Figure 2 illustrates the scanner used in the study and the output scans of one of the participants.

Fig. 2Intra oral scanner used to obtain precise, high-resolution digital dental models

Intra oral scanner used to obtain precise, high-resolution digital dental models

Intraoral scanning protocol began at the maxillary right second molar, gliding across the occlusal surfaces to the maxillary left second molar, before rolling to capture the buccal and palatal aspects in a continuous motion. The mandibular arch was scanned next, starting at the mandibular left second molar and finishing at the mandibular right second molar, ensuring full coverage of occlusal, lingual, and buccal boundaries. Finally, a bilateral buccal bite scan was captured in maximum intercuspation from the canine to the first molar regions to digitally articulate the arches.

Digital dental models were generated with incomplete or distorted scans repeated after 2 min interval. The study evaluated occlusal variables, including sagittal overjet, vertical overbite, and specific tooth alignments, molar relationship, all recorded in millimeters (mm) using standardized anatomical landmarks on the incisal edges and molar cusps.

Rigorous infection control protocols were strictly adhered to throughout all phases of the study. Disposable protective barrier sleeves were utilized for the intraoral scanner handpiece and replaced between each participant to prevent cross-contamination. Standard personal protective equipment (PPE), including examination gloves, surgical masks, protective eyewear, and disposable gowns, was worn during all clinical procedures. All non-disposable instruments were sterilized in an autoclave according to established protocols.

Educational tools, including visual aids, dental models, and age-appropriate instructional materials, were utilized to demonstrate proper oral hygiene techniques to patients and their caregivers. Oral hygiene instruction was tailored to the cognitive and motor abilities of individual participants. Figure 3 illustrates the educational tools used in the study.

Fig. 3Educational tools utilized as rewards and to demonstrate proper oral hygiene techniques

Given the behavioral characteristics commonly associated with Down syndrome, consultation with psychological services and experienced special needs educators was sought to guide appropriate communication strategies and behavior management techniques during examination. Tell-show-do techniques, positive reinforcement, and caregiver presence were employed to maximize cooperation.

Patients identified as requiring primary dental care, including restorative treatment or periodontal therapy (e.g., scaling and root planing), were appropriately referred to affiliated dental clinics or specialist providers prior to or following orthodontic evaluation as clinically indicated.

Data analysis

All collected data were coded, entered, and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. Qualitative (categorical) variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD).

As the primary objective was descriptive, no hypothesis testing was planned or performed; the analysis was limited to descriptive statistics.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical standards of Pharos University. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Pharos University in Alexandria (PUA-REC). Ethical approval was granted on January 6, 2026, with reference approval code PUA-REC-012 (Council Serial: 0220260163463), as illustrated in Appendix 1.

Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants prior to enrollment. The study objectives, examination procedures, potential risks and benefits, and the voluntary nature of participation were thoroughly explained. Caregivers were assured that participation or non-participation would not affect the standard of care provided to their dependents at the participating institutions. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained through the use of unique identification codes, with all data stored on password-protected, encrypted devices accessible only to the research team. Separate written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians for the acquisition, storage, and potential publication of clinical photographs, with explicit assurance that all identifiable facial images would have the ocular region digitally blurred to preserve anonymity.

Medical and dental history

The majority of participants, 101 individuals (86.3%), were reported to have no additional diagnosed systemic condition, while 16 participants (13.7%) had a documented systemic condition, including controlled cardiac anomalies and thyroid disorders. Discussion

The findings of the present investigation provide a comprehensive characterization of malocclusion patterns, associated craniofacial features, and orthodontic treatment needs among a cohort of 117 individuals with Down syndrome. The results unequivocally indicate a substantial burden of malocclusion and a pronounced need for orthodontic intervention, with approximately half of the participants (49.6%) exhibiting great to very great treatment requirements according to the IOTN.

Orthodontic treatment need and malocclusion patterns

The observed prevalence of definitive or borderline orthodontic treatment need (Grades 4–5: 49.6%) is consistent with previously reported figures in the international literature. However, in a cross-sectional study of individuals with Down syndrome, Alkawari documented that 81.9% required orthodontic treatment based on IOTN assessment [13]. The slight discrepancy between these figures may be attributable to variations in sample size, age distribution, geographic and ethnic differences in craniofacial morphology, or subtle differences in examiner calibration. Nevertheless, both studies converge on the overarching conclusion that the vast majority of individuals with Down syndrome present with malocclusions of sufficient severity to warrant professional orthodontic evaluation and, in many cases, intervention.

Of particular clinical significance is the finding that 49.6% of participants were categorized as IOTN Grades 4 or 5, indicating great or very great treatment need. These grades correspond to severe occlusal discrepancies including, but not limited to, increased overjet greater than 9 mm, reverse overjet greater than 3.5 mm with reported masticatory or speech difficulties, anterior or posterior crossbites with associated mandibular displacement, severe open bites, and hypodontia requiring complex interdisciplinary management. The high proportion of patients falling into these severe categories underscores the critical importance of early identification and referral to specialist orthodontic services equipped to manage complex cases in the context of intellectual and developmental disabilities.

Regarding Angle’s classification, the distribution of molar relationships in our cohort—Class I (41.0%), Class II (29.9%), and Class III (29.1%)—differs somewhat from the pattern reported by Alkawari [13], who identified Class III malocclusion as the predominant pattern (59.1%) in a relatively smaller sample (23 children aged 10–14 years). While Class III malocclusion is classically associated with Down syndrome due to maxillary hypoplasia and relative mandibular prognathism, our findings suggest a more heterogeneous distribution. Alternatively, this finding may indicate phenotypic variability within the Down syndrome population. Longitudinal follow-up of this cohort would be valuable to assess whether the prevalence of Class III malocclusion increases with age.

The prevalence of anterior open bite and posterior crossbite in our sample confirms previously reported studies on Down syndrome populations [6, 7]. These occlusal features are multifactorial in etiology, resulting from the interplay of skeletal maxillary transverse deficiency and mouth breathing. Mouth breathing was observed in 32.5% of participants, while oral respiration, either alone or combined with other breathing patterns, was found in 36.7%. These findings further support the theory possibly attributing the development of vertical and transverse occlusal problems in this population to the altered breathing patterns in the.

Oral health status and behavioral considerations

The oral hygiene in this study warrant particular attention. Only 18.8% of participants demonstrated good oral hygiene, while 81.2% were rated as having fair or poor oral hygiene. These findings, while not unexpected given the well-documented challenges of maintaining optimal oral hygiene in individuals with intellectual disabilities, highlight a critical area for intervention [15]. The high proportion of participants reporting infrequent brushing (44.4% brushing occasionally or never) underscores the need for targeted oral health education and support for both patients and caregivers [16]. However, toothbrushing frequency was apparently reported by participants or their caregivers, which may have introduced recall and social desirability bias. This might be considered as a possible limitation of our study.

Clinical implications and management considerations

The management of malocclusion in patients with Down syndrome may require a slightly different approach from conventional orthodontic treatment philosophies. The goal of treatment should not be solely defined by the achievement of ideal occlusal relationships as described by Angle, but rather by the clinician’s ability to balance biomechanical principles with the biological, behavioral, and medical realities of this population. Comparison with international literature

The findings of this study align with a growing body of international literature documenting the elevated orthodontic needs of individuals with Down syndrome. In contrast to the findings of our study, a Study by AlSarheed reported high IOTN grades and a predominance of Class III malocclusion (45% − 50%), anterior open bite, and posterior crossbite [18]. In contrast to previous study by Oredugba addressing the oral prophylaxis need and restorative clinical treatment need, the present investigation adds valuable data, highlighting orthodontic treatment need in individuals with Down syndrome [19].

Study strengths

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, the relatively large sample size (n = 117) compared to previous studies. Second, the comprehensive nature of the clinical assessment, encompassing extra-oral facial analysis, profile evaluation, intra-oral findings, functional parameters, and aesthetic considerations, provides a better characterization of the Down syndrome craniofacial and occlusal phenotype. Third, the use of calibrated examiners and a validated, internationally recognized index (IOTN) ensure methodological rigor and facilitates comparison with other studies. Fourth, the incorporation of digital intraoral scanning represents a contemporary approach to data collection in an attempt to minimize patient discomfort and provides a permanent, high-resolution record for future reference and analysis.

Study limitations

Despite the contributions of this investigation, several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

First, the study employed a non-probability convenience sampling technique, which may introduce selection bias. Participants were recruited exclusively from specialized institutions, and the findings may not be fully generalizable to individuals with Down syndrome residing in other geographic regions, those attending mainstream educational settings, or those not engaged with institutional care. Possible clustering might have added power to the current study.

Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the assessment of longitudinal changes in occlusal relationships and treatment needs over time.

Third, while the IOTN Dental Health Component is a validated and widely used index, it does not capture the full spectrum of factors influencing orthodontic treatment decisions, particularly the Aesthetic Component, patient/parent perceptions, and psychosocial considerations. The inclusion of the Aesthetic Component and quality-of-life measures in future studies would provide a more comprehensive assessment of treatment need. Moreover, Dental Health Component assesses normative need and does not necessarily reflect treatment demand, feasibility, patient preference or likely benefit.

Fourth, patient cooperation, while manageable for examination purposes in the majority of cases, posed challenges in a subset of participants (24.8% uncooperative). This may have influenced the accuracy and completeness of certain clinical assessments, particularly those requiring sustained patient compliance (e.g., intraoral scanning, detailed occlusal evaluation). Excluding individuals who could not cooperate may produce selection bias and underestimate the clinical challenges and treatment needs of individuals with more severe disability.

Fifth, the study did not include radiographic assessment (e.g., panoramic or cephalometric radiographs). Consequently, the presence of impacted teeth, congenitally missing teeth, root morphology anomalies, and precise skeletal relationships could not be fully characterized. Despite the ethical concerns regarding patient exposure to radiation, radiographic evaluation would enhance diagnostic precision and inform comprehensive treatment planning. The lack of imaging prevented assessment of skeletal relationships and dental anomalies.

Another possible limitation in the current study is the potential misclassification. Moreover, oral habits, medical history and brushing frequency appear to rely partly on caregiver reports and may be affected by recall bias. In addition, The cross-sectional design does not permit causal conclusions regarding relationships between habits, breathing patterns, oral hygiene and malocclusion. Finally, No control group of individuals without Down syndrome was included. Therefore, the study cannot determine whether the observed prevalence differs from that in the general population.

Conclusion

In this institution-based sample of individuals with Down syndrome, 22.2% had borderline orthodontic treatment need (IOTN-DHC Grade 3), while 49.6% had great or very great treatment need (Grades 4–5). Class I was the most frequent molar relationship, although Class II and Class III relationships were also common. Anterior open bite, posterior crossbite and suboptimal oral hygiene were frequently observed. These findings support the importance of accessible orthodontic assessment and preventive oral-health support for individuals with Down syndrome. However, the convenience sampling method limits generalization beyond the participating institutions.
