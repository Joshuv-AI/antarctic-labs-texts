Air Conditioning Use and Ambulance Transports for Heat-Related Illness Across Summer Months in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area
Authors: Lisa Yamasaki, Chris Fook Sheng Ng, Aurelio Tobias, Yuya Takane, Kazuki Yamaguchi, Masahiro Hashizume
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat is a growing public health threat, yet seasonal variations in adaptation including air conditioning (AC) use remain poorly understood. We examined the modifying role of electricity consumption (EC), a proxy for air conditioning (AC) use, in the association between maximum temperature and ambulance transports for heat-related illness in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area. Daily ambulance transport data (June-September, 2015–2019), alongside maximum temperature and EC data, were analyzed across seven prefectures. Using a two-stage approach, we fitted quasi-Poisson regression models to estimate relative risks (RRs) per 1 °C increase in maximum temperature under high and low EC conditions, and pooled prefecture-month-specific estimates through multivariate meta-analysis. Attributable fractions (AFs) and attributable numbers (ANs) were also calculated. A total of 88,893 transports were analyzed. In June, higher risks were observed under high EC (RR = 1.37, 95%CI: 1.32–1.42) compared with low EC (RR = 1.28, 95%CI: 1.25–1.32). In July, the pattern reversed, with lower risks under high EC (RR = 1.33, 95%CI: 1.30–1.36) than low EC (RR = 1.39, 95%CI: 1.35–1.44). EC modified the association between heat and ambulance transport in both early summer months (p = 0.01 and 0.03, respectively), but not in August or September. Across months and EC strata, heat-attributable fractions ranged from 22.1% to 34.0%. The higher risks under high EC in June may have reflected delayed or insufficient air conditioning use, whereas the lower risks in July are consistent with a possible protective role of AC emerging as the season progresses. These findings underscore the need for early season adaptation measures.

Introduction

Heat is an increasingly urgent public health issue, particularly under rising global temperatures. Heat stress is among the leading causes of weather-related mortality and often worsens existing health conditions. −

Emerging evidence shows that health risks are not uniform across the summer season. Early summer often presents greater hazards because populations are less physiologically acclimatized and slower to adopt adaptive behaviors. − For example, in the United States, mortality increased by approximately 5.0% during the first heat wave of the season compared with 2.7% in subsequent events. In France, even moderate early summer heat was linked to elevated mortality risk. In the Tokyo Metropolitan Area, early hot days in June have been associated with sharp increases in ambulance transports for heatstroke, even at relatively mild temperatures. These findings are consistent with the influence of both physiological acclimatization and behavioral responses, such as the timely use of air conditioning (AC), on seasonal differences in risk. More broadly, the increasing prevalence of AC has been identified as a key driver of the observed decline in heat-related mortality over recent decades. Electricity-powered solutions such as AC have emerged as the primary behavioral adaptation against heat, and both their adoption and timely use are critical determinants of population-level protection. However, the public health benefit of AC depends on timely and consistent use, not merely on availability.

This gap between AC availability and effective use is illustrated in Japan, where summers are increasingly hot and humid, with more than 90,000 emergency cases of heat-related illness reported in the summer of 2023 and 2024. , Of particular concern, 86% of heatstroke deaths at home in Tokyo in 2024 occurred with air conditioning not in operationwhether absent, broken, or unused. in 2024 in Tokyo. Although AC is widely available, insufficient utilization remains a concern, particularly in an aging society that is facing increasingly severe and frequent heat events. Quantifying how much AC is actually being used at the population level remains a challenge, and electricity consumption (EC) provides a practical proxy: AC accounts for approximately one-third to one-half of household electricity use depending on time of day during summer, and the temperature-sensitive component of electricity consumption in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area is primarily driven by AC demand. ,

Given the high burden of heat-related morbidity and the possibility that risks vary across summer months, this study investigates whether electricity consumption (EC), a proxy for AC use, modifies the association between daily maximum temperature and ambulance transports for heat-related illness in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area. Specifically, we examine EC-based effect modification from early to late summer to better understand how EC relates to seasonal differences in heat-related health risks, thereby informing strategies for AC adoption in seasonal adaptation.

Methods

Data

We obtained daily records of heat-related illness ambulance transports across the Tokyo Metropolitan Area, comprising seven prefectures: Chiba, Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Tochigi, Gunma, and Ibaraki, during the summer months (June to September) from 2015 to 2019. These data included all ambulance transports in which medical doctors suspected heat-related illnesses upon hospital admission, covering cases diagnosed as heat cramps, heat exhaustion, or heat stroke. Diagnoses were established based on physicians’ clinical judgment during the initial assessment.

Daily maximum temperature and relative humidity data for each prefecture were collected from the Japan Meteorological Agency, using measurements from observation stations located in the respective prefectural capitals.

Daily EC data were constructed based on the electricity data set developed by Nakajima and colleagues. , Hourly EC data were collected from 1405 distribution substations in central Japan, providing high spatial resolution within the Tokyo Metropolitan Area. After excluding 115 substations due to low building density (fewer than 50 buildings per km) or missing data, 1290 substations remained for analysis. To account for differences in urban density and building characteristics, EC was normalized by total floor area (EC per floor), using building footprint and height data from ESRI Japan’s polygon data set, enabling consistent comparisons across substations and prefectures with diverse urban forms. Prefecture-level EC was then estimated by aggregating substation-level electricity use, weighted by the total floor area of buildings each substation served.

Statistical Analysis

We investigated whether EC modifies the relationship between ambient temperature and ambulance transports for heat-related illnesses during the summer months using a two-stage approach.

In the first stage, we fitted generalized linear models with a quasi-Poisson distribution separately for each prefecture and calendar month. Daily maximum temperature was modeled assuming a linear exposure–response relationship with same-day values (lag 0), as heat-related illnesses are expected to respond acutely to temperature exposure on the day it occurs. This specification was supported by exploratory analyses, which favored a linear form over nonlinear alternatives and indicated no meaningful lag effects beyond the current day. Models were adjusted for relative humidity, day of the week, and year as a categorical variable. EC was classified as high or low according to the monthly median for each prefecture, with days above the median labeled as high consumption and those below as low. This choice was made in accordance with previous research that classified building electricity use into high and low consumption. , To assess effect modification by EC, we included an interaction term between temperature and this binary EC category. For each prefecture and month, we estimated the relative risks (RRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals associated with a 1 °C increase in temperature under both high and low EC conditions.

In the second stage, we pooled the prefecture-specific estimates of the temperature effects using a multivariate meta-analysis to derive monthly summaries for the Tokyo Metropolitan Area under high and low EC conditions. A Wald test was used to evaluate the difference in pooled log-RRs between high and low EC strata. Based on these pooled prefecture-month-specific estimates, we then calculated attributable fractions as (RR – 1)/RR and attributable numbers by multiplying the attributable fraction by the monthly observed number of heat illness-related ambulance transports within each EC category. These estimates summarize the health burden associated with a 1 °C increase in daily maximum temperature under varying levels of EC.

As a sensitivity analysis, we estimated the first-stage models replacing relative humidity with absolute humidity to assess whether the observed effect modification by EC was robust to the choice of humidity metric. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0; R Core Team, 2024), including data processing, model fitting, and meta-analysis.

Discussion

We examined whether EC modified the association between daily maximum temperature and ambulance transports for heat-related illnesses during the summer months in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area. Ambient temperature was positively associated with ambulance transports throughout the summer. Our results provide evidence of effect modification by EC in June and July, though in opposite directions. In June, a higher risk of ambulance transports was observed on days with high EC. This pattern reversed in July, with higher risk on days with low EC. EC did not appear to modify the association in August and September, although risks remained positive. While heat-related health risks are known to vary between early and late summer, the mechanisms underlying this within-season heterogeneity have remained poorly characterized. Our analysis used area-level electricity consumption data to reveal a directionally opposite pattern of EC-based effect modification between June and July, suggesting that the relationship between population-level cooling behavior and heat-related illness risk shifts as the summer progresses.

In early summer, we observed higher risks on days with high EC. In June, high electricity demand on relatively warm days may indicate early but insufficient use of cooling, which does not adequately attenuate heat-related illness risk. Several behavioral mechanisms are plausible. AC use may be inconsistent or partial, as the duration and intensity of heat have not yet prompted sustained use, resulting in higher EC without sufficient protection. AC use may also lag behind rising temperatures in early summer, as some individuals are not yet fully accustomed to the timely use of cooling systems. These factors suggest that high aggregate EC does not necessarily correspond to effective cooling at the population level.

Beyond behavioral factors, physiological mechanisms may also contribute to the elevated risks observed in June. Early season heat exposure imposes greater physiological stress because human thermoregulatory systems have not yet undergone seasonal acclimatization. Previous studies have shown that heat-related illnesses often surge during the first heatwaves of the season, when both physiological and behavioral adaptation are incomplete, and that susceptibility to heat decreases as the warm season progresses.

Older populations may further contribute to the observed higher risks in early summer despite higher EC, as they are more prone to delaying or inconsistently adopting adaptive measures against heat. In Japan, where older adults account for 29.3% of the population, the risks are amplified by the high prevalence of older adults living alone, projected to exceed 20% by 2030. Although age-specific analyses were not conducted in this study due to the limited availability of age-stratified energy consumption data, older adults comprise more than half of heat-related illness transports and represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup. Physiological changes associated with aging, including reduced skin temperature sensitivity, impaired sweating and thirst responses, lower total body water content, and diminished cardiovascular reserve, can limit the ability to perceive and dissipate heat, accelerating dehydration and heat stress. , Many older individuals additionally lack environmental and social support, and socioeconomic disadvantages such as low income and preexisting illness may further compound vulnerability, as observed globally.

In addition to physiological and socioeconomic vulnerability, older adults often delay or avoid AC use despite ownership, owing to both blunted heat perception and concerns about electricity costs. Under this interpretation, high community-level EC may coexist with suboptimal AC use among the most vulnerable individualsa mismatch that could partly explain residual heat-related illness risk even during periods of elevated area-level electricity demand. These hypotheses warrant direct examination in future studies incorporating age-stratified exposure and behavioral data. Mitigation strategies such as targeted electricity subsidies for older and low-income populations or automated AC control systems that adjust to ambient conditions may provide more immediate and equitable protection.

By midsummer, in July, the pattern reversed, with lower risks on high EC days. This shift between June and July likely reflects the gradual progression of AC adoption and physiological acclimatization across the summer season. By July, sustained high temperatures are more likely to drive widespread and consistent AC use. In addition, repeated exposure to hot conditions over preceding weeks promotes physiological acclimatization, reducing individual susceptibility to heat stress. Under these conditions, higher electricity demand may more closely track effective cooling behavior, consistent with the lower risk observed under high EC.

In August and September, EC did not appear to modify risks. In August, the hottest month, AC use may have reached a saturation point, reducing differences between high and low EC days. In September, declining temperatures and cooler nights likely reduced AC use overall, weakening the link between EC and AC operation. As a result, EC became less representative of protective cooling behaviors.

Beyond these patterns of effect modification, the absolute burden estimates of heat-attributable ambulance transports were heavily concentrated in July and August, particularly on high EC days where absolute temperatures were high and more ambulance transport cases occurred during those periods. In September, the attributable fraction was the highest across the season (approximately 34%) despite no detectable effect modification by EC. This may reflect that the AF depends on the per-1 °C relative risk rather than absolute case counts, which were small in September. These burden estimates have direct relevance for resource allocation and emergency preparedness planning.

Our findings suggest that improving the timely and consistent use of AC and other electricity-based measures could help prevent heat-related illness. Indeed, the finding of higher risk on high EC days in early summer carries important implications for public health communication and policy. In early summer, high area-level EC that coincides with high risk of heat may imply ineffective use of EC among individuals at risk, particularly older adults and those living alone. Targeted outreach and behavioral interventions are needed to promote timely and effective AC adoption at the start of the hot season, alongside monitoring of heat-related illness trends as an independent indicator of adaptation adequacy. Previous studies show that power outages during heat waves can substantially exacerbate health risks. , Promoting balanced cooling strategies, including the combined use of AC and electric fans, remains important. − Additionally, household-level measures such as encouraging energy-efficient AC technologies, improving building insulation, and integrating smart control systems could enhance the sustainability of residential cooling. At the community level, adaptation strategies such as expanding green and blue spaces, − early warning systems, , and the provision of public cooling spaces may provide additional protection, particularly for socially isolated or physiologically vulnerable older adults. Policies that balance between protection of vulnerable populations with minimizing environmental burdens may offer more sustainable approaches in the face of intensifying climate-related heat events.

Several methodological considerations should be noted in interpreting these findings. In this study, EC was used as an indicator of area-level electricity demand. The hypothesis underlying this approach is that on days when EC exceeds the monthly median, a large share of that demand can be attributed to temperature-responsive cooling use, including AC. Previous studies have shown that in regions with widespread AC penetration, fluctuations in EC during hot summer months are substantially driven by AC cooling demand. Based on national survey data, the average prevalence of AC ownership across the seven prefectures in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area was approximately 94% of households with at least two members. Given this context, day-to-day variation in EC above the monthly median can reasonably be attributed in large part to temperature-responsive cooling demand, supporting its use as a population-level indicator of cooling-related energy use during hot periods. In addition, we used daily maximum temperature as the primary heat exposure metric because of its direct physiological relevance. Maximum temperature aligns closely with the biological mechanisms of heat-related illnesses such as heatstroke, dehydration, and cardiovascular strain. Its use is further supported by existing evidence from Japan, which links maximum temperature to both heatstroke incidence and electricity consumption. , In addition to directly reflecting acute heat stress, maximum temperature is the measure commonly reported in weather forecasts in Japan, enhancing its public health relevance.

Our study has several limitations. First, the data on heat-related illness were based on physician diagnoses at the time of ambulance transport, prior to confirmatory testing. According to diagnostic criteria in Japan, heat-related illness is defined as the presence of symptoms such as dizziness or fainting following exposure to a hot environment, after excluding other underlying diseases. Therefore, patients presenting with other major conditions (e.g., stroke, cardiac event), even if heat contributed, were not included in this data set, potentially underestimating the burden. Second, EC data included industrial and commercial consumption in addition to household use. Although individual-level AC usage data were unavailable and EC necessarily includes commercial and industrial consumption in addition to household cooling, previous studies have shown that summer EC is responsive to surface temperature to a great extent, supporting its use as an area-level proxy for cooling demand. Therefore, while EC is a useful area-level proxy for cooling-related energy use that is primarily driven by AC, it also inevitably includes other uses such as refrigeration.

Conclusions

This study is the first to examine how EC modifies the association between ambient temperature and heat-related illness in the Tokyo Metropolitan Area across different summer months. We found that higher EC was linked to greater risks in June, likely reflecting delays, inconsistent use, or competing electricity demands early in the season, when physiological and behavioral adaptation to heat is incomplete. By July, higher EC appeared protective, consistent with more sustained and widespread AC use. In August and September, the modifying role of EC diminished, suggesting saturation of cooling behaviors during peak heat and reduced reliance on AC as temperatures declined. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions in early summer, such as promoting timely and consistent use of cooling, addressing misconceptions about AC, and providing support for vulnerable populations and households. At the same time, reliance on electricity alone may not ensure resilience. Policies that combine household-level measures such as energy-efficient cooling technologies, building insulation, and smart control systems with community-level adaptations including early warning systems, public cooling spaces, and urban greening can reduce vulnerability while also promoting environmental sustainability in a warming climate.
