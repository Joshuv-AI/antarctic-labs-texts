School start times and their association with rurality in British Columbia, Canada: An environmental scan
Authors: Elizabeth Keys, Tai-Lin Michon, Kayleigh Boogaart, Amy Beck
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesSince later school start times are associated with increased sleep duration, our objectives were to identify mean school start times, the proportion of schools that start at 08:30 am or later, and determine if rurality was associated with school start times.MethodsWe conducted web searches for start times of 1759 standard schools in British Columbia (BC), Canada. Schools were categorized as elementary, elementary-middle, middle, middle-high, or high school and linked to an Index of Remoteness. We calculated descriptive statistics and used Analysis of Variance to assess for start time differences by grade category. We used Spearman’s rank-order correlation to assess if there was a relationship between start time and rurality.ResultsWe found start times for 1553 (88.2%) of the included schools. The mean start time was 08:40 am (SD = 0:15) and ranged from 07:10 am to 09:45 am. There was a significant effect of grade category on start time, F (5, 1600) = 6.03, p < .001, η2 =.02, 95% CI [.006, .031] such that elementary-middle schools started significantly earlier (M = 08:34 am, SD = 0:17) than other grade categories. Overall, 1388 (86.4%) schools started at 08:30 am or later. Rurality was significantly correlated with school start time (r = −.198), such that more rural schools started earlier.ConclusionsFor the most part, school start times in BC meet recommendations that support childhood and adolescent sleep. Future research is needed to understand factors that promote the successful implementation of delayed school start times.

Statement of Significance

The implementation of later start times at schools has been demonstrated to offer students increased opportunities to obtain optimal sleep duration, which is essential for maintaining health. In a study conducted across a broad regional area in Canada, the majority of schools followed schedules that adhered to recommended sleep guidelines, including elementary, middle, and high schools. This research is noteworthy due to the extensive scope of the region assessed and the fact that the data was collected from school websites, rather than relying on self-reports from a convenience sample. Furthermore, we investigated whether rurality was associated with school start times and discovered a negative correlation, which contradicts previous research and calls for further research.

Introduction

Substantial evidence demonstrates that school start times influence sleep duration, with later school start times allowing for longer sleep [1–8]. While the link between later school start times and improved sleep duration is well-established for middle [2, 8–10] and high school students [2–6, 8], research on elementary school students is limited. Some studies suggest that earlier school start times predict less sleep for elementary students [8, 11, 12], while other researchers have found no association [13] or a more minor impact compared to middle and high school [8]. Further, later school start times for middle and high school students may confer additional benefits with respect to developmental outcomes, reduced daytime sleepiness, improved mood, fewer reports of morning tiredness, and fewer motor vehicle crashes [2, 7, 10–12, 14]. Thus, it is important to examine school start times, since they may impact sleep, as well as social and health outcomes.

Robust evidence supporting later school start times for middle and high school students has led to widespread recommendations for schools to start at 08:30 am or later [15, 16]. Delaying school start times is a proposed public health strategy to address sleep health inequities [17], as lower socioeconomic status correlates with shorter sleep duration in children and adolescents aged 0–18 years [18]. However, concerns about transportation [19, 20], school athletics [19–22], childcare [20–22], and after-school activities [20] have likely hindered implementation.

Approximately one-third of Canadian children and youth do not meet Canadian sleep duration guidelines [2, 23–26]. Although Canada lacks national recommendations for school start times, Canadian studies have indicated that later start times correlate with longer sleep duration and a higher probability of meeting sleep recommendations [2, 4, 5]. However, these findings are over 7 years old, based on self-reported data, and limited to middle and high school students. Thus, to evaluate current school start times in a Canadian setting, we conducted an environmental scan of school start times in the Canadian province of British Columbia (BC).

Our research questions were (1a) what are the average school start times for elementary, middle/junior high, and high schools? and (1b) what proportion of schools in each grade category start at 08:30 am or later? Given that British Columbia includes a mix of urban, rural, and remote schools, and people in rural areas often face health disparities, a secondary question was (2) is rurality associated with school start times? We hypothesized an average school start time of 08:30 am, aligning with Canadian middle and high schools start times reported by Patte and colleagues [4]. Given their finding of a median start time of 08:42 am, we expected at least 50% of schools to begin at 08:30 am or later. Lastly, we hypothesized that increasing rurality would be associated with later start times, as urbanicity has been linked to earlier school start times [27].

Methods

Our scan used a publicly available list of all 2329 schools in BC that included information on school district, school type (e.g., standard, alternate, distributed learning, etc.), category (i.e., public or independent), location, included grades, and student enrollment totals (http://www.bced.gov.bc.ca). For context, BC is the western-most province in Canada, has a total population of over 5 million people, and is comprised of urban, rural, and remote communities [28]. We included only standard schools (n = 1759) that included at least one grade group (including Kindergarten only). Thus, we excluded continuing education schools (e.g., schools that provide education for adult learners 18 years old and older; n = 31), schools that only delivered distributed learning programs (e.g., online learning; n = 70), alternate programing schools (e.g., schools that provide support for students whose needs are not met in the traditional school setting; n = 81), schools that were long-term provincial resource programs (e.g., providing education to students on an outreach basis or in a provincial center), youth custody/residential centers (e.g., students involved with the criminal justice system; n = 6), or elementary ungraded schools (e.g., students in courses at the Kindergarten to Grade 7 level and are not considered to be in a specific grade; n = 1). These schools were excluded because they typically do not have physical locations, have flexible or nontraditional programming, and/or no formal start time.

From June to August 2023, we conducted web searches of school websites to find each school’s start time. Data on warning bell times, school start times, and the source webpage were extracted by a primary extractor and verified by a secondary extractor (TM, KB, and AB). If schools had multiple start times for different grades, each time was recorded for the corresponding grade, and this was recorded as multiple cases. If schools had a delayed start of one day per week (e.g., a consistent start time of 09:00 am Monday to Thursday but a 10:00 am start on Fridays), all start times were recorded and the earliest start time was used for the analysis. Disagreements were resolved by consulting with a third extractor (AB or EK). Schools were categorized as elementary, elementary-middle, middle, middle-high, high school, or all grades based on the majority of grades in the school (e.g., if a school included grades 1–8, it was categorized as elementary-middle; if a school included grades 8–12, it was categorized as middle-high). Schools were considered to have missing data if start time data could not be found by any of the three extractors. Schools were not contacted to provide missing data as data extraction occurred over the summer months when schools were closed. School start time data were combined with data on school location, grades included in the school, and 2022/2023 enrollment numbers from the publicly available list. Based on their address, schools were linked to a census subdivision that corresponded to an Index of Remoteness [29]. The Index of Remoteness is determined by a community’s distance to all population centers in a given travel radium and population size. The index ranges from 0 to 1, with increasing values indicating a more remote location. Data were stored and managed in an Excel database and later exported to IBM SPSS Version 29 for analysis.

Analysis

School start times were treated as continuous data. To answer research question 1a, descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, and medians were calculated for the whole sample, as well as for each grade category. A one-way Analysis of Variance test, which is robust to non-normality [30], was used to determine if school start times differed according to grade category. To answer research question 1b, school start times were classified as yes/no for whether they started at 08:30 am or later based on their actual instructional start time (not warning bell) and percentages were calculated. To answer research question 2, a Spearman’s rank order correlation was used, due to the nonnormality of the dataset and presence of outliers. We excluded outlier start times for afternoon-only sessions (n = 1) and schools starting at 10:00 am (n = 2).

Results

In total, start time data for 1553 standard schools were obtained; 206 (11.7%) of the schools had missing start time data (i.e., no school website or start time not posted on website). Of the 1553 schools, most were public (86.3%; n = 1341), with the remaining schools being independent (13.7%; n = 212). Nearly 6% (n = 88) of schools had more than one start time. Variation in school start times was due to school days starting at different times (n = 32; e.g., Monday to Thursday 08:40 am and Friday 10:00 am), grade levels starting at different times (n = 45), both grades and school days starting at different times starting on different days (n = 8), or other (e.g., odd or even school divisions; n = 3).

The mean overall school start time was 08:40 am (SD = 0:15) and ranged from 07:10 am to 09:45 am with a median start time of 08:40 am. See Table 1 for results by grade category. There was a significant overall effect of grade category on school start time, F (5, 1600) = 6.03, p < .001, η2 =.02, 95% CI [.006, .031], with post hoc testing indicating elementary-middle schools had a significantly earlier start time than all other grade categories. Overall, 1388 (86.4%) schools started at 08:30 am or later. See Table 1 for results by grade category. Figure 1 maps the proportion of schools starting at 08:30 am or later to census subdivisions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first population-based assessment of school start times in BC and Canada. Our findings show that the majority of schools (including elementary schools) commenced at 08:30 am or later. Our findings align with previous reports of average Canadian school start times of 08:32 am [4] and 08:43 am [2], indicating relative stability since 2013/2014 and suggesting that 08:30 am or later school start times are common practice in Canada. However, our findings contrast reports that only 17.7% of U.S. schools met recommended start times [31], with average start times ranging from 08:03 am to 08:08 am [31, 32]. In a large sample of Grade 6 to 11 Russian students, only 44% of students reported a school start time of 08:30 am or 09:00 am [33]. Based on these limited comparisons, this suggests that Canada may be a good example for implementation of delayed school start times.

Despite most schools in this sample meeting recommended start times, we could find no formal record of a mandated policy change or other strategies to adopt school start times of 8:30 AM or later. The previous reports of barriers for delaying start times include a lack of tiered bus systems, conflicts with athletic programs, resistance from families and teachers [19], and reliance on older children to accompany their younger children to school [34]. Involvement of school administrators and teachers in decision-making and providing education on sleep and the benefits of delaying school start times have been suggested to facilitate implementation [19, 21]. Thus, further research, including qualitative interviews with superintendents or other educational policy makers, may help understand the facilitators and barriers to delaying school start times in a Canadian setting. Moreover, further research is warranted to explore additional factors (e.g., household characteristics, noise and light pollution, daytime activities, etc.) that may contribute to short sleep duration and/or poorer sleep health in significant proportions of Canadian children and youth [2, 23–25].

We also found a weak, yet statistically significant, negative relationship between rurality and school start time, diverging from another report indicating earlier school start times with increased urbanicity [27]. Youth in rural areas experience greater sunlight exposure and more physical activity, and are more likely to self-report being a morning chronotype. This may suggest a preference for, or acceptance of, earlier school start times [35, 36]. Still, given the link between later start times and extended sleep duration [2, 4, 10]—a key aspect of sleep health—and the link between rurality/urbanicity and sleep health [37, 38], exploring geographic variations in school start times could aid in developing public health strategies to enhance pediatric sleep health. Further research could help uncover geographic-specific factors that determine school start times.

Our study’s strength lies in using a population-based approach for examining school start times, verified by a second researcher, rather than relying on convenience samples or self-reports. It also examined school start times from kindergarten to grade 12. However, several limitations exist. Firstly, our assessment of start times was limited to only one part of Canada and may not be representative of national trends. Also, data was collected during school breaks (e.g., July and August) when schools may have been updating start times. Similarly, school web pages may not have been updated with the most current information. Lastly, it was difficult to account for schools with multiple start times and varied grade levels, resulting in more start times than schools (e.g., a delayed start on Fridays). However, these schools only accounted for 6% of the total sample and sensitivity analyses indicated excluding them did not impact the findings. Nonetheless, the large sample size and conservative use of the earliest start time enhance the rigor of our findings.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that regions can broadly adopt healthy school start times. Factors like rurality may affect these school start times and warrant further investigation. Additionally, research on factors that contribute to successful implementation of healthy school start times could provide further insight into how schools and sleep health champions could support the adoption of delayed school start times. In tandem with this research, continued examination of other modifiable structural variables that contribute to persisting short sleep duration in children and youth could be useful in developing comprehensive public health strategies to improve sleep health.
