Giant Ascending Aortic Aneurysm With Atypical Presentation as Abdominal Pain

History of Presentation

A 58-year-old woman presented with acute lower abdominal pain and diaphoresis. Because of the pelvic localization, she was initially evaluated in a gynecology clinic. During this assessment, severe hypertension (190/70 mm Hg) and a previously undocumented cardiac murmur were detected, prompting referral for cardiology evaluation.

Despite the absence of chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, or functional limitation, corresponding to NYHA functional class I, physical examination revealed findings consistent with chronic severe aortic regurgitation: wide pulse pressure (127/43 mm Hg), hyperdynamic apical impulse, and a grade IV/VI holodiastolic murmur with a thrill at the left sternal border. Peripheral signs included Corrigan's pulse, Duroziez's sign, Lian's sign, and Landolfi's sign, along with grade III jugular venous distension. Lung examination was unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The patient had a 2-year history of systemic arterial hypertension treated with losartan, without regular follow-up. She had no significant family history of cardiovascular disease. Risk factors included age, sedentary lifestyle, and an atherogenic diet.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial considerations included gynecologic and abdominal etiologies; however, cardiovascular findings redirected suspicion toward aortic pathology. Acute coronary syndrome and acute aortic syndromes, including dissection and intramural hematoma, were excluded by normal biomarkers and computed tomography (CT) angiography.

Imaging and physical examination confirmed a giant ascending aortic aneurysm with functional severe aortic regurgitation. Although causality cannot be established from a single clinical observation, the abdominal pain was considered a plausible atypical manifestation of the underlying aortic pathology.

Investigations

A 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm with T-wave inversions in V1-V3. Chest radiography showed mediastinal widening and cardiomegaly (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a dilated left ventricle with eccentric hypertrophy and a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 62%, consistent with chronic volume overload. Severe aortic regurgitation, classified as Carpentier type 1B, was identified with a vena contracta of 7 mm, a pressure half-time of 227 milliseconds, and holodiastolic flow reversal in the descending aorta. Management

Initial management prioritized stringent blood pressure control to mitigate aortic wall stress. After a multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation, urgent surgical repair was indicated given the size of the aneurysm and concomitant severe aortic regurgitation. This clinical urgency is underscored by the Law of Laplace, in which the markedly increased vessel radius drives higher wall tension, thereby supporting the indication for definitive surgical intervention. Although continuous inpatient monitoring would have been preferable given the giant aneurysm size, as illustrated in Figure 5, immediate surgery and prolonged hospitalization were not feasible because of temporary institutional constraints, including high critical care bed utilization and operating room prioritization within a high-volume tertiary center. Therefore, after confirmation of hemodynamic stability and exclusion of acute aortic syndromes on CT angiography, an expedited outpatient surgical pathway was established as an interim risk-mitigation strategy. Outcome and Follow-Up

During short-term follow-up, the patient continued medical therapy with losartan and metoprolol, along with strict activity restriction and serial echocardiographic monitoring, which demonstrated a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 62%. She remains clinically stable in NYHA functional class I under structured outpatient surveillance by the specialized Heart Failure team and remains on the urgent surgical list for definitive repair.

Discussion

This case highlights a giant ascending aortic aneurysm with an atypical presentation, where the absence of typical cardiovascular symptoms contrasts with clear signs of severe aortic regurgitation, including a wide pulse pressure, a high-grade diastolic murmur, and multiple peripheral signs on examination and imaging. Abdominal pain in this setting may plausibly be explained by increased aortic wall stress, mechanical mediastinal stretching, and shared visceral afferent pathways, potentially enabling referred pain to the abdominal quadrants. In addition, reduced effective diastolic forward flow in severe aortic regurgitation may have altered splanchnic perfusion, providing a plausible but unproven mechanism for these poorly localized symptoms. The coexistence of aortic dilation and valvular insufficiency suggests chronic hemodynamic stress, likely exacerbated by long-standing hypertension. As the aortic complex enlarges, impaired leaflet coaptation may result in functional severe aortic regurgitation,1 leading to chronic volume overload and eccentric ventricular remodeling. This compensation may preserve clinical stability despite advanced anatomic disease, while increasing wall stress and myocardial oxygen demand, predisposing to eventual cardiovascular decompensation.

According to current clinical guidelines, surgical repair is recommended for aneurysms ≥55 mm (Class I).2 In this patient, the aortic diameter reached 89 mm, markedly exceeding this intervention threshold, and the presence of severe aortic regurgitation further reinforced the indication for surgical management.3 Despite this definitive indication, immediate intervention was not feasible. Consequently, the patient was managed with strict blood pressure control and β-blockade as an interim risk-reduction strategy to minimize aortic wall stress, consistent with data supporting tailored medical stabilization in thoracic aortic disease.4 Beyond the individual clinical presentation, this case highlights the importance of timely access to definitive cardiovascular surgery once the diagnosis has been established. Advanced aortic procedures are appropriately centralized in high-volume referral centers, where specialized multidisciplinary teams consistently achieve superior outcomes. Rather than questioning this model of care, the present case illustrates how temporary institutional capacity constraints may occasionally delay definitive intervention despite appropriate referral. In these centralized networks, early diagnosis, timely multimodality imaging, coordinated multidisciplinary care, and well-defined referral pathways are essential to facilitate timely access to specialized care while minimizing treatment delays and maximizing patient safety during interim medical stabilization.

Conclusions

This case underscores that giant ascending aortic aneurysms may present atypically and remain clinically compensated despite high-risk anatomy. Recognition relies on physical examination and imaging rather than symptoms alone.
