Physician beliefs regarding venous thromboembolism prophylaxis at a large multihospital US healthcare system: a cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Stephanie R Moss, Oindrila Bhattacharyya, Benjamin G Mittman, Tarini Gunaratne, Nicola B Campoamor, John FP Bridges, Michael B Rothberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) contributes to hospitalisation-associated morbidity. Although guidelines recommend limiting VTE prophylaxis to high-risk patients, some physicians prescribe it broadly. We compared beliefs of low and high prescribing physicians.MethodsWe surveyed hospitalists and medical residents who had the opportunity to prescribe prophylaxis ≥50 times. Best-worst scaling was used to assess their beliefs. Using a balanced incomplete block design, we created seven choice tasks with seven statements regarding prophylaxis beliefs each presented four times. For each task, physicians selected the statement that most and least reflected their beliefs. We used a count method to calculate best-worst scores and a conditional logistic regression choice model to compare low and high prescribers.ResultsOf 434 invitees, 172 (40%) completed all survey questions between June and November 2023. Low (n=86, ≤62.5% prescribing rate) and high (n=86, >62.5 prescribing rate) prescribers endorsed similar beliefs with differing levels of agreement. All felt confident to prescribe prophylaxis appropriately (low: +1.13, high: +1.10, p=0.81). High prescribers expressed more concern about VTE without prophylaxis (+1.02 vs +0.65, p=0.002). Low prescribers disagreed more that prophylaxis had no downside (−1.03 vs −0.73, p=0.01). High prescribers worried less about prophylaxis risks (−0.49 vs −0.22, p=0.01), and overuse (−0.61 vs −0.34, p=0.02).ConclusionsCompared with low prescribers, high prescribers were more concerned about VTE without prophylaxis and less about harms. These differences in beliefs may underlie physician behaviour and could be targets for interventions to reduce inappropriate prophylaxis.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a common cause of preventable morbidity among hospitalised patients.13 Prophylaxis with heparin is effective for reducing the incidence of VTE, but it is not without risk. Therefore, multiple national guidelines recommend that thromboprophylaxis be limited to high-risk patients,4 5 and the National Quality Forum recommends universal risk assessment for hospitalised patients.6 To support these efforts, the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations created a core measure for VTE prevention that all medical inpatients must either receive VTE prophylaxis or have a contraindication documented.7 Being low risk for VTE could be considered a contraindication, but because there is no penalty for prescribing prophylaxis to low-risk patients, some physicians prescribe it to most or all of their patients. Recently, there has been growing concern about the overuse of prophylaxis and the potential for harm,8 9 including excess bleeding and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.10

At our institution, a locally derived risk assessment model (RAM) has been in use for more than 5 years to help physicians identify high-risk patients and optimise prescription of thromboprophylaxis.11 To understand variation in physician use of the RAM and prescription of thromboprophylaxis, we previously performed a qualitative study of 17 physicians in which we elicited perspectives on VTE, including incidence, risk factors, prevention and treatment.12 We found that providers were generally confident in their independent clinical assessment of risk, but differed markedly in their perception of the incidence of hospital-associated VTE and assessment of the harms of thromboprophylaxis. These themes were used to generate survey questions for the present study.

In our healthcare system, it is mandatory to complete a VTE risk assessment using the RAM for all admissions, but physicians are free to ignore its recommendation. As a result, there is wide variation in prophylaxis prescribing rates on the hospital medicine service. We compared beliefs regarding VTE prophylaxis of low and high prescribing physicians to understand their decisions regarding prophylaxis for acutely ill medical inpatients.

Methods

We recruited internal medicine residents and hospitalists from the Department of Hospital Medicine, which included six hospitals at the time of the study (Cleveland Clinic Main Campus, Hillcrest, Fairview, Avon, Marymount and Medina). We included physicians who had at least 50 opportunities to prescribe VTE prophylaxis during the study period. We excluded patients <18 years of age, those with positive COVID-19 test results because prophylaxis is always recommended for patients with COVID-19,1315 and those who had contraindications to prophylaxis such as therapeutic anticoagulation or active bleeding. Eligible physicians were contacted via email with a link to the electronic survey, which was available in English. By completing the survey, participants consented to participate. Three follow-up emails were sent to non-responders. This study was approved via expedited review by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB #22–321).

none.

We employed a best-worst scaling (BWS) experiment to evaluate physicians’ beliefs regarding prescribing prophylaxis for VTE. BWS estimates the relative importance of a group of objects or attributes (in this case, beliefs) based on a number of choice tasks.16 17 Specifically, we used the BWS object case method which evaluates the importance of each belief relative to the others.18 Physicians were asked to assess seven objects, which were derived from qualitative interviews of hospitalists,12 as being most and least reflective of their beliefs regarding prophylaxis. The seven objects on physician beliefs included the downside of VTE prophylaxis, prophylaxis overuse, associated risks, level of discomfort, legal risks, worry about not giving prophylaxis and personal confidence in prescribing (table 1).

Best-worst scaling (BWS) objects

The physicians evaluated a sequence of seven sets of four objects each. For each set, they indicated which object was most and least reflective of their own beliefs, resulting in two responses per set. Each set was presented singly (online supplemental figure 1). The contents of the seven sets were determined using a balanced incomplete block design in which each object on belief was presented four times in total and paired with each other twice.19 The order of the four objects within each set was randomly assigned prior to the survey, and the order of the seven sets was randomised for each physician. Our dataset included all physicians who completed all choice screens.

Five additional questions assessed physicians’ beliefs about the epidemiology of VTE and their frequency of agreement with the RAM (online supplemental figure 2). Questions included the percentage of patients they believe are at high risk of VTE, the percentage of patients who would develop VTE in the hospital if prophylaxis is not administered, frequency of physicians’ perceived agreement with the VTE RAM, and the probability of developing VTE during hospitalisation that they would consider high risk.

Once the objects for the BWS experiment and the additional questions were determined, they were reviewed with several hospitalists to ensure clarity.

We divided the physician sample at the median prescribing rate into ‘low prescribers’ and ‘high prescribers’. For the four questions, we compared responses (ordinal in nature) between low and high prescribers as frequencies and used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to assess significant differences. We analysed the BWS response data by counting the number of times each object was selected as most and least reflective across the seven sets, and by calculating a standardised score for each object. The score was computed as the difference between the frequency of being chosen as most vs least, divided by the total count of responses as most, least and null (ie, an object in a set not selected as most or least reflective of physician’s beliefs).18 19 This standardised score indicates the relative salience of each belief across all physicians in the sample. Scores ranged from −1.0 to +1.0, with scores near +1.0 signifying high reflectiveness in prescribing prophylaxis, scores near −1.0 indicating low reflectiveness, and a score of 0 indicating indifference. We report the 95% CIs for each score. A conditional logistic regression model was fit to estimate the relative importance of the objects, with the dependent variable being physicians’ choices regarding the beliefs in each choice task. We used effects coding resulting in positive and negative coefficients. As we used a convenience sample of eligible internal medicine physicians from a single academic health system, no sampling weights were applied. Analyses were restricted to physicians who completed all survey questions, and no imputation was conducted for missing data. Analyses were performed using Stata V.18.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX).

Results

The survey was sent to 434 physicians, of whom 228 (52.5%) responded between June 2023 and November 2023. Among respondents, 172 (75.4%) completed all survey questions, forming the final analytic sample (figure 1). The median prophylaxis prescribing rate was 62.5% with an IQR of 45.1%–74.3%. Physicians prescribing less than or equal to the median rate constituted the low prescriber group (n=86), while those prescribing more than the median rate were classified as high prescribers (n=86). The distribution of physician prescribing rates is shown in figure 2. The median prophylaxis rate for non-responders was 59.8% with an IQR of 35.0%–74.4%.

High and low prescribers had similar beliefs about the percentage of patients at high risk for VTE and the likelihood of developing VTE without prophylaxis. Approximately half of each group estimated that less than 50% of patients were at high risk (high: 52.3% vs low: 59.3%, p=0.36), and just over half believed that more than 5% of patients would develop VTE without prophylaxis (high: 55.8% vs low: 62.8%, p=0.35). Discussion

In recent years, there has been growing recognition that zealous efforts to prevent VTE in hospitalised patients have resulted in overtreatment of low-risk patients.9 20 Although most patients suffer no ill effects, some experience unnecessary harm. To understand potential drivers of prophylaxis overuse at the physician level, we assessed how beliefs differed between high and low prescribers of VTE prophylaxis. In this survey of hospitalists and internal medicine residents, we found that low and high prescribers endorsed similar beliefs about VTE prophylaxis but differed in the strengths of those beliefs. Compared with low prescribers, high prescribers were more concerned about patients experiencing VTE if prophylaxis was not administered and were less concerned about overuse of prophylaxis and its potential harms, including major bleeding. Specifically, low prescribers more strongly disagreed that prophylaxis has no downside, which acknowledges the potential for harm with an unnecessary intervention. Both groups disagreed that prophylaxis causes discomfort.

Both groups were similarly confident in their clinical ability to assess which patients require VTE prophylaxis. This is consistent with prior studies, both quantitative and qualitative, showing that physicians feel comfortable relying on their clinical judgement to assess VTE and bleeding risk without clinical decision support.21 22 Based on our findings that both low and high prescribing physicians overestimated patients’ risk for VTE development without prophylaxis, this confidence may be misplaced. The majority of both groups overestimated the prevalence of high-risk patients, believing >5% of untreated patients would develop VTE, when the actual number is closer to 1%.23

In a previous study in the same health system,24 we found that among >1 30 000 patient encounters, 68% of prescribing was concordant with the RAM’s recommendation. Concordance was generally high for high-risk patients, with 96% of those patients receiving prophylaxis, whereas 37% of low-risk patients also received prophylaxis, indicating that prescribers disagreed with the recommendation of the RAM. This actual discordance in prescribing is consistent with our current results, where high prescribers were more likely to express disagreement with the RAM, but even among low prescribers, only 43% perceived agreement with the RAM in >75% of cases.

Others investigating physician beliefs regarding VTE prophylaxis have documented inaccurate beliefs. Physicians incorrectly believe that VTE prophylaxis decreases mortality, morbidity and length of stay for medical inpatients.212527 At the same time, physicians have expressed doubt that patients receive appropriate prophylaxis.25 Overestimation of the benefits of prophylaxis, combined with an underappreciation of the risks and a suspicion that other physicians under-prescribe, may be drivers of prophylaxis overuse. It is not surprising that physicians have these beliefs; physicians routinely overestimate the risks of common diseases and the benefits of their treatments.28 29 Given the emphasis on VTE prophylaxis as a quality measure, physicians might reasonably infer that the benefits of prophylaxis would far outweigh the harms.

However, since both high and low prescribers similarly overestimated the risk of VTE, beliefs regarding VTE prevalence are likely not responsible for their prescribing rates. A better candidate is physicians’ degree of concern for VTE development in the absence of prophylaxis and for direct harms from prophylaxis. High prescribers were more concerned about the former and less about the latter than low prescribers. It stands to reason that if a physician worries about VTE and does not perceive much harm from thromboprophylaxis, then the physician will err on the side of overtreatment.

Based on our own institutions’ data, approximately 25%–30% of medical inpatients are at high risk for VTE as assessed by the RAM and thus potentially eligible for thromboprophylaxis. By this definition, many of the physicians in the low prescriber group could still be viewed as high prescribers. We categorised prescribing based on the median point in the data (62.5%) out of necessity. Unfortunately, comparing answers of physicians based on a cut-off of 30% would not have been possible due to small numbers.

Fear of legal consequences can also affect physician behaviour.30 In our initial qualitative study, physicians expressed concern about legal risk if prophylaxis was withheld, but in the current survey, both low and high prescribers expressed neutral feelings toward this belief. This suggests that defensive medicine is not an important driver of inappropriate prophylaxis for medical patients, at least at our institution.

To our knowledge, this is the first survey study to correlate belief patterns with observed prescribing rates. Our experimental design forced participants to choose a ‘most’ and ‘least’ option for each set of questions, allowing us to directly quantify and compare the degree to which physicians agreed or disagreed with certain beliefs. This approach offers an advantage over a Likert scale model to assess each belief, which would have made it more difficult to differentiate between high and low prescribers because they would have endorsed similar beliefs.

Understanding the beliefs associated with overprescribing prophylaxis is important because it is likely that these beliefs directly impact the observed behaviour and that interventions to reshape these beliefs might improve prescribing. It is interesting to consider the origin of prescribers’ beliefs. It is unlikely that patient factors led to the differences, as patients are randomly assigned to internal medicine teams at our institution. Instead, potential contributors include the culture of the institution in which the physicians trained, the physicians’ personal risk tolerance, the way they value sins of omission vs commission, and the way they think about quality measures.

Our study has several limitations. The survey was performed at one institution. This could limit its generalisability. However, our institution is comprised of individual hospitals which vary in their local cultures and contribute to broad physician experience. Due to the necessity of splitting low and high prescribers at the median of the sample, the low prescriber group is heterogeneous. Physicians who prescribe prophylaxis 10%–20% of the time may hold different views than those prescribing 50%–60% of the time, but we were not able to elucidate this due to small numbers. Non-responders had a slightly lower prophylaxis rate than responders (59.8% vs 62.5%) and may have different opinions that would have altered our results, leading to selection bias. However, we were not looking for a representative sample, but rather to compare high and low prescribers within the sample. Therefore, unless there was a differential response rate between high and low prescribers with regards to specific beliefs, the response rate itself should not bias the data. Moreover, our analytic approach accounted for complete response inclusion to reduce item-level bias. Our response rate is high compared with some other BWS work.31 32 Finally, although participants were asked to identify objects that were ‘most and least reflective’ of their beliefs regarding VTE prophylaxis in each survey question, no pretesting was completed to ensure participants understood the prompts.

Conclusion

Understanding physician beliefs is an important first step in targeting interventions to modify behaviours. In our population, compared with low prescribers, high prescribers were more concerned about VTE in the absence of prophylaxis and less concerned about potential harms of prophylaxis. All physicians were confident in their ability to estimate VTE risk without prophylaxis and generally overestimated it. However, high prescribers agreed with the RAM recommendations less often, leading to discordant prescribing, whereas low prescribers were more likely to trust the RAM and appropriately withhold prophylaxis from low-risk patients. Future efforts to reduce inappropriate VTE prophylaxis should focus on targeting these disparate beliefs. By further educating physicians on the harm caused by unnecessary prescription and the evidence behind the RAM, we may have more success in reducing inappropriate VTE prophylaxis.
