Unmasking Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy: A Case of Proximal Weakness in a Young Male With Co‐Occurring Vitamin D Deficiency
Authors: Ibrahim Khalil, Ahmed Fahim Faiyaz, Sunjida Amin Promi, Md Imran Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIdiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) encompasses rare autoimmune disorders causing muscle inflammation and weakness, with subtypes including dermatomyositis, necrotizing myopathy, antisynthetase syndrome, and inclusion body myositis. This case report details a 25‐year‐old South Asian male presenting with a 60‐day history of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia, alongside coexisting vitamin D deficiency (serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D: 19.3 ng/mL). Initial vitamin D supplementation (50,000 IU weekly) failed to halt symptom progression. Diagnostic workup revealed markedly elevated creatine kinase (4569 U/L), electromyography findings of myogenic motor unit potentials with abnormal spontaneous activity, and a biceps muscle biopsy showing inflammation, necrosis, and fibrosis, confirming IIM, most suggestive of likely necrotizing myopathy. Myositis‐specific autoantibodies (MSAs) were not tested, limiting definitive subtype classification. Comprehensive immunological tests were negative, ruling out other autoimmune conditions. No paraneoplastic workup was performed. Treatment with oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) and azathioprine (50 mg twice daily) led to significant improvement in muscle strength, resolution of dysphagia, and normalized CPK levels within weeks, supported by physical therapy and ongoing vitamin D supplementation. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of IIM in the presence of vitamin D deficiency, which was coincidental in this instance and may potentially exacerbate disease severity by amplifying inflammation and muscle damage, though it is not causative. However, no temporal relationship between vitamin D supplementation and symptom improvement was observed, and follow‐up vitamin D levels were not reassessed. Routine vitamin D screening in IIM patients may be considered recommended to identify and address deficiency as an adjunctive measure, potentially mitigating severity and corticosteroid‐related complications like osteoporosis. We recommend complete autoimmune myositis panels, including MSAs, and malignancy screening in similar cases. Further research is needed to define vitamin D's therapeutic role in IIM management, particularly through longitudinal studies and randomized trials.Keywords: autoimmune diseases, idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, muscle weakness, necrotizing myopathy, vitamin D deficiency

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) refers to a group of rare autoimmune diseases characterized by proximal muscle weakness, muscle inflammation, and immune‐mediated injury, often involving specific autoantibodies [1]. Contemporary classifications recognize distinct subtypes, including dermatomyositis (DM), necrotizing myopathy (NM), antisynthetase syndrome (ASS), and inclusion body myositis (IBM), each with unique clinical and histopathological features [2]. The etiology of IIM remains poorly understood, with genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and immune dysregulation implicated in its pathogenesis [3]. Recent research has highlighted the role of immune modulation in IIM, particularly the influence of vitamin D, a critical regulator of both innate and adaptive immunity [4]. Vitamin D, through its active form 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), binds to the vitamin D receptor (VDR) on immune cells such as T‐cells, B‐cells, and dendritic cells, modulating cytokine production and immune tolerance [5]. It downregulates pro‐inflammatory cytokines like interleukin‐17 (IL‐17) and interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) while enhancing regulatory T‐cell (Treg) function, which is vital for suppressing autoimmunity [6] (Figures 1 and 2). Vitamin D deficiency is prevalent in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and multiple sclerosis (MS), where it is associated with increased disease activity through immune dysregulation [7]. In IIM, studies report lower serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) levels compared to healthy controls, with up to 65% of patients showing deficiency, often correlating with elevated muscle enzymes and disease severity [8]. While vitamin D deficiency is not a primary cause of IIM, it may act as a coexisting condition that potentially exacerbates inflammation and muscle damage [9]. This case report describes a 25‐year‐old South Asian male with IIM, most suggestive of likely necrotizing myopathy, presenting with mild to severe vitamin D deficiency. Despite initial supplementation, his symptoms progressed until treated with immunosuppression, underscoring the need for comprehensive management. We aim to highlight the potential but unproven role of vitamin D screening in IIM to optimize treatment strategies and mitigate disease severity, advocating for further research into its therapeutic implications. However, in this case, the vitamin D deficiency appears coincidental, with no demonstrated impact on the clinical course.

Immune dysregulation in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) with vitamin D influence. Vitamin D deficiency activates innate immunity, leading to dendritic cell activation, pro‐inflammatory cytokine production (IL‐1β, TNF‐α), and adaptive immunity dysregulation with Th1/Th17 overactivation and autoantibody production (e.g., anti‐Jo‐1, anti‐Mi‐2), driving muscle inflammation. Vitamin D sufficiency regulates immunity, promoting anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐10, TGF‐β) and reducing inflammation.

Vitamin D and muscle pathology in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM). Vitamin D deficiency impairs calcium homeostasis, causing muscle fiber atrophy, necrosis, elevated muscle enzymes (CK, LDH), and chronic inflammation (endomysial fibrosis), leading to worsened muscle weakness. Vitamin D sufficiency optimizes calcium homeostasis, supports muscle regeneration, reduces necrosis, and improves muscle strength.

Case History/Examination

A 25‐year‐old South Asian male student presented to a tertiary medical center with progressive generalized body aches and muscle weakness over 60 days, significantly worsening in the last week. He reported difficulty standing from a seated or lying position, raising arms above shoulder level, and performing daily tasks like combing hair. In the preceding 4 days, he developed dysphagia, a nasal tone in his voice (dysphonia), and fatigue while speaking. The weakness, initially mild, worsened gradually, exacerbated by exertion but without diurnal variation. He denied skin rash, joint pain, swelling, morning stiffness, ptosis, diplopia, chewing difficulties (until recently), weight loss, tremors, involuntary movements, fever, oral ulcers, or respiratory symptoms. He had no gastrointestinal complaints. His medical history was unremarkable, with no history of hypertension, diabetes, or other systemic illnesses, and no family history of autoimmune or neuromuscular disorders. The patient reported no recent medication use beyond the prescribed vitamin D supplementation, and no known allergies. One month prior, a local physician diagnosed vitamin D deficiency (serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D: 19.3 ng/mL, normal: 30–100 ng/mL) after early symptoms of weakness and body aches, prescribing 50,000 IU weekly vitamin D supplementation for 4 weeks. Despite adherence, symptoms progressed, culminating in dysphagia and dysphonia. On examination, the patient was cooperative but anxious, with an average build, mild pallor suggestive of anemia, but no edema, cyanosis, or lymphadenopathy. Vital signs were stable, and systemic examinations (cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal) showed no organomegaly or abnormalities. Neurological evaluation revealed normal higher cerebral functions and cranial nerves, proximal muscle weakness (grade 3/5), preserved distal strength (grade 5/5), and impaired arm elevation and squatting, indicating proximal myopathy. Sensory, cerebellar, and reflex functions were intact, with mild muscle tenderness but no joint or skin abnormalities.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient's presentation of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia suggested several differential diagnoses. Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM), particularly most suggestive of necrotizing myopathy, was considered due to the pattern of weakness and recent onset. Other possibilities included metabolic myopathies (e.g., hypokalemic periodic paralysis, given the weakness), infectious myositis (e.g., viral or parasitic), and neuromuscular disorders like myasthenia gravis (due to dysphagia and dysphonia). Endocrine disorders (e.g., hypothyroidism) and toxic myopathies (e.g., statin‐induced) were less likely given the patient's history and lack of exposure. To exclude these, normal electrolytes ruled out hypokalemia, negative viral markers (HBsAg, anti‐HCV, anti‐HIV) and absence of fever or travel history to endemic areas excluded infectious causes such as viral or parasitic myositis (e.g., trichinosis). No history of allergen exposure or eosinophilia excluded allergic myositis. Thyroid profile was normal, excluding hypothyroidism. No recent medications or toxins were reported, ruling out toxic myopathy. Myasthenia gravis was less likely due to the absence of ptosis, diplopia, or fatigability, and normal cranial nerves.

Investigations

Laboratory tests revealed a normal complete blood count, CRP, electrolytes, creatinine (1.00 mg/dL), albumin (3.7 g/dL), and random blood sugar (5.65 mmol/L). Creatine phosphokinase (CPK) was markedly elevated at 4569 U/L (normal: 10–120 U/L), with mildly elevated SGPT (60 U/L, normal: 7–56 U/L), indicating muscle damage (Tables 1 and 2). Vitamin D levels remained low at 19.3 ng/mL despite supplementation. Imaging (abdominal ultrasonography, chest X‐ray) and thyroid profile were normal. Viral markers (HBsAg, anti‐HCV, anti‐HIV) and immunological tests (ANA, RF, anti‐CCP, P‐ANCA, C‐ANCA, ENA profile) were negative, ruling out other autoimmune conditions. Myositis‐specific autoantibodies (e.g., anti‐SRP, anti‐HMGCR) were not tested. No malignancy screening (e.g., CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or age‐appropriate cancer markers) was performed. Electrophysiological studies confirmed the diagnosis. Nerve conduction studies (NCS) showed normal motor and sensory responses in the median, ulnar, tibial, and peroneal nerves, with normal F‐wave latencies, excluding peripheral neuropathy. Electromyography (EMG) revealed myogenic motor unit potentials (MUPs) with full recruitment, abnormal spontaneous activity (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves), and a myogenic pattern consistent with inflammatory myopathy affecting proximal muscles. A biceps muscle biopsy provided histopathological confirmation. Gross examination showed two dark brown specimens (largest: 2.0 × 1.2 × 0.4 cm). Microscopic analysis revealed moderate fiber diameter variation, necrotic fibers, endomysial fibrosis, chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, capillary proliferation, and perimysial hemorrhage, most suggestive of consistent with IIM, likely necrotizing myopathy. No immunohistochemistry (IHC) or MHC class I staining was performed, and no biopsy images are available. These findings could also overlap with other IIM subtypes like dermatomyositis or polymyositis. Traumatic myositis was considered but deemed unlikely given the clinical context. Gastrointestinal function was not tested, as the patient had no specific gastrointestinal complaints.

Treatment

Treatment was chosen based on standard guidelines for IIM management, such as those from the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR), which recommend corticosteroids as first‐line therapy for inflammatory myopathies, with the addition of steroid‐sparing agents like azathioprine for long‐term control [1, 2]. Prednisolone, a glucocorticoid, was selected at 1 mg/kg/day due to its potent anti‐inflammatory effects, acting by suppressing immune‐mediated muscle inflammation through inhibition of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and immune cell activation. Azathioprine was added as a steroid‐sparing agent (50 mg twice daily) to minimize corticosteroid side effects; it inhibits purine synthesis, reducing T‐ and B‐cell proliferation and thereby controlling chronic autoimmunity. This combination is recommended for moderate‐to‐severe IIM cases, particularly when rapid symptom control is needed, as in this patient with dysphagia. Potential adverse reactions include, for prednisolone: osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, hypertension, and increased infection risk; for azathioprine: myelosuppression (e.g., leukopenia), hepatotoxicity, and gastrointestinal upset. Monitoring included regular complete blood counts and liver function tests, which remained within normal limits throughout treatment. This approach aligns with case reports and guidelines emphasizing early immunosuppression to prevent progression, as seen in similar IMNM cases where corticosteroids and azathioprine improved outcomes while balancing risks. The patient was started on oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) and azathioprine (50 mg twice daily) as a steroid‐sparing agent to manage IIM and minimize corticosteroid side effects. Vitamin D supplementation (50,000 IU weekly) continued to address the deficiency. Physical therapy was initiated to support muscle rehabilitation and prevent deconditioning.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient responded well to treatment. Within weeks, proximal muscle strength improved significantly, enabling independent walking and daily activities like eating and grooming, which were previously impaired. Follow‐up assessments showed near‐complete resolution of dysphagia, improved functional capacity, and normalized CPK levels, with CPK decreasing to 85 U/L (normal range) at 4 weeks posttreatment initiation, reflecting reduced muscle inflammation. Liver function tests (e.g., SGPT 45 U/L) and complete blood counts remained normal during azathioprine therapy. The patient reported relief at regaining independence, having experienced anxiety over his progressive symptoms at presentation. Ongoing management included regular monitoring of CPK and vitamin D levels, adjustments to immunosuppressive therapy, and continued physical therapy to maintain muscle strength and prevent relapse. The association of vitamin D deficiency with IIM severity was noted, suggesting its potential but unproven role as a modulator of disease activity, though not causative, emphasizing the need for comprehensive treatment strategies in such cases. Vitamin D levels were not reassessed during follow‐up.

Discussion

This case of a 25‐year‐old South Asian male with idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM), probable and most likely suggestive of necrotizing myopathy (NM), underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of IIM in the context of coexisting vitamin D deficiency. The patient's presentation with progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia, coupled with markedly elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels (4569 U/L) and histopathological findings of necrosis, fibrosis, and inflammation, aligns with NM, a subtype characterized by acute muscle fiber necrosis and minimal lymphocytic infiltration [1]. The absence of autoantibodies (e.g., anti‐SRP, anti‐Jo‐1) and negative immunological tests (ANA, ENA profile) posed a diagnostic challenge, as NM often presents with myositis‐specific antibodies [2]. However, the clinical picture, electromyography (EMG) findings of myogenic motor unit potentials with abnormal spontaneous activity, and muscle biopsy results confirmed the diagnosis, distinguishing IIM from differentials like metabolic myopathies or myasthenia gravis. Vitamin D deficiency (serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D: 19.3 ng/mL) emerged as a significant coexisting condition in this case. However, at 19.3 ng/mL, this represents mild deficiency by most rheumatologic criteria (severe typically < 10 ng/mL), and its role appears coincidental rather than influential on disease progression. Vitamin D, through its active form 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), modulates immune responses by downregulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐17, IFN‐γ) and enhancing regulatory T‐cell (Treg) function, which is critical for immune tolerance [5]. Deficiency may potentially exacerbate autoimmune inflammation by impairing Treg‐mediated suppression, as seen in conditions like systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [7]. In IIM, studies report a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (up to 65% in polymyositis [PM] and dermatomyositis [DM]), with lower 25(OH)D levels correlating with increased disease severity and muscle enzyme elevation [8]. A meta‐analysis by Xiong et al. [9] found that IIM patients with low vitamin D had a higher risk of severe disease, though causality remains unestablished. In this case, initial vitamin D supplementation (50,000 IU weekly) failed to halt disease progression, with no temporal relationship to symptom improvement, suggesting that deficiency is not a primary driver but potentially a modulator of inflammation and muscle damage. This is supported by the patient's robust response to immunosuppression (prednisolone and azathioprine), which addressed the underlying autoimmune process, while ongoing vitamin D supplementation likely supported musculoskeletal recovery. The interplay between vitamin D deficiency and IIM severity warrants further exploration. Vitamin D deficiency may potentially exacerbate muscle damage through multiple mechanisms: impaired calcium homeostasis leading to muscle fiber atrophy, increased pro‐inflammatory cytokine production (Figures 1 and 2), and reduced muscle regeneration capacity [10]. The patient's biopsy findings of necrotic fibers and endomysial fibrosis align with these effects, as vitamin D deficiency is linked to muscle weakness and elevated CPK levels in autoimmune myopathies [10]. Additionally, the patient's South Asian background may have contributed to his deficiency, as darker skin pigmentation reduces vitamin D synthesis, a known risk factor in this population [11]. This highlights the consideration for the need for routine vitamin D screening in IIM patients, particularly in high‐risk groups, to mitigate potential exacerbation of disease severity and corticosteroid‐related complications like osteoporosis. Comparing this case to prior reports provides context. Azali et al. [8] described a 42‐year‐old female with PM and vitamin D deficiency (15.2 ng/mL) who improved with corticosteroids and vitamin D supplementation, though her recovery was slower than our patient's. Yu et al. [12] reported vitamin D deficiency existed in IIM patients, which was significantly correlated with muscle enzyme. Our patient's younger age, rapid symptom progression, and near‐complete recovery with combined therapy distinguish this case, suggesting that early diagnosis and aggressive immunosuppression may improve outcomes in NM, even with coexisting vitamin D deficiency (Table 1). However, the lack of autoantibody data limits definitive NM classification, as anti‐SRP or anti‐HMGCR antibodies are often present in NM [2]. This case also raises broader implications for IIM management. The failure of vitamin D supplementation alone to alter disease progression underscores the primacy of immunosuppression in treating the autoimmune component of IIM. However, addressing vitamin D deficiency as an adjunctive measure may optimize outcomes by reducing inflammation and supporting muscle repair, particularly in patients on long‐term corticosteroids, which increase osteoporosis risk [13]. The patient's improvement in proximal muscle strength and resolution of dysphagia with combined therapy (prednisolone, azathioprine, and vitamin D) supports a multimodal approach. Drawing from methodologies like Bayesian network meta‐analysis, future studies could employ similar rigorous statistical frameworks to evaluate vitamin D supplementation's comparative effectiveness in IIM, assessing outcomes like muscle strength, CPK levels, and disease flares across diverse cohorts. Potential complications of IIM include respiratory muscle involvement leading to ventilatory failure, cardiac manifestations (e.g., arrhythmias, myocarditis), interstitial lung disease, dysphagia‐related aspiration pneumonia, and associated malignancy (paraneoplastic syndrome). In necrotizing myopathy subtypes, severe muscle necrosis can lead to rhabdomyolysis and renal failure. Long‐term immunosuppression increases infection risk, while corticosteroids may cause osteoporosis, avascular necrosis, and metabolic issues. Thrombotic events, as seen in some cases, highlight the need for monitoring hypercoagulability in inflammatory states. Limitations of this case report include its single‐patient scope, which restricts generalizability, and the speculative nature of vitamin D's role, as supplementation did not prevent disease progression. The absence of autoantibody testing (e.g., anti‐SRP) limits subtype confirmation, and no paraneoplastic workup was conducted, which is essential in adult IIM cases to rule out malignancy. Long‐term outcomes, including optimal vitamin D dosing and recurrence risk, were not assessed. We acknowledge these diagnostic gaps and recommend comprehensive evaluation, including MSAs and malignancy screening, in similar presentations. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to evaluate vitamin D supplementation's impact on IIM outcomes, ideally using randomized controlled trials. Investigating autoantibody‐specific responses to vitamin D and its effects on corticosteroid‐related complications could further clarify its therapeutic potential. Routine vitamin D screening in IIM patients, especially in populations at risk for deficiency, may enhance personalized management strategies, improving quality of life in this rare but debilitating condition.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of a thorough diagnostic workup in IIM and highlights vitamin D deficiency as a coincidental finding with potential but unproven modulation of disease severity. While not causative, low vitamin D levels may potentially exacerbate muscle inflammation, supporting consideration for routine screening and supplementation as adjunctive measures. Further studies are needed to establish vitamin D's therapeutic role in IIM management.
