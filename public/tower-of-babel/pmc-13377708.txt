Brachytherapy of skin cancer using a 90Sr/90Y beta-emitting source: design of a fixed geometry applicator
Authors: Shahriyar Seraj, Mahdi Ghorbani, Nima Hamzian, Bagher Farhood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBeta radiation is a suitable candidate for treatment of skin cancer due to its rapid dose fall-off and better protection of normal tissues. This study aimed to design and propose a set of new fixed geometry applicators with a 90Sr/90Y beta source for treatment of superficial skin tumors.MethodsFirst, the 90Sr/90Y source of the Beta-Cath™ system supplied by Novoste Corporation was simulated with the MCNPX Monte Carlo code and validated using the proposed Task Group No. 60 dosimetric parameters. Then, considering parameters including the source-to-skin distance, treatment time, the shape and thickness of the flattening filter, the size and composition of the sources, and the dimensions of the field, the optimal applicator was selected in terms of flatness, symmetry, penumbra, leakage, and dose rate at different depths (1.0–3.0 mm).ResultsThe mean difference in dose rate at the reference point, radial dose function, anisotropy function and two-dimensional dose rate compared to the corresponding published values was less than 3.0%. For a treatment depth of 3.0 mm, treatment times ranged from 6.97 to 10.22 min, the flatness values ​​of the optimal applicators in different sizes ranged from 3.36% to 14.94%, symmetry from 0.3% to 1.7%, and penumbra from 2.7 to 3.6 mm. Leakage within 1.0 mm of the applicator wall was less than 1% of the skin surface dose.ConclusionsThis is the first study to evaluate the design of a fixed geometry applicator for a 90Sr/90Y beta-emitting source for skin cancer treatment. The designed applicator exhibited good performance in terms of dose distribution parameters such as flatness, symmetry, penumbra, and leakage.Keywords: Brachytherapy, Beta applicator designing, 90Sr/90Y source, Skin cancer, Monte carlo simulation

Introduction

The World Health Organization has reported a high prevalence of skin cancer with more than 1.5 million cases worldwide in 2020 [1]. The most common forms of skin cancer are melanoma, as well as basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, which are types of non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) [2]. Mohs micrographic surgery has proven to be a successful treatment for NMSC, with a five-year cure rate of over 95% [3, 4]. However, surgery is generally not recommended for lesions in challenging locations such as the eyelids, lips, and ears, and the risk of an unattractive outcome often leads patients with facial and neck lesions to avoid surgery. Furthermore, surgery is difficult for elderly or frail patients to tolerate [5].

Compared to surgery, high-dose-rate brachytherapy (HDR BT) with radionuclides is a less invasive method of treating skin cancer [6]. BT is a type of internal radiation therapy in which radioactive sources are placed within or immediately adjacent to the target tissue. Compared with external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), BT can provide improved target coverage, better organ preservation, and shorter treatment times [7].

Currently, 192Ir-HDR BT has been developed, with applicators such as Leipzig and Valencia, and has demonstrated a local control rate of 98% after 5 years [8]. However, the high penetrating power of 192Ir-HDR gamma radiation, with mean energy of 380 keV [9], necessitates careful attention to normal tissue protection. Anagnostopoulos et al. [10] calculated the leakage of BEBIG Valencia-type surface applicators using Monte Carlo (MC) simulation to be 0.2% of the air KERMA isodose value at distances of 13.5 to 15.0 cm.

Beta radiation can stand out as a promising option due to its short range in tissue and rapid dose fall-off [11]. The limited penetration of beta particles makes them ideal for treating superficial tumors, like early-stage or low-risk squamous cell carcinomas as non-melanomas and basal cell carcinomas, especially those close to bone or sensitive structures like the eye and eyelid [12].

To date, studies have been conducted to design conformal applicators fitted to the tumor’s size for beta sources such as 90Y, 188Re, and 90Sr/90Y [13–15]. Pashazadeh et al. [13] developed a multi-well matrix applicator utilizing 90Y beta-emitting source, designed for conformal (BT) of superficial skin tumors. Their MC calculations showed a desirable reduction in the percent depth dose (PDD) to less than 10% at a depth of 4.0 mm, although inhomogeneous dose distribution was observed at a depth of 1.0 mm between the wells, and the use of unsealed sources posed radiation risks.

The present study aimed to develop BT for skin cancers that are in early stages T1 and T2 (less than 25 mm in diameter and 2.0 mm in depth). For this purpose, a novel fixed-geometry applicator was designed for BT using a 90Sr/90Y beta-emitting source. With a maximum beta energy of 2.281 MeV and a penetration depth of up to 3.0 mm, 90Sr/90Y represents a suitable option for treating most superficial skin cancers.

Materials and methods

Source description

The present study was conducted on a catheter-based system, the Beta-Cath™ system, developed by Novoste Corporation. The system contains a train of 12 beta-emitting 90Sr/90Y seeds enclosed within a 5 French catheter (Fig. 1) [16]. The parent/daughter pair 90Sr/90Y is an appropriate choice in medical applications due to its long half-life (28.7 years) and high energy. 90Sr is a pure beta emitter that decays to 90Y emitting a single beta particle with a maximum energy of 0.546 MeV. The daughter isotope 90Y, in secular equilibrium with its parent, decays to stable 90Zr emitting another beta particle with a maximum kinetic energy of 2.281 MeV and a half-life of 64.4 h [17, 18].

Structure and geometry of the Beta-Cath™ 90Sr/90Y 5 French single seed

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the source has a cylindrical core and capsule. The geometry dimensions of the source are shown in the Fig. 1. The composition of the ceramic core was assumed to be SiO2. The source is jacketed by a SS304 stainless-steel capsule (composition by weight: Fe: 67.00%, Cr: 19.50%, Ni: 10.50%, Mn: 2.00%, Si: 0.75%, N: 0.10%, C: 0.07%, P: 0.05% and O: 0.03%). The density of a ceramic core ranges from 2.6 to 4.0 g/cm3 [19]. In this study, the material densities of the core and capsule were assumed to be 4.0 g/cm3 for SiO2 and 8.02 g/cm3 for SS304. The seed activities are typically 5.0 mCi per seed. The 90Sr/90Y nuclide emits beta particles with energies in a continuum up to 2.2 MeV. The energy spectrum of the nuclide used in the calculations was taken from the report by Cross et al. [20]. According to our assumptions, the 90Sr/90Y nuclide is uniformly distributed in the SiO2 core.

Simulation and validation of the source

The dose calculation formalism for Intravascular BT recommended by the Task Group 60 (TG-60) of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) was used in this work for calculation and validation of dose parameters [21]. Dosimetric functions defined in TG-60 are determined by the source geometry and other parameters such as distance and angle. As part of the source simulation validation, and to verify the accuracy of the MC algorithm applied in this study, dosimetric parameters such as the radial dose function, anisotropy function, and 2D dose rate distribution were calculated for the 90Sr/90Y seed within a water phantom. For benchmarking purposes, the dose values from our simulation were compared to the results presented in the study by Wang and Li [22].

Due to the rapid dose fall-off near the beta sources, it is extremely difficult to obtain accurate dose experimentally. In the present study, the Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNPX 2.6.0) code [23] was applied to investigate dose distribution and particle transport in various geometries with high spatial resolution. This code utilizes the MCPLIB04 cross section library for beta particles transport. The material compositions and their density data were adopted from the National Institute of Standards and Technology website [24]. A cylindrical source with an axis along the z-axis was simulated inside a spherical water phantom with a radius of 10.0 cm to mimic full scattering conditions. For the purpose of scoring dose, a grid system composed of toruses surrounded the source.

To calculate the radial dose function, the dose calculation cells were placed at radial distances of 0.35 to 8.0 mm, and to calculate the anisotropy function, the cells were simulated at radial distances of 0.5 to 8.0 mm and angles of 0 to 90 degrees. However, to obtain the dose rate in cylindrical coordinates (according to the method presented by Wang and Li [22]), the system of cells was placed at radial and axial distances of 0.0 to 6.0 mm. In order to reduce deviation in the calculations, the cell volume increased with distance from the source.

The photon and electron importance in all the cells were set at 1, except for the vacuum space outside of the water sphere in which they were assigned a value of zero. The simulations were performed in coupled electron-photon mode. It was assumed that beta particle emission from the source was isotropic. A total of 1.5-2.0 × 108 histories were scored and the obtained maximum statistical deviation of the calculations was 4.09%. The energy cut-off of 10 keV was applied for photons and electrons and no other variance reduction techniques were used. The absorbed doses due to electrons and photons were calculated using the *F8 tally. The *F8 tally is designed to score the net energy (MeV) deposited in the cell, which was divided by the mass of that cell to convert the energy deposited into dose (MeV/g).

Designing the applicator

The general design of the proposed applicator is shown in Fig. 2. The applicator consists of a cone wall and a flattening filter. The wall ensures that radiation stays within the targeted skin area and the flattening filter creates a spatially uniform dose profile in the skin region.

Schematic diagram of the applicator designed for a tumor with a diameter of 10 mm and a margin of 2.0 mm. In this model, five Beta-Cath™ 90Sr/90Y 5 French sources were simulated in each catheter. The 18-layer conical design of the aluminum filter optimizes the radiation dose profile

Based on different tumor sizes (10, 15, 20, and 25 mm), 4 applicators were designed and labeled A1 to A4, respectively. To ensure accurate treatment delivery with radionuclide-based BT and electronic brachytherapy (eBT) applicators, a 2.0 mm extra margin should be included [6]. Therefore, the dimensions of applicators A1 to A4 were chosen as 14, 19, 24 and 29 mm, respectively.

To cover the entire tumor volume as uniformly as possible with high isodose curves and also to reduce the treatment time, two trains of 90Sr/90Y seeds of 2.6 mm length (including core and capsule, without separation between seeds) were simulated. Therefore, applicators A1 to A4 contained 5, 7, 9 and 11 source seeds per train, respectively. Two source trains were placed 0.02 mm apart in a polyethylene delivery catheter with a density of 0.94 g/cm3 and a radius of 0.4 mm.

Due to the beta dose fall-off at tissue depth, it is necessary to place the source at closer distances from the tissue, and on the other hand, closer distances reduce the uniformity of the dose distribution. Therefore, applicators were designed with a variable source-to-surface distance (SSD) between 5.0 mm and 6.0 mm. The wall of the applicator was considered conical to limit the radiation to the target volume. Young et al. [25] showed that a 5.2 mm layer of aluminum was sufficient to absorb the high-energy electrons emitted from the 90Sr/90Y source and adding 0.3 mm of lead to the primary shield significantly reduced the low-energy bremsstrahlung.

The flattening filter, between the radiation source and the skin surface, was designed in order to reduce the dose variation across the central axis of the applicator, as well as to improve the beam profile and the width of the penumbra. Many studies have been published on the design of flattening filter for gamma sources [26, 27]. To determine the filter shape, the calculations involved a combination of analytical and numerical iteration methods. Unlike the typical approach of exponential attenuation of photons, the interaction of electrons with matter is complex and the resources of analytical methods are limited. Therefore, in the design of the flattening filter for electron beams, iterative numerical methods were used until the uniformity of the dose distribution was optimized. For this purpose, a conical flattening filter was formed by stacking semi-conical layers of varying thicknesses concentrically (Fig. 2). The filter was oriented such that its narrow apex was positioned closest to the source, while its broad base was oriented toward the skin surface.

Evaluation of dose distribution and radiation leakage

For each applicator, the dose distribution in the tissue equivalent phantom was obtained, with and without flattening filter. In the simulation, the applicators (A1-A4) were placed on the phantom surface, and a rectangular mesh tally (type 1) was used to calculate the absorbed dose in each pixel. The mesh dimensions varied depending on the phantom type and all meshes had a resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 0.1 mm3.

The dose values ​​on the central axis were normalized to the dose at a depth of 3.0 mm, which corresponds to the clinically prescribed dose depth. Beam profiles (in cGy/min) were obtained at depths of 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 mm and isodose were plotted using the MATLAB R2017a (MathWorks). The output of each mesh tally (MeV/cm3) was converted to dose rate (cGy/min) using the following equation. All required unit conversions (including MeV to Joules, grams to kilograms, Gray to centigray, and seconds to minutes) together with density of the medium (ρ), the beta yield of the 90Sr/90Y decay chain (Yβ) and the activity of each source (A), were combined into a single constant factor.

Calculations were performed in tissue-equivalent phantoms. The simulations were conducted in both electron and photon modes, with an energy cutoff of 10 keV applied for each. A total of 2 × 109 electron histories were simulated to calculate the spatial dose distribution, ensuring that the statistical uncertainty of the mesh tally results remained within an acceptable level (generally below about 1.0% in the regions of interest). Following the guidelines set by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60976) [28], flatness, symmetry, and penumbra (distance between the 20% and 80% isodose lines) were calculated from dose profiles at different depths (1.0, 2.0 and 3.0 mm). To investigate the performance of the applicator wall, radiation leakage was calculated through simulations in cells 0.2 mm thick and placed 1.0 mm from the wall.

Discussion

Validation of the simulation

The emergence of novel devices for treating small skin tumors has generated significant interest in BT as a skin cancer treatment option. This study has proposed a novel skin BT applicator that utilizes beta-emitting 90Sr/90Y train sources. The short range of beta particles, unlike gamma rays, leads to a more pronounced dose gradient [29], which, although allowing for precise targeting of tissues close to the source with minimal exposure to sensitive surrounding structures, makes experimental dose estimation difficult [30]. MC simulation is the preferred method for dose calculation in cases where analytical methods are not feasible [31].

In order to ensure the accuracy of the simulation algorithm, the dosimetric parameters were calculated using the TG-60 protocol [21] and compared with the published data by Wang and Li [22]. The dose rate at the reference point for the 90Sr/90Y source with the MCNPX2.6 code was determined to be 0.541 ± 0.001 Gy/min/mCi. Wang and Li [22], also with the simulation codes EGSnrc and EGS4, obtained the dose rate at the reference point of 0.543 ± 0.000 Gy/min/mCi and 0.544 ± 0.000 Gy/min/mCi respectively, which indicate the percentage difference of 0.23 and 0.44, respectively. The mean difference of 0.33% indicates a high percentage of agreement.

The values ​​of the radial dose function were obtained using dose calculation cells at the transverse plane and are listed in Table 1. The results show remarkable consistency with Wang and Li [22] findings in the range of 0.6 to 3.25 mm, differing by less than 1.00%. The mean percentage difference between the results of the two studies is 2.85%. The maximum difference is 8.02%, which corresponds to the distance of 5.5 mm.

The anisotropy function was obtained for radial distances from 0.5 to 8.0 mm at angles of 0 to 90 degrees. Zero-degree angles showed higher uncertainty in the MC statistical calculations. For example, the uncertainty at a radial distance of 8.0 mm for an angle of 0 was 4.09% and for the angle of 5 degrees was 1.52%. This may be due to the use of a spherical dose calculation cells instead of toroidal ones at zero angle, such that the smaller volume of spherical cells increases the statistical uncertainty in the MC calculations [32]. The mean percentage difference between the present study and the study by Wang and Li [22] study was 1.74%, and as expected, the maximum difference occurred at an angle of 0 and a radial distance of 8.0 mm, equal to 7.51%.

According to Wang and Li [22] method, the two-dimensional dose rate was obtained in polar coordinates and normalized to its value at the reference point. The mean percentage difference between the results of present study with Wang and Li [22] studies was 1.70%, while the maximum difference was 8.33% (at γ and z equal to 6.0 mm). As expected, the highest statistical uncertainty values were found at γ = 0, with a maximum of 4.91% at z = 6.0 mm.

Applicator design parameters

In designing the source, two trains of source seeds were considered. This configuration resulted in better control of beta particle divergence and more uniform tumor coverage with higher isodose curves in our pilot studies. Two source trains also reduced treatment time due to the increased number of seeds.

To attenuate high-energy beta particles, the applicator body was designed with a 5.2 mm aluminum primary layer. A 0.3 mm lead secondary layer was incorporated to absorb bremsstrahlung radiation and reduce leakage to a minimum. Therefore, the leakage at a distance of 1.0 mm from the applicator body was reduced to below 0.02% of the skin surface dose in the effective field under the applicator. However, in the case of the Leipzig applicator (Stand.Appl) with 192Ir, 169Yb, and 60Co gamma-emitter sources, Safigholi et al. [33] reported maximum radiation leakage at distances greater than 20 mm from the applicator surface to be 5%, 1%, and 18%, respectively. Therefore, the advantage of the beta-emitting sources applicator designed in the present study is clearly observed in better protection of surrounding normal tissues and staff, as well as lower weight and lower costs compared to the gamma-emitter sources.

We considered a flattening filter for the applicator to achieve a uniform dose distribution in the target volume. For beta radiation, aluminum was a good option in terms of cost, availability, and the possibility of achieving smaller thicknesses. For enhanced performance, the filter was placed at the furthest point from the source, corresponding to the terminal section of the applicator. As expected, the best performance of the flattening filter was observed for larger fields and higher SSD, such that in the case of the A4 applicator we reached 3.47% flatness at a depth of 3.0 mm (Table 4). However, increasing SSD results in a reduced output dose rate and consequently longer treatment times.

Currently, there are no established reference limits for flatness, symmetry, or penumbra values for superficial applicators in BT. AAPM TG-40 protocol recommends 3.0% criterion for flatness and symmetry in electron EBRT [34]. Previous studies on gamma-emitting sources reported a flatness of 0.6% at a prescribed depth of 3.0 mm for the BVH-20 Valencia applicator and 0.4% for the BVH-30 [10]. Candela Juan et al. [35] also reported a tolerance of 7.5% for flatness at a depth of 3.0 mm for Esteya eBT system. Figure 5 illustrates that the average flatness values for all models at depths of 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 mm are 3.65%, 5.26%, and 6.93%, respectively. Notably, only models A1 and A2 exceeded 7.0% flatness at these depths. Additionally, symmetry percentages for various depths and applicator sizes (field sizes) were observed between 0.5% and 1.6%, a result that is highly favorable given the small field sizes.

Ferreira et al. [15] reported a penumbra 4.3 mm at a 5.0 mm depth for a 6 MeV electron EBRT device (Varian 21EX Clinac) with a 6 × 6 cm2 cone and a 0.5 cm bolus, compared to a penumbra of 1.6 mm on the skin surface for a 90Sr/90Y single seed source. Applicators with 192Ir gamma sources such as Valencia, with flattening filter, and Leipzig, without flattening filter also had penumbras of 1.9 and 6.2–9.5 mm, respectively, indicating the effectiveness of the flattening filter design and shape in reducing penumbra [3]. In this study, the penumbra varied from 1.7 to 3.0 mm, depending on the applicator type (field dimensions) and depth. This variation may be attributed to the use of a train source, as well as photon contamination due to flattening filter-induced dose inhomogeneity at depth.

Tumor size and pathology determine the prescribed dose, ranging from 35 to 70 Gy. The standard prescribed dose for common non-melanoma skin cancers treated with surface applicators (Leipzig, Valencia and eBT) is 40 Gy in 8 fractions at a depth of 3.0 mm. This hypofractionated regime achieved > 90% local control with acceptable toxicity in elderly patients [6]. As shown in Table 4, the dose rate for the different applicators A1 to A4 at a depth of 3.0 mm is between 55.92 and 61.97 cGy/min. This dose rate for the 500 cGy/fraction required in the hypofractionated regime is equivalent to a treatment time of 8.06 to 8.94 min. This time range is suitable for outpatient treatment.

Sealed 90Sr/90Y ophthalmic applicators with flat or concave active surfaces have been widely used for decades, particularly for treating pterygium and other superficial ocular lesions. Devices such as the concave SIA-6 and similar flat-surface models are designed with a fixed active window and rigid geometry that produce a predetermined irradiation field, typically 8–12 mm in diameter [36, 37]. While their sealed construction provides stable and reproducible dosimetric characteristics, their dose distribution and field size are inherently fixed, limiting flexibility in adapting the irradiation field to different treatment sites. In contrast, the applicator proposed in this study uses catheter-based 90Sr/90Y source trains with external flattening and shielding layers, allowing controlled field shaping up to 25 mm and improved management of beta-particle divergence and leakage radiation, which are important considerations in dermatological BT.

The results of the study showed that the designed applicator allows for non-invasive and outpatient treatment in a short time as well as optimal protection of healthy tissues along with effective dose delivery to the target volume. Sealed radioactive seeds placed inside the catheter provide enhanced safety for the treatment staff. After the treatment is completed, the seeds can be returned to their afterloader. The long half-life of the parent radionuclide reduces the need for frequent source replacement and transportation, unlike short half-life BT sources such as 192Ir that typically require replacement every few months. Low manufacturing and maintenance costs, easy use with reusability, provide significant economic advantages over external radiotherapy devices.

This applicator is intended exclusively for thin skin tumors up to 3.0 mm in thickness and maximum diameter of 25 mm, corresponding to stage T1 and T2 skin cancers. This may be considered a limitation and depends on our perspective. Another limitation of this study may be related to the flatness of the beam in small fields compared to gamma sources, in which case it may be better to make a judgment by considering the other advantages listed above for skin BT with beta source.

Conclusion

This study was the first to design a fixed geometry applicator for a 90Sr/90Y beta source in the superficial skin cancers. The results indicated that the designed applicators performed well in terms of dose distribution parameters such as flatness, symmetry, and penumbra. Due to the use of a beta-emitting source, dose leakage was significantly reduced. The use of a train of seeds instead of a single seed resulted in a high dose rate at the depth of dose administration and optimized treatment time. These dose distribution advantages, along with advantages such as low manufacturing cost, high usability, and no need for high expertise, justify the use of this applicator in treatment. However, it should be noted that this applicator set is only suitable for thin skin lesions up to 3.0 mm thick and tumors up to 25 mm in diameter.
