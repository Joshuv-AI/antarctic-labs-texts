Structural, economic, and socio-cultural barriers to birth and death registration in Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Khadijeh Asadisarvestani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCivil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS) systems are fundamental to health governance, social protection, and evidence-based policymaking. Although Iran has made significant progress in expanding civil registration coverage, the incomplete and delayed registration of births and deaths remains a critical challenge. These gaps compromise the quality of national health data, distort resource allocation, and perpetuate inequalities. This study investigates the structural, economic, and socio-cultural barriers to the timely registration of births and deaths in Iran, with a specific focus on Sistan and Baluchestan Province.MethodsA qualitative study based on seven focus group discussions was conducted between January and February 2025. Thirty-four participants were purposively selected. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis as outlined by Graneheim and Lundman.FindingsThree primary themes emerged from the analysis: (1) Legal and organizational barriers, including lack of foundational identity documents, unregistered marriages, undocumented migrants, home births and home deaths, absence of enclosed cemeteries, inaccessible registration centers, cumbersome death certification processes, and properties with unofficial documents; (2) Financial barriers, comprising poverty and financial exploitation; and (3) Socio-cultural barriers, such as lack of public awareness and poor community relations with health and registration organizations.ConclusionThe under-registration of vital events in Iran is rooted in interconnected structural, economic, and sociocultural barriers. Strengthening the CRVS system requires integrated strategies, such as linking registration with primary healthcare, decentralizing services, and launching community-based awareness campaigns. Ensuring comprehensive vital registration is not only an administrative imperative but also a critical public health priority, essential for evidence-based policymaking, equitable resource distribution, and upholding human rights.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02744-4.Keywords: Registries, Death certificates, Birth certificates, Data analysis, Data management, Civil registration, Vital statistics, Iran

Introduction

Health Information Systems (HIS) provide the essential evidence base required for effective governance, policy formulation [1], strategic identification of health needs, and efficient resource allocation [2]. Transparent reporting of HIS data is fundamental to accountability, holding policymakers responsible for health outcomes [3]. Data gaps cripple evidence-based policy, leading to a misallocation of resources, an inability to monitor health inequalities, and a failure to hold governments accountable for their health mandates, ultimately perpetuating cycles of disadvantage and poor health [4]. The Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS) system serves as the foundational element of this information ecosystem [4]. The core of the CRVS system is the legal and permanent recording of all vital events, primarily births and deaths [5]. Reliable birth registration data are foundational to evidence-informed health and social policy [6].

However, every year, millions are born and die worldwide without official registration in a national civil registration system. Birth registration is a fundamental human right and legal proof of a child’s existence and nationality that facilitates access to health services, education, and social protection [7], exercise civil rights [8], and to complete legal processes such as marriage, divorce, adoption, and inheritance [5]. Under-registration has also been linked to lower childhood immunization coverage [9]. Having proof of age can help protect against child exploitation, child trafficking, child labor, and child marriage. Birth registration is recognized in Target 16.9 of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which aims to “provide legal identity for all, including birth registration” by 2030. However, approximately 2 in 10 (150 million) children under age 5 remain unregistered globally [10].

While birth registration establishes legal identity and access to essential services, death registration completes the cycle of vital statistics, offering key information for population health monitoring and governance. Together, they form the complete life-course data that is indispensable for public health planning and monitoring. Specifically, a death certificate serves as a permanent legal record of the death of a person, which provides essential information about the decedent, including age, sex, education, date of death, along with the circumstances and cause of death, and its final disposition [11]. This information is utilized in legal matters, insurance claims, pension settlements, and real estate and personal property transfers [11]. Death registration is the primary source for understanding a population’s disease burden and guiding public health priorities [4]. Delayed registration often results in under-reporting of child deaths [5, 8], especially during the early neonatal period, because neither the birth [6, 8] nor the death is recorded [5, 12].

In sum, statistical data derived from birth and death certificates is crucial for countries and international organizations. Such data guide responses to public health challenges and the monitoring of health programs [5], and enable the tracking of fertility and mortality trends [12, 13]. Iran has made significant progress in registering births and deaths in recent years. Nevertheless, obstacles remain to achieving complete and accurate coverage. As an example, studies in Kermanshah have documented significant barriers in registering stillbirths, including the lack of a clear protocol, complex bureaucratic procedures for obtaining burial permits, and cultural stigma that discourages grieving families from engaging with the registration process [14]. Another study in a southern province has also reported inaccuracies in coding the underlying causes of death, which undermines the reliability of mortality statistics [15]. Additionally, legal and social complexities—such as the status of children born to unmarried parents—continue to limit timely and complete registration [16]. Despite these shortcomings, there are no official and accurate statistics on delays in registering these vital events and the failure to record this essential data in Iran. One of the regions where delays in vital event registration are particularly pronounced is Sistan and Baluchestan Province, a border area located in southeastern Iran. Delays in birth and death registration in this province have had significant economic, social, health, and developmental consequences. Some segments of the population are statistically invisible, which distorts national health data and leads to the systematic under-allocation of resources for healthcare, education, and social welfare. This directly undermines health equity and perpetuates cycles of disadvantage [6, 8]. Unregistered children are excluded from education, healthcare, and social services, while unregistered adults are denied access to economic, social, and health rights, as well as full citizenship. To the best of our knowledge, no previous qualitative study has examined the barriers to birth and death registration in Iran, particularly from the perspective of stakeholders in the most affected regions, highlighting the importance of the present research in addressing this gap. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the main barriers to the timely legal registration of births and deaths in Sistan and Baluchestan, Iran. The findings of this study can identify ways to enhance the registration of births and deaths as a critical part of CRVS at both national and international levels.

Methods

A qualitative approach was employed in this study. Data collection took place in Sistan and Baluchestan Province, Iran, from January to February 2025. As the second-largest province in Iran, located in the southeast bordering Pakistan and Afghanistan, Sistan and Baluchestan was selected as the study setting for several key reasons. First, it consistently demonstrates the lowest performance among Iranian provinces on key human development indicators, highlighting its vulnerability and justifying the need for detailed investigation. Second, its border location leads to significant migration flows, creating specific challenges for civil registration systems. Third, various stakeholders, including government officials and social activists, have repeatedly reported a high prevalence of undocumented residents, particularly children, in this province [16].

Data were collected through focus group discussions with 34 participants, who were recruited using purposive sampling. The number of groups was determined based on participant diversity and the need to capture perspectives from different subgroups. The inclusion criteria were: NGOs with experience supporting vulnerable populations, and individuals familiar with barriers and consequences of birth and death registration. Participants also needed to have knowledge of the socio-economic and cultural contexts of Sistan and Baluchestan Province, be at least 18 years old, and be willing to share their views in group discussions. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to participate in the study or focus group discussions, less than ten years of residency in the province, and NGOs with primarily political orientations.

According to methodological guidance on focus groups, discussions were planned for 6–12 participants [17, 18]. Actual group sizes ranged from 11 to 13 participants. The slightly larger groups were maintained to ensure representation of diverse viewpoints within each stakeholder category and to accommodate participant availability constraints [18, 19]. Details of the focus group discussions are summarized in Table 1. Each session lasted 60–90 min and was conducted in Persian with a lead researcher facilitating and a note-taker present. All sessions were digitally recorded. During the initial session, after obtaining written informed consent, participants completed a brief socio-demographic questionnaire covering age, gender, residence, and educational level.

Composition and number of discussion sessions

Seven focus group discussions were conducted in total. Data collection continued until saturation was reached, defined as the point where no new codes or categories emerged and participants began repeating previously expressed views. Saturation was achieved after the seventh session, consistent with methodological recommendations for focus groups [19, 20]. A discussion guide was developed based on the research objectives, covering five main domains: (1) personal experiences with unregistered births or deaths; (2) barriers to registration, including administrative, socio-economic, and cultural factors; (3) consequences of non-registration for families and communities, particularly for women and children; (4) perceptions of civil registration services and procedures; and (5) potential strategies and policy solutions for improving registration coverage. While these domains guided all discussions, Group 1 emphasized personal experiences, while Groups 2 and 3 focused more on community-level perspectives and institutional or policy challenges. The complete discussion guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

After each session, the research team reviewed the recordings and field notes in debriefing sessions to address any issues that emerged during data collection. All recordings were transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were then verified and corrected by the research assistant who had been present during the data collection. Data analysis was performed using qualitative content analysis following the approach of Graneheim and Lundman [21]. The process began with repeated reading of the interview transcripts to gain an overall understanding of participants’ perceptions regarding barriers to birth and death registration. The transcribed text was then divided into meaning units, which were condensed and assigned codes. These codes were compared for similarities and differences and organized into categories. Finally, categories sharing common patterns were abstracted into broader themes representing the key barriers to birth and death registration.

The analysis team comprised two independent researchers who conducted the coding, category development, and thematic analysis. Any discrepancies in analysis were resolved through discussion to minimize researcher bias. To ensure the trustworthiness of the findings, we applied Lincoln and Guba’s four criteria [22]. Credibility was established through data collection to saturation and peer debriefing within the research team. Dependability and confirmability were enhanced by involving two independent coders, maintaining detailed records of the coding and thematic development process, and resolving discrepancies through discussion. Transferability was supported by recruiting participants from diverse stakeholder groups familiar with the research issue and by providing rich descriptions of the study context and procedures, allowing readers to evaluate the potential applicability of findings to other settings.

To encourage honest and spontaneous responses, participants were not asked to introduce themselves at the beginning of the focus group discussions, following established methodological guidance [18, 20]. Before each discussion, the study aims were explained to all participants, and they were assured of confidentiality and anonymity. Every participant signed a written informed consent form, which included agreement to participate in the discussion and to audio recording of the session. Additionally, participants were reminded not to disclose any information shared during the discussions outside the group, to help maintain confidentiality within the group setting [18].

Educationally, the largest proportion of participants held a bachelor’s degree (38.2%), followed by those with a high school diploma (26.5%), a master’s degree (17.6%), and a diploma (17.6%). In terms of residence, the majority lived in urban areas (64.7%), while 35.3% resided in rural areas.

Legal and organizational barriers

This category encompasses obstacles stemming from government laws, official procedures, and bureaucratic structures within state institutions. Our analysis identified eight distinct subthemes under this category: lack of foundational identity documents, unregistered marriages, undocumented migrants, home births, absence of enclosed cemeteries, inaccessible registration centers, cumbersome death certification processes, and properties with unofficial documents.

Lack of foundational identity documents

The absence of a national ID card constitutes a primary barrier, creating a vicious cycle wherein individuals cannot register vital events due to lacking the required foundational documentation. Although official data on individuals without verified identity documents in Iran is unavailable, unofficial estimates suggest approximately 20,000 people in Sistan and Baluchestan lack any form of identity documentation. An NGO activist said, “One of the most significant reasons for this is that many of them have suspicious identities according to the Civil Status Registry Organization. These people need to have their DNA tested to determine their identity, but since the test is expensive for most people, they do not take it; therefore, they are deprived of their identity documents”.

Unregistered marriages

Legal and social barriers related to marital status take multiple forms in Sistan and Baluchestan. In addition to marriages between Iranian women and non-Iranian men, which in some cases face challenges in gaining official recognition, some domestic marriages also remain unregistered for reasons such as underage marriage, polygamy, and economic or cultural constraints. Having a registered marriage certificate is a legal prerequisite for registering the birth or death of a child; therefore, unregistered marriages directly hinder the process.

One woman stated, “Because my husband is Afghan, I cannot register my child’s birth in the local office”. “These women could not register the death of their child or spouse because their identity was not registered in the local registration office; therefore, when they died, they had to be buried in an unmarked cemetery, where they did not require a permit.”

A social activist explained: “Many marriages here take place below the legal age — 13 for girls and 15 for boys — and are only performed religiously. That’s why registering the birth of their children is often delayed for years, and if the child dies during that period, the death may never be reported.” Another social activist added: “Many polygamous marriages remain unregistered due to cultural norms, poverty, or lack of awareness, leading to delayed or incomplete registration of their children’s births and deaths.”

Undocumented migrants

Despite the Iranian government’s efforts to control its borders, many immigrants, primarily from Afghanistan, enter and reside in the country illegally. Fear of deportation and lack of legal residency status prevent a significant portion of this population from engaging with formal registration systems, rendering them statistically invisible. An NGO activist stated: “Many undocumented immigrant women give birth at home due to their lack of residence permits. Furthermore, if a family member dies, they are buried in unmarked cemeteries without official permits.” Another social activist mentioned: “I know many undocumented immigrants who temporarily relocated during Iran’s last census to avoid being counted. He claimed that according to his survey in a slum area, the actual number of immigrants differs significantly from the official census data.”

Dual citizenship is another factor that delays the registration of vital events. One participant noted: “I know many people who hold multiple passports. For them, registering births and deaths is of little importance unless they need such certificates for matters like school enrollment or inheritance transfer.”

Home births and home deaths

Although most women in Iran give birth in health facilities, a notable proportion of deliveries in Sistan and Baluchestan still occur at home due to cultural norms, financial hardship, distance from health centers, and lack of insurance or identity documents [16, 17]. Home births often delay registration because no official birth notification is generated. A participant explained: “Some families live far from the hospital, and others cannot afford the costs, so they deliver at home. They only consider obtaining a birth certificate when their children reach school age.”

Similar challenges occur with home deaths. When elderly or chronically ill individuals die at home, families often proceed with burial without notifying legal authorities. A local resident noted: “If someone dies at home, especially an older person, we bury them quickly without a death certificate. When no property has official documentation, there is no need for inheritance paperwork, so there is no reason to contact the authorities.” Another participant added: “In rural areas, due to the absence of a morgue and cultural-religious beliefs requiring swift burial, families typically avoid hospitals or forensic medicine centers.”

Absence of enclosed cemeteries

The absence of enclosed or regulated cemeteries in many rural areas of Sistan and Baluchestan Province presents a significant barrier to death registration. In these settings, burials can proceed informally without permits or official notifications, completely bypassing the registration system. One participant explained: “When a person dies at home and there are no assets requiring a death certificate, families avoid the costly and time-consuming legal procedures since no burial permit is needed.”

Inaccessible registration centers

Geographical distance to registration offices, combined with poor road conditions and limited transportation options, creates a major structural barrier to timely registration, particularly in rural and remote areas. Several participants emphasized that when civil registration services are not readily accessible and there is no immediate benefit for the child or parents, families see little reason to prioritize birth registration. As a resident stated: “When the registration office is far away and there is no financial benefit for the child or the family, why should we rush to register the birth? We only go when it becomes necessary.”

This same reasoning applies to deaths occurring at home. Registering a death requires traveling to a registration office, which is often distant or difficult to reach. Families who are grieving and managing burial rituals—especially in rural areas where prompt burial is customary—may delay the process or forgo it entirely. A participant explained: “When someone dies, the family is grieving, and the burial needs to occur quickly. Visiting the registration office requires time and money. If the deceased had no property requiring official transfer and no pension or financial benefits to be passed to the family, they often see no reason to register the death at all.”

Cumbersome death certification processes

The lengthy process of obtaining a death certificate from the Legal Medicine Organization was frequently mentioned as a significant bureaucratic hurdle that conflicts with cultural and religious norms requiring swift burial. Several participants noted that for religious and cultural reasons, people prefer to hold funeral ceremonies as soon as possible rather than navigating the extensive bureaucratic procedures of forensic medicine to confirm the cause of death and obtain a burial permit. One resident explained: “To avoid visiting forensic medicine centers, some individuals who require medical confirmation of the cause of death to secure a burial certificate bribe doctors to change the cause of death to conditions such as heart attack or other cases that do not require medical examination.”

Properties with unofficial documents

Although death registration is necessary to formalize inheritance and distribute the deceased’s property, it is important to note that many properties lack official registration. When properties are not formally registered, families perceive no legal obligation to obtain a death certificate for inheritance transfer, eliminating a key incentive for death registration. As one participant noted: “In many rural areas, properties are not officially registered; therefore, families can transfer or continue using property without needing formal death certificates.” Consequently, in the absence of legal or administrative requirements, families often choose not to register deaths, particularly in remote rural communities. Furthermore, since most properties and real estate in Iran are registered under male names, when a woman dies, there is a higher probability of delayed registration or complete non-registration of her death.

Financial barriers

This theme relates to economic constraints and incentives that directly influence the decision to register vital events. It comprises two sub-categories:

Poverty

Both childbirth and funeral practices entail considerable expenses, imposing a substantial burden on low-income households. Consequently, covering the additional costs associated with birth or death registration becomes challenging—particularly in the case of death, which often occurs suddenly, leaving families unprepared for unforeseen expenses. The direct and indirect costs of registration (including fees, transportation, and lost daily wages) are prohibitive for families living in poverty. A social activist working in suburban areas explained: “Many people here cannot even afford basic necessities, so birth or death registration fees are not a priority. However, when a cash subsidy is available for a newborn, they attempt to register the birth promptly.” Economic hardship compels families to prioritize immediate survival needs over administrative requirements, leading to delayed or missed registration of vital events.

Financial exploitation

Conversely, perverse financial incentives to avoid registration were identified. Participants described how families deliberately refrain from reporting deaths to continue receiving the deceased’s subsidies or even sell their identity documents. This practice may reflect an economic strategy shaped by poverty, but it also indicates systemic shortcomings and inadequate oversight within civil registration and social protection systems. A local man commented, “Some people avoid registering deaths to continue benefiting from financial support such as subsidies and charity. Additionally, some families sell identity documents of the deceased to those who lack proper documentation.” Another resident stated: “Many people in the province survive on very low incomes, with some relying entirely on cash subsidies. Therefore, the money obtained from selling deceased relatives’ identity documents represents a substantial amount for them.” Echoing this sentiment, another local resident noted, “I know individuals who have sold their deceased relatives’ identity documents to undocumented persons in exchange for money, while continuing to receive cash subsidies and other financial benefits themselves.”

Socio-cultural factors

This theme captures barriers rooted in community knowledge, perceptions, and relationships with authorities. It comprises two sub-categories:

Lack of awareness lack of public awareness

The findings revealed a widespread lack of knowledge regarding legal requirements, deadlines, and—more importantly—the long-term personal and societal benefits of birth and death registration. A local woman stated, “This is the first time I have heard that there are legal deadlines for birth and death registration, and I am sure many people are unaware of them.” Another resident commented, “Many people are not only unaware of the consequences of selling birth certificates but also perceive selling identity documents as a form of sacrifice and humanitarian aid.” Another local woman added, “Not only are many people unaware of the importance of timely death registration, but they also consider it unnecessary—viewing it as a waste of time that may delay funeral rituals due to bureaucratic processes.”

In some cases, this lack of awareness leads to more complex consequences. Several participants explained that some families who lost a child reused the deceased child’s birth certificate for their next newborn. As one participant described: “I know families who lost a son and later used his identity document for their newborn daughter, or vice versa. Years later, they had to visit the registration office to modify the identity document by correcting the age and first name. In such cases, neither the death of the previous child nor the birth of the new one is officially registered, creating significant gaps in vital records and identity documentation.” These statements highlight a substantial communication gap between the state and its citizens.

Poor community relations with health and registration organizations

Health centers and other local institutions, such as rural municipalities, can play an active role in recording vital events, particularly births and deaths. In recent years, healthcare centers have been tasked with registering births and deaths within their jurisdictions. Regarding death registration, one resident noted: “Due to the considerable distance between many villages and forensic and registration centers, rural municipality officials have been instructed to report local deaths to civil status registry offices. However, some officials neglect this duty due to familial relationships with the deceased’s family.” A social activist further explained: “Some community members have strained relationships with healthcare staff for various reasons. Consequently, they avoid visiting health centers even for essential services like newborn vaccinations, not to mention death registration and cause of death reporting.”

This reported distrust and poor rapport with local health officials discourages community members from utilizing their services, including vital event reporting. This barrier differs from geographical inaccessibility as it pertains to perceived social and relational distance rather than physical distance.

Discussion

The results of this qualitative research reveal a complex network of barriers to birth and death registration in Sistan and Baluchestan Province, Iran. This study set out to investigate the main barriers to timely birth and death registration in this region. Our findings not only confirm the multifaceted nature of these challenges but also elucidate their deep-rooted structural and sociocultural dimensions. These challenges are not only operational but are also deeply embedded in structural inequalities, legal frameworks, and socio-cultural factors.

Legal and organizational barriers

The identified legal and organizational barriers are major obstacles to vital event registration, effectively leading to exclusion within the system. The lack of foundational identity documents is a significant barrier, creating a vicious cycle where people cannot access rights because they lack the legal proof needed to claim them. This finding aligns with global evidence that legal identity is a basic human right and an important social factor affecting health [23, 24]. In the specific context of Sistan and Baluchestan, this issue is worsened by unregistered marriages, cross-border migration, and long-term residency challenges, similar to issues observed in border regions like those between Thailand and Myanmar [25]. The marriage of Iranian women to foreign nationals, despite recent legal changes, still creates a grey area for citizenship and registration—an issue also seen in other Middle Eastern contexts with large stateless or at-risk populations. In Kuwait, the Bidoon (a term meaning “without nationality”) are a long-standing stateless community who, despite deep historical roots, are denied citizenship and thus face severe barriers to accessing education, healthcare, and formal employment [26]. In Lebanon, discriminatory nationality laws that prevent women from passing citizenship to their children have created a generation of children born to Lebanese mothers and foreign fathers who are at high risk of statelessness and exclusion from state services [27, 28]. These examples highlight how nationality laws and gendered legal frameworks can create and sustain statelessness, similar to the barriers faced by children of Iranian women married to foreign men.

The challenge of home births fundamentally severs the critical link between health service delivery, hospitals/maternity wards, and civil registry offices, as the automatic notification to registry authorities is lost. This systemic gap is a significant contributor to under-registration [29–31]. It is documented that in countries with lower rates of facility-based births, integrating birth registration with other health services, such as immunization programs, has proven effective, as parents often bring their children for vaccinations even when the birth occurred at home [31]. A parallel and often more severe problem exists with home deaths. When deaths occur outside of hospitals without medical attention, they are far less likely to be recorded, creating a major blind spot in mortality data [32].

The inaccessibility of registration centers is another obstacle that disproportionately impacts both birth and death registration, especially in remote and rural areas, as documented also in some regions of Nepal [33], Egypt [34] and Nigeria [35]. Additionally, lengthy forensic procedures for death certification hinder timely registration, a barrier also identified in African countries [36, 37]. In Iran, administrative procedures related to death registration may not always align smoothly with religious and cultural norms that emphasize prompt burial. In addition, in contexts where families do not possess registered assets, the incentive to obtain a death certificate or engage with medico-legal authorities tends to be low, thereby perpetuating under-registration—a pattern also observed in Ghana [37]. This context-specific barrier calls for procedural reforms that decentralize and simplify vital events registration. Evidence from Bangladesh demonstrates that empowering community-level health workers to notify both births and deaths, and to conduct verbal autopsies for deaths occurring outside health facilities, has improved the completeness and timeliness of civil registration data [38]. Another context-specific barrier to death registration is the existence of no enclosed cemeteries, where burials can occur without formal documentation. Evidence from Bangladesh indicates that deaths in such cemeteries are often not linked to the national civil registration system, reducing families’ incentive to register them [39]. Similarly, in rural Senegal, the absence of a requirement for a burial permit has been shown to lower the motivation for death registration [40].

Financial barriers

Findings from this study highlight the role of economic constraints and financial costs as key barriers to both birth and death registration. Similar challenges have been reported in other low- and middle-income settings: in Indonesia, direct and indirect expenses—including transport and loss of daily income—discourage families from completing registration [41]; in Bangladesh, travel costs and distance to registration offices create financial burdens that delay timely registration [30]; and in Ghana, transportation expenses have been shown to reduce families’ motivation to register deaths [37]. While economic difficulties and the costs associated with registration are key barriers to reporting deaths, our findings suggest that the issue extends beyond financial hardship alone. In certain cases, families intentionally avoid death registration to retain benefits or subsidies linked to the deceased, indicating deliberate financial exploitation rather than simple economic constraint. Similar patterns have been observed in Ghana and other low-income settings, where weak oversight and limited data integration enable such practices to persist [37, 42].

Socio-cultural barriers

Socio-cultural barriers represent profound demand-side challenges that undermine the effectiveness of CRVS systems. A fundamental issue is the widespread lack of awareness—not only of registration procedures but, more importantly, of the personal and societal benefits of registration. Studies across low- and middle-income countries have shown that many families see little value in formal registration, perceiving it as irrelevant to their daily lives [2, 42]. In Sistan and Baluchestan, this knowledge gap is further exacerbated by relatively low literacy rates, geographical remoteness, and limited access to digital connectivity and trustworthy information channels, all of which restrict the effective dissemination of awareness regarding civil registration processes and their value. Culturally and linguistically distinct communities may not be adequately reached by standardized, top-down communication strategies originating from the central government. This can lead to a gap in awareness and understanding of state procedures. When official information does not permeate these communities through trusted channels or in relatable formats, the registration process can be perceived as less relevant to daily life, reducing the sense of urgency to comply.

Beyond a simple lack of awareness, limited trust in formal state institutions and health centres constitutes a meaningful barrier to engagement with civil registration systems. Evidence from Iran shows low institutional trust in health and government services among some communities [43, 44]. Similar dynamics have been observed among Indigenous populations in Latin America, including Mexico, where historical neglect and discrimination have led to persistent skepticism toward government-run systems [45].

Furthermore, it is crucial to recognize that these barriers are not isolated; they often interact synergistically. For instance, poverty (a financial barrier) can lead to home births (an organizational barrier), which in turn is exacerbated by a lack of awareness (a socio-cultural barrier), creating a vicious cycle of administrative invisibility.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its in-depth exploration of the main barriers to birth and death registration through a qualitative approach, which provided profound insights into the complex nature of these challenges. Furthermore, the diversity of the participants—including social activists, local residents, and individuals with direct personal experience of registration barriers—significantly enriched the comprehensiveness and depth of the findings, capturing a wide spectrum of perspectives.

However, this study has several limitations. While our findings captured the predominant barriers, some context-specific factors, such as historical avoidance of registration to evade conscription or challenges related to children born to unmarried parents, may not have been fully reflected in the focus group data. Additionally, the data were collected exclusively from Sistan and Baluchestan Province. Given Iran’s vast and diverse socio-cultural landscape, further studies in other regions are essential to validate and compare these findings. Finally, as with any qualitative study, the generalizability of the results may be limited, although the rich, contextualized insights offer valuable understanding of the local barriers.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that birth and death registration in Sistan and Baluchestan Province is hindered by a complex interplay of legal, administrative, financial, and socio-cultural barriers. The absence of foundational identity documents, economic constraints, geographical inaccessibility, and low public awareness collectively contribute to the exclusion of vulnerable populations from civil registration systems. Achieving complete birth and death registration in Iran therefore requires moving beyond technical solutions to address these fundamental social determinants of health and equity.

A comprehensive approach must include decentralized registration services, integrated health and registration systems, and targeted awareness campaigns that tackle both supply-side and demand-side barriers. Furthermore, this necessitates a pro-poor, gender-sensitive, and migrant-inclusive approach that recognizes vital registration not merely as a statistical exercise, but as a fundamental human right and cornerstone of inclusive development.

Given the transnational nature of migration in this border region, regional cooperation with neighboring countries is essential to ensure complete registration coverage. By implementing these targeted strategies, Iran can significantly strengthen its CRVS system, reduce administrative invisibility among its most vulnerable populations, and make substantial progress toward fulfilling Sustainable Development Goal Target 16.9. Ultimately, strengthening the CRVS system requires coordinated action at local, national, and international levels to uphold the right to legal identity and improve health governance.
