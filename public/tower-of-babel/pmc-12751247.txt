Glucocorticoid-mediated suppression of the CCL2-CCR2 axis drives monocyte dysfunction in severe pneumonia among immunocompromised hosts
Authors: Zhongshu Kuang, Yue Luo, Yao Chen, Yilin Yang, Chen Chen, Feixiang Xu, Yumei Chen, Yannan Zhou, Yongqi Shen, Li Yuan, Hua Su, Chaoyang Tong, Zhenju Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunocompromised hosts (ICHs) face elevated risks of severe respiratory infections and mortality. Glucocorticoids, commonly used to treat underlying conditions in ICHs, may further impair immune defenses, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsRetrospective analysis of peripheral blood immune cell profiles was conducted in pneumonia patients with ICHs. Prospective immunophenotyping of PBMCs by Cytek flow cytometry was performed to compare immune cell subsets in survivors versus deceased patients. To explore glucocorticoid effects on ICH immune mechanisms, we used a murine model of severe pneumonia (MRL/lpr mice) and an in vitro ICH infection mimic model. Glucocorticoid effects on immune cell populations and function were examined in these models.ResultsFocusing on key immune cells, we observed that ICH patients with severe pneumonia exhibit a reduction in classical monocyte subsets. Mechanistically, glucocorticoids downregulated CCL2 expression in the lung via GR/STAT3, thereby inhibiting the PA-LPS/TLR4/PI3K/AKT/STAT signaling pathway and diminishing pulmonary CCL2 and Ly6C+CCR2+ monocyte levels. Importantly, exogenous recombinant CCL2 administration restored monocyte function, reduced lung injury, and enhanced survival.ConclusionThese findings uncovered a critical immunosuppressive mechanism by which glucocorticoids, via GR/STAT3-mediated suppression of the CCL2-CCR2 axis, impair monocyte-driven antibacterial responses in severe pneumonia, highlighting the need for targeted immunomodulation in ICH patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03437-w.Keywords: Glucocorticoids, CCL2-CCR2 axis, Monocyte dysfunction, Severe pneumonia, Immunocompromised hosts

Introduction

The population of immunocompromised host (ICH) is expanding due to advancements in treatments for autoimmune diseases, organ transplantation, cancer, and other conditions, along with the widespread use of immunosuppressive drugs [1]. Glucocorticoids (GCs) represent a cornerstone of treatment for these conditions, offering potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects [2]. However, the inherent immunosuppressive properties of GCs pose a significant clinical challenge, as prolonged or high-dose exposure is associated with an increased risk of severe infectious complications. Specifically, GC therapy impairs both innate and adaptive immune responses, leading to reductions in key immune cell populations, such as monocytes and lymphocytes [3]. This compromised immune state increases susceptibility to respiratory infections, placing ICH on GC therapy at elevated risk for both common community-acquired (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, influenza) and opportunistic (e.g., Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia) infections, which frequently contribute to adverse clinical outcomes and increased morbidity and mortality [4–6]. This increased susceptibility, along with potential delays in diagnosis and treatment due to atypical presentations, results in significantly higher mortality rates, reaching 50–78% in ICH with severe pneumonia, compared to 12–36% in immunocompetent individuals [7, 8]. Consistent with other reports, we observed a high mortality rate of 60.64% among ICH patients with severe pneumonia [9]. The absence of targeted immune reconstitution strategies and a comprehensive understanding of the specific immune status of ICH patients experiencing severe infections continues to limit effective clinical immune regulation and contribute to poor outcomes.

Monocytes, play a vital role in immune and inflammatory responses, contributing to host defense against pathogens. Clinical studies have identified a decreased number of peripheral blood monocytes as an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with severe infections [10, 11]. In humans, the circulating monocyte pool is composed primarily of classical monocytes (CD14+CD16−), which account for 85%, while intermediate (CD14+CD16+) and non-classical monocytes (CD14−CD16+) comprise the remaining 15% [12]. In mice, the monocyte population is divided into classical (Ly6ChiCCR2+CX3CR1int) and non-classical (Ly6CloCCR2−CX3CR1hi) monocytes [13]. Chemokines and their receptors are essential for mediating immune cell recruitment. CC motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) is a key chemokine involved in various disease processes, including infection, inflammation, immune responses, cardiovascular diseases, and tumor development. CCL2 interacts with the C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) expressed on monocytes, inducing their migration from the bone marrow to sites of inflammation, where they directly phagocytose pathogens and release cytotoxic mediators to eliminate the infection [14, 15]. Furthermore, CCL2 regulates the migration and infiltration of other immune cells, such as dendritic cells (DCs), T lymphocytes, and natural killer (NK) cells, effectively bridging the innate and adaptive immune systems to resolve infection. Moreover, CCL2 influences macrophage polarization towards M1 or M2 phenotypes and guides the differentiation of mononuclear phagocytes into diverse cell types with specialized functions [16, 17].

Recent studies on COVID-19 associated infections have highlighted the significant role of the CCL2-CCR2 interaction in monocyte recruitment and intrapulmonary infiltration in patients [18–21]. Elevated CCL2 levels have been observed in the serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of patients with severe COVID-19, contributing to a cytokine storm and exacerbating alveolar damage [22]. However, animal studies using Ccr2 −/− mice infected with SARS-CoV-2 have shown contrasting results. These mice exhibited higher levels of neutrophils and inflammatory cytokines compared to wild-type mice, potentially linked to excessive viral load in the lungs [23]. This suggests that the CCL2-CCR2 axis may play a dual role in COVID-19, with its effects varying depending on disease severity, viral load, and the initiation of the immune response. In addition, CCL2 plays a critical role in establishing immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment (TME). CCL2 facilitates the recruitment of various immune cells and stromal cells, including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), contributing to the tumor’s ability to evade the immune system [24, 25]. Upon binding to its receptor CCR2, CCL2 activates signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK/NFκB and JAK/STAT pathways, resulting in diverse biological effects [26, 27].

Therefore, the expanding population of ICH and the widespread use of GCs for treatment create a significant clinical challenge, as GC therapy can exacerbate susceptibility to severe pneumonia and lead to high mortality rates. Considering the critical role of monocytes and the chemokine CCL2 in immune responses, particularly their interaction via the CCL2-CCR2 axis in driving monocyte recruitment to sites of infection, this study aims to investigate the specific mechanisms by which GCs disrupt CCL2-CCR2 signaling and compromise monocyte function in ICH patients with severe pneumonia. Understanding these mechanisms may identify potential therapeutic targets for improved immune regulation and better outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

A retrospective cohort of 111 patients diagnosed with pneumonia at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, between January 2018 and June 2022 was analyzed. Data extracted included blood cell counts obtained on days 1 and 7 following admission, as well as in-hospital prognosis. Of these, 86 patients were immunocompromised, and 25 were immunocompetent. Complementing this, a prospective study enrolled 10 patients with severe pneumonia [28] among ICH from Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University between February 2022 and January 2023. Patients were divided into survivors (n=5) and deceased (n=5) groups based on their in-hospital prognosis, participant demographics were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Five healthy volunteers over 18 years of age without chronic diseases were also included as controls. Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients on the days 1 and 7 following admission, and a single sample was collected from each healthy volunteer. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University (Approval number: B2021-445R). To perform deep immunophenotyping of cell subsets in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), a 36-color spectrum flow cytometry panel (Supplementary Table 2) was established, guided by the recommendations in OMIP-69 [29]. The gating strategy used is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Animal and ICH severe pneumonia model

Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 (WT) and MRL/lpr mice were obtained from Slack Laboratory Animal Company (Shanghai, China). Mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility maintained at 22 °C with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (No: 2023 − 188). MRL/lpr mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of dexamethasone (Dex, 2392-39−4, Aladin, Shanghai, China) at 5 mg/kg for six days to induce immunosuppression, as previously described [30]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA, ATCC27853), provided by the Bacteriology Department of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University, was grown in blood agar culture-medium to log phase. Following measurement of optical density at 600 nm (OD600), cultures were diluted to a fixed concentration using saline. Each mouse received an intratracheal inoculation of 30 µl of bacterial suspension, containing 2 × 106 CFU [31, 32]. Intratracheal administration of recombinant CCL2 (rCCL2, HY-P7764, MCE, Shanghai, China) was performed one hour before PA infusion.

Cell lines and cell culture

BEAS-2B and THP-1 cells, obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank, were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. To model dexamethasone treatment of lung epithelial cell damage under immunosuppression, BEAS-2B cells were pretreated with 1 µM dexamethasone (D4902, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) for 2 h. Lipopolysaccharide from pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA-LPS, L8643, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) was then added at a concentration of 0.5 µg/ml, and cell supernatant or cells were collected 24 h later for subsequent experiments.

Generating single-cell suspensions from lung issue

Lung tissues were perfused with saline through the right heart of mouse to remove blood. Dissected tissues were then digested in RPMI-1640 medium containing 100 µg/mL Liberase (5401119001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and 200 µg/mL DNase I (D8071, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 45 min at 37 °C and 180 rpm on a shaker. Single-cell suspensions were obtained by passing the digested tissue through a 70-µm cell strainer followed by red blood cell lysis.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions of lung tissue and PBMCs from mice were stained with fluorescently-conjugated monoclonal antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then washed with Fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer (2% fetal bovine serum in phosphate-buffered saline). Staining combinations and reagents are detailed in Table S3. Precision count beads (424902, BioLegend, Shenzhen, China) were added to each sample to determine absolute cell counts. Samples were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACS Celesta instrument and analyzed with FlowJo software. The gating strategy used is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Wet-to-dry weight (W/D) ratio of lung tissues

To assess lung edema in mice infected with PA, the left lung was excised. Wet weight was measured immediately, and the lobe was then incubated at 60 °C for 72 h to determine dry weight. The W/D ratio was calculated as wet weight divided by dry weight.

Histological analysis

To evaluate histological injury as previously described [33], lung tissue specimens were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in for histological evaluation. Sections were cut at 4 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Two pathologists independently assessed the histopathological scores based on the severity of neutrophil infiltration and alveolar-capillary membrane thickening.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

To determine the expression of Ly6C and CCR2 within lung tissue, a multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) protocol was employed. Lung tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. 4-µm-thick sections were cut. For mIHC staining, tissue sections were simultaneously incubated with primary antibodies against Ly6C (rabbit anti-Ly6C, 1:200, ab314120, Abcam) and CCR2 (rabbit anti-CCR2, 1:200, ab273050, Abcam). Sections were then incubated with red and green fluorophore-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies to detect Ly6C and CCR2, respectively. Sections were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to visualize cell nuclei (blue). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate excitation and emission filters to detect DAPI (blue), red, and green fluorescence.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was used to detect PA in infected lung tissue. Mouse lung tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm-thick sections. Sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval and blocking of endogenous peroxidase. Slides were pre-hybridized for 1 h at 37 °C with pre-hybridization solution (containing salmon sperm DNA). Subsequently, a hybridization solution containing a 2 µM PA specific probe (GGTAACCGTCCCCCTTGC) was applied and incubated at 42 °C overnight. Post-hybridization washes were performed. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Sections were visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Mouse lung tissue homogenate supernatant, serum, or cell supernatant were analyzed for cytokine and chemokine levels (TNF-α, IL-10, CCL2) using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China).

Cell viability quantification

Cell viability of BEAS-2B cells was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit (CCK)−8 (BS350A, Biosharp, Anhui, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

Transwell

5 × 105 BEAS-2B cells were seeded in the lower chamber of transwell and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Subsequently, 3 × 105 THP-1 cells were added to the upper chamber and co-cultured for an additional 12 h under the same conditions. Following incubation, cells within the Transwell chamber (3422, Corning, NY, USA) were fixed, stained, and counted under a high-power microscope.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (15596026CN, Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Q131-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and mRNA expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Western blot

Total protein from BEAS-2B cells was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1008, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were isolated using a kit (P0027, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Western blot details were performed as previously described [34]. The primary antibodies employed included GAPDH (A19056, 1:10000, Abclonal), Lamin B1(A11495, 1:1000, Abclonal), STAT1 (A19563, 1:1000, Abclonal), phospho-STAT1 (AP0054, 1:1000, Abclonal), STAT3 (A22434, 1:1000, Abclonal), phospho-STAT3 (AP0715, 1:1000, Abclonal), AKT (T40067, 1:1000, Abmart), phospho-AKT (T56569, 1:1000, Abmart), PI3K (T40115, 1:1000, Abmart), phospho-PI3K (TA3241, 1:1000, Abmart), Glucocorticoid Receptor (A19583, 1:1000, Abclonal).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

BEAS-2B cells (2 × 106) were washed with PBS and lysed using immunoprecipitation lysis buffer (P0013D, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against STAT3 and GR. 40 µL protein A/G agarose (P2055, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was then added to the lysates and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. After washing five times with immunoprecipitation lysis buffer, the samples were analyzed using antibodies against STAT3 or GR.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues or BEAS-2B cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, CA, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed, and RNA sequencing was carried out on the Genedovo platform. All data analysis and visualizations were conducted using R version 4.2.1. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a significance threshold of P < 0.05, an absolute log2 fold change (logFC) ≥ 1. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2(version 3.4.4). To explore the functional roles of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler (version 4.4.4). Heatmap visualizations were generated using ComplexHeatmap (version 2.13.1). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.4.4), with gene sets obtained from the msigdbr package. The reference gene set database used was MSigDB Collections (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Enrichment analysis results were visualized using ggplot2 (version 3.4.4), igraph (version 1.4.1), and ggraph (version 2.1.0) packages.

Analysis of transcription factor binding sites

The 2 kb upstream sequence of the human CCL2 gene (Entrez Gene ID: 6347) was analyzed to identify potential transcription factor binding sites. Binding site prediction was performed using the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/). The sequences of the predicted binding sites were further validated by browsing transcription factors in the Jaspar database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) and comparing them to the predicted sites within the 2 kb upstream region. To further investigate the binding patterns between the predicted transcription factors and the CCL2 gene, we utilized the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) to simulate and scoring. The results were visualized using PyMOL to provide a detailed structural representation.

Statistical analysis

Spectrum flow cytometry was performed using SpectroFlo for on-board testing, FlowJo 10.8.1 for data analysis, GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 for data plotting and statistical analysis. Student’s t-tests were used to compare normally distributed data between two groups, while Mann-Whitney tests were used for non-normally distributed data, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multi-group analysis. Chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests were employed for categorical data analysis. Statistical values are reported as mean ± standard error. All statistical tests were two-sided, and significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Poor outcomes, including increased bacterial burden, lung injury, and mortality, are relevant to depletion of monocytes in ICH -associated severe pneumonia

In our retrospective analysis of blood cell counts (Fig. 1A) from ICH patients with pneumonia, collected on days 1 and 7 after admission, the ICH pneumonia group exhibited a significant reduction in monocyte counts compared to the Non-ICH group, alongside a substantially higher mortality rate (59.3% vs. 28%) (Fig. 1B and C). To elucidate the role of immune alterations in ICH with severe pneumonia, a model was established by intratracheally infusing pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) into MRL/lpr mice (MP group) (Fig. 1A). The MRL/lpr mouse model, characterized by spontaneous autoimmune disease and immune dysregulation akin to clinical lupus, is susceptible to secondary infections under immunosuppression [35]. Dexamethasone pre-treatment (MDP group) increased mortality in MRL/lpr mice infected with PA compared to the MP group, as determined by survival analysis (Fig. 1D). H&E staining of lung tissue revealed more severe lung injury in the MDP group compared to the MP group, as evidenced by a significantly higher lung injury score at 12 h post-infection (Fig. 1E). Immunofluorescence staining of lung tissue from MP and MDP groups at 6 and 12 h post-infection revealed a significantly higher proportion of PA positive areas in the MDP group compared to the MP group (Fig. 1F). Consistently, bacterial load analysis demonstrated a higher bacterial burden in the MDP group compared to the MP group at both 6 and 12 h post-infection (Fig. 1G). The MDP group demonstrated increased lung wet/dry weight ratio and elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-10, indicating greater inflammation and fluid accumulation compared to the MP group (Fig. 1H and I). Findings from the ICH model of severe pneumonia were characterized by a significantly increased inflammatory response, lung injury, bacterial burden, and mortality. Notably, these results also suggested that monocyte depletion may be associated with the observed poor outcomes, indicating a complex interplay of pathogenic processes that drives a severe disease course associated with high mortality.

Immune cell dynamics in ICH-associated severe pneumonia

Further analysis of innate immune cell subsets in peripheral blood and lung tissue revealed several key changes in response to infection. Prior dexamethasone treatment in the MDP group resulted in a marked increase in both Ly6C+ monocyte and Ly6G+ neutrophil precents in peripheral blood and lung tissue before infection, relative to the MP group. However, these differences were less pronounced after infection (Fig. 2A and B). Both the proportion and absolute count of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes in peripheral blood and lung tissue were significantly decreased, particularly at 12 h post-infection in the MDP group (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, both groups exhibited a gradual decrease in alveolar macrophages (AMs) proportions in the lung, while interstitial macrophages (IMs) proportions and numbers increased. This increase in IMs was more pronounced in the MDP group at 12 h post-infection (Fig. 2D). While the proportion of DCs in the MDP group was elevated prior to infection, both the proportion and number of DCs were decreased in this group compared to the MP group at 6 h post-infection (Supplementary Fig. 2 C). In addition, immunofluorescence co-localization analysis of Ly6C and CCR2 showed a significant decline in their colocalization in lung tissue from MDP mice compared to MP mice after infection (Fig. 2E). Figure 2F summarized the observed immune cell alterations in peripheral blood and lung tissue. These results highlight the importance of monocyte dynamics in the pathogenesis and outcome of severe pneumonia in ICH patients.

Downregulation of classical monocytes is implicated in the pathophysiology of ICH -associated severe pneumonia

Building upon the findings from our ICH model of severe pneumonia, we further prospectively analyzed peripheral blood immune cell subsets in ICH patients with severe pneumonia. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were prospectively collected from both healthy volunteers and ICH patients with severe pneumonia on days 1 and 7 after admission (Fig. 3A). Spectral flow cytometric and tSNE analyses identified major immune cell populations, including monocytes, T cells, B cells, NK cells, and DCs (Fig. 3B to D and Supplementary Fig. 3). A key finding was the altered monocyte proportions in ICH patients: while overall monocyte proportions were elevated compared to healthy volunteers, patients in the deceased group exhibited significantly lower monocyte proportions compared to the survivors group on both days 1 and 7. Further tSNE analysis revealed that the proportion of each monocyte subset increased in both survivors and deceased groups following infection relative to healthy controls. However, the ICH severe pneumonia deceased group exhibited a downward trend of monocyte distribution compared to the survivors group on both days 1 and 7 (Fig. 3E). This difference was particularly pronounced within the classical monocyte subset, with significantly lower percentages observed in the deceased group compared to the survivors group, especially on day 1 (Fig. 3F and G). Consistent findings in both the animal model and human cohort revealed that reduced levels of classical monocytes were associated with poor outcomes.

Targeting the CCL2-CCR2 axis with rCCL2 ameliorates lung injury and improves survival in ICH -associated severe pneumonia

Building upon our finding that monocyte depletion played a critical role in ICH-associated severe pneumonia, we next investigated the mechanisms regulating monocyte recruitment. Given the importance of the CCL2-CCR2 axis in monocyte trafficking [36, 37], we hypothesized that its dysregulation contributes to the observed monocyte depletion and disease severity. To test this, we examined CCR2 expression on monocytes and CCL2 levels. Flow cytometric analysis revealed significantly decreased CCR2 expression on monocytes from the MDP group compared to the MP group, particularly in peripheral blood (Fig. 4A). Paralleling this, while serum CCL2 was upregulated in the MDP group, CCL2 expression in lung tissue showed a downward trend (Fig. 4B). These findings suggested impaired CCL2-CCR2 mediated monocyte recruitment. To explore the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis, we administered recombinant CCL2 (rCCL2) to MDP mice (Fig. 4C). MRL/lpr mice with PA infection received intratracheal rCCL2 at different doses (10, 20, and 40 µg/mouse). Survival analysis demonstrated a clear dose-dependent response: the 10 µg dose significantly prolonged survival, while higher doses showed reduced efficacy or potential adverse effects (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). Based on these findings, we selected the 10 µg/mouse dose for all subsequent experiments, which consistently showed improved survival (Fig. 4D). H&E staining revealed a significant reduction in lung injury score in the MDP + rCCL2 group (Fig. 4E). The MDP + rCCL2 group also had fewer bacteria in their lungs, as shown by both a decrease in the number of infected areas and a lower overall bacterial count (Fig. 4F and G). In addition, the MDP + rCCL2 group showed a lower lung wet/dry weight ratio and reduced TNF-α expression (Fig. 4H and I), indicating decreased inflammation and fluid accumulation. Collectively, these results revealed that exogenous rCCL2 mitigated key features of ICH-associated severe pneumonia, including lung injury and bacterial burden, ultimately improving survival.

CCL2-driven recruitment of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes reduce severity in ICH -associated severe pneumonia

Following administration of exogenous rCCL2 in the MDP group, we observed changes in immune cell populations using flow cytometry. We found a significant decrease in Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes in the peripheral blood, and conversely, a significant increase in Ly6C+ monocytes, Ly6G+ neutrophils and Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes within the lung tissue of the MDP + rCCL2 group (Fig. 5A to C). In contrast, exogenous rCCL2 treatment did not significantly affect lung macrophage (AMs, IMs) and DCs populations (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 4B). Supporting the notion of enhanced monocyte recruitment, immunofluorescence co-localization analysis confirmed significantly higher expression of both Ly6C and CCR2 in monocytes within the lung tissue from the MDP + rCCL2 group compared to the MDP group (Fig. 5E). These changes are illustrated in Fig. 5F, depicting immune cell dynamics in MDP mice after rCCL2 administration. Taken together, these data indicate that while rCCL2 modulates the migration of various immune cell populations, the specific recruitment and activation of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes to the lung is critical for reducing lung injury.

Glucocorticoids suppress chemokine activity, CCL2 expression, and monocyte migration in PA-LPS induced lung epithelial cell injury

In ICH patients, glucocorticoids like dexamethasone are frequently used for their anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects. However, a full understanding of their impact on chemokine production during severe respiratory infection is lacking. Given this clinical relevance, we utilized an in vitro model of lung epithelial cells treated with dexamethasone (Dex) and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide from pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA-LPS) to mimic the ICH infection state (Fig. 6A). Bulk RNA sequencing revealed a significant downregulation of chemokine and chemokine receptor expression in lung epithelial cells treated with Dex + PA-LPS compared to the PA-LPS group (Fig. 6B and C), with CCL2 exhibiting a particularly pronounced decrease (Fig. 6B and D). Analysis of CCL2 expression at both RNA and protein levels revealed a downregulation following dexamethasone treatment, an upregulation following PA-LPS treatment, and a subsequent downregulation after combined Dex + PA-LPS treatment (Fig. 6E and F), indicating an antagonistic effect of dexamethasone on the PA-LPS-induced upregulation of CCL2. CCK8 assays performed at 6, 12, and 24 h following treatment with BEAS-2B cells revealed a significant decrease in cell viability in the Dex + PA-LPS group compared to the Dex and PA-LPS groups at 24 h (Fig. 6G). To further investigate the impact of glucocorticoid treatment on monocyte migration, BEAS-2B and THP-1 cells were co-cultured in a Transwell system. After 24 h of co-culture, the Dex + PA-LPS group exhibited a significant reduction in THP-1 cell chemotaxis compared to the PA-LPS group (Fig. 6H). These results showed that glucocorticoids treatment suppressed chemokine activity, CCL2 expression, and monocyte migration, while reducing cell viability in a PA-LPS-induced lung epithelial cell injury model.

CCL2 expression in lung epithelial cells through a TLR4-dependent STAT signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the modulation of CCL2 production in lung epithelial cells treated with glucocorticoids, we performed KEGG differential gene analysis. Our findings suggested that CCL2 played a crucial role in several key signaling pathways, including PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, JAK-STAT, and toll-like receptor pathways (Fig. 7B and C). Putative transcription factor binding site analysis suggested that STAT (STAT1, STAT3), FOXA, and ELF factors are potential regulators of CCL2 expression (Fig. 7D and E). Furthermore, GESA revealed a downregulation of phosphorylation of STAT protein in the Dex + PA-LPS group (Fig. 7F). The structural details of the STAT1/STAT3/CCL2 transcription factor complex were presented in Fig. 7G. Furthermore, RNA expression analysis of lung epithelial cells revealed a significant upregulation of TLR4 expression in the Dex + PA-LPS group compared to the PA-LPS group, while the expression of MyD88 and NF-κB (P50, P52, P65, RelB) remained unchanged (Fig. 7H). TLR4 signaling proceeded through two main pathways: the MyD88-dependent pathway and the MyD88-independent pathway [11], our data implied that the observed TLR4 upregulation may not be mediated by the classical TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB pathway, but rather by a non-canonical pathway (Fig. 7A).

Glucocorticoids inhibit lung epithelial cell CCL2 expression by disrupting the PA-LPS/TLR4/PI3K/AKT/STAT3 pathway through GR/STAT3 interaction

The glucocorticoid receptor (GR) is a transcription factor, and dexamethasone acts as a ligand that binds to GR to regulate the expression of downstream genes [38]. Figure 8A presented a proposed signaling pathway that may explain the observed downregulation of CCL2 expression in lung epithelial cells after Dex + PA-LPS treatment. Although dexamethasone activates GR signaling, its function can be altered or dysregulated in complex ways during severe inflammatory conditions. For example, prior studies of severe infections, such as septic shock, have demonstrated a functional decline in GR, including decreased nuclear translocation, a phenomenon known as glucocorticoid resistance [39–41]. Furthermore, even when GR is active, it can participate in the formation of GR/STAT3 transcriptional inhibition complexes, which can further suppress downstream gene expression [42]. Therefore, we examined the specific effects of Dex + PA-LPS on GR-mediated regulation and CCL2 expression in our in vitro model. Western blot analysis revealed that Dex + PA-LPS treatment increased the protein levels of PI3K, phosphorylated PI3K, AKT, and phosphorylated AKT, indicating activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 8B). Concurrently, combined dexamethasone and PA-LPS treatment modulated STAT signaling: phosphorylated STAT1 levels increased, while phosphorylated STAT3 levels significantly decreased. Subcellular fractionation revealed a cytoplasmic shift of STAT1 in the Dex + PA-LPS group, with increased cytoplasmic levels and decreased nuclear levels. Cytoplasmic STAT3 levels remained unchanged, but nuclear STAT3 levels were significantly decreased, suggesting a specific effect on STAT3 nuclear localization (Fig. 8C and D). While Dex + PA-LPS treatment did not significantly alter total GR levels in lung epithelial cells, subcellular fractionation showed a similar cytoplasmic shift of GR in the Dex + PA-LPS group, with increased cytoplasmic levels and decreased nuclear levels (Fig. 8E). To further explore the relationship between GR and STAT3, we performed co-immunoprecipitation and confirmed an interaction between GR and STAT3 proteins. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments demonstrated a unidirectional interaction between GR and STAT3 in lung epithelial cells, with GR binding to STAT3 but not vice versa. Dex + PA-LPS treatment further strengthened this interaction, indicating that glucocorticoids may modulate downstream CCL2 expression by altering the GR/STAT3 interaction (Fig. 8E).

Discussion

Severe pneumonia in ICH patients is associated with significantly higher mortality, underscoring the critical role of complex immune dysfunction in disease progression. Innate host defense in the lung relies heavily on mononuclear phagocytes, which are recruited by chemokine CCL2. Studies have shown that overexpression of CCL2 in mice increased lung mononuclear phagocyte accumulation, improving pneumococcal clearance and survival [43, 44]. These results highlight the importance of CCL2-dependent mononuclear phagocyte mobilization in lung innate immunity and suggest a central role for the CCL2-CCR2 axis in regulating pulmonary defense and fibrotic responses to bacterial infection [45]. In this study, we discovered a significant reduction of classical monocytes in ICH patients with severe pneumonia. Further investigation revealed a dual impairment of the CCL2-CCR2 axis: (1) diminished Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes migration and (2) reduced glucocorticoid-suppressed CCL2 in lung epithelial cells. Mechanistically, this suppression was driven by a novel GR-STAT3 interaction disrupting key signaling pathways, inhibiting STAT3 nuclear translocation and CCL2 transcription, ultimately leading to deficits in bacterial clearance and exacerbation of lung injury. These findings highlight the critical role of GR/STAT3-mediated suppression of the CCL2-CCR2 axis in compromising monocyte function during severe pneumonia in ICH patients, underscoring the potential of this pathway as a therapeutic target (Fig. 9).

Schematic summarizing the underlying mechanism by which glucocorticoids regulate the CCL2-CCR2 axis and induce monocyte dysfunction in severe pneumonia among ICH. Severe pneumonia in ICH results in reduced classical monocyte subsets, such as Ly6C+CCR2+, associated with a poor prognosis. The glucocorticoid-mediated GR/STAT3 interaction downregulates CCL2 expression through PA-LPS/TLR4/PI3K/AKT/STAT pathway interference, impairing monocyte migration and bacterial clearance

The contribution of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes to infection outcomes is multifaceted and context-dependent, as demonstrated by various studies [46–50]. While these monocytes can promote bacterial clearance in certain infections, such as tuberculosis and klebsiella pneumonia, they can also contribute to exacerbated lung injury and mortality in virus-induced pneumonia. Moreover, in secondary bacterial infections, CCR2 deficiency can alter the balance of immune cell populations, influencing bacterial load and inflammation. These seemingly contradictory functions highlight the complexity of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocyte biology and the importance of understanding their specific role in each disease context. This complex background underscores the necessity to investigate the specific function of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes in the context of severe pneumonia in ICH. Our study identified a decrease in Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes in both the peripheral blood and lungs of ICH with severe pneumonia induced by PA, accompanied by increased bacterial load, suggesting a role for these monocytes in bacterial clearance. Furthermore, potentially driven by reduced recruitment of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes, we observed a shift in lung macrophage populations, characterized by increased IMs and decreased AMs, consistent with prior findings [51]. This transition may contribute to defective clearance of bacteria from the lung, suggesting a mechanism by which impaired monocyte recruitment exacerbates pneumonia in ICH patients.

Given the established dependency of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocyte recruitment on the chemokine CCL2, the observed decrease in these monocytes led us to investigate CCL2 levels, where we found a significant reduction in the lung. CCL2 is a chemokine that plays a multifaceted role in both disease and physiological processes. Beyond its well-established role in recruiting immune cells, such as monocytes and macrophages to inflammatory sites, CCL2 exerts diverse effects on cell behavior, including chemotaxis, adhesion, polarization, effector molecule secretion, autophagy, and killing [37, 52]. Consistent with our findings of reduced lung CCL2, we found that exogenous rCCL2 supplementation significantly ameliorated lung injury in ICH severe pneumonia, likely by restoring the recruitment of Ly6C+CCR2+ monocytes to the lung, potentially enhancing their ability to clear bacteria and reduce inflammation. This suggests that restoring CCL2 levels may serve as a viable therapeutic strategy for ICH patients suffering from severe pneumonia.

The observed therapeutic benefit of exogenous rCCL2 in our model highlights the potential of targeting the CCL2-CCR2 axis for treating severe pneumonia in ICH patients. However, understanding the regulatory mechanisms governing CCL2 expression in the lung is crucial for translating these findings into effective clinical strategies. Therefore, to identify upstream regulators of CCL2, we examined its expression under inflammatory conditions and in the presence of glucocorticoids. Investigation into the upstream regulatory mechanisms of CCL2 revealed that glucocorticoids significantly downregulated CCL2 expression in lung epithelial cells stimulated by PA-LPS. Epithelial injury plays a key role in the pathogenesis of fibroproliferative lung disorders, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [53]. These injured lung epithelial cells are a major source of CCL2, a significant proinflammatory and profibrotic mediator implicated in the development of these diseases. Previous study have established that lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major component of bacterial cell walls, triggered inflammatory responses by binding to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [54]. The PI3K-AKT pathway, a critical intracellular signaling pathway, plays a crucial role in mediating cellular responses to extracellular stimuli, promoting metabolism, proliferation, cell survival, growth, and angiogenesis [55, 56]. Signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins function as cytoplasmic signal transducers and nuclear transcription activators, regulating diverse processes including innate and adaptive immunity, hematopoiesis, growth, and development [57]. STAT1 is crucial for antiviral immunity, transducing interferon signals in the nucleus [58]. Activated by various cytokines and growth factors, STAT3 regulates a broad range of genes, including those encoding IL-10, IL-6, IFN-γ, and CCL2 [59, 60]. It has been previously demonstrated that the activation of STAT3 is a pivotal event in the progression of melanoma driven by the LPS- TLR4 signaling pathway [61].

To further elucidate how glucocorticoids suppress CCL2 expression, we next investigated their impact on the PA-LPS- induced signaling pathway, which is known to regulate CCL2 production. Our findings indicated that glucocorticoids exerted a complex regulatory effect on PA-LPS- stimulated lung epithelial cells. Specifically, glucocorticoids upregulated PI3K, AKT, and STAT1 protein levels while significantly downregulating STAT3. Concurrently, nuclear STAT1, STAT3, and GR levels decreased, while cytoplasmic STAT1 significantly increased. Importantly, a unidirectional protein-protein interaction was observed between GR and STAT3. These findings support the established anti-inflammatory role of glucocorticoids, acting through inhibition of the TLR4/PI3K/AKT/STAT signaling pathway and specifically targeting STAT3. The observed glucocorticoid-mediated inhibition of STAT3, including reduced level of phosphorylated STAT3 and decreased nuclear STAT3, likely contributes to the downregulation of CCL2. This is consistent with the known role of STAT3 as a key driver of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [62, 63]. The enhanced GR-STAT3 interaction suggests a mechanism for this inhibition, potentially involving altered STAT3 activity or subcellular localization [64, 65]. Although STAT1 phosphorylation tends to indicate activation, its reduced nuclear translocation and accumulation in the cytoplasm may indicate a functional shift, potentially towards non-transcriptional roles [66, 67], thereby limiting its pro-inflammatory activity. Further investigation, including analysis of both the mechanisms responsible for STAT1 cytoplasmic retention and its potential non-transcriptional roles in the cytoplasm, is needed to clarify its role in this context. The effects of glucocorticoids on STAT1 and STAT3 indicate a complex immunomodulatory function that extends beyond simple suppression and includes the potential for activation.

While our study provides crucial insights into the interplay between CCL2-CCR2 axis, monocytes, and glucocorticoids in severe pneumonia among ICH patients, and identifies a potential therapeutic target, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that warrant further investigation. Firstly, the clinical cohort was relatively small, and the analysis was limited to peripheral blood samples; thus, future multi-center studies with larger cohorts that include bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and lung tissue are essential. This would not only provide a more robust validation of our findings, but also offer a more comprehensive understanding of the localized immune response within the lung. Secondly, while we have confirmed an interaction between GR and STAT3 in the regulation of CCL2 expression using co-immunoprecipitation, further studies are needed to explore the exact mechanisms of this interaction. This should include investigations into whether the GR-STAT3 interaction alters the phosphorylation status of STAT3, if it changes the stability of the STAT3 protein, and to analyze the impact on their combined transcriptional activity on CCL2. These studies will be important for identifying molecular targets for therapeutic intervention. Furthermore, future studies will need to analyze a wider variety of inflammatory mediators, using multiplex analysis methods to understand the complex signaling networks that drive inflammation. And finally, it is crucial to analyze the interplay between STAT1 and STAT3 to understand glucocorticoid function more thoroughly, as the often antagonistic balance is a key determinant of cell fate in inflammation and survival [68, 69]. Addressing these limitations and taking these future directions will strengthen the impact and validity of our findings and provide vital insights into the pathogenesis and treatment of severe pneumonia in ICH patients.

In summary, our study identifies a pathologically relevant GR/STAT3-mediated suppression of the CCL2-CCR2 axis compromising monocyte function during severe pneumonia in ICH patients. This GR/STAT3 activation impairs monocyte recruitment to the lungs, resulting in ineffective bacterial clearance and worsened disease severity. Targeting the GR/STAT3-CCL2/CCR2 pathway might be a promising treatment strategy for restoring monocyte function and improving outcomes in this vulnerable population.
