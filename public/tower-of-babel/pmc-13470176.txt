Contained rupture of a thoracic aortic aneurysm at the diaphragmatic hiatus: A case report
Authors: Kyler Shin, Lauren Pupa, Molly Lieux Furrow, Milan Maheshwari, Craig Kym, Mukul Maheshwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContained rupture is a rare complication of aortic aneurysms and occurs less frequently in the thoracic aorta compared to the abdominal aorta. Rapid enlargement of a contained rupture is a high risk factor for free rupture. Radiography and cross-sectional imaging play a vital role in the prompt diagnosis of contained rupture, which is significant given its treatment may differ from that of other aortic complications. We describe a case of a 64-year-old man with multiple previous infections of the spine and bloodstream who underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis to evaluate acute hypoxic respiratory failure, hematuria, and abdominal pain. He was found to have an enlarged, contained rupture of a thoracic aortic aneurysm at the diaphragmatic hiatus. Surgical repair could not be performed due to chronic infection and underlying comorbidities.Keywords: Thoracic aortic aneurysm, Contained rupture, Acute aortic syndrome

Introduction

In aortic aneurysms, weakening of all 3 layers of the vessel wall results in progressive dilation of the vessel lumen. Aortic rupture is a feared, life-threatening complication of aortic aneurysms. Findings suggestive of impending rupture include an enlarging aneurysm size, smaller thrombus to lumen ratio, and intramural hemorrhage. Rarely, these findings may progress to aortic rupture where focal discontinuity of the vessel wall results in adjacent hemorrhage. In contained rupture, the adjacent hemorrhage is confined by surrounding structures like the spine or retroperitoneal fat which serve to tamponade the acute hemorrhage. Thus, patients with contained rupture are often hemodynamically stable with atypical symptoms [[1], [2], [3]]. Contrary to this, in free rupture, active hemorrhage accumulates in the adjacent tissue without tamponade, often leading to massive internal hemorrhage in death. Identification of contained ruptures is imperative due to the risk of progression to freerupture, which is the 13th leading cause of death in the United States [4].

While a contained rupture is present in approximately 1.5%-3% of abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA) [3], it is rarely associated with thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAA) [5]. For context, the worldwide prevalence of AAA is up to 18.5% among men and 4.2% among women [6], whereas TAA has a prevalence of up to 0.34% [7]. Additionally, only 10% of TAAs occur in the thoracoabdominal aorta, while 40% occur in the descending thoracic aorta, 10% in the aortic arch, and 60% in the aortic root or ascending aorta [8].

Here, we present a rare case of a contained rupture of a TAA at the diaphragmatic hiatus, which had enlarged 4 months after its initial discovery. We also describe the imaging findings and treatment strategies related to the intact, impending rupture, contained rupture, and free rupture subtypes of TAA.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old man with past medical history significant for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, T10-T12 transverse myelitis with residual left-sided weakness, intravenous drug use (heroin) with prolonged admission for methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteremia complicated by T5-T7 epidural abscess with discitis, osteomyelitis, and cord compression presented to the emergency department with a dislodged gastrostomy tube, hematuria, and abdominal pain. His previous hospital course was notable for recurrent hypoxic respiratory failure related to aspiration and neuromuscular weakness, which required recurrent airway-clearing bronchoscopies and subsequent tracheostomy and gastrostomy placements.

The patient was admitted to the medical intensive care unit for management of acute hypoxic respiratory failure with thick mucoid secretions from his tracheostomy, which was thought to be due to recurrent aspiration and mucoid impaction. Outpatient anticoagulation was held while awaiting hematuria workup. Chest radiograph (Fig. 1) showed a nodular opacity herniating through the medial right hemidiaphragm associated with mass effect on the heart. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis (Fig. 2) revealed a 9.6 × 7.6 cm hyperattenuating periaortic collection at the diaphragmatic hiatus. Four months prior, the thoracic aortic diameter measured up to 3.4 cm at the diaphragmatic hiatus and the hyperattenuating periaortic collection was much smaller, measuring 1.4 × 2.7 cm (Fig. 3). Together, these findings represented a rapidly enlarged contained rupture of a TAA for which vascular surgery was consulted. Unfortunately, the patient was a poor candidate for surgical repair given chronic infection (likely a superinfected aneurysm) and underlying comorbidities. A family meeting was held to discuss goals of care, and the patient was transitioned to comfort care measures until he passed away 2 weeks after initial presentation.

This was associated with leftward deviation of the heart, which was likely accentuated by the patient’s leftward rotation, as evidenced by the markedly displaced trachea (blue arrow).

The aneurysm arose along the posterior aspect of the aorta (yellow arrows) at the diaphragmatic hiatus. Note the characteristic draped aorta sign (blue arrows in A) in which the contained rupture was draped over the adjacent T12 vertebral body. In this case, the T12 vertebral body was a buffer against the development of a free rupture. The absence of erosion in the T12 vertebral body does not necessarily preclude the diagnosis of contained rupture. Additionally, there was a left pleural effusion (purple arrow in B) with an attenuation of <20 Hounsfield units, consistent with simple fluid. The absence of a hemothorax favored the diagnosis of a contained rupture over a free rupture.

The aorta (yellow arrow) measured up to 3.4 cm in diameter at the diaphragmatic hiatus. Note the calcifications (green arrow in A and C) in the wall of the contained rupture, which are seen more commonly with atherosclerosis than with an infected aneurysm.

Discussion

An aneurysm is defined as arterial dilatation of more than 50% of its diameter. Approximately 95% of patients with TAA are asymptomatic. Nevertheless, they are at risk for life-threatening complications such as rupture or aortic dissection. Acute aortic syndromes encompass a spectrum of disorders in which there is compromised aortic integrity. These disorders include impending rupture, contained rupture, and free rupture. CT is the first-line imaging modality for the workup of acute aortic syndromes with a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 98%, respectively [3].

Imaging of impending rupture

Contrast-enhanced CT findings suggestive of impending rupture include enlarging aneurysm size (with smaller thrombus to lumen ratio), contrast extravasation into thrombus, and lumen irregularity [2,4]. On unenhanced CT, intramural hematomas appear as a high-attenuating crescent sign in the wall of an aortic aneurysm or mural thrombus [2,4]. Presence of hemorrhage within the wall weakens the vessel and predisposes it to rupture [2,4]. In a retrospective study by Mehard et al. [9], the high-attenuating crescent sign was shown to have a sensitivity of 77% and specificity of 93%. In a different study by Siegel et al. [10], crescents of increased attenuation were present in 21% of patients with ruptured aneurysms, but none were seen in patients with intact aneurysms. The criteria for high-attenuating crescents included a well-defined entity of higher attenuation than the psoas muscle on enhanced CT or of higher attenuation than the patent lumen on unenhanced CT [10]. Of note, the high-attenuating crescent sign does not always indicate an impending rupture. In a 2022 retrospective analysis, Stoecker et al. [11] argues that the high-attenuating crescent sign is nonspecific in the absence of clinical or radiologic signs of rupture.

Imaging of contained rupture

If the hemorrhage fully penetrates the aneurysm wall, the aneurysm is said to have ruptured. Ruptured aneurysms can be either contained or free. According to Jones et al. [12], contained ruptures should meet the following criteria: known aortic aneurysm, previous pain symptoms that may have resolved, stable hemodynamic status, surrounding hemorrhage on CT scans, and pathologic confirmation of an organized hematoma. As discussed above, the high-attenuating crescent sign on noncontrast-enhanced CT can be seen with various complications of aortic aneurysms. However, a unique CT finding indicative of aortic wall insufficiency and contained rupture is the draped aorta sign. This occurs when the posterior aspect of the aorta wraps around and appears to drape over the adjacent vertebral body [4]. The draped aorta sign can be seen with contained ruptures even in the absence of retroperitoneal hemorrhage [4]. Osseous erosion may occur along the anterior aspect of the vertebral body due to chronic pulsatile trauma from the aneurysm, but this finding is not always seen with contained ruptures [4,13]. In our case, the patient’s contained rupture was neither associated with retroperitoneal hemorrhage at the diaphragmatic hiatus nor erosion of the T12 vertebral body (Fig. 2).

Imaging of free rupture

In contrast to a contained rupture, a free rupture of an aortic aneurysm typically leads to rapid internal bleeding and death [14]. This is because the structures adjacent to the aortic aneurysm are unable to seal off the rupture, allowing it to spread quickly. Chest radiography is considered the first-line screening modality for a ruptured thoracic aorta in both the traumatic and atraumatic settings [15]. Key diagnostic signs include mediastinal widening, a left pleural effusion, and rightward tracheal deviation [15]. If the clinical suspicion for aortic rupture remains high, multiphase enhanced and unenhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) is the best next step in management. Rupture of the thoracoabdominal aorta most commonly involves the posterolateral aorta with hemorrhage into the retroperitoneum. Our patient’s contained rupture originated from the right posterolateral aspect of the thoracic aorta (Figs. 2 and 3), which led to leftward (instead of rightward) tracheal deviation on chest radiography (Fig. 1). Less commonly, intraperitoneal rupture may occur from the anterior or anterolateral aspect of the aneurysm [4]. Direct signs of a free rupture of a TAA on CTA include the presence of discontinuity in the aortic wall or mural calcification, an intimal flap, abnormal aortic contour, change in aortic diameter, pseudoaneurysm, and contrast agent extravasation [4,15]. An important indirect sign of free rupture is mediastinal hematoma, including abnormalities in periaortic soft tissue density [15]. Additionally, the presence of a left hemothorax is highly suggestive of free rupture [16]. Our patient was found to have a simple left pleural effusion (Fig. 2), which favors the diagnosis of contained rupture over free rupture.

Risk factors for rupture

The risk of a ruptured aortic aneurysm is directly proportional to the maximum cross-sectional diameter of the aneurysm. Aneurysms less than 4 cm in diameter have a 6-year cumulative incidence of rupture of 1% [4]; our patient’s aneurysm measured up to 3.4 cm in diameter 4 months before his most recent hospital visit. The risk of rupture for aneurysms between 4 and 5 cm is estimated to be 1%-3% per year, while the risk becomes 6%-11% per year for aneurysms between 5 and 7 cm [4]. If an aneurysm is greater than 7 cm, it carries a 20% risk of rupture per year [4]. The expansion rate of an aneurysm is also widely recognized as an important determinant of the risk of rupture. An aortic aneurysm that expands at least 0.5 cm over 6 months of follow-up is considered to be at high risk for rupture [17]. In our case, the patient’s contained rupture expanded from a maximum dimension of 2.7-9.6 cm over the course of 4 months. This interval growth of nearly 7 cm in less than 6 months strongly suggested the patient’s aneurysm was at high risk for free rupture.

Treatment of intact and ruptured aneurysms

The 2022 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) have provided guidelines on the management of various aortic diseases, including aortic aneurysms [18]. Medical management of aortic aneurysms with antihypertensive, beta-blocker and statin therapies has been utilized with relative success. The goal of these therapies is to minimize risk of additional cardiovascular events (such as myocardial infarction and stroke) as well as prevent aneurysm growth and complication (such as dissection or rupture). Indications for prophylactic surgical repair of a descending TAA include size greater than 5.5 cm, rapid expansion, and symptomatic aneurysms [14]. Ideally, patients would undergo elective surgical repair prior to suffering a complication that requires consideration of urgent repair. Surgical options include open vs endovascular repair, with endovascular repair gaining popularity over the last decade in select patient populations [18].

For patients with intact TAA, etiology of the aneurysm is considered when deciding the preferred surgical approach. For patients with connective tissue and/or vascular disorders (i.e., Marfan syndrome, Loeys–Dietz, Ehlers–Danlos, and others), open repair is preferred due to patient anatomy and procedural skill required [18]. For patients with degenerative aneurysms, endovascular repair can be considered [18]. On the other hand, not all patients meet prophylactic surgical criteria for elective repair prior to a complication. For patients with a ruptured thoracic aortic aneurysm, open repair is typically recommended due to consideration of patient instability, procedural complexity and the need for customized endovascular grafts (which can take weeks to manufacture) [18]. In patients like our case, with a contained rupture of an aneurysm, there is no consensus guideline. If the patient is otherwise hemodynamically stable and has suitable anatomy, endovascular repair can be considered on a case-by-case basis.

Infectious aortitis and infected aneurysms (mycotic aneurysms)

The ACC/AHA guidelines also discuss treatment considerations for patients with “infectious aortitis,” terminology that has replaced the former “mycotic aneurysm” and is defined more broadly as any infection of the native aorta [18]. Infectious aortitis is an important consideration in patients with an aortic aneurysm and a history of infection. In our case, the patient’s history of MSSA bacteremia with infectious complications involving the T5-T7 and T10-T12 vertebral levels of the spine warranted inclusion of infectious aortitis in the differential diagnosis.

Early changes of aortitis preceding aneurysm formation include an irregular arterial wall, periaortic edema, a periaortic soft tissue mass, and periaortic gas [19]. On CT, periaortic edema can appear as fat stranding or a hypoattenuating concentric rim; on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), periaortic edema typically has low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images [19]. Concentric or eccentric periaortic inflammatory soft tissue can develop as an area of periaortic edema with homogeneous contrast enhancement on CT and MRI [19]. Occasionally, the inflammatory mass can develop necrosis, which has heterogeneous attenuation on CT and variable signal intensity on MRI; necrotic inflammatory masses may exhibit rim enhancement or poor enhancement after the administration of contrast material [19]. Periaortic mass or stranding is the most common imaging finding with infected aortic aneurysms and is found in 48% of cases [19]. On the other hand, periaortic gas is an uncommon feature [19].

When an infected aortic aneurysm develops, it commonly involves parts of the aorta that are not typically involved by atherosclerosis [19]. On CT, an infected aneurysm usually appears as a saccular, focal, contrast-enhancing dilatation [19]. Disrupted arterial wall calcification can occur adjacent to the infected aneurysm [19]. However, calcification with the infected aneurysm wall and thrombus within the infected aneurysm itself are uncommon [19].

In retrospect, our patient’s TAA was likely multifactorial in etiology given his infectious and noninfectious comorbidities like atherosclerosis. As a result, it can be challenging to determine the extent to which infection and atherosclerosis each contributed to the development of the patient’s TAA. The saccular appearance of the hyperattenuating fluid collection when the aneurysm was first discovered (Fig. 3) suggested an infectious etiology in the setting of recent vertebral infection. However, there was no periaortic edema or periaortic inflammatory mass that would have led to the formation of an infected aneurysm. Additionally, the saccular hyperattenuating fluid collection had calcifications along its wall (Fig. 3), a finding more closely associated with atherosclerosis than with infection. Furthermore, a contrast-enhanced CT performed 4.5 years before the patient’s initial diagnosis of TAA (Fig. 4) demonstrated atherosclerotic calcifications along the right posterolateral wall of the thoracoabdominal aorta, which later became the site of the contained rupture. Therefore, we suspect the patient’s underlying atherosclerosis was a greater contributor than infection toward the development and enlargement of the contained rupture of the TAA.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis performed 4.5 years before the initial diagnosis of TAA: soft tissue windowed axial plane demonstrated a normal caliber of the thoracoabdominal aorta (yellow arrows) at the level of the T12 vertebral body. Interestingly, the patient already had atherosclerotic calcifications in the right posterolateral aspect of the aorta (green arrow), which would later give rise to the contained rupture of a TAA.

Open surgical repair is the standard of treatment in patients with an infected aneurysm, according to the ACC/AHA guidelines [18]. However, in patients who are not candidates for open surgery, an endovascular “bridge procedure” can be considered as a last-resort stabilization effort until definitive open repair can be completed. This procedure comes with many risks, such as increased/persistent infections, higher long-term morbidity, and need for a second surgery (to complete device explantation/arterial reconstruction) [[9], [18]]. In our patient, this was likely not a serious consideration due to his numerous comorbidities and extensive infectious history.

Conclusion

Contained rupture of a thoracic aortic aneurysm is an uncommon but life-threatening entity. In contrast to free aortic rupture with associated hemodynamic instability, a contained rupture can present with subtle or nonspecific clinical findings. This means imaging is essential for timely diagnosis.

In our case, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated interval enlarging aneurysm size, a large hyperattenuating periaortic collection, and wrapping of the posterior aorta around the adjacent vertebral body (draped aorta sign), findings characteristic of a contained rupture. Although absent in our case, additional CT findings suggestive of an impending ruptured aneurysm include lumen irregularity and intramural hematoma (hyperattenuating crescent sign). Recognition of these findings is important, as patients may remain temporarily hemodynamically stable despite impending clinical deterioration.

Additionally, distinguishing the etiology of the TAA was an important management consideration in our case. The etiology was likely multifactorial with contributions from both atherosclerotic disease and prior bacteremia complicated by vertebral discitis/osteomyelitis. CT findings suggestive of an infected aneurysm include a saccular hyperattenuating periaortic collection, periaortic edema and/or a periaortic inflammatory mass; only the saccular periaortic collection was present in our case. Open surgical repair is preferred over endoscopic repair in patients with infected TAA, given the risk of complications (i.e., recurrent infections). Unfortunately, for patients like ours who are poor surgical candidates, treatment options are limited, leaving supportive care as the primary management strategy.

Ultimately, this case emphasizes the role radiologists play in detecting early signs of vascular instability to help guide management decisions. Urgent surgical consultation is critical as progression to hemodynamic instability can render even previous well-appearing patients unsuitable for life-saving operative intervention.
