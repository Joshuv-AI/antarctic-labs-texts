Bepirovirsen induces innate immune activation in the liver potentially through TLR8 signaling
Authors: Megan E Ermler, Jared L Delahaye, William Jordan, Usha Nivarthi, Anne DeHart, Kristen Jones, Amitabh Das, Nicholas Galwey, Bao Hoang, Rebecca L Terry, Jimmy Tarrant, Valeriia Sherina, Avijit Ray, Daren Austin, Jennifer M Singh, Dickens Theodore, Melanie Paff, Shihyun You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsBepirovirsen is an unconjugated antisense oligonucleotide in phase III trials for chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Post hoc analyses from a phase IIa study in participants with chronic HBV indicated immediate cytokine production following dosing with bepirovirsen, leading to the hypothesis that bepirovirsen may have innate immune stimulatory activity via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in hepatic non-parenchymal cells, alongside its known direct antiviral mechanism in hepatocytes.MethodsCytokine production was evaluated in phase IIa serum and plasma samples using Myriad RBM, Olink, or MSD panels. Preclinical studies used peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors (MSD, flow cytometry) and PRR-overexpressing HEK or THP-1 cells to assess innate immune activation by bepirovirsen. Cytokine protein levels were measured using Luminex or MSD assays in wild-type (WT) and human Toll-like receptor 8 (hTLR8) transgenic mice, while gene expression (reverse-transcription quantitative PCR, RNAscope) was assessed in vivo exclusively in hTLR8 transgenic mice from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center.ResultsMultiple pro-inflammatory cytokines involved in immune cell recruitment and activation were upregulated in bepirovirsen-treated participants. Similar findings were observed preclinically in PBMCs from healthy donors following in vitro exposure to bepirovirsen. Analysis of innate immune activation in PRR-overexpressing cells revealed a weak but statistically significant (p = 0.00085) induction only in the presence of human Toll-like receptor 8 (TLR8). In hTLR8 transgenic mice – but not wild-type mice – bepirovirsen treatment induced pro-inflammatory cytokines, similar to the TLR8 agonist selgantolimod. Kupffer cells and macrophages were identified as the primary responders to bepirovirsen in the livers of hTLR8 mice.ConclusionsClinical and preclinical data demonstrate that bepirovirsen elicits an innate immune response, evidenced by induction of inflammatory cytokines. This effect is mediated at least in part by TLR8 activation, complementing its established antiviral mechanism.Impact and implicationsThe aim of this study was to characterize additional mechanisms of action for bepirovirsen, including immune activation through pattern recognition receptors, based on the hypothesis that bepirovirsen may elicit an innate immune response in addition to its antiviral mechanism. Using phase IIa study samples we show that bepirovirsen induces IFN-γ, TNF, and other cytokines, while in vitro we demonstrate that bepirovirsen harbors innate immune stimulatory activity, potentially through human TLR8. Furthermore, we show that the innate immune activation induced by bepirovirsen is distinct from that of other antisense oligonucleotides. This study demonstrates that bepirovirsen can induce an innate immune response, likely through TLR8 agonism, in addition to its known antiviral mechanism; however, the clinical impact of this immunomodulatory effect remains unclear and requires further investigation.Keywords: Bepirovirsen, Antisense oligonucleotide, Innate immunity, Hepatitis B virus, HBV, Toll-like receptor 8, TLR8

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major global health problem, affecting approximately 254 million people worldwide.1 Since the chronicity of HBV infection is mediated by dysfunctional immune control due to prolonged exposure to viral antigens, it is thought that both control of HBV DNA/antigens and restoration of immunity against HBV are key to achieving functional cure (defined as sustained loss of HBsAg and unquantifiable HBV DNA at 24 weeks following treatment cessation).2,3 Bepirovirsen is a novel antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) in phase III clinical trials for the treatment of chronic HBV infection that targets HBV mRNAs for degradation. In a phase IIa study (NCT02981602), bepirovirsen demonstrated rapid and prolonged HBsAg reductions in participants with chronic HBV infection, either receiving or not receiving stable nucleos(t)ide analogue (NA) therapy.4 Transient increases in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were observed in association with HBsAg reduction and loss, possibly linked to immune clearance of infected hepatocytes.4

After subcutaneous injection, ASOs generally reach peak plasma concentrations within 3–4 h and rapidly transfer from blood to tissues, including the liver, spleen, and kidney.5 Bepirovirsen is unconjugated,6 in contrast to small-interfering RNAs currently in development, which are conjugated to a N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) for targeted delivery to hepatocytes.7 This unconjugated design may facilitate delivery to and uptake by a broader range of cells within the liver, including non-parenchymal cells, such as liver-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells) and endothelial cells.8 Bepirovirsen is a 20-nucleotide single-stranded oligonucleotide with phosphorothioate linkages and a 2′-O-methoxyethyl gapmer design (5-10-5, RNA-DNA-RNA).4 While hepatocytes are the site of HBV replication, non-parenchymal cells within the liver may play a crucial role in innate immune recognition of nucleic acid patterns via enriched expression of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs).9 Notably, bepirovirsen demonstrated superior efficacy to GSK3389404, its GalNAc-conjugate, in phase II clinical trials, suggesting a possible advantage in engaging a broader array of liver cells.4,10

In addition to its known antiviral mechanism, we hypothesize that bepirovirsen could activate PRRs that bind to single-stranded nucleic acid regions and contribute to the innate immune response. Here, we aim to characterize additional mechanisms of action of bepirovirsen, including immune activation through PRRs, using phase IIa study samples and preclinical models.

Materials and methods

Post hoc phase IIa study analyses

The phase IIa study design (NCT02981602) has been published previously.4 Thirty participants received bepirovirsen or placebo (3:1) administered on Days 1, 4, 8, 11, 15 and 22, with two cohorts: NA-naïve (bepirovirsen 150 mg, n = 6; bepirovirsen 300 mg, n = 12; placebo, n = 6) and on-NA (bepirovirsen 300 mg, n = 4; placebo, n = 2).

Plasma samples were collected from NA-naïve participants at 0.5 (baseline for plasma analysis), 4, 6 and 24 h post-dosing. Soluble plasma proteins from these samples were evaluated using the Olink 1536 Explore panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) from placebo and bepirovirsen 300 mg groups (Supplementary Methods 1.1). Plasma (placebo, 150 mg, and 300 mg treatment groups) was also analyzed for interferons (IFNs) by single molecule array (SIMOA) cytokine assay (Rules Based Medicine, RBM-a Q2 Solutions Company, Austin, TX) or U-PLEX (Meso Scale Discovery [MSD], Rockville, MD) (Supplementary Methods 1.2).

Longitudinal serum and plasma samples were collected from all participants (both NA-naïve and on-NA) on Days 1 (baseline for serum and plasma analysis), 2, 8, 15, 22, 23, 29, 36, 57, 85, 113 and 211. Longitudinal plasma samples were measured by Myriad RBM’s DiscoveryMAP v3.3 multiplexed immunoassay; the Quanterix ultra-sensitive SIMOA platform was used to measure IFN-α, IFN-β, and IFN-γ. Soluble serum proteins were evaluated using the Olink Target 96 inflammation panel from placebo and bepirovirsen 300 mg groups. Data analysis was performed using R (version 4.0.2). ALT analysis was conducted by PPD (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) using COBAS (Roche Diagnostics Operations, Inc., Indianapolis, IN) with the enzymatic method. The COBAS HBsAg II quant II (Roche) was used to quantify HBsAg (IU/ml).

Responders and non-responders to bepirovirsen were defined as >1.9 log10 IU/ml reduction and <0.2 log10 IU/ml reduction in HBsAg at Day 29, respectively. Pairwise Pearson correlations of all proteins to ALT and HBsAg were computed in responder participants. Analytes with >0.6 coefficient to HBsAg or ALT were filtered for and displayed.

All participants were required to give written informed consent prior to enrolment. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences, International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects, Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonization, and all applicable laws and regulations in participating countries. Independent Data Monitoring Committees reviewed unblinded study data.4

Preclinical models

Innate immune activation in human cells

Analyses of secreted cytokines from human PBMCs

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from seven healthy donors were used as a surrogate for liver immune cells as there were limitations in obtaining liver perfusates; culture and stimulation conditions are detailed in Supplementary Methods 1.3. Methods for MSD cytokine analysis are detailed in Section 4.1 and statistical evaluation is outlined in Supplementary Methods 1.4.

Cell and intracellular cytokine staining for flow cytometry analysis

PBMCs from three donors were cultured and stimulated as described in Supplementary Methods 1.3, before staining with an internally validated multicolor flow panel (25 colors). The panel was designed to identify the key immune subsets, activation markers (CD86, CD40, CD163), key cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-12p40, CXCL10) and cytolytic marker granzyme B (GZMB). Details on intracellular cytokine staining and flow cytometry are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.5.

Key immune subsets in the peripheral blood investigated included B cells (CD3-CD19+), CD4 (CD3+CD4+CD8-) and CD8 T cells (CD3+CD4-CD8+), mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells (CD3+Va7.2+CD161+CD56+), monocyte subsets (CD14++CD16-; CD14+CD16+; CD14dimCD16+ populations), natural killer (NK) subsets (CD16+CD56dim; CD16-CD56bright) and dendritic cell (DC) subsets (CD1c+CD11b+; CD1c+CD11b-). The frequencies of the immune subsets from each of the three donors (data not shown) and the percentages of key immune subsets producing cytokines and expressing the activation markers investigated were determined.

PRR in vitro screen

To elucidate which PRR could recognize and respond to bepirovirsen, overexpression reporter cell lines were used for human Toll-like receptors (TLRs 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, or 9), RIG-I, MDA5, STING, or mouse TLR8. Details on the cell lines and screen are provided in Supplementary Methods 1.6.

Animal models

Animal experiments performed at either GSK or WuXi AppTec were approved by their respective Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees and conducted according to the guidelines of the American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. Details are included in Supplementary Methods 1.7.

Innate immune activation in a human TLR8 (hTLR8) transgenic mouse model

WuXi AppTec performed an animal study to test cytokine expression in wild-type (WT) vs. transgenic hTLR8 mice. It was noted that hTLR8 mice appear to have more Kupffer cells and macrophages in their livers than WT mice (internal evaluation). Dosing conditions are described in Supplementary Methods 1.8.

Plasma cytokine levels were log10-transformed, and a one-way ANOVA was performed on each cytokine. Treatment means, along with 95% CIs, were calculated for comparisons. A one-way ANOVA was performed on the log10-transformed RNA fold change data. The t-statistic was used to evaluate the significance of the treatment response within each dataset, categorized by genotype (WT or hTLR8), tissue type, and gene.

Ex vivo evaluation of innate immune signaling pathways in splenocytes of hTLR8 transgenic mice

Spleens were harvested from 12-week-old WT littermate and hTLR8 transgenic mice. Details on preparation of splenocytes, stimulation of cells and MSD analysis are included in Supplementary Methods 1.9.

Immune response of hTLR8 and WT littermate mice to ASOs with different sequences

hTLR8 and WT littermate 12-week-old mice were randomized based on body weight prior to dosing with ASO-matched vehicle (PBS, Gibco, Waltham, MA; subcutaneous route), selgantolimod 3 mg/kg (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ; dissolved in 2% ethanol + 40% polyethylene glycol 300 + 58% water [w/v], oral route), bepirovirsen 40 mg/kg (subcutaneous route), or other ASOs (421856, 104838 and 141923; all 20-nucleotide long gapmers[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10] with 2′-O-methoxyethyl modification[11], [12], [13]) at 40 mg/kg (subcutaneous route). At 4 h post-dose, samples were collected as described in Supplementary Methods 1.10. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR), immunohistochemistry (IHC) and RNAscope in situ hybridization (ISH) (ACD, CA, USA) were performed. Plasma cytokine protein concentrations were log10-transformed and one-way ANOVA was performed on each cytokine. Treatment means, along with 95% CIs, were calculated for comparisons. The t-statistic was used to assess the significance of each pair-wise comparison made within each mouse strain (WT littermate or hTLR8), and the two-sided p value was reported.

Preclinical cytokine measurements

Measurement of cytokine protein by MSD and Luminex

MSD human V-PLEX assays (IL-12p40) and U-PLEX assays (all other cytokines) were used to measure a panel of cytokines in supernatant samples from in vitro human PBMC cultures.

MSD mouse U-PLEX assays were used to measure a panel of cytokines in supernatant samples from mouse splenocyte cultures or mouse plasma. In one experiment, cytokine levels from mouse plasma samples were detected with Mouse ProcartaPlex Mix&Match 9-plex Luminex assay kits (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA).

All MSD and Luminex assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Measurement of cytokine gene expression by RT-qPCR

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR protocols are detailed in Supplementary Methods 1.12 and 1.13, respectively.

Sequential fluorescent multiplexed RNAscope with IHC

Single 3.5 μm formalin-fixed paraffin embedded sections from the left lateral lobe of hTLR8 mouse livers were obtained from 5 mice per treatment group. Multiplexed fluorescent RNAscope followed by IHC was achieved and statistically analyzed as described in Supplementary Methods 1.14. Details on the RNAscope probes, fluorophores and staining used to visualize Ccl2, Ccl4, Cxcl10, Tnf mRNA targets are listed in Table S1.

Results

Acute responses upon bepirovirsen treatment include extracellular matrix maintenance markers and inflammatory response pathway

In the phase IIa trial, responses observed within 6 h of bepirovirsen treatment (Fig. 1A) included upregulation of markers for extracellular matrix maintenance (e.g., COL4A1,14 SPP1,15 SPARC,14 and APLP1).16 Additionally, chemokines including CCL20, CCL25 and ANGPT1 (involved in activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages) were statistically significantly elevated within 6 h post-dose in bepirovirsen-treated individuals compared with baseline. At 24 h following bepirovirsen treatment (Fig. 1B), key cytokines known to regulate pro-inflammatory and antiviral processes, such as IFN-γ and TNF, as well as others that induce macrophage and neutrophil (CCL2, CCL4, CSF1, CSF3) or T cell (IL-12B, CCL19) recruitment and activation, were among the upregulated analytes in bepirovirsen-treated individuals. Interestingly, some bepirovirsen-induced analytes overlap with a subset of those historically linked to TLR8 signaling, including IFN-γ, TNF, CCL4, CCL2 and IL-12A and B (encode IL-12p35 and IL-12p40 proteins, respectively).17

Additionally, plasma samples from early timepoints were assessed for the presence of IFNs (type I, II and III) (Fig. S1). An increase in IFN-γ levels could be seen in plasma samples from many participants who had received bepirovirsen. Type I IFNs (α and β) were below the limit of detection in most samples (Fig. S1A and B). For IFN-λ samples within detection limits (Fig. S1A), no obvious response to treatment was observed.

Longitudinal increase in immune mediators from responders correlates with ALT increase and HBsAg decrease

Longitudinal responses from the phase IIa study samples were measured. A heatmap with plasma responses determined by MyriadRBM’s DiscoveryMAP immunoassay for participants that were either NA-naïve or on-NA are shown in Fig. S2. Notably, type I IFNs were not elevated to levels that exceeded the detection threshold of the assay (data not shown; see Fig. S1 for early timepoints [0.5, 4, 6, 24 h] and a similar finding). In contrast, many cytokines/chemokines were upregulated similarly in both groups (NA-naïve, on-NA). Many serum proteins changed from baseline in those treated with bepirovirsen over time (Fig. 2A,B). Analytes were examined for correlation with ALT and HBsAg levels. The analysis showed that multiple serum analytes had an absolute correlation coefficient >0.6 with either ALT (Fig. 2A) or HBsAg (Fig. 2B). Qualitatively, many proteins exhibited peak levels that coincided with increasing ALT or decreasing HBsAg. Some of these analytes are historically linked to IFN-γ signaling (e.g. CXCL9, CXCL11),18 immune activation (e.g. TNF receptor superfamily member 9 [TNFRSF9] a.k.a. 4-1BB)19 and other processes. Pathway response values for ALT, IFN-γ signaling, and T-cell response gene sets in the placebo, non-responder, and responder groups over time are shown in Fig. 2C. Compared with the non-responder group, the responder group showed trends towards increased activity in the IFN-γ signaling and T-cell response pathways. Overall, the results indicate that bepirovirsen-treated participants who experience antiviral effects, such as HBsAg reduction, have stronger induction of immune mediators, including cytokines, during treatment.

Bepirovirsen induced innate immune activation in human PBMCs in vitro via monocytes, DCs and NK cells

Human PBMCs were used as a surrogate for primary liver perfusates as a source of immune cells, ensuring adherence to our rigorous compliance standards. The concentration of bepirovirsen used in vitro was expected to approximate the predicted exposure levels in the human liver.20 At 18 h after treatment, bepirovirsen significantly induced key cytokines like IFN-γ, IL-6, CXCL10, TNF-α and IL-12p40, with modest yet statistically significant IL-18 induction (Fig. 3). In contrast, no induction was observed for IL-1β (not significant) or IFN-β (not determined due to too few sample values detected). These findings align with clinical cytokine responses shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2. For earlier responses (at 6 h), the data related to Fig. 3 are presented in Fig. S3.

Next, we sought to characterize which immune cell types could respond to bepirovirsen preclinically during in vitro stimulations in PBMCs. Within CD45+ cells, cell surface markers were used to distinguish B cells, T cells, MAIT cells, NK cells, DCs and monocytes along with key sub-populations (Fig. S4). Cells that responded to bepirovirsen (as determined by induced protein expression) at 19 h included monocytes (IL-12p40, CXCL10, IFN-γ, TNF-α and GZMB), CD1c+ CD11b+ DCs (IL-12p40 and IFN-γ) and NK cells (IFN-γ [multiple donors] and for GZMB [one of three donors]) (Fig. 4). Weak induction of IL-12p40 in B cells from one of three donors was also observed. CD4+ or CD8+ T cells did not show a response to bepirovirsen in these in vitro stimulations. Fig. S5 presents an example of flow dot plots from one donor treated with bepirovirsen for 19 h, illustrating the frequencies of various cell types expressing the markers reported in Fig. 4.

Additionally, activation markers CD40, CD86 and CD163 were strongly upregulated in monocytes, with some upregulation in DCs (Fig. S6). There was also an increase in CD86 on B cells. This aligns with clinical data, where CD40 elevation (Fig. 2) and CD163 induction (Fig. S2)21 have been observed in bepirovirsen-treated participants who experienced reductions in HBsAg.

Bepirovirsen weakly induces activation in human TLR8-overexpressing cells

It is likely that the immune response elicited by bepirovirsen occurs through recognition of the RNA or DNA nucleic acid region(s) by PRRs. To test this hypothesis, TLR-overexpressing cell lines were stimulated with transfected bepirovirsen, DOTAP (1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium propane) alone, or relevant positive controls (Supplementary Methods 1.6). Of all the TLR-overexpressing cell lines tested, the HEK cell line overexpressing hTLR8 was the only one to exhibit a notable, albeit weak, positive response to bepirovirsen compared with the DOTAP control (p = 0.00085; Table 1, Fig. S7). The positive controls demonstrated substantial induction of signal in the respective TLR-overexpressing cell lines (Table 1). In HEK cells overexpressing hTLR8, an additional control using transfected single-stranded poly-uridine RNA was included to better mimic the potential magnitude of effect exerted by a transfected ASO.22 This control was effective, though produced a lower response compared with the untransfected control compound CL075 (3M-002) for hTLR8.23 In matched null cells, which do not exhibit TLR overexpression, treatment with either transfected or untransfected bepirovirsen showed no fold change compared with media controls (data not shown). In addition, cells overexpressing mouse TLR8 did not respond to bepirovirsen, as detailed in Table 1. Additional analyses in cell lines overexpressing other PRRs, which did not respond to bepirovirsen, are presented in the Supplementary Results and Fig. S7. Although NF-κB signaling was only weakly induced in HEK cells overexpressing hTLR8, this finding warranted further investigation to determine whether hTLR8 contributes to the response to bepirovirsen.

Bepirovirsen induces cytokine production via hTLR8 in a transgenic mouse model

While TLRs are generally conserved across species, important species-specific differences exist.24,25 One notable example within mammals is the function of TLR8. Human TLR8 recognizes and responds to single-stranded RNA and small molecule agonists. In contrast, the domain of TLR8 necessary for ligand recognition is absent in mouse and rat TLR8, rendering them unable to respond to known TLR8 ligands.26 To validate the involvement of hTLR8 in the recognition of bepirovirsen and the induction of innate signaling, a hTLR8 transgenic knock-in mouse model was used. Although other innate signaling pathways are presumed to be intact in this model, an initial ex vivo test using splenocytes was conducted to ensure the preservation of key nucleic acid-sensing pathways outside of TLR8 (Fig. S8). Except for selgantolimod, a bona fide TLR8 agonist,27 all other PRR agonists tested significantly induced TNF-α in both WT littermate and hTLR8 mouse splenocytes (p <0.0001 compared with matched negative controls). The robust response to selgantolimod was specific in hTLR8 splenocytes (p <0.0001 compared with media), indicating successful human TLR8 integration.

To determine whether bepirovirsen could induce innate immune activation, hTLR8 and WT mice were treated with bepirovirsen, GSK3389404, and ASO 141923 (a non-HBV ASO). Mice received preclinical antiviral-equivalent doses of bepirovirsen (40 mg/kg) and GSK3389404 (10 mg/kg) (data not shown), or ASO 141923 at the same dose as bepirovirsen, as outlined in Supplementary Methods 1.8. Minimal cytokine production was observed in the plasma of WT mice treated with any of the ASOs or selgantolimod 4 h post-dose (Fig. 5A). In contrast, hTLR8 mice had notable cytokine elevation when treated with bepirovirsen, similar to treatment with selgantolimod. Notably, induction of type I IFN (IFN-α or IFN-β) did not occur with bepirovirsen in hTLR8 mice. Induction of all cytokines remained minimal with ASOs 141923 and GSK3389404. Plasma cytokine responses were diminished at 24 h post-dose compared with those at 4 h (data not shown).

IL-12p40 and TNF-α are key cytokines typically induced upon TLR8 activation.28 As expected, selgantolimod strongly and significantly induced plasma IL-12p40 and TNF-α in hTLR8 mice (Fig. 5B). Bepirovirsen-treated hTLR8 mice showed a significant elevation in IL-12p40 and TNF-α levels compared with vehicle-treated mice. Although statistically significant induction of IL-12p40 was observed in mice treated with ASO 141923 or GSK3389404, the levels were notably lower than those induced by bepirovirsen. For ASO 141923, this may indicate that the immune activation is sequence-specific, given that both bepirovirsen and ASO 141923 are 20-nucleotide unconjugated gapmer ASOs. The reduced induction observed with GSK3389404 compared with bepirovirsen might be attributed to its lower dose (although antivirally equivalent) and its GalNAc conjugation, which facilitates higher uptake in hepatocytes compared with liver immune cells.

In line with protein expression, bepirovirsen significantly induced Il12b (the gene encoding the IL-12p40 subunit) and Tnf gene expression in the liver, and at higher levels compared with ASO 141923 (Fig. 5C). In contrast to protein induction, GSK3389404 induced Il12b and Tnf gene expression at similar levels to bepirovirsen. While statistically significant, intrahepatic Il12b and Tnf expression induced by selgantolimod was minimal and visibly lower than that elicited by bepirovirsen in the liver. Since TLR8 expression is higher in the small vs. large intestine,29 we measured cytokine gene expression in the ileum and saw strong and significant cytokine gene expression in mice treated with selgantolimod, with less induction seen in mice treated with bepirovirsen. Splenic Il12b and Tnf gene expression was relatively weak for all treatments (Fig. 5C).

Additionally, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-HBV infection did not change cytokine induction (hTLR8) or lack thereof (WT) in mice treated with a single dose of bepirovirsen vs. mice without infection at 4 h post-treatment (Supplementary Methods 1.11; Figs S9 and S10).

The profile of immune activation by bepirovirsen differs compared with other ASOs tested in vivo

Discrete features of ASOs may lead to their recognition and subsequent innate immune activation through divergent PRRs. One example is CpG motifs, which can be present in oligodeoxynucleotides that activate TLR9-mediated signaling. To determine whether the observed cytokine responses are unique to bepirovirsen treatment, WT littermate and hTLR8 mice were dosed with selgantolimod, bepirovirsen and other ASOs as per Methods 3.3.

Although ASO 421856, which has been shown to trigger a MyD88-TLR9-dependent immune response in vivo,12 induced cytokine production in both WT littermate and hTLR8 mice, bepirovirsen was active only in hTLR8 mice, where it significantly increased levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-12p40 (Fig. 6). Notably, bepirovirsen exhibited an enhanced profile of innate immune activation compared with the other ASOs tested. Levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-12p40 were significantly higher in hTLR8 mice dosed with bepirovirsen vs. ASO 141923 (TNF-α: p <0.0001; IL-6: p = 0.0169; IL-12p40: p <0.0001) or the minimally inflammatory ASO 104838 (p = 0.025, p = 0.0346, p = 0.0103).11

Kupffer cells and macrophages are the primary responders towards bepirovirsen in the liver

Based on the finding that the liver may be a major contributing site for innate immune response to bepirovirsen, we sought to identify the specific liver cell types responsible for cytokine production in response to bepirovirsen. hTLR8 mice were dosed with vehicle or bepirovirsen for 4 h and analysis was performed to determine liver cytokine gene expression by RT-qPCR. While Tnf and Cxcl10 expression were assessed throughout our in vivo studies, Ccl2 (Mcp-1) and Ccl4 (Mip-1β) were added to the panel based on their strong upregulation in clinical plasma samples (Fig. 1B) and their differential induction by the TLR8 agonist.23 Gene expression of Tnf, Ccl2, Ccl4, and Cxcl10 was significantly induced in the livers of hTLR8 mice that had been treated with bepirovirsen compared with vehicle (Fig. S11).

To identify which specific cell types in the liver responded to bepirovirsen, RNAscope ISH analysis was conducted using specific probes for target genes. Tnf, Ccl2, Ccl4 and Cxcl10 were notably induced in hTLR8 mice treated with bepirovirsen compared with vehicle, as evident from imaging (Figs 7A and S12A). While Il6 and Il12b signals were positive, their staining was weaker compared with other targets (data not shown). Quantification was performed using the percentage of ISH-positive cells, dots/cell (Fig. 7B) and H-score (Fig. S12B) in both F4/80+ (marker for Kupffer cells and macrophages) and F4/80- populations. Following bepirovirsen treatment, expression of Ccl4, Ccl2, and Tnf was significantly higher in F4/80+ Kupffer cells and macrophages than in F4/80− hepatocytes, relative to vehicle-treated controls. In contrast, Cxcl10 induction was similar and not statistically different between F4/80+ and F4/80- cells (Fig. S12C). Most responding F4/80- cells were identified as hepatocytes based on cellular morphology.

Discussion

Based on observations from the phase IIa study, we hypothesized that bepirovirsen may elicit an innate immune response in addition to its antiviral mechanism. Analyses of phase IIa clinical samples within 24 h post-dose suggest the induction of type II IFN (IFN-γ) in response to bepirovirsen treatment, while type I IFN (α, β) was mostly undetectable. Induction of IFN-γ, TNF, and other cytokines, coupled with limited detection of IFN-α, aligns with trends observed in PBMCs stimulated in vitro with TLR8 agonists23 and the cytokine profiles from clinical studies with selgantolimod.30,31 Using preclinical in vitro and in vivo systems, we demonstrated that bepirovirsen harbors innate immune stimulatory activity, potentially through human TLR8. During development, bepirovirsen was selected for its antiviral activity and counter-screened to minimize proinflammatory effects, which may explain the marginal magnitude of innate immune activation. To validate hTLR8 activation by bepirovirsen in PRR-overexpressing cell lines, we further characterized this response in a transgenic hTLR8 mouse model and demonstrated increased cytokine responses in the presence of hTLR8 that were not observed in WT mice. Additionally, we showed that the innate immune activation induced by bepirovirsen is distinct from that of other ASOs, including an ASO known to activate the TLR9/MyD88 pathway.

To identify which human immune cell types could respond to bepirovirsen, PBMCs were used as a surrogate model and were stimulated in vitro. The key cell populations identified from in vitro stimulation were monocytes, DCs and NK cells. While the response of monocytes and NK cells is consistent with a previous report assessing human liver tissue stimulated with the TLR8 ligand single-stranded RNA40, MAIT cell responses were also detected in that study.32 The minimal MAIT cell response to bepirovirsen observed in our in vitro studies may reflect weak overall activity of bepirovirsen.

In hTLR8 knock-in mice, both bepirovirsen and selgantolimod induced detectable plasma cytokines; however, the induction of cytokine gene expression by bepirovirsen was mainly observed in the liver, whereas selgantolimod induced limited hepatic cytokine expression and produced stronger responses in the ileum. This difference is likely attributable to the routes of administration: subcutaneous for bepirovirsen vs. oral for selgantolimod. To determine which liver cell types contributed to these signals, intrahepatic gene expression was assessed in hTLR8 mice dosed with bepirovirsen, revealing that Tnf, Ccl2 and Ccl4 were predominantly expressed by Kupffer cells and F4/80+ macrophages. Interestingly, Cxcl10 expression was detected to a more similar extent by both F4/80+ cells and hepatocytes (F4/80-) in response to bepirovirsen. Given the lack of expression of TLR8 in hepatocytes,9 it is possible that Cxcl10 gene induction is a secondary response resulting from exposure to cytokines such as TNF-α produced from the primary responder cells rather than a direct response to bepirovirsen.33 Supporting this, previous research shows that TNF-α can be quickly secreted by bone marrow macrophages and peak within 2 h after lipopolysaccharide stimulation.34

There are precedents in the literature of other ASOs stimulating immune responses, both in humans (ASO 353512,35 likely via TLR9) and in mice (ASOs ION-42185611,12 and 518477;12 both via TLR9). Notably, human and mouse TLR9 have different activation requirements: hTLR9 typically requires two closely positioned CpG motifs, which bepirovirsen lacks, while mouse TLR9 requires a single CpG motif.36 Previously, it was proposed that the mechanism of action of bepirovirsen is primarily driven by stimulation of innate immunity via TLR9 agonism.37,38 However, similar to previous assessments of this hypothesis,39,40 current analyses support a non-TLR9 signature. No induction of cytokines that are typically part of the TLR9 response, such as IFN-α,41 was seen in WT mice treated with bepirovirsen. Furthermore, bepirovirsen induces a distinct immune response from ASOs targeting the TLR9 pathway, highlighting its specificity for human TLR8. While TLR8 agonism by bepirovirsen was unexpected, the literature suggests that the sequence and modifications of oligonucleotides can influence TLR8 activation,[42], [43], [44], [45] and some ASOs can enhance TLR8 signaling.46 Additionally, in contrast to bepirovirsen’s signature, TLR7 agonists preferentially induce IFN-α secretion while producing less of certain cytokines, such as CCL4 (MIP-1β), highlighting differences in cytokine induction among TLRs.23

There are currently therapies that specifically agonize the TLR8 pathway in clinical trials for people living with chronic HBV infection, including the small molecules selgantolimod and CB06-036.47 TLR8 activation stimulates host innate and adaptive immunity through cytokines, chemokines, and downstream secretion of IFN-γ.30 This is consistent with the phase IIa study of bepirovirsen, in which rapid induction of cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF, CCL2, CCL4, and IL-12) was observed, similar to the cytokine response reported with selgantolimod.17 These cytokines and chemokines are known to exert antiviral effects against HBV by activating myeloid cells like mononuclear phagocytes and DCs, which in turn can promote NK and T-cell activation and proliferation, and potentially lead to destruction of infected hepatocytes.27 TLR8 activation has also been shown to promote T follicular helper cell differentiation, which can impact B-cell function.28,48 Given that selgantolimod monotherapy has demonstrated limited reductions in HBsAg in patients with chronic HBV infection,49 it is likely that additional mechanisms are needed for antiviral control.

In conclusion, results from in vivo and in vitro models demonstrate that bepirovirsen can elicit an innate immune response, as seen by induction of cytokines, likely mediated at least in part by TLR8 agonism, in addition to the previously demonstrated antiviral mechanism. This could have potential implications for the clinical efficacy demonstrated by bepirovirsen, which requires further investigation.
