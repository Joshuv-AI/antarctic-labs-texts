1.
Introduction and main results

In the present paper, we are interested in the following planar Hartree type equation with an isolated singularity at the origin:

(1.1)

-\Delta u(x)=\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\quad\text{in }B_{1}\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\alpha>0
,
B_{1}\setminus\{0\}
is the punctured ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the convolution
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\triangleq\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{u}(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}dy
. We mainly consider the singular solutions
u\in C^{2}(B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
for nonlocal equation (
1.1
) under the finite total curvature condition

(1.2)

\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Elliptic equations involving the exponential nonlinearity and/or the isolated singularity at the origin have been widely studied in recent decades, since it plays an essential role in various geometric and physical problems.
Let

(1.3)

-\Delta u=e^{u}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Using the moving planes method, all the
C^{2}

[
9

10

(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}u=e^{u}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\ (n\geq 3).

For even dimensions

and the decay condition

as

, Wei and Xu

established a complete classification of all solutions

of (

and

(m\geq 2)
, respectively. For the general integer

classified the

smooth solutions of equation (

Consequently, it is necessary for us to focus on the asymptotic behavior of singular elliptic solutions with isolated singularities.
Over the past few decades, several significant results on the asymptotic behavior of singular solutions with isolated singularities have received significant attention in the literature.
Note that if

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that

is a solution of (
1.1
) and
\alpha>0
. If
u
satisfies (
1.2
), then there exists a constant

such that

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+b\ln|x|\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where

is a solution of
-\Delta h=0
in
B_{1}
and

v(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\}}\ln\frac{5}{|x-y|}\left(\frac{1}{|y|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(y)}\mathrm{d}y.

Moreover, if
\alpha\in\left(0,2\right)
and
b>\frac{\alpha-4}{2}
, then there exists a H
\ddot{o}
lder continuous function
\varphi\in C_{loc}^{\gamma}(B_{1})
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

(1.13)

u(x)=\varphi+b\ln|x|\quad\text{near the origin}.

More generally, we will consider the following Hartree type equations in higher-order case

(1.14)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}u(x)=\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\quad\text{in }B_{1}\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

Based on the analysis in the proof of Theorem

We first consider the even-dimensional case. In this case, a fundamental solution of

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that

where

in

(1.16)

Moreover, if

Next, we consider the odd-dimensional case. Notice that when

Definition 1.3

Let

2.
Asymptotic behavior at isolated singularities in even dimension case

In this section, we focus on the singular solutions of equation (
1.21

2.1.
Representation formula

Motivated by

Proposition 2.1

(Representation Formula)
.

Let
n\geq 2
be a positive even integer and

Suppose that

(2.1)

where
\phi(x)=c_{n}\ln\frac{5}{|x|}
is a fundamental solution of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a solution of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}h=0
in
B_{1}
and
v
is given by (
1.22
).

Proof.

Define
u^{+}(x):=\max\left\{u(x),0\right\}
. It is easy to verify that
u^{+}(x)\leq e^{{u}(x)}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq C\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{u^{+}(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)u^{+}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C}{2^{\alpha}}\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}u^{+}(x)\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}.

This, together with (
1.2
), implies that

\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}u^{+}(x)\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Applying the results in
[
47
, Theorem 1.4]
with
s=0
, we conclude the proof of this proposition.
∎

2.2.
Two-dimensional case

For the case

, a fundamental solution of
-\Delta
in

is given by
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\frac{5}{|x|}

).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
u\in C^{2}\left(B_{1}\setminus\{0\}\right)
is a solution of (
1.20
) satisfying condition (
1.2
), and
K\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
is non-negative. Then u decomposes as

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+a_{0}\phi(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where

is given by (
1.22
),

h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a harmonic function in
B_{1}
satisfying
-\Delta h=0
, and the constant
a_{0}
satisfies the constraint
a_{0}<4\pi
.

Proof.

Using proposition
2.1
with
n=2
, we obtain that

(2.2)

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where
v
is given by (
1.22
). We will prove that
a_{\beta}=0
for
|\beta|=1
. By (
1.2
) and (
2.2
), we obtain that

(2.3)

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}e^{\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(y)}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}e^{\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Since
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
, we get that
C_{1}<e^{h}<C_{2}
in
B_{1/2}
for two constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. By the nonnegativity of
K
, then
e^{v}\geq 1
in
B_{1/2}\setminus\{0\}
. For
x,y\in B_{1/2}
, we have
\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\geq 1
. Hence, from (
2.3
), we yields

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}e^{\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Note that
D^{(i,j)}\phi
represents the derivative of
\phi
, taken
i
times with respect to
x_{1}
and
j
times with respect to
x_{2}
. Thus, based on the expression of the fundamental solution of
-\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we obtain that

(2.4)

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}e^{a_{0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\frac{5}{|x|}+a_{(1,0)}\left(-\frac{x_{1}}{2\pi|x|^{2}}\right)+a_{(0,1)}\left(-\frac{x_{2}}{2\pi|x|^{2}}\right)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Consider the polar coordinates

\begin{cases}x_{1}=r\cos\theta,\\
x_{2}=r\sin\theta,\end{cases}\quad 0\leq r<1,\ -\pi\leq\theta\leq\pi.

Now, we show that if
a_{(0,1)}\leq 0
, then
a_{(1,0)}=0
. Let

D_{1}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in B_{1/2}\setminus\{0\}\bigg|0\leq r<\frac{1}{2},0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\right\}.

By (
2.4
), we get that

\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{5}{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}}e^{\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[a_{(1,0)}\left(-\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\right)+a_{(0,1)}\left(-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\right)\right]}r\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

Since
a_{(0,1)}\leq 0
and
0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{4}
, we obtain that

\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\frac{5}{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}}e^{\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[a_{(1,0)}\left(-\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\right)\right]}r\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}r<\infty.

Using
\cos\theta\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
in
D_{1}
and the following equality

\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}r^{a}e^{\frac{b}{r}}\mathrm{d}r=+\infty\quad\forall a\in\mathbb{R},b>0,

we have
a_{(1,0)}\geq 0
. On the other hand, we set

D_{2}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in B_{1/2}\setminus\{0\}\bigg|0\leq r<\frac{1}{2},\frac{3\pi}{4}\leq\theta\leq\pi\right\}.

Then we can also get
a_{(1,0)}\leq 0
. Hence, if
a_{(0,1)}\leq 0
holds true, then
a_{(1,0)}=0
holds as well.

Similarly, if
a_{(0,1)}\geq 0
, we can also deduce that
a_{(1,0)}=0
, by analyzing the integral in

D_{3}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in B_{1/2}\setminus\{0\}\bigg|0\leq r<\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\pi}{4}\leq\theta\leq 0\right\}

and

D_{4}=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in B_{1/2}\setminus\{0\}\bigg|0\leq r<\frac{1}{2},-\pi\leq\theta\leq-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right\}.

Thus, we obtain
a_{(1,0)}=0
. Moreover, we can also get
a_{(0,1)}=0
.

Combined with (
2.4
), we have

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{5}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}<\infty,

which yields that
a_{0}<4\pi
.
∎

Next, we proceed to prove Theorem
1.1
. We establish the local Hölder continuity of
v
, from which the asymptotic behavior of
u
at the origin follows.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since
a_{0}<4\pi
and
a_{0}\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}\left(\ln 5-\ln|x|\right)
, by Lemma
2.2
, there exists a constant
b:=-\frac{1}{2\pi}a_{0}>-2
, such that
u
satisfies

(2.5)

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+b\ln|x|\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where
v
is given by (
1.22
) and
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a harmonic function in
B_{1}
. Here
h
has been redefined by absorbing the constant
-b\ln 5
. We will prove that if
\alpha\in\left(0,2\right)
and
b>\frac{\alpha-4}{2}
, then
v\in C_{loc}^{\gamma}(B_{1})
for some
0<\gamma<1
. By (
2.5
), we know that
v
is a non-negative solution of

\displaystyle-\Delta v(x)

\displaystyle=K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}

\displaystyle=K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}|y|^{b}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}|x|^{b}\quad\text{in }B_{1}

in the sense of distributions. According to
K\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
and (
1.2
), we have

\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}|y|^{b}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}|x|^{b}\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

We claim that
K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\in L^{p_{0}}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
p_{0}>1
. In fact, we fix an arbitrary
r\in(0,1)
. For convenience, we define
f(x):=e^{u(x)}=|x|^{b}e^{h(x)}e^{v(x)}
and
g_{r}(x):=\int_{B_{r}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y
. We divide
K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}
into two parts:

\displaystyle K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}=

\displaystyle K(x)g_{r}(x)f(x)+K(x)\left(\int_{B_{r_{1}}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{e^{u(y)}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)f(x)

\displaystyle+K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r_{1}}}\frac{e^{u(y)}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)f(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(x)+\mathcal{I}_{2}(x)+\mathcal{I}_{3}(x)\quad\text{in }B_{r},

where we choose
r_{1}
satisfying
r<r_{1}<1
.

For
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, by the results in
[
3
, Corollary 1 and Remark 2]
, we konw that
e^{v}\in L^{p}(B_{r})
for each
p>0
. Since
b>-2
, there exists
p_{f}>1
, such that
bp_{f}>-2
, whence
|x|^{b}\in L^{p_{f}}(B_{r})
. We consider two cases:

(i)
b\geq 0
;

(ii)
-2<b<0
.

For case (i), we can choose any real number
p_{f}>1
such that
|x|^{b}\in L^{p_{f}}(B_{r})
. Therefore,
f\in L^{q}(B_{r})
for any
q>1
. Since
0<\alpha<2
, by the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality, we easily get that there exists some
q_{1}>1
such that
g_{r}\in L^{q_{1}}(B_{r})
, and hence
\mathcal{I}_{1}(x)\in L^{q_{1}}(B_{r})
.

For case (ii), by the Hölder inequality, we can choose some
p_{1}
satisfying
1<p_{1}<\min\{\frac{2}{2-\alpha},-\frac{2}{b}\}
, such that
|x|^{b}\in L^{p_{1}}(B_{r})
and
f\in L^{p_{1}}(B_{r})
. Applying the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality together with the chosen value of
p_{1}
, we obtain the existence of
t>1
satisfying

(2.6)

\frac{1}{t}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{\alpha}{2}-1,

such that

(2.7)

\|g_{r}\|_{L^{t}(B_{r})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p_{1}}(B_{r})}

for some constant
C>0
. Next, we consider two cases here:

(a)
-\frac{2}{b}\geq\frac{2}{2-\alpha}
, i.e.
\alpha-2\leq b<0
,

(b)
-\frac{2}{b}<\frac{2}{2-\alpha}
, i.e.
-2<b<\alpha-2
.

For case (a), we have
1<p_{1}<\frac{2}{2-\alpha}
. Thus, combined with (
2.6
), we know that
0<\frac{1}{t}<\frac{\alpha}{2}
. By direct computation, for each fixed
b
, there exists a real number
s
such that
\frac{t}{t-1}<s<-\frac{2}{b}
when
t
is sufficiently large, and
|x|^{b}\in L^{s}(B_{r})
as well as
f\in L^{s}(B_{r})
. Combining this with (
2.7
), one can find some
q_{2}>1
satisfying
\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{t}=\frac{1}{q_{2}}
, so that

\int_{B_{r}\setminus\{0\}}\left|f(x)g_{r}(x)\right|^{q_{2}}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{B_{r}\setminus\{0\}}\left|f(x)\right|^{q_{2}}\left|g_{r}(x)\right|^{q_{2}}\mathrm{d}x\leq\|f^{q_{2}}\|_{\frac{s}{q_{2}}}\|(g_{r})^{q_{2}}\|_{\frac{t}{q_{2}}}<\infty,

where we have used the Hölder inequality. Consequently, it follows that

\mathcal{I}_{1}(x)\in L^{q_{2}}(B_{r}).

For case (b), we have
1<p_{1}<-\frac{2}{b}
. Under the assumption that
0<\alpha<2
and
b>\frac{\alpha-4}{2}
, we obtain that
\frac{\alpha-4}{2}<b<\alpha-2
and
\frac{-b+\alpha-2}{2}<\frac{1}{t}<\frac{\alpha}{2}
. Hence, for each fixed
b
, we can also find a real number
s
satisfying
\frac{t}{t-1}<s<-\frac{2}{b}
such that
|x|^{b}\in L^{s}(B_{r})
and
f\in L^{s}(B_{r})
. Using a similar method as employed in case (a), we conclude that there exists some
q_{3}>1
satisfying
\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{t}=\frac{1}{q_{3}}
such that

\mathcal{I}_{1}(x)\in L^{q_{3}}(B_{r}).

For
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, by the regularity of
u
in
\bar{B}_{r_{1}}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, we know that there exists a constant
M>0
such that
u<M
in
\bar{B}_{r_{1}}\setminus B_{r}
. Consequently, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{B_{r_{1}}\setminus B_{r}}\frac{e^{M}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)f(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{B_{2}}\frac{1}{|y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)f(x)

\displaystyle\leq Cf(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{r}.

By the similiar argument used in the analysis of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, we know that there exists some
q_{4}=p_{1}>1
such that
\mathcal{I}_{2}\in L^{q_{4}}(B_{r})
.

For
\mathcal{I}_{3}
, by the assumption (
1.2
) and
\alpha\in(0,n)
, we have

\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}e^{u(x)}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}\leq 2^{\alpha}\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

which implies that
e^{u}\in L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
. For
x\in B_{r}
and
y\in B_{1}\setminus B_{r_{1}}
, we observe that

|x-y|\geq|y|-|x|\geq r_{1}-r>0.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r_{1}}}\frac{e^{u(y)}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)f(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(r_{1}-r\right)^{-\alpha}\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{r_{1}}}e^{u(y)}\mathrm{d}y\right)f(x)

\displaystyle\leq Cf(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{r}.

By an argument analogous to that for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
,
we have
\mathcal{I}_{3}\in L^{q_{4}}(B_{r})
.

To summarize, for
\alpha\in\left(0,2\right)
and
b>\frac{\alpha-4}{2}
, we can select
p_{0}=\min\left\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}\right\}
, then
K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\in L^{p_{0}}(B_{r})
. Since
r\in(0,1)
is arbitrary, we deduce that

K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\in L^{p_{0}}_{loc}(B_{1})\quad\text{for some }p_{0}>1.

An application of the standard
W^{2,p}
elliptic regularity theory (see
[
20
]
) directly yields the improved regularity
v\in W^{2,p_{0}}_{loc}(B_{1})
. It then follows from the Sobolev embedding theorem that
v
is locally Hölder continuous, i.e.,
v\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
∎

2.3.
Even-dimensional case (
n\geq 4
)

In this subsection, following a similar detailed analysis in two-dimensional case, we study the Hartree-type equation (
1.21
) in even dimensions
n\geq 4
. The corresponding polyharmonic equations involve higher-order singularities, which introduce substantial new challenges. Thus, we need an additional decay condition (
1.15
) near the origin. Under the finite total curvature assumption (
1.2
) and condition (
1.15
), we also derive a representation formula for solutions to (
1.21
) in the even-dimensional case, and further investigate the asymptotic behavior of these solutions near the origin.

If
n\geq 4
is even, a fundamental solution of
\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}
is given by
\phi(x)=c_{n}\ln\frac{5}{|x|}
, with
c_{n}>0
being a constant. Then we have

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
n\geq 4
be an even integer and
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
u\in C^{n}\left(B_{1}\setminus\{0\}\right)
is a solution of (
1.21
) satisfying condition (
1.2
) and (
1.15
), and
K\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
is non-negative. Then u decomposes as

(2.8)

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+a_{0}\phi(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where
v
is defined by (
1.22
). The function
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a polyharmonic function in
B_{1}
satisfying
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}h=0
, and the constant
a_{0}
satisfies the constraint
a_{0}<\frac{n}{c_{n}}
.

Proof.

If
u
solves (
1.21
) and
K\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
, Proposition
2.1
with
s=0
gives the decomposition:

(2.9)

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where
v
is given by (
1.22
) and
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a solution of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}h=0
in
B_{1}
.
Next we will prove that
\sum_{1\leq|\beta|\leq n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\equiv 0
. We can split the proof in two steps.

Step 1.

We show that all coefficients corresponding to derivatives of order
2\leq|\beta|\leq n-1
must be zero.

By the Fubini Theorem and the condition (
1.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}v(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{r}}\left[\int_{B_{1}}\ln\frac{5}{|x-y|}K(y)\left(\frac{1}{|y|^{\alpha}}\ast e^{u}\right)e^{u(y)}\mathrm{d}y\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{1}}\left(\int_{B_{r}}\ln\frac{5}{|x-y|}\mathrm{d}x\right)K(y)\left(\frac{1}{|y|^{\alpha}}\ast e^{u}\right)e^{u(y)}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{n}\ln\frac{1}{r}\int_{B_{1}}K(y)\left(\frac{1}{|y|^{\alpha}}\ast e^{u}\right)e^{u(y)}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{n}\ln\frac{1}{r},\qquad\text{for }0<r<\frac{1}{2}.

Combining this with (
1.15
) and (
2.9
), we conclude that

(2.10)

\int_{B_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x=o(1)r^{n-2}\quad\text{as }r\to 0.

For the top-order derivatives
|\beta|=n-1
, we group them as

(2.11)

\sum_{|\beta|=n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)=\frac{P_{n-1}(x)}{|x|^{2n-2}},

where
P_{n-1}(x)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n-1
. Restrict
P_{n-1}
to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
by the map
\theta\mapsto P_{n-1}(\theta)
. This restriction is a continuous function on the compact set
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. We claim that
P_{n-1}\equiv 0
. In fact,
if
P_{n-1}\not\equiv 0
, then there exists at least one point
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that
P_{n-1}(\theta_{0})>0
. Let
d_{0}=\frac{1}{2}P_{n-1}(\theta_{0})
. Therefore, by continuity, there exists a geodesic ball neighborhood
U_{0}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of
\theta_{0}
such that
\left|P_{n-1}\right|\geq d_{0}>0
for all
\theta\in U_{0}
. Using polar coordinates
x=\rho\theta
with
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, the volume element becomes

\mathrm{d}x=\rho^{n-1}\mathrm{d}\rho\mathrm{d}\theta.

By homogeneity, for
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\rho>0
, we have

P_{n-1}(x)=\rho^{n-1}P_{n-1}(\theta).

Now, define the region
V_{r}=[0,r]\times U_{0}
. Then,

\int_{V_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|=n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x=\int_{U_{0}}\left|P_{n-1}(\theta)\right|\mathrm{d}\theta\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{d}\rho\geq d_{1}r

for some constant
d_{1}>0
. On the other hand, a direct computation gives

\int_{B_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-2}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{B_{r}}|x|^{-n+2}\mathrm{d}x\leq Cr^{2}.

Thus, combining these two estimates, we have

\displaystyle\int_{V_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{V_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|=n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x-\int_{V_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-2}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{V_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|=n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B_{r}}\left|\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-2}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq d_{1}r-Cr^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d_{1}}{2}r\quad\text{for small }r>0.

This contradicts (
2.10
). Hence, we conclude that
P_{n-1}\equiv 0
, which implies

\sum_{|\beta|=n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)=0.

By iterating this procedure, we conclude that

\sum_{|\beta|=j}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)=0,

for every index
2\leq j\leq n-2
.

Step 2.
We show that
\sum_{|\beta|=1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)=0
and
a_{0}<\frac{n}{c_{n}}
.

By (
1.2
), (
2.9
) and the results in step 1, we have

(2.12)

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}e^{\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(y)}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}e^{\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

To exploit this, we first note that
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
implies that
C_{1}<e^{h}<C_{2}
in
B_{1/2}
for two constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. By the nonnegativity of
K
,
e^{v}\geq 1
in
B_{1/2}\setminus\{0\}
. For
x,y\in B_{1/2}
, the kernel satisfies
\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\geq 1
. Combining these bounds with (
2.12
), we obtain the finiteness of the integral involving the remaining exponential term:

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}e^{\sum_{|\beta|\leq 1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Namely,

(2.13)

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{5}{|x|}\right)^{c_{n}a_{0}}e^{-c_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{x_{i}}{|x|^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty,

where
a_{i}=a(0,\cdots,1,\cdots,0)
. An argument parallel to the one used in proving Theorem
1.1
, we have
a_{i}=0
for every
i=1,2,\cdots,n
, and hence
\sum_{|\beta|=1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)=0
. From (
2.13
), it follows that

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{5}{|x|}\right)^{c_{n}a_{0}}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty,

which implies
c_{n}a_{0}<n
. We complete the proof.
∎

Now, we discuss the regularity of
v
. Before that, we need the following lemma to estimate
v
.

Lemma 2.4

(Exponential integrability for logarithmic potentials)
.

Let
F\geq 0
and
F\in L^{1}(B_{1})
. Define

v(x)=\int_{B_{1}}K(x,y)F(y)\mathrm{d}y,\quad\text{in }B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where the kernel
K
satisfies

(2.14)

|K(x,y)|\leq C_{1}\bigl(1+|\ln|x-y||\bigr),\quad x,y\in B_{1},

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. Then for any
p>1
, we have

e^{pv}\in L^{1}(B_{1}).

Remark 2.5
.

Lemma
2.4
does not require
F
to be finite everywhere. Any non-negative
F\in L^{1}(B_{1})
is admissible, even if
F
has isolated singularities. Since
F
is integrable, the potential

v(x)=\int_{B_{1}}K(x,y)F(y)\mathrm{d}y

is well defined almost everywhere in
B_{1}
, which is sufficient to get
e^{pv}\in L^{1}(B_{1})
.

Proof.

Since
F\geq 0
and
F\in L^{1}(B_{1})
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we may choose

\lambda>0
such that the truncation

F_{1}=F\chi_{\left\{F<\lambda\right\}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{2}=F\chi_{\left\{F\geq\lambda\right\}}

satisfying
F_{1}\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
and
\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}<\varepsilon
, where cut-off function
\chi_{\Omega}
is defined by

\chi_{\Omega}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1,&\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\quad\text{in }\Omega^{c}.\end{cases}

Therefore, for any arbitrarily fixed
p>1
, there exists
\lambda_{p}>0
such that
F_{1}\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
and
\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}<\frac{n}{2pC_{1}}
. Then the function
v
admits a decomposition
v=v_{1}+v_{2}
, in which

v_{i}(x)=\int_{B_{1}}K(x,y)F_{i}(y)\mathrm{d}y\qquad i=1,2.

By the Hölder inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\|K(x,y)\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}\|F_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\|F_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\int_{B_{2}}\left(1+\left|\ln|y|\right|\right)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle<\infty,

which implies that
e^{pv_{1}}\in L^{1}(B_{1})
. On the other hand, using (
2.14
) and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}e^{p|v_{2}(x)|}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}}e^{pC_{1}\int_{B_{1}}\left(1+\left|\ln|x-y|\right|\right)F_{2}(y)\mathrm{d}y}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{1}}e^{pC_{1}\|F_{2}\|_{1}\int_{B_{1}}\left(\left|\ln|x-y|\right|\frac{F_{2}(y)}{\|F_{2}\|_{1}}\right)\mathrm{d}y}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{1}}\int_{B_{1}}\left(e^{\left|\ln|x-y|\right|}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{F_{2}(y)}{\|F_{2}\|_{1}}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{d}y+C\int_{B_{2}\setminus B_{1}}|y|^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle<\infty.

Thus, we conclude that for any
p>1
,
e^{pv}\in L^{1}(B_{1})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we begin to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We define

b:=-c_{n}a_{0}>-n.

Since
a_{0}\phi(x)=-b\ln 5+b\ln|x|
, by Lemma
2.3
, we have

(2.15)

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+b\ln|x|.

Here
v
is given by (
1.22
) and
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a solution of

(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}h=0
in
B_{1}
, where we have absorbed the constant

-b\ln 5
into the harmonic function
h
. Therefore, we know that
v
is a nonnegative solution of

\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}v=K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\quad\text{in }B_{1}

in the sense of distributions. Using
K\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
and assumption (
1.2
), we obtain that

\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

Since
c_{n}\ln\frac{5}{|x-y|}
satisfies (
2.14
), then by Lemma
2.4
and Remark
2.5
, we have
e^{v}\in L^{p}(B_{1})
for any
p>1
.

Next, we will prove that
v\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
0<\gamma<1
.
By (
1.21
) and (
2.15
), we know that
v
is a non-negative solution of

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}v(x)

\displaystyle=K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}

\displaystyle=K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}|y|^{b}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}|x|^{b}\quad\text{in }B_{1}

in the sense of distributions. Proceeding in a manner similar to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, one finds that, provides
\alpha\in(0,n)
and
b>\frac{\alpha-2n}{2}
, it follows that

K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}|y|^{b}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}|x|^{b}\in L^{p_{0}}(B_{1})

for some
p_{0}>1
. A subsequent application of the standard
W^{n,p}
elliptic regularity theory yields
v\in W^{n,p_{0}}_{loc}(B_{1})
. It then follows from the Sobolev embedding theorem that
v\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
0<\gamma<1
.
Thus, by (
2.15
), we have

u(x)=\varphi(x)+b\ln|x|\quad\text{in }B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\},

in which
\varphi:=v+h\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
0<\gamma<1
. We finish the proof.
∎

3.
Asymptotic behavior at isolated singularities in odd dimension case

In this section, we study the isolated singularity problem for the Hartree-type equation (
1.21
) with odd dimensions
n\geq 3
. In this case, the operator
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}
is a nonlocal operator, and we mainly consider the distributional solutions defined in the space
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\frac{n}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Under the finite total curvature condition (
1.2
) and the decay condition (
1.15
), we also show that the asymptotic behavior near the origin has the same form as in the even-dimensional case. The following lemma provide the corresponding representation formula for the solutions to (
1.21
) in odd dimensions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer and
\alpha>0
. Suppose that
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\frac{n}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{n}\left(B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\}\right)
is a solution of (
1.21
) satisfying condition (
1.2
) and (
1.15
), and
K\in L^{\infty}(B_{1})
is non-negative. Then
u
decomposes as

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+b\ln|x|\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\},

where
v
is defined by (
1.22
),
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
is a polyharmonic function in
B_{1}
satisfying
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}h=0
, and the constant
b
satisfies
b>-n
.

Proof.

Let
v
be defined as in (
1.22
). Standard potential estimates imply

v\in\mathcal{L}_{\frac{n}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}v(x)=K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{1}).

Set
w=u-v
. Then

(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}w=0\qquad\text{in }B_{1}\setminus\{0\}.

Hence, for any
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}\setminus\{0\})
, we have

\langle(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}w,\psi\rangle=0,

which implies that

\mathrm{supp}\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}w\big)\subset\{0\}.

By the classical structure theorem of Schwartz, we know that

(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}w=\sum_{|\beta|\leq M}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\delta_{0},\qquad a_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}.

Using
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}\phi=\delta_{0}
, we have

w(x)=h(x)+\sum_{|\beta|\leq M}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x),

where
h
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}h=0
in
B_{1}
and
\phi(x)=c_{n}\ln\frac{5}{|x|}
is a fundamental solution of
(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}
. By the elliptic regularity, we have
h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
. Since
u,v,h\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(B_{1})
and

D^{\beta}\phi(x)\sim|x|^{-|\beta|}
,
the local integrability near
0
requires
|\beta|<n
.
Thus
a_{\beta}=0
whenever
|\beta|\geq n
, and
M\leq n-1
. Therefore, we conclude that

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+\sum_{|\beta|\leq n-1}a_{\beta}D^{\beta}\phi(x)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\}.

Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, we obtain that

u(x)=v(x)+h(x)+b\ln|x|\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}\setminus\{0\},

where
b>-n
and
h
has been redefined by absorbing a constant into the harmonic part.
∎

Now we discuss the regularity of
v
and prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Since
c_{n}\ln\frac{5}{|x-y|}
satisfies (
2.14
), then by Lemma
2.4
and Remark
2.5
, we have
e^{pv}\in L^{1}(B_{1})
for any
p>1
. By (
1.21
) and the expression of
u
, we know that
v
is a non-negative solution of

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{n}{2}}v(x)

\displaystyle=K(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*e^{u}\right)e^{u(x)}

\displaystyle=K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}|y|^{b}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}|x|^{b}\quad\text{in }B_{1}

in the sense of distributions. An argument analogous to the proof of Theorem
1.1
shows that, provided
\alpha\in(0,n)
and
b>\frac{\alpha-2n}{2}
, we have

K(x)\left(\int_{B_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{v(y)}e^{h(y)}|y|^{b}}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{d}y\right)e^{v(x)}e^{h(x)}|x|^{b}\in L^{p_{0}}(B_{1})

for some
p_{0}>1
. By the expression of
v
and the classical potential estimate (see, e.g., Gilbarg-Trudinger
[
20
]
), we obtain that
v\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
0<\gamma<1
. Consequently, we get

u(x)=\varphi(x)+b\ln|x|\quad\text{in }B_{1}\setminus\left\{0\right\},

in which
\varphi:=v+h\in C^{\gamma}_{loc}(B_{1})
for some
0<\gamma<1
.
∎
