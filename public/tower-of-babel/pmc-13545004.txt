ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesFacioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) includes approximately 10%–30% sporadic cases, where de novo pathogenic variants are hypothesized to be associated with more severe phenotypes. However, the systematic clinical characteristics and natural history of these cases remain poorly defined.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Fujian Neuromedical Center, enrolling patients with genetically confirmed FSHD who were classified as de novo (dnFSHD) when no pathogenic D4Z4 contraction was detected in blood samples from either available parent. Clinical and genetic parameters at baseline and during subsequent evaluations were compared between dnFSHD and familial FSHD (fFSHD). The fFSHD cohort was matched for sex and disease duration. Mediation analysis was applied to explore the relationships among de novo pathogenic variants, the number of D4Z4 repeats, and disease progression.ResultsA total of 92 patients with genetically confirmed FSHD who were classified as de novo cases were included, representing 17.8% of all FSHD type 1 cases. Compared with fFSHD, patients with dnFSHD exhibited an earlier age at onset (median: 10 years vs 16 years, p < 0.0001), higher clinical score (median: 8 vs 6, p = 0.0001), and smaller number of D4Z4 repeat units (RUs) (median: 3 vs 5, p < 0.0001). Survival analysis revealed significantly increased risks in patients with dnFSHD for lower extremity involvement (hazard ratio 1.55, p = 0.021). Mediation analysis indicated that D4Z4 RUs mediated 72% of the correlation between dnFSHD and lower extremity involvement and 48% of the association with age-corrected clinical severity scale.DiscussionThis study establishes dnFSHD as a distinct, high-risk FSHD subtype characterized by unique clinical features, with its severe phenotype primarily mediated by a shorter D4Z4 repeat array.

Introduction

Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) is an autosomal dominant neuromuscular disorder caused by a contraction of the D4Z4 macrosatellite repeat array to 1–10 units on a specific 4qA-permissive haplotype.1-3 This contraction is typically confirmed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)–based Southern blot analysis using the EcoRI/p13E-11 probe, and the 4qA and 4qB variants are identified using 4qA-specific and 4qB-specific probes, revealing a shortened pathogenic fragment. This method is widely used in multiple centers worldwide and is currently the gold standard for genetic diagnosis of FSHD.4-7 FSHD presents with progressive muscular weakness typically initiating in the face, shoulder girdle, and arms8,9 affecting approximately 1 in 20,000 individuals, with a penetrance of over 90% by age 20 years.

Although this inheritance pattern is well-established, a significant proportion (approximately 10%–30%) of cases occur sporadically without a positive family history.10,11 These sporadic cases primarily result from de novo pathogenic variants, molecularly defined by a pathogenic contracted fragment in the patient's genome that is absent in both parents.12,13 It is important that some sporadic cases may actually represent somatic mosaicism. In these patients, the lower proportion of cells carrying the pathogenic variant often leads to reduced penetrance and a milder clinical phenotype compared with nonmosaic individuals. This can partly explain the clinical heterogeneity observed in FSHD.

Notably, the higher prevalence of de novo pathogenic variants in early-onset FSHD may suggest a link to a more severe clinical phenotype.14 However, the complete clinical spectrum and natural history of de novo FSHD (dnFSHD) remain poorly characterized, largely because of the limitation of current evidence to case reports and small series.1,10,13,15,16 A shortage of large-scale, systematic cohort studies has resulted in an incomplete understanding of disease progression and severity specific to this subtype, posing significant challenges to the formulation of precise prognostic guidelines and effective personalized management strategies.

This retrospective cohort study included 92 patients with molecularly confirmed dnFSHD. We aimed to quantify the precise proportion of de novo pathogenic variants in an FSHD cohort, systematically characterize the clinical spectrum and natural history of this subtype, and compare key clinical parameters between de novo and familial cases. This comprehensive analysis aimed to provide a robust clinical characterization of dnFSHD, establishing an evidence-based foundation for accurate prognosis and personalized management strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The Fujian Neuromedical Centre (FNMC) is China's diagnostic center for clinical-genetic FSHD and the only 1 that uses pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)–based Southern blotting for all FSHD1 genetic testing.16 This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the FNMC, with participant recruitment spanning from 2001 to 2024. Patients with genetically confirmed FSHD1 were enrolled. The genetic diagnosis of FSHD1 was established by detecting a shortened D4Z4 repeat array of 1–10 repeat units (RU) on the 4qA permissive haplotype using PFGE-based Southern blot analysis. Patients were divided into 2 groups based on genetic testing results of the patient and their parents. De novo FSHD was defined as the presence of a pathogenic shortened D4Z4 fragment in the patient that was not detected in the blood samples of either parent. Familial FSHD (fFSHD) was defined as the presence of a pathogenic D4Z4 shortened mutation in the patient that was also present in one of the parents. Only patients with available parental blood samples were included in the dnFSHD and fFSHD groups. The fFSHD control group was drawn from the same study cohort using 1:1 frequency matching. Specifically, we matched the dnFSHD group by sex and disease duration, selecting patients with fFSHD from the source population with the same sex distribution and a comparable distribution of disease duration. To enhance reproducibility, we additionally matched the dnFSHD and fFSHD groups by sex, disease duration, and age at examination. Patients were excluded if parental blood samples were unavailable to confirm the origin of the EcoRI fragment, if they exhibited mosaic inheritance, or if they had other myopathies. Somatic mosaicism was defined as the presence of a fifth fragment hybridizing with p13E-11 in 1 individual. We further compared the group with parental samples available (PSA) and the group with parental samples unavailable (PSU), matched by sex and disease duration, to assess potential selection bias.

Clinical Assessment

This was a retrospective cohort study. All clinical data were retrospectively extracted from existing medical records. For each participant, the records included the initial evaluation and multiple subsequent clinical assessments, allowing us to evaluate disease progression based on longitudinal data. All clinical evaluations were conducted by a consistent team of examiners at both baseline and subsequent evaluations. “Age at examination” refers to the patient's age at the time of their first comprehensive evaluation at our center. Muscle strength was assessed bilaterally using manual muscle testing across 14 paired muscle groups: the deltoid, triceps, biceps, wrist extensors, wrist flexors, finger extensors, finger flexors, gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, iliopsoas, hamstrings, quadriceps femoris, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius. A total Medical Research Council (MRC) score was calculated by summing the average scores of all muscle pairs. Disease severity was further evaluated using the FSHD Clinical Score (CS; range 0–15),17,18 which measures strength and function in 6 frequently involved muscle groups, and the Clinical Severity Scale (CSS; range 0–5),2 which reflects the overall degree of muscle weakness. The age-corrected CSS (ACSS) was derived by adjusting the CSS for age at examination using the formula: ACSS = ((CSS × 2)/age at examination) × 1,000.19

Disease Progression

In the dnFSHD and fFSHD groups matched by sex and disease duration, disease progression was evaluated using 2 key end points: lower extremity involvement and independent ambulation loss. Age at symptom onset was determined as either the patient-reported age of initial symptoms or the retrospectively recognized age of first clinical signs, whichever occurred earlier.20 Lower extremity involvement was defined by the presence of both patient-reported symptoms and objective physical signs, in combination with a CSS ≥3.0. Time to lower extremity involvement was calculated from the age at symptom onset to the age at lower extremity involvement onset. Independent ambulation loss was defined as the inability to walk without assistance, based on a modified Rankin Scale score ≥4.21,22 Time to independent ambulation loss was defined as the duration from the age at symptom onset to the age at onset of this end point. For patients who did not reach either end point during the study, the time-to-event was determined as the age at the last subsequent evaluation minus the age at symptom onset.

Genetic Analysis

The genetic diagnosis of FSHD was established by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE)–based Southern blotting. The number of D4Z4 RUs was calculated from the EcoRI fragment size using the formula: (D4Z4-containing fragment length (in kb, following EcoRI/HindIII)–4.7)/3.3 or (D4Z4-containing fragment length (in kb, following EcoRI digestion) − 6.8)/3.3.22,23 In addition, the D4Z4 methylation level was measured by sodium bisulphite sequencing of 10 CpGs on the 4qA allele according to a previous protocol and was calculated as the average methylation percentage across these 10 CpGs.23

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as means ± SDs and compared using the t test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were described as medians (ranges) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test (applying a continuity correction when expected values were <5). Correlations between variables were assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficient.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of lower extremity involvement and independent ambulation loss in the dnFSHD and fFSHD groups. Cumulative hazard curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with group differences assessed using the log-rank test.

Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were used to identify risk factors for lower extremity involvement. The proportional hazards assumption for the multivariate Cox model was assessed using the Schoenfeld residuals test. For variables satisfying the proportionality assumption, the Cox proportional hazards (Cox proportional hazards) regression analysis was applied.

Mediation analysis was performed using the “mediation” package in R 4.3.2 to assess the mediating effect of D4Z4 RUs on the associations of dnFSHD with disease progression. Specifically, dnFSHD was used as the predictor variable (X), D4Z4 RUs as the mediator (M), and 2 separate outcome variables (Y) were analyzed: the survival outcome of lower extremity involvement (including time to involvement and event status) and ACSS. The analyses were adjusted for sex and D4Z4 methylation levels. A mediating effect was confirmed when all of the following criteria were met: a significant indirect effect, a significant total effect, and a positive proportion mediated.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. Written informed consent was procured from every participant enrolled in the study. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT04369209.

Data Availability

Data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Patients

As of December 2024, a total of 1,245 patients were diagnosed with FSHD1, of whom 92 (17.8%) met the inclusion criteria for this study. The patient selection process is illustrated in eFigure 1. We matched the dnFSHD group by sex and disease duration and selected 92 patients with fFSHD identical sex distribution and comparable disease duration distribution. To enhance reproducibility, we additionally matched the dnFSHD and fFSHD groups by sex, disease duration, and age at examination, resulting in 73 matched pairs for comparison (eFigure 1A). Furthermore, we compared the clinical and genetic characteristics of the group with parental samples available (PSA group, n = 213) with those of a matched group without PSA (PSU group, n = 213) (eFigure 1B). eTable 1 presents the clinical and genetic characteristics of the dnFSHD cohort, stratified by sex. The dnFSHD cohort comprised 63 men and 29 women. The median age at symptom onset was 10 years for both men (range: 3–31 years) and women (range: 2–35 years). Similarly, the median age at onset of lower extremity involvement was 16 years for men (range: 6–40 years) and 16.5 years for women (range: 2–40 years). A higher rate of independent ambulation loss was found in women (27.6%) compared with that in men (11.1%, p = 0.047). The mean FSHD clinical score was 7.7 ± 2.5 for men and 8.1 ± 2.9 for women. The median ACSS was 269.2 for men and 285.7 for women. The median number of contracted D4Z4 RUs was 3 in both groups. The median D4Z4 methylation level was 34.8% (range: 14.1–55.1) in men and 34.3% (range: 13.8–66.2) in women.

A matched cohort of 92 patients with fFSHD were selected based on sex and disease duration for comparison with the dnFSHD group (n = 92) (Table 1). Patients with dnFSHD exhibited a significantly earlier age at symptom onset (median 10 years, range: 2–35 years) compared with patients with fFSHD (median 16 years, range: 1–44 years; p < 0.0001) (Figure 1A). Similarly, the median age at onset of lower extremity involvement was earlier in the dnFSHD group (median 16 years) than in the fFSHD group (25 years; p < 0.0001). The dnFSHD group also showed a higher prevalence of independent ambulation loss (16.3% vs 4.4%; p = 0.015) and wheelchair use (15.2% vs 2.2%; p = 0.004). Comorbidities were more prevalent in the dnFSHD cohort, including pectus excavatum (33.7% vs 13.0%; p < 0.001), hyperlordosis (48.9% vs 33.7%; p = 0.036), and myopathic gait (53.3% vs 29.4%; p = 0.001). In addition, patients with dnFSHD had higher median CSS (3.5 points vs 3.0 points; p < 0.0001), clinical score (CS) (8 vs 6; p = 0.0001), and ACSS (277.0 vs 188.3; p < 0.0001) (Figure 1B). Genetically, the group had significantly shorter contracted D4Z4 arrays (median 17.0 kb vs 23.0 kb; p < 0.0001), as well as reduced median methylation levels (34.8% vs 38.5%; p = 0.018) (Figure 1, C and D). A significant positive correlation was observed between the number of D4Z4 RUs and both the age at symptom onset (r = 0.5178, p < 0.0001) and the age at lower extremity involvement onset (r = 0.5066, p < 0.0001) (eFigure 2, A and B). Conversely, significant negative correlations were noted between the number of D4Z4 RUs and both the FSHD clinical score (r = −0.3611, p < 0.0001) and the ACSS (r = −0.5186, p < 0.0001) eFigure 2, C and D). Furthermore, we selected a matched cohort of 73 patients with fFSHD based on sex, disease duration, and age at examination for comparison with the dnFSHD group (n = 73) (eTable 2). In this rematched cohort, patients with dnFSHD exhibited a significantly earlier age at symptom onset (median 10 years vs 15 years; p = 0.0005), an earlier age at lower extremity involvement onset (median 16.5 years vs 23 years; p < 0.0001), a higher rate of independent ambulation loss (15.1% vs 4.1%; p = 0.0458), a higher proportion of wheelchair use (13.7% vs 2.7%; p = 0.0311), a shorter D4Z4 repeat array (median 17.0 kb vs 20.0 kb; p < 0.0001), and lower methylation levels (34.5% vs 38.6%; p = 0.0177).

Longitudinal Outcomes

Over a median subsequent period of 4 years (range: 1–10 years), both patients with de novo and fFSHD showed disease progression (Table 2 and Figure 2). Significant increases in both the FSHD CS and CSS were observed from baseline to subsequent evaluations in both groups (Figure 2, A–D). However, patients with dnFSHD demonstrated greater progression in clinical severity compared with those with fFSHD, with a significantly higher median increase in the FSHD CS (2 points vs 1 point; p = 0.009) and CSS (0.5 vs 0; p = 0.024). Moreover, the magnitude of change in both CS and CSS was inversely correlated with the number of D4Z4 RUs (eFigure 3, A and B).

Disease Progression

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that the median time to lower extremity involvement was shorter in dnFSHD compared with fFSHD (Figure 3A). Accordingly, the risk of lower extremity involvement was significantly higher in patients with dnFSHD (hazard ratio [HR] 1.55, 95% CI 1.07–2.25, p = 0.021). In addition, patients with dnFSHD also showed a significantly higher risk of independent ambulation loss (HR 3.73, 95% CI 1.50–9.26, p = 0.005) (Figure 3B).

In univariable analysis, a smaller number of D4Z4 RUs and lower D4Z4 methylation levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of lower extremity involvement. Mediating Role of D4Z4 RU

To evaluate whether the number of D4Z4 RUs mediates the association between dnFSHD and disease progression, a mediation analysis was conducted. The outcomes assessed included the survival outcome of lower extremity involvement and the ACSS. The analysis revealed that D4Z4 RUs mediated 72% of the effect of dnFSHD on the survival outcome of lower extremity involvement (p < 0.001) and 48% of the effect on ACSS (p < 0.001) (Figure 4). These results indicate that the number of D4Z4 RUs plays a significant mediating role in the accelerated disease progression observed in dnFSHD.

Discussion

In this systematically retrospective cohort study, dnFSHD was shown to be associated with more severe clinical manifestations and faster disease progression compared with fFSHD in a Chinese population. Patients with dnFSHD exhibit earlier onset, earlier lower extremity involvement, more frequent complications, and poorer functional outcomes. In addition, dnFSHD is characterized by shorter D4Z4 repeat lengths and lower methylation levels. Our key finding is that the D4Z4 repeat length acts as the pivotal mediator for this severe phenotype, thus providing further genetic mechanistic explanation.

Previous studies have reported that sporadic cases account for approximately 10%–30% of FSHD cases; however, this proportion may not accurately reflect the true frequency of de novo cases. An Italian study reported a true de novo pathogenic variant rate of approximately 8%,24 confirmed by parental genetic testing. In this study, after strict exclusion of somatic mosaicism, the estimated de novo rate was 17.8%. This figure falls within the commonly cited 10%–30% range (closer to the midrange), yet it is lower than the upper boundary (30%) reported in previous studies of apparently sporadic cases and differs from data in Italian cohorts, possibly reflecting ethnic heterogeneity. However, this proportion is highly dependent on the definition of somatic mosaic cases. When including mosaic patients, the proportion of broadly defined de novo events in our cohort rises to 27.3%, more consistent with the reported estimates in the literature. This discrepancy suggests that previous estimates of “sporadic cases” based on clinical diagnosis likely included a significant number of mild mosaic cases, leading to an overestimation of true de novo pathogenic variant rates. Moreover, this study demonstrates that mosaic patients have a milder clinical severity compared with de novo and familial cases, which is consistent with previous findings.23 Thus, genetically distinguishing typical de novo pathogenic variants from mosaicism is critical for accurate prognostic assessment.

Previous studies on sex differences in FSHD have yielded inconsistent findings, suggesting complex underlying mechanisms. We observed a male predominance in the dnFSHD cohort, and among the unmatched familial cases (n = 375), men also accounted for the majority (226, 60.3%). We speculate that this may be related to higher societal expectations regarding physical strength in men, leading to a greater tendency for men to seek medical care. A Korean study reported no gender differences in the number of D4Z4 RUs but noted greater clinical severity in female patients.25 By contrast, our study found no significant differences between male and female patients in core clinical severity metrics or primary genetic characteristics. However, women exhibited a significantly higher rate of independent ambulation loss. This aligns with what Katz et al.26 reported: Women are more likely to progress to loss of ambulation at a faster rate. Conversely, Ricci et al. observed a different pattern where male patients displayed more severe clinical phenotypes.17 These discrepancies suggest that the impact of gender on FSHD phenotypes is likely modulated by a combination of population-specific genetic backgrounds, environmental factors, and other variables. We further analyzed the parental origin of the de novo D4Z4 contraction in the 92 patients with dnFSHD and found no significant paternal or maternal bias, suggesting that the repeat instability in the D4Z4 region may be predominantly determined by its intrinsic structural features or epigenetic status rather than by parental sex-related biological differences.

Our study revealed that patients with dnFSHD represent a distinct high-risk subgroup characterized by earlier onset age, higher CSS, and faster disease progression compared with fFSHD. Comparisons using different matching strategies consistently supported this conclusion. In addition, patients with dnFSHD face an increased risk of lower extremity involvement, with a more than 3-fold higher risk of reaching the disability milestone of independent ambulation loss. Notably, the dnFSHD cohort is enriched with “ultra-short” D4Z4 RU, providing a direct genetic explanation for the severe clinical phenotype in this subgroup. Our findings are highly consistent with the widely accepted consensus that the length of D4Z4 repeat array is the primary genetic determinant of FSHD phenotype severity.2,22,27 Consequently, early identification of patients with dnFSHD, coupled with proactive monitoring and intervention, holds significant clinical importance.

Of note, all patients with dnFSHD and fFSHD in this study were derived from the PSA group. We further compared the clinical characteristics of the PSA group with those of the PSU group and found that PSA patients had an earlier age at onset, a higher and earlier prevalence of lower extremity involvement, higher clinical severity scores and ACSS, and shorter D4Z4 contracted fragments, suggesting a certain degree of “severity enrichment” in the PSA group. This is likely because families with more severe phenotypes and greater motivation to seek medical care are more inclined to pursue genetic counseling and parental verification. Nevertheless, because both the dnFSHD and fFSHD groups originated from the same PSA source population, such severity enrichment bias is nondifferential between the 2 groups and therefore does not affect the internal validity of the intergroup comparisons.

To further elucidate risk factors for lower extremity involvement in patients with dnFSHD, univariable analysis revealed that dnFSHD, shorter D4Z4 RU, and lower methylation levels were significantly associated with time to lower extremity involvement, whereas multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that only D4Z4 RUs remained significantly associated with time to lower extremity involvement, and dnFSHD was no longer associated with the outcome of lower extremity involvement. Given the prevalence of shorter D4Z4 repeat lengths in this cohort, we explored its mediating role. Mediation analysis revealed that D4Z4 repeat array mediates 72% of the association between dnFSHD and lower extremity involvement and 48% of the association with ACSS, providing the first evidence of its critical mediating role in the severe phenotype of dnFSHD. These findings indicate that the phenotypic differences between dnFSHD and fFSHD stem primarily from the fundamental genetic characteristic of D4Z4 repeat array length, rather than the de novo status itself. This discovery offers a key genetic basis for the precise management of these patients. D4Z4 RU is the core mechanistic driver, whereas dnFSHD serves as an effective clinical classification for identifying high-risk patients in practice. Recognizing dnFSHD as a distinct subgroup facilitates the identification of high-risk patients in clinical practice. In future clinical research, we recommend analyzing dnFSHD as a distinct subgroup or incorporating D4Z4 repeat array length as a critical stratification factor to ensure the accuracy and reliability of study outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The determination of familial relationships relied primarily on patient-reported history supplemented by PFGE testing, without confirmatory paternity testing. PFGE-based Southern blotting may fail to detect low-level mosaicism, which could influence the true estimation of the de novo rate and, consequently, the interpretation of genotype-phenotype correlations. This study is retrospective in nature, with incomplete clinical records and loss of patient contact for a subset of cases, thereby precluding longitudinal assessments for the entire cohort. In addition, the analysis focused predominantly on the core genetic factors of D4Z4 repeat number and methylation levels. We did not screen for pathogenic variants in the FSHD2 genes SMCHD1, DNMT3B, and LRIF1, as well as additional epigenetic regulatory factors. Given that these modifiers can substantially influence disease severity, their absence limits the interpretability of the mediation analysis, which resulted in the inability to completely exclude or evaluate other potential confounding factors, such as patients' specific lifestyle, physical activity levels, comorbidities, or currently unidentified genetic modifiers. These factors may, to some extent, influence the severity and progression rate of the disease. Future studies incorporating these disease-modifying factors may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that dnFSHD represents a high-risk subtype of FSHD with distinct clinical characteristics, primarily mediated by shorter D4Z4 repeat array. These findings provide critical evidence to guide subsequent clinical evaluations, prognostic evaluation, and genetic counseling for patients with dnFSHD, while also offering a theoretical foundation for the design of clinical trials and patient stratification. Future research may further investigate additional genetic modifiers beyond D4Z4 repeat array length and develop predictive models of disease progression that integrate clinical and genetic data to advance precision medicine in FSHD.
