Error Estimates for the Interpolation and Approximation of Gradients and Vector Fields on Protected Delaunay Meshes in ℝ \mathbb{R} d
David M. Williams, Mathijs Wintraecken

Abstract
One frequently needs to interpolate or approximate gradients on simplicial meshes. Unfortunately, there are very few explicit mathematical results governing the interpolation or approximation of vector-valued functions on Delaunay meshes in more than two dimensions. Most of the existing results are tailored towards interpolation with piecewise linear polynomials. In contrast, interpolation with piecewise high-order polynomials is not well understood. In particular, the results in this area are sometimes difficult to immediately interpret, or to specialize to the Delaunay setting. In order to address this issue, we derive explicit error estimates for high-order, piecewise polynomial gradient interpolation and approximation on protected Delaunay meshes. In addition, we generalize our analysis beyond gradients, and obtain error estimates for sufficiently-smooth vector fields. Throughout the paper, we show that the quality of interpolation and approximation often depends (in part) on the minimum thickness of simplices in the mesh. Fortunately, the minimum thickness can be precisely controlled on protected Delaunay meshes in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} .



keywords
protected Delaunay, interpolation, high-order, higher-dimensions, error estimates

1
Introduction

The primary purpose of this article is to motivate the construction of
protected
Delaunay meshes in higher dimensions,
d>2
. In order to avoid confusion, we will refer to traditional Delaunay meshes which satisfy an empty-sphere criterion as
standard
Delaunay meshes. In contrast, a
protected
Delaunay mesh satisfies a modified empty-sphere criterion, where the sphere of each simplex is augmented by a spherical-buffer region. The original sphere with radius
R
, is effectively replaced by an augmented sphere with radius
R+r
. Here, we insist that
r\geq\delta
, for some
\delta>0
. In this context, the quantity
\delta
is called the
protection
. Broadly speaking, our goal is to make
\delta
as large as possible, as this has two positive ramifications: (i) it increases the minimum size of slivers in the Delaunay mesh, and (ii) it reduces the sensitivity of the Delaunay mesh to the locations of its points
Boissonnat et al. (2014)
. Here, we define a
sliver
element, as an element which has a very small ratio of its minimum altitude to its maximum edge length. In this work, we are primarily interested in slivers, as we can directly reduce the errors of gradient (or vector-field) interpolation and approximation by increasing the minimum size of slivers. Of course, in a
standard
Delaunay mesh without protection, the thickness of a sliver can become arbitrarily close to zero, (for
d>2
). Fortunately, in cases where the protection is non-zero, we obtain fatter simplices whose thickness is bounded away from zero. This fact was established in the pioneering work of Boissonnat, Dyer, and Ghosh
Boissonnat et al. (2013)
. More precisely, they showed that slivers naturally arise from pathological configurations of
d+2
vertices which are nearly co-spherical. These pathological configurations can be avoided by carefully perturbing the mesh points to facilitate the construction of a protected Delaunay mesh. A procedure for perturbing the points has been proposed in
Boissonnat et al. (2014)
. In addition, a detailed summary of protected Delaunay meshes appears in
Boissonnat et al. (2018)
.

The relationship between standard Delaunay meshes and protected Delaunay meshes is illustrated in Figure
1
.

A diagram of the relationship between standard Delaunay meshes and protected Delaunay meshes. The protection parameter is denoted by
\delta
.

Evidently, only a strict subset of all standard Delaunay meshes can be classified as protected Delaunay meshes.

Now, having established the concept of a protected Delaunay mesh, let us expand our discussion in order to present some broader perspectives on the entire field of Delaunay meshing. Generally speaking, standard Delaunay meshes have a very good reputation among scientists and engineers. This positive reputation has been documented in many places, including the excellent textbooks of Cheng, Dey, and Shewchuk
Cheng et al. (2013)
, and Borouchaki and George
Borouchaki and George (2017)
. However, in our opinion, the reputation of these meshes is mostly based on their optimality properties in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Unfortunately, there is a weaker justification for using standard Delaunay meshes in higher dimensions. In particular, Delaunay triangulations of nets (well-spaced point sets) still contain slivers in dimensions higher than
2
, while in 2D the quality of simplices in a standard Delaunay triangulation of a net is lower bounded (as mentioned previously).

In the next section, we will provide a short review of important properties of standard Delaunay meshes, and identify areas for potential improvement.

1.1
Background: Properties of Standard Delaunay Meshes

1.1.1
The Two-Dimensional Case

There are many important results which govern the shape and quality of triangles in standard Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For example, Sibson
Sibson (1978)
proved that a standard Delaunay triangulation of a point set
S
is guaranteed to maximize the minimum-interior angle of its triangles, relative to any other triangulation of the same points. In addition, Musin
Musin (1993)
;
Musin (1997)
proved that the standard Delaunay triangulation of
S
minimizes the average circumradius of triangles in the triangulation. In a similar fashion, Lambert
Lambert (1994)
proved that the standard Delaunay triangulation of
S
maximizes the average inradius. Furthermore, Musin
Musin (1995)
proved that the standard Delaunay triangulation minimizes the harmonic index functional, where the functional is the sum over each triangle of the squared edge lengths divided by the triangle area. In more recent work, Musin
Musin (2010)
conjectured that the mean radius functional and the
D
functional are minimized on standard Delaunay triangulations. Here, the mean radius functional is an area-weighted sum of the squared circumradii, and the
D
functional is an area-weighted sum of the squared distance between the barycenter and circumcenter of each triangle. Following this work, Edelsbrunner et al.
Edelsbrunner et al. (2017)
proved that the mean radius functional is minimized on standard Delaunay triangulations. In addition, they showed that the Voronoi functional is maximized on the same triangulations. For the sake of brevity, the precise formulation of the Voronoi functional will not be described here; the interested reader is encouraged to consult
Akopyan (2009)
for details. Furthermore, a detailed summary of the optimality properties of standard Delaunay triangulations appears in Sierra’s thesis
Sierra (2021)
.

Most of the results above, govern the shape-regularity of triangles in a standard Delaunay triangulation. Essentially, these results ensure that the triangles resemble equilateral triangles, as much as possible.

One may also obtain results which directly predict the interpolation or approximation accuracy of a standard Delaunay triangulation. In particular, Rippa
Rippa (1990)
and Powar
Powar (1992)
proved that the piecewise linear interpolations of
H^{1}
functions have minimal
roughness
on a standard Delaunay triangulation. Here, the roughness of the linear interpolation is defined as the integral of the squared magnitude of the gradient. This quantity naturally arises when a classical finite element method is applied to elliptic problems in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In
Rippa and Schiff (1990)
, Rippa and Schiff leveraged the roughness result of
Rippa (1990)
, and proved that standard Delaunay triangulations minimize the solution error for simple elliptic problems. Thereafter, Shewchuk
Shewchuk (2002)
performed an exhaustive study of piecewise linear interpolation on generic triangular and tetrahedral meshes. Here, Shewchuk presented techniques for improving interpolation error on meshes that are not necessarily Delaunay.

1.1.2
The
d
-Dimensional Case

There are fewer mathematical results for standard Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
when
d>2
. In
Rajan (1994)
, Rajan proved that the standard Delaunay mesh minimizes a functional of the weighted sum of the squared edge lengths, (see Theorem 1 of
Rajan (1994)
). In addition, Rajan proved that the standard Delaunay mesh minimizes the maximum, min-containment radius of simplices in the mesh, (see Theorem 2 of
Rajan (1994)
). These results are somewhat abstract, but they can easily be clarified with appropriate examples. In particular, Rajan’s functional in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is the sum over each 4-simplex of the 4-volume-weighted squared edge lengths of the simplex. Furthermore, the min-containment radius of a 4-simplex corresponds to the 3-sphere of minimum radius which contains the simplex.

Following the work of Rajan, Waldron
Waldron (1998)
proved that a standard Delaunay mesh minimizes the infinity error for the piecewise linear interpolation of a
C^{1}
function with pointwise-bounded second derivatives, (see Theorem 3.1 of
Waldron (1998)
). We note that Waldron’s work implicitly leverages the min-containment radius result of Rajan
Rajan (1994)
. In particular, Waldron shows that the infinity error of the linear interpolation of a
C^{1}
function over a single simplex is bounded above by the min-containment radius of the simplex, multiplied by an
L^{\infty}
-norm of the function’s second derivatives. Evidently, standard Delaunay meshes minimize the maximum min-containment radius for all simplices in the entire mesh, and therefore, provide an optimal upper bound for the pointwise, piecewise linear interpolation error.

In addition, one can prove that the standard Delaunay mesh provides optimal piecewise linear interpolation of the quadratic function,
\left|\bm{x}\right|^{2}+\bm{a}\cdot\bm{x}+b
, where
\bm{x}
is a generic point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}

Cheng et al. (2013)
. For this function, the standard Delaunay mesh minimizes the error in the
L_{p}
-norm for
p\geq 1
. This result has been leveraged in order to construct objective functions for
optimal Delaunay triangulations
, (ODTs)—see the pioneering work of Chen and Xu
Chen and Xu (2004)
. In particular, ODTs are defined based on an energy functional or objective function which takes a mesh as input. Let
f:\bm{x}\rightarrow|\bm{x}|^{2}
be the parabola.
A given mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
with length-scale
h
, induces a piecewise linear interpolation
f_{\mathrm{pl}}
, which coincides with
f
on the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and is a linear interpolation on each simplex. The energy functional (objective function) is now defined as the integrated error that the PL-interpolation makes, that is
\mathcal{F}_{\textrm{ODT}}(\mathcal{T}_{h})=\left\|f-f_{\mathrm{pl}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
. An optimal Delaunay triangulation is a mesh that minimizes this functional within the class of meshes with the same number of vertices. We stress that this means that both the positions of the vertices and the combinatorics of the mesh are not fixed in this optimization.
However, because for a fixed set of vertices the Delaunay triangulation minimizes
\mathcal{F}_{\textrm{ODT}}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, the result is always a Delaunay triangulation (assuming the vertices are in general position).
From our perspective, the only issues with the ODT approach are, (a) the lack of theoretical guarantees on the minimum size of slivers, (b) the inherent focus on piecewise linear interpolation, and (c) the potential presence of combinatorial instabilities.

Finally, we note that Musin
Musin (1997)
proved that a parabolic functional is minimized on standard Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This functional consists of a volume-weighted sum over each simplex of the squared vertex locations.

1.2
Summary of Existing Literature and New Contributions

Some of the most important work on the interpolation and approximation properties of standard Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
appears to be that of Rajan
Rajan (1994)
, Waldron
Waldron (1998)
, Chen, Xu, and coworkers
Chen and Xu (2004)
;
Chen and Holst (2011)
, and Musin
Musin (1997)
. In particular, the work of Waldron
Waldron (1998)
guarantees that standard Delaunay meshes minimize the pointwise, piecewise linear interpolation error of
C^{1}
functions with pointwise-bounded second derivatives. Unfortunately, this work is incomplete, as it does not apply to interpolation or approximation with high-order, piecewise polynomial functions.

Of course, there are many general results on high-order, piecewise polynomial interpolation and approximation on simplicial meshes—see for example the excellent finite element textbooks of Ern and Guermond
Ern and Guermond (2004)
;
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
;
Ern and Guermond (2021b)
;
Ern and Guermond (2021c)
, Brenner and Scott
Brenner and Scott (2008)
, and Di Pietro and Ern
Di Pietro and Ern (2011)
. However, these results often contain constants which depend on the mesh properties in an implicit or unspecified fashion. In addition, even the more precise results have not been specifically adapted to the context of protected Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the current paper, we provide a new set of explicit results, which establish error estimates for gradient interpolation and approximation, as well as vector-field interpolation, on protected Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our results are simultaneously more general and more specific than previous results: they are more general than previous results for standard Delaunay meshes which focused on piecewise linear interpolation, and they are more specific than previous high-order finite-element-based results which (in most cases) were not explicitly written with Delaunay meshes in mind.

1.3
Alternatives

For the sake of completeness, we note that protected Delaunay meshes are not the only avenue for addressing slivers. There are sliver-removal techniques, such as those discussed in
Cheng et al. (2000)
;
Edelsbrunner et al. (2000)
;
Li (2003)
;
Cheng and Dey (2003)
;
Tournois et al. (2009)
;
Boissonnat and Ghosh (2014)
and the references therein. However, in what remains of this work, we will primarily focus on protected Delaunay meshes, due to their elegant and concrete mathematical structure. This structure will directly facilitate our error estimates.

1.4
Paper Approach and Outline

In this work, we proceed in a straightforward fashion, and leverage vector calculus in conjunction with classical interpolation theory in order to construct error estimates. Briefly, our approach involves extending the notion of roughness, originally introduced by Rippa
Rippa (1990)
for gradients in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, to the case of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thereafter, we use this notion, along with similar ideas, to obtain a set of error estimates which govern gradient (and vector-field) interpolation and approximation on protected Delaunay meshes.

The format of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we motivate the present work by introducing a canonical, elliptic problem in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In addition, we expand the definition of roughness, originally introduced by Rippa
Rippa (1990)
, into higher dimensions. In Section
3
, we discuss the fundamental characteristics of meshes and point sets. In Sections
4
and
5
, we present new explicit error estimates that govern the interpolation and approximation of gradients on protected Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In Section
6
, we extend the results of Section
4
, in order to establish error estimates for vector fields. Finally, in Section
7
, we present some concluding remarks.

2
Mathematical Preliminaries

In this section, we define some important notation. Thereafter, we introduce an example problem in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Next, we define the notion of roughness for this problem, and explain the relationship between roughness and the solution error.
Finally, we outline our strategy for constructing error estimates.

2.1
Notation

Consider a simply-connected, polytopal domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. On this domain, we can define the Lebesgue space
L_{\varrho}(\Omega)
and its associated norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L_{\varrho}(\Omega)}
with
1\leq\varrho<\infty
as follows

\displaystyle L_{\varrho}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{f\,\Bigg|\,\int_{\Omega}\left|f\right|^{\varrho}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\varrho}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left|f\right|^{\varrho}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/\varrho},

where
f=f(\bm{x})=f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}
is a generic scalar function. For vector-valued functions, the Lebesgue space is associated with the following norm

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{f}\right\|_{L_{\varrho}(\Omega)}=\left[\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|f_{i}\right|^{\varrho}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/\varrho},

where
\bm{f}=\bm{f}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a generic vector-valued function. In a similar fashion, for matrix-valued functions, we can define the following norm

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{F}\right\|_{L_{\varrho}(\Omega)}=\left[\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|F_{ij}\right|^{\varrho}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/\varrho},

where
\bm{F}=\bm{F}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a generic matrix-valued function.

For the case of
\varrho=\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle L_{\infty}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{f\,\Bigg|\,\esssup_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\left|f(\bm{x})\right|<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\esssup_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\left|f(\bm{x})\right|,\qquad\left\|\bm{f}\right\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}=\esssup_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\left(\sup_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left|f_{i}(\bm{x})\right|\right),

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{F}\right\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\esssup_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\left(\sup_{i,j=1,\ldots,d}\left|F_{ij}(\bm{x})\right|\right).

We note that alternative infinity-norm definitions, such as

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\bm{f}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}=\esssup_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}\left(\left\|\bm{f}(\bm{x})\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\right),

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ell^{2}}
is the Euclidean norm on
\bm{f}
, are equivalent to the definitions above, as

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{f}\right\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\bm{f}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\sqrt{d}\left\|\bm{f}\right\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Next, we can define the following Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{f\in L_{2}(\Omega)\,|D^{\bm{\alpha}}f\in L_{2}(\Omega),|\bm{\alpha}|\leq 1\right\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{f\in H^{1}(\Omega)\,|\,f=0\quad\mathrm{on}\quad\partial\Omega\right\},

where

\displaystyle D^{\bm{\alpha}}f

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{|\bm{\alpha}|}f}{\partial x^{\alpha_{1}}\partial x^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\partial x^{\alpha_{d}}},\qquad\bm{\alpha}=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\qquad|\bm{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}.

Evidently, the associated norms are

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(f^{2}+\nabla f\cdot\nabla f\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/2},

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla f\cdot\nabla f\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/2},

where
\nabla=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}},\ldots,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}}\right)
.

Finally, we can define the general space
W^{1,\varrho}(\Omega)
and the associated norms

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{W^{1,\varrho}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(|f|^{\varrho}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right|^{\varrho}\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/\varrho},

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{f}\right\|_{W^{1,\varrho}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|f_{i}|^{\varrho}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\right|^{\varrho}\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/\varrho},

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{F}\right\|_{W^{1,\varrho}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|F_{ij}|^{\varrho}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left|\frac{\partial F_{ij}}{\partial x_{k}}\right|^{\varrho}\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}\right]^{1/\varrho},

where
1\leq\varrho<\infty
. Evidently, norms on the space
W^{2,\varrho}(\Omega)
are defined in an analogous fashion, with zeroth-, first-, and second-derivative terms.

2.2
Elliptic Problem in 4D

Now, consider a polytopal domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
. We are interested in solving the following elliptic problem on
\Omega

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}u

\displaystyle=f,\qquad\text{in}\quad\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega,

where
u
is the twice-differentiable solution that vanishes on the boundary
\partial\Omega
,
f
is a forcing function in
L_{2}(\Omega)
, and
\nabla^{2}=\nabla\cdot\nabla\left(\cdot\right)
is the four-dimensional Laplacian. The four-dimensional gradient operator is given by
\nabla=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\frac{\partial}{\partial y},\frac{\partial}{\partial z},\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\right)
.

We can formulate a finite element method for solving Eq. (
1
) by replacing
u
with
u_{h}
, multiplying by the test function
v_{h}
, integrating over the domain
\Omega
, and integrating by parts as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,dV-\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial x}n_{x}\,dydzdw-\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial y}n_{y}\,dxdzdw

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial z}n_{z}\,dxdydw-\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{h}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial w}n_{w}\,dxdydz=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}\,dV,

where
u_{h},v_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
dV=dxdydzdw
. Here,
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
are usually taken to be piecewise-polynomial functions. The subscript
h
’s on the functions indicate that they are defined on a mesh, with a characteristic length scale
h
.

Next, boundary conditions can be enforced by choosing
u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
so that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,dV=\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}\,dV.

We can rewrite the expression above in terms of more familiar notation

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})=L_{h}(v_{h}),

(2)

where

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,dV

(3)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial z}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial w}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial w}\right)dxdydzdw,

\displaystyle L_{h}(v_{h})

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}\,dV.

It is well known that Eq. (
2
) has a unique solution when
a_{h}
is symmetric, bilinear, and governed by the following constraints

\displaystyle|a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})|\leq\sigma\left\|u_{h}\right\|\left\|v_{h}\right\|,\qquad\tau\left\|v_{h}\right\|^{2}\leq a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h}),

(4)

where
\sigma
and
\tau
are positive constants, and

\displaystyle\left\|v_{h}\right\|=\left\|v_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\equiv\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(v_{h}^{2}+\nabla v_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\right)dV\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left[\int_{\Omega}\left(v_{h}^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial y}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial z}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial w}\right)^{2}\right)dV\right]^{1/2}.

Next, we can define the energy norm

\displaystyle\left\|v_{h}\right\|_{a}\equiv\sqrt{a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h})}=\left\|\nabla v_{h}\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

In accordance with standard elliptical theory, we can introduce the following energy functional

\displaystyle J(v_{h})\equiv a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h})-2L_{h}(v_{h}).

(5)

Here,
J(v_{h})
can be interpreted as the potential energy associated with a membrane, and
v_{h}
can be interpreted as the transverse deflection of the membrane (see
Oden (2025)
). In addition,
a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h})
characterizes the potential energy associated with internal forces, and
L_{h}(v_{h})
characterizes the energy associated with a force per unit area,
f
, applied to the membrane.

It can be shown that the minimum of the functional
J(v_{h})
is the solution of Eq. (
2
), (see
Sayas et al. (2019)
, Lemma 2.2). More precisely

\displaystyle J(u_{h})=\min_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}J(v_{h}).

Now, we are ready to introduce the notion of roughness, and its relationship to solution error. In particular, suppose that we create a pair of meshes,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, for our domain
\Omega
. These meshes are distinct, and do not necessarily possess the same number of simplices. However, we assume that these meshes both tessellate (i.e. triangulate) the same set of points and the same domain. Due to the bilinearity and symmetry of
a_{h}
, the following equality holds

\displaystyle\left\|u-u_{h,1}\right\|^{2}_{a}=J(u_{h,1})-J(u_{h,2})+\left\|u-u_{h,2}\right\|^{2}_{a}.

Naturally, if we choose
\mathcal{T}_{1}
such that

\displaystyle J(u_{h,1})\leq J(u_{h,2}),

then it follows that

\displaystyle\left\|u-u_{h,1}\right\|_{a}\leq\left\|u-u_{h,2}\right\|_{a}.

Therefore, we seek to minimize
J(u_{h})
in order to minimize the error as measured by the energy norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{a}
. In turn, due to the definition of
J(u_{h})
(see Eq. (
5
)), we seek meshes
\mathcal{T}_{1}
that minimize the quantity
a_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})
. This quantity is often called the
roughness
of the mesh.

2.3
Roughness in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

It turns out that the measure of roughness that we identified in the previous section can be extended to any number of dimensions
d
. In particular, one may define

\displaystyle a(v,v)\equiv\int_{\Omega}\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{m}}\right)^{2}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}=\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

(6)

as the measure of roughness in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, we have omitted the subscript
h
from the quantities
a
and
v
in order to simplify the notation. We note that the roughness functional (above) is merely the square of the norm on
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, introduced previously.

2.4
Alternative Strategy

In the previous sections, the primary objective was to minimize the roughness, and thereby minimize the error of the elliptic problem. Unfortunately, directly minimizing the roughness is difficult in higher dimensions,
d>2
. In this higher-dimensional setting, it is easier to minimize the distance between the exact gradient and the approximate gradient. More precisely, we seek to minimize

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u-\mathcal{I}(\nabla u)\right\|,

(7)

and in a similar fashion

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}\right\|,

(8)

where
\|\cdot\|
is a suitable norm over the domain
\Omega
, and
\mathcal{I}(\cdot)
is an interpolation operator that depends on the mesh. The error in Eq. (
7
) is the
gradient interpolation error
, i.e. the error associated with interpolating the gradient of
u
, denoted by
\mathcal{I}(\nabla u)
. Conversely, the error in Eq. (
8
) is the
gradient approximation error
, i.e. the error associated with approximating the gradient of
u_{h}
, denoted by
\nabla u_{h}
. With this distinction in mind, we will construct the following upper bounds on the gradient interpolation error on a mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u-\mathcal{I}(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{\lambda}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}\left\|\nabla(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{\lambda}(\Omega)},

(9)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla u-\mathcal{I}(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}\left\|\nabla(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{2\varrho}(\Omega)},

(10)

where
C
is a generic constant that is independent of the mesh,
\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
is a constant that depends on the mesh,
\mathdutchcal{h}
is a characteristic length scale associated with the mesh,
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
1\leq\lambda\leq\infty
,
1<\varrho\leq\infty
, and
\nabla(\nabla u)
is the Hessian of
u
. Evidently, these bounds only apply in cases where the exact solution
u
is sufficiently smooth. In addition, we note that
r
should not be confused with the radius which appeared in the Introduction.

In addition, we will construct upper bounds on the gradient approximation error

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

(11)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}\left\|\nabla(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

(12)

It is very important to note that all of the inequalities in Eqs. (
9
)–(
12
), are
dependent
on the mesh via the constants
\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
or the mesh spacing
\mathdutchcal{h}
. This suggests that some meshes may be better than others. In fact, we will show that protected Delaunay meshes allow for precise control over
\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
and (to some extent)
\mathdutchcal{h}
.

3
Fundamentals of Meshing

3.1
General Properties

Let us introduce a finite set of points
S

\displaystyle S=\left\{\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2},\ldots,\bm{p}_{l},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N_{v}}\right\},\qquad 1\leq l\leq N_{v},

where the cardinality
|S|=N_{v}
, and each point
\bm{p}_{l}
is contained within a bounded, simply-connected domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the sake of simplicity, we let
\mathrm{conv}(S)=\Omega
. Furthermore, we assume that
S
is a
(\varepsilon,\overline{\eta})
-net, which satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\Omega,\exists\bm{p}_{l}\in S

\displaystyle:\qquad\left|\bm{x}-\bm{p}_{l}\right|\leq\varepsilon,

(13)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{p}_{l},\bm{p}_{n}\in S

\displaystyle:\qquad\left|\bm{p}_{l}-\bm{p}_{n}\right|\geq\eta,

(14)

where
\varepsilon>0
,
\eta>0
, and
\overline{\eta}=\eta/\varepsilon
. Together, Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) control the density of the points in
S
.

Now, let us introduce the generic mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
whose vertices are the points of
S
, and whose elements
K
are non-overlapping. We assume that the mesh is a
triangulation
of the domain. The word
triangulation
can have slightly different connotations in different fields, so let us be more precise. We assume that each element
K
is a
d
-simplex equipped with
(d+1)
-facets of dimension
d-1
. In addition, we assume that each facet is either a boundary facet (with precisely one
d
-simplex neighbor) or an interior facet (with precisely two
d
-simplex neighbors). As a result, the mesh does not contain any hanging nodes. In accordance with these assumptions (above), we say that the mesh is a
manifold
: i.e. a pure simplicial
d
-complex which is
d
-connected, homeomorphic to the underlying space, and for which each
(d-1)
-simplex has exactly one or two
d
-simplex neighbors.

The
d+1
vertices of each simplex
K
are denoted by
\bm{p}_{K,i}
, where
i=1,\ldots,d+1
. The coordinates of each vertex are given by

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{K,i}=\left(p_{K,i}^{1},\,p_{K,i}^{2},\ldots,\,p_{K,i}^{m},\ldots,\,p_{K,i}^{d}\right)^{T},

where
1\leq m\leq d
. The absolute value of a vertex can be defined in terms of the absolute values of its components, as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,i})=\left(\left|p_{K,i}^{1}\right|,\,\left|p_{K,i}^{2}\right|,\ldots,\,\left|p_{K,i}^{m}\right|,\ldots,\,\left|p_{K,i}^{d}\right|\right)^{T}.

Note that
|\bm{p}_{K,i}|
and
\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,i})
are not equivalent quantities, as the former is the scalar magnitude of a
d
-vector, and the latter is a
d
-vector of absolute values.

Lastly, we can define the
d(d+1)/2
edges of each simplex
K
as follows

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{K,ij}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{K,j}-\bm{p}_{K,i}=\left(p_{K,ij}^{1},\,p_{K,ij}^{2},\ldots,\,p_{K,ij}^{m},\ldots,\,p_{K,ij}^{d}\right)^{T},

where
1\leq i\leq d+1
,
1\leq j\leq i-1
, and
1\leq m\leq d
.

3.2
Sizing Parameters

Let us denote the diameter of
K
by
\Delta(K)
, where the diameter is the longest edge length of
K
. In addition, we can define the maximum diameter of an element in the entire mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, as follows

\displaystyle h\equiv\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Delta(K).

Furthermore, we can define the ratio of the maximum to the minimum element diameters

\displaystyle C_{\Delta}\equiv\frac{\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Delta(K)}{\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Delta(K)}=\frac{h}{\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Delta(K)}.

(15)

Next, we can introduce the radius of the min-containment ball for the element
K
, which is denoted by
R_{K,\min}
. Generally speaking, we have that

\displaystyle\Delta(K)\leq 2R_{K,\min}.

(16)

We can define the maximum radius of the min-containment ball as follows

\displaystyle R_{\max}\equiv\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}R_{K,\min}.

(17)

Next, in accordance with
Boissonnat et al. (2012)
, we can define the thickness of a
d
-simplex
K

\displaystyle\Xi(K)\equiv\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\;d=0\\
\min_{s}\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)}{d\,\Delta(K)}\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(18)

where
1\leq s\leq d+1
, and the function
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
returns the shortest distance between the vertex
\bm{p}_{K,s}
and the affine hull of its opposite facet,
\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})
. The minimum thickness can be denoted by

\displaystyle C_{\Xi}\equiv\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\Xi(K)).

(19)

Naturally, the minimum thickness will be small for meshes which contain sliver elements, and will be large in the absence of slivers.

We can also define the following mesh-regularity parameter

\displaystyle\sigma(K)=\frac{\Delta(K)}{\rho(K)},

(20)

where
\rho(K)
is the diameter of the insphere of the element
K
. In addition, we can denote the maximum value of this parameter as

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}\equiv\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\sigma(K)).

(21)

Lastly, we introduce two functionals which act on the entire mesh.

Definition 1

(Rajan’s Functional)
.

This functional was originally identified by Rajan
Rajan (1994)
. It is the ‘weighted-sum of edge lengths squared’

\displaystyle\Theta\equiv\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\Theta(K)),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\Theta(K)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\bm{p}_{K,ij}\cdot\bm{p}_{K,ij}\right)|K|,

(23)

and
|K|
is the
d
-volume of the element
K
. Rajan’s version of the functional is obtained by rescaling the result by a factor of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(d+1)(d+2)}.

Definition 2

(Max Edge-Length Functional)
.

The following functional arises naturally during the subsequent analysis

\displaystyle C_{\Upsilon}\equiv\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\Upsilon(K)\right),

(24)

where

\displaystyle\Upsilon(K)=\frac{1}{\Delta(K)}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\bm{p}_{K,ij}\cdot\bm{p}_{K,ij}\right)}.

(25)

This functional is the maximum root-mean-square (RMS) sum of the squared edge lengths, normalized by the element diameter.

3.3
Delaunay Properties

The following statements hold true for a standard Delaunay (or protected Delaunay) mesh:

1.

The maximum min-containment ball radius,
R_{\max}
(see Eq. (
17
)) is minimized on a standard Delaunay mesh. This statement was proven in Theorem 2 of
Rajan (1994)
.

2.

Rajan’s functional
\Theta
(see Eq. (
22
)) is minimized on a standard Delaunay mesh. This statement was proven in Theorem 1 of
Rajan (1994)
.

3.

The minimum thickness
C_{\Xi}
(see Eq. (
19
)) is lower bounded on a protected Delaunay mesh.

4.

The mesh regularity parameter
C_{\sigma}
(see Eq. (
21
)) is upper bounded on a protected Delaunay mesh.

The latter two statements require additional elaboration (see below).

Remark 1

(Protected Delaunay Meshes and Thickness)
.

Broadly speaking, the minimum element thickness is difficult to control in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for arbitrary
d
. Fortunately, in Lemma 5.27 of
Boissonnat et al. (2018)
, Boissonnat and coworkers proved that the minimum thickness of simplices is bounded below on a protected Delaunay mesh, as follows

\displaystyle C_{\Xi}\geq\frac{\delta^{2}}{8d\varepsilon^{2}}.

(26)

Therefore, the minimum element thickness depends quadratically on the protection,
\delta
.

Remark 2

(Protected Delaunay Meshes and Regularity)
.

The mesh regularity parameter
C_{\sigma}
is inversely proportional to the minimum thickness
C_{\Xi}
. In order to see this, one must derive an inverse relationship between
\sigma(K)
and

\Xi(K)
. Towards this end, we introduce the following well-known identity
1
1

1

This identity can be deduced as follows. The barycenter is a distance of
1/(d+1)
times the smallest altitude from the closest face. This holds due to the properties of the barycentric coordinates. One may then observe that the resulting distance is a lower bound for the radius of the insphere.
involving the diameter of the insphere of the element
K

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(K)}{2}\geq\frac{1}{d+1}\min_{s}\left(\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right).

(27)

Upon dividing both sides of Eq. (
27
) by
\Delta(K)
, one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(K)}{\Delta(K)}\geq\frac{2}{d+1}\frac{\min_{s}\left(\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right)}{\Delta(K)}.

Equivalently, in accordance with Eq. (
18
)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho(K)}{\Delta(K)}\geq\frac{2d}{d+1}\min_{s}\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)}{d\,\Delta(K)}\right)=\frac{2d}{d+1}\Xi(K).

Therefore, by Eq. (
20
)

\displaystyle\sigma(K)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d+1}{2d}\frac{1}{\Xi(K)},

which is the desired relationship between
\sigma(K)
and
\Xi(K)
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\sigma(K))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d+1}{2d}\frac{1}{\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\Xi(K))}=\frac{d+1}{2d}\frac{1}{C_{\Xi}}.

Upon extracting the following inequality

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d+1}{2d}\frac{1}{C_{\Xi}},

and combining with Eq. (
26
), one obtains

\displaystyle C_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d+1}{2d}\frac{8d\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}=4(d+1)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}.

(28)

Therefore, the parameter
C_{\sigma}
is bounded above on a protected Delaunay mesh, where the upper bound is inversely proportional to the square of the protection,
\delta
.

Remark 3

(Maximum Protection)
.

In light of the results above, we seek protected Delaunay meshes with a maximal level of protection
\delta
for arbitrary
d
. The current best procedure for generating unstructured, protected Delaunay meshes is given by
Boissonnat et al. (2018)
. Here, the protection is guaranteed to be at least

\displaystyle\delta\sim\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{2^{d^{2}}}\right).

(29)

Alternatively, the current best procedure for generating structured protected Delaunay meshes is given by Coxeter reflection
Choudhary et al. (2020)
. In particular, the Coxeter triangulations of type
\widetilde{A}_{d}
are protected Delaunay triangulations, which are guaranteed to have the following protection

\displaystyle\delta\sim\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\right).

(30)

This level of protection is the largest of any known triangulation.

Finally, we note that the gap between the protection estimates for the unstructured and structured cases (Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
), respectively) can likely be improved. In particular, the protection estimate for the unstructured case decays exponentially with the number of dimensions
d
, and is therefore, an example of the ‘curse of dimensionality’. However, given the exceptional quality of the structured estimate, we are optimistic that the unstructured estimate can be improved.

4
Gradient Interpolation: Results for General Problems

In this section, our objective is to construct error estimates for gradient interpolation, for general (not necessarily elliptic) problems. These estimates take the following form

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla v-\mathcal{I}(\nabla v)\right\|_{L_{\lambda}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}\left\|\nabla(\nabla v)\right\|_{L_{\lambda}(\Omega)},

(31)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla v-\mathcal{I}(\nabla v)\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}\left\|\nabla(\nabla v)\right\|_{L_{2\varrho}(\Omega)},

(32)

where
1\leq\lambda\leq\infty
and
1<\varrho\leq\infty
. Throughout this section, we assume that
v
is a sufficiently smooth function, defined on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

In what follows, we refer to Eq. (
31
) as the
L_{\lambda}
error bound
, due to the presence of the
L_{\lambda}
norm on the left hand side of this equation. Similarly, we refer to Eq. (
32
) as the
L_{2}
error bound
due to the presence of the
L_{2}
norm on the left hand side of this equation.

We will first establish the
L_{2}
error bound in Eq. (
32
), as this result is the most complex, and illustrates all of the important tools for proving the remaining error bound.

4.1
L_{2}
Error Bound

Let us explain our intuition for the proof, before proceeding further. As we discussed previously, Rajan’s functional is a weighted summation of squared edge-lengths across all elements in the mesh,
\mathcal{T}_{h}
(see Definition
1
). A Delaunay mesh minimizes this edge functional, relative to all other triangulations of the same point set. Our objective is to leverage this edge functional to construct an error estimate for the gradient. In particular, we observe that it is possible to formulate a directional derivative

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{p}_{K,ij}}v\equiv\nabla v\cdot\bm{p}_{K,ij},

(33)

where
\nabla_{\bm{p}_{K,ij}}v
is the derivative of
v
in the direction of the edge
\bm{p}_{K,ij}
. With this observation in mind, we can construct a
roughness
functional of the directional derivative. Thereafter, we can prove the equivalence of the roughness functional and the
L_{2}
-norm of the gradient. Finally, we can use this equivalence, in conjunction with an interpolation estimate, to create an upper bound for the
L_{2}
-norm of the gradient error in terms of Rajan’s functional and the Hessian of
v
. Each of these steps are carried out in what follows.

Definition 3

(Roughness Functional)
.

Consider the following, non-negative functional of
v
over the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\Psi(\nabla v)\equiv\left[\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\int_{K}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\mathrm{abs}(\nabla v)\cdot\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,ij})\right)^{2}dV\right]^{1/2},

(34)

where we assume that
v
is a piecewise-
H^{1}
-scalar field on
\Omega
, and
h_{K}
is a characteristic length scale associated with each
K
. In principle,
h_{K}
can be chosen as any length scale associated with the element, such as the diameter
\Delta(K)
, insphere diameter
\rho(K)
, min-containment ball radius
R_{K,\min}
, or even the circumsphere diameter. However, for our purposes, it will often be most convenient to set
h_{K}=\Delta(K)
.

We note that the presence of the
\mathrm{abs}
symbols (in Eq. (
34
)) makes it easier for us to construct a lower bound for
\Psi(\nabla v)
in the work that follows.

Definition 4

(Gradient Norm)
.

Consider the following gradient norm of
v
over the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\equiv\left[\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{K}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\,dV\right]^{1/2},

(35)

where we assume that
v
is a piecewise-
H^{1}
-scalar field on
\Omega
.

We are now ready to show that the roughness functional and the gradient norm are equivalent.

Lemma 1

(Equivalence of the Roughness Functional and the Gradient Norm)
.

The functional in Definition
3
and the gradient norm in Definition
4
are equivalent in the following sense

\displaystyle C_{d}\,C_{\Xi}\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq\Psi(\nabla v)\leq C_{\Upsilon}\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

(36)

where
v
resides in the space of piecewise-
H^{1}
-scalar fields on
\Omega
,
C_{\Xi}
and
C_{\Upsilon}
are constants that depend on the mesh, and

\displaystyle C_{d}=\sqrt{\frac{d(d+1)}{2}}.

(37)

Proof.

We start by establishing the lower bound and recovering
C_{\Xi}
. From the definition of
\Psi(\nabla v)
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\Psi(\nabla v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\int_{K}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\mathrm{abs}(\nabla v)\cdot\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,ij})\right)^{2}dV

(38)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\int_{K}\min_{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)dV,

where we have used the following sequence of inequalities

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\mathrm{abs}(\nabla v)\cdot\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,ij})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d}|(\nabla v)^{m}||p_{K,ij}^{m}|\right)^{2}

(39)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left(|(\nabla v)^{m}||p_{K,ij}^{m}|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left((\nabla v)^{m}\right)^{2}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left[\left((\nabla v)^{m}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\min_{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}\right)\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left((\nabla v)^{m}\right)^{2}.

The second line of Eq. (
39
) follows from the observation that the sum of the squares is less than the square of the sum in cases where all the terms are non-negative.

Let us return our attention to Eq. (
38
). This equation is the centerpiece of our analysis; however the RHS of this equation is difficult to interpret in its current form. In particular, it remains for us to bound the first term in the integrand of Eq. (
38
)—i.e. the argument of the minimum function

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}.

(40)

Bounding this term requires a fairly lengthy process (see Appendix A), which leverages various geometric properties of the simplex in higher dimensions. This process yields the following inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d+1}{2d}\left(\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2},

(41)

where we recall that the function
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
returns the shortest distance between the vertex
\bm{p}_{K,s}
and the affine hull of its opposite facet,
\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})
.
Next, substituting Eq. (
41
) into Eq. (
38
), and setting
h_{K}=\Delta(K)
, yields

\displaystyle\left(\Psi(\nabla v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{\Delta(K)^{2}}\int_{K}\min_{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)dV

(42)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d+1}{2d}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{\Delta(K)^{2}}\int_{K}\min_{m}\left(\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]\right)^{2}\left(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)dV

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d+1}{2d}\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\frac{\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]}{\Delta(K)}\right)^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{K}(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v)\,dV

\displaystyle=\frac{d+1}{2d}\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\frac{\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]}{\Delta(K)}\right)^{2}\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Upon rewriting Eq. (
42
) by setting

\displaystyle\min_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\frac{\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]}{\Delta(K)}\right)^{2}=d^{2}C_{\Xi}^{2},

(43)

using the identity in Eq. (
19
), and taking the square root of both sides, we obtain the desired lower bound for
\Psi(\nabla v)
.

Next, we will construct the upper bound for the roughness functional and recover the constant
C_{\Upsilon}
. From the definition of the functional, we have

\displaystyle\left(\Psi(\nabla v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\int_{K}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\mathrm{abs}(\nabla v)\cdot\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,ij})\right)^{2}dV

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\int_{K}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\mathrm{abs}(\nabla v)\cdot\mathrm{abs}(\nabla v)\right)\left(\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,ij})\cdot\mathrm{abs}(\bm{p}_{K,ij})\right)dV

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\int_{K}\left(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\bm{p}_{K,ij}\cdot\bm{p}_{K,ij}\right)dV

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\bm{p}_{K,ij}\cdot\bm{p}_{K,ij}\right)\int_{K}\left(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)dV

\displaystyle\leq\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\frac{1}{h_{K}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(\bm{p}_{K,ij}\cdot\bm{p}_{K,ij}\right)\right)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{K}\left(\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)dV

Now, let us set the previous results aside, and shift our attention to the topic of interpolation. In what follows, we introduce some fundamental results which govern the interpolation of the gradient on our canonical mesh

Definition 5

(Local Lagrange Interpolation)
.

In a fundamental sense, Lagrange interpolation creates a set of polynomial functions which interpolate (recover) the values of a given function at a set of distinct interpolation points. The value of the interpolant at a particular interpolation point is only supported by a single Lagrange polynomial function, and the value of the interpolant between points is the summation of contributions from all Lagrange polynomials. In order to exactly represent a polynomial of degree
\leq k
, the Lagrange polynomials must be constructed based on a total of
N_{p}
interpolation points, where

\displaystyle N_{p}=\frac{(k+d)!}{k!d!}.

To fix ideas, let us consider the Lagrange interpolation of a function
f(x)
at a set of interpolation points
\{\xi_{j}\}
in one dimension, where
1\leq j\leq(k+1)
. Note that
x
denotes the coordinate in physical space, and
\xi
denotes the coordinate in reference space. The concepts of a physical space and a reference space are extremely common for the construction of finite element methods, (see the discussion in Chapter 1 of
Hughes (2003)
). The Lagrange polynomials of degree
k
are explicitly written as

\displaystyle L_{j}(\xi)=\frac{(\xi-\xi_{1})}{(\xi_{j}-\xi_{1})}\cdots\frac{(\xi-\xi_{j-1})}{(\xi_{j}-\xi_{j-1})}\frac{(\xi-\xi_{j+1})}{(\xi_{j}-\xi_{j+1})}\cdots\frac{(\xi-\xi_{k+1})}{(\xi_{j}-\xi_{k+1})}.

In addition, the Lagrange interpolant is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}f(x_{j})L_{j}(\xi(x)),

where each
x_{j}=x(\xi_{j})
is an interpolation point in physical space, the quantity
x(\xi)
is a map between the reference and physical spaces, and the quantity
\xi(x)
is the inverse map between the physical and reference spaces.

With the above discussion in mind, consider the following local interpolation operator
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\cdot)
applied to the gradient
\nabla v
on element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{K}((\nabla v)^{i})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\right),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{p}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}(\bm{x}_{j})L_{j}(\bm{\xi}(\bm{x})),

(55)

and where
i=1,\ldots,d
are coordinate indexes,
\bm{x}
is a vector of coordinates in physical space
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})
on the physical element
K
,
\bm{\xi}
is a vector of coordinates in reference space
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{d})
on the reference element
\widehat{K}
,
\bm{\xi}(\bm{x})
is the map from physical space to reference space,
\bm{x}(\bm{\xi})
is the inverse map from reference space to physical space,
\bm{x}_{j}=\bm{x}(\bm{\xi}_{j})
is an interpolation point on the element
K
, and
L_{j}(\bm{\xi})
is a multi-dimensional Lagrange polynomial which assumes the value of one at
\bm{\xi}_{j}
and zero at all other interpolation points. One may consult Figure
2
for an illustration of the relationship between the physical and reference space elements in three dimensions.

\displaystyle\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\qquad\left(\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\right)\bigg|_{K}\equiv\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg|_{K}\right),

(56)

where
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\cdot)
is defined in Eq. (

Note that, we will often apply the local or global interpolation operators

and

to vector functions. When this happens, we assume that the interpolation operators are applied component-wise, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\nabla v\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{1}}\right),\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{2}}\right),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{K}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{d}}\right)\right),

(57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\nabla v\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{1}}\right),\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{2}}\right),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{d}}\right)\right).

(58)

(Functional Estimate for Gradient Interpolation)

A measure of the error between the gradient
\nabla v

\displaystyle\Psi(\nabla v-\mathcal{I}_{h}(\nabla v))\leq C_{\Delta}C_{\mathrm{int}}C_{\varrho}R_{\max}^{(1/\varrho)}\,\Theta^{((\varrho-1)/2\varrho)}\left\|\nabla(\nabla v)\right\|_{L_{2\varrho}(\Omega)},

\Omega
,

Proof.

In order to prove the desired result, we must rely on error estimates from classical interpolation theory. Evidently, in a similar fashion

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\|\nabla v-\mathcal{I}_{K}(\nabla v)\right\|_{L_{2\varrho}(K)}^{2\varrho}\right)^{1/2\varrho}

4.3
Interpretation

Broadly speaking, our upper bounds on the gradient interpolation error (Theorem
4.1
, Eq. (
65
) and Theorem
4.2
, Eq. (
66
)) can be negatively impacted by four factors:

•

Small values of the constant
C_{\Xi}
. This constant becomes small when the mesh contains sliver elements. Recall that the element thickness can become arbitrarily small on a standard Delaunay mesh for
d>2
. However, on a protected Delaunay mesh, we are guaranteed a lower bound for the minimum thickness of our elements. This fact was established in Remark
1
.

•

Large values of Rajan’s functional
\Theta
. This functional can be minimized on a standard Delaunay mesh. This fact was established by Rajan in
Rajan (1994)
.

•

Large values of the constant
C_{\Delta}
. This constant becomes large when the ratio of the maximum and minimum diameters of the elements becomes large. This constant can be well-controlled on meshes which do not have significant variations in scale.

•

Large values of the maximum min-containment radius
R_{\max}
. This radius is minimized on a standard Delaunay mesh. This fact was established by Rajan in
Rajan (1994)
. In addition, we note that
R_{\max}
is always bounded if the point sample is a net.

Based on the discussion above, we can effectively reduce the upper bounds for gradient interpolation error by using a protected Delaunay mesh, in conjunction with a limit on the difference between the maximum and minimum element diameters.

5

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}\left\|\nabla(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

(68)

Throughout this section, we assume that

In what follows, we often refer to Eqs. (

5.1
L_{2}
Error Bounds

In the following, we leverage Cea’s Lemma and a classical interpolation result to establish an error bound.

{theo}

A measure of the error between the exact gradient
\nabla u
and the approximate gradient
\nabla u_{h}
on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is given by

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq\underbrace{C_{\mathrm{int}}}_{C}\underbrace{C_{\sigma}}_{\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

(69)

where
u
is the exact solution to the elliptic problem (Eq. (
1
)),
u_{h}
is the approximate finite element solution (Eq. (
2
)),
C_{\mathrm{int}}
is a constant that is independent of the mesh, and
C_{\sigma}
is a constant that depends on the mesh.

Proof.

In order to prove the desired result, we will leverage Cea’s lemma
Céa (1964)
. This lemma provides an upper bound for the error of the finite element solution
u_{h}
. In particular, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|u-u_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\sigma}{\tau}\left\|u-w_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

(70)

where
\sigma
and
\tau
are constants which were previously defined in Eq. (
4
), and
w_{h}
is a generic function which resides in the same space as
u_{h}
. In a similar fashion, one may also obtain a corollary to Cea’s lemma, which omits the constants
\sigma
and
\tau
, as follows

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u-u_{h},u-u_{h})

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u-u_{h},u-w_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\nabla(u-w_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

(71)

and furthermore

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|\nabla(u-w_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

(72)

where
a_{h}
is the elliptic bilinear form defined in Eq. (
3
). The second-to-last line of Eq. (
71
) holds because of Galerkin orthogonality, and the last line holds because of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Evidently, the function
w_{h}
in Eq. (
72
) can be replaced by any function in the finite element space. With this in mind, let us replace
w_{h}
with
\mathcal{I}_{h}(u)
, the projection of the exact solution
u
on to the finite element space. Upon setting
w_{h}=\mathcal{I}_{h}(u)
in Eq. (
72
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}(u))\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}.

(73)

Next, in accordance with Theorem 1.103 of
Ern and Guermond (2004)
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{K}(u))\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{int}}\sigma(K)\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\|\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{K}(u))\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{int}}^{2}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sigma(K)^{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\|\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{K}(u))\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{int}}^{2}\left(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sigma(K)^{2}\right)\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\|\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{K}(u))\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{int}}\left(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sigma(K)\right)\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}(u))\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{int}}\left(\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sigma(K)\right)\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

(74)

where
C_{\mathrm{int}}
is a constant that is
independent
of the mesh,
\sigma(K)
is the mesh regularity parameter (see Eq. (
20
)), and we can set

\displaystyle\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sigma(K)=C_{\sigma},

(75)

in accordance with Eq. (
21
). Upon substituting Eq. (
74
) into Eq. (
73
), one obtains the desired result

∎

{theo}

A measure of the error between the exact gradient
\nabla u
and the approximate gradient
\nabla u_{h}
on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla(u-u_{h})\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq\underbrace{2C_{\mathrm{int}}}_{C}\underbrace{C_{\sigma}}_{\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}\underbrace{R_{\max}}_{\mathdutchcal{h}^{r}}\left\|\nabla(\nabla u)\right\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)},

where

is the approximate finite element solution (Eq. (

)),

Interpretation

Our upper bounds on the gradient approximation error (Theorem

, Eq. (

5.1
, Eq. (

•

Large values of the constant

•

Large values of the maximum min-containment radius

Remark 7
.

The key benefit of the results in Sections
4
,
5
, and
6
, is that they provide practitioners with mathematically-supported ideas for reducing the numerical errors in their simulations of PDEs. In particular, the error estimates in this work indicate that protected Delaunay meshes should be seriously considered if one employs finite element methods to interpolate gradients or vector fields. In addition, one should strongly consider protected Delaunay meshes in conjunction with finite element methods in order to approximate solutions to elliptic problems. We note that our most general results hold for PDEs with exact solutions that are piecewise-
W^{2,p}
for suitable
p
, or vector fields that are piecewise-
W^{1,m}
for suitable
m
. If the PDE in question has a significantly less regular solution or vector field, then other error mitigation strategies will likely be required.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we developed
geometrically explicit
error estimates for gradient interpolation and approximation. Furthermore, we extended our results and developed error estimates for vector-valued functions. Our error estimates hold in any number of dimensions, and explicitly depend on the geometric characteristics of the mesh: namely, the thickness and regularity parameters associated with elements in the mesh. This is an important point, as oftentimes, the connection between mesh parameters and the quality of interpolation/approximation is not clearly articulated in the literature.

In addition to explicitly showing the connection between interpolation/approximation accuracy and mesh regularity, we also demonstrated the value of protected Delaunay meshes. In particular, we showed the many important mesh parameters are effectively controlled (and sometimes optimized) on protected Delaunay meshes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Based on our results, we have successfully shown that high-quality, high-order, piecewise polynomial interpolation and approximation can (in principle) be achieved on protected Delaunay meshes.

We note that our error estimates for gradient and vector-field interpolation are completely general, and apply to any problems with sufficiently smooth gradients or vector fields. In addition, our error estimates for gradient approximation apply to elliptic problems of the type discussed in Section
2.2
.

Finally, we note that our error estimates are not limited to the context of protected Delaunay meshes, but can be successfully applied to other simplicial meshes with similar properties.

a_{i}\neq m\end{subarray}}\epsilon_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{r-1},m,a_{r+1},\dots,a_{d}}q_{1}^{a_{1}}q_{2}^{a_{2}}\dots q_{r-1}^{a_{r-1}}\frac{n(r)^{m}}{|\bm{n}(r)|}q_{r+1}^{a_{r+1}}\dots q_{d}^{a_{d}}\right),

is the volume of the
d
-parallelepiped spanned by
\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\ldots,\bm{q}_{r-1},\frac{\bm{n}(r)}{|\bm{n}(r)|},\bm{q}_{r+1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{d}
, which is in turn equal to the volume of the
d
-parallelepiped spanned by
\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\ldots,\bm{q}_{r-1},\bm{q}_{r+1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{d}
by orthogonality. It follows that

\displaystyle|\bm{n}(r)|=(d-1)!|\mathcal{F}_{r}|,

(87)

where
\mathcal{F}_{r}
is the facet opposite
\bm{q}_{r}
.
We point out that this construction is not new (see, for example
Cho (1991)
). Indeed, for the reader that is familiar with Hodge theory, we note that the vectors
\bm{n}(r)
are the dual vectors of the Hodge dual (the image of the Hodge star operator) of the exterior product of
\bm{q}_{1}^{*},\bm{q}_{2}^{*},\dots,\widehat{\bm{q}_{r}^{*}},\dots,\bm{q}_{d}^{*}
, where
\bm{w}^{*}
denotes the dual of
\bm{w}
, (i.e. the covector of
\bm{w}
). A pedagogical introduction to the concept of Hodge duals appears in Section 7.2 of
Abraham et al. (2012)
.

Let us return our attention to the vectors themselves:
\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{q}_{r}},\ldots,\bm{q}_{d}
. These vectors can be defined so that

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{K,1}-\bm{p}_{K,\ell}=\bm{p}_{K,\ell 1},

(88)

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{K,2}-\bm{p}_{K,\ell}=\bm{p}_{K,\ell 2},

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{q}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{p}_{K,r}-\bm{p}_{K,\ell}}=\widehat{\bm{p}_{K,\ell r}}

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{K,\ell-1}-\bm{p}_{K,\ell}=\bm{p}_{K,\ell(\ell-1)},

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{K,\ell+1}-\bm{p}_{K,\ell}=\bm{p}_{K,\ell(\ell+1)},

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{d}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{K,d+1}-\bm{p}_{K,\ell}=\bm{p}_{K,\ell(d+1)}.

Here, we have shifted each vertex by
\bm{p}_{K,\ell}
. Evidently, this ensures that the vertex
\bm{p}_{K,\ell}
itself is shifted to the origin. As a result, we have that

\displaystyle|\bm{n}(r)|=(d-1)!|\mathcal{F}_{K,r}|,

(89)

where
\mathcal{F}_{K,r}
is the facet opposite the vertex
\bm{p}_{K,r}
.

Next, in accordance with Eqs. (
84
) and (
85
), we have that

\displaystyle|K|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}|\det([\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\dots,\bm{q}_{d}])|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\left|(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left((-1)^{j+1}q_{j}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,\widehat{a_{j}},\ldots,a_{d}\\
a_{i}\neq m\end{subarray}}\epsilon_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,\widehat{a_{j}},\dots,a_{d}}q_{1}^{a_{1}}q_{2}^{a_{2}}\dots\widehat{q_{j}^{a_{j}}}\dots q_{d}^{a_{d}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\left|q_{j}^{m}\right|\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,\widehat{a_{j}},\ldots,a_{d}\\
a_{i}\neq m\end{subarray}}\epsilon_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,\widehat{a_{j}},\dots,a_{d}}q_{1}^{a_{1}}q_{2}^{a_{2}}\dots\widehat{q_{j}^{a_{j}}}\dots q_{d}^{a_{d}}\right|\right).

Furthermore, we can rewrite the equation above in accordance with Eqs. (
86
) and (
88
), replacing
q_{j}^{m}
with
p_{K,\ell r}^{m}
, as follows

\displaystyle\left|K\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left(\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,\widehat{a_{r}},\ldots,a_{d}\\
a_{i}\neq m\end{subarray}}\epsilon_{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,\widehat{a_{r}},\dots,a_{d}}p_{K,\ell 1}^{a_{1}}p_{K,\ell 2}^{a_{2}}\dots\widehat{p_{K,\ell r}^{a_{r}}}\dots p_{K,\ell(\ell-1)}^{a_{(\ell-1)}}p_{K,\ell(\ell+1)}^{a_{\ell}}\dots p_{K,\ell(d+1)}^{a_{d}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left(\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|\left|n(r)^{m}\right|\right).

(90)

Upon summing Eq. (
90
) over all
\ell
, we obtain

\displaystyle(d+1)\left|K\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left(\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|\left|n(r)^{m}\right|\right)

(91)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left(\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|\left|\bm{n}(r)\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left(\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|\left|\mathcal{F}_{K,r}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\left(\max_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{K,s}|\right)\sum_{\ell=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|,\qquad s\in[1,d+1],

where we have used Eq. (
89
) on the second-to-last line of Eq. (
91
).

We note that the following identity holds by a symmetry argument

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{d+1}\left|p_{K,\ell r}^{m}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left|p_{K,ij}^{m}\right|.

(92)

Substituting Eq. (
92
) into Eq. (
91
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\frac{\left|K\right|}{\left(\max_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{K,s}|\right)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left|p_{K,ij}^{m}\right|.

(93)

The ratio
|K|/\max_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{K,s}|
on the left hand side (above) can be rewritten in terms of the minimum altitude of the simplex. In particular

\displaystyle\frac{\left|K\right|}{\left(\max_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{K,s}|\right)}=\frac{\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]}{d},

(94)

where we recall that the function
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
returns the shortest distance between the vertex
\bm{p}_{K,s}
and the affine hull of its opposite facet,
\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})
. We can substitute Eq. (
94
) into Eq. (
93
) as follows

\displaystyle\frac{(d+1)}{2}\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left|p_{K,ij}^{m}\right|.

(95)

Next, upon squaring both sides of the expression above

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(d+1)}{2}\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left|p_{K,ij}^{m}\right|\right)^{2}\leq\frac{d(d+1)}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}.

(96)

Here, we have leveraged the root-mean-square-arithmetic-mean inequality in order to reformulate the right hand side.

Equivalently, upon simplifying Eq. (
96
), we obtain the desired lower bound

\displaystyle\frac{d+1}{2d}\left(\min_{s}\left[\mathrm{dist}\left(\bm{p}_{K,s},\mathrm{aff}(\mathcal{F}_{K,s})\right)\right]\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(p_{K,ij}^{m}\right)^{2}.

(97)

9
The Interpolation Constant

In accordance with Eq. (11.14) of
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
, we have the following error estimate for interpolation

\displaystyle\left|v-\mathcal{I}_{K}(v)\right|_{W^{m,p}(K;\mathbb{R}^{q})}\leq C\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})h_{K}^{r-m}\left|v\right|_{W^{r,p}(K;\mathbb{R}^{q})},

(98)

where
v\in W^{m,p}(K;\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq q<\infty
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq m\leq r
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
l\leq r\leq k+1
, and
l,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here,
k
is the polynomial order of the finite element approximation space. We performed a very careful analysis of Lemma 11.1, Lemma 11.7, Lemma 11.9, and Theorem 11.13 in
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
, and determined that

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=C_{\text{int}}=c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}c_{4}c_{5}\frac{\Delta(\widehat{K})^{m}}{\rho(\widehat{K})^{r}},

(99)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{A}_{K}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\mathbb{A}_{K}^{-1}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\sigma(K)^{m}=\left\|\mathbb{A}_{K}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left\|\mathbb{A}_{K}^{-1}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\left(\frac{\Delta(K)}{\rho(K)}\right)^{m},

(100)

where,
c_{1}
,
\ldots
,
c_{5}
are constants,
\widehat{K}
denotes the reference element,
\Delta(\widehat{K})
is the longest edge length of the reference element,
\rho(\widehat{K})
is the diameter of the inscribed ball within the reference element,
\mathbb{A}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
is a transformation matrix, and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ell^{2}}
is the Euclidean norm of a matrix.

If we assume that our interpolant
\mathcal{I}_{K}(\cdot)
is the Lagrange interpolant, then it is also reasonable to assume (in accordance with Section 11.5.1 of
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
) that
c_{5}=1
and
\mathbb{A}_{K}=\mathbb{I}
. As a result, we find that

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=C_{\text{int}}=c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}c_{4}\frac{\Delta(\widehat{K})^{m}}{\rho(\widehat{K})^{r}},

(101)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(\mathcal{T}_{h})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{q}\sqrt{q}\left(\frac{\Delta(K)}{\rho(K)}\right)^{m}=q\left(\frac{\Delta(K)}{\rho(K)}\right)^{m}.

(102)

It remains for us to identify
c_{1}
,
\ldots
,
c_{4}
which appear in the definition of
C_{\text{int}}
above. With modest effort, we can show that:

•

c_{1}
is upper bounded by the Lebesgue constant.

•

c_{2}
is the Bramble-Hilbert constant.

•

c_{3}
is a norm-scaling constant associated with Eq. (11.7b) in
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
.

•

c_{4}
is a norm-scaling constant associated with Eq. (11.7a) in
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
.

In principle, none of these constants depend on the characteristics of the mesh,
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. In what follows, we will discuss each of these constants in more detail, and offer explanations for their descriptions (above).

The constant
c_{1}
is defined at the top of page 131 in
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
, such that

\displaystyle c_{1}\equiv\left\|\mathcal{G}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q});W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q}))}\equiv\sup_{\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\frac{\left|\mathcal{G}(\widehat{v})\right|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}},

(103)

where the operator
\mathcal{G}(\cdot)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\widehat{v})=\widehat{v}-\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v}),

(104)

\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\cdot)
is the Lagrange interpolation operator in reference space. The definition of
c_{1}
in Eq. (
103
) can be rewritten, and bounded above as follows

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\frac{\left|\widehat{v}-\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\frac{\left|\widehat{v}\right|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}+\left|\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}},

(105)

in accordance with the Triangle inequality. Furthermore, since
r\geq m
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\leq\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}.

(106)

In addition, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{v}\right|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})},

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}.

(107)

Upon substituting Eqs. (
106
) and (
107
) into Eq. (
105
), one obtains

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\frac{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}+\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}

\displaystyle=1+\sup_{\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\frac{\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}.

(108)

Evidently, we can rewrite the inequality in Eq. (
108
) as

\displaystyle c_{1}\leq 1+C_{\text{Lebesgue}},

(109)

where

\displaystyle C_{\text{Lebesgue}}\equiv\sup_{\widehat{v}\in W^{r,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}\frac{\left\|\mathcal{I}_{\widehat{K}}(\widehat{v})\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}}{\left\|\widehat{v}\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\widehat{K};\mathbb{R}^{q})}},

(110)

is a Lebesgue constant in reference space—see the relevant discussion in Section 5.5 of
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
. The Lebesgue constant is a well-known quantity, especially in the case when
r=m
. When
m
,
p
, and
q
are fixed, the value of the Lebesgue constant generally increases with the polynomial order and the number of spatial dimensions. Fortunately, the value of the constant can be controlled by carefully choosing the locations of the interpolation points. Broadly speaking, equally-spaced points produce Lebesgue constants which grow exponentially with the degree of the interpolating polynomial
Hesthaven and Warburton (2007)
. Conversely, non-uniformly distributed points demonstrate much better growth rates. Examples of this are quite numerous, and the interested reader is encouraged to consult
Warburton (2006)
;
Williams and Jameson (2013)
;
Isaac (2020)
;
Gobel et al. (2025)
;
Jiménez-Ramos et al. (2025)
and the references therein for details. In summary, we find that

\displaystyle c_{1}\leq C_{\text{Lebesgue}}(k,d,r,m,p,q).

(111)

Here, the polynomial order
k
and dimension
d
are the primary drivers of
C_{\text{Lebesgue}}
in practice.

The constant
c_{2}
is the Bramble-Hilbert constant of the well-known Bramble-Hilbert Lemma
Bramble and Hilbert (1971)
. The original formulation of the lemma is non-constructive, and no explicit formula for the constant is given. In later work by Dupont and Scott
Dupont and Scott (1980)
, a constructive proof is given, and a formula for the constant is also provided for star-shaped domains. This formula for the constant depends on
r,p
, and the
chunkiness
parameter of the domain in question, where the chunkiness parameter is essentially an aspect ratio or inverse thickness of the domain. This may initially appear to be a serious problem, especially if the domain in question is the physical element
K
, as
c_{2}
would be dependent on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Fortunately, in accordance with
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
, one may choose the domain to be the reference element
\widehat{K}
. With this in mind, the chunkiness parameter is

\displaystyle C_{\text{chunk}}(\widehat{K})=\frac{\Delta(\widehat{K})}{r(\widehat{K})},

(112)

where we define
r(\widehat{K})
as the radius of the maximal ball inscribed within
\widehat{K}
. Furthermore, in accordance with
Dupont and Scott (1980)
and Lemma 11.9 of
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
, we find that

\displaystyle c_{2}=c_{2}\left(r,p,C_{\text{chunk}}(\widehat{K})\right).

(113)

Therefore,
c_{2}
is independent of the mesh.

Lastly, the constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
are functions of the associated Sobolev norms, and the space dimension. In particular,

\displaystyle c_{3}=c_{3}(m,d),\qquad c_{4}=c_{4}(r,d).

(114)

This is discussed in the proof of Lemma 11.7 in
Ern and Guermond (2021a)
.
