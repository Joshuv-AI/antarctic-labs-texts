Intimate partner violence and relationship quality among pregnant women: insights from a multi-centre study
Authors: Taiwo Olufunmilayo Kuku-Kuye, Ayokunle Moses Olumodeji, Joy Onyinyechi Chionuma, Oladimeji Abiodun Makinde, Isaac Ian Oloke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intimate partner violence presents a complex dilemma that significantly impacts the well-being of women, particularly during pregnancy. This issue often arises from a dysfunctional relationship, characterised by inadequate emotional connections and poor-quality interactions between partners. The study identifies the interaction of intimate partner violence and the quality of the relationship between partners. A multi-centred, descriptive cross-sectional hospital-based study, in which 460 consenting pregnant women attending antenatal and postnatal care at the respective health facilities in the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Department of Lagos State University Teaching Hospital, General Hospital Ifako Ijaye, Primary Health centre Onigbogbo and were consecutively recruited over a six-month period An adapted questionnaire from the WHO Multi-Country Study on Women’s Health and Domestic Violence and Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS) to measure the quality of marital relationships. Behavioural violence was the most commonly reported, affecting 16.1% of respondents. Psychological violence was experienced by 12.9% of the participants, while 5.3% reported physical violence. Economic and sexual violence were reported by 4.6% and 4.3% of participants, respectively. Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS), which includes cohesion, consensus, and satisfaction components. Cohesion, 55.2% of participants had high scores, 26.1% scored in the mid-range, and 18.7% were classified as low. Regarding consensus, 73.6% of respondents were in the high range, 17.5% in the mid-range, and 8.9% in the low range. For satisfaction, 79.6% of respondents reported high satisfaction, while 13.7% and 6.7% fell into the mid and low satisfaction categories, respectively. Religion, education level, and experiences of sexual, physical, and psychological violence show strong associations with distress in pregnant women. These insights emphasize the complex intersection of sociocultural context and intimate partner dynamics, particularly during vulnerable periods like pregnancy.

Introduction

Violence is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against another person that either result in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, and/or psychological harm1. The United Nations defined violence against women, whether it happened within the public space or in private life, as an action that causes the woman to be damaged physically, mentally, socially, sexually, and/or and economically; to suffer, to have her honour tarnished; to lose her self-esteem; and to be subject to continuous discrimination2.

The World Health Organisation (WHO) has defined intimate partner violence IPV as ‘behaviour within an intimate relationship that causes physical, sexual or psychological harm, including acts of aggression, sexual coercion, psychological abuse and controlling behaviours’, encompassing both current and past intimate partners2. It can be subtyped into physical, sexual, economic and psychological1. IPV is a global health burden that undermines women’s fundamental reproductive health rights, human rights, mental health, and gender equality3. The methods and extent of this crisis vary across the globe, transcending all race, ethnicity, gender and socioeconomic status4.

Globally, one in three women worldwide experiences physical and/or sexual violence from an intimate partner or non-partner, with Sub-Saharan Africa and Asia having a significant share2,5. The highest rate of lifetime intimate partner violence is in Southern Asia (35%) and Sub-Saharan African countries (33%)6. Tanzania 61%, Uganda 56%, Ethiopia 34%, Ghana 39%, Sierra Leone (27% to 43), Cameroon 34%, Liberia 33%6–8. In Nigeria, it ranges between 11.5% and 79% across the geographical zones of Nigeria4. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), approximately 20% of women aged 15–49 experience intimate partner violence (IPV) across 60% of countries in the region9. Regionally, the highest prevalence of IPV is Middle Africa, with a rate of 49.3% (95% CI: 40.32–58.45), East Africa 44.13% (95% CI: 36.62–51.67), Southern Africa 39.36% (95% CI: 34.23–44.49), and West Africa at 34.30% (95% CI: 27.38–41.22)10. Approximately 1 in 4 women in the United States in their life time2.

The identified risk factors from several studies include poverty, childhood domestic violence, dissatisfaction, male dominance, economic stress, unemployment, education, age, marital duration, polygamous partners’ perception of violence, lack of discriminatory laws and policy against women, influence of recreational drugs and alcohol, culture and religion1–4,11. In India and much of South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) is linked to historical, social, and gender inequalities upheld by patriarchal norms1,12. Traditional beliefs, such as male inheritance, continuity of family name, sons as defenders of the clan and family status, contribute to violence and discrimination against women, making them more vulnerable to IPV, as they are often below the male conterpart13,14.

Pregnancy is associated with physiological and psychological changes, which may be stressful under the interplay of several hormones and this may at times affect the emotion, tolerance and vulnerability of the woman4,5,14,15. A pregnant woman is no exception to intimate partner violence and studies have shown that pregnancy may precipitate IPV15. IPV is a significant barrier to achieving safe motherhood and undermines United Nations sustainable development goals2. Experiencing physical abuse during pregnancy can lead to injuries that affect the outcomes of the pregnancy, including miscarriages, preterm birth, low birth weight, depression, stress, stunted growth and the risk of mortality for both the mother and the fetus2,4,5,15. Women who experience intimate partner violence (IPV) are more prone to unwanted, unplanned pregnancy, sexually transmitted disease, engagement in recreational drugs and alcohol and pregnancy defaulting on antenatal appointments, unsupervised birth, contributing to increased maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality3,5,12. In a multinational study involving 4 countries, there was a significant association between IPV and the presence of childhood stunted growth in India and Nepal16.

Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) may linger into childhood or endure for a lifetime2,3. Consequently, Children who witness IPV are at a high risk of becoming victims or perpetrators later in life, often facing significant behavioural, social and emotional challenges2. This creates a vicious cycle which may be difficult to outlive, creating an unhealthy future among intimate partners and the world at large2,3. According to WHO, Intimate partner violence (IPV) is often rooted in a history of exposure to violence by either partner, significant marital discord and dissatisfaction, ineffective communication between partners, and controlling behaviours displayed by men towards their partners17.

One possible solution to IPV is maintaining a healthy relationship between the partners. Couple dysfunction reflects the quality of the partners’ relationship thus a healthy relationship leads to a well-functioning family18,19. The quality of a relationship can provide insight into the risk of intimate partner violence (IPV)17–19. Dyadic adjustment is a process that measures how well a couple relates with each other20. It’s a way to assess the quality of a relationship, and it can be used to identify areas that need improvement to forestall friction in relationships20,21. The quality of marital relationships and the nature of the interactions that parents foster with their children are critically important22. Dyadic adjustment is the degree of satisfaction that individuals within the couple experience across various dimensions, including cohesion, satisfaction, and consensus22. Several studies have shown that RDAS can significantly relate the exposure to violence and sexuality among infertile women23,24. Studies have shown that higher dyadic adjustment leads to fewer couple conflict and a reversal couple with lower RDAS are prone to more disharmony22,25,26.

Several studies have reiterated the fact that marital dissatisfaction has increased IPV among couples1. IPV has a profound impact not just on the victims but also on the family, health service and nation’s economic cost. Addressing this issue is essential for the well-being of both individuals and society as a whole. It is this background that generated the need for the study. Also, there is a paucity of studies on the use of dyadic adjustment and pregnant women’s exposure to violence.

Literature review

A woman who encounters any one of the four forms of violence—economic, sexual, physical, or psychological—often finds herself vulnerable to experiencing the other subtypes as well. This interplay occurs in varying degrees, illustrating how these different forms of abuse are interconnected27,27,28. Addressing this complex issue requires a multifaceted approach that involves education, community engagement, and effective policy-making to foster safer environments for all individuals1,28,29.

Physical violence includes hurting, throwing objects, pushing, burning, kicking, biting, slapping, strangling, denying medical care, beating, forcing alcohol or drug use, threatening with any form of weapon, or using a weapon2. Psychological violence removes a woman’s sense of self-worth through constant criticism, harassment, verbal abuse, degradation, and blaming; threats; stalking, and isolation from family and friends2,3. Sexual violence involves any sexual act committed against another person’s will or when consent is not given or cannot be given, and there is a continuum of sexual activity that covers unwanted kissing, touching, or fondling; sexual coercion and rape2,13. Economic violence involves controlling a woman’s ability to gain, use and sustain economic resources, thereby threatening her economic security and potential of self-sufficiency29.

In a comparative analysis of psychological intimate partner violence against women (IPVAW) across 28 countries in the European Union, it was found that Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, and Sweden exhibited significantly higher rates than the average reported within the remaining EU member states who demonstrated a more heterogeneous spectrum of violence types13. There is, however, a strong link between psychological and physical IPVAW, since psychological violence often precedes physical IPV and it is considered one of its main risk factors13. The controlling behaviour exhibited by intimate partners is largely shaped by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including ego, masculinity, and patriarchal norms that are entrenched in societal myths, laws, and policies27,30–32.

A woman dies every 10 min, almost one in three have been subjected to physical and/or sexual violence in a lifetime. Alarmingly, only 20% seek help from the right sources. Findings reveal that only 40% of victims of family violence ask for support, and those women who do, prefer to seek help from their friends and family members, while only 10% of women report the issue to law enforcement bodies2,3,17. Interestingly, a significant number of women fail to recognise certain behaviours exhibited by their partners as any acts of violence. This lack of awareness is largely influenced by societal expectations, cultural norms and rules, which shape their perceptions and understanding of what constitutes abusive behaviour13,30. Certain individuals find themselves compelled to regard intimate partner violence as an inescapable reality within their lives. While some have successfully navigated and survived such circumstances, others have regrettably succumbed to the associated complications, resulting in detrimental effects on themselves and on the well-being of those around them.

Marital satisfaction is regarded as an ongoing process in which partners strive to adapt and enhance their relationship21. The goal is to create a harmonious partnership where both partners can thrive emotionally and relationally. The Dyadic Adjustment Scale assesses the couple’s perception of the quality of their relationship or marital satisfaction, measured through four dimensions: consensus, cohesion, affectional expression, and satisfaction21–23. Dyadic Consensus refers to the extent to which an individual aligns with their partner’s perspectives and opinions. This dimension reflects the level of agreement and shared understanding between partners, which is crucial for fostering a healthy relationship. Dyadic Satisfaction, on the other hand, captures the feelings of contentment and fulfilment that one partner experiences in relation to the other22. This aspect gauges the overall emotional happiness and satisfaction derived from the partnership, playing a vital role in the longevity and quality of the marital bond. Dyadic Cohesion indicates the degree to which partners engage in shared activities and experiences. A strong level of cohesion signifies that both individuals actively partake in recreational or daily tasks together, reinforcing their connection and partnership. Affectional Expression is the extent to which a couple expresses affection and concern for each other’s needs, both physically and emotionally (e.g., sexual relations, demonstrating emotional support)21,22,24,25.

The quality of interpersonal relationships and dyadic adjustment are critical factors that can serve as catalysts for both physical and emotional expressiveness in relationships. High levels of dyadic adjustment, are an indicator of a healthier adjustment and a higher quality of the couple’s relationship. Research conducted by Jiménez-Picón and colleagues revealed that there is a significant connection between couple dyadic adjustment and the overall health of the family unit33.

Gaps in knowledge

Despite extensive global research on intimate partner violence (IPV), limited attention has been paid to how relationship quality—measured through dyadic adjustment—intersects with IPV among pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in Nigeria. Research integrating psychological measures such as the Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS) into maternal health and IPV studies remains scarce in low- and middle-income settings. There is also a paucity of local data linking dyadic distress with different types of IPV (physical, sexual, psychological, behavioural, and economic) among pregnant women—a critical gap in understanding the relational context of IPV within antenatal populations in Nigeria.

This study is among the first in Nigeria to examine the relationship between dyadic adjustment and intimate partner violence among pregnant women using the Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS). Unlike prior research that primarily quantified IPV prevalence, this study uniquely explores how the quality of a couple’s relationship (consensus, cohesion, and satisfaction) correlates with different forms of IPV during pregnancy. It adopts a family-centred approach, emphasizing that healthy dyadic functioning within the family unit is integral to maternal and fetal well-being. The study’s integration of relational psychology with public health perspectives introduces a novel interdisciplinary framework for understanding and addressing IPV in antenatal populations.

Methodology

Sampling

A multi-centred, descriptive cross-sectional hospital-based study, in which 460 consenting booked/registered pregnant women attending antenatal and postnatal care at the respective health facilities in the Obstetrics and Gynaecology Department of Lagos State University Teaching Hospital, General Hospital Ifako Ijaye, and Primary Health centre Onigbogbo and were recruited sequentially day by day till the Intended sample size was attained over a six-month period. The required sample size for the study was estimated using a modified Kish-Leslie’s formula (n = z2p(1-p)/d2). The pregnant women in each centre were selected using a systematic random sampling technique. The record of the previous average number of pregnant women attending the ANC clinic of each selected facility was obtained from the registration book and used to determine the sampling interval. The Nth interval was calculated as the total population divided by the sample size for each centre. A sampling interval of three and five was adequate to achieve the allocated proportion of the sample size in the centre respectively. The attendance list served as the sampling frame each clinic day. The first participant was randomly selected, and then every 3rd or 5th patient on the list were added until the calculated size was reached. Based on ANC enrollees/attendees in the selected healthcare facilities in the previous year, the sample size for each healthcare facility was proportionally allocated. This was Lagos State University Teaching Hospital Ikeja (180), General Hospital Ifako Ijaiye (180) and Primary Health Centre, Oregun (100).

Instrument

The tools used for data collection will include 1. A structured socio-demographic questionnaire to include relevant characteristics (individual and birth including age, education, occupation, parity, gestational age, socioeconomic status, level of partner support, husband’s age and status and drinking/smoking status). 2. A pre-tested semi-structured interviewer-assisted questionnaire adapted from the WHO Multi-Country Study on Women’s Health and Domestic Violence was used to collect data for this study15. The questions are mixtures of Yes/No/Unsure and a Likert scale of 5 responses. 3. Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS)The RDAS is a revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale that measures the quality of marital relationships. The RDAS consists of 14 items that comprise three subscales: Consensus (item 1–6), Satisfaction (item 7–10), and Cohesion (item11–14). All items are scored on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 5, except for Item 11 which is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4. Total scores can range from 0 to 69, higher scores depict better marital relationship marital using a cut-off value of < 4811.

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Statistics). Descriptive statistics were calculated as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate, and continuous variables were analyzed using independent t-tests. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed on variables significant in bivariate analysis to control for confounders, with a significance level set at p < 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research and Ethics Committee of the Lagos State University Teaching Hospital (LREC/06/10/2486) on 30/04/2024. Permission from the Health Service Commission and Primary Health Board were also obtained from each facility respectively. There was no external funding source for the study. Informed consent was then obtained from each participant.

Classification of marital adjustment using the revised dyadic adjustment scale (RDAS)11

In this study, marital adjustment was classified using the established cut-off score of 48, as proposed by Busby et al. Participants with a total score below 48 were categorized as being in distressed relationships, while those with a score of 48 or higher were categorized as non-distressed. This binary classification was used to interpret relationship quality in the analysis. Participants were categorized into distressed and non-distressed groups based on their total scores from the Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS). The total score was calculated by summing responses from all three subscales: consensus, satisfaction, and cohesion.

Results

The age distribution showed that the majority of respondents (40.0%, n = 167) were between 26 and 30 years, followed by 29.5% (n = 123) in the 31 to 35 years range. Other groups included 15.3% (n = 64) aged 36–40, 11.5% (n = 48) aged 22–25, and 3.6% (n = 15) aged 40–46. The reported Mean ± SD for age was 2.78 ± (1.325). Regarding tribe, the sample was predominantly Yoruba (65.9%, n = 275), with 21.1% (n = 88) Igbo, 6.0% (n = 25) Hausa, and 7.0% (n = 29) from other tribes. The primary religion was Christianity (71.2%, n = 297), followed by Islam (28.8%, n = 120). No participants were recorded for the ‘None’ or ‘Others’ categories.

Educational status was high, with 69.5% (n = 290) having completed tertiary education. This was followed by 24.7% (n = 103) with secondary education, 3.6% (n = 15) with no formal education, and 2.2% (n = 9) with primary education. Occupation, 48.0% (n = 200) were self-employed. Other occupations included private sector work (32.9%, n = 137), civil service (11.3%, n = 47), and unemployed (7.9%, n = 33). The marital status of participants was listed as 99.0% (n = 413) single, 7% (n = 3) separated/divorced, and 2% (n = 1) married. However, 98.1% (n = 409) reported they lived with their husband, while 1.7% (n = 7) lived with parents/relatives and 0.2% (n = 1) lived alone.

Regarding the position of marriage, 54.7% (n = 228) were in a monogamous union, 37.4% (n = 156) were the first wife in a polygamous marriage (F-Polygamous), and 7.9% (n = 33) were the second or third wife (Polygamous). When asked who chose their husband, 51.6% (n = 215) responded ‘Both Partners’, 44.4% (n = 185) ‘Myself’, 3.6% (n = 15) ‘My Family’, 2% (n = 1) ‘God’, and 2% (n = 1) ‘Pastor’. A large majority (95.4%, n = 398) married voluntarily, with 4.6% (n = 19) reporting they did not. The most common partner occupation was in the private sector (39.1%, n = 163), followed by self-employed (36.0%, n = 150), civil servant (24.5%, n = 102), and unemployed (5%, n = 2). Analysis of behavioural and health characteristics revealed no significant differences in alcohol, cigarette, or drug use between distressed and non-distressed groups. However, more distressed women reported alcohol consumption. Involvement in physical fights and HIV status also showed no significant association with dyadic distress.

Assessment of domestic violence exposure showed that physical violence was significantly more prevalent among distressed participants (p = 0.023). Similarly, sexual violence had a highly significant association with dyadic distress (p = 0.001). Psychological violence was also significantly associated (p = 0.042), while behavioural and economic violence, though more common among the distressed group, did not reach statistical significance. These findings collectively describe the sociodemographic and experiential context of pregnant women experiencing varying levels of relationship quality in the study population.

Discussion

The most prevalent form of intimate partner violence in this study was behavioural violence, with 16.1% of respondents reporting experiences of this type of abuse.. This was followed by psychological violence at 12.9%, and both sexual and economic violence, 4.3% and 4.6%, respectively. In Nigeria, Tesfaw in a retrospective study using the NDHS study with 8061 participants showed (37.49%), (52.3%) and (14.71%) women had physical, emotional, and sexual violence, respectively, but also alluded to the interconnection among the different subtypes. Similar to findings in Turkey by Alkan27,28 In Ethiopia, a Community-based study on pregnant women reported that physical, emotional, and sexual violence were 8.1%, 24.5%, and 2.4% respectively34. In a multinational study in Sub-Saharan Africa using the NDHS of 26 countries with 14,330 women, 15–49 years, the prevalence of physical, emotional and sexual violence was about 30.58%, 30.22%, 13% violence, respectively30. In Turkey, a country characterized by alarming rates of domestic violence against women, research indicates that the prevalence of physical violence among women experiencing economic, verbal, and sexual abuse by their husbands or intimate partners stands at 39.8%, 127.35%, and 83.68%, respectively29. These statistics highlight the multifaceted nature of domestic violence against women, suggesting a significant overlap between various forms of abuse, which exacerbates the overall impact on women’s physical safety and well-being29.

The variation in the prevalence of violence may be attributed to several factors, including the existence of violence across different societies, the methodologies employed in data collection, religious influences, societal perceptions of violence, legal frameworks, and cultural contexts. Additionally, economic stressors resulting from conflicts, such as wars or pandemics like COVID-19, may also contribute to this variation27,28,30,32.

Tugut’s study in Turkey also revealed that psychological and verbal violence was the highest form of violence before the diagnosis of infertility and sexual violence was common after diagnosis35. These findings reinforce that intimate partner violence is not limited to physical aggression alone but rather includes a spectrum of controlling, degrading, and harmful behaviours. Its subtlety, leaving no visible scar, makes it easier to deny. Yet, the emotional damage can be profound, insidious and long-lasting. A multi-pronged approach incorporating prevention, identification, and support is crucial in effectively addressing and mitigating the impact of intimate partner violence on pregnant women.

The dyadic adjustment level serves as an important indicator of the well-being of the couple’s relationship21. The Relationship Dynamics Assessment Survey (RDAS) analysis unequivocally reveals an optimistic outlook regarding relationship quality among the respondents. The high levels of cohesion, consensus, and satisfaction (55%,73.6%,79.6%) demonstrate that a significant majority enjoy emotionally supportive and balanced partnerships needed during pregnancy. The remarkable levels of cohesion, consensus, and satisfaction indicate that the majority are experiencing emotionally nurturing and balanced partnerships.

However, the consistently present minority scoring mid to low on these indicators cannot be overlooked as women may be silently navigating emotionally strained relationships or low-intensity conflict situations, which could evolve into or already constitute forms of emotional abuse or neglect a subtle yet significant facet of domestic violence.

In terms of cohesion, while most pregnant women in the sample enjoy supportive relationships, nearly half experience emotional detachment or minimal joint participation with their partner, an aspect often correlated with emotional neglect or early signs of relational disengagement.

A striking 73.6% of participants reported high consensus, reflecting alignment in core values and practical life choices—a positive marker for healthy communication. Conversely, 17.5% and 8.9% scored in the mid and low ranges, respectively. These figures hint at emerging or unresolved conflict zones, which, if unchecked, could escalate into verbal arguments, tension, or even controlling behaviours all of which are red flags in assessing the potential for domestic strain or violence.

The dominant high-satisfaction score 79.1% suggests that most participants perceive their relationships as fulfilling and secure. However, the significant minority of respondents (20.4%) who reported mid to low scores on these critical indicators must be considered. A vulnerable group, possibly exposed to subtle forms of dissatisfaction, passive aggression, or unspoken emotional strain. These circumstances can evolve into or may already constitute forms of emotional abuse or neglect. This is a serious concern that highlights a critical aspect of intimate partner violence that demands attention.

Violence can significantly contribute to the deterioration of dyadic adjustment. In Turkey, Tugut et al. study showed that the RDAS of infertile women exposed to violence was low35. Santona et al. study revealed that among infertile women, dyadic adjustment predicted sexual satisfaction, and anxious attachment decreased sexual internal control24. Research has shown that families with violence have dysfunctional relationships, weak relational ties and provide minimal support and protection for their members18. Kofoworade et al. and Zeng et al. found that family dysfunction was directly related to domestic violence in pregnancy19,20. In mitigating violence, Singh et al. found that joint decisions in household matters reduce the prevalence of domestic violence and reinforce couple consensus36.

Pregnancy may significantly influence relational dynamics; however, the quality of the relationship, along with the degree of agreement and satisfaction between partners, primarily determines the risk of experiencing violence20,33,37. A robust and supportive relationship characterised by open communication and mutual respect mitigates stressors that may lead to conflict. Conversely, if a couple faces discord, misunderstanding, or dissatisfaction, the likelihood of encountering violent situations may increase. Hence, it is crucial to assess the emotional bond and mutual satisfaction within the relationship to prevent IPV. Couple dysfunction can be the backbone of IPV and is related directly to the quality and health of the relationship.

A comparative analysis paints a layered picture of the factors tied to dyadic distress, with key findings revealing that religion, education level, and experiences of sexual, physical, and psychological violence show strong associations with distress in intimate relationships among pregnant women. Several studies have implicated education as a risk factor for IPV. While education often provides women with greater access to resources and opportunities, among the distressed women with low RDAS scores, the physical, sexual and behavioural violence was significant among educated women. The association was statistically significant (p = 0.042), suggesting education may play a role in relationship harmony. Alkan reiterated the fact that education determines women’s financial dependency, 20% women with a higher education level was subjected to violence, 50% women with a lower education level, experienced domestic violence29. In contrast, some other studies have indicated that educated women are more prone to IPV, indicating that other factors may come into play, such as the partner’s level of education, difference in educational status27,33. Alebel revealed that both partners’ educational status and alcohol intake increased the risk of pregnant women to IPV33.

Conclusion

This study shows the complex interplay between intimate partner violence and relationship quality among pregnant women. While a majority reported high levels of cohesion, consensus, and satisfaction in their relationships, a significant minority experienced various forms of domestic violence, most notably behavioural and psychological abuse. These findings emphasize the importance of screening for IPV and assessing dyadic adjustment during pregnancy, as emotional strain and violence can coexist even in relationships perceived as satisfactory.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study contributes to the theoretical understanding of intimate partner violence by demonstrating the interplay between relationship quality and exposure to violence among pregnant women. It extends the application of dyadic adjustment theory to a vulnerable population, offering evidence that poor relationship cohesion, consensus, and satisfaction may increase the risk of IPV during pregnancy38–40.

In practice, the findings show the need to integrate relationship assessment and counselling into routine antenatal care. Training healthcare providers to recognise signs of relational distress and IPV may help in early identification and intervention. The results can also guide policymakers and reproductive health programmes in developing context-specific strategies for preventing IPV among pregnant women.

Limitations of the study

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between IPV and relationship quality. The use of self-reported measures may also introduce recall or social desirability bias. Furthermore, as the study was conducted in selected health facilities within Lagos State, the findings may not be generalizable to all populations or regions. Future longitudinal and qualitative studies could provide deeper insight into the temporal and contextual dynamics of IPV among pregnant women.

Recommendation

The Relationship Dynamics Assessment Scale (RDAS) is useful for assessing relationship quality and health, which has implications for intervention strategies aimed at violence prevention, couples’ education, and mental health support, particularly during pregnancy. Routine screening for intimate partner violence (IPV) and relationship distress should be included in antenatal care. Effective combat against IPV requires integration of health, police, justice, and social services, along with supportive legislation and action plans. Couple-focused interventions should enhance communication and respect to reduce violence risk. Community education programs must address gender inequities and cultural justifications for violence, while policymakers should strengthen laws for women’s safety and train health workers in supportive practices. Further longitudinal studies are necessary to understand the relationships between dyadic adjustment, partner support, and IPV, as well as the effectiveness of couple-based therapies in preventing domestic violence.
