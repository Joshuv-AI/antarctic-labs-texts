Ultra-Strongly Self-Interacting Dark Matter: From Phenomenology to Astrophysical Observables
Stefano Profumo

Abstract
We develop a minimal, testable framework for two-component self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) in which a dominant, moderately self-interacting species coexists with an ultra-strongly self-interacting subcomponent (uSIDM). A light vector mediator induces velocity-dependent self-scattering, while early-universe dynamics - standard 2 → 2 2\to 2 annihilation supplemented by interconversion χ 1 ​ χ 1 → χ 2 ​ χ 2 \chi_{1}\chi_{1}\to\chi_{2}\chi_{2} - determine the relic abundance analytically. From observations of dwarf and low surface brightness galaxy rotation curves, as well as strong cluster lensing, we place constraints on the microphysics parameters. From these constrained regions, we map the microphysics to effective ETHOS parameters and evolve the linear power spectrum in CLASS . We then confront the model with direct-detection constraints and place an upper bound on our parameter space. We identify a region where: (1) the SIDM dominant component attains σ eff / m = 20 ​ – ​ 40 ​ cm 2 ​ g − 1 \sigma_{\rm{eff}}/m=20\textup{--}40~\text{cm}^{2}\text{g}^{-1} at dwarf velocities while satisfying cluster upper bounds σ eff / m < 0.13 ​ cm 2 ​ g − 1 \sigma_{\rm{eff}}/m<0.13~\text{cm}^{2}\text{g}^{-1} ; (2) a subpercent uSIDM fraction drives accelerated gravothermal collapse in early halos, providing seeds relevant to high-redshift quasar formation and “little red dots”; and (3) the small-scale cutoff in the matter power spectrum remains consistent with Lyman- α \alpha and satellite counts, but exhibits non-standard features, potentially discernible with future observations. The allowed space can be organized by the mediator-to-DM mass ratio and the late-time uSIDM fraction, with a narrow window singled out by the combined cosmological and astrophysical requirements.



1
Introduction

The discovery of compact, red, kpc- to sub-kpc–scale sources at
z\gtrsim 4
, commonly referred to as “Little Red Dots” (LRDs), has sharpened long-standing questions about the rapid assembly of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) in the early Universe and the physical drivers of small-scale structure in the dark sector
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. One intriguing possibility is that a small subcomponent of the dark matter (DM) possesses ultra-strong self-interactions, triggering rapid gravothermal evolution and early core-collapse in nascent halos, thereby seeding massive black holes that can power LRD-like systems
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. In this ultra-strongly self-interacting DM (uSIDM) picture, only a subpercent fraction of the DM needs very large self-scattering to core-collapse quickly; the remainder can follow the successful large-scale
\Lambda
CDM phenomenology.

Independently, a broad body of work has shown that self-interacting DM (SIDM) with velocity-dependent cross sections offers a coherent explanation for several small-scale tensions, ranging from core formation and the diversity of rotation curves in dwarfs and spirals to cluster-scale density profiles, without deviating from
\Lambda
CDM on large scales
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Recent analyses explicitly demonstrate how gravothermal evolution in SIDM can amplify halo-to-halo diversity and reconcile high-density outliers, while preserving good fits to galaxies with slowly rising rotation curves
[
11
]
. On the opposite end of the mass scale, strong-lensing studies of galaxy clusters and galaxies provide complementary bounds on SIDM at higher characteristic velocities, tightening the viable parameter space and informing the velocity dependence of the cross section
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

These astrophysical observables interface naturally with the gravothermal evolution of SIDM halos. For a wide class of velocity-dependent models, the late-time evolution - including the onset and timing of core collapse - exhibits approximate universality once cast in terms of an appropriate scattering timescale, enabling robust predictions for collapse redshifts at fixed halo assembly histories
[
15
,
16
]
. In this setting, an ultra-strong subcomponent can undergo accelerated collapse even if it constitutes only a small fraction of the total DM, provided its cross section per mass is sufficiently large at the relevant internal velocities. The resulting black-hole seeds form early (e.g.,
z\sim 10
), after which baryonic accretion and feedback can generate luminous, compact systems with LRD-like spectral and morphological properties, including heavy obscuration scenarios consistent with the absence of bright far-IR/sub-millimeter detections to date
[
3
,
6
]
.

In this work we develop and test a minimal, predictive framework in which a dominant, moderately self-interacting species coexists with a subpercent uSIDM subcomponent. We: (1) lay out the microphysical setup and relic-density accounting for a light-mediator model that simultaneously fixes the late-time uSIDM fraction and the velocity dependence of self-scattering; (2) connect to halo phenomenology across dwarf-to-cluster scales via rotation curves and strong-lensing constraints; and (3) delineate the combined viable parameter space, emphasizing the mediator-to-DM mass ratio and the late-time uSIDM fraction as organizing variables. We show how future sub-GeV direct detection experiments can probe this two-component uSIDM scenario if the dark sector couples to the Standard Model through kinetic mixing of the light mediator with the photon.

2
Model Overview

We introduce our dark sector Lagrangian as follows: the fermion
\chi_{1}
represents the uSIDM component while the fermion
\chi_{2}
corresponds to SIDM. In order to limit the number of free parameters in our model, we assume that both DM particles have the same mass
m_{\chi_{1}}=m_{\chi_{2}}=m_{\chi}
. We leave the scenario of
m_{\chi_{1}}\neq m_{\chi_{2}}
to study in a future work. We also introduce a dark photon
A^{\prime}
, with mass
m_{A^{\prime}}
, to mediate the self-interactions of the dark matter particles:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm{dark}}=\overline{\chi}_{1}\left({i\not{\partial}-m_{\chi}}\right)\chi_{1}+\overline{\chi}_{2}\left({i\not{\partial}-m_{\chi}}\right)\chi_{2}-g_{\chi_{1}}\overline{\chi}_{1}A^{\prime\mu}\gamma_{\mu}\chi_{1}-g_{\chi_{2}}\overline{\chi}_{2}A^{\prime\mu}\gamma_{\mu}\chi_{2}

(2.1)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~-\frac{1}{4}F^{\prime\mu\nu}F^{\prime}_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m_{A^{\prime}}^{2}A^{\prime\mu}A^{\prime}_{\mu}.

Due to the self interactions, one could imagine that the dark matter particles form bounds states. We address this possibility in Appendix
A
, and we find that our model will not form bound states.

Phenomenologically, uSIDM is characterized by two key parameters: the uSIDM self interaction cross section,
\sigma/m
, and the uSIDM fraction,
f
. The fraction
f
quantifies the amount of dark matter that is ultra-self-interacting,
f=\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\leq 1
(but typically
f\ll 1
) where
n_{i}
is the number density of each species. If we consider the couplings of the two dark matter species to be related by
g_{\chi_{2}}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}f^{1/4}
, we obtain a strong hierarchy between the corresponding self-interaction cross sections,

\left({\sigma/m}\right)_{\rm{SIDM}}=\left(\frac{g_{\chi_{2}}}{g_{\chi_{1}}}\right)^{4}\cdot\left({\sigma/m}\right)_{\rm{uSIDM}}=f\cdot\left({\sigma/m}\right)_{\rm{uSIDM}}~,

(2.2)

such that
\left({\sigma/m}\right)_{\rm{uSIDM}}\gg\left({\sigma/m}\right)_{\rm{SIDM}}
.

To get an initial insight, some SIDM models that interact via a Yukawa potential
[
17
,
18
]
exist in the Born region of their respective parameter spaces; thus, we can get a rough idea of the non-relativistic differential scattering cross-section by considering the perturbative limit of the self-interaction for either
\chi_{1}
or
\chi_{2}
. In the following equations, the calculations apply to both
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
. Since both interact via a Yukawa potential, in the Born limit the cross-section is well posed for
\chi\overline{\chi}\to\chi\overline{\chi}
. In the center of mass frame, Rutherford-like scattering is obtained
[
19
,
20
]
:

\frac{d\sigma}{d\cos\theta}=\frac{\sigma_{0}w^{4}}{2\left[{w^{2}+v^{2}\sin^{2}\left({\theta/2}\right)}\right]^{2}},

(2.3)

where
\sigma_{0}^{i}=4\pi\alpha_{\chi_{i}}^{2}/\left(m_{\chi}^{2}w^{4}\right)
with
\alpha_{\chi_{i}}=(g_{\chi_{i}})^{2}/4\pi
and a velocity transition scale,
w=m_{A^{\prime}}/m_{\chi}
, between the constant and power-law fall off of the cross-section;
v
is the relative velocity between the two initial particles, and
\theta
is the scattering angle after the particles have interacted. In the case of
\chi\chi\to\chi\chi
, in the center of mass frame, we obtain a Møller-like differential cross section
[
21
,
22
]
:

\frac{d\sigma}{d\cos\theta}=\frac{\sigma_{0}w^{4}\left[\left(3\cos^{2}\theta+1\right)v^{4}+4v^{2}w^{2}+4w^{4}\right]}{\left(\sin^{2}\theta v^{4}+4v^{2}w^{2}+4w^{4}\right)^{2}}.

(2.4)

To specify the full model, we have four free parameters:
\left\{{m_{\chi},~m_{A^{\prime}},~g_{\chi_{1}},~f}\right\}
.

2.1
Boltzmann Equations

uSIDM
2\to 2
processes: number conserving (left), number changing (center),
\overline{\chi}_{1}\chi_{1}/\overline{\chi}_{2}\chi_{2}\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}
(right). For the DM to dark photon annihilation, we draw the t-channel; we do not draw the u-channel for simplicity but we do include its contribution to the cross-section. Similarly, for the number conserving

In order to track the evolution of the dark matter number densities, we can write a Boltzmann equation for each dark matter species, but the key property is that we will need to be careful in how we deal with the annihilation and number changing interaction channels. In particular, we can calculate the branching ratio for the tree level s-channel interaction for
\chi_{1}\overline{\chi}_{1}
. From Fig.
1
(left), we can see that the invariant scattering amplitude,
\left|{\mathcal{M}_{11}}\right|^{2}
is

\left|{\mathcal{M}_{11}}\right|^{2}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}^{4},

and from Fig.
1
(center) we obtain,

\left|{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\right|^{2}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}^{2}g_{\chi_{2}}^{2}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}^{4}\sqrt{f},

where the last scaling follows from
g_{\chi_{2}}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}f^{1/4}
. So the number changing channel is suppressed by
\sqrt{f}
compared to the number conserving channel. The corresponding ratio of interaction rates is then:

\frac{\Gamma\left({\chi_{1}\overline{\chi}_{1}\to\chi_{2}\overline{\chi}_{2}}\right)}{\Gamma_{\rm tot}}\sim\frac{\left|{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\right|^{2}}{\left|{\mathcal{M}_{11}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\right|^{2}}\sim\frac{\left|{\mathcal{M}_{11}}\right|^{2}\sqrt{f}}{\left|{\mathcal{M}_{11}}\right|^{2}+\left|{\mathcal{M}_{11}}\right|^{2}\sqrt{f}}\sim\frac{\sqrt{f}}{1+\sqrt{f}}\sim\sqrt{f}~~~\text{for $f\ll 1$},

which is also suppressed by
\sqrt{f}
. This scaling of the amplitudes will aid in solving the Boltzmann equations for
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
, which are

\displaystyle\frac{dn_{1}}{dt}+3H(z)n_{1}=-\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\left({n_{1}^{2}-n_{1,eq}^{2}}\right)-\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to 22}\left({n_{1}^{2}-n_{2}^{2}}\right),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{dn_{2}}{dt}+3H(z)n_{2}=-\left<\sigma v\right>_{22\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\left({n_{2}^{2}-n_{2,eq}^{2}}\right)-\left<\sigma v\right>_{22\to 11}\left({n_{2}^{2}-n_{1}^{2}}\right).

(2.6)

If we convert to the yield equations, the system is then given by (and note that the conversion
11\to 22
is symmetric):

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{1}}{dx}=\frac{-s}{Hx}\left[{\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\left({Y_{1}^{2}-Y_{1,eq}^{2}}\right)+\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to 22}\left({Y_{1}^{2}-Y_{2}^{2}}\right)}\right],

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{2}}{dx}=\frac{-s}{Hx}\left[{\left<\sigma v\right>_{22\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\left({Y_{2}^{2}-Y_{2,eq}^{2}}\right)+\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to 22}\left({Y_{2}^{2}-Y_{1}^{2}}\right)}\right].

(2.8)

In general, one has to solve these coupled equations numerically; however, we can compare the rough order of magnitude of each thermally averaged cross-section. The
\overline{\chi}_{1}\chi_{1}\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}
annihilation will scale as
g_{\chi_{1}}^{4}
, whereas the conversion
\bar{\chi}_{1}\chi_{1}\to\bar{\chi}_{2}\chi_{2}
scales as
g_{\chi_{1}}^{2}g_{\chi_{2}}^{2}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}^{4}\sqrt{f}
, then
\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\gg\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to 22}
and we can therefore neglect the second term in the Eq. (
2.7
) (we show this rigorously in Section
2.2
). However, for Eq. (
2.8
), we cannot yet neglect either term. While the
\overline{\chi}_{2}\chi_{2}\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}
annihilation is suppressed by
\sqrt{f}
compared to the
\bar{\chi}_{1}\chi_{1}\to\bar{\chi}_{2}\chi_{2}
conversion, due to the equal final and initial state masses, the conversion cross-section is phase space suppressed. When we neglect the second term in Eq. (
2.7
), we obtain a much simpler equation:

\frac{dY_{1}}{dx}=\frac{-s}{Hx}\left[{\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\left({Y_{1}^{2}-Y_{1,eq}^{2}}\right)}\right].

(2.9)

The above equation can be solved analytically to get
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
,

\displaystyle Y_{1}(\infty)\sim\sqrt{\frac{45}{\pi}}\frac{x_{f_{1}}}{\sqrt{g^{*}}M_{\text{pl}}\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\chi_{1}}h^{2}=\frac{h^{2}m_{\chi}s_{0}Y_{1}(\infty)}{\rho_{\text{crit}}}\sim 2.742\times 10^{8}m_{\chi}Y_{1}(\infty),

(2.11)

where
x_{f}
is the dimensionless ratio of
m_{\chi}/T_{f}
, at the approximate freeze-out temperature,
T_{f}
. This reduces the complexity of the system, as it eliminates the coupled equations. We can then rewrite Eq. (
2.8
) as:

\frac{dY_{2}}{dx}=\frac{-s}{Hx}\left[{\left<\sigma v\right>_{22\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\left({Y_{2}^{2}-Y_{2,eq}^{2}}\right)+\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to 22}\left({Y_{2}^{2}-Y_{1,\text{eff}}(x)^{2}}\right)}\right],

(2.12)

where to a good approximation
Y_{1,\text{eff}}(x)
is
Y_{1,eq}(x)
when
x<x_{f,1}
, and is
Y_{1}(\infty)
when
x>x_{f,1}
. The total energy density in the dark sector is then given by:

\Omega_{\text{DM}}\text{h}^{2}=\Omega_{\chi_{1}}h^{2}+\Omega_{\chi_{2}}h^{2}.

(2.13)

We can also calculate the freeze-out value of the uSIDM fraction, i.e., the amount of the total dark matter that is left over after freeze-out to collapse into SMBH seeds:

f_{\rm uSIDM}^{\rm\infty}=\frac{\Omega_{\chi_{1}}h^{2}}{\Omega_{\chi_{1}}h^{2}+\Omega_{\chi_{2}}h^{2}}.

(2.14)

2.2
Thermally Averaged Cross-Sections

In order to solve Eq. (
2.10
) and Eq. (
2.12
), we need to know the thermally average cross-section for each process
[
25
]
:

\left<\sigma v\right>=\frac{1}{8m_{\chi}^{4}TK_{2}^{2}\left({m_{\chi}/T}\right)}\int_{4m_{\chi}^{2}}^{\infty}\sigma(s)\left({s-4m_{\chi}^{2}}\right)\sqrt{s}K_{1}\left({\frac{\sqrt{s}}{T}}\right)ds,

(2.15)

which means that we need to calculate the center of mass cross-section for each process in Fig.
1
(center) and Fig.
1
(right). In doing so, in Fig.
2
, we plot the comparison between all of the cross-sections as a function of
x_{i}=m_{\chi_{i}}/T
. We can see from Fig.
2
, that our justification in dropping the
\chi_{1}\bar{\chi}_{1}\to\chi_{2}\bar{\chi}_{2}
conversion cross-section from Eq. (
2.7
) is validated because of the many orders of magnitude difference in the hierarchy of the cross-sections. Similarly, even though the
\overline{\chi}_{2}\chi_{2}\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}
annihilation cross-section is suppressed by
\sqrt{f}
, we can drop the conversion cross-section from Eq. (
2.12
) due to the phase space suppression being a much larger suppression factor. Thus, we can analytically solve both Boltzmann equations in the form of Eq. (
2.10
) and Eq. (
2.11
).

Given that the fully thermal averaging is numerically expensive to compute, we follow Ref.
[
26
]
and expand
\left<\sigma v\right>
in inverse powers of
x
for all channels. In general, for species which are non-relativistic during freeze-out, as our particles are, one need only keep terms up to
O(x^{-1})
in the expansion:

\left<\sigma v\right>\sim a+\frac{6b}{x}+\cdots.

(2.16)

Figure 2
:
The conversion and self-annihilation cross-sections of the two dark matter species as a function of the inverse temperature
x
. We use the values
g_{\chi_{1}}=6.3\times 10^{-1},~m_{\chi}=1~\rm{GeV},~m_{A^{\prime}}=2\times 10^{-4}~\rm{GeV}
to demonstrate the cross-section hierarchy. The conversion cross-section is many orders of magnitude smaller than the annihilation cross-sections and we are thus justified in dropping the corresponding term of the Boltzmann equations. We also plot the non-relativistic expansion (dashed lines) of the annihilation cross-sections to show that indeed at higher values of
x
, both forms agree well.

In doing so, the annihilation cross-section (for both
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
) can be expanded into both s-wave and p-wave pieces to good approximation, i.e,
\left<\sigma v\right>_{11/22\to A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\sim a+\frac{6b}{x}
. However, for the conversion cross-section, this expansion does not hold. In particular, this is because the final state particle masses are equal to the initial state particle masses, which heavily suppress the cross-section compared to the other cross-sections (see the pink curve in Fig.
2
). When calculating the expansion in this case, we find that conversion cross-section can be expanded into the half-integer powers of
x
, i.e.,
\left<\sigma v\right>_{11\to 22}\sim a/x^{1/2}+b/x^{3/2}
. This behavior for general final and initial state masses can be seen from Ref.
[
26
]
. For the annihilation cross-section, the expansion in velocity is given by (
w=m_{A^{\prime}}/m_{\chi}
):

\displaystyle\sigma v_{\rm rel}~=~a(w)+b(w)\,v_{\rm rel}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(v_{\rm rel}^{4}),

(2.17)

\displaystyle a(w)=\frac{\pi\alpha_{\chi}^{2}}{m_{\chi}^{2}}\,\frac{(1-w^{2})^{3/2}}{(1-\tfrac{w^{2}}{2})^{2}},

(2.18)

\displaystyle b(w)=\frac{\pi\alpha_{\chi}^{2}}{m_{\chi}^{2}}\,\frac{\sqrt{1-w^{2}}\,\big(20w^{10}-83w^{8}+116w^{6}-20w^{4}-64w^{2}+64\big)}{24\,w^{4}(2-w^{2})^{4}}~.

(2.19)

In the non-relativistic limit,
s=4m_{\chi}^{2}(1+\tfrac{v^{2}}{4}+\cdots)
, Eq. (
2.15
) reduces to the Maxwell-Boltzmann thermal average,

\big\langle\sigma v\big\rangle\simeq\frac{x^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{2}e^{-xv^{2}/4}~(\sigma v)~dv,

(2.20)

which allows us to take the velocity expansion and convert to an expansion in the parameter
x=m/T
via
\left<v^{2}\right>_{\rm{Maxwell}}=\frac{6}{x}
. Thus, the non-relativistic thermally averaged cross-section is given by:

\left<\sigma v\right>(x)=a(w)+\frac{6b(w)}{x},

(2.21)

with
a(w)
and
b(w)
given above. With the above expansion, we can solve Eq. (
2.13
) analytically, under the assumption that
w\ll 1
, for the relic abundance relation between
m_{\chi}
and
\alpha_{\chi}
. From the SIDM literature
[
17
,
8
]
and references therein, the typical SIDM thermal relic used is
\left({m_{\chi}/\rm GeV}\right)\sim\alpha_{\chi}/\left({4\times 10^{-5}}\right)
. We should expect some modulation to this relation in our uSIDM picture, but in the limit that
f\to 0
, they should be consistent. By plugging Eq. (
2.9
) and Eq. (
2.11
) into Eq. (
2.13
), and using that
a(w)\to\frac{\pi\alpha_{\chi}^{2}}{m_{\chi}^{2}}
for
w\ll 1
, one finds:

\Omega_{\text{DM}}\text{h}^{2}=\frac{1.07\times 10^{9}}{\sqrt{g^{*}}M_{\text{pl}}\pi}\left[{x_{f_{1}}\frac{m_{\chi}^{2}}{\alpha_{\chi_{1}}^{2}}+x_{f_{2}}\frac{m_{\chi}^{2}}{\alpha_{\chi_{2}}^{2}}}\right].

Using
g_{\chi_{2}}\sim g_{\chi_{1}}f^{1/4}
, we can simplify the ratio
\left({\alpha_{\chi_{2}}/\alpha_{\chi_{1}}}\right)^{2}=f
, and then solve for
m_{\chi}
to get:

m_{\chi}=\frac{\alpha_{\chi_{2}}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\text{DM}}\text{h}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1.07\times 10^{9}}{\sqrt{g^{*}}M_{\text{pl}}\pi}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{f1}f+x_{f2}}}.

With
\Omega_{\text{DM}}\text{h}^{2}=0.12
,
\sqrt{g^{*}}\sim 90
, and
M_{\text{pl}}=1.22\times 10^{19}~\rm GeV
, the above equation reduces to,

\left({\frac{m_{\chi}}{\rm GeV}}\right)\sim\frac{\alpha_{\chi_{2}}}{2.2\times 10^{-5}}\sqrt{\frac{20}{x_{f2}}}\left({1-\Delta}\right),\\

(2.22)

where
\Delta\sim 8.7\times 10^{-5}\left({\frac{f}{10^{-3.76}}}\right)\left({\frac{x_{f1}}{30}}\right)\left({\frac{20}{x_{f2}}}\right)
. Thus, as
\Delta\ll 1
, the uSIDM particle only introduces a small correction onto the usual SIDM thermal relic relation. Similarly, we can calculate an analytic expression for the final uSIDM fraction via Eq. (
2.14
):

f_{\rm uSIDM}^{\rm\infty}=\frac{x_{f_{1}}\frac{m_{\chi}^{2}}{\alpha_{\chi_{1}}^{2}}}{x_{f_{1}}\frac{m_{\chi}^{2}}{\alpha_{\chi_{1}}^{2}}+x_{f_{2}}\frac{m_{\chi}^{2}}{\alpha_{\chi_{2}}^{2}}}\sim 10^{-3.5}\left({\frac{x_{f_{1}}}{30}}\right)\left({\frac{20}{x_{f_{2}}}}\right)\left({\frac{f}{10^{-3.76}}}\right)~.

(2.23)

From Ref.
[
6
]

f_{\rm uSIDM}^{\rm\infty}\sim 10^{-3.5}
. We use this value as a fiducial estimate throughout this work.

4
Conclusion

We have presented a minimal two–component self-interacting dark matter framework in which an ultra-strongly self-interacting subcomponent (uSIDM) coexists with a dominant, moderately self-interacting SIDM component. The interactions are mediated by a light dark photon, and the model is specified by the parameter set
\{m_{\chi},m_{A^{\prime}},g_{\chi_{1}},f\}
, where
f
denotes the abundance fraction of uSIDM. The key phenomenological inputs are (1) the SIDM coupling constant,
g_{\chi_{1}}
, and (2) the mass ratio between the dark photon and fermions,
m_{A^{\prime}}/m_{\chi}
.

On the particle-physics side, we derived the relevant thermally averaged cross sections and solved the coupled Boltzmann system for the two species. Using an explicit non-relativistic expansion, we showed that the conversion channel
\chi_{1}\chi_{1}\to\chi_{2}\chi_{2}
is highly suppressed in the parameter space of interest and admits an expansion in half-integer powers of
x
. This justifies the neglect of this channel in the freeze-out dynamics for our benchmarks. In turn, this leads to simple analytic expressions for the relic density which establishes a clear relation between
\alpha_{\chi_{2}}
and
m_{\chi}
in Eq. (
2.22
) as well as compact expression for the late-time uSIDM fraction in Eq. (
2.23
), up to the small corrections.

On the astrophysics side, we confronted the model with a set of complementary probes. We computed the viscosity cross-section
\sigma_{V}
for Yukawa-potential scattering and evaluated an effective cross section
\sigma_{\rm eff}(V_{\max})
using the velocity distribution appropriate for halos. Imposing
\sigma_{\text{eff}}/m=20-40~\text{cm}^{2}\text{g}^{-1}
at
V_{\rm{max}}=75\pm 25~\text{km}~\text{s}^{-1}
while requiring
\sigma_{\text{eff}}/m<0.13~\text{cm}^{2}\text{g}^{-1}
at
V_{\rm{max}}=1000\pm 55~\text{km}~\text{s}^{-1}
, we identified viable regions in
(\alpha_{\chi_{2}},~m_{A^{\prime}}/m_{\chi})
parameter space consistent with dwarf and cluster-scale observations. We computed the transfer function and verified that the scales affected by the light mediator and subpercent uSIDM fraction remain compatible with large-scale structure and Lyman-
\alpha
considerations for our benchmarks. In the space of direct detection we employ a photon/dark-photon kinetic mixing,
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
, and map the SIDM-nucleon cross section compared to current experimental limits for a representative choice
\varepsilon_{\gamma}\sim 10^{-10}
(motivated by supernova and stellar-cooling considerations
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
). As the uSIDM is depleted into black-hole seeds at early times, only the SIDM component of DM contributes today.

The resulting overlays imply a mediator-to-DM mass ratio requirement and a corresponding range for the dark matter interaction strength for viable parameter space,

10^{-4}\lesssim\frac{m_{A^{\prime}}}{m_{\chi}}\lesssim 10^{-3}~,\quad 10^{-6}\lesssim\alpha_{\chi_{2}}\lesssim 10^{-4}~,

(4.1)

for which: (1) the relic density of the dominant SIDM is obtained with moderate couplings as per Eq. (
2.22
), (2) the effective self-scattering satisfies requirements at dwarf galaxy and galaxy cluster scales, (3) the linear matter power spectrum remains within current bounds, and (4) representative direct-detection limits do not exclude the model for a maximal kinetic mixing. Within this window, the subpercent uSIDM fraction can achieve the ultra-strongly self-scattering required for rapid gravothermal evolution in early halos, providing a path to early black-hole seeding without disrupting late-time small-scale structure traced by the dominant SIDM.

Several directions follow directly from the ingredients developed here. A more exhaustive mapping of
\sigma_{\rm eff}(V_{\max})
to rotation-curve diversity using the same microphysical inputs would provide further insights into the viable parameter space of the uSIDM model. Additionally, more advanced modeling of cluster members using stellar kinematics as well as more precise observations of strong-lensing in galaxy clusters will further clarify the SIDM impacts on strong-lensing which can lead to further constraints in the uSIDM parameter space. The calculations performed here on the impacts of uSIDM on the linear matter power spectrum can be expanded to include a wider range of parameter space in
f
and
\xi
. Lastly, we assume throughout this work that the two DM components are of equal mass. Relaxing this assumption via the inclusion of a finite mass splitting could alter the cosmological history of the uSIDM model in interesting ways. Each of these can be addressed without extending the particle content, using the formalism we have established.

The minimal two-component uSIDM model presented here admits a simple, analytic model of the relic density and a clear organizing role for the mass ratio
m_{A^{\prime}}/m_{\chi}
. The phenomenology of uSIDM is simultaneously consistent with dwarf and cluster observations, linear matter power spectrum constraints, and representative direct-detection bounds. Coupled with the ability to produce Little Red Dots in the correct abundance, we have shown that the uSIDM model is both cosmologically consistent, as well as phenomenologically compelling.
