The evaluation of a lean healthcare concept to improve the implementation of Digital Health innovations in secondary health care: a qualitative study within a Dutch hospital setting
Authors: Hanna A. Kellner, Eric R. Edelman, Nicole van Eldik, Perry Hunen, Marieke Spreeuwenberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDigital health innovations are considered a promising avenue in transforming the resourcing, organization and delivery of healthcare services and are often nurtured in organization-based innovation labs. One of their biggest challenges is developing effective processes for sifting through ideas. To improve the existing work process, innovation management is often rooted in lean thinking principles. To illustrate the optimization of the efficiency of innovation processes, an example of a lean innovation process concept will be evaluated in this study.MethodsThe setting of this study was the Health Innovation Lab (HIL) of the Maastricht University Medical Center + (MUMC +) in the Netherlands. The aim was to evaluate its lean “speed dating” concept to improve the implementation of Digital Health innovations in secondary health care. A qualitative study was performed based on a focus group and semi-structured interviews. 36 stakeholders were selected by purposive sampling. Data was collected from 11 respondents. The transcripts of the focus group and interviews were coded according to the open and axial coding phases of the methods of Grounded Theory.ResultsFive major themes emerged during the thematic analysis of the transcriptions: (1) Preparation before the Sign in, (2) Mapping out the process between Sign in and Sign out, (3) Defining the roles and deliverables of experts, (4) Guiding the idea owner, and (5) Supporting capacity of resources.ConclusionsThrough multidisciplinary interaction, by encouraging creative thinking and by fostering innovative behavior, innovation labs cultivate the development of innovative solutions. While the creative phase of innovation commonly exhibits non-linearity, most innovation pilots and implementations follow a linear path. This linearity seems to contradict the importance of flexible approaches to prevent waste in the context of innovation. However, our results suggest that improving cooperation between stakeholders may be a strategy to mitigate this effect. This paper broadens the knowledge on how multidisciplinary interaction during a lean innovation process can be improved, thereby enhancing the implementation of DH innovations.

Background

Important demographic and epidemiological trends affect the demand for healthcare services. Noteworthy influences include a declining birth rate and higher life expectancy, a rise in the old-age dependency ratio, and an increase in mobility and urbanization [1]. Significant issues facing modern healthcare policy involve the increasing age of the population, the emergence of chronic illnesses at earlier stages of life, and the unequal distribution of multimorbidity and its impacts due to socioeconomic factors [2]. The combined result is an ever-increasing demand placed on healthcare services. The current capacity and resources available for providing cure and care are already proving insufficient to maintain the levels of service society expects [3]. Improving and expanding services under these conditions is even harder [4]. To illustrate, the World Health Organization estimates the current shortage of healthcare workers worldwide is already around 4.3 million. This number will grow to 10 million by 2030 [5, 6].

To address this widening gap between supply and demand, many organizations, researchers, governments and others have turned to the growing possibilities of digital health(DH) with the hope that they will contribute to transforming the resourcing, organization and delivery of healthcare services [7]. The integration of technologies such as remote monitoring, data analytics, and artificial intelligence may help expand the capacity of preventive, diagnostic and therapeutic options [8]. Because DH innovations are considered such a promising avenue of innovation, their number has grown exponentially over the past years. This in turn has resulted in an increasing number of healthcare stakeholders (such as managers, healthcare professionals, and patients) to be led into an overwhelming and cluttered DH landscape of novel ideas [9]. As described by Bates et al.:

“Developing effective processes for sifting through ideas for innovation is one of the biggest challenges facing health care innovation centers. Only a few of the ideas that are presented are likely to have a major impact on clinical care or prove to be commercially successful, so it is essential to weed out most-yet it is extremely hard to predict which concepts will bear fruit.” [10].

“Developing effective processes for sifting through ideas for innovation is one of the biggest challenges facing health care innovation centers. Only a few of the ideas that are presented are likely to have a major impact on clinical care or prove to be commercially successful, so it is essential to weed out most-yet it is extremely hard to predict which concepts will bear fruit.” [10].

This naturally leads to the question how this sifting should be performed. While Bates et al. recommend developing a number of ideas simultaneously, the authors also suggest assessing multiple dimensions of the innovations as to elicit their quality and value. This way only the most promising need to be nurtured. Though originally aimed at achieving commercial success, the method described by the authors for screening innovations can be applied more generically. It consists of forming experienced multidisciplinary panels that include members with the relevant expertise to make the final decision to continue development or not. In addition to filtering only the most promising ideas, multidisciplinary collaboration achieved with these panels is also considered crucial in enhancing the development of innovations [11].

Increasingly, innovation labs play an important role within organizations “in supporting the enhancement of innovation capabilities and developing and testing innovative ideas and solutions” [12]. Fostering an atmosphere of cross-disciplinary collaboration and diversity is intended to promote creativity, which in turn may result in an increase in the quality and productivity of innovative solutions, and also ties in well with the multidisciplinary approach described above for prioritizing innovations with high potential [13]. But this creative agility does not always lead to efficiency in the process of innovation itself [14]. Partly, this is inherent to the exploration of new technologies, products, and services, which is what innovation encompasses [15]. However, the greater organisation an innovation lab is expected to operate in may also present various barriers. These include risk aversion, prioritizing short-term goals, ineffective leadership, bureaucratic policies and procedures, resource limitations, and external resistance [16].

These barriers can present a significant obstacle to keeping innovation lean and efficient. For many organizations, their innovation management is rooted in the fundamental principles of lean thinking: “to add value to customers, improve effectiveness, eliminate waste, minimize operating costs, and reduce time-to-market through the redesign of the innovation processes and their automation” [17]. The primary objective of lean thinking should be to optimize efficiency of the innovation process.

Considering the optimization of the efficiency of innovation processes, it is interesting to investigate: what might such a lean innovation process look like that enables multidisciplinary collaboration and still leaves enough room for creativity? This paper describes our evaluation of an example of a lean innovation concept.

Methods

Setting of the study and aim

An example of an innovation lab that plays an important role in developing and testing innovative ideas and solutions, is the Health Innovation Lab (HIL) of the Maastricht University Medical Center + (MUMC +) in The Netherlands. Here, the assessment of innovative ideas and solutions is performed by applying an Innovation Loop (Fig. 1). This framework was created to stimulate cooperation between healthcare workers so that eventually high-potential ideas and solutions devised by hospital employees (idea owners) can be better distinguished from less promising ones. Additionally, it offers a practical platform for executing and coordinating the hospital’s strategy concerning digital health.Fig. 1Innovation Loop and speed dating concept

Innovation Loop and speed dating concept

In phase 1a (“Problem exploration”), idea owners and employees of the HIL sit down together to develop the idea into a presentable format. The steering committee for digital healthcare in the MUMC + then uses the Quintuple Aim model to identify which ideas hypothetically meet the criteria to realize real quality and value: improving patient experience, improving provider experience, reducing healthcare costs, enhancing population health, and advancing health equity [18]. Based on cooperation between employees of different departments of the hospital and the idea owner, only the most hypothetically successful ideas are allowed to continue to iterative prototyping, implementation and scaling up (the phases 1b “Pilot”, 2 “Implementation” and 3 “Managing”).

After the Innovation Loop was implemented in 2018, a qualitative research study among healthcare employees (n = 20) who worked in the hospital and used the framework, confirmed that there were still several organizational challenges in the use of the Innovation Loop. For the expert group, seven organizational problems were identified: an unstructured work process, lack of multidisciplinary communication, information asymmetry, insufficient resources, unclear division of roles, low quality of documentation, and an inefficient work approach (A full description is given in Appendix A.) The study also showed that the idea owners experienced insufficient cooperation from other healthcare stakeholders their projects depended on. Not surprisingly, the employees of the HIL who coordinate the innovation process strongly felt that the existing Innovation Loop needed optimization.

Since the biggest organizational challenges among the three groups was a feeling of insufficiency in the level of collaboration between healthcare stakeholders, the HIL added the concept of “speed dating” to the Innovation Loop in 2020 (Fig. 1). This allows the experts to meet the idea owners and work together in multidisciplinary panels, enabling more awareness of who is currently working on which step in the process from idea to innovation. The speed dating concept consists of the following parts:Sign in: The idea owner pitches the innovation to the whole panel. This is followed by speed dating sessions between the idea owner and the experts. These sessions are used to determine which conditions need to be met for the pilot phase to be started. These conditions may include the availability of resources, budget, documentation and signed contracts with third parties.Sign out: A follow-up meeting after the pilot phase between the idea owner and experts to evaluate whether the (usually more extensive) conditions have been met for implementation and/or scaling up to be started. This meeting results in a possible implementation plan.

The Sign in and Sign out are anchored to the start and finish of the pilot phase: the phase in which an innovation idea is developed into a prototype [19].

From February 2021 till May 2022, the processing of nine DH innovations was used to gain experience with the speed dating concept. An important question was what the experts, idea owners and employees of the HIL thought of the speed dating concept. To find out, the following research question was formulated: How is the speed dating concept experienced by experts, idea owners and employees of the HIL and what are their suggestions for improvement? The aim of this study is to evaluate this lean healthcare concept to improve the implementation of Digital Health innovations in secondary health care.

Study design

To achieve this study aim, a qualitative research design was utilized. This study design was useful for our purposes, because it aims to comprehend and investigate non-numerical data in a contextual and interpretative way [20]. The qualitative study design was primarily based on a focus group. This group provided insight into the experiences and possible improvements described by the experts, idea owners and employees of the HIL with regard to the speed dating concept. It can be defined as a method in which a researcher gathers a group of people to discuss a particular subject, with the intention of drawing on personal experiences and perceptions [21]. As Sim and Waterfield put it, on the one hand, a focus group is considered a meeting (reflecting the planned and structured intent), while on the other hand it is considered a conversation (reflecting the spontaneous integrative approach) [22]. In addition to the focus group, semi-structured interviews were organized as an alternative for the stakeholders who were unable to join the focus group.

Characteristics of participants

36 stakeholders were selected by purposive sampling and approached to participate in this study. To ensure the most relevant respondents, a number of inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined. The inclusion criteria were that participants were currently working at the MUMC + in an expert field, as employee of the HIL, or as another employee and idea owner of an innovation. They must have been present at a minimum of one Sign in session, and understand and speak Dutch. Participants who were unable to join the focus group or the semi-structured interviews were excluded. Although initially to only plan a focus group with as much as possible stakeholders, a lot of invited stakeholders were not able to join the focus group session because of time constraints or other practical issues. Therefore, also semi-structured interviews were planned to include more stakeholders. Finally, data was collected from 11 respondents. Six of them were included in both the former qualitative research study as in the current qualitative study. From the 11 respondents, six respondents participated in the focus group and five respondents participated in the semi-structured interviews. The distinction between employees of the HIL, experts and idea owners was based on their job titles and their predefined roles in the speed dating concept. Of all eleven respondents, six participants (R5, R6, R8, R9, R10, and R11) were employed as experts. Their job titles were Information Manager, Chief Information Security Officer, Patient Communication Specialist, Medical Device Regulation Specialist, Technical Application Manager, and Functional Application Manager. Two participants (R4 and R7) were employees of the hospital and took part in the speed dating concept as idea owners. Finally, three participants (R1, R2 and R3) were employees of the HIL. Although anonymity could not be achieved among the participants during the focus group because everyone knew each other, the characteristics of the participants have been pseudonymised in the tables below for the publication of this study (Tables 1 and 2). Data collection

The focus group was organized in the MUMC + on 13 May 2022 and lasted one and a half hours. Experts, idea owners and employees of the HIL had the chance to discuss their experiences with the speed dating concept and suggest improvements. Discussion was prompted by the lead researcher by using a predetermined topic list (Appendix B). No validated questionnaires were used. A topic list was prepared in order to structure the discussion into general experience, positive experience, negative experience and future. The questions to prompt these topics were:What is your experience with the speed dating concept?What is your negative experience with the speed dating concept?What is your positive experience with the speed dating concept?If you look back at the positive and negative experiences, how can the speed dating concept be further improved?

The semi-structured interviews included the same questions as the focus group. Based on the insights of the focus group, the principal author asked further questions when themes from the focus group were recognized.

Data processing and analysis

The audio recordings of the focus group and interviews were transcribed verbatim. Coding was performed in two phases by the principal author and supervised by the last author according to the methods of Grounded Theory [23]. During open coding, relevant text fragments which were associated with the research question were identified and separated from the transcripts into discrete components: the identified fragments were summarized into codes consisting of one to five words. During the subsequent axial coding phase, the relations between the codes were considered. In this phase, codes were organized by category. Each category was created by including a variety of codes. Afterwards, the resulting categories were cleaned and summarized, primarily bundling content-comparable codes and deleting duplicate codes.

Preparation before the Sign in

The HIL shares a short description of the innovation with the experts prior to the Sign in. This enables the experts to read up on the innovation and think ahead about questions to ask during the Sign in. Three respondents described being satisfied with this short description, while six respondents claimed that experts still missed important information about the innovation. They would have preferred to have this information available at an earlier stage:

“[…] when we are far into the process: ‘Isn’t it useful to check whether [the innovation] has a digital privacy impact analysis?’ Then I think: ‘Why didn’t we ask that question at the beginning?’.” (R5).

“[…] when we are far into the process: ‘Isn’t it useful to check whether [the innovation] has a digital privacy impact analysis?’ Then I think: ‘Why didn’t we ask that question at the beginning?’.” (R5).

Besides the information intended for the experts, three respondents also described missing information for idea owners prior to the Sign in. This especially concerned the pitch, in which the idea owner presents the innovation to the experts. Several experts experienced that the pitches often differed in their levels of preparation and depth. Two respondents specifically mentioned the need to remind idea owners to present in regular Dutch, not using Latin or English medical terminology and abbreviations.

Two respondents from the HIL did not agree with this need for additional information prior to the Sign in, as presented by the experts and idea owners. They explained that the Sign in was initially intended to only pitch the idea. One respondent warned that over-preparing and informing experts and idea owners would make the speed dating concept feel akin a regular application process, which would be at the expense of the current interaction between experts and idea owners:

“[Otherwise] you will see that the idea owner says: Is that so? If I fill in ‘yes’ here [on a preparatory questionnaire], then: Oh, what a hassle that will be! I’ll fill in ‘no’!.” (R2)

“[Otherwise] you will see that the idea owner says: Is that so? If I fill in ‘yes’ here [on a preparatory questionnaire], then: Oh, what a hassle that will be! I’ll fill in ‘no’!.” (R2)

However, three respondents suggested still letting the idea owner complete a “smart form” prior to the Sign in to provide additional information to the experts. This form could serve as a quick scan to analyse and assess DH ideas at an early stage. The group proposed designing a questionnaire to collect details about the innovation idea, such as the target population, motive for development, method of development, and whether certain requirements had already been met. An important question would be whether the innovation will be self-developed or purchased as an existing product. Second to this, it would be helpful to know if the innovation would be considered a medical device and would need to be CE marked:

“To quickly determine ‘he needs that or not’. [To] see what needs to be done […], what risks [there are], ‘does the idea meet certain elements?’ of which we can say: ‘Then can we take the next step?’.” (R5).

“To quickly determine ‘he needs that or not’. [To] see what needs to be done […], what risks [there are], ‘does the idea meet certain elements?’ of which we can say: ‘Then can we take the next step?’.” (R5).

In addition to gaining a better understanding of the innovation, it was expected that this questionnaire would enable experts to provide more specific advice. This could also mean attaching a different expert to the innovation than usual, due to specific knowledge or experience. One respondent also expected the information to make it easier to determine whether a similar innovation already existed within the hospital. One respondent suggested designing the smart form as a web application.

Mapping out the process between Sign in and Sign out

All respondents described feeling involved with the innovation during the Sign in. For example, one respondent felt that the Sign in increased cooperation between experts and idea owners, due to the time spent together and getting to know each other at a personal level. Two respondents described that this made it easier to plan follow-up appointments.

Despite this general feeling of involvement (and the expectation that this would be the same during the Sign out), six respondents (mainly experts) indicated that the experts did not feel involved in the phase between the Sign in and the Sign out:

“We have had the Sign in and some follow-up sessions, but now it is quiet. […] I am not aware of the development.” (R10)

“We have had the Sign in and some follow-up sessions, but now it is quiet. […] I am not aware of the development.” (R10)

Additionally, two respondents (both idea owners) found it difficult to understand which steps needed to be taken between the Sign in and the Sign out:

“[…] then you do have those contacts [but] I don’t know yet […] in which order I should see those names. […] What are the steps I have to go through? And in what order? […] When do I have to deliver something? And which documents?” (R7).

“[…] then you do have those contacts [but] I don’t know yet […] in which order I should see those names. […] What are the steps I have to go through? And in what order? […] When do I have to deliver something? And which documents?” (R7).

To strengthen the involvement of experts with innovation, four respondents proposed digitally monitoring the progress of projects between the Sign in and Sign out. One respondent suggested doing so using a website, newsletter or Excel file. This would effectively force idea owners to provide updates and have the HIL employees share these with the experts. This would make it easier for experts to intervene when necessary:

“Maybe […] with a line with dots. That you see ‘[these expert fields are] connected… we’ll try this in a month’s time’. [That] you know: it is going well, stops for a while or the work is in progress. […] with colours you can indicate a kind of stage of progress.” (R8).

“Maybe […] with a line with dots. That you see ‘[these expert fields are] connected… we’ll try this in a month’s time’. [That] you know: it is going well, stops for a while or the work is in progress. […] with colours you can indicate a kind of stage of progress.” (R8).

One respondent expected that it should be feasible for an idea owner to regularly write a summary of the progress. It was mentioned that the HIL could facilitate this by developing a standard reporting format. One respondent also expected that digital monitoring would make it possible for experts to perform a risk analysis of the innovation more efficiently:

“[That] certain expert fields already say [to] the rest of the experts: […] ‘I will get started with it, you take it easy for a while.” (R2)

“[That] certain expert fields already say [to] the rest of the experts: […] ‘I will get started with it, you take it easy for a while.” (R2)

To support idea owners in understanding which steps exist between the Sign in and Sign out, three respondents suggested creating a general roadmap, visualizing when an idea owner should contact a certain expert or when a certain deliverable becomes essential, such as a contract. It was noted, however, that this might be difficult to realize, because each new idea differs sufficiently from the previous ideas for the steps to be different as well.

Defining the roles and deliverables of experts

All respondents described that the speed dating concept successfully facilitated contact between idea owners and experts. This in turn led to a clearer understanding of which deliverables (such as documents, designs, risk assessments, and recommendations etc.) were needed per expert field in preparation of the Sign in and subsequent pilot phase. Four respondents remarked that this had made their work more efficient. Nevertheless, four other respondents still felt that it was unclear for idea owners what the division of roles and responsibilities between them and the experts was exactly. This example shows how a required document (in this case a digital privacy impact analysis) should have been drafted by a different expert in advance, but because this was not done, the question now lands with the idea owner, who is insufficiently equipped to complete this task:

“Then [an expert asked]: Has a digital privacy impact analysis been prepared already? [The idea owner responds:] Never heard of it. What do you need from me?” (R6)

“Then [an expert asked]: Has a digital privacy impact analysis been prepared already? [The idea owner responds:] Never heard of it. What do you need from me?” (R6)

Three respondents indicated an additional uncertainty. Some experts were unaware of who exactly the other experts were and what services they delivered during the innovation process. This sometimes resulted in confusion and was mainly explained by the freedom of the innovation process. Five respondents suggested that this was partly due to an overlap in the services provided by the various experts. One respondent did not agree though and suggested that it was due to the patient missing as an expert during the Sign in. The Communication expert effectively taking the place of the patient further contributed to the indistinct roles.

Various suggestions for improvement for the speed dating concept were mentioned, mainly focussing on combatting the lack of clarity. One idea owner suggested creating a list of names, job titles, and short descriptions of the deliverables per expert field:

“So that you have an idea why [for example] someone from Contract Management is present and what he wants to know about me [as an idea owner] and that I can take care of that.” (R7)

“So that you have an idea why [for example] someone from Contract Management is present and what he wants to know about me [as an idea owner] and that I can take care of that.” (R7)

Two experts proposed organizing masterclasses about the expert fields, deliverables, and expected questions for the idea owner during the Sign in. This could be supplemented with information about the mission and vision of the speed dating concept. A document containing this information would make it easier for colleagues to familiarize themselves with it. This might benefit both idea owners as experts.

Three respondents from the HIL did not agree with the suggested forms of improvements to clarify the roles and deliverables of the experts, as presented by the experts and idea owners. They explained that the roles and deliverables of the experts can only be clarified by cooperation between experts and idea owners. One respondent noted that in this cooperation it is up to the experts themselves to clarify to the idea owners what their roles and deliverables are and therefore also when they are required to proceed:

“Experts are experts for a reason. […] They know better than anyone else when they are needed in the entire development process. […] but […] this [clarification] takes time.” (R2)

Guiding the idea owner

One respondent specifically pointed out being satisfied with the concept of idea owners itself, because this makes it clear who is responsible for the development process.

More practically speaking, two respondents described being satisfied with the guidance idea owners receive during the Sign in. An idea owner, for example, found it very helpful that notes are made containing all the names of the experts who ask questions and with whom follow-up appointments are to be made.

Experts, however, indicated that the guidance idea owners receive might still be lacking, because the pitches often differed greatly in the amount of information being presented. One respondent also experienced that idea owners were often insufficiently aware of the responsibilities of an idea owner. Additionally, the complexity of project leadership is regularly underestimated:

“[It] will depend on the project and idea owner, [but] often they are inexperienced people who have a nice idea, but don’t actually know about the process. […] We are not trained in that.” (R7)

“[It] will depend on the project and idea owner, [but] often they are inexperienced people who have a nice idea, but don’t actually know about the process. […] We are not trained in that.” (R7)

Despite this desire among experts and one idea owner to expand the current guidance of idea owners, three respondents from the HIL warned about the risk of idea owners then simply transferring tasks to others, at the expense of the idea owner concept:

“I think the HIL is precisely intended to […] make use of the innovative power we have in this organization!” (R3)

“I think the HIL is precisely intended to […] make use of the innovative power we have in this organization!” (R3)

Four respondents proposed introducing an innovation coach; someone who might assist the idea owner in the process of managing projects and making contacts. Three respondents suggested that the innovation coach should also regularly evaluate the progress of the innovation among the experts. It was expected that this would result in even stronger cooperation between experts and a clearer picture of what the speed dating concept entails, especially for experts that work outside the hospital walls:

“'(So) a person who collects what everyone has done and who contacts the other experts in the meantime: 'how are you doing?', 'have you already been in contact?' […] 'can I assume that you will take care of that? ' [And] also providing feedback to the Health Innovation Lab: 'these are the agreements made’’. (R11).

“'(So) a person who collects what everyone has done and who contacts the other experts in the meantime: 'how are you doing?', 'have you already been in contact?' […] 'can I assume that you will take care of that? ' [And] also providing feedback to the Health Innovation Lab: 'these are the agreements made’’. (R11).

Two respondents suggested that the Sign in and the Sign out should be chaired by the innovation coach in the future to provide a clear overview of the involved stakeholders. The innovation coach could also manage time during the Sign in and take final responsibility for forwarding the pitch presentation afterwards. An additional suggestion was to let the innovation coach invite previous idea owners to the Sign in to remind experts of past successes and maintain their enthusiasm.

Supporting capacity of resources

Four respondents described being satisfied with the current workload and being able to handle it well with the available capacity of resources, such as finances, time, and employees. However, six respondents voiced concern that these resources will not be sufficient in the long term when two or three innovations per month will need to be processed. According to four respondents, this insufficiency was already becoming noticeable, since some experts are regularly absent from the Sign in, hurting the innovation process:

“If two or three expert fields are missing, the added value decreases enormously.” (R6)

“If two or three expert fields are missing, the added value decreases enormously.” (R6)

For this reason, two respondents suggested organizing the speed dating concept at a higher organizational level within the hospital, with the aim of using the resources of different expert departments more efficiently. For example, one respondent suggested adding the Chief Information Officer or the Director of Patient Care as a sponsor to the speed dating concept to ensure approval of the resources requested. Alternatively, four respondents suggested letting the heads of the idea owner’s or experts’ departments act as sponsors in case of capacity issues. The reasoning being that this sponsor would be able to realize more specific resources, since C-level executives and directors are already responsible for a large number of projects and are generally less directly involved in the development process. In addition to adding a sponsor, it was suggested by one respondent to continue planning the Sign in for when as many experts as possible would be available.

Discussion

Creativity, innovative thinking, and the acceptance of controlled risk associated with entrepreneurship is only possible within an environment where these values are not only tolerated but stimulated. Besides being a phenomenon in an organization’s culture, it has distinct advantages to offer a physical location where various stakeholders can meet to develop their skills and ideas. Innovation labs are intended to provide such a space, generally offering more freedom to employees to explore new solutions and opportunities than the organization at large [13].

To allow this freedom in a fairly hierarchical organizational structure—such as typically seen in professional organizations like hospitals—an innovation lab is often positioned relatively separate from the rest of the organization. This might fit the independent culture intended for the lab to have, but also creates certain risks. Primarily, innovation labs do not directly control resources located elsewhere within the organization, such as manpower and available funds. This can have an impact on how effectively an innovation lab can realize its goals, especially considering the typically low budgets and small teams allocated to innovation. Consequently, most labs must rely heavily on convincing others within or outside the organization to collaborate and provide the required resources. This limits their ability to independently achieve widespread change through a sector, such as regional or national healthcare [24].

However, in the setting of secondary health care, innovation labs are still expected to play an important role in the development and introduction of DH innovations. Through multidisciplinary interaction, encouraging creative thinking and fostering innovative behavior, innovation labs cultivate the development of innovative solutions [25]. In this process, the innovation process itself can be defined as follows: “[It] is a non-linear cycle of divergent and convergent activities that may repeat in unpredictable ways over time. It is highly iterative, and organisations may enter the process at different stages and backtrack to earlier points but engaging in innovation follows a broadly agreed life cycle” [26]. While innovation processes commonly exhibit this non-linearity, in practice, most innovations follow a linear development path when an organization attempts to eliminate waste in this process [27]. On this path, innovations progress through a series of distinct developmental stages with minor transitions facilitating adjustments between each stage [28]. This linearity might seem to contradict the importance of flexible approaches to allow room for emergent creation. Although the lean speed dating concept discussed in this paper has a linear approach with its distinct Sign in and Sign out phases, it also contains room for the creative process in the form of the Innovation Loop, as previously described. This combines the minimalization of waste in the overall process, but still leaves room for emergent learning and the flexible inclusion of stakeholder perspectives.

Since multidisciplinary interaction is so crucial to the efficiency with which innovations can be initiated, filtered for success, and made to grow, it is perhaps not surprising that the most important improvements suggested by our results are also associated with improving cooperation. These include developing smart forms for idea owners to enhance information collection prior to the first meeting with the experts, digital monitoring of an innovation’s progress, clarifying the roles and deliverables of the expert fields by various media (documents, master classes), introducing an innovation coach role to assist the idea owner with project management and introduce him/her to relevant contacts, and adding a sponsor to establish and solidify the lab’s position within the organization. While all these results are associated with improving cooperation, we expect the results on progress monitoring, clarifying deliverables, and introducing coaches to be easily combined with one another. They all improve multidisciplinary collaboration between stakeholders and take place between the Sign in and the Sign out.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, the study population was limited to employees of our own hospital and only a limited number of the invited participants participated. Although we are convinced many of our results are applicable to innovation labs and hospitals in general, having our study include participants from other labs might have resulted in a higher external validity. Additionally, not all stakeholders that emerged in the results were included in the initial study population. For example, C-level executives, directors and patients were not interviewed. Healthcare organizations are historically dependent on traditional and linear innovation models, also known as technology-push models, in which patients have often been seen as passive recipients rather than influential participants on the innovation [29]. Following these principles of traditional and linear innovation models, no major effect is expected of not including patients in our study since in the linear speed dating concept, patients are also not involved in the innovation process. However, this can still be seen as a missed opportunity to guarantee the relevance and acceptance of innovations among its end-users [30].

Secondly, the experiences shared by the participants in the focus group may differ from reality as a result of peer pressure. There is always a chance participants had a motivation to describe their experiences untruthfully or incompletely.

Thirdly, not all participants were interviewed during the focus group because they were not able to attend. Although every attempt was made to provide as much context during the individual interviews as was discussed during the focus group, the different setting may have resulted in a misinterpretation of the reactions. Although it might have been tempting to schedule only semi-structured interviews given the possible bias due to peer pressure in a focus group, organizing a focus group also had an important advantage. Data obtained from focus groups holds substantial promise for delving into knowledge that is uniquely formed through interactions with others [31].

Conclusions

Innovation labs are crucial in fostering an environment that stimulates experimentation and scaling of (digital) healthcare innovations. Considering that they strongly depend on communication and collaboration to access external expertise and resources, the results of this study may be interpreted as a number of suggestions for making optimal use of an innovation lab within a large organization, such as a secondary health care center wanting to improve its portfolio of digital innovations.

For these organizations, we recommend examining the possibilities to introduce a similar process as the MUMC + ’s speed dating concept. Our study results confirm the advantages of such an approach, but also highlight the necessity of continuous improvement. These improvements should focus on the coordination of joint efforts between stakeholders and the securing of resources. For example, clarifying the roles and deliverables of the experts and assisting the idea owner with project management are forms of improvement which are expected to increase the innovation lab’s efficacy. Additionally, adding a sponsor who would be able to acquire specific resources might give innovation labs more stability and independence.

These results may inspire other hospitals in improving the implementation of DH innovations in the setting of secondary health care.
