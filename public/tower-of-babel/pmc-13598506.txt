Epididymal and testicular abscesses: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Abdulaziz Saleh A. Alghamdi, Ata B. Gungor, Oleg Mironov, Lorraine Min-Shan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epididymal and testicular abscesses are uncommon complications of epididymo-orchitis traditionally managed with open surgical incision and drainage or orchiectomy. We report a 75-year-old man with polymicrobial scrotal abscess presenting with dysuria, pain, swelling, and mild leukocytosis. Diagnosis was confirmed by scrotal ultrasound, and he was treated with ultrasound-guided percutaneous catheter drainage and antibiotics. Improvement in pain and swelling followed with resolution of leukocytosis. This case and our narrative review demonstrate that image-guided drainage is a feasible organ-preserving option for selected patients with accessible collections, although evidence remains limited to case reports and small series.

Introduction

Epididymal and testicular abscesses are uncommon but clinically significant complications of epididymo-orchitis, urinary tract infection, trauma, immunosuppression, tuberculosis, and Crohn's disease.1,2 In the modern antibiotic era, abscess formation has become less frequent, yet the consequences of delayed recognition remain serious; testicular loss is a real outcome when drainage is deferred.3,4 For most of the twentieth century, management defaulted to open surgical incision and drainage (I&D) or orchiectomy.5,6 That picture is shifting. A small but growing body of case reports and retrospective series describes ultrasound-guided needle aspiration and percutaneous catheter or tube drainage as organ-preserving alternatives in selected patients.7,8

No prospective comparative trial of drainage modalities has been conducted in this population.9 The existing literature primarily consists of case reports, small series, and expert opinion. We present this narrative synthesis of the literature alongside a case from our institution to illustrate the clinical decision-making and management considerations in patients presenting with scrotal abscesses.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old male with no previous urological history presented from an assisted living facility with a 10-day history of progressively worsening left-sided scrotal pain and swelling. He also reported dysuria but no other infectious symptoms. His past medical history was significant for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, abdominal aortic aneurysm, Wernicke encephalopathy, and ataxia. Prior to presentation, he had completed a 7-day course of oral cefuroxime without clinical improvement.

On presentation, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Physical examination demonstrated an enlarged and tender left hemiscrotum with mild overlying erythema and a collection inseparable from the left testicle. The collection was clinically felt to be located within the testicular parenchyma beneath the tunica albuginea, raising concern for an intratesticular abscess.

Scrotal ultrasonography revealed a 4.0 × 3.9 × 3.0 cm heterogeneous extratesticular collection within the left hemiscrotum containing echogenic foci with ring-down artifact suggestive of gas locules and concerning for abscess formation. Laboratory investigations demonstrated mild leukocytosis of 14.3 × 109/L. Other laboratory findings including urinalysis were unremarkable.

Despite imaging findings suggesting an extratesticular abscess, the collection remained clinically inseparable from the testicle on examination. Given the concern for possible intratesticular involvement, bedside needle aspiration was avoided because of the potential risk of testicular injury. Operative incision and drainage was also considered undesirable in this medically complex patient, as it would have required general anesthesia, endotracheal intubation, and exposed the patient to the inherent risks of surgical intervention.

Given these considerations, the patient underwent an ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage of the left scrotal abscess under local anesthesia and conscious sedation by an interventional radiologist. An 8-Fr drain was inserted using a trocar technique, yielding purulent fluid that was sent for microbiological analysis. The drain was left in situ following the procedure and had a total output of 40 mL over the subsequent 48 hours.

He was started on empiric antibiotics and later was transitioned to ceftriaxone and metronidazole after infectious diseases consultation. Culture of the drained purulent material demonstrated a polymicrobial infection with mixed aerobic and anaerobic organisms, including Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, Bacteroides fragilis, and Clostridium species. Urine cultures remained negative.

Following drainage and antibiotic therapy, the patient demonstrated progressive clinical improvement with resolution of leukocytosis and significant reduction in scrotal pain and swelling.

Discussion

Etiology and risk factors

Most epididymal and testicular abscesses arise from untreated or refractory epididymo-orchitis. The predominant bacterial pathogens in immunocompetent adults are Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and sexually transmitted organisms including Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae.1,10 A Chinese tertiary-center series of 442 epididymitis admissions identified abscess formation in 24 patients (5.4%); caput epididymitis was more likely to involve concurrent orchitis and yielded positive cultures more often than corpus or caudal disease.1

Immunosuppression shifts the microbiology substantially. Fungal abscesses caused by Candida parapsilosis and Aspergillus fumigatus have both been reported, the former in a diabetic patient and the latter in a renal transplant recipient diagnosed on fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC).3,4 Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) is the most important pathogen in endemic regions; genitourinary TB can present as a chronic, indolent abscess that mimics malignancy and may be complicated by calcification, sinus tracts, and fistulae.5,6 Brucellosis is a close second in endemic areas: in a retrospective Turkish series of 25 cases of brucellar epididymo-orchitis, virtually all patients recovered on prolonged combination antibiotic therapy, though one patient with a discrete testicular abscess required surgical drainage.8 Crohn's disease is a rare but increasingly recognized precipitant; at least one case has described a testicular abscess as the first manifestation of penetrating perianal Crohn's disease, ultimately requiring orchiectomy and fecal diversion.11

Diagnosis and imaging

Scrotal ultrasonography is the diagnostic cornerstone. High-resolution grayscale imaging demonstrates hypoechoic or heterogeneous lesions with or without internal echoes; color Doppler interrogation characterizes vascularity and separates viable from necrotic parenchyma.12,13 Associated pyocele, reactive hydrocele, and epididymal thickening are identified on the same examination. In a Saudi Arabian referral cohort of 63 epididymo-orchitis patients, ultrasonography detected abscesses in 11.1% and hypoechoic lesions in a further 4.8%.2 Point-of-care ultrasound achieves high specificity for scrotal pathology in emergency settings and can accelerate the decision to drain 13.

Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) complement ultrasound when abscess extent is uncertain or a secondary source is suspected, for example prostatic or Crohn's disease involvement. FNAC serves a dual role: it confirms the microbiological diagnosis in ambiguous cases, particularly TB and fungal infection, while simultaneously decompressing unilocular collections.5,6 In brucellar disease, combining color Doppler sonography with serology and fine-needle aspiration avoids misdiagnosis as a testicular tumor and prevents unnecessary orchiectomy.14

Treatment modalities

Antibiotics alone

Systemic antibiotics are a required component of management regardless of the drainage strategy chosen. For small, well-defined abscesses in hemodynamically stable, non-immunocompromised patients who respond promptly, antibiotics alone may suffice. In brucellar epididymo-orchitis, virtually all patients in a 25-case Turkish series cleared the infection on prolonged dual therapy without drainage; only one, with a discrete testicular abscess, required surgical intervention.8 TB similarly responds to antitubercular therapy in most cases, with drainage reserved for collections that fail to resolve.5,6 Failure to improve clinically within 48 to 72 hours is the standard threshold for escalation.

Needle aspiration and fine-needle aspiration cytology

Needle aspiration is the least invasive interventional option and serves both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. FNAC has confirmed TB etiology from epididymal collections when urine culture was negative,5 established the diagnosis of Aspergillus fumigatus abscess in a transplant recipient,4 and allowed organ-sparing treatment of brucellar testicular abscess mimicking malignancy.14 MRI-guided seminal vesicle aspiration in a patient with concurrent testicular involvement achieved decompression without open surgery.15

Percutaneous catheter and tube drainage

Percutaneous catheter or tube drainage sits between simple aspiration and open surgery. Direct scrotal evidence is sparse but consistent. In a 12-day-old infant with acute pyocele of the tunica vaginalis, ultrasound-guided transcutaneous aspiration under local anesthesia achieved complete resolution, avoiding both general anesthesia and open exploration; this was the first reported use of percutaneous drainage for neonatal pyocele.16 A spermatic cord abscess complicating epididymo-orchitis in a diabetic patient on hemodialysis was initially controlled with ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage; when pus reaccumulated in the scrotum, open I&D completed the treatment, with full resolution at two months.17 Pancreatic fluid tracking retroperitoneally into the scrotum resolved after percutaneous pigtail catheter placement without scrotal exploration.18 Across these cases, image-guided drainage was effective for accessible, non-complex collections in stable patients; no prospective trial exists in this population.

Open incision and drainage

Open I&D remains the most common surgical approach in the published series. In a five-year retrospective study of 97 epididymo-orchitis patients, 15 (15.4%) developed testicular abscess; of those, 53.3% were managed with I&D, 40% required orchiectomy, and only one (6.7%) underwent ultrasound-guided aspiration.19 In a prepubertal boy with polymicrobial intratesticular abscess, operative I&D with primary wound closure and drain placement achieved testicular preservation.20

Orchiectomy

Orchiectomy is reserved for non-viable parenchyma, failed drainage, or cases where malignancy cannot be excluded. In 14 neonates with acute suppurative scrotal infection, early surgical exploration within 72 hours was associated with testicular preservation in 12 cases; both neonates explored after 72 hours required orchiectomy.9 Even large brucellar abscesses apparently replacing the entire testis have been salvaged by organ-sparing I&D combined with appropriate antibiotics, confirming that abscess size alone does not mandate removal if vascularity is preserved.7 TB orchitis with intraoperative evidence of ischemia and infarction represents the scenario where surgical removal is unavoidable.6

Proposed management algorithm

Direct comparative evidence for tube drainage versus open I&D does not exist in this population; the evidence base is case reports and small retrospective series throughout. Five decision points follow from the collective experience reviewed here.

Antibiotics alone are appropriate for small, unilocular abscesses in stable, non-immunocompromised patients who show prompt clinical improvement, particularly in brucellar and tuberculous disease.7,8 Needle aspiration or FNAC is the first interventional step when microbiological diagnosis is needed or when the collection is unilocular and accessible, as in most TB, brucellosis, and fungal cases.4,5,14 Percutaneous catheter or tube drainage is appropriate for accessible single or early multilocular abscesses in clinically stable patients without suspected malignancy or parenchymal necrosis.16,17 Open I&D is indicated when percutaneous access is inadequate, when the abscess is complex or multiloculated, when minimally invasive attempts have failed, or when intraoperative viability assessment is required.19 Orchiectomy follows when the parenchyma is necrotic or non-salvageable, or when malignancy cannot be ruled out.6,9 (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Scrotal ultrasonography showing a collection on the left hemiscrotum.

Limitations and future directions

The literature is dominated by single cases and small single-center series with no prospective comparative data. Reporting is inconsistent: abscess dimensions, catheter type, drainage duration, microbiological results, and follow-up imaging are frequently missing. These gaps make it impossible to define the precise role of tube drainage relative to open I&D from the current evidence. Structured prospective registries at burn and urological centers, with agreed minimum data sets, would be a practical first step. Future reports should specify imaging characteristics, drainage method, catheter dwell time, culture results, and testicular outcome at follow-up.

Conclusion

Epididymal and testicular abscess management has moved beyond the binary of antibiotics versus orchiectomy. Minimally invasive options, needle aspiration, and percutaneous catheter or tube drainage can achieve resolution without testicular loss in selected patients, particularly those with unilocular or accessible collections and intact parenchymal vascularity.7,8,14,15 Open I&D and orchiectomy remain essential when disease is advanced, multiloculated, or necrotic.6,9,19 The evidence base is thin and largely observational; prospective reporting of tube drainage outcomes is the most pressing unmet need in this field.
