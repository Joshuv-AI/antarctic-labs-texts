Solute Carrier Family 2 Member 1 Gene Mutation Presenting as Adult-Onset Paroxysmal Exercise-Induced Dyskinesia Without Epilepsy
Authors: Yun Su Hwang, Eungseok Oh
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

The solute carrier family 2 member 1 (SLC2A1) gene that encodes the glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) is responsible for glucose transport across the blood–brain barrier. This gene is associated with an epileptic encephalopathy referred to as GLUT1 deficiency syndrome, which typically presents with early-onset refractory epilepsy, developmental delay, and complex movement disorders.1 SLC2A1 variants induce various phenomenologies, including mild paroxysmal exercise-induced dyskinesia (PED) with or without epilepsy and delayed development.2 We report a patient with a nonclassical type of SLC2A1 mutation who developed PED in early adulthood with no other neurological symptoms and a suspected maternal history of abnormal movement.

A 35-year-old male visited with intermittent dystonia of the lower legs that had started around the age of 20 years. The attacks occurred once or twice daily after prolonged walking of over 2 hours or after an estimated 5,000 steps. The symptom manifestation involved his ankles becoming stiff and immovable, and the muscles on the soles of his feet feeling twisted, which resulted in difficulty in walking that lasted for about 30 minutes. His past medical history was unremarkable, with no seizure, developmental delay, or mental retardation.

A neurological examination revealed that each hand adopted a dystonic posture after repeated finger tapping, which disrupted the speed and amplitude of finger movements similarly to that seen in parkinsonism. Leg dystonia was not evident in the clinic (Supplementary Video 1 in the online-only Data Supplement), but dystonic posturing of right foot was observed during prolonged walking (Supplementary Video 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). Among his family, mother had history of intermittent paroxysmal gait disturbance due to involuntary leg movement which first occurred at the age of 24 years. Her frequency of attack was about once a month, and the attacks did not occur after the age of 60 years without specific treatment.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging and laboratory findings including complete blood counts, renal and liver function test, electrolyte, thyroid function test, serum copper and ceruloplasmin, folate, vitamin B12, and glucose were normal. The dystonia panel identified a heterozygous missense variant c.667C>T (p.Arg223Trp) in the SLC2A1 gene (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the online-only Data Supplement) that was previously reported to be a pathogenic variant.3 The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) glucose level was not measured. His mother had a history of intermittent paroxysmal gait disturbance due to involuntary leg movements that first occurred at the age of 24 years.

He declined a ketogenic diet, which has been recommended as the first-line treatment for GLUT1 disorder, and instead took 150 mg of levodopa twice daily. After 4 weeks the frequency of PED had reduced to two to three times weekly. However, since it was unclear whether this reduction was caused by the levodopa or by him quitting his job, he stopped taking levodopa and was monitored in an off-medication state. During the next 3 months his symptoms remained stable at fewer than three attacks per week with long-term rest and limited walking.

GLUT1 deficiency syndrome is a rare genetic condition causing impaired brain glucose metabolism that presents with diverse manifestations from confusion and uncontrolled seizure to mild paroxysmal movements.1 The condition is diagnosed based on suggestive clinical findings, the glucose level being lower in the CSF than in serum (CSF/blood glucose ratio: 0.19–0.59), and the identification of a heterozygous pathogenic variant in SLC2A1.2 Although a ketogenic diet is known to be effective for GLUT1 disorder,4 the present patient refused this intervention. When encountering patients with such a compliance problem, diets that are less restrictive such as the Modified Atkins diet and a low-glycemic index diet should be offered since they have shown noninferior results and are effective in the presence of SLC2A1 mutations.56

Our patient had experienced his first attack 15 years previously with no other neurological symptoms, but he had a maternal history of paroxysmal gait disturbance. The co-occurrence of PED and epilepsy has been occasionally reported, but adult-onset PED without epilepsy has been rarely reported; most previous cases of PED were childhood onset with or without epilepsy,17 or adult onset with the co-occurrence of seizure or intellectual disability.8 The present case might belong to the third of the three categories classified by Leen et al.,3 with an adult phenotype and only subtle symptoms, rather than the nonclassical phenotype. Unfortunately, CSF and familial genetic testing were not conducted in the present case. Since this disorder mainly exhibits autosomal-dominant inheritance, it is possible that the similar history of the patient’s mother was also related to the SLC2A1 gene. This might be consistent with mild PED cases that we have seen, in that the symptoms are milder and more variable in the familial type, while the symptoms are more severe in the de novo and sporadic forms.9

Advances in genetic studies are resulting in novel genetic mutations causing PED continuing to be discovered in addition to the known causative genes associated with paroxysmal movement disorders (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement), and heterogeneous phenotypes such as epilepsy and migraine are shared among those genes.10 The phenotypes of GLUT1 deficiency are heterogeneous, and so SLC2A1 testing should be considered in a broad range of dyskinetic patients, even though there is no history of epilepsy.
