Molecular Mechanisms and Biomarkers Driving the Transition From Chronic Atrophic Gastritis to Gastric Cancer
Authors: Yerlan Suleimenov, Ainur Samigatova, Bakhytzhan Seksenbayev, Nursulu Altaeva, Ayazhan Zhorayeva, Aruzhan Bakytzhan, Assylkhan Bekbayev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastric cancer continues to be a major contributor to global cancer morbidity and mortality. Although the Correa cascade is widely used as a model for gastric carcinogenesis, the molecular mechanisms driving the transition from chronic atrophic gastritis to invasive cancer have not been comprehensively integrated into a unified molecular framework. This review aims to synthesize current knowledge of the molecular events associated with each stage of the Correa cascade and to evaluate the clinical relevance of established and emerging biomarkers for risk stratification, early diagnosis, and personalized surveillance.This review summarizes the principal molecular mechanisms involved in the progression from chronic gastritis through chronic atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and ultimately gastric cancer. These mechanisms include inflammatory cytokine networks, epigenetic modifications, genomic instability, disruption of signaling pathways, clonal evolution, and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Particular emphasis is placed on contemporary tissue and circulating biomarkers, their diagnostic and prognostic significance, and the application of advanced technologies including multi-omics analysis, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell sequencing, and liquid biopsy.The available evidence supports a conceptual model of gastric carcinogenesis as a continuous molecular evolutionary process in which chronic inflammation initiates progressive epigenetic and genetic alterations that promote clonal selection and malignant transformation. Integrating molecular biomarkers with conventional morphological assessment may improve risk stratification, facilitate earlier detection of disease progression, and support the implementation of personalized strategies for gastric cancer prevention, surveillance, and treatment.

1. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health concern, consistently ranking among the leading causes of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN, 2022), over 968,000 new cases are reported annually, accounting for 4.8% of all malignant neoplasms, with more than 660,000 deaths each year. Gastric cancer is the fifth most common cancer after lung (12.4%), breast (11.5%), colorectal (9.6%), and prostate (7.3%) cancers. It ranks fourth in cancer-related mortality (6.8%), following lung (18.7%), colorectal (9.3%), and liver (7.8%) cancers. 1 GC remains a significant health concern in Kazakhstan. In 2023, there were over 2,809 new cases and more than 1,539 deaths related to this disease. Stomach cancer represented 7.8% of all cancer cases, showing a slight decrease from 8.3% in 2022. It ranked second in cancer-related mortality at 11.9% (12.0% in 2022), following lung cancer (15.8%) and preceding breast cancer (8.1%). 2 These indicators demonstrate the high mortality rate of the disease and its substantial impact on overall cancer mortality rates. Epidemiological data underscore the need for comprehensive research into the biological and molecular genetic features of gastric cancer in the Kazakhstani population, considering regional and ethnogenetic factors. Such research is crucial for advancing early diagnosis, prognosis, and personalized treatments.

Although diagnosis and treatment have improved, the pathogenesis of gastric cancer remains complex and involves genetic, epigenetic, infectious, and microenvironmental factors. The Correa cascade is the most widely accepted model, outlining the progression of the gastric mucosa from chronic inflammation to atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and ultimately invasive carcinoma. 3

Chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG) is a key precancerous condition characterized by loss of glandular tissue, mucosal remodeling, and metaplastic epithelium formation. Although CAG increases cancer risk, only a minority of patients develop malignancies, indicating that specific molecular factors influence progression. Evidence shows that the shift from chronic inflammation to neoplasia involves genomic damage, epigenetic changes, activation of oncogenic pathways, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Persistent inflammation, often due to Helicobacter pylori infection or autoimmune gastritis, triggers oxidative stress, DNA damage, cell cycle disruption, and impaired apoptosis.4,5

Recent research has focused on molecular biomarkers that reflect different stages of carcinogenesis and support the early detection of high-risk patients. These biomarkers include altered expression of tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes, DNA methylation changes, dysregulated microRNAs, and circulating markers such as tumor DNA and exosomal components of the blood. The integration of multiomics data offers new opportunities for risk stratification, personalized surveillance, and targeted prevention.6,7

Although substantial data have been collected, several issues remain to be resolved. These include accurately identifying patients at high risk of progression, standardizing molecular markers, and effectively integrating them into clinical practice. Another challenge is the heterogeneity of the population studies. Most data come from diverse ethnic and geographic groups, and regional or ethnogenetic factors can significantly influence the molecular profile and clinical course of the disease.

This review aims to develop a conceptual model of gastric carcinogenesis that incorporates inflammation, epigenetic changes, genomic instability, and clonal evolution in gastric cancer. It also evaluates the clinical relevance of molecular biomarkers for risk stratification, early diagnosis, and personalized follow-up. Although individual aspects of gastric carcinogenesis are better understood, a comprehensive and clinically relevant model explaining the progression from chronic atrophic gastritis to gastric cancer is still lacking. Key gaps include limited knowledge of how inflammatory, epigenetic, and signaling pathways interact, their combined impact on cancer risk, and how related biomarkers can be applied in clinical settings.

We propose that the progression from chronic atrophic gastritis to gastric cancer is driven by a pathogenetic axis that connects inflammation-induced epigenetic changes to genomic instability and clonal evolution. In this model, the tumor microenvironment exerts selective pressure, favoring the growth of cell clones with survival and proliferation advantages, thereby driving malignant transformation and disease progression. 2. Chronic Gastritis

Chronic gastritis represents the initial stage of the Correa cascade, in which persistent Helicobacter pylori infection triggers a sustained inflammatory response that initiates the multistep process of gastric carcinogenesis. The primary bacterial virulence factors, CagA and VacA, play key roles in compromising epithelial barrier integrity, modulating host cell signaling pathways, and stimulating both innate and adaptive immune responses.

Chronic Helicobacter pylori (H.Pylori) infection is the main external cause of chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG) and subsequent gastric cancer. The pathogenic effects of H.Pylori result from interactions between bacterial virulence factors, epithelial cells, and the host immune system. 8 The oncoprotein cytotoxin-associated gene A (CAG A) plays a key role in entering epithelial cells through the type IV secretion system, undergoing tyrosine phosphorylation, and interacting with intracellular signaling molecules such as SHP2, Grb2, and Crk. 9 This activates oncogenic cascades, including the MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, and β-catenin-dependent pathways, which increase proliferation, impair cell adhesion, and disrupt epithelial polarity. 10

VacA toxin causes mitochondrial dysfunction, vacuole formation, and apoptosis. It also alters the immune response, which facilitates bacterial persistence and immune evasion. 11 Chronic activation of NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways results in sustained production of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), creating a self-perpetuating inflammatory microenvironment through a positive feedback loop. 12 Collectively, cytokine networks provide a mechanistic link between chronic inflammation and malignant transformation by sustaining epithelial injury, promoting genomic instability, and facilitating the accumulation of oncogenic molecular alterations.

The persistent inflammatory microenvironment is sustained by a complex cytokine network that links chronic inflammation to gastric cancer development. IL-6 is a key activator of the JAK/STAT3 pathway, driving epithelial cell proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. IL-1β and TNF-α amplify inflammation by activating NF-κB, increasing proinflammatory mediators, and contributing to epithelial injury and mucosal atrophy. IL-17 from Th17 cells recruits neutrophils and maintains tissue damage, reinforcing chronic inflammation.13,14 TGF-β has context-dependent roles, acting as a tumor suppressor early in disease but later promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition, tissue remodeling, fibrosis, and immune suppression. Through interactions with NF-κB and STAT3, these cytokines create a self-sustaining inflammatory environment that leads to epithelial instability, genomic damage, and accumulation of molecular changes, bridging chronic gastritis and malignant transformation.15,16

The inflammatory environment promotes mucosal infiltration by neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes. These immune cells generate high levels of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and RNS), which induce oxidative DNA damage, including base modifications, single- and double-strand breaks, and replication errors. The accumulation of these lesions promotes genomic instability and facilitates the acquisition of oncogenic mutations. 17 Persistent cytokine stimulation impairs epithelial regeneration and facilitates the accumulation of molecular damage. In parallel, persistent oxidative stress disrupts DNA repair mechanisms, promotes microsatellite instability through impairment of the DNA mismatch repair system, and accelerates telomere shortening, thereby further increasing chromosomal instability and facilitating the clonal expansion of genetically altered epithelial cells. 18 Together, these processes initiate early epigenetic alterations that progressively accumulate during the transition from chronic gastritis to chronic atrophic gastritis, where stable DNA methylation patterns become a defining molecular feature.

Beyond inflammatory signaling, persistent H. pylori infection is also associated with activation of cyclin D1 and disruption of cell cycle regulation, inactivation of suppressor genes (including through promoter methylation), induction of microsatellite instability, and disruption of intercellular contacts through the degradation of E-cadherin (CDH1).19,20 Another key mechanism is the disruption of regulation of gastric epithelial stem cells, resulting in increased cell turnover. Sustained stem-cell dysregulation during chronic inflammation promotes the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic damage. H. pylori therefore acts both as an initiator of inflammation and as a direct modifier of the signaling and epigenetic processes that drive atrophy and neoplastic transformation. 21 Sustained inflammatory signaling also maintains the activation of NF-κB- and STAT3-dependent transcriptional programs, leading to increased expression of genes involved in cell-cycle progression, resistance to apoptosis (BCL-2 family), angiogenesis (VEGF), and immune evasion. Collectively, these molecular alterations enable genetically damaged epithelial cells to survive, proliferate, and accumulate additional oncogenic events.22-24

In cases of Helicobacter pylori-associated chronic gastritis, confirmation of active infection remains essential for diagnosis and is typically achieved through the 13C urea breath test, detection of H. pylori antigen in stool, and histological examination. 25 Although serological methods may be employed in specific clinical scenarios, they do not reliably confirm active infection. The measurement of serum pepsinogens, which reflect both the functional and morphological status of the gastric mucosa, offers valuable insights into gastric secretory function. While alterations in pepsinogen levels may be detected in the early stages of disease, these biomarkers attain their highest diagnostic value as atrophic changes progress. 26

Although the molecular mechanisms underlying chronic gastritis are actively being studied, no molecular biomarker has yet demonstrated adequate diagnostic or prognostic efficacy for independent clinical application. Consequently, risk assessment should rely on a comprehensive evaluation of H. pylori detection, histological assessment of the gastric mucosa, and functional indicators. This integrated approach enables more precise stratification of patients by risk of progression to chronic atrophic gastritis and supports the selection of optimal strategies for ongoing monitoring.

Early diagnosis of chronic gastritis associated with H.Pylori represents an important window of opportunity to interrupt the Correa cascade before irreversible precancerous changes develop. According to current international guidelines, timely detection and eradication of H.Pylori eliminate active inflammation, prevent further progression of gastric mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, and, in some patients, achieve regression of atrophic changes. The greatest reduction in the risk of developing gastric cancer is achieved with eradication therapy before severe chronic atrophic gastritis develops. 27

Persistent chronic inflammation resulting from prolonged H.Pylori infection or delayed treatment initiation causes ongoing epithelial damage and impaired regenerative processes, ultimately leading to progressive loss of the gastric glands. This process marks the transition to chronic atrophic gastritis, which represents the first irreversible precancerous stage in the Correa cascade. This stage is characterized by marked structural, functional, and molecular alterations that substantially elevate the risk of subsequent neoplastic transformation.

Beyond the well-established role of Helicobacter pylori, growing evidence suggests that additional infectious agents may also contribute to the progression of premalignant gastric lesions. In particular, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) has attracted increasing attention because of its potential involvement in the early stages of gastric carcinogenesis. While EBV is recognized as a distinct molecular subtype of gastric cancer, recent evidence indicates that latent EBV infection may also contribute to earlier stages of gastric carcinogenesis by promoting chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and epigenetic alterations, including aberrant DNA hypermethylation of host genes. These processes may create a microenvironment that increases epithelial instability and susceptibility to neoplastic transformation. Unlike H. pylori, the precise role of EBV in the development of chronic atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, or dysplasia remains incompletely understood. Current studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the presence of EBV in premalignant gastric lesions, and a causal relationship has not yet been established. Therefore, EBV is currently regarded as a potential modifier of gastric carcinogenesis rather than an initiating driver of premalignant disease. Although chronic atrophic gastritis is primarily driven by persistent H. pylori infection or autoimmune mechanisms, EBV has also been proposed as a potential cofactor capable of modulating the molecular microenvironment during this premalignant stage.28-30

3. Chronic Atrophic Gastritis

Chronic atrophic gastritis is characterized by progressive loss of native gastric glands, impaired gastric secretory function, and remodeling of the gastric mucosa, reflecting the transition from predominantly inflammatory changes to irreversible structural damage. As structural atrophy progresses, epigenetic dysregulation becomes a major driver of disease progression, while persistent oxidative stress and accumulated DNA damage further impair cellular repair mechanisms.

Although chronic Helicobacter pylori infection remains the predominant cause of chronic atrophic gastritis worldwide, autoimmune gastritis represents an alternative etiological pathway characterized by immune-mediated destruction of gastric parietal cells. 31 Autoimmune gastritis develops when autoreactive CD4+ T lymphocytes target the gastric H+/K+-ATPase on parietal cells. This T-cell response also activates B lymphocytes, leading to the production of anti-parietal cell and intrinsic factor antibodies. These antibodies serve as valuable diagnostic biomarkers but are not considered the primary mediators of tissue injury. Impaired regulatory T-cell (Treg) function further contributes to the loss of immune tolerance, promoting ongoing inflammation and progressive destruction of the gastric mucosa.32,33

Despite distinct initiating mechanisms, both forms converge on common downstream molecular events, including progressive glandular atrophy, epigenetic dysregulation, genomic instability, and an increased risk of gastric neoplasia. However, despite these shared downstream events, important molecular differences remain. H. pylori-associated gastritis is triggered by bacterial virulence factors such as CagA and VacA. In contrast, autoimmune gastritis arises independently of microbial infection and is primarily driven by dysregulated adaptive immune responses. Autoimmune gastritis is further characterized by marked hypochlorhydria, compensatory hypergastrinemia, enterochromaffin-like cell (ECL) hyperplasia, and the presence of anti-parietal cell and intrinsic factor antibodies, which help differentiate it from H. pylori-associated atrophic gastritis.34,35

Clinically, autoimmune gastritis is associated with an increased risk of both intestinal-type gastric adenocarcinoma and type I gastric neuroendocrine tumors, although the underlying mechanisms differ. Gastric adenocarcinoma development is primarily linked to chronic inflammation, progressive atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and the accumulation of molecular alterations. In contrast, chronic hypergastrinemia stimulates ECL-cell hyperplasia and represents the principal mechanism underlying the development of type I gastric neuroendocrine tumors.33,36 At the molecular level, both etiologies share progressive DNA methylation, genomic instability, and impaired epithelial regeneration, ultimately contributing to the development of intestinal metaplasia and subsequent gastric carcinogenesis.37-39

Progressive epigenetic alterations, particularly promoter hypermethylation of the tumor suppressor genes CDH1 and MLH1, are key molecular events at this stage. Hypermethylation-mediated silencing of CDH1 disrupts E-cadherin expression, impairing cell-cell adhesion and epithelial integrity. Loss of CDH1 function further compromises epithelial architecture by disrupting adherens junctions that maintain epithelial polarity. As a result, intercellular adhesion is reduced, cellular motility is enhanced, and epithelial integrity deteriorates, thereby facilitating progressive mucosal remodeling and disease progression. 40

In contrast, MLH1 hypermethylation compromises DNA mismatch repair, increases genomic instability, and facilitates the accumulation of somatic mutations. Loss of MLH1 function results in defective mismatch repair, enabling the accumulation of replication errors and further increasing genomic instability during disease progression. 41 Loss of E-cadherin function also creates molecular conditions that favor epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), although this process becomes more prominent during the later stages of gastric carcinogenesis, particularly in diffuse-type gastric cancer. 42 Collectively, these molecular alterations impair epithelial regeneration, promote irreversible mucosal remodeling, and establish the molecular basis for the transition to intestinal metaplasia, the subsequent stage of the Correa cascade.43-45

Beyond these intracellular molecular events, progressive loss of acid-producing parietal cells also profoundly alters the gastric microenvironment. The resulting hypochlorhydria or achlorhydria leads to increased intragastric pH, facilitating gastric microbiota dysbiosis and the enrichment of non-Helicobacter pylori bacterial species that are normally suppressed by gastric acidity. This shift can worsen chronic inflammation, increase oxidative stress, and disrupt epithelial homeostasis, creating conditions that favor neoplastic transformation. Evidence implicates bacteria such as Fusobacterium nucleatum in maintaining chronic inflammation, modulating the immune microenvironment, and increasing epithelial cell proliferation, though the exact mechanisms in carcinogenesis remain unclear. Gastric microbiota dysbiosis is also linked to altered microbial metabolism and disrupted host-microorganism interactions, which may contribute to disease progression. Thus, microbiota dysbiosis represents an additional mechanism connecting chronic atrophic gastritis to intestinal metaplasia and may play a role in the development of gastric cancer.46,47

3.1 Gastric Microbiome Beyond Helicobacter pylori

Although Helicobacter pylori remains the primary initiating factor in gastric carcinogenesis, recent research indicates that, following the development of chronic atrophic gastritis, disease progression is influenced not only by the persistence of H. pylori but also by significant alterations in the gastric microbiome. As gastric atrophy and hypochlorhydria advance, the bactericidal capacity of gastric juice diminishes, resulting in disruption of the gastric microbiome. Consequently, the stomach becomes vulnerable to colonization by microorganisms that typically cannot survive in highly acidic conditions. Next-generation sequencing studies have revealed increased microbial diversity and the accumulation of bacteria from the genera Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Lactobacillus, Peptostreptococcus, and others, most of which originate from the oral microbiota. These microbiome changes are now regarded not only as a consequence of mucosal atrophy but also as a contributing factor to the progression of precancerous gastric lesions. 48

Non-Helicobacter pylori microbiota are implicated in carcinogenesis through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Persistent colonization by these microorganisms sustains chronic inflammation, elevates oxidative stress, disrupts epithelial homeostasis, and fosters conditions conducive to neoplastic transformation. Many of these bacteria stimulate the production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-17, thereby perpetuating chronic immune activation. This inflammatory response is associated with increased generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, resulting in DNA damage, genomic instability, and the accumulation of somatic mutations. Additionally, altered microbial metabolism leads to the production of potentially carcinogenic metabolites, including N-nitroso compounds, secondary bile acids, and other reactive molecules that exacerbate epithelial injury and hinder regeneration. Collectively, these processes contribute to the development of a pro-inflammatory and pro-neoplastic microenvironment. 49

The translocation of oral microorganisms to the stomach is a prominent area of research. Under normal physiological conditions, the acidic gastric environment inhibits their colonization. However, in hypochlorhydric states, oral bacteria can persist in the stomach for extended periods. Species such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, Parvimonas micra, Peptostreptococcus stomatis, Prevotella, and Veillonella have been consistently identified in tissues affected by chronic atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and gastric cancer. While a definitive causal relationship has not been established, current evidence suggests these bacteria can enhance inflammation, modulate immune responses, stimulate epithelial cell proliferation, facilitate immune evasion, and remodel the extracellular matrix. The role of Fusobacterium nucleatum is particularly well characterized, with its involvement previously demonstrated in colorectal cancer and now under active investigation in gastric cancer. 47

Successful eradication of Helicobacter pylori does not consistently result in complete restoration of the normal gastric microbial composition. Although eradication therapy significantly reduces the risk of cancer, particularly when administered before severe atrophy develops, patients with pre-existing chronic atrophic gastritis or intestinal metaplasia frequently exhibit persistent dysbiosis. Longitudinal studies indicate that microbial diversity and composition often fail to return to levels observed in healthy gastric mucosa following eradication, and colonization by non-Helicobacter pylori bacteria persists. This phenomenon may partially account for the residual risk of gastric cancer development after successful H. pylori eradication, especially among patients with irreversible structural changes in the gastric mucosa. 50

The current concept identifies microbial dysbiosis as an additional mechanism contributing to the progression of gastric carcinogenesis. Alterations in the microbiota interact with epithelial cells, the immune system, and the stromal microenvironment, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation, promoting oxidative DNA damage, disrupting epithelial barrier function, inducing immune dysregulation, and facilitating the formation of carcinogenic metabolites. However, in most of the identified studies, these associations remain correlational; definitive evidence of causality requires further experimental investigation. 51

Investigation of the gastric microbiome presents new opportunities for enhancing gastric cancer prevention. Microbiota profiling may complement established histological and molecular biomarkers in stratifying risk among patients with chronic atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia. In addition, active research is focused on microbiome biomarkers, microbial metabolites, and interventions to correct dysbiosis through probiotics, prebiotics, and other targeted microbiota modification strategies. Nevertheless, these approaches remain experimental and require standardized research methodologies, validation of reproducibility, and large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies before they can be incorporated into routine clinical practice and international guidelines for gastric cancer prevention and surveillance. 52

Clinically validated serological and histopathological biomarkers reflect the structural and functional consequences of chronic atrophic gastritis. Serum pepsinogen I and II concentrations, especially a reduced pepsinogen I/II ratio, are the most established non-invasive indicators of gastric corpus atrophy. These biomarkers reflect both the functional and structural integrity of the gastric mucosa, and a decreased pepsinogen I/II ratio is closely linked to progressive glandular loss and impaired gastric secretion. 53

Serum gastrin-17 enhances pepsinogen assessment by offering additional insight into antral gland function and gastric acid secretion, especially in patients with hypochlorhydria. In autoimmune chronic atrophic gastritis, circulating anti-parietal cell and intrinsic factor antibodies serve as important serological markers supporting the diagnosis and differentiating this condition from H. pylori-associated atrophic gastritis. 54 While these biomarkers provide clinical value in detecting gastric atrophy and identifying patients at elevated risk of disease progression, none exhibits adequate sensitivity or specificity to independently predict malignant transformation. Consequently, interpretation of biomarkers should be combined with histopathological assessment and clinical evaluation.

Histopathological staging using the Operative Link on Gastritis Assessment (OLGA) system is central to risk stratification in chronic atrophic gastritis, as it incorporates both the severity and anatomical distribution of glandular atrophy. Individuals with advanced OLGA stages (III-IV) are at a markedly higher risk of progressing to intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and gastric cancer. The Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia Assessment (OLGIM) complements the OLGA system by assessing the extent of intestinal metaplasia and enhancing the precision of cancer risk evaluation. 55 Consistent with this risk-adapted approach, the current ESGE MAPS II guidelines recommend individualized endoscopic surveillance based on OLGA/OLGIM stage, the extent of atrophic and metaplastic changes, and additional clinical risk factors. This integrated strategy supports early detection of disease progression and reduces unnecessary surveillance for low-risk individuals. 56

Accurate identification of patients with advanced chronic atrophic gastritis is essential, as management and surveillance strategies are primarily guided by the estimated risk of progression to gastric cancer. Integrating histopathological staging, validated serological biomarkers, and individual clinical risk factors enables personalized risk stratification and supports tailored surveillance programs. This approach facilitates the early detection of precancerous progression while minimizing unnecessary endoscopic procedures in low-risk individuals. Patients with extensive gastric atrophy, advanced OLGA or OLGIM stage, persistent Helicobacter pylori infection, autoimmune gastritis, or a family history of gastric cancer require closer endoscopic follow-up. Accordingly, risk-adapted surveillance has become a cornerstone of current international recommendations for gastric cancer prevention, facilitating earlier detection of progression to intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and ultimately gastric cancer.

4. Intestinal Metaplasia

Intestinal metaplasia is widely recognized as the pivotal premalignant stage of the Correa cascade, characterized by replacement of the normal gastric epithelium with intestinal-type cells. This change signifies the transition from predominantly reversible inflammatory injury to persistent mucosal remodeling and represents one of the principal precancerous lesions of the stomach. At the molecular level, intestinal metaplasia is characterized by disrupted epithelial differentiation and aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which promotes cell proliferation, maintains stem cell-like properties, and impairs normal epithelial renewal. Activation of this pathway results in the stabilization and nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, leading to transcription of genes involved in proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Notably, aberrant nuclear localization of β-catenin is detectable as early as the stages of intestinal metaplasia and dysplasia, underscoring the pivotal role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the early phases of gastric carcinogenesis. 57

At the same time, epigenetic alterations become increasingly prominent, including the development of the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) and promoter hypermethylation of genes involved in cell cycle regulation, epithelial differentiation, and DNA repair. 58 Dysregulated microRNA expression and changes in the gastric stem cell compartment reinforce the intestinal phenotype and facilitate disease progression. Together, these molecular events contribute to the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic abnormalities that define the transition from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia. 59

Beyond these molecular alterations, recent evidence indicates that gastric stem-cell populations serve as the cellular origin of expanding clones driving progression from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia and ultimately gastric cancer. Under normal conditions, long-lived stem cells in the gastric glands maintain epithelial homeostasis through self-renewal and differentiation. Lgr5-positive (Lgr5+) stem cells are of particular interest due to their longevity and capacity to accumulate oncogenic alterations, making them likely candidates for the cells of origin of gastric neoplasia. Lineage-tracing studies demonstrate that Lgr5+ stem cells can survive chronic inflammation, expand clonally, and develop into metaplastic, dysplastic, and neoplastic lesions after acquiring oncogenic genetic and epigenetic changes.60,61 Chronic Helicobacter pylori infection and persistent inflammation accelerate this process by promoting oxidative DNA damage, increasing epithelial turnover, and creating selective pressures that favor the expansion of genetically altered stem-cell clones. Concurrently, inflammatory remodeling of the stem-cell niche and interactions between stem cells and metaplastic cell populations further drive clonal expansion and disease progression. Collectively, these findings indicate that gastric carcinogenesis results not only from the accumulation of molecular alterations but also from the selective expansion of mutated stem-cell-derived clones that drive progression from intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia and invasive gastric cancer. 62

The progressive molecular and cellular alterations accompanying intestinal metaplasia are reflected by several tissue and molecular biomarkers that have been proposed to improve diagnosis and risk stratification at this stage of gastric carcinogenesis. Among tissue biomarkers, loss of the gastric transcription factor SOX2 and ectopic expression of CDX2 represent complementary molecular hallmarks of intestinal metaplasia. Physiologically, SOX2 maintains gastric epithelial differentiation, whereas CDX2 functions as the master regulator of intestinal lineage specification. Progressive downregulation of SOX2, together with aberrant activation of CDX2, promotes the transcription of intestine-specific genes and drives the phenotypic conversion of gastric epithelial cells toward an intestinal phenotype. MUC2, an intestinal-type mucin expressed downstream of CDX2 activation, serves as a characteristic marker of goblet cell differentiation. The combined assessment of SOX2, CDX2, and MUC2 collectively reflects the loss of the gastric phenotype and acquisition of the intestinal phenotype, providing valuable biomarkers for the diagnosis and biological characterization of intestinal metaplasia.63-65

In addition to tissue biomarkers, DNA methylation markers have emerged as valuable tools for risk assessment and early detection. Promoter methylation changes involving CDH1, RUNX3, and MLH1 are among the most extensively studied and correlate with increasing severity of precancerous gastric lesions and an elevated risk of progression to gastric cancer. Circulating methylated DNA fragments, exosomal microRNAs, and methylation-based biomarker panels are currently being investigated as non-invasive approaches for identifying individuals at high risk. Although these biomarkers have demonstrated encouraging diagnostic performance, further prospective validation is required before their routine implementation in clinical practice.66-69

The identification of intestinal metaplasia carries significant clinical implications, as it indicates a markedly increased risk of gastric cancer. International guidelines recommend histological risk stratification through the Operative Link on Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia Assessment (OLGIM) system, which assesses the extent and distribution of intestinal metaplasia. Individuals with advanced OLGIM stages (III–IV), extensive or incomplete intestinal metaplasia, persistent Helicobacter pylori infection, or a family history of gastric cancer are classified as high risk and should receive periodic endoscopic surveillance. Consequently, accurate risk stratification using the OLGIM system and appropriate endoscopic surveillance are essential for the timely detection of dysplasia and prevention of gastric cancer. 70 Despite appropriate surveillance, a subset of patients continues to accumulate additional genetic and epigenetic alterations, ultimately leading to the development of dysplasia, the final precancerous stage preceding invasive gastric cancer.

5. Dysplasia

Gastric dysplasia is the final precancerous stage in the Correa cascade, characterized by genetic and chromosomal abnormalities that precede invasive carcinoma. Inactivation of TP53 is a key molecular event that impairs DNA damage response, cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and genomic integrity. This disruption allows genetically unstable epithelial cells to survive and expand. TP53 alterations are among the earliest driver events that distinguish dysplasia from non-neoplastic precursor lesions and become more frequent as the disease progresses toward invasive gastric adenocarcinoma. 71

Loss-of-function alterations in ARID1A, a key component of the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling complex, contribute to neoplastic progression by disrupting chromatin organization, transcriptional regulation, and genomic stability. Furthermore, ARID1A deficiency interacts with oncogenic signaling pathways and induces epigenetic dysregulation, thereby facilitating malignant transformation.72,73 Chromosomal instability (CIN) represents the dominant genomic hallmark of gastric dysplasia and is characterized by progressive accumulation of aneuploidy, copy-number alterations, chromosomal gains and losses, and clonal evolution. Increasing chromosomal instability accelerates the accumulation of driver genetic alterations, promotes intratumoral genomic heterogeneity, and provides the molecular basis for malignant transformation. 74 Collectively, TP53 inactivation, ARID1A deficiency, and progressive chromosomal instability establish the molecular framework for the transition from gastric dysplasia to invasive adenocarcinoma.

These molecular alterations are accompanied by changes in tissue and circulating biomarkers that facilitate the diagnosis of dysplasia and improve assessment of the risk of progression to invasive carcinoma. TP53 and Ki-67 are the most extensively studied tissue biomarkers in gastric dysplasia. Aberrant TP53 expression detected by immunohistochemistry reflects underlying TP53 alterations and correlates with increased dysplastic severity and a heightened risk of progression to invasive gastric adenocarcinoma. In clinical practice, immunohistochemical assessment of TP53 complements conventional histopathological evaluation, particularly in lesions with equivocal morphological features. 75

Elevated Ki-67 expression reflects increased cellular proliferation and expansion of the proliferative compartment beyond the normal glandular neck region. Loss of normal surface maturation together with increasing Ki-67 labeling indices correlates with progressive architectural and cytological atypia, reflecting the biological progression of dysplastic lesions. When interpreted alongside routine histopathological findings, Ki-67 provides valuable complementary information for the assessment of gastric dysplasia. 76

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker for detecting tumor-associated genetic and epigenetic alterations during gastric carcinogenesis. Preliminary studies suggest that ctDNA may improve risk stratification and facilitate longitudinal monitoring of disease progression. However, its application in patients with gastric dysplasia remains investigational. Further prospective studies are required before ctDNA can be integrated into routine clinical practice. 77 Consequently, no single biomarker currently demonstrates sufficient diagnostic or prognostic accuracy to independently predict malignant transformation. Biomarker assessment should therefore be interpreted in conjunction with conventional histopathological evaluation and the overall clinical context.

Prompt clinical intervention is warranted following the diagnosis of gastric dysplasia, as it represents the final precancerous stage of the Correa cascade and carries a substantial risk of progression to invasive gastric adenocarcinoma, particularly in cases of high-grade dysplasia. The primary goal of treatment is complete removal of the dysplastic lesion before invasive transformation occurs.

Current international guidelines recommend endoscopic resection as the treatment of choice for visible gastric dysplasia, primarily using endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), depending on lesion size, morphology, and the likelihood of superficial invasion. These techniques provide definitive treatment while allowing comprehensive histopathological evaluation of the resected specimen, including assessment of invasion depth, resection margins, and other adverse pathological features. Surgical resection should be considered when endoscopic treatment is not technically feasible, when invasive carcinoma is suspected, or when unfavorable histopathological findings are identified following endoscopic resection. After treatment, patients require individualized endoscopic surveillance because they remain at increased risk of synchronous and metachronous neoplastic lesions. 70 Timely recognition and appropriate management of gastric dysplasia therefore represent the final opportunity to interrupt the Correa cascade before the development of invasive gastric adenocarcinoma.

6. Gastric Cancer

The progression from gastric dysplasia to invasive gastric adenocarcinoma involves the accumulation of complex genetic, epigenetic, and chromosomal alterations, resulting in pronounced molecular heterogeneity. In contrast to earlier stages of the Correa cascade, gastric cancer is defined by both progressive genomic instability and the emergence of distinct molecular subtypes, each exhibiting unique biological behavior, prognosis, and therapeutic vulnerabilities.

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) provides the most widely accepted molecular classification of gastric cancer, categorizing it into four major subtypes: Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-positive, microsatellite instability (MSI), chromosomal instability (CIN), and genomically stable (GS) tumors. 78

The EBV subtype represents approximately 9% of gastric cancers and is characterized by extensive CpG island hypermethylation, frequent PIK3CA mutations, amplification of PD-L1 and PD-L2, and dense immune-cell infiltration. These molecular characteristics contribute to a highly immunogenic tumor microenvironment, accounting for the increased sensitivity of this subtype to immune checkpoint blockade.79-82

The MSI subtype arises primarily due to defects in the DNA mismatch repair system, most often resulting from epigenetic silencing of MLH1. These tumors display a hypermutated phenotype, characterized by numerous insertion-deletion mutations and an exceptionally high tumor mutational burden, which leads to increased neoantigen formation and enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy. MSI gastric cancers frequently develop in the context of chronic atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, constituting a principal molecular pathway within the Correa cascade. 83

The chromosomal instability (CIN) subtype is the most prevalent form of gastric cancer, characterized by widespread aneuploidy, copy-number alterations, and frequent TP53 mutations. Amplification of receptor tyrosine kinases, such as HER2, EGFR, MET, and FGFR2, leads to persistent activation of proliferative signaling pathways, including MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR. CIN tumors are predominantly associated with the intestinal histological subtype and typically develop through the classical Correa cascade following prolonged chronic inflammation.84-87

In contrast, genomically stable (GS) tumors are typically associated with diffuse-type gastric cancer and exhibit relatively few chromosomal abnormalities. These tumors are instead defined by alterations affecting cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization, such as mutations in CDH1 and RHOA or CLDN18-ARHGAP gene fusions, which promote loss of epithelial integrity and diffuse tumor infiltration. 88

Beyond the TCGA classification, dysregulation of several oncogenic signaling pathways is central to gastric cancer progression. Constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway promotes cellular proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, stimulates angiogenesis, and facilitates metabolic adaptation. Aberrant EGFR signaling further drives tumor growth through downstream activation of the MAPK and PI3K pathways. Concurrently, activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) leads to reduced E-cadherin expression, increased cellular motility, invasion, metastatic dissemination, and resistance to systemic therapy. Collectively, these molecular alterations contribute to extensive intratumoral heterogeneity, which underpins differences in clinical behavior and therapeutic response among patients with gastric cancer.82,89-91

In addition to aberrant signaling pathways, metabolic reprogramming has emerged as another hallmark of gastric cancer progression and is closely associated with remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Tumor cells rely on increased aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) to rapidly generate the energy and metabolites needed for proliferation. Lactate accumulation in the microenvironment fosters immunosuppression by inhibiting cytotoxic T lymphocytes and promoting macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype. Activation of factors such as HIF-1α further supports cellular adaptation to hypoxia and drives tumor progression. 92

The increasing molecular complexity of gastric cancer is reflected by an expanding repertoire of tissue and circulating biomarkers that support diagnosis, prognostic assessment, therapeutic decision-making, and disease monitoring. Assessment of HER2 status among tissue biomarkers is essential, as HER2 overexpression or gene amplification identifies patients eligible for anti-HER2 therapy. PD-L1 expression, typically quantified using the combined positive score (CPS), serves as a predictive biomarker for immune checkpoint inhibitors. Determination of microsatellite instability (MSI) or mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) status identifies tumors with high immunogenicity and favorable responses to immunotherapy. More recently, CLDN18.2 has emerged as a clinically relevant therapeutic target, and FGFR2b expression has gained importance with the development of novel targeted agents.93-97

Circulating biomarkers are emerging as valuable complementary tools for the non-invasive management of gastric cancer. Among them, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) enables the detection of tumor-specific genetic and epigenetic alterations in peripheral blood, providing dynamic insight into tumor evolution and molecular heterogeneity. Liquid biopsy offers several advantages, including minimal invasiveness, repeatability, and the ability to monitor treatment response, detect minimal residual disease, identify acquired resistance mechanisms, and facilitate earlier detection of disease recurrence.98-100

Despite these promising developments, most circulating biomarkers remain under clinical validation and require confirmation in large prospective studies before routine implementation. Tumor mutational burden (TMB), reflecting the total number of somatic mutations within the tumor genome, has also emerged as an important biomarker associated with increased tumor immunogenicity and improved response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in selected patients. 101 Together, tissue and circulating biomarkers provide complementary information that supports molecular classification, prognostic assessment, therapeutic decision-making, and longitudinal monitoring of gastric cancer.

Advancements in the understanding of gastric cancer biology have shifted clinical management from a morphology-based approach to precision oncology, where treatment decisions are guided by the molecular profile of the tumor rather than histological appearance alone. International guidelines currently recommend comprehensive molecular profiling of advanced gastric cancer to identify clinically actionable alterations. Routine assessment of clinically actionable biomarkers has therefore become an integral component of the diagnostic work-up for advanced gastric cancer. Patients with HER2-positive tumors are eligible for HER2-targeted therapy, while those with CLDN18.2-positive tumors may receive recently approved targeted agents. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown significant clinical benefit in patients with MSI-H/dMMR or PD-L1-positive gastric cancers, and EBV-positive tumors are also recognized as highly immunogenic, supporting the use of immunotherapy in selected cases.102,103

In addition to informing treatment selection, molecular biomarkers enable prognostic stratification, prediction of therapeutic response, identification of resistance mechanisms, and optimization of treatment sequencing. Incorporating liquid biopsy into clinical practice can further support real-time monitoring of disease progression, early detection of recurrence, and adaptive therapeutic decision-making. The progressive accumulation of molecular alterations throughout the Correa cascade results in a biologically heterogeneous disease that necessitates individualized therapeutic strategies. Comprehensive molecular characterization therefore underpins precision oncology by translating the molecular evolution of gastric carcinogenesis into biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies and personalized patient care.104,105

7. Future Directions

Future research on gastric carcinogenesis is shifting toward integrative approaches that address the complex molecular and cellular interactions driving disease progression. The use of multi-omics technologies, such as genomics, epigenomics, and transcriptomics, enables the comprehensive and precise characterization of tumors and precancerous conditions. These methods support the identification of molecular signatures that improve risk prediction for malignant transformation and aid in the development of biomarker panels with high sensitivity and specificity. 106

Research on microbiome-host interactions beyond Helicobacter pylori is a promising field. Alterations in the gastric and intestinal microbiota can drive chronic inflammation, immune modulation, and metabolic changes, all of which may influence cancer development. Understanding these interactions could help identify new biomarkers and therapeutic targets, especially in patients who remain at risk after infection eradication. 107

Preventive immunomodulation strategies offer a promising approach to correct the pro-tumor microenvironment early in the disease. By targeting inflammatory signaling pathways, immune checkpoints, and cytokine networks, these approaches may disrupt the progression from chronic inflammation to neoplastic transformation. This creates opportunities for genuine cancer prevention rather than focusing solely on treatment in the later stages. 108

Although there has been significant progress, the application of molecular advances in clinical practice remains limited because of insufficient validation. Prospective longitudinal cohort studies are necessary to determine the prognostic value of potential biomarkers, establish clinically relevant cut-off values, and clarify their roles in monitoring and treatment decisions. Standardizing methodologies and unifying study designs are essential for reproducibility and integrating findings into clinical guidelines. 109

7.1 Emerging Single-Cell and Spatial Technologies

Recent advances in single-cell and spatial technologies have fundamentally reshaped current concepts of gastric carcinogenesis by enabling detailed characterization of cellular and molecular heterogeneity throughout the Correa cascade. In contrast to traditional transcriptome analysis methods, which measure average gene expression across entire tissues, single-cell RNA sequencing enables transcriptomic profiling at the level of individual cells, thereby uncovering previously unrecognized populations of epithelial, stromal, immune, and stem cells. This enhanced resolution has revealed that gastric cancer development is influenced not only by the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations but also by dynamic changes in tissue cellular composition associated with chronic inflammation and neoplastic transformation. 110

Beyond revealing cellular heterogeneity, single-cell RNA sequencing has provided unprecedented insight into the cellular mechanisms underlying progression from chronic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia to dysplasia and invasive carcinoma. Trajectory inference analyses have revealed rare transitional epithelial cell populations with mixed transcriptomic profiles, which represent intermediate states between normal epithelium and tumor cells. Notably, research on gastric stem cell biology has revealed functionally distinct populations with differing capacities for self-renewal, differentiation, and accumulation of oncogenic alterations. Collectively, these findings indicate that neoplastic transformation is a gradual process characterized by sequential reorganization of cellular differentiation programs, rather than a single discrete event.111,112

Despite its exceptional molecular resolution, single-cell RNA sequencing inevitably loses spatial information because tissue dissociation disrupts the native architecture of the gastric mucosa. Spatial transcriptomics addresses this limitation by preserving tissue architecture while enabling analysis of the spatial distribution of gene expression. Integrating spatial transcriptomics with single-cell sequencing data facilitates the reconstruction of the molecular organization of gastric mucosal lesions, while preserving the precise localization of epithelial, immune, stromal, and vascular cells. This combined approach has substantially advanced the characterization of the spatial organization of intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and early tumor lesions, and has enabled the identification of local molecular programs that drive transitions between successive stages of carcinogenesis. 113

Recent computational analyses of ligand-receptor signaling have elucidated complex intercellular communication networks that coordinate pathological processes within the gastric mucosa. These studies indicate that epithelial cells, gastric stem cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and various immune cell populations form highly interconnected signaling networks that regulate chronic inflammation, tissue remodeling, angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and tumor progression. Rather than acting independently, these cellular populations collectively influence the tumor microenvironment through reciprocal signaling pathways that facilitate disease progression. As a result, gastric carcinogenesis is now conceptualized as a complex multicellular ecosystem in which intercellular communication is as critical as the accumulation of somatic mutations within epithelial cells. 114

Equally important is the growing recognition that epithelial and immune cells engage in dynamic bidirectional interactions throughout gastric carcinogenesis. Recent advances in spatial technologies have demonstrated that chronic inflammation leads to substantial restructuring of the immune microenvironment, including changes in the functional states of macrophages, T lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and other components of both innate and adaptive immunity. Additionally, immune cells are organized within tissues into specialized microniches that directly affect the behavior of epithelial and stem cells, clonal selection, and neoplastic transformation. These insights clarify the mechanisms of immune evasion during the early stages of carcinogenesis and suggest new opportunities for the development of preventive immunomodulatory strategies. 113

One of the most promising applications of single-cell technologies is the identification of premalignant cellular states that emerge before morphologically detectable dysplasia. These approaches have facilitated the identification of transitional epithelial populations defined by activated proliferation programs, impaired differentiation, metabolic reprogramming, and early oncogenic signaling pathway activation. These populations are increasingly considered to represent the initial stages of neoplastic evolution and are promising candidates for the development of highly sensitive biomarkers for early diagnosis and novel preventive therapeutic strategies. 115

Collectively, these advances are redefining the conceptual framework of gastric carcinogenesis by shifting its investigation from descriptive histopathology toward high-resolution molecular and cellular profiling. Looking ahead, integrating these technologies is expected to enhance the accuracy of molecular risk stratification, optimize endoscopic surveillance programs, identify new therapeutic targets, predict malignant transformation, and enable personalized preventive and therapeutic interventions. Nonetheless, widespread clinical adoption remains limited by high testing costs, technical complexity, lack of standardized analytical algorithms, interlaboratory variability, and the requirement for large, prospective, multicenter studies. However, additional prospective clinical validation is essential before these technologies can be incorporated into international guidelines for the diagnosis, surveillance, and management of precancerous gastric lesions and gastric cancer.

8. Limitations and Controversies

Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating the molecular mechanisms of gastric carcinogenesis, several important challenges remain. First, although inflammation, epigenetic alterations, genomic instability, and clonal evolution are recognized as interconnected drivers of disease progression, the temporal sequence and causal relationships among these processes remain incompletely understood. Second, many proposed biomarkers - including methylation signatures, circulating miRNAs, ctDNA, and single-cell-derived molecular profiles - have shown promising results but still lack prospective multicenter validation and standardized analytical methods. Third, marked interpatient heterogeneity, dynamic tumor evolution, and regional differences in gastric cancer biology limit the generalizability of existing biomarker models. Finally, most available evidence originates from retrospective studies or heterogeneous cohorts, highlighting the need for large prospective longitudinal studies to validate biomarker-based risk stratification and facilitate integration into routine clinical practice.

9. Conclusion

Gastric carcinogenesis is a complex, multistage process driven by the interplay of chronic inflammation, epigenetic alterations, genomic instability, dysregulated signaling pathways, clonal evolution, and dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Although the Correa cascade remains the cornerstone morphological model of gastric carcinogenesis, accumulating molecular evidence indicates that progression through its stages is governed not only by sequential histopathological changes but also by the continuous accumulation of interconnected molecular alterations.

Recent advances in molecular biology have substantially improved our understanding of gastric carcinogenesis and enabled the identification of diverse tissue and circulating biomarkers that reflect distinct stages of disease progression. These discoveries have expanded opportunities for molecular risk stratification, early diagnosis, and personalized surveillance. Nevertheless, most emerging biomarkers-including liquid biopsy assays, multi-omics panels, spatial molecular technologies, and single-cell approaches-require further standardization and validation in large prospective studies before they can be incorporated into routine clinical practice.

Future integration of molecular, morphological, and clinical data through multi-omics technologies, artificial intelligence, and systems biology has the potential to shift gastric cancer management from reactive treatment toward personalized prevention. Such an integrated approach may improve risk stratification, enable earlier identification of individuals at high risk of malignant transformation, optimize endoscopic surveillance, and facilitate the development of personalized preventive and therapeutic strategies. Collectively, these advances provide the foundation for a unified molecular framework of gastric carcinogenesis, bridging the classical Correa cascade with contemporary precision oncology.
