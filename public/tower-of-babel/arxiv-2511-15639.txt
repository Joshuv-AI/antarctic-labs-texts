An Analytical Formula for Gravitational Faraday Rotation in the ADM Split of Spacetime
Mark T. Lusk

Abstract
An analytical expression is derived for the rate of gravitational Faraday rotation measured by Eulerian observers. The reference frame is a Fermi-Walker triad aligned with the spatial wave vector. Attention is restricted to the ADM split of Kerr spacetime and geometric optics. Our exact, closed-form GFR formula is implemented and verified to be consistent with numerical predictions. The approach offers a new perspective on Faraday rotation, and it allows a single Eulerian observer to compare experimentally measured polarization holonomy with analytical prediction. Sliced spacetime does not suffer from a mathematical singularity at the ergosphere associated with Boyer-Lindquist coordinates in the threading decomposition. These physically intuitive coordinates can therefore be used to analytically produce and study GFR predictions for transits of light that pierce the ergosphere.

Introduction

In ADM (

\alpha

Eulerian observers move orthogonal to the slices with 4-velocity

A general spacetime metric can be expressed in matrix form as

[g_{\mu\nu}]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(-\alpha^{2}+\beta_{k}\beta^{k}\right)&\beta_{j}\\
\beta_{i}&\gamma_{ij}\\
\end{array}\right]\;.

(1)

U^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\{1,-\beta^{i}\}\;.

(2)

U_{\mu}=\{-\alpha,0,0,0\}\;.

(3)

VII.1
Parallel Transport Equation

In the (3+1) slicing decomposition of a stationary spacetime, the photon frequency (energy) measured by Eulerian observers is identified as
{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}

e^{i{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}t}

{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}:=-U_{\mu}k^{\mu}\equiv\alpha k^{0}\,.

(24)

The spacetime Maxwell equations can then be subjected to an Eikonal ansatz and a perturbation expansion, using
{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}
to identify terms of differing order. Within this geometric optics approximation, the wave vector and the polarization are both governed by the parallel transport equation

;
14

Assume a vacuum setting and consider vector potential
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf A}}}
in the Lorenz gauge. The requisite short-wavelength ansatz is

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf A}}}(x)=\real\{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf a}}}(x)\,e^{iS(x){\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}}\},\quad{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}\gg 1\;,

(25)

with real phase,
S
, and complex amplitude,
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf a}}}
, assumed to be slowly varying.

The
spacetime wave 1-form
is defined in terms of derivatives of the Eikonal phase, S:

k_{\mu}=\nabla_{\mu}S\equiv\partial_{\mu}S\;.

(26)

Insert this into Maxwell’s equations and collect powers of
{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}
. Define the

\lambda

k^{\mu}=\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\lambda}.

(27)

At leading order (

g(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k})=0\;.

(28)

Using

\displaystyle k^{i}=

\displaystyle\gamma^{ij}k_{j}-\frac{{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}}{\alpha}\beta^{i}

\displaystyle k_{i}=

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}k^{j}+\frac{{\omega_{\!\scriptscriptstyle E}}}{\alpha}\beta_{i}\,.

(29)

After applying the Lorenz gauge,
(\boldsymbol{k},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf a}}})=0

(\boldsymbol{k},\bm{\nabla})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf a}}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(4)}}{{{\bf a}}}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})=0\;.

Expand and simply this equation after substituting in the unit-valued polarization,

XI.4
Evaluation of

The Lie derivative defined in Eq.

implies that

\bm{\nabla}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\boldsymbol{U}}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}=\mathcal{L}_{\bf{\scriptscriptstyle U}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}+\bm{\nabla}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}}{\boldsymbol{U}}\;,

and taking the inner product with

(70)

Here and throughout, subscripts
\{a,b\}\in\{1,2\}
. Using Eq.
16
, this can be re-expressed in terms of the extrinsic curvature:

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}\,=\,g({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{\bf{\scriptscriptstyle U}}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}})-K_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}\;.

(71)

The Lie derivative of lapse function,
\alpha
, is simply a directional derivative:

\mathcal{L}_{\bf{\scriptscriptstyle U}}\alpha=({\boldsymbol{U}},\bm{\nabla}\alpha)\;.

(72)

In Kerr spacetime, though,
\alpha=\alpha(r,\theta)
, so its gradient only has components in the
r
and
\theta

2
by noting that
\bm{\beta}
is non-zero only in the
\phi

\mathcal{L}_{\bf{\scriptscriptstyle U}}\alpha=0\;.

(73)

Using the definition of the hypersurface normal, Eq.
2
, we then have

\mathcal{L}_{\bf{\scriptscriptstyle U}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}-\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)\;.

(74)

Substitute this into Eq.
71
and invoke the linearity of the inner product:

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)-K_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}.

(75)

None of the steps above would change if we had reversed the assignment of
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{1}}
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{2}}
, so Eq.
75
can be written with the first two indices reversed:

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}\hat{0}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}\big)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}\big)-K_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}.

(76)

The spin antisymmetry of Eq.

implies that

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}-\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}\hat{0}}\right)\;.

(77)

Inserting Eqs.
75
and
76
into the right side of this relation gives

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)-g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)-\alpha K_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}\big)-g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}\big)-\alpha K_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}\right)\;.

(78)

Since the extrinsic curvature is symmetric, the curvature terms cancel leaving

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)\,-\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\,-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left(\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}}\big)\,-\,g\big({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}\big)\right)\;.

(79)

We next show that the first pair of terms on right side of Eq.
XI.4
is equal to zero. The orthonormality relation,

\gamma_{ij}\,e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j}=\delta_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}\;,

(80)

can be differentiated with respect to
t
, giving

\partial_{t}\big(\gamma_{ij}e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j}\big)=0\;.

(81)

Expand the derivative, with attention restricted to stationary spacetimes so that
\partial_{t}\gamma_{ij}=0
:

\gamma_{ij}\,(\partial_{t}e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i})\,e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j}+\gamma_{ij}\,e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}(\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j})=0\;.

(82)

It will be convenient to label these terms,

\displaystyle T^{(2)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{ij}\,(\partial_{t}e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i})\,e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j},

(83)

\displaystyle T^{(3)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{ij}\,e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,(\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j})\;

(84)

so that Eq.

is equivalent to

T^{(2)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}+T^{(3)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}=0\;.

(85)

Since the indices can be reversed, we also have

T^{(2)}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}+T^{(3)}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}=0\;.

(86)

Subtract Eq.
86
from Eq.
85

0=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(T^{(2)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}-T^{(2)}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}\big)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(T^{(3)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}-T^{(3)}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}\big)\;.

Noting that

\displaystyle T^{(2)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}-T^{(2)}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{ij}\big(\partial_{t}e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j}-\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}}{}^{i}\,e_{\hat{a}}{}^{j}\big)

\displaystyle T^{(3)}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}}-T^{(3)}_{\hat{b}\hat{a}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{ij}\big(e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j}-e_{\hat{b}}{}^{i}\,\partial_{t}e_{\hat{a}}{}^{j}\big)\;,

(88)

and adding these gives

\gamma_{ij}\,e_{[\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}]}{}^{j}=0\;.

(89)

Because

\gamma_{ij}\,e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}e_{\hat{b}})^{j}=\gamma_{ij}\,e_{\hat{a}}{}^{i}\,\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}}{}^{j}\;.

Apply Eq.

\displaystyle g({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}})\,-

\displaystyle\,g({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}})

\displaystyle\,2\,\gamma_{ij}\,e_{[\hat{a}}{}^{i}\partial_{t}e_{\hat{b}]}{}^{j}=0\;.

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\bigl(g({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}}-g({\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{a}},\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{b}})\bigr)\;.

This reduces Eq.

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}\,=\,-\frac{1}{2\alpha}e_{\hat{a}}^{i}e_{\hat{b}}^{j}\,S\big(\partial_{i},\partial_{j}\big)\;.

Now use Eq.
6

\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}\partial_{j}=[\bm{\beta},\partial_{j}]=-\partial_{j}\beta^{i}\,\partial_{i}\;.

(95)

Project Eq.

\partial_{i}

\gamma\left(\partial_{i}\,,\,\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\scriptscriptstyle\beta}}\partial_{j}\right)=-\gamma_{iq}\,\partial_{j}\beta^{q}\ \;.

Apply this to bilinear operator,

(97)

In turn, this can be utilized in Eq.

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\rm(4)}}{{\omega}}_{\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{0}}\,=\,-\frac{1}{2\alpha}e_{\hat{a}}^{i}e_{\hat{b}}^{j}\,\bigl(\gamma_{jq}\partial_{i}\beta^{q}-\gamma_{iq}\partial_{j}\beta^{q}\bigr)\;.

XII
The Shift Tetrad

The GFR rate of Eq.

52

Since it is Lense-Thirring precession that causes any screen basis to rotate relative to a local FW frame, it makes sense to construct a new screen frame,
\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{1}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{2}}\}

{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{2}}

{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{1}}:={\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{2}}\crossproduct{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}},\quad{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{2}}:=\frac{{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}\crossproduct\bm{\beta}}{|{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}\crossproduct\bm{\beta}|}\;.

(100)

Provided that the photon trajectory can be expressed using standard functions, the spatial wave vector, the shift vector, and ultimately the GFR rate of Eq.
68
, can be evaluated in closed form as well.

The prescence of

XVI
Conclusions

A continuum of Eulerian observers along the trajectory of a photon can individually measure an instantaneous rate of polarization rotation with respect to a local frame of reference. The reference screen dyad is governed by Fermi-Walker rotation about the local spatial wave vector instead of the photon trajectory. Path integration then quantifies the accumulation of GFR. Within the ADM split of Kerr spacetime, these Eulerian spotters have zero angular momentum and, from their perspective, the rotation of the polarization of light relative to FW frames is due to the frame dragging deformation of the hypersurface, completely characterized by its extrinsic curvature. When applied to a closed trajectory
17
, this allows a single stationary observer to compare a measured polarization holonomy with theoretical prediction.

The rate of GFR is formulated using a Shift Dyad,
\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{1}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{2}}\}
, for which
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\grave{2}}\,\,{\scriptstyle\propto}\,\,{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}}\crossproduct\bm{\beta}
. The spatial gradient of the shift vector then has a component which produces a differential rotation of the screen basis about the foliation normal. This distills into an exact, closed-form rate of GFR proportional to the
K_{\grave{1}\grave{2}}
–component of the extrinsic curvature in our bespoke frame. Such a curvature-based formulation offers an Eulerian perspective on GFR that is complementary to the more standard gravitomagnetic perception of a Lagrangian ghost within the threading (1+3) split of spacetime.

The new rate of GFR accumulation has been formulated using a specific polarization gauge wherein spatial polarization is orthogonal to the spatial wave vector. GFR is invariant with respect to gauge, though. This has been numerically checked by adopting a gauge for which the spatial polarization is orthogonal to the photon trajectory instead of the spatial wave vector. It is worth emphasizing that the GFR perceived by an Eulerian observer is not the same as that measured by a Lagrangian observer. This is because the reference triads are aligned with the spatial wave vector and spatial photon trajectory, respectively.

The Eulerian formulation of GFR does not suffer from the mathematical singularity at the ergosphere present for Boyer-Lindquist coordinates in the threading split. These physically intuitive coordinates can therefore be used to analytically produce and scrutinize GFR predictions for transits of light that pierce the ergosphere.
