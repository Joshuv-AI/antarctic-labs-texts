Post‑pandemic shifts in the age distribution of RSV, influenza A, and rhinovirus infections: A four‑year pediatric surveillance study
Authors: Hongbo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether age distributions of common pediatric respiratory viruses changed uniformly after the COVID‑19 pandemic remains unclear. Understanding pathogen‑specific patterns is important for guiding surveillance and prevention strategies in the post‑pandemic period.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective hospital‑based cohort study of 19,900 laboratory‑confirmed single‑virus infection episodes caused by RSV, influenza A virus (FluA), and human rhinovirus (HRV) among children aged 0–17 years who presented to a provincial women’s and children’s hospital in China from 2022 to 2025. These data describe age distributions among detected cases and do not represent population‑level incidence. Age distributions were examined using kernel density estimation and quantile regression. Annual proportions of children aged ≥ 3 years were compared across pathogens and years, and multivariable logistic regression was used to assess adjusted associations with older age. Sensitivity analyses included outpatient‑only models, RSV‑only models, and an alternative age cutoff of ≥ 5 years.ResultsRSV infections occurred mainly in the youngest children (median 1.4–2.6 years), FluA affected older children (median up to 5.9 years), and HRV showed a stable intermediate pattern (median 2.9–3.5 years). FluA demonstrated clear shifts toward older ages in 2023 and 2025, whereas RSV showed only modest variation and HRV remained stable across years. Compared with RSV, FluA (OR 5.69) and HRV (OR 3.29) were strongly associated with being ≥ 3 years old. Findings were consistent across all sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsAge redistribution after the pandemic differed by pathogen. FluA shifted toward older children, RSV remained concentrated in early childhood, and HRV returned to a stable pre‑pandemic profile. These.results highlight the value of pathogen‑specific, age‑stratified approaches to pediatric respiratory virus surveillance in the post‑pandemic era.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28011-x.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza A virus (FluA), and human rhinovirus (HRV) are major causes of acute respiratory infections in children and contribute substantially to outpatient visits and hospitalizations worldwide [1–3]. Before the COVID‑19 pandemic, the age distributions of these viruses were highly consistent across settings, with similar patterns reported in multiple countries [4–8]. RSV infections occurred mainly in infants and young toddlers, FluA affected a broader age range but imposed the greatest burden on preschool‑aged children, and HRV—despite its wide circulation—remained concentrated in early childhood [2–9]. These stable age profiles informed clinical management, vaccination strategies, and planning for pediatric healthcare services.

The widespread use of non‑pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the pandemic markedly reduced children’s exposure to respiratory viruses and altered the early‑life immunity landscape. Reduced viral circulation and fewer early‑childhood infections may have created an “immunity debt,” particularly among young children who missed opportunities for immune priming [10–13]. After NPIs were relaxed, several regions reported atypical rebounds of RSV and FluA, raising concerns that pre‑pandemic age‑related susceptibility patterns may have shifted [11, 12, 14, 15]. However, it remains unclear whether these changes occurred consistently across different viruses, persisted over multiple years, or varied between outpatient and inpatient populations.

Most existing studies have focused on single pathogens, limited geographic areas, or short post‑pandemic intervals, limiting their ability to assess broader structural changes in pediatric susceptibility. Although our analysis is also based on a single provincial tertiary maternal and child health hospital in China, it provides a multi‑pathogen, four‑year, age‑focused description from a large referral center in central China, without fully resolving the limitation of geographically restricted data. Key questions remain regarding whether age distributions shifted in a pathogen‑specific manner and whether these patterns have stabilized or continued to evolve in the years following the pandemic.

To address these gaps, we conducted a four‑year analysis of RSV, FluA, and HRV infections among children from 2022 to 2025 at a large provincial tertiary women’s and children’s hospital. Using kernel density estimation, quantile regression, and multivariable modeling, we characterized post‑COVID age distributions, examined year‑to‑year changes in pediatric age structure, and evaluated the consistency of these patterns across outpatient and inpatient settings. This study provides updated evidence on pathogen‑specific age patterns in the evolving post‑pandemic period.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective observational study based on hospital‑based surveillance of laboratory‑confirmed respiratory virus infections at a large provincial tertiary women’s and children’s hospital in China. The study describes age distributions among detected cases rather than population‑level incidence. The study covered the period from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2025, representing four consecutive years after the lifting of strict COVID‑19 public health restrictions in China.

Laboratory testing

Outpatients and emergency department patients were routinely tested using a limited viral panel (parainfluenza viruses [PIV], influenza A/B [FluA/B], RSV, adenovirus [AdV]) with standardized commercial multiplex PCR assays; positivity was defined according to manufacturer‑recommended cycle threshold criteria. A small subset of outpatients also underwent an expanded panel at the treating physician’s discretion (e.g., severe or prolonged illness).

Inpatients were tested based on clinical need, receiving either the same limited panel or an expanded multiplex PCR panel detecting FluA/B, RSV, PIV, HRV, human metapneumovirus, human coronaviruses (NL63, OC43, 229E, HKU1), AdV, human bocavirus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Chlamydia pneumoniae. Sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid culture was performed when bacterial co‑infection or severe pneumonia was suspected.

Study population and case definitions

We included children aged 29 days to 17 years who presented with symptoms of acute respiratory infection and underwent virologic testing during the study period. The analysis focused on three predefined viruses: RSV, FluA, and HRV. A virus‑specific episode was defined as a positive RT‑PCR result for one target virus with no other respiratory pathogens detected in the same encounter; other detected pathogens were used solely to identify and exclude mixed infections. Each new illness episode associated with a separate virologic test was treated as an independent encounter. A single child could contribute multiple episodes if encounters were separated by more than 21 days. Repeat positive tests for the same pathogen within 21 days were considered part of the same episode. The 21‑day interval was used as a practical window in respiratory virus surveillance to distinguish new clinical episodes from short‑interval repeat testing. Encounters with missing age or missing virologic test results were excluded.

Variables and definitions

Age was recorded in years and analyzed both as a continuous variable and in predefined groups (< 6 months, 6–<12 months, 1–<3 years, 3–<6 years, and ≥ 6 years). The primary age outcome was a prespecified binary indicator of ≥ 3 years versus < 3 years, selected based on established epidemiologic and clinical distinctions: children younger than 3 years exhibit distinct immune maturation, higher incidence and severity of RSV and HRV infections, and a transition in contact patterns (e.g., entry into childcare settings). An alternative cutoff of ≥ 5 years was used in sensitivity analyses. Continuous‑age methods (kernel density estimation and quantile regression) were additionally applied to characterize full distributional shifts. Sex and month of encounter were available for all episodes and were summarized descriptively but were not included as model covariates, as they were not central to the prespecified interannual objective. Main exposure variables included calendar year (2022–2025), viral pathogen (RSV, FluA, HRV), and visit type (outpatient/emergency vs. inpatient). Secondary outcomes included age distributions assessed by kernel density estimation, year‑specific shifts evaluated using quantile regression, age‑group composition, and adjusted odds ratios for age ≥ 3 years estimated from multivariable logistic regression.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by year and pathogen. Age distributions were visualized using kernel density estimation, and year‑specific shifts were evaluated with quantile regression. Age‑group composition was displayed using stacked bar charts. To examine factors associated with age ≥ 3 years, multivariable logistic regression was performed with calendar year, pathogen, and visit type as prespecified predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, using 2022, RSV, and outpatient visits as reference categories. Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were conducted: outpatient‑only, RSV‑only, and an alternative age cutoff of ≥ 5 years. Temporal trends in the proportion of children aged ≥ 3 years were assessed using the Cochran–Armitage trend test. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2), and two‑sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 80,721 pediatric encounters underwent virologic testing for acute respiratory infections during the study period. After excluding encounters that tested negative for all respiratory pathogens and all forms of co‑infection—including co‑detection of ≥ 2 target viruses (RSV, FluA, HRV) or co‑detection of a target virus with any non‑target pathogen—19,900 single‑virus infection episodes were included in the final analytic cohort: 6,357 RSV, 9,191 FluA, and 4,352 HRV infections.

Baseline characteristics showed clear differences in age distribution across pathogens and years (Table 1; Fig. 1). RSV consistently affected the youngest children, with median ages ranging from 1.4 to 2.6 years. Infants < 6 months accounted for 11.1–23.5% of annual RSV detections, and children aged 1–<3 years represented the largest proportion each year (31.2–44.0%). FluA infections occurred in older children. Median ages exceeded 5.8 years in 2023 and 2025, and children ≥ 6 years accounted for nearly half of detections in those years (49.9% and 47.8%, respectively), while infants contributed only 1.9–4.8% of cases. HRV showed an intermediate and comparatively stable age profile, with median ages of 2.9–3.5 years. The 1–<3‑year and 3–<6‑year groups together accounted for approximately 60–70% of annual HRV detections, and the proportions of infants and older children varied only modestly across years.

Age distribution

The age distribution of infections differed markedly across pathogens and years (Fig. 2). Kernel density curves revealed distinct patterns: RSV demonstrated a sharply left‑skewed distribution with a pronounced peak in infancy across all years. HRV displayed a broader distribution with a stable peak in early childhood and minimal temporal variation. In contrast, FluA exhibited the oldest age profile, with density curves peaking between approximately 3 and 6 years and showing marked rightward shifts in 2023 and 2025, reflecting older ages at infection in those years.

Quantile regression of age over time

Quantile regression revealed pathogen‑specific and quantile‑specific age shifts from 2022 to 2025 (Fig. 3). For RSV, predicted ages at lower quantiles remained low with minor fluctuations (0.10 quantile: 0.2–0.4 years; 0.25 quantile: 0.5–1.0 years), whereas the median and upper quantiles showed more pronounced variation. The median age increased from 1.4 years in 2022 to 2.6 years in 2023, decreased to 1.9 years in 2024, then rose again to 2.4 years in 2025; the 0.90 quantile ranged from 3.4 to 6.3 years, indicating that temporal changes were driven primarily by older RSV‑infected children rather than infants.

Quantile regression trends showing year‑specific shifts in the age distribution of pediatric RSV, influenza A, and rhinovirus infections from 2022 to 2025

For FluA, age shifts were larger and more systematic across quantiles. Predicted ages increased substantially between 2022 and 2023 at all quantiles (e.g., median: 3.1 to 5.9 years; 0.90 quantile: 6.1 to 10.3 years), followed by a partial decline in 2024 and a renewed increase in 2025 (median: 5.8 years; 0.90 quantile: 11.0 years). This pattern is consistent with a marked post‑pandemic shift of FluA infections toward older children, particularly at the upper tail of the age distribution.

In contrast, HRV exhibited only modest temporal variation: predicted ages increased slightly from 2022 to 2023 across quantiles (e.g., median: 2.9 to 3.5 years; 0.90 quantile: 6.1 to 7.4 years) and then remained relatively stable through 2024–2025, suggesting limited age redistribution for this pathogen.

Adjusted associations with being ≥ 3 years old

Multivariable logistic regression identified strong and pathogen‑specific associations with the likelihood of being ≥ 3 years old (Table 2; Fig. 4). Compared with 2022, the odds of being ≥ 3 years increased markedly in 2023 (OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.82–2.26) and 2025 (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.24–1.55), with no significant difference in 2024 (OR 1.09, 95% CI 0.98–1.22). Viral pathogen was a major determinant of age: relative to RSV, FluA‑infected children had the highest odds of being ≥ 3 years (OR 5.69, 95% CI 5.28–6.12), followed by HRV (OR 3.29, 95% CI 3.00–3.60). Inpatient encounters were substantially less likely to involve children ≥ 3 years than outpatient encounters (OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.41–0.48), consistent with the younger age profile of hospitalized patients.

Temporal trends in the proportion of children aged ≥ 3 years

The annual proportion of children aged ≥ 3 years varied across the four post‑pandemic years (Supplementary Table S1). The proportion increased from 42.3% in 2022 to 70.6% in 2023, then declined to 53.7% in 2024 and rose again to 58.6% in 2025. However, the Cochran–Armitage trend test indicated no significant linear trend across years (Z = − 0.19, p = 0.853), reflecting the non‑monotonic pattern driven by pathogen‑specific epidemic dynamics rather than a consistent temporal shift.

Sensitivity analyses

Findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S2–S4). In the outpatient‑only analysis, the odds of being ≥ 3 years were significantly lower in 2025 versus 2022 (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.61–0.78), and markedly higher for FluA (OR 7.58, 95% CI 6.87–8.38) and HRV (OR 2.49, 95% CI 1.05–6.31) relative to RSV. In the RSV‑only analysis, older age remained more likely in 2023–2025 versus 2022 (ORs 1.87–3.23), and inpatient encounters continued to involve younger children (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.61–0.76). Using an alternative age cutoff of ≥ 5 years yielded consistent results, with higher odds in 2023–2025 relative to 2022 and substantially elevated odds for FluA (OR 10.93, 95% CI 9.95–12.03) and HRV (OR 5.99, 95% CI 5.28–6.81) versus RSV. Together, these analyses demonstrate that the observed age shifts were robust to visit type, virus‑specific restriction, and the choice of age threshold.

Discussion

In this four‑year analysis of pediatric respiratory infections, we observed clear and pathogen‑specific differences in the post‑COVID age distribution of RSV, FluA, and HRV. FluA showed the most evident rightward shift, with older ages at infection in 2023 and 2025, consistent with reports describing post‑pandemic immunity gaps and delayed exposure among school‑aged children [16, 17]. RSV exhibited only modest and variable changes, aligning with evidence that its early‑childhood susceptibility window re‑establishes rapidly once viral circulation resumes [18–21]. HRV maintained a comparatively stable age profile across years, consistent with observations that its transmission was less affected by pandemic‑related mitigation measures [22]. Taken together, these findings indicate that post‑pandemic age redistribution occurred in a pathogen‑specific manner, with FluA showing transient shifts toward older children, while RSV and HRV returned more quickly to their characteristic pre‑pandemic age patterns. These patterns were consistently observed across both the binary age outcome and the continuous‑age analyses (kernel density estimation and quantile regression), supporting the robustness of the interannual shifts.

Several factors may help contextualize this differential redistribution. FluA appears more sensitive to interruptions in viral circulation, as effective protection relies on short‑lived effector responses and strain‑specific B‑cell memory that require periodic antigenic restimulation; when circulation is reduced, memory pools contract and antibody levels decline, leaving older children without recent boosting [23, 24]. This vulnerability is reinforced by the age‑structured contact patterns that sustain influenza transmission: school‑aged children form the core transmission layer in seasonal influenza dynamics, and the disruption of school‑based contact networks—key determinants of epidemic intensity and age distribution—substantially reduced exposure in the age groups that typically drive transmission [25, 26]. Taken together, reduced immune maintenance and altered contact‑mediated exposure likely contributed to the shift toward older pediatric age groups once circulation resumed.

In contrast, susceptibility to RSV is shaped largely by early‑life biology. Pediatric airway epithelium is intrinsically more permissive to RSV replication, with immature innate antiviral responses and enhanced viral entry and spread in infant airways [27, 28]. These features, together with the rapid decay of maternally derived antibodies and the absence of durable population‑level immunity, anchor primary RSV infection within early childhood and support the rapid re‑establishment of its characteristic age distribution once circulation resumes [29]. HRV follows a different ecological pattern: it circulates perennially across school and community settings, with repeated introductions and overlapping transmission chains maintaining continuous exposure [30]. Its extensive genetic and antigenic diversity—spanning numerous co‑circulating types with limited cross‑protective immunity and heterogeneous clinical expression—prevents the development of stable, long‑lasting population immunity [31, 32]. These features allow HRV to re‑occupy ecological space quickly after perturbation, contrasting with the more pronounced shifts observed for FluA and the biologically constrained stability of RSV. Asynchronous viral resurgence may also have contributed, with FluA re‑emerging into a less competitive environment than RSV [33–35]. Taken together, these pathogen‑specific biological and ecological characteristics help contextualize why FluA showed notable age shifts, whereas RSV and HRV remained comparatively stable.

These pathogen‑specific patterns have practical implications for respiratory virus preparedness. The rightward shift observed for influenza A suggests that immunity gaps in older children may influence transmission dynamics, underscoring the importance of maintaining vaccination coverage in school‑aged populations. The relative stability of RSV’s age profile, despite substantial disruptions, supports continued prioritization of infant protection through maternal immunization, long‑acting monoclonal antibodies, and pediatric vaccination. HRV’s continuous circulation throughout the pandemic and post‑pandemic periods highlights its ecological resilience and its role as a consistently present respiratory pathogen [30–32]. More broadly, these observations support the value of multi‑pathogen, age‑aware surveillance strategies that integrate information on co‑circulating viruses and population immunity to guide public health responses in an evolving respiratory virus landscape.

This study has several strengths, including a large pediatric cohort, four consecutive post‑pandemic years of virologic testing, consistent case definitions across pathogens, and complementary analytic approaches. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single‑center design may limit generalizability to settings with different healthcare‑seeking behaviors or testing practices. Second, virologic testing was clinically driven rather than population‑based, introducing the potential for selection bias. Third, our multivariable model focused on a prespecified set of variables most directly relevant to the study’s interannual objective (calendar year, pathogen, and visit type). Other patient‑level or temporal factors, such as sex and within‑year seasonal variation, were not included and may warrant consideration in future work. Fourth, the absence of immunologic, genomic, and microbiome data prevented direct assessment of underlying biological mechanisms. Finally, the post‑pandemic period continues to evolve, and age patterns may shift further as immunity rebuilds and contact structures stabilize. These limitations indicate that our findings should be interpreted as an early but informative step toward understanding pathogen‑specific age redistribution in the post‑COVID era.

Conclusion

This study provides direct evidence that post‑pandemic age redistribution among respiratory viruses is pathogen‑specific. FluA shifted toward older children, RSV remained concentrated in early childhood, and HRV rapidly returned to its pre‑pandemic profile. These findings support age‑stratified, pathogen‑specific approaches to pediatric respiratory infection surveillance and prevention in the post‑pandemic period.
