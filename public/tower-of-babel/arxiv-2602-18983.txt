A generalized Helmholtz-type decomposition of symmetric tensor fields and applications to ray transforms
Antti Kykkänen, Rohit Kumar Mishra, Suman Kumar Sahoo

Abstract
We study a solenoidal-potential type decomposition of a symmetric m m -tensor field in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , and its implications to injectivity questions for the momentum and elastic ray transforms. For symmetric tensor fields, a general decomposition with a restriction on the dimension and order of the decomposition was proved in [ 15 ] . We extend the result to dimension 2 2 under a mean-zero assumption. We use the decomposition in 2 2 dimensions to prove the injectivity of the momentum and elastic ray transforms. We also prove a connection between the two integral transforms for 2 2 -tensors. Later, we use our decomposition to prove the injectivity of integral transforms, including longitudinal and elastic ray transforms, without any mean-zero assumption on tensor fields. Next, we explore connections between different integral transforms and use these to relate their corresponding properties.



Keywords:
Helmholtz Decomposition, Elastic Ray transform, Momentum Ray Transforms, Symmetric
m
-Tensor Fields, Integral Geometry

Introduction

The well-known Helmholtz Decomposition states that a “sufficiently smooth, rapidly decaying” vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be uniquely decomposed into a curl-free and a divergence-free part. The curl-free and divergence-free parts are often referred to as the potential part and solenoidal part, respectively. Generalizing this decomposition, Sharafutdinov
[
24
]
proved that a symmetric
m
-tensor field in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be uniquely decomposed into a solenoidal part and a potential part of the given tensor field. In a recent work
[
15
]
, the authors introduced
k
-solenoidal and
k
-potential tensor fields as a generalization of solenoidal and potential tensor fields in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A more general decomposition of symmetric
m
-tensor fields into
k
-solenoidal and
k
-potential tensor fields was discussed in
[
15
]
. Their decomposition is proven under a restriction on the dimension and the order of tensor fields, which makes the decomposition not applicable for
n=2
. Improving on this, we prove that the decomposition result holds in
2
dimensions as well, under a mean-zero condition on the tensor field. Furthermore, we proved that the imposed condition cannot be removed, as we also prove that existence of such a decomposition implies the mean-zero condition. There are other kinds of decompositions studied in the literature, specifically for problems in integral geometry; please refer to
[
7
,
21
,
11
]
and the references therein.

Such decompositions play a fundamental role in many applications. In fluid dynamics, the Helmholtz decomposition allows one to split a velocity field into its divergence-free (solenoidal) part and its irrotational (gradient) part. This separation is essential in the analysis of incompressible flows, where the pressure term acts as a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the divergence-free constraint. In particular, by projecting the Navier–Stokes equations onto the space of divergence-free vector fields via the Helmholtz decomposition, one eliminates the pressure term and obtains the Stokes operator as the linear part of the dynamics. This viewpoint is fundamental in the linearization of the Navier–Stokes equations around equilibrium states and in the study of well-posedness and stability, see for instance
[
4
,
9
]
. The Helmholtz decomposition also plays an important role in electrodynamics and medical imaging. More generally, such decompositions provide powerful tools for characterizing the kernels of integral transforms and for establishing injectivity modulo the identified kernel. In our work, we apply this framework to momentum ray transforms and to elastic ray transforms of tensor fields.

For momentum ray transforms, we showed that the first two integral moments of a symmetric
2
-tensor field are zero if and only if the tensor field is the Hessian of a scalar, real-valued function. To prove injectivity, we did not require the mean-zero condition (which was essential for the decomposition), since it is automatically satisfied if the momentum transforms are zero. Momentum ray transform appears to study Calderón type inverse problems for biharmonic and polyharmonic operators, see for instance
[
2
,
22
,
3
]
.
Such injectivity results are studied by many authors in different settings; the author may refer to
[
23
,
15
,
8
,
14
,
13
,
12
,
1
,
17
,
6
,
7
,
18
,
5
,
20
,
19
]
and some references therein.

Elastic ray transforms arise as linearizations elastic travel times around a homogeneous and isotropic background (see
[
11
]
for details). We extend the proof of the Helmholtz-type decomposition of
[
11
]
to
2
-dimensional domains. For elastic tensor fields the potential part of the tensor field consists of two not mutually disjoint parts (see
2
for definitions of the operators, c.f.
[
11
]
). We use our decomposition to extend the injectivity result from
[
11
]
into the
2
-dimensional setting.

Finally, we conclude the article by relating various integral transforms (the elastic ray transform, the momentum ray transforms, and the mixed ray transforms) and use the observed relations to characterize the kernel of the aforementioned transform in the case of
2
-tensors.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the various notations and definitions that will be used in the article. Section
3
focuses on the main decomposition result of our manuscript. Next, in Sections
4
and
5
, we apply our decomposition to the momentum ray transform and the elastic ray transforms of symmetric tensor fields in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively. The relation between various integral transforms is discussed in Section

A decomposition result for symmetric
2
-tensor fields in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

The goal of this section is to prove that any symmetric
2

2

2

Lemma 2

(
[

[

.

Given a tensor field

and
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})
such that the following decomposition of
f
holds:

\displaystyle f(x)=g(x)+i^{2}_{x}v(x),\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})

(5)

and

satisfies
j^{2}_{x}g(x)=0

(x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})
. For

, the fields

\displaystyle v(x)=\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{|x|^{4}}f_{ij}(x)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad g_{ij}(x)=\sigma(i,j)\left(\delta_{i}^{k}\delta_{j}^{\ell}-\frac{x^{i}x^{j}x^{k}x^{\ell}}{|x|^{4}}\right)f_{k\ell}(x).

(6)

\displaystyle\lvert g\rvert\lesssim(1+\lvert x\rvert)^{-n},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lvert\mathrm{d}^{\ell}v\rvert\lesssim(1+\lvert x\rvert)^{2-\ell-n},\quad\mbox{for $\ell=0,1$}.

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
denotes the space of tempered distributions. Applying Fourier transform to the equations
g+\mathrm{d}^{2}v=0
and
\delta^{2}g=0
, we get
\widehat{g}(y)+\mathrm{i}^{2}i_{y}^{2}\widehat{v}(y)=0
and
j_{y}^{2}\widehat{g}(y)=0
. By Lemma
2
we have
\widehat{g}(y)=\widehat{v}(y)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{0}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
,
i.e.,
the support of distributions is contained in
\{0\}.
Thus
\widehat{g}
and
\widehat{v}
can be written as a finite linear combination of derivatives of the Dirac delta distribution.
Therefore

\widehat{g}=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq p}c_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}\delta_{0},\quad\mbox{ for some positive integer }p,

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta distribution.
Again
\partial^{\alpha}\delta_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any multi-index
\alpha
. Taking the inverse Fourier transform of this equation in the sense of tempered distributions, we obtain that
g
is a polynomial of degree at most
p
. But
g(x)\rightarrow 0
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
implies
g=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
One can argue similarly and conclude that
v(x)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

4
Applications to momentum ray transforms

In this section, our aim is to prove an injectivity result for momentum ray transforms. The result we prove here improves the earlier result
[
15
, Theorem 4.3]
, which had a dimensional restriction. Because of the restriction, the result
[
15
, Theorem 4.3]
was not true for
m=2
and
n=2
.

Definition 5
.

For a non-negative integer
q\geq 0
, the
q
-th integral moment transform of a symmetric
2
-tensor field is the operator
I^{q}:{\mathcal{S}}(S^{2})\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}(T\mathbb{S}^{1})
given by
[
23
]
:

\displaystyle I^{q}f(x,\xi)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{q}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\,\xi^{i}\xi^{j}dt.

(11)

We also define the operator
J^{q}:\mathcal{S}(S^{2})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}))
by extending
I^{q}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}

J^{q}f(x,\xi)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{q}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\,\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\,dt\quad\mbox{for}\quad(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}.

(12)

It has been shown in
[
13
]
that the data
(I^{0}f,I^{1}f,I^{2}f)
and
(J^{0}f,J^{1}f,J^{2}f)
are equivalent for and there are explicit relations between these operators:

\displaystyle J^{0}\!f(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=|\xi|I^{0}\!f\left(x-\frac{\langle x,\xi\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\xi,\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)

\displaystyle J^{1}\!f(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\langle\xi,x\rangle}{|\xi|}\,I^{0}\!f\left(x-\frac{\langle x,\xi\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\xi,\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)+I^{1}\!f\left(x-\frac{\langle x,\xi\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\xi,\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)

\displaystyle J^{2}\!f(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\xi,x\rangle^{2}}{|\xi|^{3}}\,I^{0}\!f\left(x-\frac{\langle x,\xi\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\xi,\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)-2\frac{\langle\xi,x\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\,I^{1}\!f\left(x-\frac{\langle x,\xi\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\xi,\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+|\xi|^{-1}\,I^{2}\!f\left(x-\frac{\langle x,\xi\rangle}{|\xi|^{2}}\xi,\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right).

The Fourier transform of a symmetric
2
-tensor field
f\in\mathcal{S}(S^{2})
is defined component-wise, that is,

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{ij}(y)=\widehat{f}_{ij}(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\widehat{h}(y)
denotes the usual Fourier transform of a scalar function
h
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

The Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}(T\mathbb{S}^{1})\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}(T\mathbb{S}^{1})
is defined as follows, see
[
24
, Section 2.1]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(y,\xi)=\widehat{\varphi}(y,\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\int_{\xi^{\perp}}e^{-ix\cdot y}\varphi(x,\xi)\,dx,

(13)

where
dx
is the arc-length measure on the line
\xi^{\perp}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle x,\xi\rangle=0\}.

This definition of the Fourier transform is used to compute the following Fourier transform of
q
-th integral moment transform of
f
:

\displaystyle\widehat{I^{q}f}(y,\xi)=(2\pi)^{1/2}i^{q}\langle\xi,\partial_{y}\rangle^{q}\left(\widehat{f}_{ij}(y)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\right).

For
q=0
, the above equality reduces to

\displaystyle\widehat{If}(y,\xi)=(2\pi)^{1/2}\widehat{f}_{ij}(y)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}.

Theorem 6
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(S^{2})
be a symmetric
2
-tensor field then
I^{q}f=0
for
q=0,1
if and only if
f=\mathrm{d}^{2}v
, for some smooth function
v
satisfying
v(x)\rightarrow 0
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

To prove the ‘if’ part of the theorem, we start by assuming
f=\mathrm{d}^{2}v
for some
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfying
\mathrm{d}^{\ell}v\rightarrow 0
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
for
\ell=0,1
. Then a simple application of integration by parts gives

I^{\ell}(f)=I^{0}(\mathrm{d}^{2-\ell}v)=0,\qquad\mbox{ for }\ell=0,1.

Conversely, let
f\in\mathcal{S}(S^{2})
be symmetric
2
-tensor field that satisfies
I^{q}f=0
for
q=0,1
. Since,
I^{0}f(x,\xi)=0
for
(x,\xi)\in T\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We will use this information to conclude
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)dx=0
, so that we can apply our Decomposition Theorem
3
.

Let us fix
\xi=e_{1}:=(1,0)
then for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle I^{0}f((0,x_{2}),e_{1})=0\Longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{11}(t,x_{2})dt=0\Longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{11}(t,x_{2})dtdx_{2}=0\Longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{11}(x)dx=0.

Repeating the same process as above by choosing
\xi
to be unit vectors
(0,1)
and
\displaystyle\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt{2}}
, we can show

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{22}(x)dx=0\qquad\&\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{12}(x)dx=0.

Therefore, we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)dx=0
and hence from our Decomposition Theorem
3
,
f
can be decomposed as follows:

f=g+\mathrm{d}^{2}v,\quad\delta^{2}g=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad v\,\rightarrow 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad|x|\rightarrow\infty.

Using
[
15
, Lemma 4.2]
, we have

\displaystyle J^{0}f(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle J^{0}\tilde{f}^{i}(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,2\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\tilde{f}^{i}=(f_{i1},f_{i2}).

This implies

\displaystyle I^{0}\tilde{f}^{i}(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,2.

Then, taking the Fourier transform, we get for
y\perp\xi

\displaystyle\widehat{(\tilde{f}^{i})}_{j}(y)\xi^{j}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\widehat{f}_{ij}(y)\xi^{j}=0,\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,2.

From the Fourier transform and our Decomposition Theorem
3
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(y)+i_{y}^{2}\widehat{v}(y)=\widehat{f}(y)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\qquad\xi^{j}\widehat{g}_{ij}(y)+\xi^{j}y_{i}y_{j}\widehat{v}(y)

\displaystyle=\xi^{j}\widehat{f}_{ij}(y),\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,2.

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\qquad\ \ \quad\qquad\qquad\xi^{j}\widehat{g}_{ij}(y)

\displaystyle=\xi^{j}\widehat{f}_{ij}(y)=0,\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,2.

From above, we can obtain two independent relations
\langle\widehat{g},\xi\otimes\xi\rangle=0
and
\langle\widehat{g},y\otimes\xi\rangle=0
. Furthermore
\delta^{2}g=0
implies
\langle\widehat{g},y\otimes y\rangle=0
. Combining all these three conditions, we have
\widehat{g}(y)=0
for
y\neq 0
. Since
\widehat{g}(y)
is an integrable function, therefore as a distribution the support of
\widehat{g}\subseteq\{0\}
. Amending the arguments used in the proof of the uniqueness part of the Theorem
3
, we get
g=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Putting
g=0
in the decomposition above, we achieve
f=\mathrm{d}^{2}v
, which completes the proof of the converse part as well.
∎

Applications to Elastic ray transform

We start with recalling the definition (see
[
11
]
) of
elastic ray transform
acting on elastic tensor fields from
\mathcal{S}({E^{m}(n))}
. For a given direction vector
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we denote the hyperplane passing through the origin and perpendicular to
\xi
by
\xi^{\perp}
. For a given unit vector

\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\{(\xi,\zeta):\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\mbox{ and }\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\xi\cup\xi^{\perp}\}

to set of such pairs; unit vectors and corresponding polarization vectors.

Definition 7
.

We define the elastic ray transform as a map from
\displaystyle X^{m}\colon\mathcal{S}(E^{m}(n))\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{Z})
defined by

\displaystyle X^{m}f(x,\xi,\zeta):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle f(x+t\xi),(\xi\otimes\zeta)^{\otimes m}\rangle\,dt.

(14)

Our primary aim is to study the injectivity of the elastic ray transform for elastic 2-tensor fields in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 8
.

[
11
, Theorem 1]

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{1}(n))
then
X^{1}f=0
if and only if
f=\mathrm{d}^{2}v
for some smooth function

such that

as
\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty
.

In this section, we will utilize similar ideas as in Section
3
to prove the kernel description of the integral transform
X^{2}
when
n=2
. To this end, we recall some differential operators from
[
11
]
that will be used repeatedly in the discussion that follows.

Recall (from Section
2
), the operator
\displaystyle H:\mathcal{S}(E^{1}(n))\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(n))
and its formal
L^{2}
-adjoint
H^{*}:\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(n))\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}(E^{1}(n))
are defined explicitly in coordinates as follows:

\displaystyle(Hv)_{ijk\ell}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}v_{k\ell}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{\ell}}v_{ij}\right)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(H^{*}w)_{ij}:=\sum_{k,\ell}\frac{\partial^{2}w_{ikj\ell}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{\ell}}.

And their corresponding operators on the Fourier side are given by (for any non-zero
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
)

\displaystyle(\widehat{H}_{y}v)_{ijk\ell}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(y_{i}y_{j}v_{k\ell}+y_{k}y_{\ell}v_{ij}\right)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(\widehat{H}^{*}_{y}w)_{ij}:=\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k}y_{\ell}w_{ikj\ell}.

We will also need one more first-order differential operator
K:\mathcal{S}(S^{1})\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(n))
defined below:

\displaystyle Kv

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon\bigl(\mathrm{d}v\otimes I\bigr),\qquad\mbox{ here }\ I_{ij}=\delta_{ij}

(15)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad(Ku)_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial x_{\ell}}+\frac{\partial u_{\ell}}{\partial x_{k}}\right)\delta_{ij}.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(16)

The formal
L^{2}
adjoint of
K
,
K^{*}:\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(n))\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}(S^{1})
is given by

\displaystyle(K^{*}w)_{i}:=-\sum_{k,j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}w_{ijkk}.

(17)

Again, on the Fourier side, these operators take the following form:

(\widehat{K}_{y}u)_{ijk\ell}=\frac{1}{4}(y_{i}u_{j}+y_{j}u_{i})\delta_{k\ell}+\frac{1}{4}(y_{k}u_{\ell}+u_{\ell}v_{k})\delta_{ij}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}w)_{i}:=\sum_{k,j}y_{j}w_{ijkk}.

(18)

We aim to prove a decomposition of an elastic tensor field
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(n))
of the following form:

f(x)=Hv(x)+Ku(x)+g(x),\quad K^{*}g(x)=H^{*}g(x)=0,\mbox{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In the above decomposition,
v\in C^{\infty}(E^{1}(n))
,
u\in C^{\infty}(S^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
, and
g\in C^{\infty}(E^{2}(n))
. We first show that such a decomposition is possible pointwise by going to the Fourier domain; then, the idea is to use the Fourier inversion to obtain the required decomposition. To obtain the pointwise decomposition, we start with fixing a
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and for this fixed
y
all the operators defined above on the Fourier side (
\widehat{H}_{y}
,
\widehat{K}_{y}
,
\widehat{H}_{y}^{*}
, and
\widehat{K}_{y}^{*}
) can be treated as linear operators between the various spaces of tensors.

The following lemma is presented in
[
11
, Lemma 10]
. Here we give a different proof.

Lemma 9

(Decomposition of elastic
2
-tensors)
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Then the space of elastic
2
-tensors admits the following (interior) direct sum decomposition

E^{2}(n)=A_{y}\oplus B_{y}\oplus C_{y},

where

\displaystyle A_{y}

\displaystyle:=\{\widehat{K}_{y}u:u\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;u\perp y\}

\displaystyle B_{y}

\displaystyle:=\{\widehat{H}_{y}v:v\in E^{1}(n)\}

\displaystyle C_{y}

\displaystyle:=\{g\in E^{2}(n):\widehat{K}_{y}^{*}g=\widehat{H}_{y}^{*}g=0\}.

Moreover, the projections onto the subspaces
A_{p}
,
B_{p}
, and
C_{p}
depend

0
-homogeneously on
y
.

Proof.

The aim is to prove that any elastic
2
-tensor
f\in E^{2}(n)
has the following decomposition:

f=\widehat{H}_{y}v+\widehat{K}_{y}u+g,\qquad\widehat{H}_{y}^{*}g=\widehat{K}_{y}^{*}g=0,\quad u\perp y.

(19)

The main idea to achieve this goal is to derive the expressions of
u
,
v
, and
g
in terms of the given elastic tensor
f
. Assume equation (
19
) is true, applying
\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}
to equation (
19
) and using
\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}g=0
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}f=\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}\widehat{H}_{y}v+\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}\widehat{K}_{y}u.

(20)

A direct computation, along with the fact that
u\perp y=0
, entails

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}\widehat{K}_{y}u\right)_{i}=\frac{n}{4}\lvert y\rvert^{2}\,u_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}\widehat{H}_{y}v\right)_{i}=\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{2}}{2}y_{i}\sum_{k}(v_{kk}+y_{k}v_{ik}).

(21)

This further gives

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}f\right)_{i}=\frac{n}{4}\lvert y\rvert^{2}\,u_{i}+\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{2}}{2}y_{i}\sum_{k}(v_{kk}+y_{k}v_{ik}).

(22)

Next, we apply
\widehat{H}^{*}_{y}
to equation (
19

\displaystyle\widehat{H}^{*}_{y}f

\displaystyle=\widehat{H}^{*}_{y}\left(\widehat{H}_{y}v+\widehat{K}_{y}u\right)

\displaystyle\implies\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{2}}{4}(y_{i}u_{j}+y_{j}u_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\left(y_{i}y_{j}\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k}y_{\ell}v_{k\ell}+\lvert y\rvert^{4}v_{ij}\right).\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(23)

Using
u\perp y
in equation (
5
), we also deduce

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\lvert y\rvert^{4}\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k}y_{\ell}v_{k\ell}

\displaystyle\implies\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{k,\ell}y_{k}y_{\ell}v_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert y\rvert^{4}}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad

(24)

Next multiplying
y_{i}
to the equation (
22

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i}y_{i}\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}f\right)_{i}=\lvert y\rvert^{4}\sum_{k}v_{kk}+\lvert y\rvert^{2}\sum_{i,j}y_{i}y_{j}v_{ij}.

(25)

Consequently inserting the value of

from (
5

\displaystyle\sum_{k}v_{kk}=\frac{1}{\lvert y\rvert^{4}}\left(2\sum_{i}y_{i}\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}f\right)_{i}-\frac{1}{\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}\right).

(26)

Inserting this into equation (

\displaystyle\frac{n}{4}\lvert y\rvert^{2}\,u_{i}+\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{2}}{2}y_{j}v_{ij}=\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}f\right)_{i}-\frac{y_{i}}{2\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\left(2\sum_{i}y_{i}\left(\widehat{K}^{*}_{y}f\right)_{i}-\frac{1}{\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}\right).

(27)

Similarly from (
5

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k,\ell}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{4}}{4}u_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left(y_{i}y_{k}y_{\ell}\lvert y\rvert^{2}v_{k\ell}+\lvert y\rvert^{4}y_{j}v_{ij}\right).

(28)

This along with equation (

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{4}}{4}u_{i}+\frac{\lvert y\rvert^{4}}{2}y_{j}v_{ij}=\sum_{j,k,\ell}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}-\frac{y_{i}}{2\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,\ell}y_{i}y_{j}y_{k}y_{\ell}f_{ijk\ell}.

Next, we perform

.

And the components

), and (

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{v}_{ij}(y)\rvert\leq C\lvert y\rvert^{-2}\quad\mbox{for $\lvert y\rvert\leq 1$}.

On the other hand,

is rapidly decreasing. Therefore, by taking the inverse Fourier transform, we find the elastic vector field
u
required for the decomposition. This
u
is smooth, since
\hat{u}
is rapidly decreasing. The same line of reasoning also proves smoothness of
g
and
v
. However,
\widehat{v}_{ij}(y)
is not integrable near the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
due to the estimate (
34
). But, this estimate improves if
f
has mean zero
i.e.,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f=\widehat{f}(0)=0
, which implies the integrability of
\widehat{v}
and the existence of
v
(again by Fourier inversion) needed for the decomposition (see the proof of Theorem
3
for the argument). Once, we have determined
u
and
v
then
g
can be defined as

g(x)=f(x)-\left(Hv(x)+Ku(x)\right).

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

With this decomposition result at hand, we state the main result of this section, which is about the kernel description of
X^{2}
. In dimensions
n\geq 3
, this result was proved in
[
11
]
; however, the exact kernel description of
X^{2}
was not proven in
[
11
]
because their proof of the generalized trace-free solenoidal potential decomposition is not applicable for
n=2
. It is possible to prove the decomposition result under an additional mean zero condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{ijk\ell}\,\mathrm{d}x=0
when
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(2))
.

Theorem 11
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(2))
, we have the following characterization of the kernel of the transform
X^{2}
:

X^{2}f=0\iff f=Hv+Ku,\quad\mbox{for some $v\in C^{\infty}(E^{1}(2))$ and $u\in\mathcal{S}(S^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$}

with the property that
v
and partial derivatives of
v
and
u
tends to
0
as
\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose that

f=Hv+Ku,

where
v
and
u
, together with all partial derivatives of
v
, tend to zero as
\lvert x\rvert\to\infty
.

Now applying
X^{2}
on the above expansion of
f
and using integration by parts (along with decay of
v
and
w
at infinity), we get

X^{2}f=0.

Conversely, assume that
X^{2}f=0
. By the Fourier slice theorem, this implies that

f
has mean zero; see
[
11
, Lemma 7]
. Since
f
has
mean zero, it admits the decomposition

f=g+Hv+Ku,\qquad\text{where }K^{*}g=H^{*}g=0.

Applying
X^{2}
to this decomposition gives
X^{2}f=X^{2}g=0.

Together with the conditions
K^{*}g=H^{*}g=0
, this implies that
g=0
.
∎

Before stating our next results, we need to define a new notation for a specific collection of elastics 2-tensors with certain properties. We denote
\mathcal{A}_{2}
to be the subset of
E^{2}(n)
satisfying the following properties:

1.

\displaystyle f\in C^{\infty}(E^{2}(n))\cap L^{2}(E^{2}(n))

2.

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{1-n}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

3.

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(y)
is smooth away from
y=0
and rapidly decreasing.

Please note
\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(n))\subset\mathcal{A}_{2}
and therefore
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is dense in
L^{2}(E^{2}(n))
. Further, we also define the space
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{H}
to be the set of smooth elastic 1-tensor fields
v
such that
Hv\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
and the space
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{K}
to be the set of vector fields
u
satisfying
Ku\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
. For
n\geq 3
, the following Helmholtz-type decomposition result was proved in
[
11
, Lemma 12]
:

Lemma 12

(Helmholtz-type decomposition for elastic
2
-tensors)
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Then for any elastic
2
-tensor field
f\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
there exist unique tensor fields
h,g\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
satisfying

f(x)=h(x)+g(x),\quad\mbox{ with }\quad H^{*}g(x)=0,\quad K^{*}g(x)=0.

Further, the potential part
h
can be further decomposed as
h(x)=Ku(x)+Hv(x)
for some vector field
u\in\mathcal{A}_{0}^{K}
and some elastic
1
-tensor field
v\in\mathcal{A}_{1}^{H}
. Moreover,
u
and
v
satisfies the following decay estimates

\lvert u(x)\rvert\leq C(1+\lvert x\rvert)^{1-n},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lvert v(x)\rvert\leq C(1+\lvert x\rvert)^{2-n}.

We now state the result in two dimensions for any elastic 2-tensor fields with the property that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{ijk\ell}\mathrm{d}x=0
for
1\leq i,j,k,\ell\leq 2
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{2}(2))
be an elastic 2-tensor field satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{ijk\ell}\mathrm{d}x=0
. Then there exist unique tensor fields
h,g\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
satisfying
f
satisfying

f(x)=h(x)+g(x),\quad\mbox{ with }\quad H^{*}g(x)=0,\quad K^{*}g(x)=0.

Further, the potential part
h
can be further decomposed as
h=Ku+Hv

for a vector field
u\in\mathcal{A}_{0}^{K}
and an elastic
1
-tensor field
v\in\mathcal{A}_{1}^{H}
.

6
Connections between two types of transform

In this section, we give a relation between the integral moment transform
\{I^{0},I^{1}\}
and elastic ray transforms
X^{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We show that the kernel of
\{I^{0},I^{1}\}
is exactly the same as the kernel of
X^{1}
. Recall that

\displaystyle X^{1}f(x,\xi,\zeta):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\xi_{i}\zeta_{j}\quad\mbox{for $f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{1})$ and $\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\xi\cup\xi^{\perp}$}.

(35)

Since
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{1})=\mathcal{S}(S^{2})
, using this identification we write the operator
X^{1}
as
X^{1}f=(I^{0}f,Mf),
where
Mf
is the mixed ray transform which is given by

\displaystyle Mf(x,\xi,\zeta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\xi_{i}\zeta_{j}\quad\zeta\perp\xi.

(36)

Let
\eta\neq 0
then we choose
\zeta=P_{\xi}\eta=\eta-\langle\xi,\eta\rangle\xi\in\xi^{\perp}
for unit vectors
\xi
. Then
Mf
is given by

\displaystyle Mf(x,\xi,\eta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\xi_{i}(P_{\xi}\eta)_{j}.

(37)

With this definition at hand, we have the following result.

Proposition 14
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
(recall, this is the same as the space
\mathcal{S}(E^{1}(n)))
then we have
\ker{\{I^{0}f,I^{1}f\}}=\ker{X^{1}f}
.

Proof.

Recall the operators
J^{0}f,J^{1}f
(defined in equation (
12
)), which are extensions of
I^{0}f
,
I^{1}f
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, respectively. It is easy to drive the following relation satisfied
J^{0}f,J^{1}f
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi_{j}}J^{0}f(x,\xi)-\partial_{x_{j}}J^{1}f(x,\xi)=2J^{0}f_{j}(x,\xi)=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\,\xi_{i}\mathrm{d}t.

(38)

Let
\eta\in\xi^{\perp}
, then multiplying the above relation by
\eta_{j}
and sum it over
j
to get

\displaystyle\eta_{j}\left(\partial_{\xi_{j}}J^{0}f(x,\xi)-\partial_{x_{j}}J^{1}f(x,\xi)\right)=2\,Mf(x,\xi),

(39)

where
\displaystyle Mf(x,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\,\xi_{i}\eta_{j}\,\mathrm{d}t
is the
mixed ray transform
of
f
.
Thus, given
I^{0}f,I^{1}f
, we can recover the information of
X^{1}f
.

Conversely, assume that we have given the data
X^{1}f
, then taking
\eta
derivative of
Mf
we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta_{k}}Mf(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\xi_{i}\epsilon_{jk}(\xi)\mathrm{d}t

(40)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ik}(x+t\xi)\xi_{i}\mathrm{d}t-\lvert\xi\rvert^{-2}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{ij}(x+t\xi)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\mathrm{d}t.

(41)

Simplifying above and using the relation (
38
) we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta_{k}}Mf(x,\xi)+\frac{\xi_{k}}{\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}}J^{0}f(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\xi_{k}}J^{0}f(x,\xi)-\partial_{x_{k}}J^{1}f(x,\xi)\right)

(42)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{k}}J^{1}f(x,\xi)=\partial_{\xi_{k}}J^{0}f(x,\xi)-2\left(\partial_{\eta_{k}}Mf(x,\xi)+\frac{\xi_{k}}{\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}}J^{0}f(x,\xi)\right).

(43)

As a result we recover
J^{1}f
from the data
X^{1}f
.
∎

As a corollary, we have the following result.

Corollary 15
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}(E^{1})
, then the following conditions are equivalent

1.

X^{1}f=0
.

2.

f=\mathrm{d}^{2}\phi
for some
\phi\in C^{\infty}
such
\phi
and
\partial_{x_{i}}\phi
converges to as
\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty
.

3.

The generalized Saint-Venant operator
\mathcal{R}^{1}f=0
, where
\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}^{1}f)_{ijk}=\sigma(j,k)\left\{\frac{\partial f_{ij}}{\partial x_{k}}-\frac{\partial f_{ik}}{\partial x_{j}}\right\}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
14
we have that
X^{1}f=0\iff I^{0}f=I^{1}f=0
. Hence, the result follows from Theorem
6
for
n=2
and from
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
15
, Theorem 4.3]
for
n\geq 3
.
∎
