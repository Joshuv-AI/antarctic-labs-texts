Meaning of home among community-dwelling older adults with health concerns: a reflexive thematic analysis
Authors: Kazuma Haida, Yuria Yamasaki, Nanami Sugawara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHome is more than a place of care delivery, yet little is known about how community-dwelling older adults with health concerns in Japan understand its meaning. This study explored the shared meanings of home among them and derived implications for home care.MethodsThis qualitative study involved reflexive thematic analysis within a non-positivist framework. Fifty-four adults aged 65–95 years living in urban, suburban, and rural areas were purposively recruited through home-visit nursing services and an online survey panel. Semi-structured interviews were conducted in Japanese. Interview data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed iteratively in Japanese.FindingsFour themes were generated: “autonomy and independence,” “residential environment that sustains daily life,” “familiar place with attachment,” and “personally meaningful connections with others.” Home was understood as more than a physical residence: a familiar, cherished, and personally meaningful foundation for sustaining a self-determined and independent life, personal identity, and relationships with others, extending beyond the dwelling to the surrounding community.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that home care professionals should understand what home means as a lived place to each older adult, support independence and safety while respecting autonomy, familiarity, and preferences, and facilitate meaningful connections at their preferred relational distance.

Introduction

Numerous older adults worldwide wish to continue living in their homes as they age (de Jong et al., 2022). When long-term care becomes necessary, home care is often preferred to support aging in place (de Jong et al., 2022), and this tendency is also seen in Japan (Cabinet Office Japan, 2018). However, unlike hospitals and institutional settings, homes are not primarily designed for treatment or care. Rather, home is more than a physical structure; it is a place imbued with personal meanings (Milligan, 2016). Therefore, introducing home care may turn private homes into public arenas and disrupt older adults’ daily lives (Jarling et al., 2018). As such, home care professionals need to understand that home is a personally meaningful place for older adults, not merely a context for care delivery.

The meaning of home has long been the subject of interdisciplinary enquiry in diverse fields, including philosophy, anthropology, gerontology, and nursing. Reviews and syntheses of this body of knowledge indicate that home is a multidimensional and dynamic phenomenon associated with identity, independence and autonomy, emotional attachment, a sense of safety and security, meaningful activities, and connections with others and the surrounding community (Aclan et al., 2023; Mallett, 2004; Molony, 2010). Of particular importance in understanding home in later life is the interaction between people and their physical and social environments. In environmental gerontology, an integrative conceptual framework has been proposed to provide a comprehensive account of person–environment exchange. Within this framework, agency and belonging are conceptualised as interrelated person–environment processes that influence identity and autonomy as interdependent outcomes (Chaudhury & Oswald, 2019). A more recent study focused on the meaning of home and older adults experiencing health challenges also highlighted the importance of independence and autonomy in daily life, self-identity supported by attachment rooted in memories and cherished possessions, and the continuation of personally meaningful activities (Hugøy et al., 2025). Furthermore, the surrounding community is important in shaping the meaning of home (Ecarnot et al., 2022; Kylén et al., 2019). A previous study with older adults, including nursing home residents, reported that the continuation of their longstanding occupations contributed to a sense of being at home, whether they lived in institutional settings or private residences (Sahraoui et al., 2026). This evidence highlights that the meaning of home does not arise simply from living in a private residence, underscoring the importance of exploring what homes mean to older adults. From an environmental gerontology perspective, well-being has been conceptualised, alongside autonomy and identity, as an outcome of person–environment interchange processes (Wahl et al., 2012). Consistent with this perspective, empirical studies have found that the perceived meaning of home is associated with older adults’ well-being (Kylén et al., 2017; Oswald et al., 2007). The meaning of home may also support active aging, conceptualised as the process through which people strive to maintain well-being as they age (Slaug et al., 2024). Beyond its relevance to well-being, the home has been described as an important source of support for older adults’ health (Fänge & Dahlin-Ivanoff, 2009; Hugøy et al., 2025). Thus, home care that respects the meanings that older adults attach to their homes may support not only daily life but also well-being and health in later life.

Although the meaning of home among older adults has been explored internationally, few studies have been conducted in Asian countries. A qualitative meta-synthesis of 46 studies published between 2006 and 2022 included only five studies involving participants in Asian countries and none involving participants in Japan (Aclan et al., 2023). Similarly, a recent bibliometric analysis found that research on the meaning of home has been concentrated in North America and Europe (Olfat, 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that evidence on how older adults in Japan understand the meaning of home remains limited. Within this understudied population, community-dwelling older adults with health concerns warrant particular attention because some may already be receiving home care, whereas others may require it in the future. Given that home care may adversely affect the meaning of home (Jarling et al., 2018), understanding the meanings these older adults attach to home is important for providing care that respects these meanings. Accordingly, this study aimed to explore the shared meanings of home among community-dwelling older adults with health concerns in Japan and derive corresponding implications for home care.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a non-positivist qualitative approach and employed reflexive thematic analysis, which recognises the researcher’s active role in the analysis and emphasises the value of information-rich datasets rather than the attainment of theoretical saturation (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Reflexive thematic analysis was particularly appropriate given the study’s exploratory nature. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR; O’Brien et al., 2014).

Participants and recruitment

A purposive sampling design was employed. To include older adults with diverse health conditions and living circumstances, participants were recruited in Japan via two routes: home-visit nursing services and an online survey panel provider. The inclusion criteria were being a community-dwelling older adult aged 65 years or older and having health concerns. Older adults living in residential care facilities were excluded. However, no restrictions were placed on housing type (detached or multi-unit housing), housing tenure (owner-occupied or rental housing), or living arrangement (living alone or with others).

Using personal professional networks, the first author contacted the administrators of three home-visit nursing services and asked them to arrange for the study to be introduced to clients who met the eligibility criteria. The clients received a brief overview of the study from their home-visit nurses, who emphasised that participation was entirely voluntary. The first author was present during nursing visits to clients who expressed an interest in the study and provided them with a formal explanation of the study. It was re-emphasised that participation or non-participation would not affect their future use of home-visit nursing services. Additionally, the first author asked an online survey panel provider to disseminate a brief overview of the study to registrants who met the eligibility criteria and identify registrants who expressed interest in the study and permitted the first author to contact them. The first author contacted the identified registrants and provided a formal explanation of the study via an online meeting platform. These recruitment activities resulted in 55 individuals providing consent to participate. One individual was excluded because a change in their physical and mental condition made it difficult to complete the interview safely. The final analytic sample therefore included 54 participants: nine recruited through home-visit nursing services and 45 recruited through the online survey panel provider. The first author had no prior relationship with the participants before recruitment.

Sample adequacy was considered based on the concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2016). As this study sought to identify shared meanings across multiple participants rather than conducting an in-depth analysis of individual cases, it addressed a relatively broad phenomenon. Although the participants constituted a target group relevant to the study aim, their meanings of home were diverse and shaped by their personal histories, health conditions, and living environments. Additionally, research on the meaning of home among community-dwelling older adults in Japan is limited. Therefore, a relatively large sample was needed to obtain sufficient information power. Accordingly, the final sample of 54 participants was considered appropriate for addressing the study aim.

Data collection

The first author conducted a semi-structured interview in Japanese with each participant between September 2024 and September 2025. Most interviews were conducted via an online meeting platform. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim. Interviews with participants recruited through home-visit nursing services were conducted face-to-face in their homes because of their limited familiarity with internet-based technologies. Interviews lasted 12–47 min, with a mean duration of 25.3 min. Interview duration varied because participants differed in how explicitly they articulated the meaning of home.

At the beginning of each interview, basic information was collected, including age, sex, living arrangement, mobility status, use of home care services, health concerns, housing type, and type of residential area. The interview then focused on participants’ meanings of home. To facilitate participants’ narratives about the meaning of home, the first author used questions such as “What makes your home feel like home to you?”, “What is important to you in your life at home?”, and “What do you appreciate about your current home?” These questions were used flexibly according to the flow of the participants’ narratives, and the first author proceeded with the interview by identifying questions that helped participants speak more easily about their own meanings of home. Further, participants were asked whether and how the neighbourhood and surrounding environment contributed to their meaning of home.

Data analysis

The first author led the analysis, conducted following the six phases of reflexive thematic analysis: “familiarising with the dataset”; “coding”; “generating initial themes”; “developing and reviewing themes”; “refining, defining, and naming themes”; and “writing up” (Braun & Clarke, 2022). First, the first author engaged deeply with the data by repeatedly reading the verbatim transcripts to thoroughly comprehend the dataset. Second, text segments related to the meaning of home were identified while preserving their contextual integrity. The identified text segments served as the basic units of analysis and were coded by assigning labels that concisely captured their meanings. The codes were generated at a range of levels, from very explicit to more implicit meanings, while reflecting the researcher’s interpretive engagement with the data. Third, the codes were synthesised into subthemes and themes through the researcher’s active engagement in discerning broader, shared meanings across the dataset. Through repeated review, the subthemes and themes were refined and their final names were assigned. Finally, in writing up the findings, text segments that exemplified each subtheme and theme were selected, and interpretive accounts were developed.

The first author adopted two strategies to enhance analytical quality and rigour. First, before beginning the analysis, the first author engaged in and documented reflexive reflection to critically examine subjectivity. Specifically, the first author reflected on their personal values related to the study topic and their nursing practice and research experience. The first author had clinical nursing experience in hospital and home care settings and had long been engaged with this study topic. Therefore, the first author had a prior understanding that older adults’ expressions of wanting to “remain at home” may carry meanings rooted in their personal histories and values. Because the first author had experience with multiple qualitative analytical approaches, the first author attended to the methodological characteristics of reflexive thematic analysis throughout the analytical process to avoid conflating it with other approaches. Reflexive reflection continued throughout the analytical process and remained an ongoing consideration. Second, through discussions with the co-authors, the first author examined whether the identified text segments were relevant to the research question, the labels clearly and concisely captured the content of the codes, the boundaries among subthemes and among themes were clear, and the names of the subthemes and themes clearly represented the shared meanings across the codes they comprised. The analysis was conducted manually and iteratively, without the use of qualitative data analysis software; Microsoft Excel was used only to organise the data. The analysis was conducted using the Japanese verbatim transcripts, and only the portions required for reporting the findings were translated into English. The translation was led by the first author, with careful attention paid to preserving the nuances of the original text, and was checked and discussed by the co-authors.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Graduate School of Medicine, Nagoya University (Approval No. 2024-0057-3). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before data collection, the first author explained the study purpose and procedures to all participants both orally and in writing and obtained written informed consent from all participants. Participation was entirely voluntary. All collected information was kept confidential and de-identified to protect participants’ privacy.

Findings

The participants were 54 older adults with a mean age of 73.3 years (range: 65–95 years). Of the participants, 33 were men and 21 were women. Regarding walking ability, 51 participants were able to walk independently, and three used walking aids. The most common health concerns were musculoskeletal, followed by cardiovascular and cognitive concerns. Sixteen participants lived alone and 38 lived with others. Regarding housing type, 36 participants lived in detached houses and 18 in multi-unit housing; 48 lived in owner-occupied housing and six in rental housing. Regarding residential area, 19 participants lived in urban areas, 29 in suburban areas, and six in rural areas. The participant characteristics are presented in Table I. The analysis generated eight subthemes, which were organised into four themes: “autonomy and independence,” “residential environment that sustains daily life,” “familiar place with attachment,” and “personally meaningful connections with others.” An overview of the themes and subthemes is presented in Table II.

Autonomy and independence

Living on one’s own terms without interference from others

As illustrated by one participant’s statement, “I think being able to do whatever I want—to have the freedom to do anything—is something unique to being at home” (ID24, female, late 60 s), participants commonly construed freedom as one of the meanings they attached to home. Specifically, they valued being able to determine their own daily routines and lifestyles, engage freely in hobbies, and arrange their living spaces according to their preferences. When health concerns had a more evident impact on daily life, the importance of freedom within the home was particularly emphasised:

Because of my physical condition, it is difficult for me to go out, enjoy different things, or experience much excitement. So, I feel that being at home gives me the only real freedom I have. (ID25, male, late 60 s)

Participants’ narratives of freedom were also associated with not being directed, constrained, or controlled by others, reflecting an underlying desire to avoid unwanted interference in their daily lives:

By nature, I have never liked people interfering with me or telling me what to do. It is not just that I dislike it—I tend to resist it. You could say I am not the kind of person suited to working for a large corporation. I still help out with some work, but it is basically like being self-employed. More than anything, I think freedom is what matters most. Even without much money, I feel that as long as I have freedom, I can manage somehow. (ID35, male, early 70 s)

As another participant explained, “I think the best thing is being able to do things at my own pace. It protects my privacy—or perhaps it is like a kind of shelter” (ID49, male, late 70 s). Thus, the freedom described by participants was also closely related to privacy. In particular, participants who lived with a partner or another person commonly emphasised the importance of having separate private rooms, which allowed each person to spend time without interfering with the other.

Maintaining independence without burdening others

Based on participants’ narratives, independence was interpreted as one of the meanings they attributed to home.

As I’ve mentioned several times, I would say that what I really want is to remain independent. I probably have about 10 more years of healthy life expectancy, so during that time, I want to devote myself to doing what I truly want to do. (ID42, male, mid-60s)

Although the participants wished to remain in their homes until the end of their lives, their narratives were also interpreted as expressing concerns about losing the ability to walk independently and placing a caregiving burden on their families:

I would like to stay at home for as long as possible. However, I do not know how I would feel if I reached the point where I caused trouble for my family, especially my children, or required them to take care of me. (ID50, male, late 60 s)

The participants’ attention to their physical and mental condition, including exercise and cognitive training, was interpreted as reflecting efforts to sustain their independence in the face of these concerns. Participants’ narratives about home care were interpreted as conveying a willingness to accept selective support focused on concrete difficulties in daily life, such as concerns about their own health or the caregiving burden on family members.

Residential environment that sustains daily life

Living in a safe and secure residential environment

Participants’ narratives were interpreted as positioning home not as a temporary place to stay but as a place in which daily life could be sustained over time. Within this meaning, both safety and security were emphasised. Regarding the safety of the dwelling, participants’ narratives conveyed concerns about the risk of fires caused by gas stoves. One participant with concerns regarding her vision reflected on an experience that prompted her to replace her gas stove with an induction cooktop:

Because the flame was under bright light, I did not notice at all that it was still burning. When I came home and realised that the flame had been left on, I was so terrified that I nearly collapsed. (ID13, female, early 70 s)

Participants’ narratives were also interpreted as emphasising the importance of the capacity of a dwelling to withstand natural disasters as part of the meaning of a safe home. When asked whether experiencing a major earthquake had changed what home meant to him, one participant responded:

On the contrary, I felt fortunate that my home withstood such a major earthquake. While the buildings around it were completely destroyed, my home sustained only partial damage. (ID33, male, late 60 s)

Security from crime was also interpreted as an important aspect of the meaning participants attached to home. Participants’ narratives commonly emphasised living in neighbourhoods with little crime. In particular, those who chose multi-unit housing as their home referred to security features such as automatic entrance locks, surveillance cameras, and on-site building managers. These security features were interpreted as becoming increasingly important as participants grew older. At the same time, the repeated entry of various people into participants’ homes, including home care professionals, was interpreted as potentially diminishing the meaning of home by undermining participants’ sense of security.

Living in a convenient and tranquil neighbourhood

Participants’ narratives were interpreted as positioning home as a place where they could continue living over time. Within this meaning, they valued a neighbourhood that combined convenience with the tranquility provided by the natural environment. Therefore, the area surrounding the dwelling was also interpreted as part of the meaning of home.

Rather than having a special sense that this house is my home, it is really the surrounding environment. Being close to the sea, enjoying the sea breeze, and seeing the scenery—such as the changing colours of the rice fields—are all things I really appreciate. So, it is more about the surrounding environment than the dwelling itself. (ID32, female, late 80 s)

Participants commonly emphasised the importance of nearby shopping facilities, public transportation, and healthcare facilities when describing neighbourhood convenience.

As I get older, my legs inevitably become weaker, so I think it is important to have a supermarket nearby where I can do my daily shopping and to have a train station close by. I also think the most important thing is having healthcare facilities nearby. They do not have to be large hospitals, but it is important to have clinics within walking distance, such as those specialising in internal medicine, general surgery, and otolaryngology. (ID20, male, early 70 s)

As health concerns became more salient with age, being able to meet daily needs within walking distance and having healthcare facilities nearby were interpreted as particularly important aspects of the meaning of home.

Familiar place with attachment

Feeling at ease in a familiar place

Participants’ narratives were interpreted as positioning a sense of familiarity developed through long-term living experience as part of the meaning of home.

I have lived here for almost 40 years, so perhaps feeling at ease is the expression that fits best. I enjoy travelling, but home is still the best. No matter what happens, sleeping at home in my own room is where I feel most at ease and can sleep most peacefully. (ID10, female, mid-60s)

This sense of familiarity was interpreted as developing through becoming accustomed to the everyday living environment, including the sounds, smells, scenery, spaces, and objects within the dwelling and surrounding neighbourhood. This familiarity was also interpreted as fostering positive emotions among participants. They described these emotions in various ways, including a sense of ease and calm, relaxation, and the feeling that home was their own castle.

Feeling attached through personal history and preferences

Participants’ narratives were interpreted as positioning home as a place interwoven with their personal histories and preferences, toward which they felt a strong sense of attachment. One participant described the relationship between personal history and attachment as follows:

This is where I was born and raised. My parents, my older sister, and I all lived here together, so I feel a very strong attachment to this place and to the land itself. (ID50, male, late 60 s)

Personal history was also expressed through specific memories associated with family photographs, items from their children’s childhood, marks their children had left on the dwelling, and familiar local scenery.

Attachment to home was interpreted as being associated not only with personal history but also with personal preferences. One participant described a need to prioritise her personal preferences over addressing the mismatch between the dwelling space and her physical functioning, as follows:

We designed the interior around what we both really love, so my husband and I feel deeply attached to our home. I have felt especially attached to it since we renovated it. This is a three-story dwelling, so there are many stairs. It is certainly difficult for me to get around, especially given the current condition of my legs. Even so, I still do not want to leave this place. (ID46, female, early 70 s)

Thus, personal preferences that fostered attachment were grounded in having a living space arranged according to one’s own tastes. Participants also emphasised the importance of being surrounded by cherished possessions. At the same time, their narratives were interpreted as reflecting conflict concerning these cherished possessions. Participants felt caught between the perceived need to declutter as part of preparing for the end of life and their desire to retain possessions that remained personally meaningful.

Personally meaningful connections with others

Living with others at a comfortable distance

Participants’ narratives were interpreted as positioning connections with others, including family members and neighbours, as one aspect of the meaning of home. One participant described connections with others as a fundamental condition of home and continued:

For example, it would feel completely different to come back to a place where no one was there and I did not know anyone nearby. At present, my wife lives with me, my daughter lives next door, and my grandchildren are also there. I think coming home to them feels entirely different. (ID27, male, early 70 s)

Another participant stated, “I suppose my way of thinking is that this is my home base, and what matters is how I connect with the outside world from here” (ID14, male, mid-60s). Home was therefore interpreted as functioning as a base from which participants maintained their connections with others. The importance of connections with neighbours was commonly emphasised across participants and was expressed particularly vividly by one participant living in a rural area:

Neighbours really matter. We’ve got to keep talking and work together, because we can’t get by all by ourselves. Otherwise, we just can’t manage. I’m not going anywhere. I know that for sure. (Interviewer: Is that because the people around you are good people?) That’s right. The neighbours are all good people. (ID8, male, mid-80s)

However, not every form of connection was considered desirable. Participants sought relationships that maintained a degree of closeness they experienced as appropriate and comfortable. One participant living alone reflected on having previously lived with her sister’s family but moving out because they did not get along, and continued:

I think living alone suits me better. But sometimes I still feel as though my sister might suddenly appear from somewhere nearby. I suppose those feelings are partly mixed with my memories of when we lived together. So I think the way things are now is probably best—just seeing her from time to time, maybe once every other day. (ID6, female, mid-70s)

Supporting one another through roles

Participants’ narratives were interpreted as positioning mutual support within the family and community as part of the meaning of home.

All of our relatives live nearby—or really, in the same area—so I just can’t imagine leaving. If my wife’s parents become unwell, we can get to them right away, and if anything happens to us, my sister can come over and help us straight away. With everyone close by like this, I’d find it really hard to leave this community. (ID48, male, late 60 s)

One participant also described a role within the family as part of the meaning of home:

(Interviewer: So, from what you’ve said, do you feel you need to maintain the home as a place where your family can return and gather?) In the end, I think that is probably what matters most about a home. Whenever we hear that one of our children who lives far away is coming home, we let the other one know. Then the whole family gets together for a meal. That has become a regular tradition in our home. (ID37, female, late 70 s)

Roles within the family took various forms, including maintaining a place where family members could gather, working to sustain the home, caring for family graves, looking after pets, and performing household tasks. Participants’ health concerns were interpreted as heightening the importance of support from neighbours as part of the meaning of home.

There are many kind people around here, and they really help me out. A vegetable vendor comes by every Friday in a small truck, and five or six neighbours usually come to shop. When I’m not feeling well, I just give one of them a note with what I need, and they bring it to me. (ID1, female, mid-90s)

Discussion

Main findings

This study generated four themes representing shared meanings of home among community-dwelling older adults with health concerns in Japan: “autonomy and independence,” “residential environment that sustains daily life,” “familiar place with attachment,” and “personally meaningful connections with others.” For the participants, home was understood not merely as a physical residence but as a foundation imbued with familiarity and personal attachment to sustain a self-determined and independent daily life and connect with others; this meaning also encompassed the characteristics of the surrounding community. The meanings identified included those related to the self—autonomy and independence, as well as familiarity and attachment rooted in personal histories and preferences; those related to the physical and neighbourhood environment—a functional dwelling and a convenient and tranquil neighbourhood; and those related to social relationships—connections with family and community members. These findings can also be situated within the perspective of person–environment exchange in environmental gerontology. In this study, living according to one’s own wishes and making use of or adapting aspects of the dwelling and neighbourhood environments as needed can be understood in relation to agency, whereas attachment to places imbued with personal histories and preferences, together with meaningful connections with family and community members, can be understood in relation to belonging (Chaudhury & Oswald, 2019). Thus, home may be understood as a context in which agency and belonging interact to support older adults’ autonomy and identity. The multidimensional meanings of home identified in this study are broadly consistent with those reported in international studies (Aclan et al., 2023; Ecarnot et al., 2022; Kylén et al., 2019; Mallett, 2004; Molony, 2010).

These similarities with international research suggest that the self-related, environmental, and social dimensions constituting the meaning of home may be relatively widely recognised among older populations with differing cultural backgrounds. However, the specific ways in which these broader meanings are expressed may reflect older adults’ social and environmental contexts. For example, the meaning of independence identified in this study reflects a desire to avoid burdening family members with care. Japanese respondents have been shown to be more concerned than Dutch respondents about becoming a burden on relatives at the end of life (Groenewoud et al., 2020). Therefore, this relational meaning of independence may reflect a concern that is particularly salient in Japan. Additionally, in this study, living in a dwelling capable of withstanding natural disasters was described as part of the meaning of home in relation to sustaining daily life. This may reflect Japan’s environmental context, characterised by recurrent exposure to diverse natural disasters (Cabinet Office Japan, 2024). Health status may also shape how these broader meanings of home are expressed. In this study, concerns about the future sustainability of living at home, adapting dwelling features to changes in physical functioning, and a local environment where daily needs can be met were prominent in participants’ narratives. Similar narratives have also been reported among healthy and independently living older adults (Kylén et al., 2019). However, the findings suggest that, for older adults who perceived health deterioration as a more immediate possibility, access to healthcare within walking distance, in addition to meeting everyday needs locally, became an important condition for sustaining life at home.

Overall, this study did not identify entirely new meanings of home among older adults. Rather, to our knowledge, this study provides the first empirical evidence that the meanings of home among community-dwelling older adults with health concerns in Japan are broadly consistent with the self-related, environmental, and social dimensions identified predominantly in Western research. At the same time, the findings demonstrate that the specific expression of these meanings is context-dependent, underscoring the need to explore the meaning of home in relation to each individual’s particular circumstances.

Implications for home care

The findings suggest the importance of balancing three considerations in home care: supporting older adults’ independence threatened by health concerns without diminishing their sense of autonomy; ensuring patient safety while preserving their personal preferences and familiarity with their living environment; and supporting meaningful connections while respecting older adults’ preferred relational distance. By grounding these three balances in older adults’ meanings of home, this study provides an integrated account of how home care may preserve each person’s home as a lived and personally meaningful place, rather than merely as a setting for care delivery.

First, the findings indicate that older adults’ meanings of home involve a need to live on their own terms without unwanted interference from others, while health concerns heighten their anxiety about maintaining independence. Previous studies exploring the experiences of older adults receiving home care have similarly highlighted the importance of independence and a sense of control over daily life (Jarling et al., 2018; Larsson Gerdin et al., 2025; Silverglow et al., 2021). Therefore, home care that respects older adults’ meanings of home may need to balance supporting them in managing health-related challenges in daily life with preserving their sense of autonomy.

Second, the findings indicate that older adults desire to remain in familiar spaces imbued with their personal preferences, even when these spaces present physical challenges. However, unlike hospitals and residential care facilities, the home is a less controlled environment and may contain various patient safety risks, such as limited space and clutter (Lang et al., 2015). A previous study has similarly shown that older adults may prioritise familiarity with their living environment over safety risks in the home (Lee et al., 2025). Therefore, home care that respects older adults’ meanings of home, particularly when environmental modifications are considered, may need to avoid the unilateral application of standardised safety measures and instead balance patient safety with older adults’ personal preferences and familiarity with their living environment.

Finally, the findings indicate that maintaining personally meaningful connections with others is an important aspect of older adults’ meanings of home. Social isolation and loneliness are associated with health risks (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015), and long-term reciprocal relationships between frailty, social isolation, and loneliness have also been reported (Mehrabi et al., 2024). Changes in health and functional status may therefore make it difficult for older adults to maintain established relationships with family members and people in their communities, suggesting a role for home care in supporting the continuation of these relationships. However, the participants did not simply seek more social contact; rather, they valued relationships that were personally meaningful and maintained at a comfortable distance without excessive interference. A previous study similarly showed that although preventing loneliness is important, time spent alone may contribute to well-being for some older adults (Axén et al., 2025). Therefore, home care that respects older adults’ meanings of home may need to support social connections without focusing solely on increasing the amount of social contact and to explore with older adults which relationships they value, what meanings they attach to those relationships, and the degree of involvement they prefer.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations concerning the transferability of the findings and the preservation of nuances in translation.

First, the findings should be interpreted within the sociocultural and housing context of community-dwelling older adults in Japan and may not be transferable to older adults in residential care facilities or other cultural contexts.

Second, recruitment relied on three home-visit nursing services and an online survey panel provider. Because the home-visit nursing services that assisted with recruitment were selected through the first author’s professional network, the older adults recruited through this route came from a limited number of geographical areas. The addition of the online survey panel partly mitigated this geographical limitation by enabling the recruitment of older adults living in different regions of Japan, including those who did not use home care services such as home-visit nursing. However, older adults with limited digital access or literacy may have been less likely to participate. Additionally, both recruitment routes relied on self-selection by older adults interested in the study. Thus, those with little interest in reflecting on the meaning of home may have been less likely to participate. Consequently, the transferability of the findings to older adults who neither use home care services nor have adequate digital access or literacy, as well as to those with little interest in reflecting on the meaning of home, may be limited.

Third, participant characteristics may limit the transferability of the findings concerning how meanings of home are expressed in specific contexts. Most participants were in their 60 s or 70 s and were independently mobile; most lived in owner-occupied housing; and only six lived in rural areas. Accordingly, the findings may not fully capture how these meanings are expressed among oldest-old adults, those with greater care needs, those living in rental housing, or those living in rural areas. Moreover, because the analysis focused on shared meanings across participants, it could not fully examine how the expression of these meanings varied according to participant characteristics such as living arrangement, housing tenure, health condition, use of home care services, and residential area. Future research should include more diverse participants and examine how these meanings are expressed across groups with different characteristics.

Fourth, interviews were conducted either face-to-face in participants’ homes or online. Differences in access to contextual and nonverbal cues, as well as in interactional dynamics, may have influenced how participants articulated the meanings of home. Because the interview setting corresponded to the recruitment route, the influence of setting could not be separated from differences in the characteristics of participants recruited through each route. These differences in interview setting, together with their correspondence to recruitment route, should therefore be considered when assessing the transferability of the findings.

Finally, although the translations were reviewed and discussed among the authors, they were not independently verified by a Japanese–English bilingual researcher. Some nuances of Japanese expressions related to home may therefore not have been fully conveyed in the English presentation of the findings.

Conclusions

This study generated four themes representing shared meanings of home among community-dwelling older adults with health concerns in Japan: “autonomy and independence,” “residential environment that sustains daily life,” “familiar place with attachment,” and “personally meaningful connections with others.” The findings indicate that home extends beyond the physical dwelling to encompass the surrounding neighbourhood and serves as a foundation for sustaining a self-determined and independent daily life, familiarity and attachment rooted in personal histories and preferences, and personally meaningful connections with others. The findings also provide empirical evidence that the self-related, environmental, and social dimensions identified predominantly in Western research are broadly reflected in the meanings of home expressed by older adults with health concerns in Japan, while their specific expression is shaped by individual health conditions and social and environmental contexts. Home care that respects older adults’ meanings of home should therefore support independence without undermining autonomy, ensure patient safety while preserving familiarity with and personal preferences for the living environment, and sustain meaningful connections while respecting each older adult’s preferred relational distance. Exploring what home means to each individual may help home care professionals preserve each person’s home as a lived and personally meaningful place rather than merely a setting for care delivery.
