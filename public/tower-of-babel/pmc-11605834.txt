Risk of long-term ophthalmological complications in Taiwanese preterm infants: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Chia-Yu Huang, Sio-Ian Tou, Heng-Jun Lin, Hung-Rong Yen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim was to determine whether different maturity levels and birth weights could change the risk of long-term ophthalmological complications in preterm infants.DesignThis was a cohort study.SettingThis was a nationwide population-based study.ParticipantsNewborns born between 2009 and 2012 were identified; newborns with congenital deformities, newborns with missing data on urban area, sex, gestational week at birth or birth weight and newborns who died before the age of 8 years were excluded.InterventionsInfants were divided into full-term, preterm and preterm with retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) cohorts based on their basic characteristics at birth.Primary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were ophthalmological complications, including strabismus, refractive error, amblyopia, cataracts and glaucoma.ResultsUltimately, 735 702 infants were included in the analysis. There were 727 403, 7165 and 1134 newborns in the full term, preterm without ROP and preterm with ROP cohorts, respectively. Premature infants, whether without ROP or with ROP, had an elevated risk of various ophthalmic complications, with adjusted ORs (aORs) ranging from 1.48 to 2.74 and 1.90 to 10.34, respectively. Extremely low birth weight contributes to an increased risk of various ophthalmic complications, with aORs ranging from 2.21 to 6.55. Moreover, a higher number of negative variables, such as preterm birth or low body weight, increased the risk of various ophthalmological complications, with the aOR falling within the range of 1.03–23.86.ConclusionsImmaturity and low body weight were risk factors for ophthalmological complications in preterm infants. The presence of both of these two risk factors increased the risk of ophthalmological complications. Our results are essential for caregivers and health policy-makers to design comprehensive follow-up plans for preterm infants.Keywords: Neonatology, Ophthalmology

Introduction

The definition of a preterm infant is an infant born earlier than 37 weeks gestation, and the incidence rate of preterm birth is increasing yearly worldwide: the incidence of preterm birth is 19.6%, 9.1%, 6.2%, 8.1% and 10.6% in Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America and North America, respectively.1 Many comorbidities are related to preterm birth, such as necrotising enterocolitis (NEC), intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH), bronchopulmonary dysplasia and retinopathy of prematurity (ROP).2 ROP has been noted as a risk factor for poor vision, and low birth weight (LBW), another characteristic of preterm infants, has also been found to have a relationship with ophthalmological complications.3 The presence of both risk factors (preterm birth and LBW) contributes to a higher risk of neurological abnormalities.4 The neurological and ocular systems are important components of visual system maturation.5 However, the influence of preterm birth and LBW on ocular complications is still not fully understood.

Considering cost-effectiveness, the early detection and treatment of visual complications from preterm birth are essential.6 Strabismus,7 refractive errors,8 amblyopia,9 cataracts10 and glaucoma11 are the major ophthalmological disorders in the preterm population; associations between these conditions and both LBW and ROP have been reported. The prevalence of strabismus, refractive errors, amblyopia and glaucoma in preterm infants is greater than that in full-term infants.12,16 Additionally, preterm individuals with ROP have a higher incidence of strabismus.12 LBW has also been found to be a risk factor for cataract development in preterm infants.17 Untreated strabismus can cause unclear vision and amblyopia to develop.18 Refractive errors include myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism and presbyopia. The prevalence of myopia in Asian schoolchildren is increasing, but the situation in children younger than 6 years is less studied, especially in those who may be at higher risk, such as preterm infants and preterm infants with ROP.19 Amblyopia also causes a decrease in visual acuity. The effectiveness of treatment for amblyopia differs with age. A meta-analysis reported that the treatment results were the best in children before the age of 7 years.20 Glaucoma damages the development of the optic disc, and a higher treatment response rate was found in children before the age of 10 years.21 22 Cataracts may cause blindness in children, but this condition could be improved by surgery at an early age.23 Most related studies have been performed in Europe and North America, and information from Asian infants is limited.14

It is not easy to observe neonates for a long time in clinical trials. The aim of this study was to use a large-scale long-term follow-up health database to determine the risk of ophthalmological complications in Taiwanese neonates. The results could be useful for parents, caregivers and health policy-makers to design care plans more completely.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We conducted a nationwide, population-based, retrospective cohort study using the Taiwanese National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), which has been used as a long-term follow-up database since 1995. Furthermore, the NHIRD records the medical services of the National Health Insurance (NHI) programme, and more than 99.6% of Taiwanese individuals are covered by the NHI. In other words, the NHIRD is also a large-scale database because it contains data on more than 20 million Taiwanese individuals.

The dataset employed in our study comprised a registry for beneficiaries, inpatient expenditures by admissions and ambulatory care expenditures by visits from the NHIRD. Disease coding in this database was performed using the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) and ICD-10-CM.

Study subjects and variables

Infants who were born from 1 January 2009 to 31 December 2012 were identified (figure 1). Infants who had congenital deformities, missing data on urban status, sex, gestational week at birth or birth weight and infants who died before the age of 8 years were excluded. Based on the week of gestation and the presence of ROP, the infants were divided into three cohorts: the full term (≥37 weeks gestation), preterm (<37 weeks gestation) without ROP and preterm with ROP cohorts. Infants were followed up until 31 December 2020 or until ophthalmological complications were diagnosed (after at least three clinical visits or one hospital admission); these complications included strabismus, refractive error, amblyopia, cataracts and glaucoma.

Flow chart of patient selection. NHIRD, National Health Insurance Research Database; ROP, retinopathy of prematurity.

Covariate assessment

The sociodemographic factors were sex and urbanisation level in our study. There were four levels of urbanisation: level 1 (highest) to level 4 (lowest). The infants were divided into four subgroups according to birth weight: normal birth weight (>2500 g), LBW (1501–2500 g), very LBW (VLBW) (1000–1500 g) and extremely LBW (ELBW) (<1000 g). Comorbidities were defined as conditions that occurred ≥3 times in outpatient records or ≥1 time in inpatient records coded by the ICD-9-CM/ICD-10-CM before the end of the study or as ophthalmological complications were diagnosed, including transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTNB), chronic respiratory disease arising in the perinatal period (CRDA), respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), sensory hearing loss (SHL), failure to thrive (FT), NEC and IVH.

Statistical analyses

We used the χ2 test to compare the baseline characteristics and comorbidities of term infants, preterm infants without ROP and preterm infants with ROP. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate crude ORs and adjusted ORs (aORs) of (1) infants at different maturity levels and with ROP, (2) infants at different birth weights in subgroups with or without ophthalmological complications and (3) infants at different maturity levels and with LBW. The aOR was obtained by adjusting for sociodemographic factors, birth weights, outcomes and comorbidities. Statistical analysis was performed, and figures were generated using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute) and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We considered p<0.05 in two-tailed tests to indicate statistical significance.

Patient and public involvement

Our research question and outcome measures are based on the clinical observation and published studies discussed on this topic. We used NHIRD to process our investigation, there is no patient involve in study design, recruitment or conduct the study. The personal data in the NHIRD could not be identified, thus we could not give specific patient about the result of this study.

Discussion

We used a nationwide, long-term follow-up database to investigate the association between the risk of ocular complications and the characteristics of preterm infants. The results showed that the risk of long-term ocular complications increased with increasing age and body weight. The same trends could be noted for some ocular complications when considering the presence of both LBW and preterm birth. The majority of our three cohorts comprised male infants. Most of the infants lived in areas within the first and second levels of urbanisation. Most of the preterm infants had LBW, and most preterm infants with ROP had VLBW. The three most common ocular complications in the full term, preterm and preterm with ROP cohorts were refractive error, amblyopia and strabismus. The three most common comorbidities in preterm infants and preterm infants with ROP were RDS, CRDA and TTNB. Each comorbidity was noted in <1% of the full-term infants.

The findings of our study could guide public health policy design, and the distribution of urbanisation levels of the included infants was compatible with the findings of previous studies.24 25 However, the distribution of preterm infants with ROP has never been mentioned. Although the prevalence of preterm birth and preterm birth with ROP was similar at the highest level of urbanisation, the prevalence of preterm birth with ROP at the third and lowest levels of urbanisation was slightly greater. In other words, eye health screening is essential in these areas.

The prevalence of a disease may vary in different regions. The number of participants and the follow-up period could also have affected the results. Previously published clinical trials or cross-sectional studies have included small sample sizes and short observation periods.10 Thus, the prevalence rates of ocular complications in infants in our study may not be fully compatible with those of studies published in other regions. However, our results were similar to those of a small study conducted in Taiwan; thus, the risk of selection bias is low.26 Furthermore, the possibility of television overuse and a learning burden may increase the prevalence of refractive error in Taiwanese children.27 This is the variable factor that could not be monitored.

Gestational age and birth weight are two important characteristics, but with restrictions of follow-up interruption, medical caregivers may not read birth information completely. Thus, our study design could compensate for the limitations of clinical trials. According to a previous investigation, variations in these two characteristics may affect development in children.28 We evaluated the outcomes of ocular complications in children. LBW, as previously reported, increases the risk of cataract development. However, even a preterm infant may have a normal birth weight. Thus, the interactions and outcomes of different levels of variable factors should be analysed and presented. According to our results, maturity and birth weight could be independent risk factors contributing to ocular complications in infants. The risk of ocular complications decreased with decreasing gestational age and birth weight. Although the coexistence of these negative variables increases the risk of ocular problems, LBW is not a definite risk factor for cataracts when preterm infants have ROP at the same time.

The limitations of our study are that data on gestational weeks could not be obtained from the database; thus, we used the ICD codes to determine the maturity status of the included infants. Data on some comorbidities, such as congenital heart disease, gestational diabetes mellitus and gestational hypertension, could not be found in the database. However, there was no evidence to support the relationships between these problems and ocular outcomes.

Conclusions

Preterm birth and LBW were risk factors for developing ocular complications in infants. A younger gestational age and lower birth weight increase the risk of ocular complications. Furthermore, the presence of these two factors was associated with a greater risk than was the presence of each individual factor for most of our outcomes. Our results could be helpful for designing a comprehensive care plan for preterm infants to detect possible ocular problems as early as possible.
