Challenges and Opportunities for Visual Analytics in Jurisprudence
Daniel Fürst 0000-0002-0407-2867, Mennatallah El-Assady 0000-0001-8526-2613, Daniel A. Keim 0000-0001-7966-9740, Maximilian T. Fischer 0000-0001-8076-1376

Abstract
Legal exploration, analysis, and interpretation remain complex and demanding tasks, even for experienced legal scholars, due to the domain-specific language, tacit legal concepts, and intentional ambiguities embedded in legal texts. In related, text-based domains, Visual Analytics (VA) and Large Language Models (LLMs) have become indispensable tools for navigating documents, representing knowledge, and supporting analytical reasoning. However, legal scholarship presents distinct challenges: it requires managing formal legal structure, drawing on tacit domain knowledge, and documenting intricate and accurate reasoning processes – needs that current VA systems designs and LLMs fail to address adequately. We identify previously unexamined key challenges and underexplored opportunities in applying VA to jurisprudence to explore how these technologies might better serve the legal domain. Based on semi-structured interviews with nine legal experts, we find a significant gap in tools and means that can externalize tacit legal knowledge in a form that is both explicit and machine-interpretable. Hence, we propose leveraging interactive visualization for this articulation, teaching the machine relevant semantic relationships between legal documents that inform the predictions of LLMs, facilitating the enhanced navigation between hierarchies of legal collections. This work introduces a user-centered VA workflow to the jurisprudential context, recognizing tacit legal knowledge and expert experience as vital components in deriving legal insight, comparing it with established practices in other text-based domains, and outlining a research agenda that offers future guidance for researchers in Visual Analytics for law and beyond.

Background

Jurisprudence is underpinned by a substantial body of literature outlining methodologies for legal research and interpretation, particularly emphasizing
doctrinal analysis

Although jurisprudence traditionally relies on such analysis, legal scholars increasingly recognize the importance of

For example, legal practitioners need to weigh up a conflict of objectives as part of the European General Data Protection Regulation

Empirical approaches

Visual Analytics

Systematic Relationships
—
The interplay of these diverse legal sources constitutes the fabric of the German legal system
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 460)
.
Understanding individual components requires knowledge of their relationships within the broader system.
These relationships are formally structured, hierarchical, and semantic, necessitating tacit knowledge to navigate their complexity effectively.
Applying legal sources follows a systematic process that relies on explicit procedures
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 412)
.
Before applying legal norms, practitioners must first understand the sources that constitute the law.
Then, the application process typically unfolds through a series of distinct steps
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 414)
.
First, practitioners establish the facts of the case, carefully analyzing the situation to identify relevant details.
Experienced practitioners often intuitively classify the case within a specific legal area or discipline
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 413)
.
A particularly challenging aspect of this process involves identifying the legal norms relevant to the case.
This task requires understanding the formally structured, hierarchical, and semantic relationships within the legal system
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 460)
.
Interpreting these legal texts depends on contextualizing a given norm within its broader legal framework, as its meaning and implications are frequently defined by its connections to others.
Determining these connections relies heavily on the practitioner’s expertise and familiarity with the legal system.
Practitioners must continuously evaluate these relationships, considering both explicit legal provisions and implicit analogies or concepts derived from prior decisions and academic literature
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 413)
.
After identifying the relevant norms, practitioners assess whether the case fulfills the specific elements outlined by these norms.
This process, known as
subsumption
, involves comparing the facts of the case with the requirements of the legal provisions
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 413)
.
If the elements are satisfied, the practitioner determines the legal consequences accordingly.
However, cases are often not straightforward, and legal scholars can have differing opinions.

Nevertheless, through this systematic and knowledge-intensive process, practitioners bridge the gap between abstract legal principles and real-world cases, underscoring the critical role of domain expertise in legal reasoning.
While many professionals engage with this legal system, our work focuses on
jurisprudence
, the scientific debate and analysis of law.
However, the tasks and workflow described in the following also apply broadly to other legal professions.

3.2
Tasks

Legal scholars engage in tasks that span multiple levels of complexity, which can be categorized into fundamental information retrieval, understanding structural relationships, advancing legal reasoning, and specialized tasks.

Fundamental Information Retrieval
(
T_{1}
) —
Scholars focus on identifying and retrieving relevant legal sources at the foundational level.
These tasks include filtering sources pertinent to a particular topic
Resck et al.
, understanding the formal legal organization of these sources, and determining the location of specific legal norms within the broader legal corpus
La Cava et al.
.
Efficient information retrieval serves as the groundwork for deeper analytical tasks.

Understanding Structural Relationships
(
T_{2}
) —
Building on information retrieval, scholars analyze the structural relationships within legal texts. The analysis involves evaluating the chronological order of statutes
Resck et al.
and interpreting both explicit and implicit relationships between legal norms
Lettieri et al.
;
Resck et al.
. Grasping these relationships is critical for understanding how individual legal norms interact within the broader legal framework, enabling scholars to navigate the complexities of formal legal structures effectively.

Advancing Legal Reasoning
(
T_{3}
) —
At the highest level, legal reasoning tasks require synthesizing information to uncover patterns and identify conflicts across legal texts
Gomez-Nieto et al.
;
McLachlan and Webley
. Scholars engage in activities such as detecting inconsistencies between norms, identifying legal precedents, and formulating arguments. Additionally, they analyze legal conflicts
Burkhardt and Nazemi
and derive insights that inform decision-making processes.

Specialized Tasks
(
T_{S}
) —
Beyond these general categories, certain tasks are unique to specific legal disciplines or application scenarios. Examples include
Policy Modeling
as analyzing the implications of proposed laws and policies
Burkhardt and Nazemi
,
resolving natural language queries
through interpreting and answering complex legal queries
La Cava et al.
or
comparative law analysis
which involves comparing legal systems or statutes across jurisdictions
Lettieri et al.
.

4
Subject Matter Expert Interviews

To deepen our understanding of the tasks performed by legal scholars and their associated challenges, we conducted semi-structured interviews with domain experts, discussing their established workflows and their opinions on potential technical support.

4.1
Methodology

The semi-structured interviews with subject matter experts (
n=9
) from the legal domain, averaging one hour each, followed a protocol but allowed for follow-ups to capture detailed insights. We conducted the interviews at the participants’ workplace. Each interview began by gathering information about the participants’
specializations and backgrounds
, asking them about their legal expertise as well as their professional and academic experiences.
Next, we inquired about the
established workflows
for working with legal texts in each of these specializations, including their tasks. The participants used their familiar setup to demonstrate how they practice their workflows. We asked them to showcase these workflows using recent examples of their research. We also wanted the participants to describe the tools they use and their role in their workflows.
We also encouraged participants to
compare their practices
in previous roles to establish diverse tasks.
Finally, we asked the experts to identify specific
obstacles
they face throughout their workflows with the tools they described, providing examples. Looking forward, we inquired about their
needs and expectations
for applications and tools that could improve their workflows.

5
Addressing Challenges in Legal Research: A Three-Phase Workflow Design

Despite the digitization of law and recent advancements in AI, our interviews (refer to
subsection 4.3
) reveal challenges that cannot be resolved solely through technological solutions.
The research process of legal scholars is fraught with difficulties, ranging from accessing relevant legal materials amid restrictive license models to navigating formal legal structures and relationships between legal documents.
Moreover, synthesizing tacit knowledge into transparent and well-reasoned arguments poses a significant challenge.
A syntactic keyword search in commercial databases often proves inadequate to capture these nuanced interpretation tasks, highlighting the limitations of current UIs and underscoring the need for support contextualized to the user.

In line with these insights, and building on the bottom-up findings from our subject-matter expert interviews, we observe that legal scholars follow structured steps in their research
(
Rüthers et al.
, pp. 412–413)
.
These phases require different types of support.
While scholars typically switch between these phases effortlessly, addressing the need for support and the difficulties of the legal research process from a technical perspective necessitates a coherent structure to the workflow of legal scholars.
This allows for using different support mechanisms in each phase
Pirolli and Card
.
Most participants begin by conducting a keyword search to identify relevant laws and legal norms.
However, a single attempt is usually insufficient for satisfactory results, so keyword search becomes an iterative process
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 413)
.
It serves as a tool to facilitate the analysis of legal materials, but it is not an end in itself.
Often, key insights emerge not through targeted searches but during comprehensive reading.
Subsequently, legal scholars transition to contextualizing laws and legal norms with each other, using commentary and other legal materials
(
Rüthers et al.
, pp. 93, 459–461)
.
This involves connecting the initially narrow scope of documents with a broader landscape of legal materials to enable systematic interpretation.
Often, identifying individual legal norms is not sufficient, but connecting them to legal concepts is necessary to facilitate legal reasoning.
Given the vast legal document collections, this process can be cognitively taxing for scholars.
Therefore, they rely on documentation throughout the process
Cantatore and Stevens
.
Documenting their legal insights enables synthesis, which is used to construct legal arguments and constitute legal knowledge.
While many of the recurring challenges faced by researchers arise during their work, some are more prevalent at specific phases.
Based on these observations and the literature, we identify a three-phase workflow that consists of an initial overview, a strategic analysis and contextualization, and synthesis.

One can categorize the points raised by the interviewees into several distinct and recurring

1.

5.

Reliance on Tacit Knowledge:
The relationships between legal materials are essential for sophisticated legal reasoning. However, many of these relationships are not explicitly encoded in legal documents. Instead, scholars must draw on their domain expertise, which includes implicit relationships to interpret norms. Integrating their implicit knowledge with the explicit knowledge conveyed in current interfaces is labor-intensive and error-prone.

Apart from the licensing issues, these findings emphasize a need for more adequate tooling support in the legal domain. VA offers ways to address these challenges through collaboration between humans and AI by enabling semantic exploration and systematic analysis of legal texts. The individual phases in the workflow (refer to
subsection 5.1
) need different types of support for these challenges. As discussed previously (refer to
section 2
), some of the challenges align with standard practices in text-based research domains, which allows us to reuse some existing concepts, but with the need to adapt and integrate them to the specific needs of law scholars.

In computer science, a separation of concerns is fundamental to the architecture of our modern computers.
It separates input and output from data management and representation, and algorithmic instructions
von Neumann 1945
.
In recent interpretation, VA also considers the interaction of humans with computers from three perspectives: data, model, and visualization
Sacha et al.
.

Data
describes the information related to the analytical problem, which, in law, is not only the raw text but also their interdependency and metadata.
A
model
can refer to descriptive statistics or a sophisticated computational algorithm that processes the data, which is akin to the cognitive capabilities of human legal reasoning.

Visualization
refers to the visual representation of data and models, which can be a line-by-line block of text on a display in a specific formatting or a flow chart of causal relationships between norms in the legal domain.
These aspects frame the three related but distinct perspectives we apply to the recurring challenges previously identified as leverage points
Pirolli and Card
.

We consider the following aspects to contextualize the three perspectives with the identified challenges and the legal domain.
User interfaces of legal databases visualize document collections and enable their navigation, but are currently complex and inefficient.
The complexity results from intricate formal legal structures, and the inefficiencies are due to the lack of contextual and semantic understanding in syntactic keyword searches.
VA can support this navigation of the information space (refer to
subsection 6.1
).
The explicit and implicit knowledge encoded in legal documents through references and semantic formulations must be sufficiently represented (refer to
subsection 6.2
) in the form of data structures and relations.
Since tacit knowledge is not readily encoded in the data, it is also partly responsible for the current inefficiency of user interfaces and the difficulties in modeling this information.
The logical analysis and reasoning, a manual process so far, can be supported through the navigation and tailored interfaces by making relevant information more accessible and connected (refer to
subsection 6.3
).
In all three perspectives, AI solutions can be employed to support data filtering, to improve the context-dependent prediction of relevant documents, and to help with analytical reasoning, for example.

6.1
Data Navigation

In VA, data navigation encompasses the exploration of and the movement in information spaces
Shrinivasan and Van Wijk
.
For jurisprudence, this translates to navigating formally structured legal document collections.
The
traditional UI
of legal databases (refer to
Figure 2
) relies on a collapsible tree-based list to represent hierarchies.
However, this approach fails to provide semantic context during navigation.
Since legal structures can be nested several levels deep
(
Bundesministerium für Justiz
, p. 152)
, their tree representation can become cumbersome.
Additionally, tree-based lists inadequately support the simultaneous navigation of multiple legal structures – a common requirement in jurisprudential research.

Merkl and Schweighofer
introduced the concept of navigating such corpora using a
Hierarchical Feature Map

Merkl and Schweighofer
, a
Treemap
variant that organizes clusters across layers representing the hierarchy.
Each layer corresponds to a hierarchical level, with subsequent layers revealing sub-clusters.
Despite their improvement, the static, semi-automatically generated maps require manual input for cluster titles, limiting scalability.
Building on this idea,
Lettieri et al.
started employing Treemaps
Johnson and Shneiderman
in the
Legal Doctrine Semantic Navigator
for navigating formally structured legal documents through drill-down and roll-up operations
Lettieri et al.
.
Later, they introduced the
sliding Treemap
, a variation, enabling the navigation of rulings by the European Court of Justice and other legal documents on mobile devices
Lettieri et al.
.
Treemaps with interactive drill-downs can display multiple paths simultaneously across hierarchies and can visually encode guidance by enlarging documents of higher relevance
Lettieri et al.
(refer to
Figure 2
, a).

Beyond Treemaps,
Icicle Plots

Kruskal and Landwehr
have emerged as an effective visualization method for hierarchical data.
DH researchers employ these plots for document comparison
Tytarenko et al.
.

Kim et al.
use Icicle Plots to visualize a hierarchy of topics during their interactive modeling
Kim et al.
, while
Tytarenko et al.
also visualize a hierarchy of topics to compare documents
Tytarenko et al.
(refer to
Figure 2
, a).

We
propose
a hybrid approach combining the strengths of both visualization techniques based on the following observations.
Since Treemaps perform worse at displaying large hierarchies than Icicle Plots
Macquisten et al.
, distributing the visual representation between these complementary visualizations is beneficial.
A Treemap would display the first few layers of the currently selected path as an overview, while a juxtaposed Icicle Plot would present the remainder of the path in the hierarchy.
Employing the two visualizations in parallel mitigates scalability issues and leverages the benefits of interactively linking both visualizations
Burch et al.
, offering complementary perspectives on legal corpora.
This combination would support legal scholars during the initial workflow phase (refer to
W_{\texttt{P1}}
), facilitating the discovery and scoping of legal document collections.

Alternative hierarchical visualization techniques such as Bubble Treemaps or Sunbursts
Macquisten et al.
can also be employed but perform worse for small hierarchies than our proposed approaches.
Still, we emphasize the need for exploring other design alternatives such as circular grid layouts
Qiu et al.
and hierarchical edge bundling
Holten
for radial visualizations like a radial dendrogram.
Though less prevalent in the legal domain, these alternatives are worth considering for expanding the design space.

6.2
Knowledge Representation

The interactions with visualizations for data navigation heavily depend on the user’s prior knowledge, explicit and tacit
Wang et al.
.
In jurisprudence, explicit knowledge can manifest as connections between legal documents
La Cava et al.
, where one norm references another legal norm, establishing a relationship.
The
traditional UI
of legal databases displays these relationships through backlinks, with a popup and metadata, thereby representing explicit knowledge.
The tree-based list of the law’s hierarchy to the left and the list of related legal documents on the right also express explicit knowledge contained in legal documents.
Meanwhile, legal scholars’ tacit knowledge about the applications of these legal norms is internalized as part of legal scholarship. However, it is not articulated
Rüthers et al.

subsubsection 5.1.2

In the literature, various types of visualizations, such as stacked graphs and geographic maps, have been employed to visualize the metadata of legal documents

Similarly,
Resck et al.
visualize the structure of legal documents by semantically segmenting them into paragraphs
Resck et al.

Meanwhile, other works statically analyze the complexity of legal documents by visualizing explicit references between their articles

Beyond jurisprudence, explicit knowledge of text-based documents is visualized in the context of discussion forum posts

and document comparison
Tytarenko et al.

However, these examples, both within and outside jurisprudence, often neglect to facilitate the articulation of tacit knowledge.
Instead, VA should consider tacit knowledge

For instance,
Wagner et al.
allow experts for cybersecurity to classify behavioral rules of software for identifying malware

Mistelbauer et al.
enhance medical visualizations by ranking views based on the experience of medical experts
Mistelbauer et al.

Endert et al.
define several semantic interactions that support analytical reasoning for text analytics
Endert et al.
.
The authors also provide an implementation, letting users determine the importance of phrases through text highlighting.
Accordingly, documents containing the exact phrase become closer in a spatial layout (refer to

Similarly, a diagram of concepts connected by labeled arcs constitutes a
Concept Map

Cañas et al.
(refer to
Figure 2

Translating these solutions from VA to jurisprudence, we
observe
that visually laying out legal concepts in a map is an established approach
Burkhardt and Nazemi
(
Ginevra Peruginelli and Faro
, p. 48)
Onami et al.
.
Concept maps can enhance VA for jurisprudence by converting legal scholars’ domain knowledge into explicit knowledge through semantic interactions, thereby improving the effectiveness of LLMs.
With
KMTLabeler
,
Wang et al.
propose a labeling tool for medical texts integrating VA with LLMs

.
This tool allows experts to express their domain knowledge by adjusting keyword weights and defining labeling rules.
The resulting explicit knowledge is incorporated into the embedding generation of the LLM and used to improve the labeling of the medical texts.
We can also connect the knowledge representation to legal reasoning since a

Both support the latter two phases of the jurisprudence workflow (refer to

While networks are suitable for supporting this workflow phase, node-link diagrams may not be the most effective visualization method. Matrices, i.e., tabular displays of graphs, are proven methods for visualizing network data

integrate natural language querying into network navigation, enabling users to filter nodes efficiently
La Cava et al.
(refer to
Figure 2
, c).
Since analytical artifacts externalize tacit knowledge as explicit
Wang et al.
, they can act as a countermeasure to the confirmation bias in hypothesis generation
Pirolli and Card
and enable collaboration
Pérez-Messina et al.
, which can reinforce diverse opinions in legal decision-making.
When persisted
Pérez-Messina et al.
, these artifacts can help users recall and replicate insights
Ragan et al.
.

Analytical provenance
additionally fosters transparency, which is particularly important in the legal domain, where the validity and reliability of insights must be meticulously documented.
Integrating these aspects, for example, through 2D clustering for overview, graph networks for relations, and provenance trees for documentation, shows the significant potential of VA for jurisprudence.

7
Emergent Challenges for Visual Analytics Design

While numerous approaches exist for data navigation in the legal domain, knowledge representation and analytical reasoning are often left to analysts to perform manually, as we have seen in the previous discussion.
We have analyzed several examples of existing VA techniques to address these shortcomings in
section 6
.
Crucially, no existing solution integrates all these areas of VA into a unified framework to exploit their synergetic effects.
In the following, we explore the
opportunities and challenges
of developing such an integrated approach, building upon the proposed solutions.

7.1
Addressing Jurisprudence

Traditionally, German legal scholars predominantly use keyword-based search interfaces, the prevalent UI paradigm
Verlag C.H.BECK oHG
;
juris GmbH
.
However, this poses several challenges to legal scholars, hindering effective workflow support.
While familiar, the paradigm limits scholars to primitive input, lacking the expressiveness required in the legal domain.
Hence, we propose combining existing visualization techniques into a novel VA workflow (refer to
Figure 1
) for jurisprudence, leveraging LLM-assisted contextual understanding.

As part of the initial workflow phase (refer to
W_{\texttt{P1}}
), the scholar scopes their research verbally. Instead of a syntactic keyword search, they articulate analysis goals using natural language. The system processes the verbalization and embeds the query into a semantic context by inferring references to legal entities such as laws, legal norms, and court rulings. We imagine an overview displaying clusters of legal concepts and individual documents relevant to the query, enabling scholars to leverage their domain expertise to refine the selection. For its implementation, the overview leverages a network visualization augmented with colored shapes that indicate clusters. For details, a scholar can preview individual documents on-demand during selection, highlighting explanations for their relevance.
After refining the scoping, legal scholars transition to the second workflow phase (refer to
W_{\texttt{P1}}
). In VA, the UI transitions into a detailed view upon selecting a cluster or document of interest. We propose juxtaposing the formal legal structure with a branching visualization such as an Icicle Plot. The former displays the legal documents of the scope with their hierarchy, enabling efficient navigation. Meanwhile, the Icicle Plot provides the contextualized path of the selected document in its hierarchy. Together, these visualizations address the legal domain’s unique requirement for
simultaneous navigation across multiple hierarchies
.

During the analysis & interpretation, legal reasoning takes place, which must utilize the scholar’s domain knowledge. While the proposed VA techniques can initially visualize the explicit knowledge from the legal documents, they cannot draw on evolving domain knowledge. To achieve this, VA must provide interaction for users to articulate their tacit knowledge. Therefore, at any point during the workflow, if users find a relevant document missing from the current selection, they can establish a relationship to include it. The Treemap and Icicle Plot visualizations facilitate this interaction by integrating empty cells with suggestive icons as visual placeholders. By clicking these icons, the analyst can relate a selected document to others represented in the visualizations. These interactions reveal user preferences relevant to the subsequent recommendation of related legal documents. With these user preferences, we augment a knowledge graph (KG) of the legal documents selected during the first workflow phase.
Yang et al.
demonstrate the benefit of such an augmentation with
LegalGNN
, a framework for recommending legal documents based on a graph neural network trained with a legal KG and the contents of legal documents
Yang et al.
. We exploit the resulting recommendations to personalize the VA workflow with the expert’s domain knowledge. Further, we draw tacit knowledge from its externalization through analytical artifacts.

Besides the Treemap and the Icicle Plot, we propose to add a concept map to the top side of the suggested user interface. The map allows users to synthesize and document evidence acquired throughout the legal reasoning process, forming a crucial part of the third workflow phase (refer to
W_{\texttt{P3}}
). Scholars capture and connect relevant artifacts within the concept map to organize their legal insights. Nodes represent primitive artifacts resembling individual legal entities, such as legal norms or court rulings. In contrast, compound artifacts describe the links that relate multiple primitives, representing causal connections in the legal realm. Based on these and the graph augmented during the previous workflow phase, we enhance documentation of the legal reasoning process through retrieval-augmentation generation (RAG).
Edge et al.
propose
GraphRAG
, which performs RAG on the KG of text corpora
Edge et al.
. The authors leverage the hierarchy of the KG to enable the summarization of documents at varying levels of abstraction. This approach aligns with the nature of legal corpora and can benefit the documentation of legal reasoning in jurisprudence. For instance, in analyzing racial profiling by the German Federal Police (refer to
subsection 5.1
), a scholar might document that the police are responsible for securing train stations (§ 3 BPolG) and are permitted to identify individuals if necessary for fulfilling their duties (§ 23 BPolG). However, this authority must be balanced against the third-party effect of the German Basic Law (Art. 3 GG), which ensures equality and non-discrimination.

While the concept map functions as an isolated visualization, we propose a novel augmentation that merges it with the network visualization used during the first workflow phase (refer to
W_{\texttt{P1}}
). This integration creates a seamless transition between overview and detail, addressing a gap in current applications for jurisprudence. The unified design enables scholars to maintain their analytical context while moving between different levels of abstraction. The design can reduce the cognitive load associated with context switching by preserving the semantic relationships between legal materials across visualizations. This integration also facilitates the progressive refinement of legal arguments, as insights developed in detailed analysis can immediately inform and restructure the broader research scope. Our
unified design
creates a continuous analytical environment that mirrors the non-linear nature of legal reasoning.

Implementing the proposed VA workflow
faces challenges
.
The suggested visualization techniques for VA may suffer from issues with
visual scalability
. For vast legal datasets, node-link diagrams, Treemaps, and Icicle Plots are of limited use.
However, since the datasets are hierarchically structured, we can progressively unveil parts of the hierarchy to limit the information these visualization techniques need to convey.
Further, leveraging progress in
natural language understanding
remains difficult: legal text data combines domain-specific language
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 123)
and precise phraseology with intentional ambiguity at times
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 128)
.
This ambiguity allows for statutory interpretation
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 22)
, enabling laws to evolve alongside societal norms and values, but is challenging for LLMs.
Although trained on vast datasets,
Large Language Models
still struggle to apply these nuances of legal reasoning, particularly open foundation models.
For example, we found that Llama 3.1 70B still sometimes confuses § 212 StGB (Strafgesetzbuch, i.e., criminal code), which defines homicide for murder (§ 211 StGB).
However, by definition of the criminal code, they are two strictly separate criminal offenses.
While such models can describe the differences in great detail when explicitly asked, during reasoning, they can tend to collapse any distinctions without further warning.
Equally, hallucinations, particularly during the subsumption phase, are critical as LLMs struggle with completeness.
Therefore, it is crucial to perform extensive fine-tuning of foundational LLMs to align them with the specific language of the legal domain.
This also presents ample opportunity for VA design to facilitate collaboration between human scholars and LLMs during the legal reasoning process.

7.2
Implications for Visual Analytics Design

The lingering challenges in data navigation, knowledge externalization, and analytical reasoning cannot be sufficiently addressed with AI and computational solutions alone.
Therefore, we advocate for a human-
is
-the-loop design
Endert et al.
, which positions the VA system as an integral part of the scholar’s workflow. This view contrasts designs that rely on the common human-
in
-the-loop
Keim et al.
;
Sacha et al.
approach.
While the latter assumes the domain expert to become a part of their VA system, the former prerequisites the VA system to become part of the domain expert and their workflow instead.
To achieve this, the VA design process requires a user-centered approach that honors the workflows of the domain expert, supporting them throughout the sense-making loop
Pirolli and Card
;
Endert et al.
.
Therefore, we highlight the following
key techniques
and higher-level considerations regarding jurisprudence and generalization to other domains.

User-Centered Design
— The development of effective VA design requires adherence to user-centered design principles
Gibbs and Owens
;
Hinrichs et al.
.
These principles promote broader adoption of VA designs that meet specific audience needs.
These needs vary across different application domains, valuing various visual qualities, such as page layout in law.
While these aspects may not seem directly related to the effectiveness or efficiency of VA design, they can significantly influence data navigation in information spaces.
This variation in user requirements presents ample opportunity for employing design studies or conducting pilot interviews with domain experts.
These methodological approaches allow for gaining critical insights into the audience’s specific needs, workflows, and mental models, like the importance of tacit knowledge, the lack of contextualized search, and the three-phase research process, which we discussed in
section 4
.

Knowledge Externalization
— In application domains, subject matter experts possess tacit knowledge regarding domain-specific problems.
It encompasses implicit understanding, experiential insights, and intuitive reasoning processes not readily encoded in explicit knowledge.
The externalization process captures these domain-specific analytical approaches in shareable analytical artifacts.
If not articulated, VA design inherently lacks access to this knowledge, potentially compromising analytical effectiveness.
While explicit knowledge may be available in substantial quantities through text documents, for example, integrating with tacit knowledge is imperative to enhance analytical outcomes
Federico et al.
.
VA design must strive to externalize tacit knowledge through user interaction, KGs, and analytical artifacts.

Provenance Tracking
— Analytical artifacts manifest knowledge externalization processes within VA systems.
These artifacts serve as persistent representations of analytical reasoning and decision-making trajectories throughout the analytical workflow
Pérez-Messina et al.
.
Since users often struggle to maintain holistic awareness of past analytical steps, access to provenance information is essential for branching and revising the analysis process.
VA design must facilitate the creation and storage of analytical artifacts through comprehensive provenance tracking, fostering the user’s awareness of the analysis process.
Concurrently, these artifacts can be systematically utilized to guide subsequent analytical processes.
VA design must exploit this recursive employment of analytical artifacts to create a self-reinforcing system that progressively refines the analytical workflow based on accumulated insights and knowledge.

Natural Language Inference
— While mining user interactions with VA interfaces can constitute guidance, it presents inherent methodological challenges in deducing higher-order analytical objectives
Gotz and Zhou
.
Interaction patterns alone provide insufficient semantic context for accurately inferring analytical goals that guide user behavior.
With recent advancements in LLMs, these models demonstrate sophisticated semantic parsing and intention recognition capabilities that can be leveraged to extract structured analytical objectives from natural language
Zhao et al.
.
VA design must enable users to articulate these analytical objectives through natural language interfaces to capture nuanced analytical intentions while going beyond a simple chat-based interface design.

Unified Design
— To achieve effectiveness and efficiency in addressing domain-specific problems, VA design must integrate the aforementioned key techniques.
User-centered design facilitates identifying and formalizing domain-specific workflows, as demonstrated through our structured interviews with subject matter experts (refer to
section 4
).
Such approaches reveal critical insights into domain-specific analytical processes that may not be apparent through conventional design approaches.
Likewise, knowledge externalization provides systematic access to tacit knowledge inherent to analysts.
This externalization process transforms implicit knowledge into explicit, computationally accessible representations that can inform VA.
Provenance tracking can effectively reflect domain-specific workflows by capturing the structure of analytical processes.
Minimizing cognitive load induced by context switching between applications resulting from fragmented VA design is imperative.
Instead, it must unify analytical functionalities within a coherent interface to optimize cognitive resource utilization and enhance analytical performance.

7.3
Limitations

In this unified design, we focus on legal tech from the perspective of VA while adopting a definition of visualization
(
Card et al.
, p. 1)
from the visualization community in our
literature analysis
. Meanwhile, the legal community borrows a broader definition of visualization
Khalil
. This difference leads to papers from the legal community that visualize legal data but are not considered VA. Although these approaches are not the primary focus of our work, we incorporate their visualization concepts, which are familiar to legal scholars, in our proposed VA design (refer to
section 6
). For example, we use
Concept Maps
, which are commonly used in the legal community to visualize cases and causal relationships between legal norms (e.g.,
Cantatore and Stevens

Cantatore and Stevens
), to capture tacit knowledge (refer to
subsection 6.2
) during legal reasoning. Thus, visualization concepts from the legal domain can be incorporated into VA design, fostering interdisciplinary research.
To enhance our understanding of how legal scholars operate, we describe different levels of tasks (refer to
subsection 3.2
). These levels are supported by research on applications in legal tech, often emerging from the intersection of law and computer science. Consequently, the labels for these task levels resemble technical terms from VA, such as information retrieval. However, these tasks are grounded in the operations of scholars as described in literature from the legal domain (refer to
subsection 3.1
). Additionally, these tasks are linked to the three-phase workflow (refer to
subsection 5.1
), which is based on interviews with legal scholars (refer to
section 4
).

These results of the
subject matter expert interviews
(refer to
section 4
) take common workflows and recurring challenges faced by German scholars from various legal disciplines into account.
Still, they are not exhaustive, as further disciplines exist in the German legal system and beyond.
To accommodate the differences between legal disciplines, we have decided on a semi-structured interview, where we adapt the follow-up questions to the interviewees’ backgrounds and experiences.
Although we assume differences in the details, we expect the significant challenges distilled in
section 6
to be similar as they concern all disciplines.
We elicit our observations from subject matter experts who work exclusively with the German civil law system and European legislation.
So far, we have not incorporated experiences from countries that practice civil law or other legal systems like common law (refer to
section 1
), which is practiced in the United States, for example.

To illustrate the three-phase workflow design (refer to
subsection 5.1
) and to critically discuss traditional UI for legal research in our
case study
(refer to
section 6
), we have focused on the
Bundespolizeigesetz
(Federal Police Act).
This specific legal research context highlights the challenges described in a broader context by participants during the interviews (refer to
section 4
) using a concrete example.
Challenges arise from accessibility issues, navigational complexity, the inefficiency of the search interface, and the reliance on tacit knowledge, which extend to other laws and legal research contexts.
This generalization allows our case study to transcend the specific example chosen and the German legal system.
To illustrate the traditional UI used in jurisprudence as part of our case study (refer to
section 6
), we use
beck-online
as an example. Although
beck-online
is just one of many commercial and scientific applications for jurisprudence, it is a suitable representative. In the German legal system context,
beck-online
is among the most widely used legal research applications by practitioners. Additionally,
beck-online
features a design similar to widely used applications such as
Westlaw

Thomson Reuters
and
PACER

Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts
, which helps generalize our findings to case law.
While we have theoretically examined the workflow design in subject matter expert interviews (refer to
section 4
) for various legal disciplines, our work lacks empirical evaluations of the proposed VA workflow in these specific settings.
We partially rely on empirical evaluations of visualization techniques that we propose for use in a legal setting. However, the unified design incorporating these techniques has not been evaluated accordingly.
Furthermore, the existing empirical evaluations have been conducted in related domains but may not necessarily generalize to the legal field.

7.4
Future Work and Generalization

To address the limited evaluation, we plan to implement the proposed VA workflow in preparation for a
systematic evaluation
.
In this context, corresponding user studies should involve controlled experiments conducted with legal scholars.
These experiments should compare the efficacy and usability of traditional UI, which utilizes syntactic and keyword-based search interfaces, with the proposed VA-enhanced workflow.
Additionally, the experiments should assess the reduction in cognitive load experienced when utilizing interactive visualizations in legal research tasks.

Beyond legal scholars’ evaluations, future research should focus on communicating law and legal concepts to the general public.
This necessitates research into
adaptive, personalized visualization
techniques that cater to the user’s expertise level rather than assuming the expertise of legal scholars.
Personalized visualization also allows us to explore the adoption of specific visual metaphors prevalent in legal sub-disciplines like administrative law or criminal law.

Our work focuses primarily on the German civil law system.
While the civil law system works similarly, countries sometimes have substantial differences regarding style, structure, and methodology.
For example, EU legislation and German law differ in articulating their goals.
In EU legislation, goals are clearly outlined, while German law lacks such explicit statements, sometimes to be found in the law justifications.
Additionally, the two systems vary in their teleological and systematic interpretations.
There are also differences in the integration of certain aspects of case law into EU legislation as a supranational legal system.
Future work should explore the particularities of extending our approach to
other legislative systems
and, in particular, to case law, with more considerable differences.
Regardless, we expect that our distilled three-phase workflow (refer to
subsection 5.1
) still applies. The three key areas of VA also benefit jurisprudence in these legal systems due to their generalizability.

Unfortunately, open legal datasets necessary for training and fine-tuning are limited and need more diversity.
While several German legal datasets include fundamental law and some court rulings (refer to
Table 1
), law commentary, interpretation, and the vast majority of legal cases and statements of claims are rarely available.
However, access to law commentary is fundamental for the legal reasoning process
(
Rüthers et al.
, p. 254)
.
In the future, more work must be done to offer more structured
open legal data
.
One possible direction forward is through open data initiatives, digitalizing legal systems, or scanning older literature that is automatically transformed into legal archives with semantic LLM-based methods alongside reforms in copyright legislation and fair use.

While we have presented the textual analysis with a strong focus on jurisprudence, some methods and solutions exhibit substantial overlap with workflows in related, text-based research domains
Zeng et al.
;
Tian et al.
.
These workflows share core elements of data navigation, knowledge representation, and analytical reasoning, as outlined in
section 6
.
Consequently, our VA workflow (refer to
subsection 7.1
) can be
transferred
onto these usage scenarios, albeit with domain-specific adaptations.
For example, the types of analytical artifacts must align with the target discipline.
In the context of scientific literature, scholars writing a survey about visualization types might use the workflow to stash evidence regarding papers covering specific visualization techniques.
In this case, a primitive artifact could represent an individual visualization figure, while a compound artifact could encapsulate a collection of papers discussing the same visualization type.
This adaptability stems from the workflow’s abstraction into the broader sense-making loop
Pirolli and Card
, as described by
Pirolli and Card
.
Implementations of this loop are evident in various domains, including healthcare
Federico et al.
and sports
Chung et al.
.
For instance, the VA system proposed by
Chung et al.
for analyzing rugby performance
Chung et al.
uses a projection of game events to facilitate data navigation.
This approach parallels the projection of medical texts employed by
KMTLabeler

Wang et al.
.
Additionally, both systems enable domain experts to incorporate their tacit knowledge through weight modifications during analysis
Chung et al.
;
Wang et al.
.

Thus, our VA workflow can provide a guiding framework for visualization researchers across diverse domains, where
tacit knowledge
remains a driving force for analytical reasoning and decision-making.
Several existing systems externalize tacit domain knowledge as explicit rules, often as tables
Chung et al.
.
While functional, these representations may appear overly generic and disconnected from the specific workflows of individual domains.
Therefore, future research should prioritize the development of
domain-specific metaphors
for externalizing tacit knowledge
Wang et al.
.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we explore the integration of Visual Analytics (VA) into jurisprudence, addressing the unique challenges of legal texts’ complex, formal, and hierarchical nature.
Through semi-structured interviews with legal experts, we identify a typical workflow in jurisprudence and analyze its commonalities with workflows in other disciplines.
This analysis reveals an abstraction in the sense-making loop, emphasizing the need for VA systems that integrate data navigation, knowledge representation, and analytical reasoning within a unified design.
To address these gaps, we propose a human-is-the-loop VA design that leverages legal scholars’ tacit knowledge, enhancing their ability to navigate, interpret, and reason with legal documents.
We critically discuss the necessary transformations from a traditional UI commonly employed to a VA-based interface, alongside its challenges.
Our proposed approach facilitates informed legal reasoning by combining human expertise with machine intelligence.
It provides a scalable framework for other text-intensive domains and the blueprint for future development of such systems.
Our findings extend beyond jurisprudence, offering valuable insights into the design of VA systems for related fields.
This work lays a foundation for future advancements in knowledge-assisted VA systems by addressing the interplay between tacit knowledge and sense-making processes.
