Truthful Text Sanitization Guided by Inference Attacks Journal: Information Sciences
Ildikó Pilán, Benet Manzanares-Salor, David Sánchez, Pierre Lison

Abstract
The purpose of text sanitization is to rewrite those text spans in a document that may directly or indirectly identify an individual, to ensure they no longer disclose personal information. Text sanitization must strike a balance between preventing the leakage of personal information (privacy protection) while also retaining as much of the document’s original content as possible (utility preservation). We present an automated text sanitization strategy based on generalizations , which are more abstract (but still informative) terms that subsume the semantic content of the original text spans. The approach relies on instruction-tuned large language models (LLMs) and is divided into two stages. The LLM is first applied to obtain truth-preserving replacement candidates and rank them according to their abstraction level. Those candidates are then evaluated for their ability to protect privacy by conducting inference attacks with the LLM. Finally, the system selects the most informative replacement shown to be resistant to those attacks. As a consequence of this two-stage process, the chosen replacements effectively balance utility and privacy. We also present novel metrics to automatically evaluate these two aspects without the need to manually annotate data. Empirical results on the Text Anonymization Benchmark show that the proposed approach leads to enhanced utility, with only a marginal increase in the risk of re-identifying protected individuals compared to fully suppressing the original information. Furthermore, the selected replacements are shown to be more truth-preserving and abstractive than previous methods.

Introduction

Text documents play a key role in many sectors, such as healthcare, legal services or public administrations. However, those documents often contain personal data that, if released without adequate protection, can pose significant privacy risks to the individuals they concern. Regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
[
15
]
impose strict constraints how personal information should be protected. In particular, such data cannot be shared with third parties or employed for secondary purposes without a proper legal ground, such as the explicit consent of the individuals the data refers to. However, if the data can be fully anonymized, those restrictions no longer apply, as the resulting data is by definition no longer considered personal. Many approaches have been proposed to perform this anonymization
[
24
]
, although their compliance relative to legal frameworks such as GDPR remains unclear
[
44
]
.

Text sanitization is a type of text anonymization technique that involves i)
detecting
and ii)
protecting
sensitive text spans. Most approaches in the existing literature focus on the detection step, and employ straightforward methods to protect the detected text spans, such as suppressing them or replacing them by generic semantic categories indicating named entity types such as
PERSON
or
LOCATION

[
21
,
46
,
47
,
18
,
25
,
9
,
30
]
. However, this relatively crude level of protection can undermine the usability of the protected data for research and analysis, which is the ultimate goal of data sharing. As highlighted in the data privacy literature
[
19
]
, data protection should aim at balancing the trade-off between the attained level of privacy and the preservation of data utility. For textual data, this means designing effective
text sanitization
mechanisms that preserve the meaning of the original text as much as possible while fulfilling the privacy needs of the data release.

1.1
Contributions and plan

Large Language Models (LLMs) are particularly effective for text rewriting tasks, among others, due to their ability to understand both the detailed and broader contextual aspects of language. This enables them to rephrase content while preserving its intended meaning, making them valuable for applications like text simplification
[
22
]
and editing
[
41
]
. Although LLMs can be adapted for text rewriting through fine-tuning on task-specific data, a simpler approach is to rely on instruction-tuned LLMs to directly prompt the model with rewriting instructions.

In this paper, we propose a novel text sanitization approach, named INference-guided Truthful sAnitization for Clear Text (INTACT). INTACT leverages instruction-tuned open-weights LLMs to replace personal information with less specific, but truth-preserving
generalizations
that retain the original semantic content to the greatest extent possible, while preventing inference attacks from recovering the initial data.

Unlike previous privacy-oriented text rewriting methods
[
5
,
13
]
, our method adopts a two-stage strategy. Given a text span to replace, we first (1) generate a sorted list of generalization candidates at different abstraction levels, and then (2) select the most suitable replacement among those alternatives. Crucially, this selection of the best replacement is done by testing whether the LLM can guess the original span from the replacement and the rest of the text (inference attack). As LLMs have been shown to be effective adversaries in anonymization tasks in previous studies
[
31
]
, leveraging their predictions is a crucial step towards ensuring the privacy of the generated replacements, particularly truthful ones. Separating these steps not only allows balancing and customizing the privacy-utility trade-off, but it also enables a more granular evaluation of the rewriting process.

We evaluate and compare our method against several baselines on the Text Anonymization Benchmark (TAB)
[
32
]
, a dataset explicitly tailored for the evaluation of text sanitization methods. As text sanitization is by definition a process that involves the processing of (sometimes highly sensitive) personal data, our approach relies on the use of open-weights models, as those can be run locally without necessitating the transfer of data to third-party cloud solutions. Empirical results show that our method achieves the best privacy-utility trade-off, while keeping the protected data truthful to their original content. We also show how privacy and utility can be measured without reference annotations.

4

2
Background and related works

Text anonymization aims to remove or mask any personally identifiable information (PII) that may lead to re-identifying the subject(s) referenced in the text and/or disclose their confidential information.

For detecting PII, the vast majority of automatic text anonymization approaches rely on
Named Entity Recognition
(NER)
[
21
,
46
,
47
,
18
,
25
,
9
]
, which assume that named entities (NEs), due to their specificity, may facilitate re-identification and should therefore be subjected to protection. However, because not all PIIs fit within the limited set of NE types supported by NER tools, NER-based methods may miss a significant amount of disclosive terms
[
24
]
. To mitigate this issue, other approaches grounded on the broader notion of disclosure risk have been proposed
[
17
,
30
,
35
]
.

The above approaches provide solutions for the detection of disclosive terms, but do not specify how those terms ought to be edited in the document. Two editing strategies are possible. Whereas document
redaction
directly suppresses or blacks out terms, thereby maximizing privacy at the cost of the utility and readability of the protected documents, text
sanitization
replaces terms with broader, more abstract generalizations
[
8
]
. The latter achieves a better privacy-utility trade-off while preserving the truthfulness of the information, as generalizations, by definition, should retain the core semantic content of the original terms.

The majority of current text anonymization methods use either strict redaction or replace the disclosive terms by their corresponding NE category (such as
PERSON
or
LOCATION
), which is a basic form of sanitization
[
24
]
. Both strategies retain little to no meaningful information from the original terms. More sophisticated approaches leverage structured knowledge bases, such as ontologies, or, more recently, language models, to retrieve potential replacements. We survey those methods in the following subsections.

2.1
Sanitization from Knowledge Bases

Automatic text sanitization methods have traditionally leveraged structured knowledge bases
[
10
,
3
,
2
,
37
,
38
,
36
,
17
,
29
]
, such as WordNet, the now defunct Open Directory Project (ODP) or YAGO, to retrieve term generalizations to be used as replacements.

They propose different mechanisms to select the most appropriate generalization from a list of candidates. In
[
17
]
, the authors leverage the Word2Vec
[
28
]
word embedding model to identify terms that are semantically related to the individual to be protected. If a term’s embedding has a cosine similarity with the individual’s name above a user-defined threshold, it is considered disclosive, and it is replaced by its most specific generalization (retrieved from YAGO) whose cosine similarity falls below the threshold. In
[
37
,
36
]
the authors employ a similar approach, but this time selecting the most appropriate replacement through measures of the term informativeness and mutual information.
[
3
]
propose to select generalizations so that they encompass a number of specializations (or hyponyms) that would result in
t
or more
plausible
documents.
[
2
]
employs a contingency table that quantifies the degree of correlation between each pair of textual terms and a taxonomy modeling term generalizations. Finally,
[
29
]
rely on human judgments to select the most appropriate generalization for each sensitive term based on its context.

Nonetheless, sanitization methods based on “static” knowledge bases have limited applicability due to their relatively narrow coverage of disclosive terms. This limited coverage is particularly salient for named entities, which are rarely present in such knowledge bases, while they are often one of the most crucial type of terms to sanitize. Moreover, the largest knowledge bases, such as YAGO or ODP may not provide consistent generalizations, as they originate from arbitrary categories, labels and descriptions (such as Wikipedia categories) that do not fulfill the subsumption property; and this may hamper the truthfulness of the sanitized outcomes.

2.2
Sanitization with Language Models

More recently, a number of studies have also explored LLMs for text anonymization, both for detecting and for protecting sensitive text spans. Such models successfully overcome the aforementioned coverage issues of knowledge bases as they can (1) generate replacements for any input span and (2) can ensure those replacements can be inserted in the document in a grammatically meaningful manner.

The MAPA anonymization toolkit
[
5
]
is an open source tool capable of both detecting a variety of PII types and replacing them with alternatives sampled from the most likely next tokens based on BERT language models fine-tuned for a variety of European languages and domains including administration, health and law.
[
1
]
relies on the same type of models, but without fine-tuning for PII replacement. Those two approaches, however, generate replacements that are restricted to the same token length as the original sensitive term. This restriction has two important shortcomings. The first is that it rules out replacements that are either shorter or longer (in terms of number of tokens) than the original text span. Furthermore, this restriction may be easily exploited by an adversary seeking to infer the original content of the replaced span.

More recently, larger, instruction fine-tuned language models such as those offered by ChatGPT
[
45
]
have also been proposed for protecting sensitive information. Instead of fine-tuning, a common way to get these models to perform a task is via an instruction (
prompt
) and optional examples. Such prompting methods are also integrated in existing de-identification tools, such as Microsoft Presidio. Given a text, the PII detected with the Presidio pipeline can be replaced with GPT 3.5-generated alternatives in a single step
1
1

1

https://github.com/microsoft/presidio/blob/main/docs/samples/python/synth_data_with_openai.ipynb
.

None of the LLM-based works mentioned above focus explicitly on generating
truthful
replacements – that is, replacements that subsume the semantic content of the original text span. As a result, the selected replacement may significantly distort the original terms’ meaning. A study in this direction on a closely related tasks is described in
[
13
]
, who look at abstracting text spans containing
self-disclosure
, “the communication of personal information to others”. The categories of personal information employed in this study are a fine-grained version of those typically considered by previous anonymization approaches, and mostly fall under demographic attributes. The sensitive text spans in
[
13
]
, however, are often phrases and clauses (written in first person) rather than words or named entities. Their proposed task can be thus viewed as a specialized case of protecting sensitive information. PII protection is performed step-wise, where self-disclosure spans are first detected with a BERT model fine-tuned on a manually annotated dataset. The text spans expressing personal information are then re-written with an open-weights instruct LLM (Llama-2-7B) fine-tuned on a dataset with replacements automatically generated using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.

In contrast, our work leverages open-weights instruct LLMs without additional fine-tuning to iteratively select
truthful
generalizations from a set of alternatives with different abstraction levels, using inference attacks to test whether a given replacement effectively conceals the initial content of the span.

2.3
Evaluation

A standard evaluation strategy for text anonymization methods is to contrast their outcomes against manual annotations, which are considered as ground truths. The anonymization quality is quantified via the precision and recall metrics, which are used as proxies of the preserved utility and the attained privacy protection, respectively
[
24
,
32
,
35
,
16
,
21
]
.

Precision measures the percentage of text spans replaced by the automatic method that human annotators also deemed disclosive. Because any additional replaced text spans are deemed unnecessary, lower precision figures are interpreted as poorer utility preservation. On the other hand, recall quantifies the percentage of disclosive text spans, as identified by human annotators, that the automatic method replaced. Because untouched disclosive spans are considered privacy failures, lower recall figures are understood as weaker privacy protection.

However, these evaluation metrics are only applicable to assess the quality of the detection step, and are unable to assess the suitability of the replacements used for sanitization.
Indeed, evaluating sanitized texts against a manually constructed ground truth is challenging because i) there can be multiple valid (
i.e.
, non-disclosive) combinations of replacements for a given text and, ii) replacement candidates are, quite often, unbounded. Establishing a single ground truth for the evaluation is thus impossible. Moreover, requesting human annotators to provide privacy-preserving replacements (or, at the very least, chose appropriate replacements for predefined lists of candidates) requires even more effort than the –already quite costly– detection of disclosive text spans
[
29
]
. As a result, the few sanitization strategies proposed in the literature are largely untested
[
10
,
20
,
2
,
37
,
38
,
36
,
17
,
29
]
.

3
Text Sanitization Method

Our text sanitization approach, INTACT, is based on instruction-fine-tuned LLMs and breaks down the task into two separate steps:

1.

Generating replacement candidates for disclosive terms with different levels of generalization

2.

Selecting the most appropriate replacement among those candidates through the use of inference attacks.

The purpose of this two-stage process is to select replacements that satisfy three conditions:

1.

they should be
privacy-preserving
, i.e. they should conceal the original content of the span, also to adversaries seeking to infer their values based on the selected replacements and the context they appear in.

2.

they should be
truthful
, i.e. they should subsume the semantic content of the original span.

3.

they should be
as informative as possible
(to maximize the data utility) given that the two above conditions are satisfied.

We will assume throughout this paper a document
D
in which we have detected a set of text spans
T=\{t_{1},t_{2},\allowbreak\ldots,t_{n}\}
that may disclose personal information and ought to be edited. The task of detecting those PII-related spans in text documents is outside the scope of this paper, but has already been studied in numerous works, such as
[
27
,
24
,
32
]
. Common approaches to this task rely on standard NER tools or sequence labeling models tailored for detecting PII-related information. We will also assume that each of those detected text spans
t_{i}
in
D
has been tagged with a corresponding entity type label
l_{i}
, such as
ORG
or
PERSON

3.1
Replacement Generation

In this first step, we prompt an LLM to generate a list of generalized replacement candidates for the provided text span in its context. We explicitly request replacements with different levels of abstraction that the LLM is then required to sort from most to least specific. This sorting is done within the same step in order to limit computational costs. To enhance the quality of the generated candidates, we also include a one-shot example containing a sentence with a target span (of the same label as the span to sanitize) and some possible generalizations. We provide the text span in its local context, defined here as the sentence it occurs in.

More formally, given an open-weights instruction fine-tuned language model
\texttt{LLM}_{\texttt{instr}}
and an input text span
t_{i}
occurring in
D
that should be sanitized, we prompt the LLM to generate a list of
m
replacements candidates
C_{i}=\{c_{i,1},c_{i,2},\ldots,c_{i,m}\}
, where the candidates
c_{i,j}
are sorted by order of abstraction (from the most specific to the most generic). The list of replacement candidates
C_{i}
for the text span
t_{i}
is thus defined as:

C_{i}=\texttt{LLM}_{\texttt{instr}}(p_{C},e_{C},t_{i},s_{i})

(1)

where
p_{C}
is the prompt used to instruct
\texttt{LLM}_{\texttt{instr}}
to generate the candidates,
e_{C}
is the one-shot example,
t_{i}
is the input text span that should be edited, and
s_{i}
is the sentence in
D
containing that text span.

The text spans
\{t_{1},t_{2},\allowbreak\ldots,t_{n}\}
are processed according to their order of occurrence in
D
. For entities that occur multiple times through the same document
D
, we only generate replacements for the first mention of that entity and reuse the selected replacement for all subsequent mentions.

For direct identifiers – such as person names or phone numbers – which can reveal identity even in isolation, replacement candidates are not obtained through Eq.
1

[
19
]
,
C_{i}
of direct identifiers are assigned to either:

1.

a generic token (
e.g.
, “
***
”);

2.

an entity type (
e.g.
,
PERSON
) corresponding to the label
l_{i}
;

3.

a more human-readable variant of this category (
e.g.
, “the person”).

This not only helps maintaining privacy, but it also prevents accidental identification,
i.e.
, generated names or codes matching actual entities.

3.2
Replacement Selection

The second step of the method identifies the
best
replacement
c_{i}^{*}
among the possible replacement candidates
C_{i}
for the original text span
t_{i}
. The best replacement is here defined as the most specific candidate that does not reveal, directly or indirectly, the original content of the span
t_{i}
. In other words, the best replacement should be the most informative replacement among those that do not constitute a disclosure risk. To determine whether a replacement candidate
c_{i,j}
constitutes a disclosure risk, we carry out an
inference attack
– that is, we use the instruction-tuned LLM to produce a set of guesses for the original span given some context. These guesses are then compared to the original span to assess whether the replacement is acceptable (i.e. if none of the guesses match the original span) or whether it would allow an adversary to find back the original text span. Crucially, those guesses are generated by taking into account not only the replacement candidate, but the full (edited) document as context, in order to allow the LLM to take advantage of all information expressed in the document to predict the original content of a given span.

Inference attack

For each text span
t_{i}
and replacement candidate
c_{i,j}
for this span, we generate a set of
p
guesses
G_{i,j}=\{g_{i,j}^{1},g_{i,j}^{2},\allowbreak\ldots,g_{i,j}^{p}\}
using the instruction-tuned LLM:

G_{i,j}=\texttt{LLM}_{\texttt{instr}}(p_{G},e_{G},c_{i,j},D^{\prime})

(2)

in which:

1.

p_{G}
is the prompt employed for the generation of guesses.

2.

e_{G}
is an one-shot example composed of a short purpose-written text, with one span substituted with a synthetic LLM replacement and a set of synthetic LLM guesses.

3.

c_{i,j}
is the candidate replacement for the original input text span
t_{i}
.

4.

D^{\prime}
is the current version of the sanitized document. More precisely,
D^{\prime}
is obtained from the original document
D
by replacing each original text span
t_{j}
with its best replacement
c_{i}^{*}
, if it has already been selected, or with the most specific replacement candidate
c_{i,1}
if is has not.

Matching

We then need to define what constitutes a “successful” guess from the point of view of the adversary. A naive approach would be to define a guess as successful if it returns the exact same string as the original text span. This approach, however, fails to account for guesses that are closely related but not entirely identical to the original string. To capture those variations, we define a
\small\textsc{match}(t_{i},g_{i,j}^{k})
condition, which returns
true
if any of the following conditions hold:

1.

Lemma Overlap
: There is an overlap between the set of lemmas (base forms of words,
e.g.
,
dog
for
dogs
) of
t_{i}
and
g_{i,j}^{k}
.

2.

N-gram Overlap
: If
t_{i}
is a named entity, an overlap exists between the n-grams (sequences of
n
characters) of
t_{i}
and
g_{i,j}^{k}
.

Non-alphabetic tokens, stopwords and high-frequency lemmas being sanitized are excluded from both
t_{i}
and
g_{i,j}^{k}
when computing lemma overlap. Acronyms are handled by merging the first letters of all title-cased words in
t_{i}
into one word, doing the same for
g_{i,j}^{k}
, and expanding the respective lemma lists with these automatically coined (potential) acronyms. In all comparisons, the text spans are normalized to lowercase characters.

Based on this matching function, we can then define a replacement candidate
c_{i,j}
as risky from a privacy perspective if at least one of the guesses matches the original text span
t_{i}
. More formally, we can thus define the following boolean function:

\small\textsc{risky\_replace}(t_{i},c_{i,j})=\begin{cases}\textsf{true}&\text{if }\exists\ g_{i,j}^{k}\in G_{i,j}\text{ where }\small\textsc{match}(t_{i},g_{i,j}^{k})\!=\!\textsf{true}\\
\textsf{false}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

4
Evaluation metrics

As discussed in Section
2.3
, the current metrics for text anonymization evaluation are not suitable to assess the quality of sanitized replacements. To address this shortcoming, we propose two novel automatic evaluation metrics. Instead of relying on manual annotations, our metrics quantify
data utility
as a function of the proportion of semantic content preserved in the selected replacements relative to the original text span, and
privacy protection
as the inverse of the observed residual re-identification risk.

4.1
Utility assessment

The utility purpose of anonymized data is to produce outcomes in downstream tasks that closely align with those resulting from the original data.

In SDC for structured databases
[
19
]
, this alignment is evaluated by measuring the
similarity
between the anonymized and the original data, as the two are known to be correlated. However, while quantifying similarity for structured numerical data is straightforward, doing so for unstructured text data is more challenging.

We propose a new utility metric for text anonymization that quantifies the similarity between an original document and its sanitized version from a semantic perspective, as the preservation of the semantic content of the text is directly related to its utility. Our proposal assesses the impact on utility of these replacements based on two factors:

1.

the
information content
of the disclosive text span to be protected within the original document, which accounts for the amount of semantics encompassed by the original text span.

2.

the
semantic similarity
of the replacement relative to the original text span, which expresses the proportion of semantic content preserved by the replacement.

4.1.1
Information content

In natural language processing, the informativeness of a text span, or its
information content
(IC), can be expressed according to Shannon’s information theory as the negative logarithm of its probability of occurrence
[
40
]
:

\small\textsc{IC}(t)=-log(\Pr(t))

(3)

This implies that the less probable (or predictable) a text span
t
is, the more information it provides. Conversely, if the text span is highly probable (or predictable), its information content is low.

Under this premise, we calculate the information content of a document
D
(that is, the amount of semantics it conveys) by summing the IC values of all its text spans. We name this
text information content
(TIC):

\small\textsc{TIC}(D)=\sum_{t\in D}{\small\textsc{IC}(t)}

(4)

As all text spans we consider the set of non-overlapping segments that are either a noun phrase, a named entity or a word other than a stopword.

We then measure the
relative information content
(RIC) of a text span
t
within a document
D
as the proportion of the text span’s IC relatively to the TIC of
D
:

\small\textsc{RIC}(t,D)=\frac{\small\textsc{IC}(t)}{\small\textsc{TIC}(D)}

(5)

Various methods are possible to practical compute the probability
\Pr(t)
in Eq.
3
, such as using their frequency of appearance within a corpus
[
34
]
(even leveraging the Web as corpus
[
37
,
35
]
), considering the number of hypernyms and/or hyponyms within an ontology
[
39
,
7
]
, or evaluating their predictability with a language model
[
32
]
. The latter approach utilizes the general language understanding capabilities of language models (specifically BERT
[
12
]
) to predict words based on context. The model receives the context of the text span (
e.g.
, the surrounding sentence) with the target text span substituted by the
[MASK]
token. The probability of the text span is then determined by the model’s probability of predicting it from the context. For multi-token text spans, the minimum probability is selected. This predictability technique is the only estimate of
\Pr(t)
that accounts for compositionality, which states that the meaning of an expression is derived from the meanings of its parts and the rules for combining them.
Moreover, this technique is also the only capable of estimating probabilities for any text span, not requiring it to be present in a specific corpus or ontology.

As in
[
32
]
, we rely on BERT (bert-base-uncased
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/google-bert/bert-base-uncased
) for computing the IC of our text spans. However, contrary to
[

]
, here we must compute the IC for all text spans within a document. Using the method proposed in
[
32
]
would involve substituting all text spans with
[MASK]
simultaneously, depriving the model of the necessary context to make accurate probability predictions. To solve this issue, we alternate the
[MASK]

[MASK]
for one of every
N

[MASK]
placements. For instance, with
N=2
, two predictions are needed: one with
[MASK]
applied to the odd text spans while using the even spans as context, and another with
[MASK]
applied to the even spans while using the odd spans as context. Increasing
N
linearly increments the number of predictions (and thus the computational cost), but it is expected to enhance the quality of the probability estimates by providing more context for each prediction.

4.1.2
Semantic similarity

As introduced above, the
semantic similarity
between an original text span and its replacement indicates how well the semantic content of the former has been preserved by the latter. A well-established measure of semantic similarity between text spans consists of calculating the cosine similarity between their embeddings in a language model
[
43
,
11
,
4
]
.

A cosine similarity of 1 corresponds to identical spans, while 0 indicates no particular similarity, and -1 depicts completely unrelated text spans. Given that replacements are not expected to be unrelated to the original text span, we constrain the similarity calculation to the
[0,1]
range:

\small\textsc{Similarity}(t_{i},c_{i}^{*})=\max(0,\small\textsc{cos\_sim}(\small\textsc{embed}(t_{i}),\small\textsc{embed}(c_{i}^{*})))

(6)

Where
t_{i}
is the original text span,
c_{i}^{*}
the selected replacement span, and
embed
is the embedding model.

As embedding model, we leveraged
paraphrase-albert-base-v2
4
4

4

https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/paraphrase-albert-base-v2
in our experiments, an off-the-shelf language model trained with Sentence-BERT
[
33
]
that has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art results in multiple benchmarks for semantic similarity
[
33
,
23
]
.

4.1.3
Text Preserved Similarity

Building upon the aforementioned measures, we now introduce
text preserved similarity
(TPS), an utility metric for text sanitization:

\small\textsc{TPS}(D,D^{\prime})=\sum_{(t_{i},c_{i}^{*})\ \in\ (D,D^{\prime})}\small\textsc{RIC}(t_{i},D)*\small\textsc{Similarity}(t_{i},c_{i}^{*})

(7)

Where
D^{\prime}
represents the sanitized document,
D
the original document,
t_{i}
the original span and
c_{i}^{*}
its selected replacement.

For unedited text spans,
c_{i}^{*}
is identical to
t_{i}
, resulting in a similarity of 1 and thus no loss in utility. TPS can be interpreted as the arithmetic mean of semantic similarities for all text spans, weighted by their relative informativeness. The TPS metric range from 0, indicating a loss of all original content, to 1, where
D^{\prime}
conveys precisely the same meaning as
D
(
e.g.
, no text spans have been altered).

4.2
Privacy assessment

The ultimate goal of data anonymization is to minimize the re-identification risk.
Considering that the greatest disclosure risk comes from the
combination
of disclosive text spans
[
24
]
,
a sound privacy evaluation should consider the residual re-identification risk of the chosen replacements as a whole. In
[
26
]
we proposed a privacy metric for anonymized text, named
text re-identification risk
(TRIR) to precisely account for this.

More specifically, TRIR involves simulating a plausible re-identification attack on the protected documents by leveraging, as background knowledge, a collection of public documents of a population of individuals that encompasses the actual individuals to be protected. For example, if the individuals to be protected are hospital patients and the anonymized documents are their medical reports, all the citizens of the hospital’s town could be the population, and the public documents leveraged as background knowledge could be a collection social media posts of those citizens.

To construct the attack, a language model with a classifier head is first trained to exploit this background knowledge to predict the individual corresponding a given document. This classification model is thus trained to identify the information most closely linked to each individual from the public documents. When presented with an anonymized document, the model can then leverage the learned knowledge to re-identify the corresponding individual. If the predicted public individual is in fact the corresponding individual to be protected, re-identification is successful. TRIR is therefore computed as the re-identification accuracy on the set of anonymized documents.

Matching function

To assess whether the LLM was able to correctly guess the original text span based on its replacement and the rest of the document context, we defined a matching function (see Section

We also check for n-gram overlap if the text span corresponds to a named entity. We choose 4-grams to account for words with the same, longer base (

We evaluated the residual disclosure risk of the strategies by using the TRIR metric introduced in Section

The use of TRIR requires the specification of a body of

The annotated court decisions from TAB were derived from the same source, this corpus constitutes therefore a strict superset of the TAB documents. However, assuming that each adversary has access to this full corpus of rulings is somewhat unrealistic, as such an adversary would already have access to all original documents, and would therefore have no need to re-identify the sanitized versions of those same documents. On the other hand, considering that the knowledge of the adversary strictly encompasses the protected individuals is also unrealistic, as they would rarely known the exact population sample in the protected dataset. As expected, the replacements with the most-specific LLM generalisations yield the worst TRIR scores. However, regardless of the actual TRIR, using non-truthful replacements as Presidio does, may allow an attacker to differentiate between protected and non-protected terms, thereby providing an advantage that can be exploited to conduct case-specific attacks focused on inferring only the protected terms. 5.7
Truthfulness and abstraction level

An important characteristic of the INTACT approach is its emphasis on producing
truth-preserving
replacements whose semantic content subsumes the content of the original text span. Such replacements should therefore correspond to generalisations that encompass and abstract away the original text span. However, the truthfulness and abstraction level of the replacements are nuances that are challenging to evaluate automatically in a reliable manner. Their impact in combination is reflected in the results of our automatic metrics, but these do not provide explicit information about replacement truthfulness and abstraction level separately.

To address this, we conducted a manual evaluation focused on the truthfulness and abstraction level of the selected replacements. This evaluation was performed on a subset of TAB documents, and comprised 600 instances, each consisting of a PII span mention with 70 characters preceding and following it as context.
For each document, we randomly sample a mention for each entity and then select a random subset of all these sampled mentions, using an equal size of 100 instances per each entity type, excluding the
PERSON
and
CODE
categories. The instances were rated by a single evaluator with NLP background, who was also allowed to look up concepts on the internet.

The selected replacements are assessed using two criteria: abstraction level and truthfulness.
A replacement is considered more abstract if it constitutes a
hypernym
or provides fewer details than the original PII span.
A replacement is truthful if it retains the same overall content of the original span. When approximate equivalence can be assumed in the context of the span (
e.g.
,
28 days
and
1 month
), it is counted as truth-preserving. Partially true spans (
e.g.

6
Conclusions and future work

We presented INTACT, a two-stage approach to automatically sanitize disclosive text spans in documents by leveraging instruction-tuned large language models. In contrast with related works, our approach is not limited by the narrow scope of structured knowledge bases and focuses on generating
truthful
replacements, which are of outmost importance in secondary uses of the data, such as medical research, insurance or law. We ensure the replicability of our method by using open-weights LLMs, which can also be run locally to guarantee private and secure handling of sensitive data. Empirical results show that our method provides the best privacy/utility trade-off, while avoiding distorting the original semantic content of the document as other LLM-based approaches do.

We have also proposed automatic utility and privacy metrics for text anonymization, which capture the notion of semantics and disclosure risk better than standard precision and recall metrics, and do not require (costly) manual annotations as ground truth.

In future work, we aim to enhance both the ordering of replacement candidates and their grammatical integration within sentences. Even though a grammatically correct output is specifically requested in the replacement generation prompt, some replacements would still produce ungrammatical phrases (e.g., double prepositions like
in in
) if inserted, partly due to inconsistencies in the inclusion of these within the manually annotated spans of the dataset used. Additionally, while the replacement suggestions generally range from the most specific to the most abstract, the ordering isn’t always consistent. We also plan to explore additional methods for matching the original spans against those guessed by the LLM. These enhancements could potentially be implemented through additional LLM steps to further improve the quality of the sanitized text.
