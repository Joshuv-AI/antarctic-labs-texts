A monotone finite element method for an elliptic distributed optimal control problem with a convection-dominated state equation
SeongHee Jeong, Seulip Lee, Sijing Liu

Abstract
We propose and analyze a monotone finite element method for an elliptic distributed optimal control problem constrained by a convection-diffusion-reaction equation in the convection-dominated regime. The method is based on the edge-averaged finite element (EAFE) scheme, which is known to preserve the discrete maximum principle for convection-diffusion problems. We show that the EAFE discretization inherits the monotonicity property of the continuous problem and consequently preserves the desired-state bounds at the discrete level, ensuring that the numerical optimal state remains stable and free of nonphysical oscillations. The discrete formulation is analyzed using a combination of the EAFE consistency result and a discrete inf-sup condition, which together guarantee well-posedness and yield the optimal convergence order. Comprehensive numerical experiments are presented to confirm the theoretical findings and to demonstrate the robustness of the proposed scheme in the convection-dominated regimes. Keywords: Edge-averaged finite element (EAFE), elliptic optimal control problems, convection-dominated problems, monotone schemes, desired-state bounds, bound-preserving

Introduction

In this work, we consider an elliptic distributed optimal control problem constrained by a convection-diffusion-reaction equation in the convection-dominated regime. Elliptic distributed optimal control problems and their numerical approximations have been extensively studied in the literature
[
9
,
13
,
14
,
34
,
12
,
25
]
, with numerous applications in engineering, aeronautics, aerodynamics, and related fields, see
[
41
,
33
]
for comprehensive discussions. Among these problems, convection-dominated optimal control problems are particularly challenging, as strong convective effects substantially influence both the analytical properties and the numerical approximation. Optimal control problems constrained by convection-dominated equations have been investigated in
[
31
,
24
,
35
,
9
]
, which revealed essential differences between solving a single convection-dominated equation and solving an optimal control problem constrained by such an equation.

A key analytical observation in this work is the role of the maximum principle, one of the fundamental properties of elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs).
This principle is guaranteed by a sufficient condition known as the monotonicity property, which asserts that the inverse of the elliptic operator is nonnegative.
Although this structure is inherent at the continuous level, it has not always been emphasized in the optimal control literature, particularly in the convection-dominated setting.
When the monotonicity property is considered in the saddle-point system associated with the optimal control problem, the nonnegativity of the operator’s inverse induces a stability relation between the desired state
y_{d}
and the optimal state
\bar{y}
:

\begin{cases}0\leq\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})\leq y_{d}(\mathbf{x})&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\[3.01385pt]
y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.\end{cases}

These desired-state bounds are not additional constraints imposed in the optimization formulation; rather, they arise naturally from the intrinsic stability of the elliptic operator and the coupled structure of the PDE–constrained optimal control problem, in which the state and adjoint variables are linked through the saddle-point formulation.
As a consequence, the optimal state inherits the same monotone behavior as the underlying PDE, leading to stable and physically consistent solution behavior even in convection-dominated regimes.

From a numerical perspective, convection-dominated equations are well known to pose significant challenges in the design of stable and accurate numerical schemes.
The presence of sharp boundary layers near the outflow boundary often leads to spurious oscillations in the numerical solution if no special stabilization is employed
[
40
,
39
,
27
,
28
]
.
To address these difficulties, a wide range of stabilization techniques within the finite element framework have been proposed, including the upwind/Petrov-Galerkin (UPG) methods
[
19
,
10
,
6
]
, the edge-averaged schemes
[
42
,
1
,
20
]
, local projection methods
[
29
,
32
]
, the algebraic flux corrections (AFC) method
[
7
]
, and discontinuous Galerkin (DG) formulations
[
30
,
4
,
3
,
2
]
, among many others
[
40
]
.
The same numerical difficulties arise in optimal control problems governed by convection-dominated equations. However, the associated saddle-point system consists of two coupled convection-dominated equations with opposite convection directions, which makes the design of stable numerical schemes even more delicate.
As demonstrated in
[
31
,
24
]
, boundary layers can induce nonphysical oscillations that propagate into the interior of the domain when Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed strongly, even in the presence of stabilization. DG methods are effective in mitigating this issue, as they impose boundary conditions weakly and naturally incorporate upwind stabilization
[
35
,
31
]
. Nevertheless, DG methods may overlook extremely thin boundary layers, which is undesirable in applications where accurate resolution of the layer structure is required, even on relatively coarse meshes.

To address this numerical challenge, we adopt a monotone conforming finite element method, namely the edge-averaged finite element (EAFE) scheme
[
42
]
.
This monotone discretization preserves the desired-state bounds in the discrete setting, thereby eliminating nonphysical oscillations and yielding stable, physically consistent numerical solutions.

Our contributions in this work are twofold.
First, we discretize the optimal control problem using the EAFE method and derive a concrete error estimate.
To achieve this, we combine the consistency results of
[
42
]
with the discrete inf-sup condition established in
[
13
]
.
The inf-sup condition not only guarantees the well-posedness of the discrete problem,
but also provides a direct framework for deriving
a priori
error estimates.
This analysis can be regarded as an extension of the results in
[
42
]
to optimal control problems.
We believe this is the first work to establish a concrete error estimate for the EAFE method applied to optimal control problems.
While our estimate does not explicitly track the dependence on the diffusion coefficient
\varepsilon
and convection field
\bm{\zeta}
, which would require a more delicate analysis, we refer to
[
35
]
for results that include such parameter-dependent estimates.
Nevertheless, we show that, for sufficiently small
h
, the EAFE method achieves the optimal convergence order in the
H^{1}
norm, consistent with the findings in
[
42
]
.

Second, we prove that the monotone EAFE scheme preserves the desired-state bounds in the discrete setting, thereby guaranteeing the absence of spurious oscillations.
Several numerical studies have addressed convection-dominated optimal control problems using stabilization techniques such as streamline upwind/Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG)
[
22
,
24
]
, AFC
[
8
]
, and DG formulations
[
31
,
44
]
.
While these approaches successfully demonstrated numerically that oscillations can be mitigated, a rigorous analytical explanation of this property has not yet been established.
To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first mathematical proof that a monotone scheme preserves the stability relation (desired-state bounds) and completely eliminates nonphysical oscillations for optimal control problems constrained by convection-dominated equations.
This monotonicity property is ensured by the
M
-matrix condition of the stiffness matrix associated with the discrete convection-diffusion operator, which determines the direction of the inequalities in the discrete saddle-point system.

2
Continuous Problems and Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the notation and basic definitions used throughout the paper, formulate the continuous optimal control problem, and recall key analytical properties such as the maximum principle and the associated desired-state bounds.
These results provide the theoretical foundation for the monotone discretization and analysis developed in the later sections.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. For any real number
s\geq 0
, we denote by
H^{s}(\mathcal{D})
the Sobolev space of order
s
, equipped with the norm norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{D})}
and seminorm
|\cdot|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{D})}
. In particular,
H^{0}(\mathcal{D})=L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product.
We denote by
H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D})
the subspace of
H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
consisting of functions with vanishing trace on
\partial\mathcal{D}
.

2.1
Optimal Control Problems

We consider the following elliptic distributed optimal control problem. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded convex polygonal domain, let
y_{d}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a given desired state, and let
\beta>0
be a fixed regularization parameter. The goal is to find the optimal state-control pair
(\bar{y},\bar{u})
that minimizes the cost functional:

\left(\bar{y},\bar{u}\right)=\argmin_{(y,u)}\left[\frac{1}{2}\|y-y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\beta}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right],

(2.1)

subject to the constraint that
(y,u)\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
if and only if the state equation

a(y,v)=(u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(2.2)

is satisfied. The bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined by

a(y,v):=\int_{\Omega}\left(\varepsilon\nabla y+\bm{\zeta}y\right)\cdot\nabla v\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega}\gamma yv\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x},

(2.3)

where
\varepsilon({\bf x})\geq\varepsilon_{0}>0
, the vector field
\bm{\zeta}({\bf x})\in[W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)]^{2}
, and the reaction coefficient
\gamma({\bf x})\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
is nonnegative.
We further assume that

\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\bm{\zeta}\geq\gamma_{0}>0,

(2.4)

which guarantees the well-posedness of the state equation (
2.2
).
In this work, we focus on the convection-dominated regime, characterized by

\|\varepsilon\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\ll\|\bm{\zeta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Following
[
33
,
41
]
, the solution of (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) is characterized by

\displaystyle a(q,\bar{p})

\displaystyle=(\bar{y}-y_{d},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle\forall q\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\bar{p}+\beta\bar{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle a(\bar{y},z)

\displaystyle=(\bar{u},z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle\forall z\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.5c)

where
\bar{p}
is the adjoint state.
After eliminating
\bar{u}
, we arrive at the saddle-point problem

\displaystyle a(q,\bar{p})-(\bar{y},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-(y_{d},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle\forall q\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.6a)

\displaystyle-(\bar{p},z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\beta a(\bar{y},z)

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall z\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.6b)

By standard saddle-point theory
[
13
,
18
,
5
]
, the system (
2.6
) has a unique solution. For simplicity, let
\beta=1
and we consider the following saddle-point problem,

\displaystyle a(q,\bar{p})-(\bar{y},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-(y_{d},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle\forall q\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.7a)

\displaystyle-(\bar{p},z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(\bar{y},z)

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall z\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.7b)

Note that, by integration by parts, the saddle-point problem (
2.7
) involves two convection-diffusion operators that are formally adjoint to each other, acting in opposite convection directions. Specifically, for all
(q,z)\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
,

\displaystyle\left[(\varepsilon\nabla\bar{p},\nabla q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(\bm{\zeta}\bar{p},\nabla q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+((\gamma-\nabla\cdot\bm{\zeta})\bar{p},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]-(\bar{y},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-(y_{d},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle-(\bar{p},z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\left[(\varepsilon\nabla\bar{y},\nabla z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\bm{\zeta}\bar{y},\nabla z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\gamma\bar{y},z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

The saddle-point problem (
2.7
) can be written in the concise form

\mathcal{B}((\bar{p},\bar{y}),(q,z))=-(y_{d},q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall(q,z)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.9)

where

\mathcal{B}((p,y),(q,z)):=a(q,p)-(y,q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(p,z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a(y,z).

(2.10)

Introducing the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
allows the coupled state-adjoint system to be represented as a single variational problem, making the analysis of discrete inf-sup conditions and convergence more transparent and structurally unified.

2.2
Maximum Principle and Stability via Desired-State Bounds

We discuss a fundamental mathematical property of the optimal control problem constrained by the state equation. The state equation involves the elliptic operator

\mathcal{L}y=-\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\nabla y+\bm{\zeta}y)+\gamma y.

Together with the boundary condition
y\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, the weak maximum principle ensures that the inverse of
\mathcal{L}
is nonnegative. In other words, even in the convection-dominated regime, if
\mathcal{L}y(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
, then
y(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
.
This property is often referred to as the monotonicity of the operator
[
23
, Theorem 2, Section 6.4]
.

From the saddle-point system (
2.7
), we obtain the following implications: For all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})-y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\quad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\bar{p}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,

\displaystyle\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})-y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\quad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\bar{p}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0.

Consequently, the optimal state
\bar{y}
satisfies the following bounds determined by the desired state
y_{d}
:

\begin{cases}0\leq\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})\leq y_{d}(\mathbf{x})&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\[3.01385pt]
y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq\bar{y}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(2.11)

We call these inequalities as the
desired-state bounds
.

Remark 2.1
.

It is important to emphasize that these bounds are not imposed as explicit constraints in the optimization problem.
Rather, they arise naturally as a stability property of the optimal control problem, inherited from the saddle-point problem and the monotonicity of the elliptic operator. In other words, the output of the optimization problem (the optimal state
\bar{y}
) remains bounded by the given data of the problem (the desired state
y_{d}
), which directly reflects the concept of stability even in convection-dominated regimes.

A monotone numerical scheme provides a discretization of
\mathcal{L}
that preserves its monotonicity property; that is, if
\mathcal{L}_{h}y_{h}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
, then
y_{h}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
. Such schemes are well known to yield stable numerical solutions for convection-dominated problems without spurious oscillations (see
[
42
,
20
]
for further details). For optimal control problems constrained by convection-dominated state equations, we demonstrate that the monotone scheme preserves the desired-state bounds at the discrete level, preventing spurious oscillations (see Section
3.3
).

3
A Monotone Scheme for Optimal Control Problems

In this section, we develop and analyze a monotone discretization for optimal control problems constrained by convection-diffusion equations. Our approach builds on the edge-averaged finite element (EAFE) method, originally introduced as a monotone scheme for convection-diffusion problems, and extends it to the optimal control setting. We first formulate the discrete saddle-point system based on the EAFE bilinear form, and then show that the resulting scheme inherits the stability properties of the continuous problem. In particular, we establish that the discrete optimal state satisfies the discrete version of the desired-state bounds, which ensures robustness of the scheme and prevents spurious oscillations in convection-dominated regimes.

3.1
Edge-Averaged Finite Element Discretization

The EAFE discretization, originally introduced in
[
42
]
, is a well-known monotone scheme for convection-diffusion problems. We present a flux-based formulation of the EAFE method that employs locally defined edge potentials together with mass lumping in the Nédélec space. This approach is particularly effective in convection-dominated regimes, where it provides stable and physically consistent numerical solutions.

For the discrete setting, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular triangulation of
\Omega
, where each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a triangle.
For any element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote its diameter by
h_{T}
, and let
h=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}
denote the mesh size of the triangulation.

3.1.1
Exponentially Fitted Flux and Its Approximation

We first define the flux by

J(y):=\varepsilon\nabla y+\bm{\zeta}y.

If there exists a scalar function
\psi
such that
\nabla\psi=\varepsilon^{-1}\bm{\zeta}
, then the flux can be rewritten as

J(y)=\kappa\nabla(e^{\psi}y),

where
\kappa({\bf x}):=\varepsilon({\bf x})e^{-\psi({\bf x})}
.
In general, when
\textbf{curl}(\varepsilon^{-1}\bm{\zeta})\not=0
, such a global potential function
\psi
may not exist. However, along any line segment
L
between
{\bf x}_{0}
and
{\bf x}_{1}
, we can define a one-dimensional edge potential
\psi_{L}
through the relation

\psi_{L}({\bf x}_{1})-\psi_{L}({\bf x}_{0})=\frac{1}{|L|}\int_{L}\varepsilon^{-1}(\bm{\zeta}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{L})\;{\rm d}s,

(3.1)

where
\bm{\tau}_{L}={\bf x}_{1}-{\bf x}_{0}
is the scaled tangent vector to
L
with
|\bm{\tau}_{L}|=|L|
.
With this definition, we set
\kappa_{L}:=\varepsilon e^{-\psi_{L}}
, and the flux along
L
satisfies

\left.J(y)\cdot\bm{\tau}_{L}\right|_{L}=\left.\kappa_{L}\nabla(e^{\psi_{L}}y)\cdot\bm{\tau}_{L}\right|_{L}.

For the discrete formulation, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a conforming triangulation of the domain
\Omega
, and let
V_{h}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
denote the associated piecewise linear finite element space defined on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. In the discrete setting, we consider the local flux

\left.J(y_{h})\right|_{T}=\left.(\varepsilon\nabla y_{h}+\bm{\zeta}y_{h})\right|_{T}

for
y_{h}\in V_{h}
and each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
For each edge
E\subset\partial T
, let
\bm{\tau}_{E}
denote the scaled tangent vector such that
|\bm{\tau}_{E}|=|E|
.
We then define an edgewise potential function
\psi_{E}
according to the relation (
3.1
).
With this definition, the flux along
E
can be expressed as

\left.J(y_{h})\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E}\right|_{E}=\left.\kappa_{E}\nabla(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E}\right|_{E}.

(3.2)

We denote by
\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
the lowest-order local Nédélec space, consisting of functions of the form

\mathbf{p}_{0}+\mathbf{x}^{\perp}q_{0},\quad\mathbf{p}_{0}\in(\mathcal{P}_{0}(T))^{2},\ q_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(T),

where
\mathbf{x}^{\perp}
denotes a
90^{\circ}
rotation of
\mathbf{x}
such that
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\perp}=0
.
For any
{\bf v}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
, the degrees of freedom are defined edgewise by

\text{dof}_{E}(\mathbf{v})=\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E}\;{\rm d}s=\left.(\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E})\right|_{E}.

The canonical basis functions
\{\bm{\chi}_{E_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{3}\subset\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
are then characterized by

\text{dof}_{E_{i}}(\bm{\chi}_{E_{j}})=\delta_{ij},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq 3.

Applying
\text{dof}_{E}
to the scaled flux
\kappa_{E}^{-1}J(y_{h})|_{T}
in (
3.2
), we obtain

\text{dof}_{E}(\kappa_{E}^{-1}J(y_{h}))=\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h}):=(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})({\bf x}_{j})-(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})({\bf x}_{i}),

(3.3)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}
are the endpoints of edge
E
, and
\bm{\tau}_{E}={\bf x}_{j}-{\bf x}_{i}
.
Based on this relation, we define the flux approximation
J_{T}(y_{h})\in\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
for
J(y_{h})|_{T}
by prescribing its degrees of freedom along each edge
E\subset\partial T
:

\text{dof}_{E}(J_{T}(y_{h}))=\left.(J_{T}(y_{h})\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E})\right|_{E}=H(\kappa_{E})\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h}),

(3.4)

where
H(\kappa_{E})
denotes the harmonic average of
\kappa_{E}=\varepsilon e^{-\psi_{E}}
over the edge
E
, defined as

H(\kappa_{E})=\left(\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\kappa_{E}^{-1}\;{\rm d}s\right)^{-1}.

(3.5)

Thus, the flux approximation
J_{T}(y_{h})\in\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
can be written in terms of the canonical basis functions as

J_{T}(y_{h})=\sum_{E\subset\partial T}\text{dof}_{E}(J_{T}(y_{h}))\bm{\chi}_{E}=\sum_{E\subset\partial T}H({\kappa}_{E})\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})\bm{\chi}_{E}.

Moreover, since
\left.\nabla v_{h}\right|_{T}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
, it admits a similar representation:

\nabla v_{h}=\sum_{E\subset\partial T}(\nabla v_{h}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E})\bm{\chi}_{E}=\sum_{E\subset\partial T}\delta_{E}(v_{h})\bm{\chi}_{E}.

3.1.2
Edge-Averaged Finite Element (EAFE) Scheme

Let
\{\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}_{i=1}^{3}\subset\mathcal{P}_{1}(T)
denote the barycentric coordinates associated with the vertices
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
of an element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
We begin by introducing a mass-lumping approximation for the local mass matrix in the Nédélec space
\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
:

\int_{T}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}\;{\rm d}\mathbf{x}\approx\sum_{E\subset\partial T}\omega_{E}^{T}\left.(\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E})\right|_{E}\left.(\mathbf{w}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{E})\right|_{E},\quad\forall{\bf v},{\bf w}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}(T),

(3.6)

where the edge weights
\omega_{E}^{T}
are defined by

\omega_{E}^{T}=-\int_{T}\nabla\lambda_{i}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{j}\;{\rm d}\mathbf{x}\quad\text{for the edge $E$ with }\bm{\tau}_{E}=\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i}.

Since both
J_{T}(y_{h})
and
\nabla v_{h}|_{T}
belong to
\mathcal{N}_{0}(T)
, we apply the mass-lumping approximation to their inner product:

\int_{T}J_{T}(y_{h})\cdot\nabla v_{h}\;{\rm d}\mathbf{x}\approx\sum_{E\subset\partial T}\omega_{E}^{T}H({\kappa}_{E})\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})\delta_{E}(v_{h}).

This leads to the definition of the local EAFE bilinear form:

a_{h}^{T}(y_{h},v_{h}):=\sum_{E\subset\partial T}\omega_{E}^{T}H(\kappa_{E})\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})\delta_{E}(v_{h}),

where
H(\kappa_{E})
is the harmonic average of
\kappa_{E}
on edge
E
, as defined in (
3.5
). The corresponding global EAFE bilinear form is then given by

a_{h}(y_{h},v_{h})=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}a_{h}^{T}(y_{h},v_{h}).

(3.7)

Lemma 3.1

(Consistency estimate)
.

Assume that
\varepsilon\in W^{1,\infty}(T)
and
\bm{\zeta}\in[W^{1,\infty}(T)]^{2}
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\left|a(y_{h},v_{h})-a_{h}(y_{h},v_{h})\right|\leq Ch\lVert y_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert v_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(3.8)

A proof of this estimate can be found in
[
42
]
.

Remark 3.2
.

Regarding the bilinear forms, note that
\bar{\kappa}_{E}>0
and that

\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}\lambda_{i})\delta_{E}(\lambda_{j})=-e^{\psi_{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i})}<0.

This implies that the off-diagonal entries of the stiffness matrix associated with the global bilinear form are nonpositive if and only if

\omega_{E}^{T}+\omega_{E}^{T^{\prime}}\geq 0

for every interior edge
E=\partial T\cap\partial T^{\prime}
.
This condition is equivalent to the Delaunay condition on the triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
in two dimensions (see
[
42
]
for further details).
Therefore, the stiffness matrix corresponding to the EAFE bilinear form is an
M
-matrix if and only if the stiffness matrix for the Poisson problem (with weights
-\omega_{E}^{T}
) is also an
M
-matrix. In particular, any invertible
M
-matrix has a positive inverse.

Remark 3.3
.

For implementation purposes, the coefficients
\varepsilon
and
\bm{\zeta}
are approximated along each edge
E\subset\partial T
by their midpoint averages, denoted by
\varepsilon_{E}
and
\bm{\zeta}_{E}
, respectively.
For example,

\varepsilon_{E}=\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\varepsilon(\mathbf{x}_{j})}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\zeta}_{E}=\frac{\bm{\zeta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\bm{\zeta}(\mathbf{x}_{j})}{2},

where
{\bf x}_{i}
and
{\bf x}_{j}
are the endpoints of edge
E
.
The corresponding edgewise potential function is then defined as

\psi_{E}(\mathbf{x})={\varepsilon_{E}}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{E}\cdot\mathbf{x},

which is uniquely determined up to an additive constant.
With this approximation, the degrees of freedom in (
3.4
) can be explicitly evaluated as

H(\kappa_{E})\delta_{E}(e^{\psi_{E}}y_{h})=\varepsilon_{E}\mathbb{B}({\varepsilon_{E}}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{E}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}))y_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\varepsilon_{E}\mathbb{B}({\varepsilon_{E}}^{-1}\bm{\zeta}_{E}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i}))y_{h}({\bf x}_{i}),

(3.9)

where
\mathbb{B}(x)
denotes the Bernoulli function,

\mathbb{B}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}&x\not=0,\\[8.61108pt]
1&x=0.\end{array}\right.

(3.10)

3.2
A Monotone Scheme for Optimal Control Problems

We now present the monotone discretization for the optimal control problem. The discrete problem is to find
(\bar{p}_{h},\bar{y}_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{h}((\bar{p}_{h},\bar{y}_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))=-(y_{d},q_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall(q_{h},z_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h},

(3.11)

where the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as

\mathcal{B}_{h}((p,y),(q,z))=a_{h}(q,p)-(y,q)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(p,z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a_{h}(y,z),

(3.12)

with
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
3.7
).
Equivalently, this can be expressed as the following saddle-point system:

\displaystyle a_{h}(q_{h},\bar{p}_{h})-(\bar{y}_{h},q_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-(y_{d},q_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in V_{h},

(3.13a)

\displaystyle-(\bar{p}_{h},z_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a_{h}(\bar{y}_{h},z_{h})

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall z_{h}\in V_{h}.

(3.13b)

Remark 3.4
.

Note that
a_{h}(q,p)
may not be the EAFE discretization of the bilinear form
a(q,p)
. However, we define our discrete problem using
a_{h}(q,p)
so that we can utilize the dual property in the analysis.

Remark 3.5
.

We could also consider a general saddle-point formulation: find
(p,y)\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
such that

\mathcal{B}((p,y),(q,z))=(f,q)+(g,z),\quad\forall(q,z)\in{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\times{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)},

(3.14)

where
(f,g)\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}\times{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
2.10
). The EAFE discretization of (
3.14
) is to find
(p_{h},y_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{h}((p_{h},y_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))=(f,q_{h})+(g,z_{h}),\quad\forall(q_{h},z_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}.

(3.15)

This general formulation is more practical if an external force is included in the state equation (
2.2
), it is also useful when designing numerical experiments.

3.3
Preservation of Desired-State Bounds

We now establish a discrete version of the desired-state bounds (
2.11
), demonstrating that they are preserved by a monotone scheme for optimal control problems subject to convection-dominated state equations.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\bar{y}_{h}
be the discrete optimal state of the saddle-point problem (
3.13
), and let
y_{d}
denote the desired state.
For each vertex
\mathbf{x}_{i}
of the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, let
\phi_{i}\in V_{h}
be the corresponding canonical nodal basis function, where
1\leq i\leq N_{V}
and
N_{V}
is the number of vertices in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Then, for every
1\leq i\leq N_{V}
, the discrete optimal state satisfies the inequalities

\begin{cases}0\leq(\bar{y}_{h},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq(y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\[2.15277pt]
(y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq(\bar{y}_{h},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Additionally, the discrete adjoint state
\bar{p}_{h}
satisfies the inequalities

\begin{cases}\bar{p}_{h}\leq 0&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\[2.15277pt]
\bar{p}_{h}\geq 0&\text{for }y_{d}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

Define the coefficient vectors corresponding to the discrete optimal state
\bar{y}_{h}
and the adjoint state
\bar{p}_{h}
:

\bar{\mathsf{y}}=\left[\bar{y}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ \bar{y}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{2}),\ \cdots,\ \bar{y}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{N_{V}})\right]^{\top},\quad\bar{\mathsf{p}}=\left[\bar{p}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ \bar{p}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{2}),\ \cdots,\ \bar{p}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{N_{V}})\right]^{\top}.

The discrete saddle-point problem (
3.13
) with the EAFE discretization leads to the linear system

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}^{\top}\bar{\mathsf{p}}-\mathsf{M}\bar{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{y}_{\mathsf{d}}),

(3.16a)

\displaystyle-\mathsf{M}\bar{\mathsf{p}}-\mathsf{A}\bar{\mathsf{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{0},

(3.16b)

where
\mathsf{A}_{ij}=a_{h}(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})
is the stiffness matrix satisfying the
M
-matrix condition,
\mathsf{M}_{ij}=(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is the mass matrix with nonnegative entries, and
(\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{y}_{d}))_{i}=(y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is the right-hand side vector for
1\leq i,j\leq N_{V}
.

Consider first the case
y_{d}\leq 0
and assume

(\bar{y}_{h}-y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq N_{V}.

(3.17)

Then,
\mathsf{M}\bar{\mathsf{y}}-\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{y}_{\mathsf{d}})\geq\mathsf{0}
, i.e., each component of this vector is nonnegative.
Since
\mathsf{A}
is an invertible
M
-matrix with a nonnegative inverse,

\bar{\mathsf{p}}\geq(\mathsf{A}^{\top})^{-1}(\mathsf{M}\bar{\mathsf{y}}-\mathsf{f}(\mathsf{y}_{\mathsf{d}}))\geq\mathsf{0}.

From (
3.16b
), we obtain
\mathsf{A}\bar{\mathsf{y}}=-\mathsf{M}\bar{\mathsf{p}}
, and the
M
-matrix property implies

\bar{\mathsf{y}}=\mathsf{A}^{-1}\mathsf{M}(-\bar{\mathsf{p}})\leq\mathsf{0},

hence
\mathsf{M}\bar{\mathsf{y}}\leq\mathsf{0}
. Combining this with assumption (
3.17
), we deduce

(y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq(\bar{y}_{h},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0.

In the opposite case
y_{d}\geq 0
, if

(\bar{y}_{h}-y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq N_{V},

then an identical argument yields

(y_{d},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq(\bar{y}_{h},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq 0.

∎

Remark 3.7
.

This result in Theorem
3.6
is consistent with the desired-state bounds satisfied by the continuous optimal state
\bar{y}
.
Since each canonical basis function
\phi_{i}
is nonnegative, the
L^{2}
inner products
(\bar{y}_{h},\phi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
reveal that the discrete optimal state
\bar{y}_{h}
is bounded strongly on one side by zero and weakly on the other side by the desired state
y_{d}
.
Thus, the preservation of desired-state bounds in the discrete setting ensures that the discrete optimal state
\bar{y}_{h}
remains free from spurious oscillations, as such oscillations would otherwise produce overshoots or undershoots beyond the desired-state bounds.

4
Error Analysis

In this section, we analyze the well-posedness and convergence of the EAFE discretization (
3.11
) for the optimal control problem (
2.7
).
We first establish the discrete inf-sup condition for the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
, which guarantees the stability and uniqueness of the discrete solution.
We then derive convergence results based on the consistency of the EAFE bilinear form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
3.8
) and the regularity of the continuous solution.

4.1
Well-Posedness of the Discrete Problem

We now establish a discrete inf-sup condition for (
3.11
), ensuring its well-posedness via standard saddle-point theory (see
[
18
,
5
]
). We follow the approach of
[
42
, Section 6]
and
[
13
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
(p_{h},y_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}
and for sufficiently small
h
,

\sup_{(q_{h},z_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{h}((p_{h},y_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))}{\|q_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|z_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\geq C\left(\|p_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|y_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

(4.1)

Proof.

Given
(p_{h},y_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}
, choose the test functions
q_{h}=p_{h}-y_{h}
and
z_{h}=-p_{h}-y_{h}
in the bilinear form (
3.12
). Then, we obtain

\mathcal{B}_{h}((p_{h},y_{h}),(p_{h}-y_{h},-p_{h}-y_{h}))=a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})+(y_{h},y_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(p_{h},p_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+a_{h}(y_{h},y_{h}).

(4.2)

We estimate only the term
a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})
, since the analysis for
a_{h}(y_{h},y_{h})
is identical. Following
[
42
, Lemma 6.2]
, we decompose

a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})=a(p_{h},p_{h})+\left[a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})-a(p_{h},p_{h})\right].

(4.3)

By integration by parts and the assumption (
2.4
), we obtain

a(p_{h},p_{h})=\|\varepsilon\nabla p_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{\Omega}\left(\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\bm{\zeta}\right)p_{h}^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathbf{x}\geq\varepsilon_{0}\|\nabla p_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma_{0}\|p_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(4.4)

Furthermore, by the consistency estimate (
3.8
), it follows that

|a(p_{h},p_{h})-a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})|\leq Ch\|p_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

(4.5)

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
h
.
For sufficiently small
h
, combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) yields

a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})=a(p_{h},p_{h})+[a_{h}(p_{h},p_{h})-a(p_{h},p_{h})]\geq C\|p_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(4.6)

Consequently, we obtain

\mathcal{B}_{h}((p_{h},y_{h}),(p_{h}-y_{h},-p_{h}-y_{h}))\geq C\left(\|p_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|y_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

(4.7)

Using the parallelogram identity,

\|p_{h}-y_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|p_{h}+y_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=2\left((\|p_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|y_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right),

(4.8)

we conclude that the discrete inf-sup condition (
4.1
) follows immediately from (
4.7
) and (
4.8
).
∎

4.2
Convergence Results

Based on the discrete inf-sup condition established in Lemma
4.1
, we derive the following error estimates for the EAFE discretization of the optimal control problem.

Let
I_{h}:H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow V_{h}
be the standard nodal interpolation operator. Then, the standard approximation estimates hold (cf.
[
16
]
):

\displaystyle\|\bar{y}-I_{h}\bar{y}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{p}-I_{h}\bar{p}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\big(\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{p}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\big),

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\|\bar{y}-I_{h}\bar{y}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{p}-I_{h}\bar{p}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\big(\|\bar{y}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{p}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\big),

(4.9b)

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
h
.
In addition, the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the continuity condition

|a(y,v)|\leq C\lVert y\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert v\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

(4.10)

with constant
C>0
independent of
h
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\bar{p},\bar{y})
be the solution of (
2.9
) and
(\bar{p}_{h},\bar{y}_{h})
be the solution of (
3.11
).
Assume that
\bar{p},\bar{y}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
. Then, for
h
sufficiently small, the following error bound holds:

\|\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq Ch.

(4.11)

Proof.

By choosing
p_{h}=I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h}
and
y_{h}=I_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}
in the discrete inf-sup condition (
4.1
) of Lemma
4.1
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|I_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\sup_{(q_{h},z_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h},I_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))}{\|q_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\|z_{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}.

From the definition of the discrete bilinear form
\mathcal{B}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
, together with (
3.11
) and (
2.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h},I_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p},I_{h}\bar{y}),(q_{h},z_{h}))-\mathcal{B}_{h}((\bar{p}_{h},\bar{y}_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p},I_{h}\bar{y}),(q_{h},z_{h}))+(y_{d},q_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p},I_{h}\bar{y}),(q_{h},z_{h}))-\mathcal{B}((\bar{p},\bar{y}),(q_{h},z_{h})).

Expanding
\mathcal{B}_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathcal{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we decompose the difference into four contributions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p},I_{h}\bar{y}),(q_{h},z_{h}))-\mathcal{B}((\bar{p},\bar{y}),(q_{h},z_{h}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\ \underbrace{a_{h}(q_{h},I_{h}\bar{p})-a(q_{h},\bar{p})}_{(\textrm{I})}\ +\ \underbrace{(\bar{y}-I_{h}\bar{y},q_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}_{\textrm{(II)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad+\ \underbrace{(\bar{p}-I_{h}\bar{p},z_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}_{\textrm{(III)}}\ +\ \underbrace{a(\bar{y},z_{h})-a_{h}(I_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})}_{\textrm{(IV)}}.

Estimate of
(I)
:
Applying the triangle inequality, the EAFE consistency estimate (
3.8
), the continuity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
(
4.10
), and the interpolation error bounds (
4.9
) (neglecting
h^{2}
-terms for sufficiently small
h
), we obtain

(I)

\displaystyle\leq|a_{h}(q_{h},I_{h}\bar{p})-a(q_{h},I_{h}\bar{p})|+|a(q_{h},I_{h}\bar{p})-a(q_{h},\bar{p})|

\displaystyle\leq Ch\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert I_{h}\bar{p}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert\bar{p}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

Estimate of
(II)
:
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the interpolation estimate (
4.9
),

\displaystyle\textrm{(II)}\leq\lVert\bar{y}-I_{n}\bar{y}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{2}\lVert\bar{y}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Estimate of
(III)
:
Similarly,

\textrm{(III)}\leq Ch^{2}\lVert\bar{p}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Estimate of
(IV)
:
Proceeding as in (I),

(IV)

\displaystyle\leq|a(\bar{y},z_{h})-a(I_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})|+|a(I_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-a_{h}(I_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert{\bar{y}-I_{h}\bar{y}}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+Ch\lVert{I_{h}\bar{y}}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\lVert\bar{y}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Collecting the above estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{h}((I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h},I_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}),(q_{h},z_{h}))\leq Ch\left(\lVert\bar{p}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\bar{y}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)\left(\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right).

Substituting into the discrete inf-sup condition yields the desired bound:

\displaystyle\lVert I_{h}\bar{p}-\bar{p}_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\lVert I_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq Ch\left(\lVert\bar{p}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\bar{y}\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

The estimate (
4.11
) shows that, for sufficiently small
h
, the EAFE discretization (
3.11
) converges with first-order accuracy, i.e., at a rate of
O(h)
. This result does not account for the influence of the coefficients
\varepsilon
and
\bm{\zeta}
, whose effects are considerably more subtle to analyze. For a more detailed convergence analysis incorporating these effects in the context of an upwind DG scheme, we refer to
[
35
]
.

In particular, we consider an example that exhibits pronounced boundary layers near the outflow boundaries. 6
Conclusion

In this work, we developed and analyzed a monotone finite element scheme for elliptic distributed optimal control problems constrained by a convection-diffusion-reaction equation.
By employing the EAFE discretization, we established a framework that not only ensures monotonicity but also preserves the desired-state bounds in the discrete setting.
This property guarantees that the numerical optimal state remains stable and free from nonphysical oscillations, even in convection-dominated regimes.

The theoretical analysis combined the consistency of the EAFE scheme with a discrete inf-sup condition, leading to a rigorous proof of well-posedness and an
O(h)
convergence order in the
H^{1}
norm.
Extensive numerical experiments further validated the theoretical results, confirming that the EAFE method accurately captures both boundary and interior layers without introducing spurious oscillations.

Future research directions include extending the present framework to adaptive mesh refinement strategies (e.g.,
[
43
,
44
]
) and preconditioning techniques (e.g.,
[
26
,
17
,
37
,
38
]
) specifically tailored for the EAFE method in optimal control problems. It is also interesting to consider pointwise control constraints
[
11
,
9
]
and state constraints
[
15
,
36
,
12
]
.
Another promising direction is to generalize the approach to more complex multiphysics optimal control settings, such as those involving coupled flow and transport phenomena or high-dimensional convection-diffusion systems.
