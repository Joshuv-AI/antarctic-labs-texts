Renal dynamic scintigraphy using 3D-ring CZT cameras: comparison with NaI(Tl)
Authors: Gilles Metrard, Gilles Le Rouzic, Benoit Chapelle, Diane Darsin-Bettinger, Manon Dekeyser, Matthieu Bailly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsotopic renography is pivotal for assessing upper urinary tract obstruction and provides renal functional parameters. New 3D-CZT cameras offer enhanced sensitivity and innovative full SPECT protocols that need to be validated on phantoms. Current renal phantoms are often complex and suitable for 2D imaging. Using a new printed 3D renal phantom, we compared planar and full SPECT dynamic imaging. Methods: A 3D-printed renal phantom mimicking pediatric/adult kidneys was designed. Each kidney subunit, connected to a bladder-bag, was programmably infused with 7.5 MBq of [99mTc]-Pertechnetate to simulate normal nephrograms. An asymmetry of renal activity was simulated using different activity ratios. Each acquisition was conducted on both conventional NaI(Tl) and 3D-CZT cameras. Results: The 3D printed phantom enabled the acquisition of consistent and reproducible nephrograms. Tmax, T1/2, and A20 − min/max ratios showed no statistically significant differences between planar and SPECT. Simulated asymmetric renal function demonstrated a strong linear correlation between activity and AUC with r = 0.9455 and 0.9471 (p < 0.0001), respectively for conventional and 3D cameras. Conclusion: This innovative 3D phantom allowed the acquisition of reproducible nephrograms with parameters comparable to those of clinical examinations. At low activity, 3D-CZT camera acquisitions provided equivalent values to conventional cameras, supporting their use for qualitative imaging and follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-025-01351-2.

Background

Renography is a diuretic renal scintigraphy imaging used to assess suspected or confirmed upper urinary tract obstruction. Two radiopharmaceuticals are primarily used, [99mTc]-MAG3 and [99mTc]-DTPA, with [99mTc]-MAG3 [1] having an advantage in a better extraction fraction with higher quality images [2, 3]. It is applicable in both adult and pediatric patients, being among the three most performed procedures in pediatrics [4, 5].

In pediatric use, the administered dose is adapted to weight and standardized with joint guidelines from the European Nuclear Medicine Association (EANM) and Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI). This dose should be kept as low as reasonably achievable, with a minimum posology of 15 MBq for a 3 kg newborn to ensure an adequate qualitative level [6].

The time-activity curves (TACs) derived from renography using regions of interest (ROIs) on dynamic acquisitions enable the assessment of functional parameters, including split renal function, and provide valuable insights into the degree of resistance to urine outflow [2]. Since this examination is dynamic and functional, clinical comparison on different cameras remains challenging. Therefore, the use of physical phantoms enables standardized acquisitions to be reproduced on different systems and compared [7–9]. These phantoms can be used to adapt the administrated dose to the camera performance. However, the few developed phantoms are often complex and tailored to planar imaging [10–13], and there is currently no established gold standard for dynamic SPECT phantom design. A recent study by Kamiya et al. hinted at the possibility of a simpler version [14].

A new generation of digital 3D-ring CZT SPECT cameras, with advanced 3D dynamic imaging capabilities, has recently entered the market, offering higher performances compared to the previous planar gamma-cameras [15–18]. The initial in vivo pilot studies have shown the feasibility of performing SPECT dynamic renal imaging [19, 20]. In this study, we present an innovative and cost-effective renal 3D phantom and evaluate the performance of both planar and 3D-ring CZT cameras using this novel tool.

Methods

Phantom design

Phantom design

Phantom design

The renal phantom for each kidney consisted of a 3D-printed shape using PLA material (Fig. 1A). Each kidney was divided into 2 subunits to enable the separation of the upper and lower renal activity. For this, a 6 mm gap was created to improve visualization and positioning the Regions Of Interest (ROI) during image processing.

(B) Positioning of the renal phantom on conventional NaI(Tl) camera during planar acquisitions

Each renal subunit had a height of 50 mm and an outer cylinder diameter of 46 mm with a central cavity diameter of 38 mm to minimize attenuation phenomena. It featured a double helical groove with a pitch of 10 mm, both upward and downward, to standardly coil a 1.5 mm inner diameter disposable infusion set (1159.80 Luer-Lock M/F, Lectro-Spiral™) for a better homogeneity. The excess tubing simulated the ureter, with its end connected to a 250 ml bag (PB02502852T20CPP149, SLB Medical™) representing the bladder. Unlike renal anatomy, the ureters were voluntarily positioned outside to avoid overlap of incoming and outgoing activities. Each subunit was designed to correspond to the dimensions of a pediatric kidney, and the superposition of 2 units matched adult kidney dimensions.

The spacing between the 2 kidneys was maintained at a constant distance by a spacer, and all tubing was grouped into a central tube simulating the aorta. All these parts were 3D-printed with PLA material.

Each of the 4 tubes feeding each renal subunit was connected to a 4 lines programmable pump (BeneFusion nSP, Mindray™ and 50 ml Original Perfusor Syringe, B Braun™) via an extension set with Y-connector to inject the activity (PS3310R, Cair-LGL™).

Phantom validation

Phantom validation

Phantom validation

As in the study by Kamiya et al. [14], we tested various perfusion flow rates of the phantom over four sequential phases (vascular, secretory, early and late excretory) using a radiopharmaceutical activity diluted in 2.2 mL—corresponding to the estimated cortical volume—with acquisitions performed on a conventional gamma camera. The optimal parameters were determined to reproduce isotopic nephrograms most closely resembling a normal clinical pattern. These parameters are detailed in the following section.

The phantom allowed adjustment of acquisition and kinetic parameters to reproduce either physiological or pathological conditions, such as those observed in obstructive uropathy, but it did not allow variations in renal volume as seen in hydronephrosis.

An obstructive uropathy could therefore be simulated, from normal pattern, by increasing the volumetric activity of the radiopharmaceutical, reducing the perfusion flow rate during the secretory and excretory phases respectively resulted in a higher peak amplitude, a prolonged Tmax, and an increased T½ as well as a greater residual activity at 20 min in excretory phases. Conversely, a very low volumetric activity combined with globally reduced perfusion flow rates in the phantom can simulate a model of severe renal dysfunction, leading to nearly flat time–activity curves.

This phantom is freely available for research purposes. The STL files are provided in Supplementary Materials section (3D Phantom part 1,2,3 - Fig. 1A).

Preparation and acquisitions

Preparation and acquisitions

Preparation and acquisitions

Acquisitions were performed on NaI(Tl) dual head SPECT/CT (Discovery NM670, General Electric™) and 3D-ring CZT SPECT/CT (Starguide, General Electric™).

The phantom was positioned on the camera table just beneath the outer envelope of an anthropomorphic phantom (ECT/TOR/P, Data Spectrum Corporation™) to ensure reproducible placement of the heads at a clinically relevant distance (Fig. 1B). This methodology was established to be reproducible and applicable to 3D-ring CZT systems utilizing infrared beams for precise positioning of detection modules as close to the patient as possible.

All tubes and syringes were pre-filled with tap water, and the bags were elevated to the upper level of the phantom to prevent gravity-induced flow.

For simulating normal examinations, an activity of 7.5 MBq of [99mTc]-Pertechnetate was diluted in a volume corresponding to each renal subunit (2.2 mL) and injected into the Y connector. The activity was then flushed with 2 mL of tap water through each line to propel the activity bolus into the line. This 7.5 MBq per subunit was selected to represent the lowest pediatric activity administered per kidney [6]. Measurements were repeated three times on each system.

For simulating renal activity asymmetries, two additional series were acquired on each system with activity ratios ranging from 5% to 50% of the total activity of both kidneys.

Preliminary studies defined four sequential infusion rate phases: 9.6 mL/min for 10 s, 0.55 mL/min for 4 min, 0.183 mL/min for 6 min, and 0.066 mL/min for 10 min, corresponding to the vascular, secretory, early excretory, and late excretory phases, respectively.

Planar acquisitions were conducted with a Low Energy High Resolution and Sensitivity (LEHRS) collimator [21], employing a 256 × 256 matrix in dynamic mode (1s * 60 frames followed by 20 s * 60 frames), with a zoom factor of 1 asing in 2.2 × 2.2 mm² pixel size. The energy window was set at 140 keV ± 10%. Planar activity was calculated using the geometric mean. This acquisition was the routine F-0 protocol [22]. On the 3D-ring camera, 4D acquisitions were performed using a 232 × 232 × 112 matrix (for the phantom’s field of view) in dynamic mode (2s * 60 frames followed by 20 s * 60 frames), with an energy window of 140 keV ± 10%. Reconstruction was carried out using the factory Q.Clear algorithm (10 iterations, 10 subsets, Voxel size 4.92 × 4.92 × 4.92 mm3, Relative Differences for Maximum (RDP) regularization method, Gamma = 2, Beta = 0.2) with resolution recovery correction but without attenuation nor scatter correction.

The Q.Clear scintigraphic reconstruction method, derived from PET [23–26], utilizes the Block Sequential Regularized Expectation Maximization (BSREM) algorithm in conjunction with RDP noise reduction regularization [27, 28]. This approach can achieve full convergence while controlling noise levels, directly within the iterative reconstruction loop. The Beta parameter defines the penalty weight controlling the preservation in image differences, resulting in a progressively smoother image as its value increases. The specific RDP gamma parameter helps to preserve edge definition. A higher gamma value allows for more accurate rendering of structural contours.

The default factory settings of this reconstruction method were used to remain within the most general and standardized configuration. Outside of the factory settings, any adjustment of these parameters would require dedicated studies to assess their impact, particularly regarding quantitative accuracy.

Acquisitions started immediately upon activation of the perfusion pumps.

Processing

Processing

Processing

The acquisitions, stored in a DICOM-standardized format with a data volume comparable to that of conventional non-dynamic SPECT studies, were processed with the clinical commercial version of the dynamic imaging processing software of the Xeleris V workstation (General Electric™) to measure the time to maximum activity peak (Tmax), the time from maximum activity peak to half maximum activity (T1/2), the ratio of 20 min activity to maximum peak activity (20-min/max count ratio, A20 − min/max), and activity ratios using the integral method. This method was defined by the ratio of area under the curve (AUC) between 90 and 150 s as recommended for [99mTc]-MAG3 [2].

The values of the Time-Activity Curve (TAC) were normalized based on the maximum activity peak.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed in Prism 10.2.1 software (GraphPad™).

The data of nephrograms were compared with paired t tests and expressed as mean and standard deviation.

Correlation was computed with a one-tailed Pearson correlation test. The normality test was performed with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

Statistical tests were performed at the 5% level of significance.

Discussion

Firstly, this study aimed to develop a dynamic renal phantom suitable for use on both conventional 2D planar NaI(Tl) and new 3D-ring CZT systems. The phantom needed to be cost-effective, capable of reproducing normal nephrograms, and scalable for studying both pediatric and adult kidneys.

On a gold-standard planar dual head NaI(Tl) camera, the phantom values of Tmax, T1/2 and A20 − min/max count ratio were within normal clinical ranges. In a retrospective study involving 106 healthy subjects for kidney donation, Esteves et al. measured a Tmax of 3.57 ± 2.1 and 3.16 ± 1.0 min, a T1/2 of 5.64 ± 2.3 and 5.36 ± 1.4 min, A20 − min/max of 0.24 ± 0.14 and 0.22 ± 0.08%, respectively for the right and left kidneys [29]. In their study, Ishii et al. found lower values with a Tmax about 3 min and a 20-min/max count ratio of 19.3% [30].

This dynamic phantom appeared to be reproducible with a CV measured at 9.8% for Tmax and 12.8% for T1/2. These values remained slightly higher than those observed in the major studies, with CV of 6.3, and 8.7 for Tmax, respectively for the studies by Kamiya and Sabbir. For the T1/2, it was 7.4, and 9.4% respectively for the studies by Sabbir and Kamiya [12, 14].

The Tmax and CV values were relatively similar but slightly lower with the 3D camera, possibly due to the higher latency of this camera type at the start of the acquisition (setting the motors in motion). Regarding T1/2 and A20 − min/max, although the differences in values were not statistically significant, the CV was markedly higher: 37.1% and 35.4% with the 3D system compared to 12.8% and 19.2% with the conventional camera, respectively, for T1/2 and A20 − min/max. Volume acquisition tends to dilute the information across a greater number of voxels compared to pixels. This lower voxel count statistics is likely responsible for the increase in the coefficient of variation.

This low signal requires an image reconstruction process designed to enhance the acquired signal. For the Q.Clear factory protocol, the performance of this method is primarily governed by the Beta coefficient, which defines the penalty factor. With a Beta value set at 0.2, the image is more heavily smoothed to reduce noise. Another factor that may influence spatial resolution—while still aiming to preserve an adequate signal—is the use of a 4.92 × 4.92 × 4.92 mm3 pixel size parameter, as opposed to 2.2 × 2.2 mm² pixel size in the plantar view. It should be noted that the Q.Clear algorithm requires additional SPECT/CT imaging assessment.

For the vascular phase, the conventional camera acquired data at 1 s per frame, whereas the 3D-ring CZT camera acquired data at 2 s per frame but had a greater impact on the image quality with more counts per image. In fact, this difference resulted from a limitation in the total number of frames allowed in 4D acquisitions. While the 3D-ring CZT camera supports a temporal resolution of up to 1 s per frame, this limitation did not influence the results, as key parameters such as Tmax, T1/2, and A20 − min/max were derived from data recorded after the vascular phase. Furthermore, activity values were expressed as counts per second and normalized to the peak value.

This study also demonstrated a good reproducibility and linear correlation between activity and AUC for both cameras. This correlation was important because the ratio of AUCs enables accurate estimation of split renal function, often obviating the need for cortical scintigraphy [31].

However, our study did not assess attenuation, which did not affect model validation, as attenuation does not influence Tmax, T1/2 and PLA is a material with low attenuation properties. In clinical practice, patient attenuation may reduce the number of events detected by the camera; however, this decrease in sensitivity was not considered significant on first clinical acquisition (Fig. 4) and could be partially corrected by applying attenuation correction methods [32, 33].

The advent of 4D imaging with 3D-ring CZT cameras allows for a combined functional and anatomical examination. In a study involving 20 patients admitted to the emergency department for acute renal colic, Ochoa et al. demonstrated that 4D imaging enabled the detection of acute ureteric obstruction while providing additional clinical information. This was achieved by combining dynamic obstruction data with CT findings, such as direct visualization of the calculus [34].

It has also been demonstrated that renal dynamic acquisitions can detect small-volume urinomas [35], while adding a SPECT/CT acquisition at the conclusion of a planar examination provides additional information for accurately localizing fistulas [36]. A full SPECT/CT acquisition performed immediately after injection could allow earlier spatial localization in cases of significant leaks.

Another advantage of direct full SPECT/CT acquisition is improved renal depth evaluation. Indeed, an inaccurate estimation of renal depth can impact the assessment of split renal function. Zheng et al. demonstrated that SPECT/CT acquisition using a CZT camera allows for better assessment of renal depth without the need for CT [37], which is particularly advantageous in pediatric patients. SPECT/CT with low dose CT can also effectively correct attenuation [38]. A preliminary study conducted using a 3D-CZT cardiac camera found improved assessment of split renal function and glomerular filtration rate using Gate’s method, compared to planar acquisitions [20].

More recently, Beytur et al. demonstrated how the dynamic SPECT approach combines high-resolution anatomical imaging with the functional assessment of renal drainage and parenchymal integrity in a single imaging session. This integration provides an alternative to conventional planar scintigraphy and [99mTc]-DMSA scans, offering enhanced diagnostic accuracy and holding promise as a state-of-the-art tool for the preoperative evaluation of obstructive uropathies [39].

Renal 4D imaging using 3D-ring CZT cameras opens perspectives for both research and clinical applications.

While the overall acquisition time remained unchanged, the higher volumetric sensitivity provided by 3D-ring CZT technology [40] offers the potential for a reduction in patient dosimetry, without compromising image quality. Dose-reduction studies are usually performed using list-mode acquisition, which, to our knowledge, is not available for temporal resampling on the different 3D-ring CZT gamma cameras. Indeed, temporal data sampling is governed by the acquisition time required for a complete detector sweep. This limitation will therefore require the use of alternative methods to determine the optimal administered activity.

Three-dimensional volumetric acquisition enables a more precise delineation of the renal cortex from pyeloureteral retention areas in cases of upper urinary tract obstruction, thereby improving the reliability of quantitative indices such as cumulative nephrograms and residual activity measurements. Dynamic SPECT in volumetric mode further reduces the overlap of anatomical structures, allowing a clearer temporal and spatial distinction between cortical and excretory components. This enables the generation of parametric images in which the early frames predominantly visualize the renal cortex, whereas the late images mainly represent the excretory tract.

The integration of CT, primarily used in adult pathologies, paves the way for a one-stop-shop examination, reducing the need for additional anatomical imaging. CT imaging additionally provides anatomical information that can facilitate the segmentation of the different renal structures and the use of semi-automatic contouring methods. Moreover, the inclusion of the abdominal aorta within the renal field of view, combined with attenuation correction, may further enhance quantitative analyses by enabling access to the arterial input function. However, the use of CT remains optional, which represents a significant advantage for minimizing radiation exposure in pediatric studies.

Nevertheless, achieving reliable quantitative accuracy will require rigorous standardization of acquisition and reconstruction parameters to ensure reproducibility and cross-center comparability. In parallel, manufacturers will also need to adapt their software bundle to incorporate more dedicated 3D processing tools for dynamic data analysis.

Conclusion

This novel 3D-phantom printed design, optimized for 4D SPECT/CT, allowed the acquisition of reproducible nephrograms with similar parameters to those observed in clinical settings. Using this phantom and minimal pediatric radiotracer activity, the study demonstrated that 4D SPECT acquisitions on 3D-ring CZT cameras provide similar values to those obtained with planar dynamic imaging on conventional NaI(Tl) gamma cameras. This enables consistent qualitative imaging and follow-up regardless of the camera type.
