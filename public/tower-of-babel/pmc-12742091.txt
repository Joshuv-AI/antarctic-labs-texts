Burden and risk factors of mental and substance use disorders among children aged 5–14 in Asia from 1990 to 2021: results from the Global Burden of Disease study
Authors: Yanyan Wang, Huifang Yang, Yizhen Wu, Jiahui Li, Chikyu Tsin, Shuyang Ren, Jun Zhang, Yuling Qiao, Xiwang Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract‌ObjectiveTo analyse trends in mental and substance use disorders among Asian children aged 5–14 years, identify key risk factors (eg, bullying, abuse, lead exposure) and compare gender/age disparities using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data.‌DesignCross-sectional analysis of the GBD 2021 database, focusing on prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and risk factor associations.‌SettingPopulation-based study across Asian countries, examining the burdens of mental health, especially substance use disorders in high-prevalence regions.‌ParticipantsChildren aged 5–14 years in Asia, with gender-stratified subgroups (boys vs girls).‌ResultAnxiety, conduct disorders and autism were primary contributors to mental health burdens. Substance use disorders, though less prevalent, rose notably among boys. Girls showed higher burdens of anxiety/depressive disorders. Bullying and childhood abuse were strongly linked to these conditions. Gender disparities in DALYs highlighted boys’ vulnerability to substance use and girls to internalising disorders.‌ConclusionUrgent, region-specific interventions are needed to address bullying, lead exposure and abuse, with gender-sensitive strategies. The study calls for targeted research and policies to mitigate rising mental health challenges in Asian children.

Introduction

Since the peak onset age for certain mental disorders is observed during adolescence,1 mental health has gained increased attention and become a critical public health concern in the global context.2 Nevertheless, most previous studies focus on European or American adult populations, leading to a specific lack of research targeted to adolescents in Asia.

Notably, an increasing prevalence of various mental disorders has been observed in Asia.3 The latest data from the WHO on suicide rates positions South-East Asia as having the second highest incidence globally. During the period from 1990 to 2019, there was a steady rise in the occurrence of major depressive disorder and dysthymia in China.4 In Thailand, cannabis consumption rose from 0.9% in 2019 to 2.2% in 2021,5 in which a comparable pattern of substance use is also observed in Hong Kong, China.6 Meanwhile, the relative share of mental illnesses in the overall disease burden has nearly doubled in India.7 And also, a study in Nepal in 2019 highlighted a prominent prevalence of psychotic disorder, agoraphobia, major depressive disorder and anxiety disorders, particularly in children, with a notable 8.7% displaying suicidal inclinations.8 Regarding other countries in Asia, such as China, a steady rise in the occurrence of major depressive disorder and dysthymia has been demonstrated in the previous decades.9

Furthermore, the incidence of most mental disorders is dramatically higher in the young generation compared with the elder.10 Empirical studies demonstrated that adolescents are more susceptible to depression, anxiety, psychosis and other mental disorders11 because it is a crucial stage in human development, characterised by swift enhancements in brain structure and function, facilitating individuals’ learning of growth and social function.12 From 2005 to 2014, a considerable increase in the global incidence of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) diagnoses is observed, which rose from 6.8% to 14.4%.

Specifically for the adolescent population, mental issues may result in negative consequences beyond individual suffering. They not only compromise personal well-being but also impose a considerable burden on societal resources. For instance, a previous study indicated that a 20% population segment accounted for 42% to 100% of economically costly outcomes, including healthcare use, unemployment and absenteeism.13 Furthermore, children exposed to adverse experiences, such as child abuse, are more likely to belong to these high-cost segments and even multiple high-cost segments, exacerbating the strain on social systems and economies.14 These findings highlight the need for early detection and intervention to reduce the long-term societal and economic impacts of children’s mental health disorders, as neglecting this issue may escalate healthcare costs and productivity losses.

In general, suffering from mental disorders among the young generation can be attributed to numerous factors, predominantly categorised into genetic and environmental domains. In the genetic perspective, infected genes are inherited to children via maternal infections or a parent’s psycho-pathological history.15 Meanwhile, gender presents an additional risk factor, in which girls are more prone to developing anxiety disorders.16 Regarding the situational factors, mental disorders are associated with strained familial bonds and the extravagant academic pressure exerted by parents.17 Besides, excessive engagement in activities such as gambling, gaming and internet usage also contributes to mental issues in young individuals.18 Surprisingly, an empirical study revealed an unexpected finding: the Western diet, which is marked by excessive consumption of sweets and desserts, processed and red meat, and a lack of fruits, vegetables and whole grains, heightens the risk and intensity of depressive disorders in young people.19 In conclusion, factors like family dynamics, academic stress, internet use and diet significantly impact adolescent mental health.

Potentially, a dearth of psychiatric research targeted to the adolescent demographic inhibits the development of mental healthcare in Asia.10 First of all, insufficient financial support is recognised as the most challenging barrier.10 Second, the stigma towards mental health problems among Asians is considerably higher compared with Europeans and Americans, in which the cultural norm supposes that having mental disorders is shameful.10 Ultimately, limited governmental support, such as the deficiency in well-established data collection systems or organisations, may also lead to the lack of mental health research.20

In summary, there has been a rise in both the prevalence and severity of mental disorders in Asia over the past decades, particularly among adolescents.21 Multiple risk factors lead to the emergence of mental disorders in youth, including parental infections, gender, family relationships, academic pressures, excessive internet use, dietary patterns, among other attributes.22 This study focuses on Asian youth to provide insights that may help prevent mental health disorders and advance psychiatric research in this specific region.

Materials and methods

Study design

We present the findings of Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021, which employs all available data sources to estimate the global burden of disease for 354 diseases and injuries in 204 countries and territories. As estimates from the GBD 2021 are actively used by policy makers, service planners, resource allocators and researchers worldwide, they hold substantial influence in shaping global health strategies.23 The results were retrieved from the GBD Results tool. Mental disorders encompassed within the GBD framework include depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), conduct disorder, idiopathic developmental intellectual disability, eating disorders and personality disorders. Mental disorders are defined based on the criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders—Fifth Edition or the International Classification of Diseases. Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) stand for the aggregated average of total health loss, calculated by adding the years of life lost and years lived with disability for each cause under mental and substance use disorders. We report 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) derived from 1000 posterior distributions, accounting for data heterogeneity across regions and time periods, based on the 25th and 975th draws at each stage of the burden estimation process. Moreover, we report GBD trends in disease-specific and gender-specific DALYs from 1990 to 2021. For the 2021 data, we also detail the proportion of disease burden attributable to different risk factors and compare the prevalence of mental illness by country and gender in the Asian region. Additionally, we identified three crucial factors contributing to mental disorders and substance abuse and examined the distinct impacts on men and women in terms of disease burden.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This study relies solely on secondary analysis of aggregated data from the GBD 2021 database.

Data source

This study used data from the 2021 GBD study, which provides estimates from 204 countries, to offer a comprehensive assessment of the health status of the population in Asian countries. The GBD study provides information on the prevalence, morbidity and mortality of various health conditions, including mental and substance use disorders, with a reported global prevalence of 6.7% for child mental disorders in 2016. This emphasises existing data gaps that may impact regional comparisons, with the data disaggregated by age, gender and location.

Data selection

We selected GBD data on the prevalence and risk factors of substance abuse and psychiatric disorders among children aged 5–14 years in Asia.24 Data were extracted by mental health status, age (5–14 years) and gender (male and female). See figure 1 for the data extraction workflow.

Data extraction flowchart. DALYs, disability-adjusted life years; GBD, Global Burden of Disease.

Data cleaning and preparation

The data were cleaned and prepared for analysis through the standard GBD procedures, which encompass removing outliers, conducting imputation of missing data and adjusting for disparities in data quality and completeness across locations and time periods. Additionally, we scrutinised the data for any errors or inconsistencies and made the necessary corrections.

Data analysis

We implemented descriptive statistical methods to summarise and present the data. Specifically, we used the estimated figures of mental and substance use disorders, DALYs and prevalence rate by countries and gender, and presented the outcomes in tables and graphs. All statistical analyses and data visualisations were performed using R (V.4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within RStudio (V.2024.04.2+764; Posit Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

Mental disorder DALYs increased by 19%, with anxiety, conduct disorders and autism being major contributors. Risk factors like lead exposure, bullying and abuse showed significant associations, with gender-specific impacts.

The number of DALYs caused by mental disorders among Asian youth aged 5–14 increased by 19% from 1990 to 2021, from 6 027 660 (95% UI 4374.39 to 8020.50) to 7 180 220 (95% UI 5142.18 to 9586.73). Among these disorders, anxiety disorders, conduct disorders and ASDs were the top three contributors, with increases of 29%, 16% and 10%, respectively, from 1990 to 2021. For example, between 1990 and 2021, the age-standardised DALY rate for anxiety disorders increased by 30% (95% UI 22% to 38%), with significant variation across sub-regions (p<0.05). During this period, the largest increase in DALYs was major depressive disorder (61%) and bulimia nervosa (59%). In contrast, idiopathic intellectual development disorder was the only condition that decreased, with a 5% reduction.

In terms of DALYs caused by substance use disorders in Asia, there was an overall decrease of 2380 cases from 1990 to 2021. Specifically, the DALYs caused by alcohol use disorder declined from 21 770 (95% UI 11.84 to 36.71) to 19 170 (95% UI 9.29 to 33.77). However, the number of DALYs caused by drug use disorder increased by 220 (online supplemental table 1).

From 1990 to 2021, anxiety disorders emerged as a growing concern, with a significant rise in DALYs per 100 000 population. For substance use disorders, the total DALYs in 2021 were 22 615 (95% UI 11 122 to 38 226.1). Among them, alcohol use disorders accounted for the majority (84.75%), while drug use disorders represented 13.25%, including marijuana use disorder contributing 14.40%, cocaine use disorder 0.83% and other drugs 0.01% (figure 3b).

The prevalence rates indicate that mental and substance use disorders are generally higher among boys than girls across most Asian countries. Anxiety disorders and depressive disorders contribute significantly to the overall burden, with a higher prevalence among girls. In contrast, substance use disorders, particularly drug use and alcohol use disorders, are more prevalent among boys. Other mental disorders, including ADHD and conduct disorder, also contribute substantially to the prevalence rates in both genders. Notably, countries such as Iran, Afghanistan and Syria show higher prevalence rates (figure 4a).

When measuring the disease burden in terms of DALYs, the trend remains consistent, with boys experiencing a greater burden than girls. Substance use disorders, particularly drug and alcohol use, contribute significantly to DALYs among boys, indicating a higher impact on health and daily functioning. Conversely, anxiety and depressive disorders play a dominant role in the disease burden among girls. Countries with lower socioeconomic conditions, such as Afghanistan, Syria and Iran, exhibit higher DALY rates (figure 4b).

Among the risk factors contributing to mental disorders, 845 459.61 (95% UI 1 518 918 to 371 314) DALYs were attributed to three primary risk factors: lead exposure, bullying victimisation and childhood sexual abuse. Lead exposure accounted for the largest share, representing 51.1%, approximately 432 129.33 (95% UI 773 521 to 186 718) DALYs, with girls slightly more affected (50.4%) compared with boys (49.6%). Bullying victimisation followed, contributing 401 797 DALYs, or 47.5% of the total, with a significantly higher impact on boys (54.5%) than girls (45.5%). Childhood sexual abuse was responsible for 1.4% of DALYs, with a higher proportion in boys (55.9%) compared with girls (44.1%).

For substance use disorders, the gender differences are obvious in all three risk factors of drug use, alcohol use and childhood sexual abuse. Alcohol use was the predominant risk factor, accounting for 81.7% (19 166.17 (95% UI 33 769 to 9292)) of DALYs. Among these cases, boys comprised 70.9%, which is approximately 2.4 times the proportion of girls (29.1%). Drug use was responsible for 14.7% of DALYs, with boys (66.8%) being two times as affected as girls (33.2%). Childhood sexual abuse accounted for only 3.6% of DALYs, with a stark gender disparity: 83.5% were boys, while only 16.5% were girls (figure 5).

Discussion

This study analyses the burden of mental illness and substance abuse among Asian adolescents from 1990 to 2021 and highlights the mental health challenges and vulnerabilities of those aged 5–14.

Although typically healthy with low mortality, children aged 5–14 years represent a critical future workforce. Thus, the growing mental health burden in this group threatens national development and productivity. Mental disorders are already leading causes of YLD and DALY in Europe and the Americas and are likely to become one of the major public health challenges of the 21st century25—a trend increasingly seen in Asia.

According to GBD’s all-age data for Asia, depression (37.1%), anxiety (21.5%) and schizophrenia (13.8%) are the three major contributors to mental disorders DALYs. Among children aged 5–14 anxiety and conduct disorders are most prevalent.26 27

The COVID-19 pandemic increased anxiety and depression prevalence in the general population,28 including children aged 5–14 likely contributing to increased DALYs observed in 2020 and 2021.

Bullying contributes 401 797 DALYs, or 47.5% of the total, affecting boys (54.5%) more than girls (45.5%). This disparity may stem from social norms discouraging male help-seeking, worsening long-term outcomes. With 30.5% of adolescents aged 12–17 experiencing recent bullying,29 enhanced social and familial support is critical.

In Confucian-influenced East Asian cultures, sexual minority adolescents are at higher risk of bullying due to traditional gender norms and family expectations, alongside reduced support access.30 Also, teachers in these collectivist settings are more likely to perpetrate bullying. This reflects authoritarian educational structures, unlike in Western contexts where student-on-teacher bullying is more common.31

Most risk factors in childhood affect boys more than girls; however, this pattern reverses after age 14, potentially due to economic hardship, discrimination, stigma, gynaecological problems, limited access to resources, insufficient family support and marital disharmony.32

Biological mechanisms also contribute: pubertal hormonal changes, especially oestradiol and testosterone, affect mental health trajectories, with oestradiol associated with internalising symptoms and testosterone with externalising behaviours.33 And also, detection and reporting biases may contribute, as boys’ externalising problems are more readily observed in childhood, while girls’ internalising problems often emerge or become clinically recognised in adolescence and adulthood.

Despite higher overall mental disorder rates in boys, suicidal thoughts and anxiety are more common in girls across most countries.34 Given their greater severity and fatality risk, targeted governmental strategies are essential to support female adolescents’ mental health—especially considering women’s vital role in the global workforce, including over 500 million employed women and high labour participation in East Asia and the Pacific.35 36

Limitations

However, this study has limitations: the 2016 GBD database reveals critical gaps in adolescent mental health data, with only 6.7% global prevalence coverage and 66% of countries lacking key disorder-specific data.37 Asian data presents additional challenges: stark disparities in depression reporting (Japan 95% vs Myanmar 30%), cultural stigma masking 60% of Indian suicide cases and 80% of Korean alcohol disorders and methodological issues like Syria’s 80% reduced post-traumatic stress disorder criteria. Diagnostic inconsistencies across ADHD/ASD tools and underreported childhood sexual abuse (1.4% DALYs) further complicate regional comparisons. Particularly concerning is poly-substance use: 57.5% of adolescents report lifetime substance exposure, with five-substance users facing 16 times higher suicide risk.38 Urgent priorities include funded surveys to improve prevalence estimates, standardised diagnostic protocols and culturally tailored interventions for substance use disorders, particularly in high-risk populations.

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of child mental health and substance use disorders burden in Asia, revealing distinct gender patterns and risk factor priorities among children aged 5–14 from 1990 to 2021 across Asian countries. Our findings reveal a significant increase in DALYs due to mental disorders, with anxiety, conduct disorders and ASDs being the primary contributors. Notably, risk factors such as lead exposure, bullying and childhood abuse are strongly associated with the rising burden, with gender-specific impacts observed across the region.

Promoting school-based mental health promotion and intervention

Schools should conduct regular mental health screenings for high-risk students (eg, those experiencing bullying or family adversity) and provide access to counselling. Gender-sensitive approaches are needed to address internalising disorders like anxiety and depression.39 Anti-bullying efforts require clear policies, teacher training and student education.

Strengthening policy and systemic support

Primary healthcare institutions and general hospitals must provide initial mental health assessments and referrals to streamline access to specialists. Public health actions to reduce lead exposure through environmental management and blood lead screening are urgently needed.40 Laws against child abuse require stronger enforcement, better reporting and multidisciplinary victim support. Cross-sector collaboration among health, education and social services is critical to forming a coordinated response.

Developing multidisciplinary and accessible services

Evidence-based interventions like cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) and dialectical behavioural therapy need expansion in school and community. Non-pharmacological interventions (eg, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, transcranial direct current stimulation) require more research for children.41 Family support programmes and parenting education can foster nurturing home environments. Public awareness campaigns are needed to reduce stigma and promote early help-seeking.42

Addressing comorbidity and integrated care

Integrated treatment is essential for comorbid mental and substance use disorders, combining therapies like motivational interviewing, CBT and medication. Emotion regulation and nutrition are also key to holistic recovery and relapse prevention.43

This study advances knowledge by outlining trends, risk factors and disparities in Asian children’s mental health, though aggregated data may mask country-specific variations.

Future research should refine country-level data, explore regional differences and investigate the long-term impacts of substance use on mental health in children. Prioritising harmonised national surveillance systems and testing culturally adapted anti-bullying/lead exposure interventions is critical. As socioeconomic conditions evolve,44 understanding these shifts is vital for developing targeted early intervention policies in Asia. Furthermore, it is crucial to consider the specific historical period during which pandemics like COVID-19 influenced the development of adolescents’ cognitive disorders.45 Special attention should also be given to vulnerable adolescent populations, such as street children who are particularly prone to drug abuse and self-harm.46
