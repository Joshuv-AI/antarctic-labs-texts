Application value of radiomics features based on PSMA PET/CT in diagnosis of clinically significant prostate cancer: a comparison between manual and automatic segmentation
Authors: Yujun Geng, Ming Zhang, Jingwen Li, Xinzhi Wang, Junzhang Tian, Xiaofen Ma, Kebing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiomics shows promise for non-invasive detection of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) using prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA-PET/CT). However, the impact of segmentation method—manual versus automated—on feature reproducibility and diagnostic performance remains unclear.ObjectivesTo compare the diagnostic value of radiomics features extracted from manual versus convolutional neural network (CNN)-based segmentations in PSMA-PET/CT for csPCa detection, and to evaluate their performance against maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax).MethodsRadiomics features (n = 1155) were extracted from both manual and CNN-based (3D U-Net) segmentations in 110 patients. The cohort was split into training and test sets (7:3). Key features were selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Feature consistency between methods was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cohen’s kappa. Diagnostic models were built on the training set and validated in the independent test set using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and accuracy (ACC).ResultsOf the 1155 features, 258 (22.24%) showed strong agreement (kappa ≥ 0.7) and 484 (41.90%) moderate agreement (0.4 < kappa < 0.7) between segmentation methods. Among the 10 LASSO-selected features, 4 showed strong and 3 moderate agreement. The CNN-based radiomics model achieved the highest diagnostic performance in the test set (AUC: 0.895, ACC: 0.873), outperforming both the manual model and SUVmax (AUC: 0.822).ConclusionRadiomics features from PSMA-PET/CT offer superior diagnostic performance for csPCa compared to SUVmax. The use of CNN-based segmentation facilitates the development of more efficient, objective, and stable radiomics models, with reduced inter-observer variability and improved reproducibility.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men worldwide, with approximately 1.9 million new cases and over 800,000 deaths annually, highlighting the urgent need for accurate early diagnosis and risk stratification [1]. In recent years, positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) using radiolabeled prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) tracers has emerged as a powerful imaging modality in the management of prostate cancer. PSMA, a type II transmembrane glycoprotein highly overexpressed in prostate cancer cells, serves as an ideal molecular target for PET imaging and has become one of the most widely studied probes in nuclear medicine [2, 3]. PSMA-PET/CT has demonstrated high diagnostic performance in lesion detection [4, 5], pathological score prediction [6] and postoperative recurrence and assessment of postoperative recurrence and metastasis [7, 8]. Numerous studies have indicated that PET/CT has excellent diagnostic and predictive abilities [9], especially when the lesion is located in the central and transitional zones of the prostate [10], and its diagnostic ability is much better than that of multiparameter magnetic resonance imaging (mp-MRI) [11, 12].

Despite these advantages, conventional interpretation of PSMA-PET/CT relies heavily on semiquantitative metrics such as maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), which reflects only local tracer intensity and fails to capture spatial heterogeneity, morphological patterns, or textural characteristics of tumor uptake [13]. Moreover, small lesions may be overlooked due to limited spatial resolution or partial volume effects [14]. Radiomics offers a promising alternative by extracting high-dimensional, quantifiable features from medical images to uncover imaging phenotypes associated with underlying tumor biology [15, 16]. However, the reproducibility and clinical translation of radiomics models depend critically on the transparency and completeness of methodological reporting across all steps of the radiomics workflow [17]. As recently emphasized [18], parameters used in image segmentation—such as delineation method, resampling, bin width, and smoothing filters—can significantly influence the stability and interpretability of radiomic features, particularly in PET imaging where noise and reconstruction algorithms add further variability. Therefore, rigorous documentation of technical parameters is essential to ensure reproducibility and facilitate multicenter validation.

Manual segmentation, though widely used, is time-consuming, subjective, and susceptible to inter- and intra-observer variability—particularly for lesions with ill-defined boundaries. This variability can compromise the consistency of radiomic features and limit model generalizability. A recent study on PSMA PET images [19] demonstrated significant variation in radiomic features across different segmentation methods, underscoring the need for more standardized approaches. However, that study did not incorporate deep learning–based automation. In contrast, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly 3D U-Net architectures, offer a more efficient, objective, and reproducible alternative for tumor delineation [20, 21]. These models reduce human bias, minimize clinical workload, and enable consistent application across datasets—critical advantages for scalable radiomics applications. Yet, comparative studies evaluating radiomic features from manual versus CNN-based segmentations in PSMA-PET/CT remain limited.

Therefore, this study aims to identify robust radiomic signatures for diagnosing clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa), compare their performance with SUVmax, and comprehensively assess the diagnostic value and stability of features derived from manual versus CNN-based segmentations. By ensuring transparent reporting of all preprocessing and segmentation parameters, we aim to contribute to the development of more reliable and clinically translatable radiomics models.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital of Jinan University. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the anonymous and non-interventional nature of the study, in accordance with ethical guidelines for retrospective research.

Study population

A total of 255 patients who underwent ¹⁸F-PSMA-PET/CT at the Department of Nuclear Medicine, Meizhou People’s Hospital, were initially identified for potential inclusion. All cases were histopathologically confirmed as either clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) or non-clinically significant prostate cancer (non-csPCa) following transrectal or transperineal prostate biopsy. The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) Availability of diagnostic-quality PET/CT images with definitive pathological confirmation; cases were further validated through clinical and imaging follow-up (repeat imaging, PSA trends, or additional biopsy) when necessary to ensure diagnostic certainty, Follow-up duration ranged from 12 to 24 months [22]. (2) A Gleason score was assigned based on histopathological analysis of biopsy specimens. (3) The time interval between PET/CT imaging and pathological biopsy did not exceed two months. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Prior partial or radical prostatectomy, or systemic treatment (e.g., androgen deprivation therapy) before PET/CT scanning; (2) Presence of severe comorbidities or distant metastases involving other organs; (3) Significant motion artifacts, metallic artifacts, or poor image quality that impaired accurate segmentation and radiomic analysis; (4) Incomplete clinical or laboratory data.

After applying these criteria, 110 patients (mean age: 70.3 ± 8.05 years; age range: 48–89 years) were included in the final analysis. Among them, 55 were diagnosed with csPCa (Gleason score ≥ 7), and 55 with non-csPCa (Gleason score < 7). Clinical variables collected for analysis included age and serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

PET image acquisition and reconstruction

All PET/CT scans were performed using a Siemens Biograph 20 PET/CT scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Patients received an intravenous injection of ¹⁸F-PSMA-1007 at a dose of 2.5 MBq/kg body weight, followed by a 90-minute uptake period before imaging. Scanning was performed from the skull base to the mid-thigh. A low-dose non-contrast-enhanced CT scan was acquired first for attenuation correction and anatomical localization, with the following parameters: tube voltage, 120 kV; automated tube current modulation, 30–210 mA; pitch, 0.56:1; slice thickness and reconstruction interval, 3.75 mm. Subsequently, PET emission data were acquired in 3D mode over 7–9 bed positions (depending on patient size), with 2.5 min per bed position. The acquisition matrix was 256 × 256, resulting in a reconstructed slice thickness of 3.27 mm. PET images were reconstructed using iterative reconstruction (2 iterations, 28 subsets) with CT-based attenuation correction. Multiplanar reformatted images, maximum intensity projection (MIP), and fused PET/CT images were generated using the institutional PACS workstation. Imaging parameters included a matrix size of 256 × 256, with in-plane voxel dimensions of 1.523 mm × 1.523 mm and a slice thickness of 3.0 mm.

For quantitative analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated around areas of focal tracer uptake in the prostate gland by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians (with 8 and 12 years of experience, respectively), blinded to clinical and pathological outcomes. In cases of discordance, consensus was reached through discussion. Within each ROI, the following parameters were measured: Maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax): the highest single-voxel SUV within the lesion; Mean SUV (SUVmean): the average SUV of all voxels within the ROI; Metabolic tumor volume (MTV): defined as the volume of tumor tissue with SUV ≥ 2.5, segmented using a fixed threshold method; Total lesion PSMA (TLP): calculated as TLP = SUVmean × MTV, representing a volumetric metabolic burden metric [23].

Region of interest (ROI) segmentation and feature extraction

All PET/CT images were imported into the open-source platform 3D Slicer (version 5.0.3, https://www.slicer.org) for three-dimensional visualization and manual segmentation [24]. The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the entire prostate gland [25], rather than a focal tumor subregion, for the following reasons: (1) clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) often presents with multifocal or diffuse patterns of PSMA uptake, especially in intermediate- to high-risk cases; (2) whole-gland analysis has been shown to improve diagnostic performance in radiomic models by capturing global heterogeneity and background parenchymal changes [19]; and (3) precise delineation of intraprostatic tumor foci on PSMA-PET can be challenging due to limited spatial resolution and variable tracer distribution, making whole-prostate segmentation a more robust and reproducible approach in routine clinical practice.

Manual segmentation of the prostate was performed independently by two attending nuclear medicine physicians (with 8 and 12 years of experience in genitourinary imaging), who were blinded to clinical and pathological outcomes. Segmentation was conducted slice-by-slice on the axial PET and CT images, with multiplanar reformats used for guidance. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion, with arbitration by a senior radiologist (15 years of experience) when necessary.

Radiomic features were extracted using the open-source Python package PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) [26]. The following parameters were used to ensure reproducibility: Image resampling: voxel spacing was resampled to isotropic 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ using B-spline interpolation. Intensity discretization: a fixed bin width of 5 SUV was applied for grey-level discretization. Intensity normalization: voxel intensities were normalized to a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1, then scaled to a range of 0–100 using a shift of 1000 and scale factor of 100. Filtering: both Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) (with σ = 1, 3, 5 mm) and Wavelet (using ‘haar’ wavelet, levels 1–3) filters were applied. Feature classes: all available feature classes were enabled, including: First-order statistics (20 features), Morphological (shape) features (28 features), Texture features: GLCM (24), GLDM (14), GLRLM (16), GLSZM (16), NGLDM (5), Features from filtered images (Wavelet and LoG). Feature enabling: all default-disabled features (e.g., Energy, TotalEnergy, Compactness1, Elongation, etc.) were explicitly enabled to ensure comprehensive feature extraction prior to selection.

To assess inter-observer agreement in feature values, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated between the two physicians using a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement. Features with ICC < 0.70 were excluded from further analysis due to poor reproducibility. The remaining stable features were subjected to feature selection using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 5-fold cross-validation, which penalizes coefficients of non-informative features toward zero.

Model development and hyperparameter selection

We evaluated three classifiers: Logistic Regression (LR) with L1 regularization (Lasso), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF). For LR, the regularization strength (C) was optimized via LassoCV (5-fold internal CV, C ∈ [0.01, 10]) to automatically select the most predictive feature subset. Features were standardized before training. The SVM used linear kernel with C = 1.0 and degree is 3. RF used 50 trees with a maximum depth of 4, selected based on internal validation to avoid overfitting. All models were implemented using scikit-learn (v1.3.0). Hyperparameter choices were guided by established defaults and internal cross-validation to ensure reproducibility.

CNN segmentation model establishment

In this study, we used an improved ResUnet image segmentation network model (Fig. 2); compared with the traditional Unet network model, it adds a residual module in each layer to facilitate the retention of original image information, and also adds a Dropout function in the each last layers to prevent overfitting. For more information about the model, please see Fig. 3. We used 30 of the cases to train the network model and used Dice similarity coefficient loss as the loss function to measure the performance of the model and optimize the parameters of the network model. We resize each cases into 24*128*128. Before training the network model, we used methods such as flipping, rotation, cropping, affine transform to enhance the data to achieve better results. For the optimization strategy, we chose the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1*e-5 for total of 1000 epoches for training and it’s platform was based on NVIDA RTX 3090 with 25.4GB GPU memory.

Statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were performed with SPSS software and Python 3.8 (version 1.11.1). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated by matplotlib (version 3.8.0). The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare differences in continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used for categorical variables. In this study, we established three different machine learning classification models, linear regression model (LR), random forest model (RF), and support vector machine model (SVM). In the training set, we obtain the best parameters of the model based on 5-fold cross validation (CV) and fit it to the independent test set. The Spearmanr correlation coefficient is used to measure the consistency between two groups of variables, where a Kappa value greater than 0.7 is considered a strong correlation, greater than 0.4 and less than 0.7 are considered a moderate correlation, and less than 0.4 are considered a weak correlation. The area under the curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), 95% confidence interval (95% CI), and F1-score were calculated to compare each predictor. A P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient clinical characteristics

A total of 110 patients were included in this study; 60 were confirmed to have prostate adenocarcinoma by pathological biopsy, including 5 with a Gleason score of 6 and 55 with a Gleason score > = 7. Among all the patients, 28 had PI-RADS scores of 1–2 (25.4%), 20 had PI-RADS scores of 3 (18.2%), 18 had PI-RADS scores of 4 (16.4%), and 44 (40.0%) had PI-RADS scores of 5. The PRIMARY score results indicated 50 (45%) negative cases (1–2) and 60 (55%) positive cases (3–5). Among all malignant cases, 6 involved concurrent lymph node and bone metastases; 6 had only involved bone metastases (Table 1).

CNN segmentation results

Among all 30 cases used for training, the Dice similarity coefficient reached 0.860; the modified ResUnet segmentation model we trained this time also achieved good results in the test set (N = 80) with a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.823 (Fig. 3).

In the two different sources of radiomics features (N = 1155), 258 groups (22.34%) of features had strong consistency, with a Kappa value greater than 0.7, and another 484 groups (41.90%) of radiomics features reached moderate correlation (0.4 < Kappa < 0.7).

Features selection and comparison

A total of 92 sets (7.97%) of radiomic features were deleted due to their lower intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs < 0.70). In contrast, no features were excluded from the CNN-based segmentation group, as all features demonstrated excellent reproducibility with ICCs greater than 0.90. After LASSO regression and 5-fold cross-validation, a total of 10 groups of features were selected including original_shape_Sphericity, original_glcm_Correlation, sigma-1-mm-3D_glcm_ Correlation, sigma-1-mm-3D_glcm_Imc2, sigma-3-mm-3D_gldm_Dependence-Non- Uniformity Normalized, sigma-3-mm-3D_glrlm_ShortRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis, wavelet-LLH _glcm_Autocorrelation, wavelet-HLL_glcm_Idmn, wavelet-HHL_ gldm_Dependence Entropy, wavelet-HHH_firstorder_Median.

Among these ten sets of radiomics features, only four sets of features have a Kappa coefficient greater than 0.7 in manual and CNN ROI segmentation, they are original_glcm_Correlation (Kappa = 0.745), sigma-1-mm-3D_glcm_Correlation (Kappa = 0.741), sigma-1-mm-3D_glcm_Imc2 (Kappa = 0.731) and sigma-3-mm- 3D_glrlm_ShortRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis (Kappa = 0.800) (Table 2; Fig. 4).

In terms of accuracy, there are also 6 groups of radiomics features from the automatic segmentation model that are higher than the manually delineated features. They are original_glcm_Correlation (Manual ACC:0.836, CNN ACC:0.846), sigma-1-mm-3D_glcm_Imc2 (Manual ACC:0.818, CNN ACC:0.836), sigma-3-mm-3D_gldm_DependenceNonUniformityNormalized (Manual ACC:0.809, CNN ACC:0.818), sigma-3-mm-3D_glrlm_ShortRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis (Manual ACC:0.827,CNN ACC:0.873), wavelet-HLL_glcm_Idmn (Manual ACC:0.600, CNN ACC:0.609), wavelet-HHL_gldm_DependenceEntropy (Manual ACC:0.609, CNN ACC:0.627). But, the results of Delong-test show that the differences between all features are not statistically significant (Table 3).

Machine learning models

In the three different machine learning models, the results of the LR model showed that the model based on the automatically segmented radiomics features (AUC:0.895, ACC:0.879) was better than the manually segmented model (AUC:0.801, ACC:0.818) in terms of diagnostic performance in the test set, but the results of the RF and SVM showed that the model based on the manually segmented radiomics features (AUC:0.876,0.872, ACC:0.788,0.849) was better than the automatically segmented model (AUC:0.857,0.853, ACC:0.727,0.788)(Table 4).

In the six models established, the LR model based on automatic segmentation of radiomics features and the SVM model based on manual segmentation of radiomics features had better diagnostic performance than the important parameters SUVmax (AUC:0.822, ACC:0.818) and PSA (AUC:0.681, ACC:0.697) based on PSMA-PET/CT in the test set (Fig. 6).

Subsequently, we performed 5-fold cross-validation to compare radiomic features extracted from manually segmented regions with those from deep learning–based segmentations in all patients. The model using deep learning–derived features achieved superior performance with an AUC of 0.933, accuracy of 0.857, sensitivity of 0.875, specificity of 0.850, and F1 score of 0.862, outperforming both the manually segmented radiomic model (AUC: 0.835, accuracy: 0.742, sensitivity: 0.768, specificity: 0.718, F1: 0.748) and conventional metrics such as SUVmax and TLP (AUC: 0.848 and 0.827, accuracy: 0.732 and 0.709, respectively).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a radiomics model using PSMA PET/CT to identify clinically significant prostate cancer. As shown in Table 4, The model achieved strong diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.895, ACC: 0.879), outperforming conventional indicators such as SUVmax (AUC: 0.822) and serum PSA (AUC: 0.681). Given the absence of an external validation cohort, we applied 5-fold cross-validation on the entire dataset to ensure robust internal evaluation—a practical and data-efficient approach when external data are limited. We acknowledge that some studies [19], adopted a train-set-only CV strategy followed by a hold-out test set. While this design enhances generalizability assessment, our approach allows more comprehensive evaluation across all available samples, which is particularly valuable in single-center studies with moderate sample sizes [27].

The key advancement of our work lies in the integration of a deep learning–based model for automatic tumor segmentation. We found only 22.34% of radiomic features showed strong correlation between manual and CNN-based segmentations, highlighting the sensitivity of radiomics to delineation methods (please see Tables 2 and 3). Importantly, As detailed in Table 4, features extracted from the AI-generated regions yielded superior diagnostic performance—the model using CNN-based features outperformed the manually segmented counterpart. This suggests that deep learning segmentation not only reduces radiologist workload and improves reproducibility but can also capture more informative tumor heterogeneity patterns. Our study emphasizes that automation in radiomics should not merely replace manual effort—it can enhance model performance when properly designed.

Moreover, it is also worth mentioning that the cut-off value for SUVmax for diagnosing clinically significant prostate cancer of our research was determined to be 12.6, which closely aligns with the findings of Lv et al. [9], whose cut-off value was 11.5, with a diagnostic AUC of 0.864. In fact, many studies on PSMA PET/CT for diagnosing prostate cancer heavily rely on SUVmax values. A score of 5 in the PRIMARY is based on whether SUVmax exceeds 12.0 [28]. According to the study by Liu et al. [29], the SUVmax of a normal prostate gland is approximately 3.40 (range: 2.00-4.40), and a value greater than 3.50 can be considered the focal concentration. Compared to visual identification, radiomics features based on image pixel values demonstrate greater objectivity and repeatability. In addition to the pathological type of the lesion, it is also associated with many biological indicators such as whether there is a gene mutation or whether it is suitable for a certain treatment method [15]. However, this research still faces many challenges. First, before extracting features, physicians need to manually delineate the region of interest of the whole prostate gland, introducing observer errors. Second, its interpretability is not high [30]. Therefore, the current importance of deep learning-based medical image segmentation techniques is evident [31]. On the one hand, this approach can significantly reduce the workload of clinical physicians, and on the other hand, it can assist researchers in quantifying certain biological parameters, such as the tumour volume [32] and prostate gland area [33].

At present, an increasing number of research results have shown that PSMA PET/CT has the best prostate diagnostic ability [2], with performance that is far better than that of mp-MRI [11], especially when low-signal lesions are located in the central and transitional zones of the prostate [10]. Besides that, MRI result should be interpreted with caution, as categorizing cases with a PI-RADS score of 3 as either benign or malignant is problematic, given that it inherently represents a 50% probability of being malignant or benign. But it is worth mentioning that in our study, a total of 20 patients had a PI-RADS score of 3, and only 2 (10%) of these cases were malignant. Our study results are consistent with the findings of Guo et al.’s team [11], who reported that approximately 91.5% of cases with a PI-RADS score of 3 were negative. According to the study by the Emmett team [28], this figure is approximately 76.92% (20/26). Similarly, a PI-RADS score of 5 plays a crucial role in diagnosing prostate cancer [34]. In our study, there were approximately 44 patients with a PI-RADS score of 5, with only 6.82% (3/44) of the lesions being malignant. These findings align closely with those of Guo et al. [11], who reported that approximately 25.05% of patients had a PI-RADS score of 5 but contributed to 41.3% (92/223) of the malignant cases.

Radiomics [35] and various deep-learning techniques [36] have been widely applied in various clinical studies in recent years, such as detection of prostate cancer lesions, benign-malignant classification of lesions, and prediction of prognosis. For instance, the study by Yi’s team [14] confirmed that radiomics features can effectively identify malignant lesions that appear negative in PET/CT, and scholars such as Emmett et al. [37] have demonstrated that radiomics features can efficiently diagnose lymph node metastasis and extracapsular extension in prostate cancer. In our study, We used 5-fold cross validation to compare the diagnostic performance of radiomics and other parameters based on PET/CT images. The results showed diagnostic model based on radiomics achieved good diagnostic results in both the training set and the test set. Although radiomics features from different sources have different diagnostic results, and some extracted features have significant numerical differences, in this study, the model based on LR model performed better than the RF and SVM models in the test set. This shows that the radiomics features obtained by automatic image segmentation still have considerable diagnostic significance, and research on it should also be taken seriously.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although our cohort of 110 patients enabled the development of a predictive model, the sample size remains relatively small. Multi-center studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate our findings and improve generalizability across different clinical settings. Second, we performed whole-prostate radiomic analysis rather than focusing on focal areas of PSMA uptake. While whole-gland segmentation captures global heterogeneity and avoids challenges in precise lesion localization, it may dilute signal from small but metabolically aggressive foci [38]. The spatial pattern and intensity of focal tracer accumulation may carry critical information about tumor aggressiveness. Future studies should explore lesion-specific radiomic analysis—delineating individual suspicious foci on PSMA-PET—to assess whether this approach improves diagnostic accuracy for clinically significant prostate cancer [39]. Third, while we employed LASSO for feature selection, we did not evaluate the selection frequency of radiomic features during cross-validation—a method recently demonstrated in PSMA-PET studies to assess feature stability and interpretability [19]. Incorporating this analysis in future work will help identify the most robust features and improve model transparency. Additionally, our clinical model included only age and PSA. Expanding integration to include PSAD, SUVmax, (e.g., PI-RADS), and genomic markers may further enhance diagnostic accuracy. Finally, external validation in independent cohorts is essential to confirm the clinical utility of our model. We plan to share our feature extraction protocol to support reproducibility and standardization in the field.

Conclusion

This study presented a comprehensive analysis of PSMA PET/CT radiomics for differentiating clinically significant from non-significant prostate cancer. The model based on deep learning–derived features demonstrated high diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 89.5%, accuracy of 87.9%, sensitivity of 85.7%, specificity of 89.5%, and F1-score of 85.7%. Notably, using a convolutional neural network for automatic tumor segmentation not only improved reproducibility by eliminating inter-observer variability but also enhanced diagnostic accuracy compared to manual delineation. Furthermore, it significantly reduced clinical workload, enabling efficient and standardized analysis. As a result, radiomics features extracted from CNN-based segmentations may serve as a reliable, non-invasive tool for prostate cancer risk stratification, reducing reliance on invasive biopsies. The integration of automated segmentation with radiomics offers a clinically practical, scalable, and operator-independent approach that can be readily implemented in routine PET/CT workflows to support personalized decision-making.
