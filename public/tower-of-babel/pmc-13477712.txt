Burden of Diabetes and Temporal Trends in Clinic Attendance at a Sub-Urban Primary Diabetes Centre in South West Nigeria: A Five-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Ayotunde Oladunni Ale, Olubiyi Fidelis Adesina, Funmilayo Deborah Adediji, Oluwabukola. Oluwatamilore Ale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Diabetes mellitus (DM) is an increasing public health burden in sub-Saharan Africa, yet longitudinal primary care trends remain underreported. This study assessed the burden of DM and temporal trends in clinic attendance at the Talabi Diabetes Centre (TDC) in Isara-Remo, Ogun State, from 2021 to 2025Methodology:A retrospective review of 4,475 patients managed between 2021 and 2025 was conducted. Data on socio-demographic characteristics, clinical diagnoses, and service utilisation were analysed using descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, Cochran–Armitage trend analysis, and multinomial logistic regression. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.Results:The studied mean age was 57.4 ± 9.8 years, with 80.0% aged ≥50 years and 67.5% female. DM accounted for 61.5% of cases, hypertension 18.7%, and other conditions 19.8%. Most patients (76.3%) were overweight or obese. Annual clinic attendance fluctuated significantly (χ2= 68.07, p < 0.0001), rising from 948 in 2021 to 1,026 in 2025 with a mid-period decline. New registrations (13.6% of attendees) decreased significantly over time (Z = −2.41, p = 0.016). DM showed a significant declining trend in clinic burden, peaking at 69.5% in 2023 and falling to 54.4% in 2025 (p < 0.001). DM-specific new attendances also declined (p = 0.006), while hypertension remained stable and other conditions increased. Female sex, older age (≥60 years), and obesity were strongly associated with higher odds of DM and comorbidity (OR up to 8.10).Conclusion:DM remains the leading cause of clinic attendance, though its proportional burden is declining alongside rising non-diabetic conditions. Ageing, female sex, and obesity are key determinants of disease burden. These findings highlight the need for strengthened integrated chronic disease management in primary care settings.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a growing public health challenge worldwide, which disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries such as Nigeria [1]. In 2021, an estimated 537 million adults were living with diabetes globally, with projections rising to 783 million by 2045 if current trends persist [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa is expected to experience one of the fastest increases in diabetes prevalence, with the number of affected adults projected to rise by more than 130% between 2021 and 2045 [1,2]. Although prevalence in the region is lower than in high-income countries, a substantial proportion of cases remain undiagnosed, contributing to delayed presentation and an increased risk of complications [2].

In Nigeria, the burden of diabetes has increased markedly over recent decades. A systematic review in 2018 by Uloko et al. reported a pooled national prevalence of 5.77%, with significant regional variation [3]. More recent evidence from a 2024 meta-analysis by Olamoyegun et al. indicates a pooled prevalence of approximately 7.0%, suggesting a continuing upward trend [4]. Southern regions, including South-West Nigeria, consistently report higher prevalence rates compared with northern regions, likely reflecting differences in urbanization, lifestyle factors, and access to healthcare services [3,4].

Specialized diabetes care centres play a pivotal role in improving outcomes through early diagnosis, routine monitoring, and structured patient education [4]. The Talabi Diabetes Centre (TDC) serves as a primary care centre for diabetes management and long-term follow-up. Understanding patterns of clinic attendance, patient characteristics, and disease distribution is essential for effective service planning, workforce deployment, and optimal resource allocation. However, documentation on longitudinal clinic attendance patterns and diabetes trends in Nigeria, particularly within specialized clinics, remains limited [3,5,6].

To address this gap, this study seeks to describe the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of patients, annual clinic attendance trends, and determine the DM burden. Findings from this study are expected to inform service planning and guide improvements in diabetes care delivery.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective descriptive study that reviewed patients’ records from the TDC clinic between January 2021 and December 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of Olabisi Onabanjo University Teaching Hospital, with reference number OOUTH/HREC/01/001/E556/2026AP dated 10th February 2026. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards, and patient records were anonymised to ensure confidentiality.

Study Setting

The study was conducted at the TDC, a non-profit, non-governmental primary diabetes care centre located in Isara Remo, Remo North Local Government Area, Ogun State, South-West Nigeria, and affiliated with Olabisi Onabanjo University. The Centre provides specialised medical services to adjoining communities, villages, and towns within and beyond the local government area.

The Centre operates outpatient clinics from Monday to Friday, including a weekly diabetologist-led specialist clinic on Wednesdays. Services include diagnosis, routine monitoring, structured diabetes self-management education, follow-up care, and medication access through a drug-revolving scheme. It also conducts community outreach screening programmes and trains primary healthcare workers across Ogun State in diabetes education and management.

Study Population

Participants’ eligibility criteria included records of all adult patients (≥18 years) who attended the TDC clinic during the study period, comprising individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), diabetes with hypertension, hypertension without diabetes, and other disease categories.

Patients with incomplete records, particularly those lacking key demographic variables such as age or sex, were excluded from the analysis.

Data Collection

Clinic records were systematically reviewed, and data were extracted from routinely maintained clinic records using a standardized data extraction form. The following information was collected: Sociodemographic variables: age, sex, occupation; Clinical characteristics: body mass index (BMI), blood pressure; Clinic utilization data: annual attendance figures, number of new attendees, number of follow-up visits; Disease distribution: documented diagnoses including diabetes, hypertension, and other comorbid conditions.

Study Variables

The primary outcomes were annual clinic attendance trends, distribution of diabetes, hypertension, and other disease categories, and temporal trends in clinic utilisation. Secondary variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, occupation) and clinical characteristics (BMI categories and blood pressure status).

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 25.0. Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The Chi-square test and the Cochran–Armitage trend test were used for comparisons and trend analysis, respectively. Relative risk (RR) and multinomial logistic regression (reported as ORs with 95% CIs) were used to assess associations. A p-value < 0.05 was

considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 4,475 patients were managed at the TDC between 2021and 2025. The mean age of the patients was 57.4 ± 9.8 years, with the majority (80.0%) being aged ≥50 years, indicating that the clinic population was predominantly older adults. Females comprised 67.5% of attendees, showing female preponderance.

Regarding occupation, Retirees (36.7%) and traders (27.6%) were the most represented groups. Nutritional status assessment showed that more than three-quarters of patients (76.3%) were either overweight or obese (BMI ≥25 kg/m2), with females constituting the majority of individuals with overnutrition.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) was the predominant diagnosis, accounting for 61.5% of the study clinic population. Of these patients, 250 (9.1%) had concomitant hypertension. Hypertension without DM constituted 18.7%, and Other disease categories constituted (19.8%) managed like malaria, diarrheal diseases, and urinary tract infections (Table 1).

Clinic Attendance Trends (2021–2025)

Annual clinic attendance increased slightly from 948 in 2021 to 960 in 2022, followed by a decline to 824 in 2023 and 717 in 2024, before rising sharply to 1,026 in 2025 (Figure 1). There was significant heterogeneity in clinic utilisation across the study period (χ2= 68.07, df = 4, p < 0.0001), indicating statistically significant fluctuations in service utilisation over time.

New Patient Registration Trends

A total of 607 new patients were registered during the study period, representing 13.6% of all clinic attendees (Figure 2).

The highest proportion of new registrations occurred in 2023 (15.9%), while the highest absolute number was recorded in 2022 (n = 151). Thereafter, a progressive decline in new patient registration was observed.

Trend analysis using the Cochran–Armitage test demonstrated a significant downward trend in new registrations over time (Z = −2.41, χ2trend = 5.81, p = 0.016). Compared with 2021, the likelihood of being a new registrant decreased by 26% by 2025 (RR = 0.74).

Annual Distribution of Clinic Cases by Disease Category

Diabetes mellitus consistently accounted for the largest proportion of clinic cases, peaking at 69.5% in 2023 before declining to 54.4% in 2025. Hypertension proportions fluctuated between 13.7% and 24.2%, without a clear directional pattern.

In contrast, other disease categories increased progressively from 13.3% in 2021 to 25.2% in 2025, reflecting a diversification of clinic case mix (Figure 3).

Overall Five-Year Clinic-Based Burden of New Attendances

Over the five-year period, diabetes mellitus accounted for 395 cases (8.8%) of the total clinic population (4,475), comprising7.3% with DM alone and 1.5% with DM coexisting with hypertension. Hypertension alone accounted for 101; 2.3%, while other disease categories contributed 134, 3.0% of the clinic population.

Factors Associated with Disease Categories (Multinomial Logistic Regression)

Female sex was associated with significantly higher odds of disease outcomes, including DM, hypertension, and comorbidity (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.35–2.02; p < 0.001).

A strong age gradient was observed. Patients aged ≥60 years had markedly increased odds of all outcomes compared with those aged 20–29 years, with a similar but less pronounced effect in the 50–59 age group (p < 0.001).

Body mass index demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship. Overweight individuals had increased odds of DM and hypertension, while obesity showed stronger associations across all outcomes (p < 0.001).

Occupational status also influenced disease distribution, with retirees exhibiting higher odds of DM and comorbidity compared with teachers. Overall, age and adiposity showed the strongest associations, while sex and occupation had moderate but consistent effects (Table 4).

Discussion

This study reported a five-year overview of clinic attendance patterns, sociodemographic characteristics, and disease distribution in a specialized diabetes clinic in South-West Nigeria. The findings highlight a predominance of older adults and females, a high burden of overweight and obesity, fluctuating service utilisation and a consistent dominance of diabetes among clinic attendees. These trends reflect the growing impact of diabetes on specialized healthcare services and mirror the broader epidemiological transition toward non-communicable diseases in low- and middle-income countries [4].

The sociodemographic profile of clinic attendees provides important context for the observed disease patterns. Attendees were predominantly older adults (mean age 57.4 ± 9.8 years) and female (67.5%), consistent with previous Nigerian and sub-Saharan Africa reporting higher diabetes burden in middle-aged and older adults, as well as greater healthcare utilisation among women [1–5]. A substantial proportion were retired, underscoring the influence of age distribution and socioeconomic determinants on clinic utilization [5]. These characteristics likely shape the observed disease distribution, particularly the concentration of cardiometabolic conditions.

Overweight and obesity were highly prevalent (79.3%), particularly among females, mirroring national trends of increasing overnutrition in urban and semi-urban populations [11,12]. Given the well-established association between excess adiposity and insulin resistance, this likely contributes significantly to the high burden of Type 2 diabetes observed and reinforces the need for integrated lifestyle interventions within routine diabetes care [13].

Clinic attendance patterns showed significant variability, with a decline in 2023–2024 followed by a rebound in 2025. Similar disruptions in healthcare utilisation have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa, often driven by economic constraints, health system inefficiencies, and post-pandemic recovery challenges [14,15]. Nigerian studies also document reduced healthcare utilisation during periods of economic stress and system strain, followed by rebound effects when access improves [14,16].

The observed decline in new patient registration suggests reduced case detection or barriers to healthcare access, or changes in referral patterns. This was mainly contributed to by a decline in the proportion of diabetes cases over time. This is consistent with evidence that more than half of individuals with diabetes in sub-Saharan Africa remain undiagnosed due to weak screening systems and limited primary care capacity [17,18]. This underscores the urgent need for strengthened community-based screening and early detection strategies

Overall, the observed trends indicate a dynamic pattern of health service utilisation, characterised by an initial decline in clinic attendance followed by recovery, alongside a shifting disease burden marked by a relative reduction in diabetes predominance and increasing representation of other medical conditions.

The regression analysis identified key determinants of disease patterns. Increasing age showed a strong graded association with all outcomes, with individuals aged ≥60 years having the highest odds of diabetes, hypertension, and comorbidity, likely reflecting cumulative metabolic risk, vascular ageing, and reduced physiological reserve [19-21]. Similarly, body mass index demonstrated a dose–response relationship, with overweight and obesity significantly increasing disease odds, underscoring the central role of adiposity in insulin resistance and cardiometabolic dysfunction and supports its prioritisation in preventive strategies [22-24].

Female sex was associated with modestly higher odds across disease categories, possibly due to greater healthcare utilisation and post-menopausal metabolic changes [25,26]. Occupational status also influenced disease distribution, with retirees showing a higher odds of diabetes and comorbidity compared with teachers, likely reflecting reduced physical activity and age-related metabolic clustering [27].

The five-year clinic-based burden of diabetes of new attendees (8.8%) within the clinic population is higher than previous national estimates (5–7%), suggesting that specialized clinics may encounter a concentration of cases with higher disease severity or complications [1,4]. The relatively lower burden of hypertension and other conditions reflects either lower clinic presentation rates in specialized DM clinic or potential underdiagnosis. These findings highlight the important role of specialized clinics in managing chronic diseases and providing comprehensive care [5,28].

Beyond cardiometabolic conditions, nearly one-fifth of cases comprised other disease categories such as malaria and respiratory infections, etc. This reflects the dual function of the facility as both a specialised diabetes centre and a primary care provider, with implications for expanded clinical capacity and resource allocation.

Strengths of this study include its relatively large sample size and longitudinal design, enabling assessment of trends over five years within a community-based specialist centre. However, its retrospective design and reliance on routine clinical data may introduce information bias. Additionally, as a single-centre study, generalizability is limited. Incomplete records in 10 patients (0.2%); DM duration, data on disease progression, glycemic control, and complications were not captured, limiting the ability to assess long-term outcomes.

Future multicenter and prospective studies incorporating detailed outcome measures are warranted to better characterize regional variations and long-term clinical trajectories.

Conclusion

Diabetes mellitus remains the predominant condition in this suburban primary care setting, driven largely by older age and high levels of overweight and obesity. However, the declining trend in diabetes burden and new registrations, alongside increasing representation of other conditions, indicates a shifting pattern of healthcare utilisation. Strengthening early detection, expanding integrated primary care services, and prioritising obesity prevention are essential to address the evolving burden of cardiometabolic diseases in this setting.
