Associations of non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among general population
Authors: Songfeng Zhao, Liqiaona Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) is a novel lipid indicator for predicting mortality across populations, but its link to mortality in the general population is underestimated. A total of 17,334 participants from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2005–2018) were analyzed. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were employed to investigate the association between NHHR and all-cause and cardiovascular (CVD) mortality. We employed restricted cubic splines (RCS) to assess potential nonlinear relationships. Over a median follow-up of 88 months, we documented 1878 deaths. According to the RCS analysis, NHHR exhibited an L-shaped association with all-cause and CVD mortality. Inflection points were identified at NHHR = 2.30 for all-cause mortality and NHHR = 2.64 for CVD mortality. Below these thresholds, each unit increase in NHHR was associated with a 39% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR: 0.61, 95% CI 0.50–0.75) and a 34% reduction in CVD mortality (HR: 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.88). Above these thresholds, no significant associations were observed. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger inverse associations in adults aged ≥ 60 years (P for interaction < 0.0001) and those with underweight or overweight statuses (P = 0.0032). Our findings highlight an L-shaped association between NHHR and both all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death in the United States, accounting for one-third of all deaths in 20211. This burden extends globally, with elevated non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) levels estimated to have contributed to nearly four million deaths in 20172.

Epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that optimal lipid profiles are critical for cardiovascular risk reduction3,4. Traditional lipid markers such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) are widely used to guide therapy, yet they capture only part of the atherogenic burden. Epidemiological and clinical studies show that patients with normal or even low LDL-C can develop coronary heart disease5. This has spurred interest in composite lipid indices that reflect the balance between pro-atherogenic and protective lipoproteins. Among these, the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) has emerged as a novel composite lipid indicator that takes into account both atherogenic and protective lipid components6. Notably, early research suggested that lipoprotein ratios such as total cholesterol/HDL-C and LDL-C/HDL-C predict coronary heart disease better than single lipid measures7.

In recent years, NHHR has been explored in various clinical populations8–11. For instance, among adults with diabetes or prediabetes, NHHR demonstrated a U-shaped relationship with all-cause mortality and an L-shaped relationship with cardiovascular mortality9. In a cohort of U.S. cancer survivors, lower NHHR values were associated with higher all-cause mortality11. These findings suggest that both extremely low and elevated NHHR values may be detrimental, indicating a potential non-linear association between NHHR and mortality. However, most existing studies focus on disease-specific cohorts, are limited by relatively small sample sizes or short follow-up, and may not generalize to the broader population.

To clarify the prognostic value of NHHR in the general population, we analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative cohort of U.S. adults. We aimed to characterize the dose–response association between NHHR and long-term all-cause and CVD mortality and determine the potential nonlinear relationship between NHHR and mortality.

Method

Study population

NHANES is a nationally representative survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It collects comprehensive health and nutritional data on non-institutionalized U.S. civilians through household interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. The present study period spanned from 2005 to 2018, which included seven consecutive cycles.

Participants aged 20 years or older with complete data on serum total cholesterol (TC) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were included. Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals lacking complete data for NHHR calculation, those with missing mortality data and fasting data. The detailed participant selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of participants enrollment.

Calculation of NHHR

Blood lipid parameters are collected at the mobile examination centers (MECs). The Non-HDL-C were calculated as total cholesterol (TC) minus HDL-C, while the NHHR was calculated as non-HDL-C divided by the HDL-C12.

Mortality ascertainment

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, with cardiovascular mortality as secondary outcomes. Mortality status was ascertained through linkage to the National Death Index records. Causes of death were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes.

Covariates

We collected comprehensive data on potential confounding factors, including demographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity), socioeconomic status (education level, poverty-income ratio), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption), and clinical variables (body mass index, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and CVD). Race and ethnicity were categorized as Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and other races based on self-reported data. Educational level was classified as Lower than high school, high school graduate or equivalent, and college or higher.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses incorporated survey weights to account for the complex sampling design of NHANES and generate nationally representative estimates. The fasting subsample weights were used for weighted analyses. Baseline characteristics differences across NHHR quartiles were analyzed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Results were shown as means with standard errors for continuous data and as proportions for categorical data.

We used weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between NHHR and mortality outcomes. We constructed three sequential models: Model 1 is unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; and Model 3 further adjusted for education, poverty-income ratio, smoking status, alcohol consumption, body mass index, and medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and CVD. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations were detected (p > 0.05). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with all values < 2, indicating no concerning collinearity. Potential nonlinear relationships were examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Segment Cox proportional hazards regressions were conducted to explore the piecewise association between NHHR and mortality.

To explore potential effect modification, we conducted stratified analyses by age groups, sex, BMI, and presence of major chronic conditions including hypertension, DM, and CVD. Tests for interaction were performed by including interaction terms in the models. The missing rate of each covariate is summarized in Table S1. Missing data were imputed using R package “missForest” with 10 iterations and 100 trees per forest13. Given its ability for handling different types of variables, MissForest is an appropriate imputation tool for the NHANES database.

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the results. First, we excluded participants who died within the first 2 years of follow-up to mitigate reverse causality. Second, we further adjusted for baseline lipid-lowering therapy to account for the influence of lipid-modifying treatment. Third, we performed Cox regression using complete cases to evaluate potential bias introduced by data imputation. Finally, we repeated the primary analysis in participants who provided non-fasting blood samples to assess the consistency of the findings.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance set at P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Association of NHHR with all-cause and CVD mortality

During a median follow-up of 88 months, we documented 1878 deaths from all causes, accounting for 8.01% of the study population. The association between NHHR and all-cause mortality was assessed using weighted Cox proportional hazards regression models (Table 2). In the crude model, elevated NHHR was significantly associated with decreased all-cause mortality risk. However, after partial and full adjustment, the magnitude of association attenuated, yet remained statistically significant (Model3: HR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.90–0.99). Specifically, the HR for all-cause mortality in the highest NHHR quartile (Q4) compared to the lowest quartile (Q1) was 0.82 (0.71, 0.95), indicating a 18% decrease in mortality risk. We further investigated the association between NHHR and CVD mortality. Similarly, NHHR was negatively associated with CVD mortality in the crude model (Model1: HR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.84–0.99). After full adjustment, the HR for CVD mortality in Q2 compared to Q1 was 0.77 (0.59, 1.00).

Nonlinear relationship

The RCS plot revealed a nonlinear L-shaped connection between NHHR and both all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (P for nonlinear = 0.037; Fig. 2). Through segmented Cox regression analysis, inflection points were found at NHHR values of 2.30 for all-cause mortality and 2.64 for CVD mortality. These inflection points indicate a threshold-dependent relationship, where NHHR shows a negative correlation with both outcomes below these levels, but no significant link above them (Table 3). For NHHR values below the inflection points, one-unit increase in NHHR was associated with a 39% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR: 0.61, 95% CI 0.50–0.75) and a 34% reduction in cardiovascular mortality (HR: 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.88), respectively.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

The association between NHHR and all-cause mortality remained broadly consistent across most subgroups, though several notable variations emerged. For instance, a significant inverse association was observed among individuals aged ≥ 60 years compared to younger counterparts (P for interaction < 0.0001; Fig. 3A), indicating age-dependent effect modification. Similarly, NHHR exhibited significant associations with all-cause mortality in both underweight and overweight subgroups (P for interaction = 0.0032). Furthermore, the interaction between age group and NHHR extended to cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality (Fig. 3B), underscoring the modifying role of age across outcomes. Importantly, these results remained consistent across all sensitivity analyses (Tables S2–S5).

Discussion

This large, nationally representative cohort study investigated the association between the NHHR and mortality risks among U.S. general population. Our findings highlight an L-shaped association between NHHR and both all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. NHHR values below these thresholds were associated with substantial reductions in mortality risk (39% reduction in all-cause mortality and 34% reduction in CVD mortality per unit increase in NHHR), whereas NHHR values above the inflection points showed little additional benefit. This pattern differs from the traditional expectation that lower cholesterol ratios always translate into lower risk.

The L-shaped pattern may reflect the complex biology of HDL particles. Although HDL-C has long been considered protective, recent studies have shown that extremely high HDL-C may paradoxically increase mortality14,15. In non-diabetic Korean adults, a U-shaped relationship between HDL-C and all-cause mortality was observed. It’s reported that men in the highest HDL-C group (≥ 80 mg/dL) had a 31% higher risk of death compared with those in the reference range after adjustment for confounders16. A recent multicenter cohort study found that among patients with coronary artery disease, HDL-C > 80 mg/dL was associated with a 96% higher risk of all-cause death (HR 1.96, 95% CI 1.42–2.71) and 71% higher risk of cardiovascular death (HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.09–2.68) compared with HDL-C 40–60 mg/dL17. These data suggest that very high HDL-C might be dysfunctional or a marker of pathological states such as chronic alcohol consumption18.

At the opposite extreme, very low non-HDL-C may not be biologically benign. Large cohort studies of elderly Chinese adults reported that both low and high non-HDL-C levels increased all-cause and CVD mortality, with low non-HDL-C (< 1.8 mmol/L) associated with higher risk of cancer and other mortality19. This paradox may stem from malnutrition or chronic illness. Low non-HDL-C often reflects either low total cholesterol or very high HDL-C and may be a marker of underlying wasting conditions or inflammatory states19,20. Another study in patients with coronary heart disease showed that the worse prognosis in the low non-HDL-C group (< 2.2 mmol/L) disappeared after adjusting for malnutrition, indicating that poor nutritional status mediates the association between low non-HDL-C and mortality21. Therefore, the sharp mortality reduction with increasing NHHR below the threshold in our study likely reflects the benefit of avoiding extremely low non-HDL-C relative to HDL-C, while the loss of benefit at higher NHHR may correspond to the adverse effects of very high HDL-C.

Our results diverge from prior studies reporting linear or U-shaped associations between lipid ratios and all-cause mortality. For example, NHHR exhibits a U-shaped association with all-cause mortality in diabetic populations, where extremes in either direction heighten risk9, while a linear relationship was reported between NHHR and 28-day mortality among patients with sepsis10. In diabetics and pre-diabetics, NHHR was U-shaped for all-cause mortality and L-shaped for CVD mortality, with inflection points at 2.72 and 2.83; below these points, each unit increase in NHHR reduced all-cause mortality by 24% and CVD mortality by 30%, while above the thresholds the all-cause mortality risk increased by 11%9. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in study populations, particularly variations in baseline health status, comorbidities, and competing risks. Further research is needed to elucidate whether these findings generalize to other cohorts.

For CVD mortality, the lack of a significant overall association except in Q2 (24% risk reduction vs. Q1) may reflect limited statistical power due to fewer CVD events. The weaker association with CVD mortality compared to all-cause mortality could also imply that NHHR captures broader metabolic or systemic health factors beyond cardiovascular-specific pathways.

In our stratified analysis, NHHR was inversely associated with mortality in participants ≥ 60 years but not in younger individuals. This could reflect reverse causality, wherein terminal illnesses concurrently reduce non-HDL-C levels and increase mortality risk. Moreover, aged HDL may lose its protective functions, transforming NHHR into a marker of broader metabolic decline rather than atherogenic burden. This interpretation aligns with studies showing that critically low lipid levels in older populations frequently signal increased incidence of atrial fibrillation and mortality22,23. Age-related shifts in lipoprotein functionality may critically modulate NHHR’s role. For instance, aging diminishes HDL-C’s capacity for cholesterol efflux and antioxidative activity, decoupling its traditional atheroprotective effects from circulating levels24,25. HDL isolated from older individuals promotes significantly less cholesterol efflux than HDL from young individuals (41.7% vs. 49.0%; P = 0.013)26. Compellingly, experimental models indicate that HDL particles in older adults may even acquire pro-atherogenic properties through mechanisms such as increased lipid influx27. Additionally, the heightened effect in participants who were underweight or overweight could indicate that NHHR is particularly salient in metabolically compromised populations, which has been widely reported in previous research9,28. Taken together, our findings highlight the importance of personalized risk assessment, especially among high-risk populations where NHHR may have distinct implications.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the observational design precludes causal inference. Second, residual confounding may persist despite extensive adjustments. Third, the NHHR data were derived from a single baseline assessment, limiting our ability to capture the dynamic changes in NHHR levels. Future longitudinal cohorts with repeated NHHR measurements are needed to clarify temporal patterns and validate the threshold effects observed in our research. Fourth, we didn’t account for competing risk from other outcomes, such as non-CVD deaths, which may have introduced bias in estimating CVD mortality risk. Finally, while the NHANES sample reflects the general U.S. population, our findings may not be fully generalizable to other populations worldwide.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrated an L-shaped association of NHHR with all-cause and CVD mortality. These findings suggest that NHHR may serve as a novel biomarker for mortality risk stratification, particularly in individuals aged 60 or older. Further research should validate the thresholds and investigate the potential biological mechanisms driving the non-linear relationship. Clinicians mayconsider integrating NHHR into multivariable risk assessment tools, leveraging its threshold-dependent relationship to optimize preventive strategies.
