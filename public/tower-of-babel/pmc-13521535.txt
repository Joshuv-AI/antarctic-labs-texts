Enhancing deep learning-based dental caries detection through feature pyramid network-based image segmentation
Authors: Maya Fitria, Maulisa Oktiana, Muhammad Aditya Yufnanda, Muhammad Keysha Al-Yassar, Nova Rosdiana, Kahlil Muchtar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:Dental caries is a multifactorial disease that may lead to pain, infection, and tooth loss if left untreated, and it requires early detection to prevent complications and to reduce costs. Advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has enabled automated caries detection using intraoral images. However, many deep learning models rely on raw images, such as gums, tongue, and lighting variations, resulting in reduced diagnostic accuracy. Tooth region segmentation is therefore essential to focus on relevant features and to produce more reliable predictions.Aims:This study aims to improve the performance of classification models by employing a feature pyramid network (FPN)-based tooth segmentation method before classification.Settings and Design:The FPN technique was utilized for the teeth image segmentation model, which is integrated with feature extraction backbones.Materials and Methods:Three deep learning architectures, namely DenseNet169, InceptionV3, and MobileNetV2, were also employed for caries classification, and were trained with and without FPN-based segmentation. Segmentation was evaluated using IoU and Dice Similarity Coefficient for performance.Statistical Analysis Used:A comparative analysis was conducted on FPN-based segmentation by comparing models on segmented images and original images employing accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.Results:The findings show that employing FPN-based segmentation before the classification process consistently enhances the performance. DenseNet169 model outperformed InceptionV3 and MobileNetV2 models, yielding 89.28% of classification accuracy.Conclusions:The improvement in classification results also confirmed that employing segmentation techniques reinforces feature extraction, leading to more reliable classification, with strong potential for AI-based caries early screening using intraoral images.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries is a complicated dental illness caused by the interaction between bacteria, substrates, and host factors, leading to the demineralization of hard tissues.[1] The main risk factors of this dental illness include excessive sugar consumption, poor oral hygiene, and lack of access to dental care.[2] Studies indicate that children and teenagers are particularly vulnerable to dental caries.[3] Environmental factors such as eating habits and parenting styles also contribute to the risk of dental caries, as it has strong correlation to the prevalence of dental caries in children.[45] In addition, a study in Peshawar confirmed that 72% of adolescents often consume enormous amounts of fermentable carbohydrate-rich foods, underscoring the need for improvement in nutritional education and prevention in dental care programs.[6]

Untreated dental decay can progressively lead to pain, infection, and tooth loss. Recent data shows that Indonesia faces the ongoing challenges in oral health as the dental caries prevalence stroked to 57.6%, reaching 7.1 of a national Decay, Missing, and Filling Teeth (DMF-T) index.[78] The need of early dental caries detection is necessary, aiming to prevent severe complications, reduce the treatment costs, and enhance overall oral health outcomes.[9]

Compared to conventional radiographic methods, the utilization of clinical intraoral imaging for early caries detection offers significant benefits. Digital intraoral imaging is considered safer and more accessible, due to its non-invasive examination without radiation exposure.[1011] Furthermore, intraoral imaging can be conveniently taken utilizing digital cameras or smartphones, allowing for quick screening in a variety of clinical settings, including remote locations. Research has also shown that intraoral imaging can achieve diagnosis accuracy comparable to dentists’ visual inspections, and artificial intelligence (AI) can further improve the analytical process.[121314]

Clinical intraoral imaging indeed provides great advantages for the caries examination process. However, challenges in intraoral images arise during direct classification. The presence of irrelevant background elements, including gums, tongue, and lighting variations during image capturing, can lead to misclassification.[15] The classification process is further complicated due to divergence in acquisition conditions and image quality, which makes the identification between carious and non-carious regions challenging.[1617]

Several studies have been conducted to explore the effectiveness of early detection of dental caries using clinical dental images. One such study by Fitria et al.,[13] successfully developed a dataset of clinical dental images consisting of 700 images, captured from five intraoral perspectives: anterior (labial), left side (left buccal), right side (right buccal), upper occlusal (inner upper teeth), and lower occlusal (inner lower teeth). The dataset was then augmented to increase the quantity and diversity of data before being used to train a classification model based on the ResNet-50 architecture. The training results showed that the model trained with a learning rate of 10−4 achieved the highest accuracy of 0.7952 and a loss value of 0.4225.[13]

The next study, conducted by Supiyandi et al.,[18] utilized a Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)-based approach for segmentation in detecting dental caries in humans. The objective was to develop and evaluate a deep learning model based on YOLOv5 to identify dental conditions in intraoral images, including normal teeth, caries, root remnants, and fillings. The dataset consisted of 1767 labeled and augmented images, which were then divided into training data (80%), validation data (10%), and testing data (10%). A deep learning-based object detection method was implemented to assist doctors in identifying patient conditions more quickly and accurately. The evaluation results showed that the best CNN model achieved a mean average precision (mAP) of 84% and a testing accuracy of 82%, whereas the YOLOv5 model achieved an accuracy of up to 97%.[18]

Unlike previous studies, the research conducted by Fitria et al. developed an automatic dental condition detection system based on the DMF-T index using deep learning models YOLOv5 and YOLOv8. Using a dataset of 294 clinically annotated images with bounding boxes, which was expanded to 708 images through mosaic augmentation, the YOLOv5l model achieved a precision of 0.97, a recall of 0.858, and a mAP of 90.4%. Meanwhile, the YOLOv8m model performed slightly better with an mAP of 90.6% but required a longer training time.[19] On the other hand, the study by J. Kühnisch et al. employed MobileNetV2 with transfer learning to classify healthy teeth, noncavitated caries, and cavitated caries, achieving an accuracy of 92.5% in dental caries detection.[20]

Recent research has further developed end-to-end learning systems and AI-based diagnostic frameworks for detecting dental caries using intraoral images.[142122] Comprehensive discussions on AI in conservative dentistry and its prospective applications have additionally drawn attention to the transformative role of deep learning in supporting clinical diagnosis and decision support.[2324] These studies show the promising diagnostic performance and showcase the development of AI-based caries detection. Nevertheless, the majority of these systems rely on direct object detection from entire intraoral images without explicitly isolating the tooth region. Consequently, the nondental structures, such as the gingiva, tongue, and lips, may affect the feature extraction and reduce model robustness. While lightweight architecture and optimization techniques have been explored to improve development efficiency, attention to structured segmentation-classification procedures that systematically minimize background noise remains limited.

Many deep learning studies continue to rely on direct classification without segmentation, such as ResNet-50, which processes entire images, including nonrelevant background elements, resulting in noise and reduced accuracy.[2526] In addition, differences in how images are captured, such as camera angle and lighting, can affect how features are recognized and how well the model can perform under various conditions.[27] These challenges underscore the necessity of segmentation-based approaches to enhance classification accuracy and robustness.

To address these limitations, this study integrates a segmentation step using the feature pyramid network (FPN), which constructs high-level semantic feature maps at multiple scales through a top-down pathway with lateral connections. The implementation of FPN-based segmentation before classification aims to improve the performance of dental caries detection by isolating the irrelevant dental structures, such as the tongue, lips, gums, and medical instruments, and by decreasing the impact of background noise. The irrelevant dental regions can interfere with the ability of models to focus on the caries region, potentially leading to a reduction in accuracy. FPN has demonstrated superior performance in object detection and instance segmentation, making it highly suitable for dental image analysis.[28]

The remaining part of this paper is broken down into: The proposed framework is described in Section 2, the experimental results and discussion are explained in Section 3, and the conclusion of this work is provided in Section 4.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The objectives of this study are to improve the performance of dental classification while minimizing the impact of irrelevant background features in clinical intraoral images by incorporating the FPN segmentation approach before the deep learning process. Along with FPN-based segmentation, three deep learning architectures were implemented for pre-trained feature extraction. These architectures included DenseNet169, InceptionV3, and MobileNetV2. The procedures involved in this research are divided into seven primary stages, namely (1) dataset preparation; (2) dataset labelling; (3) segmentation model training and validation; (4) evaluation of segmentation model; (5) classification model training and validation; (6) evaluation of classification model; and (7) result analysis. The details of each step are explained in further depth in the following subsection.

Dataset

The additional clinical dental images from the Dental and Oral Polyclinic at Zainoel Abidin Regional Hospital in Banda Aceh, Indonesia, were added to the dataset collected by Fitria et al.,[13] and gathered to be used for the experimental study. The dataset comprises intraoral clinical images of patients aged 18–45 years, captured using a Nikon D5300 camera (24MP) with an ISO sensitivity range of 100–12,800 and an aperture setting of f/5.6. The dental images of patients were captured in a dental clinical room under room lighting conditions, with a fixed distance of 20 cm between the camera lens and the subject, ensuring consistency in data collection.

The final collected dataset consists of 1.274 images, consisting of 721 normal teeth images and 553 carious teeth images. As illustrated in Figure 1a-e, the dataset includes five perspectives of intraoral images, including anterior, right and left buccal, and upper and lower occlusal, and each image was resized to an image resolution of 224 × 224 pixels. The dataset diversity ensures the proposed method can provide an effective generalization across various elements in dental images and their imaging conditions, by providing a robust foundation for training and evaluating both segmentation and classification models.

Data preprocessing

To ensure an optimal model performance, the raw dataset was pre-processed prior to the segmentation and classification model training. The original dataset was split into training, validation, and testing datasets, with a ratio of 80:10:10,[29] resulting in sets of 1019, 127, and 128 images accordingly. The data augmentation techniques were applied to the training image set, aiming to improve the capability of the model to generalize the data and enhance its robustness. The augmentation technique used in this work was image rotation by 180 degrees, as this method effectively increases the variation of the datasets while maintaining the semantic integrity of the original images.[30] This augmentation technique effectively doubled the training dataset from 1019 to 2038 dental images. Furthermore, the variety of image orientations gained from rotated images enhances the feature extraction capability of models in recognizing dental structures from different orientations. Research also found that the accuracy and robustness of deep learning models are improved when data augmentation is applied, particularly when using a limited number of datasets.[31] Thus, the preprocessing step is essential to optimize the performance of the segmentation and classification model for automated caries detection. However, the augmentation process was not implemented to the validation and testing dataset, aiming to prevent evaluation bias. Thus, the doubled number of training dataset includes both original and augmented images, whereas the validation and testing datasets remain original.

Data labeling

To annotate the dataset, Label Studio was used as an annotation framework. The process was done manually by involving creating pixel-wise segmentation masks for the teeth in the image. The main purpose of the segmentation process conducted in this study was to localize the tooth area from the background element, rather than specifically segment the pathological areas. The segmentation model was trained and validated utilizing these masks as ground truth. To ensure the precise segmentation results, the annotations were carefully constructed to precisely represent the edges and structure of the teeth. The annotation process for both normal and carious teeth used a similar general methodology by annotating the entire visible area of the teeth. All visible tooth structures were masked as one group (tooth region), whereas nontooth areas, such as tongue, lips, gums, and dental instruments, were considered as image background [Figure 1f and g]. In addition, regions classified as carious parts were not masked as a separate class, and the segmentation did not specifically contain the location where the carious considerably altered the tooth surface [Figure 1h and i]. The goal of this approach was to reduce background noise prior to classification by enabling the segmentation model to serve as an initial stage for region-of-interest extraction.

Segmentation training and validation

The FPN architecture is commonly utilized for multi-scale object recognition and image segmentation, and works effectively for medical image segmentation in particular.[32] Two primary components of FPN architectures, namely the bottom-up pathway (encoder) and the top-down pathway (decoder), link to each other through lateral connections to improve feature representation at various sizes.[33] Convolutional and pooling layers are used by the encoder to extract the spatial features to gradually reduce image resolution while increasing the feature depth. In addition, the feature maps are reconstructed by the decoder through up-sampling and lateral connection to ensure preservation of fine details across different scales.[34] EfficientNetB1 was employed as the backbone architecture for model development. The segmentation model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5 for 100 epochs and a batch size of 4, employing intersection over union (IoU) Loss as the loss function. Model performance was assessed throughout training using a validation process to ensure its effectiveness on unseen validation data. The optimizer parameters were set to β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, and ∈ = 10−8, following commonly used default configurations. These parameters were selected to ensure stable convergence during training.

Segmentation model evaluation

The dice coefficient (DC) and IoU are utilized to evaluate the performance of the segmentation model. These metrics are commonly used in medical image segmentation as they effectively measure the degree of alignment between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth. Classification training and validation

For classification development purposes, both the original and segmented images, which were obtained using a pre-trained dental segmentation model, were separately trained using three deep learning architectures, namely DenseNet169, InceptionV3, and MobileNetV2. To ensure a fair comparison, the models were trained using the stochastic gradient descent for the optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5, CrossEntropy as the loss function, and a batch size of 8. To monitor the performance and prevent the model from over-fitting, a validation process was conducted during training.

Classification model evaluation

A comprehensive assessment of the classification model’s performance was conducted using various evaluation metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score. These metrics were computed based on the Confusion Matrix, consisting of true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative (FN). Accuracy represents the proportion of correct predictions relative to the number of testing dataset (Equation 3). Precision denotes the proportion of correctly predicted positive cases relative to all positive prediction, corresponding to the percentage of correctly diagnosed caries tooth from all instances predicted as caries (Equation 4). In addition, recall evaluates the ability of the model in identifying actual positive cases, indicating the proportion of actual caries cases that the model correctly detects (Equation 5), whereas the F1-Score signifies the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing both metrics and providing a balanced measure of a model’s ability to detect caries while minimizing FPs and FNs (Equation 6).[37] This evaluation process offers an understanding of the strengths and limitations of the model in detecting dental caries.

DISCUSSION

The experimental results obtained from this work justified the effectiveness of an FPN-based segmentation approach integrated before the classification phase for dental caries detection. With a validation IoU of 95.66% and a Dice Score of 96.26%, the proposed segmentation model showed a robust quantitative performance. These findings indicate a high degree of alignment between ground truth and predicted mask, demonstrating the effective generalization of the model. Qualitatively, the model consistently isolated the dental structures under challenging circumstances, such as cases involving missing and filled teeth. This precise isolation of the area of interest is necessary as it successfully removes unimportant background elements such as gums, lips, tongue, and other medical instruments, which typically create noise and limit feature extraction in traditional raw-image classification techniques.

The constant performance enhancements observed across all deep learning architectures demonstrate the positive effects of the segmentation process. Training the model on a segmented dental dataset outperformed the performance of the model trained on the original dataset, in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. With the increasing accuracy from 86.31% on original images to 89.28% on segmented data, DenseNet169 is suggested as the best overall performance. This progressive improvement results validate the hypothesis that lowering background noise enables the classifier to effectively focus on differentiating dental features, resulting in more accurate diagnostic results. This is further reinforced by the learning curves, which indicate a stable convergence in the trained model on segmented images and demonstrate a lower validation loss compared to the non-segmented dataset.

A key discovery of this study is that segmentation significantly improves lightweight architectures, particularly MobileNetV2, where a substantial increase in precision (from 0.6943 to 0.7844) and recall (from 0.6979 to 0.7865) was observed, whereas heavier models like DenseNet169 showed only slight improvements. Fewer parameters and lower representational capacity on a lightweight model make it typically more prone to background noise interference. By providing the model with strongly segmented data, MobileNetV2 was able to obtain a performance level competitive with larger architectures by providing the model with strictly segmented dental data, which significantly decreased the complexity of the classification task. This conclusion implies that segmentation is a prerequisite for the implementation of AI-driven diagnostic tools on mobile or on resource-constrained devices.

To contextualize findings on this work, the proposed approach performs better than similar caries detection studies. For example, using a comparable intraoral dataset, Fitria et al.[13] obtained a model accuracy of 79.52% using ResNet-50, whereas the proposed framework outperforms it by yielding an accuracy of 89.28% using DenseNet169, indicating the efficacy of the segmentation-classification pipeline. However, it is noteworthy that other research by Kühnisch et al.[20] provided a higher performance with an accuracy of 92.5%, as the investigation showed that the research concentrated on specific lesion phases, while this work used a diverse dataset with notable variations in camera angles and lighting conditions. This discrepancy may be explained by the variances in dataset complexity, implying that while the proposed approach is robust, this performance gap can be potentially addressed with further domain adaptation and dataset standardization.

From a clinical perspective, including the segmentation stage not only obtains accuracy improvement but also the reliability of the diagnostic process. An automated system that is capable of isolating teeth and identifying caries with a rough accuracy of 89% can be adopted as an initial screening tool, which could facilitate clinical decision-making. Although the proposed work yielded promising results, the challenges remain, particularly the learning curves for MobileNetV2. The sign of over-fitting remains in the curves, which is seen from the gap between validation and training accuracy remaining after segmentation. Accordingly, while feature quality is improved after segmentation, a more diverse dataset and varied augmentation methods must be explored in the future to further enhance the capability of lightweight models for generalization in the clinical environment.

Limitations and future work

Despite its promising results achieved in this study, several limitations remain challenges to be addressed further. First, the limited dataset in terms of size and diversity employed in this work affects the capability of the model in generalizing the dataset to broader clinical settings. It is suggested that future research should concentrate on enhancing the robustness and generalizability of models by expanding more varied intraoral images from multiple clinical centers or hospitals. Subsequently, the limitations related to computational complexity and time inference need to be examined thoroughly. Future research should address this to enable the deployment of real-time mobile systems.

CONCLUSIONS

This research demonstrates how incorporating dental segmentation into deep learning models can improve the accuracy of classification in dental caries detection. The proposed segmentation framework, which uses the FPN, significantly improves the feature extraction by separating the relevant dental structures while reducing background noise. The findings show that classification models trained on the segmented images result in outstanding performance across all the tested architectures. The highest performance on segmented data was yielded by DenseNet169, reaching an accuracy of 89.28%. This performance is followed by MobileNetV2 and InceptionV3, highlighting the necessity of segmentation in AI-driven dental diagnostics. Despite these advancements, issues such as over-fitting, notably in MobileNetV2, indicate the importance of additional optimization strategies, such as data augmentation and fine-tuning. To improve model generalization, future research should focus on improving segmentation techniques, exploring different deep learning frameworks, and enlarging the dataset. The findings of this work demonstrate the critical importance of the segmentation stage in AI-based dental caries detection and its potential application in clinical decision-support systems, allowing for more accurate and reliable diagnostic outcomes in dentistry.
