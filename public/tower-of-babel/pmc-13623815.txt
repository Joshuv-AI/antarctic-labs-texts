Microfragmented Adipose Tissue in Pain Management: Bridging Regenerative Biology and Clinical Therapeutics
Authors: Y Van Tran, Phong Van Pham, Miguel Narvaez Encinas, Piercarlo Sarzi Puttini, Dariusz Myrcik, Pierfrancesco Dauri, Giacomo Farì, Christopher G Gharibo, Matteo Luigi Giuseppe Leoni, Marco Mercieri, Giustino Varrassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegenerative medicine has emerged as a transformative paradigm in contemporary healthcare, shifting the therapeutic focus from symptomatic management toward the restoration of tissue structure and function through biologically active interventions. Within this framework, adipose-derived products have attracted substantial interest owing to their relative abundance, ease of harvesting, and rich cellular and paracrine composition, including mesenchymal stromal cells, pericytes, and bioactive mediators with immunomodulatory potential. Among these technologies, microfragmented adipose tissue (MFAT) constitutes an innovative, minimally manipulated approach because it preserves the native stromal vascular niche and extracellular matrix architecture while avoiding enzymatic processing. This characteristic not only maintains biological integrity but also facilitates regulatory compliance in multiple jurisdictions. This narrative review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence on microfragmented adipose tissue in pain management, integrating biological rationale, mechanistic insights, and clinical applications across musculoskeletal disorders and chronic pain conditions. Particular attention is devoted to its capacity to modulate inflammatory pathways, promote angiogenesis, and support tissue regeneration within complex pathological environments. In addition, the review critically appraises the methodological limitations of existing clinical studies, including heterogeneity of design and limited high-quality randomized evidence. Finally, future perspectives are explored, emphasizing the integration of precision medicine approaches, biomarker-driven patient stratification, and combinatorial regenerative strategies to optimize therapeutic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40122-026-00877-0.Keywords: Regenerative medicine, Microfragmented adipose tissue, Mesenchymal stem cells, Chronic pain, Osteoarthritis, Immunomodulation, Tissue regeneration

Key Summary Points

Chronic pain and musculoskeletal disorders remain major clinical challenges, with current treatments largely limited to symptomatic relief and lacking disease-modifying efficacy.Microfragmented adipose tissue (MFAT) represents a minimally manipulated regenerative approach, including the Lipogems system, which preserves the native stromal vascular niche and extracellular matrix architecture.MFAT exerts multimodal biological effects, including immunomodulation, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodelling, and neuroimmune regulation, which collectively target key mechanisms underlying pain and tissue degeneration.Emerging clinical evidence indicates durable improvements in pain and function, particularly in osteoarthritis, but remains limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneity, and scarcity of high-quality randomized trials.Further progress requires high-quality randomized controlled trials, standardized protocols, and biomarker-guided patient selection to establish the role of MFAT in precision regenerative pain medicine.

Introduction

Regenerative medicine has shifted from symptom-oriented treatment toward biologically driven restoration of tissue integrity and function, a paradigm particularly relevant in chronic pain and degenerative disorders [1]. Conventional strategies like NSAIDs, opioids, intra-articular corticosteroids, and radiofrequency procedures address symptoms without modifying underlying disease biology and carry substantial risks of gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and dependency-related adverse effects [2, 3 ].

Among regenerative substrates, adipose tissue has emerged as a preferred source owing to its abundance, ease of harvest, and high yield of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), pericytes, and immunomodulatory mediators, surpassing bone marrow aspirates in progenitor cell density per unit volume [4, 5 ]. Importantly, the regenerative capacity of adipose-derived products is increasingly understood to be mediated by paracrine signaling, immunomodulation, and microenvironmental support rather than by direct cellular differentiation [5, 6 ]. Microfragmented adipose tissue (MFAT) represents a clinically translatable refinement of this approach: it is generated by closed, mechanical systems that fragment lipoaspirate without enzymatic digestion, the Lipogems device (Lipogems International SpA, Milan, Italy) being the most extensively studied example, preserving the native perivascular niche, pericyte populations, and extracellular matrix architecture [7]. Importantly, MFAT is not synonymous with a single proprietary device: several regulatory-compliant, non-enzymatic mechanical or filtration-based platforms, including mesh- or blade-based micro-sizing systems (e.g., Tulip Medical mechanical sizing, Rigenera micrografting, and the Arthrex AutoPose Restore system) and membrane-filtration systems (e.g., Puregraft), can generate minimally manipulated adipose products, although their cellular yield, secretory profile, and resizing characteristics differ and remain incompletely standardized across devices [8, 9 ]. This structural integrity ensures compliance with regulatory minimal-manipulation criteria while positioning MFAT at the interface between advanced cell therapies and conventional injectables, offering both symptomatic relief and potential disease-modifying effects [10, 11 ].

Preliminary comparative evidence supports a durable clinical advantage of MFAT over intra-articular corticosteroids and hyaluronic acid in knee osteoarthritis [12, 13 ], with adipose-derived therapies more broadly demonstrating longer-lasting benefits than viscosupplementation, though data heterogeneity limits definitive conclusions [14]. Emerging data further suggest advantages over platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in advanced degenerative conditions requiring structural scaffolding, whereas PRP may perform comparably in early-stage disease, underscoring the importance of patient phenotyping [15, 16 ]. Nevertheless, high-quality randomized controlled trials comparing MFAT with standard-of-care treatments remain scarce, and the risk-benefit profile of MFAT relative to systemic pharmacotherapy, particularly in elderly or comorbid populations at high pharmacological risk, has not been rigorously quantified [17].

This narrative review synthesizes current biological and clinical evidence on MFAT and critically appraises its role within the evolving landscape of regenerative medicine and chronic pain management.

Methods

Although a formal Methods section is not mandatory for narrative reviews, it is reported here to render the search and selection process explicit, reproducible, and auditable, in keeping with current expectations for transparent narrative synthesis and with the SANRA framework. This narrative review was conducted in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA), ensuring methodological transparency, comprehensive coverage of the literature, and critical appraisal of the available evidence [16]. Although narrative in design, efforts were made to minimize selection and interpretative bias through a structured and reproducible search strategy, predefined eligibility criteria, and rigorous source verification. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Scopus, covering the period from January 2005 to March 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords to ensure both sensitivity and specificity. Core queries targeted microfragmented adipose tissue and regenerative medicine using terms such as “microfragmented adipose tissue”, “regenerative medicine”, “tissue repair”, and “mesenchymal stromal cells”. Additional searches addressed clinical applications in pain-related conditions, including “chronic pain”, “osteoarthritis”, and “tendinopathies”, as well as musculoskeletal and orthopaedic settings. Comparative effectiveness was explored by combining regenerative therapies with established interventions, including PRP, corticosteroids, and hyaluronic acid. Boolean operators and truncation were adapted to each database, and manual screening of reference lists was performed to ensure completeness. Studies were considered eligible if they were peer-reviewed, indexed in major biomedical databases, and reported clinical (randomized or non-randomized), translational, or preclinical investigations of MFAT or adipose-derived therapies, with outcomes related to pain, functional improvement, tissue repair, or biological mechanisms, and published in English. Conference abstracts without full text, non-peer-reviewed sources, and studies lacking methodological transparency were excluded. Titles and abstracts were screened by three independent reviewers (YVT, PVP, MNE), followed by full-text evaluation. Data extraction included study design, population, intervention characteristics, comparators, outcomes, and follow-up. Methodological quality was appraised qualitatively according to the SANRA framework, and findings were synthesized using thematic and mechanistic approaches, with explicit consideration of bias, heterogeneity, and limitations.

Biological Rationale of MFAT

Cellular Composition and Structural Preservation

Adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (ASCs) constitute the principal regenerative cell population of this tissue: they are recovered in substantially higher yield per unit volume than bone marrow-derived MSCs, express the canonical MSC immunophenotype (CD73, CD90, and CD105, with absence of CD34/CD45 after culture), retain tri-lineage differentiation potential, and act predominantly through a potent immunomodulatory and trophic secretome rather than through direct engraftment or differentiation. Adipose tissue is a heterogeneous organ comprising adipocytes, endothelial cells, immune cells, pericytes, and MSCs embedded within a dynamic ECM, providing structural support and biochemical signaling [18]. Its stromal vascular fraction (SVF) orchestrates regenerative processes predominantly through paracrine signaling, extracellular vesicle release, and immunomodulatory interactions rather than direct cellular differentiation[19], a paradigm reinforced by translational studies highlighting the central role of the adipose secretome, including cytokines, growth factors, and exosomes, in modulating inflammation and promoting tissue repair [20].

Unlike enzymatic digestion, which disrupts cellular architecture and may alter the cell phenotype, mechanical microfragmentation employs gentle mechanical processing to preserve the structural integrity of adipose tissue, yielding a MFAT product enriched in pericyte-like cells and MSC precursors retained within their native microenvironment [7, 21 ]. Preservation of ECM components and microvascular structures sustains cell-cell communication and prolonged paracrine activity, increasingly recognized as key determinants of regenerative efficacy [22, 23 ]. Pericytes, abluminal MSC progenitors central to angiogenesis, vascular stability, and tissue regeneration [24], are retained within MFAT, enabling context-dependent MSC-like activation in response to injury signals; pericyte-immune cell crosstalk further contributes to macrophage polarization and modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment [25]. Adipose-derived extracellular vesicles and exosomes additionally serve as critical mediators of regenerative signaling, influencing gene expression, angiogenesis, and anti-fibrotic pathways [26]. Therefore, MFAT represents not merely a cellular therapy but also a structurally preserved, biologically active microenvironment that integrates cellular, matrix, and paracrine components to optimize regenerative outcomes.

Paracrine Signaling and Secretome

The therapeutic efficacy of MFAT is predominantly mediated through paracrine and trophic mechanisms rather than direct cellular engraftment or differentiation. MSCs, pericytes, and other stromal vascular niche components secrete a dynamic repertoire of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) [27], all of which contribute to angiogenesis, anti-inflammatory signaling, and extracellular matrix remodelling, which collectively orchestrate tissue repair, angiogenesis, ECM remodelling, and immunomodulation.

EVs and exosomes have emerged as principal effectors of this activity, transferring microRNAs, proteins, and lipids, which modulate target cell gene expression and sustain regenerative signaling [28, 29 ] while driving a pro- to anti-inflammatory microenvironmental shift characterized by M2 macrophage polarization and attenuation of TNF-α and IL-1β [30]. Such immunological reprogramming is particularly relevant in chronic pain states, where persistent low-grade inflammation and neuroimmune dysregulation are central pathogenic drivers [31]. These mechanisms position MFAT as a biologically active signaling platform that modulates local tissue environments and promotes functional recovery.

Immunomodulatory Effects

MFAT exerts broad immunomodulatory effects through coordinated interactions with both innate and adaptive immune systems, encompassing macrophage polarization, T-cell regulation, and remodelling of the cytokine milieu [32]. Central to this activity is the paracrine-driven shift toward an M2 macrophage phenotype, accompanied by downregulation of TNF-α and IL-1β and upregulation of IL-10 and TGF-β, collectively supporting anti-inflammatory responses, tissue repair, and matrix remodelling [33, 34 ]. Recent well-controlled studies have demonstrated that this immunological reprogramming is mediated by both soluble factors and EV/microRNA signaling, which modulate gene expression across immune and stromal cell populations [35]. Additionally, MFAT promotes regulatory T-cell induction and restrains effector T-cell proliferation, contributing to immune tolerance and resolution of chronic inflammation [36]. These mechanisms closely align with contemporary models of chronic pain, which increasingly emphasize the role of neuroimmune crosstalk, peripheral sensitization, and central immune activation in the persistence of pain states [37].

Mechanisms of Action in Tissue Repair

Anti-inflammatory Effects

Chronic musculoskeletal disorders, including osteoarthritis, are driven not only by structural degeneration but also by persistent low-grade intra-articular inflammation [38]. Synovial immune activation and catabolic mediator release collectively drive cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodelling, and peripheral nociceptor sensitization [39], establishing inflammation as a central mechanistic link between tissue pathology and pain generation. MFAT addresses this inflammatory milieu through a multifaceted biological action (Fig. 1): experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates downregulation of articular TNF-α and IL-1β alongside microRNA-mediated suppression of NF-κB signaling and related inflammatory cascades implicated in osteoarthritis progression. As summarized in Fig. 1, by limiting TNF-α/IL-1β-driven activation of the IKK–IκB–NF-κB axis, MFAT attenuates downstream pro-inflammatory and pro-algesic effectors, including IL-6, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) [40]. Importantly, these anti-inflammatory effects are closely linked to the modulation of neuroimmune interactions, which are now understood to play a critical role in pain chronification [37]. By attenuating synovial inflammation and reducing the release of algogenic mediators, MFAT may simultaneously preserve structural tissue integrity and reduce peripheral sensitization and nociceptive input [41]. This positions MFAT as a biologically coherent intervention within contemporary pain models, where inflammation is both a driver of tissue pathology and a key determinant of pain persistence.

Angiogenesis and Tissue Regeneration

Neovascularization is a fundamental prerequisite for tissue repair, as adequate vascular supply is essential for oxygen delivery, nutrient exchange, and metabolic clearance [42]. In degenerative and chronically inflamed tissues, impaired microcirculation and endothelial dysfunction sustain a hostile microenvironment that limits intrinsic healing capacity [43]. Within this context, MFAT addresses this through multiple coordinated pro-angiogenic mechanisms [44]. Its cellular constituents and secretome release a broad spectrum of angiogenic mediators, including VEGF, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and angiopoietins, that collectively stimulate endothelial proliferation, migration, and capillary network formation (Fig. 1). Mechanistically, as described in Fig. 1, VEGF engages its principal receptor VEGFR2 to activate the PI3K-AKT-eNOS and PLCγ-PKC cascades that drive endothelial survival and sprouting, whereas angiopoietin-1 (Angpt1) signaling through the Tie2 receptor promotes pericyte recruitment and vessel maturation and stabilization [18, 45 ]. Beyond soluble factors, recent well-controlled studies have highlighted the contribution of EV-associated microRNAs in regulating endothelial gene expression and sustaining angiogenic signaling [46]. The preservation of perivascular niches within MFAT further supports vascular regeneration by maintaining pericyte-endothelial interactions, which are critical for vessel stabilization and maturation [47].

Importantly, enhanced neovascularization not only facilitates structural repair but also supports functional recovery by improving tissue perfusion and modulating local inflammation. Emerging evidence further suggests that restored microvascular integrity may indirectly attenuate nociceptive processing by reducing ischaemia-induced peripheral sensitization and limiting the release of algogenic mediators [48]. All these findings position MFAT as a promising therapeutic approach that integrates angiogenic, immunomodulatory, and regenerative mechanisms within a unified biological framework.

Extracellular Matrix Remodelling

The ECM is a dynamic macromolecular network that provides structural support, regulates cellular behaviour, and serves as a reservoir for signaling molecules (Fig. 1), maintained by a tightly regulated balance between MMP-mediated degradation and TIMP-mediated inhibition [49, 50 ]. Disruption of this balance, as in osteoarthritis (OA), leads to pathological remodelling characterized by excessive degradation or fibrosis [51, 52 ]. In OA specifically, TNF-α- and IL-1β-driven amplification of MMP and aggrecanase activity, combined with insufficient TIMP expression, perpetuates progressive cartilage destruction [53–55]. MFAT modulates this process through multiple complementary mechanisms. Adipose-derived stromal cells regulate MMP/TIMP dynamics, reducing matrix degradation and promoting tissue repair while enhancing collagen synthesis via paracrine and juxtacrine signaling [50, 56, 57 –]. Experimental models show that MFAT reduces degradative mediators such as MMP-9, increases TIMP-1, and downregulates inflammatory pathways, including TLR4 and NF-κB, thereby attenuating ECM breakdown [36, 59 ]. MSC-derived EVs provide an additional regulatory layer by delivering bioactive molecules that modulate gene expression and ECM turnover [60], promote matrix reconstruction, and exert anti-fibrotic effects through ERK/MAPK signaling and miR-192-5p–mediated targeting of the IL-17RA/Smad axis [61, 62 ].

Proteomic and miRNA analyses further highlight MFAT’s cartilage-protective profile, including promotion of anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization [63], while clinical evidence demonstrates increased glycosaminoglycan content following MFAT injection, consistent with enhanced matrix regeneration [64]. Additionally, inflammatory priming may further enhance the anti-fibrotic and remodelling capacity of MFAT, supporting a context-dependent therapeutic effect [56].

Mechanisms of Action in Pain Modulation

The tissue-repair mechanisms outlined above (attenuation of synovial inflammation, restoration of microvascular integrity, and rebalancing of extracellular-matrix turnover) also contribute indirectly to analgesia by lowering the local concentration of algogenic mediators and limiting peripheral sensitization. The present section focuses on the more direct neuro-modulatory mechanisms through which adipose-derived products are proposed to influence nociceptive signaling while explicitly distinguishing effects that are exerted locally at the injection site from those that have only been demonstrated for distinct experimental preparations. At the systems level (Fig. 1), these combined actions are proposed to attenuate nociceptive processing in a hierarchical manner, reducing peripheral sensitization, dampening spinal glutamatergic transmission mediated by N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors in the dorsal horn and thereby decreasing supraspinal pain perception.

Neuro-modulatory Effects

Emerging evidence indicates that adipose-derived products modulate nociceptive pathways by attenuating neuroinflammation and reducing peripheral nerve sensitization through cytokine regulation, glial cell modulation, and extracellular vesicle signaling [39, 65 ]. Chronic pain is sustained by maladaptive neuroimmune interactions in which activated glial cells release pro-inflammatory mediators perpetuating central sensitization and neuronal hyperexcitability [66–68]. MFAT counteracts these processes through several converging mechanisms, most of which have been characterized using culture-expanded adipose- or bone marrow-derived MSCs, their conditioned medium, or systemically/intrathecally administered extracellular vesicles rather than locally injected bulk MFAT: preclinical studies demonstrate reversal of nociceptive hypersensitivity and reduction of TNF-α and IL-6, partly through M2 microglial polarization [69–71]; MSC-derived EVs promote nerve repair, neurite outgrowth, and upregulation of BDNF, NGF, and GDNF by transporting bioactive molecules across neural barriers (an effect documented for isolated, systemically, or intrathecally delivered vesicles rather than for peripherally injected MFAT) [72–75]; and the adipose-derived secretome suppresses microglial activation while modulating purinergic signaling [76, 77 ]. Additionally, MFAT exerts antioxidant effects by reducing reactive oxygen species and restoring redox homeostasis, thereby limiting oxidative stress-driven neuronal hyperexcitability [78, 79 ]. By simultaneously targeting neuroinflammation, glial activation, neurotrophic signaling, and oxidative stress, MFAT represents a mechanistically coherent, multimodal approach in mixed pain conditions characterized by overlapping nociceptive and neuropathic mechanisms [80, 81 ], though further translational studies are required to fully elucidate the underlying pathways and optimize clinical application (Table 1). An important translational caveat must be emphasized. The central neuro-modulatory effects summarized above (modulation of microglial and astrocytic activation, suppression of spinal NF-κB signaling, and attenuation of central sensitization) derive almost exclusively from experimental paradigms employing culture-expanded MSCs, cell-conditioned medium, or extracellular vesicles delivered systemically (intravenously) or directly into the central nervous system (intrathecally), and not from peripherally injected bulk MFAT. Locally administered MFAT is retained within the target joint, tendon, or peri-articular tissue and is not expected to cross anatomical barriers in sufficient quantity to act directly on central glial populations. A clear conceptual distinction should therefore be drawn between two categories of effect: modulation of the local peripheral nociceptive and immune microenvironment by intact MFAT at the injection site, for which biological and clinical support is comparatively direct, and putative remote or central actions mediated by the MFAT secretome and its isolated vesicles, which remain hypothetical and, to date, unproven in human MFAT applications. Animal studies relying on systemic or intrathecal cell or vesicle delivery should accordingly be regarded as establishing biological plausibility rather than as direct evidence for the central action of clinically administered peripheral MFAT injections.

Clinical Applications of MFAT

Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) represents the most extensively investigated clinical indication for MFAT, reflecting both its high global prevalence and the persistent limitations of conventional therapies [84]. Increasing evidence suggests that MFAT may provide clinically meaningful improvements in pain and function by targeting underlying inflammatory and degenerative processes rather than offering purely symptomatic relief [10]. Early clinical investigations have provided encouraging results. Prospective studies reported sustained and statistically significant improvements in clinical conditions at 12 months following intra-articular MFAT injection in patients with knee OA [85, 86 ]. Russo et al. [87] similarly demonstrated meaningful reductions in pain intensity and improvements in joint function extending beyond the typical duration associated with corticosteroid injections, findings consistent with a disease-modifying rather than a purely analgesic mechanism, subsequently confirmed at 3-year follow-up [88]. More recent systematic reviews have further contextualized these findings within the broader landscape of intra-articular therapies, supporting promising efficacy and acceptable safety of adipose-derived therapies for knee OA, but robust evidence of superiority over standard injectable treatments is still insufficient [89, 90 ]. These data support the emerging role of MFAT as a biologically driven therapeutic option in OA (Table 2). Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains predominantly observational, underscoring the need for rigorously designed randomized controlled trials to precisely define its comparative efficacy and optimal clinical positioning.

Tendinopathies

MFAT has increasingly been explored as a regenerative strategy for chronic tendinopathies, including rotator cuff disease, lateral epicondylitis and Achilles tendinopathy, conditions characterized by failed healing responses, disorganized collagen architecture, and persistent low-grade inflammation. Clinically, Usuelli et al. [94] reported significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes following MFAT injection in chronic Achilles tendinopathy, supporting both the feasibility and therapeutic potential of this approach. These findings are mechanistically grounded: adipose-derived stromal cells and pericyte-rich niches within MFAT promote tenogenic processes through paracrine release of VEGF and TGF-β, supporting collagen synthesis and matrix remodelling [82]. Preclinical and translational studies further show that adipose-derived products can modulate tendon-derived cell activity, enhance type I collagen deposition, and reduce inflammatory mediators implicated in tendinopathy progression [101]. Andia et al. [102] highlighted the capacity of biological therapies to stimulate tendon regeneration through local microenvironment modulation, while more recent experimental data confirm that adipose-derived EVs contribute anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative signals within tendon tissue [103, 104 ]. These findings position MFAT as both a cellular therapy and a biologically active scaffold capable of restoring tendon homeostasis. Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains limited, and well-designed randomized controlled trials are required to definitively establish its efficacy and comparative effectiveness against established treatments such as PRP or structured rehabilitation programs (Table 2).

Sports Medicine and Orthopaedics

MFAT is increasingly being investigated as a regenerative strategy to accelerate tissue recovery and reduce time to return to activity [13, 105 ]. Its application spans a range of sports-related injuries, including focal cartilage defects, muscle injuries, and ligamentous lesions, all of which are characterized by limited intrinsic healing capacity and a high risk of incomplete recovery. The biological rationale resides in MFAT's pericyte-rich stromal vascular niche, which modulates inflammation, promotes angiogenesis, and supports matrix remodelling within damaged tissues. Clinical evidence, although still evolving, is encouraging. For example, Zaffagnini et al. [91] conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial comparing MFAT with PRP in knee osteoarthritis, a condition frequently encountered in athletic populations, and demonstrated significant clinical improvement in both groups, confirming the regenerative potential of MFAT in joint pathology (Table 2). This finding has since been independently corroborated by an investigator-initiated randomized controlled trial, which reported that a single intra-articular injection of MFAT was equivalent to platelet-rich plasma for pain and functional improvement in knee osteoarthritis at 12 months, with neither treatment demonstrating superiority [106]. For focal chondral defects, injectable biologics including MFAT have yielded promising symptomatic and functional outcomes [92, 93 ]. At the experimental and translational level, adipose-derived products have been shown to enhance muscle regeneration and ligament healing through paracrine signaling and modulation of local inflammatory responses, with a comprehensive review further supporting the role of adipose-derived stem cells in musculoskeletal regeneration [107]. All these data position MFAT as a promising adjunct in sports-related injury management. However, high-quality, injury-specific randomized controlled trials, particularly addressing muscle and ligament repair, remain limited, underscoring the need for more robust evidence to precisely define its role in sports medicine.

Chronic Pain Syndromes

Beyond structural tissue repair, MFAT is increasingly explored as a biologically oriented intervention in chronic pain conditions, including chronic low back pain (LBP), facet joint arthropathy, and selected forms of neuropathic pain, indications where conventional therapies frequently provide limited and transient benefit [80, 85, 108 ]. The rationale is grounded in MFAT's capacity to modulate local inflammatory processes, influence neuroimmune interactions, and attenuate peripheral sensitization. Although high-quality randomized trials remain scarce, early clinical evidence is accumulating. Noriega et al. [109] demonstrated that cell-based regenerative approaches (using allogeneic, culture-expanded bone marrow-derived MSCs, a product mechanistically and regulatorily distinct from minimally manipulated adipose tissue) can improve both pain and disc degeneration, supporting the biological plausibility of MFAT in spine pain, while others have reported feasibility and clinical improvement using adipose-derived regenerative approaches in low back pain [96].

These observations are mechanistically supported by experimental evidence showing that adipose-derived products downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulate glial activation, both of which are central to chronic pain pathogenesis [110, 111 ]. Moreover, EVs derived from adipose stromal cells influence neuronal signaling pathways and reduce neuroinflammation, providing a plausible biological basis for their application in mixed pain syndromes [83, 112 ]. Importantly, the concept of mixed pain, characterized by overlapping nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic mechanisms, aligns well with MFAT's multimodal biological activity. By simultaneously targeting inflammation, tissue degeneration, and neuroimmune dysregulation, MFAT may offer a more integrative therapeutic approach than conventional analgesics [81].

Low Back Pain and Discogenic Pain

LBP, frequently driven by intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD), involves nucleus pulposus cell loss, catabolic overactivity, reduced ECM synthesis, and TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6-mediated chronic inflammation, culminating in structural deterioration and nociceptive sensitization [1, 113–115 –]. Current strategies remain largely symptomatic, failing to reverse the underlying degenerative process [117].

Adipose-derived MSCs represent promising regenerative candidates owing to their accessibility, chondrogenic potential, and immunomodulatory capacity [5, 118 ]. Preclinical studies confirm their ability to survive within the hostile disc microenvironment, enhance ECM production, and attenuate inflammation [95, 97 ]. Early clinical data report favorable safety and preliminary efficacy following intradiscal administration [96, 119 ], supported by evidence from bone marrow-derived MSCs that reinforces biological plausibility despite variable clinical outcomes [109, 120, 121 ]. MFAT represents a further refinement by preserving the stromal vascular niche and sustaining paracrine activity [7, 10 ], with preliminary reports indicating feasibility as both standalone therapy and surgical adjunct (Table 2) [108, 122 ]. Mechanistically, MFAT may attenuate inflammation, modulate matrix degradation, and promote nucleus pulposus anabolism via EV-mediated signaling [40, 116, 123 ], though delivery route, dosing, patient selection, and long-term efficacy require validation in rigorous RCTs [114, 124, 125 ].

Facet joint arthropathy contributes to 15–45% of chronic axial LBP cases, presenting with cartilage loss, subchondral sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation analogous to peripheral osteoarthritis [126–128]. Corticosteroid injections and radiofrequency neurotomy provide only transient relief without modifying disease progression [129]. Adipose-derived approaches offer a biologically grounded alternative, delivering MSCs, pericytes, and bioactive mediators that modulate inflammation, support cartilage homeostasis, and attenuate nociceptive signaling [10]. Observational data indicate sustained pain improvement [98], and early-phase bone marrow MSC trials confirm intra-articular safety and feasibility [130]. MFAT may confer additional advantages through stromal vascular niche preservation and simplified preparation [7, 10 ], though high-quality MFAT-specific evidence remains lacking and robust controlled trials are urgently needed.

Other Emerging Indications

Emerging applications of MFAT extend beyond musculoskeletal indications to include wound healing, reconstructive surgery, and urological disorders. Clinical and translational studies suggest that adipose-derived products enhance tissue regeneration, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation in chronic wounds and soft-tissue defects, while also showing potential in conditions such as stress urinary incontinence [99, 100 ].

Safety Profile and Regulatory Considerations

MFAT is generally considered safe, with a low incidence of adverse events that are predominantly minor and procedure-related. Clinical series and observational studies consistently report favorable safety profiles without serious procedure-related complications. Importantly, the closed mechanical processing of MFAT generally satisfies “minimal manipulation” criteria, aligning with US FDA HCT/P 21 CFR Part 1271 (Section 361) and European regulatory frameworks for homologous use, thereby facilitating clinical adoption compared with enzymatically processed cell therapies [87, 131, 132 ].

Limitations of Current Evidence and Future Directions

MFAT represents a biologically plausible and clinically applicable regenerative strategy, bridging advanced cell-based therapies and routine practice. Its multimodal mechanisms, anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and immunomodulatory, align with contemporary disease models emphasizing immune dysregulation and impaired tissue repair, targeting key drivers of chronic pain and degeneration. However, the field remains at an early translational stage. Current evidence is constrained by substantial heterogeneity in study design, a predominance of observational data, and a paucity of high-quality randomized controlled trials. Small sample sizes, variability in harvesting and processing techniques, short follow-up durations, and placebo effects, particularly relevant in pain outcomes, further limit the robustness and comparability of findings, and incomplete mechanistic understanding warrants cautious interpretation. MFAT should therefore be considered within a multimodal, patient-centred framework integrating rehabilitation and pharmacological strategies. Future progress will depend on precision regenerative approaches incorporating biomarkers, advanced imaging, and clinical phenotyping to optimize patient selection. Combination therapies and artificial intelligence-driven decision support may enhance efficacy. Crucially, large-scale randomized trials, standardized methodologies, and long-term outcome data are needed to establish definitive clinical utility.

This narrative review carries inherent limitations. Its non-systematic design may introduce selection and interpretative bias, despite adherence to SANRA criteria. The available literature is heterogeneous, with a predominance of small observational studies and limited high-quality randomized controlled trials. Moreover, variability in MFAT preparation and administration techniques limits comparability across studies. Additionally, follow-up durations are often insufficient to assess long-term efficacy and safety. Finally, mechanistic insights remain incompletely defined, and publication bias cannot be excluded, particularly given the emerging nature of the field.

Conclusions

MFAT represents a promising and biologically sound approach in regenerative medicine. Its ability to modulate inflammation, promote tissue repair, and potentially influence pain pathways positions it as a valuable tool in the management of musculoskeletal and chronic pain conditions. Future research should focus on methodological rigour, mechanistic elucidation, and integration into personalized treatment paradigms to fully realize its clinical potential.
