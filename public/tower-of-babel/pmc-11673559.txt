Mung bean protein enhances the expansion of corn starch during twin‐screw extrusion
Authors: Joshua Bernin, Preston Watanabe, Caleb E. Wagner, Stephanie Smith, Girish M. Ganjyal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the effects of the inclusion of mung bean protein (MBP) on the direct expansion characteristics of corn starch during twin‐screw extrusion. Six blends of corn starch and MBP isolate (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% w/w) were hydrated to three different moisture contents (MCs) (16%, 19%, and 21% w.b.). The blends were extruded using a twin‐screw extruder at three screw speeds (SSs) (300, 400, and 500 rpm). The resulting extrudates were evaluated for their water solubility index, water absorption index, expansion ratio (ER), true density, unit density, and porosity. As the protein content increased, the porosity of the extrudates increased. The ER of all extrudates ranged from 2.90 to 5.46, with the largest ER observed at an SS of 400 rpm, an MC of 19%, and 25% MBP inclusion. The porosity of the extrudates ranged from 1.79% to 11.42%. SS and protein content had a significant impact (p < 0.05) on the porosity and ER of the extrudate.Practical ApplicationThis work provides valuable information for the industry on utilizing mung bean protein in direct expanded corn starch‐based extruded snacks. The information could be useful in the development of high‐protein extruded snacks and breakfast cereals.

INTRODUCTION

Many worldwide health organizations recommend an increased intake of plant‐derived foods (Kerbaol et al., 2006), and the demand for innovative ways to utilize a wider variety of crops has increased (Brishti et al., 2021; Du et al., 2018; Espín et al., 2007; Ramadhani et al., 2020). Although mung bean (Vigna radiate L.) has over 2000 years of cultivation history in Asia, it has been underutilized in the food industry because it is more difficult to mill than other pulses and requires additional steps to process relative to other plant‐based protein sources (Brishti et al., 2017; Du et al., 2018; Espín et al., 2007; Table S2).

Mung bean contains high concentrations of starch and protein, approximately 55%–65% and 25%–28% d.b., respectively (Brishti et al., 2017; Du et al., 2018). Garcia and Coffmann (1977) found that mung bean protein (MBP) isolate contains all amino acids, with methionine, cysteine, and tryptophan being deficient, as is typical of many legume proteins. Mung beans are commonly fractionated to produce starch, whereas the protein is treated as a coproduct utilized for livestock feed (Brishti et al., 2017; Du et al., 2018; Ramadhani et al., 2020). MBP has been reported to have higher water solubility and gel strength relative to soy and wheat proteins (Brishti et al., 2017; Langton et al., 2020; Lee & Chin, 2013; Schmidt et al., 1984). MBP can be used in food applications and is currently being used to create plant‐based cheese (Langton et al., 2020), egg, and meat alternatives (Brishti et al., 2021), which has been shown to improve product characteristics during the extrusion of a compound noodle (Diao et al., 2022).

There has been relatively little research on how adding MBP may impact directly expanded products produced by extrusion. However, there are reported studies focused on the rheological, physiochemical, and thermal changes to MBP with the addition of corn starch (Hossain Brishti et al., 2021; Tarahi, Hedayati, et al., 2022; Tarahi, Shahidi, et al., 2022) and texturization of MBP during high moisture extrusion (Hossain Brishti et al., 2021; Seetapan et al., 2023). The reported studies have not investigated the incorporation of MBP in direct expanded extruded starches.

The characteristics of an extruded direct expansion product (such as hardness and crispness) are determined by the extrudates expansion ratio (ER), porosity, and density. These properties are dependent on the ability of the product to expand (Ek et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2022). Extrusion has a unique way of expanding starch products by utilizing high pressure, shear, and depolymerization of starch molecules. During the expansion process, the viscoelastic properties of the starch will significantly impact product expansion (Alvarez, 2020; Kiristiawan & Valle, 2020; Sereno et al., 2007). The addition of other components, such as protein, will affect the viscosity and viscoelastic properties of the molten material and, therefore, alter the expansion characteristics of the starches (Ditudompo et al., 2016; Downs et al., 2023; Gomez & Aguilera, 1984).

Despite having desirable functional and nutritional attributes such as quality amino acid profile, solubility, water‐holding capacity, and film‐forming ability, proteins derived from mung beans have not yet been fully explored in extrusion processing and specifically for direct expansion (Du et al., 2018). Thus, this research aimed to explore the impacts of the inclusion of MBP with corn starch during the direct expansion process.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ingredients and blend preparation

MBP isolate (protein: 77.86 ± 3.43, fiber 2.14 ± 0.14, ash 3.41 ± 0.51, starch 0.32 ± .01, fat 3.20 ± 0.89, moisture, 7.03 ± 0.02) was purchased from Green Boy Group. Native corn starch was provided by Tate and Lyle Ingredients Americas LLC. For the physicochemical characterization of the raw materials, ten 100 g blends of MBP and corn starch were made, each with a different MBP inclusion level of 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%, 40%, and 50% (w/w) to show the effect of MBP at concentrations above 25%. The blends were mixed for 2 min using a Uni Lab stand mixer (ONiLAB OS20‐S, OniLAB Scientific) to ensure an even distribution of protein and starch. All tests were run in triplicate unless specified.

Proximate analysis

The total fiber was analyzed using an Ankom fiber analyzer (Model 200, Ankom Technology) using the American Oil Chemists Society method Ba 6a‐05. The protein content was measured using the AOAC method 920.87 with a nitrogen conversion factor of 5.7. The moisture, ash, and crude fat contents were measured by AACCI method 44‐15.02, 0.8‐01.01, and 30‐25.01, respectively. Starch was measured using a total starch assay kit (K‐TSTA‐50A 03/20, Megazyme Intl., Bray, Co.).

Extrusion blend preparation

MBP was blended with corn starch at concentrations of 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% (w/w). The moisture content (MC) of each blend was adjusted to 16%, 19%, or 21% (±0.5% w.b.) using a spray bottle and confirmed using AOAC method 930.15. The samples were mixed in a Hobart Mixer (Model A‐200) for 5 min. Hydrated blends were sealed in polyethylene bags and refrigerated for 24 h to equilibrate the moisture.

Pasting properties

The pasting properties of the blends were analyzed per the method described by Wagner et al. (2023) using a ViscoQuick (Model 803600, Brabender GmbH & Co.). To prepare the samples, 10 g of each blend was standardized to 14% w.b. in 105 mL of water. For each blend, this solution was mixed in an overhead mixer for 1 min at 1000 rpm (ONiLAB OS20‐S, OniLAB Scientific). The pasting temperature, peak viscosity, trough viscosity, and final viscosity were measured as described by Wagner et al. (2023). A viscosity, time, and temperature graphs were generated for each blend.

Extrusion processing

Extrusion was conducted with a 20 mm corotating parallel twin‐screw extruder (Brabender TSE 20/40, CW Brabender Instruments Inc.) with an L/D ratio of 19.5, as described by Ikuse et al. (2024). The blends with different protein inclusion levels were extruded in a fully randomized order at a constant feed rate of 60 g/min. The screw speed (SS) was adjusted to 300, 400, or 500 rpm as per the experimental design. The raw ingredient blends were fed into the extruder with a twin‐auger volumetric feed system (Model 15‐37‐000, Brabender Technology).

The screw profile included conveying elements, compression elements, and one reverse kneading element (Figure 1) (Wagner et al., 2023). The internal temperature controllers of the extruder were used to set the temperatures of the different zones: die section (130°C), die adaptor (130°C), compression and metering section (100°C), and conveying section (50°C). A round hole die with 3.15 mm diameter was used (Wagner et al., 2023). The torque, back pressure, temperature, and SS were recorded by the built‐in Intelli‐Torque Plasti‐Corder data acquisition system (CW Brabender Instruments Inc.). The specific mechanical energy (SME) was calculated from the torque, SS, and feed rate per Godavarti and Karwe (1997). Extrudates were collected and dried in a horizontal‐flow convection oven (Model 414004‐568, VWR International) at 45°C for 24 h. The dried extrudates were then sealed in airtight polyethylene bags and stored in a cool and dry place for further analysis.

Screw profile used in this study. The conveying screw elements (SE) are in reference to three different pieces of information: screw pitch (20 or 30 mm), screw element length (20 or 30 mm), and directionality (left or right). The KP is code for lobed kneading blocks that show four pieces of information: the angle between the different lobes (45°), the number of lobes (5), the screw length (20 mm), and directionality (left).

Expansion ratio, unit density, true density, and total porosity

The radial ER was calculated using the method previously reported by Wagner et al. (2023). Briefly, the diameter of 3 randomly selected extrudates from each sample was measured 15 times at different places with a digital vernier caliper (Model DCLA‐0605, Clockwise Tools Inc.). The average extrudate diameter was divided by the die orifice diameter to calculate the ER.

The unit and true density (TD) of the dried extrudate were measured as per Ek et al. (2021). Briefly, the unit density (UD) was determined by using 10 g of the extrudate and placing it in a 100 mL graduated cylinder. The graduated cylinder was then filled to 50 mL with 1 mm glass beads to find the volume via displacement. The volume was measured, and the UD was calculated by dividing the weight of the extrudate by the sample volume. For the TD determination, dried extrudates were first milled in a cyclone mill with a 0.5 mm screen (Model 3010‐017, Udy Corporation). After milling, 10 g of powder was placed in a 10 mL graduated cylinder and tapped 25 times. The volume was measured, and the TD was calculated by dividing the weight of the milled extrudate by the sample volume (Ek et al., 2021). The total porosity of each sample was calculated using the method reported by Sereno et al. (2007).

Water absorption index and water solubility index

The water solubility and absorption indices (water solubility index [WSI] and water absorption index [WAI], respectively) of the extrudates were analyzed similarly to the protocol used by Pietrysiak et al. (2020). Briefly, 50 g of the extrudate was milled in a cyclone mill (Model 3010‐030, UDY Corporation) with a 0.5 mm steel screen. Then, 7.5 g of each sample was mixed with 30 mL of water at 30°C for 30 min and vortexed every 5 min. The samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was poured into an aluminum dish. The supernatant was dried at 105°C for 24 h (Pietrysiak et al., 2020).

Statistical analysis

A 3 × 3 × 6 factorial experimental design with three levels of SSs (300, 400, and 500 rpm), three levels of MCs (16%, 19%, and 21% w.b.), and six concentrations of MBP (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% d.b.) was used. RStudio base version 4.2.1 and the plugin Agricolae version 1.3‐5 (RStudio) were used for the statistical analysis. All analyses used a significance level of α = 0.05. Three‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze each response variable (WAI/WSI; TD, UD, porosity, and ER). The figures associated with WAI/WSI; TD, UD, porosity, and ER were averaged based on SS due to the lack of interactions between SS and other independent variables, such as protein content and MC. The data that detail the effect of SS that has on the dependent variable are in Table S1. Statistically significant (α = 0.05) trends in the pasting properties were detected using one‐way ANOVA.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Pasting properties

The pasting curves of the raw blends are depicted in Figure 2. The gelatinization temperature, peak, setback, and breakdown values are shown in Table 1. The beginning of gelatinization was significantly (p < 0.05) affected by the MBP level (Table S2) and ranged from 69.63 to 73.8°C (Table 1). The protein did significantly affect this process because the protein denatures fully and interacts with the starch at these temperatures because of the high MC (Brishti et al., 2017). This protein denaturation exposes hydrophobic regions that interact with the amylopectin and amylose chains at these temperatures and shear forces (Du et al., 2018; Tarahi, Hedayati, et al., 2022). Tarahi and Shahidi (2022) reported the pasting properties between native corn starch and MBP isolate. Their findings corroborate the trend observed in this study that MBP does have a significant effect on gelatinization temperature, reporting a gelation temperature range of 69.69–72.21°C and significantly affecting the peak viscosity, breakdown, and setback.

The peak torque changed as a function of the MBP inclusion, ranging from 101.05 BU at the highest MBP level to 604.67 BU (Table 1), and was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table S1). The breakdown, setback, and onset of gelatinization values all decreased with the increase of MBP after 5% MBP inclusion (Table 1). The pasting properties were mainly dependent on the dominant component of the sample, corn starch, in this study. As more of the protein is added to the corn starch, the corn starch will have a smaller effect on the pasting properties (Wang et al., 2020). The primary interaction that occurs between protein and starch is hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen bonding occurs between the protein after it has been denatured, exposing hydrophilic amino acids and amylose and amylopectin chains of the starch matrix (Tarahi, Hedayati, et al., 2022; Tarahi, Shahidi, et al., 2022). Electrostatic and van der Waals forces also interact with the hydrophilic sections of the protein and the amylopectin side chains (Brishti et al., 2017). The increase in the setback viscosity when 5% MBP was included, followed by the subsequent decrease in pasting properties, can be explained by the protein binding to the amylose and amylopectin during retrogradation (Tarahi & Shahidi, 2022). The decrease in the peak torque when MBP was added can also be attributed to the decrease in water solubility (Table 3), as the MBP can reduce the swelling of the starch granules preventing the amylose from leaching out and interacting with water (Tarahi, Shahidi, et al., 2022). Furthermore, Niu et al. (2018) show the storage modulus decreases because of the interactions between carboxyl groups of amino acids and leached amylose preventing amylose gelatinization (Niu et al., 2018). When the protein inclusion is added, the large MBP molecules will physically disrupt the starch matrix formed during gelatinization, further decreasing the viscosity of the matrix (Brishti et al., 2017). This relationship was also reported by Yang et al. (2019), who reported the whey protein acting as an inactive filler, preventing the amylose arrangement into a gel. The other reason is that the further addition of MBP will result in large protein agglomerates that will disrupt much of the starch matrix (Tarahi, Hedayati, et al., 2022).

Extrusion process responses

The extrusion process responses recorded from extrusion are pressure and torque, whereas SME is calculated with mass flow rate, torque, and SS. It is important to record these responses as they provide key information about the rheological properties of the melt through the extrusion process. These properties have a significant impact on the final extrudate physical attributes (Kowalski et al., 2020). The protein content had a significant (p < 0.05; Table 2) impact on the torque and back pressure. The SS did contribute significantly (p < 0.05) to the back pressure but not the torque (Table 2). There were no significant (p < 0.05) interactions between the independent variables influencing torque. However, there was a significant (p < 0.05) interaction between the SS and protein content affecting pressure (Table 2). The torque ranged from 23.15 to 47.8 N m (Table S1), and die back pressure ranged from 2.47 to 8.38 MPa. The torque decreased with the increase in MC. This is to be expected as the moisture acts as a plasticizer in the extrusion system, reducing the viscosity of the melt. The torque was also significantly different (p < 0.05) from the control to 5% MBP inclusion (Table 2). The torque increased from 0% MBP inclusion to 5% MBP inclusion, indicating an increase in the viscosity of the melt and interactions between the MBP and cornstarch and MBP and water. The pressure decreased with the increase in MC for all MBP levels (Table S1). This can also be attributed to the plasticization effect, decreasing the viscosity of the melt (Downs et al., 2023). This plasticization effect for both torque and pressure will also reduce the energy applied to the melt via viscous dissipation.

For all the extrusions, the torque increased with the increase in SS between 300 and 400 rpm, whereas the torque at 500 rpm was approximately the same as at 400 rpm (Table S1). The torque and pressure values at 400 and 500 rpm were higher than at 300 rpm (Table S1). This is unexpected as the increased starch depolymerization should have decreased the torque and pressure in extrusion. The increase in pressure and torque with the increase in SS can be attributed to the hydrophilic groups (such as –COOH, –SH, –NH2, and –OH) that are exposed, broken, and then form electrostatic and hydrogen bonds with the water and starch as well as form protein aggregates (Tarahi & Shahidi, 2022). The hydroxyl groups can also be used as a primary binding site for water and starch molecules (Tarahi & Shahidi, 2022). These interactions can strengthen the starch matrix, resulting in less starch depolymerization with higher shear forces (Brishti et al., 2017; Tarahi & Shahidi, 2022). The increased SS will also decrease the residence time, resulting in even less starch depolymerization. The result of starch not undergoing high degrees of starch depolymerization can increase melt viscosity (Richter, Pietrysiak, et al., 2022). This increase in viscosity can increase torque and back pressure.

The SME can reflect the physical changes, such as viscosity, and chemical changes, such as starch depolymerization, that occur during extrusion (Chinnaswamy, 1993; Pansawat et al., 2008; Philipp et al., 2018). SME depends on multiple factors, including torque and SS, extruder power, and feeding rate. Due to the limited factors being analyzed for SME, the SME follows the trends found in torque when comparing various blends at a single SS. The SME values ranged from 888.25 to 1983.52 kJ/kg (Table S1). The SME was significantly (p < 0.05) affected by the SS and MC, and there were no significant interactions between the independent variables (Table 1). As the MC increased, the SME values decreased for all samples (Figure 3). However, it is important to note that protein content also had an effect on the SME but is not statistically significant with a p‐value of 0.062 (Table 2). As previously described, the increase in MC will decrease the viscosity of the melt. This decrease in the viscosity of the melt will also decrease the viscous dissipation of the physical shear forces through the melt (Roland et al., 2019). The physical shear forces stem from the SS of the machine. As the SS increases, the amount of viscous dissipation also increases as the increased shear force is transformed into heat, further decreasing the viscosity of the melt (Fratte et al., 2022). The increase in moisture that results in the subsequent decrease in viscosity decreases the amount of total energy applied to samples with increased MC.

Expansion ratio

The ER was significantly (p < 0.05) affected by SS, protein content, and MC (Table 2). A significant (p < 0.05) interaction between protein and MC affected the ER. The ER of all extrudates ranged from 2.90 (0% MBP, 19% moisture, and 500 rpm) to 6.18 (25% MBP, 19% moisture, and 400 rpm) (Table 3). There was a slight increase in the ER from 0% to 10% MBP, and after this point, the ER began to not have a discernible pattern from 15% to 25% MBP (Figure 4a). This suggests that a critical MBP inclusion level is needed to start affecting the starch melt rheology (Figure 4a). The findings in this study are in agreement with the findings of de Mesa et al. (2009), who included soy protein isolate in corn starch. de Mesa et al. (2009) found that there is a critical level of soy protein that can be added to corn starch before the ER decreases. They reported that the expansion decreases slightly beyond 15% protein inclusion (de Mesa et al., 2009). They explained that the reason for the decrease in ER is due to the increase of SME and physical protein disruption of the starch matrix. This disruption weakens the starch matrix and reduces the elasticity of the cell walls. The protein disrupts the starch matrix by forming large aggregates that create weak spots in the melt and reduce protein–starch interactions.

The MC had a significant (p < 0.05) effect on the ER of the extrudate. Chinnaswamy and Hanna (1988) extruded native corn starch at different MCs. They observed that the expansion of corn starch increased and then rapidly decreased when the raw material reached a critical MC. In our study, the optimal MC for the largest ER was dependent on the SS and protein content. At 300 rpm, the optimal MC was at 16% for 5% MBP, whereas the optimal MC for 10%–25% MBP was at 19%. At 400 rpm, the optimal MC was at 19% for 5%–10% MBP, then it increased to 21% moisture for 15% MBP and 19% moisture for 20% and 25% MBP (Table 3). At 500 rpm, the optimal MC for 5% MBP was at 19%, then it increased to 21% moisture for 10% MBP, and down to 19% moisture at 15%–25% MBP (Table 3). The inconsistency in the trends can be attributed to the amount of depolymerization of starch, the subsequent formation of interactions with the protein, and temporal differences in phase transitions between the protein and starch during extrusion. Starch molecules undergo phase change during extrusion before the protein does (Moisio et al., 2015). This causes the molten starch to experience more energy than the protein, as the particle friction increases during this period because of the phase change. The addition of water can lower the denaturation temperature of the MBP. Unfolded MBP can interact more with the starch, creating hydrogen bonding that strengthens the starch matrix. However, if there is too much protein, the protein will begin to aggregate with other protein molecules, reducing the interactions between the starch and protein. The samples with an optimal MC of 16% need more mechanical energy to unfold the protein to create the synergistic relationship between the starch and protein that strengthens the starch melt. As seen in this study, this occurs at low concentrations of MBP except at 300 rpm. At 300 rpm, the starch experiences less SME than the higher SSs reducing the depolymerization and therefore can support more protein in the system at lower MCs. At 400 and 500 rpm, the starch experiences more SME, increasing the degree of starch depolymerization and resulting in a weaker starch matrix. This is why the optimal MCs for the higher SSs increased (Table 3).

Unit density, true density, and total porosity

The protein content and SS had a significant (p < 0.05) effect on the UD, with no interactions among SS, MC, and protein content being significant (Table 2). UD ranged from 0.07 to 0.51 g/cm3, as samples with higher concentrations of MBP had lower UD values (Table 3). The UD decreased between 16% and 19% MC and then increased at 21% MC and decreased with increasing MBP concentrations (Figure 4c). This can result from the MBP strengthening the starch matrix by cross‐linking between the starch and the protein, creating more pockets for air to inhabit (Ghorpade et al., 1997). Yu et al. (2013) analyzed the effects of soy protein isolate in corn flour. They reported that MC was the major factor contributing to UD rather than protein content and SS, in contrast to our findings, where we found that protein and SS were significant. MBP exhibits a high amount of hydrophobic amino acids that will open and bond with the starch polymers, which will then create a stronger starch matrix, which increases the size of the pores and, thus, decreases the UD (Du et al., 2018; Philipp et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2013).

The TD of the exudates ranged from 0.77 to 0.87 g/cm3 and did not exhibit a statistically significant relationship to the level of MBP inclusion (data not shown). This was an expected outcome because the TD ranges between the UD of the extrudate and the density of water (Thymi et al., 2005). There was no discernable pattern in the TD data.

Porosity is defined as all the air trapped inside a substance (Ditudompo et al., 2016). SS, MC, and protein content had a significant (p < 0.05) impact on the porosity of the extrudate (Table 2). There was a significant (p < 0.05) interaction between protein content and MC that affected the porosity of the sample (Table 2). The porosity increased from 16% moisture to 19% moisture with no MBP added at 5% MBP (Figure 4b). The porosity increases with the addition of MBP. The porosity of the extrudates ranged from 1.65% to 11.42% (Table 3). The largest porosity was observed at an SS of 500 rpm, with 25% MBP inclusion at 19% moisture (Table 3). As more air is trapped in the extrudate, it will result in a product with a higher porosity and lower UD. The 16% moisture blends have undergone the most rigorous starch depolymerization because of the smaller water content. This results in more starch–protein interactions due to the smaller starch chains; however, the strength of the matrix will decrease if the starch molecules become too small to support the force of the moisture flash‐off at the exit of the die. The 21% moisture blends did not experience high shear forces because of the plasticization effect occurring in the raw blend and strengthening the starch matrix. This strengthening can result in a larger expansion, porosity, and UD, as extrudate size contributes to all these. This means there is an interaction between the MC and how the protein affects the rheological properties of the melt. The protein and moisture could both strengthen the melt and allow a larger porosity while also decreasing the resistance of the melt for bubble growth (Downs et al., 2023). This implies that there is an optimal range of shear, MC, and protein content that will form a strong melt but has little resistance to bubble growth.

Water solubility index (WSI) and water absorbance index (WAI)

The WSI indicates the solubility of the molecular components of the system in excess water (Richter, Gu, et al., 2022). The WSI was significantly (p < 0.05) impacted by the protein content, SS, and MC. There were significant (p < 0.05) interactions between the protein content and MC that affected the WSI (Table 2). The WSI data ranged from 30.72 to 84.52 g/g (Table 3). The WSI decreased with the increase of MBP inclusion, with the WSI leveling out after 15% MBP content (Figure 5a). This is to be expected because the MBP does not become depolymerized, and as more MBP is added, the starch concentration decreases, resulting in a dilution effect (Allen et al., 2007; Dey et al., 2021). However, it is also key to mention that, in general, the WSI increased with the increase in SS (Table 3). The increased amount of shear, in this case SS, increases the amount of mechanical depolymerization of the starch molecules. The decrease in the starch molecular weight leads to a higher solubility of the starch molecules (Kowalski et al., 2020). As the concentrations of MBP increased, the water solubility generally decreased because of the protein–starch interactions, protein–protein aggregation, and protein–water interactions (Brishti et al., 2017; Day & Swanson, 2013). Similar interactions were reported by Allen et al. (2007), who found that the WSI decreased as the concentration of whey protein increased in corn starch extrudates. The WSI decreased with the increase in MC. This is expected as water acts as a plasticizer and reduces the amount of pressure, viscous dissipation of energy, and mechanical shear forces on the melt.

Water absorbance index (WAI) (a) and water solubility index (WSI) (b) of different blends, as a function of moisture content (%) and grouped by protein content (%). Error bars represent standard error (n = 9).

The WAI is related to the ability of the material to absorb water. In high starch systems such as the blends used in this study, water absorption is governed by the concentration of starch and surface hydrophobicity of the protein (Sompong et al., 2011). The WAI was significantly (p < 0.05) influenced by the protein content, SS, and MC (Table 2). There were also significant (p < 0.05) interactions between protein content and MC that affected the WAI (Table 2). The WAI ranged from 0.79 to 5.49 g/g (Table 3). The WAI increased with the increased concentrations of MBP to 10% (Figure 5b). However, at 15%, the WAI begins to level off with generally small differences between 15% and 25% MBP (Figure 5a). These results correlate with Chen et al. (2017), who extruded corn starch with the inclusion of soy protein and noted that the WAI values increased as a function of protein inclusion to a level of 20% protein, where the WAI then decreased. There are multiple potential reasons for this pattern, as the authors of the study mentioned above concluded that the cause is the decrease of starch in the system, resulting in less starch gelatinization in the system, leading to lower WSI values (Chen et al., 2017). When the protein level becomes too high, the proteins can aggregate and form larger protein molecules (Downs et al., 2023). These large protein aggregates can potentially physically disrupt the starch matrix, reducing the amount of water it can hold. The generally smaller differences of WAI between 15% and 25% MBP protein show that the maximum amount of MBP before the WAI stops being affected is at 15%. This demonstrates a critical amount of MBP where amounts exceeding 15% will begin to disrupt the starch matrix, resulting in a lower WAI. The SS did not have any significant three‐way interactions between all independent variables, SS, moisture, or protein content. In addition, the water might also interact with the three‐dimensional structure of the protein. At the highest SS, the WAI has the highest values at an MC of 21% at 20% MBP (Table 3). This suggests that with the decreased MC, the starch will not be able to undergo retrogradation because of a smaller degree of gelatinization. Additionally, protein–starch interactions between the hydrophilic groups can increase the retrogradation rate after extrusion.

CONCLUSION

This study highlighted that MBP significantly affects the expansion characteristics and porosity of the corn starch extrudates. The extrudates that exhibited the largest ER and porosity did not have a dependable pattern and occurred at varying levels of moisture, MBP, and SS. Interestingly, the maximum expansion was observed at an MC of 19% and the highest MBP inclusion level of 25% at 400 rpm. This goes against our hypothesis that the highest expansion would be observed at the lowest MC, the lowest mung bean inclusion level, and the lowest SS. Our results indicate that MBP can help increase the ER and porosity of extrudates with proper extrusion processing parameters. Further research is needed to identify and understand molecular interactions between the MBP and starch during extrusion processing.
