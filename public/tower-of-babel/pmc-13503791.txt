In silico augmentation strategies for enhanced machine learning performance in fracture recognition
Authors: Ming Xu, Zhiqiang Wang, Guanhong Liu, Chenxi Wu, Hong Jiang, Xiangqi Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a machine learning framework for fracture risk prediction and in silico validation of synthetic biomedical data. A retrospective dataset comprising 169 patient records with clinically relevant variables, including age, sex, weight, height, medication status, and bone mineral density (BMD), was analyzed. Multiple classification models, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Support Vector Machine, and ensemble voting classifiers, were evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Synthetic data fidelity was assessed through statistical distribution alignment, correlation preservation, and predictive transferability between real and synthetic domains. Among the evaluated models, the Voting Hard ensemble achieved the highest classification performance with an accuracy of 85.8% and F1-score of 0.822, while Logistic Regression demonstrated the highest discriminative capability (AUC = 0.88). Synthetic data showed strong agreement with real data in marginal feature distributions but weaker preservation of inter-feature correlations. The findings demonstrate the potential of ensemble machine learning methods for fracture risk prediction while highlighting the importance of rigorous validation when utilizing synthetic biomedical datasets. This framework provides a foundation for future development of privacy-preserving and clinically relevant synthetic data applications in biomedical machine learning.

Introduction

Machine learning offers a transformative potential for enhancing the detection accuracy and accessibility of medical image analysis, particularly in regions lacking specialized radiological services1. This capability is particularly vital given the global burden of fractures, with hundreds of millions occurring annually, necessitating accurate and timely diagnosis to mitigate substantial healthcare system strains2. The development of sophisticated deep learning algorithms, coupled with advancements in general-purpose graphics processing unit hardware, has enabled significant progress in automated disease detection from medical images, leading to improved diagnostic sensitivity and efficiency3. However, the performance of these AI models is heavily reliant on the availability of extensive, high-quality, and diverse datasets for training4,5. This is particularly critical in small datasets, where the risk of overfitting is high and models may otherwise memorize training samples instead of learning generalizable patterns. Data augmentation and synthetic data generation therefore play an essential role in improving model robustness and generalization. The inherent scarcity of such meticulously labeled medical image datasets, particularly for less common pathologies or specific fracture types, frequently impedes the full realization of deep learning’s potential in clinical settings4,6. The ultimate goal of these methods is to synthesize new data samples that closely align with the underlying distribution of real medical images, thereby enhancing the generalization and robustness of the model, which is particularly desirable when tasked to identify a fracture given an X-ray image7–9. In particular, the methods such as You Only Look Once and its variations have proven to be quite promising in detecting anomalies with X-ray images automatically, performing expert-like in the localization and classification of fracture10,11. This is frequently achieved by using architectures such as the Vision Transformer and its variants, which using transfer learning on large datasets are found to have better results in detecting complex patterns that indicate fractures in complex X-ray images, despite having little domain specific data3,12. Also, the use of AI in medical imaging, as well as detecting fractures in particular, has shown that it may succeed in total accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity when compared to the existing diagnostic methods13. The ability to detect minute patterns and features that may indicate the existence of a fracture that may not be seen by human users is the reason behind this enhancement, which makes deep learning networks, such as Convolutional Neural Networks, more effective and could potentially reduce inter-observer variance14. However, the implementation of such models into clinical use is frequently constrained by the fact that such models require large, annotated datasets in order to train them, which are not always readily available with the advanced deep learning models15,16. These types of augmentations play a critical role in enhancing the model performance of object detection algorithms such as YOLOv8 in the medical field more so in pediatric wrist fracture detection7. This approach directly addresses the challenges associated with obtaining large, diverse datasets in medical imaging by synthetically expanding the training data, thereby improving the diagnostic accuracy of AI systems in detecting subtle radiological findings.

Despite the growing demand for high-quality synthetic data in biomedical research, particularly for conditions such as osteoporosis, current in silico validation frameworks lack comprehensive evaluation pipelines. While various studies have implemented generative models to mimic biomedical data, few have rigorously compared real versus synthetic datasets using both statistical and machine learning metrics, especially for sensitive clinical indicators like bone mineral density (BMD). Furthermore, age- and sex-specific patterns in fracture prediction are often overlooked in synthetic modeling approaches, creating a gap in contextual fidelity.The development of robust and biologically faithful synthetic datasets is critical in biomedical domains where data privacy, scarcity, and ethical concerns hinder access to real-world patient data. Motivated by the need to ensure that synthetic data not only replicates statistical distributions but also preserves predictive relevance, this study proposes an integrated evaluation framework. It rigorously assesses the fidelity of synthetic data through statistical divergence, classification performance, and model generalizability from real to synthetic cohorts. Such work can accelerate data-sharing while preserving clinical utility, particularly in aging and sex-stratified populations prone to osteoporosis.This study aims to construct a robust, in silico machine learning framework that evaluates the fidelity and performance of synthetically generated biomedical data. Specifically, it seeks to: Analyze real-world BMD data stratified by age and sex to understand clinical patterns.Generate and evaluate synthetic data using statistical metrics such as KL divergence and visual distribution comparisons.Train machine learning models—including Voting Classifier ensembles—on real data and evaluate their generalizability on synthetic datasets.Assess feature importance and model explanations through SHAP values to ensure alignment between real and synthetic data domains.Offer a validation pipeline that can be adapted for future synthetic data engines, ensuring relevance, accuracy, and ethical usability in biomedical applications.

Methodology

This study presents a comprehensive framework integrating machine learning-based outcome prediction and in silico validation for synthetic biomedical data. The methodological pipeline is divided into distinct phases covering data preparation, supervised learning, evaluation, statistical comparison, and structural validation to ensure reproducibility, explainability, and biological plausibility.

Dataset source and characteristics

The dataset used in this study was obtained from the publicly available FracAtlas dataset repository, comprising a total of 169 patient records. The dataset includes clinically relevant variables for fracture risk prediction, such as age, sex, weight (kg), height (cm), medication status, and bone mineral density (BMD), along with a binary outcome variable indicating fracture versus non-fracture.

Inclusion criteria required records to contain complete information for key clinical variables, including age, sex, weight, height, and BMD. Records with missing or inconsistent entries were retained and handled during preprocessing using appropriate imputation techniques to ensure dataset usability. No additional exclusion criteria based on demographic or clinical conditions were applied due to the limited dataset size.

As the dataset is publicly available and fully anonymized, no ethical approval was required.

Data preprocessing

To ensure data quality, consistency, and model compatibility, a structured preprocessing pipeline was implemented:

Data cleaning and missing value handling

The dataset was first inspected for missing and inconsistent entries. Missing numerical values (e.g., weight, height, BMD) were imputed using median imputation to reduce the influence of outliers. For categorical variables (e.g., medication), missing values were handled using forward-fill or mode imputation where appropriate.

Categorical encoding

Categorical variables such as sex and medication status were transformed using one-hot encoding to ensure compatibility with machine learning models.

Feature engineering and transformation

Age was discretized into clinically meaningful groups (< 40, 40–60, 60–80, 80+) to enable subgroup analysis. No feature removal was performed to preserve all clinically relevant variables.

Feature Scaling

Numerical features were normalized using either StandardScaler or MinMaxScaler depending on the model requirements. Scaling was particularly applied for distance-based and kernel-based models such as SVM and KNN.

Data consistency across domains

The same preprocessing pipeline was applied identically to both real and synthetic datasets to ensure a fair and unbiased comparison during in silico validation.

This preprocessing workflow ensures reproducibility and minimizes bias introduced during data preparation.

The selection of machine learning algorithms in this study was guided by the need to evaluate a diverse set of models representing different learning paradigms commonly used in biomedical tabular data analysis.

Feature contribution analysis

To further evaluate the influence of clinically relevant variables on fracture prediction performance, a supplementary feature contribution analysis was conducted. The analysis examined the relative importance of major clinical features, particularly bone mineral density (BMD), age, weight, and height, through comparative model behavior, SHAP-based interpretability assessment, and performance consistency across classifiers.

In addition, comparisons between standalone classifiers and ensemble-based approaches were evaluated to assess the contribution of ensemble learning to predictive robustness and generalization. The analysis was performed within the same 5-fold stratified cross-validation framework used throughout the primary experiments.

In silico evaluation of synthetic data

To support in silico validation and evaluate synthetic data fidelity, a synthetic dataset was generated based on the statistical characteristics of the original dataset containing 169 patient records. A total of 169 synthetic samples were generated to maintain consistency in dataset size and class distribution with the real dataset. The synthetic data were not used to artificially balance the classification performance during the primary model training stage. Instead, they were utilized to assess statistical similarity, biological coherence, and downstream predictive transferability between real and synthetic domains.

The generated synthetic dataset was subsequently compared with the real dataset using distribution analysis, correlation preservation, KL divergence, and cross-domain prediction testing. This evaluation framework enabled assessment of how effectively the synthetic samples preserved clinically meaningful relationships relevant to fracture prediction.

Downstream Predictive Transferability

To evaluate the practical utility and generalization capability of the synthetic dataset, a cross-domain predictive transferability analysis was conducted. Initially, all machine learning models were trained and validated using the real dataset through 5-fold stratified cross-validation. The best-performing model, identified as the Voting Hard ensemble classifier, was subsequently retrained on the complete real dataset and evaluated on the synthetic dataset without additional fine-tuning.

This evaluation strategy was designed to assess whether the synthetic data preserved sufficient structural and statistical characteristics to support meaningful predictive inference. Performance on the synthetic dataset was measured using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The obtained F1-score ranged approximately between 0.46 and 0.55, indicating moderate transferability between the real and synthetic domains.

The results suggest that the synthetic dataset successfully preserved important marginal feature distributions and partial learnable patterns relevant to fracture prediction. However, reduced predictive performance compared to the real-data evaluation indicates that the synthetic data did not fully preserve complex inter-feature dependencies and biological relationships. This observation is further supported by the correlation matrix analysis, where weaker correlations were observed among key clinical variables in the synthetic dataset.

Results

The results of this study provide a comprehensive evaluation of the machine learning models applied for fracture risk prediction using clinically relevant features. The analysis includes model performance metrics, statistical assessments across various demographic subgroups, and detailed visualizations that collectively validate the robustness of the proposed approach. By comparing multiple classifiers and ensemble strategies, the study identifies the best-performing methods, highlighting the predictive advantage of ensemble learning—particularly hard voting—in accurately stratifying fracture risk. These results are further contextualized through statistical group comparisons and model explainability outputs, offering insights into feature importance and demographic-specific trends that reinforce the clinical utility of the findings.

Figure 3 presents a detailed comparison of multiple supervised machine learning models applied to fracture prediction using real biomedical data. The evaluated models include baseline classifiers such as Logistic Regression, Support Vector Classifier (SVC), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), as well as advanced ensemble techniques including Random Forest (default and hyperparameter-tuned), Gradient Boosting, Stacking, and both Voting classifiers (hard and soft variants). Model performance was evaluated using five metrics, i.e. Accuracy, F1_macro, Precision_macro, Recall_macro, and F1_weighted. The Voting Hard classifier was the overall best followed by the highest score in most of the measures particularly Accuracy and F1_weighted. Tuned Random Forest and Gradient Boosting also showed competitive results which justifies the performance of ensemble learning. KNN and SVC, comparatively, though, did poorly in a variety of measures. These findings confirm the applicability of ensemble-based methods to the improvement of the strength of prediction in the biomedical machine learning setting.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the Voting Hard ensemble model achieved the highest predictive performance for fracture risk, with accuracy (85.8%) and F1-score (0.822) surpassing all individual classifiers (e.g., RF, XGBoost, SVM). This finding aligns with recent reports that ensemble methods consistently outperform single algorithms in fracture prediction. For example17, found that a Super Learner ensemble (combining multiple ML algorithms) markedly outperformed any single model (AUC ≈ 0.76, accuracy 95.6%) for osteoporotic fracture risk. Similarly18, reported a staged ensemble model (combining clinical and DXA-imaging features) that achieved an AUC of 0.954 and accuracy of 0.919 for hip fracture prediction, far exceeding simpler models. Random forests (RF) placed next best (accuracy = 85.2, F1 = 0.816), once again in line with results of others that RF can be highly performative. A case in point is that19 reported (RF) model with test AUC = 0.88 to predict the occurrence of postoperative vertebral re-fracture, which is comparable to our RF performance. Other simpler models like the logistic regression or the SVM, however, had a lower F1-score (0.745–0.787) in our study which is the relative low performance of the models in other cohorts of fractures. In sum, the elite performance of the ensemble performance in our work is an indication of the concurrence of the recent literature indicating that the combination of various learners consider complementary trends in the data of fracture risks. The importance of data augmentation and synthetic data becomes particularly evident in small datasets, where models are prone to memorization rather than learning meaningful clinical patterns. By increasing data variability, synthetic augmentation can help mitigate overfitting and improve generalization.

The supplementary feature contribution analysis further emphasized the importance of clinically meaningful predictors in fracture risk assessment. In particular, BMD and age consistently demonstrated strong influence on model predictions, supporting their established clinical relevance in osteoporosis-related fracture evaluation. Furthermore, the improved stability of ensemble-based models compared with standalone classifiers suggests that combining complementary learning strategies enhances robustness and generalization in small biomedical datasets.

At this point, it can be demonstrated that ML is much more effective in predictive accuracy than traditional tools. A 91.2% accuracy and an AUC = 0.739 of XGBoost model (including genetic risk scores) in20 with major osteoporotic fractures is significantly higher than FRAX (Net reclassification improvement= −22.6). Despite not being as precise as some of these studies (e.g. 95.6% in Wu 2025 or 91.2% in our Voting ensemble can be viewed in its context: we have a mixture of both clinical and BMD characteristics, and the macro-averaged F1 is reported to be between the classes. In addition, a recent systematic review study by21 synthesized ML-based fracture predictors, because they found a mean C-index of approximately 0.75 (AUC = 0.75) with sensitivities of 0.76–0.79. Seeing that our ensemble outperforms that average C-index (by a significant margin due to its high performance and F1) shows that highly sophisticated ML techniques are capable of significantly increasing the performance of prediction. Interestingly enough, in the case of particular subgroups (e.g. diabetes)22, pointed out that the existing tools are incomparably worse: both FRAX and Garvan do not enable effective predictions, and AI-technologies, in their turn, have amazing opportunities to predict. This is so: in the absence of diabetes-specific modelling, our ML model would actually model the interactions between features (e.g. the effect of age, BMD, comorbidity): which FRAX would not discover.

From a clinical perspective, sensitivity (recall) is a critical metric in fracture prediction, as failing to identify a true fracture case may result in delayed treatment and increased risk of complications. Therefore, while accuracy and F1-score provide a general measure of model performance, greater importance should be placed on recall for the positive (fracture) class. In this study, the selected models demonstrated reasonably balanced recall values, particularly the Voting Hard and Logistic Regression models, indicating their potential suitability for clinical decision support. Future work should further optimize models specifically for sensitivity-driven performance, even at the cost of a slight reduction in precision.

The measures of statistics which we adopted in our study provide complementary data. Validity (correct classification rate in totality) is intuitively valid, but misleading in the situation of unequal classes. We also report F1-score which is a compromise between precision and recall. F1 of The Voting Hard model = 0.822 which is a good sensitivity-specificity balance as also emphasized in the other research on ML fracture. For example8, evaluated seven ML models for fracture in diabetics and found F1 scores up to 0.97 for their best model (Probabilistic Classification Vector Machine). Our F1 is lower, reflecting differences in cohort size, feature set, and threshold optimization. Nonetheless, the general improvement in F1 over simpler models (our LR F1 ≈ 0.745 vs. Voting F1 ≈ 0.822) confirms that our ensemble better captures the “true positives” of fracture risk.

Importantly, we also examined Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence as a measure of calibration (i.e. how closely predicted probability distributions match the true outcome distribution). This is uncommon in clinical fracture studies, but relevant: recent ML research shows that minimizing KL divergence during model tuning leads to predicted probabilities that align better with actual risks without sacrificing AUC. In practice, a lower KL for our Voting ensemble would imply its predicted risk scores are well-calibrated to observed fracture rates, complementing the accuracy/F1 findings. While few fracture prediction papers report KL explicitly, the concept of calibration is implicit. For instance23, emphasize that high AUROC (e.g. 0.92 for vertebral fracture prediction) indicates excellent discrimination, but they also caution that heterogeneous reporting (uneven sensitivity/specificity) complicates cross-study comparisons. Our incorporation of KL divergence addresses this by quantifying distributional alignment. In our results, the ensemble’s KL divergence was lower than that of single models (data not shown), suggesting it not only classifies well but also produces probability estimates that reflect true fracture prevalence an important consideration for clinical risk thresholds.

Our findings broadly align with published literature on ML-enhanced fracture risk prediction. Meta-analyses agree that traditional methods have limited accuracy; for example21, noted conventional regression models frequently plateau around C-index ~ 0.7. In contrast, ML ensembles routinely break into the 0.8–0.9 range. The World Journal of review by reported that AI algorithms achieved mean AUC ≈ 0.93 and, astonishingly, claimed ~ 99% accuracy versus ~ 70% for FRAX/DXA (though we view that figure as optimistic). Similarly24, indicated that the ML models, which were trained on partial FRAX, had a marginally better performance compared to the FRAX score (AUC 0.66 vs. 0.62). In our study, we have not calculated FRAX but the tendency is the same: ML is a predictor. Indeed, because of the accuracy and F1 of our ensemble (85.8 and 82.2 respectively), the effect would be very large compared to the results that would have been achievable with FRAX-type models only.

The quantitative differences between our findings and some of the reports are mainly quantitative: the values of the absolute metrics are based on its data and traits. Indicatively, xgboost-genetic-enhanced model offered by20 gave an accuracy of 91.2 which is larger than ours, though there is obviously an impact of cohort (25,772 women with genomics) and inclusion of GRS on the accuracy. Likewise, AUC was also extremely high in18 with DXA images (0.954) - once again, a more plentiful source of data. The availability of the clinical/BMD variables that we have used in our research is a good thing; it is encouraging that despite the availability of this background information the ensemble will still be the undoubted winner. Thus, the degree of such improvement varies, although the qualitative conclusion is the same: advanced ML (not least, ensembles) can be applied to predict fractures better than existing risk factors or simpler models.

Finally, there are two implications of these results. First, they endorse the potentiality of ML to educate individualized fracture danger evaluation. An adeptly adjusted Voting ensemble can be better adapted to identify high-risk patients that can be missed by linear models. Second, the use of different measures (accuracy, F1, KL divergence) underline the fact that the assessment of models should not be reduced to the reports containing only a single score. Namireddy et al. emphasize that the value of F1 and KL should be considered in addition to the value of AUROC as the notion of predictive validity is more detailed. These performance limits should be pushed further by future work including larger external validations and incorporation of other features (e.g. genomics or advanced imaging).

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the dataset is retrospective in nature, which may introduce selection bias, as the included cases may not fully represent the broader patient population. This can affect the generalizability of the developed models to different clinical settings or populations.

Second, retrospective datasets are often subject to incomplete or missing records, which may introduce uncertainty despite the use of imputation techniques. Although preprocessing methods such as median imputation were applied to mitigate missing data issues, these approaches may not fully capture the true underlying data distribution and could influence model performance.

Third, the relatively small dataset size (n = 169) limits the ability of machine learning models to learn complex patterns and increases the risk of overfitting, despite the use of cross-validation techniques.

Fourth, the synthetic dataset evaluated in this study, while showing strong alignment in marginal distributions, failed to preserve inter-feature correlations. This limits its effectiveness for tasks that rely on complex feature interactions and may impact downstream predictive performance.

Finally, no external validation using independent datasets was performed. As a result, the robustness and clinical applicability of the proposed models require further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts.

Conclusion

To sum up, this paper has managed to determine the effectiveness of machine learning techniques in forecasting the emergence of fractures by applying a wide range of clinically related factors, such as age, sex, bone mineral density (BMD), weight, and height. By implementing highly stringent data preprocessing techniques, exploratory analysis and applying a wide range of classification algorithms we were able to come up with a very elaborate framework that is not only able to provide at-risk individuals but also improve interpretability of patterns of fractures in particular demographic subgroups. The performance comparison analysis showed that ensemble-based learning methods have a great advantage over individual models of bases. The hard voting classifier was found to be the most effective and gave better predictive accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. This plan exploited the complementary advantages of having many classifiers, giving it strong generalization throughout the population. Statistical validation methods further showed such performance as well as stratified performance on the age and sex cohort which supported the model as flexible to clinical heterogeneity. Interestingly, the younger population below 40 years showed a relatively higher average BMD with the older population and especially males above 80 showing significantly lower values validating existing clinical trends on osteoporosis and fracture vulnerability. With the addition of model interpretability tools, including the use of SHAP-based feature importance and correlation heatmaps, there was an improved insight into how particular variables affect the prediction of fractures. Such observations can also inform practitioners to move to more specific diagnostic plans and preventive measures, especially in older adults where early detection is very important. Our research helps to increase the trust of AI-based clinical decision-support systems and makes it possible to apply them more widely by aligning machine learning results with clinical reasoning. Moreover, the current study adds value to the ongoing research on personalized medicine. The ability to combine multidimensional clinical data and derive actionable insights is a major progressive change in the creation of intelligent healthcare solutions. It confirms the role of artificial intelligence as a change agent in preventive care particularly when used in resource-limited environments where proactive screening can significantly lower the incidence of osteoporotic fractures.

In general, the research provides a solid basis to the future implementation of predictive analytics in the practical clinical process. To enhance the effect further, future studies are advised to consider real-time implementation in electronic health record (EHR) systems, extension to other clinical biomarkers and validation by means of prospective and multi-centre cohorts. These guidelines will be necessary to move the focus away towards retrospective analysis and towards proactive, AI-supported clinical decision-making that primarily entails early intervention, profiling of risk threats based on the individual patient, and better long-term outcomes.
