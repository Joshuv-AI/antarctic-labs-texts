A QR code-based intervention to improve nursing documentation quality: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Sajjad Shokri, Sepideh Mohammadi, Mojtaba Qanbari Qalehsari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-quality nursing documentation is fundamental to patient safety. However, its quality often remains suboptimal in resource-limited settings relying on paper-based systems. Quick Response (QR) codes offer a promising low-cost strategy for providing just-in-time access to guidelines, yet no prior study has examined their effect on nursing documentation quality in such contexts. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a QR code-based intervention on the quality of nursing documentation.MethodsA cluster quasi-experimental study with a pre-test/post-test design was conducted. Two hospitals were randomly allocated (coin toss) to intervention or control arms. Within each hospital, 30 nurses were randomly selected from medical and surgical wards to participate (intervention group: n = 30; control group: n = 30). The intervention group received a training session, plus access to a dedicated website—hosting categorized documentation guidelines and nursing process protocols—via unique QR codes displayed at the nursing station. Weekly follow-up was provided over two months. The control group continued routine practice. Documentation quality was assessed at baseline and post-intervention using a validated 29-item checklist.ResultsNo significant differences existed between groups at baseline (p = 0.587). Post-intervention, the intervention group’s documentation quality score (Mean = 88.37, SD = 17.71) was significantly higher than the control group’s (Mean = 68.30, SD = 17.52; p < 0.001). This represents a mean score increase of approximately 29% for the intervention group compared to the control group. The effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 1.14).ConclusionQR code-based support package significantly enhanced nursing documentation quality. This practical, low-cost, and scalable approach can serve as a viable digital support layer to improve documentation practices and patient safety in settings where comprehensive electronic health records are not feasible.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Nursing documentation, QR codes, Documentation quality, Point-of-care access, Quasi-experimental study, Paper-based records

Introduction

High-quality nursing documentation is a cornerstone of patient safety and continuity of care, serving as a vital tool for communication, clinical evaluation, and legal accountability [1, 2]. Nursing documentation is a valuable document for continuous patient care, as principled and effective nursing care requires obtaining comprehensive and dynamic information about the patient’s health status, as well as diagnostic, therapeutic, care, and educational actions [3]. At the same time, it is considered the most compelling document in legal proceedings for defending nurses [3]. High-quality nursing documentation is directly associated with a reduction in medical errors, enhanced accuracy in patient monitoring, facilitated shift handovers, and strengthened professional accountability among nurses [4]. Furthermore, precise and comprehensive nursing records can provide valuable data for clinical research, staff training, and the continuous improvement of care processes [5]. Despite its critical role, the quality of nursing documentation is not uniform across healthcare settings [6]. A pertinent example can be found in Iran, where nursing documentation is traditionally recorded by hand in patients’ medical records. Although the importance of accurate documentation is evident, recent national studies have demonstrated that its quality in many hospitals remains suboptimal. Common issues include incomplete recording of information, lack of standardized protocols, and insufficient ongoing training for nursing staff [7]. Findings from a study in Iran indicated that the quality of nursing records was rated as desirable in only 21.4% of cases, moderate in 53.6%, and poor in 25% [8].

Numerous studies have implemented various interventions, including educational and follow-up methods, to improve nursing documentation [9, 10]. However, these studies concluded that the improvements in documentation were not sustained, with the quality declining again following the conclusion of the interventions. Consequently, it has been suggested that future interventions should not be short-term in nature. Instead, they should provide nurses with ongoing, just-in-time opportunities to utilize and reinforce documentation skills. In this context, technology-based interventions that offer persistent support are particularly promising [11].

In this context, the adoption of innovative technologies that can provide rapid, accurate, and structured access to standardized content, clinical protocols, and sample forms becomes paramount [12]. While comprehensive Electronic Health Records (EHRs) represent an ideal integrated solution, their cost and complexity limit feasibility in many settings. QR codes, as a low-cost and accessible technology, present a pragmatic alternative capable of delivering structured support directly into the existing paper-based workflow [13, 14]. Given these advantages, the implementation of QR codes in clinical settings can be proposed as an innovative and effective strategy for improving the quality of nursing documentation. However, no prior study has empirically tested the use of QR codes as a sustainable, just-in-time support tool specifically designed to enhance the quality of nursing documentation within paper-based systems, particularly where traditional training interventions have failed to yield lasting improvements. Motivated by the recurrent challenge of documentation deficiencies and the potential of QR codes to provide sustained support, the investigators conducted a systematic literature search and this search revealed that while QR codes have been used in healthcare education and patient follow‑up, no prior study has empirically tested their use as a just‑in‑time, workflow‑integrated support tool specifically designed to enhance the quality of nursing documentation within paper‑based systems in resource‑limited settings. Therefore, this quasi-experimental study aimed to evaluate the impact of a QR code-based intervention, designed for seamless integration into the nursing workflow, on the quality of nursing documentation.

Methods and materials

Study design

This cluster quasi-experimental study employed a pre-test and post-test design with control and intervention groups to investigate the effect of implementing a QR code-based intervention on the quality of nursing documentation.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted from January 2024 to July 2025 at two teaching hospitals in Babol, northern Iran. These hospitals are the leading centers in Mazandaran province.

The study sample consisted of 60 nurses working in medical or surgical wards of two teaching hospitals affiliated with Babol University of Medical Sciences. It is important to note that both hospitals are teaching hospitals affiliated with the same university and operate under the same national nursing documentation standards and university-level training frameworks. Although minor local variations in workflow or form layout might exist, the core documentation requirements, paper-based admission report format, and essential documented items were identical across both sites, ensuring that the control and intervention groups operated under comparable baseline documentation expectations. Eligibility for participation required a minimum of six months of experience in a general ward, possession of at least a Bachelor’s degree in Nursing, ownership of a smartphone with reliable internet access, and an expressed intention not to leave their current job or transfer from the study ward during the two-month intervention period. Participants were excluded from the final analysis if they withdrew their consent for continued cooperation or if they attended any in-service training sessions related to documentation during the study period, and transfer or departure from the study ward (e.g., transfer to another department) during the two-month intervention period.

This study used a two-stage cluster sampling and allocation approach. First Stage (Cluster Allocation): To prevent cross-contamination of the intervention, the two hospitals were randomly allocated as intact clusters to either the intervention or control group using a simple random method (coin toss). Hospital A was assigned to the intervention group, and Hospital B to the control group. Second Stage (Participant Recruitment within Clusters): Within each hospital (cluster), eligible nurses from the medical and surgical wards were invited to participate. A target of 15 nurses per ward (30 per hospital) was set. From the list of eligible nurses in each ward of the intervention hospital, 15 were randomly selected (Fig. 1). The same procedure was followed in the control hospital. This ensured that all participants within a cluster (hospital) received the same condition (intervention or control), aligning with the cluster-randomized design.

Sample size calculation

Since a researcher-developed instrument was used in this study and no prior empirical data were available regarding the mean and standard deviation of the primary outcome, a sample size estimation based on an expected effect size was performed. In the absence of a previous directly comparable study, a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8) was selected based on Cohen’s conventional benchmark for behavioral science research. This conservative approach was chosen to ensure the study would be adequately powered to detect a substantial, clinically meaningful improvement in documentation quality, should the intervention prove effective. With a statistical power of 80% and a confidence level of 95%. The sample size was calculated as 52 participants. To account for potential attrition, we aimed to recruit 58–60 participants. We successfully enrolled 60 eligible nurses (30 in each hospital cluster), No attrition occurred during the study period, which can be attributed to our strict eligibility criterion requiring participants to express no intention of leaving their ward or job during the study, combined with our weekly follow-up and close communication with ward supervisors. Therefore, all 60 participants were included in the final analysis.

Data collection instruments

In this study, data were collected using two instruments: a demographic questionnaire and the Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist.

1. Demographic Questionnaire:

The demographic questionnaire was designed to capture essential background information of the participating nurses. It collected data on key variables including age, gender, clinical ward, level of education, marital status, and work experience.

2. Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist:

The nursing documentation quality was evaluated using the Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist, a researcher-developed instrument specifically created for this study. It was developed based on patients’ admission reports and essential documentation items and had not been used in any prior research. The instrument’s development was grounded in a comprehensive review of national nursing documentation standards, existing hospital protocols, and relevant scientific literature. To ensure content validity, a panel of 12 expert faculty members in nursing evaluated the initial 27-item instrument using both qualitative assessment and quantitative measures (CVR and CVI). Based on their expert feedback, one item was removed due to insufficient CVR, while three new items were added to better capture essential documentation elements, resulting in a final 29-item checklist. All items demonstrated acceptable validity thresholds, with CVI values exceeding 0.78 and CVR values surpassing the Lawshe’s table criterion of 0.56. The instrument’s reliability was established through a pilot study involving 20 nursing records, with internal consistency assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in SPSS version 25. The analysis demonstrated excellent reliability, with the checklist achieving an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84. Furthermore, all individual items showed strong consistency, with corrected item-total correlations above 0.80. Each item is scored on a three-point scale: 2 = “Accurately and completely documented” (all required information is present and correct), 1 = “Partially documented” (some but not all required information is present, or the information is ambiguous), and 0 = “Not documented” (the item is absent). To ensure consistent scoring, operational definitions were established. For categorical items (e.g., “Patient’s mode of arrival”), a score of ‘1’ was assigned if the mode was noted but the prior location was missing, or vice versa. For scale-based or descriptive items (e.g., “Consciousness status”), a score of ‘1’ was assigned if a screening was mentioned (e.g., “GCS checked”) but the specific score or description was not recorded, or if the result was recorded in an unstructured comment field rather than the dedicated section. To ensure objective and consistent application of the scoring criteria, a comprehensive scoring guide with operational definitions for ‘partial’ documentation for each of the 29 items was developed and used by the assessor during all evaluations. The rigorous validation process confirms the instrument’s robustness for assessing nursing documentation quality in clinical settings. For instance, the following four items are representative sample items from the checklist: “Consciousness status, evaluated using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) or a descriptive assessment of consciousness level, “ Pressure injury risk and the corresponding preventive interventions implemented, based on the Braden Scale criteria”, “Thromboembolism risk and the consequent prophylactic measures taken, as per the Wells Criteria”, “Patient’s mode of arrival to the ward (e.g., on foot, by wheelchair, or stretcher) and the patient’s prior location (e.g., home, another hospital ward, the operating room)”. The Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist used in this study is available as supplementary file alongside this article. The checklist was designed to produce a continuous total score reflecting the degree of adherence to documentation standards, rather than to classify records into predefined quality categories.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted after obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences (Ethical Code: IR.MUBABOL.HRI.REC.1403.173). The research process adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the commencement of the study, the objectives and procedures were thoroughly explained to all eligible nurses. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all participants provided written informed consent. They were assured of their right to decline participation or withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences to their professional status, employment, or relationships with their institution. To ensure confidentiality, all collected data were anonymized. Each participant was assigned a unique code, and this code was used on all data collection tools and documents instead of any personal identifiers. The demographic questionnaires and completed checklists were stored securely, with access restricted to the principal investigator.

Procedure

Phase one: development and deployment of the QR code-based intervention

The first phase of this study involved the development and implementation of the technological intervention. A dedicated website was designed to serve as a comprehensive digital repository for nursing documentation guidelines. The website was built to be responsive, ensuring optimal display on both smartphones and computers, and used a simple, hierarchical structure for intuitive navigation. The content was systematically organized into distinct categories to facilitate easy access. Key categories included: Principles of accurate nursing documentation, emphasizing exemplars of correctly completed records, detailed instructions for writing the nursing process within patient reports and a compilation of essential standardized assessment tools and criteria mandated for documentation, such as the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Braden Scale (for pressure injury risk), Morse Fall Scale, and others relevant to patient status. To ensure immediate utility, the content was organized into five primary categories aligned with nursing workflow: (1) Admission Assessment (e.g., exemplars for documenting patient arrival mode and prior location), (2) Risk Assessment Protocols (step-by-step guides for Braden, Morse, and Wells scales, including sample documentation of preventive measures), (3) Nursing Process Documentation (templates and examples for nursing diagnoses, interventions, and outcomes), (4) Procedure & Communication (guides for documenting physician notifications and interventions with required elements like time and performer), and (5) Sample Completed Forms (downloadable exemplars of fully documented admission reports). Each QR code linked directly to one of the five primary content categories. The website was intentionally designed without a keyword search function to keep the interface simple and fast. Instead, each category’s landing page presented a structured sub‑menu listing its specific guidelines (e.g., under “Risk Assessment Protocols”: Braden Scale, Morse Fall Scale, Wells Criteria). This allowed nurses to navigate to the required content within 2–3 clicks, minimizing time spent searching. To ensure the clinical relevance and validity of the website content, it was rigorously reviewed and approved by a panel of experts. This panel comprised five faculty members from the nursing school and two head nurses and a supervisor from the participating hospital. The experts evaluated the content for accuracy, completeness, clarity, and applicability to the clinical setting. Their feedback was incorporated into the final version of the website before the intervention phase began.

Following the website development and validation, a unique QR code was generated for each individual content category. All QR codes were then consolidated and printed on a single, clearly labeled poster. A brief, standardized instruction on how to scan the QR codes was also provided to the intervention group at the start of the study. This poster was prominently displayed at the nursing station in the wards assigned to the intervention group. This strategic placement provided nurses with immediate, on-demand access to the specific guidance they required at the point of care. By simply scanning the relevant QR code using their smartphones, nurses could instantly retrieve the corresponding documentation data, protocols, and exemplars, thereby integrating just-in-time learning and support directly into their workflow. Figure 2 provides a detailed schematic of the QR code-based support system structure. The central poster housed five QR codes, each linking to a main content category. Each category’s landing page offered a structured sub-menu of specific guidelines, tools, and exemplars—such as nursing process templates, sample completed forms, and standardized assessment tools (e.g., Glasgow Coma Scale, Braden Scale)—enabling nurses to quickly locate the required reference during documentation.

Phase two: intervention implementation and data collection

Pre-test data collection

Following the approval of the study protocol by the institutional ethics committee, written informed consent was obtained from all participating nurses. Prior to the initiation of the intervention, baseline data on the quality of nursing documentation were collected for both the control and intervention groups. Due to logistical constraints, the principal researcher (who was involved in the intervention delivery) also performed the outcome assessments. To mitigate potential assessment bias, several measures were implemented. First, all admission reports were de-identified prior to evaluation: patient names, national identification numbers, admission and discharge dates, and any references to ward or hospital names were removed or redacted. This prevented the assessor from knowing whether a report originated from the intervention or control hospital. Second, a rigorously validated and highly objective 29-item checklist was used. Each checklist item required a trinary factual judgment (e.g., “documented” vs. “not documented,” “complete” vs. “incomplete”), minimizing the need for subjective interpretation. Third, all assessments were based solely on the written content of the admission reports, without any interaction with the participating nurses during the evaluation process. These procedures collectively aimed to maximize objectivity and minimize ascertainment bias. The principal researcher evaluated two admission reports for each participant in both groups using this Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist.

Intervention procedure for the intervention group

Subsequently, the intervention group received a multi-component support package consisting of: (1) a single, 90-minute training session held in the hospital conference hall, which focused on reinforcing the principles of accurate documentation and providing a hands-on demonstration of QR code access; (2) permanent access to the QR code poster (hosting categorized documentation guidelines) installed at the nursing station. Following the training, the poster containing all QR codes was installed at the nursing station, with the formal permission of the head nurse. The nurses in the intervention group were instructed to refer to the QR codes during their documentation process whenever they needed assistance, using the provided exemplars as a reference for correct formatting and content, and (3) weekly follow-up visits by the principal researcher over the two-month intervention period. These visits were primarily intended to troubleshoot technical issues, ensure the poster remained accessible and functional, and verbally encourage use (i.e., fidelity checks), but were explicitly structured not to provide any additional instructional content or training beyond the initial session.

The intervention period lasted for two months. To monitor intervention fidelity and provide ongoing support, the principal researcher conducted weekly follow-up visits with all participants in the intervention group. During these visits, the researcher checked the physical placement of the QR code poster, verbally confirmed its use with the nursing teams, observed nurses scanning the codes in practice when opportunities arose, and offered immediate assistance with any technical or procedural difficulties they encountered. While this provided direct, qualitative confirmation of the tool’s integration into the workflow, the study did not employ backend website analytics to track the exact frequency, timing, or specific content pages accessed via the QR codes.

Control group protocol

Throughout this two-month period, the control group continued their standard nursing documentation practices without receiving the educational session, the QR code poster, or any follow-up visits from the researcher.

Post-test data collection

Upon completion of the two-month intervention period, post-test data were collected. The principal researcher again evaluated two new, separate patient admission reports for each participant in both the control and intervention groups, using the same Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist to assess any changes in documentation quality.

Post-study ethical consideration

In adherence to ethical principles and following the completion of data analysis, which demonstrated the positive impact of the intervention, the QR code-based educational resource was also implemented for the nurses in the control group. They received the same training session and instructional materials on how to utilize the QR codes to support their nursing documentation, thereby ensuring that all participants ultimately benefited from the study’s findings.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables, were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the participants. To compare the demographic and baseline variables between the intervention and control groups, independent statistical tests were employed: Chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test where applicable) for categorical variables and independent samples t-test for continuous variables.

The normality of the distribution of total documentation quality scores (for both pre-test and post-test data) was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The results confirmed that the data for both groups at both time points did not significantly deviate from a normal distribution (p > 0.05), thereby justifying the use of parametric tests for group comparisons.

The primary objective of comparing the quality scores of nursing documentation between the intervention and control groups after the intervention was assessed using an independent samples t-test on the post-test scores. The magnitude of the intervention’s effect was calculated using Cohen’s d. The effect size was interpreted as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), or large (d ≥ 0.8). For all analyses, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The Nursing Documentation Quality Checklist consisted of 29 items, with each item scored on a 3-point scale (0 to 2). Consequently, the total possible score for a single admission report ranged from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 58. In this study, for each participant, to obtain a more stable and reliable measure less susceptible to random variation or transient situational factors (e.g., workload on a specific shift), the total scores from two separate admission reports were summed for both the pre-test and post-test analyses. Therefore, the overall score for each participant at each time point (pre-test/post-test) ranged from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 116. This aggregated score was used as the primary data point for all subsequent statistical comparisons between the groups.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 60 nurses participated in this study, with 30 nurses allocated to the intervention group and 30 to the control group. The demographic characteristics of the participants in both groups are summarized in Table 1. The results of the comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups at baseline for the key demographic and work-related variables (p ≥ 0.05). While the p-value for gender distribution was borderline (p = 0.197), the clinical composition was similar, with both groups predominantly female (Intervention: 73.3%, Control: 86.7%). More importantly, the mean age and work experience, which are more directly relevant to clinical practice and documentation familiarity, showed no significant difference (p = 0.55 and p = 0.75, respectively). This indicates that the groups were homogenous and comparable for the purpose of this study.

The majority of participants in both groups held a Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BScN). To account for baseline scores and increase precision, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with the post‑intervention score as the dependent variable and the pre‑intervention score as a covariate. The ANCOVA confirmed a strong and significant effect of the intervention (F(1,57) = 189.21, *p* < 0.001, partial η² = 0.768), indicating that the intervention explained approximately 76.8% of the variance in post‑test scores after adjusting for baseline. The pre‑intervention score was also a significant predictor of the post‑intervention score (F(1,57) = 398.06, *p* < 0.001, partial η² = 0.875). The overall model accounted for 90.6% of the variance in post‑intervention documentation quality (R² = 0.906, adjusted R² = 0.903).

A supplementary, exploratory analysis was conducted to identify which specific checklist items showed the greatest improvement attributable to the intervention. This was determined by calculating the difference in the percentage of ‘Completely Documented’ scores between the intervention and control groups at post-test. The five items with the largest positive differences were: Item 11 (Pressure injury risk & interventions): Improved by 47%, reflecting better use of the Braden Scale and documentation of preventive care. Item 7 (Consciousness & admission vital signs): Improved by 43%, indicating more complete and structured initial assessments. Item 4 (Reason for hospitalization): Improved by 40%, showing clearer documentation of medical diagnoses and linked nursing interventions. Item 24 (Provided nursing care): Improved by 38%, suggesting a more comprehensive recording of specific care activities performed. Item 16 (Patient/companion education): Improved by 35%, demonstrating more consistent coverage of mandatory educational topics.

Discussion

The primary finding of this quasi-experimental study demonstrated that the implementation of a QR code-based intervention led to a statistically significant and substantial improvement in the quality of nursing documentation, as evidenced by the marked increase in the post-test scores of the intervention group compared to the controls. The large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.14) underscores the potency of this low-cost technological solution in addressing a pervasive challenge in clinical practice, particularly in settings where comprehensive electronic health record (EHR) systems are not financially feasible.

Our pre-test findings, which indicated suboptimal documentation quality in both groups prior to the intervention, are unfortunately not an isolated phenomenon but rather a reflection of a widespread issue documented in both national and international literature. Studies from various healthcare contexts have consistently reported deficiencies in nursing records, characterized by incompleteness, lack of standardization, and omissions of critical patient data [7, 15]. The baseline assessment in our study aligns with findings from a systematic review by Wang et al. which highlighted that documentation quality is often uniformly poor in the absence of targeted, sustained interventions [16]. This consistent pattern across different settings, including our context in Iran, underscores the inadequacy of traditional documentation methods and short-term training programs, which fail to provide the ongoing support necessary for maintaining high standards [17].

This finding resonates with the growing body of evidence supporting technology-enabled “point-of-care” learning. For instance, a study by Park Eun-Woo and colleagues (2019) in Korea demonstrated that a QR code-based education program improved competence among operating room nurses, attributing success to the immediate accessibility of content [18]. Similarly, the work of Patting Fan et al. (2023) in China confirmed the utility of QR codes as an efficient channel for patient follow-up [19]. In contrast to traditional short‑term educational interventions cited earlier [9, 10], which failed to sustain improvements, our QR code‑based approach provided persistent, integrated support directly within the workflow. The effectiveness of this intervention likely extends beyond mere accessibility; it reduces cognitive load by providing instant answers, empowers nurses with on‑demand standardized guidance, and seamlessly integrates into the documentation routine due to its ease of use. The key differentiator of our study is its demonstration that this simple technology can serve as an immediate and practical support mechanism within the existing documentation system, offering a feasible and low‑cost alternative to a full EHR in the short to medium term. Long‑term studies are needed to assess whether this approach can sustainably compensate for structural limitations over time.

The significant post-intervention improvement observed in our study can be robustly explained by the intervention’s core design principle: providing persistent, just-in-time, and on-demand access to standardized guidelines. This approach is strongly supported by established educational theories. The concept of “just-in-time learning” posits that information is best absorbed and applied when it is readily available at the precise moment of need, directly within the workflow [20]. Furthermore, theories of workplace learning and situated cognition emphasize that learning is most effective when it is embedded in the actual context of practice [21]. Beyond these, the intervention also aligns with principles of self‑regulated learning, as nurses could independently access guidance to monitor and adjust their documentation practices, and with habit formation theory, since repeated, context‑triggered use of QR codes can help internalize documentation standards into routine practice. Our QR code intervention operationalized these principles perfectly by placing critical information at the nurses’ fingertips during the documentation process itself, thereby reducing cognitive load, minimizing reliance on fallible memory, and facilitating the immediate application of knowledge.

A critical implication of our study lies in its strategic relevance for resource-limited healthcare systems. In many contexts, including Iran, the widespread implementation of integrated EHRs remains a distant goal due to prohibitive costs, infrastructural challenges, and complex implementation processes [12]. Our QR code-based intervention presents a pragmatic and highly cost-effective alternative to bridge this technological gap. By leveraging ubiquitous smartphone technology and free online resources, this approach delivers a significant improvement in documentation quality without the need for massive capital investment. It effectively creates a “low-fidelity” digital support layer over the existing paper-based system, mitigating some of its most critical shortcomings, such as difficult access to standardized protocols and the inefficiency of searching through physical manuals. The simplicity, low cost, and minimal infrastructure requirements of this intervention suggest high transferability to other low‑resource or paper‑based settings globally, where similar challenges in nursing documentation persist. This demonstrates that meaningful quality improvement is achievable through innovative, appropriate-technology solutions even in the absence of full-scale digitalization. For sustainable long-term integration into ward culture, the QR code system should be transitioned from researcher-led implementation to hospital management ownership. Nursing education departments or clinical supervisors could assume responsibility for maintaining the QR code posters, ensuring they remain prominently displayed at nursing stations. The website content should be designated as a living document, reviewed and updated periodically by hospital nursing committees to reflect current clinical guidelines, protocols, and documentation standards. Furthermore, the QR code system could be incorporated into orientation programs for newly recruited nurses, establishing it as a standard reference tool from the outset of employment. Hospital quality improvement units might also consider monitoring QR code usage patterns and periodically assessing documentation quality to evaluate the intervention’s ongoing effectiveness. By embedding these strategies within existing hospital structures—rather than relying on researcher involvement—the QR code-based support system can become a permanent, self-sustaining component of the clinical environment, reinforcing accurate documentation practices as an integral part of daily nursing routine.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations. The most important methodological limitation of this study is the lack of blinding of the outcome assessor, which introduces a potential for assessment bias. Although we employed a highly structured and validated checklist to minimize subjectivity, the lack of blinding remains a methodological weakness. The quasi-experimental design carries a risk of selection bias, although random allocation was employed to mitigate this. The study design does not allow us to disentangle the specific effect of the QR code technology from the potential combined effects of the initial training session and the increased attention from weekly follow-up visits (a Hawthorne effect). The intervention was deliberately designed as a pragmatic, bundled support package for clinical settings. Therefore, the observed improvement should be interpreted as the result of this integrated package, which includes a technological aid coupled with minimal training and proactive support for its use. Future studies with multiple arms could separate the effects of the technology itself from the effects of training and researcher attention. The two-month intervention period does not allow us to draw conclusions about the long-term sustainability of the improvement. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up periods and use QR code analytics to objectively track usage patterns. The use of a newly developed, context-specific checklist, while rigorously validated, limits the direct comparability of our absolute documentation quality scores with findings from other studies that employed different instruments. Sixth, the checklist yields a continuous adherence score without established clinical cut-off points to define ‘good’ or ‘poor’ documentation quality. Furthermore, while the use of a detailed scoring guide minimized subjectivity, the application of a uniform 3-point scale across all items may not capture granular quality differences with equal sensitivity. Future research could develop and validate more differentiated, domain-specific scoring rubrics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that a multi-component support package incorporating QR code technology can significantly enhance nursing documentation quality by integrating standardized support directly into the daily workflow. This approach empowers nurses with immediate, on-demand access to clinical standards, effectively weaving continuous learning and guidance into the documentation routine. For healthcare systems grappling with the high cost and complexity of EHR implementation, this approach offers a practical, immediately actionable, and cost-effective strategy to improve documentation quality, enhance patient safety, and foster a culture of continuous learning, thereby delivering a high-impact solution at a remarkably low cost. Future research should build on these findings by incorporating backend website analytics to objectively track QR code scan frequency and usage patterns. Such data would enable investigators to examine dose-response relationships between intervention utilization and documentation quality, identify the most frequently accessed content categories, and further optimize the design and deployment of QR code-based support systems in clinical settings.
