In Memoriam: Maria Iandolo New, 1928-2024
Authors: Phyllis W Speiser, Richard J Auchus, Perrin C White
Published: 2024

Maria I. New, MD, past president of the Endocrine Society, was a pioneering figure in pediatric endocrinology and disorders of the adrenal cortex. Born and raised in New York City, she overcame family, economic, and academic challenges to pursue her goal of becoming the first physician in her family. As an undergraduate scholarship student at Cornell University, she majored in chemistry. There she met and married her husband, Bertrand (Buzz) New. They were the first married couple to matriculate at the University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine, graduating in 1954. Starting out in an internal medicine residency, she switched after a year to pediatric training at Cornell Medical College–New York Hospital. She then spent the next few years in research, culminating with a 3-year National Institutes of Health (NIH)-sponsored fellowship at Cornell with Dr Ralph Peterson, thus establishing her life-long fascination with adrenal steroid disorders. Shortly after joining the faculty of Cornell Medical College, she founded the Division of Pediatric Endocrinology, and remained as division director for nearly 40 years. She rose rapidly through the academic ranks, acquiring new responsibilities as director of the Pediatric Clinical Research Center, and in 1980 was named chair of the Department of Pediatrics at a time when there were very few women serving as academic department chairs. During this time, Dr New established a fellowship program in pediatric endocrinology, mentoring several generations of trainees from the United States and abroad, among them at least 18 division chiefs and many other noted leaders in the field. In 2004, Dr New moved to the Mount Sinai School of Medicine as a professor of Pediatrics and Human Genetics and director of the Adrenal Steroid Disorders Program, where she remained for the rest of her career.

In 1985 to 1986, she served as president of the Pediatric Endocrine Society (then known as “The Lawson Wilkins Pediatric Endocrine Society”) and in 1991 to 1992 as president of the Endocrine Society. During her tenure as president, Dr New championed the development of the Women's Caucus, which was a forum for “women to meet and discuss issues that affect women, not only in endocrinology but in their careers as well.” She served on the nominating committee, board of directors, and executive committee, among other duties. Her awards and honors from the Endocrine Society include the Robert H. Williams Distinguished Leadership Award in 1988, the Rhone-Poulenc Rorer Clinical Investigator Award in 1995, and in 2003, the Fred Conrad Koch Lifetime Achievement Award, the Society's highest honor. In 2010, she also received the Judson J. Van Wyk Award, the Pediatric Endocrine Society's most prestigious award, in recognition of outstanding career achievement. Dr New was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and inducted into the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) Hall of Honor, being among the longest-tenured grant recipients in NICHD history. She served as editor-in-chief of The Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism from 1994 to 1999.

Her own research yielded more than 400 peer-reviewed publications, plus numerous monographs, textbooks, and chapters. Her painstaking description of patients’ clinical and hormonal phenotypes led to the delineation of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) due to various degrees of 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Her carefully curated collection of affected kindreds provided a springboard for identifying linkage of the trait for 21-hydroxylase deficiency to the HLA complex, in work done with Drs Lenore S. Levine, Sharon E. Oberfield, and Bo Dupont. This led to cloning, sequencing, and identification of various CYP21A2 genotypes, in collaboration with Dr Perrin C. White and colleagues, to subsequent extensive studies of genotype-phenotype relationships in this disease. Dr New sought to expand the reach of CAH treatment to prenatal glucocorticoid administration in at-risk pregnancies. Seeking to more selectively apply such treatment, she collaborated on next-generation sequencing techniques for noninvasive prenatal diagnosis. Among her last papers was a genotype-phenotype correlation study of more than 1500 patients with 21-hydroxylase deficiency seen in her clinics and research centers, the largest series ever described. She also was among the first to describe the clinical and fertility challenges of adults with 21-hydroxylase deficiency, after glucocorticoid therapy became widely available.

Dr New and her colleagues developed immunoassays for a wide range of steroids and employed those assays in detailed inpatient research protocols with dynamic testing, including dexamethasone suppression, cosyntropin stimulation, salt-loading, and drug challenges. Their analyses of hormone profiles from baseline through dynamic stimuli enabled the elucidation of several novel human steroid biosynthesis disorders, including 2 inherited forms of hypertension, apparent mineralocorticoid excess, and glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism (dexamethasone-suppressible hyperaldosteronism). She also made important contributions to early studies of syndromes of aldosterone resistance and deficiency and rare forms of congenital adrenal hyperplasia, such as 11β-hydroxylase deficiency.

The consummate physician-scientist, Dr New was constantly exploring new ideas and asking important clinical questions, while at the same time, she was passionately devoted to the care of her patients and their families. She sat at the bedside of infants having salt-wasting crises overnight to adjust their medications and fluids. She consoled and guided parents distraught at the prospect of caring for children with rare diseases.

She also established American and international collaborations to study rare diseases. She compared notes and compiled case series with Prof Maguelone Forest in France, Berenice Mendonça in Brazil, and groups around the world. She would have Dr Jean Wilson, another great endocrinologist, visit from Texas for several weeks to discuss enigmatic cases and plan additional studies and treatment plans. Her case conferences were famous for involving the input of experts, who were often stumped, but also led to the description of new disorders.

Maria New was predeceased by her husband. She is survived by 2 daughters and 1 son (all physicians), as well as 8 grandchildren and 4 great-grandchildren. We extend our condolences to all.
