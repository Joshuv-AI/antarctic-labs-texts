Preoperative red cell width distribution to albumin ratio predicts early radial artery occlusion following coronary intervention via conventional transradial access
Authors: Yanbin Song, Wenhua Li, Jianqiang Xiao, Gaojun Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe red cell width distribution to albumin ratio (RAR) has recently been linked to various cardiovascular diseases. Radial artery occlusion (RAO) is a critical complication in patients undergoing coronary angiograph or percutaneous coronary intervention (CAG/PCI) via conventional transradial access (cTRA). However, the relationship between RAR and RAO remains unclear. This study investigates the association between RAR with the risk of early RAO. We retrospectively analyzed data from 389 participants in the CONDITION study. Participants underwent CAG/PCI via cTRA access and were divided into two groups based on the optimal cutoff value of RAR from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Multivariate logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed to explore the association between RAR and RAO. A total of 26 participants (6.6%) developed early RAO as confirmed by ultrasonography. Patients with higher RAR levels had a significant higher prevalence of RAO. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that elevated RAR levels were associated with an increased risk of RAO (OR: 3.673, 95% CI 1.566–8.615, P = 0.003). Moreover, a larger preoperative radial artery inner diameter was significantly protective against RAO (OR 0.299, 95% CI 0.109–0.846, P = 0.020). RCS analysis revealed a linear relationship between RAR and the risk of RAO. The current study showed a significant positive association between RAR and early RAO in individuals undergoing CAG/ PCI via cTRA. Introduction

To date, following the endorsement of the “radial-first” strategy, radial artery puncture access has emerged as the predominant percutaneous intervention route for cardiovascular diseases in clinical practice1,2. Although the use of distal transradial access (dTRA) for coronary angiograph and percutaneous coronary intervention (CAG/PCI) is increasing globally3,4, conventional transradial access (cTRA) remains a crucial approach in intervention procedures5–7. However, the prevalence of pre-procedure testing for hand circulation and the evaluation of radial artery occlusion (RAO) remains low8. As the most frequent vascular complication following cTRA, RAO can affect up to 30% of patients9. RAO poses a significant challenge for subsequent CAG/PCI procedures via cTRA. Given the latent nature of RAO, it is essential to focus on the early identification of high-risk patients.

The mechanism of RAO remains controversial. RAO may result from compression phenomena, induced locally by the catheterization process, such as edema or hematoma10. The patient’s coagulation status may also influence the occurrence of RAO8. Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated that the inflammatory response plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of RAO11–13. During sheath insertion, damage to the intima of the radial vessel can trigger inflammation and activate coagulation cascade, leading to the occurrence of RAO14. Recent studies have also reported that puncture or material-associated trauma and inflammation response are important factors in the development of RAO11,15.

The Red cell width distribution to albumin ratio (RAR) , a novel combined parameter, has been reported as a promising biomarker of baseline inflammatory process. It is cost-effective and can be derived from routine laboratory tests upon admission. Furthermore, RAR has been identified as having the potential to provide effective risk stratifcation in patients with severe diseases16,17. Numerous studies have indicated that RAR is associated with infammation-related conditions such as cancer18, acute myocardial infarction19, carotid plaques20, sepsis21 and atrial fibrillation17.

Nevertheless, to our knowledge, no prior studies have explored the association between RAR and RAO in the literature. In this present study, we aim to investigate the relationship between preoperative RAR and the early risk of RAO among patients hospitalized for primary percutaneous coronary angiograph and intervention at a single center.

Participants and methods

Study participants

This study comprised 389 subjects participating in the CONDITION study. The CONDITION (Comparison of Long-term Radial Artery Occlusion in Coronary Diagnosis and/or Intervention via Distal vs. Conventional Transradial Access) study was a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wujin Hospital, Jiangsu University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration number: NCT05253820).

These participants successfully underwent CAG/PCI via cTRA access between February 2022 and December 2022 at our center. Eligible subjects were included according to the following inclusion criteria: patients aged ≥ 18 years, first time recipients of CAG/PCI, underwent Doppler ultrasound examination of the radial artery 24 h before and after the procedure, and willingness to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria were patients with a stenosed radial artery or a negative Allen test, prior percutaneous intervention via cTRA, infection or broken skin at the puncture site, acute ST elevated myocardial infarction, and incomplete clinical data.

Puncture procedure and perioperative data

During the CAG/PCI procedure, a standardized protocol was followed. The procedure was performed by experienced interventional cardiologists. The right radial artery was the preferred puncture site. After administering local anesthesia through lidocaine, the Seldinger technique was meticulously applied to puncture the radial artery at its pulsation point, approximately 2–3 cm proximal to the wrist stripe. A guidewire was then routinely inserted into the radial artery sheath, followed by the administration of appropriate doses of nitroglycerin and 3000U of heparin. If PCI was required, 100 U/kg of heparin was administered. The arterial sheath was promptly removed upon completion of the procedure. Postoperatively, an elastic bandage or a radial compression device (TR Band, Terumo Medical, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized to compress and control bleeding. Proper post-procedural techniques and care for the radial arterial access site were strictly adhered to.

Perioperative data were collected on the preoperative radial artery inner diameter, puncture time, number of attempts, operative duration, hemostasis time, hemostasis methods, sheath size, and access site-related complications including local hematoma, radial artery spasm, arteriovenous fistula, pseudoaneurysm. Additionally, the doses of heparin and contrast agent were recorded.

Pre-puncture evaluation of the radial artery was conducted using Doppler ultrasound with an L18-4MH frequency probe. Postoperatively, Doppler ultrasound was performed at 24 h to assess the patency of the RAO in all study participants. The RAO was characterized by the presence of thrombus, interrupted blood flow, and the absence of the arterial pulse waveform. The examination was conducted by an experienced assessor. Access site-related complications and RAO were evaluated.

Clinicodemographic and laboratory characteristics

The medical history, including hypertension, diabetes, cerebral infarction, coronary artery disease (CAD), Non-ST elevated acute coronary syndrome (ACS), heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF), smoking, and proportion of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) were collected from medical records. Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, as well as physical examination such as postoperative systolic blood pressure (SBP), postoperative diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and body mass index (BMI), along with medication use (aspirin, clopidogrel, tegretol, indobufen, statins and anticoagulants, were documented by cardiologists.

Venous blood samples were collected from all study participants upon hospital admission, prior to any intervention procedures, following a minimum fasting period of 8 h. Biochemical parameters, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, uric acid (UA), fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), red cell width distribution (RDW), albumin, white blood cell (WBC), haemoglobin, neutrophils, platelet count, fibrinogen, and D-dimer were measured and recorded. The RAR was calculated using the formula: RDW/albumin. Participants were stratified into two groups based on the median RAR value.

Statistical analysis

The normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while the nonnormally distributed continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR). Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA or the nonparametric Mann‒Whitney test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square was used to compare categorical variables between the two groups. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of RAR in assessing the risk of RAO. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for RAO. The results of the logistic regression analyses are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was employed to evaluate the potential linear relationship between RAR and RAO. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0.

Results

Baseline clinicodemographic and laboratory characteristics of included participants

A total of 801 participants were screened, of whom 389 were included in the data analysis (Fig. 1). All participants successfully underwent CAG/PCI via cTRA. The baseline clinicodemographic and laboratory characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. Overall, the median (range) age was 67(58, 73) years, and 216 (55.5%) were male. Among these participants, 26 (6.6%) developed early RAO. The use of aspirin, clopidogrel, tegretol, indobufen, statins, and anticoagulants made up 44.2%, 36.5%, 13.1%, 4.9%, 87.7% and 19.8% respectively. Additionally, 32.9% of individuals received a combination of antiplatelet therapies, while 9.3% received a combination of antiplatelet and anticoagulants therapies.

A ROC curve illustrating the predictive performance of RAR for RAO was presented in Fig. 2. The optimal cut-off value for RAR was determined to be 3.39, yielding a sensitivity of 69.2% and a specificity of 62.8%. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for RAR was 0.642 (95% CI 0.533–0.750, P = 0.016).

ROC curve of RAR for predicting RAO.

Based on the optimal cutoff value of RAR, participants were stratified into two groups. A significant increase was observed in the prevalence of non-ST-elevated ACS and HF among participants with high RAR compared to those with low RAR. Compared to the low RAR group, participants in the high RAR group were significantly older, and exhibited lower levels of BMI, FBG, TC, TG, LDL-C, and higher levels of neutrophils. Furthermore, participants in the high RAR group exhibited higher creatinine, RDW and D- dimer levels, as well as lower albumin levels (all P < 0.01). However, the proportion of anticoagulants and combined antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapies were not significant difference between the two groups.

In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding the proportion of males, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, cerebral infarction, AF, use of antiplatelet medications and statins, or levels of ALT, AST, BUN, UA, HDL-C, WBC, platelets count, fibrinogen, postoperative SBP, and postoperative DBP (all P > 0.05).

Comparison of puncture and perioperative data

All the included participants successfully underwent puncture via cTRA. The puncture and perioperative data for both the high and low RAR groups are provided in Table 2. Doppler ultrasound revealed no significant difference in the preoperative radial artery inner diameter between the two groups (P = 0.411). Patients with high RAR levels were more likely to experience RAO (11.3% vs. 3.8%, P = 0.004). The median operative duration for patients with high RAR tended to be longer compare to those with low RAR; however, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.238). Subjects with high RAR were more likely to receive higher doses of heparin (> 3000U) compared to those with low RAR. However, there were no significant difference in the puncture time, number of attempts, hemostasis methods, sheath size, access site-related complications, proportion of PCI, or doses of contrast agent (all P > 0.05).

Relationship between RAR and the risk of early RAO

The univariate logistic regression analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 1. RAR, statin use, doses of heparin (> 3000U), and preoperative radial artery inner diameter were all significantly associated with early RAO in patients undergoing primary CAG/PCI via cTRA.

As shown in Table 3, after adjusting for confounding factors, RAR was significantly associated with the risk of early RAO when analyzed as a continuous variable (OR: 3.239, 95% CI 1.614–6.499, P = 0.001). When RAR was categorized, the high RAR group demonstrated a threefold increased risk of early RAO compared to the low RAR group (OR: 3.673, 95% CI 1.566–8.615, P = 0.003). A larger preoperative radial artery inner diameter and higher doses of heparin (> 3000U) were significantly protective against RAO (OR 0.299, 95% CI 0.109–0.824, P = 0.020; OR 0.225, 95% CI 0.051–0.985, P = 0.048). Statin use was not independently associated with RAO risk (P = 0.261).

Subgroup analyses stratified by sex indicated that the association between RAR levels and RAO risk was not consistent between male and female individuals (males: OR 5.166, 95% CI 1.368–19.138, P = 0.015; females: OR 2.252, 95% CI 0.664–7.639, P = 0.193). Discussion

This study found that 6.6% of participants developed early RAO. There was a significantly positive correlation between elevated RAR level and an increased risk of early RAO among individuals undergoing CAG/PCI via cTRA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the predictive value of RAR with early RAO in patients.

Transradial artery access has emerged as the preferred approach for cardiovascular interventions, demonstrating superior outcomes compared to the traditional transfemoral method 22. RAO is the most frequently reported complication of conventional transradial access (cTRA) , with an incidence rate ranging from 1 to 30%14.

As described in our previous study, a distal transradial access inner diameter-to-sheath outer diameter ratio less than 1 was identified as an independent risk factor for RAO23. Consistent results were observed in the present study, particularly among male patients. Preoperative ultrasound assessment of vessel inner diameter and the selection of a sheath with a smaller outer diameter may help reduce the risk of RAO.

The exact biological mechanisms underlying the RAO remain unclear. It is well established that the inflammatory response plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of various cardiovascular diseases24,25. The vascular endothelium maintains vascular homeostasis by releasing a wide array of vasoactive molecules, which function as both protective and detrimental regulators of vascular tone, structural remodeling, inflammation, and atherogenesis. Previous study has demonstrated that damage to the intima of the radial vessel can trigger an inflammation response and activate the coagulation cascade, ultimately leading to the development of RAO14,15. Additionally, chronic inflammation plays a crucial role in the pathophysiological mechanisms underling endothelial dysfunction and vascular diseases26. Chronic inflammation impairs the stability of the vascular microenvironment, affects vascular cell plasticity, and leads to adverse cardiovascular events27.

Red cell width distribution (RDW) is a simple and cost-effective parameter that reflects the degree of heterogeneity in erythrocyte volume. This variability may be indicative of various underlying metabolic abnormaliies, including oxidative stress, inflammation, poor nutritional status, erythrocyte fragmentation, and altered erythropoietin function28. A recent review summarized the latest evidence from key studies on the relationship between RDW and AF recurrence following catheter ablation, as well as elucidated the mechanism linking elevated RDW to heightened systemic inflammation29. Additionally, RDW can serve as a predictor of adverse outcomes in patients with acute and chronic inflammation disease30,31. Elevated RDW has also been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, including carotid atherosclerosis, coronary heart disease, and both macrovascular and microvascular complications32–34. Albumin, a biochemical marker of nutritional status, not only influences the progression of oxidative stress-related diseases but is also intricately associated with systemic inflammation levels. A low albumin level exerts pro-infammatory effects and has been associated with increased oxidative stress, platelet activation and aggregation, thereby elevating the risk of all-cause mortality risk in patients with acute myocardial infarction35. Albumin has also been recognized as a multifaceted factor with anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory properties that influence vascular events and prognostic outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke36. Moreover, hypoalbuminemia refects an infammatory state and is related with small vessel disease37. The reduction in albumin levels is correlated with high blood viscosity, impaired endothelial function, and atherogenesis and thrombosis38–40. These may represent a potential mechanism linking hypoalbuminemia to the risk of arterial disease.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) functions as a highly sensitive systemic indicator of acute inflammation condition and tissue damage. A retrospective study involving 403 patients identified hs-CRP as a novel independent predictor of the development of RAO. The study also revealed that monocyte count was significantly elevated in the RAO group compared to the control group11. RAR, an integrated inflammatory-related index that combines two classic parameters, has been recognized as a simple yet relatively reliable parameter for identifying high-risk patients in infammation-related conditions17–21. These researches indicate that it functions not only as an acute inflammation factor but also plays a crucial role in chronic inflammation responses, demonstrating superior performance compared to CRP. Furthermore, RAR has been reported to be superior to RDW or albumin when used individually41. However, to date, limited research has focused on the diagnostic and prognostic value of RAR in peripheral artery disease (PAD), particularly in radial artery complications following sheath insertion during cardiovascular interventions. A recent study involving diabetic patients revealed a significant association between elevated RAR and the risk of PAD, particularly among those aged 65 years or older42. In addition, RAR has been identified as a prognosis-related risk factor for patients with aortic aneurysms43. Furthermore, RAR has been proved to be associated with an elevated risk of limb atherosclerosis in patients with T2DM, suggesting that the RAR ratio may serve as a significant clinical marker for vascular complications in this patient population44. Thus, the relationship between RAR and RAO can be at least partially explained.

In our study, we observed a markedly higher prevalence of inflammatory diseases, such as coronary artery disease (CAD), non-ST-elevated acute coronary syndrome, and heart failure (HF), among participants with high RAR compared to those with low RAR. Furthermore, participants in the high RAR group were significantly older and exhibited lower levels of albumin, BMI, FBG, TC, TG, LDL-C, and hemoglobin, as well as higher levels of RDW, D-dimer, and neutrophil counts. These findings suggest that patients with elevated RAR levels are at an increased risk of developing inflammatory conditions, experiencing oxidative stress, undergoing platelet activation and aggregation, suffering from impaired endothelial function, and facing a higher likelihood of thrombosis. Consequently, these factors contribute to the progression of vascular atherogenesis and occlusion. Subsequent logistic regression demonstrated that RAR remained a significant independent risk factor of early RAO, even after adjusting for clinicodemographic characteristics and puncture and perioperative factors. Our study also demonstrated a linear relationship between RAR and RAO. Moreover, subgroup analysis revealed that RAR was associated with the risk of early RAO in both females and males. The finding suggests that increase in RAR level may cause a parallel deterioration in endothelial function in patients catheterized for CAG/PCI. We surmise that RAO may be more likely occur in patients with chronic inflammatory conditions due to the sheath insertion and endothelial injury during the procedure.

In future clinical practice, greater attention should be given to patients with elevated radial artery resistance (RAR) levels. Potential management strategies may involve pre-procedural assessment of the radial artery using Doppler ultrasound, selection of appropriately smaller sheath sizes during the procedure, administration of an optimized dose of heparin, and intensified statin therapy. Furthermore, evidence from long-term clinical trials supports the use of colchicine for secondary prevention of vascular events45. Therefore, a short-term, low-dose colchicine regimen may offer additional benefits for patients with high RAR, especially those with underlying inflammatory conditions.

RAR can be rapidly and easily measured during admission laboratory tests, aiding in the early detection of RAO. Further prospective studies are warranted to establish RAR as a reliable biomarker for predicting RAO. However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, this is a single-center study, which may introduce selection bias in the study population. Multicenter, prospective studies are necessary to validate these findings. Secondly, important inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were not included in the analysis. Lastly, variations in post-intervention hemostasis practices among different cardiologists may have influenced the results. We plan to further investigate this relationship in future prospective studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated for the first time that a high RAR level is associated with an increased risk of early RAO in patients underwent primary CAG/PCI via cTRA. A linear relationship was observed, with a threshold value of 3.29 as the infection point. Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed that the association remained consistent across both male and female participants. Therefore, RAR can serve as a valuable reference for the prevention and risk stratification of RAO in patients.
