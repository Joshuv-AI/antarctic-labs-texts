Effectiveness of AI and rule-based conversational agents for depression, anxiety and stress: A meta-analysis
Authors: Saeed Mokhtari Masoumi Alamdarloo, Ali Mirzakhani, Mohamad Azhdarloo, Elaheh Haghani-Samani, Abouzar Nazari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression, anxiety, and stress are significant global health burdens worsened by restricted access to care. Conversational agents (CAs), encompassing both AI(Artificial Intelligence)- and rule-based systems, provide scalable mental health interventions. This systematic review and meta-analysis analyzed the efficacy of CA interventions in 48 randomized controlled trials covering 28,071 participants. It demonstrated small-to-moderate but significant effects in minimizing symptoms of depression (SMD: −0.27), anxiety (SMD: −0.20), and stress (SMD: −0.26). A Robust Variance Estimation (RVE) approach was used to address within-study dependencies, with results consistent with the random-effects model. Subgroup analyses investigated sources of heterogeneity and indicated greater effects in clinical populations and shorter-duration interventions. Meta-regression identified no appreciable effect of outcome timing. Risk of bias was largely low, although some studies had issues due to missing data or selective reporting. Publication bias was negligible. These results provide evidence for the utilization of conversational agents as effective interventions for lessening psychological symptoms. Additional research is required to evaluate their long-term effectiveness and how best to integrate them into mental healthcare systems.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Depression, anxiety disorders, and stress-related disorders are the most disabling and prevalent mental disorders globally. As estimated by the World Health Organization (WHO), over 280 million individuals suffer from depression1 and around 301 million individuals suffer from anxiety disorders annually2, and stress-related disorders have an equally large percentage of the global burden of disease and disability-adjusted life years. These conditions, in addition to diminishing the well-being and functioning of the people, put substantial pressure on public health systems and national economies in terms of excess use of healthcare, absenteeism, and lost productivity3. Even though they exert significant impacts, provision of effective and timely mental health care is predominantly limited by structural factors such as a lack of trained staff, cost of treatment, geographical disparity, and persistent stigma against mental illness, particularly in low- and middle-income environments4.

Responding to these challenges, there has been a massive advancement in creating and applying digital interventions for mental health. Amongst these, conversational agents (CAs) have garnered significant interest because of their potential to offer low-cost, accessible, and scalable psychological therapy. Conversational agents are software-based entities that engage users with the use of natural language interfaces, normally simulating human conversation. They have found application in numerous mental health treatments such as mood monitoring, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) delivery, and psychoeducation5–7. The compounds can function on multiple platforms—cell phones, messaging apps, and the web—and are particularly appealing in that they can provide on-demand, anonymous support, thus circumventing some of the most recognized barriers to traditional care8,9.

Interestingly, conversational agents may be typically characterized by their architecture and response generation mechanisms. At one end are AI-based agents, which implement natural language processing (NLP), machine learning (ML), or large language models (LLMs) to look at user inputs dynamically and generate context-apt responses. These may be chatbots like Woebot, which deliver interventions in the form of CBT through NLP, or more sophisticated agents employing affective computing to facilitate emotion detection and regulation of CBT responses10–12. At the other extreme are rule- or script-based systems, that follow pre-scripted decision trees or conversation flows and do not enable adaptive learning. While less personalized, they are simpler to put in place and have been used successfully in structured intervention formats13.

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have increasingly examined the role of AI-based conversational agents in mental health. For instance, Li et al. (2023)14 demonstrated the effectiveness of AI-driven conversational agents in improving mental health outcomes across diverse populations. Similarly, Feng et al. (2025)15 reported positive effects of AI-based chatbots in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety among young people. More recent evidence, including Du et al. (2025)16, has begun to compare rule-based and large language model (LLM)-based systems, highlighting potential differences in adaptability and therapeutic engagement. In addition, emerging meta-analyses such as Sohn et al. (2026)17 further confirm the growing evidence base supporting chatbot interventions in mental health care.

Despite these advances, most existing reviews focus either on AI-based systems alone or on specific populations or disorders, and few provide a comprehensive comparison between AI-based and rule-based conversational agents across multiple psychological outcomes. This gap underscores the need for updated and integrative evidence synthesis11.

Despite the growing body of papers supporting the use of both AI-based vs rule-based Cas in mental health contexts, few have explicitly compared their relative effectiveness over well-used psychological outcomes such as depression, anxiety, and stress14,15,18,19. Most existing reviews either take a broad view of digital mental health interventions or focus exclusively on AI-driven systems14,20–22, often neglecting comparisons between different conversational agent architectures. This leaves an important gap in understanding how agent type—whether adaptive and AI-based or rule-based and scripted—may influence therapeutic outcomes23. This study aims to address this significant gap in knowledge. In addition, the accelerating pace of AI feature development—particularly for generative models—requires ongoing assessments of their relative advantages compared to more conventional digital tools16,17.

Therefore, this study systematically evaluates the effectiveness of AI-based and rule-based conversational agents in reducing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress.

Results

Results of Systematic Review

Database searches turned up 3976 records in all 2787 records were left for title and abstract screening after 693 records and 496 duplicates were eliminated using automated tools. Of these, 66 full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility after 2721 were eliminated. For various reasons, including non-RCT design, irrelevant outcomes, or ineligible interventions, 18 studies were eliminated. In the end, the systematic review contained 48 studies (Fig. 1). Additionally, the first 200 records retrieved from Google Scholar were screened, but no new articles were identified. A subset of these randomized controlled trials provided sufficient data for inclusion in the meta-analysis focused on depression, anxiety, and stress.

Among the included studies, 41 trials assessed depression-related outcomes24–64, 34 reported on anxiety10,24–26,28–32,34–39,41,44–48,51–53,56,58,60,61,63–67, and 19 evaluated stress34,36,39,43,46,47,51–54,59,61,62,64,68–72 (Fig. 2).

The included studies had sample sizes ranging from 17 to more than 19,643 participants, and included 28,071 participants from 31 different countries. Given the quick rise in interest in AI and conversational agent (CA)-based mental health interventions, the earliest included study was published in 2015, and the most recent was in 2025. University students, medical professionals, community samples, and people with diagnosed mental health conditions were among the clinical and non-clinical populations included in the majority of studies, which were carried out in high-income nations.

Conversational agents used in the included studies comprised different types of systems, including rule-based (scripted or decision-tree–based) agents (n = 18, 58.1%), retrieval-based or NLP/ML-based systems (n = 9, 29.0%), and generative AI-based models (n = 2, 6.5%), while a small number employed hybrid or unspecified approaches (n = 2, 6.5%). These agents were integrated into a variety of platforms, including mobile applications (n = 18, 58.1%), web-based systems (n = 8, 25.8%), instant messaging platforms (n = 5, 16.1%), and mixed or multi-platform delivery modes (n = 3, 9.7%). Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) was the most frequently used therapeutic approach (n = 20, 64.5%), and the majority of interventions incorporated psychotherapy or psychoeducational components (n = 26, 83.9%). Additional features such as gamification, mood tracking, personalization, and empathic responses were reported in several studies (n = 11, 35.5%). The duration of interventions varied substantially, ranging from single-session or brief interventions (n = 6, 19.4%) to multi-week programs lasting 12 weeks or longer (n = 12, 38.7%), with the remainder falling between these durations (n = 13, 41.9%). Most studies employed text-based interaction modes (n = 26, 83.9%), while a smaller subset used multimodal interaction including voice or embodied avatars (n = 5, 16.1%). Finally, a subset of studies incorporated adaptive dialogue techniques, personalization algorithms, or emotion recognition features such as sentiment analysis (n = 10, 32.3%).

Regarding the timing of outcome assessment and follow-up periods, there was substantial variability across studies. Outcome measurements were most commonly conducted immediately post-intervention and at 4 weeks (both 29.2%, n = 14/48), followed by 8 weeks (16.7%, n = 8/48) and 2 weeks (14.6%, n = 7/48). Fewer studies assessed outcomes at 12 weeks (8.3%, n = 4/48) or 24 weeks (6.3%, n = 3/48), while assessments at 1, 6, 7, 16, 20, 22, and 28 weeks were rare. Follow-up durations showed a similar pattern, with the most common follow-up periods being 4 weeks (29.2%, n = 14/48), 8 weeks (16.7%, n = 8/48), and 2 weeks (14.6%, n = 7/48). In contrast, 18.8% (n = 9/48) of studies did not include any follow-up assessment. Overall, the evidence base is characterized by a predominance of short-term evaluations, with limited long-term follow-up data.

Less than half of the studies specifically addressed safety procedures, human backup, or adverse event monitoring, despite the fact that safety and ethical issues are becoming more and more important in AI mental health applications. Furthermore, few studies systematically evaluated user engagement’s moderating role, despite the fact that it was frequently mentioned as a crucial element of intervention success. Risk of Bias Assessment

The majority of included studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias in domains related to the randomization process (n = 34, 79.1%), deviations from intended interventions (n = 41, 95.3%), missing outcome data (n = 35, 81.4%), and measurement of the outcome (n = 40, 93.0%). In contrast, concerns were more frequently identified in the domain of selection of the reported result, where 12 studies (27.9%) were rated as having some concerns. Overall, 25 studies (58.1%) were judged to have a low risk of bias, 11 studies (25.6%) had some concerns, and 7 studies (16.3%) were rated as having a high overall risk of bias. Most high-risk judgments were driven by issues related to missing outcome data and selective reporting, often due to incomplete reporting or lack of pre-specified analysis plans. These findings indicate that while a substantial proportion of studies demonstrated acceptable methodological rigor, a non-trivial subset exhibited potential sources of bias, warranting cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Certainty of Evidence

Evidence certainty ranged from moderate for depression and stress outcomes to low for anxiety outcomes, based on the GRADE approach (Supplementary Table 1). Depression and stress showed consistent effects across studies with low heterogeneity, supporting a moderate level of confidence. However, the certainty for anxiety was downgraded due to potential publication bias and imprecision in some subgroup estimates.

Results of meta-analysis

A total of 31 RCTs(53 reports)24–29,31–36,38–41,44–48,50–59,66 assessed depression outcomes and were included in the meta-analysis. Conversational agent-based AI interventions showed a significant small-to-moderate effect in reducing depressive symptoms compared to controls (SMD = −0.27; 95% CI: −0.32 to −0.21; p < 0.001). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 11.2%), indicating consistent effects across diverse populations and settings (Fig. 4). Most studies used validated tools like PHQ-9, BDI-II, and DASS-21, with intervention durations ranging from brief to multi-week formats.

Discussion

This meta-analysis and systematic review pooled data from randomized controlled trials that evaluated the effectiveness of conversational agent (CA) interventions in symptom reduction of depression, anxiety, and stress. In general, our evidence suggests that these digital interventions have small to moderate beneficial effects on psychological measures, with meta-analytic standardized mean differences (SMD) of −0.27 for depression, −0.20 for anxiety, and −0.26 for stress. Such effects were also continuously monitored using validated instruments such as the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and PSS, demonstrating a robust and clinically relevant pattern of psychological change.

Our findings are consistent with previous meta-analyses of internet and mobile-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) which observed similar effect sizes73,74. The present findings expand the existing evidence base by demonstrating that both AI-based and rule-based conversational agents serve as promising tools for alleviating common mental health symptoms. These results align with prior research highlighting the effectiveness of digital mental health interventions across diverse delivery formats14,15,75.

Several mechanisms may explain the observed benefits. The majority of interventions employed evidence-based psychotherapeutic approaches, such as CBT and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), which likely drive therapeutic success independent of the underlying software architecture. Moreover, conversational agents offer a scalable, accessible, and anonymous channel for support. This modality can enhance user engagement, decrease perceived stigma, and facilitate compliance—especially among demographic groups hesitant to seek face-to-face care7,10,28. The interactive, dialogical interface mimics therapeutic dialogue, and can further induce user reflection, the processing of emotions, and the modification of maladaptive behaviors.

Subgroup analyses indicated some variation in effect sizes across population subgroups, with relatively larger point estimates observed in nonclinical groups and among older adults; however, these differences were not statistically significant. This pattern may partly reflect differences in baseline characteristics such as comorbidity burden and motivation for self-care, which previous literature suggests influence engagement with digital health interventions30,39,76.

In addition, interventions incorporating mobile applications and more advanced AI-based systems—particularly those employing generative models—tended to show larger effect estimates compared to non-dynamic, rule-based systems. These systems may enable more adaptive, personalized, and human-like interactions, which could enhance user engagement, perceived usability, and the therapeutic alliance77. However, given that the subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance, these observations should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Clinically, the findings are consistent with the potential of conversational agents to expand the access of mental health services, especially in environments with workforce shortages or where stigma and access hurdles remain. CAs can be used as cost-efficient, low-intensity interventions for symptom management, early intervention, or adjunctive treatment—highly valuable in times of crisis such as the COVID-19 pandemic, when access to face-to-face services is restricted78.

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting our findings. Our exclusion of dissertations, conference abstracts, unpublished manuscripts, and restriction to English-language publications may have introduced publication bias and limited the comprehensiveness of our review. While many studies demonstrated a low risk of bias, approximately one-third were rated as having some concerns or a high risk of bias, predominantly due to missing data, selective reporting, and a lack of participant or assessor blinding. Variability in conversational agent type, delivery platform, therapeutic content, and intervention duration limits the generalizability and precision of our effect estimates. Most studies relied on self-reported outcomes and lacked long-term follow-up assessments, reducing confidence in the sustainability of the therapeutic effects. Finally, few studies evaluated adverse events, long-term outcomes, or user trust, which are critical determinants of real-world viability and effectiveness. Importantly, even though we have examined both AI-based and non-AI-based interventions, the rapid evolution of generative AI necessitates further study of its relative impact, risks, and ethics.

Future research must look beyond basic efficacy to bolster the evidence base and support practical application. The question of whether these conversational agents work in a vacuum is not the only one. To determine the feasibility of deploying these tools at scale—particularly in resource-constrained settings—researchers should conduct formal cost-effectiveness analyses. Furthermore, it is essential to investigate the human-AI interaction dynamics. Engagement and clinical outcomes are significantly influenced by trust, empathy, and user preferences regarding human involvement in care. Qualitative research is also needed to explore user experiences, expectations, and the factors that promote or inhibit sustained use. Ultimately, to ensure that conversational agents deliver lasting public health benefits, an integration of mixed methods and real-world perspectives is required. Otherwise, even the most promising tech risks falling flat outside the laboratory.

In conclusion, both rule-based and AI-based conversational agent interventions show statistically significant reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress. Although these reductions are small to moderate and are not significantly different from routine face-to-face interventions (and in some cases show smaller effect sizes), they remain promising given the scalability and accessibility of these technologies. With thoughtful, evidence-based development and implementation, conversational agents can make a valuable contribution to global mental health efforts.

Methods

Search Strategy

The PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines were followed in the conduct of this systematic review. Supplementary Table 5 contains a completed PRISMA checklist. Three significant electronic databases—PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science—were thoroughly searched in order to find relevant studies. The initial search was performed on May 8, 2025 and Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English between January 2010 and May 2025 were included in the search, as this is when conversational agent and artificial intelligence technologies have become more prevalent in digital mental health interventions.

The search strategy was created to find research assessing AI or conversational agent-based digital interventions that address stress, anxiety, or depression symptoms. Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms pertaining to conversational agents, chatbots, artificial intelligence, digital mental health, and psychological outcomes were combined. Supplementary Table 6 provides comprehensive search strategies for each database.

To identify additional relevant studies beyond those indexed in peer-reviewed databases, grey literature was systematically searched using Google Scholar. We screened the first 200 records returned by keyword searches sorted by relevance, using combinations of terms such as “chatbot”, “conversational agent”, “AI”, “mental health”, “depression”, “anxiety”, “stress”, and “RCT”. Filters were applied to include only English-language and full-text accessible studies. We also examined the reference lists of all included full-text articles and pertinent systematic reviews (retrieved from database searches) to identify additional eligible studies. This backward snowballing approach was complemented by forward citation tracking using Google Scholar, allowing us to include studies that cited any of the included articles. In addition, when required data for effect size calculation were missing or unclear, attempts were made to contact the corresponding authors via email (up to two attempts). All retrieved citations were imported into EndNote 20, and duplicates were removed prior to screening. The review protocol (ID: CRD420251059269) was registered in PROSPERO.

Selection of Studies

All references retrieved from the database searches were imported into EndNote 20 for reference management and duplicate removal. Title and abstract screening was conducted independently by two reviewers (A.M. and M.A.) to identify potentially eligible studies. The full texts of selected articles were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same reviewers according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (A.N.) was consulted. All reviewers had prior experience in conducting systematic reviews and meta-analyses in the field of mental health research.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (A.M. and M.A.) using a standardized data extraction form. The following study features were sourced from the data extracted: first author name, year of publication, country, sample size, type of population (clinical vs. nonclinical), participants’ mean and range of age, method of blinding, conversational agent type used (rule-based vs. AI-based), therapeutic approach type used (e.g., CBT, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)), delivery platform (e.g., web, mobile app), method of response generation (i.e., the mechanism by which the conversational agent generates responses, including rule-based/scripted systems, retrieval-based approaches, and generative artificial intelligence–based models), control condition, outcome measures (e.g., GAD-7, PHQ-9, PSS), timing of post-intervention assessment, and findings reported for depression, anxiety, and stress. Means and standard deviations (SDs) were derived for baseline and post-intervention in both the intervention and control groups. Whenever studies had reported standard errors (SEs), confidence intervals (CIs), or interquartile ranges (IQRs) instead of SDs, respective conversion formulas were applied to calculate SDs so that there was uniformity in effect size calculation. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and where necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (A.N.).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers(E.H.S. and M.A.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB-2) tool79. Random sequence generation, allocation concealment, participant and staff blinding, outcome assessment blinding, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting are among the domains that are evaluated by this tool. “Low,” “high,” or “some concerns” were assigned to each domain. Any differences of opinion were discussed and decided by consensus. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to ensure consistency and reliability of assessments.

Certainty Assessment (GRADE)

Two reviewers (A.N. and M.A.) independently applied the GRADE approach to assess the certainty of evidence for each pooled outcome, based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Statistical Analysis

Effect sizes were calculated as Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) using mean change from baseline for both intervention and control groups80. This approach was specifically chosen to account for baseline imbalances observed in several included trials and to provide a more precise estimate of the therapeutic effect by removing between-person variation (Cochrane Handbook, Section 6.5.1.1)81. If S.D was not reported in each of individual study; it was calculated using following formula: S.Dchange = square root [(S.Dbaseline2 + S.Dfinal2)-(2 × R × S.Dbaseline × S.Dfinal)]. A correlation coefficient of 0.8 was considered as R-value of the above-mentioned formula82. Where standard error of mean (S.E.M) was only reported, standard deviation (S.D) was calculated by S.D = S.E.M × √n (n is the number of participants in each group). Finally, When data were only presented in graphical formats, figures were digitized using WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.6) to extract numerical values. The extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. For studies where data required for effect size calculation were missing or unclear, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors via email up to two times to request the necessary statistical information83,84.

Estimates of effect sizes were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a random-effects model. A random-effects model was selected as the primary analytical approach to account for potential between-study variability arising from differences in populations, interventions, and measurement instruments across studies. This model assumes that the true effect size may vary between studies and incorporates both within-study and between-study variance in the weighting process. Studies were weighted using the inverse of their variance, with additional adjustment for between-study heterogeneity.

In addition to the random-effects model, a Robust Variance Estimation (RVE) approach was also applied to account for potential dependencies within studies. The RVE method, performed using the robumeta command in Stata, considered the correlation between effect sizes within studies (with an assumed correlation of 0.8). This method provides more accurate standard errors for the estimated effect sizes by adjusting for dependencies between effect sizes within studies. The RVE analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the results and confirm that the findings were consistent across different methodological approaches.

Furthermore, to explicitly account for the hierarchical structure of the data and the dependency introduced by multiple time points within the same trial, we conducted a three-level multilevel meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. This model partitioned the total variance into between-study variance and within-study (time-level) variance, enabling us to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates and determine whether repeated measurements significantly impacted overall heterogeneity.

Pre-defined subgroup analysis based on country, sample size, sample type, age, therapeutic approach, CA type, and outcome timing was conducted to detect potential sources of heterogeneity. The sensitivity analysis was conducted by the one-study remove (leave-one-out) approach, to explore the impact of each study on the pooled effect size. Meta-regression was performed to examine whether the timing of outcome assessment moderated the observed effect sizes. Timing of outcome assessment was defined as the duration (in weeks) between the end of the intervention and the measurement of outcomes. When multiple follow-up time points were reported within a study (e.g., 4 weeks, 8 weeks, 16 weeks), each time point was extracted and included as a separate effect size.

Publication bias was evaluated using visual assessment of funnel plots and Egger’s weighted regression tests. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software version 21 (StataCorp. College Station, Texas, USA). P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
