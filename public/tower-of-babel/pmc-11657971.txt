Plasmodium falciparum and Schistosoma mansoni coinfections among the general population in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abebaw Setegn, Gashaw Azanaw Amare, Wagaw Abebe, Wassie Alemayehu Damtie, Gebremariam Wulie Geremew, Abaynesh Fentahun Bekalu, Tekletsadik Tekleslassie Alemayehu, Fentahun Megabiaw, Yenesew Mihret Wondmagegn, Zufan Yiheyis Abriham, Yilkal Abebaw Wassie, Getasew Kibralew, Getu Girmay, Muluneh Assefa, Berihun Agegn Mengistie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the overlapping endemic regions and the high burden of both infections, coinfection with Plasmodium falciparum and Schistosoma mansoni poses distinct public health concerns that require coordinated, multifaceted interventions. There are epidemiological studies on the coinfection of these two parasites in Ethiopia, and the results differ and are inconsistent from one region to another. Thus, the goal of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to ascertain Ethiopia’s pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection.MethodsThe preferred reporting item review and meta-analysis guidelines were followed in this study. PubMed, Google Scholar, Research4Life, Scopus, African-Wider, and EMBASE were the primary search databases. The final analysis included six studies in total. Stata software version 11 was used for analysis after Microsoft Excel was used to extract the data. The critical appraisal tool developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the studies. Publication bias was checked via a funnel plot, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test. The pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection was estimated via a random effect model via the Der Simonian–Laird method. The heterogeneity of the studies was evaluated via I2 statistical tests.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections in Ethiopia was 10.496% (95% CI 6.134, 14.859). Subgroup analysis of geopolitical regions revealed that the highest and lowest pooled prevalence rates of coinfection were 11.808% (95% CI 0.304, 23.312) and 8.600% (95% CI 5.755, 11.445), respectively, in the Amhara and Benishangul Gumuz regions.ConclusionsThis study highlights the substantial prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection in Ethiopia’s general population, with considerable geographical variation. Targeted and intensive public health interventions are necessary because it has the highest incidence in the Amhara region, and ongoing efforts are needed to address the relatively lower but still considerable prevalence in the Benishangul Gumuz region. These results direct future research, integrated control strategy design, and resource allocation to successfully manage Ethiopia’s combined burden of malaria and schistosomiasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-024-05192-7.Keywords: P. falciparum, S. mansoni, Coinfection, General population, Ethiopia, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

Malaria is one of the most significant health issues in developing nations. Approximately 50% of the population on the planet is thought to be at risk of malaria [1, 2]. The genus Plasmodium is the cause of this complex and fatal parasitic disease. Globally, Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax are the predominant species responsible for most of the disease burden [3] According to the most recent World Malaria Report, in contrast to 244 million cases in 2021, there were 249 million cases of malaria in 2022. Malaria deaths are predicted to have globally decreased to 608,000 in 2022 from 610,000 in 2021 [4].

The burden of malaria worldwide is still disproportionately heavy in the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region. Approximately 94% of all malaria cases and 95% of deaths in 2022 occurred in the region. The majority of malaria deaths in the region (78% of all deaths) were in children under 5 years of age. Just over half of all malaria deaths globally occurred in four African countries: Mozambique (4.2%), Uganda (5.1%), Nigeria (26.8%), and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (12.3%) [4].

Individuals who are infected with malaria display mild clinical signs, which can range from minor symptoms such as fever, increased body temperature, perspiration, shaking, vomiting, and extreme headache in simple cases to serious sequelae, including acute renal failure, hypoglycaemia, and severe anaemia [5].

Five species of Schistosoma are the cause of schistosomiasis, a neglected tropical disease that is second only to malaria [6]. These parasitic worms are members of the genus Schistosoma; in sub-Saharan Africa, the species Schistosoma mansoni, which causes intestinal schistosomiasis, and Schistosoma haematobium, which causes urogenital schistosomiasis, are the main forms of human schistosomiasis [7]. Anaemia, developmental retardation (frequent in school-age children), dysuria, and nutritional deficiencies are a few of the signs and illnesses of people with infections caused by either S. haematobium or S. mansoni [8], bladder cancer, pelvic pain, haematuria, hepatosplenomegaly, and infertility (in extended cases in females) [9].

In Africa's tropical and subtropical regions, malaria and schistosomiasis are still amongst the leading causes of morbidity and death [10]. Because of the ideal environment and water bodies that act as intermediate hosts and vector breeding grounds for P. falciparum and S. mansoni, coinfection of these two parasites is common [11].

The prevalence of coinfections with malaria and schistosomiasis is 15% in Ethiopia; in contrast to individuals with malaria alone, schistosomiasis significantly increases the prevalence of anaemia [12]. Prior studies revealed an argumentative connection between coinfections with malaria and helminths [13]. Although intestinal schistosomiasis and malaria appear to be antagonistic, the way the two interact may vary depending on the age of the afflicted individuals and the intensity of the eggs that S. mansoni lays [14]. A study in humans revealed that coinfection with high-intensity schistosomiasis and heavy Plasmodium parasitaemia are highly correlated [15].

Information on the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum–S. mansoni coinfection is needed to improve clinical management, control strategies, and research guidance, standardize diagnostic and treatment protocols, understand epidemiological trends, increase public awareness, and monitor and evaluate health interventions, especially in coendemic areas of malaria and schistosomiasis. Furthermore, published statistics on the pooled prevalence of coinfection in Ethiopia with P. falciparum and S. mansoni are lacking. Therefore, the main objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to estimate the current pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections among the general population in Ethiopia.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

All longitudinal studies (cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort) that reported the prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection among the general population in Ethiopia and were published in peer-reviewed journals were included. Article searching was not restricted by publication year; however, only English-language full-text articles were considered for review. All participants regardless of age or sex, who were coinfected with P. falciparum and S. mansoni were included. Studies that had prevalence data on P. falciparum with S. mansoni coinfection were also included.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria included duplicate reporting, insufficient data of the outcome variable for extraction, and articles with missing outcomes of interest (prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection or adjusted odds ratio at 95% CI). Findings from personal opinions, editorial reports, letters to the editors, systematic reviews, and qualitative studies were excluded. In addition, studies with coinfection prevalence of P. falciparum with other Schistosoma species were not included in this review.

Study protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement. The search strategy and selection of studies for the review were conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Additional file 1). The study protocol has been registered with the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Reference number: CRD 42024541973).

Search strategy

Published data on the prevalence of coinfections between P. falciparum and S. mansoni in Ethiopia were used to conduct this systemic review and meta-analysis. The main databases used to search for peer-reviewed journal articles included PubMed, Google Scholar, Research4Life, Scopus, African-Wider, and EMBASE. Articles that met the eligibility criteria were retrieved from a list of studies. A search strategy was established for each database by using a keyword and combining it with Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”). The following medical subject heading (MeSH) and/or text words in any field, “Plasmodium falciparum” OR “P. falciparum” AND “Intestinal schistosomiasis” OR “Bilharziasis” OR “Neglected tropical disease (NTD)” OR “Schistosoma mansoni” OR “S. mansoni” AND “Coinfection” OR “concomitant” OR “mixed infection” OR “concurrent” AND “general population” AND “Ethiopia” combination rules, were used to obtain relevant articles. A slight modification was made to the search strategy based on the requirements of various databases. The references of retrieved articles were also checked for any additional studies.

Study selection

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, all the searched articles were transferred to Endnote × 9, a reference management software, to remove duplicate studies. After duplications were removed, two reviewers independently read the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles to identify both potentially eligible articles and any articles for which the determination could not be made from the title and abstract only. The selected full-text articles were subsequently examined for eligibility.

Quality appraisal

The quality of the included articles in this review was assessed via the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical assessment checklist for cross-sectional studies [16]. This quality assessment checklist comprises nine items, ranging from 0–9 (0–4—low, 5–7—medium, and 8 and above good quality). The final analysis was based on articles of medium and high quality (greater than or equal to 5) (Additional file 2).

Outcome measures

The prevalence of coinfections between P. falciparum and S. mansoni in Ethiopia was the main outcome that was measured. The studies included in this review described the species of Schistosoma involved (S. mansoni) with P. falciparum coinfection. The prevalence was determined by dividing the number of individuals with both P. falciparum and S. mansoni infection by the total number of individuals included in the study (sample size) and multiplying by 100.

Data extraction

Two authors (AS and BAM) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the published articles according to the inclusion criteria. These two authors then independently extracted information from each of the included studies via an Excel spreadsheet. The data extraction format included the author’s name, publication year, study area (classified on the basis of the geopolitical regions), sample size, study design, and prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection. Disagreements between the two authors were resolved by other reviewers or authors (GAA and WA). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was performed to determine how reliable the meta-analysis results are.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All the statistical data analyses were carried out via STATA statistical software (version 11) after the retrieved data were exported. After that, texts, tables, and forest plots were used to display the data. The standard error of the prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection for each primary or original study was analysed via binomial distribution. The Higgins I-square (I2) test was used to evaluate the heterogeneity of the original studies. Higgins I-square values of 25%, 50%–75%, and above 75% were used to classify the computed values of I2 heterogeneity among the included studies as low, moderate, or high, respectively.

A random-effects meta-analysis model with Der Simonian and Laird’s method was used to determine the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections in the Ethiopian context. In addition, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the potential cause of heterogeneity in the pooled meta-analysis. The pooled estimates of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were then displayed via forest plots and tables.

Funnel plots were also used to evaluate the presence of publication bias; a large, symmetrical inverted funnel indicated a lower likelihood of publication bias. Graphic assessment was supported by statistical regression methods such as Egger's test, and publication bias was deemed statistically significant if the p-value was less than 5%.

Subgroup analysis and heterogeneity

Subgroup analysis was carried out to compare the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection among the general population in geopolitical regions of Ethiopia. A random-effects model was employed to perform a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of a specific study on the combined prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection.

Results

Study selection and characteristics of the included studies

Overall, 2330 studies were retrieved through all database searches, involving manual searches; 1648 duplicates were removed; the remaining 682 studies were eligible for screening; 668 studies were excluded based on the title and abstract screening from the remaining 14 full articles; and 8 studies were removed (3 due to insufficient data for extraction, 5 not related to the outcome variable). Finally, 6 eligible studies were included in the final meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study selection for meta-analysis of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections among the general population in Ethiopia

A total of 6 eligible published articles from three different regions of Ethiopia with a total of 2683 study participants were included in this meta-analysis. Concerning the distribution of the studies across the geopolitical regions, three were conducted in the Amhara region, two in the Oromia region, and the remaining one in the Benishangul Gumuz region (Table 1).

Heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

The pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection differed significantly among studies (heterogeneity I2 = 94.1%, p-value = 0.00). Thus, to evaluate possible sources of variability, a subgroup analysis was performed in the geopolitical regions. Consequently, the highest and lowest pooled prevalence rates of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection were 11.808% (95% CI 0.304, 23.312) and 8.600% (95% CI: 5.755, 11.445), respectively, in the Amhara and Benishangul Gumuz regions (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was used to assess the heterogeneity of the included studies and ascertain how each study’s findings affected the pooled prevalence of coinfection between P. falciparum and S. mansoni in this meta-analysis. The findings showed that all values were within the estimated 95% confidence interval, indicating that a leave-one-out analysis had no significant effect on the combined estimate of this meta-analysis (Table 3).

Discussion

Malaria can occur in association with schistosomes such as S. mansoni and S. haematobium, which are endemic helminths in tropical areas, especially in Africa and East Asia, and coinfections have been associated mostly with P. falciparum [17].

Coinfection can result from similar life cycles and environmental conditions that make it easier for malaria and helminths to spread. For example, populations near water sources where Anopheles species reproduce are more likely to have malaria; this may be because these sources which many people can exploit for household use or consumption; contain helminth eggs and larvae [18].

Furthermore, several factors, including living circumstances and the concentration of impoverished people in places where both infections are widespread, may increase the incidence of coinfection, especially in nations with low resources such as Ethiopia. Thus, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections in Ethiopia's general population.

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections among the general population in Ethiopia was 10.496% (95% CI 6.134, 14.859).

This result was higher than the results of a systematic review and meta-analysis, which reported that the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections was 6.7% among children living in different endemic regions [19], 4.3% in Colombia [18], and 3.4% in Nigeria [20]. A mixture of biological, healthcare, social, and environmental factors could be responsible for this disparity. The varied topography and climate of Ethiopia provide favourable circumstances for P. falciparum and S. mansoni [21, 22].

Higher rates of coinfection are caused by the existence of freshwater bodies, which are essential to the life cycle of S. mansoni snails and provide breeding sites for Anopheles mosquitoes [23]. Additionally, many rural communities depend on rivers, lakes, and ponds for daily activities such as washing, fishing, and bathing, which increases exposure to both parasites. Additionally, the risk of schistosomiasis is increased by frequent contact with potentially polluted water sources because of agricultural practices, particularly in rural and low-income areas, with limited access to clean water and inadequate sanitation facilities [24].

Moreover, the migration and movement of people within Ethiopia, particularly from nonendemic areas to endemic areas and vice versa, as well as densely populated areas with inadequate infrastructure, are more likely to lead to higher rates of transmission for both schistosomiasis and malaria, which can aid in the spread of both infections [25].

However, the findings of this study were lower than those of cross-sectional studies conducted in different East African countries. The incidence of coinfection was 19.3% among school-age children in Kenya [26], 71.0% among children aged 10–15 years in Uganda [27], 23.51% among school-aged children in Uganda [28], 22.6% among school-aged children in Tanzania [29], 22.4% among school-aged children in Tanzania [30], and 27.2% among school-aged and preschool children in Tanzania [31].

This lower pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections among the general population in Ethiopia than that reported in cross-sectional studies among schoolchildren in East African nations such as Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania could be attributed to several factors.

The distributions of the parasites S. mansoni and P. falciparum may differ within and between nations. The ecological niches of Ethiopia may not overlap much with those of Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, the other countries where these parasites are found [32, 33].

Compared with the general population, school-aged children are frequently more susceptible to parasitic infections because of increased exposure in school settings and possibly weakened immunity, which comprises adults with more mature immune systems, a wider age range, and perhaps different exposure risks that might lead to lower prevalence rates [34]. Additionally, the methodologies of the studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis differ from those used in cross-sectional studies. The reported prevalence rates of coinfection may vary depending on the sample size and diagnostic methods employed.

There was a significant heterogeneity (I2 = 94.1%, p value = 0.000) across the studies included in this meta-analysis. Accordingly, a subgroup analysis based on geopolitical regions revealed that the Amhara region had the highest prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection (11.808%; 95% CI 0.304, 23.312), whereas the Benishangul Gumuz region had the lowest prevalence (8.600%; 95% CI 5.755, 11.445).

This discrepancy might be explained by the potential abundance of water bodies and irrigation systems in the Amhara region, which provide ideal habitats for snails that act as intermediate hosts of S. mansoni. These water bodies also support the breeding of mosquitoes that transmit P. falciparum [35].

Standing water is a common feature of agricultural practices, such as rice farming, which also increases the risk of S. mansoni and P. falciparum coinfections. Comparing the Amhara region to the Benishangul Gumuz region, there might be a greater prevalence of these farming practices. Moreover, cultural practices and behaviours that increase contact with water bodies or expose individuals to mosquito bites can influence infection rates [36].

Strengths of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis was the first report on P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfections in Ethiopia’s general population. The review’s strengths include a thorough search of several databases and other sources to find relevant studies on the general population infected by both P. falciparum and S. mansoni as well as data generation for policymakers to adjust or enhance current control and preventive measures.

Study limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis have shortcomings. Primary studies conducted in different countries were used to discuss the findings due to lack of relevant systematic reviews which makes impossible to determine the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection among the general population. There was a substantial degree of heterogeneity across the included studies in this review. Nevertheless, Egger’s test was unable to reach statistical significance; therefore, publication bias cannot be completely ruled out.

Conclusion

In this review, the pooled prevalence of P. falciparum and S. mansoni coinfection in Ethiopia’s general population highlights a serious public health issue that calls for effective management and reduction of the burden of these infections through targeted resource allocation, ongoing research, and robust policy development. Moreover, focused interventions such as mass drug administration (MDA) for schistosomiasis and improved malaria control strategies such as indoor residual spraying (IRS) and insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) might be beneficial for areas with high coinfection prevalence.
