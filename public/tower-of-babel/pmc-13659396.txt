The maturation of precision medicine into pragmatic, patient‐centered care: A commentary on the 2026 EHA CLL Guidelines
Authors: Stefano Molica, Talha Munir, David Allsup
Published: 2026

The 2026 EHA Guidelines for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) mark a paradigm shift in the disease management moving beyond the novel introduction of targeted agents toward a highly refined, individualized approach to clinical decision‐making. 1 Rather than merely endorsing specific regimens, their true value lies in introducing a holistic framework that seamlessly harmonizes disease biology, patient profiles, clinical feasibility, and personal preferences into a unified therapeutic strategy. 1

One of the most notable conceptual advances in the 2026 EHA Guidelines is the replacement of the traditional term “watch and wait” with “active surveillance.” 1 While seemingly semantic, this change reflects a broader shift in the management of early CLL management. Rather than implying therapeutic passivity, “active surveillance” emphasizes proactive interventions, including vaccination, infection prevention, secondary cancer screening, and optimization of comorbidities. 1 This reframing acknowledges that morbidity and mortality in CLL are influenced not only by disease progression but also by immune dysfunction, infectious complications, and age‐related comorbidities. 2 Consequently, the pre‐treatment window should be conceptualized not as an interval of clinical latency, but as a dynamic phase of proactive, preventive intervention. 1

Historically, CLL management focused on achieving durable disease control through continuous therapy or repeated treatment cycles. 3 Today, the emphasis increasingly lies on attaining deep remissions that allow treatment discontinuation without compromising long‐term outcomes. 4 Venetoclax‐based combinations have become central to this strategy. Cumulative evidence from long‐term follow‐ups of the CLL14 trial, integrated with robust data from the CLL13, GLOW, CAPTIVATE, FLAIR, AMPLIFY, and CLL17 trials, substantiates the efficacy of fixed‐duration regimens. These approaches consistently achieve prolonged progression‐free survival (PFS) and high rates of undetectable measurable residual disease (uMRD), ultimately enabling sustained treatment‐free intervals (TFIs). 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Importantly, the absence of a definitive superiority signal to date for continuous therapy over time‐limited regimens bolsters the clinical rationale for prioritizing finite treatment strategies where clinically feasible. 11

Rather than reducing therapeutic selection to a binary comparison of competing efficacy curves, the 2026 EHA Guidelines for CLL adopt a more holistic paradigm. A central contribution of this framework is the explicit recognition that precision medicine in CLL extends far beyond genomic and molecular profiling. 1 While genomic determinants—principally TP53 aberrations and IGHV mutational status—remain fundamental to prognostic risk stratification, the real‐world viability of any therapeutic strategy is heavily contingent upon patient‐specific clinical variables. 1

This paradigm is underscored by a rigorous focus on comorbidities and polypharmacy. 1 Specifically, mandating systematic cardiovascular assessments prior to BTK inhibitor initiation and baseline renal evaluations before venetoclax‐based therapy reflects a growing recognition that host‐specific factors dictate clinical outcomes. 2 Similarly, prioritizing drug–drug interactions addresses the clinical realities of managing an aging, medically complex population. 12 Crucially, the integration of practical decision‐support tools—such as treatment “heat maps”—operationalizes these multidimensional variables, translating complex guidelines into routine clinical practice. 1

In many respects, the guidelines implicitly redefine the concept of optimal therapy. Rather than pursuing maximal efficacy in isolation, they advocate for maximizing net clinical benefit. This distinction is particularly relevant in real‐world settings, where treatment adherence, monitoring capacity, toxicity management, and patient preferences often determine success as much as biological activity. 1 , 13 Consequently, a regimen demonstrating marginally lower efficacy in clinical trials may emerge as the optimal clinical choice if it offers a superior safety profile, a reduced monitoring burden, or closer alignment with individual patient values and lifestyle. This approach marks a critical transition from a purely biologically driven model of precision oncology toward a pragmatic, patient‐centric framework of personalized care. 1

The complexity of modern CLL management becomes even more apparent in the relapsed and refractory setting. 1 Here, the guidelines emphasize mechanism‐based sequencing and formally recognize clinically distinct categories such as single‐refractory, double‐exposed, and double‐refractory disease. 1 While targeted therapies have revolutionized outcomes in early‐line settings, they have concurrently generated a novel patient cohort characterized by double‐refractory disease exhausted of both BTK‐ and BCL2‐inhibitor pathways. 2 For this high‐risk population, non‐covalent BTK inhibitors such as pirtobrutinib offer promising therapeutic avenues. 14 Nonetheless, clinical guidelines heavily prioritize enrollment in clinical trials, underscoring that novel investigational therapeutics remain vital to addressing this formidable clinical challenge. 1

A similar theme emerges in the section devoted to Richter transformation. 1 In this setting, the guidelines advocate for a structured diagnostic algorithm incorporating positron emission tomography (PET)‐computed tomography (CT), histological confirmation, and determination of clonal relatedness, while concurrently acknowledging the efficacy limitations of conventional standard‐of‐care regimens. 1 Accordingly, the pronounced emphasis on novel immunotherapies, non‐covalent BTK inhibitors, bispecific antibodies, CAR T‐cell therapies, and clinical trial enrollment underscores a consensus that improving outcomes in this ultra‐high‐risk cohort remains contingent upon ongoing therapeutic innovation. 1

Importantly, the incorporation of the ESMO Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO‐MCBS) provides a broader perspective on value‐based care. 1 In an era characterized by multiple effective but costly therapeutic options, the challenge is no longer simply identifying active treatments but determining which interventions deliver the greatest meaningful benefit to patients. 15 By systematically integrating efficacy, toxicity, quality of life, and treatment burden, the ESMO‐MCBS bolsters the patient‐centered framework of these guidelines. This multidimensional evaluation reinforces the paradigm that clinical benefit in CLL must be measured using endpoints of mutual significance to both patients and clinicians. 1 , 15

Ultimately, the 2026 EHA Guidelines illustrate the maturation of CLL management into a truly multidimensional discipline (Figure 1). Their central message is that optimal treatment cannot be determined by a single biomarker, a single clinical trial, or a single efficacy endpoint. 1 The future of CLL care will undoubtedly continue to be shaped by new drugs and emerging technologies, but the lasting contribution of these guidelines may be their recognition that precision medicine is most effective when it is also pragmatic, individualized, and firmly centered on the needs and circumstances of the patient.

Conceptual framework of the 2026 EHA Guidelines for chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), illustrating a shift toward therapeutic maturity. The framework prioritizes active surveillance, multidimensional clinical decision‐making, and value‐based care to optimize patient outcomes and maximize clinical utility.

Future CLL guidelines should incorporate structured prehabilitation across the continuum of care. Proactively mitigating modifiable physical, nutritional, and emotional risk factors prior to and during therapy optimizes patient functional capacity, treatment tolerability, and overall quality of life.
