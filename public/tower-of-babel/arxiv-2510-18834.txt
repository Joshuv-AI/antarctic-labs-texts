Testing Risk Difference of Two Proportions for Combined Unilateral and Bilateral Data
Jia Zhou, Chang-Xing Ma

Abstract
In clinical studies with paired organs, binary outcomes often exhibit intra-subject correlation and may include a mixture of unilateral and bilateral observations. Under Donner’s constant correlation model, we develop three likelihood-based test statistics (the likelihood ratio, Wald-type, and score tests) for assessing the risk difference between two proportions. Simulation studies demonstrate good control of type I error and comparable power among the three tests, with the score test showing slightly better stability. Applications to otolaryngologic and ophthalmologic data illustrate the methods. An online calculator is also provided for power analysis and risk difference testing. The score test is recommended for practical use and future studies with combined unilateral and bilateral binary data. Keywords: Combined unilateral and bilateral data; Donner’s ρ \rho model; Risk difference; Likelihood ratio test; Wald-type test; Score test



1
Introduction

In comparative clinical studies, binary outcomes are commonly used to assess treatment efficacy or disease occurrence, where each observation represents a dichotomous event such as success/failure or cured/uncured. In many medical applications, particularly those involving paired organs, such as eyes and ears, responses are measured bilaterally. In practice, however, datasets often contain a mixture of unilateral and bilateral observations, as some subjects contribute paired organ data while others provide only a single measurement. Such imbalance may arise due to prior surgical removal, congenital absence, or missing measurements in one organ. Analyzing these combined unilateral and bilateral data jointly, rather than excluding unilateral cases, is essential to avoid loss of information and to reduce potential bias in statistical inference.

A key challenge in analyzing bilateral outcomes lies in appropriately accounting for the intra-subject correlation between paired organs. Several statistical models have been proposed to handle this correlation. Rosner
[
1
]
introduced the “constant
R
” model, which assumes equal dependence between paired organs within an individual and tests for equality of proportions across treatment groups. Dallal
[
2
]
identified limitations of Rosner’s model when the marginal prevalences differ substantially between groups and proposed an alternative model assuming a constant conditional probability of response in one organ given the other. Donner
[
3
]
later developed a constant correlation model that parameterizes the intra-subject dependence through a single correlation coefficient
\rho
, which Thompson
[
4
]
demonstrated to be robust through simulation studies. Moreover, Clayton
[
5
]
proposed a general model for association in bivariate data using the Clayton copula, which expresses the joint distribution of two variables in terms of their marginal cumulative distribution functions and a dependence parameter. Although originally developed for bivariate survival or event-time data, the Clayton copula model can be extended to correlated binary outcomes and has been applied in paired organ studies to capture intra-subject dependence
[
6
,
7
]
.

Building on these correlation structures, numerous likelihood-based methods have been developed for inference with correlated binary data. For instance, Tang
et al.

[
8
]
and Pei
et al.

[
9
]
proposed asymptotic methods for testing equality of proportions between two groups. Ma and others
[
10
,
11
]
investigated three asymptotic tests (likelihood ratio, Wald-type and score) for testing homogeneity of proportions among
g\geq 2
groups. Other studies have considered relative measures, such as relative risk and odds ratio, and their interval estimations have been studied under different correlation models, with or without stratification (e.g., see
[
12
,
13
]
).

While tests for equality of proportions have been extensively studied, practical research questions often concern the difference in risk (or proportion) between two treatment groups, referred to as the
risk difference
. Compared with relative measures such as odds ratio or relative risk, the risk difference provides a more direct and interpretable measure of treatment effect, especially in clinical and public health contexts where absolute risk reduction is of primary interest
[
14
]
. It also forms the basis for important inferential quantities such as the number needed to treat (NNT)
[
15
,
16
]
. Several studies have examined inference procedures for the risk difference in correlated binary data. Pei
et al.

[
17
]
compared three methods (profile likelihood, asymptotic score and Wald-type with three estimated variances) for constructing confidence intervals for differences in proportions in a two-arm randomized trial. More recently, Sun
et al.

[
18
]
studied various confidence intervals of common risk difference in stratified unilateral and bilateral data based on the Dallal’s model. In this paper, we focus on testing the risk difference between two proportions under Donner’s constant
\rho
model. We derive three likelihood-based test statistics: the likelihood ratio, Wald-type, and score tests, respectively, to evaluate their performance through extensive simulation studies. To facilitate practical implementation, we also develop an online calculator for power analysis and hyptohesis testing using user-supplied data.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents the construction of the three likelihood-based test statistics under Donner’s model. Section
3
reports numerical results, including simulation studies (Section
3.1
) evaluating empirical type I error rates and powers, and two real-world applications in otolaryngologic and ophthalmologic studies (Section
3.2
). Section
4
concludes the paper.

The proportion of organs that are cured (or affected) in the

Pr\left(Z_{ijk}=1\right)=\pi_{i}
. Under Donner’s constant
\rho
model
[
3
]
, the intra-subject correlation satisfies
{\mathrm{Corr}}\left(Z_{ijk},Z_{ij,3-k}\right)=\rho
. Therefore, given
m_{+i}
and
n_{+i}
in the
i
-th group, the counts
\left(m_{0i},m_{1i},m_{2i}\right)
follow a trinomial distribution, and
\left(n_{0i},n_{1i}\right)
follow a binomial distribution,

\left(m_{0i},m_{1i},m_{2i}\right)\sim Trinomial\left(m_{+i},p_{0i},p_{1i},p_{2i}\right),\quad n_{1i}\sim Binomial\left(n_{+i},\pi_{i}\right),

(1)

where the joint probabilities
p_{ri}
’s (
r=0,1,2
) are given by

\displaystyle p_{2i}=Pr\left(Z_{ij1}=1,Z_{ij2}=1\right)=E\left(Z_{ij1}Z_{ij2}\right)=\pi_{i}\left(\pi_{i}+\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\rho\right),

(2)

\displaystyle p_{1i}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}Pr\left(Z_{ijk}=1,Z_{ij,3-k}=0\right)=2\left(\pi_{i}-p_{2i}\right)=2\pi_{i}\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\left(1-\rho\right),

\displaystyle p_{0i}=Pr\left(Z_{ij1}=0,Z_{ij2}=0\right)=1-p_{1i}-p_{2i}=\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\left(1-\pi_{i}+\pi_{i}~\rho\right).

Consequently, the log-likelihood as a function of
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\rho\right)^{T}
can be written as

\displaystyle\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\bigg\{m_{0i}\log\left[\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\left(1-\pi_{i}+\pi_{i}\rho\right)\right]+m_{1i}\log\left[2\pi_{i}\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\left(1-\rho\right)\right]

(3)

\displaystyle+m_{2i}\log\left[\pi_{i}\left(\pi_{i}+\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\rho\right)\right]+n_{0i}\log\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)+n_{1i}\log\left(\pi_{i}\right)\bigg\}+\text{const},

where the term ‘const” denotes a constant that depends only on the counts
m_{ri}
and
n_{ri}
.

Likewise, by reparameterizing in terms of the risk difference
\delta=\pi_{2}-\pi_{1}
, the log-likelihood as a function of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta,\pi_{1},\rho\right)^{T}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\ell_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)=

\displaystyle m_{01}\log\left[\left(1-\pi_{1}\right)\left(1-\pi_{1}\left(1-\rho\right)\right)\right]+m_{02}\log\left[\left(1-\delta-\pi_{1}\right)\left(1-\left(\delta+\pi_{1}\right)\left(1-\rho\right)\right)\right]

(4)

\displaystyle+m_{11}\log\left[2\pi_{1}\left(1-\pi_{1}\right)\left(1-\rho\right)\right]+m_{12}\log\left[2\left(\delta+\pi_{1}\right)\left(1-\delta-\pi_{1}\right)\left(1-\rho\right)\right]

\displaystyle+m_{21}\log\left[\pi_{1}\left(\pi_{1}+\left(1-\pi_{1}\right)\rho\right)\right]+m_{22}\log\left[\left(\delta+\pi_{1}\right)\left(\delta+\pi_{1}+\left(1-\delta-\pi_{1}\right)\rho\right)\right]

\displaystyle+n_{01}\log\left(1-\pi_{1}\right)+n_{02}\log\left(1-\delta-\pi_{1}\right)+n_{11}\log\left(\pi_{1}\right)+n_{12}\log\left(\delta+\pi_{1}\right)+\text{const}.

Our primary interest is to test whether the risk difference between the two groups equals a pre-specified constant. Formally, the hypotheses are

H_{0}:~\delta=\delta_{0}\quad\text{vs}\quad H_{1}:~\delta\neq\delta_{0},

(5)

for some given constant
\delta_{0}
.

In what follows, we derive the unconstrained maximum likelihood estimates (MLEs) of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta,\pi_{1},\rho\right)^{T}
, as well as the constrained MLEs of
\left(\pi_{1},\rho\right)
under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\delta=\delta_{0}
. Based on these estimates, we then construct three likelihood-based test statistics (likelihood ratio, Wald-type and score) to assess the hypothesis.

2.1
Maximum Likelihood Estimates

2.1.1
Unconstrained MLEs

The unconstrained MLEs of
\left(\delta,\pi_{1},\rho\right)
can be derived from the global MLEs of
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\rho\right)^{T}
by using
\hat{\delta}=\hat{\pi}_{2}-\hat{\pi}_{1}
.
The global MLEs of
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\rho\right)^{T}
can be obtained by solving the following normal equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\pi_{i}}=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{0+}\left(2\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{i}+\rho-2\right)}{\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)\left(1-\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{i}\right)}+\frac{m_{1+}\left(1-2\pi_{i}\right)}{\pi_{i}\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)}+\frac{m_{2+}\left(2\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{i}+\rho\right)}{\pi_{i}\left(\rho\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)+\pi_{i}\right)}

(6a)

\displaystyle-\frac{n_{0+}}{1-\pi_{i}}+\frac{n_{1+}}{\pi_{i}}=0,\quad\left(i=1,2\right),

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\frac{m_{0i}\pi_{i}}{1-\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{i}}+\frac{m_{2i}\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)}{\rho\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)+\pi_{i}}\right]-\frac{m_{1+}}{1-\rho}=0.

(6b)

The equation (
6a
) leads to a cubic equation

a_{i}\pi_{i}^{3}+b_{i}\pi_{i}^{2}+c_{i}\pi_{i}+d_{i}=0,

(7)

for
i=1,2
, where

\displaystyle a_{i}=\left(1-\rho\right)^{2}\left(2m_{+i}+n_{+i}\right),

\displaystyle b_{i}=\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\left(3\rho-2\right)m_{0i}+3\left(\rho-1\right)m_{1i}+\left(3\rho-4\right)m_{2i}+\left(\rho-1\right)n_{0i}+2\left(\rho-1\right)n_{1i}\right),

\displaystyle c_{i}=\rho\left(\rho-2\right)m_{0i}+\left(\left(\rho\left(\rho-4\right)+1\right)m_{1i}+\left(\rho\left(\rho-4\right)+2\right)m_{2i}+\left(\rho\left(\rho-3\right)+1\right)n_{1i}\right),

\displaystyle d_{i}=\rho\left(m_{1i}+m_{2i}+n_{1i}\right).

It can be shown that the analytic root for the cubic equation (
7
)
[
11
]
,

\hat{\pi}_{i}=\frac{-b_{i}+2\sqrt{\Delta_{0}}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{3}-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)}{3a_{i}},

(8)

yields the maximum of the log-likelihood in (
3
) for a given
\rho
, where

\theta=\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{\Delta_{1}}{2\Delta_{0}^{3/2}}\right),

and

\Delta_{0}=b_{i}^{2}-3a_{i}c_{i},\quad\Delta_{1}=2b_{i}^{3}-9a_{i}b_{i}c_{i}+27a_{i}^{2}d_{i}.

The global MLEs of
\pi_{i}
and
\rho
can be obtained through the iterative procedure outlined as follows.

1.

Update

\pi_{i}
: at the
t
-th step, for a given
\hat{\rho}^{(t)}
, compute
\hat{\pi}_{i}^{(t)}
using equation (
8
).

2.

Update

\rho
: at the
\left(t+1\right)
-th step, update
\hat{\rho}^{(t+1)}
via the Newton-Raphson method:

\hat{\rho}^{(t+1)}=\hat{\rho}^{(t)}+\left(\left.-\frac{\partial^{2}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)}{\partial\rho^{2}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}}\right)^{-1}\left.\frac{\partial\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)}{\partial\rho}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}},

where
\partial\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)/\partial\rho
corresponds to the middle expression in equation (
6b
), and
\partial^{2}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)/\partial\rho^{2}
is the associated Hessian term and takes the following form

\frac{\partial^{2}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)}{\partial\rho^{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[-\frac{m_{0i}\pi_{i}^{2}}{\left(1-\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{i}\right)^{2}}+\frac{m_{1i}}{\left(1-\rho\right)^{2}}+\frac{m_{2i}\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)^{2}}{\left(\rho\left(1-\pi_{i}\right)+\pi_{i}\right)^{2}}\right].

3.

Check convergence
: repeat
Steps 1 - 2
until
\hat{\rho}
converges, as measured by

\delta^{(t+1)}=\left|\hat{\rho}^{(t+1)}-\hat{\rho}^{(t)}\right|.

The iteration stops when
\delta^{(t+1)}<\delta_{c}
for a sufficiently small tolerance, such as
\delta_{c}=10^{-6}
.

After obtaining
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, the MLE of the risk difference
\delta
is
\hat{\delta}=\hat{\pi}_{2}-\hat{\pi}_{1}
.

2.1.2
Constrained MLEs

The constrained MLEs of
\pi_{1}
and
\rho
under
H_{0}:~\delta=\delta_{0}
can be obtained by solving the following normal equations,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\pi_{1}}\right|_{\delta=\delta_{0}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=

\displaystyle-\frac{m_{01}+m_{11}+n_{01}}{1-\pi_{1}}+\frac{m_{11}+m_{21}+n_{11}}{\pi_{1}}-\frac{m_{02}+m_{12}+n_{02}}{1-\delta_{0}-\pi_{1}}+\frac{m_{12}+m_{22}+n_{12}}{\delta_{0}+\pi_{1}}

(9a)

\displaystyle-\frac{m_{01}\left(1-\rho\right)}{1-\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{1}}+\frac{m_{21}\left(1-\rho\right)}{\rho+\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{1}}-\frac{m_{02}\left(1-\rho\right)}{1-\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\delta_{0}+\pi_{1}\right)}+\frac{m_{22}\left(1-\rho\right)}{\rho+\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\delta_{0}+\pi_{1}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\rho}\right|_{\delta=\delta_{0}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\rho}\left(\frac{m_{01}}{1-\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{1}}+\frac{m_{21}}{\rho+\left(1-\rho\right)\pi_{1}}+\frac{m_{02}}{1-\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\delta_{0}+\pi_{1}\right)}+\frac{m_{22}}{\rho+\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\delta_{0}+\pi_{1}\right)}\right)

(9b)

\displaystyle-\frac{m_{++}}{1-\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Since there is no closed-form solution for
\pi_{1}
or
\rho
from equations (
9a
–
9b
), we compute the constrained MLEs using the Fisher scoring method. Specifically, given the estimates
\left(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t)},\hat{\rho}^{(t)}\right)^{T}
at the
t
-th step, the updates at the
(t+1)
-th step are obtained as

\left(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t+1)},\hat{\rho}^{(t+1)}\right)^{T}=\left(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t)},\hat{\rho}^{(t)}\right)^{T}+\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}_{0}\left(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t)},\hat{\rho}^{(t)}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}\left(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t)},\hat{\rho}^{(t)}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{U}\left(\pi_{1},\rho\right)=\left(\partial\ell_{1}/\partial\pi_{1},\partial\ell_{1}/\partial\rho\right)_{\delta=\delta_{0}}^{T}
is the score function, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}_{0}\left(\pi_{1},\rho\right)^{-1}
is the inverse of the
2\times 2
Fisher information matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}_{0}\left(\pi_{1},\rho\right)
with respect to
\pi_{1}
and
\rho
, which coincides with the lower-right block of the
3\times 3
Fisher information matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\delta=\delta_{0},\pi_{1},\rho\right)
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\pi_{1},\rho\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{22}&I_{23}\\
I_{32}&I_{33}\end{array}\right)_{\delta=\delta_{0}},

(10)

with
I_{22},I_{23},I_{32},I_{33}
the elements in
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\delta,\pi_{1},\rho\right)
provided in Appendix
A
.

This updating process is repeated until convergence, which is assessed by the change in successive estimates falling below a pre-specified tolerance. Specifically, let

\delta^{(t+1)}=\sqrt{\left(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t+1)}-\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(t)}\right)^{2}+\left(\hat{\rho}^{(t+1)}-\hat{\rho}^{(t)}\right)^{2}},

and stop updating when
\delta^{(t+1)}<\delta_{c}
for a sufficient small tolerance, such as
\delta_{c}=10^{-6}
.

2.2
Likelihood-based Test Statistics

Based on the unconstrained and constrained MLEs, we construct three likelihood-based test statistics: (i) the likelihood ratio, (ii) Wald-type, and (iii) score tests, for testing the hypotheses in (
5
).
Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left(\hat{\delta},\hat{\pi}_{1},\hat{\rho}\right)
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}=\left(\hat{\pi}_{1,0},\hat{\rho}_{0}\right)
be the unconstrained and constrained MLEs, respectively. Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:~\delta=\delta_{0}
, the three test statistics are defined as follows.

(i) Likelihood Ratio Test

The likelihood ratio (LR) test is given by

Q_{LR}=2\left[\ell_{1}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)-\ell_{1}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}\right)\right].

(11)

According to Wilks’ theorem, under
H_{0}
the LR test asymptotically follows the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom.

(ii) Wald-type Test

The Wald-type statistic is constructed based on the asymptotic normality of the MLEs,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim AN\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)^{-1}\right).

Let
\boldsymbol{C}^{T}=\left(1,0,0\right)
, such that under
H_{0}:~\boldsymbol{C}^{T}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\delta_{0}
. Then, the Wald-type test statistic is given by

Q_{W}=\left.\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}-\delta_{0}\right)\left[\boldsymbol{C}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{C}\right]^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{C}^{T}\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\delta_{0}\right)\right|_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=\left(\hat{\delta}-\delta_{0}\right)^{2}/I^{11}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right),

(12)

where
I^{11}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{-1}\right)_{11}
denotes the
\left(1,1\right)
-th element of the inverse Fisher information matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)
.
For a given
3\times 3
Fisher information matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)=\left(I_{ij}\right)_{i,j=1}^{3}
, it is straightforward to show that

I^{11}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)=\left(I_{11}-\left(I_{12},I_{13}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{22}&I_{23}\\
I_{32}&I_{33}\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}I_{12}\\
I_{13}\end{array}\right)\right)^{-1}.

(13)

The explicit form of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)
is provided in Appendix
A
.
The Wald-type test in (
12
) asymptotically follows the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom.

(iii) Score Test

The score test under
H_{0}:~\delta=\delta_{0}
is defined as

Q_{S}=\left.\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\right|_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}\right)},

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)=\left(\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\delta},\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\pi_{1}},\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\rho}\right)
is the score function, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)^{-1}
is the inverse of the Fisher information matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)
given in Appendix
A
. When evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}\right)
, the score function reduces to
\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\right)=\left(\left.\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\delta}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}\right)},0,0\right)
,
and the score test simplifies to

Q_{S}=I^{11}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\left.\left(\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\delta}\right)^{2}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left(\delta_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}\right)},

(15)

where
I^{11}\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)
is defined in (
13
), and

\frac{\partial\ell_{1}}{\partial\delta}=-\frac{m_{02}+m_{12}+n_{02}}{1-\delta-\pi_{1}}+\frac{m_{12}+m_{22}+n_{12}}{\delta+\pi_{1}}-\frac{m_{02}\left(1-\rho\right)}{1-\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\delta+\pi_{1}\right)}+\frac{m_{22}\left(1-\rho\right)}{\rho+\left(1-\rho\right)\left(\delta+\pi_{1}\right)}.

Likewise, the score test
Q_{S}
asymptotically follows the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom.

Results

3.1
Simulation Studies

We conduct simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the three likelihood-based test statistics proposed in Section
2.2
by investigating their empirical type I error rates (TIEs) and powers.
The following parameter settings are used in common for both empirical type I error and power evaluations:

\rho=0,0.5,0.9
,
\pi_{1}=0.1,0.2,0.3
,
m_{+1}=m_{+2}=m=50,100,150
and
n_{+1}=n_{+2}=n=50,100,150
.

3.1.1
Empirical Type I Error

Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:~\delta=\delta_{0}
with
\delta_{0}=0.1,0.2,0.3
, and for given values of
\rho
,
\pi_{1}
,
m
, and
n
, datasets are randomly generated according to the distributions in (
1
). The three likelihood-based test statistics,

,

, and

, respectively defined in (
11
), (
12
) and (

is rejected if

indicates the test type, and
\chi^{2}_{1-\alpha,1}
denotes the
\left(1-\alpha\right)
-quantile of the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom. Each simulation scenario is replicated

present the empirical TIEs at the nominal significance level

3.1.2
Empirical Powers

The empirical powers are computed in the same manner as the empirical TIEs, except that the datasets are generated under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}:~\delta=\delta_{1}
with
\delta_{1}=0.1,0.2,0.3
, against the null hypothesis
H_{0}:~\delta=0
. Tables
-
present the empirical power results at the nominal significance level
\alpha=0.05
for
\rho=0
,
0.5
and
0.9
, respectively. The results show that the empirical powers of the three tests are generally comparable under the same parameter settings. Moreover, for each given value of
\rho
, the empirical powers increase with the sample size and risk difference in proportions
\delta_{1}
(hereafter referred to as the
effect size
). On the other hand, for fixed sample size and effect size, the empirical powers decrease as the intra-subject correlation
\rho
increases.

4
Conclusions

In this study, we investigated three likelihood-based test statistics: the likelihood ratio test (
Q_{LR}
), Wald-typ test (
Q_{W}
) and score test (
Q_{S}
), respectively, for testing the risk difference of two proportions for combined unilateral and bilateral data. Through extensive simulation studies, we evaluated their empirical TIEs and powers under various parameter settings. The results show that all three tests maintain fairly good control of the type I error across a wide range of scenarios. Among them, the score test consistently demonstrates slightly better perfomrance, showing narrower variability in empirical TIEs and power estimates that are comparable to those of the other two tests.

Power analysis further revealed that, as expected, power increases with sample size and effect size (risk difference), but decreases with higher intra-subject correlation. A useful
online calculator
has been developed to assist researchers in determining sample sizes, power, and effect sizes under arbitrary parameter settings.

Two real examples from otolaryngologic and ophthalmologic studies were analyzed to illustrate the application of the proposed methods. In addition, the
online calculator
enables users to perform the proposed risk difference test on their own datasets.

Overall, the score test provides marginally superior control of type I error and comparable power, making it a recommended choice for practical applications and future studies involving combined unilateral and bilateral data.

Finally, we note that the proposed statistics rely on large sample asymptotic properties and thus perform best with sufficiently large sample sizes. For small sample situations, exact methods may be more appropriate and will be explored in future work.
