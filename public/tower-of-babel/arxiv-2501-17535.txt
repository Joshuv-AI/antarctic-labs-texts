Probabilistic interpretation of the Selberg–Delange Method in analytic number theory
Maximilian Janisch

Abstract
In analytic number theory, the Selberg–Delange Method provides an asymptotic formula for the partial sums of a complex function f f whose Dirichlet series has the form of a product of a well-behaved analytic function and a complex power of the Riemann zeta function. In probability theory, mod-Poisson convergence is a refinement of convergence in distribution toward a normal distribution. This stronger form of convergence not only implies a Central Limit Theorem but also offers finer control over the distribution of the variables, such as precise estimates for large deviations. In this paper, we show that results in analytic number theory derived using the Selberg–Delange Method lead to mod-Poisson convergence as x → ∞ x\to\infty for the number of distinct prime factors of a randomly chosen integer between 1 1 and x x , where the integer is distributed according to a broad class of multiplicative functions. As a Corollary, we recover a part of a recent result by Elboim and Gorodetsky under different, though related, conditions: A Central Limit Theorem for the number of distinct prime factors of such random integers.

Mod-

13 Definition

(
[

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

. Assume that for

\phi_{n}(z)\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{e}^{zX_{n}}\right)

have finite moments of all orders.) Fix furthermore an infinitely divisible distribution

\eta(z)\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\ln\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{zx}\,\mathrm{d}\phi(x)\right)

with

such that

\psi_{n}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\exp(-t_{n}\eta)\phi_{n}\to\psi\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}_{\text{loc}}(\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)})\text{ as }n\to\infty,

(10)

where

Since

is admissible with average value
\rho
by assumption and since it is non-negative, from Theorem
11
, we get

\sum_{n=1}^{x}\alpha(n)=x(\ln x)^{\rho-1}(\lambda_{0}(\alpha)+O(1/\ln x)),\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty.

(11)

Now, let
y\in\mathbb{C}
and let
\alpha_{y}
be defined as in Lemma
10
. Since
\alpha
is admissible++, by Proposition
10
,
\alpha_{y}
is admissible with average value
y\rho
. We note furthermore that
\alpha_{y}
is admissible for the same
c_{0}
in Definition
3
as
\alpha
. Thus, by Theorem
11
, noting that
|y^{\omega(n)}\alpha|=|y|^{\omega(n)}\alpha
is admissible too, we get

\sum_{n=1}^{x}y^{\omega(n)}\alpha(n)=x(\ln x)^{\rho y-1}\left(\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{y})+O(1/\ln x)\right),\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty,

(12)

where by convention we set
\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{y})
equal to
0
if
\rho y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
.

The idea of the proof is to now combine (
11
) and (
12
) to obtain asymptotic estimates for the moment-generating function
\mathbb{E}\left(y^{\omega(N_{x,\alpha})}\right)
, as will be done in (
13
) below. Note that mod-
\phi
convergence requires not only pointwise-in-
y
convergence, but it requires locally uniform convergence (see equation (
10
)). As already mentioned, the
c_{0}
from Definition
3
are the same for
\alpha_{y}
as they are for
\alpha
, and furthermore
\delta=1
for both. The constants implicit in the
O
can thus be chosen the same for
\alpha
and
\alpha_{y}
, as long as
y
varies in a compact set, as the constant implicit in (
8
) depend only on
c_{0},\delta
and locally uniformly on the average value of the multiplicative function, as well as
M
, but the latter varies continuously depending on
y
by Proposition
10
.

We thus conclude that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left(y^{\omega(N_{x,\alpha})}\right)&=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{x}y^{\omega(k)}\alpha(k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{x}\alpha(k)}\\
&=\frac{x(\ln x)^{\rho y-1}\left(\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{y})+O\left(1/\ln x\right)\right)}{x(\ln x)^{\rho-1}\left(\lambda_{0}(\alpha)+O\left(1/\ln x\right)\right)}\\
&=\frac{(\ln x)^{\rho(y-1)}}{\lambda_{0}(\alpha)}\left(\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{y})+O\left(1/\ln x\right)\right)\left(1+O\left(1/\ln x\right)\right)\\
&=(\ln x)^{\rho(y-1)}\frac{\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{y})}{\lambda_{0}(\alpha)}\left(1+O\left(1/\ln x\right)\right),\end{split}

(13)

where for any fixed
B\in(0,\infty)
, the constant implicit in the
O
can be chosen uniformly for all
y\in\mathbb{C}
with
|y|\leq B
.

It follows that for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, and any
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{e}^{z\omega(N_{x,\alpha})}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{\rho(\ln\ln x)(\mathrm{e}^{z}-1)}\frac{\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{y})}{\lambda_{0}(\alpha)}\left(1+O\left(1/\ln x\right)\right),

where for any fixed
B\in(0,\infty)
, the constant implicit in the
O
can be chosen uniformly for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\lvert z\rvert\leq B
.

Recall that the Laplace transform of the Poisson distribution with parameter
1
is given by

z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{e}^{z}-1}.

Therefore, we have mod-Poisson convergence of the
\omega(N_{x,\alpha})
as
x\to\infty
with limiting function

\psi(z)=\frac{\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{\mathrm{e}^{z}})}{\lambda_{0}(\alpha)}

and parameters
t_{x}=\rho\ln\ln x
on the entire complex plane. The speed of convergence is
O\left(1/\ln x\right)
.
∎

2.4
Generalization to additive functions other than
\omega

In this section we will discuss generalizations of Theorem
15
to
g(N_{x,\alpha})
, where
g
is some additive function. Theorem
15
corresponds to the choice
g=\omega
.

16 Definition

(Additive function)
.

A function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is called
additive
if, for any co-prime integers
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

g(nm)=g(n)+g(m).

For example, the functions
\omega,\Omega
(see Example
18
), the logarithm restricted to natural numbers and the function mapping an integer to the sum of the distinct prime divisors of that integer are additive functions.
In order to establish mod-Poisson convergence of
g(N_{x,\alpha})
for an additive function
g
, we need asymptotic estimates as
\mathbb{N}\ni x\to\infty
for the sum

\sum_{n=1}^{x}y^{g(n)}\alpha(n)

for
y
in at least a subset of the complex plane. In principle, it would be best if we could get such asymptotic estimates for all
y\in\mathbb{C}
, however, as we will see in Example
18
below, even for fairly mundane choices of
g
, such as choosing
g=\Omega
, where
\Omega
is the function counting the number of prime factors of an integer with multiplicity, will lead to
n\mapsto y^{g(n)}\alpha(n)
being non-admissible for some
y\in\mathbb{C}
.

We briefly discuss how to adjust the proof of Theorem
15
to this more general case. In order to obtain (
11
) as well as (
12
), we need that
n\mapsto y^{g(n)}\alpha(n)
is admissible for all
y
of interest, which for mod-
\phi
convergence means for all
y
in some set
\{\exp(z):z\in\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}\}
, borrowing notation from Definition
13
. We furthermore need that the constant implicit in the
O
obtained from Theorem
11
varies locally uniformly with
y
.

We summarize the previous discussion in the following Theorem.

17 Theorem

(Main result, most general version)
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a multiplicative function which is not identically equal to
0
. Furthermore, let
c,d\in[-\infty,\infty]
satisfy
c<d
, and let
\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}\subset\mathbb{C}
denote the set
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:c<\operatorname{Re}z<d\}
. Fix an additive function
g:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
and assume that all of the following hold:

1.

there exist
\rho\in[0,\infty)
,
\delta\in(0,1]
and
c_{0}\in(0,1)
, as well as a function
M:\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}\to(0,\infty)
such that for every
z\in\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}
, the function
\alpha_{\mathrm{e}^{z}}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C},n\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{zg(n)}\alpha(n)
, is admissible for that
\delta,c_{0}
and for
M=M(z)
with average value
\rho\mathrm{e}^{z}
;

2.

for any compact set
C\subset\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}
,
\sup_{z\in C}M(z)<\infty
.

Let
(N_{x,\alpha})_{x\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of random variables such that

\mathbb{P}(N_{x,\alpha}=k)=\frac{\mathds{1}_{k\in\{1,\dots,x\}}\alpha(k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{x}\alpha(j)},\qquad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Then the family of random variables
(g(N_{x,\alpha}))_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
converges mod-Poisson on
\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}
with limiting function

\psi(z)=\frac{\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{\mathrm{e}^{z}})}{\lambda_{0}(\alpha)},

where
\lambda_{0}(\alpha_{\mathrm{e}^{z}})
is given by (
9
) for
\mathrm{e}^{z}\rho\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, and by
0
for
\mathrm{e}^{z}\rho\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
.

The parameters of the convergence are
t_{x}=\rho\ln\ln x
and the speed of convergence
O(1/\ln x)
.

We hope to illuminate this more general Theorem with the following example.

18 Example

(Prime factors counted with multiplicities)
.

We consider the function
g=\Omega
, with
\Omega(n)
defined as the number of prime factors of
n\in\mathbb{N}
, counted with multiplicity. We choose, for simplicity, the multiplicative function
\alpha(n)=1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
y\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|y|<2
, the Dirichlet series

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{y^{\Omega(n)}}{n^{s}}
is absolutely convergent when
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
and we can thus expand it into the Euler product

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{y^{\Omega(n)}}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{k}}{p^{ks}}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\left(1-\frac{y}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1}.

In particular, choosing
\rho=y
,

\zeta(s)^{-y}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{y^{\Omega(n)}}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{y}(1-yp^{-s})^{-1}.

This product has a pole whenever
y=p^{-s}
, which is the reason why the function
n\mapsto y^{\Omega(n)}
is not admissible for all
y\in\mathbb{C}
. However, assuming that
|y|\leq 2^{-\operatorname{Re}s}
, we may take the logarithm to obtain

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}y\ln(1-p^{-s})-\ln(1-yp^{-s})=\sum_{p\text{ prime}}-yp^{-s}+yp^{-s}+O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-2s})=\sum_{p\text{ prime}}O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-2s}).

In particular, this is uniformly convergent on the half-plane
\left\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}\right\}
. Thus, we have admissibility on this half-plane assuming that
|y|<\sqrt{2}
. Furthermore, one can check that the constant
M
in (
3
) depends locally uniformly on
y
. This means that we can apply Theorem
17
for the strip
\mathcal{S}_{(-\infty,\ln(2)/2)}
and thus obtain mod-Poisson convergence for the
(\Omega(N_{x}))_{x\in\mathbb{N}}
, where each
N_{x}
is a random variable distributed uniformly on
\{1,2,\dots,x\}
.

19 Remark

(Erdős-Kac Theorem for
\Omega
)
.

Based on the discussion in Example
18
and the results of section
3.1
, we remark that we obtain a Central Limit Theorem for the number of prime factors with multiplicity of a uniformly randomly chosen integer in
\{1,\dots,x\}
as

\frac{\Omega(N_{x})-\ln\ln x}{\sqrt{\ln\ln x}}\to Z\text{ in distribution as }x\to\infty,

where
N_{x}
denotes a uniformly on
\{1,2,\dots,x\}
distributed random variable, and
Z
denotes a normally distributed random variable with mean
0
and variance
1
.
This is a well-known variant of the classical Erdős-Kac Theorem.

3.1
Central Limit Theorem

Mod-
\phi

\omega(N_{x,\alpha})

21 Proposition

(Central Limit Theorem for random variables that converge mod-
\phi
)
.

If
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of random variables converging mod-
\phi
with
\eta
and
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as in Definition

\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0

(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

Y_{n}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\frac{X_{n}-t_{n}\eta^{\prime}(0)}{\sqrt{t_{n}\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)}}

converges in distribution to a standard normal distribution as
n\to\infty
.

22 Remark

(Condition
\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
)

Note that
\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)
equals the variance of a random variable
X
which is
\phi
-distributed (for details see Remark
28

\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
is equivalent to the condition that the support of
\phi
contains at least two distinct points. If this condition is not met, then instead
X_{n}-t_{n}\eta^{\prime}(0)
converges in distribution towards a random variable that is
\psi
-distributed, where
\psi
is as in Definition
13
, by a direct application of Lévy’s continuity Theorem.

Proposition
21
follows from a Taylor expansion of the cumulant generating function
\eta

A

5
5

5

Similar stronger results are also available for non-lattice distributions, see
[

The Central Limit Theorem gives us asymptotics for the probability

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\geq t_{n}\eta^{\prime}(0)+\sqrt{t_{n}\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)}y\right)

23 Theorem

(Extended Central Limit Theorem, Corollary of
[
4

)

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables that converges mod-
\phi
on any strip where
\phi
is an infinitely divisible probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}
whose support is contained in some set of the form
\gamma+\lambda\mathbb{Z}

\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\lambda\in(0,\infty)

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\geq t_{n}\eta^{\prime}(0)+\sqrt{t_{n}\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)}y_{n}\right)=(1-\Phi(y_{n}))(1+o(1)),\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

Since the Poisson distribution is infinitely divisible, has its support contained in

(Extended Central Limit Theorem for the

3.2
Precise large deviations

Mod-
\phi
convergence implies precise large deviation principles. The main result of this section will be a precise large deviation result for the
\omega(N_{x,\alpha})
, see Corollary
30
.

In order to motivate this result, we recall first the basic theory of large deviations, most notably, Cramér’s large deviation principle.

26 Theorem

(Cramér’s large deviation principle)
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent, identically distributed real random variables. We consider the cumulant generating function
\Lambda(t)\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\ln\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{e}^{tY_{1}}\right)
, which we assume to be finite and well-defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
6
6

6

In particular, the random variables
Y_{n}
have finite moments of all orders.
We now define the
Fenchel-Legendre transform
of the cumulant generating function as

\Lambda^{*}(s)=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}ts-\Lambda(t),\qquad\text{for }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, for all
s>\mathbb{E}(Y_{1})
, with
X_{n}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{n}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\geq sn)=-\Lambda^{*}(s).

Large deviation principles say, intuitively, that, for some sequence of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\geq sn)=\exp\left(-n\Lambda^{*}(s)+o(n)\right)
for
s>\mathbb{E}(X_{1})
fixed as
n\to\infty
. We would like to establish
precise
large deviations, meaning that we want better control of the
o(n)
term.

Precise large deviations can be obtained for random variables that converge mod-
\phi
, as illustrated in the following result.

27 Theorem

(
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

Let
\phi
be an infinitely divisible probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}
whose support is contained in some
\gamma+\lambda\mathbb{Z}
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, and let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables converging mod-
\phi
on
\mathcal{S}_{(c,d)}
with
c<0<d
and parameters
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with limiting function
\psi
(borrowing the notation from Definition
13
). Let
\eta
be as in Definition
13
and assume
\eta^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
. Then, for
s\in(\eta^{\prime}(c),\eta^{\prime}(d))
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\geq t_{n}s)=\exp(-t_{n}\eta^{*}(s))\frac{\psi(h)}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-h}}(1+o(1)),\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where

\eta^{*}(s)\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}ts-\eta(t),\qquad\text{for }s\in\mathbb{R},

and
h
is defined through the implicit equation
\eta^{\prime}(h)=s
.

28 Remark

(Well-definedness of
h
)
.

Consider
\phi,\eta
as in Definition
13
, and let
X
be a
\phi
-distributed random variable. Then note that, for
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\eta^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(X^{2}\mathrm{e}^{zX}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{e}^{zX}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(X\mathrm{e}^{zX}\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{e}^{zX}\right)^{2}},

which is the variance of
X
under the measure
\mathbb{Q}_{z}
given by
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{z}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{zX}}{\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{e}^{zX})}
. Therefore,
\eta
restricted to
\mathbb{R}
is a convex function. Furthermore,
\eta
is strictly convex unless
\phi
is supported on a single point.

It follows that
h
in Theorem
27
is well-defined and unique. Furthermore, we have
\eta^{*}(s)=sh-\eta(h)
when
\eta^{\prime}(h)=s
.

29 Remark

(The term
\frac{\psi(h)}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-h}}
)
.

The term
\frac{\psi(h)}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-h}}
in Theorem
27
is undefined when
h=0
. In this case, we implicitly define this term as
\psi^{\prime}(0)
.

30 Corollary

(Precise large deviations for the
\omega(N_{x,\alpha})
)
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an admissible multiplicative function with average value
\rho\in\mathbb{C}
. Then for
s\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(N_{x,\alpha}\geq s\rho\ln\ln x)=\exp(-\rho\ln\ln x(1+s(\ln(s)-1)))\frac{\psi(\ln s)}{1-\frac{1}{s}}(1+o(1)),\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty,

where, for
s=1

\frac{\psi(\ln s)}{1-\frac{1}{s}}
as
\psi^{\prime}(0)
.

4
Examples of admissible++ functions

In this section, we give examples of families of multiplicative functions which are admissible++, and for which we can thus deduce a result of mod-Poisson convergence as in Theorem
15
.

4.1
\alpha(k)=\theta^{\omega(k)}
for a
\theta>0

A well-known class of multiplicative functions, studied also in
[
2
]
, is given by
\alpha(n)=\theta^{\omega(n)}
for a parameter
\theta\in(0,\infty)
. To show that such an
\alpha
is admissible, by Proposition
10
, it is enough to prove that
\alpha\equiv 1
is admissible. However,
\alpha\equiv 1
has the Dirichlet series
\zeta(s)
, which directly shows that this
\alpha
is admissible with average value
1
, as discussed in Example
4
. Admissibility++ holds for
\rho=1
and any
c_{0}<\frac{1}{2}
.

4.2
\alpha(p^{s})=B^{s}
for a
B\in(0,2)

Consider now a fixed
B\in(0,2)
and let
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
be the multiplicative function which satisfies for all
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
as well as all primes
p
the identity
\alpha(p^{s})=B^{s}
. Then we have the Euler product

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(n)}{n^{s}}\zeta(s)^{-\rho}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{\rho}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B^{s}}{p^{ks}}\right)\\
=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{\rho}\frac{1}{1-\frac{B}{p^{s}}}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{\rho}\left(1+\frac{B}{p^{s}-B}\right).

(14)

We note that the second term in the product has a pole when
p^{s}=B
. Thus, on the half-plane
\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}(s)>1-c_{0}\}
for
c_{0}\in(0,1/2)
, we need to restrict ourselves to
B\in(0,2^{1-c_{0}})
.
Choosing
\rho=B
, and taking the logarithm of the right-hand side of (
14
), we get

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}B\ln(1-p^{-s})+\ln\left(1+\frac{B}{p^{s}-B}\right),

which is

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}-Bp^{-s}+Bp^{-s}+O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-2s}).

Checking admissibility as well as admissibility++ is now straightforward.

4.3
\alpha(p^{k})=a+O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-\varepsilon k})
for some
\varepsilon>0

We finally consider the case where for some
a>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\alpha(n)=a+O(n^{-\varepsilon})
, for
n\to\infty
restricted to the set
n\in\mathcal{P}
, where

\mathcal{P}\overset{\text{Def.}}{=}\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\{p^{k}:p\text{ prime}\}.

We assume for simplicity that
\alpha
is non-negative. Then we note first that

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\alpha(n)}{n^{2}}<\infty.

Second, fix a
c_{0}\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
. Then

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(p^{k})}{p^{k(1-c_{0})}}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{p\text{ prime}}2\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a}{p^{k(1-c_{0})}}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{o_{p\to\infty}(1)}{p^{k(1-c_{0})}}\right)^{2}\right),

which is indeed finite.

Finally, we consider the compensated Euler product for
\rho=a
,

\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a+O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-\varepsilon k})}{p^{ks}}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{a}.

Taking the logarithm we obtain

\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\left(O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-2s})-\frac{a}{p^{s}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a+O_{p\to\infty}(p^{-\varepsilon k})}{p^{ks}}\right),

where the first term comes from the quadratic terms of the logarithm, while the second term comes from
(1-p^{-s})^{a}
and the last term comes from the first term in the Euler product.
The sum is convergent whenever
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\max(\frac{1}{2},1-\varepsilon)
. We have thus established admissibility and, together with the previous reasoning, thus admissibility++.

(15)
