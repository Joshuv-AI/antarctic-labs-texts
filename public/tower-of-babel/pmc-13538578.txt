Authentication status and AI triage concordance among care seekers in a US health system
Authors: Bilal A Naved, Quintan M Slott, Adeel Malik, Melody Hmaidi, Yuan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnline AI triage tools (symptom checkers) are widely deployed at the digital front door of US health systems, but little is known about how users’ pre-stated care intent aligns with the AI recommendation or how that alignment shapes downstream engagement. We conducted a prospective cohort analysis of 6772 randomly selected users completing AI self-triage on either the public website (unauthenticated) or patient portal (authenticated) of a large integrated US health system (September 2023 to May 2024). Users reported their planned site of care (pre-intent), and encounters were classified as validated (matching the AI triage recommendation) or re-directed (differing); re-directed encounters were sub-classified as escalated or de-escalated. Downstream digital engagement was captured as interaction with any call-to-action. Of 6772 users, 508 (7.5%) were unauthenticated and 6264 (92.5%) authenticated; 89% of unauthenticated self-care pre-intenders were escalated by the AI, and 42% of authenticated office-visit pre-intenders were re-directed. Call-to-action interaction was approximately twice as high when the AI recommendation matched pre-intent. Of 636 non-engagers responding to a follow-up survey (9.4% response rate), all reported plans to seek care off-platform. Authentication status and pre-intent-to-recommendation alignment are strong correlates of digital care-seeking engagement, and merit targeted user-experience design.Subject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

One of the primary functions of A.I. triage platforms is to capture patients’ care-seeking intent in real time. Patients often turn to these online tools at the onset of symptoms, seeking advice on whether (and where) to obtain care. In fact, up to 71% of symptom checker encounters occur outside of traditional clinic hours, suggesting that digital triage is particularly valuable when conventional healthcare services are less accessible1–4. During the COVID-19 pandemic, health systems rapidly deployed web-based A.I. triage applications to manage surges in demand, divert non-urgent cases, and reduce pressure on emergency services5–8. These experiences highlight the dual role of digital triage systems: they not only capture the initial intent to seek care but also help direct patients to appropriate care pathways based on their symptoms.

Beyond measuring intent, A.I. triage systems qualify patient care by stratifying the urgency and type of care needed. By guiding users through a series of targeted questions and providing tailored recommendations, ranging from self-care at home to emergent evaluation, these platforms help ensure that patients are directed to the most suitable care setting6,9–14. Integration of such tools into health system websites has been associated with operational benefits, including shorter clinic wait times and improved patient flow, further emphasizing their value6–8,11,12,15.

Importantly, the advent of A.I. triage has introduced new considerations regarding the characteristics of those seeking care via digital access channels (i.e., digital care seekers). Early evaluations suggest that users of online symptom checkers, while diverse, tend to differ in demographics from the broader patient population. For instance, one study reported that over two-thirds of users were female and that the average user was in their mid-30s, indicating a skew toward younger, female users12,16–19. Moreover, health systems typically offer symptom checker access both to authenticated users (those logged in with patient portal accounts) and to unauthenticated visitors (guests). These groups may differ not only demographically but also in terms of their engagement and subsequent care-seeking behavior.

To address these gaps, we conducted a prospective cohort analysis of digital care seekers using an online A.I. triage system within a large, integrated health system in the U.S. South. This study is the first in the literature to report on the behavior and characteristics of authenticated versus unauthenticated digital care seekers, specifically examining their intents prior to interacting with the A.I. triage system and their subsequent care-seeking actions following receipt of clinically validated triage recommendations. By elucidating these differences, our work aims to inform more tailored digital engagement strategies that enhance patient guidance.

On-platform CTA engagement is about twice as high when pre-intent matches the AI triage recommendation as when it does not

Engagement with Calls to Action (CTAs) (e.g. selecting a slot to book) varied by pre‑intent clarity: 19.5% of “unsure” users interacted with booking or contact options, compared to 26.4% of users with a defined pre‑intent (Suppl. Fig. 2). On-platform CTA interaction was higher for users whose pre-intent was urgent care or virtual care (41%) than for users whose pre-intent was a retail-clinic visit (38%) or a primary care or specialist visit (28–29%). On this platform, urgent care and virtual care CTAs offer the deepest one-click fulfillment, whereas PCP and specialist visits are commonly fulfilled off-platform (e.g., by phone), so this differential partly reflects fulfillment depth rather than care preference per se. Among users whose pre-intent matched the AI recommendation (validated), 54% interacted with a CTA, compared with 37% among those who were de-escalated by the AI and 27% among those who were escalated by the AI. Validation of pre-intent was therefore associated with approximately twofold higher on-platform CTA interaction (Fig. 2).

Digital care seekers most commonly seek to qualify between PCP vs. specialist and Urgent Care vs. lower levels of in-person care

Among unauthenticated respondents who answered the pre-intent question, 31% selected more than one potential care option (Fig. 3). The three most common dyads were Urgent Care + Retail Clinic (n = 949, 16%), Primary Care + Home (n = 658, 11%), Primary Care + Specialty (n = 535, 9%), and Urgent Care + ED (n = 462, 8%) (Fig. 3).

Distribution and pairings of pre-intent responders.

Of care seekers who did not mark unsure in their pre-intent, 65% had a single clear intent with the remaining 35% indicating multiple possible intents (i.e., still choosing between multiple options). Care seekers with more than one indicated intent engaged with CTAs at a higher rate of 41% vs. those with a single intent engaging at a rate of 29%. The most prevalent dyads for those who were choosing between two sites of care were urgent care + retail clinic, primary care + specialist, and urgent care + primary care. Intuitively, this suggests that care seekers using an AI triage system may often be unsure if they should seek non-emergent care immediately via an urgent care as opposed to lower-levels of care (e.g., retail clinic or primary care) or are unsure about whether or not they need a specialist as opposed to their PCP.

Across these dyads, the proportion whose pre-intent matched the triage recommendation (validated) varied substantially: 31% for Urgent Care + Primary Care, 72% for Urgent Care + Retail Clinic, and 70% for Primary Care + Specialty; in the aggregate multiple-response cohort (all users selecting > 1 response; n = 1751), 22% were validated (Fig. 4). When pre-intent was validated, downstream digital engagement was consistently high: 50% (Urgent Care + Primary Care), 48% (Urgent Care + Retail Clinic), 55% (Primary Care + Specialty), and 40% in the aggregate multiple-response cohort interacted with booking or contact CTAs (Fig. 4).

Validation of two-option pre-intents and downstream engagement.

Among users who selected exactly two care options, validation rates varied by dyad: Urgent Care + Primary Care (n = 50) 72% validated; Urgent Care + Retail Clinic (n = 67) 70% validated; Primary Care + Specialty (n = 68) 22% validated. In the aggregate multiple-response cohort (n = 1181), 22% were validated. When validated, call-to-action (CTA) engagement was consistently high—40%, 48%, and 55% across the three dyads, and 50% in the aggregate—indicating that affirmation of a short-listed plan reliably drives digital follow-through.

Focusing on encounters where pre-intent did not match the triage recommendation (re-directed), the number re-directed was 21 for Urgent Care + Primary Care, 30 for Urgent Care + Retail Clinic, 78 for Primary Care + Specialty, and 1208 in the aggregate multiple-response cohort (Fig. 5). The type of re-direction differed by dyad: Urgent Care + Primary Care was predominantly de-escalated (72%) with 25% escalated; Urgent Care + Retail Clinic was predominantly escalated (79%) with 4% de-escalated; Primary Care + Specialty showed 0% escalated and 100% de-escalated; in the aggregate multiple-response cohort, 0% were escalated and 100% de-escalated (Fig. 5).

When two-option pre-intents are re-directed, patterns differ by dyad.

Urgent Care + Primary Care (n = 56) was entirely de-escalated (100%); Urgent Care + Retail Clinic (n = 80) was also entirely de-escalated (100%) with 90% of all de-escalations to Telemedicine; Primary Care + Specialty (n = 208) was entirely mostly escalated (79% escalated, 21% de-escalated), overwhelmingly to Urgent Care. In the aggregate multiple-response cohort (n = 3221), re-direction was largely de-escalatory (72% de-escalated, 28% escalated), underscoring a dominant shift to lower-acuity modalities. Engagement after re-direction is lower, largely in the 20–30% range. However, in the dyad selecting between Urgent Care and Primary Care, a significant increase in CTA engagement is revealed (52%). Among re-directed users, CTA engagement was 52% for Urgent Care + Primary Care, 33% for Urgent Care + Retail Clinic, 24–33% for Primary Care + Specialty, and 23–32% in the aggregate multiple-response cohort.

CTA engagement declined when users were re-directed relative to when they were validated, but again varied by dyad: 32% (Urgent Care + Primary Care), 23% (Urgent Care + Retail Clinic), 33% (Primary Care + Specialty), and 24% in the aggregate multiple-response cohort engaged with CTAs after a re-direction (Fig. 5). Notably, the Urgent Care + Primary Care dyad, where options are clinically adjacent, retained comparatively higher engagement among re-directed users. Overall, multiple-response users demonstrated strong engagement when their initial plan was affirmed, and among those re-directed, the dominant pattern was de-escalation to lower-acuity modalities (e.g., virtual care), especially for Primary Care + Specialty.

100% of care seekers who do not engage with calls to action at the end of a A.I. triage report planning to proceed with seeking care

A total of 636 of the included 6772 surveyed (9.39%) who did not click any CTA reported subsequent plans to seek care: 49% intended to book care directly, 31% planned to go to an emergency or urgent care setting, and 20% planned to call their physician’s office. Notably, none of them reported not planning to go seek care. Among those who had received an ED recommendation, 62% indicated they would instead book care or call a provider rather than present to the ED. Similarly, 72% of those given a primary care recommendation reported plans to call a doctor or schedule an appointment (Suppl. Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study offers a detailed examination of digital care-seeking behavior within a large, integrated U.S. health system, specifically being the first study in the literature to compare authenticated (portal-based) and unauthenticated (public website) digital care-seeking behaviors using an A.I. triage system. Our findings reveal three key insights: (1) a high rate of misalignment between patient intent and clinically optimal triage recommendations, (2) substantial behavioral differences between authenticated and unauthenticated digital care seekers, and (3) clear engagement implications tied to the concordance of pre-intent and post-triage outcomes.

The A.I. triage system in question was built in partnership with the co-author of the same telephone triage protocols trusted in 95% of the nurse call centers in the USA1,9,20. The development of this system was done by further refining the co-author’s content for the purposes of digital self-triage by a panel of board-certified physicians across primary care, virtual care, emergency medicine, behavioral health, surgical specialties, and pediatrics9. We use this engine here as a clinically reviewed reference benchmark, not as a gold standard. The engine has been independently evaluated against physician triage in prior work, but the comparator throughout the present study is the AI recommendation, which is itself an automated output and not a substitute for physician adjudication. We are also mindful that several authors (BAN, AM, MH, QMS) are employees of the company that produces the engine and supplied the data while YL is not. Conflict-of-interest considerations therefore preclude us from making comparative claims about this engine relative to other AI triage engines9,20.

We compared the pre-intent of digital care seekers to the recommendations provided by the AI triage system, which we treat as a panel-adjudicated reference benchmark (the triage logic was developed by a multi-specialty board-certified physician panel adjudicating every possible triage pathway in a deterministic corpus of triage algorithms spanning hundreds of chief complaints. The underlying protocols are used in the majority of U.S. nurse call centers). The vast majority of users began with a clear pre-intent (86%). However, more than 70% of these users received an AI recommendation that differed from their pre-intent, indicating a widespread divergence between user-stated and AI-recommended sites of care. We acknowledge that while random sampling of retrospective AI triage conversations is conducted for ongoing quality assurance, not every individual encounter is independently re-adjudicated at the per-encounter level.

The majority of users began with a clear pre-intent (86%). However, more than 70% of these users received an AI recommendation that differed from their pre-intent, indicating a widespread divergence between user-stated and AI-recommended sites of care. The AI engine is derived from nurse-triage protocols used widely across U.S. nurse call centers and was further reviewed for digital self-triage by a panel of board-certified physicians spanning primary care, virtual care, emergency medicine, behavioral health, surgical specialties, and pediatrics. We therefore consider it a defensible ref. 1, 9. Discordance between pre-intent and AI recommendation was especially pronounced among users whose pre-intent was self-care or an emergency department (ED) visit. More than 90% of these encounters received an AI recommendation at a different acuity. These observations extend prior descriptive work showing that symptom-based self-assessment by lay users frequently differs from clinically reviewed digital triage recommendations even when a clear initial preference is reported. We make no claim about which of pre-intent or AI recommendation is clinically correct in the absence of physician adjudication.

Second, authentication status correlated with both initial pre-intent and the direction of any AI re-direction. Unauthenticated users were more likely to pre-intend self-care. In 89% of these encounters the AI recommendation was urgent care or higher acuity (an AI escalation relative to pre-intent). Several non-mutually-exclusive explanations are possible: (i) information-seeking rather than care-intent (the absence of a one-click path to a real appointment (see Methods) may make “home care” the path of least resistance for anonymous users); (ii) limited access to care (uninsured status, absence of a portal account, no PCP attribution) for which a self-care pre-intent may reflect economic or logistical constraint rather than a clinical judgment; (iii) symptom mix that genuinely warranted higher-acuity care; and/or (iv) selection (the website-only path is more often used by anonymous, novel, or out-of-network visitors). Our data cannot adjudicate among these. Authenticated users, by contrast, more often pre-intended an office visit. 42% of these encounters received an AI recommendation that differed from pre-intent (escalation to urgent care or de-escalation to virtual care). These differences may reflect a combination of authentication-related access asymmetries, differences in symptom mix, and differences in health literacy and digital engagement.

Engagement with the care recommendations (i.e., interaction with CTAs) varied significantly based on the concordance between pre-intent and triage result. Users whose care plans were affirmed by the triage tool engaged with CTAs at a rate almost twice as those whose intents were de-escalated or escalated, respectively. This suggests that affirmation of a patient’s self-assessed care plan may increase their trust in the system and willingness to act. Conversely, re-direction, while clinically necessary, may introduce friction or skepticism, leading to lower uptake of suggested actions. Notably, even among users who did not engage digitally, follow-up surveys revealed that all still intended to seek care, suggesting that lack of CTA interaction does not equate to inaction but may reflect a preference for traditional booking channels or lingering doubt in the triage guidance. Because CTA interaction captures only on-platform behaviors, our engagement metric likely underestimates true adherence to the AI recommendations. Among users who did not click any CTA, 100% nevertheless reported plans to seek care, and roughly two-thirds (49% book directly, 20% call a clinician) chose pathways that are commonly fulfilled off-platform (phone, in-person, or external sites).

Together, these findings reinforce the dual value proposition of A.I. triage: enhancing clinical appropriateness of care while optimizing the use of provider and facility time. However, the findings also point to areas for refinement. Tailoring user experience based on authentication status and initial intent may improve user trust and downstream engagement. For instance, unauthenticated users may benefit from more assertive interface nudges or real-time education about symptom severity due to their disproportionate inclination toward inappropriate self-care. Authenticated users, meanwhile, might be offered more granular care options within the portal to better match their initial intent with triage outcomes.

Integrating brief educational components into the triage process, offering users insight into why their care plan was changed, may enhance transparency and trust. The engagement and economic implications of digital triage concordance are increasingly relevant as health systems seek to optimize resource allocation and patient flow. Prior studies have shown that digital triage tools can safely redirect patients to appropriate levels of care, with high user satisfaction and a reduction in uncertainty regarding care pathways, but the impact on actual healthcare utilization and cost savings remains variable and context-dependent21–23. For example, real-world evaluations of digital triage platforms for specific complaints, such as chest pain, have demonstrated that these tools can safely identify high-risk cases and potentially reduce unnecessary emergency department (ED) visits, though they tend to be more conservative than physician decision-making23. Similarly, digital kiosks in EDs have shown high sensitivity for identifying high-acuity patients, but their specificity for low-acuity cases is limited, and over-triage rates remain high, which may limit operational efficiency gains24.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results reported here: (1) Reference standard - The AI triage output is the panel-adjudicated reference throughout. We did not have an independent physician adjudication of the correct disposition for each encounter. (2) On-platform engagement - CTA engagement reflects on-platform interaction (a click on a bookable, callable, or initiable element) and not subsequent care completion or arrival. Users who completed care off-platform (by phone, in person, or with a different vendor) are not counted as engaged, and PCP-visit pre-intenders in particular have low platform engagement because PCP visits on this platform are typically booked off-platform. (3) Group-size disparity - The 7.5%/92.5% (unauthenticated/authenticated) split reflects natural traffic distribution. The smaller unauthenticated subgroup is more sensitive to outliers, and within-subgroup estimates should be interpreted accordingly. (4) Exclusion of 911/ED routings from the pre-intent question - Users routed directly to 911 or ED were not shown the pre-intent question for safety reasons. The highest-acuity AI dispositions are therefore under-represented in the pre-intent denominator, and the absolute escalation rate is understated. (5) Single-system generalizability - the analysis was conducted in one integrated system in the southern United States, which may limit external validity. (6) Self-report - pre-intent and the follow-up survey are subjective and may be subject to recall and social-desirability bias. (7) Conflict of interest - several authors are employees of the company that produces the AI triage engine and supplied the data, the senior author YL is not. The conflict is disclosed in full in the Declarations and is not a basis for any cross-vendor comparative claim in this manuscript. (8) Alternative explanations for the unauthenticated cohort - the patterns we observe in unauthenticated users (more frequent self-care pre-intent, more frequent AI escalation) are also consistent with access barriers (uninsured status, no portal account) rather than clinical mis-assessment per se. Our data cannot distinguish among these.

Additionally, users routed directly to a 911/ED disposition were not shown the pre-intent question for safety reasons (described in detail in Methods, “Excluded encounters”). As a structural consequence, the highest-acuity AI dispositions are under-represented in the pre-intent denominator, and the absolute escalation rate is understated. The qualitative direction of the headline finding (more re-direction than validation and more de-escalation than escalation among re-directed encounters) is conservative with respect to this exclusion. This also suggests that the 11.5% of care seekers who pre-intended ED or 911 but were not classified by the AI triage as needing emergency care represent a potentially immediately deflectable group to more appropriate use of less acute care as the AI (Suppl. Fig. 2).

One pattern visible in Fig. 2 is the large portion of users whose pre-intent was self-care that proceed to engage with telemedicine (virtual care) and the small portion of overall who proceed to engage with self-care. This is mainly caused by the fact that even in self-care results pages, users are still presented with options for virtual care if they’d “still like to be seen”. This is because many self-care pre-intenders presented with symptoms that could plausibly resolve at home but for which a same-day virtual visit is the lowest-acuity safe disposition the protocol typically issues (e.g., low-grade upper respiratory symptoms with mild fever). Moreover, virtual care is the platform’s lowest-friction CTA option (no commute, often same-day) and is therefore the natural endpoint of any “you could probably handle this at home but if you’d still like to be seen” pathway. The asymmetry in Fig. 2 is therefore expected as a safety feature to ensure patients always have an option to easily access care if they prefer, even after receiving a “self-care” result.

We note that the behavioral pattern observed here (lower CTA interaction when the AI re-directs the user, and especially when the AI escalates) mirrors observations from non-AI nurse-triage and telephone-based care navigation, in which a clear, plain-language rationale for re-direction has repeatedly been associated with higher patient satisfaction and improved adherence to the recommended pathway. This literature, although not voluminous, supports embedding a brief, structured explanation into the AI triage results page when the AI re-directs the user. Such a feature is a low-cost intervention with a reasonable prior, and a target for prospective testing in this platform.

As the first study in the literature to compare digital care-seeking behavior between authenticated (portal-based) and unauthenticated (public-website) users of an AI self-triage tool in a large, integrated U.S. health system, we found that more than 70% of digital care seekers received an AI triage recommendation that differed from their pre-intent. Unauthenticated users more often pre-intended self-care that the AI escalated to higher-acuity care, while authenticated users more often pre-intended an office visit that the AI redirected (typically de-escalating to virtual care or escalating to urgent care). When the user’s pre-intent matched the AI recommendation, on-platform CTA interaction was approximately twice that of de-escalated or escalated re-directions. The AI triage output is the comparator throughout built as a physician-adjudicated reference standard on top of gold-standard nurse triage protocols. Results should be read as a behavioral comparison of user intent against a clinically reviewed reference recommendation. From a portfolio-management perspective, these patterns are consistent with a modest net per-user economic gain at the platform level (approximately $7.51 per user on average).

These insights highlight both the promise and the challenge of A.I. triage: by validating patient expectations, systems can build trust and drive follow-through, yet widespread misalignment signals a need for refined user experiences. Tailored educational prompts for unauthenticated visitors, more nuanced scheduling options for portal users, and transparent explanations of recommendation changes could enhance adherence and equity. Future work should assess long-term clinical outcomes, economic effects, and the generalizability of these findings across diverse health systems and patient populations.

Methods

Study population

The study population comprised 6772 randomly selected digital care seekers who used the AI self-triage system of a large U.S. health system ( > 40 hospitals; ambulatory and acute-care sites) headquartered in the southern United States. The AI symptom checker is exposed at two entry points: (1) the system’s public consumer website (unauthenticated), open to any visitor, and (2) the patient-portal home page (authenticated), accessible only to users logged into the EHR-linked patient portal. Encounters were sampled continuously over the study window of a 3.5-month (9/3/23–5/20/24). All encounters originating from either entry point during the study window were eligible. From the eligible pool (n = 18,774), 6772 encounters were drawn by simple random sampling without replacement using a uniform sampler over encounter ID. Authentication status was not used as a stratification variable. The 7.5% (n = 508) unauthenticated to 92.5% (n = 6264) authenticated split therefore reflects the natural traffic distribution to the symptom checker over the study window, in which portal-side traffic dominates because the patient-portal home page and outreach campaigns directly link enrolled members into triage, while the consumer website surfaces the tool to anonymous visitors only via the digital front door. We did not down-sample the authenticated arm to balance group sizes, in order to preserve all available pre-intent and engagement data; we account for the imbalance analytically (within-group proportions and χ² tests for categorical comparisons). Baseline characteristics of the cohort overall and stratified by authentication status are reported in Table 1.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

According to NIH human subject’s regulations, category 2 (Exemption 2) because the data obtained for the study was recorded in such a manner that the identity of the human subjects cannot readily be ascertained, directly or through identifiers (i.e., subject id numbers) linked to the subjects and the investigators had no ability to contact the subjects nor to re-identify subjects this study considered exempt from human subjects regulations.

Measuring care-seeking intents

Before digital care seekers (those seeking care via digital access channels) received their A.I. triage results, a randomly selected set were asked what they were planning on doing for their care. This was referred to as their “pre-intent”. Care seekers were asked what care options they were thinking of using and given options to select from including “I’m not sure”, “Taking care of the problem at home”, “Going to see a primary care doctor”, “Going to see a specialist”, “Having a video call with a healthcare provider”, “Going to a walk-in clinic”, “Going to an urgent care”, “Going to an emergency department”, “Calling an ambulance / 911”, or “None of the above”.

This analysis only looked at care seekers that answered the pre-intent question with a clear intent or “unsure” remark. Those that didn’t answer the question or remarked “None of the above” were excluded. Additionally, any users who were calculated to require a “911” or “Emergency Room” recommendation were not shown the additional pre-intent question and were immediately offered emergency care.

Calculating the self-triage of the same patients whose care-seeking intents were measured

Using a widely deployed commercial AI triage engine that is built on nurse-triage protocols used across U.S. nurse call centers and that has been further reviewed for digital self-triage by a panel of board-certified physicians9,20, a triage recommendation for each of the 6772 care seekers with collected pre-intent was calculated. The triage level included any number of categories including self-care, primary care, specialty care, virtual care, urgent care, or emergent care.

Comparing the calculated self-triage and the measured care-seeking intents

For the study population, the collected pre-intent was compared to the calculated A.I. triage. If the pre-intent matched the A.I. triage, then the individual was classified as having their pre-intent “validated”. If the pre-intent mismatched the A.I. triage, then the individual was classified as having their pre-intent “re-directed”. Of the re-directed individuals, those whose pre-intent was to a site of care that was less emergent than the calculated A.I. triage were sub-classified as having their pre-intent “escalated”. Those whose pre-intent was to a site of care that was more emergent than the calculated A.I. triage were sub-classified as having their pre-intent “de-escalated”. In sum, individuals could be classified into any one of three groups: “validated”, “escalated”, or “de-escalated”. The latter two being sub-classifications of “re-directed”. Both pre-intent options and AI triage outputs are placed on an acuity ladder. An encounter is classified as “validated” if the pre-intent rank equals the AI triage rank, “re-directed” otherwise. Among re-directed encounters: “escalated” = pre-intent rank lower than AI rank (i.e., the AI recommended higher-acuity care than the user planned); “de-escalated” = pre-intent rank higher than AI rank (i.e., the AI recommended lower-acuity care than the user planned) (Table 2).

In-depth analysis on multiple pre-intents

We further examined users who selected more than one option to the pre-intent question. We further examined the pairwise comparisons to understand the most common choices care seekers are picking between.

Using the same triage engine and definitions as the primary analysis, we classified each encounter as validated (pre-intent matched triage) or re-directed (mismatch). Re-directed encounters were sub-classified as escalated (triage recommended a higher-acuity site than pre-intent) or de-escalated (lower-acuity site). Within each dyad and in the aggregate multiple-response cohort, we calculated: (1) the proportion validated; (2) the proportion re-directed, (3) the proportions escalated vs de-escalated; and (4) the post-results digital engagement (interaction with any call-to-action [CTA] (e.g., bookable options)) as defined in the above methods. A more detailed description with examples of CTAs are provided in the next section below.

Measuring the proportion of individuals that engaged with AI-suggested options for care

As individuals in the study population completed the triage questionnaire, they were met with a results page that clearly stated the triage at the top (topline triage). Then, below the topline triage, the individuals would find options for booking or initiating the desired care (fulfillment options). These options included buttons to book, modals to fill in zip code and retrieve specific day/time slots, phone numbers to call, location finders for their nearest facilities, or instructions for initiating virtual care. As every one of these fulfillment options required some form of user interaction to book, call, or initiate, the proportion of individuals that engaged with the calculated options for care could be measured. Any individual that interacted to book, call, or initiate was classified as having engaged with the calculated option for care. Ultimately, the proportion of individuals who interacted with the calculated option for care was calculated in aggregate across the study population.

Comparison of authenticated versus unauthenticated users

The study population was stratified by authentication status. Users arriving via the public website (“unauthenticated”) versus users logged in to the patient portal (“authenticated”) and their pre-intent distributions and AI triage recommendations were compared using χ² tests for categorical variables. Authenticated and unauthenticated users were exposed to the same set of CTA categories on the results page (book office visit, start virtual care, find urgent care, find ED, call clinician). In both flows, PCP visits are integrated as a schedulable option. The difference is fulfillment depth (pre-filled portal identity for authenticated users, identity capture during scheduling for unauthenticated users). Walk-in / urgent-care location finders, virtual-care initiation via the partner vendor, and ED-finder CTAs were available to both groups. This asymmetry is important for interpretation of the CTA-engagement results, since CTA engagement is in part a property of the platform rather than of the user.

The AI triage output serves as the panel-adjudicated reference standard throughout this analysis. The triage logic encoded in the engine was developed by a multi-specialty panel of board-certified physicians (primary care, emergency medicine, pediatrics, and virtual care) who systematically adjudicated every possible triage pathway via clinical vignettes. The underlying nurse-triage protocols are used in the majority of U.S. nurse call centers. While random sampling of retrospective AI triage conversations is conducted for ongoing quality assurance, not every individual encounter is independently re-adjudicated by a physician at the per-encounter level. Accordingly, we use neutral, behavior-anchored language throughout (validated, re-directed, escalated, de-escalated). We did not have a parallel physician adjudication for each encounter. Accordingly, we use neutral language throughout (“validated,” “re-directed,” “escalated,” “de-escalated”) rather than “correct” or “inappropriate.”

Pre-intent options and AI triage outputs were placed on a single 8-step acuity ladder from lowest to highest acuity, as follows: 1 = self-care at home, 2 = specialist visit, 3 = video visit (virtual care; placed above “specialist” because virtual care typically carries a same-day urgency that a specialist visit does not), 4 = primary care visit, 5 = retail/walk-in clinic, 6 = urgent care, 7 = emergency department, and 8 = 911/ ambulance (Table 2). An encounter was “validated” if its pre-intent rank equaled its AI rank; “re-directed” otherwise. “Escalated” was defined as pre-intent rank lower than AI rank (i.e., the AI recommended higher-acuity care than the user planned). “De-escalated” was defined as pre-intent rank higher than AI rank. For users who selected more than one pre-intent option, the pre-intent severity was computed as the mean acuity rank across the selections and compared against the AI rank to classify the encounter. Dyad-level analyses are also reported separately (Figs. 3–5).

Users whose triage flow led directly to a 911/ED disposition were not shown the pre-intent question, for safety reasons. As a structural consequence, the highest-acuity AI dispositions are under-represented in the pre-intent denominator. CTA engagement reflects on-platform interaction and not subsequent care completion or arrival. That is a click on a bookable, callable, or initiable element on the results page. Users who completed care off-platform (by phone, in person, or with a different vendor) are not counted as engaged here. CTA engagement is therefore an under-estimate of true adherence to the AI recommendation. We address this caveat further in Limitations.

Follow-up survey of non-engagers

A follow-up questionnaire asking “How do you now plan to seek care?” was offered to users who had not interacted with any call-to-action (CTA) on the results page (n = 636 offered) prior to clicking the button to exit. This question was deliberately about how the user planned to engage with care (e.g., book on-platform, call the office, present to UC/ED, or not seek care) rather than about the level of care they were planning to seek. The four response options were: (a) book care through this site, (b) call the doctor’s office, (c) go to the ER or urgent care, and (d) I don’t plan to book care. The follow-up survey was offered to all users who did not click any CTA prior to attempting to exit the experience. 636 users responded, yielding a response rate of 636/6,772 (9.4%) of the total pre-intent-measured cohort. The narrative results below refer to this responding subset.
