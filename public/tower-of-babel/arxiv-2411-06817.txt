Ultrastrong coupling, nonselective measurement and quantum Zeno dynamics
Stefano Marcantoni, Marco Merkli

Abstract
We study the dynamics of an open quantum system linearly coupled to a bosonic reservoir. We show that, in the ultrastrong coupling limit, the system undergoes a nonselective measurement and then evolves unitarily according to an effective Zeno Hamiltonian. This dynamical process is largely independent of the reservoir state. We examine the entanglement breaking effect of the ultrastrong coupling on the system. We also derive the evolution equation for systems in contact with several reservoirs when one coupling is ultrastrong. The effective system dynamics displays a rich structure and, contrarily to the single reservoir case, it is generally non-Markovian. Our approach is based on a Dyson series expansion, in which we can take the ultrastrong limit termwise, and a subsequent resummation of the series. Our derivation is mathematically rigorous and uncomplicated.

Introduction

An open quantum system is modeled as a bipartite system-reservoir (
{\rm S}{\rm R}
) complex, described by a Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R},

and has a Hamiltonian of the form

H=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm R}+\lambda G\otimes\varphi.

Here,
H_{\rm S}
and
H_{\rm R}
are the system and reservoir Hamiltonians, and
G
,
\varphi
are hermitian operators on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}
determining the interaction. The parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a coupling constant. It is understood that
{\rm S}
is a small system, which for us here means that
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}<\infty
, and that
{\rm R}
is a large system, which means here that
\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm R}=\infty
and that
H_{\rm R}
has a continuum of modes (continuous spectrum). The reduced system state is given by the density matrix

\rho_{\rm S}(t)={\rm tr}_{\rm R}\,\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}\big(e^{itH}\cdot e^{-itH}\big),

where
\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}
is the initial (factorized) system-reservoir state and
{\rm tr}_{\rm R}
denotes the partial trace over the reservoir degrees of freedom. As we explain below,
\omega_{\rm R}
can be a density matrix of
{\rm R}
— but should generally be understood as an ‘expectation functional’.

\tau=\lambda^{2}t

\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. We show that it gives rise to a nonselective measurement of the system followed by a quantum Zeno dynamics.

Of course,
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
depends on the coupling parameter
\lambda

\lambda
. The two extreme cases
\lambda=0
and ‘
\lambda=\infty

, respectively. In the first case, the system
{\rm S}
does not interact with the reservoir
{\rm R}
and it evolves separately, undisturbed by the environment. The case of infinitely strong (ultrastong) coupling is understood in a limit sense when
|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty
. Between the two extreme cases one finds other regimes. The
ultraweak
coupling regime, also called the
van Hove weak coupling regime
, is defined by taking the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
and at the same time, looking at the dynamics for long times

, such that
\lambda^{2}t=\tau
takes finite values
[
44
,

. The intuition is that for weaker interaction strength, one has to wait for a longer time to detect a sizable influence on the system caused by the reservoir — noticeable interaction effects happen over a
coarse grained
time scale parametrized by
\tau
. One can show that the Markovian approximation for the reduced system dynamics is valid for certain systems in the ultraweak coupling regime — leading to an effective approximate evolution given by the Markovian master equation. This was first proved in
[
8
,
9
]
with subsequent refinements
[
36
,
37
]
. If
\lambda
is small but fixed (without taking
\lambda\rightarrow 0
) then we are said to be in the
weak coupling regime
. The smallness of
\lambda
is taken in comparison for example with the Bohr energies of
{\rm S}
(which are the nonvanishing energy eigenvalue differences of
H_{\rm S}
). In this regime, too, the correctness of the Markovian master equation can be validated for certain models
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, using the so called quantum resonance theory. The Markovian regime is particularly suited to describe, for example, matter-radiation interactions and quantum optical systems.

Towards the other extreme, the
strong coupling regime
is characterized by values of
\lambda
which are large compared to the ‘hopping’ terms in
H_{\rm S}
, namely the part of
H_{\rm S}
which does not commute with the interaction operator
G
. In this regime, one can perform a ‘polaron transformation’ after which the
{\rm S}{\rm R}
dynamics is explicitly solvable in the absence of the hopping terms (but in which
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}
are coupled with arbitrary strength
\lambda
). The hopping terms are then treated as a perturbation. This strategy is particularly adapted to the treatment of, for example, the Förster and Marcus theories, describing the excitation energy and charge transfers in quantum chemical and biological processes
[
15
,
19
,
22
,
42
,
26
,
23
]
. A further approach to describe the strong coupling regime is the reaction coordinate method, in which one incorporates some degrees of freedom of the reservoir into the system
[
41
,
1
,
2
]
. The (ultra)strong coupling regime is of interest for static, not only dynamical, properties as well in particular in quantum equilibrium and non-equilibrium thermodynamics

,

,

,

,

.

While there are somewhat general mathematical approaches to describe the ultraweak coupling regime (and the non-interacting one), the mentioned techniques to treat the weak and strong coupling settings are tailored to specific models and a general solution of the problem is not available. A discussion of this fact can be found for instance in
[
43
]
. In the following, we focus on the ultrastrong coupling limit and present a detailed analysis of the open system dynamics in this regime.

There is another,
a priori
quite different perspective on the theory of strong interactions, coming from the study of the quantum Zeno effect. This effect describes the fate of a system subjected to frequent measurements. It is formalized as follows. A system’s density matrix
\rho_{\rm S}
evolves unitarily, according to a Hamiltonian
H_{\rm S}
,

\rho_{\rm S}\mapsto\mathcal{U}_{t}(\rho_{\rm S})=e^{-itH_{\rm S}}\rho_{\rm S}\,e^{itH_{\rm S}}.

Let
\{P_{n}\}
be a complete set of orthogonal projections (
P_{n}P_{m}=\delta_{mn}P_{n}
,
\sum_{n}P_{n}=1
) — think of the
\{P_{n}\}
as the spectral projections of a system observable to be measured. Those projections describe the non-selective measurement on a density matrix of
{\rm S}
by

\rho_{\rm S}\mapsto{\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{\rm S})=\sum_{n}P_{n}\rho_{\rm S}P_{n}.

The evolution of
{\rm S}
interspersed with
N
measurements at time intervals of duration
t/N
each, is

\rho_{\rm S}(t,N)=\big(\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{U}_{t/N}\big)^{N}\rho_{\rm S}.

It is then shown that
[
32
,
11
,
13
,
10
]

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{\rm S}(t,N)=e^{-itH_{\rm Z}}\big(\sum_{n}P_{n}\rho_{\rm S}P_{n}\big)e^{itH_{\rm Z}},

where the right hand side is called the
Zeno dynamics
, with the
Zeno Hamiltonian
given by

H_{\rm Z}=\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{\rm S}P_{n}.

The action of the frequent measurement is thus to cut in
\rho_{\rm S}
correlations between different subspaces
{\rm Ran}P_{n}
— called the
Zeno subspaces
— and to evolve each one independently by the projected Hamiltonian
P_{n}H_{\rm S}P_{n}
. While the dynamics within a block is still carrying a mark of the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm S}
, the partitioning of the space into blocks is determined entirely by the measurement projections
P_{n}
.

The basic intuition for the
connection between the Zeno and the ultrastrong coupling
is that the quantum von Neumann measurements are supposed to happen instantaneously, and the associated very short (zero) time would correspond to a very strong (infinitely strong) interaction with an apparatus. In the above description of the Zeno effect of a system
{\rm S}
, however, there is no explicit mention of an apparatus, or reservoir
{\rm R}
. The question then is,

Q:

Does an ultrastrong
{\rm S}{\rm R}
coupling cause a nonselective measurement and subsequent Zeno dynamics?

Our contribution in the current work is to answer this question in the positive for a large class of open systems where a finite dimensional
{\rm S}
is coupled to a reservoir of bosonic modes
k\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with creation and annihilation operators satisfying
[a(k),a^{\dagger}(l)]=\delta(k-l)
. We consider interaction operators of the form
G\otimes\varphi(g)
, where
G
is a hermitian observable of
{\rm S}
and
\varphi(g)
is the field operator,

G=\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}P_{n},\qquad\varphi(g)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big(g(k)a^{\dagger}(k)+{\rm h.c.}\big)d^{3}k,

the
\gamma_{n}
(which are distinct for distinct
n
) and
P_{n}
are the eigenvalues and eigenprojections of
G
, and
g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3},d^{3}k)
is called the form factor, determining how strongly each mode
k
is coupled to
{\rm S}
. This class of models includes the famous spin-Boson model. Let
\rho_{\rm S}(t)={\rm Tr}_{\rm R}\,\rho_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}(e^{itH}\cdot e^{-itH})
be the reduced system density matrix, obtained by tracing out the reservoir, as introduced above. Our results are as follows.

\bullet
Our
main result
is that for all times
t>0
,

\lim_{|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{\rm S}(t)=e^{-itH_{\rm Z}}\Big(\sum_{n}P_{n}\rho_{\rm S}P_{n}\Big)e^{itH_{\rm Z}}

with Zeno Hamiltonian
H_{\rm Z}=\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{\rm S}P_{n}
. Our result holds for initial states
\omega_{\rm R}
of the reservoir drawn from a large class — including all Gaussian states, such as equilibrium states at any temperature. The ultrastrong coupling limit has an effect on the system (right hand side of the above equation) which is independent of the reservoir state. This answers the above question:

A:

The ultrastrong coupling implements a nonselective measurement of the system coupling operator
G
and a subsequent associated Zeno dynamics.

We complement this main finding with further results:

\bullet
When
{\rm S}
is a many-body system we show that for typical choices of the coupling operator
G
, the ultrastrong coupling limit breaks entanglement between the subunits of the system.

\bullet
We find the dynamics of complexes when
{\rm S}
is coupled to several reservoirs, when one of them is coupled ultrastrongly to
{\rm S}
. We show that this ultrastrong coupling causes a Zeno dynamics for
{\rm S}
and the residual reservoirs. The reduction to
{\rm S}
gives a rich, generally non Markovian dynamics — in contrast to the case of a single reservoir.

Related previous work on the Zeno effect in open systems.
In the previous literature, one line of investigation examines the frequent measurements setup with the unitary dynamics
\mathcal{U}_{t}
replaced by the action of a CPTP (completely positive, trace preserving) semigroup generated by operators in the standard GKSL (Gorini-Kossakovski-Sudarshan-Lindblad) form
[
4
]
. That approach differs from ours because it considers the effect of the reservoir implicitly — the reservoir is already ‘traced out’ before the frequent measurements are performed — and it is assumed that the system dynamics is
Markovian
(GKSL semigroup). This is a different physical model from ours as we investigate a microscopic
{\rm S}{\rm R}
model and show that the ultrastrong coupling produces a Zeno effect on
{\rm S}
. There is a remarkable current activity in the mathematical analysis aimed at finding explicit error bounds for the deviation of the Zeno dynamics from the dynamics caused by frequent (but not infinitely frequent) measurements, under generic assumptions on the structure of the semigroups. See for instance the works
[
4
,
5
,
33
,
39
]
.

Some literature is more in line with our approach, where the reservoirs are explicitly included in the description. In
[
42
]
the author derives a Markovian master equation in the strong coupling limit, starting from a system-bath model. They exhibit the emergence of the Zeno dynamics and corrections to it (large but finite
\lambda
). Their method is based on second order perturbation theory in the hopping terms in
H_{\rm S}
, with uncontrolled higher order terms.
In
[
34
]
the authors analyze an unstable (3-level) system interacting with a radiation field (oscillators) and they compare the decay, or de-excitation rates (Fermi Golden Rule) of the system in the presence and in the absence of an additional strong laser field illuminating the system (the measurement apparatus), revealing that the decay is slowed down by the laser, in accordance with the Zeno effect. In
[
12
]
the authors find that the decay rates of a three level system can also be enhanced, depending on which level transitions the laser couples strongly to (‘inverse’ or anti-Zeno effect). In
[
16
]
a Schrödinger particle in a Coulomb field subjected to a strong interaction with a monochromatic electromagnetic wave is analyzed. The author shows that for small times, the decay of the particle follows a law consistent with the Zeno effect (as opposed to an exponential decay). In
[
40
]
the authors consider the spin-boson model (discrete modes) and analyze the short-time decay rates of the initially excited spin. They detect that depending on the strength of the interaction with the bath, the decay rate is decreasing or increasing in the coupling parameter — hence revealing a Zeno or anti-Zeno behaviour. In
[
35
]
quantum measurement processes were studied in view of the Zeno effect, and connected to experiments on inhibited stimulated emissions in a matter-radiation systems. In comparison with these works, our results are quite general (valid and independent for a large class of reservoir states) and are mathematically rigorous and quite uncomplicated. That said, so far we only consider
|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty
, which is a simpler regime than
\lambda
finite but large. Work on that harder regime is under way.

Setup and main results

2.1
The model

A
d

k\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and has an associated bosonic creation and annihilation operators
a^{\dagger}(k),a(k)
, satisfying the canonical commutation relation
[a(k),a^{\dagger}(l)]=\delta(k-l)

H=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm R}+\lambda G\otimes\varphi(g),

(1)

where

is the system Hamiltonian, that is a
d\times d

H_{\rm R}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\omega(k)a^{\dagger}(k)a(k)d^{3}k

(2)

is the reservoir Hamiltonian. In (
2

\omega(k)\geq 0

(3)

\varphi(g)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big(g(k)a^{\dagger}(k)+{\rm h.c.}\big)d^{3}k,

We give a proof of Theorem

2.3
Ultrastrong coupling breaks entanglement within
{\rm S}

Consider a system
{\rm S}
consisting of
N
subsystems
{\rm S}_{1},\ldots,{\rm S}_{N}
, described by the Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}={\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm S},1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm S},N},

possibly of varying finite subsystem dimensions. To each subsystem
n
, we associate a hermitian interaction operator
G_{n}
acting on
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm S},n}
and we write for simplicity,

G_{n}\equiv\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}.

Wen consider total interaction operators of the form

H_{\rm SR}=G\otimes\varphi(g),\qquad G=F(G_{1},\ldots,G_{N}),

(14)

where
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function of
N
variables. Examples are,

F(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}G_{n},\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad F(G_{1},\ldots,G_{N})=G_{1}\cdots G_{N}.

(However, see (
19
) for reasonable interactions which are not of this form — and which lead to outcomes different from the ones discussed here.)
The
{\rm S}{\rm R}
Hamiltonian is given by

H=H_{\rm S}+H_{\rm R}+\lambda H_{{\rm S}{\rm R}},

(15)

where
H_{\rm S}
is a hermitian operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}
,
H_{\rm R}
is given by (
2
) and
H_{{\rm S}{\rm R}}
is as in (
14
). We denote the spectral decomposition of
G_{n}
by

G_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{\nu_{n}}\gamma_{n}(l)P_{n}(l),

where
1\leq\nu_{n}\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm S},n}
and the
\{\gamma_{n}(l)\}_{l=1}^{\nu_{n}}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
G_{n}
with associated eigenprojections
P_{n}(l)
of dimensions
\geq 1
. The eigenvalues of
G
are

{\rm spec}(G)=\big\{F(\gamma_{1}(l_{1}),\ldots,\gamma_{N}(l_{N}))\ :\ l_{j}=1,\ldots,\nu_{j}\big\}.

(16)

We will call the coupling
nondegenerate
if all eigenvalues of
G
are simple. This necessitates in particular that
\dim P_{j}(l)=1
for all
j
and all
l
. As we explain below, the nondegeneracy can be viewed as a
generic
situation. For nondegenerate couplings each eigenvalue

\Gamma(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})\equiv F(\gamma_{1}(l_{1}),\ldots,\gamma_{N}(l_{N}))

of
G
has the associated rank one eigenprojection

P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})\equiv P_{1}(l_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N}(l_{N}),

leading to the spectral decomposition

G=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}\Gamma(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}).

The density matrix of the system resulting from the nonselective measurement on
S
implemented by the strong coupling with the reservoir is (Theorem
1
),

\rho_{\rm S}(0_{+})\equiv\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})\rho_{\rm S}P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}p_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}P_{1}(l_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N}(l_{N}),

where the
0\leq p_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}\leq 1
satisfy
\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}p_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}=1
(they are the diagonal matrix elements of
\rho_{\rm S}
, therefore probabilities). Furthermore, from (
12
),

H_{\rm Z}=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})H_{\rm S}P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}h_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}P_{1}(l_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N}(l_{N}),

(17)

where
h_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}
are the matrix elements of
H_{\rm S}
. Then

e^{-itH_{\rm Z}}=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}e^{-ith_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}}P(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})

and we obtain from Theorem
1
that for all
t>0
,

\rho_{\rm S}(t)=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}p_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{N}}P_{1}(l_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{N}(l_{N}).

(18)

The relation (
18
) shows that for nondegenerate couplings,
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
is time independent (
t>0)
and
separable
, regardless of whether the system state
\rho_{\rm S}
before the contact with
{\rm R}
was separable or entangled. We conclude that the ultrastrongly coupled reservoir acts as an entanglement breaking channel on
{\rm S}
. The entanglement breaking effect is happening independently of the particular choice of the coupling
G
of the form (
14
), and regardless of whether
H_{\rm S}
is a local Hamiltonian or not.

Genericness of the nondegeneracy.

In a sense, the nondegeneracy of the spectrum of
G
is a generic situation, even in ‘homogeneous’ systems. Consider for instance
N
qubits,
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm S},n}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
for each
n
, each one coupled to the reservoir via
G_{n}=\sigma_{x}
(Pauli
x
operator with eigenvalues
\pm 1
). If
F
, (
14
) is a symmetric function in its
N
variables, then of course the corresponding spectrum (
16
) is degenerate. For instance, for
F(G_{1},\ldots,G_{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}G_{n}
, the eigenvalue
\Gamma=0
of
G
is
N\choose N/2
-fold degenerate (
N
even). For
F(G_{1},\ldots,G_{N})=G_{1}\cdots G_{N}
, the degeneracies of the two eigenvalues
\pm 1
of
G
are even higher, equal to
2^{N-1}
. These exact symmetries leading to eigenvalue degeneracy are very special and unstable, though. Indeed, each qubit, even if being fabricated of the same material, will not generally have
exactly
the same energy levels (or eigenvalues of
G
), because variations naturally occur due to production imprecision or laboratory operating conditions. One may then consider that the levels of each operator
G_{n}
will slightly deviate from the precise values
\pm 1
. This can be modeled by taking for
G_{n}
a random matrix of the form, say,

G_{n}=\sigma_{x}+\mu\xi_{n},

where
\mu
is a strength parameter and the
\xi_{n}
are a family of
N
real-valued independent, identically distributed random variables with a continuous distribution, like a centered Gaussian. As mentioned above, for
\mu=0
the eigenvalue zero of
G=\sum_{n=1}^{N}G_{n}
for instance, and the eigenvalues
\pm 1
of
G=G_{1}\cdots G_{N}
are degenerate. But as soon as
\mu\neq 0
, all eigenvalues are simple, almost surely (in the sense of probability theory).

Example 1.

We close our discussion with an example showing that entanglement can be preserved if the interaction
G
is not of the form (
14
). Consider
{\rm S}
to be made of two qubits and let

G=\sigma_{+}\otimes\sigma_{-}+\sigma_{-}\otimes\sigma_{+},

(19)

where
\sigma_{\pm}
are the raising and lowering operators in the
\sigma_{z}
eigenbasis
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
, where
\sigma_{z}|0\rangle=|0\rangle
,
\sigma_{z}|1\rangle=-|1\rangle
. The Bell states

\ket{\psi_{\pm}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}\pm\ket{11}),\qquad\ket{\phi_{\pm}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{01}\pm\ket{10})

are the eigenvectors of
G
:
G\ket{\phi_{\pm}}=\pm\ket{\phi_{\pm}}
and
G\ket{\psi_{\pm}}=0
. Therefore, the projections appearing in Theorem
1
are

P_{+}=\ket{\phi_{+}}\bra{\phi_{+}},\quad P_{-}=\ket{\phi_{-}}\bra{\phi_{-}},\quad P_{0}=\ket{\psi_{+}}\bra{\psi_{+}}+\ket{\psi_{-}}\bra{\psi_{-}}.

It is manifest that
\rho_{\rm S}(0_{+})
, given by (
11
) with
t=0_{+}
, can be entangled. For instance the initial state
\rho_{\rm S}=|\phi_{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{+}|
is invariant under the projective measurement. It will therefore remain entangled after the action of the measurement. The difference with (
14
) is that there, each subsystem involves only one operator
G_{j}
, while in (
19
) two non-commuting ones are involved for each qubit:
\sigma_{+}
and
\sigma_{-}
.

2.4
Multiple reservoirs, multiple measurements

Consider the system
{\rm S}
coupled to two independent reservoirs
{\rm R}1
and
{\rm R}2
, according to the Hamiltonian

H=H_{\rm S}+H_{{\rm R}1}+\lambda_{1}G_{1}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1})+H_{{\rm R}2}+\lambda_{2}G_{2}\otimes\varphi_{2}(g_{2})

(20)

acting on the Hilbert space

{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm R}1}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm R}2}.

It is understood in the notation that
H_{{\rm R}1},\varphi_{1}
and
H_{{\rm R}2},\varphi_{2}
are observables pertaining only to the first and second reservoir, respectively, while
H_{\rm S},G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are hermitian operators on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}
.

Formally, one may understand (
20
) to be of the form (
1
), with the pair
{\rm S}
and
{\rm R}1
making a new ‘system’. By keeping
\lambda_{1}
fixed and taking
|\lambda_{2}|\rightarrow\infty
, Theorem
1
would then show that the dynamics of
{\rm S}
plus
{\rm R}1
is given by a nonselective measurement relative to the spectral projections
P_{l}
of
G_{2}
followed by a Zeno dynamics with Hamiltonian

H_{\rm Z}=\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}\big(H_{\rm S}+H_{{\rm R}1}+\lambda_{1}G_{1}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1})\big)P_{l}.

(21)

The caveat is that Theorem
1
was shown for a finite dimensional
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm S}
, so it is not immediately applicable to this situation. Nevertheless, we now demonstrate that this result is correct, provided the state
\omega_{{\rm R}1}
satisfies the following regularity condition: For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\;\sup_{0\leq t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{j}\leq t}\Big|\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(\varphi_{1}(e^{i\omega t_{1}}g_{1})\cdots\varphi_{1}(e^{i\omega t_{j}}g_{1})\Big)\Big|\leq B_{n},

(22)

for some numbers
B_{n}\geq 0
satisfying

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{n!}B_{n}<\infty\qquad\mbox{for any $\alpha>0$.}

(23)

The conditions (
22
) and (
23
) hold in particular for quasifree states
\omega_{\rm R}
for which Wick’s theorem applies (such as equilibrium states at any temperature). Those states satisfy for
n\in\mathbb{N}

[
3
]

\omega_{\rm R}\big(\varphi(f_{1})\cdots\varphi(f_{2n})\big)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\prod_{(p_{1},p_{2})\in p}\omega_{\rm R}\big(\varphi(f_{p_{1}})\varphi(f_{p_{2}})\big),

where the
f_{1},\ldots,f_{2n}
are arbitrary functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},d^{3}k)
(and odd moments vanish). The number of pairings
p
is
|\mathcal{P}|=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}
and by using
|\omega_{\rm R}(\varphi(f)\varphi(g))|\leq C
one readily verifies that (
22
) and (
23
) hold. This gives a rich and physically relevant class of reservoir states we can treat.

Theorem 3
.

Assume the condition (
22
), (
23
) and let
P_{l}
,
l=1,\ldots,\nu
be the spectral projections of
G_{2}
.
Then, for any observable
A
of the system and any

we have

\lim_{|\lambda_{2}|\to\infty}\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\Big(e^{itH}(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\rm R}2})e^{-itH}\Big)=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(e^{itH_{\rm Z}}\big(\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}AP_{l}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{{\rm R}1}\big)e^{-itH_{\rm Z}}\Big),

(24)

where
H_{\rm Z}
is the Zeno Hamiltonian (
21
).

We present a proof of Theorem
3
in Section
3.2
. Note that the system part of the coupling operator in
H_{\rm Z}
, (
21
)
after
the ultrastrong coupling interaction
|\lambda_{2}|\rightarrow\infty
is

G^{\prime}_{1}=\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}G_{1}P_{l}.

(25)

Therefore, if we perform the
|\lambda_{1}|\rightarrow\infty
limit (after
|\lambda_{2}|\rightarrow\infty
), then the resulting nonselective measurement of
{\rm S}
is performed according to the observable
G^{\prime}_{1}
which commutes with
G_{2}
. As a consequence, by having
{\rm S}
in contact with several reservoirs and sequentially taking ultrastrong coupling limits to the different reservoirs, one cannot implement sequential nonselective measurements associated to incompatible (not commuting) observables. This stems from the fact that the first ultrastrong coupling affects the interaction operators of all following coupling processes.

If one did want to implement successive nonselective measurements of non-commuting system observables
G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}
, one would need to couple the system
{\rm S}
to
m
individual environments in a successive manner, one at the time. This is the
collision model
setup, where
{\rm S}
interacts (‘collides’) with
{\rm R}1
alone first, then is decoupled from
{\rm R}1
and collides with
{\rm R}2
alone, and so on. The resulting system state after the first collision is given by (
11
) (take
H_{\rm S}=0
)
\rho_{\rm S}^{(1)}=\sum_{l_{1}}P^{(1)}_{l_{1}}\rho_{\rm S}P^{(1)}_{l_{1}}
. This is the initial state for the second collision, after which the system state is
\rho_{\rm S}^{(2)}=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}}P^{(1)}_{l_{2}}P^{(1)}_{l_{1}}\rho_{\rm S}P_{l_{1}}^{(1)}P_{l_{2}}^{(2)}
. After
m
collisions, the system is in the state

\rho_{\rm S}^{(m)}=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{m}}P^{(m)}_{l_{m}}\cdots P^{(1)}_{l_{1}}\rho_{\rm S}P^{(1)}_{l_{1}}\cdots P^{(m)}_{l_{m}},

where
P_{l}^{(j)}
are the spectral projections of
G_{j}
, the operator describing the interaction of
{\rm S}
with the
j
th reservoir (as per (
1
)).

Let us finally discuss the system density matrix
\rho_{\rm S}(t)
associated to (
24
), which is defined by setting
{\rm tr}_{\rm S}(\rho_{\rm S}(t)A)
equal to (
24
) for all
A
. Suppose that all the projections are rank one,
P_{l}=|\psi_{l}\rangle\langle\psi_{l}|
, that is, all the eigenvalues of
G_{2}
are simple, then

e^{-itH_{\rm Z}}=\sum_{l}P_{l}\otimes e^{-it(h_{l}+H_{{\rm R}1}+\lambda_{1}\eta_{l}\varphi(g_{1}))},

(26)

where
\eta_{l}=\langle\psi_{l},G_{1}\psi_{l}\rangle
and
h_{l}=\langle\psi_{l},H_{\rm S}\psi_{l}\rangle
. Substituting (
26
) into (
24
) we see that the system density matrix is constant in time after the first measurement and is in a state which only depends on the interaction with
{\rm R}2
.
The system is entirely decoupled from
{\rm R}1
by the ultrastrong coupling to
{\rm R}2
.
This happens if all measurement projections (associated to
G_{2}
) have rank one.
However
, a
G_{2}
with degenerate spectrum does not in general decouple
{\rm S}
from
{\rm R}1
and leads to a rich, usually non-Markovian, dynamics of
{\rm S}
. We illustrate this with an example.

Example 2.

Take a two qubit system interacting with two reservoirs
{\rm R}1
and
{\rm R}2
as per (
20
), with a coupling

G_{2}=\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{2}+\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{1}\otimes\sigma_{z}.

Here,
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{1},\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{2}
are the identities on the first and second qubit, respectively (so
G_{2}
acts on both qubits).
The spectral projections of
G_{2}
are
P_{+}=\ket{00}\bra{00}
,
P_{-}=\ket{11}\bra{11}
(both rank one) and
P_{0}=\ket{01}\bra{01}+\ket{10}\bra{10}
(rank two). A nontrivial evolution will generally take place in the two dimensional Zeno subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm eff}:={\rm Ran}P_{0}
, which can be identified as the state space of an
effective single qubit
. The dynamics generated by the Zeno Hamiltonian leaves
{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm eff}
invariant — leading to a dynamics of the effective qubit. Namely, let

\rho_{\rm S}=\frac{1+z}{2}\ket{01}\bra{01}+\frac{1-z}{2}\ket{10}\bra{10}+\frac{c}{2}\ket{01}\bra{10}+\frac{c^{*}}{2}\ket{10}\bra{01}

(27)

be an initial density matrix, with
-1\leq z\leq 1
and
0\leq|c|^{2}\leq 1-z^{2}
.
After the measurement and Zeno evolution to time
t>0
, the effective qubit state is again of the form (
27
), with time dependent
z(t)
and
c(t)
. For instance, if

H_{\rm S}=G_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{2}+\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{1}\otimes\varepsilon_{2}\sigma_{z}

we find that
z(t)=z
(constant populations), while the coherence is

c(t)=e^{2it\Delta}D(t)c,\qquad D(t)=\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(W\big(\lambda_{1}\Delta\tfrac{e^{i\omega t}-1}{i\omega}g_{1}\big)\Big),

with
\Delta=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
. This is the well known pure dephasing model. If
\omega_{{\rm R}1}
is in thermal equilibrium at inverse temperature
\beta
, the decoherence function
D(t)
has the explicit expression,

D_{\beta}(t)=\exp\Big[-\lambda^{2}_{1}\Delta^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|g_{1}(k)|^{2}\coth(\beta\omega(k)/2)\frac{\sin^{2}(\omega(k)t/2)}{\omega^{2}(k)}d^{3}k\Big].

Depending on the explicit form of
g(k)
and on the value of
\beta
, the decoherence function
D_{\beta}(t)
may be non-monotonic in
t
, thus describing a non-Markovian quantum evolution
[
18
]
. In this respect the system dynamics resulting from the ultrastrong coupling in the presence of several reservoirs is richer than for a single reservoir — the latter is always Markovian because it is the composition of a projective measurement and a unitary evolution. By choosing appropriate
H_{\rm S}
and
G_{1}
one will get dissipative qubit evolutions where populations are modified as well.

The following relations are well known,

\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}\big(e^{itK}\big(B(\lambda)\otimes W(h)\big)e^{-itK}\mathcal{D}^{*}\big)=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}\Big(\big(\mathcal{D}e^{itK}\big(B(\lambda)^{*}\otimes W(-h)\big)e^{-itK}\big)^{*}\Big)

we see that
T_{3}
is the complex conjugate of
T_{2}
with
B(\lambda)
replaced by
B(\lambda)^{*}
and
h
replaced by
-h
. So we obtain from (
52
),

\displaystyle\lim_{|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty}T_{3}=\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}\omega_{\rm S}\big(P_{l}AP_{l}(e^{-itP_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})\big)\omega_{\rm R}\big(W(e^{i\omega t}h)\big).

(53)

\bullet
We take the limit
|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty
of
T_{4}
(see (
31
)). We have

\displaystyle T_{4}=

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}\big(\mathcal{D}e^{itK}\big(B(\lambda)\otimes W(h)\big)e^{-itK}\mathcal{D}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{0},r_{0}=1}^{\nu}\,e^{-\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{2}(\gamma^{2}_{l_{0}}-\gamma^{2}_{r_{0}}){\rm Im}\langle g,\frac{e^{-i\omega t}+it\omega}{\omega^{2}}g\rangle}

\displaystyle\ \times\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{\rm R}\big(\mathcal{D}P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}W\big((e^{it\omega}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})+e^{i\omega t}h\big)\mathcal{D}^{*}\big).

(54)

Next, from the definition of
\mathcal{D}
, (
29
)

\displaystyle DP_{l_{0}}

\displaystyle AP_{r_{0}}W\big((e^{it\omega}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})+e^{i\omega t}h\big)\mathcal{D}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 1}i^{n}(-i)^{m}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}\int_{0\leq s_{m}\leq\cdots\leq s_{1}\leq t}

\displaystyle\times H_{\rm S}(t_{n})\cdots H_{\rm S}(t_{1})P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}W\big((e^{it\omega}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})+e^{i\omega t}h\big)H_{\rm S}(s_{1})\cdots H_{\rm S}(s_{m}).

(55)

We use Lemma
1
to get

\displaystyle H_{\rm S}(t_{n})\cdots H_{\rm S}(t_{1})P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}W\big((e^{it\omega}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})+e^{i\omega t}h\big)H_{\rm S}(s_{1})\cdots H_{\rm S}(s_{m})

\displaystyle\,=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}=1}^{\nu}\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}=1}^{\nu}P_{l_{n}}H_{\rm S}P_{l_{n-1}}\cdots P_{l_{1}}H_{\rm S}P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}H_{\rm S}P_{r_{1}}\cdots P_{r_{m-1}}H_{\rm S}P_{r_{m}}

\displaystyle\quad\times W\big((e^{it_{n}\omega}-1)(f_{l_{n}}-f_{l_{n-1}})\big)\cdots W\big((e^{it_{1}\omega}-1)(f_{l_{1}}-f_{l_{0}})\big)W\big((e^{it\omega}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})+e^{i\omega t}h\big)

\displaystyle\quad\times W\big((e^{is_{1}\omega}-1)(f_{r_{0}}-f_{r_{1}})\big)\cdots W\big((e^{is_{m}\omega}-1)(f_{r_{m-1}}-f_{r_{m}})\big)

\displaystyle\,=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}=1}^{\nu}\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}=1}^{\nu}e^{-i\Phi_{4}}P_{l_{n}}H_{\rm S}P_{l_{n-1}}\cdots P_{l_{1}}H_{\rm S}P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}H_{\rm S}P_{r_{1}}\cdots P_{r_{m-1}}H_{\rm S}P_{r_{m}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\times W\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(e^{it_{j}\omega}-1)(f_{l_{j}}-f_{l_{j-1}})\big)+\sum_{k=1}^{m}(e^{is_{k}\omega}-1)(f_{r_{k-1}}-f_{r_{k}})+(e^{i\omega t}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})+e^{i\omega t}h\Big).

(56)

The phase
\Phi_{4}
comes from the commutation relations of the Weyl operators when we combine their products into a single one. It satisfies

\Phi_{4}=0\qquad\mbox{if $l_{n}=\cdots=l_{1}=l_{0}=r_{0}=r_{1}=\cdots=r_{m}$.}

The expectation of the Weyl operator in (
56
) in
\omega_{\rm R}
is,

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{4}\big\|\mathcal{C}^{1/2}\big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{e^{it_{j}\omega}-1}{i\omega}\lambda(\gamma_{l_{j}}-\gamma_{l_{j-1}})g+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{e^{is_{k}\omega}-1}{i\omega}\lambda(\gamma_{r_{k-1}}-\gamma_{r_{k}})g+\frac{e^{it\omega}-1}{i\omega}\lambda(\gamma_{l_{0}}-\gamma_{r_{0}})g+e^{i\omega t}h\big]\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}}}.

(57)

By replicating the proof of Lemma
2
we see that unless
\gamma_{l_{n}}=\cdots=\gamma_{l_{0}}=\gamma_{r_{0}}=\cdots=\gamma_{r_{m}}
, we have that the limit of (
57
) as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
is zero, for all
t>0
and all
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n},s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
(except possibly a set of measure zero for the
(t_{1},\ldots,s_{m})
). Using this together with (
54
), (
55
) (
56
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}T_{4}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{m\geq 1}i^{n}(-i)^{m}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}\int_{0\leq s_{m}\leq\cdots\leq s_{1}\leq t}

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}\omega_{\rm S}\big((P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l})^{n}P_{l}AP_{l}(P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l})^{m}\big)\omega_{\rm R}(W(e^{i\omega t}h))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}\omega_{\rm S}\big((e^{itP_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})P_{l}AP_{l}(e^{-itP_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})\big)\ \omega_{\rm R}(W(e^{i\omega t}h)).

(58)

Summing up the limits as
|\lambda|\rightarrow\infty
of
T_{1}
to
T_{4}
according to (
39
), (
52
), (
53
) and (
58
), we arrive at the result (
13
). This completes the proof of Theorem
2
. ∎

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Theorem
3
is proved similarly to Theorem
2
. We highlight the details that differ. Define a new operator
K
(compare with (
28
))

K=H_{{\rm R}1}+H_{{\rm R}2}+\lambda_{2}G_{2}\otimes\varphi_{2}(g_{2}),

so that

e^{itK}=e^{itH_{{\rm R}1}}\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}e^{it(H_{{\rm R}2}+\lambda_{2}\gamma_{l}\varphi_{2}(g_{2}))}.

Here, the
P_{l}
and
\gamma_{l}
are the spectral projections (of dimension
\geq 1
) and the distinct eigenvalues of
G_{2}
,

G_{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}\gamma_{l}P_{l}.

Formally the Dyson series reads

e^{itH}e^{-itK}=\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}+\mathcal{D},\qquad\mathcal{D}=\sum_{n\geq 1}i^{n}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}\widetilde{H}(t_{n})\cdots\widetilde{H}(t_{1})

where (as in Lemma
1
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{itK}(H-K)e^{-itK}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,r=1}^{\nu}P_{l}\left(H_{\rm S}+\lambda_{1}G_{1}\otimes\varphi_{1}(e^{i\omega t}g_{1})\right)P_{r}

\displaystyle\qquad\times W_{2}\big((e^{i\omega t}-1)(f_{l}-f_{r})\big)e^{-\frac{i}{2}{\rm Im}\big\langle(e^{i\omega t}-1-i\omega t)(f_{l}-f_{r}),(f_{l}+f_{r})\big\rangle}.

A technical difference with respect to the previous case (single reservoir) is that the presence of the field operator
\varphi_{1}
makes
\widetilde{H}(t)
an unbounded operator. The condition (
22
) makes sure the Dyson series converges in the weak sense, that is, when the state
\omega_{{\rm R}1}
is applied, as we explain below. As in the proof of Theorem
2
, we set

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\big(e^{itH}\big(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\big)e^{-itH}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad=

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\big(e^{itK}\big(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\big)e^{-itK}\big)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\big(\mathcal{D}e^{itK}\big(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\big)e^{-itK}\big)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\big(e^{itK}\big(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\big)e^{-itK}\mathcal{D}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\big(\mathcal{D}e^{itK}\big(A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\big)e^{-itK}\mathcal{D}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\equiv

\displaystyle T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}

and we take
|\lambda_{2}|\to\infty
in each term. Here,
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}
is the identity operator acting on both reservoir Hilbert spaces. In the expression of
T_{1}
the free Hamiltonian of the first reservoir
H_{{\rm R}1}
drops out (the propagator commutes with
A\otimes\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}
) and the expression reads exactly as (
37
) with
h=0
and
\lambda_{2}
in place of
\lambda
. Therefore, thanks to Lemma
1
, we obtain (see also (
39
) with
h=0
),

\lim_{|\lambda_{2}|\to\infty}T_{1}=\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}\omega_{\rm S}(P_{l}AP_{l}).

(59)

Next we analyze
T_{2}
. Proceeding as in the derivation of (
43
) we now obtain

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}2}\Bigg(\sum_{n\geq 1}\,\sum_{l_{0},\ldots,l_{n},r_{0}=1}^{\nu}\!\!\!i^{n}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}\!\!\!\!\!e^{-\frac{i}{2}\Phi(\lambda_{2}^{2})}P_{l_{n}}\widehat{H}(t_{n})P_{l_{n-1}}\cdots P_{l_{1}}\widehat{H}(t_{1})P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}

\displaystyle\quad\times W_{2}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(e^{it_{j}\omega}-1)(f_{l_{j}}-f_{l_{j-1}})+(e^{it\omega}-1)(f_{l_{0}}-f_{r_{0}})\Big)\Bigg),

(60)

where

\widehat{H}(t)=H_{\rm S}+\lambda_{1}G_{1}\otimes\varphi_{1}(e^{i\omega t}g_{1}).

We now show that the right side of (
60
) is well defined. We have
|\omega_{{\rm R}2}(W_{2}(f))|\leq 1
for any
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{3}},d^{3}k)
and

\left|\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\left(P_{l_{n}}\widehat{H}(t_{n})P_{l_{n-1}}\cdots P_{l_{1}}\widehat{H}(t_{1})P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}\right)\right|\leq\|A\|2^{n}C^{n}B_{n},

where
C=\max\{\|H_{\rm S}\|,|\lambda_{1}|\,\|G_{1}\|\}
and
B_{n}
is as in (
22
). Then, due to (
23
),

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\,\sum_{l_{0},\ldots,l_{n},r_{0}=1}^{\nu}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}\left|\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\left(P_{l_{n}}\widehat{H}(t_{n})P_{l_{n-1}}\cdots P_{l_{1}}\widehat{H}(t_{1})P_{l_{0}}AP_{r_{0}}\right)\omega_{{\rm R}2}\Big(W_{2}\big(\lambda_{2}h_{n}\big)\Big)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leq\|A\|\sum_{n\geq 1}\nu^{n+2}\frac{(2Ct)^{n}}{n!}B_{n}<\infty.

(61)

Furthermore, the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}
on the left side in (
61
) converges uniformly in
\lambda_{2}
and so we can pull the limit
|\lambda_{2}|\rightarrow\infty
inside the sum in (
60
) to obtain,

\displaystyle\lim_{|\lambda_{2}|\to\infty}T_{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}\sum_{n\geq 1}i^{n}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}P_{l}\widehat{H}(t_{n})P_{l}\cdots P_{l}\widehat{H}(t_{1})P_{l}AP_{l}\Big)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}\big(e^{it(H_{{\rm R}1}+P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}+\lambda_{1}P_{l}G_{1}P_{l}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1}))}e^{-itH_{{\rm R}1}}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\big)P_{l}AP_{l}\Big).

(62)

The first equality in (
62
) is obtained as before using Lemma
2
which constrains
r_{0}=l_{0}=l_{1}=\ldots=l_{n}
(then also making the phase
\Phi
in (
60
) vanish). To arrive at the second equality in (
62
) we used the Dyson series expansion

e^{it(H_{{\rm R}1}+P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}+\lambda_{1}P_{l}G_{1}P_{l}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1}))}e^{-itH_{{\rm R}1}}=\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}+\sum_{n\geq 1}i^{n}\int_{0\leq t_{n}\leq\cdots\leq t_{1}\leq t}P_{l}\widehat{H}(t_{n})P_{l}\cdots P_{l}\widehat{H}(t_{1})P_{l}.

Just as in the single reservoir case, the term
T_{3}
can be obtained from
T_{2}
by a suitable complex conjugation (see before (
53
)),

\lim_{|\lambda_{2}|\to\infty}T_{3}=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}AP_{l}(e^{itH_{{\rm R}1}}e^{-it(H_{{\rm R}1}+P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}+\lambda_{1}P_{l}G_{1}P_{l}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1}))}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})P_{l}\Big).

(63)

Finally, the term
T_{4}
is again treated similarly. We need to make sure the double Dyson series in
n
and
m
converges weakly (compare with (
55
)). It suffices check that the absolute double series converges, which is shown by noticing that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\alpha^{n+m}}{n!\,m!}B_{n+m}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k\geq n}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{n!(k-n)!}B_{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{k!}B_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{k}\binom{k}{n}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{2^{k}\alpha^{k}}{k!}B_{k}<\infty.

We then obtain,

\displaystyle\lim_{|\lambda_{2}|\to\infty}T_{4}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\rm S}\otimes\omega_{{\rm R}1}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{\nu}P_{l}(e^{it(H_{{\rm R}1}+P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}+\lambda_{1}P_{l}G_{1}P_{l}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1}))}e^{-itH_{{\rm R}1}}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})P_{l}AP_{l}

\displaystyle\quad\times(e^{itH_{{\rm R}1}}e^{-it(H_{{\rm R}1}+P_{l}H_{\rm S}P_{l}+\lambda_{1}P_{l}G_{1}P_{l}\otimes\varphi_{1}(g_{1}))}-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul})P_{l}\Big).

(64)

Finally, summing the four contributions (
59
), (
62
), (
63
) and (
64
) gives the result (
24
). This completes the proof of Theorem
3
.
