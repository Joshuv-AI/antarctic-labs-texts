Evaluation of a canine hemoglobin A1c point-of-care assay: analytical validation, clinical utility, and establishing a population-based reference interval
Authors: Denzel Adam, Nicholas Noto, Michael F Rosser, Sarah Connolly, Arnon Gal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) assay validation is essential for establishing analytical reliability, evaluating discrimination between diabetic and non-diabetic (NDM) dogs, and establishing reference intervals.Hypothesis/ObjectivesValidate a canine HbA1c point-of-care analyzer (Vet Chroma™), evaluate its diagnostic performance, and establish a reference interval in healthy dogs.AnimalsFifty-one diabetic, 44 NDM controls, and 42 healthy client-owned dogs.MethodsProspective validation of precision, linearity, interference, and storage stability. Method comparison used an immunoturbidimetric comparator assay (Olympus DxC 700 AU). Diagnostic performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, likelihood ratios, and odds ratios.ResultsAverage intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were 3.92% and 8.47%. Linearity over the clinical range (0.86%-6.27% HbA1c%) was strong (R2 = 0.973) with minor constant bias. HbA1c remained stable for 5 days at 4 °C and up to 3 months at both -20 and -80 °C, but not reliably at 6 months. Lipemia and icterus exhibited variable interfering effects. Method comparison indicated non-interchangeability with the comparator assay. Diabetic dogs had significantly higher HbA1c than NDM (mean 5.10% vs 1.58%). ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.969, with a cutoff > 3.22% achieving 90.20% sensitivity and 100% specificity. The positive likelihood ratio was not estimable, and the odds ratio was 22.90 (95% CI, 3.60–146.90). Healthy young dogs’ reference interval was 1.25% (90% CI, 1.19–1.59) to 2.75% (90% CI, 2.56–2.90).Conclusions and clinical importanceThe Vet Chroma™ provides reliable HbA1c measurements in canine whole blood and effectively discriminates diabetic from non-diabetic dogs. Results should be interpreted alongside clinical and clinicopathologic findings.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is among the most frequently diagnosed endocrine diseases in dogs.1 Although effective management is often achievable, dogs with poorly controlled DM frequently develop secondary complications, including diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome, and cataract formation.1,2 The diagnosis of DM in dogs is based on the presence of characteristic clinical signs, such as polyuria (PU), polydipsia (PD), polyphagia (PP), and weight loss, together with clinicopathological evidence, primarily persistent fasting hyperglycemia and glucosuria.1,2 Measurement of serum fructosamine is also commonly used to assess average glycemic status over the preceding 1-2 weeks.3 Fructosamine measures glycated serum proteins, primarily albumin, and its concentration might be influenced by serum albumin levels, disorders affecting albumin metabolism (eg, protein-losing nephropathy/enteropathy, hyperthyroidism), or hyperlipidemia.4,5 Current approaches to monitoring glycemic control in insulin-treated dogs include assessment of clinical response, serial blood glucose (BG) curves, continuous glucose monitoring systems, serum fructosamine concentrations, and urine glucose testing.3 Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), a glycated form of hemoglobin, increases in proportion to BG concentration and provides an estimate of long-term glycemic control. In contrast to fructosamine, HbA1c is less affected by non-glycemic variables and reflects average BG over the lifespan of erythrocytes, which is approximately 3-4 months in dogs.6 However, HbA1c% might also be influenced by anemia and other conditions that alter erythrocyte lifespan or turnover.7 In human medicine, HbA1c, expressed as a percentage of total hemoglobin (HbA1c%), is the primary marker used to evaluate glycemic control and to predict diabetes-related complications.8 Although HbA1c% measurement is described in dogs,9–11 its limited clinical use in North America likely reflects the absence of broadly adopted assay standardization for dogs, the limited availability of validated veterinary-use platforms, and the lack of established canine reference intervals.

The Vet Chroma™ (Vet Chroma, Boditech Med Inc., Chuncheon, Republic of Korea) is a commercially available point-of-care (POC) benchtop analyzer that employs analyte-specific cartridges to measure multiple blood and serum analytes. Using an immunofluorescent method, it quantitatively reports HbA1c as a percentage of total hemoglobin, with a working range of 0.6%–15%. The objectives of this study were to analytically validate the Vet Chroma™ for measurement of canine HbA1c%, to assess its ability to discriminate DM from NDM dogs, and to establish a population-based reference interval for HbA1c% in young healthy dogs.

Materials and methods

Detailed information is provided in the Appendix.

Study design and samples

This analytical validation study evaluated Vet Chroma™, a POC fluorescence immunoassay for canine HbA1c%. We used excess EDTA whole blood from client-owned dogs. We classified dogs as diabetic based on a previous diagnosis or compatible clinical and clinicopathologic findings and/or discharge for insulin therapy; non-diabetic (NDM) dogs lacked DM history or signs, had fasting BG ≤ 100 mg/dL, and had no condition expected to alter erythrocyte lifespan. Method comparison included 113 samples, and diagnostic analyses included 51 diabetic and 44 NDM dogs. A separate cohort of 42 clinically healthy young dogs aged 6 months to 2 years established the reference interval. Samples were refrigerated within 12 h; aliquots for later testing were stored at -80 °C.

Assay validation

Vet Chroma™ measurements followed the manufacturer’s procedure: 5 μL of whole blood was mixed with prepared detection buffer, and 75 μL were dispensed to a cartridge, incubated for 12 min at 30 °C, then read.

Intra-assay imprecision was assessed by 25 sequential measurements of 3 whole-blood pools and 2 levels of QCM; inter-assay imprecision used 4 measurements daily for 5 days (pools) and duplicates for 5 days (QCM).12 We calculated the total observed error (TEobs) as 2 × CV + |bias|.13

We evaluated linearity in triplicate at 10 levels spanning 0.86%–6.27% HbA1c%.12

We evaluated lipemia and icterus at 4 graded concentrations.14 We assessed stability in low- and high-HbA1c% samples at 4 °C for 5 days and at -20 and -80 °C for 180 days.12

Statistical analysis

We estimated level-specific imprecision using restricted-maximum-likelihood mixed-effects models, with day and run nested within day as random effects.15 We assessed stability and interference using paired t-tests.12 For method comparison, results ≤ 0.60% and duplicate outliers exceeding 4 times the instrument-specific mean absolute duplicate difference were excluded; remaining duplicates were averaged. Passing-Bablok regression and Bland–Altman analyses assessed method agreement.16–19 ROC analysis with the Youden index estimated diagnostic performance and the optimal cutoff.20 We calculated the reference interval using the robust method with 90% CIs derived from 1000 bootstrap samples.21,22 Analyses were performed in SAS 9.4; P < .05 was significant.

Results

Method validation

The intra-assay %CVs for the low-, intermediate-, and high-HbA1c% pools were 5.36%, 4.04%, and 2.36%, respectively. In the inter-assay reproducibility study, mean (±SD) HbA1c% values for the low-, intermediate-, and high-HbA1c% pools were 0.80% ±0.13%, 2.85% ±0.16%, and 6.44% ±0.26%, respectively; the corresponding %CVs were 15.78%, 5.55%, and 4.07% (Figure 1). The linear mixed model estimated inter-assay and total imprecision %CVs for the low-, intermediate-, and high-HbA1c% samples were 15.42% and 16.93%, 3.14% and 5.69%, and 3.44% and 4.30%, respectively. Level 1 (low) QCM had an intra-assay %CV of 1.81% and an inter-assay %CV of 3.37%, and level 2 (high) QCM had an intra-assay %CV of 3.77% and an inter-assay %CV of 1.35%. The observed total error was 12.21% for the low QCM and 11.69% for the high QCM, with an average of 11.95%. Regression analysis of the linearity study demonstrated strong assay linearity (R2 = 0.973; r = 0.986) across the HbA1c% range of 0.86% to 6.27%. The 95% CI for the y-intercept (0.24–0.71) did not include zero, indicating a positive constant bias. In contrast, the 95% CI for the slope of the regression line (0.94-1.06) included 1.00, indicating no evidence of proportional bias across the measured HbA1c range (Figure 2).

In the interference study, lipemia resulted in absolute deviations from baseline HbA1c% ranging from 0.36% to 1.24% (with the greatest change observed at the 1+ lipemia level), and icterus resulted in absolute deviations ranging from -0.38% to 1.86% (Table S1). No significant differences from baseline were detected for both; however, because this screening experiment used a single base sample and duplicate measurements at each interferent level, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be taken as definitive evidence of the absence of interference.

To evaluate storage stability, samples with mean HbA1c% of 1.64% and 4.68%, representing low and high values, were analyzed (Tables S2 and S3). At 4 °C, isolated significant differences from baseline were observed on day 4 in both samples; however, these changes were not sustained on day 5. At -20 °C storage, the high-HbA1c% sample decreased from 4.68% to 0.60% at 6 months, corresponding to an absolute change of -4.08 HbA1c percentage points (-87.17%; P = .020). The low-HbA1c% sample also decreased at 6 months, from 1.64% to <0.60%, corresponding to an absolute change of -1.04 HbA1c percentage points (-63.41%); however, this change was not significant (P = .127). At -80 °C, the high-HbA1c% sample did not differ from baseline at 6 months (4.68% vs 4.68%, P = 1.000), whereas the low-HbA1c% sample decreased from 1.64% to 0.60% at 6 months (absolute change, -1.04 HbA1c percentage points; -63.41%), although this change was not significant (P = .127). Because values at 6 months fell below the assay’s lower limit of quantification (0.6%), these storage conditions were not considered analytically acceptable for long-term stability.

Method comparison

A total of 113 dog samples were analyzed concurrently on the Vet Chroma™ analyzer and the Olympus DxC 700 AU HbA1c assay for the method comparison study. The mean absolute duplicate difference was 0.137 for the Vet Chroma™ and 0.100 for the Olympus DxC 700 AU, corresponding to acceptability limits of 0.548 and 0.401, respectively. One Vet Chroma™ sample and 6 Olympus DxC 700 AU samples exceeded these limits, resulting in exclusion of 7 unique Sample IDs. After duplicate screening, 106 Sample IDs remained. 3 additional Sample IDs lacked a usable Vet Chroma™ result after filtering, leaving 103 complete paired samples for downstream method-comparison analyses. Passing-Bablok regression yielded a y-intercept of -6.41 (95% CI, -7.35 to -5.35) and a slope of 1.84 (95% CI, 1.66–2.03), indicating both negative constant and positive proportional bias (Figure 3). Using Olympus DxC 700 AU HbA1c% as x and Vet Chroma™ HbA1c% as y, the resulting equation was: Vet Chroma™ HbA1c% = -6.41 + 1.84 × Olympus DxC 700 AU HbA1c%. Bland–Altman analysis (Figure 4) demonstrated a mean HbA1c% bias (Vet Chroma − Olympus DxC 700 AU) of -1.89 points, indicating that the Vet Chroma™ generally reported lower HbA1c% than the Olympus DxC 700 AU. The limits of HbA1c% agreement (mean bias ±2 SD) ranged from -3.93 to 0.15 points, suggesting that for most samples the Vet Chroma HbA1c% result might be up to ~ 3.9 points lower or ~ 0.15 points higher than the Olympus DxC 700 AU. Visual inspection of the Bland–Altman plot suggested proportional bias, with differences tending to become less negative as HbA1c% increased.

Comparison of HbA1c% between diabetic and control dogs

Fifty-one diabetic and 44 NDM control dogs were included. Diabetic dogs had a mean (±SD) HbA1c% of 5.10% ±1.51, compared with 1.58% ±0.33 in NDM dogs (Figure 5). Diabetic dogs had significantly higher HbA1c% than NDM dogs (mean estimated difference ± SE, 3.53% ±0.22, 95% CI, 3.10–4.00, P < .001).

Individual symbols (circles and dashes) represent individual dogs. Diabetic dogs had a higher mean HbA1c% (5.10%) compared with non-diabetic dogs (1.58%). Abbreviations: DM = diabetes mellitus; HbA1c = hemoglobin A1c; NDM = no diabetes mellitus.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (Figure 6) showed excellent discrimination for HbA1c% in identifying diabetic dogs, with an AUC of 0.969 ± 0.021 (95% CI, 0.928–1.000). An HbA1c% cutoff of > 3.22% provided optimal diagnostic performance, with 90.20% sensitivity (95% CI, 78.6–96.7) and 100% specificity (95% CI, 92.0–100.0). At this threshold, the positive likelihood ratio (LR+) was not estimable (no false positives), and the negative likelihood ratio (LR-) was 0.078. In the logistic regression model, each 1-percentage-point increase in HbA1c% was associated with 22.9 times higher odds of diabetes (OR, 22.9; 95% CI, 3.6–146.9).

The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.969 ± 0.021 (95% confidence interval, 0.928–1.000), indicating excellent discrimination between diabetic and non-diabetic dogs. Establishing population-based reference interval

Forty-two clinically healthy dogs aged between 6 months and 2 years were included in the reference interval (RI) analysis. The group of healthy dogs in our study comprised 20 females (14 spayed, 6 intact) and 22 males (17 neutered, 5 intact) of multiple breeds, including 12 mixed-breed dogs and a diverse distribution of purebred dogs (Table S4).

HbA1c% measured using the Vet Chroma™ analyzer was approximately normally distributed based on visual inspection of histograms and q-q plots, with no evidence of departure from normality by the Shapiro–Wilk test (P = 0.681), and no significant outliers were identified. The robust 95% RI for HbA1c% was 1.25%–2.75%. The lower reference limit (1.25%) 90% bootstrap CI was 1.19%–1.59%, and the upper reference limit (2.75%) 90% bootstrap CI was 2.56%–2.90%. The lower and the upper CI widths (0.40% and 0.34%) were higher than 0.2 × the RI width (0.2 × 1.47 = 0.29%).

Discussion

This study analytically validated the Vet Chroma™ POC analyzer for measuring canine HbA1c%, evaluated its diagnostic performance in distinguishing DM from NDM dogs, and established a population-based reference interval in young healthy dogs. The assay demonstrated acceptable analytical precision, strong linearity, and excellent discrimination between diabetic and NDM dogs, supporting its clinical utility as an adjunct in-clinic tool for the assessment of glycemic control.

In the validation study, HbA1c% ranged from 0.74% to 7.70%. The mean HbA1c% among NDM dogs was 1.58% (range: 0.74%–2.07%). In humans, an HbA1c% value < 5.70% is considered normal, 5.70%–6.40% indicates prediabetes, and >6.40% indicates diabetes.23 The markedly lower HbA1c% values observed in dogs compared with humans are likely attributable, at least in part, to species-specific differences in erythrocyte glucose permeability. Canine erythrocytes exhibit reduced glucose permeability relative to human erythrocytes,24 which plausibly accounts for the lower HbA1c%. Similar ranges of HbA1c are reported in dogs.9,11

Analytical precision of the Vet Chroma™ analyzer was demonstrated by a mean intra-assay %CV of 3.92% (range, 2.36%–5.36%) and a mean inter-assay %CV of 8.47% (range, 4.07%–15.78%) across the evaluated samples. The greatest variability was noted at the lower end of the HbA1c% range; however, the absolute standard deviation was relatively consistent across HbA1c% levels, and the elevated %CVs at low HbA1c% reflect the small denominator effect of the mean analyte value. The additional model-based %CVs reflect the variance components (eg, day/run and residual error) rather than the pooled dispersion used in descriptive %CVs and support the same conclusion that imprecision was greatest at low HbA1c%. At the low HbA1c% level, the small difference between the total imprecision %CV to inter-assay %CV (ie, 16.93% vs. 15.42%) indicates that the residual contribution to the total imprecision %CV was minimal, and the majority of variation originated from the variance components day and day/run; thus we suspect that it could reflect decreased stability at the low end of the reportable HbA1c% range over the 5-day period.

Our inter-assay variability results are comparable to those obtained with other reported methodologies,11,25 though they are higher than the variability documented with some non-POC methods,9,26 which typically yield superior analytical precision. Notably, previous evaluations of POC analyzers for canine HbA1c% measurement report intra-assay variability comparable to that observed in the present study; however, inter-assay variability was not assessed on dog samples in these studies.26–28

While established performance thresholds for intra- and inter-assay %CVs are not available for canine HbA1c%, ligand-binding assays generally accept %CVs up to 20% as analytically permissible.29 The variability observed with the Vet Chroma™ falls well within these bounds, supporting its suitability as a POC diagnostic tool for canine HbA1c% measurement.

The average observed total error for the Vet Chroma™ was 11.95%. A total allowable error (TEa) for HbA1c measurement in dogs has not been universally established, owing to the limited availability and standardization of canine-specific commercial assays and the absence of consensus in the veterinary literature.

The Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988 (CLIA) specify a TEa of 3% for HbA1c% measurement in humans, whereas the College of American Pathologists (CAP) has set an acceptance criterion of 6%.30 The National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program (NGSP) currently permits a TEa of up to 8%.31 These performance targets are based on the biological variability of HbA1c% in humans and, although informative, should be applied cautiously when establishing quality goals for canine HbA1c% measurement. In dogs, TEobs should be interpreted as an assay-specific analytical performance metric rather than as a clinically validated allowable-error target; clinical decisions should instead rely on assay-specific reference intervals, assay-specific cutoffs, and the overall clinical context. Future development of QC material better suited to the dog linear range can yield a more relevant TEobs value.

The Vet Chroma™ demonstrated excellent linearity across the clinically relevant range of canine HbA1c%. Specifically, a strong correlation (r = 0.986) was observed between 0.86% and 6.27%, which encompassed the majority of values in the study group. Evaluation of the 95% CI for both the slope and y-intercept indicated no evidence of proportional bias; however, a slight positive constant bias was detected. The presence of constant bias might reflect preanalytical factors, such as dilutional error, or analytical imprecision. Given the inherent variability of the analyzer, random error could also contribute, although this influence was mitigated by performing all dilutions in triplicate.

Lipemia (turbidity), icterus (bilirubin), and hemolysis (free hemoglobin) are common preanalytical interferents in veterinary clinical pathology that can variably affect assay performance. The effect of hemolysis was not evaluated in this study because deliberate lysis of red blood cells is an integral step in the test procedure. The interference study did not detect a significant effect of lipemia or icterus across the graded concentrations evaluated; however, measurable absolute changes in HbA1c% were observed, and the limited design precludes concluding that clinically relevant interference is absent. Thus, grossly lipemic or icteric samples should be avoided, when feasible, until larger, dedicated interference studies are performed.

The storage stability study indicated that HbA1c in canine whole blood remains stable for up to 5 days at 4 °C, and for up to 3 months at both -20 and -80 °C. Isolated significant deviations from baseline were observed on day 4 in both the 4 °C low- and high-HbA1c% samples; however, these changes were not sustained at day 5, and the absence of a progressive trend suggested that they were likely attributable to preanalytical variation, such as pipetting or sample handling, rather than to true loss of analyte stability. Nevertheless, because one day-4 measurement in the low-HbA1c% sample fell below the assay’s lower limit of quantification, a conservative recommendation was made that whole blood should be stored at 4 °C for no longer than 4 days before analysis. Based on the frozen storage data, samples should not be stored at -20 °C for 6 months before HbA1c% measurement with this assay. This condition was analytically unacceptable, as shown by the marked decrease in the high-HbA1c% sample from 4.68% to 0.60% at 6 months. Therefore, frozen storage at -20 °C should not exceed 3 months before analysis. Six-month storage at -80 °C preserved analytical recovery for the high-HbA1c% sample, but should be interpreted cautiously for low-HbA1c% samples, because the low-HbA1c% sample decreased to the assay’s lower limit of quantification despite not differing significantly from baseline by paired t-test. Overall, the practical recommendation is that fresh whole-blood analysis is preferred, refrigerated storage is acceptable only for short-term holding, storage at 4 °C should not exceed 4 days, and frozen storage at -20 and -80 °C should not exceed 3 months. As a POC assay, the Vet Chroma™ is designed for use with fresh whole blood, providing rapid and clinically actionable HbA1c% measurements. The observed stability under different storage conditions is considered acceptable for its intended clinical application, though fresh sample analysis remains the preferred approach.

Currently, no established “gold standard” exists for the measurement of canine HbA1c%. Although high-performance liquid chromatography is considered the reference method for human HbA1c analysis,32 this designation has not been validated for dogs. In the absence of a recognized reference method, a previously utilized automated immunoturbidimetric assay (Olympus DxC 700 AU HbA1c) was employed as a comparator in our study.9,25 Method comparison revealed that the Vet Chroma™ and Olympus DxC 700 AU assays are not directly interchangeable. Most discordance was observed at lower HbA1c%, likely reflecting both methodological differences and the correction formulas used by each assay. Many of the human HbA1c assays, including Olympus DxC 700 AU, apply the following formula: ((HbA1c/total hemoglobin) × 91.5) + 2.15, yielding a minimum value of 2.15% plus the assay’s limit of detection,33 whereas the Vet Chroma™ reports a minimum of 0.6%. This constant bias was most clinically relevant at lower HbA1c% values, where the Passing-Bablok regression suggested substantial between-method disagreement; Vet Chroma™ values of 1%–3% corresponded to predicted Olympus DxC 700 AU values of approximately 4.0%–5.1%, indicating absolute differences of about 2.1%–3.0%. Bland–Altman analysis showed wide limits of agreement, indicating that the observed between-method spread is not acceptable for clinical interchangeability, particularly near the discriminatory cutoff, where a difference of this magnitude could shift an individual dog across clinically relevant interpretive thresholds. Given that the observed between-method differences are of similar magnitude to clinically relevant decision thresholds, use of different analyzers could lead to discordant clinical classification. These findings underscore that currently available HbA1c analyzers should not be used interchangeably, and that analyzer-specific and species-specific reference intervals are required. Accordingly, serial HbA1c% measurements in an individual dog should ideally be performed using the same analytical platform. If a clinician or laboratory switches platforms, a new method-specific baseline should be established before interpreting longitudinal changes, and absolute HbA1c% values obtained from one platform should not be directly translated to the other platform.

A significant difference in HbA1c% was observed between diabetic and NDM dogs, consistent with prior reports.10,11,25,27,34 Diabetic dogs exhibited a mean HbA1c% of 5.10%, whereas the control group had a mean of 1.58%. Some overlap between groups was noted, likely reflecting heterogeneity within the diabetic cohort, which included dogs with varying levels of glycemic control, ranging from well-controlled to those presenting with DKA. Lower HbA1c% values in certain diabetic dogs are presumed to indicate optimal glycemic control, consistent with expected lower circulating glucose levels.

ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent discrimination of the Vet Chroma™ between dogs with overt DM and clearly NDM dogs in this study sample, with an AUC of 0.969. At an HbA1c% cutoff > 3.22%, the assay achieved a sensitivity of 90.20% and a specificity of 100%, indicating strong separation between these groups within this study sample. However, because this comparison was performed using a case–control framework, with diabetic dogs compared to dogs unlikely to have dysglycemia, these estimates likely overstate diagnostic performance relative to the more heterogeneous populations in which HbA1c testing would be applied clinically. Furthermore, at this HbA1c% cutoff > 3.22%, the positive likelihood ratio was not estimable, indicating that all dogs exceeding this threshold were correctly classified as diabetic, thereby demonstrating strong diagnostic confidence in confirming DM in this study population. A positive likelihood ratio greater than 10 provides strong evidence to rule in a diagnosis in most cases.35 Further analysis also showed an odds ratio of 22.9, indicating that each 1-percentage-point increase in HbA1c% was associated with approximately a 23-fold increase in the odds of DM, underscoring the strong discriminative power of Vet Chroma™ for identifying DM dogs. The wide 95% CI around the odds ratio indicates limited precision of this estimate, likely reflecting the modest sample size and strong separation between groups in this selected study population.

This cutoff is consistent with the previously reported 3.30% cutoff,11 though it is lower than the 6.20% cutoff reported in another study.10 These findings highlight assay-dependent variability in canine HbA1c% and support the use of HbA1c% as an indicator of DM in dogs; however, because a small risk of misclassification persists, and because the derived cutoff reflects discrimination between overtly diabetic and clearly NDM dogs in this study sample, results should be interpreted using assay-specific reference ranges and in conjunction with clinical presentation and additional clinicopathologic findings.

The estimated 95% reference interval (RI) for HbA1c of 1.25% (90% CI, 1.19–1.59) to 2.75% (90% CI, 2.56–2.90) in young, clinically healthy dogs was slightly lower than values previously reported in broader canine cohorts.9 The group of healthy dogs in our study consisted exclusively of dogs between 6 months and 2 years of age with normal body condition and no history of systemic illness or medication use. This restriction was intentional and was designed to define a baseline HbA1c% interval in dogs least likely to have altered glucose metabolism. Restricting enrollment to this narrowly defined, healthy age group likely minimized biological variability associated with age-related metabolic changes and subclinical disorders that could increase insulin resistance. Furthermore, excluding dogs with potential systemic or inflammatory diseases could have reduced the influence of insulin resistance or altered erythrocyte turnover on HbA1c%, thereby yielding a more precise estimate of baseline HbA1c% in healthy dogs. Accordingly, this RI should be interpreted as applying specifically to young, clinically healthy dogs and not as a general interval for all canine populations. Values above this reference interval should not be interpreted in isolation but should prompt interpretation in light of clinical findings and other clinicopathologic data.

The use of the robust approach, as recommended by ASVCP guidelines, ensured that the derived RI was minimally affected by distributional assumptions. Furthermore, bootstrapped 90% CI were applied to quantify the uncertainty surrounding the reference limits, providing robustness given the modest sample size. The narrow CI observed in this study indicate low variability and support the analytical precision of the Vet Chroma™ assay for HbA1c measurement. However, because the CI width exceeded 0.2 × the RI width, the precision of the RI limits would be expected to improve with additional reference individuals. The inclusion of multiple breeds also enhances the generalizability of the results. Establishing a reliable reference interval for HbA1c% is essential for the clinical interpretation of results in both healthy and diabetic dogs. Given the convenience, rapid turnaround time, and small sample volume required by the Vet Chroma™ analyzer, this POC method might serve as a practical and accurate alternative for in-clinic monitoring of long-term glycemic control.

Based on our storage stability data, the blood samples could only be reliably preserved for a duration of 3 months, thereby imposing a technical limitation on our capacity to bank samples for subsequent analysis. Another limitation of this study is the absence of a defined total allowable error for this assay in dogs or an established gold standard for canine HbA1c% measurement. Although the Olympus DxC 700 AU HbA1c assay was used as a comparative method, neither method should be considered inherently superior or more accurate than the other, and results between these 2 methods are not interchangeable. The linearity assessment did not encompass the full manufacturer-stated analytical range, although HbA1c% observed in DM dogs were well below the upper reporting limit and are unlikely to reach clinically relevant extremes. An additional limitation is that diagnostic accuracy was assessed in a selected case–control population comparing overtly diabetic dogs with clearly NDM dogs, which likely introduced spectrum bias and might have overestimated discrimination compared with more heterogeneous clinical populations. Lastly, because the diabetic cohort was not prospectively stratified by disease duration or newly diagnosed versus previously diagnosed status, and because referral histories were not sufficiently standardized to support accurate retrospective classification, this study could not assess the diagnostic utility of HbA1c% specifically at the time of initial diagnosis. Future prospective studies should evaluate assay performance in dogs undergoing first-time diagnostic evaluation for DM.

In conclusion, the Vet Chroma™ POC analyzer demonstrated excellent analytical performance, ease of use, and rapid turnaround time, supporting its suitability for clinical application in veterinary practice. The assay effectively discriminated between diabetic and nondiabetic dogs, achieving a sensitivity of 90.20% and a specificity of 100% at a cutoff value > 3.22% HbA1c. Nevertheless, HbA1c% results should be interpreted in conjunction with the animal’s clinical presentation and complementary clinicopathologic findings. Although these findings support the diagnostic utility of HbA1c%, its comparative performance relative to established diagnostic methods and its role in monitoring glycemic control in treated diabetic dogs remain incompletely defined.
