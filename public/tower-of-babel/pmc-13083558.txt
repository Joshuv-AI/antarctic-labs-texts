Sleep Behaviour and Subjective Sleep Quality in Esports Athletes: A Multilevel Analysis of Night-to-Night Variability
Authors: Andrew Kidcaff, Mitchell Nicholson, Tristan J. Coulter, Craig McNulty, Remco Polman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsports training is typically self-directed and frequently scheduled late in the day, which may disrupt habitual sleep–wake patterns and contribute to poor sleep outcomes. Although previous esports research has identified delayed sleep timing and reduced sleep duration at the group level, less is known about how night-to-night fluctuations in sleep behaviour and stable individual characteristics jointly influence perceived sleep quality. Examining both intra-individual and inter-individual determinants may provide a more nuanced understanding of sleep health in esports athletes.MethodsTwenty-four esports athletes (male: n = 16, age M = 20.13 ± 2.42 years; female: n = 8, age M = 25.13 ± 4.02 years; range 18–33) from Brisbane, Australia, completed the study between October 2023 and March 2024. The protocol comprised baseline questionnaires assessing sleep hygiene, habitual sleep quality, chronotype, and daytime sleepiness, followed by approximately seven consecutive nights of wrist-worn actigraphy and daily sleep diaries (187 valid nights). Subjective sleep quality was rated each morning on a 5-point scale. Multilevel modelling was used to examine within-person associations between nightly deviations in sleep duration and bed/wake times, as well as the timing of esports gameplay relative to bedtime (no gameplay, < 1 h, 1–2 h, > 2 h). Between-person predictors included sleep hygiene, habitual sleep timing and duration, and age.ResultsParticipants averaged approximately 6 h 42 min (± 51 min) of sleep per night and reported moderate subjective sleep quality. The intraclass correlation coefficient indicated that 20% of the variance in sleep quality was attributable to between-person differences, with the remaining 80% reflecting within-person variability. At the nightly level, longer-than-average sleep duration was associated with higher perceived sleep quality, whereas nightly bed and wake times were not significant predictors. Nights in which gameplay ended 1–2 h before bedtime were associated with poorer sleep quality compared with nights without gameplay. At the between-person level, poorer sleep hygiene was associated with lower sleep quality, whereas later habitual bedtimes were associated with higher sleep quality ratings across the monitoring period.ConclusionsSubjective sleep quality in esports athletes is shaped by both nightly variability in sleep duration and stable behavioural characteristics. These findings highlight the importance of sleep regularity, behavioural routines, and training schedules in esports, and support the use of multilevel approaches to capture dynamic sleep processes in performance-based gaming contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01021-0.

Background

The unstructured nature of esports training can disrupt habitual sleep patterns and contribute to daytime sleepiness. Unlike other competitive domains with regulated training environments, esports athletes often train without standardised routines or facility constraints, enabling continuous, self-directed engagement [1]. Flexible scheduling allows players to coordinate training and competition with teammates and opponents located in different time zones. However, this same flexibility may reinforce maladaptive pre-sleep behaviours, such as late-night gaming and caffeine consumption, which elevate physiological arousal before bedtime [2–4]. When sustained over consecutive nights, these behaviours can contribute to irregular sleep–wake patterns, characterised by inconsistent bed and wake times, delayed sleep onset, and reduced total sleep time [5, 6]. Such irregularity in sleep timing has been linked to poorer subjective sleep quality and greater daytime sleepiness, even when overall sleep duration falls within recommended ranges [7, 8].

Previous research in esports populations has consistently reported delayed bed and wake times, reduced sleep duration and efficiency, and poor subjective sleep quality [5, 9–11]. These group-level findings suggest that esports athletes, on average, display suboptimal sleep characteristics, which may be partly explained by the high prevalence of evening chronotypes within this population [1, 5, 9, 10]. However, mean-level data provide only a partial view and cannot capture the night-to-night fluctuations in sleep behaviour that may also influence recovery and performance. Although some night-to-night variation in sleep is typical, larger fluctuations may indicate poorer sleep regularity. Examining these fluctuations therefore provides a more complete picture of sleep–wake patterns, an aspect increasingly recognised as important for health and performance outcomes [7]. To capture the complexities of sleep behaviour in this context, it is necessary to move beyond group-level means and consider night-to-night variability.

Recent research has examined night-to-night variation in individual sleep patterns, defined as changes in sleep timing and duration relative to one’s average across multiple nights [12]. Intra-individual variability (IIV) has emerged as a key behavioural marker of sleep regularity, with growing evidence linking greater variability to poorer cognitive and emotional functioning. Even when average nightly sleep duration meets recommended guidelines for adolescents (8–10 h) and adults/young adults (7–9 h) [13], higher IIV has been associated with poorer subjective sleep quality and impaired daytime functioning, including reduced attentional capacity, memory difficulties, and mood disturbances [12, 14, 15]. These adverse outcomes may be attributable to circadian misalignment, characterised by a disruption in the stable relationship between biological rhythms and external environmental cues such as the light–dark cycle [16]. Poor sleep hygiene can exacerbate circadian misalignment and increase IIV [4, 7]. Most sleep recommendations focus solely on sleep duration, overlooking other components of healthy sleep, such as sleep hygiene [17].

Sleep hygiene encompasses behavioural and environmental practices that promote healthy sleep, including consistent bed and wake times, avoidance of evening stimulants, and reduced exposure to bright light before bedtime [4]. Among esports athletes, poor sleep hygiene is common, with frequent reports of irregular sleep schedules, late-night caffeine intake, and prolonged screen exposure [1]. Evidence from both general and performance-based populations indicates that improving sleep hygiene enhances sleep regularity, increases sleep duration, and improves subjective sleep quality [18–21]. Despite this, the relationship between habitual sleep timing, sleep hygiene, and variability in sleep outcomes remains underexplored in esports. Given that both IIV and sleep hygiene practices influence how individuals perceive the restorative value of their sleep, subjective sleep quality emerges as a key marker for evaluating sleep health in esports.

Subjective sleep quality is widely recognised as a core dimension of sleep health, reflecting an individual’s perceived satisfaction with their sleep and its restorative value. Buysse et al. identified sleep quality as one of five key components of healthy sleep, alongside duration, efficiency, timing, and alertness, highlighting its ecological validity and relevance to multiple health outcomes [17]. In the present study, subjective sleep quality refers to each participant’s nightly rating of how restful or satisfactory their sleep was, a metric relevant to daily recovery and readiness to perform. Poor sleep quality is frequently accompanied by greater daytime dysfunction [22], having been associated with impairments in attention, reaction time, decision-making, and working memory, with meta-analytic evidence showing the most significant effects on tasks requiring sustained attention [23, 24]. Sleep quality is influenced by both nightly fluctuations and broader lifestyle factors, with characteristics such as age and chronotype interacting with modifiable behaviours like caffeine use, irregular sleep schedules, and evening light exposure [20]. Understanding how these influences operate at different levels of analysis may help clarify the factors shaping esports athletes’ sleep outcomes.

Although previous esports studies have documented delayed sleep timing and poorer sleep outcomes at the group level, less is known about how nightly fluctuations in sleep behaviour interact with stable behavioural characteristics to influence perceived sleep quality. In particular, the relative contributions of intra-individual variability in sleep patterns and broader behavioural factors, such as sleep hygiene and habitual sleep timing, remain poorly understood in esports populations. Addressing this gap may provide greater insight into the mechanisms shaping sleep quality in esports athletes and inform strategies to support recovery and wellbeing in performance-based gaming contexts.

The objective of this study was to examine both intra-individual and inter-individual determinants of subjective sleep quality during a week of typical sleep behaviour in esports athletes. Specifically, we investigated whether night-to-night deviations in total sleep duration and the timing of esports gameplay relative to bedtime were associated with perceived sleep quality within individuals. It was hypothesised that, within individuals, nights with longer total sleep duration relative to their personal average would be associated with higher perceived sleep quality, whereas gameplay occurring closer to bedtime would be associated with lower perceived sleep quality. In addition, we examined whether stable between-person characteristics, including sleep hygiene practices, habitual sleep timing and duration, and age, accounted for differences in overall perceived sleep quality across participants. It was hypothesised that better sleep hygiene and more favourable habitual sleep characteristics, including earlier and more stable sleep timing and longer habitual sleep duration, would be associated with higher overall perceived sleep quality, with age also examined as a potential predictor.

Methodology

Design

The study employed an observational, repeated-measures design to examine within-person and between-person predictors of sleep quality in esports athletes. Nightly sleep observations (Level 1) were nested within participants (Level 2) over approximately seven consecutive nights. Objective sleep parameters were measured using wrist-worn actigraphy, while daily self-report diaries captured subjective sleep quality and contextual behaviours. Stable individual characteristics, including demographic and behavioural factors such as sleep hygiene, were collected at baseline via an online questionnaire. The design enabled the simultaneous investigation of short-term nightly fluctuations and between-person differences.

Participants

Recruitment targeted Brisbane-based competitive gaming communities via online advertisements, university mailing lists, and social media platforms. Eligibility criteria required participants to be aged 15 years or older, actively involved with seasonal esports competitions (e.g., ranked online matches, intercollegiate leagues), and hold a current in-game rank. This broader age criterion was used to capture a wider esports player base. However, all participants who enrolled and completed the study were aged 18 years or older, and no minors were included in the final analytic sample. All participants provided written informed consent.

Measures

Subjective Measurement of Sleep and Esports Engagement

Baseline Sleep Questionnaire

Participants completed a questionnaire including demographic items (e.g., age, gender), an esports profile (title, gaming platform, ranking), and standardised self-report measures of habitual sleep quality, sleep hygiene, daytime sleepiness, and chronotype. Additional items assessed average weekly esports activity and daily caffeine intake. Details of the baseline sleep questionnaire items are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Sleep Quality

Subjective sleep quality over the past month was assessed using the brief version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [25], a six-item measure derived from the original scale [22]. Higher global scores (range 0–15) indicate poorer sleep quality. The brief scale demonstrates good construct validity and internal consistency comparable to the full version [26, 27].

Sleep Hygiene

Sleep-related behaviours were measured using the Sleep Hygiene Index [4], a 13-item scale assessing practices that facilitate or interfere with sleep. Total scores range from 13 to 65, with higher scores reflecting poorer sleep hygiene. The index shows established associations with sleep quality and daytime sleepiness, demonstrating adequate internal consistency [4, 28].

Daytime Sleepiness

Perceived daytime sleepiness was assessed using the Pediatric Daytime Sleepiness Scale [29], an eight-item measure rated on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater sleepiness (range 0–32). The scale was originally developed for adolescent populations and has since shown good internal consistency in young adult and esports samples [10, 18].

Chronotype

Preferred sleep–wake timing was evaluated using the reduced Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire [30], a five-item measure yielding scores from 4 to 25, with lower values indicating greater evening preference. The reduced version correlates strongly with the original 19-item scale [31] and demonstrates acceptable construct validity in young adult samples, including associations with preferred sleep timing and duration [32].

Daily Sleep Diary

Participants completed a brief online sleep diary each morning to supplement the objective actigraphy data. A standardised list of sleep items was adapted from the Consensus Sleep Diary protocol [33], which recorded the previous night’s bed and wake times, estimated sleep onset latency, and a single-item rating of perceived sleep quality on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“very poor”) to 5 (“very good”). Additional items captured contextual behaviours from the preceding day, including the duration and timing of esports gameplay and caffeine intake (beverage type, approximate number of servings, and time of last consumption). Diary links were accessed via a QR code provided at the initial session, with participants instructed to complete entries as close to waking as possible to minimise recall bias.

Objective Measurement of Sleep

Actigraphy

Objective sleep–wake patterns were measured using wrist-worn triaxial accelerometers (ActiGraph GT9X Link, ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA), worn on the non-dominant wrist to minimise interference from dominant-hand movements. Devices were initialised to record raw acceleration data in 60-second epochs, consistent with established field-based protocols [34, 35]. Data were processed using the manufacturer’s ActiLife software (version 6.13.4), applying the Cole-Kripke algorithm [36], which is widely validated for adult populations and demonstrates high agreement with polysomnography for sleep–wake classification [34, 37]. Self-reported bed and wake times from the daily sleep diary were used to guide and validate actigraphy scoring, with manual editing applied when discrepancies arose, consistent with best-practice recommendations for improving sleep–wake classification accuracy [37].

Data Inclusion Criteria for Actigraph

A night was considered valid if the device was worn for at least 90% of the diary-reported time in bed, the main sleep episode was identifiable in both actigraphy and diary records, and no technical failures occurred (e.g., battery depletion, device malfunction). As only nocturnal sleep periods were analysed, brief device removal during daytime wake periods did not affect data inclusion. Nights with missing actigraphy data or diary entries were excluded from analyses using pairwise deletion, while participants’ remaining valid nights were retained. Across the monitoring period, 23 nights (12.3% of total) were excluded from analysis due to device removal during the main sleep episode (n = 13 nights) or incomplete diary entries (n = 10 nights). Procedure

Participants who had completed the online sleep questionnaire were invited and eligible to participate in the study. Eligible participants were scheduled to attend an initial in-person session at the university, where they received study information and completed written informed consent. A wrist-worn Actigraph was fitted to monitor sleep at home for approximately seven consecutive nights, which is considered sufficient for reliable sleep IIV estimates [12, 37]. During this period, participants completed an online sleep diary each day and maintained their usual daily routines. Participants contributed a median of 7 nights of valid actigraphy data (range 4–12), totalling 187 nights of sleep data across the sample. Following the monitoring period, devices were returned via pre-paid postal satchel. Study compliance was monitored through daily diary submissions and device wear-time data. Upon completing all study requirements, participants entered a prize draw to win one of five $100 (AUD) online gift cards. Data were collected over approximately six months from October 2023 to March 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the Queensland University of Technology Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 6112).

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 30). Data were screened for outliers and normality at both the nightly (Level 1) and participant (Level 2) levels. Minor deviations in skewness and kurtosis were observed for several subjective and objective sleep variables, consistent with the expected distribution of a young adult esports sample (e.g., delayed bedtimes and late-night wake patterns). For Pearson’s correlations, assumptions were evaluated by inspecting scatterplots for linearity and homoscedasticity, and by screening for univariate skewness and kurtosis. Given the modest sample size (N = 24), bias-corrected accelerated bootstrap confidence intervals (based on 1000 samples) were applied to provide robust estimates of effect sizes. For the multilevel models, assumptions were examined by inspecting Q–Q plots for normality of residuals and scatterplots to assess linearity and homoscedasticity. Influence diagnostics, including Cook’s distance and leverage values, indicated a small number of potentially influential cases, primarily from a single participant. However, preliminary analyses conducted with and without this participant showed no meaningful change in the pattern of results. On this basis, all participants were retained in the final analyses. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

To provide context for the multilevel models, Pearson product-moment correlations were calculated between baseline questionnaire measures and person-level averages of actigraphy-derived sleep parameters to examine convergence and complementary roles of subjective and objective indicators of sleep behaviour. Effect sizes for correlations were interpreted using Cohen’s benchmarks (r = .10 small, 0.30 medium, 0.50 large) [38].

Multilevel models were then conducted to examine predictors of nightly subjective sleep quality, which was treated as a continuous outcome. Evidence supports this approach for 5-point Likert scales [39], and the distribution in the present dataset showed adequate use of all response categories without substantial floor or ceiling clustering. At Level 1, predictors represented nightly deviations in bed/wake times and sleep duration from each participant’s mean across the monitoring period. These variables were group-mean centred to isolate within-person effects. Gameplay timing was also included as a categorical predictor at Level 1. This variable was dummy-coded to indicate whether participants ceased gaming < 1 h, 1–2 h, or > 2 h before bedtime, with “no gameplay that day” as the reference category. At Level 2, predictors included sleep hygiene scores, habitual sleep timing and duration, calculated as the mean across the monitoring period for each participant, and age. All level 2 predictors were grand mean centred to facilitate interpretation and reduce multicollinearity. Effect sizes for fixed effects were obtained using established conversion procedures for multilevel models, with r reported for continuous predictors as an index of effect magnitude [40].

The analysis began with an unconditional means model (random intercept, no predictors) to estimate variance components and the intraclass correlation coefficient. Level 1 predictors were then entered sequentially as nightly deviations in bed/wake times, sleep duration, and gameplay timing, followed at Level 2 by sleep hygiene scores, habitual sleep timing and duration, and age. All models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, which provides less biased estimates of variance components in smaller samples [40]. As restricted maximum likelihoods are not directly comparable across models with differing fixed effects, marginal and conditional R2 values are reported as descriptive indices of variance explained rather than as formal tests of model improvement. Specifically, marginal R2 reflects the variance explained by fixed effects alone, while conditional R2 captures the variance explained by both fixed and random effects [41]. The final model incorporated all predictors from both levels.

To assess robustness to modelling assumptions, the final model was re-estimated using [1] an ordinal multilevel specification (cumulative logit) with a random intercept for participant, and [2] a linear multilevel model incorporating an AR(1) within-person residual covariance structure to account for potential serial dependence across adjacent nights. Lastly, daily caffeine intake was considered as a potential covariate to account for short-term stimulant effects on perceived sleep quality [1]. However, due to frequent missing or incomplete data, caffeine intake was excluded from the analysis.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

The sample comprised 24 participants (male = 16; female = 8) aged 18–33 years (M = 21.79, SD = 3.81), with a mean age of 20.13 years (SD = 2.42) for males and 25.13 years (SD = 4.02) for females. Participants reported engagement across a range of esports titles, including League of Legends (42%, n = 10), Overwatch 2 (21%, n = 5), Super Smash Bros. Ultimate (17%, n = 4), Valorant (12%, n = 3), and Rocket League (8%, n = 2). The sample comprised both casual players who only competed in ranked online matches (42%, n = 10) and competitive esports athletes who also participated in organised tournaments or leagues (58%, n = 14). All participants are considered non-elite based on the elite continuum defined by Poulus et al. [42] At baseline, participants reported a median of 12.5 h of gameplay per week (IQR = 10.5–18.5) and an average daily caffeine intake of 1.6 servings (SD = 1.8). Daily diaries collected during the monitoring period indicated lower engagement, with participants averaging 2.05 h of gameplay per day (SD = 2.21) and 0.8 caffeine servings per day (SD = 1.39).

Baseline questionnaire responses indicated generally moderate sleep outcomes, including good overall sleep quality, average sleep hygiene, moderate daytime sleepiness, and evening chronotype. Exploratory comparisons revealed no significant gender differences across baseline measures (all ps > 0.05), although females reported slightly poorer sleep quality than males. During the monitoring period, participants averaged approximately 6 h 42 min of total sleep per night (SD = 51 min) and rated their nightly sleep quality as moderate (Median = 3, IQR = 3–4). Consistent with the baseline comparisons, no significant gender differences were observed in nightly sleep duration or perceived sleep quality (both ps > 0.05). Correlational Analyses

Bivariate correlations were examined to explore associations between baseline questionnaire scores and actigraphy-derived sleep parameters. Age and sleep hygiene scores showed similar patterns of association with other sleep outcomes. Both were positively correlated with longer sleep onset latency and higher sleep quality and daytime sleepiness scores (medium-to-large effect sizes), indicating that older age and poorer sleep hygiene were associated with poorer sleep quality and greater daytime sleepiness. In addition, older age was associated with earlier bedtimes and wake times and greater morningness (medium effect), whereas poorer sleep hygiene was linked with later bedtimes and wake times (small-to-medium effects) and greater eveningness (medium to large effect). Evening chronotypes were associated with poorer sleep quality and sleep efficiency, later bedtimes and wake times, longer sleep onset latency, and greater wake after sleep onset (medium-to-large effects). Bedtime and wake time were positively correlated (large effect), and sleep duration was positively correlated with wake time (large effect) while showing little association with bedtime. Finally, poorer sleep quality was significantly correlated with longer sleep onset latency (large effect), greater wake after sleep onset, and reduced sleep efficiency (medium to large effects). Multilevel Modelling

The final model revealed significant variance in subjective sleep quality ratings, both within and between participants, with the intraclass correlation coefficient indicating that approximately 20% of the variance was attributable to between-person differences and the remaining 80% to within-person variability. Nights with greater sleep duration relative to an individual’s usual amount were associated with higher sleep quality ratings, F(1175) = 4.03, p < .05, r = .15 (small effect), whereas nightly bedtimes and wake times were not significant. Nights in which gameplay ended 1–2 hours before bedtime were associated with poorer perceived sleep quality compared with nights without gameplay, F(1177) = 5.14, p = .03, r = .17 (small effect). Gameplay ending less than 1 h or more than 2 hours before bedtime was non-significant. At Level 2, poorer sleep hygiene predicted lower overall sleep quality, whereas later habitual bedtimes were associated with higher sleep quality ratings (both ps < 0.05). Habitual sleep duration, habitual wake time, and age were not significant predictors. The final model explained 24.1% of the variance in subjective sleep quality through fixed effects (marginal R2) and 39.3% when both fixed and random effects were considered (conditional R2).

Sensitivity analyses addressing distributional assumptions and potential within-person serial dependence produced a consistent pattern of results, with key predictors retaining the same direction and statistical significance. Discussion

The present study investigated within-person and between-person predictors of sleep quality in esports athletes using nightly actigraphy and subjective diary data collected over one week. The findings highlight the importance of both nightly sleep duration and stable behavioural characteristics in shaping perceived sleep outcomes. Across the monitoring period, participants averaged approximately 6.7 h of total sleep per night, with a mean bedtime of 12:40 a.m. and wake time of 9:01 a.m. Additional sleep parameters indicated moderate sleep efficiency (~ 79%), a relatively short sleep onset (~ 13 min), and extended wake after sleep onset (~ 91 min), while IIV analyses indicated modest night-to-night variability in sleep duration and continuity. These characteristics are consistent with prior research in esports populations, which reports delayed sleep timing, insufficient sleep duration, and reduced efficiency [5, 10], suggesting that the sample was representative of the broader player base. Correlation analyses further supported this interpretation, showing that poorer sleep hygiene and evening chronotype were associated with later and more fragmented sleep patterns, while poorer subjective sleep quality aligned with objective indicators of sleep disruption (i.e., delayed sleep onset and night-time awakenings).

Night-to-Night Variability and Sleep Quality

The present findings suggest that subjective sleep quality reflects both the average nightly sleep duration and its stability across nights. Consistent with the study expectations, nights in which individuals obtained more sleep than their usual amount during the monitoring period were associated with higher perceived sleep quality, indicating that relative nightly deviations in sleep duration may influence perceptions of restfulness. These results reinforce the role of IIV as a behavioural marker of sleep regularity [43]. Within the framework of the two-process model of sleep regulation [44, 45], longer-than-average nights may facilitate the greater dissipation of homeostatic sleep pressure (Process S), while shorter or irregular nights leave residual pressure that undermines perceptions of restfulness. Variability in nightly sleep patterns may also heighten susceptibility to circadian misalignment, or “social jetlag”, which has been linked to poorer sleep quality and wellbeing even when adequate sleep duration is obtained [8, 16]. Such mechanisms may be particularly consequential in esports, where delayed sleep timing and irregular schedules are common among esports athletes [5, 10].

Gameplay Timing and Sleep Outcomes

The multilevel analysis identified nightly deviations in sleep duration as the most robust predictor of perceived sleep quality, with the timing of esports gameplay contributing additional explanatory value. Specifically, nights where gameplay ended 1–2 h before bedtime were associated with poorer sleep quality compared to nights without gameplay, after controlling for other predictors. The findings align with systematic reviews that show adult gaming is linked to delayed bedtimes, shorter sleep duration, and poorer sleep quality, with evening gameplay and longer gaming sessions posing greater risk [6, 46]. For competitive esports athletes, these risks may be amplified, as late-night training and competition can elevate arousal, extend light exposure, and sustain cognitive and emotional activation, thereby reducing the likelihood of restorative sleep [1].

The absence of a significant effect when gameplay ended within one hour of bedtime warrants consideration. One possibility is that gameplay during this window may differ in intensity, with players engaging in more casual, low-stakes activity such as routine practice sessions rather than cognitively demanding or competitive matches. Such sessions are unlikely to elicit the same physiological or emotional arousal as high-pressure gameplay, thereby reducing their potential to interfere with sleep [5]. This pattern may also reflect individual differences in sensitivity to the arousing properties of evening gameplay, with some players less affected by its effects or by light exposure. In addition, the impact of late-night gaming may be further reduced when individuals adopt pre-sleep routines that lower arousal, such as relaxation techniques, dimming screens, or engaging in less stimulating activities before bed [1, 6]. Alternatively, the null effect may be due to limited statistical power, given the moderate sample size and relatively few nights represented in this gameplay category.

Between-Person Predictors of Sleep Quality

At the between-person level, stable characteristics differentiated overall perceptions of sleep quality across the monitoring period. Poorer sleep hygiene was associated with lower sleep quality ratings, consistent with previous studies showing that maladaptive routines and pre-sleep behaviours contribute to delayed sleep timing and reduced efficiency in esports athletes [5, 10]. In contrast, later habitual bedtime was associated with higher average sleep quality. This pattern diverges from broader findings in young adults and esports populations, where later bedtimes and evening chronotype are commonly associated with poorer sleep outcomes [5, 8, 10, 47]. In the context of esports, later bedtimes may reflect behavioural routines in which training and gameplay activities frequently extend into the evening hours. For some players, these schedules may be more compatible with their habitual sleep timing, potentially contributing to more favourable perceptions of sleep quality despite later clock times. Habitual sleep duration, wake time, and age were not significantly associated with sleep quality in the present model, suggesting that behavioural routines and sleep hygiene practices may play a more prominent role in shaping perceived sleep outcomes than demographic characteristics or average sleep duration.

These between-person findings can also be considered alongside the nightly analyses reported earlier. While longer-than-average sleep duration was associated with higher perceived sleep quality on a given night, habitual sleep duration across the monitoring period was not related to average sleep quality. This distinction suggests that perceptions of sleep quality may reflect both short-term fluctuations in sleep behaviour and broader behavioural routines. Occasional nights with longer sleep may provide short-term restorative benefits, whereas stable behavioural patterns, particularly sleep hygiene practices and habitual sleep timing, may shape individuals’ overall perceptions of sleep quality across time. Together, these findings highlight the importance of sleep hygiene, regularity, and the alignment of sleep–wake schedules with the behavioural demands of esports training and competition.

Study Implications

The present findings have several implications for both research and applied practice in esports. First, the robust within-person association between nightly sleep duration and perceived quality underscores the importance of sleep regularity for recovery processes. Interventions that promote consistent sleep–wake schedules, even when the average duration appears sufficient, may enhance restfulness and readiness to perform. Second, the exploratory evidence that gameplay timing relative to bedtime contributes to subjective sleep quality highlights a potential behavioural target for intervention. Educating players and coaches about the risks of late-night gaming and encouraging earlier training sessions or pre-sleep routines that reduce arousal may help minimise sleep disruption. Third, the identification of sleep hygiene as a between-person predictor mirrors established findings in broader sleep science, suggesting that known determinants of sleep outcomes remain relevant in esports populations. Identifying poor sleep habits and providing education may help address the different challenges esports athletes face, supporting healthier routines and improving overall sleep quality in esports populations. Finally, the methodological application of multilevel modelling demonstrates the value of distinguishing nightly fluctuations from stable characteristics, highlighting the importance of looking beyond mean-level estimates to capture intra-individual variability in sleep behaviour.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the present findings. The modest sample size reduced statistical power, particularly for detecting effects in less frequent gameplay timing categories and limits the generalisability of the results to the broader esports population. All participants were from Queensland, Australia, reducing the representativeness of the global esports population. The sample size also prevented analyses by competitive tier, restricting the ability to examine whether sleep outcomes or training demands differed between casual players and competitive esports athletes. Self-selection bias also needs to be considered as a limitation, as participation may be skewed towards more motivated or health-conscious players. The one-week monitoring period provided initial insight into short-term nightly fluctuations but may not capture longer-term sleep patterns or compensatory recovery that unfold across multiple weeks of training and competition. This limitation was compounded by the timing of data collection, which extended beyond the competitive season for many players and their respective esport, likely contributing to lower gameplay engagement during the monitoring period.

The inclusion of multiple esports titles also introduces variability, as competitive calendars differ and may not align consistently across participants. Prior studies show that both sleep patterns [5] and training demands [48] shift as competition intensifies, underscoring the importance of considering seasonal context when evaluating sleep behaviour in esports. Finally, while caffeine intake was recorded, limited variability and reliance on self-report restricted the capacity for detailed analysis of its influence on nightly sleep quality. Other aspects of participants’ daily routines, such as work or study schedules, physical activity, and dietary behaviours, were not systematically recorded and may also influence sleep behaviour and perceived sleep quality.

Despite actigraphy being a validated tool for field-based monitoring, it remains an indirect proxy for sleep–wake behaviour, derived from movement, and cannot capture detailed aspects of sleep architecture or physiological arousal that may be particularly relevant in esports contexts. Additionally, daily sleep diaries are subject to self-report bias, in which players may report healthier sleep behaviours due to factors such as social desirability, leading to a more favourable impression. Future research could incorporate multimodal designs that combine actigraphy with physiological markers such as heart rate variability and cognitive measures to capture downstream performance effects. Intervention studies targeting sleep hygiene and sleep regularity may also be valuable for translating these findings into practice, with strategies such as maintaining consistent bed and wake times, managing caffeine intake, and establishing pre-sleep routines to reduce arousal.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both nightly fluctuations in sleep behaviour and stable individual characteristics shape subjective sleep quality in esports athletes. Longer-than-average nightly sleep duration was associated with higher perceived sleep quality, highlighting the influence of intra-individual variability on restorative perceptions of sleep. Gameplay timing contributed modestly at the nightly level, with gameplay ending 1–2 h before bedtime linked to poorer sleep quality relative to days without gaming. At the between-person level, sleep hygiene and habitual bedtime accounted for meaningful variance in overall sleep quality. Collectively, these findings underscore the intertwined roles of sleep regularity, behavioural routines, and lifestyle factors in shaping sleep outcomes among esports athletes, and reinforce the importance of evaluating both within-person and between-person determinants when examining sleep health in performance-based gaming contexts.
