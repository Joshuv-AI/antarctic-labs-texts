Ultrasound-guided diffuse optical tomography: an adjunct to ultrasound that can reduce unnecessary breast biopsies
Authors: Quing Zhu, Debbie Bennett, Ian S Hagemann, Jaimee Mannix, Kimberly Wiele, Megan Luther, Jingqin Luo, Steven P Poplack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast biopsy is recommended for any lesion in which the radiologist deems the likelihood of malignancy to be greater than 2% and more than 75% of biopsies return with benign results. This study assesses whether the total hemoglobin concentration (HbT) and blood oxygen saturation (%sO2) of malignant and benign breast lesions, obtained using Ultrasound-guided diffuse optical tomography (US-guided DOT), can help reduce benign breast biopsy rates.Methods238 women consecutively recruited were examined with US-guided DOT before their US-guided biopsy. Three or four radiologists independently provided BI-RADS assessments using pre-procedure US and mammography (when available), first without and then with adjunctive US-guided DOT data. Radiologists and study personnel were blinded to biopsy outcomes. HbT and %sO2 were computed and correlated with histopathology. One-way ANOVA test was used on multiple groups followed by a post-hoc Tukey test. Student t-test was performed on any two groups.Results226 women (mean 52 years, 19–85 years) were analyzed, with histopathology showing: 70 invasive carcinomas (grade 3, n = 25, and grade 1&2, n = 45), 7 DCIS, 9 high-risk lesions, and 140 benign lesions. The integration of DOT-derived HbT and %sO2 reduced benign biopsies by 24.54% with a false-negative rate (FNR) of 1.78%. The HbT of the malignancy group (invasive carcinoma and DCIS) was significantly higher than that of all benign lesions (P < 0.001) and the %sO2 was significantly lower than in benign lesions (P = 0.033). The HbT of grade 3 carcinomas was significantly higher than that of grade 1&2 carcinomas (P = 0.042), while the %sO2 was significantly lower (P = 0.015). The HbT of carcinomas smaller than 2 cm (measured by ultrasound ) was not significantly different from that of carcinomas larger than 2 cm (P = 0.781). However, %sO2 differed significantly between these two size groups (P = 0.023).ConclusionsIntegration of HbT and %sO2 into the radiologists’ BI-RADS imaging assessments of ultrasound-visible breast lesions can reduce benign biopsy rates by 25%, while maintaining a FNR below 2%. The correlation of HbT and %sO2 with invasive carcinoma grade, and the size-independent HbT contrast and size-dependent %sO2 level of invasive carcinomas may guide preferential diagnosis of malignancies.Keywords: Breast cancer diagnosis, Reduction of benign breast biopsy, Ultrasound, Ultrasound-guided diffuse optical tomography, Tumor angiogenesis, Tumor hypoxia

Background

Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels, is required for breast tumor growth and metastasis [1–2]. As breast tumors grow, their metabolic demand causes local hypoxia [3], stimulating angiogenesis [4]. Advancements in imaging techniques that require contrast agents have been explored to image tumor angiogenesis [5–8]. Near-infrared (NIR) imaging using diffused light is a promising non-invasive tool for evaluating breast tumor hemoglobin content, which is related to tumor angiogenesis, and blood oxygenation [9–11]. Diffuse optical tomography (DOT) utilizes laser diodes to emit light at multiple NIR wavelengths and measures the transmitted or reflected light from breast tissue. The detected light can be used to compute total hemoglobin concentration (HbT) and blood oxygen saturation (%sO2) from optical absorption maps obtained at multiple wavelengths. HbT and %sO2 are functional parameters that can characterize breast lesion vascularity and metabolism without the need for exogenous contrast agents [9]. Diagnostic US is typically indicated to evaluate mammographic abnormalities other than calcifications [12], as well as palpable abnormalities that are not clearly benign on mammography [13]. While US can usually distinguish solid from cystic masses, it has limited capacity to differentiate benign from malignant solid masses due to morphological feature overlap and lack of functional information [14]. Elvirici et al. assessed the predictive value of BI-RADS descriptors of 186 non-palpable low (BI-RADS 4a), moderate (BI-RADS 4b) and high suspicion (BI-RADS 4c) pathology proven solid breast masses [15]. Only shape and lesion orientation were significantly different between benign and malignant lesions, and no combination of descriptors was sufficiently discriminatory to omit biopsy. As a result, a biopsy is recommended for any lesion in which the radiologist deems the likelihood of malignancy to be greater than 2% (BI-RADS final assessment of suspicious BI-RADS 4 or highly suggestive of malignancy BI-RADS 5) and more than 75% biopsies return with benign results [16]. Improving the specificity of diagnostic ultrasound would decrease benign biopsy rates and associated patient anxiety, morbidity, and medical costs [16]. DOT and US are complementary methods. US provides structural information while DOT provides functional information on vascular content (HbT) and lesion metabolism (%sO2). This synergy results in improved diagnosis [10]. US-guided DOT improves DOT imaging reconstruction by providing information on lesion location and therefore overcoming the limitation of light scattering-induced reconstruction inaccuracy. US-guided DOT has shown promise as an adjunct to US in improving the specificity of lesion assessment and in evaluating neoadjuvant treatment response [17–20]. Mammography-guided DOT has been explored by several research groups combining either 2-D mammography or 3-D tomosynthesis with DOT using the fusion of high-resolution breast lesion structure and DOT data to improve breast cancer diagnosis [21–22]. MRI-guided DOT has also been extensively investigated to leverage the high-resolution MRI images to guide the DOT reconstruction and improve the DOT reconstruction accuracy for accurate breast cancer diagnosis [23–25]. Compared with mammography-guided DOT and MRI-guided DOT, US-guided DOT systems are more easily integrated into clinical operations as commercial US systems using hand-held probes can be retrofitted for simultaneous US and DOT data acquisition and imaging. However, most earlier studies have not engaged radiologists to prospectively integrate US-guided DOT into clinical decision-making [18–19]. Our initial prospective multi-reader study of US-guided DOT in a smaller cohort of 63 patients demonstrated a 23.5% reduction in benign breast biopsy rates while maintaining high sensitivity [17]. This study builds upon previous work by integrating US-guided, DOT-derived %sO2 and HbT into the radiologists’ prospective BI-RADS assessments of US-visible breast lesions recommended for biopsy with a larger cohort of 226 patients. Additionally, it reports the characteristics of HbT and %sO2 in both malignant and benign breast lesions, and examines the correlation of these parameters with invasive tumor grade and size within the cohort. The HbT and %sO2 analysis helps explain the radiologist assessments and guide future studies.

Methods

Patients

This single-site study was approved by the IRB and was HIPAA compliant (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03842358). Women 18 years and older with ultrasound-visible breast abnormalities referred for an ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy were screened by the study coordinator and assessed by one of the four study radiologists (R1–R4) with 32, 35, 11, and 4 years of breast US experience, to confirm lesion eligibility. Participants were excluded if the lesion was located within 5 mm of the skin, beneath the nipple-areolar complex, at the site of a recent (< 6 months) biopsy, or in the same quadrant as additional imaging abnormalities or if there was an abnormality in the mirror image location of the contralateral breast or prior contralateral mastectomy. Lesions of any size and depth were included, including non-mass-like lesions provided that US identified the region of interest as distinct from background tissue. Additionally, if a patient had more than one lesion for biopsy, the lesion with the highest suspicious level was imaged. Eligible patients were contacted and those who agreed signed the informed consent on the day of the biopsy.

Image acquisition

Details of US and US-guided DOT examinations have been reported [17]. Briefly, a US examination was performed by a US technologist, supervised by a study radiologist, and a US-guided DOT exam was performed subsequently by a DOT engineer. The breast US was acquired in approximately 5–10 min and was used to localize the lesion of interest for subsequent US-guided DOT. The DOT engineer was directed to the target lesion and then performed US-guided DOT measurements of the index lesion and the mirror location of the contralateral breast. The measurements obtained from the mirror contralateral breast served as a normal reference for calculating perturbation and background optical absorption and scattering properties used for DOT reconstruction. DOT measurement can be affected by the chest wall’s angle and depth and tissue heterogeneity, especially when the thickness of the breast tissue is less than 2 cm. Our earlier studies showed that the mirror contralateral measurements of the index lesion were a more reliable reference for computing the perturbation, the normalized difference between lesion and reference measurements, and average optical properties used for DOT reconstruction [26]. Co-registered real-time US can be readily used to identify the matched chest-wall depth and angle on the mirror location of the contralateral breast.

US-guided DOT system and total hemoglobin and %sO2 computation

The hand-held probe consists of 9 source fibers and 14 light guides coupled to 14 parallel detectors (see Fig. 1) [27]. A US probe is inserted into the middle slot of the 10-cm diameter probe during US-guided DOT acquisition. Each DOT dataset consists of measurements obtained from 9 sources and 14 detectors. Four laser diodes (730, 785, 808, and 830 nm) were used to illuminate the tissue sequentially, while the reflected light signals at each wavelength were acquired simultaneously. The entire four-wavelength dataset consists of 504 (4 × 9 × 14) measurements. Multiple DOT datasets were acquired of the lesion (8–10 datasets) and the contralateral site (5–8 datasets). The DOT acquisition took < 5 s per dataset, and the entire US-guided DOT examination took approximately 5–10 min.

A US-guided DOT system and a hand-held probe with US transducer inserted in the middle of the 10 cm diameter probe during US-guided DOT examination. B Zoom-in view of the probe

The normalized perturbation at each wavelength was used for DOT reconstruction. Data outliers were filtered out before DOT reconstruction [28]. The DOT reconstruction algorithm has been reported elsewhere [29]. In brief, the DOT reconstruction uses ultrasound lesion identification to segment the imaging volume into a region of interest (ROI) and background to improve the reconstruction. The ROI is two to three times larger in spatial dimensions than the tumor size (as measured by co-registered US) due to the low spatial resolution of diffused light. A tighter ROI in the depth dimension is set by using co-registered US to accommodate the poor depth resolution of diffused light. The optical absorption distribution at each wavelength was reconstructed using a regularized Conjugate gradient optimization method for inversion, and the total hemoglobin concentration (HbT), oxygenated-hemoglobin (oxyHb), and deoxygenated-hemoglobin concentration (deoxyHb) maps were computed from optical absorption distributions of four wavelengths. %sO2 was computed as the ratio of mean oxyHb measured from the full width of half maximum of oxyHb map over mean HbT measured from the full width of half maximum of HbT map. An average maximum value of HbTs and an average %sO2s were obtained from 8 to 10 lesion measurements acquired from the index tumor to characterize each lesion. The data processing and reconstruction were done in about 20 min. The study engineers, blinded to biopsy results, submitted the DOT form for each patient with an HbT map, average maximum HbT, and %sO2 to the study coordinator for US-guided DOT assessment by study radiologists.

BI-RADS assessment

Interpretation of conventional breast imaging (CI), (i.e., US and mammography if available), and DOT data was performed by four study breast radiologists, consisting of two readers (R1, R2) who participated in the initial 63 patient cohort [17] and two new readers (R3, R4). The two new readers (R3, R4) underwent training in DOT interpretation using early data (see details in Ref. 17). No consensus mechanism was implemented among readers. The US images were recorded in longitudinal and transverse planes as well as Doppler images in one plane per American College of Radiology guidelines. Study radiologists first provided an assessment based on pre-procedure ultrasound (and mammography if available) termed conventional imaging BI-RADS assessment (CI-BI-RADS), which may have differed from the assessment documented in the original exam report. The readers were then provided with the HbT map and quantitative HbT and %sO2 values and re-assessed the lesion by integrating information from both conventional imaging and DOT (CI&DOT-BI-RADS). Based on the readers’ availability, three (35% of the cases) or four (65% of the cases) radiologists independently evaluated each lesion and assigned CI-BI-RADS and CI&DOT-BI-RADS. The integration of DOT information into the readers’ CI&DOT-BI-RADS assessments was guided by previously published thresholds (HbT typically < 50 µM for benign lesions and ≥ 78 µM for malignant lesions) [17–18]. Additionally, readers were also aware that low %sO2 (< 55%) was typically associated with malignant lesions and high %sO2 (> 70%) with benign lesions. These %sO2 thresholds were derived from a retrospective analysis of a previous study involving 63 patients using the same US-guided DOT system [17], and were applied prospectively to the current study cohort. In general, when conventional imaging demonstrated a low to moderate suspicion lesion (BI-RADS 4a or 4b) and HbT was below 50 µM, the lesion assessment may be downgraded or unchanged based on their CI assessment. Lesions with HbT ≥ 78 µM were typically upgraded or unchanged based on their CI appearance. BI-RADS assessments of lesions with HbT in the 50–78 µM range were downgraded or upgraded based on a combination of the CI appearance and whether the HbT was in the lower or upper part of the intermediate range. The spatial distribution of HbT provided with HbT maps was also incorporated into CI&DOT-BI-RADS assessment. In general, focal HbT pattern or peripheral distribution of HbT increased the level of suspicion, while a diffuse HbT pattern decreased the level of suspicion. The study radiologists, blinded from biopsy results, submitted CI -BI-RADS and CI&DOT-BI-RADS assessment forms to the study coordinator who entered these data into a research database, (OnCore™), developed by the WUSTL School of Medicine.

Histopathology

Based on pathology reports, lesions were categorized by the study pathologist as invasive carcinoma, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), high-risk, or benign lesions. High-risk lesions reported at the time of biopsy included fibroepithelial lesion not definitively diagnosed as a fibroadenoma; intraductal and sclerosing papillomas; radial scars/complex sclerosing lesions; fibromatosis; and atypical lobular hyperplasia [30]. This is a group of diagnoses designated by our radiology group for surgical referral and consideration for excision. Benign lesions consisted of fibroadenomas, fibrocystic changes, and other benign results (Table 1).

When more than one diagnosis was made, the case was classified based on the most pathologically significant diagnosis. Similarly, for cases in which surgical excision was performed, the case was also classified based on the most pathologically significant diagnosis. For patients directly undergoing surgery without any neoadjuvant treatment, the tumor size was ascertained from surgical pathology reports. A breast pathologist with 13 years of experience was responsible for ascertaining core biopsy and related surgical results.

Statistical analysis

The reader agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient between two readers, and Fleiss’ kappa coefficient among four readers for CI, CI&DOT readings. One-way ANOVA was performed on HbT of 5 histology groups followed by a post-hoc Tukey honest test. The Student t-test was used to compare invasive carcinoma & DCIS vs. benign lesions & high-risk lesions, invasive carcinomas of grade 3 versus grade 1&2, and invasive carcinomas of US size less than 2 cm versus greater than 2 cm. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman’s rho was used to assess the correlation of two variables. The statistical software Minitab 19 (Minitab, State College, PA, USA) was used for statistical calculations.

Results

A total of 238 consecutively recruited patients met eligibility criteria and provided consent for the US-guided DOT imaging study from July 2020 to August 2024 (see Fig. 2). 12 patients were excluded from the data analysis for the reasons given in Fig. 2. The remaining 226 patients (mean age = 52 years, range 19–85 years) constituted the study group having undergone US and US-guided DOT imaging and subsequent core needle biopsy of the index breast abnormality.

Patient chart. A total of 238 patients were enrolled and 12 were excluded. The final data analysis included 226 patients with 226 lesions

Tumor characteristics

The 226 lesions were categorized into 5 groups based on histopathology: (1) invasive carcinomas (n = 70) and DCIS (n = 7); (2) high-risk lesions (n = 9; initially n = 10 prior to surgical upgrade); (3) fibrocystic changes (n = 46); (4) fibroadenomas (n = 63); and (5) other benign lesions (n = 31) (Table 1). The three benign categories were used to explore which benign histological group may benefit the most from the addition of US-guided DOT data. Seven high-risk lesions were excised to reveal one invasive lobular carcinoma, one borderline phyllodes tumor, one benign phyllodes tumor, and other benign findings (n = 4) (Table 1). One high-risk sclerosing papilloma was upgraded to invasive lobular carcinoma upon surgical excision. Thus the invasive carcinoma group totaled 70, and the high-risk lesions were reduced to 9 for analysis (Table 1). Among the 70 invasive carcinomas, 48 had tumor sizes available from the upfront surgery. The remaining had neoadjuvant therapy (n = 19) and no further follow-up information (n = 3) (Table 1).

BI-RADS assessment based on the inclusion of US-DOT data

A total of 823 initial CI-BIRADS assessments and 823 subsequent CI&DOT-BIRADS assessments were provided by study radiologists. Of note, study readers downgraded 45 assessments from the assessment documented in the clinical report. Table 2 shows the change in BI-RADS assessment from the clinical report, i.e. clinical radiologist assessment vs. study radiologist assessment, as well as the change in study radiologist assessment due to US-DOT integration. There was no change in the biopsy recommendation for 630 reads. In 136 reads, a review of US-DOT information led to a downgrade in the BI-RADS assessment to a non-biopsy recommendation (BI-RADS 2 or 3). On average benign lesions were appropriately downgraded in 24.54% (119/485), while only 1 downgrade (3.03%, 1/33) occurred in the high-risk group. Malignancy was inappropriately downgraded in 1.78% (5/281), involving three patients.

Three of the five false negative (FN) reads occurred in a 35 year-old lactating patient who presented with a palpable mass. On conventional imaging, the lesion was assessed as low suspicion (BI-RADS 4a) by 3 readers and moderate suspicion (BI-RADS 4b) by 1 reader with differential diagnosis favoring lactating adenoma, focal lactational change, or fibroadenoma. The HbT map showed a low maximum value of 52.5 µM in a diffuse distribution. The %sO2 was moderate at 61.4%. The low HbT in this case was likely caused by the known phenomenon of decreased HbT levels during lactation [31]. The three readers initially graded the lesion as BI-RADS 4a but later downgraded their assessments to BI-RADS 3 for follow-up. One incorrect downgrade involved a 68-year-old patient with a clinical history of trauma and a hyperechoic mass on ultrasound, thought to be fat necrosis. HbT was low at 55.6 µM, and %sO2 was moderate at 65.2%. The lesion was graded as moderate suspicion (BI-RADS 4b) based on CI and was downgraded to a probably benign assessment (BI-RADS 3) by one of 4 study radiologists. Surgical pathology revealed a 2.2 cm invasive lobular carcinoma. The last FN occurred in a 63 year-old patient who presented with an oval 1.3 cm mass with very low HbT of 21.8 µM almost indistinguishable from background and high %sO2 of 83.3%. The lesion was initially graded as high to moderate suspicion (BI-RADS 4c, 4b, 4b) based on CI, but one of the three study radiologists later downgraded it to a probably benign assessment (BI-RADS 3). Biopsy revealed 0.9 cm, grade 2 DCIS.

Reader agreement

All four readers demonstrated moderate interobserver agreement for both CI-BIRADS and CI&DOT-BIRADS. R1 and R2 reviewers who were experienced with US-guided DOT interpretation having participated in the initial study of 63 patients [17] had a Cohen’s kappa of CI and CI&DOT of 0.453 and 0.432 of the current study, respectively. The R3 and R4 reviewers were trained on DOT before participating in the study and had a kappa of 0.581 and 0.475, respectively. The Fleiss’ kappa of the four readers for CI and CI&DOT were moderate, 0.551 and 0.426, respectively.

HbT and %sO2 characteristics and statistics

One-way ANOVA followed by a post-hoc Tukey honest test showed that the HbT of the invasive carcinoma and DCIS group (all malignancies) was significantly higher than those of fibroadenoma (adjusted Pa < 0.001), fibrocystic changes (adjusted Pa < 0.001), and other benign lesions (adjusted Pa = 0.003), respectively (Fig. 5). The HbT of high-risk lesions was not statistically different from that of malignant lesions. %sO2 did not differentiate the malignancy, high-risk, and other benign groups. When US-guided DOT data for all malignancies was compared to all benign lesion groups including high-risk lesions, the HbT was significantly higher than benign lesions (P < 0.001), and the %sO2 of all malignancies was significantly lower than that of all benign lesions (P = 0.033) (Fig. 6). Note that One-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests (e.g., Tukey) provides a more rigorous approach to evaluating group differences by adjusting for multiple comparisons to control the Type I error rate. These corrections make the test more conservative. In contrast, a t-test between two groups is designed to detect specific pairwise differences without such adjustment.

CI&DOT-BI-RAD scores were prospectively assessed by four readers based on training with prior HbT and %sO2 data. To evaluate how continuous values and threshold guidelines influenced their decisions, we computed Spearman’s rho between CI&DOT-BI-RAD scores and both HbT and %sO2, using continuous data and threshold-based scales (Table 5).

The Spearman’s rho values from continuous values were slightly higher than those from threshold-based scales, though both were comparable. These results suggest that the readers integrated their clinical breast imaging experience with DOT data rather than relying solely on predefined thresholds.

The US-guided, DOT-derived HbT and %sO2 are best used as an adjunct to US in reduction of benign biopsies while maintaining the acceptable false positive rate. We also evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of HbT and %sO2 alone using different thresholds from this patient cohort. An HbT threshold of 70 µM provided a sensitivity of 71.4% and specificity of 68.5%, while a %sO2 threshold of 65% resulted in a compromise sensitivity of 65.8% and specificity of 51.1%. Additionally, HbT and %sO2 measurements of individual invasive carcinomas, grouped by biopsy results as either Grade 3 or Grade < 3, help characterize the invasiveness of carcinomas. Furthermore, HbT and %sO2 values of invasive carcinomas, categorized by US size as either > 2 cm or < 2 cm, reveal how these functional parameters correlate with tumor growth. Both tumor grade and US size analyses were performed exclusively on invasive carcinomas. These findings provide valuable guidance for radiologists in future prospective clinical trials.

Discussion

In this prospective, blinded study we have correlated US-guided DOT-derived measures of vascular content (HbT) and metabolism (%sO2) with five histopathology subgroups representing the entire spectrum of breast lesions in a large patient cohort. HbT alone significantly discriminated malignant from benign lesions but did not differentiate malignancy from high-risk pathology. While this may be viewed as a limitation, it remains important to diagnose high-risk pathology for future breast cancer risk estimation due to an associated low, but potential 0.2–5% risk of upgrade to malignancy from core biopsy to surgical excision [30, 32]. One abnormality, with HbT and %sO2 of 78.6 µM and 54.5%, initially diagnosed with high-risk sclerosing papilloma on core biopsy was upgraded to malignancy on surgical excision. The original BI-RAD scores were 4c, 4c, and 4b, and the later CI-DOT scores were 4c, 4c, and 4c. However, the small sample size prevents assessment of the likelihood of an upgrade based on DOT parameters.

This study also highlights the value of US-guided DOT in reducing false positive biopsy recommendations. Fibroadenoma had the highest downgrade rate of all benign subgroups. Our results suggest that when a mass has sonographic features suggestive of a FA and a sufficiently low HbT (< 50µM), the assessment category can be safely downgraded. In the future, it may be possible to downgrade masses that meet probably benign criteria, i.e. oval shape, circumscribed margins, and no posterior features (BIRADS 5th ed), to a benign assessment, although this approach requires validation in the research setting.

In this study, inappropriate downgrades to follow-up occurred in two cancer and one DCIS cases, two of which had unusual or misleading presentations. Three of the five FN reads involved a lactating patient and the phenomenon of suppressed HbT in the setting of lactation was unknown to study readers. One case of a 2.2 cm ILC presented with a history of trauma and the combination of clinical history and relatively low HbT likely led to a FN interpretation. The third case is a 0.9 cm intermediate grade 2 DCIS which may not have neovascularity as reflected by low HbT contrast. While any missed cancer case is unacceptable, the false negative rate of adjunctive US-DOT at 1.78% is notably low, especially considering the masking effect of lactational changes in 3 of the 5 false negative cases. According to BI-RADS guidelines, a BI-RADS 3 assessment (probably benign) carries a malignancy risk of ≤ 2%. Nevertheless, it is important to recognize these pitfalls so they can be avoided as the technology becomes integrated into clinical practice. In comparison with photoacoustic imaging (PAI), a competitive optical imaging technology, the pivotal study of PAI involving 1690 patients and 7 study radiologists, showed a net reduction of benign lesion biopsy of 14.9% with a 2.6% FN rate [33]. As for functional information, both DOT and PAI provide HbT and %sO2, while DOT provides quantitative HbT and PAI computes relative HbT which can be system dependent. However, the DOT resolution is lower compared with PAI which has the same resolution as the US used by the PAI system. Regarding system cost, DOT systems employ low-cost Class II laser diodes priced under a hundred dollars, while PAI systems require high-cost pulsed lasers, typically Class IIIB, costing tens of thousands of dollars and providing sufficient energy for breast imaging. The adjunctive use of US Shear Wave Elastography in 1288 BIRAD 3 or 4 lesions resulted in 24.24% fewer benign biopsies and 1.98% undetected malignancies [34]. However, since SWE probe breast lesion stiffness, they may be limited in non-palpable and non-mass-like breast lesions [35–36]. As a comparison, US-guided DOT requires that lesions be US visible, however, no limitations are based on lesion palpability. As for non-mass-like lesions, as long as the suspicious region was visibly different than the surrounding tissue in US, DOT can map out lesion HbT distribution and compute the %sO2 value. Regarding system cost and operation, DOT system is low cost can be readily integrated with any conventional US system, while SWE requires special US transducers to generate shear waves and additional software that can be readily integrated with conventional ultrasound systems.

In the United States, over one million breast biopsies are performed each year, with 75–80% showing benign results. This high number of benign outcomes underscores the importance of improved accuracy of diagnostic examinations. The combination of information from conventional (structural) imaging with functional (physiologic) imaging techniques has the potential to reduce the number of benign biopsies and associated healthcare costs and burdens. This study also demonstrates that breast radiologists can be readily trained to incorporate information from US-guided DOT to assign a more accurate combined BI-RADS final assessment than conventional imaging alone. Even with the readers’ diverse clinical experience, the inter-observer agreements of CI-BI-RADS and CI&DOT-BI-RADS were at moderate levels. The agreement of CI&DOT-BI-RADS assessments of two new readers was slightly lower than for conventional imaging alone. Further standardizing framework, rubric, or scoring tool can improve reproducibility across readers with different levels of experience. Ongoing work is also focused on the machine-learning-based integration of US BI-RAD reading with DOT data into a decision tree to streamline the breast biopsy decision-making which could help in a broader clinical adoption, particularly outside of experienced academic settings. It is known that angiogenesis is critical in the development, progression, and metastasis of solid tumor cells. Low levels of O2 trigger hypoxia-inducible factors that regulate the expression of multiple genes responsible for stimulating metabolism, angiogenesis, and cell division. Local angiogenesis is one of the tumor microenvironment’s long-term primary adaptation to low O2 levels [37]. Our study demonstrated that HbT is significantly higher in grade 3 tumors than that of grade 1 and 2 tumors, and %sO2 is significantly lower in grade 3 tumors than that of grade 1 and 2 combined. This suggests that HbT and %sO2 measured by US-guided DOT can characterize tumor aggressiveness. Additionally, %sO2 demonstrated as a good indicator of characterizing the large tumor local hypoxia condition, valuable for guiding breast cancer diagnosis and therapy. Furthermore, in our study, 70% of invasive cancers had tumor size less than 2.0 cm and HbT correlated with malignancy independent of tumor size. This suggests that the functional parameters of HbT and %sO2 have the potential to characterize one important metric of cancer aggressiveness in T1 tumors. Note that %sO2 estimation is in general less robust than HbT. However, our latest US-guided DOT system [27] has a much higher signal-to-noise ratio compared to earlier prototypes [18–19], and the reported patient cohort includes a large sample size of both malignant and benign lesion groups. The ability of technology to identify biologically meaningful breast cancer is critical to mitigate overdiagnosis, defined as the detection of breast cancers through screening methods, such as mammograms, that would not have caused any harm or symptoms during a person’s lifetime if left undetected. Overdiagnosis has been estimated to occur in 1–10% of mammography screening trials [38] and may be higher with screening tomosynthesis [39]. When compared to mammography, contrast-enhanced MRI (CE-MRI) has also been shown to preferentially identify higher-grade breast cancer [40]. Advanced imaging tools like CE-MRI also detect functional vascular changes, but require intravenous injection of contrast agents, potential monitoring of renal function, and risk of allergic reaction. CE-MRI is also disadvantaged by high intrinsic expense and restricted access. In contrast, US-guided DOT is low-cost and does not require intravenous injection. With the advancement of wireless US probes and systems, the US-guided DOT can be readily integrated with US as one unit for seamless clinical translation [41]. This study has several limitations. While consecutive patients were reviewed for eligibility, a proportion of patients were not approached for study entry due to factors that could compromise the DOT acquisition including: superficial (≤ 5 mm from epidermis) and/or subareolar lesion location and recent biopsy in the area. Because the US transducer is located at the center of the combined probe, the minimum DOT source-detector separation must be maintained at 2.5 to 3 centimeters, which reduces DOT sensitivity to superficial lesions. These exclusions consisted a small percentage of patient population referred for US-guided biopsy. Only US visible abnormalities undergoing biopsy were evaluated, so results may not apply to the general diagnostic imaging population. The study occurred in a single academic institution and study readers were exclusively breast radiologists thereby the generalizability of the study outcomes remains to be validated. US-guided DOT technology requires the contralateral breast measurements to compute perturbation and background tissue optical properties used for DOT reconstruction. A longer data pre-processing and DOT reconstruction time is needed which hinders real-time interpretation by radiologists. As a relatively new technique, US-guided DOT is still under development to optimize study protocols, data processing, image reconstruction, and interpretation. Our recent technical advances in US-guided DOT have focused on a robust deep-learning (DL)-based automated data processing pipeline [42], DL-based DOT difference imaging by using target measurements only [43], hybrid DL model for robust DOT reconstruction [44–45]. Furthermore, we have developed a DL fusion model that combines a US-based neural network and DOT neural network to achieve a more accurate classification than a single model approach can provide [46]. By validating and implementing this series of DL-based models, we can further improve US-guided DOT and bring this technology to clinics.

Conclusions

In conclusion, US-guided DOT is a promising adjunct to diagnostic breast ultrasound. Integration of US-guided, DOT-derived HbT and %sO2 into the BI-RADS assessment of ultrasound visible suspicious breast lesions can reduce false positive biopsy recommendations by 25%. However, this reduction has occurred at the expense of a false negative rate of 1.78%, which is lower than 2% based on BI-RADS 3 assessment (probably benign). As the US-DOT technology matures and radiologists learn its limitations, we anticipate that this FN rate can be further reduced.

HbT and %sO2 have the capacity to identify functional differences between malignant and benign lesions. Additionally, HbT contrast and %sO2 levels correlate with invasive carcinoma grade. HbT contrast is independent of carcinoma size measured by ultrasound, whereas %sO2 is size-dependent. These important findings may help guide preferential diagnosis of malignancies in future clinical trials.
