Comparing cognitive impairment using MACFIMS in patients with multiple sclerosis and healthy controls: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amirreza Nasirzadeh, Mohammad Mohammadi, Melika Arab Bafrani, Aynaz Mohammadi, Hossein Bakhtiari-Dovvombaygi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder affecting the central nervous system, leading to a range of symptoms that impact physical, psychiatric, and cognitive functions. Cognitive dysfunction is prevalent among patients with MS (pwMS), affecting at least 65% of patients, and includes deficits in processing speed, attention, learning, memory, and executive function. Despite the significant impact on daily life, cognitive impairment in MS patients is often underrecognized in clinical settings.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate cognitive function using the Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis (MACFIMS) battery among pwMS patients and healthy controls (HCs). A comprehensive search of the Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases was conducted on January 2024 following the PRISMA guidelines. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed research assessing the validity of the MACFIMS in adult MS patients. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed using standardized tools, and statistical analyses were conducted using R4.2.3.ResultsEight studies met the inclusion criteria, including a total of 1,481 pwMS and 1,072 HCs. The meta-analysis revealed significant cognitive deficits in pwMS patients compared to HCs across all the MACFIMS subtests, including language, spatial processing, new learning and memory, processing speed, and executive function. Processing speed and working memory were the most affected domains, with 36% of pwMS showing impairment on the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT). Subgroup analyses indicated that the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score significantly influenced cognitive impairment, while disease duration had a limited impact.ConclusionsThe MACFIMS effectively discriminates between pwMS patients and HCs, demonstrating its validity as a comprehensive cognitive assessment tool for MS. Routine cognitive screening, particularly for processing speed and working memory, is crucial for early detection and intervention. Future research should focus on the sensitivity and specificity of the MACFIMS across diverse MS subtypes and cultural contexts to enhance its global applicability in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-024-03943-2.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Cognitive impairment, MACFIMS

Background

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder affecting the central nervous system that is characterized by a combination of inflammatory and degenerative mechanisms [1]. The disease presents in distinct clinical phenotypes: Clinically Isolated Syndrome (CIS), the first symptomatic episode suggestive of MS [2]; Relapsing-Remitting MS (RRMS), marked by relapses followed by periods of remission with no apparent disease progression [3]; Secondary Progressive MS (SPMS), which evolves from RRMS and involves gradual neurological decline with or without relapses [4]; and Primary Progressive MS (PPMS), characterized by continuous disease progression from onset without relapses [4].

In patients with multiple sclerosis (pwMS), inflammation, demyelination, and neurodegeneration of the central nervous system (CNS) lead to a range of symptoms that can affect physical, psychiatric, and cognitive functions. MS affects more than 2.8 million people worldwide [5] and most commonly manifests in adults between the ages of 20 and 50, with the average age of onset being around 33 to 34 in recent studies [6]. Cognitive dysfunction is prevalent among patients with multiple sclerosis, afflicting at least 65% of patients across diverse disease presentations [7]. These deficits commonly encompass reduced processing speed, impaired complex attention, difficulties in learning and memory, and compromised executive function [8]. Language and verbal fluency issues, such as difficulty finding words and reduced verbal fluency, are also common among pwMS [9, 10]. Additionally, visuospatial and visuoconstructive impairments are frequent and are marked by deficiencies in visual perception, visual-motor integration, and spatial abilities [10, 11]. The cognitive challenges associated with MS have a significant impact on daily activities, including household chores, interpersonal relationships, financial management, and employment maintenance [12]. Furthermore, they often coincide with other “invisible” symptoms, such as mood disorders and fatigue [12]. Detecting cognitive impairment in MS patients poses challenges, as patients may present as cognitively competent during clinical interviews despite experiencing substantial difficulties in daily functioning [12]. Objective neuropsychological evaluations are often necessary to identify specific cognitive deficits [12, 13]. Remarkably, approximately 30% of pwMS may progress to severe cognitive impairment, potentially meeting the criteria for dementia [12, 14]. Unfortunately, this severely affected subgroup is frequently overlooked, despite the significant impact on their daily functioning [12, 14]. Effective management requires a multidisciplinary approach, including screening, ongoing monitoring, and personalized interventions [12, 13]. Neurologists often struggle to identify cognitive impairment related to MS during routine consultations [15]. There is a growing consensus among pwMS and professionals that regular cognitive testing should be integrated into clinical practice to guide management [16]. However, objective cognitive testing is rarely administered [17, 18]. Both the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) recommend annual cognitive assessments for MS [19]. Regular monitoring of cognition in pwMS can help with appropriate management and facilitate specialist referrals for further assessment and intervention [20, 21]. Once cognitive impairment is detected, healthcare professionals can adjust their approach and monitor associated risks such as driving accidents, falls, unemployment, and disease management [22]. In 2012, an international committee of MS experts developed a review process to select scales for a feasible, valid, and globally applicable MS cognitive assessment. They considered psychometric and pragmatic standards and selected subtests, including the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), the first five learning trials of the California Verbal Learning Test (CVLT-II), and the first three learning trials of the Brief Visuospatial Memory Test-Revised (BVMT-R) [23]. These tests are known for their reliability and sensitivity to MS-related cognitive impairment.

The Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis (MACFIMS), a recommended battery for assessing cognitive impairment in pwMS, comprises various neuropsychological tests [24]. These tests include processing speed and working memory assessments using the SDMT and Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test (PASAT), tests of episodic memory such as the CVLT-II and BVMT-R, evaluations of executive function via the Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (D-KEFS) Sorting Test and Controlled Oral Word Association Test (COWAT), and assessments of visual-spatial processing such as the Judgment of Line Orientation (JLO) [13].

The SDMT is a brief, sensitive, and culturally unbiased tool that screens for organic cerebral dysfunction and cognitive changes, assessing memory, lexical access speed, and processing speed [25]. The PASAT evaluates information processing capacity and speed, sustained and divided attention, auditory processing speed, flexibility, and calculation ability, serving as a cognitive measure in the MS Functional Composite [26]. The CVLT-II assesses verbal learning and memory using a “shopping list” format, measuring recall across multiple learning trials and providing insights into different memory processes and strategies [27]. The BVMT-R is a versatile tool for assessing visuospatial memory, suitable for criterion, screening, or longitudinal evaluation purposes, offering multiple equivalent forms and normative data across various age groups [28, 29]. The D-KEFS is a comprehensive neuropsychological battery assessing verbal and nonverbal executive functions, consisting of nine standalone tests and providing detailed evaluations of higher-order cognitive abilities and the impact of brain dysfunction [30].

The COWAT measures verbal fluency by prompting individuals to generate words within specific categories or starting with designated letters, assessing memory retrieval, language organization, and executive functions related to language and thought organization [31]. The JLO is a standardized test that assesses visuospatial skills primarily linked to the function of the right parietal lobe, evaluating an individual’s ability to match the angle and orientation of lines in space [32, 33].

The MACFIMS offers a standardized and validated approach to routine cognitive assessment in MS patients and was developed based on findings from a 2001 international conference [34]. This battery includes tests targeting processing speed, episodic memory, executive function, and other cognitive domains commonly affected in MS [34, 35]. A 2006 study involving 291 pwMS patients and 56 healthy controls validated the MACFIMS, demonstrating its ability to differentiate between MS course types and predict vocational status [34]. This study confirmed that the MACFIMS is recognized as a reliable tool for routine neuropsychological assessment in MS patients [34].

Several validation studies have confirmed its effectiveness across different languages and populations. For example, a French study established regression-based norms and validated the battery in French-speaking healthy individuals and pwMS [36]. Similarly, a Czech study assessed the validity of the Czech translation and compared it with that of the BICAMS [37]. The MACFIMS has also been validated in Italian and other languages, highlighting its cross-cultural applicability [36, 37]. These studies have consistently demonstrated the ability of the MACFIMS to identify cognitive impairment in pwMS patients, with deficits detected in 32.7–55% of the MS patients studied [36, 37].

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, our aim was to comprehensively evaluate cognitive function using the MACFIMS battery among pwMS patients and healthy controls. We sought to investigate the cognitive profiles of pwMS patients compared to those of healthy individuals by employing a battery of tests designed to assess various cognitive domains affected in MS, including executive functioning, learning and memory, information processing speed and working memory, and visual perceptual ability. By comparing the cognitive performance of pwMS patients to that of healthy controls, we aimed to gain insights into the specific cognitive impairments associated with MS and to further elucidate the impact of the disease on cognitive function. Through this comparative analysis, we endeavour to contribute to a better understanding of the cognitive manifestations of MS and potentially inform the development of targeted interventions aimed at improving cognitive outcomes in individuals living with this condition.

Methods

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement as a standardized guide to perform the present systematic review and meta-analysis [38]. A comprehensive systematic search of the Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus and Cochrane databases (conducted in January 2024) revealed an absence of prior reviews addressing the proposed topic. Consequently, the present review serves as the first synthesis of studies pertaining to the validation of the MACFIMS.

Systematic literature search

The MeSH terms and keywords used to retrieve records and studies are presented in Table S1. These keywords were searched within the full texts of the Web of Science, PubMed and Embase databases in January 2024. For Scopus, keywords were searched within the title, abstract, or keywords of the studies (Table S1).

Eligibility criteria

To enhance the rigor of the present review, a strict set of eligibility criteria was implemented for the inclusion of studies. The following inclusion criteria were used to refine the studies: (1) Peer-reviewed studies without publication date restrictions; (2) The samples were collected from adult individuals diagnosed with any clinical subtype of MS or a combination of subtypes (3) Studies comparing the cognitive performance of pwMS with HC using MACFIMS battery.

Additionally, studies were eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis if they reported quantitative data for the MACFIMS subscales (mean, standard deviation, and sample size) for both MS patients and HCs.

All titles and abstracts were reviewed and screened based on the eligibility criteria by two independent reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved by consulting a third reviewer (Fig. S1).

Data extraction

A structured Excel sheet was designed to facilitate data extraction from full-text articles and to assess their eligibility for inclusion in the systematic review, meta-analysis, or both. The extracted data from the full texts and their study characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The average scores of MACFIMS sub-tests are presented in Table 2. The abovementioned data were obtained from numerical information in texts, tables and graphs and statistical analysis.

Quality assessment

The quality assessment of the included studies in the current systematic review was conducted using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), version 2018 [39]. The MMAT is a critical appraisal tool designed to assess the methodological quality of quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods studies. It consists of five sections corresponding to different study designs: qualitative, quantitative randomized controlled trials, quantitative nonrandomized, quantitative descriptive, and mixed methods. Each section contains a series of criteria with a “yes,” “no,” or “cannot tell” rating system, allowing for a standardized and comprehensive evaluation of methodological rigor across diverse research methodologies. The studies were evaluated by two independent authors, and any disagreements were resolved by consulting the third author. To calculate the overall score for each study, if all five criteria were met (i.e., all “yes” responses), the study was considered high quality; if four criteria were met, the study was considered moderate quality; if three or fewer criteria were met, the study was regarded as low quality.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R4.2.3. To compare the MACFIMS subscales between the MS participants and HC participants, the mean difference (MD) [95% confidence interval (95% CI)] was reported using a random effect model. Additionally, we performed a subgroup meta-analysis stratified by Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score and disease duration.

To confirm the robustness of our results, we performed meta-regression to identify and account for any confounding factors that might have influenced our analyses. We conducted a leave-one-out meta-analysis and funnel plots to examine the impact of studies with extreme results.

We also calculated the pooled prevalence of cognitive impairment based on each subscale using proportional meta-analysis. To compare the ratio of individuals with cognitive impairment between MS patients and HCs, the odds ratio (OR) of each subscale was calculated. The logit transformation was applied to calculate the OR. To determine heterogeneity, we reported the I2 statistic and the Cochrane Q statistic.

Compliance with ethics statement

This study is a secondary analysis of previously published research and does not report on any original studies involving human participants or animals conducted by the authors.

Results

Study selection

Following the elimination of duplicate records, our comprehensive search yielded 363 articles (as illustrated in Figure S1). A preliminary screening based on titles and abstracts led to the exclusion of 344 studies from this initial pool of articles. Subsequently, we conducted a comprehensive review of the full texts of the remaining 19 articles. Ultimately, 8 studies met our predefined inclusion criteria and were thus included in our systematic review [24, 37, 40–45].

Study characteristics

The study characteristics, participant demographics and disease information are available in Table 1. Of the included studies, 3 used Italian [40, 44, 45], 2 used English [24, 41], 1 used Persian [42], 1 used French [43], and 1 used Czech [37]. The studies included a total of 1,481 pwMS patients and 1,072 HCs. The age of the pwMS ranged from 34 to 49.6 years, with an average of 41.1 years. The age of the HC participants ranged from 33.7 to 51.3 years, with an average of 43 years. Among both the MS and HC participants, the population was predominantly female. The percentage of females with pwMS ranged from 61.6 to 78, and the percentage of HCs with pwMS ranged from 52.9 to 95.4%. Years of education averaged 14.25 for pwMS patients and 14.2 for HCs.

Most of the studies included RRMS patients. One study included only RRMS patients [45], while other studies included a mix of different types of MS. Two studies also recruited CIS patients from their sample [44]. The average disease duration was 8.6 years, ranging from 6.1 to 15.2 years. The average EDSS score was 2.4, indicating that, on average, the participants were in the mild disability range and could walk unaided.

Risk of bias

The overall quality of the studies ranged from low to high according to the MMAT [39]. Two studies were of high quality (score 5), one study was of moderate quality (score 4), and the remaining studies were of low quality (scores 2 and 3). All included studies demonstrated adherence to items 2 and 5, suggesting the consistent utilization of appropriate measurement instruments and adherence to intended methodologies. However, a majority of studies exhibited shortcomings in items 1 and 3, indicating deficiencies in participant recruitment strategies for achieving representative samples of the target population and incomplete reporting of outcome data. Accordingly, future studies should pay more attention to sampling and data collection processes to increase the quality of the data. Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to identify, synthesize, and appraise the literature on effectiveness of MACFIMS battery in discriminating between pwMS and HC in terms of cognitive performance. The results of this systematic review demonstrated the wide application of the MACFIMS across diverse linguistic, cultural, and geographic contexts. It encompasses a wide range of clinical samples, including various disease subtypes, durations, and severities. Most studies included an HC sample with similar sociodemographic features, particularly age and education. Although the MACFIMS is intended to be administered by diverse healthcare professionals, the studies predominantly involved neuropsychologists or psychology graduates administering the assessment. However, this information has not been consistently reported across studies. Notably, the majority of studies presented a gender recruitment bias toward female participants in both control and experimental samples. This disparity aligns with the higher incidence of MS in females, with an approximate female-to-male ratio of 3:1 [46].

The results of this study indicate that processing speed and working memory are the most prevalent cognitive impairments in MS patients (36%), according to the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT). These results align with earlier studies emphasizing the susceptibility of these cognitive functions in pwMS [9, 47]. The prevalence of impairment in processing speed and working memory domain were 23% and 26% according to PASAT 2 and PASAT 3 respectively, confirming the result of SDMT. Processing speed and working memory are critical cognitive functions that enable individuals to efficiently process and manipulate information in real time.

In MS patients, impairments in these domains can have significant implications for daily functioning, including difficulties with multitasking, decision-making, and adapting to changing environments [17]. SDMT is a widely used measure to assess processing speed and working memory in MS patients [48]. The SDMT has been shown to be a sensitive and reliable tool for detecting cognitive impairment in MS patients, with strong correlations with other measures of cognitive functioning and brain imaging findings [49]. Research has demonstrated the PASAT’s validity in detecting cognitive impairments common in MS, particularly in processing speed and working memory. However, its reliability can be influenced by factors such as auditory processing and mathematical ability, leading some researchers to recommend alternative or complementary tests for a more comprehensive assessment [50].

The reasons behind the high frequency of processing speed and working memory issues in MS patients may be linked to various factors. MS is characterized by the loss of myelin and damage to nerve fibers in the central nervous system, which can interfere with the smooth communication of nerve signals and result in cognitive impairment. Moreover, the existence of brain lesions, especially in the frontal and parietal lobes, has been linked to difficulties in processing speed and working memory [51]. The findings of this study underscore the importance of routine cognitive screening in MS patients, with a particular emphasis on assessing processing speed and working memory. Early detection of cognitive impairment can facilitate timely interventions and support strategies to mitigate the impact of these deficits on daily functioning and quality of life [52]. Cognitive rehabilitation interventions, such as computerized cognitive training and strategy-based approaches, have shown promising effects on improving processing speed and working memory in MS patients [53, 54].

Additionally, managing fatigue and promoting healthy lifestyle habits, such as regular exercise and cognitive stimulation, may help to preserve cognitive function in MS patients [10].

These findings emphasize the need for routine cognitive screening and targeted interventions to address these deficits and improve the daily functioning and quality of life of individuals with MS. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating the effectiveness of cognitive rehabilitation strategies specific to processing speed and working memory in the MS population.

The analysis revealed that the highest odds ratio (OR) of 8.7 was observed for New Learning and Memory, as assessed by the BVMT. Accordingly, pwMS seem to be more likely to experience impairments in new learning and memory than HCs are. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have underscored the pivotal role of new learning and memory in MS. For instance, previous research found that delayed memory recall, as measured by the BVMT, could be a strong predictor of cognitive decline in MS patients [20, 55]. Another study published in the Multiple Sclerosis Journal revealed that impairments in new learning and memory were associated with reduced hippocampal volume, a key region involved in memory formation [56].

The delayed BVMT is a valid and reliable test assessing visuospatial memory, the type of memory that plays an important role in episodic memory [29]. Impairments in visuospatial memory have been linked to disruptions in the neural networks involved in memory formation, including the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex [57]. We hypothesized that in pwMS, demyelination and axonal damage can disrupt these networks, leading to impairments in new learning and memory.

The analysis we conducted showed a high chance, indicating that difficulties in new learning and memory could play a crucial role in the progression of cognitive decline in pwMS. This finding aligns with the idea of “cognitive reserve,” indicating that people with greater cognitive skills can better manage neural damage and preserve cognitive function [58]. In MS, difficulties acquiring new learning and memory can diminish cognitive reserve, increasing vulnerability to cognitive deterioration in pwMS.

Our findings have important implications for the management of cognitive decline in MS patients. Efforts to enhance new learning and memory, such as cognitive training and memory rehabilitation, could help lower the chances of cognitive decline [59]. Moreover, detecting impairments in new learning and memory early on may enable earlier intervention and potentially delay or prevent cognitive decline. Overall, the present analysis highlights the critical importance of new learning and memory in the development of cognitive decline in MS patients. The analysis showed a high likelihood ratio, indicating that cognitive decline may result from poor new learning and memory, and interventions to enhance these abilities could lower the risk.

We investigated the confounding effects of BDI score, age, education level, EDSS score, and disease duration on cognitive impairment. Notably, our analysis revealed that disease duration did not emerge as a significant confounder in most MACFIMS measures, with the exception of the PASAT-2 score. In contrast, the EDSS score was identified as a confounder of SDMT, BVMT-R IR, and CVLT-II DR. Both subgroup and meta-regression analyses consistently suggested that the EDSS score exerts a more profound influence on cognitive impairment relative to disease duration in this study. Therefore, we recommend that cognitive screening using the MACFIMS be integrated into routine check-up visits for pwMS patients with an EDSS score of 2 or higher.

This finding contrasts with the widely accepted clinical understanding that cognitive impairment tends to worsen over time in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) [51, 58]. One potential reason for this discrepancy could be the relatively young age of participants in the included studies, with an average age of 41.1 years. Additionally, most patients had only mild to moderate disability, as reflected by the mean EDSS score of 2.4. Cognitive reserve, which helps to protect against cognitive decline, tends to diminish more rapidly after age 50—a threshold that was not well-represented in the study samples [60]. As a result, the effects of disease duration on cognition may not have been fully captured in this younger cohort. Future research should aim to focus on older MS patients and those with longer disease durations to better understand this relationship.

The educational level of pwMS and HC participants significantly influenced performance on several MACFIMS measures, including the PASAT-3, CVLT-II IR, DKEFS-CCS, and COWAT. The finding that education level acts as a confounder in both groups underscores its pivotal role in modulating cognitive performance, regardless of disease type. Consequently, we recommend that future studies prioritize the examination of education level as a determinant of cognitive impairment in the context of various chronic conditions, particularly when utilizing the aforementioned cognitive assessments. Moreover, the cofounding effect of BDI and age was negligible among pwMS, while in HC participants, they affected four and three of the measures, respectively. In summary, the findings suggest that the passage of time itself has a limited impact on cognitive decline in pwMS, whereas the disability score emerges as a more critical factor that should be taken into account during regular patient evaluations.

Strength and limitations

A key strength of this paper lies in its novelty. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis conducted to evaluate existing research on the studies comparing cognitive performance between pwMS and HC using MACFIMS battery. The comprehensive analysis in this study comprised a range of effect sizes, including odds ratios, prevalence, and mean differences, providing a robust assessment of previous findings. The results demonstrate that MACFIMS effectively discriminates between pwMS patients and HCs. This finding offers promising evidence supporting the application of the MACFIMS as a precise and comprehensive cognitive assessment tool within the field of MS.

In addition to these strengths, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations inherent to the present study. The restriction to English-language publications may have inadvertently excluded studies published in other languages, potentially impacting the comprehensiveness of the review. Furthermore, the search strategy employed in this review was limited to the use of specific keywords, namely, “Multiple Sclerosis” and “MS”. This narrow approach may have resulted in an underestimation of the total number of relevant studies, as MS can be described using a range of terms, including those related to its autoimmune etiology. Additionally, the review’s eligibility criteria were restricted to peer-reviewed studies, which may have led to the exclusion of potentially relevant gray literature, such as thesis publications that were not published, from the analysis. Last but not least, the studies included in this review were predominantly from European and North American countries, which could impact the generalizability of the findings. Significant differences in terms of healthcare systems, accessibility, economic status, and access to general MS support facilities must be considered when interpreting the results [61, 62].

The population included in the reviewed studies had a relatively young mean age (43 years), shorter disease duration (mean 8.6 years), and lower EDSS scores (mean 3.4), which may limit the generalizability of our findings to older MS patients or those with more advanced stages of the disease. Future studies should focus on including a broader range of participants, particularly older individuals and those with longer disease durations, to better understand the cognitive effects of MS across different stages of the disease.

4.3 Future research recommendations

The establishment of a standardized international validation protocol, coupled with global collaborative efforts, has successfully elevated the MACFIMS to a widely accepted international neurological battery test for assessing cognitive function in pwMS. This systematic review highlights the need for future studies to investigate the sensitivity and specificity of the MACFIMS in diverse forms of MS or in patient groups with varying degrees of disability, particularly patients with longer disease durations and more progressive disease subtypes. Additionally, this initiative has the potential to serve as a paradigm for other neurological batteries, thereby enhancing awareness, understanding, evaluation, and management of cognitive impairment. Furthermore, it is anticipated that future research examining the feasibility of MACFIMS in clinical settings will optimize its application in routine consultations for evaluating cognitive status in pwMS. We suggest that other researchers in other areas of the world conduct studies for the assessment and transcultural adaptation of MACFIMS in their native language.

Conclusion

The MACFIMS has undergone translation and cultural adaptation in 6 countries, establishing its validity as a global measure of cognitive functioning in MS patients. Cross-cultural studies have consistently demonstrated the ability of the MACFIMS to detect cognitive impairment in MS patients compared to HCs. This comprehensive review contributes to the growing recognition of MS-related cognitive impairment and facilitates the widespread implementation of MACFIMS in clinical practice, enabling the assessment and management of cognitive dysfunction across diverse cultural and geographical contexts.
