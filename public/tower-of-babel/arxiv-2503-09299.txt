Low-Rank Graphon Estimation: Theory and Applications to Graphon Games
Olga Klopp ESSEC Business School Fedor Noskov HSE University

Abstract
We study low-rank estimation of an unknown sparse graphon from sampled network data under operator-norm loss, motivated by targeted interventions in graphon games. Starting from the observed adjacency matrix, we construct low-rank surrogates by singular value thresholding and, for smooth graphons, by block averaging followed by thresholding. We obtain non-asymptotic bounds on both the operator-norm error and the rank of the resulting estimator for stochastic block model, Hölder, and analytic graphons, and we complement these results with minimax lower bounds showing that the rates are essentially sharp for these classes. Our analysis highlights that low rank is valuable here primarily for computation: while it does not improve the minimax operator-norm rate, it yields operator-norm accurate surrogates with substantially smaller rank. We then apply these estimators to linear-quadratic graphon games and derive non-asymptotic stability bounds showing that the welfare loss incurred by using an estimated graphon is controlled by the operator-norm perturbation. This yields near-optimal guarantees for targeted interventions computed from the estimated graphon, together with substantial computational savings. For zero baseline heterogeneity and under a spectral-gap condition, we also establish matching lower bounds for intervention regret. Numerical experiments illustrate the trade-off between statistical accuracy, retained rank, and runtime.



Keywords:
Graphon Model, Network Games, Rank Estimation, Targeted Interventions

Introduction

Large networks are ubiquitous in today’s world, present in various systems such as social networks, epidemic models, power grids, the Internet of Things, and more. These networked systems possess a unique characteristic: the agents within them are interconnected via the network. Consequently, the efficiency of algorithms operating on these systems scales with the size of the network, denoted as
n
.
For instance, consider linear-quadratic network games, where computing a Nash equilibrium of the game exhibits a worst-case complexity of
O(n^{3})

(
Galeotti et al., 2020
)
. In practical scenarios,
n
can reach substantial magnitudes; for instance, Facebook boasts over 2 billion users. Consequently, for computationally intensive tasks, such algorithms can become practically intractable.
To address this challenge, the theory of graphons has emerged as a valuable tool. For example, in the context of network games, graphons have been employed to mitigate computational complexities
(
Parise and Ozdaglar, 2023
)
.

Formally, a graphon is a measurable function
W
given by
W:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
. A graphon can either be interpreted as a limit of a sequence of finite networks, or as a probabilistic model that generates random finite networks
(
Lovász, 2012
)
. While, traditionally, networked systems have been modeled on finite networks, these models have been extended to graphons, e.g.,
(
Parise and Ozdaglar, 2023
)
extended network games to graphon games. These works have shown that modeling networked systems as graphon systems has three benefits: (i) graphons provide a mathematically elegant, unified framework for representing and comparing networked systems of various sizes, (ii) a graphon system can closely approximate the behavior of a networked system if the network has been sampled from the graphon, (iii) under certain conditions on the graphon, the dimension of some computational problems involving the networked system can be significantly reduced, thus leading to computationally efficient methods.

Crucially, the works on graphon games mentioned above assumed that the true graphon that generates
finite networks is known to the planner. In practice, however, the underlying graphon is unknown,
and the planner only observes one (or a few) realized networks. This motivates the problem of
estimating a graphon from sampled network data, a topic extensively studied in the literature
(
Chatterjee, 2015
;
Gao et al., 2015
;
Klopp et al., 2017
;
Klopp and Verzelen, 2019
;
Xu, 2018
)
.

In connection to our motivating application, targeted interventions that maximize social welfare at a Nash
equilibrium, the estimator must be accurate in a metric that controls the intervention loss.
Our non-asymptotic stability result (Theorem
1
) shows that the achieved welfare is Lipschitz (with explicit constants) in the operator norm distance

\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. This suggests that, for the downstream objective (
6
), an estimator should satisfy:

(P1)
it is close to the underlying graphon in operator norm.

At first glance, one might then simply take the empirical graphon associated with the observed adjacency matrix
(or equivalently use the adjacency matrix itself), since in operator norm the adjacency matrix already achieves
the minimax-optimal scaling over standard low-rank/SBM-type families. In fact, our Theorem
2
recovers this
benchmark: setting the SVT threshold

\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{0}=\mathbf{A}
and an operator-norm

\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}

Instead, the role of low-rank structure is primarily

6

In contrast, existing results in graphon games show that if the graphon has rank

(
Parise and Ozdaglar, 2023

the estimator should have low (effective) rank, because the rank is the algorithmic bottleneck

To achieve (P1)–(P2) simultaneously, we use a singular value thresholding (SVT) estimator. Given a network of
size

\rho_{n}W
, we form a low-rank approximation of the adjacency
matrix by retaining only those singular components whose singular values exceed a threshold

\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}
. We then define the estimated graphon as the empirical (piecewise-constant)
graphon associated with
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}
.

A key feature is that
\lambda
acts as a
computational knob
. Theorem
2
bounds the operator-norm error by
an additive
\lambda

\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}
), so taking
\lambda
at
(or slightly above) the noise level preserves the optimal statistical scaling in operator norm while potentially
shrinking the retained rank

R(\lambda)\;:=\;\mathrm{rank}(\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda})\;=\;\#\{i:\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{A})\geq\lambda\}.

This rank directly governs both (i) the cost of computing
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}
via truncated SVD methods
(up to rank
R(\lambda)
) and (ii) the dimension of the reduced SDP used to compute interventions. We then show
that for structured families (SBM, Hölder, analytic graphons) one can obtain explicit bounds on the rank of the
SVT estimator, which grow slowly with
n
, thereby yielding operator-norm accurate surrogates that make the
intervention computation tractable.

In Section

Preliminaries

We start with a brief recap of graphon theory and its connection with network theory. We refer the interested reader to
Lovász (2012)

Graphons as operators

Throughout this paper the term “graphon” denotes a symmetric measurable function
W:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
.
Let
\mathcal{W}
denote the space of all bounded symmetric measurable functions
W:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

In the following, it will be useful to think of graphons as operators on

[\mathbb{W}f](x)=\int_{0}^{1}W(x,y)f(y)dy

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{W}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{f\in L_{2}[0,1],\;\|f\|_{L_{2}}=1}\|\mathbb{W}f\|_{L_{2}}.

Graphons as random network models

In relation to graphs, graphons can be seen both as the limit of a sequence of graphs when the number of nodes tends to infinity, as well as a way to generate random networks. Specifically, given a graphon

1.

Sample

.

2.

Fix a sparsity parameter
\rho_{n}\in[0,1]
.

1

. Hence, the density of the graph is of the order

.

3.

Sample a weight matrix

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{ij}=\rho_{n}W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})\quad\forall(i,j)\in[n]^{2},

(1)

.

4.

Sample an adjacency matrix

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\textrm{w.p. }\bm{Q}_{ij},\\

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{ii}

Estimation problem

In this paper we are interested in the problem of estimating the graphon
W
from the observation of a sample network
\bm{A}
generated from it. While several works addressed this problem before (see, e.g.,
(

3
Targeted Interventions in Network Games

We provide an example of application that motivate our study, Targeted Interventions in Network Games. Network games serve as models to elucidate strategic behaviors of agents within networked environments, finding applications across various domains such as public goods

(
Bramoullé and Kranton, 2007
)
, R&D, crime
(
Bramoullé et al., 2014
)
, and others
(
Galeotti et al., 2010
;
Parise and Ozdaglar, 2021
)
. Recently,
Galeotti et al. (2020)
addressed the challenge of a central planner intervening in a network game to enhance agents’ welfare, a task presenting challenging optimization hurdles, particularly for large networks.
In response,
Parise and Ozdaglar (2023)
introduced the concept of graphon games, formalizing the asymptotic behavior of a network game as the population size approaches infinity. They proposed a method to derive interventions for large network games from solutions in the graphon domain, assuming knowledge of the graphon. Our aim is to extend these findings to scenarios where the graphon is unknown, yet the planner can observe a realized network. We start by providing an overview of graphon games and the associated optimization problem.

3.1
Graphon games and interventions

In a graphon game, the interval
[0,1]
is the set of all players. The players interact over a graphon
W
. We will consider linear-quadratic (LQ) graphon games. For a general framework, we refer the reader to
Parise and Ozdaglar (2023)
. In an LQ graphon game, each player
x\in[0,1]
chooses a strategy
s(x)\in\mathbb{R}
. A strategy profile is a measurable function
s:x\mapsto s(x)
in
L_{2}[0,1]
. For a given strategy profile
s
, the utility of player
x
is given by

\displaystyle U\left(s(x),z_{W}(x\mid s),\theta(x)\right)=-\frac{1}{2}s(x)^{2}+s(x)\cdot\left(\gamma z_{W}(x\mid s)+\theta(x)\right).

(3)

Here,
\gamma>0
is the peer-effect parameter,
z_{W}(x\mid s)
is the local aggregate sensed by player
x
via the graphon, defined as

\displaystyle z_{W}(x\mid s):=\int_{0}^{1}W(x,y)s(y)\mathrm{d}y,

(4)

and
\theta(x)
is a parameter modeling heterogeneity among the players.
We denote a graphon game compactly as
G(W,\theta)
.
A strategy profile
s^{*}
of a graphon game is a Nash equilibrium if almost all players have no incentive to unilaterally deviate from their strategy, i.e.,

\displaystyle U\left(s^{*}(x),z_{W}\left(x\mid s^{*}\right),\theta(x)\right)\geq\sup_{\tilde{s}\in\mathbb{R}}U\left(\tilde{s},z_{W}\left(x\mid s^{*}\right),\theta(x)\right)\quad\text{ a.e. in }[0,1].

(5)

We refer to a Nash equilibrium of a graphon game as a graphon equilibrium. Let
\mathrm{NE}(G(W,\theta))
be the set of all graphon equilibria of
G(W,\theta)
.

For the utility in (
3
), for any fixed local aggregate
z\in\mathbb{R}
the payoff

U(s(x),z,\theta(x))=-\frac{1}{2}s(x)^{2}+s(x)\,(\gamma z+\theta(x))
is strictly concave in
s(x)
, hence each player
has a unique best response. Writing the local aggregate as
z_{W}(x\mid s)=(\mathbb{W}s)(x)
, the best-response
mapping takes the linear form

\mathrm{BR}(s)(x)=\gamma(\mathbb{W}s)(x)+\theta(x).

Therefore, any graphon equilibrium
s^{\star}
must satisfy the fixed-point equation

s^{\star}=\gamma\mathbb{W}s^{\star}+\theta,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad(I-\gamma\mathbb{W})s^{\star}=\theta.

Under the standard stability condition
\gamma\|\mathbb{W}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<1
(assumed throughout our intervention
analysis and in Theorem
1
), the operator
(I-\gamma\mathbb{W})
is invertible on
L^{2}[0,1]

(e.g., by a Neumann series argument), and the equilibrium exists and is unique, with the closed form

s^{\star}=(I-\gamma\mathbb{W})^{-1}\theta.

We refer to
Parise and Ozdaglar (2023)
for a broader discussion of equilibrium in graphon games and for the
LQ equilibrium characterization used in our setting.

Next consider a central planner who wants to maximize the average utility of the players at a graphon equilibrium. For this, the planner adds an intervention
\hat{\theta}(x)
to the heterogeneity
\theta(x)
of each player
x
, subject to a budget constraint. Thus, the central planner wants to solve the problem

\displaystyle\max_{\hat{\theta}\in L_{2}[0,1]}

\displaystyle\quad T_{(W,\theta)}(\hat{\theta}):=\int_{0}^{1}U\left(s(x),z_{W}(x\mid s),\theta(x)+\hat{\theta}(x)\right)dx

(6)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad s\in\mathrm{NE}\left(G(W,\theta+\hat{\theta})\right),\quad\|\hat{\theta}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq B.

Here,
\|\hat{\theta}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq B
is the budget constraint, where
B>0
.

Solving problem (
6
) in practice presents two main challenges: i) the planner typically doesn’t know the graphon and ii) problem (
6
) is, in general, computationally intractable.
To obviate problem i), we assume that the planner can observe one network realized from the graphon model and use this information to infer the underlying graphon. The planner could then solve problem (
6
) for the estimated graphon, instead of the true graphon. The next theorem shows that it is possible to bound the suboptimality of such a solution for the original graphon intervention problem in terms of the graphon estimation error in the operator norm.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbb{W}_{1},\mathbb{W}_{2}:L_{2}[0,1]\to L_{2}[0,1]
be two different integral operators such that

\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}<1/\gamma
, and let
W_{1},W_{2}
be their kernels. Assume that
W_{1},W_{2}
are symmetric and

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}W_{1}^{2}(x,y)dxdy,\int_{[0,1]^{2}}W_{2}^{2}(x,y)dxdy<+\infty.

Let

be corresponding target functions of problem (

\displaystyle T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})\geq T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})-\frac{4\gamma\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}(\|\theta\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+B)}{(1-\gamma[\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}])^{3}}.

Remark 1

(Why the operator norm naturally appears for interventions)

Appendix
A

In particular, when

If
\bm{\theta}
is not orthogonal to the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
, the problem of finding optimal interventions in the network
\mathbf{A}
with agent heterogeneity
\bm{\theta}
can be reformulated as finding a value of
L
in the interval
(-\infty,-(1-\gamma\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\operatorname{op}}/n)^{-2})
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}=-\left[\mathbf{I}+L(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{A}/n)^{2}\right]^{-1}\bm{\theta}

(7)

satisfies
\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}=B
, see Lemma
10
in Appendix
N
.
If the network
\mathbf{A}
is sparse, an approximate solution can be obtained iteratively using the Conjugate Gradient method. This procedure generates a monotone sequence
L_{1},L_{2},\dots
that converges to the optimal solution
L
(see
(
Gander et al., 1989
;
Reinsch, 1967
)
). However, each iteration of the algorithm may require
O(n\cdot|E|)
operations, where
|E|
is the number of edges in
\mathbf{A}
, since solving the linear equation

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{I}+L(\mathbf{I}-\gamma\mathbf{A}/n)^{2}\right]\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

via the Conjugate Gradient method has this complexity. We refer the reader to
Gander et al. (1989)
for the full details and the description of the algorithm.
The degenerate case where
\bm{\theta}
is orthogonal to the eigenspace of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
requires a separate treatment. The eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
and its corresponding eigenspace can be computed efficiently using the Lanczos algorithm, which has a complexity of
O(T\cdot|E|)
for
T
iterations.
In contrast, the proposed SVT estimator has a significantly lower computational cost of
O(nR)
, which corresponds to the complexity of performing a randomized truncated singular value decomposition (SVD), where
R
is the target rank and
n
is the network size.

Remark 3
.

There are several other scenarios where a low-rank estimator of the underlying graphon function is essential. One notable example is the optimal control of networked dynamical systems. In such systems, the objective is to determine a control input that minimizes a total running cost and a terminal cost, both of which depend on the system state and control input.
However, for large-scale networks, solving this optimization problem becomes computationally intractable—even when the system dynamics are linear and the cost function is quadratic. To address this challenge, the theory of
graphon dynamical systems
was introduced in
(
Gao and Caines, 2020
;
Gao and Caines, 2019
)
. This framework establishes that as the number of nodes in a networked dynamical system grows indefinitely, the system behavior converges to a limiting
graphon system
, enabling more tractable analysis and control.

4
Low-rank graphon estimation

In this section, we introduce a graphon estimator that satisfies Properties (P1) and (P2). Our approach consists of two main steps. First, we construct a matrix estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A})
for
\mathbf{Q}
using singular value thresholding (see Section
4.1
). Then, we show that the empirical graphon associated with
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A})
possesses the required properties (see Section
4.2
).
The problem of estimation of
\bm{Q}
from
\bm{A}
was considered mainly in Frobenius and cut norms, see, for example,
(
Chatterjee, 2015
;
Gao et al., 2015
;
Klopp et al., 2017
;
Klopp and Verzelen, 2019
;
Xu, 2018
)
and references therein. Our goal here is different as we are interested in the estimation of the matrix
\bm{Q}
in spectral norm.

4.1
Hard thresholding estimator

We follow the classical approach relying on spectral properties of the observed matrix
\bm{A}
. For the adjacency matrix
\bm{A}
, consider its singular value decomposition

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}.

Then, the hard thresholding estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i\in[n]:\sigma_{i}\geq\lambda}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}.

(8)

We start by proving a general upper bound on the error of
\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}
measured in spectral norm.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a weight matrix
\bm{Q}
and an adjacency matrix
\bm{A}
sampled from it. Let
\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}
be the hard thresholding estimator of
\bm{Q}
as defined in (
8
). Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\lambda+7\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}+\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(n/\delta)}.

In particular, for
\lambda=9\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}
we have

\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 16\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}+\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(n/\delta)},

(9)

where
C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}
is an absolute constant defined in Appendix
Q
.

Remark 4
.

In
(
Klopp and Verzelen, 2019
)
, the authors consider the problem of estimating the matrix
\bm{Q}
in cut-norm and show that for
\rho_{n}>1/n
, there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}\sup_{\bm{Q}\in\text{SBM}(2,\rho_{n})}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}\|_{\square}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{n}}{n}},

where
\text{SBM}(2,\rho_{n})
denotes a Stochastic Block Model (SBM) with two communities.
3
3

3

We say that a matrix
\bm{Q}
belongs to
\text{SBM}(k,\rho_{n})
if there exists a symmetric matrix
\bm{S}\in[0,1]^{k\times k}
such that
\max_{k_{1},k_{2}}\bm{S}_{k_{1},k_{2}}=\rho_{n}
, and a mapping
z:[n]\to[k]
such that
\bm{Q}_{ij}=\bm{S}_{z(i)z(j)}

Since
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq n\|\cdot\|_{\square}
(see Appendix
Q
), this implies that for
\lambda=O(\sqrt{n\rho_{n}})
the estimator
\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}
is minimax optimal in spectral norm, since

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}\sup_{\bm{Q}}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq n\inf_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}\sup_{\bm{Q}}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}\|_{\square}\geq n\inf_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}\sup_{\bm{Q}\in\text{SBM}(2,\rho_{n})}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}\|_{\square}\geq C\sqrt{\rho_{n}n}.

Remark 5

(On the
\lambda
-term in Theorem 2)
.

The additive
\lambda
term in Theorem 2 reflects the bias induced by hard thresholding and cannot be removed
uniformly: even in a noiseless setting
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}
, if
\mathbf{Q}
has a singular value
\sigma\in(0,\lambda)

then
\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}
discards the associated component and
\|\mathbf{Q}-\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\sigma
.

Remark 6

(Operator norm versus Frobenius norm)
.

Most of the graphon/matrix estimation literature measures error in (normalized) Frobenius norm or related

L^{2}
-type metrics. In our setting, these norms lead to a very different fluctuation scale than the operator norm.
Indeed, since the entries of
\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}
are independent, centered, and bounded with

\mathrm{Var}(A_{ij})\leq Q_{ij}\leq\rho_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i\neq j}\mathrm{Var}(A_{ij})\lesssim n^{2}\rho_{n},\qquad\text{so typically}\qquad\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n\sqrt{\rho_{n}}).

By contrast, non-commutative (matrix) concentration yields the sharper spectral-scale bound

\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}+\sqrt{\log n}\right),

which is the scale captured (up to logarithmic terms) by Theorem
2
.

This distinction matters for our application: Theorem
1
controls the difference of intervention objectives and
maximizers through
\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, hence we require operator-norm accuracy. While one always has

\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{F}
, using a Frobenius guarantee to control operator norm would lead to an

O_{\mathbb{P}}(n\sqrt{\rho_{n}})
bound, which is looser by a factor
\sqrt{n}
compared to the intrinsic spectral noise
scale
\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}
.

The proof techniques also differ accordingly. Frobenius-norm analyses typically exploit “energy” control such as

\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{A})<\lambda}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{2},

which can be bounded via tail-energy/approximation arguments (and yields improvements when the signal is
approximately low rank).
In contrast, the operator-norm bound in Theorem
2
relies on two ingredients tailored to worst-direction control:
(i) a matrix concentration inequality for
\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, and (ii) the basic spectral property of
hard thresholding that
\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{Q}}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\lambda
because the discarded singular
values are all below
\lambda
.

Remark 7
.

To simplify results, in our subsequent discussion, we fix
\lambda=9\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}
so that we can use (
9
). All our results can be generalized for arbitrary
\lambda>7\sqrt{n\rho_{n}}+\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(n/\delta)}
.

4.1.1
Generalization to missing links

Theorem
2
can be extended to the case of missing links. In some applications the network of interactions is not fully observed, or the size of networks is so huge that we need to subsample the network. In such cases, we may consider the matrix
\widetilde{\bm{A}}=\bm{M}\circ\bm{A}
, where
\bm{M}
is a symmetric mask with independent entries
\bm{M}_{ij}
, each a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
p
.

In this setting,
\widetilde{\bm{A}}
can be considered as a sample from the sparse graphon model with sparsity parameter
\rho_{n}p
. Thus, the singular value thresholding estimator based on the matrix
\widetilde{\bm{A}}
satisfies the following theoretical guarantees:

\displaystyle\|p\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{A}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\lambda+7\sqrt{np\rho_{n}}+\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(n/\delta)}

with probability at least
1-\delta
, leading to the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\widetilde{\bm{A}}
be obtained as above. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{p}\widetilde{\bm{A}}\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\lambda}{p}+7\sqrt{\frac{n\rho_{n}}{p}}+\frac{1}{p}\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(n/\delta)}

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

The corollary above generalizes all subsequent results in the context of estimators to the case of missing links.

4.2
Graphon estimator

In this section we use the empirical graphon of the hard thresholding estimator,
W_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}}
, as an estimator of the graphon
W
. Note that by triangular inequality, and by Lemma
1

\displaystyle\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\bm{Q}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\mathbb{W}_{\bm{Q}}-\mathbb{W}_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\bm{Q}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

We obtained a bound for the second term above in Theorem
2
.
Hence, to get a bound on the error of our estimator, it is enough to estimate the approximation error
\|\rho_{n}W-W_{\bm{Q}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, which measures the distance between the true sparse graphon
\rho_{n}W
and its discretized version, the empirical graphon
W_{\bm{Q}}
,
sampled at the unobserved random points
\xi_{1}\leq\dots\leq\xi_{n}
.

It is clear that the approximation error depends on the topology of the space of the considered graphons. We will study two cases usually considered in the literature,

step function graphons
(see Sect
4.2.1
) and
smooth graphons
(see Sect
4.2.2
). For these two cases, we derive bounds on the estimation error and show that the rank of our estimator is sublinear in
n
.

5.1
Lower bound for targeted interventions

While targeted interventions problem for LQR games naturally motivates us to study graphon estimation in the spectral norm, it is an interesting problem how can one maximize the social welfare
T_{(\rho_{n}W,\theta)}(\cdot)
of an unobserved sparse graphon
\rho_{n}W(\cdot,\cdot)
in general based on an observed network
\bm{A}
. In other words, setting

\displaystyle\theta^{*}\in\argmax_{\tilde{\theta}\in L_{2}[0,1],\|\tilde{\theta}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq B}T_{(\rho_{n}W,\theta)}(\tilde{\theta}),

we are interested in minimizing the regret of an intervention
\tilde{\theta}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Regret}(\tilde{\theta})=T_{(\rho_{n}W,\theta)}(\theta^{*})-T_{(\rho_{n}W,\theta)}(\tilde{\theta}).

Our paper suggests constructing an intervention
\widehat{\theta}
based on an estimator
W_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}
for some estimator
\widehat{\bm{Q}}
of the matrix
\bm{Q}
of connection probabilities, where
W_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}
aims to estimate the sparse graphon
\rho_{n}W
in the spectral norm. The upper bound on the regret that follows from Theorem
1
is of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{Regret}(\widehat{\theta})\leq C\cdot\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

for some constant
C
depending on
\mathbb{W},\rho_{n},B
and
\theta(x)
.

In a special case when

and
\mathbb{W}
has a spectral gap, this upper bound can be improved through the Davis–Kahan theorem, which leads to the following result.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\theta(x)\equiv 0
and
\mathbb{W}_{1}
has a spectral gap
\mathtt{g}=\lambda_{1}(\mathbb{W}_{1})-\lambda_{2}(\mathbb{W}_{1})>0
. Let
T_{1}(\cdot)
be the welfare function associated to the graphon
W_{1}
, initial heterogeneities
\theta
and budget
B
, and let
\hat{\theta}_{1}
be its maximizer. Let
\hat{\theta}_{2}
be the optimal solution of the social welfare problem associated with graphon game
G(W_{2},\theta)
with the same initial heterogeneity parameter and budget
B
for some other graphon
W_{2}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})\geq T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})-\frac{8\gamma\sqrt{B}(\|\theta\|_{L_{2}}+2\sqrt{B})}{(1-\gamma[\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}])^{3}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{\mathtt{g}}.

Note that the above bound is better than one provided by Theorem
1
only if
\mathtt{g}\gtrsim\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. In what follows, we refer to the latter inequality as the spectral gap assumption. Since the spectral gap of a sparse graphon
\rho_{n}W
scales linearly in

, the spectral gap assumption implies the following upper bound on the regret of
\widehat{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Regret}(\widehat{\theta})\leq C^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\widehat{\bm{Q}}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{\rho_{n}},

(13)

for which we are able to find a matching minimax lower bound, see Theorem
10
below.

It is interesting to generalize this result beyond the case
\theta(x)\equiv 0
. It would be enough to derive a Davis–Kahan type result for the generalized eigenvalue problem of the form (see
(
Gander et al., 1989

and Appendix

\displaystyle\text{Find maximal }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and a corresponding function $u(\cdot)$ such that }

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{A}[u](x)=\lambda u(x)+z(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\|u\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=B,

when

and bounded self-adjoint operator

are perturbed. Unfortunately, we are not aware of any result of this type in the literature.

We will construct information-theoretic lower bounds for the regret using the following theorem, which relates regret and the deficit of a triangle inequality for some spectral norms. The proof of the theorem is given in Appendix

Suppose that for graphons

\displaystyle\|(\bm{I}-\gamma\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}_{0})^{-2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|(\bm{I}-\gamma\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}_{1})^{-2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq s+\|(\bm{I}-\gamma\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}_{0})^{-2}+(\bm{I}-\gamma\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}_{1})^{-2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

6
Conclusion

Driven by applications in graphon games, we developed an estimator for an unknown graphon based on a sampled network. Under standard regularity conditions for the graphon, our estimator possesses two key features: (i) it approximates the unknown graphon in the operator norm, and (ii) it is of low rank. We established upper bounds for the convergence rate and rank of the estimator. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the estimator yields near-optimal solutions for the social welfare problem in linear-quadratic graphon games, with efficient computation. We also quantified the convergence rates of the associated costs and the computational complexity relative to the network size.

Step 1. Reduction to the difference of resolvents.
The problem of optimal interventions in graphon can be restated as follows:

\displaystyle\max_{\hat{\theta}}T(\hat{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\left(\bm{I}-\gamma\mathbb{W}\right)^{-1}(\theta+\hat{\theta})\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\|\hat{\theta}\|^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq B,

see the proof of Proposition 1 in
(
Parise and Ozdaglar, 2023
)
. From the self-adjointness of the operator
\mathbb{W}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{i}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle(\bm{I}-\gamma\mathbb{W}_{i})^{-2}[\theta+\hat{\theta}],\theta+\hat{\theta}\right\rangle,\quad i=1,2.

Let
\hat{\theta}_{i}
be the argument of the maximum of
T_{i}(\hat{\theta})
. Then, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})+T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})-T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})=T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})+T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})-T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{2})+T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{2})-T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})+T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})-T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{2})+T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{1})-T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1}),

(16)

where we used
T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{2})\geq T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{1})
by the definition of the argument of the maximum in the last inequality. For brevity, we denote the resolvent by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\gamma,\mathbb{W})=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma\mathbb{W})^{-1},

so

\displaystyle T_{1}(\widehat{\theta}_{2})-T_{2}(\widehat{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle(\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2}))[\theta+\hat{\theta}_{1}],\theta+\hat{\theta}_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\theta+\theta_{2}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\|\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{op}}(\|\theta\|^{2}_{L_{2}}+B).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{1})-T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})\geq-\|\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{op}}(\|\theta\|^{2}_{L_{2}}+B).

Combining the above with (
16
), we obtain

\displaystyle T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})\geq T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})-2\|\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\cdot(\|\theta\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+B).

(17)

Step 2. Analysis of squared resolvents.
To bound the operator norm of the difference between
\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{i})
, we use decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W})=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\gamma^{i}\mathbb{W}^{i}.

The right-hand side converges because
\gamma\|\mathbb{W}_{i}\|<1
for
i=1,2
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(i+1)\gamma^{i}(\mathbb{W}_{1}^{i}-\mathbb{W}_{2}^{i}).

Finally, we bound
\|\mathbb{W}_{1}^{i}-\mathbb{W}_{2}^{i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
by induction on
i
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{W}_{1}^{i}-\mathbb{W}_{2}^{i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq i\cdot\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\cdot(\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}})^{i-1}.

The base
i=1
is trivial. Next, we obtain

\displaystyle{\|\mathbb{W}_{1}^{i}-\mathbb{W}_{2}^{i}\|}_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{W}^{i-1}_{1}(\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2})+\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mathbb{W}^{i-1}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}^{i-1})\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{i-1}\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbb{W}^{i-1}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}^{i-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{I.H.}}{\leq}\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{i-1}\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+(i-1)\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}(\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}})^{i-2}

\displaystyle\leq i\cdot\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\cdot(\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}})^{i-1}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{1})-\mathcal{R}^{2}(\gamma,\mathbb{W}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)(i+2)\gamma^{i+1}(\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}})^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{(1-\gamma[\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}])^{3}}.

(18)

Then, the above, together with (
17
), implies

\displaystyle T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{2})\geq T_{1}(\hat{\theta}_{1})-\frac{4\gamma\|\mathbb{W}_{1}-\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}(\|\theta\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+B)}{(1-\gamma[\|\mathbb{W}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vee\|\mathbb{W}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}])^{3}},

which completes the proof.

i) Upper bound on the convergence rate.

We build the proof on that of Proposition 3.2 in
(
Klopp et al., 2017
)
. However, we provide bounds in probability instead of bounds in expectation and we use the operator norm.
Define a function
\phi:[0,1]\to[k]
, such that
\phi(x)=a
iff
x\in P_{a}
and a function
\psi:[0,1]\to[k]
such that for any
x\in\left(\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}\right),\psi(x)=\phi(\xi_{i})
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-{\mathbb{W}}_{\bm{Q}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-{\mathbb{W}}_{\bm{Q}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}\sqrt{\int_{[0,1]^{2}}(\bm{S}_{\phi(x)\phi(y)}-\bm{S}_{\psi(x)\psi(y)})^{2}dxdy}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}\sqrt{\mu\bigl(\psi(x)\neq\phi(x)\text{ or }\psi(y)\neq\phi(y)\bigr)}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{n}\sqrt{2\mu\bigl(\psi(x)\neq\phi(x)\bigr)}.

Define
\widehat{e}_{a}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\operatorname{I}\left\{\xi_{i}\leq e_{a}\right\}}.

Note that
\{x\mid\phi(x)=\psi(x)\}\subseteq\bigcup_{a=1}^{k-1}[\max(e_{a-1},\hat{e}_{a-1}),\min(e_{a},\hat{e}_{a})]
, where we assume that the segment

is empty if
\min(e_{a},\hat{e}_{a})<\max(e_{a-1},\hat{e}_{a-1})
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mu\{\phi(x)\neq\psi(x)\}\leq\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}|\widehat{e}_{a}-e_{a}|.

We are left to bound
|e_{a}-\widehat{e}_{a}|
for each
a\in[k-1]
. Fix any
a\in[k-1]
. Applying the Bernstein inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\widehat{e}_{a}-e_{a}\right|\geq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\operatorname{I}\left\{\xi_{i}\leq e_{a}\right\}}-e_{a})\right|\geq nt\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}t^{2}/2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}({\operatorname{I}\left\{\xi_{i}\leq e_{a}\right\}})+nt/3}\right).

Since
\operatorname{Var}({\operatorname{I}\left\{\xi_{i}\leq e_{a}\right\}})=e_{a}(1-e_{a})\leq e_{a}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|e_{a}-\widehat{e}_{a}|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2(e_{a}+t/3)}\right)

for each
a=[k-1]
. We choose
t=t_{a}
to ensure that the right-hand side is at most
\delta/(2k)
. For our purposes, it is sufficient to set

\displaystyle t_{a}=\frac{2}{3n}\log\left(\frac{4k}{\delta}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2e_{a}}{n}\log\left(\frac{4k}{\delta}\right)}.

Hence, by the union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, it holds

\displaystyle\mu\{\psi(x)\neq\phi(x)\}\leq\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}t_{a}\leq\frac{2k}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}+\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sqrt{\frac{2e_{a}}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}.

(19)

The second term in the right-hand side can be bounded using the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sqrt{\frac{2e_{a}}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2k}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}e_{a}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}.

Assumption

\displaystyle\frac{2k}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2k}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}.

Substituting the above two displays into (
19

\displaystyle\mu\{\psi(x)\neq\phi(x)\}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8k\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}e_{a}}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\bm{Q}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\rho_{n}\sqrt{2\mu(\psi(x))\neq\rho(x))}\leq\rho_{n}\left(\frac{32k\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}e_{a}}{n}\log\frac{4k}{\delta}\right)^{1/4}

(20)

with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.
Bounding
\|\bm{Q}-\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{\lambda}\|
via Theorem
2
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
and applying Lemma

we obtain the result.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
4

We use the standard framework based on hypotheses testing and adapt the proof technique from
(
Klopp et al., 2017
)
. As discussed in Section
Q.5
, we should construct two SBM graphons
U
and
V
such that

1.

\|\rho_{n}U-\rho_{n}V\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\rho_{n}n^{-1/4}/8
;

2.

the corresponding distributions
\mathbb{P}_{U}
and
\mathbb{P}_{V}
satisfy
\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{V}\|\mathbb{P}_{U}\right)\leq 1/2
.

We introduce a family of graphons parametrized by some real number
m\in(-1/2;1/2)
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{m}(x,y)=\begin{cases}3/4,&\text{either $x,y\leq 1/2+m$ or $x,y\geq 1/2+m$},\\
1/4,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We choose
U
to be
W_{0}
and
V
to be
W_{\Delta}
for
\Delta
specified in the sequel. First, we bound the difference between
W_{0}
and
W_{\Delta}
in the operator norm.

Proposition 3
.

We have

\displaystyle\|W_{0}-W_{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Delta(1-\Delta)}.

Proof.

Due to symmetry, we may assume that
\Delta>0
. Note that a graphon
W^{\prime}=W_{\Delta}-W_{0}
is an SMB with three communities:

\displaystyle W^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2;1/2+\Delta)\right\}}{\operatorname{I}\left\{y\in(0;1/2)\right\}}+\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(0;1/2)\right\}}{\operatorname{I}\left\{y\in(1/2;1/2+\Delta)\right\}}

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91684pt\quad-\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2+\Delta;1)\right\}}{\operatorname{I}\left\{y\in(1/2;1/2+\Delta)\right\}}-\frac{1}{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2;1/2+\Delta)\right\}}{\operatorname{I}\left\{y\in(1/2+\Delta;1)\right\}}.

Since for any function
f
,
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}f
can be represented as

\displaystyle(\mathbb{W}^{\prime}f)(x)=f_{1}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(0;1/2)\right\}}+f_{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2;\Delta)\right\}}+f_{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2+\Delta;1)\right\}}

for some numbers
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
,
any eigenfunction
\bm{v}
of
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
has the form:

\displaystyle\bm{v}(x)=v_{1}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(0;1/2)\right\}}+v_{2}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2;{1/2+}\Delta)\right\}}+v_{3}{\operatorname{I}\left\{x\in(1/2+\Delta;1)\right\}}.

Note that, the matrix of connection probabilities between communities of the SBM
W^{\prime}
has rank
2
and
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
has zero trace, so the graphon
W^{\prime}
has two different non-zero eigenvalues
\lambda
and
-\lambda
,
\lambda>0
. We find
\lambda
from the system
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}
. We have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\Delta}{2}v_{2}&=\lambda v_{1}\\
\frac{1}{2}v_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}v_{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\Delta\right)&=\lambda v_{2}\\
-\frac{\Delta}{2}v_{2}&=\lambda v_{3}.\end{cases}

From the first and the third equations, we deduce
v_{1}=-v_{3}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\Delta}{2}v_{2}&=\lambda v_{1}\\
\frac{1}{2}v_{1}(1-\Delta)&=\lambda v_{2}.\end{cases}

We multiply these equations, and get
\lambda^{2}=\Delta(1-\Delta)/4
. Therefore, the proposition follows.
∎

Next, we bound the KL-divergence between distributions
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}
corresponding to graphons models
\rho_{n}W_{0}
and
\rho_{n}W_{\Delta}
respectively.

Proposition 4
.

We have that
\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}\|\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)\leq 16n\Delta^{2}
.

Proof.

By the definition, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}\|\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)=\sum_{A}\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}(\bm{A}=A)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}(\bm{A}=A)}{\mathbb{P}_{0}(\bm{A}=A)},

where the sum is taken over all adjacency matrix
A
.
We introduce a new random variable
\theta=\max\{k\mid\xi_{k}\text{ belongs to the first community}\}
, with the convention that
\theta=0
if
\xi_{k}
belongs to the second community. Subject to
\theta
, the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}(\cdot\mid\theta)
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}(\cdot\mid\theta)
are identical, since both of them are random graph models with the same matrix of connection probabilities
\bm{Q}
:

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{ij}=\begin{cases}3\rho_{n}/4,&\text{if $i,j\leq\theta$ or $i,j>\theta$,}\\
\rho_{n}/4,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For brevity, we define
p_{\bm{A}|\theta}(A\mid t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\bm{A}=A\mid\theta=t)=\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}(\bm{A}=A\mid\theta=t)
,
q(t)=\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}(\theta=t)
and
p(t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\theta=t)
. Then, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}\|\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)=\sum_{A}\sum_{t}p_{\bm{A}\mid\theta}(A\mid t)q(t)\log\frac{\sum_{t}p_{\bm{A}\mid\theta}(A\mid t)q(t)}{\sum_{t}p_{\bm{A}\mid\theta}(A\mid t)p(t)}.

Since the function
(x,y)\to x\log(x/y)
is convex, due to the Jensen inequality, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}\|\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)\leq\sum_{t}q(t)\log\frac{q(t)}{p(t)}.

The latter is the KL-divergence between two binomial distributions
Binom(n,1/2+\Delta)
and

Binom(n,1/2)
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t}q(t)\log\frac{q(t)}{p(t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{n}\binom{n}{t}(1/2+\Delta)^{t}(1/2-\Delta)^{n-t}\log\frac{(1/2+\Delta)^{t}(1/2-\Delta)^{n-t}}{2^{-n}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{KL}\left(Bern^{\otimes n}(1/2+\Delta)\|Bern^{\otimes n}(1/2)\right)

\displaystyle=n\operatorname{KL}\left(Bern(1/2+\Delta)\|Bern(1/2)\right)\leq{4}n\Delta^{2},

where we use the fact that KL-divergence does not exceed chi-square divergence.
∎

We choose
\Delta=({2}\sqrt{2n})^{-1}
, so
\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}\|\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)\leq 1/2
and
\|\rho_{n}W_{0}-\rho_{n}W_{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\rho_{n}n^{-1/4}/8
. Application of Theorem
13
finishes the proof.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

We analyze the bias part
\mathcal{S}_{m}(\bm{Q})-\bm{Q}

\mathcal{S}_{m}(\bm{A})-\mathcal{S}_{m}(\bm{Q})
of the error matrix

(22)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}_{m}(\bm{Q})_{ij}-\bm{Q}_{ij}|

\displaystyle|W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})-W(\xi_{(i^{\prime})},\xi_{j^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle|W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})-W(\xi_{(i^{\prime})},\xi_{j^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle\leq 4C(H,\alpha)\left(\Delta^{\beta}+\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^{\beta}\right).

(23)

Then, by Corollary
4

\tau

\overline{\bm{E}}

\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}

\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}{\operatorname{I}\left\{|\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}|>\tau\right\}}.

It will be convenient to choose

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\overline{\bm{E}}_{a,b}|>\tau\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2s^{2}m^{2}}

By the Bernstein inequality, if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\overline{\bm{E}}_{a,b}|>\tau\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{m^{2}\tau^{2}/2}{\rho_{n}+\tau/3}\right).

If
a=b

\displaystyle p=\begin{cases}m&\text{ if }a\leq s-1\\
m+\ell&\text{if }a=s.\end{cases}

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}|>\tau\right)

So, we set

\displaystyle\tau=\sqrt{\frac{32\rho_{n}\log(8s^{2}m^{2}/\delta)}{m^{2}}}+\frac{11\log(8s^{2}m^{2}/\delta)}{m^{2}},

for which (
30

Step 2. Applying matrix concentration.
Next, we apply Corollary
3
to
\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}-\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}

\displaystyle\max_{a}\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}(\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}-\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau})_{ab}^{2}\leq\max_{a}\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}(\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau})_{ab}^{2}\leq\max_{a}\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}(\overline{\bm{E}}_{0})_{ab}^{2}\leq s\rho_{n}/m^{2},

Therefore, by Corollary

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}-\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 6\sqrt{s\rho_{n}/m^{2}}+4\tau\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(2s/\delta)}

with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,
which implies

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+6\sqrt{s\rho_{n}/m^{2}}+4\tau\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(2s/\delta)}

1-\delta/2

\displaystyle 0=\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}_{0}=\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}+\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau},

we obtain

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{E}}^{\leq\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+6\sqrt{s\rho_{n}/m^{2}}+4\tau\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(2s/\delta)}

1-\delta/2

Step 3. Bounding
\|\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\|\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\|\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq s\max_{a,b}\mathbb{E}|\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}|\leq s\max_{a,b}\mathbb{P}(|\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}|>\tau)\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}

due to (
30

Then, we bound
\|\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{E}}^{>\tau}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{b\in[s]}{\operatorname{I}\left\{|\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}|>\tau\right\}}=1\right)\leq\sum_{b=1}^{s}\mathbb{P}\left(|\overline{\bm{E}}_{ab}|>\tau\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2sm^{2}}.

In particular,

with probability at least
1-\delta/2

\displaystyle\|\overline{\bm{E}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}+6\sqrt{\frac{s\rho_{n}}{m^{2}}}+4\tau\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(2s/\delta)}

\displaystyle\leq 6\sqrt{\frac{s\rho_{n}}{m^{2}}}+4\left(\sqrt{\frac{32\rho_{n}\log(8s^{2}m^{2}/\delta)}{m^{2}}}+\frac{12\log(8s^{2}m^{2}/\delta)}{m^{2}}\right)\sqrt{C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}\log(2s/\delta)}.

Since the latter bound holds for any realization of

i)
Upper bound on the convergence rate.
We build the proof on that of Proposition 3.5 in

By the definition of the empirical graphon

\displaystyle W_{\bm{Q}}(x,y)=\rho_{n}\sum_{ij}W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})\cdot I\{x\in[(i-1)/n,i/n]\}\cdot I\{y\in[(j-1)/n,j/n)\}.

\Delta_{ij}=[(i-1)/n,i/n)\times[(j-1)/n,j/n)

\displaystyle|W(x,y)-W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})|\leq\left|W(x,y)-W\left(i/n,j/n\right)\right|+\left|W\left(i/n,j/n\right)-W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})\right|.

Analogously, we have

\displaystyle\left|W\left(i/n,j/n\right)-W(\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)})\right|\leq C(H,\alpha)\left(|\xi_{(i)}-i/n|^{\alpha\land 1}+|\xi_{(j)}-j/n|^{\alpha\land 1}\right).

\displaystyle\|\rho_{n}\mathbb{W}-\mathbb{W}_{\bm{Q}}\|_{F}^{2}

Lemma 10
.

For a network
\bm{A}
of size
n
and heterogeneities
\bm{\theta}
, let

be a network LQ game with peer-effect parameter
\gamma
and budget
B
. Suppose that
\bm{\theta}
has non-zero projection on the eigenspace of the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\bm{A})
of
\bm{A}
. Then, there exists a number
L\in\left(-\infty;(1-\gamma\|\bm{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}/n)^{-2}\right)
, such that the optimal interventions can be computed as follows:

The optimal intervention problem can be restated as follows:

\displaystyle\max\|(\bm{I}-\gamma\bm{A}/n)^{-1}(\bm{\theta}+\widehat{\bm{\theta}})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}\leq B,

see the proof of Proposition 1 in

Parise and Ozdaglar, 2023
)
. Expanding the squared norm, we obtain the following equivalent problem

\displaystyle\min_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}\quad\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\left[-(\bm{I}-\gamma\bm{A}/n)^{-2}\right]\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-2\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}(\bm{I}-\gamma\bm{A}/n)^{-2}\bm{\theta},

where
\bm{C}
,
\bm{b}

(
Forsythe and Golub, 1965
;
Gander et al., 1989
;
Moré, 2006
)
. It admits solution in the following form:

\displaystyle x=(\bm{C}-L\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{b},\quad\|x\|^{2}_{2}=\beta,

where
L\in(-\infty;\lambda_{\min}(\bm{C}))
, assuming
\bm{b}
has non-zero projection on the eigenspace of

Q.1
Concentration inequalities

First, we state a standard concentration inequality for sums of independent random variables.

Proposition 6

(Bernstein’s inequality, see Equation (2.10) in
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables such that
\max_{i}|X_{i}|\leq M
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(X_{i})\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})\right)+Mt/3}\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(X_{i})\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})\right)+Mt/3}\right).

Then, we introduce a concentration inequality for the operator norm of random matrices with independent entries.

Proposition 7

(Matrix concentration inequality, see Corollary 3.12, Remark 3.13 in
(
Bandeira and van Handel, 2016
)
)
.

Let
\bm{X}
be an
n\times n
matrix whose entries
\bm{X}_{ij}
are independent, centered and bounded random variables. Then, for any
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
, there exists a universal constant
c_{\epsilon}
such that for every
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)2\sqrt{2}\sigma+t\right)\leq ne^{-t^{2}/c_{\epsilon}\sigma_{*}^{2}},

where we have defined

\displaystyle\sigma:=\max_{i}\sqrt{\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}(\bm{X}_{ij}^{2}}),\quad\sigma_{*}:=\max_{ij}\|\bm{X}_{ij}\|_{\infty}.

The above proposition yields the following corollary for symmetric matrices.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\bm{X}
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix whose entries
\bm{X}_{ij},1\leq i\leq j\leq n,
are independent, centered and bounded random variables. Then, for any
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
, there exists a universal constant
c_{\epsilon}
such that for every
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)4\sqrt{2}\sigma+t\right)\leq 2ne^{-t^{2}/4c_{\epsilon}\sigma_{*}^{2}},

where we have defined

\displaystyle\sigma:=\max_{i}\sqrt{\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}(\bm{X}_{ij}^{2}}),\quad\sigma_{*}:=\max_{ij}\|\bm{X}_{ij}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Set
\bm{X}^{+}
to be the upper triangular part of
\bm{X}
, i.e.,
\bm{X}^{+}_{ij}=\bm{X}_{ij}
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
\bm{X}^{+}_{ij}=0
otherwise. Set
\bm{X}^{-}=\bm{X}-\bm{X}^{+}
. By Proposition
7
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}^{+}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)2\sqrt{2}\sigma+t/2\right)\leq ne^{-t^{2}/4c_{\epsilon}\sigma_{*}^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}^{-}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)2\sqrt{2}\sigma+t/2\right)\leq ne^{-t^{2}/4c_{\epsilon}\sigma_{*}^{2}}.

Then, by the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)4\sqrt{2}\sigma+t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}^{+}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\bm{X}^{-}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)4x\sqrt{2}\sigma+t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}^{+}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)2\sqrt{2}\sigma+t/2\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bm{X}^{-}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq(1+\epsilon)2\sqrt{2}\sigma+t/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2ne^{-t^{2}/4c_{\epsilon}\sigma_{*}^{2}}.\qed

In our paper, we choose
\epsilon=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{4}-1\approx 0.06
. Then,
(1+\epsilon)4\sqrt{2}=6
. We denote the constant
c_{\epsilon}\vee 1
by
C_{\text{Pr.\ref{proposition: matrix concentration}}}
.

To derive concentration inequality for order statistics of uniform random variables, we use the following celebrated result of Massart, who extended earlier theorem of Dvoretzky, Kiefer and Wolfowitz
(
Dvoretzky et al., 1956
)
.

Theorem 12

(
(
Massart, 1990
)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random samples from a distribution with defined by CDF
F(\cdot)
. Consider the empirical CDF function
\widehat{F}(\cdot)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\operatorname{I}\left\{X_{i}\leq x\right\}}.

Then, for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{n}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F(x)-\widehat{F}(x)|\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-2t^{2}}.

Applying the above theorem to
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
and noting that
F(\xi_{(i)})=\xi_{(i)}
in this case, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
. Then, for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|\xi_{(i)}-i/n|\geq\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq 2e^{-2t^{2}}.

Q.2
Norms and singular values of matrices

Proposition 8

(Weyl’s inequality, see
(
Bisgard, 2020
)
)
.

Let
\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be arbitrary matrices, and let
\sigma_{k}(\bm{A})
and
\sigma_{k}(\bm{B})
,
k\in[n]
be their singular values in descending order. Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{i+j-1}(\bm{A}+\bm{B})\leq\sigma_{i}(\bm{A})+\sigma_{j}(\bm{B})

for
1\leq i,j,i+j-1\leq n
. If
\bm{A},\bm{B}
are symmetric, then the same inequality holds for their eigenvalues.

Proposition 9

(Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem, see
(
Bisgard, 2020
)
)
.

Let
\bm{A}
be an arbitrary matrix and
\sigma_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{n}
be its singular values. Then, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\rank(\bm{B})\leq r}\|\bm{A}-\bm{B}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i>r}\sigma_{i}^{2}},

and the minimizer is unique.

Q.3
Norms of matrices and graphons

The cut norm of a matrix
\bm{A}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}\|_{\square}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\max_{S,T\subseteq[n]}\left|\sum_{i\in S,j\in T}A_{ij}\right|.

(52)

We have that
\|\bm{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq n\|\bm{A}\|_{\square}.

Proof.

Let
S^{*},T^{*}
be the sets that provide the maximum in the definition of
\|\bm{A}\|_{\square}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}\|_{\square}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left|\sum_{i\in S^{*},j\in T^{*}}A_{ij}\right|.

Define
\bm{u}^{S^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\bm{u}^{S^{*}}_{i}=1
if
i\in S^{*}
,
\bm{u}^{S^{*}}_{i}=0
if
i\notin S^{*}
. Similarly, define
\bm{u}^{T^{*}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|\bm{A}\|_{\square}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\langle\bm{A}\bm{u}^{T^{*}},\bm{u}^{S^{*}}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|\bm{A}\bm{u}^{T^{*}}\|\|\bm{u}^{S^{*}}\|\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|\bm{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\bm{u}^{T^{*}}\|\|\bm{u}^{S^{*}}\|\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|\bm{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{n}\sqrt{n}=\frac{\|\bm{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{n},

where the first inequality is the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and the second inequality is due to submultiplicativity of the
2
-norm.
∎

Q.4
Rank of kernels

Let
\mathbb{W}
be the integral operator associated with the graphon
W
defined as

[\mathbb{W}f](x)=\int_{0}^{1}W(x,y)f(y)dy
for all
x\in[0,1]
, for all
f\in L_{2}[0,1]
.
The operator
\mathbb{W}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. In particular,
\mathbb{W}
is a compact operator, meaning it possesses a discrete spectrum. Hence,
\mathbb{W}
admits a spectral decomposition.

W(x,y)\sim\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}f_{k}(x)f_{k}(y)

The rank of a kernel
W
is determined by the rank of its associated kernel operator
\mathbb{W}
. While this rank is typically infinite, our focus lies on cases where it’s finite.
If the rank
r
of
W
is finite, then the spectral decomposition of
W
will be finite. In particular,

W(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\lambda_{k}f_{k}(x)f_{k}(y).

holds almost everywhere.
Note that every SBM graphon (Definition
1
) has a finite rank. Indeed, if
W
is a stepfunction with
m
steps, then every function in the range of
\mathbb{W}
is a stepfunction with the same
m
steps, and so the dimension of this range is at most
m
.
It is also easy to see that the sum and product of
two kernels with finite rank have a finite rank (see
(
Lovász, 2012
, Sections 7.5, 14.4)
).

Q.5
Minimax bounds

Here, we briefly describe the simplest framework for establishing the minimax lower bounds on a risk. For deeper introduction, see
(
Tsybakov and Zaiats, 2009
)
. Suppose that we have some family of distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\theta\in\Theta,
over a sample space
\mathcal{X}
parametrized by parametric space
\Theta
. Let
\mathcal{R}:\Theta\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a semi-distance on
\Theta
. Any function
\widehat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\Theta
is referred to as an estimator of the parameter
\theta
. Given a sample
X
drawn from the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
, the quantity
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\theta}(X),\theta)
serves as a natural measure of the estimator’s quality. A fundamental question in statistical estimation is determining the minimax lower bound on the risk
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\theta}(X),\theta)
, representing the best achievable performance across all estimators.

If two distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}
are close in some sense, they generate similar samples
X
from the sample space
\mathcal{X}
. When
X
is low-dimensional, distinguishing between
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
becomes challenging, particularly if both parameters are high-dimensional, making it difficult for any estimator to separate them effectively. To formalize this intuition, we examine the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between two discrete distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\right)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}(X=x)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}(X=x)}{\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(X=x)}.

It admits several basic properties:

1.

it is non-negative and equals zero if and only if
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}
coincide;

2.

the KL-divergence does not exceed the chi-square divergence:

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\right)\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}(X=x)-\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(X=x)\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(X=x)};

3.

for tensor product of measures, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{1}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\prime}\|\mathbb{P}_{2}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\prime}\right)=\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{1}\|\mathbb{P}_{2}\right)+\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\prime}\|\mathbb{P}_{2}^{\prime}\right).

If
\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}\right)
is small, any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
is expected to struggle to distinguish between
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. More precisely, the following theorem establishes that if
X\sim\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}
, then the risk
\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\theta}(X),\theta)
is at least
\mathcal{R}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})/2
, with a probability that increases as
\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}\right)\to 0
.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
be any two elements of parametric space
\Theta
such that
\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{2}}\right)\leq\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\max_{\theta\in\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left\{\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\theta},\theta)\geq\mathcal{R}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})/2\right\}\geq\max\left\{e^{-\alpha}/4,\frac{1-\sqrt{\alpha/2}}{2}\right\}.

For proofs, see Chapter 2 from
(
Tsybakov and Zaiats, 2009
)
.
For general distributions such that
\mathbb{P}_{1}\ll\mathbb{P}_{0}
, the KL-divergence can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{1}\|\mathbb{P}_{0}\right)=\int\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{1}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}\right)d\mathbb{P}_{1}=\int\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{1}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}\right)\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{1}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}d\mathbb{P}_{0}.

It can be useful to upper bound the KL-divergence by the chi-square divergence, that is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0})=\int\left(\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{1}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}-1\right)^{2}d\mathbb{P}_{0}=\int\left(\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{1}}{d\mathbb{P}_{0}}\right)^{2}d\mathbb{P}_{0}-1,

which may be easier to compute in some cases. In particular, the following theorem provides a lower bound on the risk of any estimator
\widehat{\theta}
based on the chi-square divergence between
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{j}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
for
j=1,\ldots,M
.

Theorem 14

(
(
Tsybakov and Zaiats, 2009
)
,Theorem 2.6)
.

Assume that
M\geq 2
and suppose that the parameter space
\Theta
contains elements
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M}
defining distributions
\mathbb{P}_{0},\ldots,\mathbb{P}_{M}
such that

1.

d(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\geq 2s
for all
0\leq i<j\leq M
,

2.

\mathbb{P}_{j}\ll\mathbb{P}_{0}

Data-processing inequality

Lemma 11

(Data-processing for KL under a Markov kernel)
.

Let
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{F})
and
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{G})
be measurable spaces and
let
K(\cdot\mid u)
be a Markov kernel from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{A}
.
For
i\in\{0,1\}
let
\mu_{i}
be probability measures on
\mathcal{U}
and define the induced
marginal laws on
\mathcal{A}
by

P_{i}(B):=\int_{\mathcal{U}}K(B\mid u)\,\mu_{i}(du),\qquad B\in\mathcal{G},

i.e.
P_{i}=\mu_{i}K
.
Assume
\mu_{1}\ll\mu_{0}
so that
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{0})<\infty
.
Then

\mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\|P_{0})\ \leq\ \mathrm{KL}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{0}).

Proof.

Define joint probability measures on
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{A}
by

\widetilde{P}_{i}(du,da):=\mu_{i}(du)\,K(da\mid u),\qquad i\in\{0,1\}.

By construction, the
\mathcal{A}
-marginal of
\widetilde{P}_{i}
is
P_{i}
.

(1) KL increases under adding variables (equivalently, decreases under marginalization).

Using the chain rule for KL divergence (factorizing by
A
),

\mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{P}_{1}\|\widetilde{P}_{0})=\mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\|P_{0})\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P_{1}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(\widetilde{P}_{1}(U\mid A)\ \|\ \widetilde{P}_{0}(U\mid A)\big)\right].

The conditional KL term is nonnegative, hence

\mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\|P_{0})\ \leq\ \mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{P}_{1}\|\widetilde{P}_{0}).

(2) Compute the joint KL: the kernel cancels.

Since
\widetilde{P}_{i}(du,da)=\mu_{i}(du)K(da\mid u)
share the same conditional law
K(\cdot\mid u)
,

\frac{d\widetilde{P}_{1}}{d\widetilde{P}_{0}}(u,a)=\frac{d\mu_{1}}{d\mu_{0}}(u)\quad\text{for }\widetilde{P}_{1}\text{-a.e. }(u,a),

and thus

\mathrm{KL}(\widetilde{P}_{1}\|\widetilde{P}_{0})=\int_{\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{A}}\log\!\left(\frac{d\mu_{1}}{d\mu_{0}}(u)\right)\,\widetilde{P}_{1}(du,da)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\log\!\left(\frac{d\mu_{1}}{d\mu_{0}}(u)\right)\,\mu_{1}(du)=\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{0}).

Combining (1) and (2) gives
\mathrm{KL}(P_{1}\|P_{0})\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{1}\|\mu_{0})
.
∎

Lemma 12

(KL contraction under a measurable map)
.

Let
S:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be measurable and let
P,Q
be probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
.
Then

\mathrm{KL}(P\circ S^{-1}\,\|\,Q\circ S^{-1})\leq\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q).

Proof.

This is the data-processing inequality for KL.
Define the Markov kernel
K(B\mid x):=\mathbf{1}\{S(x)\in B\}
(deterministic channel).
Then
P\circ S^{-1}=PK
and
Q\circ S^{-1}=QK
, hence

\mathrm{KL}(P\circ S^{-1}\|Q\circ S^{-1})=\mathrm{KL}(PK\|QK)\leq\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)
.
∎
