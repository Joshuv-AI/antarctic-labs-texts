Cutaneous metastasis secondary to nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Yang Yang, Yong-Liang Hu, Ting-Hui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCutaneous metastasis (CM) is an uncommon manifestation of internal malignancies, with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) as an exceptionally rare primary source. NPC, a subtype of head and neck carcinoma, demonstrates geographic variability and aggressive behavior, including potential for distant metastasis. Despite the rarity of cutaneous dissemination from NPC, accurate diagnosis requires comprehensive clinical evaluation supported by histopathological and immunohistochemical assessment.Case presentationA 59-year-old male with previously treated non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma of the nasopharynx presented with multiple erythematous papules and nodules in the right axillary region over three weeks. Histopathological examination revealed dense dermal infiltration by atypical epithelial cells arranged in nests. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated tumor cell reactivity for pan cytokeratin (CK), CK5/6, and CK7, with a high Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 75%. These findings confirmed a diagnosis of CM secondary to NPC. Following histopathological confirmation, the patient continued multidisciplinary management under general medical care.ConclusionsThis case highlights that NPC may present with CM, even years after initial treatment. Early recognition of cutaneous lesions in patients with a history of malignancies is critical, as definitive diagnosis relies on integrative evaluation combining clinical history, morphological assessment, and molecular pathological analysis. Accurate identification of CM directly impacts therapeutic strategies and prognostic evaluation, emphasizing the importance of maintaining high diagnostic suspicion for cutaneous metastasis in at-risk patient populations.

Background

The skin, as the largest organ of the human body, can occasionally serve as a site for metastatic dissemination from internal malignancies such as head and neck carcinomas, breast carcinoma, and neuroendocrine tumors. Cutaneous metastatic involvement may occur via hematogenous or lymphatic pathways or through direct invasion from adjacent anatomical structures, resulting in cutaneous metastatic carcinoma. Despite these potential pathways, cutaneous metastasis (CM) remains a relatively rare clinical presentation [1, 2].

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant epithelial neoplasm originating from the mucosal lining of the nasopharynx. It demonstrates a distinct geographic distribution, with over 75% of global cases reported in Southeast Asia and southern China. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, approximately 129,000 new cases were reported in 2018, comprising only 0.7% of all malignancies [3, 4]. CM originating from NPC is exceptionally uncommon. The present report describes a rare case of CM in a patient with a prior diagnosis of NPC, in whom dermatological manifestations and histopathological evaluation were critical for establishing the diagnosis.

Case report

A 59-year-old male patient presented to the dermatology outpatient clinic on March 7, 2024, with a three-week history of progressive dermatological changes in the right axillary region. The initial presentation involved several erythematous papules and nodules, each approximately the size of a sesame seed, were observed without an identifiable triggering factor. These lesions progressively enlarged and increased in number, accompanied by mild tenderness on palpation.

The patient was initially diagnosed with nasopharyngeal carcinoma in December 2020 at an external hospital due to nasal congestion, runny nose, and headache symptoms with positive EBV by molecular pathology testing. Subsequently, the patient sought treatment at the Department of General Medicine of our hospital and were hospitalized for treatment, receiving NPC radical radiotherapy combined with carboplatin and paclitaxel chemotherapy for 1–2 cycles; [5] In May 2021, the patient felt that their symptoms had worsened compared to before, so they adjusted to second-line treatment (gemcitabine + lobaplatin) for 8–9 cycles; In early July 2022, the patient was found to have enlarged lymph nodes on the right clavicle. Paclitaxel liposome chemotherapy and Toripalimab (intravenous drip, per 3 weeks) immunotherapy were given, with a total of 7 cycles of intermittent maintenance treatment until March 2024. At the same time, several nodules were found on the skin of the right axilla, and subsequent pathological diagnosis was NPC with skin metastasis. After being diagnosed with skin metastasis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, due to the local progression of nasopharyngeal cancer, the patient was treated with capecitabine 1000 mg twice daily by mouth and teprotumumab 240 mg infusion in March 2024, combined with local radiotherapy in the right axilla. After this, the skin lesion in the right axilla of the patient shrank. Subsequently, the patient discontinued the treatment due to intolerance of the digestive tract reactions caused by oral capecitabine. From September to December 2024, the patient’s NPC skin metastases (multiple nodules) began to increase, and on the basis treatment of Toripalimab, the patient was given fluorouracil 2.0 g by chemotherapy. During the period, PET/CT tracking showed no distant metastasis of the primary lesion (Fig. 1), but the metastatic lesion gradually involved the right chest and back, accompanied by lymph node metastasis. At present, the patient is still hospitalized in the general practice department for observation and treatment.

Dermatological examination identified over ten firm, mildly tender, erythematous papules and nodules ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 cm in diameter in the right axillary area. The lesions had smooth surfaces and were not associated with increased local temperature (Fig. 2).

Histopathological evaluation demonstrated dense dermal infiltration by atypical epithelial cells, organized in nests and scattered throughout the dermis (Fig. 3A, B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive expression of pan cytokeratin (CK), CK5/6, and CK7, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 75% (Fig. 3C–F). These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of cutaneous metastasis secondary to NPC. Following diagnostic confirmation, the patient continued to receive multidisciplinary care under the general medicine department.

The patient is survived but with progression of skin lesion at the last follow-up after 4 years.

Discussion

CM typically occurs in proximity to the primary neoplastic site and may serve as clinical feature of the underlying malignancy. In approximately 6.4% to 7.8% of cases, CM may precede the identification of other distant metastases [6]. Given the wide variability in clinical presentation, early recognition of CM is often challenging. For instance, Gaitán-Gaona et al. reported a case of pigmented breast carcinoma with CM that was clinically similar to malignant melanoma [7]. The pigmentation observed was hypothesized to result from damage to epidermal melanocytes or the transfer of melanin into tumor cells.

NPC, classified within the spectrum of head and neck carcinomas, is characterized by its aggressive nature and potential for distant metastasis, including cutaneous dissemination. In a retrospective cohort study by Bohli et al., among 820 individuals diagnosed with NPC over two decades, only two cases of CM were identified, one during radiotherapy and the other within three months following treatment [8]. These lesions, located on the trunk and back, appeared as painless nodules and erythematous plaques, highlighting the rarity of this manifestation.

Andres Gonzalez Garcia et al. reported a rare case of NPC skin metastasis with simulated herpes zoster like presentation in the early stages. The patient presented with painful erythema and nodules in the left chest, accompanied by symptoms such as fatigue [9]. Fadila Kouhen et al. reported a case of underage patient with scalp metastasis of NPC (stage III undifferentiated carcinoma) [10]. Unfortunately, both patients ultimately died due to the rapid development and metastasis of the primary lesion. From this, it can be seen that skin metastasis, as a “warning sign” of NPC, usually indicates that the patient is in a state of low immunity and also suggests that the disease is in the late stage and has a poor prognosis. Therefore, clinical doctors should achieve early detection with a full understanding of the relevant medical history, especially paying attention to identifying non-specific skin lesions, and if necessary, combining relevant auxiliary examinations for final diagnosis and overall evaluation. Generally speaking, the clinical features of primary squamous cell carcinoma of the skin are mainly characterized by red wart like proliferative plaques, which may be covered with scales and prone to ulceration accompanied by bleeding; Its histological features are mainly related to its degree of differentiation, with most cases being highly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, with small cellular atypia, and common keratinized beads and intercellular bridges within the cancer tissue. In clinical practice, skin metastatic NPC usually presents as several red nodules of varying sizes with or without pain, and generally does not rupture; The histological manifestations are closely related to the degree of differentiation of its primary lesion. Most of them are non keratinized cancers, mainly characterized by nest like distribution of cancer cells, no obvious keratinization beads or intercellular bridges, and significant cellular atypia. In addition, it is often necessary to combine the patient’s detailed medical history, especially to understand whether there is EB virus infection.

Due to the clinical presentation of nasopharyngeal carcinoma skin metastasis as single or multiple red or light red papules or nodules accompanied by pain or no symptoms, it is necessary to differentiate it from skin diseases such as herpes zoster, cutaneous mycobacterial infection, seborrheic inflammation, and sarcoidosis. Among them, herpes zoster is mainly manifested as clustered erythema and blisters distributed along one side of the nerve, usually accompanied by severe pain or numbness; Skin mycobacterial infection often has a history of trauma, and the skin lesions are polymorphic, which can manifest as papules, nodules, plaques, or ulcers; Seborrheic inflammation mainly affects the subcutaneous fat layer, manifested as single or multiple tender nodules, and may be accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever, joint pain, etc.; Sarcoidosis also clinically manifested as subcutaneous nodules or plaques, which may be accompanied by multiple organ damage and lymph node lesions. The above-mentioned diseases often require a comprehensive judgment based on laboratory tests, imaging, and pathology.

In the present case, the patient had a prior confirmed diagnosis of NPC. Although initial treatment resulted in tumor regression, subsequent imaging findings and the emergence of suspicious cutaneous lesions raised concern for metastatic progression. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed CM, with high proliferative activity and cytokeratin expression supporting the diagnosis.

From a diagnostic perspective, distinguishing between primary cutaneous malignancies and secondary cutaneous involvement originating from non-cutaneous derived tumors requires detailed assessment of histopathological features in conjunction with immunohistochemical profiling. Accurate interpretation of staining patterns, differentiating those associated with normal skin architecture from those of metastatic tumor cells is essential [11]. Therefore, an integrative diagnostic approach that encompasses clinical history, morphological assessment, and molecular pathological analysis is vital to ensure diagnostic accuracy and to prevent misclassification.

Conclusion

In patients with a history of malignancies such as NPC, the emergence of solitary or multiple infiltrative cutaneous lesions, particularly in the absence of preceding trauma or localized irritation, should prompt consideration of cutaneous metastasis. Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological evaluation supported by immunohistochemical analysis. Early and accurate identification of CM is critical, as it directly impacts therapeutic strategies and prognostic evaluation.
