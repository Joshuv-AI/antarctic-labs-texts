Longitudinal trajectories and predictors of glycated hemoglobin among type 2 diabetes patients concomitant with hypertension: spline based linear mixed-effects model
Authors: Mulat Belay Simegn, Habtamu Guaguahu Feleke, Temesgen Baylie, Elyas Melaku Mazengia, Muluye Gebrie Mengie, Bekalu Endalew, Abiot Aschale, Kebere Dagnaw Birhanu, Eskeziyaw Agedew, Kidist Teshome Dugassa, Habtamu Geremew, Adugnaw Bantie Kebie, Werkneh Melkie Tilahun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonitoring glycated hemoglobin biomarkers is very essential to be early aware of that metabolic abnormality and poorly controlled blood sugar. The presence of type 2 diabetes has been associated with an increased risk of hypertension and vice versa, and their coexistence increases the risk of micro and macro-complications and poor glycemic control. The aim of the study is to assess HbA1c trajectories and identify its predictors among type 2 diabetes patients concomitant with hypertension. An institution-based retrospective follow-up study was conducted at Debre Markos Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital. A total of 648 were sampled, and 600 were included in the final analysis among type 2 diabetes patients concomitant with hypertension between June 30, 2014, and July 01, 2023. The best time function, covariance structure, and model comparison were conducted, and those that had the lowest AIC and/or BIC were selected. A linear mixed-effects model with cubic natural spline random intercepts and slopes has been constructed to model the complete true history of HbA1c for each subject by considering the effects of baseline covariates and socio-demographic factors on HbA1c evolution. Finally, statistical significance was declared at 95% CI with a P-value less than 0.05. Patients were followed for different follow-up times, with a median follow-up period of 85.2 months (IQR: 25.1). Injection medication (β = − 0.16, 95% CI: [− 0.29, − 0.02]) and treatment duration > = 6 years (β = − 0.12, 95% CI: [− 0.21, − 0.03]) were associated with lower HbA1c level, while obesity (β = 0.12, 95% CI: [0.01, 0.22]), presence of nephropathy (β = 0.35, 95% CI: [0.25, 0.51]), higher weight (β = 0.09, 95% CI: [0.01, 0.21]), uncontrolled hypertension (β = 0.89, 95% CI: [0.23, 1.14]), and family history of diabetes (β = 0.22, 95% CI: [0.12, 0.36]) were associated with higher biomarker level. The mean glycated hemoglobin was high when compared to the American Diabetes Association. This study revealed that injection medication, obesity, presence of nephropathy, higher weight, family history of diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, and ≥ 6 years of treatment duration were significant predictors of HbA1c level. Therefore, prioritize patients with uncontrolled hypertension and any other comorbidity, and integrating weight management into a routine system for risk assessment and glycemic control is highly recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57264-0.Keywords: Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), Type 2 diabetes mellitus, HypertensionSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Endocrinology, Medical research, Nephrology, Risk factors

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic disease caused by either lack of enough insulin production or when the body fails to use the produced insulin1,2. Globally, number of diabetic patients is estimated to be 463 million people by 2019, and expected to rise to 1578 million by 20303. However, Low and middle income countries takes a huge share this burden. Diabetes can result in vascular injury and dysfunction4, thereby elevating the risk of hypertension development and progression. Conversely, hypertension (HTN) heightens the risk of diabetes-related complications, including heart disease, stroke, and kidney disease5. Glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) assesses recent blood sugar control by reflecting the average blood sugar level over the preceding 2 to 3 months6.

Previous studies conducted across the world indicated that factors such as Longer duration of type 2 DM (> = 6 years)7–14, obesity11,14, Poor adherence to medication8,15, no education7,16,17, chat chewing17, using a combinations of drug (either insulin with oral anti-diabetic drugs or combination of oral anti-diabetic drugs)15,18, age of participants8,14, family history of DM11, presence of comorbidities like hypertension8, total cholesterol level of 200 mg/dl or more10, Marital status9,19, inadequate physical exercise7,8, smoking and alcohol use7 have been associated with poor glycemic control13.

The incidence of hypertension in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is twofold higher than without the disease20 and their coexistence leads to poor glycemic controls, serious vascular complication21,22. In addition to medical treatment, various strategies like frequent patient visits23, home blood pressure and blood glucose monitoring24 have been reported to improve achievement of glycemic control and blood pressure targets. However, the burden of diabetes is continue to be a public health concern and becomes sever when diabetes and hypertension coexists.

Ethiopian studies have relied on cross-sectional designs and fasting blood glucose measurements rather than repeated HbA1c assessments. Moreover, no evidence exists regarding longitudinal HbA1c trajectories and their predictors among patients with concomitant hypertension, a clinically high-risk subgroup with potentially different metabolic profiles and treatment responses. In addition, nonlinear patterns of HbA1c change over time have not been evaluated yet in low-resource settings like Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess longitudinal HbA1c trajectories and identify their predictors among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension at Debre Markos Comprehensive and Specialized Hospitals (DMCSH).

Method and materials

Study area, design and period

An institutional-based retrospective longitudinal study was conducted at Debre Markos Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital (DMCSH) among type 2 diabetes patients concomitant with hypertension having follow up between June 30/2014 and July 01/2023. It is located in the Misraq Gojjam Zone Debre Markos town of Amhara region. DMCSH is the only specialized hospital found in East Gojjam Zone. This hospital serves more than 3.5 million people in its catchment area.

Source and study population

All patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and concomitant hypertension who had follow-up at DMCSH were considered the source population. From this group, patients diagnosed with both type 2 diabetes and hypertension during the study period and who met the eligibility criteria were included in the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All type 2 diabetic patients with concomitant hypertension who were diagnosed from June 30/2014 to July 01/2023 were included in the study. However, those whose date of initiation was not recorded and those who had less than two HbA1c measurements were excluded.

Sample size determination

By considering a two sided significance level (α = 5%), power 80%, number of repeated measurements (m = 8), true mean difference (δ = 0.84), and the subject correlation estimate from similar cohorts (ρ = 0.16)25,26.

The longitudinal model sample size was determined using the Diggle formula taken for repeated measurements27,28.

After considering the 10% incomplete ness, the final sample size becomes 648.

Sampling technique and procedures

After identifying patients who fulfilled the selection criteria, the sampling frame was prepared by collecting their medical record numbers (MRNs) from the hospital registration book. This registry contains comprehensive records of all patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes concomitant with hypertension within a specified timeframe (June 30/2014, to July 1, 2023). Then, study subjects were selected using a simple random sampling technique, generated by a random number generator.

Data collection tool and follow up

Data for this study is secondary data, and it was obtained from diabetic and hypertensive patients under follow-up from June 30/2014, to July 01/, 2023. A structured checklist was prepared and used for the extraction of relevant information from patient charts. Data was extracted from the patient’s intake form, follow-up card, chronic disease registration book, electronic data sources, and clinical records, including laboratory results of biomarkers from September 01/, 2024, to October 30/2024. A total of four BSc nurses and two master’s holders were assigned as data collectors and supervisors, respectively. Each data collector follows the prepared checklist to extract specific data points, including socio-demographic information, clinical details, treatment history, and behavioral factors. The process involves finding each patient’s medical chart, verifying the patient’s identity using their medical card number, and thoroughly extracting the required information on the checklist. The starting point for the retrospective follow-up was the time of the first confirmed diagnosis of type-2 diabetes concomitant with hypertension. The recruitment period spans from June 30/2014, to July 01/, 2023. Patients after 2023 July 01 was not considered, hence they could not provide adequate (two) HbA1c measurements. The endpoint of follow-up was the date of death, loss to follow-up, last contact, or the end of the follow-up period observation period which is July 01, 2023.

Data quality assurance

In order to maintain the quality of the data, well-experienced data collectors and supervisors were recruited. Two-day training was given for the data collectors on the objective of the study and how to review the documents as per the data extraction checklist. Before actual data collection time, the checklist (tool) was pretested using 5% of the sample size, and possible adjustments were made to the tool. The variables like glucometer usage and self-monitoring of glucose were excluded from the checklist due to not being recorded at any of the registers. Besides, daily monitoring of data collection for completeness and consistency, the principal investigator was closely following the supervisors and data collectors. There was a double-check data entry process.

Data management and analysis

The extracted data were entered into Epidata version 4.6.0.2 and were exported to STATA statistical software Version 17 for further management and analysis. All Descriptive statistics were computed considering the nature (categorical/continuous) and distribution (normal vs. skewed) and presented using tables, graphs, and texts. The categorical variables were described using frequency with percentages, while the continuous variable was described using mean with standard deviation and median with interquartile range to summarize normal and skewed distributions, respectively. The missing value was treated before the model was fitted for covariates that have more than 5% missing (Occupation 11%, neuropathy 18%, education 8%, and marital status 14%). The missingness mechanism for the independent variables was checked by the Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) test, and a p-value < 0.05, suggesting that the data were not MCAR. Logistic regression models were fitted for predicting the missingness indicator for each covariate using observed variables. Finding significant variables supports the assumption that the data are missing at random (MAR), as the missingness is conditionally dependent on observed covariates. Multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) were performed with 20 imputations for missing at random. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by comparing the imputed datasets with the complete-case dataset. The parameter estimate between complete case analysis and multiple imputation is not that much different. Detailed comparisons of estimates between the two methods are presented in (Supplementary Table 1).

Covariates that have significant effects on the mean change of HbA1c measurements over time have been assessed. A linear mixed effect model with cubic spline random intercepts and slopes has been constructed using the maximum likelihood estimation technique to model the complete true history of HbA1c for each subject by considering the effects of baseline covariates on HbA1c evolution. Knot locations for the cubic spline were determined based on selected percentiles of the follow-up time distribution, specifically the 25th, 50th, and 70th percentiles. These corresponded to approximately 24, 36, and 50 months, respectively. This percentile-based approach is widely recommended in the literature and was chosen to ensure both statistical robustness and clinical interpretability, allowing the model to flexibly capture meaningful changes in HbA1c trajectories over time37. Additionally, Knot locations for the cubic splines were also chosen based on the observed distribution of potential curvature points of follow-up time using a kernel density plot (Supplementary Fig. 1): Knot 1 at ~ 24 months (main peak), Knot 2 at ~ 35 months (second peak), and Knot 3 at ~ 60 months (late-phase change). This knot location indicated almost a similar curvature points. Sensitivity analyses using alternative knot locations produced similar trajectory patterns and predictor estimates, supporting the robustness of the selected model specification and details of result is supported by (Supplementary Table 2).

Cubic spline basis variables were generated from the time variable using these knots and incorporated into the linear mixed-effects model as fixed and random effects to account for nonlinear trends and subject-specific variations in HbA1c trajectories over time. Random effects were specified at the individual level, including a random intercept and spline-based random slopes, to capture subject-specific deviations in HbA1c trajectories. The normality of residuals was assessed using a Q-Q plot, which indicates approximate normality (points fall roughly along the reference line (Fig. 1). This graphical evidence was also supported by the Shapiro–Wilk statistical test, which accepts the normality of HbA1c data with the Shapiro–Wilk test (W = 0.98, p = 0.061).

Selecting best time function

After confirming the need for Random Intercept/Slope and a higher degree function within the model from the individual profile and mean structure. Candidate time function models include the random intercept, random slope, Quadratic Polynomial Mixed Effect Model, 3 DF Polynomial Mixed Effect Model, cubic-spline mixed effect model, and Cubic-Spline Mixed Effect Model with spline random effect. As the model comparison reveals, the Cubic-Spline Mixed Effect Model with spline random effect was the best model due to its lowest AIC and/ or BIC, and its indication as the time function pattern shows (Supplementary Table 3).

Selecting the best covariance structure

The best covariance structure for both the within-individual covariance structure and the random effect covariance structure is selected based on AIC/BIC. So, the AIC/BIC model comparison shows that the first-order Autoregressive from within individual and unstructured covariance structure from random effect covariance structure is best (Supplementary Table 4).

On the other hand, less than one-third (26.16%) of the patients have no education at all. Similarly, more than half (56.17%) was also from urban residence. Nearly one-third (30.67%) of patients were single. Of all patients, 27% had no occupation (Table 1).

Factors associated with glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) variability

Linear mixed effect model with cubic spline random intercept and slope model was employed. The variables, including medication type, BMI, family history of diabetes mellitus, nephropathy, treatment duration, uncontrolled hypertension, and weight, had a statistically significant association with HbA1c change. The estimated standard deviation for the random intercept was 0.46, indicating considerable variability in baseline HbA1c levels across patients. For the spline based random effects of slope, the first spline component of the slope had a standard deviation of 0.12, while the second spline component of the slope had a standard deviation of 0.09, reflecting individual differences in HbA1c trajectory. The correlation structure among the random effects revealed negative correlation (− 0.05) between the intercept and the first time spline component, suggesting that individuals with higher baseline HbA1c levels tend to follow slower decrease over first time spline component. The second spline component had a positive (0.03) correlation with intercept, which indicates patients with higher baseline HbA1c have faster decrease over the second time spline component. Random slope between the first spline term and second spline term have a negative (− 0.01) correlation, indicating that patients had large HbA1c at the first spline component tend to follow slower decrease at the second spline component.

Patients who took injection medication had lower HbA1c levels by 0.16 units on average [β = − 0.16, 95%CI; − 0.29, − 0.02] as compared to no medication, while keeping other variables constant. Those obesity patients had a 0.12 unit higher in HbA1c compared to normal [β = 0.12, 95%CI; 0.01, 0.22]. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension had, on average, higher HbA1c levels over follow-up compared with patients with controlled hypertension [β = 0.89, 95%CI; 0.23, 1.14]. Patients had a family history of diabetes had higher HbA1c level with 0.22 units on average [β = 0.22, 95%CI; 0.12, 0.36] when compared to their counter parts. Treatment duration greater than six year lower the HbA1c by 0.12 unit [β = − 0.12, 95%CI; − 0.21, − 0.03] compared with having treatment duration less than six year. Those patients who developed nephropathy had, on average, higher glycated hemoglobin level [β = 0.35, 95%CI; 0.25, 0.51] as compared to did not have nephropathy. One Kg increase in weight had 0.09 unit higher in HbA1c level [β = 0.09, 95%CI; 0.01, 0.21]. These effects are contextualized within a model explaining 69% of HbA1c variability (conditional R2 = 0.69), indicates substantial variability explained by both fixed and random effects (Table 3).

Discussion

The mean baseline HbA1c of 8.37 in this study of patients with comorbid type 2 diabetes and hypertension showed suboptimal glycemic control, falling above recommended clinical targets. This finding is consistent with studies of similar comorbid populations of patients with diabetes and hypertension, frequently reported baseline HbA1c levels hovering around 8.0–8.538. This might be the added cardiovascular risk from hypertension, which may sometimes lead to cautious intensification of glycemic therapy, potentially contributing to higher average HbA1c, where the management of both conditions often presents a therapeutic challenge. Whereas, this mean is lower than values reported in some real-world studies of underserved or poorly resourced settings, where means can exceed 9.039. The reason might be that in our setting, the study was conducted at a specialized hospital, which enhances the treatment quality and is supported by the finding that longer treatment duration and good glycemic control were associated with better outcomes. However, the result is higher than the study reported from the American Diabetes Association value of < 7.040. This is due to the tight control target of ≤ 6.5, and the association followed. This indicates a substantial gap in effective glycemic management at the population level in this study.

The longitudinal data analysis predictors for change in HbA1c were identified. Besides, medication type (injection), family history of diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, obesity, presence of nephropathy, treatment duration ≥ 6 years, and higher weight had statistically significant associations with HbA1c level. Patients using injection medication had lower the HbA1c level when compared to those not using medications at all, controlling for other factors. This evidence was supported by several studies10,15,18,41. This consistency might be that patients on injection medication, particularly insulin, show greater reductions in HbA1c compared to those receiving no medication because insulin directly compensates for the body’s insufficient endogenous insulin production and more effectively lowers blood glucose levels42,43. Patients with higher body mass index (obesity) were associated with higher HbA1c level as compared to normal BMI. This finding was also evidenced by different studies8,44,45. This association might be that elevated BMI is associated with excessive insulin production as the pancreas tries to compensate for insulin resistance. Over time, this can lead to beta-cell dysfunction, diminishing insulin secretion, and worsening blood glucose control reflected in higher HbA1c46. Patients had family history of diabetes was associated with higher HbA1c levels. This may reflect a combination of genetic predisposition and shared lifestyle factors that contribute to more severe diabetes management. This is due to the familial aggregation of diabetes, which is linked to poorer glycemic control and earlier onset of complications47. The presence of nephropathy was also linked to higher HbA1c. This finding highlights a bidirectional relationship: poor glycemic control leads to nephropathy, and the presence of nephropathy can complicate diabetes management48. Higher weight was associated with higher HbA1c. This reinforces the finding for obesity under the BMI category and underscores the direct relationship between adiposity and glycemic dysregulation, as increased adipose tissue promotes inflammation and insulin resistance49. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension had higher HbA1c levels over follow-up by 0.89 units compared with patients with controlled hypertension. This finding was consistent with other studies conducted including USA50,51. This large association observed between uncontrolled hypertension and HbA1c trajectories may indicate the complex metabolic and vascular interaction between hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension reflect poorer adherence to treatment, suboptimal lifestyle modification, or greater comorbidity burden, potentially contributing to persistently elevated HbA1c levels. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously due to some important time-varying predictors, including medication adjustments, antihypertensive treatment intensification, and longitudinal blood pressure changes, were not available in the retrospective records and therefore could not be incorporated into the model.

Strength and limitation of study

This study was conducted using an advanced statistical approach; Use of longitudinal HbA1c changes using linear mixed effect with cubic spline models, improving inference accuracy. It also considered a long follow-up time up to 10 years, which enhances the study’s reliability. However, it is not without limitations. Study limits causal inference due to its retrospective nature and depends on data accuracy recorded in medical charts, and some important factors, such as lipid profile, inflammatory markers, diet, and physical activity, were not available in records. In addition, variation in the timing of HbA1c measurement over the 10-year period could have introduced measurement bias. The frequency of HbA1c testing differed between patients, reducing the temporal resolution of longitudinal trajectories. In addition, some important time-varying covariates such as changes in antihypertensive or antidiabetic medications, treatment intensification, and longitudinal blood pressure trajectories were not available in the medical records. The absence of these variables may have introduced residual confounding and influenced the estimated associations.

Conclusion and recommendation

In this study, the mean glycated hemoglobin was high when compared to the American Diabetes Association. This study revealed that injection medication, obesity, presence of nephropathy, higher weight, family history of diabetes, uncontrolled hypertension, and > = 6 years treatment duration were significant predictors of changes in HbA1c. Health professionals strengthen patient-centered education emphasizing weight control, screen and monitor patients with complications and comorbidities, including family diabetes, prioritize patients with high baseline HbA1c and rapidly rising HbA1c, and incorporate Hb1c trajectory analysis into the routine system for risk assessment and glycemic control.
