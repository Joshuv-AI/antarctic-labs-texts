Protocol for designing and evaluating antibody-drug conjugates to promote endocytosis and lysosomal trafficking
Authors: Xinlei Zhuang, Li Lin, Liqiang Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryReceptor endocytosis and lysosomal delivery are critical for efficient intracellular delivery of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), yet many cancer targets exhibit limited internalization. Here, we present a protocol for engineering and evaluating ADCs to induce receptor ubiquitination, thereby promoting receptor uptake and transport toward lysosomal compartments. We describe steps for designing antibodies fused with E3 ligase-binding modules, drug conjugation, and cellular assays to quantify ubiquitination-dependent internalization and lysosomal accumulation in cancer cell models.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Zhuang et al.1

Before you begin

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as a promising class of targeted therapeutics that combine the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potency of cytotoxic payloads.2,3 However, their efficacy remains limited by inefficient receptor uptake and delivery to lysosomal compartments, which restrict payload liberation inside cells and consequently attenuate antitumor activity.4,5 Many tumor-associated antigen, including EGFR and HER2, exhibit slow endocytosis or recycling to the plasma membrane, resulting in suboptimal payload delivery and reduced therapeutic index.6,7

Recently, our group reported a receptor-ubiquitination-targeting ADCs (ubitaADCs) platform designed to utilize membrane E3 ligase–dependent ubiquitination for promoting receptor uptake and trafficking toward lysosomes (Figure 1A).1 By fusing a modified RSPO2 FU module capable of interacting with the transmembrane E3 ligases RNF43 and ZNRF3 to a receptor-targeting antibody (Figure 1B), ubitaADCs promote rapid ubiquitination of the target receptor. This ubiquitination serves as the initiating signal for receptor endocytosis, thereby accelerating intracellular trafficking toward lysosomes and enhancing intracellular payload delivery. Using EGFR-ubitaADC as a prototype, we demonstrated that ubiquitination-driven receptor trafficking results in enhanced internalization, lysosomal delivery, receptor degradation, and improved tumor suppression compared with conventional ADCs. As with conventional EGFR-targeting antibodies and ADCs, the primary tumor selectivity of EGFR-ubitaADC is determined by EGFR expression. In addition, the ubitaADC platform requires recruitment of the membrane-associated E3 ubiquitin ligases RNF43 and ZNRF3, which are highly expressed in many colorectal cancers. Thus, efficient receptor ubiquitination and lysosomal trafficking depend on the presence of both EGFR and RNF43/ZNRF3, providing an additional level of tumor selectivity. In this protocol, we outline the detailed procedures for designing, producing, and evaluating EGFR-ubitaADC, including the construction of the EGFR-ubiquitination-targeting antibody (EGFR-ubitaAb), site-specific chemoenzymatic conjugation, and functional assays for EGFR-ubitaADC-induced EGFR ubiquitination and degradation, EGFR-ubitaADC internalization, lysosomal trafficking, as well as in vitro and in vivo efficacy. This protocol provides a generalizable framework for applying ubiquitination-driven trafficking control to optimize ADC performance and other receptor-targeted biologics.

Innovation

The ubitaADCs introduce a mechanistic and design innovation that rewires receptor fate through engineered ubiquitination. By co-targeting tumor-associated receptors and membrane-tethered E3 ligases, these conjugates actively trigger receptor ubiquitination and redirect receptor trafficking toward lysosomal degradation. This built-in mechanism enhances payload delivery into lysosome-rich degradative compartments, even for receptors such as EGFR that otherwise favor recycling, thereby providing a generalizable and directional strategy to potentiate cytotoxic payload release within tumor cells. Beyond a single target, ubitaADCs establish a modular and broadly applicable platform that can be readily adapted to different receptor-ligase pairs, enabling precise control of receptor internalization and degradation. We demonstrate this flexibility by constructing ubitaADCs targeting both EGFR and HER2 in combination with distinct E3 ligases (RNF43 and ZNRF3), achieving consistent improvements in lysosomal trafficking and cytotoxic efficacy across multiple cancer cell lines and xenograft models. Importantly, ubitaADCs integrate a dual-function mechanism that synergistically combines targeted receptor ubiquitination-mediated degradation with efficient cytotoxic payload delivery, allowing simultaneous silencing of oncogenic surface receptors and selective elimination of tumor cells. This unified design provides a mechanistically informed framework for optimizing ADC performance and expanding their therapeutic potential against refractory solid tumors.

Design and functional screening of EGFR-ubitaAbs with improved cellular uptake and receptor ubiquitination-dependent degradation capabilities

Timing: 7–10 days (depending on transient transfection efficiency, antibody expression yield, purification, and completion of downstream functional assays)

Timing: 7–10 days (depending on transient transfection efficiency, antibody expression yield, purification, and completion of downstream functional assays)

This section outlines the design, production, and functional evaluation of EGFR-targeting receptor-ubiquitination antibodies (EGFR-ubitaAbs) developed to engage membrane-associated E3 ubiquitin ligases and promote EGFR uptake together with ubiquitination-driven receptor degradation in colorectal cancer cells.1.Design and generation of EGFR-ubitaAbs.a.Design and cloning of EGFR-ubitaAb expression constructs (Figure 2A).i.Use cetuximab heavy-chain (HC) and light-chain (LC) sequences as the parental antibody framework.ii.For EGFR-ubitaAb-v1, attach the RSPO2 FU domain carrying the F109A mutation to the N-terminus of the HC using a flexible (Gly4Ser)3 linker.iii.For EGFR-ubitaAb-v2, connect the RSPO2 FU domain containing the F109A mutation to the N-terminus of the LC through the same flexible linker.iv.Clone all HC and LC constructs into pcDNA3.1(+) mammalian expression vectors. The amino acid sequences of the parental cetuximab heavy and light chains, the RSPO2 FU domain carrying the F109A mutation, and the engineered EGFR-ubitaAb fusion constructs are provided in the supplemental information (Table S1).Note: The RSPO2 FU domain carrying the F109A mutation preserves its ability to interact with RNF43/ZNRF3 while eliminating binding to LGR4-6 receptors.b.Transient transfection for antibody expression.i.Prepare HEK-293F or Expi293 cells in suspension culture using SMM 293-TI Expression Medium.ii.Adjust cell density to 5×106 cells/mL prior to transfection.iii.For each transfection, mix a total of 600 μg plasmid DNA (HC and LC plasmids at a 1:1 mass ratio) with SMM 293-TI Expression Medium.iv.Add 2 mL of PEI (1 mg/mL stock solution) to the DNA mixture, vortex briefly, and incubate at room temperature for 15 min to allow complex formation.v.Add the DNA-PEI complexes dropwise to the cell culture while gently swirling.vi.Culture transfected cells at 37°C with 5% CO2, shaking at 120 rpm.vii.Maintain cells in SMM 293-TI Expression Medium without medium exchange.viii.Harvest culture supernatants 3–4 days post-transfection by centrifugation at 3,000×g for 10 min to remove cells and debris.Note: HEK-293F and Expi293 cells are suspension-adapted mammalian expression cell lines that are widely used for transient antibody production because they combine high transfection efficiency with high recombinant protein yield while supporting appropriate protein folding and mammalian post-translational modifications, including glycosylation.CRITICAL: Maintain a DNA:PEI mass ratio of approximately 1:3 to ensure efficient transfection and antibody expression.c.Antibody purification and buffer exchange.i.Clarify supernatants by filtration through a 0.45 μm filter.ii.Load supernatants onto a Protein A affinity chromatography column pre-equilibrated with PBS.iii.Wash the column thoroughly with PBS to remove unbound proteins.iv.Elute bound antibodies using 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 3.0)Elute bound antibodies in a single batch using 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 3.0). Collect the eluate over approximately 2–3 column volumes into a single collection tube.v.Immediately neutralize the acidic eluate by gradually adding small aliquots of 1 M Tris-HCl while gently mixing. Monitor the pH using pH indicator paper during neutralization and continue adding Tris-HCl until the eluate reaches approximately pH 7.0, then proceed with buffer exchange.vi.Pool antibody-containing fractions and exchange into PBS using desalting columns or ultrafiltration.vii.Concentrate the antibodies to approximately 1–5 mg/mL using ultrafiltration as needed. Measure the antibody concentration using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. If necessary, continue ultrafiltration until a final concentration of 1–5 mg/mL is achieve.d.Quality control of purified antibodies.i.Determine the concentration of purified antibodies by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Calculate the total antibody yield from the measured concentration and the final sample volume.ii.Load 2–5 μg of purified antibody per lane and evaluate antibody purity and structural integrity by SDS-PAGE using a 4%–20% gradient gel under both reducing and non-reducing conditions. (Figure S2A).iii.Use the same gel composition for both reducing and non-reducing analyses to facilitate comparison of antibody integrity and confirm correct heavy- and light-chain assembly prior to downstream functional assays.2.Evaluation of EGFR binding and E3 ligase recruitment.a.Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) instrument setup and sensor chip activation.i.Conduct SPR measurements on a WeSPR One Auto system at 25°C. Alternative SPR instruments, such as Biacore platforms, may also be used provided that comparable experimental conditions are maintained, including ligand immobilization strategy, analyte concentration series, experimental temperature, and kinetic analysis using the same 1:1 Langmuir binding model.ii.Perform all binding analyses using carboxylate-functionalized sensor chips provided by WeSPR. Equivalent carboxymethylated sensor chips compatible with other SPR platforms (e.g., CM5 sensor chips) may also be used, provided that comparable ligand immobilization chemistry and immobilization levels are maintained.iii.Initiate sensor chip activation by flowing a 1:1 solution of 0.4 M EDC and 0.1 M NHS (100 μL each) across the surface at 10 μL/min.b.Ligand immobilization.i.Prepare recombinant EGFR-ECD-Fc, RNF43-ECD-Fc, or ZNRF3-ECD-Fc at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL using 10 mM MES buffer (pH 5.0).ii.Apply 100 μL of each ligand to the activated sensor chip surface to obtain an immobilization level of approximately 770 response units (RU).iii.Set up reference flow channels using the same activation and blocking procedures but without immobilizing ligand proteins.iv.Quench residual active ester groups with a casein-containing blocking solution supplied by WeSPR.CRITICAL: Maintain comparable immobilization levels across ligand surfaces to ensure accurate affinity comparison.c.Antibody binding measurements.i.Generate serially diluted solutions of EGFR-Ab (cetuximab), EGFR-ubitaAb-v1, and EGFR-ubitaAb-v2 using PBST buffer.ii.Flow antibody analytes across both ligand-coated and reference sensor surfaces at 10 μL/min.iii.Monitor and record both the association and dissociation kinetics during each analyte injection.iv.In our experiments, sensor surface regeneration was generally not required because fresh ligand-coated sensor surfaces were used when necessary. This step describes the rational conversion of the lead receptor-ubiquitination antibody identified in Major Step 1 (EGFR-ubitaAb-v2) into a site-specifically conjugated antibody-drug conjugate (EGFR-ubitaADC), and the systematic evaluation of its biochemical integrity, receptor and E3 ligase engagement, intracellular trafficking, and cytotoxic activity. Based on the superior internalization efficiency and ubiquitination-dependent EGFR degradation observed for EGFR-ubitaAb-v2, this antibody was selected as the scaffold for ADC development. Expected outcomes

This protocol enables the reproducible generation, characterization, and functional evaluation of EGFR receptor-ubiquitination-targeting antibodies (EGFR-ubitaAbs) and their corresponding antibody-drug conjugates (EGFR-ubitaADCs) (Figure 1). Successful completion of this protocol generates biochemical, cellular, and in vivo evidence showing that engagement of membrane-associated E3 ubiquitin ligases promotes EGFR internalization, trafficking to lysosomes, and antitumor efficacy.

The design and functional evaluation of EGFR receptor-ubiquitination antibodies generate EGFR-ubitaAbs that retain strong binding affinity toward EGFR while gaining the capacity to recruit the membrane-associated E3 ubiquitin ligases RNF43 and/or ZNRF3. Surface plasmon resonance analyses typically show preserved EGFR binding together with newly introduced interactions with RNF43-ECD-Fc and ZNRF3-ECD-Fc, whereas the parental EGFR antibody binds EGFR alone. In EGFR-expressing colorectal cancer cells, EGFR-ubitaAbs display accelerated internalization kinetics and enhanced EGFR downregulation relative to conventional EGFR antibodies. Immunoblot analysis further reveals ubiquitination-dependent receptor degradation, enabling identification of a lead EGFR-ubitaAb variant with superior intracellular trafficking properties (Figures 2 and S2).

Engineering EGFR-ubitaADCs to promote receptor ubiquitination–mediated internalization, lysosomal transport, and tumor cell cytotoxicity enables the generation of homogeneous EGFR-ubitaADC products with defined drug-to-antibody ratios through site-specific chemoenzymatic conjugation. SDS-PAGE and HIC-HPLC analyses confirm conjugation integrity and narrow DAR distributions. Compared with conventional EGFR-ADCs, EGFR-ubitaADC shows increased cellular uptake and more efficient lysosomal delivery in colorectal cancer cells. Confocal microscopy demonstrates stronger colocalization of EGFR-ubitaADC with lysosomal markers and elevated ubiquitin signals associated with EGFR-positive compartments. These trafficking differences are accompanied by enhanced cytotoxic activity in cell viability assays (Figures 3 and S3).

The dependence of EGFR-ubitaADC–induced endocytosis and lysosomal trafficking on E3 ubiquitin ligase activity is investigated using a simplified HEK-293T model stably expressing EGFR-eGFP. Without ectopic E3 ligase expression, both EGFR-ADC and EGFR-ubitaADC are predominantly localized at the cell surface. Following ZNRF3 overexpression, EGFR-ubitaADC exhibits pronounced cellular uptake and enrichment within LAMP-1–positive lysosomal compartments, whereas the conventional EGFR-ADC displays only limited intracellular trafficking. Inhibition of ubiquitin activation using pharmacological agents or disruption of the ZNRF3 RING domain through genetic modification eliminates this phenotype, indicating that the catalytic activity of the E3 ligase is essential for intracellular trafficking of EGFR-ubitaADC (Figure 4).

The requirement of receptor ubiquitination for EGFR-ubitaADC activity, as well as its ability to maintain antitumor efficacy despite partial target depletion, is confirmed through complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments. Inhibition of ubiquitin activation or competitive blockade of RNF43/ZNRF3 engagement markedly attenuates EGFR-ubitaADC cytotoxicity in colorectal cancer cells. Despite inducing partial depletion of surface EGFR upon prolonged exposure, EGFR-ubitaADC retains robust killing activity, indicating resilience to target self-consumption. In HT29 xenograft models, EGFR-ubitaADC treatment results in superior tumor growth inhibition compared with conventional EGFR-ADCs, without significant body weight loss. Analysis of tumor lysates reveals enhanced EGFR downregulation without changes in E3 ligase expression, supporting ubiquitin-mediated receptor degradation as the dominant mechanism in vivo (Figure 5).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.4. Data are expressed as mean ± s.d. or mean ± s.e.m., as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Analyses of EGFR internalization in HT29 cells, including time-dependent studies and comparisons among distinct EGFR-ubitaAb constructs, were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests based on data collected from three independent biological experiments. For animal experiments, analyses of tumor growth and body weight were carried out as described in the relevant figure legends. Body weight measurements are presented as mean ± s.e.m. and were statistically evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Tumor volume data from xenograft models (n = 5 mice per treatment group) are shown as mean ± s.e.m., and differences between groups at the indicated time points were assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Unless otherwise noted, all experiments were independently reproduced at least three times with consistent outcomes. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, whereas P > 0.05 was considered not significant (ns). Detailed information regarding exact P values, sample numbers, and statistical methods is provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Limitations

This protocol is designed to interrogate receptor ubiquitination-driven endocytosis and lysosomal trafficking using EGFR and the membrane-bound E3 ligases RNF43/ZNRF3 as a defined model system. Its robustness therefore depends on adequate expression, membrane localization, and catalytic activity of these ligases, and the magnitude of EGFR-ubitaADC-mediated effects may vary across cell types or tumor models with distinct endocytic landscapes. In addition, mechanistic dissection relies in part on engineered HEK-293T systems and pharmacological inhibition of ubiquitin activation, which enable controlled analysis but may not fully capture the complexity of endogenous receptor regulation or avoid broader effects on cellular ubiquitin homeostasis. Finally, in vivo validation is performed in immunodeficient xenograft models, and factors such as tumor microenvironment, vascularization, and immune interactions may influence ADC trafficking and efficacy beyond what is observed in vitro, warranting further evaluation in additional disease-relevant settings.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low yield or poor integrity of EGFR-ubitaAbs after transient expression. Related to: Step 1 (Engineering and production of EGFR-ubitaAbs).

Potential solution

Low antibody yield is most commonly caused by suboptimal transfection efficiency or incorrect heavy- and light-chain stoichiometry. Ensure that HEK-293F or Expi293 cells are in logarithmic growth phase and that cell viability exceeds 95% prior to transfection. Strictly maintain the recommended DNA:PEI mass ratio and confirm that heavy- and light-chain plasmids are mixed at an equimolar ratio. If antibody degradation is observed, reduce the culture duration to 72 h and include protease inhibitors during purification. Alternative mammalian expression systems (e.g., ExpiCHO) may be used, provided that comparable antibody folding and glycosylation are achieved.

Problem 2

EGFR-ubitaAbs show reduced EGFR binding affinity compared to parental cetuximab. Related to: Step 2 (Evaluation of EGFR binding and E3 ligase recruitment).

Potential solution

Reduced EGFR binding may arise from steric interference caused by improper positioning of the RSPO2 FU domain. Verify correct fusion orientation and linker length by sequencing and SDS-PAGE. In SPR experiments, confirm that EGFR-ECD-Fc is immobilized at comparable response units across channels and that ligand integrity is preserved during regeneration. If necessary, reduce immobilization density or perform single-cycle kinetics to minimize avidity-related artifacts.

Problem 3

Minimal differences in internalization rates between EGFR-Ab and EGFR-ubitaAbs in flow cytometry assays. Related to: Step 3 (Flow cytometry-based antibody internalization assay).

Potential solution

Insufficient temperature control during surface binding is a frequent cause of high background internalization. Ensure that all binding and labeling steps are performed strictly at 4°C and that pre-chilled buffers are used. Confirm that secondary antibody labeling is restricted to surface-retained antibodies and that fluorescence signals are normalized to the 0 h time point. If internalization remains weak, extend the internalization window or increase antibody concentration within the non-saturating range.

Problem 4

EGFR degradation is not detectable by immunoblotting despite prolonged antibody treatment. Related to: Step 4 (Immunoblot-based analysis of EGFR degradation).

Potential solution

Failure to detect EGFR degradation may result from insufficient antibody concentration, incomplete receptor internalization, or suboptimal lysis conditions. Verify that cells reach appropriate confluency and that treatment duration is sufficient to allow lysosomal degradation. Use freshly prepared RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and ensure equal protein loading across samples. Increasing gel resolution or extending exposure times during chemiluminescence detection may improve sensitivity.

Problem 5

Low or heterogeneous drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) after chemoenzymatic conjugation. Related to: Step 5 and Step 6 (Site-specific conjugation and ADC characterization).

Potential solution

Incomplete glycan trimming or inefficient GalNAz installation can compromise subsequent payload conjugation. Confirm Endo S activity using SDS-PAGE mobility shifts and ensure that GalT(Y289L) and UDP-GalNAz are freshly prepared. Maintain reaction temperatures and incubation times precisely as specified. While alternative payloads or click chemistries may be compatible, modification of the Fc glycan site or reaction sequence is not recommended, as this may alter ADC homogeneity.

Problem 6

EGFR-ubitaADC fails to show enhanced lysosomal trafficking in E3 ligase-dependent models. Related to: Steps 9–12 (E3 ligase-dependent trafficking analyses).

Potential solution

Insufficient E3 ligase activity or loss of inhibitor efficacy may obscure ubiquitination-dependent effects. Confirm expression and membrane localization of ZNRF3 or ZNRF3 ΔRING constructs prior to imaging. For MLN7243 experiments, ensure continuous presence of the inhibitor throughout the internalization period and verify that cell viability is not compromised. Imaging parameters should be kept constant across conditions to allow accurate comparison of lysosomal colocalization.
