Tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity: case report of a rare special type of breast cancer and review of the literature
Authors: Haijuan Wang, Zhengyang Li, Jingjing Na, Wenkai Ma, Yu Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tall cell carcinoma of the breast with reversed polarity (TCCRP) is a rare and diagnostically challenging subtype of triple-negative invasive breast carcinoma. Herein, we report a case of a 60-year-old female presenting with a two-year history of a left breast mass that exhibited progressive enlargement. Histopathological examination of the excised tumor revealed distinctive morphological features, including expansive nests of neoplastic cells with sharply demarcated borders and a spectrum of architectural patterns encompassing solid, follicular, and papillary configurations. Notably, papillary structures contained delicate fibrovascular cores infiltrated by foamy macrophages. The tumor cells demonstrated characteristic tall columnar to cuboidal morphology with nuclei displaying reversed polarity (basal-to-luminal nuclear polarization). Immunophenotypic analysis revealed a triple-negative profile (ER −/PR −/HER2 −) with co-expression of CK5/6 and GATA-3, while P63 staining confirmed the absence of myoepithelial differentiation. Molecular characterization identified an IDH2 p.R172S hotspot mutation, further corroborating the diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of integrating histomorphological features, immunohistochemical profiles, and molecular biomarkers to distinguish TCCRP from other triple-negative breast carcinomas. Recognition of this indolent entity is critical for avoiding overtreatment and ensuring appropriate clinical management, given its favorable prognosis compared to conventional triple-negative breast cancers.

Background

Tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity (TCCRP), initially characterized by Eusebi et al. in 2003 [1], has been formally recognized in the 2019 WHO Classification of Tumours of the Breast as a distinct histological subtype of invasive breast carcinoma [2]. While its histomorphological features—such as tall columnar cells with basal-to-luminal nuclear polarization—mirror those of the tall cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, TCCRP is distinguished by unique molecular and immunophenotypic profiles. Notably, TCCRP lacks thyroid-specific alterations (e.g., BRAFmutations or RET rearrangements) but demonstrates recurrent IDH2 p.R172 hotspot mutations, a molecular signature absent in conventional thyroid neoplasms [3].The diagnostic complexity of TCCRP arises from its histological mimicry of both benign and malignant breast lesions, including florid epithelial hyperplasia in intraductal papilloma, solid papillary carcinoma, and encapsulated papillary carcinoma. This overlap frequently leads to misinterpretation, particularly on core needle biopsy specimens. Despite sharing a triple-negative phenotype (estrogen receptor ER-, progesterone receptor PR-, HER2-) with aggressive triple-negative breast carcinomas (TNBCs), TCCRP exhibits markedly indolent behavior, emphasizing accurate diagnosis to guide therapeutic decision-making [4].

Case presentation

A 60-year-old woman presented with a gradually enlarging left breast mass first noted two years prior. No significant medical history or familial cancer predisposition was documented. Breast ultrasound identified a hypoechoic nodule (BI-RADS 4a) alongside bilateral lobular hyperplasia. Subsequent MRI revealed a mass-like enhancing lesion (BI-RADS 5), prompting surgical excision with sentinel lymph node biopsy. Histopathological evaluation confirmed a diagnosis of TCCRP. The tumor measured 1.5 cm in greatest dimension, with no lymphovascular invasion or nodal involvement (0/8 sentinel nodes). Microscopic analysis demonstrated well-circumscribed tumor nests exhibiting expansive growth within a hyalinized stroma (Fig. 1A). Architectural diversity was evident, including solid sheets, follicular structures, and papillary formations with delicate fibrovascular cores containing clusters of foamy macrophages (Fig. 1B, C). Focal cystic spaces contained eosinophilic colloid-like material, reminiscent of thyroid follicular secretions (Fig. 1D). Neoplastic cells displayed uniform cytomorphology: abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, round-to-oval nuclei with minimal pleomorphism, and rare mitotic activity. Tall columnar cells were arranged in palisading patterns, characterized by reversed nuclear polarity (basal-to-luminal nuclear orientation) (Fig. 1E, F). Discussion

Tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity (TCCRP) represents a rare and morphologically distinct subtype of invasive breast carcinoma. We conducted a retrospective analysis and summary of the clinical and pathological characteristics of 30 TCCRP cases reported in the literature over the past decade. Statistical analysis revealed an age range at diagnosis of 40–79 years (mean: 62.8 years) and a Tumor size range of 0.8–8.5 cm (mean: 1.7 cm)[5–15]. This average age aligns with the previously reported mean of 60 years [4]. Furthermore, TCCRP typically lacks distinctive clinical features and is often detected incidentally during screening examinations [5].

Notably, TCCRP demonstrates striking morphological overlap with the tall cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, sharing features such as nuclear grooves, pseudoinclusions, and colloid-like secretions. However, its mammary origin is confirmed by GATA3 positivity and absence of thyroid-specific markers (TTF −1 −/PAX8 −) [2]. While classically indolent, rare cases exhibit lymphovascular invasion or nodal metastasis [5], underscoring the need for thorough sampling and staging. The near-universal absence of necrosis and minimal proliferative activity (Ki-67 < 20% in most cases) further distinguishes TCCRP from aggressive triple-negative breast cancers.

In routine clinical practice, the pathological diagnosis of TCCRP poses significant challenges. While core needle biopsies and intraoperative frozen section analyses are standard methods for evaluating breast lesions, the identification of reversed nuclear polarity in TCCRP can be diagnostically elusive. Tumor cells exhibit a deceptively bland morphology, lacking the uniform low-grade nuclear features or overt high-grade atypia characteristic of most invasive breast carcinomas. The coexistence of solid, papillary, and nested architectural patterns creates a heterogeneous cellular landscape that may mimic non-clonal proliferations, particularly in limited biopsy specimens. The defining histopathological hallmark—basal-to-luminal nuclear polarization (characterized by nuclear displacement away from the basement membrane)—serves as a critical diagnostic clue. This feature is optimally visualized at the tumor-stroma interface, where columnar cells demonstrate perpendicular alignment to stromal boundaries. Stromal hyalinization, though nonspecific, provides additional contextual support when observed in conjunction with architectural diversity. Pathologists must maintain a high threshold of suspicion for TCCRP in triple-negative lesions with indolent cytomorphology, as diagnostic oversight may lead to misinterpretation as benign hyperplasia or reactive changes.

Immunohistochemical analysis holds critical value in the diagnostic workup of TCCRP. Current evidence from published cases, including our analysis, demonstrates a characteristic immunoprofile: ER −, PR −, and HER2 − negativity (triple-negative immunophenotype), often accompanied by diffuse CK5/6 positivity and variable expression of GATA3 and GCDFP-15. While weak focal ER/PR reactivity may occasionally occur, the absence of thyroid lineage markers (TTF-1/NKX2-1 −, thyroglobulin −) reliably excludes metastatic thyroid carcinoma. Calretinin expression—whether diffuse or focal—should be interpreted cautiously given its lack of specificity. The neoplastic cells typically exhibit low proliferative activity (Ki-67 index < 20%) and show no peripheral myoepithelial cell layer (p63 −, SMA −), confirming invasive growth. Our case aligns with prior reports [5, 16], displaying concordant CK5/6 and GATA3 positivity with retained mammary differentiation markers. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating immunohistochemical patterns with histological features to distinguish TCCRP from mimics such as metastatic papillary thyroid carcinoma or triple-negative ductal carcinomas.

The presence of IDH2 R172 hotspot mutations represents a molecular hallmark of TCCRP [2, 5, 17]. Absence of RETrearrangements and BRAF mutations distinguishes TCCRP from the tall cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, confirming their biological independence [16]. Genomic analyses by Chiang et al. on 13 histologically confirmed TCCRP cases (2005–2014) revealed IDH2 R172 mutations in 77% (10/13) of tumors, with concurrent PIK3CA pathway alterations in 62% (8/13) [2]. Our meta-analysis of 17 TCCRP cases reported between 2015 and 2024 demonstrates similar molecular patterns: excluding 2 cases with undetermined genetic status and 1 BRAF-negative case, 85.7% (12/14) harbored IDH2 R172 mutations (84% concordance with Elghobashy et al. [3]). Subtype distribution among mutated cases included R172S (4/12), R172G (3/12), R172W (1/12), R172T (1/12), and unspecified R172 variants (3/12).

The high prevalence of IDH2 R172 mutations (> 80% across studies) strongly implicates these alterations as oncogenic drivers underlying TCCRP’s characteristic reversed polarity phenotype [2]. This mutation exhibits diagnostic specificity for TCCRP when integrated with clinicopathological features, while simultaneously presenting a potential therapeutic target. Notably, IDH2 mutations are recurrent in diverse malignancies—including gliomas, acute myeloid leukemia (AML), angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma, and chondrosarcoma—where they induce neomorphic production of the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), disrupting cellular differentiation and promoting tumorigenesis [18]. Early-phase clinical trials demonstrate that IDH2 inhibitors (e.g., enasidenib/AG-221, ivosidenib/AG-120) effectively suppress 2-HG levels and show efficacy in hematologic malignancies [2, 18].

This structured approach integrates histomorphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular profiling to resolve diagnostic ambiguities [20, 19].

TCCRP is associated with a favorable prognosis due to its indolent clinical course. Despite exhibiting a triple-negative phenotype, cases with lymph node or distant metastases are exceedingly rare. Immunohistochemistry for IDH2 has been reported as a highly sensitive diagnostic marker, detecting 93% of molecularly confirmed cases; however, molecular testing remains the more specific assay to confirm the presence of characteristic IDH2 R172 hotspot mutations [5].

Conclusion

This study presents a confirmed case of the rare tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity (TCCRP) and synthesizes clinicopathological insights through a comprehensive literature review. Our analysis delineates the diagnostic hallmarks of TCCRP, providing actionable criteria to aid pathological recognition and clinical management. Given its rarity, pathologists may overlook TCCRP’s defining features—most notably the basal-to-luminal nuclear polarization in papillary architectures—leading to diagnostic misinterpretation. For morphologically suspicious cases exhibiting triple-negative immunophenotype (ER −/PR −/HER2 −), targeted molecular testing for IDH2 R172 hotspot mutations is imperative to confirm diagnosis.

Accurate identification of TCCRP carries critical therapeutic implications: unlike conventional triple-negative breast carcinomas, TCCRP demonstrates indolent behavior with favorable prognosis, potentially obviating the need for neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy [15]. Future studies should employ advanced molecular techniques to elucidate the oncogenic mechanisms underlying TCCRP’s unique polarity defects and explore targeted therapeutic strategies against IDH2-mutant pathways.
