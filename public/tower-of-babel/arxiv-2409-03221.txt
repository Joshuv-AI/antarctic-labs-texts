Abstract
. The critical length phenomenon of the Korteweg-de Vries equation is well known, however, in higher dimensions, it is unknown. This work explores this property in the context of the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili equation, a two-dimensional generalization of the Korteweg-de Vries equation. Specifically, we demonstrate observability inequalities for this equation, which allow us to deduce the exact boundary controllability and boundary exponential stabilization of the linear system, provided the spatial domain length avoids certain specific values, which is a direct consequence of the Paley-Wiener theorem. To the best of our knowledge, our work introduces new results by identifying a set of critical lengths for the two-dimensional Kadomtsev-Petviashvili equation.

1.
Introduction

The renowned Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation

u_{t}+uu_{x}+u_{xxx}=0

was first derived by Boussinesq
[
2
]
and Korteweg-de Vries
[
21
]
to model the unidirectional propagation of small amplitude, long wavelength water waves in shallow canals. This equation encapsulates the interplay between nonlinear and dispersive effects, which are crucial for the formation and stability of solitary waves, often referred to as solitons. The KdV equation is essential in the study of nonlinear dispersive wave systems, offering profound insights into wave dynamics in one spatial dimension.

In scenarios where wave propagation extends to two spatial dimensions, phenomena characterized by weak transverse effects and weak nonlinearity are introduced by Kadomtsev and Petviashvili
[
22
]
, giving the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation:

(1.1)

\left(u_{t}+uu_{x}+au_{xxx}\right)_{x}+bu_{yy}=0,

where
u=u(x,y,t)
is the wave profile, and
a
and
b
are real constants. The KP equation generalizes the KdV equation to account for the transverse spatial variable
y
, incorporating weak transverse perturbations into the dynamics. It arises naturally in systems where the longitudinal nonlinear and dispersive effects dominate, while transverse variations introduce secondary corrections.

Notice that, through suitable scaling transformations, the coefficients
a
and
b
can be normalized. Specifically, by applying the transformations
x\mapsto a^{1/4}x
and
y\mapsto|b|^{1/2}y
, the equation simplifies to a form with
a=1
and
b^{2}=1
. Consequently, we consider the rescaled and simplified version of (
1.1
) for further analysis:

u_{t}+uu_{x}+u_{xxx}+b\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{yy}=0,\quad b=\pm 1.

The parameter
b
determines the type of KP equation: when
b=-1
, the equation is referred to as KP-I, which is relevant for situations where surface tension effects are dominant, leading to a focusing regime. Conversely, when
b=1
, it is called KP-II, representing a defocusing regime often associated with gravity-dominated systems. These two cases exhibit distinct mathematical and physical wave behaviors.

1.1.
Problem setting

Numerous aspects of the KP equation have been thoroughly examined, such as its well-posedness, stability of solitary waves, its integrability, etc (see
[

20

The controllability problem involves determining whether it is possible to “manipulate” the solution at specific points in space and time to transition the system from an initial to a final state. Moreover, controllability and the asymptotic behavior of solutions are closely related: selecting an appropriate control mechanism can achieve exponential stability of the solutions. In this context, controllability and stabilization problems for one-dimensional dispersive equations and systems, such as KdV, Kawahara, and Boussinesq equations, posed on various domains have been successfully addressed using different techniques (e.g
[
33
,
16
,
15
,
9
]
and references therein). Specifically, the internal controllability of the KP-II equation on a periodic domain was studied in
[
32
]
, and the asymptotic behavior of solutions for the KP-II and their fifth-order generalization, called Kawahara-Kadomtsev-Petviashvili-II equation, or shorter, K-KP-II equations with internal feedback mechanisms on bounded domains was examined in
[
18
,
29
]
, respectively. Additionally, the exponential stabilization of the K-KP-II equation with internal damping and time-delayed feedback was investigated in
[
5
]

Two important facts need to be spotlighted:

(1)

Due to the regularity of the solutions for the KP-II equation and the dimension of the domain, the Sobolev embeddings employed in the one-dimensional cases cannot be used to address the well-posedness for the nonlinear case, precisely as
H^{1}(\Omega)
is not embedding in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the fixed point argument does not hold. Therefore, we deal with the linear version of the KP-II.

(2)

The term
\partial_{x}u
, called drift, could modify the behavior of the operator in a bounded rectangular domain. Switching to traveling coordinates is not applicable in the bounded domain problem without incurring a real cost. By changing variables to
u(x,t)=u(x+t,t)
, we can eliminate the problematic term in the evolution equation. However, the boundary condition is now applied at a varying spatial point, and the problem is defined in the unusual domain
\{(x,t)\colon t\geq 0,x+t\geq 0,L\geq x\}
instead of a traditional rectangular domain.

Let us consider the linear KP-II within a rectangular domain
\Omega:=(0,L)\times(0,L)
,

u_{t}+u_{x}+u_{xxx}+\partial_{x}^{-1}(u_{yy})=0,\quad(x,y)\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T),

\begin{cases}u(0,y,t)=u(L,y,t)=0,\quad u_{x}(L,y,t)=h(y,t),&y\in(0,L),\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,0,t)=u(x,L,t)=0,&x\in(0,L),\ t\in(0,T),\\

The study of controllability typically involves finding appropriate control functions that act on the system, and the choice of these controls can depend on the specific structure of the equation. Therefore, the next question related to the exact control arises:

Problem A:
Given an initial state

A feedback process is one in which the system state determines the way the control has to be exerted at any time. Therefore, the notions of control and stability are extremely related and play an important role in applications. In this framework, we say that the control is given by a feedback law, then the natural issue appears.

Problem B:

If this is the case, can we give the decay rate?

To our knowledge, the boundary control properties of the KP-II equation posed on a rectangle are completely open. This paper aims to investigate boundary observability and, consequently, obtain exact boundary controllability and the asymptotic behavior of the solutions with one control.

In real-world applications, achieving control or stabilization with a minimal number of inputs is often desirable. In our case, we impose the boundary conditions (

\begin{cases}u_{t}+u_{xxx}+u_{x}=0,&(x,t)\in(0,L)\times(0,T),\\

(1.7)

\mathcal{N}:=\left\{\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{k^{2}+kl+l^{2}};\quad k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\right\}.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}[u_{x}^{2}(L,y)-u_{x}^{2}(0,y)]\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}[f^{2}(L,y)-f^{2}(0,y)]\,dy.

Therefore, we have that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y)dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y))^{2}\,dy\leq 0.

Similarly, let
\theta\in D(A^{\ast})
and
g_{x}(x,y)=\theta_{y}(x,y)
with
g(0,y)=0
. Then

\displaystyle\left\langle\theta,A^{\ast}\theta\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}[\theta_{x}^{2}(L,y)-\theta_{x}^{2}(0,y)]\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(g^{2}(x,y))_{x}\,dx\,dy.

From (
2.4
), it yields that

\left\langle\theta,A^{\ast}\theta\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\theta_{x}^{2}(L,y)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left((\partial_{x}^{-1})^{\ast}\theta_{y}(L,y)\right)^{2}\,dy\leq 0.

Thus, from
[
30
, Corollary 4.4, page 15]
, the proposition holds.
∎

Now, we can state the following theorem regarding the existence of solutions to the Cauchy problem.

Theorem 2.3
.

Then, for each initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exists a unique mild solution
u\in C\left([0,\infty),L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
for the system (
2.1
). Moreover, if the initial data
u_{0}\in D(A)
the solution
u
belongs to

C\left([0,\infty);D(A)\right)\cap C^{1}\left([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega)\right).

Proof.

From Proposition
2.2
, it follows that
A
generates a strongly continuous semigroup of
contractions
\{S(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
(see
[
30
, Corollary 1.4.4]
).
∎

The next proposition provides valuable estimates for the mild solutions of the equation (
2.1
), including the energy estimate, Kato smoothing effect, and the existence of the traces.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then the map
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mapsto u\in\mathcal{B}_{H^{1}}
is well-defined, continuous and satisfies

(2.6)

\|u(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

and

(2.7)

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C(T,L)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

for some positive constant
C:=C(T,L)=\frac{2\left(2T+L\right)}{3}>0
.
Moreover, if the initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we get the following trace estimate

(2.8)

\|u_{x}(0,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,L))}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

and the estimate

(2.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt.

Proof.

Observe that (
2.5
) implies that

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy=\int_{\Omega}u(t)\left(-u_{x}(t)-u_{xxx}(t)-{\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{yy}(t)\right)\,dx\,dy=\left\langle Au(t),u(t)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 0.

Integrating in
[0,s]
, for
0\leq s\leq T
we get (
2.6
).

To see that
u\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))
we multiply the equation (
2.1
) by
xu
. Integrating by parts in
\Omega\times(0,T)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle u_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{T}{2}+L\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\|u(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\|u_{x}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dt\leq\frac{2\left(2T+L\right)}{3}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

giving (
2.7
). On the other hand, for the trace estimate, we multiply the equation (
2.1
) by
u
and integrate by parts in
\Omega\times(0,T)
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy.

\displaystyle\|u_{x}(0,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,L))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{x}(0,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)\times(0,L)}^{2}+\left\|{\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,\cdot,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T)\times(0,L)}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

Finally, multiplying the equation (
2.1
) by
(T-t)u
and integrating by parts in

yields that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt,

showing (
2.9

∎

2.2.
Adjoint system

We consider the following time-backward homogeneous problem

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\theta_{t}+\theta_{x}+\theta_{xxx}+\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}\right)^{\ast}\theta_{yy}=0,&(x,y)\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T),\\

Note that, by the variable change
t\mapsto T-t
the problem (

(2.11)

\begin{cases}\theta_{t}-\theta_{x}-\theta_{xxx}-\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}\right)^{\ast}\theta_{yy}=0,&(x,y)\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T),\\

That is equivalent to abstract Cauchy problem
\theta_{t}=A^{\ast}\theta
with initial data

, where
A^{\ast}
is defined by (
2.3
) with dense domain (
2.4
), by using Lemma
2.1
we can stablish the well-posedness for the problem (
2.11
) and consequently the well-posedness for (

) yields. Precisely, we get the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Then, for each initial data
\theta_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exists a unique mild solution
\theta\in C\left([0,\infty),L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
for the problem (
2.11
). Moreover, if the initial data
\theta_{0}\in D(A^{\ast})
the solutions are classical such that

\theta\in C\left([0,\infty),D(A^{\ast})\right)\cap C^{1}\left([0,\infty),L^{2}(\Omega)\right).

2.3.
KP-II equation with feedback

Let us first pay attention to the existence and regularity issues for the solutions for (
1.2
) with a feedback mechanism given by (
1.10
). Gathering this information, we get the system

(2.12)

\begin{cases}u_{t}+u_{x}+u_{xxx}+\partial_{x}^{-1}(u_{yy})=0,&(x,y)\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(0,y,t)=u(L,y,t)=0,&y\in(0,L),\ t\in(0,T)\\
u(x,0,t)=u(x,L,t)=0,\quad u_{x}(L,y,t)=-\alpha u_{x}(0,y,t),&y\in(0,L),\ t\in(0,T)\\

We introduce the operator
\tilde{A}:D(\tilde{A})\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
defined by

\tilde{A}u:=-u_{x}-u_{xxx}-{\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{yy}

on the dense domain given by

D(\tilde{A}):=\left\{u\in H_{x}^{3}(\Omega)\cap X^{2}(\Omega)\left\lvert\begin{aligned} &\ u(0,y)=u(L,y)=u(x,0)=u(x,L)=0\\
&\ u_{x}(L,y)=-\alpha u_{x}(0,y),\ 0<|\alpha|\leq 1\end{aligned}\right.\right\}.

By analogous arguments as in Lemma
2.1
we get that
\tilde{A}
is closed and the adjoint
\tilde{A}^{\ast}:D(\tilde{A}^{\ast})\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
by

\tilde{A}^{\ast}v=v_{x}+v_{xxx}+\left(\partial_{x}^{-1}\right)^{\ast}v_{yy}

with dense domain

D(\tilde{A}^{\ast}):=\left\{v\in H_{x}^{3}(\Omega)\cap X^{2}(\Omega)\left\lvert\begin{aligned} &\ v(0,y)=v(L,y)=v(x,0)=v(x,L)=0\\
&\ v_{x}(0,y)=\alpha v_{x}(L,y),\ 0<|\alpha|\leq 1\end{aligned}\right.\right\}.

Proposition 2.6
.

The operator
\tilde{A}
is the infinitesimal generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Similarly as the proof of the Proposition
2.2
, we consider
u\in D(\tilde{A})
and then

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{A}u,u\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=-\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})}{2}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y))^{2}\,dy\leq 0.

Similarly, let
v\in D(\tilde{A}^{\ast})
therefore

\displaystyle\left\langle v,\tilde{A}^{\ast}v\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=-\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})}{2}\int_{0}^{L}v_{x}^{2}(L,y)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(({\partial_{x}^{-1}})^{\ast}v_{y}(L,y)\right)^{2}\,dy\leq 0.

Thus, from
[
30
, Corollary 4.4, page 15]
, the proposition holds.
∎

Therefore, we are in a position to present the subsequent theorem concerning the existence of solutions for the Cauchy abstract problem:

Theorem 2.7
.

For the initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique solution
u\in C\left([0,\infty),L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
for the KP-II equation (
2.12
). Moreover, if the initial data
u_{0}\in D(\tilde{A})
the solutions are classical such that
u\in C\left([0,\infty),D(\tilde{A})\right)\cap C^{1}\left([0,\infty),L^{2}(\Omega)\right).

Proof.

From Proposition
2.6
, it follows that
\tilde{A}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup of
contractions
(\tilde{S}(t))_{t\geq 0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
(see
[
30
, Corollary 1.4.4]
).
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

For any mild solution of (
2.12
), the energy
E(t)
is non-increasing and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(2.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(t)

\displaystyle\leq-C\left[\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy+\int_{0}^{L}({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy\right]

where

is given by

.

Proof.

Note that

From the proof of Proposition
2.6
, we conclude (

).
∎

The following estimates for the mild solution
u(\cdot)=\tilde{S}(\cdot)(u_{0})
of the KP-II equation provide useful information for the regularity and the asymptotic behavior. The first is standard energy estimates, and the last one reveals that a slightly different Kato smoothing effect holds.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and the mild solution given by
u(\cdot)=\tilde{S(\cdot)}(u_{0})
. Then,

(2.14)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy=&\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt,\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt,\end{split}

for any
T>0
. Moreover, we have

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq C(T,L,\alpha)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where
C:=C(T,L,\alpha)=\left(\frac{4T}{3}+\frac{2L}{3(1-\alpha^{2})}\right)>0
.

Proof.

Following the same arguments that in Proposition
2.4
and taking into account the boundary conditions of the KP-II equation (
2.12
) we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy\\
=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt,

\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt\\
+\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}(T-t)\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt,

and

(2.15)

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt.

Note that, from (
2.14
),

\displaystyle(1-\alpha^{2})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha^{2})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle\leq 4E(0).

Therefore (
2.15
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu_{0}^{2}(x,y)\,dx\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L+T(1-\alpha^{2})}{2(1-\alpha^{2})}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\|u(\cdot,\cdot,t)\|_{H_{x}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dt\leq\left(T+\frac{2L+T(1-\alpha^{2})}{3(1-\alpha^{2})}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Summarizing,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{4T}{3}+\frac{2L}{3(1-\alpha^{2})}\right)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

showing the result.
∎

2.4.
Nonhomegenous system

Let us consider problem (
1.2

. Given the lack of appropriate hidden regularity results that would allow us to achieve optimal existence and uniqueness outcomes within the framework of the usual Sobolev spaces, the transposition (or duality) method yields the existence of weak solutions.

As usual, we begin with a formal computation. Fix
\theta_{0}\in D(A^{*})
arbitrarily, where

is given by (
2.4

1.2

\displaystyle L_{S}(u_{0})=-\left\langle u_{0},\theta_{0}\right\rangle_{D(A^{*})^{\prime},D(A^{*})}-\int_{0}^{S}\int_{0}^{L}h(y,t)\theta_{x}(L,y,t)\,dy\,dt.

Now, we can establish the well-posedness for the nonhomogeneous problem, which is classic (for details, we encourage to see
[
9
, Proposition 2.6]

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
T>0
,
u_{0}\in D(A^{*})^{\prime}
and

. Then there exists a unique solution

of (
1.2
)-(

3.
Boundary observations

3.1.
Observation for controllability

It is well known in control theory
[
23
,
27
]
that the exact controllability of a system is equivalent to proving an observability inequality. Taking into account the following observability will ensure the controllability of the KP-II system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
L\in(0,+\infty)\setminus\mathcal{R}
and
T>0
. Then exists
C(T,L)>0
such that
\theta_{T}(x,y)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies

(3.1)

\|\theta_{T}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left|\theta_{x}(L,y,t)\right|^{2}\,dy\,dt,

where
\theta
is the solution of (
2.10
) with initial data
\theta_{T}(x,y)
.

Proof.

To prove this, first, making the change of variables
t\mapsto T-t
,
x\mapsto L-x
, transform (
2.10
) into the homogeneous problem (
2.1
). Then, (
3.1
) is equivalent to

(3.2)

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left|u_{x}(0,y,t)\right|^{2}\,dy\,dt.

To establish the observability inequality (
3.2
) we proceed in several steps.

First step.
By contradiction, assume that (
3.2
) does not hold. Then, there exists a sequence
\left(u_{0}^{n}\right)_{n}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

(3.3)

1=\left\|u_{0}^{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}>n\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left(u_{x}^{n}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt,

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that (
3.3
) is equivalent to the next two assertions:
\left\|u_{0}^{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=1,
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left(u_{x}^{n}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy\,dt\longrightarrow 0,\quad\text{in }L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,L)),

where
u^{n}
is the solution of (
2.1
) with initial data
u_{0}^{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From (
2.7
) and (
3.3
) we get that
\left(u^{n}\right)_{n}\text{ is bounded in }L^{2}(0,T;H_{x}^{1}(\Omega)).
Then, the sequence defined by

u_{t}^{n}=-u_{x}^{n}-u_{xxx}^{n}-{\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{yy}^{n}

is bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega)).
Since
u^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\in H_{x}^{1}(\Omega)
, thus
u_{x}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\subset H^{-2}(\Omega)
. Moreover, thanks to the fact that
u_{x}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we get that
u_{xxx}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\in H^{-2}(\Omega)
.

Claim 1.
Note that

{\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{yy}^{n}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega)).

Indeed, we can recognize
L^{2}(\Omega)
as a pivot space once we have
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\subset H^{-2}(\Omega)
. Observe that, as
u^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\in H_{x}^{1}(\Omega)
implies that
u_{x}^{n}(\cdot,\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Now, defining a function

u(\cdot,\cdot,T)=u_{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Now, we use the adjoint system to give an equivalent condition for the Definition
2
. The condition is the following one.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u_{0},u_{T}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then there exists
h\in L^{2}\left((0,T)\times(0,L)\right)
, such that the solution
u
of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) satisfies
u(x,y,T)=u_{T}(x,y)
if and only if

(4.1)

\int_{\Omega}\theta_{T}(x,y)u(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}h(y,t)\theta_{x}(L,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{\Omega}\theta(x,y,0)u_{0}(x,y)\,dx\,dy,

for any
\theta_{T}(x,y)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\theta
being the solution of the adjoint problem (
2.10
).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Notice that the relation (
4.1
) may be seen as an optimality condition for the critical points of the functional
\Lambda(\theta_{T}):L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by

(4.2)

\Lambda(\theta_{T})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\theta_{x}(L,y,t)\right\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,L))}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}\theta_{T}u(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy,

where
\theta
is the solution of (
2.10
). A control may be obtained from the solution of the homogeneous system (
2.10
) with the initial data minimizing the functional
\Lambda
. Hence, the controllability is reduced to a minimization problem. To guarantee that
\Lambda
defined by (
4.2
) has a unique minimizer, we use the next fundamental result in the calculus of variations.

Theorem 4.2

(See
[
3
]
)
.

Let
H
be a reflexive Banach space,
K
a closed convex subset of
H
and
\Lambda:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a function with the following properties:

(1)

\Lambda
is convex and lower semi-continuous;

(2)

If
K
is unbounded then
\Lambda
is coercive. Then
\Lambda
attains its minimum in
K
, i. e. there exists
x_{0}\in K
such that

\Lambda\left(x_{0}\right)=\min_{x\in K}\varphi(x).

Note that
\Lambda
defined in (
4.2
) is continuous and convex. The existence of a minimum is ensured if we prove that
\Lambda
is also coercive, which is obtained with the
observability inequality
given by (
3.1
). Thus,

\displaystyle\Lambda(\theta_{T})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\theta_{x}(L,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,L))}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}\theta_{T}u(x,y,T)\,dx\,dy\geq\frac{C^{-1}}{2}\|\theta_{T}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, the KP-II equation (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) is exactly controllable.
∎

4.2.
Boundary stabilization

We aim to show that the KP-II equation (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) can be stabilized by selecting an appropriate feedback-damping control law. Specifically, by choosing
h(y,t)=-\alpha u_{x}(0,y,t)
with
0<|\alpha|\leq 1
, we can establish our second main result. Recall that the energy of the KP-II equation, defined by (
1.4
), is a nonincreasing function due to (
1.11
).

Proof of the Theorem
1.4
.

Through the utilization of energy dissipation, specifically (
2.13
), in conjunction with the
observability inequality
(
3.16
), we obtain the exponential stabilization. Indeed, notice that, integrating (
2.13
) over
[0,T]
, it follows that

E(T)-E(0)\leq-C\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}({\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy\,dt\right].

Thus, we have that

E(T)-E(0)\leq-\frac{1}{C}E(0).

Since the energy is dissipative, it follows that
E(T)\leq E(0)
, thus

E(T)\leq E(0)-\frac{1}{C}E(0)\leq E(0)-\frac{1}{C}E(T)\implies E(T)\leq\delta E(0),\ \text{where}\ \delta=\frac{C}{1+C}<1.

Now, applying the same argument on the interval
[(m-1)T,mT]
for
m=1,2,\ldots
, yields that

E(mT)\leq\delta E((m-1)T)\leq\cdots\leq\delta^{m}E(0).

Thus, we have

E(mT)\leq e^{-\mu_{0}mT}E(0)\quad\text{ with }\quad\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{T}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{C}\right)>0.

For an arbitrary
t>0
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
(m-1)T<t\leq mT
, and by the nonincreasing property of the energy, we conclude that

E(t)\leq E((m-1)T)\leq e^{-\mu_{0}(m-1)T}E(0)\leq\frac{1}{\delta}e^{-\mu_{0}t}E(0),

showing uniform exponential stability.
∎

4.3.
An explicit decay rate

Finally, by using Lyapunov’s approach, we can give an explicit decay rate for the solutions of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
).

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Firstly, let us consider the following Lyapunov’s functional

V(t)=E(t)+\gamma V_{1}(t),

where
\gamma
is a constant to be fixed later,
E(t)
is given by (
1.4
) and

V_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy.

Note that
E(t)
and
V(t)
are equivalent in the following sense
E(t)\leq V(t)\leq(1+\gamma L)E(t)
and observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(x,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dx\,dy.

Recalling that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(t)=-\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})}{2}\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\left(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y,t)\right)^{2}\,dy

and gathering the results holds that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[L\alpha^{2}\gamma+\alpha^{2}-1\right]\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy\\
-\frac{3}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy\,dt-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(x,y,t))^{2}\,dx\,dy.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V(t)+2\nu V(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[L\alpha^{2}\gamma+\alpha^{2}-1\right]\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(0,y,t))^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy\,dt

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(x,y,t))^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy+\frac{\nu\gamma}{2}\int_{\Omega}xu^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[L\alpha^{2}\gamma+\alpha^{2}-1\right]\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy-\frac{3}{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu(1+L\gamma)+\gamma}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy.

Consequently, applying the Poincaré inequality yields that

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V(t)+\nu V(t)\leq&\frac{1}{2}\left[L\alpha^{2}\gamma+\alpha^{2}-1\right]\int_{0}^{L}u_{x}^{2}(0,y,t)\,dy\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\left[(\nu(1+L\gamma)+\gamma)L^{2}-3\gamma\right]\int_{\Omega}u_{x}^{2}(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy.\end{split}

Follows from (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V(t)+\nu V(t)\leq 0
, then by the Gronwall’s inequality and the equivalence between
E(t)
and
V(t)
the proof is complete.
∎

5.
Final remarks

This work dealt with the KP-II equation posed on a rectangle, a bi-dimensional generalization of the KdV equation. Under certain hypotheses of the spatial length
L
, that is,
L\in(0,+\infty)\setminus\mathcal{R}
, with
\mathcal{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:=\left\{\frac{\pi}{4n}\sqrt{(3m_{1}+2m_{2}+m_{3})(m_{1}-2m_{2}-m_{3})(m_{1}+2m_{2}-m_{3})(m_{1}+2m_{2}+3m_{3})}\quad s.t.\right.

\displaystyle\left.n,m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\in\mathbb{N},\ \text{with}\ \ |m_{1}-m_{3}|>2m_{2}>0\right\},

the boundary controllability and stabilization are achieved by using the compactness-uniqueness method, which reduces the problem to show a unique continuation property. This property is a consequence of a spectral problem associated with the KP-II operator. Additionally, to relax the condition over
L
, we employed Lyapunov’s approach to finding an explicit decay rate for the solutions of the feedback-closed KP-II system.

Since this work is the first one in the direction to prove control properties with critical length, several issues can be addressed in the research agenda, let us point out some of them. Observe that due to the lack of regularity, we can not address the nonlinear problem, that is,

(5.1)

\begin{cases}u_{t}+u_{x}+u_{xxx}+uu_{x}+{\partial_{x}^{-1}}u_{yy}=0,&(x,y)\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(0,y,t)=u(L,y,t)=0,\quad u_{x}(L,y,t)=h(y,t),&y\in(0,L),\ t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,0,t)=u(x,L,t)=0,&x\in(0,L),\ t\in(0,T),,\\
u(x,y,0)=u_{0}(x,y),&(x,y)\in\Omega.\end{cases}

So, in the nonlinear context, the natural questions are:

(
\mathcal{A}
)

Is the system (
5.1
) well-posed in the class
C\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}(0,T;H_{x0}^{1}(\Omega))
?

(
\mathcal{B}
)

Is the nonlinear system (
5.1
) controllable for any
L>0
? In this sense, work related to the KdV
[
12
,
13
]
shows that the nonlinear term will give the exact control property on critical lengths and motivates future research to be addressed.

(
\mathcal{C}
)

Is there a feedback lack such that the nonlinear system (
5.1
) is exponentially stable?
