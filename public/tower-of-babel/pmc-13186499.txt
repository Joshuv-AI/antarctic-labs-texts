Pulmonary mycobacterial spindle cell pseudotumour: Report of a case and systematic review of literature
Authors: Rakesh Kodati, Narendra Kumar Narahari, Shantveer G. Uppin, Ranganath Ratnagiri, Sujata Patnaik, Kavitha Nair, Paramjyothi Gongati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterial spindle cell pseudotumour (MSP) is a rare benign disease characterized by proliferation of spindle-shaped histiocytes containing acid-fast mycobacteria. Most of the reported cases occur in lymph nodes and skin, predominantly in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or after organ transplantation. The occurrence of MSP in the pulmonary parenchyma is less common. We describe a case of MSP arising in a lung sequestration in an immunocompetent host and present a systematic review of all cases of pulmonary MSP reported in the English literature.

INTRODUCTION

Mycobacterial spindle cell pseudotumour (MSP) is an inflammatory tumour-like manifestation commonly reported with tubercular and non-tubercular mycobacterial infections. It is usually seen in immunocompromised subjects, especially in subjects infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).[1] It poses a diagnostic challenge owing to limited awareness of this rare entity among physicians and pathologists. Lymph nodes and skin are the commonly reported sites, involvement of lungs being less common. The diagnosis is confirmed by detection of acid-fast bacilli (AFB) in spindle-shaped histiocytes, which do not have any significant atypia or mitoses.[2] Here in, we report a case of MSP arising within a lung sequestration in an immunocompetent woman and conduct a systematic review of the existing literature on pulmonary MSP. The primary objective of this systematic review is to describe the clinical presentations and outcomes of reported cases of pulmonary MSP.

CASE REPORT

A 31-year-old woman presented to us with complaints of cough and dyspnoea of two years duration associated with one episode of haemoptysis. Breathlessness was of insidious onset, gradually progressive and was graded 2 on the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale. Cough was predominantly dry with no diurnal or postural variation and had no allergic triggers. She also had right-sided intermittent non-pleuritic chest pain. She denied history of orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, or any constitutional symptoms. She was on levothyroxine 25 mcg/day for hypothyroidism. She was a never smoker and had no other prior medical illnesses. On examination, the pulse rate was 100 beats per min, respiratory rate 20 breaths per min, blood pressure 140/90 mmHg, and temperature 99°F. Pulse oximetric saturation was 96% while breathing room air. Systemic examination of the respiratory system and other systems was unremarkable.

Complete blood counts, liver function tests, and renal function tests were normal. Chest radiograph showed round homogenous opacity seen occupying the right mid and lower zones [Figure 1a]. Computed tomography (CT) scan of chest was conducted which revealed well-defined contrast enhancing soft tissue density lesion (8.4 × 8.2 × 6.0 cm) in the right lower lobe [Figure 1b and d]. Ultrasound guide biopsy of the lesion done was suggestive of benign vascular neoplasm. In view of symptomatic disease, thoracotomy and surgical resection of the tumour was performed. Intraoperative bronchoscopy revealed extrinsic compression of the lower lobe bronchus with no apparent endoluminal disease. An encapsulated tumour (8 × 7 cm) between the right middle and lower lobes covered by visceral pleural layer with dense adhesions was noted during the time of surgery. As the tumour was separately identified from the lung lobes between the fissure, CT contrast images were reviewed. An aberrant artery was identified arising from the arch of aorta supplying the lesion [Figure 1c]. A diagnosis of extra-lobar sequestration was made during the hospital stay. Her post-operative course was uneventful, and she was discharged after a week. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen showed proliferation of spindle cells on sheets, focal storiform pattern with no atypia. In addition, there are areas showing inflammatory cells composed of lymphocytes, plasma cells along with few neutrophils and eosinophils. Areas showing necrosis with multiple granulomas comprising of epithelioid histiocytes and foreign body type of giant cells [Figure 2a-e]. Ziehl Neelsen stain for AFB was positive [Figure 2f]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed positivity for CD 68 and SMA (smooth muscle actin) [Figure 2g and h]. A final diagnosis of MSP in an extralobar sequestration of the lung was made. Xpert MTB-RIF assay conducted on archived formalin fixed paraffin embedded blocks was positive for mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) with no rifampin resistance. She was started on anti-tuberculous therapy (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol). She was asymptomatic and tolerating anti-mycobacterial therapy (ATT) well at 4 months of follow up.

Systematic review of literature

We (RK, NKN) searched the PubMed database (from inception till 31 May 2025) to identify the case reports or case series describing pulmonary MSP and reported it as per PRISMA recommendations.[3] We used the following search strategy: “mycobacterial spindle cell pseudotumor” OR “mycobacterial spindle cell tumor” OR “Pulmonary mycobacterial spindle cell tumour” OR “Pulmonary mycobacterium spindle cell tumour” OR “Pseudotumoural tuberculosis” OR “Pulmonary pseudotumour”. We further supplemented the search by reviewing our files and the references of the articles included. The citations were screened by titles and abstracts independently to identify case studies reporting MSP involving the lung. The full texts and cross-references were then obtained and reviewed in detail. The location of the pseudotumour and presence of spindle cell morphology with mycobacterial staining were assessed in all the reviewed case studies. Non-English articles, review articles, pseudotumours occurring at non-pulmonary sites and pseudotumours lacking characteristic spindle cell morphology were excluded.

Our search yielded 482 citations; two conference papers presented at American thoracic Society International Conference were also included.[45] Following the initial screening of titles and abstracts, 205 studies were excluded. The remaining 279 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 10 studies comprising 12 subjects were included in the final analysis. The mean (SD) age of the patients (including index case) was 45.9 (15) years; six of them were men. The diagnosis was confirmed in all the patients by histopathological examination. Of the 12 cases, six were associated with HIV/AIDS, and four involved organ transplant recipients receiving immunosuppressive therapy. Endobronchial disease with distal lung disease (collapse, consolidation or cavitation) is the most common imaging finding, seen in six cases (50%). The other abnormalities found on imaging are lung nodules, diffuse miliary nodules, lung masses, and mass with nodules. Six cases were diagnosed by bronchoscopic biopsy, three by image-guided biopsy, two after lobectomy, and one after autopsy. One case also had concomitant involvement of the cervical and supraclavicular lymph nodes along with pulmonary disease.[4] In nine out of the 12 cases, the organism was identified using either culture or molecular diagnostic techniques. The most common bacteria identified are Mycobacterium avium intracellulare (six cases), followed by MTB, M. xenopi, and M. malmoense (one each). Granulomatous inflammation, a hallmark of classical mycobacterial infections, is less commonly observed in MSP. Among the 12 examined cases, only four demonstrated granulomatous inflammation, which was typically ill-defined.

Of the 12 cases, complete details regarding case management were available for 9. Six patients received ATT alone and four underwent resection of the lesion [endobronchial debulking (2) and lobectomy (2)]. Among the cases that underwent intervention, three also received ATT post-procedure. The details of the clinical outcomes were available in eight cases. The clinical symptoms improved after treatment, despite some patients having persistent disease diagnosed by radiology or culture during follow-up. One case had recurrence of disease at the surgical skin incision site after 2 years.[6] The index case had no identifiable risk factors, was diagnosed following surgical resection, and was subsequently treated with ATT without any reccurence.

DISCUSSION

This article reports the case of a 31-year-old woman with MSP arising in an extralobar lung sequestration, and reviews 12 additional cases of pulmonary MSP reported in the literature. The diagnosis is made by histopathological examination of the tumour-like lesion. The optimal approach to management, ATT, or surgical resection is not well defined.

Tuberculosis presenting as parenchymal tumour-like masses is well known. In the absence of drainage into the bronchus, enlarging necrotic areas may appear as parenchymal masses.[7] However, MSP is a unique presentation of mycobacteria which is different from the necrotic mass-like appearances of mycobacterial infections. It is a benign lesion characterized by tumour-like proliferation of spindle-shaped histiocytes containing acid-fast mycobacteria. The histologic findings are different from the classical granulomatous reaction found in mycobacterial infections. The reason for this unique reaction is not known. Spindle cell reaction or previously known as ‘histoid’ was initially characterized in Mycobacterium leprae by Wade et al. in 1963.[8] Lymph nodes and skin are the common sites involved. Involvement of other organs, like lung, appendix, spleen, nasal septum, bone marrow and brain, are less common. It is seen in immunocompromised subjects, especially HIV subjects and solid organ transplant recipients.

These lesions have a close resemblance pathologically with other mesenchymal neoplasms occurring in the lung. Kaposis sarcoma (KS), spindle cell melanoma, neuroendocrine tumour, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour, and inflammatory pseudotumour are the differential diagnoses of the lesions.[2] Spindle cell sheets tend to have an expansile tumour-like growth pattern rather than an infiltrative pattern and positive for histiocytic markers on IHC (CD68). KS is a malignancy that is closely related to HIV infection and is known to closely resemble it. MSP is characterized by a loose storiform pattern of spindle cell arrangement, while KS has longer and more compact fascicles with intercellular slit like spaces filled with erythrocytes.[2] These spindle cells often lack significant atypia or mitoses, which distinguish MSP from high-grade sarcomas. The granulomatous reaction is vague and sparse, leading to a delay in the diagnosis of MSP in the initial stages.

Lung sequestration is a congenital anomaly of the lower airway consisting of a non-functioning mass of lung tissue. It lacks normal communication with the tracheobronchial tree and receives arterial blood supply from the systemic circulation. The association between lung sequestrations and tubercular infections is less common.[910] The occurrence of MSP in sequestered lung tissue is exceedingly rare, and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first case in the literature.

Clinical features of pulmonary MSP vary from asymptomatic disease to life-threatening dyspnoea due to endobronchial obstruction. They are diagnosed on histopathological examination without any suspicion before the biopsy. Meticulous histopathological examination of the biopsy specimen for any granulomatous inflammation and staining for AFB is crucial. Demonstration of AFB within spindle cells is diagnostic of MSP. IHC confirms the histiocytic origin of spindle cells (positive for CD68). The standard treatment of pulmonary MSP is not well known. Medical therapy may lead to regression of the lesion; however, some cases are diagnosed only after surgical resection. Anti-mycobacterial drugs are given even after complete removal of the lesions.[6] Surgery is often required both to prevent compressive symptoms caused by space-occupying lesions in vital organs and to establish a definitive diagnosis through histopathological examination. The recurrence of the disease post-surgical or medical intervention is unclear.

CONCLUSION

MSP is a unique tumour-like manifestation of mycobacterial infection, commonly observed in immunocompromised individuals. Its occurrence within sequestered lung tissue and in an immunocompetent patient is exceptionally rare. This case highlights the importance of considering MSP in the differential diagnosis of pulmonary masses, even in immunocompetent individuals. Early recognition and confirmation through mycobacterial staining techniques can facilitate accurate diagnosis and may help avoid unnecessary surgical intervention.
