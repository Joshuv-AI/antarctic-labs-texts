Increasing dietary leucine in Ross broilers affects gut permeability but not growth responses
Authors: MT Kidd, SC Wells, WK Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe investigated dietary branched-chain amino acid responses in commercial broilers with a factorial design (2 × 2 × 7) containing a Leu titration. Ross 344 x Ross 308 male broilers (2,304) randomized across 192 floor pens were fed nutritionally adequate diets from d 1 through 14 and provided dietary treatments from d 15 to 35. Dietary treatments had 6 replications and consisted of varying digestible amino acids relative to Lys: 0.76 and 0.80 Val, 0.65 and 0.68 Ile, 1.15, 1.25, 1.35, 1.45, 1.55, 1.65, and 1.75 Leu, and a titration comparison positive control diet containing 1.35 Leu. Interactions (P < 0.05) were limited to Ile and Val and occurred for BW gain, feed intake, and carcass yield. Birds fed a Val to Lys 0.80 and Ile to Lys of 0.65 had improved BW gain and feed intake that birds fed a Val to Lys 0.76 and Ile to Lys of 0.65. However, improved carcass yield occurred in birds fed Val to Lys of 0.76 and Ile to Lys of 0.65 or Val to Lys 0.80 and Ile to Lys of 0.68 with birds fed Val to Lys 0.80 and Ile to Lys of 0.65 being intermediate. BW gain of birds fed 1.35 Leu to Lys in the titration or control diet did not differ (P = 0.19), and birds fed the titration diet with 1.35 Leu to Lys had higher (P < 0.05) feed intake and feed conversion than birds fed the control diet. Although linear or quadratic Leu to Lys live performance responses were not detected (P > 0.05), birds fed increasing Leu to Lys had worsened intestinal permeability (linear; P = 0.04). Birds fed the Leu to Lys titration diet of 1.35 had higher (P < 0.05) fat and breast yields than bird fed the control diet with equal Leu to Lys, which may be attributed to increased feed intake. Decreasing quadratic responses (P < 0.05) to increasing Leu to Lys occurred for both Pectoralis minor and leg quarter yields. Dietary Leu by Val and Ile interactions did not occur, indicating the ability of Ross 308 male broiler to be fed diets with relatively high Leu. However, as dietary Leu increased, broilers had compromised intestinal barrier integrity. Amino acid levels tested mimic industry relevance and necessitates the need for more branched-chain amino acid research involving intestinal health.Keywords: Branched-chain amino acids, Leucine, Isoleucine, Valine, Broiler

Introduction

Amino acid interactions in broilers involving Lys with Arg (Jones, 1961) and Leu with Val and Ile (D’Melllo and Lewis, 1970) are well characterized and thought to be of little practical relevance. Broiler industry ingredient use, however, is challenging the former. Hence, the increase in affordability, availability, and use of feed grade amino acids (Kidd et al., 2013) has resulted in US broiler, turkey, and layer diets containing more grains and grain co-products, and less oilseed meals. Oilseed meals are higher in Arg relative to Lys (NRC, 1994). Further, corn and its co-products contain an inordinate proportion of Leu in comparison to Val and Ile when compared to wheat (NRC, 1994). Although Leu antagonism has been investigated with corn and corn co-products from wet milling (e.g., corn gluten meal) (Waldroup et al., 2002), there is a renewed interest in investigating Leu antagonism in low CP diets due to the increase use of corn in combination with corn-distillers dried grains with solubles, a corn co-product from dry milling (Maynard et al., 2021).

It has been indicated that relatively high dietary levels of Leu, albeit practical, necessitate an increase of Val or Ile or both to positively influence broiler performance and processing traits (Kidd et al., 2021) and mRNA expression of mechanistic target of rapamycin pathway related genes (Ospina-Rojas et al., 2020). In the former work, design limitations existed due to testing 3 amino acid levels to estimate potential Leu antagonistic effects. Kriseldi et al. (2022) assessed dose responses of Leu, Val, and Ile in a central composite design (5 levels of each amino acid) and determined that increased Leu and Ile independently improved breast meat yield. As both l-Val and l-Ile are commercially available, increasing their nutrient minimums in least cost formulation to offset any negative consequence of excess Leu is possible, but the quantitative need of higher Val and Ile to counteract a practical Leu antagonism does not exist in broilers. Our hypothesis was that moderate increases in the Val to Lys and Ile to Lys ratios as achieved by adding the amino acids in l-form would counteract any loss in performance or yields as a function of high dietary Leu. In the research reported herein, therefore, our objective was to assess live performance gut integrity, and processing responses in broilers fed dietary Leu dose responses with varying levels of Val and Ile.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The methodology for the care and use of the commercial-broiler chickens used in this research was approved by the Division of Agriculture Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number: 18095) of the University of Arkansas System.

Birds, experimental design, and management

Ross 308 broilers (5,000) were obtained from a commercial hatchery (Simmons Foods, Inc., Siloam Springs, Arkansas) and feather sexed. Approximately 2,500 male chicks were placed into pens at 1 d of age and reared identically until the experimental period (15 to 35 d). At the onset of 15 d, pens were equalized to 12 birds per pen (0.093m2/bird) and birds were fed one of the experimental diets (28 factorial diets of 6 replicate pens across 168 pens and 1 control diet for each titration diet with 6 replicate pens across 24 pens) in a broiler house containing 192 pens (2,304 broilers at d 15) on a concrete slab at the tunnel fan half of a 12 × 122 m commercial broiler house. Each pen was equipped with built-up used softwood shavings from commercial flock grow-outs, a nipple drinker line containing 4 nipples per pen, and a Konavi pan feeder with 13 feeder space openings and a 16 kg feed storage hopper. Four continuous nipple drinker lines with regulators served each row of 48 pens and flow rates were measured weekly (20 mL/min at placement, 35 mL/min at d 15, which was increased weekly to 55 mL/min at d 30). A Rotem environmental controller (Diversified, Eatontown, NJ) was used to control house heating, ventilation, and lighting of the negative pressure solid-sided house. The pen half of the commercial house contained 13 vent boards each on both the north and south side walls, 2 side-wall fans for minimum ventilation, 8 pancake propane brooders for heat, 11 circulation fans for air stratification, and 8 tunnel fans at one end of the floor pens. Two forced air-natural gas furnaces (L. B. White, Onalaska, WI) were used for supplemental brooding heat. Whole house lighting consisted of 24 h light from placement to 24 h of age, 23 h light to 7 d of age, and was decreased to 20 h to 15 d of age and maintained to 35 d at am intensity of 10 lux.

Diets and analyses

Test diets used in this research (Table 1) were formulated with corn, soybean meal, and feed-grade amino acids representing the protein-based ingredients. The titration diet contained the first 8 limiting amino acids in feed-grade form to His and the control diet contained the first 5 limiting amino acids in feed-grade form to Ile. Dietary formulation with the former protein contributing ingredients was based on amino acid analytical data after analyses in duplicate. After formulation and prior to research initiation, composite samples of the titration diet and control diet were analyzed for amino acids and analyzed Val, Ile, and Leu (Table 1). All diets contained 1,000 units of phytase providing 0.1% Ca and 0.18% non-phytate P and salinomycin Na (60 g/907 kg) for prevention of coccidiosis. All diets were isocaloric and the control diet contained Leu to Lys of 1.35 with surfeit Val and Ile.

Increasing the Val, Ile, and Leu levels relative to Lys to create dietary treatments was achieved by adding l-Val, l-Ile, and l-Leu (commercial freebase forms; CJ Cheiljedang Bio, Seoul, South Korea) at the expense of the dietary inert filler (e.g., sand). Treatments represented the Val, Ile, and Leu calculated dietary levels relative to Lys were 0.76, 0.65, and 1.15, respectively (Table 2). In order to create test diets, 4 mash diets were created containing Val and Ile levels of 0.76 and 0.65, 0.80 and 0.65, 0.76 and 0.68, and 0.80 and 0.68, respectively. All 4 mash diets contained 1.15 Leu to Lys and after mash diets were mixed they were briefly stored. The 4 diets were subdivided into 8 test diets representing the original 4 diets and the 4 diets with 1.75 Leu to Lys as from l-Leu additions. The 8 diets were then blended in proportions to contain all treatment combinations (Table 2), mixed, and pelleted. The full factorial of 28 treatment diets replicated 6 times encompassed 168 pens. Thus, control diets with 6 replicates were included in all 4 Val and Ile combinations resulting in 24 pens of control fed birds. Composite samples of the 28 pelleted factorial diets were analyzed for free l-Leu, rather than protein bound l-Leu. Briefly, composite treatment feed samples were ground, diluted with room temperature hydrochloric acid, agitated, filtered, and analyzed by chromatography. The former was done in order to quantify free l-Leu concentrations in all 28 experimental diets without liberating any protein-bound Leu.

Measurements

Live performance measurements consisted of BW gain, feed intake, feed conversion, and mortality from d 15 to 35. Feed conversion was adjusted by subtracting mortality BW from feed intake data. After live performance measurements were obtained, two birds per pen (384 birds) were randomly removed from pens, placed in coops, transported to a pilot processing facility, and processed for yields and myopathy occurrence as previously described (Kidd et al., 2025). Intestinal barrier integrity was measured with serum a procedure utilizing fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran procedure as previously described (Tabler, 2020). Briefly, 1 bird per pen was orally gavaged with fluorescein isothiocyanate (8.32 mg/kg, MW ∼4 kDa, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) from the flowing treatment pens (all Leu to Lys pens in the 0.76 and 0.80 Val to Lys with a 0.65 Ile to Lys, and control pens). One hour post gavage, blood was collected from the brachial wing vein, serum separated, and fluorescence was measured (Ex 428nm/Em 528 nm) using a multi-mode micro plate reader (Synergy HTX plate reader: BioTek, Winooski, VT). Hence, only half the pens were sampled in this assay due to the time constraint from gavaging to bleeding in order to assess intestinal barrier integrity in the Leu titration and control fed birds.

Statistical analyses

All variables were analyzed by the generalized linear mixed models procedure of SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) with the pen of broilers serving as the analyzed experimental unit. Birds were allocated to pens at d 15 in a randomized manner with any bird appearing lame removed. The full factorial of pens with control pens were blocked to account to tunnel fan air movement across pens resulting in a randomized complete block design of pens. Preplanned orthogonal contrasts were used to compare effects between iso dietary Leu treatments (e.g., the test diet containing 1.35 Leu to Lys (24 replicate pens) from intact protein ingredients and l-Leu vs the control diet formulated to contain 1.35 Leu to Lys (24 replicate pens), devoid of l-Leu). Results and discussion

Test diet analyses

The analyzed total dietary Leu to Lys ratio in the averaged test diets was 1.13 and in close agreement with the target digestible Leu to Lys of 1.15 (Table 1). Digestible targets for Val and Ile ratios to Lys at the lowest levels were 0.76 and 0.65, respectively, and were analyzed to be 0.79 and 0.67 total ratios to Lys (Table 1). All four Leu dose response treatments including 7 Leu titration levels were analyzed for free l-Leu (Table 2). Total free Leu can be converted to digestible Leu at 100% (Tillman, 2019). Calculated l-Leu levels to achieve the 1.15 and 1.75 treatments were 0.00 and 0.69, respectively. Analyzed l-Leu in the four dose response titrations ranged from <0.02 to 0.03 and 0.60 to 0.65 for the 1.15 and 1.75 treatments, respectively (Table 2). Moreover, analyzed l-Leu represented a valid dose response, although Leu analyzed values were below calculations (Table 2).

Diet isoleucine by valine interactions

Interactions in this study were limited to Ile by Val for BW gain, feed intake, and carcass yield (Table 3). Broilers fed 0.65 Ile to Lys and 0.80 Val to Lys had the highest (P = 0.017) feed intake with the highest (P = 0.001) BW gain which was equal to broilers fed 0.68 Ile to Lys and 0.80 Val to Lys. However, either the 0.65 Ile to Lys and the 0.76 Val to Lys or the 0.68 Ile to Lys and the 0.80 had the highest (P = 0.03) carcass yields with broilers fed 0.65 Ile to Lys and 0.80 Val to Lys having an intermediate yield.

Allen and Baker (1972) assessed Val and Ile responses in diets with Leu supplementation and found that 0.5% l-Leu added to a purified diet with 1.2% l-Leu decreased Val and Ile efficacy to 92.9 and 93.6%, respectively. Our lack of Leu interactions on Val and Ile indicate that the Ross x Ross 308 broiler can withstand diets elevated in the Leu to Lys ratio up to 1.75 without negative consequences on growth performance or processing yields and is in agreement with similar work in Cobb broilers (Maynard et al., 2022). Moreover, Val and Ile interactions (Table 3) indicate that higher Val to Lys (0.80) with lower Ile to Lys (0.65) improves BW gain as a function of increased feed intake, and the reversal of these amino acids (Val to Lys of 0.76 and Ile to Lys of 0.68) suppresses carcass yield. Kriseldi et al. (2022) showed that carcass yields of Ross 708 broilers were optimized with increasing Ile to Lys (0.68 to 0.74) in the presence of 1.90 dietary Leu to Lys. Although the former Leu is above levels tested herein, more work is needed to further assess Ile and Val needs of carcass yields in Ross broilers. Further, Kidd et al. (2021) evaluated Leu, Ile, and Val interactions in male and female broilers (e.g., similar to Ross 308 performance) and demonstrated that Ile, but not Val, resulted in improved carcass yields.

Diet leucine and broiler performance

Broilers fed the titration diet with 1.35 Leu to Lys had higher (P = 0.041) feed intake and higher (P = 0.001) feed conversion than birds fed the control diet containing 1.35 Leu to Lys (Table 4). This response is likely due to the higher inclusion of soybean meal in the positive control diet which resulted in higher levels of less limiting amino acids (e.g., His, Phe, and Gly). However, BW gain and mortality were not statistically different in broilers fed differing diets containing the 1.35 Leu to Lys level. Furthermore, BW gain, feed intake, and feed conversion were not affected by the dietary Leu dose responses. Although the main effect of Leu on mortality occurred (P = 0.04), linear (P = 0.97) and quadratic (P = 0.88) responses did not.

Assessing Val and Ile responses in diets with surfeit Leu (1.10 Leu:Lys) is not relevant for poultry nutritionists in the U.S. because practical dietary Leu to Lys can range from 1.35 to 1.85 depending on grain type and grain by-products used in formulation (Kriseldi et al., 2022). Recent work in Ross 344 x Ross 708 demonstrated that Leu level and response criteria assessed resulted in variable Val and Ile needs in diets that ranged from 1.10 to 1.85 dietary Leu to Lys (Kriseldi et al., 2022). The decreased efficacy of elevated dietary Leu on Val and Ile in broilers has been shown to be partly attributable to a decreased feed consumption (Smith and Austic, 1978). Linear or quadratic responses to feed consumption herein did not occur, indicating that the titration used (1.15 to 1.75 Leu to Lys) was not high enough to impair growth responses (Table 4).

Diet leucine and Intestinal integrity

Increasing dietary Leu linearly decreased (P = 0.04) broiler intestinal barrier integrity (Table 3). Importantly, birds fed 1.35 Leu to Lys either from the titration diet or the control diet had statically equal intestinal barrier integrity. The former indicates that feeding diets reduced in CP with additional feed grade amino acids did not alter intestinal barrier integrity. For example, Barekatain et al. (2019) evaluated intestinal permeability in broilers and found that as dietary CP was reduced, intestinal barrier integrity was decreased. Specific amino acids critical for villus epithelial cell growth and mucin formation are Met, Cys and Thr (Moran, 2016). However, both Val and Ile are involved in the intestinal core proteins of bottle-brush units (Moran, 2016).

Although work herein only assessed intestinal permeability and not protein digestion, absorption, or expression, our proposed mechanism below is tied to an amino acid antagonism. Research demonstrating the growth depressing effects of excess dietary Leu in broilers (D’Melllo and Lewis, 1970), work in rats elucidated antagonistic effects of dietary Leu on isoleucine (Harper et al., 1954) and valine (Benton et al., 1956) efficacy. Subsequent work in rats that focused on elucidating the growth depressive effect of increased dietary Leu included both metabolic studies and those that focused on digestion and absorption. Szmelcman and Guggenhein (1966) conducted absorption experiments with rats and found that increasing Val and Ile inhibited Leu absorption and that increasing Leu inhibited Val and Ile absorption. Because Val and Ile are critical in protein formation of microvilli (Moran, 2016), excess dietary Leu may decrease their intestinal absorption thereby compromising villi health and nutrient digestibility.

Liu et al. (2023) fed Val, Ile, and Leu levels 25% above know ratio needs in a reduced CP diet in Cobb male broilers during an Eimeria challenge and noted improvements in intestinal integrity. Similarly, dietary limitations of both Val and Ile have been shown to decrease broiler performance and intestinal health during a necrotic enteritis challenge (Goo et al., 2025). Clearly, all branched-chain amino acids are important for intestinal health (as reviewed by Kim et al., 2022). However, as our dietary levels of Val and Ile were surfeit and not limiting, it is suggested than future work assessing the importance of the branched-chain amino acids for intestinal integrity focus on dietary Leu from Leu to Lys ratio typical formulation levels to excess amounts (e.g., 1.50 to 1.80 Leu to Lys), rather than limiting Val and Ile levels which is no longer industry relevant.

Diet carcass traits

Broilers fed the titration diet with 1.35 Leu to Lys had higher carcass peritoneal cavity adipose tissue (P = 0.004), and Pectoralis major (P = 0.025) and total breast meat (P = 0.046) yields compared to broilers fed the 1.35 control diet (Table 5). The dietary Leu titrations resulted in both Pectoralis minor (Quadratic P = 0.038) and leg quarter yields (Quadratic P = 0.039) having a deceasing and then increasing response as diet Leu increased.

Recent research has assessed the branched-chain amino acid antagonism on broiler processing traits. It has been shown that increasing dietary leucine beyond estimated requirement levels decreases breast meat yields (Franco et al., 2017). Carcass traits in Lowman Indian River male and female broilers were assessed in a Box-Behnken design with Val, Ile, and Leu (Kidd et al., 2021). It was determined that Ile improved breast meat yield in females, but not males, but live performance of females was best with the lowest levels of Leu and Ile (Kidd et al., 2021), and the breed used in this work is similar to the strain used herein. Both carcass and breast meat yields of Ross 708 male broilers were increased with Ile to Lys and Leu to Lys ratios from 0.68 to 0.74 and 1.90, respectively (Kriseldi et al., 2022). It must be pointed out that the Ile and Leu dietary levels in the former work were 9% higher than herein. Thus, at some point birds fed dietary Leu to Lys levels above 1.75 have more branched-chain interactions on live performance and carcass traits that appear to be both criteria and strain dependent. However, Maynard et al. (2022) fed Cobb 500 male broilers Leu to Lys levels of 1.15, 1.26, 1.37, 1.48, 1.59, and 1.70 and found no negative consequences indicating that Ile and Val levels fed in practice should overcome any detrimental effect of increased Leu within this range. In conclusion, increasing dietary Leu to Lys from 1.15 to 1.75 did not impair growth performance and no major Leu × Val or Leu × Ile interactions were measured. Caution should be used as increasing Leu was associated with increased intestinal permeability. And future studies should assess former antagonistic mechanisms with additional assays to determine the repeatability and degree of impact of high Leu on gut function.
