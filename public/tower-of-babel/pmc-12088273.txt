Building High-performance Teams in Pediatric Orthopaedic Surgery: The Importance of Psychological Safety and Creating a Trusting Environment
Authors: Steven L Frick, Virginia F Casey, Benjamin J Shore, Peter M Waters
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric orthopaedic surgeons are the central figures in the delivery of specialized musculoskeletal care for children. Our work transcends individual skills, relying heavily on high-performance teams (HPTs) of professionals who deliver care cohesively across ambulatory clinics, hospital, and operating room settings. Furthermore, collaboration with hospital administrators is essential to secure the people, buildings, equipment, and resources for successful long-term patient care strategies. Thus, every pediatric orthopaedic surgeon needs to develop leadership skills that facilitate optimal patient care by creating a work environment that promotes peak performance by all team members. This article explores the foundational elements necessary to build and sustain HPTs in pediatric orthopaedic surgery, with a particular emphasis on psychological safety and trust. We will also provide recommendations for promoting a culture of excellence and mutual care.Keywords: Leadership, Teamwork, Psychological safety

The importance of building high-performance teams in pediatric orthopaedics

High-performing team members collaborate effectively, share a common vision, and consistently deliver high-quality outcomes. The importance of high-performance teams (HPTs) to achieve desired results in high-risk, even life-threatening endeavors, has been emphasized in military operations, space travel, car racing, international politics, and, of course, complex surgical care. With every operative case, parents entrust us with their most precious asset, their children. The stakes are high. Outcomes are contingent not only on our technical proficiency and surgical knowledge but also on our nontechnical skills of leadership and teamwork. Failures of communication, contingency planning, and care coordination can lead to medical errors with dire consequences, including surgical complications, delayed or suboptimal recovery, and even death. Conversely, when teams are functioning at their highest potential, they foster improved decision-making, enhanced problem solving, and optimal patient outcomes. A learning, collaborative, and rewarding professional work environment for team members enhances the safety and quality of care (see Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3).

In pediatric orthopaedics, effective HPTs encompass not only all the surgical professionals (attendings, postgraduate medical trainees, advanced practice providers [APPs]) but also the anesthesiologists, scrub and circulating nurses, surgical technicians, postoperative staff, other subspecialists, and all those involved from “wheels in to wheels out” of the operating room. No job is too small to be undervalued on the team. We also cannot expertly care for our patients in surgery without all the professionals outside the operating room who prepare our patients for surgery and help us care for them afterward. These include APPs, residents/fellows, physical and occupational therapists, radiologists, other pediatric subspecialists, and administrative staff. They all play critical roles in facilitating excellent patient care. Everyone involved needs to be a valued member of our team. The complexity of cases, the vulnerability of patients, and the critical nature of decision-making require seamless collaboration among all healthcare professionals and employees.

Traditional team building for outstanding team performance and goal accomplishment involves long-term working relationships with the same group of people, each with specific jobs or roles. Many orthopaedic surgeons have a history of close ties to athletics. Valuable lessons can be learned from sports participation as well as high performance in the arts and sciences. Some of these lessons were reviewed recently in a Harvard Business Review article by Tom Brady, widely regarded as the greatest American football quarterback of all time, and a leader of one of sports’ great teams, the New England Patriots [1]. Noteworthy points Brady made with coauthor Harvard Business School professor Nitin Nohria are:

1)Achieving greatness rarely happens alone. To be our best, we rely on teammates. This is consonant with the following proverb: “If you want to go fast, go alone; if you want to go far, go together.” A key concept in the article is not how Brady himself excelled but how he helped his teammates improve and play better.

While this article has many sports references to team building and performance, a focus purely on “winning” in medicine can at times be harmful and unprofessional. Organizational psychologist Adam Grant has commented on this distinction: “In sports, success is beating the competition—in life, success is contributing. In sports, you win when others lose—in life, you win by helping others win. In sports, no one receives points on how they treat others—in life, how you treat others is what matters most” [5]. Leaders in medicine should view “winning” as doing what is in the best interests of the patient, the community, their group, and the healthcare system in a manner that is professional, collaborative, and respectful of all the individuals. This allows teams, and systems to provide excellent care to patients.

Currently, in the US healthcare environment, there are multiple barriers to creating a common group of professionals working together on pediatric orthopaedic patients. During the COVID pandemic, the workforce changed with increased utilization of traveling nurses and scrub technicians; this made building surgical teams difficult, especially in on-call situations. These teams often disband after the case is completed; an example is a long “on-call” surgical case. This is the ideal situation for “teaming” as described by Amy Edmondson in “Teamwork on the Fly” [6]. We often work with teams that vary in the duration of participation, with constantly shifting membership, pursuing moving targets or patient-care goals. In teaming, individuals acquire knowledge, skills, and networks as a “way to get work done while also figuring out how to do it better.” Teaming cannot occur without psychological safety, where even new members feel comfortable speaking up and disagreeing when it is important for achieving the best patient care outcomes. Leaders facilitate this psychological safety by expecting team members to speak up, modeling behaviors such as asking thoughtful clarifying questions, acknowledging personal ignorance, and conveying awareness of potential fallibility. The surgeon can lead by listening carefully to all team members, regardless of rank or seniority in the team, and especially by showing deep interest in novel ideas. Edmondson describes this as “executing and learning at the same time,” which is an essential attribute of high-performance surgical teams. All of us are familiar with these concepts in the high-risk operating room environment and are hopefully at least modeling these behaviors daily in our presurgical timeouts and postsurgical debriefs. We have to rely on these skills when an unexpected, at-risk change occurs in the operating room.

These same behaviors of teaming in the operating room can also be equally effective in the outpatient clinics, our subspecialty multidisciplinary teams, and hospital meeting rooms. Edmondson noted the accomplishment of highly complex, complicated tasks requiring multiple layers of expertise and experience often requires not just an HPT but teams of teams. We can also relate to this in our hospital environments, where our pediatric orthopaedic teams rely on teams from nursing, anesthesiology, hospital administration, radiology, and others to safely accomplish technically challenging surgical cases on medically fragile children.

The role of psychological safety in team performance

Psychological safety is the foundation of any HPT. Defined as the belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, or concerns, psychological safety fosters open communication, which is crucial in high-stake environments such as pediatric orthopaedic surgery. Edmondson, a scholar in organizational behavior and expert on this concept, highlights that psychologically safe environments encourage team members to voice concerns and offer suggestions without fear of retribution, ultimately leading to better learning and performance outcomes [7]. Leaders should create an environment where all team members feel safe to speak up on behalf of patients, patient safety, and team culture to create HPTs, making the chances of medical errors going unnoticed or uncorrected lower. All team players should focus on operational goals with confidence that other team members understand everyone's role, with each committed to doing their job well and holding others accountable for doing their respective jobs well [8]. We all currently experience this in our daily operating room playbook with an emphasis on surgical timeouts, contingency plans, and team communication, and we can demonstrate pace-setting and coaching leadership by being active role models in these situations. No one wants to be involved in a wrong-patient, wrong-site, wrong-procedure situation.

How to foster psychological safety:

1)Leadership modeling: surgeons, especially senior members of the team, must foster psychological safety. By openly discussing their own mistakes or uncertainties, they normalize this behavior for others.

2)Inclusive communication: team leaders should create an environment where all voices are valued, asking for input from all team members, regardless of their role or position in the hierarchy. Regular debriefs after surgeries, where everyone involved in the case can share their perspective, can help. A commitment to continuous improvement as a surgeon includes setting aside reflective time each day after surgery to review cases with others and consider how to be better next time.

Trust as the cornerstone of team dynamics

The traditional leadership narrative focuses on the leader: charisma, character, courage, competency, and vision are highlighted characteristics. Scholars Frei and Morriss, in “Begin with Trust,” state that “real leadership is about your people and creating conditions for them to realize their capacity and power” [10]. The authors describe the trust triangle that leaders must build, consisting of authenticity, logic, and empathy, noting “people will trust you when they sense authenticity, faith in your judgment, and they believe you care about them.”

Trust is an essential element of effective teamwork performance, especially in the operating room. Trust enables team members to rely on each other's skills and judgments. In pediatric orthopaedics, where surgeries are often complex and outcomes are critical, trust allows for improved transitions and faster decision-making. Trust between the surgeon and the anesthesiologist, for example, ensures that anesthetic protocols are followed precisely during complicated procedures, allowing the surgeon to focus on surgical execution. Similarly, trust between the surgeon and nursing staff guarantees that postoperative care is administered effectively. Without trust, team members may engage in redundant tasks, micromanagement, or worse, fail to communicate crucial information to all team members—all of which impair the efficiency of care delivery, negatively impact patient outcomes, and increase the risk of medical error.

Trusting HPTs takes time to develop and depends on consistent commitment and responsiveness by all team members. Once trust has been developed, it is tested daily in team interactions and as work is completed. A leadership maxim to consider is that trust is “gained in drops but can be lost in buckets.” Trusting your team makes you vulnerable, yet this vulnerability can positively deepen relationships with those on your team. Professionals, especially surgeons, doctors, and nurses, will not follow people they do not trust. People only trust people they think are real (authentic and vulnerable) [11] and who act in everyone else's best interest.

Effective, clear communication builds trust, which is the cornerstone for healthy team dynamics. Because trusting creates vulnerability, some may shy away from trust as a defense mechanism and prefer a cynical approach, viewing others as untrustworthy. This protective approach has been studied by Stanford professor Jamil Zaki, PhD, who found initial cynicism unfounded and that an optimistic, trusting approach leads to improved team function [12]. Cynics who start as nontrusting may never get a chance to have their trust validated by high-level performance, behavior, and accomplishment. Optimists who start as trusting may occasionally be burned but think of trust as something set up to be tested and perhaps microfractured. In the repair process from trust “microfracture” (as a fractured bone cyst that can become stronger through healing), there is an opportunity to rebuild deeper trust [13].

1)Consistency: teams build trust when there is consistency in behavior, communication, and expectations. Consistently following through on promises and being dependable in high-pressure situations helps team members rely on each other.

Creating a culture of collegiality and commitment to excellence

Beyond psychological safety and trust, teams thrive in an environment where collegiality and a commitment to excellence are paramount. Collegiality refers to the cooperative relationship between colleagues is characterized by mutual respect and a shared sense of responsibility toward the team's goals. In a pediatric orthopaedic context, collegiality allows for shared decision-making, mentorship, and a collective approach to problem-solving.

A culture of excellence, on the other hand, goes beyond meeting minimum standards. It demands continuous improvement and a commitment to the highest levels of patient care, research, innovation, and education. In such cultures, team members are not only focused on their immediate tasks but are also invested in the long-term success of the team by continuous focus on the wellbeing of patients and staff.

Promoting collegiality and excellence:

1)Mentorship and professional development: senior surgeons and administrators can foster collegiality by mentoring junior staff, fostering an environment of learning and professional growth. Building trust in organization: the OrthoCarolina Mentorship Program.

Virginia Casey, MD, Medical Director OrthoCarolina.

The OrthoCarolina Mentorship Program exemplifies our commitment to fostering the development and success of our physicians in alignment with our core value, “Make Lives Better.” Beyond delivering exceptional patient care, this program aims to cultivate emotionally intelligent and self-actualized clinicians, who are known to achieve better patient outcomes [1]. Through mentorship, we emphasize the importance of making explicit our organizational values, setting clear behavioral expectations, and establishing norms via peer-to-peer coaching and accountability practices. The program encourages operating together and exchanging clinical insights to reinforce group values and performance standards. By normalizing mentorship throughout a physician's career, we aim to ensure continuous professional growth and alignment with our high standards.

OrthoCarolina has 125 physicians, with 17 currently participating as mentees. The mentorship process begins with a formal kick-off event, where new physicians and their mentors connect through discussions on the therapeutic relational model for high-achieving professionals, moderated by the Medical Director. This initial meeting includes dialogs with our CEO and his mentor, underscoring the long-term value of mentor–mentee relationships [2]. Mentees are matched with one to two mentors, specific to their clinic and specialty, and follow a structured two-year curriculum covering topics such as corporate governance, billing, health equity, and work-life balance. To accommodate varying schedules, sessions are available in both virtual and in-person formats. Mentors are encouraged to go beyond clinical discussions, addressing overall physician wellbeing [3]. Progress is monitored through biannual surveys, and feedback is incorporated to ensure alignment with the physician's professional trajectory before presenting them for a shareholder position.

High-fidelity surgical teams: Boston Children's Neuromuscular Orthopaedic Team.

Benjamin J.Shore,MD,MPH,FRCSC.

The COVID-19 pandemic created a widespread effect throughout health care since the first reports of the viral outbreak in December 2019 [1]. While the pandemic created tremendous stress in our healthcare systems, it also provided a unique opportunity for rapid iterative change to improve healthcare delivery. At Boston Children's Hospital during the pandemic, operating room access was significantly contracted, yet the demand for surgery/operating room access for children with neuromuscular conditions increased. To maximize operating room efficiency and utilization, we elected to merge the schedules of two surgeons and build a two-surgeon neuromuscular operative team to help improve operating room utilization, efficiency, and access to care for our neuromuscular patients and families.

In recent years, there has been an increase in the literature surrounding the utilization of two attending surgeons for more complex surgical cases [2]. Specifically, the effect of two attending surgeons on complex surgical cases, similar to that of single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS) commonly performed in neuromuscular children, has identified benefits of decreased hospitalization, blood loss, and shorter operative times [[3], [4], [5]]. There are numerous studies in the adult and pediatric spine literature but scarce literature on the effect of two attending surgeons performing SEMLS in children with cerebral palsy.

To facilitate this new two-surgeon team initially, several elements of team dynamics needed to be operationalized. First, from a scheduling/logistical perspective, two neuromuscular surgeons allocated one day every other week to facilitate combined bilateral surgeries. This day was protected and consistent. Success was imparted because both surgeons respected this day and did not violate the collaboration with other responsibilities or surgeries. The collaboration started every other week, but as volume and practice grew, this blossomed into 1–3 operating room days per week for combined surgery to manage the volume of operative neuromuscular cases. Neither surgeon was able to schedule cases into this block, and open communication between surgeons and schedulers was paramount to success. Second, the surgeons developed an operative protocol to facilitate simultaneous surgery for bilateral lower extremities neuromuscular surgeries. Standard SEMLS-type surgeries were partitioned into sections that outlined important equipment, personnel, and C-arm positioning. This protocol was discussed in open communication with the physicians, schedulers, operating room nurses, and members of the sterilizing and processing department of the operating room. Specialized kits were created to facilitate bilateral simultaneous surgery with two drills, mallets, chisels, and duplicate equipment as needed. Third, the modified protocol was put into place and communicated with the anesthesia department and operating room schedulers to allow for multiple cases to be booked in a single operating room and to trust this new scheduling process.

Four years after implementation, our two-surgeon neuromuscular team has demonstrated decreased operative time, total blood loss, and operating room costs. Our model has been adopted by several orthopaedic and nonorthopaedic teams across the enterprise. Several factors are responsible for the success of this highly specialized surgical team. A sense of community among the surgeons, operating room nurses, and surgical schedulers has created a trusting environment that has facilitated collaboration across the enterprise. Open communication has created a sense of trust between the surgeons and anesthesiologists responsible for operating room scheduling, which has facilitated the ability to “massage” the system to improve operative efficiency.

Conclusion

Building HPTs in pediatric orthopaedic surgery is essential to delivering top-tier care to patients and ensuring the long-term success of your group and healthcare system. By fostering psychological safety, trust, and commitment to excellence, leaders can create an environment where team members are motivated, engaged, and committed to excellence. Using another valuable sports analogy, this aligns with the longstanding goals of team-building by highly successful Davidson College basketball coach Bob McKillop. He emphasized the TCC concept so much that his most famous athlete Stephen Curry had TCC tattooed on his wrist—Trust each other, Care about each other, and Commit to excellence [18]. A caring and collaborative team culture not only enhances patient outcomes but also contributes to the personal and professional growth of all team members. By implementing these recommendations, pediatric orthopaedic teams can continue to provide exceptional care while navigating the challenges of an ever-evolving healthcare landscape.
