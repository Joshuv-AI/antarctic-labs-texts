Preconception health risk profiles among women planning pregnancy in northern Ethiopia: a latent class analysis
Authors: Gebremedhin Gebreegziabher Gebretsadik, Andargachew Kassa Biratu, Alemayehu Bayray Kahsay, Afework Mulugeta, Amanuel Gessessew, Zohra S Lassi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAlthough the WHO and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) classify preconception health risks (PCHRs) into biomedical, behavioural and social categories, this classification remains theoretical, mainly inconsistent and lacks a scientifically robust framework. Data-driven clustering techniques may help clarify this complexity for policymakers and healthcare providers. This study aimed to assess the status of PCHRs and identify latent classes of these risks among women preparing for pregnancy.Design and methodsThis community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 31 July to 16 August 2024 in Tigray, Ethiopia, among 865 married women planning to conceive within the next 6 months. Data were gathered through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. Risk factor indicators covering lifestyle behaviours, substance use, nutritional risks and related factors were developed based on guidelines from the WHO, the CDC and national recommendations. Latent class analysis (LCA) was employed to identify distinct classes of PCHRs, with the optimal number of classes determined using statistical fit indices, adequacy criteria and interpretability. The study also evaluated the overall distribution of PCHRs among participants.Setting and participantsThe study took place in Tigray, Ethiopia, among married women intending to become pregnant within 6 months.OutcomeBurden of PCHRs and identified distinct latent classes of these risks within the participants.ResultsAll participants were exposed to at least four PCHRs, with 84.2% experiencing between 6 and 12 risk factors. The optimal LCA model identified four distinct classes of PCHRs: lifestyle behavioural risks (n=458, 52.9%), reproductive health risks and chronic medical conditions (n=106, 12.25%), nutritional risks and environmental exposure (n=149, 17.23%) and social determinants of health (n=152, 17.57%).ConclusionsOur study reveals a high baseline level of PCHRs, with all participants exhibiting multiple risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes. The identification of four distinct risk profiles underscores the need for tailored risk-specific interventions, particularly in conflict-affected settings. Our findings point out the need for targeted preconception care and risk stratification in national health strategies to improve maternal and child health outcomes.Keywords: Risk Assessment, Nutrition, SEXUAL MEDICINE, REPRODUCTIVE MEDICINE

Introduction

Addressing preconception health risks (PCHRs), including abnormal weight, smoking, alcohol use, mental illness, chronic medical illness, substance abuse and use of teratogenic medications, presents a vital opportunity to enhance maternal and child health outcomes across generations.1,3 These modifiable risk factors not only threaten maternal well-being but also disrupt fetal development and predispose offspring to metabolic disorders and chronic diseases later in life.4,7 Adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs), which are a significant global public health concern, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa,8 9 are strongly linked to PCHRs.10 Recognising this connection, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Preconception Care (PCC) Work Group emphasised identifying PCHRs as a key strategy to enhance women’s health and reduce APOs.11 Besides, according to Rosenstock’s Health Belief Model,12 individuals are more likely to adopt health behaviours when they perceive a significant risk of adverse outcomes if no action is taken. Key factors such as perceived susceptibility and severity of a condition influence decisions like seeking screening or counselling driven by a personal sense of threat.12 Despite their critical importance, research on PCHRs remains scarce in Ethiopia, highlighting a significant gap in addressing these modifiable risk factors.

The preconception period presents a critical opportunity to address health risks and promote lifestyle changes. 5PCC encompasses interventions aimed at identifying and mitigating biological, behavioural and social risks that affect pregnancy outcomes through preventive and management strategies, including public awareness campaigns, risk assessments and primary care education.11 However, effective implementation depends on understanding the prevalence of these risks.

In Ethiopia, APOs affect between 18.3% and 32.5% of pregnancies.13,15 Those outcomes are driven by several risk factors including underweight status (36.2%),16 alcohol use (68.7%),17 folate deficiency (84%),18 long birth intervals (20.8%),19 low levels of physical activity (53.2%),20 chronic illnesses (16.6%),21 khat chewing (the practice of consuming an euphoria-inducing stimulant plant common in Eastern Africa)22 (27.6%),23 smoking (20.3%)24 and coffee intake (68.7%).25 Consequently, Ethiopia ranks among the top 10 countries with the highest neonatal mortality rates.26 The conflict in Tigray has further strained the health system, exacerbating preconception health issues. An estimated 43.3% of reproductive-aged women experienced gender-based violence,27 and the prevalence of sexually transmitted infections (including HIV) increased.28 The incidence of neural tube defects had more than doubled from 13129 to 262.5 per 10 000 births30 and maternal mortality also surged, rising from 266 to 840 deaths per 100 000 live births.31

Despite the need for comprehensive risk assessment, current approaches remain fragmented and likely underestimate the actual burden of PCHRs.32 This study is the first to assess PCHRs from the perspective of women planning pregnancies, categorising participants into risk-based classes using latent class analysis (LCA).33 LCA identifies distinct subgroups based on symptoms, medical history or health outcomes, enabling more targeted interventions and the efficient allocation of resources. In alignment with national PCC guidelines and CDC/WHO recommendations,34 35 this community-based study seeks to assess the burden of PCHRs and identify distinct latent classes of these risks among women intending to conceive.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in Tigray, a region comprising seven administrative zones, with Mekelle serving as both the regional capital and a special zone. The research focused on six selected districts spanning two zones: Kilite Awlaelo, Tsirae Womberta and Wukro town in the Eastern Zone, as well as Ahferom, Adwa district and Adwa town in the Central Zone. These areas collectively contain 68 villages (kebeles) and have a combined population of 549 419, of which an estimated 129 015 were women of reproductive age. In Tigray, the conflict led to inadequate access to maternal healthcare and other essential health services36 (figure 1).

Map of the study setting.

Study design and population

A community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted among married women of reproductive age who intended to become pregnant within 6 months of data collection.

Data collection methods and measurements

The community-based survey was conducted from 31 July to 16 August 2024, in Tigray, using an interviewer-administered questionnaire that included mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) assessments. Data collection was facilitated through a digital platform (KoBoToolbox) with questionnaires administered in the local language ‘Tigrigna’ by trained healthcare professionals, including public health officers, midwives and nurses with BSc, MSc or MPH qualifications. The questionnaire, adapted from validated tools such as published literature, the Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey, WHO and CDC surveys,1035 37,40 was tailored to the local context and pretested with 44 women for refinement. The questionnaire was designed to be concise for respondent convenience, and the average interview duration was 35 min. Data quality was ensured through a multifaceted approach. Field staff received a three-day extensive training on the questionnaire, MUAC measurement, ethics and protocols. Furthermore, KoBoToolbox’s real-time monitoring further enhanced data quality, automated validation features (eg, skip logic, constraints) and rigorous on-site supervision to verify questionnaire completeness and consistency. The adapted tools were based on validated instruments from published studies, ensuring reliability and relevance. The survey was supported by the Tigray Regional Health Bureau and local health authorities, reinforcing its robust implementation. The questionnaire is found in online supplemental S1 appendix.

Preconception health risks

We measured women’s PCHRs using indicators from the CDC and WHO recommendations35 and the Ethiopian national guidelines.34

Reproductive health risks

Subfertility is defined as the inability to conceive within 6 months of attempting pregnancy for women over 35 years of age and within 12 months for those under 35 years.41 42 Maternal parity risk is classified by a history of five or more deliveries.43 In addition to maternal age, participants were considered at risk based on age-related thresholds: younger than 20 years or 35 years and older. APOs encompass both fetal and maternal complications, such as neonatal mortality, preterm birth, low birth weight, stillbirth, congenital anomalies, miscarriage, Rh incompatibility, postpartum haemorrhage, obstructed labour, postpartum depression, antepartum haemorrhage (APH), ectopic pregnancy and pregnancy-induced hypertension.15

Genetic risk factors

Pregnant women were classified as having a genetic risk if they answered ‘yes’ to any of the following: do you/your partner, previous children or other relatives have a birth defect, genetic condition, developmental delay or learning disability; two or more miscarriages; prior pregnancy ended due to birth defect genetic issues; or an age of 35 or older at delivery?

Insufficient sleep

We defined this as receiving fewer than 6 hours of sleep per night, based on self-reported usual sleep duration on weekdays and weekends.44

Nutritional assessment

MUAC is a well-supported, simple and effective tool to screen for undernutrition in women, especially in community and resource-limited settings.45 Women with a MUAC of ≥23 cm were classified as well nourished, and those with MUAC <23 cm as undernourished.46 Trained healthcare professionals measured MUAC by averaging three readings taken midway between the shoulder and elbow of the left arm, with the arm hanging freely.47 48 Dietary diversity was assessed by asking women about their consumption of a list of 10 food groups over 24 hours with ‘yes’ or ‘no’ options. Consumption of foods from five or more groups was defined as adequate dietary diversity49. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire was used to assess the type, frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate and walking activities over the past week.50 51 Activity levels were then categorised as low, moderate or high based on the scoring protocol.52

Substance use

Current use of substance: using at least one specific substance for non-medical purposes within the last 3 months (khat, tobacco and others).53 Medication use was assessed by asking, ‘do you currently use any of the following medications?’ Participants responded ‘yes’ to categories including self-medication use, over-the-counter medications, herbal or natural remedies, weight-loss products, and sports supplements.44 Besides, we assessed coffee intake by asking, ‘do you currently drink coffee?’ Respondents answered ‘yes’ or ‘no’, and those who answered ‘yes’ were further categorised by daily intake as either fewer than three cups or three or more cups during the current pregnancy.54 We assessed intake of alcohol by asking ‘did you drink any amount of alcohol in the last 12 months?’ and reporting when participants responded ‘yes’. The study assessed smoking history, including both active and passive smoking. Passive smoking was measured by exposure to tobacco smoke for at least 1 hour per day in a room, home, car or office with one or more smokers.

History of chronic medical illness

We assessed dental health by asking, ‘do you currently have any of the following: red or swollen gums, tender or bleeding gums, pain when chewing, loose teeth (tooth decay), or dental or gum pain?’ If a woman answered ‘yes’ to any of these questions, we reported it as a dental health problem. The history of infectious diseases includes the history of HIV/AIDS, hepatitis B, malaria, sexually transmitted diseases and tuberculosis. The history of the medical conditions included a history of diabetes mellitus, epilepsy, hypertension, cancer, chronic kidney problems, chronic heart problems, asthma and thyroid disorder. To assess psychological distress, we used a 10-question scale with responses ranging from 1 to 5, resulting in total scores between 10 and 50. The cut-off scores indicate 10–19 for no mental disorder, 20–24 for mild disorders, 25–29 for moderate disorders and 30–50 for severe disorders.55 56 The level of appetite during the current pregnancy was assessed by asking if the woman experienced anorexia during this time.

Social determinants of health risks include having an education level below high school or not being a member of a community health insurance programme.35

Status of PCHRs

If a woman suffered at least one of the following risks during the preconception period: history of partner violence, chronic medical conditions, infectious disease, poor dietary practice, medication use, tobacco use, alcohol use, intake of coffee above optimal, history of infertility/subinfertility, high maternal parity, maternal age risks, abnormal weight status, psychosocial distress, dental health problem, history of genetic risks, sleeping problems, low physical activity levels, member of community health insurance, social risk education, poor eating habits and exposure to environmental and/or occupational hazards; any history of APOs (stillbirth, neonatal death, low birth weight, preterm, congenital anomaly, perinatal death, miscarriage, eclampsia, pre-eclampsia, APH and recurrent abortion).57 58

Awareness of PCC

We measured preparation for pregnancy use by asking participants, ‘what preparation or actions did you take before your current pregnancy?’. The response options were: (1) none, (2) prepared on my own at home and (3) consulted healthcare providers. We measured awareness of PCC with a single question: ‘have you ever heard about prepregnancy care?’. Women who answered ‘yes’ were considered to have information about PCC.

Maternal health services characteristics

Cervical cancer screening status was defined by whether participants had undergone screening at least once in the past 5 years (yes/no). Preconception vaccination status was determined by asking if participants received tetanus diphtheria (Td1–Td4), COVID-19, human papillomavirus vaccines and hepatitis B vaccines before the current pregnancy (yes/no). Fasting status was measured by asking if participants abstained from animal-based foods (meat, dairy and eggs) for religious reasons on Wednesdays, Fridays and during extended fasting periods (yes/no).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula. Although PCHRs in Italian and Canadian women (smoking, alcohol intake, low physical activity, chronic diseases and poor diet) range from 10% to 52%,59 60 a conservative 50% proportion was used to maximise the sample size, with a 95% confidence level (Zα/2=1.96) and 5% margin of error (d);

The initial sample size calculation resulted in 384 participants. After applying a design effect of 2 and adding a 15% contingency for non-response, the final minimum sample size is 883 participants.

Sampling procedure and techniques

The study used multistage sampling at the kebele and household levels. Six districts from two zones in the Tigray region were selected: three from the Central Zone (Ahferom, Adwa District and Adwa Town) and three from the Eastern Zone (Kilite Awlaelo, Tsirae Womberta and Wukro Town). In the first stage, 30 clusters (kebeles) were chosen using simple random sampling from a total of 68 clusters across these six districts (six from Ahferom, six from Adwa District, three from Adwa Town, six from Kilite Awlaelo, six from Tsirae Womberta and three from Wukro Town). A survey, conducted by health extension workers and women’s development group leaders, targeted married women or those cohabiting. The survey employed a single-item reproductive life plan tool (‘would you like to become pregnant in the next 6 months?’), a widely accepted measure of pregnancy intention.59 From this survey, a list of 14 776 women who answered ‘yes’ was compiled, with the following distribution: 2386 from Ahferom, 2463 from Adwa District, 2648 from Adwa Town, 2000 from Kilite Awlaelo, 2478 from Tsirae Womberta and 2801 from Wukro Town. Finally, from the list of 14 776 registered women, 883 participants were randomly selected for this study using proportional allocation: 143 from Ahferom, 147 from Adwa District, 158 from Adwa Town, 120 from Kilite Awlaelo, 148 from Tsirae Womberta and 167 from Wukro Town (figure 2).

Statistical analyses

We used descriptive statistics to evaluate the prevalence of PCHRs, reporting frequencies, percentages and means.

Latent class analysis

It is a statistical method used to identify distinct, unobserved subgroups within a population that may share similar observable traits. As a data-driven approach, LCA classifies individuals into homogeneous latent classes using response patterns, such as binary outcomes (eg, 1 or 0), to define the fewest and most interpretable groups.60 Using all 22 indicators, we estimated latent class models in R (V.3.6.2, package poLCA) software without making any assumptions about the ideal number of classes.61 We started with a one-class model and expanded it to a 10-class model, estimating parameters using maximum likelihood.60 To streamline presentation, only models up to seven classes were reported. Determining the number of classes is critical, though no definitive consensus exists on the best criteria for model selection. Researchers generally agree on three key principles: (a) multiple fit statistics should be examined or reported, (b) the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) is a reliable fit index and should always be included,62 and (c) theoretical interpretability must guide the final solution. We did not predefine the optimal number of classes. Instead, we compared model fit using the AIC and the BIC, where lower values indicate a better fit, supplemented by average latent class posterior probabilities and the Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin (VLMR) test to assess class separation. The final model was selected for its balance of simplicity and meaningful class structure. Individuals were assigned to latent classes based on their highest posterior probability of membership.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or public were involved in any aspect of this study, including recruitment, data collection, analysis, interpretation, manuscript writing or editing, or result dissemination.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Of 883 total participants, 865 women were enrolled in the study, yielding a response rate of 98%. Those 2% of women who declined participation were because of being overwhelmed by multiple tasks like caring for their child, animal husbandry and daily household activities. Among the respondents, 28.5% were aged 35 years or older and 27.3% were between 25 and 29 years of age. The mean age of participants was 29.92 years (SD±6.48). Regarding educational status, 15.4% women were illiterate. The majority (64.7%) resided in rural areas. Additionally, a quarter, 25.5% of participants had married before the age of 18, and 43% reported owning either a radio or a television. Only 9% women held government jobs, and 41% reported limited access to health facilities within a walking distance of at least 30 min (one way) to reach the nearest health facility (table 1).

Maternal health services of participants

Of the total 807 participants with a history of pregnancy, 22.7% women had not received any antenatal care (ANC) services. Approximately 37.2% women attended four ANC visits, while only 3% of the participants received eight or more ANC visits. About one-fourth, 24.3% of women had never used contraceptives, while 41.1% were current users. Among those using contraceptives, 38.3% women relied on implants, and 52.4% used injectable contraceptives. Only 17.5% women had stopped drinking alcohol following healthcare provider advice. Nearly all, 76.3% of participants consumed coffee, and approximately 88.8% of women reported engaging in fasting. Regarding vaccination status, 86.7% of women had received the COVID-19 vaccine, while 40% had received the Td4 or higher vaccine. Additionally, 89% of women had undergone HIV testing, whereas only 3.5% of women had been tested for cervical cancer in the past 5 years. A total of 64.6% of the participants received information from HEWs, and 9.6% were enrolled in community health insurance. Currently, only 11.3% of the participants consulted healthcare providers for pregnancy preparation. A significant proportion, 47.4% of women, held an unfavourable attitude towards PCC (table 3).

Latent class analysis

We evaluated model fit for solutions ranging from 2 to 7 classes. Multiple fit statistics supported the 4-class model as the optimal choice. Notably, the value of BIC was lowest for the 4-class solution (16 599.8) compared with the other classes, including the 5-class model (16 633.2), indicating a good fit. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) also showed statistically significant results with p values, further supporting this solution. Additional fit indices, such as the VLMR adjusted LRT, were also used to compare models by testing whether one solution fits significantly better than another. Classification diagnostics revealed robust class assignment with average posterior probabilities of 99.3% (Class 2), 94.2% (Class 3) and 92.4% (Class 4), all notably higher than Class 6 (89.7%). The 4-class model also ensured practical utility, with each class comprising at least 5% of the total sample. This solution offered the most logically interpretable class structure, aligning with substantive theory (table 4).

The classes were labelled based on the highest reported risk factors and their theoretical interpretability. Based on the 22 PCHR indicators, a group of individuals identified in the four-class model was categorised as follows: Class 1: lifestyle behavioural risks (n=458; 52.95%), characterised by the highest probabilities for insufficient sleep and loss of appetite. Class 2: biomedical (reproductive health and chronic medical condition-related risks) (n=106; 12.25%), with the highest probabilities for chronic medical illness, maternal age-related risks and a history of APOs. Class 3: social determinants of health risks (n=152; 17.57%), marked by the highest probabilities in items related to educational and other social risk factors. Class 4: nutritional risks and environmental exposure (n=149; 17.23%), with the highest probabilities for poor dietary practices, low physical activity and environmental exposure. Discussion

In Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, previous studies have explored individual PCHR factors; however, none have systematically assessed these risks using the comprehensive set of CDC preconception health indicators35 in conjunction with the national PCC guideline.34 To our knowledge, this represents the first comprehensive study in Ethiopia to assess both the burden of PCHRs and characterise participant risk profiles using a robust LCA model.

In our study, all participants had at least four PCHR factors for APOs, with some reporting up to 18, highlighting the critical need for evidence-based preconception health interventions. Using LCA on 22 risk factors, we identified four distinct risk profiles. Lifestyle behavioural risks marked the most common profile, seen in 52.95% of participants.

Identifying risk factors for APOs during the preconception period is essential for guiding PCC. The Health Belief Model, grounded in Rosenstock’s psychological and behavioural theory,12 suggests that individuals are more likely to adopt healthy behaviours when they perceive a serious threat to their health from inaction. A key component of this model is the perception of personal risk, which significantly influences preventive actions such as health screenings and counselling.12 In this study, all participants had at least four PCHRs for APOs, with approximately 84% exposed to between 6 and 12 different types, which is substantially higher than the findings from India, where only 41.2% of women had at least one risk factor.63 In Canada, 55% of women planning pregnancy had risks in only one preconception risk category, with common concerns including overweight (50.4%), reproductive health issues (62.8%) and medication use (52.0%).58 Similarly, a study among urban Indian women reported that all participants had at least one risk factor, with anaemia (61%) and malnutrition (41%) being the most prevalent.2 The discrepancy could be attributed to factors such as economic status, education, healthcare-seeking behaviour and disparities in PCHR indicators. In this study, all indicators except MUAC data were collected through self-reported data. The Tigray conflict, which began in 2020,64 severely disrupted the region’s healthcare system, worsening maternal health services.64 For example, the use of contraception in Tigray dropped from 37%65 to 22.9%,36 and household hunger rose to 60.1%.66 Although Ethiopia prioritised PCC with a 2020 national policy34 and promising early progress (53.54% of healthcare providers demonstrated PCC practice),67 postconflict recovery efforts have focused on revitalising basic services.68 This has diverted attention from newer programmes like PCC, which aims to APOs. To address these gaps in Tigray, the government and stakeholders should urgently integrate PCC into maternal health services and implement effective awareness campaigns. Evidence suggests that programmes such as preconception health insurance can significantly reduce health risks and APOs.69

The CDC defines PCC as a set of interventions aimed at addressing biomedical, behavioural and social risks that may affect women’s health and pregnancy outcomes.11 Building on this concept, the Core State Preconception Health Indicators Working Group developed key indicators,70 primarily derived from the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System and the Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), to assess these risks across populations.35 In 2016, a set of 10 priority indicators was recommended for state-level monitoring.71 Other studies have examined modifiable risk factors from the 2010 BRFSS (71) and identified additional relevant indicators in countries such as Italy,72 India63 and Australia.73 In alignment with these approaches, our study measured PCHRs using 22 variable indicators, each encompassing multiple subvariables. We employed LCA to examine these indicators, a robust statistical approach for identifying distinct population subgroups from the categorical data.33 60 Particularly valuable for health behaviour research, LCA classifies individuals by risk profiles to enable precision interventions. Advanced LCA methods revealed four distinct PCHR profile domains: (1) lifestyle behavioural risks, (2) reproductive health risks and substance use, (3) social determinants of health risks and (4) nutritional risks and environmental exposure. Our findings align somewhat with the CDC’s theoretical framework, which categorises preconception risk factors that affect maternal and pregnancy outcomes through preventive measures.11 However, other studies and experts have classified these risks differently.6371,73 To our knowledge, this is the first study to categorise PCHRs using the scientific LCA model. Using robust statistical models to categorise PCHR factors provides critical insights for optimising PCC strategies, including prevention, health promotion, education, counselling and risk management to enhance pregnancy outcomes.

Furthermore, additional research is necessary to validate and generalise these component categorisations of PCHRs. In our study, of the four categories, the majority of participants were classified under the lifestyle behavioural risk group, which exhibited the highest PCHRs. This finding is concurrent with other studies.58 According to the global action plan for the prevention and control of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) 2013–2030, a key priority is reducing modifiable risk factors and addressing underlying social determinants by fostering health-promoting environments. The most burdensome NCDs, including diabetes, cancer and chronic respiratory illnesses, are largely preventable through improved health behaviours and a key intervention for improving the risk factors.74 In our study, 12.25% of the participants were primarily exposed to biomedical risks like chronic medical illness and reproductive health risks. In this study, most participants were primarily exposed to lifestyle-related behavioural risks, which act as early warning indicators or contributing factors to various health issues, such as chronic medical conditions and reproductive health risks. Since modifying these behaviours can decrease the likelihood of biomedical risks, behavioural preconception care serves as a valuable proxy for broader health interventions and calls for targeted prevention strategies like education rather than medical treatment. Evidence shows that there are direct and indirect intervention approaches to PCHRs and the above medical conditions and nutritional risks, focusing on direct intervention approaches to health issues.75 The growing burden of NCDs in low middle-income countries (LMICs) stems from epidemiological transitions characterised by urbanisation, ageing populations, behavioural risk factors (including smoking and physical inactivity), dietary changes and biological determinants.76 China’s National Free Preconception Health Examination Programme exemplifies an effective community-level intervention, achieving 85% population coverage through comprehensive services, including health screenings, education, counselling, risk assessments and medical advice on behaviour modification.57

Approximately 17.23% of participants in this study were primarily affected by nutritional risks and environmental exposure, highlighting a significant public health concern. In LMICs, reproductive-aged women and adolescents are disproportionately affected by stunting, underweight status and micronutrient deficiencies.77 Over 20 years, developing nations saw a threefold rise in the double burden of malnutrition (undernutrition and overweight) due to poor diets, inactivity and substance use.78 Malnutrition impairs cognition, increases substance abuse risk and contributes to chronic diseases, worsening fertility and intergenerational health.79 Alarmingly, 43.2% of women in our study were underweight, far exceeding typical rates, though Sub-Saharan Africa (10.1–36.2%) and prewar Tigray had high prevalence.32 Malnutrition fuels a harmful cycle, elevating risks for chronic diseases and poor maternal–child outcomes.79 Tackling this crisis requires improved nutrition programmes, dietary support and increased access to healthcare to break the cycle and enhance overall well-being.

Implications for practice

Identifying PCHRs for APOs is crucial for designing targeted health interventions that effectively address the needs of these populations. The Health Belief Model suggests that risk perception motivates preventive behaviours such as screening.80 Our findings show that addressing prevalent PCHRs through policy prioritisation, tailored PCC programmes, reproductive life planning for risk detection and health education can significantly improve maternal outcomes under universal health coverage frameworks. This study reveals critical PCHRs in Tigray, notably lifestyle behavioural risks such as environmental exposures, excessive coffee intake and alcohol use, which call for strengthened preventive strategies in Ethiopia’s reproductive health policies. Profiling PCHRs enables more targeted interventions and helps guide health system priorities, especially in addressing the high-risk profiles associated with lifestyle behavioural risk factors. Furthermore, integrating community- and facility-based data will enhance risk prediction and validate classification models, providing a robust evidence base to support clinicians and policymakers in optimising maternal health outcomes. Future studies with larger samples are recommended to validate the identified preconception health risk categories and to mitigate limitations associated with data sparsity.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability biases, which can potentially affect estimates of risk factors. Second, since most risks were reported rather than clinically diagnosed, their prevalence is likely underestimated, as diagnosis depends on access to healthcare. Third, a cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Fourth, generalisability is limited to similar conflict-affected or resource-limited regions. Fifth, while this is the first study to use LCA for categorising PCHRs into four distinct groups, this classification has not yet been externally validated. However, a key strength is that this study is among the first to comprehensively assess preconception health status in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia. Additionally, the use of LCA to classify participants into risk profiles represents a novel and valuable methodological contribution.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that all participants exhibited four or more PCHRs for APOs, indicating alarmingly high baseline risk levels. Using LCA, we identified four distinct risk profiles, with the most prevalent characterised by lifestyle behavioural risk factors. These evidence-based risk stratifications offer actionable guidance for (1) health systems to develop targeted interventions tailored to specific risk profiles; (2) policymakers to design PCC initiatives aligned with universal health coverage goals and (3) healthcare providers to enhance risk assessment through structured reproductive life planning. The findings underscore the critical role of public health education in addressing modifiable risks, especially lifestyle behavioural risks. By integrating clinical, policy and educational strategies, this comprehensive approach holds significant potential to improve preconception health outcomes both at the individual and population levels. Overall, this study establishes a critical foundation for targeted preconception care in conflict-affected settings, emphasising the need to incorporate risk stratification into national reproductive health strategies.
