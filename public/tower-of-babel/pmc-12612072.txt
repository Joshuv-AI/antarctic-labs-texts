A neuromorphic approach to early arrhythmia detection
Authors: Manjur Kolhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate detection of arrhythmias from electrocardiogram (ECG) signals is crucial for timely diagnosis and effective management of cardiovascular diseases. This paper introduces a novel bio-inspired approach for ECG arrhythmia detection, leveraging Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) inspired by biological neural mechanisms. The proposed methodology employs a structured pipeline, beginning with signal preprocessing involving normalization and filtering. Continuous ECG signals are then transformed into spike trains using rate coding. The core of the approach utilizes leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons in combination with spike timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), modeling synaptic plasticity observed in biological neurons. The network dynamically updates synaptic weights based on the timing of input and output spikes, enabling it to learn complex temporal patterns from encoded ECG data. The SNN model was trained and evaluated using a comprehensive 12-lead ECG dataset aimed at classifying various arrhythmic conditions. The developed SNN achieved a high overall accuracy of 94.4% in arrhythmia detection tasks. Accuracy, sensitivity, and F1-scores exceeded 0.88 across all arrhythmia classes. Notably, the model demonstrated exceptional performance in identifying left bundle branch block (LBBB) and right bundle branch block (RBBB), attaining F1-scores of 1.00 and 0.99, respectively. The bio-inspired SNN approach effectively captures temporal dynamics critical for accurate arrhythmia classification. With high accuracy and reliability demonstrated across various arrhythmic conditions, particularly in distinguishing LBBB and RBBB, this model holds significant potential for enhancing automated ECG interpretation and supporting clinical decision-making. Introduction

Electrocardiography (ECG) has long been instrumental in diagnosing heart conditions1. With the rising prevalence of cardiovascular diseases, the accurate interpretation of ECG signals has become more critical than ever2,3. Traditionally, ECG analysis involves healthcare providers manually reviewing recordings—a time-consuming process whose accuracy can vary depending on individual expertise4. The advent of artificial intelligence and deep learning (DL) has transformed this landscape, offering automated solutions that can process and analyze ECG signals with high precision. Among these approaches, bio-inspired Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) stand out by mimicking the neural processing mechanisms of the human brain5. This research explores the application of SNNs for ECG analysis, highlighting not only their advantages but also the challenges they face and the potential impact on healthcare. By combining computational principles with medical signal processing, our goal is to demonstrate how SNNs can enhance the accuracy and efficiency of ECG interpretation. Unlike conventional neural networks that rely on continuous activation values, SNNs communicate via discrete spikes, making them particularly suited for temporally structured data like ECG signals. Moreover, SNNs offer energy-efficient computation, enabling the integration of machine learning algorithms into portable and embedded medical devices. The primary objective of ECG classification is to detect cardiac abnormalities directly from raw ECG signals. Conditions such as fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, and bundle branch blocks must be promptly diagnosed to prevent severe complications, including stroke, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death6,7. Traditional machine learning (ML) models and deep learning networks, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have demonstrated strong performance in ECG classification8. However, their reliance on matrix operations and continuous data processing can be computationally intensive, limiting real-time applicability9. ECG classification is further complicated by signal variability and interference arising from real-world conditions10. Differences in ECG waveforms exist both within and between patients, influenced by factors such as patient motion, electrode placement, and physiological states11. To address these challenges, preprocessing steps—such as noise filtering, signal normalization, and feature extraction—are typically applied before feeding ECG data into learning models12. Additionally, effective encoding methods are critical for preserving temporal features of ECG signals to enable spike-based learning. SNNs excel in this domain due to their ability to utilize biologically inspired learning rules, including Spike Timing Dependent Plasticity (STDP) and gradient-based learning. STDP allows neurons to adaptively communicate to identify patterns in neural activity, while surrogate gradient learning provides a differentiable approximation to train SNNs in a supervised manner. Combining these strategies allows SNNs to learn representations tailored for ECG analysis effectively.

Recent advances in neuromorphic computing have facilitated the deployment of SNNs for medical applications. Platforms like Intel’s Loihi and IBM’s TrueNorth are designed for spike-based computations with lower energy consumption than traditional GPUs, enabling real-time ECG classification on wearable and portable devices. This capability has significant implications for remote healthcare, wearable monitoring technologies, and point-of-care diagnostics where energy efficiency is crucial13. To validate the performance of SNNs, standardized ECG datasets such as the PhysioNet PTB Diagnostic ECG Database14 and the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database15 provide annotated recordings for model evaluation. Despite their promise, SNNs face challenges, including the difficulty of training due to non-differentiable spike activations and the lack of standardized clinical guidelines for implementation. Collaboration among neuroscientists, ML researchers, and healthcare professionals is vital to integrate SNNs effectively into ECG-based tools.

While existing deep learning models like CNNs and RNNs achieve high accuracy, their computational demands and continuous-valued operations limit their feasibility for real-time wearable applications. Neuromorphic approaches using SNNs offer energy-efficient alternatives but often rely on ANN-to-SNN conversion or shallow STDP-trained networks, which can result in information loss and scalability issues. To overcome these limitations, we propose an end-to-end trained SNN using surrogate gradient backpropagation with Leaky Integrate-and-Fire neurons and dropout regularization. This model learns directly from ECG signals and incorporates SNOMED CT–based clinical label mapping, event-driven computation for long ECG segments, and imbalance handling via RandomOverSampler. The resulting model is compact, low-power, and clinically interoperable.

Methodology

This study utilizes SNN for classifying ECG with the goal of improving the precision, effectiveness and real time usability of automated heart monitoring. The research makes use of data from sources like the ECG research database developed by Chapman University and hospitals, in Shaoxing and Ningbo16. The dataset includes electrocardiograms (ECGs), with 12 leads taken from 45,152 patients at a sampling rate of 500 Hz that detect heart rhythms and cardiovascular issues identified by experienced professionals, in the field. The datasets of superior quality and extensive scope play a role in developing machine learning models that can accurately identify arrhythmias with both sensitivity and specificity. ECGs often show noise because of patients shifting around during the test and variations in how electrodense is placed and interference from electrical sources.

To improve the model’s performance level and accuracy measures are taken such as wavelet denoise filters being used and implementing bandpass filtering, between 0.5 and 50 Hz range for ECG signals to ensure consistency in standardization and segmentation length. One important stage involves converting ECG wave patterns into centric formats using rate encoding and time-based coding techniques that correspond with the event focused behavior of SNN frameworks (refer Fig. 1). The coded spike patterns allow the brain network to effectively capture changes in ECG signals over time. The research uses a neural network structure that relies on LIF neuron models. The design of the model involves a layer that encodes ECG features, into spike-based representations followed by layers utilizing synaptic weight adjustments via Spike Timing Dependent Plasticity (STDP) and a final layer responsible, for categorizing ECG signals based on different heart conditions.

Proposed model.

SNN simulations require multiple simplifying assumptions to achieve both biological accuracy and computational speed. The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire model serves as a simplified neuron model to replace Hodgkin–Huxley equations because it maintains spike behavior while reducing computational requirements. The representation of synaptic transmission uses fixed weights together with basic plasticity rules such as STDP to prevent detailed biochemical modeling. The simulation of neural activity through digital time steps allows computational processing but introduces small inaccuracies in spike timing. The encoding of inputs into spike trains through Poisson or fixed-rate methods affects both network performance and robustness.

The trained model’s performance is assessed by looking at metrics such as accuracy and sensitivity well as the area under the ROC curve specifically. The research also evaluates effectiveness by comparing energy usage and speed of operation with deep learning models like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs). The findings highlight the benefits of SNNs in terms of usability and energy efficient processing, indicating their suitability for use, in wearable medical gadgets. SNNs naturally operate with event-driven sparsity at inference time yet we added a dropout layer following the initial LIF stage. The decision to use dropout was based on both training-phase behavior and generalization requirements. The firing rates remain high during the initial training epochs especially when using rate-based or latency temporal encoding together with surrogate-gradient optimization. The high initial activation density emerges from the training process even though sparse activations appear later. The dropout mechanism functions as a stabilizing regularizes which protects against early overfitting before the natural emergence of sparsity. The model exhibits two types of sparsity: activity sparsity which means few spikes and parameter sparsity which includes redundant or co-adapting weights. The occurrence of sparse spike events does not stop hidden units from adapting their weights together. The representational level operation of dropout disrupts co-adaptation between units which leads to better generalization performance especially when working with our class-imbalanced dataset and RandomOverSampler for training fold balancing.

The model performs spike output averaging across multiple time steps for classification purposes. The temporal pooling stage becomes prone to overfitting when it encounters transient patterns or noise-driven or lead-specific information without dropout. The controlled stochasticity introduced by dropout enhances the temporal representation’s resistance to such artifacts. The computational cost of applying a dropout mask to GPU or CPU operations remains minimal compared to matrix multiplication operations. The model disables dropout during inference operations to prevent runtime or energy penalties which makes it suitable for real-time and wearable-device applications. The dropout mechanism provides biological plausibility because it simulates the random synaptic breakdowns and membrane fluctuations which occur in cortical neurons. We chose dropout as our SNN-specific regularizes because of its simplicity and reproducibility and because it maintained consistent validation stability throughout our experiments.

Dataset

Our model standardized all ECG signals to 5000 samples through resampling or padding to achieve consistent network input at the expense of minimal temporal distortion. The research concentrated on four SNOMED classes which included AFIB, SR, LBBB and RBBB because these classes were considered most important for this task. The training stability improved through z-score normalization of each signal while the method eliminated absolute amplitude data. Random oversampling of minority classes occurred during cross-validation to address severe class imbalance because researchers believed duplication would improve minority detection without causing significant overfitting. The assumptions enabled stable training and balanced evaluation and computational efficiency in the constrained simulation environment.

The ECG dataset provided by the curators contained a noise-free version that underwent preprocessing to eliminate baseline drift and muscle artifacts and powerline interference. The system removed all records which contained invalid ECG signals or missing metadata during the data loading process. The remaining signals received z-score normalization per record to eliminate amplitude offsets and achieve standard scaling across patients. This survey of literature situates our results in relation to prior research and shows how our method is consistent with, and even goes beyond, the current state of the art. The initial attempts for arrhythmia detection were based on conventional machine learning approaches which used hand crafted features and statistical models. Martis and colleagues27utilized methods to categorize atrial arrhythmia setting the groundwork, for future implementations of deep learning techniques. However, because these initial models were trained on limited data sets and did not incorporate sophisticated deep learning techniques, they often failed to generalize across varied ECG patterns. Deep learning, with the emergence of CNN, was a revolutionary change in ECG signal analysis. Rajpurkar et al.22, showed that CNNs can perform comparable to cardiologists in arrhythmia detection and are better than conventional methods by directly learning hierarchical features from the raw ECG signal. Hannun et al28. and25 have also built upon this breakthrough by using deep neural networks on ambulatory ECG data to achieve high diagnostic accuracy across multiple arrhythmia types. The field was further enhanced by recurrent neural networks (RNNs), including long short-term memory (LSTM) networks26, which captured temporal dependencies in ECG signals. In their study, from 2018, Yildirim and colleagues23 and30 used bidirectional LSTM networks to enhance accuracy in detecting arrhythmias by considering the pattern of ECG data. These methods underscored the significance of incorporating changes to classify arrhythmia precisely.

Table 4 shows the SNN achieved consistent strong classification results throughout the 5-fold stratified cross-validation process with minimal performance variations which indicated reliable generalization. The detection of LBBB and RBBB showed near-perfect results with recall values of 1.000 and minimal standard deviations because the model identified these conduction abnormalities with stability. The detection of AFIB achieved high recall performance at 0.940 ± 0.012 and maintained balanced precision which demonstrated its robustness in identifying irregular heart rhythms. The SR class demonstrated high precision, but its recall rate was slightly lower at 0.852 ± 0.008 which indicated occasional misclassification of other heart rhythms. The model demonstrates both high accuracy and stability between folds which makes it appropriate for reliable arrhythmia detection in various ECG datasets.

Recent research has delved into combining sophisticated structures. Wang et al.19 proposed Res-BiANet, which combines residual networks with bidirectional attention mechanisms for effective integration of structural information from sequences of contiguous intervals. It achieved an F1-score of 0.92 on the PhysioNet 2020 challenge dataset. To capture long-term dependencies in ECG signals, Chen et al.18 proposed ECG-Transformer, which achieves high classification accuracy by leveraging self-attention mechanisms. These studies are consistent with the shift toward more powerful architecture that can train to recognize the complex patterns of ECGs. In our study, a novel, bio inspired approach to arrhythmia detection is offered using SNNs. Unlike traditional deep learning models, SNNs mimic biological neurons’ spike-based communication and could therefore improve interpretability and energy efficiency. We conducted studies that aimed at categorizing heart rhythm abnormalities such as fibrillation (AFIB) normal sinus rhythm (SR) left bundle branch block (LBB) right bundle branch block (RBB) and different levels of atrioventricular (AV) blocks. In Experiment 1, our model achieved test accuracy of 94.4% and in Experiment 2, it achieved 94.3%, which is either on par with or better than the results obtained in comparable research. For example, Katal et al.17, observed 91.2% accuracy using lightweight CNN models, but our SNN-based approach has better generalization capability especially for complex arrhythmic patterns. In our study, precision and recall metrics were consistently high, and F1-scores exceeded 0.90 for most classes, showing the robustness of the model. However, our results also demonstrate some challenges, such as the difficulty in distinguishing arrhythmia with relatively minor morphological variation. The difference between AFIB and SR, for example, is like the findings made by Attia et al.24 who pointed out that it was challenging to determine whether a patient has AFIB when they are in sinus rhythm. In the same manner, Chen et al.18 observed that WTS provides a better perspective of the sequence of events and is, therefore, vital in identifying arrhythmias with similar characteristics. The incorporation of ROC curves and confusion matrices, in our assessment offered an understanding of the model’s effectiveness. High values of the area, under the curve (ROC AUC) showing ability across various classes were observed in this study—a trend also seen in previous research, by Hannun et al.21and Ribeiro et al.29,—where deep neural networks trained on extensive ECG datasets exhibited high AUC values. Furthermore, our approach is consistent with the current shift to hybrid and multimodal models. Liu et al.20 showed that incorporating ECG and photoplethysmogram (PPG) signals enhanced arrhythmia detection, suggesting that combining different bio signals may be beneficial.

In this section we provide performance analysis of our approach compared with the state-of-the-art methods presented in31 (refer Table 5). Because of its deep convolutional architecture and well-established training techniques, VGG16 outperforms the spiking neural network (SNN) model. Capable of learning rich hierarchical features from ECG signals that capture both local and global patterns, VGG16 has sixteen layers, including thirteen convolutional layers, which enables it to effectively identify subtle morphological differences between classes like atrial fibrillation and sinus rhythm. Another factor is that VGG16 models are usually trained on large scale datasets such as ImageNet and hence act as a good initialization when fine tuning for medical datasets. This pretraining results in faster convergence and better generalization. On the other hand, SNNs are usually trained from scratch and rely on discrete, spike-based representations, which although inspired by biology, may not capture the same level of spatial detail and signal complexity. However, VGG16 also gains from the continuous valued processing and backpropagation through standard activations like ReLU for gradient updates. Standard activations and surrogate gradients based on binary spike events are harder to optimize and may constrain the learning dynamics of SNNs. In addition, VGG16 has been extensively optimized in previous research with well-tuned hyperparameters and regularization strategies which add to its performance.

Conclusion

Our research specifically examined ECG signals. Found that the strong classification results indicate that SNN could efficiently capture attributes from ECG data without additional input sources. Our research adds to the conversation, on detecting arrhythmia by introducing spiking networks as a potential substitute for conventional deep learning models. The model achieved high accuracy and precision and recall and F1-scores for most arrhythmia classes with the best performance on atrial fibrillation, sinus rhythm, left bundle branch block, and right bundle branch block. The SNN achieved comparable or higher scores than conventional models in these major classes, which demonstrates its potential as a viable alternative for ECG classification in resource-efficient environments. The model demonstrates several strengths in its performance. The model demonstrated lower accuracy in detecting first-degree atrioventricular block and other conditions with minor morphological variations. The model demonstrates good generalizability because of its diverse dataset but it still lacks sufficient representation of rare arrhythmia classes which could affect its performance in real-world imbalanced scenarios.

Future work can also extend to hybrid models that combine SNNs with attention mechanisms, or with multimodal inputs, building on the foundations of studies for improving the automation of ECG analysis. Furthermore, our future work could focus on reducing the misclassification rates, especially for 1-degree atrioventricular block, to further enhance model performance.
