The critical curve of long-range percolation on oriented trees
Sandro Gallo

Abstract
We consider a long-range percolation model on homogeneous oriented trees with several lengths. We obtain the critical surface as the set of zeros of a specific polynomial with coefficients depending explicitly on the lengths and the degree of the tree. Restricting to the case of two lengths, we obtain new bounds on the critical parameters, monotonicity properties, as well as continuity of the critical curve, plus some partial results concerning its convexity. Our proofs rely on the study of the properties of the characteristic polynomial of the transition matrix of a multi-step Markov chain related to the model.



Keywords:
Long range percolation, critical surface, absorbing Markov chain.

1
Introduction, definitions and results

At each vertex of the rooted oriented
d
-ary tree, we add edges of lengths
k_{1}<k_{2}<\dots<k_{m}
, pointing to its
d^{k_{i}}
descendants at
k_{i}
generations below.
For
i=1,\dots,m
, we let edges of length
k_{i}
be
open
with probability
p_{i}\in(0,1)
, independently of all other edges.
An
open path
is a sequence of vertices connected by open edges. For any pair of vertices
u
and
v
, we write
u\rightarrow v
if there exists an open path starting at
u
and ending at
v
.
We denote
\mathcal{C}(v):=\{w:v\rightarrow w\}
and say that
there is percolation
if
\theta(\mathbf{p})>0
, where
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
is the probability parametrising vector,

\theta(\mathbf{p}):={\mathbb{P}}(|\mathcal{C}(o)|=\infty)

and
o
denotes the root of the tree.

Note that
\theta
is non-decreasing in
\mathbf{p}
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{c}\subseteq[0,1]^{m}
denote the boundary between the set of parameters
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})
for which there is percolation and the set of
\mathbf{p}
for which there is no percolation.
We refer to this region
\mathcal{S}_{c}
as the
critical surface
(or, when
m=2
, the
critical curve
).
The goal of this paper is to analyse
\mathcal{S}_{c}
.

A general result for the multi-edge model.

Let
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{k_{m}}
and denote its elements as
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k_{m}})
.
Consider the following matrix doubly-indexed by
\{0,1\}^{k_{m}}\setminus\{0\}^{k_{m}}
:

Q(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-p_{j})^{x_{k_{m}-k_{j}+1}}&\text{if}&\,y_{k_{m}}=0\,,\,\,y_{i}=x_{i+1},\,i=1,\dots,k_{m}-1,\\
1-\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-p_{j})^{x_{k_{m}-k_{j}+1}}&\text{if}&\,y_{k_{m}}=1\,,\,\,y_{i}=x_{i+1},\,i=1,\dots,k_{m}-1.\end{array}\right.

(1)

We denote by
P_{Q}
the characteristic polynomial of
Q
(which is parametrized by
\mathbf{p}
).

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\gcd\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}=1
.
Then

\mathcal{S}_{c}\subseteq\{\mathbf{p}\in[0,1/d^{k_{1}}]\times\dots\times[0,1/d^{k_{m}}]:P_{Q}(1/d)=0\}.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section
2.1
.

Our next results will give further properties of the critical curve for the case
m=2
. But before that, let us illustrate Theorem
1
with the simplest examples.

Whenever
m=2
, we will use
(l,k,p,q)
instead of
(k_{1},k_{2},p_{1},p_{2})
, to lighten the notation.
In this case, we define the functions

\displaystyle p_{c}(q):=\inf\{p\in(0,1):\theta(p,q)>0\}\text{ and }q_{c}(p):=\inf\{q\in(0,1):\theta(p,q)>0\},

leaving the dependency on
d
,
l
and
k
implicit.

For
l=1
and
k=2
, the matrix
Q
, indexed by
\{01,10,11\}\times\{01,10,11\}
, becomes

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1-p&p\\
q&0&0\\
0&(1-p)(1-q)&1-(1-p)(1-q)\end{array}\right).

(2)

According to Theorem
1
, the critical curve is the set of
(p,q)\in[0,d^{-1}]\times[0,d^{-2}]
satisfying
P_{Q}(1/d)=0
,
where
P_{Q}
is the characteristic polynomial of
Q
.
So we get the following equation for the critical curve:

\left\{(p,q)\in[0,1/d]\times[0,1/d^{2}]\,:\,pq^{2}-\frac{pq}{d^{2}}-\frac{pq}{d}+\frac{p}{d^{2}}-q^{2}+\frac{q}{d^{2}}+\frac{q}{d}-\frac{1}{d^{3}}=0\right\}.

Solving this equation, we get, for
q<d^{-2}
,

p_{c}(q)=\frac{q^{2}-\frac{q}{d}-\frac{q}{d^{2}}+\frac{1}{d^{3}}}{q^{2}-\frac{q}{d}-\frac{q}{d^{2}}+\frac{1}{d^{2}}}.

(3)

Using Theorem
1
, one can make plots of the critical surface at any fixed
d,m,k_{1},\dots,k_{m}
that is computationally feasible.
For that, one needs to work with the characteristic polynomial of a (
2^{k_{m}}-1)\times(2^{k_{m}}-1
) matrix whose entries depend on the vector
\mathbf{p}
, and are computed using (
1
).
We refer to Figure
1
for
m=2
with
k_{1}=1,k_{2}=2
.
For larger values of
k_{2}
, we can hardly distinguish between the critical curve and the lower bound. Figure
2
shows the case where
m=3
and
k_{1}=1,k_{2}=2,k_{3}=3
.

The two-edge model.

We obtain further results when restricting to the case
m=2
.
Let us start with a simple observation.

Proposition 1
.

For every
p\in[0,1/d^{l}]
,

\frac{1-pd^{l}}{d^{k}}\leq q_{c}(p)\leq\frac{1-pd^{l}}{(1-p)d^{k}}.

The lower bound is immediate by noticing that the percolation model can be embedded in a Galton-Watson tree with offspring expectation
pd^{l}+qd^{k}
.
For the upper bound, we consider a reduced model, whereby long edges originating at each given vertex can be open only when the corresponding short edges are closed.
On the one hand, this new model is dominated by the original model and, on the other hand,
\mathcal{C}(o)
is in perfect correspondence with a Galton-Watson tree having offspring expectation
pd^{l}+(1-p)qd^{k}
.

The following theorem gives tighter bounds for the critical parameters.

Theorem 2
.

Fix any
d\geq 2
and
1\leq l<k<\infty
.
For every
p\in[0,1/d^{l}]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1-pd^{l}}{d^{k}(1-p^{k-l+1})}\leq q_{c}(p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-pd^{l}}{d^{k}-pd^{l}}.

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
2.2
.

Two interesting consequences of this theorem are the following. First, the lower bound implies that the critical curve lies strictly above the straight line that connects
(d^{-l},0)
to
(0,d^{-k})
, for every
1\leq l<k<\infty
(as remarked in
de Lima et al. 2023
when
l=1
). Second, the bounding curves are differentiable, and when
l=1
they have equal slope
\frac{-d}{d^{k}-1}
at
p=d^{-1}
, implying that
p\mapsto q_{c}(p)
is also differentiable at
p=d^{-1}
, and has the same slope.

Figure 2:
The critical surface for
m=3
with
k_{1}=1,k_{2}=2,k_{3}=3
.

Our last theorem states that the critical curve is continuous and strictly decreasing.

Theorem 3
.

Fix any
d\geq 2
and
1\leq l<k<\infty
.
If
q^{\prime}>q^{\prime\prime}>0
are such that
p_{c}(q^{\prime\prime})>0
, then
p_{c}(q^{\prime})<p_{c}(q^{\prime\prime})
.
If
p^{\prime}>p^{\prime\prime}>0
are such that
q_{c}(p^{\prime\prime})>0
, then
q_{c}(p^{\prime})<q_{c}(p^{\prime\prime})
.

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in Section
2.3
.

The first handmade sketches of the critical curve show it as a concave function.
It turns out that this perhaps intuitive depiction is misleading.
The function
q\mapsto p_{c}(q)
given by (
3
) is convex near
q=\frac{1}{4}
; equivalently,
p\mapsto q_{c}(p)
is convex near
p=0
, as can be seen on Figure
1
.
This seems to be the case for
l=1
and every
k\geq 2
.

Conjecture 1
.

For the case
m=2
and
l=1
, there exists
\delta=\delta(k,d)>0
such that
p\mapsto q_{c}(p)
is not concave on
[0,\delta]
.

We provide a justification for this conjecture in Section
3
, based on an assumption that we also conjecture to be true.

Relation with previous works.

de Lima et al. 2019
considered the special case in which
m=2
and
l=1
.
They proved that
q_{c}
is continuous and strictly decreasing in
p
, and also strictly decreasing in
k
. Later on,
de Lima et al. 2023
obtained the first two terms of the asymptotic expansion of
q_{c}
as
k
diverges, and in particular that the critical curve lies strictly above the line
q=\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}}
.

Let us first observe that, even in the broader context where
1\leq l<k<\infty
, the fact that
q_{c}=q_{c}(p,d,l,k)
is strictly decreasing in
k
follows from Proposition
1
above.

Now let us position our results referring to the critical surface.
Theorems
2
and
3
hold for every
l
and
k
, without assuming that
l=1
.
Theorem
2
implies that the critical curve lies strictly above the line connecting
(0,d^{-k})
to
(d^{-l},0)
, and when
l=1
, that it is differentiable at
p=d^{-1}
with slope
\frac{-d}{d^{k}-1}
, steeper than said line.

Let us also mention that our method of proof for Theorem
1
does not involve percolation arguments as such used by
de Lima et al. 2019
and
de Lima et al. 2023
. Much more in the spirit of
Gallo & Rodriguez 2018
and
Gallo & Pena 2023
, we transform the problem into that of studying the decay of the probability of finding long open paths along one fixed direction, and, in a second step, this probability is related to the absorption time of a Markov chain.

The remaining of the paper is dedicated to proving the results (Section
2
) and justifying the conjecture (Section
3
).

2
Proofs of the results

We start by defining a multi-step Markov chain whose asymptotic absorption rate is related to connectivity decay of the multi-range percolation model along a fixed branch.
We then use it to prove Theorem
1
.
Finally, Theorems
2
and
3
are proved in separate subsections using the same construction.

2.1
Identification of the critical surface

Here we prove Theorem
1
by analysing a multi-step Markov chain that will be used in later subsections.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
denote the set of vertices of the rooted oriented
d
-ary tree.
For
v,w\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we write
w\geq v
if either
v=w
or the oriented path from the root
o
to
w
passes by
v
, and in this case denote by
d(v,w)
the length of the oriented path from
v
to
w
.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{(n)}:=\{v\in{\mathcal{V}}:d(o,v)=n\}
,
{\mathcal{V}}^{\geq w}:=\{v\in{\mathcal{V}}:v\geq w\}
.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow}
consist of a fixed sequence of sites that form an infinite oriented path starting from the origin, and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow n}:=\{v\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow}:d(o,v)\geq n\}
.
(Here we consider the edges of the original tree rather than the long-range ones of our percolation model.)

Denote by
u_{n}=u_{n}(\mathbf{p})
the quantity

u_{n}(\mathbf{p}):={\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{C}(o)\cap{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow n}\neq\emptyset).

Observe that
u_{n}(\mathbf{p})
is non-increasing in
n
and non-decreasing in
\mathbf{p}
.
We will study the asymptotic behaviour of
(u_{n})_{n}
through the following construction.

Fix
n\geq 1
for now.
We enumerate the first
n
vertices in
{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow}
backwards as
v_{n},\dots,v_{1},v_{0}
, so that
v_{n}=o
,
v_{0}\in{\mathcal{V}}^{(n)}\cap{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow}
and
d(v_{i},v_{0})=i
for
i=0,\dots,n
.
We now define a sequence of binary random variables

Y^{n}_{i}=\mathds{1}\{v_{i}\rightarrow{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow n}\},\ i=1,\dots,n,

(4)

so that in particular

\{Y^{n}_{n}=1\}=\{\mathcal{C}(o)\cap{\mathcal{V}}^{\swarrow n}\neq\emptyset\}

and therefore

u_{n}={\mathbb{P}}(Y^{n}_{n}=1).

(5)

Notice that
(Y^{n}_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
can be constructed as a
(k_{m}-1)
-step Markov chain started from
Y_{-k_{m}+1}=\dots=Y_{0}=1
with transition probabilities

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=0|Y_{i-1}=y_{i-1},\dots,Y_{i-k_{m}}=y_{i-k_{m}})=\prod_{j=1,\dots,m}(1-p_{j})^{y_{i-k_{j}}}.

(6)

These transition probabilities do not depend on
n
, so we removed
n
from the superscript and we are no longer taking
n
fixed.

This
(k_{m}-1)
-step Markov chain can be seen as a proper Markov chain
(X_{j})_{j\geq 0}
on
\{0,1\}^{k_{m}}
, by putting
X_{j}=(Y_{j-k_{m}+1},\dots,Y_{j})
for
j=0,1,2,\dots
.
This chain starts from
X_{0}=\mathbf{1}
and has transition matrix given as follows: for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{k_{m}}

\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-p_{j})^{x_{k_{m}-k_{j}+1}}&\text{if}&\,y_{k_{m}}=0\,,\,\,y_{i}=x_{i+1},\,i=1,\dots,k_{m}-1,\\
1-\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-p_{j})^{x_{k_{m}-k_{j}+1}}&\text{if}&\,y_{k_{m}}=1\,,\,\,y_{i}=x_{i+1},\,i=1,\dots,k_{m}-1.\end{array}\right.

(7)

Observe that
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})=1
, so
\mathbf{0}
is an absorbing state. We therefore consider the matrix
Q
, sub-matrix of
\mathcal{Q}
restricted to
\{0,1\}^{k_{m}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, which is precisely the one defined in (
1
).
Now let

\rho_{Q}=\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})=\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\text{ is an eigenvalue of }Q\}.

Since the entries of
Q
vary continuously as functions of
\mathbf{p}
, and
\rho_{Q}
varies continuously with respect to the entries of the matrix, we automatically have that
\rho_{Q}
varies continuously with
\mathbf{p}
.

The next lemma relates
\rho_{Q}
to the asymptotic behavior of
u_{n}
.

Lemma 8
.

The
\lim u_{n}^{1/n}
exists and equals
\rho_{Q}
.

Proof.

For matrices doubly indexed by
\{0,1\}^{k_{m}}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, consider the norm

\|A\|_{1}=\sup\{|\mu A|_{1}:|\mu|_{1}=1\}.

By Gelfand’s formula,

\rho_{Q}=\lim_{n}(\|Q^{n}\|_{1})^{1/n}.

We develop

\displaystyle\|Q^{n}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}}\Big|\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}\mu(\mathbf{x})Q^{n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Big|:\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}|\mu(\mathbf{x})|=1\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\sum_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}}\mu(\mathbf{x})Q^{n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}\mu(\mathbf{x})=1\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}(X_{n}\neq\mathbf{0}):\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}\mu(\mathbf{x})=1\bigg\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{\delta_{\bf 1}}(X_{n}\neq{\bf 0}),

where the second equality holds because the entries of
Q
are non-negative, and the last equality holds by attractiveness of the model (larger configurations at time
0
yield larger configurations at time
n
).

By definition of
X_{n}
, we have

\|Q^{n}\|_{1}={\mathbb{P}}_{\delta_{\mathbf{1}}}(\cup_{i=n-k_{m}+1}^{n}\{Y_{i}=1\}).

Since
{\mathbb{P}}_{\delta_{\mathbf{1}}}(Y_{i}=1)=u_{i}
, which is non-increasing in
i
, this yields

u_{n-k_{m}+1}\leq\|Q^{n}\|_{1}\leq k_{m}u_{n-k_{m}+1}.

Applying Gelfand’s formula, we get

\lim u_{n}^{1/n}=\rho_{Q}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 9
.

If
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})<1/d
, then
\theta(\mathbf{p})=0
and if
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})>1/d
, then
\theta(\mathbf{p})>0
.

Proof.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consider the random set

\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{w\in{\mathcal{V}}^{(n)}:\mathcal{C}(o)\cap{\mathcal{V}}^{\geq w}\neq\emptyset\}

and observe that
d^{n}u_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{A}_{n}|\leq d^{k_{m}}d^{n}u_{n}
.

We start by proving the first statement.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, on the event that
|\mathcal{C}(o)|=\infty
,
we have
\mathcal{A}_{n}\neq\emptyset
.
Hence,

\theta(\mathbf{p})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}_{n}\neq\emptyset)\leq d^{k_{m}}d^{n}u_{n}.

Assuming
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})<\frac{1}{d}
, we can take
\alpha<\frac{1}{d}
such that
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})<\alpha
.
By Lemma
8
, we have
u_{n}<\alpha^{n}
for all sufficiently large
n
.
Thus
d^{k_{m}}d^{n}u_{n}<d^{k_{m}}(d\alpha)^{n}\rightarrow 0
, implying that
\theta(\mathbf{p})=0
. This concludes the proof of the first part.

We now prove the second part.
Assuming that
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})>\frac{1}{d}
, we can take
\alpha>\frac{1}{d}
such that
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})>\alpha
. Since
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})=\lim_{n}u_{n}^{1/n}
, we can take
n
such that
u_{n}>\alpha^{n}
.
We will define a random subset
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(o)
distributed as a Galton-Watson branching process whose offspring size has the same distribution as
|\mathcal{A}_{n}|
.
Since

\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{A}_{n}|\geq d^{n}u_{n}>\left(\alpha d\right)^{n}>1,

we have

\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{T}|=\infty)>0
, and therefore

\theta(\mathbf{p})>0
.

Let us define
\mathcal{T}
.
The
0^{\text{th}}
generation of
\mathcal{T}
is the root
o
.
The offspring of a vertex
w
belonging to some generation of
\mathcal{T}
is defined as follows.
Define the random set

\mathcal{A}_{n}^{w}:=\{v\in\mathcal{V}^{w+n}:\mathcal{C}(w)\cap\mathcal{V}^{\geq v}\neq\emptyset\},

where
\mathcal{V}^{w+n}:=\{x\in\mathcal{V}^{\geq w}:d(w,x)=n\}
.
For each
v\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{w}
, select, among all vertices
u\in\mathcal{V}^{\geq v}
such that
w\to u
, one that minimizes
d(w,u)
, to belong to the offspring of
w
.
With this construction, the size of the offspring of
w
equals
|\mathcal{A}_{n}^{w}|
, which has the same distribution
|\mathcal{A}_{n}|
.
Moreover, the offspring of
w
is conditionally independent of the offspring of other vertices in the same generation, showing that
\mathcal{T}
is indeed distributed as a Galton-Watson branching process.
∎

The next lemma is a simple consequence of Lemma
9
.

Lemma 10
.

The critical surface of the multi-edge percolation model is contained in

\{\mathbf{p}\in[0,1/d^{k_{1}}]\times\dots\times[0,1/d^{k_{m}}]:\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})=1/d\}.

Proof.

If
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})>1/q
, by continuity of
\rho_{Q}
this inequality (and thus
\theta(\mathbf{p})>0
by Lemma
9
) holds in a neighbourhood of
\mathbf{p}
, which implies that
\mathbf{p}
is away from the critical surface.
On the other hand, if
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})<1/q
, by continuity of
\rho_{Q}
this inequality (and thus
\theta(\mathbf{p})=0
by Lemma
9
) holds in a neighbourhood of
\mathbf{p}
, which again implies that
\mathbf{p}
is away from the critical surface.
Therefore, on the critical surface, we can only have
\rho_{Q}=1/d
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Suppose
\gcd\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}=1
and
\mathbf{p}>\mathbf{0}
.
Then
Q
is primitive. In particular, by the Perron-Frobenius Theorem,
\rho_{Q}
is a real eigenvalue of
Q
.

Proof.

The proof is somewhat trivial and only uses modular arithmetic.
Assume
\gcd\{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\}=1
and let
M=k_{1}\cdots k_{m}
.
We know that there is an integer linear combination
a_{1}k_{1}+\dots+a_{m}k_{m}=1
.
In particular,
a_{1}k_{1}+\dots+a_{m}k_{m}=1\mod M
. Taking
\tilde{a}_{j}=a_{j}\mod M
, we find positive coefficients such that
\tilde{a}_{1}k_{1}+\dots+\tilde{a}_{m}k_{m}=bM+1
for some
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
So from any given position
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we can reach both
i+bM
and
i+bM+1
.
From there, we can reach
i+2bM
,
i+2bM+1
and
i+2bM+2
, and so on.
In the end, we can reach
i+k_{m}bM,\dots,i+k_{m}(bM+1)
.
Starting from any
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
, taking some
i\in\{1,\dots,k_{m}\}
such that
x_{i}\neq 0
, after
k_{m}(bM+2)
steps we can reach
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{1}
with positive probability, and from there we can reach any other
\mathbf{x}
within another
k_{m}
steps, also with positive probability, proving that
Q
is primitive.
∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

For
\mathbf{p}
on the critical surface, Lemma
10
says that
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p})=1/d
.
It remains to justify that
1/d
is actually an eigenvalue of
Q(\mathbf{p})
.
If
\mathbf{p}>\mathbf{0}
, then Lemma
11
says that
\rho_{Q}
is an eigenvalue of
Q(\mathbf{p})
and there is nothing left to prove.
So suppose
p_{j}=0
for some
j
.
Note that, since
\mathbf{p}
is on the critical surface, we have
\theta>0
in part of the neighbourhood of
\mathbf{p}
.
Consider
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}=\mathbf{p}+(\varepsilon,\dots,\varepsilon)
.
By monotonicity of
\theta
we have
\theta(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})>0
, thus
\rho_{Q}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\geq 1/q
, and by the previous lemma the matrix
Q(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})
has a real eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\geq 1/q
.
Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we see that
Q(\mathbf{p})
has a real eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\geq 1/d
.
Since
|\lambda|\leq\rho_{Q}
for every eigenvalue
\lambda
, we conclude that
\lambda_{0}=1/d
, concluding the proof.
∎

2.2
Recursive bounds

Here we will prove Theorem
2
. We are assuming that
m=2
and denoting
k_{1}=l
,
k_{2}=k
,
p_{1}=p
and
p_{2}=q
.

Recall the process
(Y_{n})_{n}
defined in Section
2.1
, and
u_{i}={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=1)
.
For
i\geq k

\displaystyle u_{i}={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=1)=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=0,Y_{i-l}=1,Y_{i}=1)+{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=1,Y_{i-l}=0,Y_{i}=1)+

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=1,Y_{i-l}=1,Y_{i}=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=0,Y_{i-l}=1)p+{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=1,Y_{i-l}=0)q+

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=1,Y_{i-l}=1)(p+q-pq)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-l}=1)p+{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=1)q-{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=Y_{i-l}=1)pq.

(12)

The third term prevents us from solving this recursive equation explicitly.
However, we can replace it by lower and upper bounds.
Observing that

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{{i-l}}=Y_{{i-k}}=1)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{{i-l}}=1)=u_{i-l},

we obtain

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle\geq p(1-q)u_{i-l}+qu_{i-k}.

(13)

In the other direction, observing that

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{{i-l}}=Y_{{i-k}}=1)={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{{i-l}}=1|Y_{{i-k}}=1)u_{i-k}\geq p^{k-l}u_{i-k},

we obtain

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle\leq pu_{i-l}+q(1-p^{k-l+1})u_{i-k}.

(14)

We can use these recursive equations to prove our bounds.

We start by proving the upper bound in Theorem
2
using (
13
).
Consider the recurrence

v_{i}=p(1-q)v_{i-l}+qv_{i-k}.

(15)

The polynomial associated to it is

g(x)=x^{k}-p(1-q)x^{k-l}-q.

By Descartes Rule of Signs, we have: (i) if
k
is even, then
g
has exactly two real roots, one positive and one negative, and (ii) if
k
is odd then it has exactly one real root and it is positive.
In both cases, there is a unique positive root, that we denote
\rho
.

Consider the sequence
v_{i}=c\rho^{i}
, which satisfies (
15
).
The constant
c>0
is taken so that
u_{i}\geq v_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
Now note that the same inequality can be extended for
i=k+1,k+2,\dots
by using (
13
) and (
15
) inductively.
In particular,
\lim_{n}u_{n}^{1/n}\geq\rho
.

Finally, since
g(x)<0
for
0<x<\rho
and
g(x)>0
for
x>\rho
, we have the following equivalence:

g({1}/{d})<0\Longleftrightarrow\rho>{1}/{d}.

Thus, for every
(p,q)
such that

\frac{1}{d^{k}}-p(1-q)\frac{1}{d^{k-l}}-q<0,

we have
\lim_{n}u_{n}^{1/n}>1/d
and thus, using Lemmas
8
and
9
,
\theta(p,q)>0
.
In other words, if

q>\frac{1-pd^{l}}{d^{k}-pd^{l}}

then
\theta(p,q)>0
.
This proves the upper bound of Theorem
2
.

Finally, let us prove the lower bound using (
14
).
Applying the exact same reasoning as above we get that, for every
(p,q)
such that

\frac{1}{d^{k}}-p\frac{1}{d^{k-l}}-q(1-p^{k-l+1})>0,

we have
\lim_{n}u_{n}^{1/n}<1/d
and thus
\theta(p,q)=0
.
In other words, if

q<\frac{1-pd^{l}}{d^{k}(1-p^{k-l+1})}

then
\theta(p,q)=0
.
This proves the lower bound of Theorem
2
.

2.3
Strict monotonicity of the critical curve

Here we prove Theorem
3
. Recall that we are assuming that
m=2
and denoting
k_{1}=l
,
k_{2}=k
,
p_{1}=p
and
p_{2}=q
.

Lemma 16
.

For all
q>0
, we have
p_{c}(q)<d^{-l}
.
For all
p>0
, we have
q_{c}(p)<d^{-k}
.

Proof.

We claim that

\rho_{Q}\geq(p^{k}+q^{l}-p^{k}q^{l})^{1/lk}
.
Indeed, let
x_{j}:={\mathbb{P}}(w\in\mathcal{C}(o))
for any
w\in{\mathcal{V}}^{(jkl)}
.
This sequence is positive supermultiplicative, that is
x_{i+j}\geq x_{i}x_{j}
for any
i,j\geq 0
.
Therefore
\lim_{j}x_{j}^{1/j}=\sup_{j}x_{j}^{1/j}
.
Now note that, among all possible open paths to connect two vertices that at distance
kl
apart, one consists of
k
short edges and another, independent of this one, consists of
l
long edges.
Hence,
x_{1}\geq p^{k}+q^{l}-p^{k}q^{l}
and therefore
\lim_{j}x_{j}^{1/j}\geq p^{k}+q^{l}-p^{k}q^{l}
.
On the other hand,

u_{jkl}\geq x_{j}

and thus
\lim_{n}u_{n}^{1/n}=\lim_{j}u_{jkl}^{1/jkl}\geq\lim_{j}(x_{j}^{1/j})^{1/kl}\geq(p^{k}+q^{l}-p^{k}q^{l})^{1/kl}
.
This proves the claim.

By Lemmas
8
and
9
,
\lim_{n}u_{n}^{1/n}>1/d
implies that
\theta(p,q)>0
, concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma 17
.

For
q=0
,
\rho_{Q}=p^{1/l}
.
For
p=0
,
\rho_{Q}=q^{1/k}
.

Proof.

For the first claim, it suffices to observe that

{\mathbb{P}}_{\delta_{\mathbf{1}}}(Y_{n}=1)=p^{\lceil n/l\rceil},

thus

\lim_{n}({\mathbb{P}}_{\delta_{\mathbf{1}}}(Y_{n}=1))^{1/n}=p^{1/l},

which by Lemma
8
means
\rho_{Q}=p^{1/l}
.
The proof of the second statement is identical.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
3
.
We can assume that
\gcd\{l,k\}=1
, for otherwise we can consider an equivalent model with parameters
l^{\prime}=l/n
,
k^{\prime}=k/n
and
d^{\prime}=d^{n}
, where
n=\gcd\{l,k\}
.
So we can use Lemma
11
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
q^{\prime}>q^{\prime\prime}>0
and suppose
p_{c}(q^{\prime\prime})>0
.
We want to prove that
p_{c}(q^{\prime})<p_{c}(q^{\prime\prime})
.
We can assume that
p_{c}(q^{\prime})>0
, otherwise the claim holds trivially.

By Lemma
10
,
\rho_{Q}(p_{c}(q^{\prime}),q^{\prime})=1/d
and
\rho_{Q}(p_{c}(q^{\prime\prime}),q^{\prime\prime})=1/d
.
Suppose by contradiction that
p_{c}(q^{\prime})=p_{c}(q^{\prime\prime})=p_{c}
.
By monotonicity of
\rho_{Q}
, we would have
\rho_{Q}(p_{c},q)=1/d
for every
q\in[q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime}]
.
By Lemma
11
,
1/d
would be an eigenvalue of
Q_{p_{c},q}
for every
q\in[q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime}]
, that is,
P_{Q}(1/d;p_{c},q)=0
for every
q\in[q^{\prime\prime},q^{\prime}]
.
Since
P_{Q}
is a polynomial in all its three variables, we would deduce that
P_{Q}(1/d;p_{c},q)=0
for every
q\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and in particular
\rho_{Q}(p_{c},q=0)=1/d
.
However, by Lemma
17
we have
\rho_{Q}(p_{c},q=0)=p_{c}^{1/l}
and by Lemma
16
we have
p_{c}^{1/l}<1/d
, a contradiction.

This proves the first claim of the theorem. The second claim is analogous.
∎

3
Convexity of the critical curve

In this section,
we provide a justification for Conjecture
1
based on the following ansatz regarding small values of
p
.
Let
d\geq 2
,
m=2
,
k_{1}=1
and
k_{2}=k\geq 2
be fixed.

Assumption 18
.

There exists a positive constant
C(k,d)
such that,
for all
p<\frac{1}{2d}
and
\frac{1}{2d^{k}}<q<\frac{2}{d^{k}}
, for all
n>k
,

{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n-k}=1|Y_{n-1}=1)\leq C(k,d)p.

(19)

We believe that this assumption is true.
Indeed, when
0<p\ll q
, knowing that
Y_{n-1}=1
, most likely the position
n-1
is reached by a chain of open edges that end with several long edges (of length
k
).
In such a configuration, we have
Y_{n-1-k}=1
and, in order to also have
Y_{n-k}=1
we need to open a short edge near the end of this chain of long edges, and this happens with probability proportional to
p
.

In the remainder of this section we justify Conjecture
1
under the above assumption.

Recall (
2.2
) and note that it can be rewritten as

u_{i}=pu_{i-1}+qu_{i-k}-pqr_{i}u_{i-1},

(20)

where

r_{i}:={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i-k}=1|Y_{i-1}=1)=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{i-1}(1)=X_{i-1}(k)=1|X_{i-1}\neq{\bf 0})}{{\mathbb{P}}(X_{i-1}(k)=1|X_{i-1}\neq{\bf 0})}.

Recall the construction of the
(k-1)
-step Markov chain
(X_{j})_{j}
done in Section
2.1
.
The theory of quasi-stationary distributions for finite Markov chains (see
Collet et al. 2012
for instance) tells us that

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{i}=\mathbf{x}|X_{i}\neq\mathbf{0})\to\lambda(\mathbf{x}),

where
\lambda
is a probability measure supported on
\{0,1\}^{k}\setminus\{(0,\dots,0)\}
, called Yaglom limit.
So the conditional probabilities
r_{i}
have a limit, namely

r_{p,q}:=\lim_{i}r_{i}=\frac{\lambda(\{\mathbf{x}:x_{1}=x_{k}=1\})}{\lambda(\{\mathbf{x}:x_{k}=1\})}.

Now notice that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have for sufficiently large
i
that

pu_{i-1}+qu_{i-k}-pq(r_{p,q}+\varepsilon)u_{i-1}\leq u_{i}\leq pu_{i-1}+qu_{i-k}-pq(r_{p,q}-\varepsilon)u_{i-1}.

(21)

Just as we obtained bounds on
q_{c}=q_{c}(p,d,1,k)
using recursive bounds (
13
) and
14
on
u_{i}
, we obtain, using the recursive bounds given by (
21
),

\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}-p(r_{p,q_{c}}-\varepsilon)d}\leq q_{c}\leq\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}-p(r_{p,q_{c}}+\varepsilon)d}.

Since these inequalities hold for every
\varepsilon>0
, we get

q_{c}=\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}-pr_{p,q_{c}}d}.

Since the critical curve is strictly above the line
q=\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}}
(by Theorem
2
), which in turn has slope
-d^{1-k}
, Conjecture
1
follows from the fact that

q_{c}^{\prime}(0)=-d^{1-k}.

It remains to justify this fact.
For
p
small and
r
also small, we expand the previous expression for
q_{c}
in terms of
p
and
r
as two independent variables, thus getting

q_{c}=\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}-prd}=d^{-k}+\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}-prd}\bigg]_{p=0,r=0}p+\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\frac{1-pd}{d^{k}-prd}\bigg]_{p=0,r=0}r+o(p+r).

The first partial derivative gives
-d^{1-k}
and the other vanishes.
Assumption (
19
) implies that
r_{p,q_{c}(p)}=O(p)
,
so the above expansion with
r=r_{p,q_{c}(p)}
gives

q_{c}=d^{-k}-d^{1-k}p+o(p)
, showing that

q_{c}^{\prime}(0)=-d^{1-k}
as claimed.
