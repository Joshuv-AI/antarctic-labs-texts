Boosting lignocellulosic bioethanol production: fermentation of corn stalk using a lignin-reduced brown midrib 2 maize mutant
Authors: Jinyue Wang, Huachuan Tuo, Peng Cheng, Fangying Chen, Yuxiao Li, Mengyu Yuan, Wenji Lin, Xiaonan Bao, Junjie Sun, Yaowen Guo, Ziwei Wang, Ying Wang, Haochuan Li, Xiaohuan Mu, Qin Zhang, Yanwen Yu, Mingyue Gou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe recalcitrance of lignin is a major bottleneck in the efficient conversion of lignocellulosic biomass to bioethanol. Genetic reduction of lignin content represents a key strategy to overcome this barrier. This study focuses on characterizing the brown midrib2 (bm2) maize mutant to assess its potential for improving bioethanol production.ResultsUsing a near-isogenic line (BC4F5) harboring the bm2 mutation, an 8.01% reduction in acid-insoluble lignin content in stalks was observed, with no significant change in cellulose or hemicellulose. This lignin reduction led to a 25.17% increase in glucose release upon sulfuric acid pretreatment. Most importantly, the bm2 mutant showed significantly higher lignocellulosic bioethanol yields: 3.05 g/L Ethanol 1 from the pretreatment hydrolysate (fermentation via Pichia stipites) and 25.88 g/L Ethanol 2 from the cellulose residue (fermentation via Saccharomyces cerevisiae), corresponding to 59.07% and 38.58% increases over the wild-type control, respectively.ConclusionsOur results provide direct evidence that the bm2 mutation enhances lignocellulosic ethanol production by reducing lignin content and improving saccharification efficiency. This work underscores the value of bm2 in breeding specialized corn varieties for sustainable biofuel feedstock.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-025-02722-6.

Background

Current global energy systems remain heavily dependent on non-renewable fossil fuels. According to the World Energy Outlook 2024 by the International Energy Agency (IEA, 2024), petroleum, coal, and natural gas collectively account for approximately 80% of the total primary energy supply. This heavy reliance on finite resources has led to the rapid depletion of fossil fuel reserves, contributing to unprecedented energy crises that threaten international political and economic stability. Furthermore, the entire fossil fuel value chain, spanning extraction, processing, transportation, and end-use combustion, generates substantial environmental externalities and causes widespread ecological degradation [1]. There is therefore an urgent need to develop alternative energy solutions that address both environmental sustainability and energy security to support sustainable societal development [2–5]. Among various alternatives, corn stover has attracted significant attention as an abundant source of lignocellulosic biomass and a green renewable resource [6, 7]. Its utilization as feedstock for biofermentation offers considerable economic benefits and represents a promising emerging industry. The conversion of lignocellulose into biofuel primarily involves pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, and microbial fermentation [8–10]. As a renewable and clean energy source, cellulosic ethanol offers several advantages, including broad raw material availability and environmental friendliness. Large-scale production of cellulosic ethanol can help reduce environmental pollution, promote energy diversification, and decrease dependence on traditional fossil fuels [11–13].

During lignocellulosic fermentation, cellulose and hemicellulose are hydrolyzed and fermented into ethanol, whereas lignin remains largely unconverted. More critically, lignin physically hinders the effective separation of cellulose and hemicellulose. Furthermore, lignin inhibits microbial growth and enzyme activity during saccharification and fermentation, significantly reducing ethanol conversion efficiency [14–16]. Conventional lignocellulosic fermentation processes therefore rely on pretreatment to mitigate the negative effects of lignin. However, pretreatment not only substantially increases production costs, but also generates inhibitory compounds, such as phenolics and furfurals, that suppress microbial activity and reduce ethanol productivity [17]. Beyond optimizing pretreatment technologies, reducing the intrinsic lignin content in stover has emerged as a promising strategy to improve bioethanol fermentation efficiency and lower overall production costs [18–20].

As a core component of plant secondary cell walls, lignin is primarily synthesized through the phenylpropanoid pathway, which involves multiple synthases and transcriptional regulators [21–24]. Recent studies have explored genetic modifications targeting key lignin biosynthesis genes in various plant species. For instance, caffeic acid o-methyltransferase (COMT), supported by the methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), catalyzes the methylation reaction of the C6-phenyl ring of monolignols. Suppression of SAMS and COMT in switchgrass significantly improved enzymatic hydrolysis and saccharification efficiency [25, 26]. Similarly, the expression of engineered MOMT4, a novel o-methyltransferase (OMT) variant that catalyzes the 4-o-methylation of monolignols, in poplar altered lignin monomer composition and cell wall structure, resulting in markedly enhanced bioethanol yields [27]. However, research on lignin-related genes in maize remains limited and has primarily focused on brown midrib (bm) mutants, which are named for their distinctive reddish-brown midrib phenotype. These bm mutants, which exhibit reduced lignin accumulation, have been commercially applied to improve silage digestibility [28, 29]. Recent studies have shown that targeted mutation of ZmNST2, a key regulatory gene in maize lignin biosynthesis, not only effectively reduces lignin content but also significantly decreases the accumulation of fermentation inhibitors, such as phenolic compounds and furfurals, during processing [18]. This genetic modification enhances cellulose hydrolysis into monosaccharides and ultimately improves ethanol production efficiency from corn stalk fermentation [18]. This breakthrough identifies a novel genetic target for optimizing bioethanol production from corn stover and provides crucial theoretical foundations for improving biomass crops through molecular breeding approaches.

To investigate the universal applicability of engineered lignin synthesis-related genes for enhancing bioethanol production from straw, a maize mutant, bm2, with significantly reduced lignin content was characterized. Map-based cloning revealed that the bm2 mutant carries a mutation in a methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene involved in lignin biosynthesis, which impairs lignin deposition [30]. Lignin biosynthesis relies on the phenylpropanoid pathway, which is catalyzed by a series of enzymes including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamic acid 4-hydroxylase (C4H), 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL), cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), caffeic acid 3-o-methyltransferase (COMT), and caffeoyl-CoA 3-o-methyltransferase (CCoAOMT) [21–24]. The BM2/MTHFR enzyme catalyzes the conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, ultimately affecting the biosynthesis of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), a methyl donor required by COMT and CCoAOMT for the methylation of the aromatic ring in monolignols [30] (Fig. 1a). In switchgrass and sorghum, alteration of SAM also affects lignin biosynthesis, and simultaneous downregulation of MTHFR and COMT even leads to growth defects [31–33]. Methods

Plant materials

The germplasm materials carrying the bm2 mutation used in this study were obtained from the Maize Genetics Cooperation Stock Center (MGCSC) in the United States. To establish genetically uniform experimental materials, bm2 mutants and wild-type (WT) controls with consistent genetic backgrounds were developed through successive backcrossing and hybridization. Specifically, bm2 plants were backcrossed to the inbred line B73 (WT) and subsequently self-pollinated to the BC4F5 generation, yielding both wild-type and homozygous mutant plants. All maize plants were cultivated under field conditions at the experimental stations of Henan Agricultural University, located in Zhengzhou, Henan Province, and Sanya, Hainan Province, China. Standard local agronomic practices were followed for field management throughout the growth period. Plant height was measured after pollination. Biomass was measured after the plants had been harvested and dried.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from 4-week-old maize seedling leaves using TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit. The RT-qPCR was performed using the LightCycler 480 II system (Roche) and ZmUBQ was used as the internal control for normalizing gene expression. The relative expression of mRNA was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method [34]. Primers used in the RT-qPCR analyses are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Dual-luciferase assay using maize protoplasts

The pGreenII 0800-LUC and pM999 vector was used to generate the reporter construct of firefly luciferase (FLUC) driven by the promoter of the BM2 gene (pBM2::FLUC) and the effector construct of ZmNST2 driven by the CaMV 35S promoter (p35S::ZmNST2). Leaves from 1-week-old seedlings of maize inbred line B73 were used to isolate protoplasts and then the protoplasts were transfected with the reporter and effector plasmids by a polyethylene glycol/calcium-mediated method following Cao et al. [35]. Luminescence quantification was performed using the Duo-Lite Luciferase Assay System (Vazyme Biotech, China) with the luminometer Mithras LB940 (Berthold, Germany). Luminescence values for FLUC were normalized against renilla luciferase (RLUC). Primers used in this assay are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Measurement of lignin and cellulose

Corn stalks were harvested, air-dried, crushed, and sieved to prepare samples for the measurement of lignin and cellulose content. Acid-insoluble lignin, acid-soluble lignin, cellulose (glucan), and hemicellulose (xylan) were quantified following the Standard Biomass Analytical Procedures established by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) of the United States [36, 37]. The experiment included five independent biological replicates, with each replicate consisting of a pooled sample from three individual plants.

Fermentation of corn stalk

The fermentation of corn stalk was carried out following a previously established method which included three parts: pretreatment, fermentation and measurement of Ethanol 1, and fermentation and measurement of Ethanol 2 [18].

Pretreatment

Pretreatment and fermentation were performed using five biological replicates of corn stover powder (20–40 mesh), with each replicate weighing 800 g. The powder was subjected to hydrolysis with 4% (v/v) dilute sulfuric acid at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:5 (w/v) and treated at 121 °C for 30 min. A portion of the resulting hydrolysate was used to quantify inhibitory compounds, including phenolic compounds and furfural that may affect fermentation. The remaining hydrolysate was detoxified with Ca(OH)2 and decolorized using macroporous adsorption resin prior to fermentation, following established procedures [38].

Fermentation and measurement of Ethanol 1

The hydrolysate was adjusted to a total reducing sugar concentration of 30 g/L, mainly composed of glucose and xylose, and used for ethanol fermentation with Pichia stipitis (strain CICC1960, China Center of Industrial Culture Collection), which has a good ability to utilize both glucose and xylose [39, 40]. The fermentation procedure was conducted as follows: a single colony of P. stipitis was inoculated into YPD liquid medium and cultivated in a shaking incubator at 30 °C and 150 rpm for 18–24 h. The culture was then adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of 1.0 and transferred into seed medium at a 10% (v/v) inoculation rate. After further incubation under the same conditions for 18–24 h, the seed culture was adjusted again to OD₆₀₀ = 1.0 and inoculated at 10% (v/v) into the fermentation medium. Fermentation was carried out for 72 h under the following conditions: shaking at 120 rpm for the first 12 h, 90 rpm from 12 to 24 h, and static incubation from 24 to 72 h with an air-tight seal. After fermentation, the broth was collected and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was distilled using a rotary evaporator (Shanghai Yarong RE-52AA; 50 °C, 90 rpm), and the ethanol concentration (denoted as Ethanol 1) was quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Waters 2695, USA) equipped with a refractive index detector (RID) and an Aminex HPX-87H column (300 mm × 7.8 mm, Bio-Rad, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 5 mM H2SO4 at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 60 °C, the RID temperature at 55 °C, and the injection volume was 20 μL.

Fermentation and measurement of Ethanol 2

The cellulose residue obtained after pretreatment was neutralized by adding 1% (w/v) NaHCO₃ solution at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 to eliminate residual sulfuric acid. The material was then rinsed with tap water until a neutral pH (6–7) was reached, dried, and subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis. Hydrolysis was performed by suspending the dried cellulose substrate at 20% (w/v) in citrate buffer (0.05 M, pH 4.8). Cellulase (Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, 400 U/mg) was added at a loading of 30 mg/g of dry substrate. The reaction was carried out in a thermostatic shaker at 50 °C and 250 rpm for 48 h. After hydrolysis, the mixture was centrifuged, and the glucose concentration in the supernatant was quantified via HPLC (Waters 2695, USA). The reducing sugar concentration was adjusted to 50 g/L by concentration using a rotary evaporator (Shanghai Yarong, Model RE-52AA) at 70 °C and 120 rpm, and the solution was used for ethanol fermentation with Saccharomyces cerevisiae, which is the industry-preferred yeast for its high ethanol yield and fermentation rate from glucose (S. cerevisiae P1 strain provided and preserved by the research group of Zhang Qin, Anhui Polytechnic University) [41]. A single colony of S. cerevisiae was inoculated into YPD liquid medium and grown in a shaking incubator at 30 °C and 160 rpm for 18–24 h. The culture was adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 1.0 and transferred into seed medium at a 10% (v/v) inoculation rate. After additional shaking under the same conditions for 18–24 h, the seed culture was again adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 1.0 and inoculated at 10% (v/v) into the fermentation medium. Fermentation was conducted over 72 h under the following conditions: shaking at 120 rpm for the first 12 h, 90 rpm from 12 to 24 h, and static incubation from 24 to 72 h with an air-tight seal. The fermented broth was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was distilled using a rotary evaporator (Shanghai Yarong RE-52AA; 50 °C, 90 rpm). The resulting ethanol concentration (denoted as Ethanol 2) was analyzed by HPLC under the same conditions described earlier.

Detection of glucose, xylose, and fermentative inhibitors in hydrolysate

Total phenolic content was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, following previously described procedures [42, 43]. Concentrations of glucose, xylose, and furfural were determined by HPLC equipped with a photodiode array detector (PDA) and an Aminex HPX-87H column (300 mm × 7.8 mm, Bio-Rad, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 5 mM H2SO4 flowing at a rate of 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 60 °C, and detection was performed at 210 nm with an injection volume of 20 µL.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with five biological replicates and three technical replicates. Statistical significance of differences between groups was assessed using Student’s t-test after the assumptions of normality and variance analysis (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01).

Results

BM2 is regulated by ZmNST2 in lignin biosynthesis

To verify whether ZmNST2 regulates the expression of BM2, total RNA was extracted from 4-week-old maize seedlings. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression of BM2 was significantly reduced in the zmnst2-1 and zmnst2-2 mutants compared to that in the WT plants (Fig. 1b). In addition, a dual-luciferase assay was conducted by transient expression of p35S::ZmNST2 and pBM2::FLUC in maize protoplasts. Compared to the control co-transformed with the empty vector, the FLUC/RLUC signal ratio of pBM2::FLUC was remarkably decreased when ZmNST2 overexpression constructs were co-transformed, indicating that BM2 is a target of ZmNST2 and ZmNST2 may affect lignin biosynthesis by transcriptionally activating BM2 (Fig. 1c).

Isolation of the bm2 mutant

Subsequently, a bm2 mutant line was obtained from MGCSC and identified through sequencing based on previously reported polymorphisms [30] (Supplemental Figure 1). Through successive backcrossing to the maize inbred line B73 and selfing cycles, combined with phenotypic selection for the brown midrib trait, BC4F5 generation plants segregating into bm2 homozygous mutants and WT controls were obtained (Fig. 2a). Compared to WT plants, the plant height and dry biomass of bm2 mutants were significantly reduced (Fig. 2b, c). Analysis of glucose, xylose and fermentation inhibitors content in fermentation broth

The aforementioned results confirmed a significant reduction in lignin content in the bm2 mutant stover. To examine whether this change influences cellulose hydrolysis, mature stalks of the bm2 mutant were subjected to a standard lignocellulosic ethanol fermentation process, including pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis. Analysis of fermentation intermediates via HPLC showed that the glucose content in the bm2 mutant hydrolysate was 25.17% higher than that of the wild-type (WT) controls, while the xylose content did not differ significantly (Fig. 4a). These results indicate that reduced lignin content in the bm2 mutant improves the efficiency of cellulose-to-glucose conversion without impairing hemicellulose hydrolysis. In the zmnst2 mutant, the contents of total phenolic compounds and furfural derivatives, known inhibitors of stalk fermentation, were significantly reduced. Discussion

The complex chemical interactions between lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose represent a major constraint on bioethanol fermentation efficiency. In addition to optimizing pretreatment processes, reducing the intrinsic lignin content of crop residues has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate this limitation [18–20]. This study demonstrates that BM2 is a transcriptional target of ZmNST2, and functional disruption of this gene leads to a significant decrease in acid-insoluble lignin content in crop stalks, accompanied by a marked improvement in bioethanol fermentation efficiency. Lignin reduction facilitates three critical enhancements: improved separation and purification of cellulose, increased efficiency of cellulose hydrolysis, and possible mitigation of phenolic compound-induced inhibition during microbial fermentation. Together, these effects synergistically improve the overall efficiency of lignocellulosic ethanol production. In this study, the reduced lignin content in the bm2 mutant could increase the biomass’s accessibility to chemicals during acid pretreatment, resulting in increased glucose levels and more extensive removal of hemicellulose in the acid hydrolysates. Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a microorganism that cannot utilize xylose, and the presence of xylose competes with glucose for transport and metabolic flux, which reduces the ethanol production efficiency of this yeast [44, 45]. We suspect that the lower content of hemicellulose in the hydrolyzed residue of bm2 may result in a relatively higher ratio of glucose but a lower ratio of xylose in the fermentative sugar solution after cellulase hydrolysis. Therefore, a higher concentration of Ethanol 2 produced in the bm2 sample could be due to a relatively higher glucose content and a less inhibitory effect of xylose on Saccharomyces cerevisiae metabolism. In addition, although no significant differences in phenolic compound and furfural derivative concentrations between bm2 and WT were observed, we could not exclude the impacts of specific phenolic compounds or other inhibitory compounds on the subsequent ethanol fermentation. It is possible that some specific inhibitory phenolic compound was reduced in the bm2 mutant, thereby benefiting ethanol production.

These improvements offer a more economical and efficient feedstock for industrial bioethanol production while contributing important scientific insights to sustainable biomass energy development. As a fundamental constituent of secondary cell walls, lignin plays essential roles in normal plant development and stress adaptation [46, 47]. In this study, the reduction of lignin in the bm2 mutant, while beneficial for saccharification, had adverse effects on plant height and straw biomass (which were reduced by 9.69% and 7.22%, respectively; Fig. 2). This trade-off could potentially be ameliorated through the construction of hybrids or the introduction of other genes that maintain the reduced lignin content and high ethanol yield of the bm2 mutant while restoring plant vigor. Furthermore, given the reported roles of lignin in disease resistance and lodging prevention [48–50], a thorough understanding of how lignin reduction influences crop productivity and stress tolerance is crucial for the practical application of this strategy.

Preliminary findings regarding the reduced expression level of BM2 in the zmnst2 mutant and the transcriptional activation of the BM2 promoter by ZmNST2 suggest a genetic interaction between BM2 and ZmNST2. However, the effects of these two genes on lignin biosynthesis and stalk fermentation efficiency differ significantly. For instance, both acid-insoluble lignin, acid-soluble lignin, and key fermentation inhibitors were substantially reduced in the zmnst2 mutant [18], whereas only acid-insoluble lignin was decreased in the bm2 mutant (Figs. 3 and 4). This difference likely stems from ZmNST2’s role as a master regulator of the phenylpropanoid pathway, while BM2 has a more specific catalytic function. Phenotypically, this is evidenced by the presence of the brown midrib only in the bm2 mutant (Fig. 2a). Compared to the zmnst2 mutant, the bm2 mutant has a less adverse effect on plant growth but a higher Ethanol 2 yield (Fig. 5) [18]. Therefore, attempting to mitigate its potential drawbacks represents a key next step for applying BM2 in lignocellulosic fermentation.

Given the complexity of the lignin biosynthetic regulatory network and the large number of genes involved, further studies should systematically identify genetic targets that can reduce stalk lignin content without adversely affecting plant growth, stress resilience, or grain yield. Such targets would hold significant potential for applications in the bioethanol industry. Lignocellulosic ethanol production represents a major direction for the development of renewable biofuels and aligns closely with national sustainable energy strategies. However, its industrialization is currently hindered by high costs and technically complex processes. This study addresses a core bottleneck, the inhibitory role of lignin, by utilizing the bm2 mutation to reduce lignin content. This strategy not only increases ethanol yield, but also streamlines pretreatment, improves enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation efficiency, lowers production costs, and facilitates industrialization. Ultimately, this approach supports more valuable uses of crop residues and can contribute to increased agricultural income.

Conclusions

In this study, the maize lignin synthase gene BM2 was further proved as a transcriptional target of ZmNST2, and the mutant of this gene exhibited an 8.01% reduction in acid-insoluble lignin content compared to the wild type, while no significant differences were observed in cellulose or hemicellulose content. Determination of sugar concentration after straw hydrolysis and ethanol content after fermentation showed that the bm2 mutant produced 25.17% more glucose than the wild type, along with significant increases in ethanol yield (59.07% for Ethanol 1 and 38.58% for Ethanol 2). These results demonstrate that reducing lignin content via the bm2 mutation enhances the efficiency of cellulose hydrolysis and improves the conversion rate of bioethanol fermentation, offering a promising strategy for developing maize varieties better suited for lignocellulosic ethanol production.
