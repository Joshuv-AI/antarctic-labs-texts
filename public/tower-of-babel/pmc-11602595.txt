The proteome and metabolome changes distinguish the effect of dietary energy levels on the development of ovary in chicken during sexual maturity
Authors: Lingqian Yin, Xinyu Wang, Donghao Zhang, Zhongzhen Lin, Yan Wang, Chunlin Yu, Hang Jie, Feng Xu, Chaowu Yang, Yiping Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To deeply understanding the impact of peripheral energy level on the development of ovaries during the sexual maturation of chicken, in this study, the ovaries and serum of sexually mature and immature chickens at the same age from different energy level groups were collected, and the proteome and metabolome were detected. The results of ovarian and serum metabolomics revealed that dietary energy levels affected the energy metabolism and fatty acid oxidation of ovary in chicken, including the up-regulated expression of dihydroacetone phosphate and α-linolenic acid in high energy level groups. The results of proteomics showed that peripheral energy levels affected the catecholamine biosynthesis and metabolism in ovary before sexual maturation. The integrating analysis revealed that increased energy flux may influence ovarian development by regulating cholesterol reserves and steroid hormone synthesis in the ovaries. In vitro, the cultivation of chicken primary granulosa cells showed that sterol carrier protein 2 played a role in fatty acid synthesis and metabolism but did not significantly affect progesterone synthesis. Overall, dietary energy levels may be involved in the development of the ovaries during sexual maturation by influencing energy metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and steroid hormone within the ovaries.

Introduction

Sexual maturity in birds typically signifies the point at which a bird is capable of reproduction. In poultry, the onset of laying in females and crow in males, as well as the complete development of secondary sexual characteristics, are commonly used as external markers of sexual maturity. The development of sexual maturity of commercial poultry is closely related to its production efficiency. The time of sexual maturity had a direct impact on the laying rate, egg quality and bone quality of avian species (Tumova and Gous, 2012; Dunn et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). Generally, birds that reach sexual maturity earlier were able to commence egg-laying sooner, thus enhancing overall production efficiency. Nonetheless, sexual premature may also result in impaired growth and diminished production performance in hens (Pishnamazi et al., 2014; Thofner et al., 2021). Therefore, understanding and controlling the sexual maturation process of birds is of great significance for improving the efficiency and sustainability of poultry production.

The completion of sexual maturity of birds depends on the complete development of the reproductive system. As an important part of the reproductive system of female birds, the ovary and its accessories were mainly responsible for ovulation and hormone secretion (Cui et al., 2022). The avian ovaries primarily comprise cortical and medullary tissues (Smith et al., 2008). During sexual maturity, there were visible changes in the volume and mass of the cortical in ovaries of birds. The main manifestations were the growth and development of follicles and the deposition of yolk (Pishnamazi et al., 2014). This process is regulated by numerous factors, including genetics, environment, endocrine and nutrition factors. The development and functional status of ovary varies among different poultry breeds due to their distinct genetic backgrounds, such as the local chicken breeds and commercial layer breeds (Tang et al., 2023). Among the environmental factors, light has been demonstrated to have a significant impact on the regulation of reproductive organ development in poultry (Cui et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2021). The endocrine studies mainly focus on the regulation of hormones and factors in hypothalamus, pituitary, gonadal, thyroid and liver on ovarian development (Richards, 1980; Macklon et al., 2006; Clelland and Peng, 2009). The hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is currently recognized as a crucial regulatory axis for the growth and development of ovary, which involves gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH), gonadotrophin-inhibitory hormone (GnIH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), growth hormone (GH) and other hormones (Maddineni et al., 2008; Hrabia et al., 2011; Tan et al., 2021). The thyroid-ovarian axis is another network that has been found to be involved in the regulation of ovarian function (Sechman, 2013). The liver-ovary axis has been found to be involved in the ovarian function as it is the main source of yolk precursor substances (Gloux et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2020).

In addition, peripheral nutrient levels also seem to have an impact on ovarian development. During sexual maturity, the levels of lipid and lipoprotein in circulating blood have been confirmed to be possibly related to gonad development (Peebles et al., 1996; Schienkiewitz et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2023a). The nutritional status of body affects the growth and development of the ovary and its appendages, the follicle environment, and the activity of oocytes. Energy deficiency may impede the growth of ovarian stroma, follicles, and oviduct, resulting in delayed sexual maturation (Renema et al., 1999; Lu et al., 2023), affect the synthesis of steroid hormones in follicles and the activity and maturation of oocyte (Montrezor and Urbinati, 2017; Costermans et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020). Therefore, adequate energy is essential for the growth and development of the ovaries and their accessories and the completion of sexual maturity. However, excessive energy reserves can also negatively impact the development and function of the ovaries. Feeding with higher energy levels during rapid growth and development often leads to accelerated ovarian development and precocious puberty, which may affect the reproductive function of the majority of animals (Gasser et al., 2006; Bauman, 2023; Catandi et al., 2024). In addition, other nutrients deficiency was also observed to affect ovarian function, including protein, microelement and amino acid (Zhang et al., 2020; Nwachukwu et al., 2021; Pokorska-Niewiada et al., 2021).

In general, the body energy state has an important influence on the growth, development and function of the ovary and its appendages, while the underlying mechanism regulating the development of ovary during sexual maturation in poultry remains confusing. In this study, Guangyuan Grey chicken was chosen as the experimental subject. This local breed is characterized by grey feather and skin. However, it exhibits low reproductive efficiency with late sexual maturity, low laying rate, and the average egg production at 300 days of age is around 77 (Yin et al., 2023a). The liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) technology was employed to systematically assess alterations in the ovarian proteome and metabolome, along with the circulating blood metabolome, pre- and post-sexual maturation of chickens under varying energy feeding conditions. The objective was to investigate the crucial factors influencing the development of ovary during sexual maturity, as well as elucidated the impact of the peripheral energy status on the development of ovary. These results would help us to further understand the regulatory mechanisms of gonad development during sexual maturation.

Materials and methods

This trial was conducted under the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Certification No. SYXK2019-187). All research work was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Sichuan Agricultural University Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics.

Animals and treatments

All chickens were raised at Sichuan Tianguan Ecological Farming and Animal Husbandry Co., LTD. A total of 1000 one-day old female chicks were randomly selected and raised to 10 weeks of age in a closed room controlled by illumination, temperature and humidity. At 10 weeks of age, 630 chickens with similar body weight were selected and transferred to single cage. All cages were at the same height from the light source. All chickens were divided into three groups, each consisting of five replicates, with 42 chickens per replicate. Each group fed a diet with different calculated metabolizable energy levels (group L: 2573 kcal/kg, group C: 2836 kcal/kg, group H: 3122 kcal/kg), and the other components of diets were consistent. The diets and feeding amounts of the three groups were in accordance with the recommendations of the Feeding Standards of Meat-type Chicken Breeder (2004) and were consistently maintained. All chickens were free to access water from nipple drinkers. The details of feed and nutritional composition can be found in the previous study (Yin et al., 2023a). The feeding trial spanned over a duration of eight weeks. From 19 weeks of age, all three groups were fed diets with the same energy level until the end of the trial.

Sample collection and preparation

From 18 weeks of age, the first egg of each chicken was recorded. After laying eggs, the hens and the sexual immature chickens were chosen for sample collection. In brief, the ranked top nine laying hens at the age of first egg and the nine sexually immature chickens at the same age in group L, C and H were selected randomly for blood collection from the wing veins (Table S1). The serum was isolated after centrifuging for 10 min at 3600 rpm at 4 °C. Subsequently, animals were submitted to respiratory anesthesia and decapitation. The ovaries were collected and removed follicles with a diameter greater than 1 mm, blood vessel and mesovarium. Every three ovaries were placed in a cryotube and stored in liquid nitrogen immediately. The samples were cryoground and separated in triplicate for subsequent analysis. The detail of sample collection and processing can be found in Fig. S1. According to the findings outlined in our most recent study, the physiological and biochemical parameters, as well as the onset of laying performance differed significantly between groups L and H (Yin et al., 2023a). Therefore, the serum and ovaries samples from group L and H were chose to performed proteomic and metabolomic analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics and bioinformatic analysis

Every six samples of serum (SMH, SML, SIH and SIL) and ovaries (OMH, OML, OIH and OIL) from the sexually mature and immature groups fed with high and low energy level diets were submitted to untargeted metabolomics analysis. The preparation of ovaries samples were based on the reported methods (Yin et al., 2023b). The serum samples were thawed at 4 °C. 100 μL of each sample was added to 400 μL of precooled methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) for protein removal. Centrifuge at 14,000 xg for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was dried in a vacuum centrifuge and dissolved in 100 µL acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) solvent. It was then centrifuged at 14,000 xg for 15 min. at 4 °C. The supernatant was used for ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) analysis. Equal sample sizes were pooled to prepare four quality control (QC) samples.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed in the Agilent 1290 Infinity LC (Agilent, USA) and AB Triple TOF 6600 (AB SCIEX, USA). The sample was analyzed using the Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra-high performance liquid chromatography system equipped with an HILIC column. The samples were submitted to gradient elution program and then separated by Agilent 1290 Infinity LC. Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX) was used to perform mass spectrometry in positive and negative ion modes of electrospray ionization. The primary m/z detection range was 60 to 1000 Da. The secondary m/z detection range was 25 to 1000 Da. The cumulative scanning time for primary mass spectrometry was 0.20 s/spectrum, and for secondary mass spectrometry was 0.05 s/spectrum. Information dependent acquisition mode (IDA) was employed for obtaining the secondary mass spectrometry.

The raw MS data was converted to MzXML files using ProteoWizard MSConvert, then imported into XCMS software with parameters: centWave m/z-10 ppm, peak width=c (10,60), pre filter=c (10, 100) for peak identification and extraction, peak integration and alignment, and retention time correction. Peaks were categorized using the parameters: bandwidth of 5, mass width of 0.025, and minimum fraction of 0.5. R-package CAMERA (Collection of Algorithms for Metabolite Profile Annotation) were performed to annotate isotopes and adducts. In the extracted ion features, only at least one set of variables with non-zero measurements greater than 50 % were retained. For the identification of metabolites, the retention time, molecular weight (within a molecular weight error of 25 ppm), MS/MS spectra, and collision energy information of the metabolites were compared with the in-house database established by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. After sum-normalization and quality control, the data was submitted to R package (ropls) for further analysis, including the quantity statistics and classification of identified metabolites, analysis of differences in metabolite abundance between groups, bioinformatics analysis of differential abundance metabolites (DMs). Univariate statistical analysis was used to calculate the fold change between groups and the significance P values. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was used to evaluate model stability and calculate the variable importance in the projection (VIP) values in the OPLS-DA model. OPLS-DA VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were set as the criteria to screen metabolites with significant abundance changes between groups. Student's t-test was conducted to assess the significance of differences between two groups of independent samples. Based on Pearson correlation analysis, the correlation between DMs was analyzed. Biological function prediction of DMs was conducted in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (KEGG, http://www.kegg.jp/).

4D-label-free quantitative proteomics and bioinformatic analysis

Every three samples of ovaries from the sexually mature and immature groups fed with high and low energy level diet were used to determine proteomic profile (OMH. OML, OIH and OIL, Figure S1). The samples were lysed with SDT buffer (4 % SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) to extract proteins, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Bio-Rad, USA). The proteins was degraded with trypsin following the Filter-Aided Sample Preparation (FASP) method (Wisniewski et al., 2009). Peptides were digested and desalted on a C18 column (Empore™ SPE C18, 7 mm diameter, 3 ml volume, Sigma), then concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 40 µL of 0.1 % (v/v) formic acid.

LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Easy nLC system (Thermo Scientific). The peptides were loaded onto a reverse-phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap100) connected to a reverse-phase chromatography column (Thermo Scientific Easy column) in aqueous 0.1 % formic acid solution. The peptides were separated with a linear gradient of 84 % acetonitrile and 0.1 % formic acid at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The mass spectrometer operated in positive ion mode, acquiring data using a data-dependent top 20 method with dynamic selection of precursor ions for higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation scans (300 to 1800 m/z). Key parameters included AGC target of 1e6, maximum injection time of 50 ms, and dynamic exclusion set at 30 s. Full scan measurements were done at 60, 000 resolution for m/z 200, while HCD spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15,000 for m/z 200 with an isolation width of 1.5 m/z.

The MS raw data were combined and searched using the MaxQuant 1.6.14 for identification and quantitation analysis. The reference database was uniprot Gallusgallus_51560_20230524 with reverse pattern. The label free quantitation (LFQ) was used for protein quantification. Posterior error probability (PEP) was used to assess the reliability of protein identification and the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 was set for protein and peptide screening. The criterion as fold change (FC) > 2 and P value < 0.05 was set to screen for significantly differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). To identify homologous sequences, the sequences of DEPs were searched using NCBI BLAST+ client software and InterProScan. Then, gene ontology (GO) term mapping and KEGG pathway mapping were performed for biological function prediction analysis. Enrichment analysis was applied based on the Fisher’ exact test. Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing was further applied to adjust derived P values. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) information was retrieved from STRING software (http://string-db.org/). The results of PPI networks were visualized by Cytoscape software (http://www.cytoscape.org/, version 3.2.1).

Integrated analysis of proteome and metabolome

The results of the proteomics and metabolomics were integrated and analyzed to identify potential molecular regulatory network associated with ovarian development during sexual maturation from heterogeneous data. In this study, the KEGG enrichment pathways of DEPs and DMs were applied for joint analysis via R software (R Version 3.5.1). The quantitative information of DEPs and DMs was standardized by log2 transformation for Pearson correlation analysis in R software. The results were visualized by CytoScape (Version 3.5.1).

Cell isolation, culture and transfection

The sexual mature Guangyuan Grey female chicken was chosen for primary granulosa cells collection. Chickens were submitted to respiratory anesthesia and sacrificed by exsanguinations. The hierarchical follicles were collected. After removing the blood vessels and connective tissue, follicles were rinsed with phosphate buffered solution (PBS, Solarbio, China). A small incision was made on the surface of the follicle to expel excess yolk, followed by washing through shaking in cold PBS. After inverting the follicles, the granulosa cell layer was meticulously separated and collected in clean PBS. The granulosa cell layer was sheared and digested in collagenase type II (Biofroxx, Germany) for 10 min. The mixture was filtered using a 70 μm pore size cell strainers to gain pure granulosa cells. The cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10 % fetal calf serum (Gibco, USA) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA). Subsequently, the cells were incubated at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 and 95 % humidity. Granulosa cells were transfected with three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting SCP2, which were synthesized by GenePharma Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The specific sequences of the siRNAs were provided in Table S2. The siRNA oligonucleotides were diluted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) and Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The resulting mixture was subsequently added to the cultured cells.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from granulosa cells using a Trizol-based method, and cDNA reverse was conducted according to the instructions of PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR) was performed in a 10 μL reaction system. with the specific reagents and their respective dosages detailed in Table S3. The mRNA expression level of genes associated with fatty acid synthesis and metabolism (ACACA, SREBP1, FASN, ATGL, PPARγ, CPT1A) and steroid hormones synthesis (StAR, HSD3B1 and CYP11A11) were detected. All primers were designed using Prime Premier 6 and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with the primer sequences provided in Table S4. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the endogenous control. The relative expression level of the genes was calculated by 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting analysis

Proteins were extracted from ovary and granulosa cell following the guidelines of the total protein extraction kit (Bestbio, Jiangsu, China). The protein concentration was determined with the BCA assay kit (Bio-Rad, USA). The primary antibodies synthesized in ZEN-BIOSCIENCE (Chengdu, China) and ABclonal (Wuhan, China), including cytochrome P450 family 19 subfamily A member 1 (CYP19A1, 1:000, 71.51 % specificity), Tenascin C (TNC, 1:1000, 72.30 % specificity), integrin subunit beta 1 (ITGB1,1:1000, 84.21 % specificity), proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1, 1:1000, 86.89 % specificity), steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR, 1:750, 72.36 % specificity), cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1, 1:500, 51.43 % specificity), hydroxy-delta-5-steroid dehydrogenase, 3 beta- and steroid delta-isomerase 1 (HSD3B1, 1:1000, 60.4 %) and sterol carrier protein 2 (SCP2, 1:1000, 65.07 % specificity). Beta-Tubulin was used as an internal control. The detailed experimental procedures were referred to the previous report (Tang et al., 2023). The ChemiScope 6200 (CLINX, Shanghai, China) was utilized for quantitative analysis. Quantification of the western blot strips was performed using Image J 1.51j8 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA). Data processing was conducted with Excel 2021 (Microsoft, Redmond, USA) and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (Graphpad Software, San Diego, USA).

Immunofluorescence assay

The ovaries and follicles from sexually mature Guangyuan Grey hens were sampled, washed with PBS, and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. Tissue was embedded in paraffin using standard methods. The wax block was trimmed and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Slices underwent deparaffinization and rehydration in three changes of xylene for 10 min each, followed by three changes of pure ethanol for 5 min each. Slices were placed in preheated citric acid antigen repair solution and heated under high pressure. A 3 % BSA solution blocked non-specific binding at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibody SCP2 rabbit pAb (1:50, 65.07 % specificity) was added and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Slides were washed in PBS for 5 min and shaken on a decoloring shaker three times for 5 min each. Secondary antibody fluorescein Isothiocyanate goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:200) was added and incubated at room temperature for 50 min in the dark. 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole solution was added and incubated at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. Washing steps in PBS were repeated. Autofluorescence quencher B solution was applied for five minutes, followed by rinsing with running water for 10 min. Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade medium, and images were collected using fluorescent microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Oil red O staining

The cells cultured in vitro were collected and discarded the culture medium. Wash three times with pre-cooled PBS, followed by 4 % paraformaldehyde fix at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated in 0.3 % filtered oil red O for 1 h. Excess oil red O was removed by washing with 60 % isopropanol for 10 sec, and the cells were subsequently stained with hematoxylin for 2 min. PBS was added for microscopy using an Olympus microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Oil red O was extracted with 100 % isopropanol for 20 min. The supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for optical density detection, with absorbance measured at 510 nm.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The cell culture fluid was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the supernatant. Progesterone content was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (mlbio, Shanghai, China) per the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 50 μL of standard solution was added to a 96-well plate, followed by 10 μL of test sample and 40 μL of sample diluent. Each well received 100 μL of horseradish peroxidase-labeled antibody except blanks. The wells were sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After discarding the liquid, wells were dried on absorbent paper, washed with buffer for 60 sec, and repeated five times. Substrate A and B (50 μL) were added to each well and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min. Finally, 50 μL of stop solution was added, and OD values were measured at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using SPSS 26 (SPSS for Windows, Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student's test were utilized to examine the statistical differences between the means of groups. Least significant difference (LSD) was performed for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 indicated that the results were statistically significant and extremely significant. GraphPad Prism 9.0 (Graphpad Software Inc, USA) was applied for visualizing graphical representations of results.

Results

Metabolomics analysis of the ovary

In this study, the ovaries of high- and low-energy feeding groups before and after sexual maturity (OMH, OIH, OML, OIL) were used for metabolome detection. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) and the results of PCA indicated small variation caused by the instrumental errors during the experiment (Figure S2), demonstrating good reproducibility. A collective of 1563 metabolites were detected under both positive and negative ion modes.

The difference of energy metabolism in ovary between different energy level groups

In the comparisons fed with high- and low-energy level diets, the high abundance of metabolites of high-energy groups were related to energy homeostasis and lipid metabolism, such as PE(P-18:0/18:1), PC(14:0/16:0), lovastatin hydroxy acid and glycocholic acid (Fig. 1A). In terms of the high abundance of metabolites in low-energy groups, most of them were related to glucose metabolism, glycolysis, and energy production (Fig. 1A). This phenomenon was more prominent in the sexual mature groups, such as the higher abundance of dihydroxyacetone phosphate, D-glucose 6-phosphate, alpha-D-Glucose 1-phosphate and citrate in the ovary form sexually immature chicken with low energy level (OIL). KEGG enrichment analysis confirmed these findings, highlighting pathways such as FoxO signaling, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, and glycerolipid metabolism (Fig. 1B). The GnRH signaling pathway was found to be a notable enrichment pathway of DMs with significantly upregulated arachidonic acid in the ovary from sexually mature chicken with high energy level (OMH). The differences in energy requirements in ovaries between sexual maturity and immaturity groups

The comparisons between sexually mature and immature stages revealed distinct DMs related to energy requirements and angiogenesis. Ovarian energy demand surged post-maturity, indicated by increased levels of octanoylcarnitine, linoleoylcarnitine, and oleoyl-l-carnitine in OMH and the ovary from sexually mature chicken with low energy level (OML) (Fig. 2A). High levels of alpha-D-Glucose 1-phosphate and D-glucose 6-phosphate in OIL suggested enhanced glycolytic activity in the low-energy group. KEGG enrichment analysis showed DMs concentrated in unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism, glycerophoric metabolism, and starch and sucrose metabolism (Fig. 2B). Metabolomics analysis of the serum

The metabolite profile of serum in high- and low-energy level dietary groups at sexual maturity and immaturity (SIH, SMH, SIL, SML) was analyzed. The results of quality control, including TIC and PCA, indicated small variation caused by the instrumental errors during the experiment (Figure S3). A total of 1513 metabolites was identified in four groups. In the serum of high-energy level groups, numerous DMs involved in fatty acid oxidation were identified, such as myristoyl-l-carnitine, oleoyl-l-carnitine, linoleoylcarnitine, (2r)-3-hydroxyisovaleroylcarnitine and acetylcarnitine, carnitine (Figure S4A). The DMs between different energy level groups were mainly enriched in biosynthesis of amino acids, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism and primary bile acid biosynthesis (Fig. 3A). In terms of sexual maturity, more phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine derivates were enriched in the sexually mature group instead of the immature, including PE(16:0/0:0), LPE(18:1/0:0), PC (16:1(9Z)/16:1(9Z)) and PC(14:0/16:0) (Figure S4B). The DMs between sexual maturity and immaturity were mainly enriched in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, biosynthesis of amino acids and ABC transporters (Fig. 3B). Proteome analysis of ovary

The proteome of ovaries in groups fed with high- and low-energy levels before and after sexual maturity was identified (OIH, OMH, OIL, OML). The results of quality control indicated that the mass error of peptide segments was mainly distributed within 10 ppm, and more than 70.92 % of peptide segments with Andromeda scores above 60 in MS2 spectra (Figure S5), indicating low deviation during data acquisition and reliable identification results. A total of 1390 DEPs were found in four groups, which were selected for further functional analysis.

The difference of catecholamine biosynthesis and metabolism in ovary between different energy level groups

In the comparisons of different energy level diets, 63 and 129 DEPs were identified respectively. The DEPs related to catecholamine biosynthesis and metabolism, involving solute carrier family 6 member 2 (SLC6A2), GTP cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1), tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and synuclein alpha (SNCA) were significantly enriched between different energy groups (Figure S6A). These DEPs were primarily involved in norepinephrine transport, catecholamine uptake and dopamine transport (Fig. 4A). In addition, the DEPs involving in lipid transport, such as low density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) and CCZ1 vacuolar protein trafficking and biogenesis associated homolog (CCZ1), were significantly up-regulated in OMH compared to OML. The DEPs involving steroid hormone receptor binding, including TATA-box binding protein associated factor 10 (TAF10), SMAD family member 3 (SMAD3) and aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT), were up-regulated in OIH compared to OIL. Notably, SMAD3 was enriched in TGF-beta signaling pathway, common to both comparisons (Fig. 4B). Steroid hormone biosynthesis was significantly enriched with the up-regulated hydroxysteroid 11-beta dehydrogenase 1 like (HSD11B1L) in OMH. The difference of vitellogenesis and extracellular matrix formation in ovary between sexual maturity and immaturity groups

In comparation of sexual maturity and immaturity, a total of 362 and 836 DEPs were identified respectively. In mature groups, up-regulation of vitellogenin 1-3 (VTG1-3), apovitellenin 1 (APOV1), LDLR, and fatty acid binding protein 5 (FABP5) suggested a link to vitellogenesis (Figure S6B). Integrated analysis of proteomic and metabolomics of ovary and serum

The investigation into the regulation of energy metabolism during ovarian development utilized an integrated analysis of KEGG pathways derived from proteomic and metabolomic data of the ovary. A total of 5, 9, 14 and 35 pathways were identified in the four comparations, respectively. The top 10 KEGG pathway were illustrated in Figure S7. Besides, the integration analysis of serum and ovarian metabolomics were also performed to pinpoint key metabolic molecules that mediate the regulation of energy metabolism on ovarian development. There were 17, 14, 26 and 36 pathways were identified across the four comparative groups. The top 10 KEGG pathway were presented in Figure S8. The aforementioned KEGG pathways were further analyzed concerning various energy levels and stages of sexual maturation.

Integrating proteome and serum metabolomics revealed the biosynthesis pathway of unsaturated fatty acids across all comparations (Table 2). Alterations of steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism during sexual maturity

Steroid hormone synthesis increased in high energy groups. Proteomic results showed significant enrichment of steroid hormone receptor binding, metabolism, and biosynthesis in sexual immature groups. Notably, steroid hormone biosynthesis was enriched in high energy comparisons versus low energy. The DEPs and DMs involved in these processes were the significantly up-regulated TAF10, SMAD3, ARNT, HSD3B1 and the highly abundance β-estradiol 3-sulfate, cholesteryl sulfate, estrone 3-sulfate in OIH. In particular, cholesteryl sulfate was found to be highly abundant in serum and ovary of OIH. To further assess the function of SCP2 in the synthesis of steroid hormones in granulosa cells, the steroid hormone biosynthesis in granulosa cells was explored. After Si-SCP2-1 interference, the significantly decreased of the mRNA expression of HSD3B1 and CYP11A1 was observed (Fig. 8H). Discussion

The ovaries are essential for the reproductive and endocrine functions in female animals. Their development and condition directly influence the reproductive capabilities of females, particularly in egg-laying poultry. The rearing phase is critical for achieving sexual maturity in birds. In our previous studies, it was found that varying dietary energy levels during this period could impact the growth of chicken reproductive organs, thus affecting their sexual maturation (Yin et al., 2023a). However, the specific mechanisms through which energy levels influence ovarian development remain elusive. In this study, the ovaries of chickens fed diets with different energy levels during the growing period were studied at sexual maturity and immaturity. The differences of ovarian proteome and metabolome in different states were compared and the metabolic factors and pathways promoting ovarian development were also analyzed in combination with serum metabolome. The results implied that peripheral energy levels may participate in ovarian development by modulating energy storage, catecholamine metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid and steroid hormone synthesis.

Adequate energy levels are crucial for successful reproduction. During sexual maturity, the body reallocated energy for gonadal development (Grossmann and Wittert, 2021). Variations in energy metabolism occurred pre- and post-maturity across species (Das et al., 2017; Ding et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2024). Previous research showed that diets with different energy levels have a significant effect on the development of ovary, follicle and oviduct in chickens (Lu et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2023a). This study confirmed these findings in ovaries from different energy level groups before and after sexual maturity. Compared to the immature group, more DEPs and DMs were enriched in energy metabolism pathway in the high and low energy level dietary groups at sexual maturity, including PKM, PDHB, CKI and PRPS2 enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, galactose metabolism, citrate cycle (TCA cycle) and pentose phosphate pathway in sexual mature groups. In addition, the differences in energy metabolism were also observed in the comparisons of sexual maturity and immaturity. In particular, PDHB and aldo-keto reductase (AKR) were significantly up-regulated in sexual mature groups. As a vital metabolic enzyme connecting glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, PDH regulates cellular energy homeostasis (Jiang et al., 2023). The upregulation of PDHB may be related to the increase in energy demand. AKR was an aldo-ketone reductase, which was found to be involved in the synthesis and metabolism of steroid hormones in cells (Detlefsen et al., 2022). Besides, it was also been confirmed to participate in the metabolism of fructose, mannose and galactose (Everard et al., 1997; Kim et al., 2011; Banerjee et al., 2023). It could be speculated that AKR may play an important role in mediating the regulation of energy levels in steroid hormone synthesis and metabolism. After integrating multiple omics data, an important metabolite, dihydroxyacetone phosphate, was identified to be significantly upregulated in OIH and OML. Dihydroxyacetone phosphate was an important intermediate product of glycolysis, which mediated the conversion of glycerol and glucose in animals (Hajra et al., 2000). It was also reported to be involved in the cellular perception of glucose availability via the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway (Orozco et al., 2020). These findings highlighted the alterations in the requirements and utilization of energy in the ovaries during sexual maturity.

Catecholamines are a class of neurotransmitters containing catechol structures, mainly including dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine and their derivatives (Banerjee et al., 2023). Catecholamines were mainly involved in the contraction and relaxation of blood vessels and smooth muscle, regulation of nervous system sensitivity, energy metabolism and hormone secretion, which were primarily mediated by α and β adrenergic receptors and dopamine receptors (Tank and Wong, 2015). Studies confirmed catecholamine neuron subsets influenced ovarian development during puberty (Dees et al., 2006). Norepinephrine regulated follicular development in the chicken ovary by promoting granulosa cell proliferation and influenced the mRNA expression of StAR and FSHR (Jiang et al., 2022). Diet-induced obesity was found to increase norepinephrine levels in the ovaries (Fernandois et al., 2023). This study found that differential enrichment of DEPs related to dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine synthesis in high and low energy groups at different maturity stages. SLC6A2 was the gene encoding the norepinephrine transporter, which regulated the bioavailability of NE (Bonisch and Bruss, 2006). In this study, SLC6A2 was only observed in OIH, indicating greater norepinephrine utilization before sexual maturity. Dopamine, traditionally linked to neural pathways, also contributed to gonadal development (Patterson Rosa et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2023). Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) was the rate limiting enzyme in catecholamine biosynthesis, which catalyzed the formation of tyramine, dopamine, adrenaline, and noradrenaline from tyrosine (Molinoff and Axelrod, 1971). Herein, TH was found to be up-regulated in low energy group compared with the higher, and was abundant in sexually mature groups as against the immature groups. Besides, as an essential coenzyme for TH (Ichinose et al., 1999), GCH1 has also been identified in the OML. Overall, dopamine and norepinephrine may mediate energy induced the development and maturation of ovary.

Unsaturated fatty acids play crucial roles in reproduction, influencing oocyte maturation, follicle development, prostaglandin and steroidogenesis, and cell membrane integrity (Zeron et al., 2002; Tahara and Yano, 2004; Ishak et al., 2008; Mahla et al., 2023). Their role in sexual maturity has been shown across species (Smith et al., 1989; Pilevar et al., 2011; Grzesiak et al., 2020). Key unsaturated fatty acids, including arachidonic acid (ARA), linoleic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid, were reported to regulate follicle and oocyte function (Pati and Habibi, 2002; Nikoloff et al., 2017; Maillard et al., 2018; Roura et al., 2018). Both in the metabolic profile of serum and ovaries, docosatetraenoic acid, arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and palmitic acid were identified. Compared to low energy level groups, docosahexaenoic acid and ARA were highly abundant in OMH, while linoleic acid and palmitic acid were highly abundant in OIH. These results suggested that dietary energy levels altered the composition of unsaturated fatty acids in the ovaries. The protein SCP2, which may regulate unsaturated fatty acid metabolism (Atshaves et al., 2003), was detected in various groups. Moreover, we found that the expression of genes related to fatty acid synthesis and metabolism were increased in granulosa cells after SCP2 interference, confirming SCP2′s role in this process. In granulosa cells of chicken ovarian follicles, the expression of SCP2 was high, but as the follicles mature, the expression of SCP2 decreased (Pfeifer et al., 1993b). Similarly, in this study, we also found that as the hierarchical follicles matured, the expression of SCP2 in the granulosa cells gradually decreased. In addition, the highest level of SCP2 was observed in granulosa cells at F6. It was inferred that SCP2 may be involved in follicle selection, potentially regulated by estrogen (Pfeifer et al., 1993b). Furthermore, SCP2 was implicated in the synthesis of steroid hormones (Pfeifer et al., 1993a). This study supported that SCP2 interference in granulosa cells significantly reduced the mRNA and protein levels of CYP11A1 and HSD3B1, linked to steroid synthesis, with only a minor decrease in progesterone levels. These results suggested SCP2′s direct role in pregnenolone synthesis in mitochondria, rather than affecting downstream progesterone synthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum.

Ovarian function integrity tightly correlates with steroid hormone synthesis. Estradiol and progesterone were the two main types of steroid hormones, primarily synthesized by the granulosa cells and theca cells of the follicle (Xiao et al., 2019). Previous studies showed significant fluctuations in E2 and progesterone levels in chicken serum pre- and post-sexual maturity (Hrabia et al., 2011; Bahry et al., 2023). Cholesterol, sourced mainly from plasma lipoproteins, serves as a crucial precursor for steroid hormone synthesis (Dong and Tong, 2019). Under the catalysis of cytochrome P450 family members and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, cholesterol was utilized to synthesize estradiol and progesterone (Lee and Bahr, 1990). This study identified differential expression of numerous cytochrome P450 family members across different energy level groups. Before sexual maturity, CYP11A1 and CYP17A1 were up-regulated in the high energy level group, while CYP19A1 was up-regulated in the low energy level group. After sexual maturity, CYP17A1 and CYP19A1 were upregulated in the high-energy group, while CYP11A1 was upregulated in the low energy level group. Differential expression of hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases was also noted, with HSD3B elevated in high-energy groups pre-maturity and upregulated in low-energy groups post-maturity. Another hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases, HSD17B2 was observed to be increase in low-energy level groups overall process. Given the roles of HSD3B1, CYP11A1, and CYP19A1 in progesterone and estradiol synthesis (Sechman et al., 2020), high peripheral energy levels may regulate the synthesis of steroid hormones in the ovary by affecting the expression of steroidogenesis genes. Moreover, there were differences in the impact of elevated periphery energy levels on the synthesis of estradiol and progesterone around sexual maturity. Notably, TAF10, ARNT, and SMAD3 were significantly upregulated in high-energy groups pre-maturity, implicating their roles in estradiol synthesis and estrogen receptor binding (Hao et al., 2007; Li et al., 2010; An et al., 2021). Hence, it was speculated that high energy levels intake may regulate the development of ovary by affecting the production of sex hormones and the downstream signaling pathways before sexual maturity (Fig. 10). Additionally, elevated β-estradiol 3-sulfate, estrone 3-sulfate, and cholesteryl sulfate were found in OIH, indicating their roles in sex hormone and cholesterol metabolism(Shimizu et al., 2003; Bjerregaard-Olesen et al., 2016; Ma and Yates, 2018). These results suggested that high energy level intake promotes the storage of cholesterol and the production of sex hormones in the ovaries, potentially explaining high energy-induced precocious puberty.Fig. 10Schematic of steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism in the sexual immature groups fed with high energy level diet.Fig 10

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the effects of feeding different energy levels of diet during the rearing period on ovarian development in chickens during the process of sexual maturity. The proteomic and metabolomic results of the ovaries and serum indicated that differences in peripheral energy levels affect glucose and lipid metabolism, unsaturated fatty acids, and steroid hormone synthesis in the ovaries pre- and post-sexual maturation. The differential abundance metabolite, dihydroxyacetone phosphate, may mediate the glucose-lipid interconversion and steroid production across different energy groups. Prior to sexual maturity, catecholamine synthesis and metabolic pathways, including norepinephrine and dopamine, may regulate ovarian development in response to peripheral energy levels. Furthermore, the cholesterol stores and the synthesis of steroid hormones were observed in high energy level groups due to the up-regulated SCP2, APOV1, LDLR, SMAD3, HSD3B1, β-estradiol 3-sulfate, cholesteryl sulfate, estrone 3-sulfate. SCP2 was abundantly expressed in the theca cells and granulosa cells of follicles at different developmental stages, participating in lipid transport, cholesterol metabolism, and steroid hormone synthesis in granulosa cells.
