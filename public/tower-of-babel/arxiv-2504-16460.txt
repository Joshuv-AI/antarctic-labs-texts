T-VEC: A Telecom-Specific Vectorization Model with Enhanced Semantic Understanding via Deep Triplet Loss Fine-Tuning
Vignesh Ethiraj, Sidhanth Menon, Divya Vijay

Abstract
The specialized vocabulary and complex concepts of the telecommunications industry present significant challenges for standard Natural Language Processing models. Generic text embeddings often fail to capture telecom-specific semantics, hindering downstream task performance. We introduce T-VEC (Telecom Vectorization Model), a novel embedding model tailored for the telecom domain through deep fine-tuning. Developed by NetoAI, T-VEC is created by adapting the state-of-the-art gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct model using a triplet loss objective on a meticulously curated, large-scale dataset of telecom-specific data. Crucially, this process involved substantial modification of weights across 338 layers of the base model, ensuring deep integration of domain knowledge, far exceeding superficial adaptation techniques. We quantify this deep change via weight difference analysis. A key contribution is the development and open-sourcing (MIT License) of the first dedicated telecom-specific tokenizer, enhancing the handling of industry jargon. T-VEC achieves a leading average MTEB score (0.825) compared to established models and demonstrates vastly superior performance (0.9380 vs. <0.07) on our internal telecom-specific triplet evaluation benchmark, indicating an exceptional grasp of domain-specific nuances, visually confirmed by improved embedding separation. This work positions NetoAI at the forefront of telecom AI innovation, providing the community with a powerful, deeply adapted, open-source tool.



I
INTRODUCTION

Text embeddings, dense vector representations of text, are fundamental to modern Natural Language Processing (NLP), powering applications ranging from semantic search to chatbots [1]. While general-purpose embedding models pre-trained on vast web corpora have achieved remarkable success [2], [3], their efficacy often diminishes significantly when applied to specialized domains characterized by unique vocabularies, ambiguous terminology, and intricate contextual dependencies [4].

The telecommunications (telecom) sector exemplifies such a domain. It features a rapidly evolving landscape of technical standards (e.g., 5G, LTE, SDN, NFV), complex network architectures (RAN, Core, Transport), specific operational jargon (e.g., handover, RRC states, QoS parameters), and dense regulatory frameworks. Standard NLP models frequently struggle to disambiguate terms like "cell" (wireless vs. spreadsheet), "sector" (antenna vs. business unit), or "RAN" vs. "Core Network," and fail to grasp the semantics of countless domain-specific acronyms (e.g., MME, SMF, UPF, gNB, eNB, O-RAN). This semantic gap severely limits the effectiveness of AI solutions for critical telecom tasks such as automated network fault analysis from logs, intelligent customer support, semantic search across technical documentation (standards, manuals), and compliance verification. Bridging this gap requires models with a deep, nuanced understanding of telecom language.

To address this critical need, we developed T-VEC (Telecom Vectorization Model), a high-performance text embedding model specifically engineered for the telecommunications domain via deep fine-tuning. Our contributions are threefold:

1.

Curated Telecom Dataset:
We meticulously curated a large-scale, high-quality dataset specifically covering diverse telecom concepts, terminologies, standards, and operational contexts. This involved over three months of intensive manual effort by domain experts to generate relevant text pairs and triplets.

2.

Deep Triplet Loss Fine-Tuning:
We fine-tuned the powerful gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct model [5] on this dataset using a triplet loss objective. Critically, this was not a superficial adaptation; we modified weights across
338 layers
of the transformer architecture, enabling the model to learn deep telecom-specific semantic relationships.

3.

Open-Source Telecom Tokenizer:
We developed and are releasing the first open-source (MIT License) tokenizer specifically trained on telecom corpora. Inherently part of T-VEC, it significantly improves the handling of industry-specific jargon compared to general-purpose tokenizers.

Our comprehensive evaluations demonstrate that T-VEC achieves state-of-the-art performance on standard benchmarks (leading MTEB average score) while exhibiting dramatically superior understanding of telecom semantics compared to its base model and other strong general-purpose models on our domain-specific benchmarks. This work underscores the necessity and effectiveness of deep domain adaptation and positions NetoAI as a leader in providing powerful, open, and practical AI tools for the telecommunications industry.

II
RELATED WORK

Generating effective text representations is a cornerstone of NLP. Sentence-Transformers [1] popularized the use of siamese network structures with pre-trained models like BERT [2] to create semantically meaningful sentence embeddings. Subsequent research has produced numerous powerful general-purpose embedding models, including MPNet-based models (’all-mpnet-base-v2’), E5 [10] (’e5-base-v2’), BGE [11] (’bge-base-en-v1.5’), GTE [12] (’gte-base’, now including Qwen2-based variants like our base model), Jina Embeddings [13], and instruction-tuned models like Instructor [14]. These models excel on general language tasks due to training on vast, diverse web corpora.

However, the limitations of general models in specialized domains are well-documented [4]. Domain adaptation techniques aim to bridge this gap. Methods range from continued pre-training on domain corpora [4], [16] to fine-tuning on domain-specific downstream tasks. Fine-tuning using objectives like triplet loss [17] is particularly effective for learning fine-grained semantic similarity relevant to tasks like search and retrieval within a specific domain. While some domain adaptations might only involve fine-tuning the final layers or adding small adapter modules, our work pursues
deep fine-tuning
, modifying a significant portion of the base model’s weights to fundamentally reshape its representational space for the target domain.

Our base model, gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct [5], part of the Qwen2 family, provides a strong foundation with its robust multilingual capabilities and instruction-following performance. T-VEC differentiates itself through: (1) its exclusive focus on the telecom domain, (2) the scale and expert curation of its telecom-specific fine-tuning dataset based on triplet loss, (3) the
depth
of architectural modification (338 layers adapted), and (4) the release of a novel, open-source telecom-specific tokenizer.

III
METHODOLOGY

III-A

Base Model

We selected Alibaba-NLP/gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct as the base model. This powerful transformer model features 1.5 billion parameters, produces embeddings of dimension 1536, and supports a maximum input sequence length of 32,000 tokens. Its foundation in the Qwen2 family [5] provides strong multilingual capabilities and instruction-following performance, making it an excellent starting point for domain-specific fine-tuning.

III-B

Telecom-Specific Dataset Curation

The effectiveness of T-VEC stems directly from its specialized fine-tuning dataset. Over a period of three months, telecom domain experts at NetoAI curated an extensive dataset comprising over 100,000 triplets for training, specifically designed to teach the model telecom-specific semantics. This curation process involved the following key aspects:

•

Topic Coverage:
Ensuring comprehensive representation across diverse telecom areas, including wireless standards (3G, 4G/LTE, 5G NR, Wi-Fi), network domains (Access, RAN, Core, Transport, OSS/BSS), network functions (e.g., AMF, SMF, UPF, gNB, eNB), operational procedures (e.g., fault management, performance monitoring, configuration), vendor documentation, technical specifications, and regulatory guidelines.

•

Vocabulary Emphasis:
Prioritizing the correct interpretation of telecom-specific jargon, acronyms, and potentially ambiguous terms, always considering their contextual meanings within the telecom domain.

•

Triplet Generation:
Crafting high-quality triplets
(a,p,n)
—anchor, positive, and negative—is essential for effective training with triplet loss. Positives were chosen to be semantically close to the anchor within the telecom context, while negatives were contextually relevant but either incorrect or semantically distinct. The creation of informative triplets, especially challenging "hard negatives" (i.e., those semantically close yet incorrect), played a critical role in enhancing model robustness.

III-C

Fine-tuning with Triplet Loss

We adapted the gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct model using the triplet loss objective function [17]. The goal is to learn an embedding function
E(\cdot)
that maps text inputs to vectors such that semantically similar items are close in the vector space, while dissimilar items are far apart. For a triplet
(a,p,n)
, the loss is defined as:

L(a,p,n)=\max(0,d(E(a),E(p))-d(E(a),E(n))+\alpha)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a distance metric (we used cosine distance, so
d(x,y)=1-\text{cosine\_similarity}(x,y)
), and
\alpha
is a margin hyperparameter. The loss penalizes the model if the negative sample
n
is not further away from the anchor
a
than the positive sample
p
by at least the margin
\alpha
. This encourages the model to create distinct clusters for related telecom concepts. Our training incorporated query-like anchors (questions, short descriptions) to specifically boost performance on search, retrieval, and reranking tasks within the telecom domain.

III-D

Deep Model Architecture Modifications

A crucial aspect of our approach is the
depth
of fine-tuning. Unlike methods that only update output layers or add small adapters, our fine-tuning process involved modifying the weights of
338 layers
within the gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct model architecture. This represents a substantial portion of the model’s parameters.

To quantify the extent of these changes, we calculated the L2 norm of the difference between the fine-tuned weights and the original base model weights for each modified parameter tensor. The average L2 norm difference across these tensors was computed to be
0.7735
. This significant value indicates that the fine-tuning process induced substantial changes throughout the adapted layers, fundamentally altering the model’s internal representations to align with telecom-specific semantics.

Figure
1
provides visual evidence of this deep adaptation, showing the top 20 layers/parameter tensors with the most significant weight changes (measured by L2 norm difference). The affected layers span various parts of the transformer blocks, including MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron) gate, up, and down projection weights across numerous layers (e.g., layers 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8…). This distributed pattern confirms that T-VEC’s specialization is not superficial but results from deep, pervasive modifications across the model architecture. This extensive fine-tuning is key to enabling T-VEC to capture and represent nuanced semantic relationships specific to the telecommunications domain far more effectively than models relying solely on pre-trained weights or minimal fine-tuning.

V
DISCUSSION

The evaluation results strongly validate our deep fine-tuning approach. By adapting the gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct model using triplet loss on a large, expert-curated telecom dataset and modifying a substantial portion of its architecture (338 layers, Avg. L2 Diff 0.7735, see Fig.
1
), we produced T-VEC, a model demonstrating both robust general semantic capabilities (evidenced by top MTEB score and strong STS performance) and exceptional domain specialization.

The crux of our findings lies in the dramatic improvement shown in the telecom-specific triplet evaluation (Table
IV
, score 0.9380 vs. <0.07 for others) and the clear visual confirmation of improved semantic separation (Figures
2
,
3
). This unequivocally demonstrates that the intensive data curation and, critically, the
deep
fine-tuning process successfully imbued T-VEC with a nuanced understanding of telecom language far exceeding that of general-purpose models or even its highly capable base model. This deep adaptation is vital for building effective AI solutions in the telecom industry, enabling tasks like accurate semantic search over dense technical standards, correlating network events based on log semantics, and providing intelligent automation for customer support or network operations that understand the user’s specific telecom context.

The observed performance decrease on the general AllNLI task (Table
III
) is an important result, highlighting the well-known specialization-generalization trade-off common in domain adaptation. While T-VEC excels in its target telecom domain, its intense focus may lead to reduced performance on unrelated, general tasks compared to models explicitly optimized for those broader tasks. This reinforces the argument for using domain-specific models like T-VEC when high accuracy and nuanced understanding of specialized language are required, rather than relying solely on general-purpose models.

Our decision to release T-VEC and its novel telecom-specific tokenizer under the permissive MIT license provides a valuable, practical asset to the AI and telecom communities. This fosters transparency, enables verification of our results, and empowers researchers and engineers to build upon our work, accelerating the development of advanced AI applications within the telecommunications sector. This contribution positions NetoAI as a leader in practical, domain-focused AI innovation.

VI
CONCLUSION

We introduced T-VEC, a 1.5B parameter telecom-specific text embedding model derived from gte-Qwen2-1.5B-instruct. Through extensive and deep fine-tuning – adapting 338 layers – on a large, manually curated telecom dataset using triplet loss, T-VEC achieves state-of-the-art performance on telecom-specific semantic understanding tasks, significantly outperforming general models and its own base model. It simultaneously maintains strong performance on standard benchmarks, evidenced by its leading MTEB average score. T-VEC, including its novel, open-source telecom-specific tokenizer, is released under an MIT license. This work underscores the efficacy and necessity of deep domain adaptation for specialized fields like telecommunications and provides a powerful, openly accessible tool for the industry, affirming NetoAI’s leadership in specialized AI solutions.

Future work includes expanding the T-VEC fine-tuning dataset with even more diverse telecom data, exploring architectural enhancements specifically for telecom NLP tasks, and deploying T-VEC in real-world telecom applications to further validate and refine its capabilities.
