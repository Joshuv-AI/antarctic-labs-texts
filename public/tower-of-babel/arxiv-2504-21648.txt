Moment estimates for solutions of SPDEs with Lévy colored noise
Raluca M. Balan, Juan J. Jiménez

Abstract
In this article, we continue the investigations initiated by the first author in [ 2 ] related to the study of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) with Lévy colored noise on ℝ + × ℝ d \mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d} . This noise is constructed from a Lévy white noise on ℝ + × ℝ d \mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d} (which is in turn built from a Poisson random measure on ℝ + × ℝ d × ℝ 0 \mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0} with intensity d ​ t ​ d ​ x ​ ν ​ ( d ​ z ) dtdx\nu(dz) ), using the convolution with a suitable spatial kernel κ \kappa . We assume that the Lévy measure ν \nu has finite variance. Therefore, the stochastic integral with respect to this noise is constructed similarly to the integral with respect to the spatially-homogeneous Gaussian case considered in [ 14 ] . Using Rosenthal’s inequality, we provide an upper bound for the p p -th moment of the stochastic integral with respect to the Lévy colored noise, which allows us to identify sufficient conditions for the solution of an SPDE driven by this noise to have higher order moments. We first analyze this question for the linear SPDE (in which the noise enters in an additive way), considering as examples the stochastic heat and wave equations in any dimension d d , for three examples of kernels κ \kappa : the heat kernel, the Riesz kernel, and the Bessel kernel. Then, we present a general theory for a non-linear SPDE with Lipschitz coefficients, and perform a detailed analysis in the case of the heat equation (in dimension d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 ), and wave equation (in dimension d ≤ 3 d\leq 3 ), for the same kernels κ \kappa . We show that the solution of each of these equations has a finite upper Lyapounov exponent of order p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 , and in some cases, is weakly intermittent (in the sense of [ 19 ] ). In the case of the parabolic/hyperbolic Anderson model with Lévy colored noise, we provide the Poisson chaos expansion of the solution and the explicit form of the second-order Lyapounov exponent.

Introduction

The study of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) is an active area of research in probability theory, which has been growing tremendously in the last four decades, since the publication of Walsh’ lecture notes
[
41
]
. Many investigations focus on equations driven by Gaussian noise, which may be white or colored in space or time.

A significant portion of the literature is dedicated to equations driven by a pure-jump
space-time Lévy white noise
, which does not have a Gaussian component. This noise is given by a collection
\{\Lambda(A);A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
of random variables indexed by the bounded Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and is defined by:

\Lambda(A)=b|A|+\int_{A\times\{|z|\leq 1\}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{A\times\{|z|>1\}}zN(dt,dx,dz),

where
b\in\mathbb{R}
,
N
is a Poisson random measure (PRM) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
of intensity
\mu(dt,dx,dz)=dtdx\nu(dz)
,
\widehat{N}(F)=N(F)-\mu(F)
is the compensated version of
N
, for Borel sets
F
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
, and
\nu
is a
Lévy measure
on
\mathbb{R}
i.e.
\nu(\{0\})=0
and

\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}(|z|^{2}\wedge 1)\nu(dz)<\infty.

(1)

In this theory,
\mathbb{R}_{0}:=\mathbb{R}\verb 2\2\{0\}
is equipped with the distance
d(x,y)=|\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{y}|
, so that “bounded sets” in
\mathbb{R}_{0}
are in fact those bounded away from
0
. One motivation for this comes from the symmetric
\alpha
-stable Lévy motion
\{Z(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
, which has Lévy measure:

\nu_{\alpha}(dz)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha|z|^{-\alpha-1}1_{\{|z|>0\}}dz\quad\mbox{with}\quad\alpha\in(0,2),

and for which the underlying PRM has points of the form
(T_{i},Z_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha})
where
(T_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. uniform on
[0,1]
,
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are Rademacher variables, and
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are the points of a Poisson process on
[0,\infty)
of intensity 1. Since
|Z_{i}|\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, and a PRM has a finite number of points in any compact set, we must exclude 0 from the “jumps space”.

The moments of
\Lambda(A)
are closely related to integrability properties of
\nu
: for any
p>0
,
\mathbb{E}|\Lambda(A)|^{p}<\infty
if and only if
\int_{|z|>1}|z|^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty
. Therefore, if we assume that

m_{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{2}\nu(dz)<\infty,

(2)

then
\Lambda(A)
has finite variance.
If in addition, we choose
b=-\int_{|z|>1}z\nu(dz)
, we obtain a centered process:

L(A)=\int_{A\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3)

Since

\mathbb{E}\big[L(A)L(B)\big]=m_{2}|A\cap B|,

the theory of stochastic integration with respect to
L
is the same as for the space-time Gaussian white noise
W
. Moreover, SPDEs with noise
L
share many similarities with the corresponding equations driven by
W
, with some notable exceptions, such as: the existence of higher-order moments, and the regularity of the sample paths.
Throughout this work, we will assume that (
2
) holds, and we let
L
be defined by (
3
).

We start by recalling the major contributions for SPDEs with general Lévy white noise, for which the techniques are very different compared to the Gaussian case. Then we return to the finite variance case, which is the topic of the present article. Note that for our literature review, we mention only the references based on Walsh’s random field approach. For a different approach to SPDEs with Lévy noise, we refer the reader to the monograph
[
36
]
by Peszat and Zabczyk, and the references therein.

The
stochastic heat equation
with general Lévy noise:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u(t,x)+\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\dot{\Lambda}(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(4)

was studied for the first time in
[
38
]
, where it was shown that if
\sigma
is Lipschitz, (
4
) has a unique solution, and this solution has a finite
p
-th moment, provided that
p<1+\frac{2}{d}
and

m_{p}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty.

(5)

Condition (
5
) is not satisfied by the
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise, with
\alpha\in(0,2)
. In
[
33
]
, Mueller proved the existence of a solution of (
4
) driven by an
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise with
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and a non-Lipschitz function
\sigma(u)=u^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>0
. The same problem for the case
\alpha\in(1,2)
was treated by Mytnik in
[
34
]
. The compact support property for solutions to stochastic
heat equations with
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise has been recently established in
[
23
]
.

The problem of existence of a solution of a general SPDE with
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise and
Lipschitz function
\sigma
remained open until 2014. In
[
1
]
, the first author of this paper established the existence of a solution of a large class of SPDEs on bounded domains, driven by the
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise. This was achieved by solving first the equation with truncated noise, obtained by removing the “large jumps” that exceed a flat level
N
.

In 2017, Chong
[
11
]
, introduced a space-dependent truncation technique, and improved significantly the results of
[
1
,
38
]
by showing the existence of a solution of (
4
). His condition:

\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}|z|^{p}\nu(dz)<\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{\{|z|>1\}}|z|^{q}\nu(dz)<\infty\quad\mbox{for some $0<q\leq p$},

(6)

uses different exponents
p
and
q
for the “small jumps” region
\{|z|\leq 1\}
versus the “large jumps” region
\{|z|>1\}
, and is satisfied by the
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise.
Subsequently, the authors of
[
12
]
proved the regularity of the
sample paths of the solution of (
4
) as a process with values in a Sobolev space.
The uniqueness of the solution of (
4
) was proved in
[
10
]
, in the case when
\sigma(u)=\lambda u
for some
\lambda>0
, and
\nu(-\infty,0)=0
. As far as we know, for a general Lipschitz function
\sigma
, the uniqueness of the solution remains an open problem.

In the hyperbolic case, the first author of this paper established in
[
3
]
the existence of a solution of the
stochastic wave equation
:

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\dot{\Lambda}(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad(d\leq 2)

(7)

using the same truncation and mixed-exponent techniques as in
[
11
]
.
In the recent article
[
26
]
, the second author of this paper proved the uniqueness of the solution of (
7
).

We return now to the case of the finite variance Lévy white noise. The existence and uniqueness of the solution of an SPDE with noise
\dot{L}
was established in
[
5
]
, using the same techniques as for the space-time Gaussian white noise. The higher order moments of the solution were studied in
[
4
]
for the wave equation in dimension
d=1
. The linear stochastic heat and wave equations with this type of noise have random field solutions only in spatial dimension
d=1
, because
the fundamental solutions of these equations are not square-integrable on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
d\geq 2
.
We recall that a
space-time Gaussian white noise
is a zero-mean Gaussian process
\{W(A);A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
with covariance:

\mathbb{E}[W(A)W(B)]=|A\cap B|,

where
|A|
is the Lebesque measure of
A
. This can be extended to an isonormal Gaussian process indexed by
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which in turn induces a zero-mean Gaussian process
W=\{W_{t}(\varphi);t\geq 0,\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
with covariance:

\mathbb{E}[W_{t}(\varphi)W_{s}(\psi)]=(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(x)dx.

In the case of Gaussian noise, a well-established procedure for obtaining random field solutions to SPDEs in higher dimensions is to introduce some “color” in the spatial structure of the noise. This idea appeared for the first time in
[
16
]
and
[
32
]
for the stochastic wave equation in dimension
d=2
, and was extended to general SPDEs in
[
14
]
. More precisely, in these references, the noise is given by a zero-mean Gaussian process
F=\{F_{t}(\varphi);t\geq 0,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
with covariance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(\varphi)F_{s}(\psi)]

\displaystyle=(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(y)f(x-y)dxdy,

(8)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
is the Fourier transform of a tempered measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In
[
14
]
, it was proved that if the measure
\mu
satisfies the (now-called)
Dalang’s condition
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\mu(d\xi)<\infty,

(9)

then the stochastic heat and wave equations with noise
\dot{W}
have random field solutions for any
d\geq 1
, respectively for any
d\leq 3
. The extension to case
d\geq 4
was studied in
[
13
]
.

The noise
\dot{F}
induces a
stationary random distribution
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an object which was introduced by Itô in
[
25
]
in the case
d=1
. Its defining property is that the covariance is invariant under translations. In higher dimensions, such a process was studied by Yaglom in
[
42
]
, who called it a
homogeneous generalized random field
. For this reason,
F
is usually referred to as a “spatially-homogenous” Gaussian noise.

An important observation is that
F
can be constructed from
W
, by letting

F_{t}(\varphi)=W_{t}(\varphi*\kappa),\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(10)

for a suitable kernel
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
for which
\varphi*\kappa\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is because the covariance of
F
:

\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(\varphi)F_{s}(\psi)]=(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi*\kappa)(x)(\psi*\kappa)(x)dx,

matches the right hand-side of (
8
), provided that
\kappa*\widetilde{\kappa}=f
, where
\widetilde{\kappa}(x)=\kappa(-x)
.

On the other hand, we have the following expression which involves only
\mu
:

\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(\varphi)F_{s}(\psi)]=(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(\xi)}\mu(d\xi),

where
\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\varphi(x)dx
is the Fourier transform of
\varphi
. This leads to the following alternative definition:

F_{t}(\varphi)=\widehat{W}_{t}(\mathcal{F}\varphi\cdot\mathcal{F}\kappa),\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

provided that the kernel
\kappa
satisfies the condition:
\mathcal{F}\varphi\cdot\mathcal{F}\kappa\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Here
\widehat{W}
is the “Fourier transform” of
W
, defined by
W_{t}(\varphi)=\widehat{W}_{t}(\mathcal{F}\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of
\mathbb{C}
-valued square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The existence of random field solutions of linear SPDEs with noise
\dot{F}
, of the form

\mathcal{L}u(t,x)=\dot{F}(t,x),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for a second-order partial differential operator
\mathcal{L}
,
can be done using only Fourier transforms, without requiring the existence of the kernel
f
. But the study of non-linear equations with Lipschitz coefficients
\sigma
and
b
:

\mathcal{L}u(t,x)=\sigma\big(u(t,x)\big)\dot{F}(t,x)+b\big(u(t,x)\big),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

is greatly simplified,
if
we assume the existence of the kernel
f
. This foundational study
was performed by Dalang in the seminal article
[
14
]
, and is now the backbone for a huge area of research.

In 2015, at Dalang’s suggestion, the first author of this paper introduced in
[
2
]
a similar “coloring” procedure in the Lévy case, and defined the
Lévy colored noise
:

X_{t}(\varphi)=\widehat{L}_{t}(\mathcal{F}\varphi\cdot\mathcal{F}\kappa),\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

based on the “Fourier transform”
\widehat{L}
of
L
.
Article
[
2
]
developed an integration theory with respect to
X
, which is then applied for the study of linear SPDEs with noise
X
.

After 10 years, in the present article, we continue this line of investigation by considering the next step: the study of non-linear equations with noise
X
. As in the Gaussian case considered in
[
14
]
, we assume for simplicity the existence of the kernel
f
. More precisely, by analogy with (
10
), we define the Lévy colored noise
X
by:

X_{t}(\varphi)=L_{t}(\varphi*\kappa),\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We postpone the general case (which does not require the existence of
f
) for future work.

Throughout this article, we assume that
\nu
satisfies condition (
2
).
Therefore, modulo the constant
m_{2}
, the covariance of
L
is the same as that of
W
:

\mathbb{E}[L_{t}(\varphi)L_{s}(\psi)]=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(x)dx,

and hence, the covariance of
X
will be (modulo the constant
m_{2}
) the same as that of
F
:

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}(\varphi)X_{s}(\psi)]=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi*\kappa)(x)(\psi*\kappa)(x)dx.

This means that most problems that were studied in
[
14
]
and are related to second-order moments can be extended immediately to the Lévy case. The difficulties arise when we start to consider higher order moments. These moments will be the main focus and constitute the novelty of the present paper.

More precisely, the goal of the present paper is to provide some sufficient conditions under which the solution of the non-linear SPDE:

\mathcal{L}u(t,x)=\sigma(u(t,x))\dot{X}(t,x)+b(u(t,x))\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(11)

(with Lipschitz functions
\sigma
and
b
), has moments of order
p\geq 2
, and analyze the asymptotic behaviour of these moments when
t\to\infty
.
We present the general theory for the case when
\mathcal{L}
is a second order partial differential operator (with coefficients that do not depend on the space variable), and then
perform a detailed analysis in the case when
\mathcal{L}
is the heat or wave operator, for three examples of kernels
\kappa
: the heat kernel, the Riesz kernel, and the Bessel kernel.

We denote by
G_{t}
the fundamental solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
, with the convention:

G_{t}(x)=0\quad\mbox{if $t<0$}.

(12)

We suppose that the initial condition is given by the constant
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
.
More precisely, we assume that the solution
w
of the equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
with the same initial conditions is the constant function
w(t,x)=\eta
.
For instance,

(i) if
\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta
is the heat operator and the initial condition is
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)=\eta
, then
w(t,x)=(G_{t}*u_{0})(x)=\eta
;

(ii) if
\mathcal{L}=\partial_{tt}-\Delta
is the wave operator and the initial conditions are
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)=\eta
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(0,x)=v_{0}=0
, then
w(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(G_{t}*u_{0})(x)+(G_{t}*v_{0})(x)=\eta
.

Following
[
41
,
14
]
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

A predictable process
u=\{u(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is a
(mild) solution
of equation (
11
) with constant initial condition
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, if it satisfies the integral
equation:

u(t,x)=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma\big(u(s,y)\big)X(ds,dy)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b\big(u(t-s,x-y)\big)G_{s}(dy)ds.

To explain our strategy for handling moments, we recall that in the case of stochastic integrals with respect to discontinuous martingales, the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality does not provide useful bounds, and one has to rely on Rosenthal’s inequality instead. This inequality (which we recall in Proposition
2.3
), is applied in Theorem
3.6
to obtain a moment estimate for the
p
-th moment of the stochastic integral

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}S(s,x)Z(s,x)X(ds,dx)

(13)

with respect to the Lévy colored noise
X
, when
S
is deterministic and
Z
is random.
Compared to the bound given by Theorem 5 of
[
14
]
for the spatially-homogeneous Gaussian noise
F
, our estimate contains an extra term which involves
\|S(s,\cdot)*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.
To see that this term is natural, assume for simplicity that
Z=1
. Then the integral in (
13
) coincides with the integral of
S(s,\cdot)*\kappa
with respect to
L
(see Remark
3.8
). This integral has a finite
p
-th moment if and only if the integrand is in
L^{p}([0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
m_{p}<\infty
, according to Corollary
2.5
.
By exploiting this idea, we provide in Section
4
some sufficient conditions which guarantee that the solutions of the linear heat and wave equations (with
\sigma=1
and
b=0
) have finite
p
-th moments, in the case the three examples of kernel mentioned above. Then, in Section
5.1
, we extend this result to the non-linear equations.

In Theorem
3.7
, we provide an alternative bound for the
p
-th moment of integral (
13
), in which the constant does not depend on
t
.
This result is used in Section
5.2
to obtain more precise bounds for the
p
-th moments of the solution
u
of equation (
11
) without drift (i.e. with
b=0
), and conclude that the solution
u
has a finite
upper Lyapunov exponent
:

\overline{\gamma}(p):=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{p}.

(14)

Note that
\overline{\gamma}(p)
does not depend on
x
since
u
is stationary in space, a property which is due to the space homogeneity of the noise and the fact that the initial condition is constant.

This leads us immediately to the question of intermittency, which has been studied extensively in the Gaussian case. Following
[
19
]
, we say that

is
weakly intermittent
if

\overline{\gamma}(p)<\infty\ \mbox{for all $p\geq 2$},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{\gamma}(2)>0.

In Section
6
, using a similar argument as in
[
19
]
for the Gaussian case, we give some sufficient conditions on the kernel
\kappa
under which
\overline{\gamma}(2)>0
, in the case of the heat equation in dimension
d\geq 1
and the wave equation in dimension
d\leq 2
. Then we identify some cases for which the solutions of these equations are weakly intermittent.

Finally, in Section
7
, we consider the particular case of the
the
parabolic/hyperbolic Anderson model
. This is the name given to
equation (
11
) when
\mathcal{L}
is the heat/wave operator,
b=0
and

\sigma(u)=\lambda u
. In the case of the wave operator, we assume that

d\leq 2
. Using tools from Malliavin calculus on the Poisson space, we identify the chaos expansion of the solution and compute explicitly
\overline{\gamma}(2)
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the Lévy white noise and the Lévy colored noise and discuss some of their properties.

2.1
Lévy white noise

In this section, we introduce the stochastic integral with respect to the Lévy white noise
L
, and we present a new result which states that this integral has a finite
p
-th moment if and only if the (deterministic) integrand is
p
-integrable and
m_{p}<\infty
.

Let
N
be the PRM specified in the introduction and
L
be the Lévy white noise given by (
3
).
We recall the following properties:

•

L
is a
(
\sigma
-additive) random measure
, i.e. if
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
are disjoint and
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}A_{n}
is bounded, then

L\Big(\bigcup_{n\geq 1}A_{n}\Big)=\sum_{n\geq 1}L(A_{n})
a.s.

•

L
is
independently scattered
, i.e.
L(A_{1}),\ldots,L(A_{n})
are independent, for any disjoint sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

•

L
is
infinitely divisible (ID)
, i.e. for any
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
L(A)
has an ID distribution. More precisely, the characteristic function of
L(A)
is given by:

\mathbb{E}(e^{iuL(A)})=\exp\left\{|A|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}(e^{iuz}-1-iuz)\nu(dz)\right\},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

We include below two examples of Lévy white noise with finite moments of all orders.

Example 2.1

(Gamma white noise)
.

A
Gamma white noise
with parameters
\alpha>0,\beta>0
is an independently scattered random measure
\gamma=\{\gamma(A);A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
such that

\gamma(A)\sim{\rm Gamma}(\alpha|A|,\beta)\quad\mbox{for any $A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

Here the Gamma
(\alpha,\beta)
distribution has
density
f(x)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}1_{\{x>0\}}
. (The word “white” does not mean “zero-mean”.)
Then,

\mathbb{E}[e^{iu\gamma(A)}]=\left(1-\frac{iu}{\beta}\right)^{-\alpha|A|}=\exp\left\{\alpha|A|\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{iuz}-1)\frac{1}{z}e^{-\beta z}dz\right\},\quad u\in\mathbb{R},

and the Lévy measure
\nu(dz)=\alpha z^{-1}e^{-\beta z}1_{\{z>0\}}dz
satisfies:

m_{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z|^{p}\nu(dz)=\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{p-1}e^{-\beta z}dz=\frac{\alpha\Gamma(p)}{\beta^{p}}\quad\mbox{for any $p>0$}.

The centered Gamma white noise
L
defined by

L(A):=\gamma(A)-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}|A|
is a Lévy white noise of form (
3
),
for which
\mathbb{E}|L(A)|^{p}<\infty
for all
p>0
.

Example 2.2

(Variance Gamma white noise)
.

In this example, we give an extension of the variance gamma (VG) process defined in
[
30
]
.
A Gamma white noise with “mean rate”
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and “variance rate”
\nu>0
is a Gamma white noise with parameters
\alpha=\mu^{2}/\nu
and
\beta=\mu/\nu
, as defined in Example
2.1
. Let
\gamma_{p}
and
\gamma_{n}
be independent Gamma white noises with mean and variance rates
(\mu_{p},\nu_{p})
and
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
given by:

\mu_{p}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\theta^{2}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\nu}}+\frac{\theta}{2},\quad\nu_{p}=\mu_{p}^{2}\nu\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\theta^{2}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\nu}}-\frac{\theta}{2},\quad\nu_{n}=\mu_{n}^{2}\nu,

for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma>0
and
\nu>0
. The process
\{X(A);A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
defined by

X(A)=\gamma_{p}(A)-\gamma_{n}(A)\quad

is called a
variance gamma (VG) white noise
with parameters
(\theta,\sigma,\nu)
.

The values
(\mu_{p},\nu_{p})
and
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
satisfy:

\frac{\mu_{p}^{2}}{\nu_{p}}=\frac{\mu_{n}^{2}}{\nu_{n}}=\frac{1}{\nu},\quad\quad\quad\frac{\nu_{p}}{\mu_{p}}\cdot\frac{\nu_{n}}{\mu_{n}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}\nu}{2},\quad\quad\quad\frac{\nu_{p}}{\mu_{p}}-\frac{\nu_{n}}{\mu_{n}}=\theta\nu.

Therefore, the characteristic function of
X(A)
can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{iuX(A)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{iu\gamma_{p}(A)}\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-iu\gamma_{n}(A)}\big]=\left(1-\frac{iu\nu_{p}}{\mu_{p}}\right)^{-\frac{\mu_{p}^{2}|A|}{\nu_{p}}}\left(1+\frac{iu\nu_{n}}{\mu_{n}}\right)^{-\frac{\mu_{n}^{2}|A|}{\nu_{n}}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-iu\theta\nu+\frac{u^{2}\sigma^{2}\nu}{2}\right)^{-\frac{|A|}{\nu}}.

This means that
X(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y(A)
for any
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where

Y(A)=B_{\gamma(A)}

is defined by “subordinating” a classical Brownian motion
B_{t}=\theta+\sigma W_{t}
(with
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a standard Brownian motion) to an independent Gamma white noise
\{\gamma(A);A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
with mean rate
1
and variance rate
\nu
.
Unlike the classical case (see e.g. Theorem 30.1 of
[
39
]
), this subordination procedure does not yield an independently scattered process:
for disjoint sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and bounded continuous functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(Y(A_{j}))\Big]=\mathbb{E}\big[G(\gamma(A_{1}),\ldots,\gamma(A_{n}))\big]

with function
G(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(B_{s_{j}})\Big]
that cannot be transformed into a product. This means that the processes
\{X(A)\}
and
\{Y(A)\}
do not have the same distribution, although they have the same marginal distributions.

Clearly,
\mathbb{E}|X(A)|^{p}<\infty
for any
p>0
. In fact, the mean and variance of
X(A)
are:

\mathbb{E}\big[X(A)\big]=\theta|A|\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\rm Var}\big(X(A)\big)=\left(\frac{5}{8}\theta^{2}\nu+\sigma^{2}\right)|A|.

The characteristic function of
X(A)
can be written also as:

\mathbb{E}[e^{iuX(A)}]=\exp\left\{|A|\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{iuz}-1)\nu^{*}(dz)\right\},\quad u\in\mathbb{R},

with Lévy measure
\nu^{*}
given by:

\nu^{*}(dz)=\left[\frac{\mu_{p}^{2}}{\nu_{p}}\cdot\frac{1}{z}e^{-\frac{\mu_{p}}{\nu_{p}}z}1_{\{z>0\}}+\frac{\mu_{n}^{2}}{\nu_{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{|z|}e^{-\frac{\mu_{n}}{\nu_{n}}|z|}1_{\{z<0\}}\right]dz.

The
centered VG white noise

L
defined by

L(A):=X(A)-\theta|A|
is a Lévy white noise of form (
3
) with Lévy measure
\nu^{*}
,
for which
\mathbb{E}|L(A)|^{p}<\infty
for all
p>0
.

We continue now with a discussion about the stochastic integral with respect to
L
.
We let
L(1_{A})=L(A)
. The map
1_{A}\mapsto L(1_{A})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is an isometry which can be extended to
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by linearity and a density argument.
We denote this map by:

L(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(t,x)L(dt,dx)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\varphi\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

More precisely, the following isometry property holds: for all
\varphi,\psi\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\mathbb{E}[L(\varphi)L(\psi)]=m_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(t,x)\overline{\psi(t,x)}dtdx.

(15)

For any
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
L(\varphi)
has the Poisson representation:

L(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\varphi(t,x)z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz),

(16)

and characteristic function

\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda L(\varphi)})=\exp\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}\big(e^{i\lambda z\varphi(t,x)}-1-i\lambda z\varphi(t,x)\big)dtdx\nu(dz)\right\},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

For any
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the process
L_{t}(\phi)=\{L(1_{[0,t]}\phi)\}_{t\geq 0}

is a Lévy process and a zero-mean square-integrable martingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. This process has a càdlàg modification. We work with the modification. We let
L_{t}(B)=L_{t}(1_{B})
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The “Fourier transform” of the noise
L
in space is defined by:

\widehat{L}(\varphi):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\varphi(t,\cdot)(x)L(dt,dx),

for any
\varphi\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We will use the notation
\widehat{L}(\varphi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(t,\xi)\widehat{L}(dt,d\xi)
.

For any
t>0
, we let

\widehat{L}_{t}(\phi):=L_{t}(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\phi)\quad\mbox{for any $\phi\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

(17)

Next, we consider the case of random integrands.
We consider the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0}\vee\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0}=\sigma(L_{s}(A);s\leq t,A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\mathcal{N}
is the null
\sigma
-field.

We say that
\{g(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is an
elementary
process if it is of the form

g(\omega,t,x)=Y(\omega)1_{(a,b]}(t)1_{A}(x),

(18)

where
0\leq a<b
,
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
Y
is a bounded
\mathcal{F}_{a}
-measurable random variable. The stochastic integral with respect to
L
of a process
g
of form (
18
) is given by:

(g\cdot L)_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x)L(dt,dx)=Y(L_{t\wedge b}(A)-L_{t\wedge a}(A)).

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of linear combinations of elementary processes, and
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}=\sigma(\mathcal{E})
be the
predictable
\sigma
-field
on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. (We refer the reader to Lemma
A.2
for an alternative representation of
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.) A process
g=\{g(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is
predictable
if it is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

For any predictable process
g\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the
Itô integral
of
g
with respect to
L
, and this integral satisfies the isometry property:

\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x)L(dt,dx)\right|^{2}=m_{2}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(t,x)|^{2}dxdt.

(19)

In addition,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x)L(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}g(t,x)z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz),

(20)

where on the right hand-side we have the Itô integral with respect to
\widehat{N}
.

We recall Rosenthal’s inequality: for any càdlàg martingale
M=(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
p\geq 2
,

\|\sup_{s\leq t}|M_{s}|\|_{p}\leq B_{p}\left(\|\langle M\rangle_{t}^{1/2}\|_{p}+\|\sup_{s\leq t}|(\Delta M)_{s}|\|_{p}\right),

where
\langle M\rangle
is the predictable quadratic variation of
M
, and
(\Delta M)_{t}=M_{t}-M_{t-}
is the jump size of
M
at time
t
. As an application, we obtain the following inequality, which was proved in
[
4
]
, and plays an important role in the present article. See also Theorem 1 of
[
31
]
.

Proposition 2.3

(Rosenthal Inequality for
L
)
.

Let
\Phi=\{\Phi(t,x);t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be a predictable process such that
\Phi\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. If
p\geq 2
is such that
m_{p}<\infty
,
then

\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)L(dt,dx)\right|^{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Phi(t,x)|^{2}dxdt\right)^{p/2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Phi(t,x)|^{p}dxdt\right\},

(21)

where
\mathcal{C}_{p}=2^{p-1}B_{p}^{p}(m_{2}^{p/2}\vee m_{p})
.

In the case of a deterministic integrand, it is possible to give a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the
p
-th moment of the stochastic integral with respect to
L
. To prove this, we will use the following result, inspired by Theorem 25.3 of
[
39
]
(which is stated for a classical Lévy process, indexed by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
).

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\Phi\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a locally integrable function which is “sub-multiplicative”, i.e.

g(x+y)\leq ag(x)g(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, for some
a>0
. If

\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)L(dt,dx)\right)\right]<\infty,

then

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|>1\}}g\big(z\Phi(t,x)\big)\nu(dz)dxdt<\infty.

Proof.

Since
\Phi
is Itô integrable with respect to
L
, it is also
L
-integrable, in the sense of the definition given on page 460 of
[
37
]
. By Theorem 2.7 of
[
37
]
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|z\Phi(t,x)|^{2}\wedge 1\big)\nu(dz)dxdt<\infty.

(22)

As mentioned in the introduction,
L(A)
can be written as:

L(A)=b|A|+\int_{A\times\{|z|\leq 1\}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{A\times\{|z|>1\}}zN(dt,dx,dz),

with
b=-\int_{\{|z|>1\}}z\nu(dz)
.
This means that
L
has characteristic triplet
(b,0,\nu)
, as defined in Theorem 1.0.9 of
[
24
]
. By Theorem 2.3.10 of
[
24
]
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)L(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[b\Phi(t,x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\Phi(t,x)\big(1_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|\leq 1\}}-1_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}\big)\nu(dz)\right]dxdt

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|\leq 1\}}z\Phi(t,x)\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|>1\}}z\Phi(t,x)N(dt,dx,dz)

\displaystyle\quad=:B+X+Y.

We denote by
P_{X}
be the law of
X
and
P_{Y}
the law of
Y
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)L(dt,dx)\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\big[g(B+X+Y)\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(B+x+y)P_{X}(dx)P_{Y}(dy).

Since this expectation is finite,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(B+x+y)P_{Y}(dy)=\mathbb{E}\big[g(B+x+Y)\big]<\infty
for
P_{X}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}[g(x^{\prime}+Y)]<\infty\quad\mbox{for $x^{\prime}=B+x$ and $P_{X}$-almost all $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

(23)

Recall that
\mu(dt,dx,dz)=dtdx\nu(dz)
. Let

\mathcal{R}=\{(t,x,z)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0};|z\Phi(t,x)|>1\}.

From (
22
), we know that
\mu(\mathcal{R})<\infty
. Then, the restriction of
N
to
\mathcal{R}
has the following representation (in law):

N|_{\mathcal{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\delta_{(T_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})},

where
K
is a Poisson random variable with mean
\mu(\mathcal{R})
,
\{(T_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. with law
\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{R})}\mu|_{\mathcal{R}}
, and are independent of
K
. Here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. Hence,

Y=\int_{\mathcal{R}}z\Phi(t,x)N(dt,dx,dz)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}Z_{i}\Phi(T_{i},X_{i})=:Y^{\prime}.

Since
\{W_{i}:=Z_{i}\Phi(T_{i},X_{i});i\geq 1\}
are i.i.d.,
Y^{\prime}
has a compound-Poisson distribution. Let
F
be the law of
W_{1}
. For any measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
, we have:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(y)F(dy)=\mathbb{E}[h(W_{1})]=\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{R})}\int_{\mathcal{R}}h\big(z\Phi(t,x)\big)dtdx\nu(dz),

and

\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[h(Y^{\prime})]=\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(y)F^{*n}(dy).

where
F^{*n}
is the law of
X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}
.
Applying this formula to the function
h(x^{\prime})=g(x^{\prime}+\cdot)
, and using (
23
), we obtain that, for
x^{\prime}=B+x
and
P_{X}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x^{\prime}+y)F^{*n}(dy)<\infty.

By Lemma 25.5 of
[
39
]
, there exist
b>0
and
c>0
such that

g(x)\leq be^{c|x|}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,
g(y)\leq ag(-x)g(x+y)\leq abe^{c|x|}g(x+y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that

\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(y)F^{*n}(dy)\leq abe^{c|x^{\prime}|}\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x^{\prime}+y)F^{*n}(dy)<\infty,

for
x^{\prime}=B+x
and
P_{X}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, for
n=1
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(y)F(dy)=\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{R})}\int_{\mathcal{R}}g\big(z\Phi(t,x)\big)dtdx\nu(dz)<\infty.

∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\Phi\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
p\geq 2
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)L(dt,dx)\right|^{p}<\infty

if and only if

\Phi\in L^{p}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\mbox{and}\quad m_{p}<\infty.

(24)

Proof.

Let
Z=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(t,x)L(dt,dx)
.
If (
24
) holds, then
\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}<\infty
, by Proposition
2.3
.

For the converse implication, suppose that
\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}<\infty
. Let
g(x)=(1+|x|^{2})^{p/2}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
g
is sub-multiplicative, and
\mathbb{E}\big[g(Z)\big]<\infty
. By Theorem
2.4
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|\geq 1\}}g\big(z\Phi(t,x)\big)dtdx\nu(dz)<\infty,

and hence
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|\geq 1\}}|z\Phi(t,x)|^{p}dtdx\nu(dz)<\infty
. On the other hand, by (
22
),

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|\leq 1\}}|z\Phi(t,x)|^{p}dtdx\nu(dz)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z\Phi(t,x)|\leq 1\}}|z\Phi(t,x)|^{2}dtdx\nu(dz)<\infty.

Hence,
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|z\Phi(t,x)|^{p}dtdx\nu(dz)=m_{p}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}|\Phi(t,x)|^{p}dtdx<\infty
.
∎

2.2
Lévy colored noise

In this section, we introduce the Lévy colored noise, paying attention to impose only minimal assumptions on the kernel
\kappa
.

We first recall some basic terminology and notations. We say that a
C^{\infty}
-function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
rapidly decreasing
if

s_{\alpha,\beta}(\varphi):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x^{\alpha}D^{\beta}\varphi(x)|<\infty

for all multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}),\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, where
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}
and
D^{\beta}=\frac{\partial^{|\beta|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\ldots\partial x_{d}^{\beta_{d}}}
.
We let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of all rapidly decreasing
C^{\infty}
-functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
in
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
s_{m}(\varphi_{n}-\varphi)\to 0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where

s_{m}(\varphi)=\sup_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq m}s_{\alpha,\beta}(\varphi),\quad\mbox{with}\quad|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}.

We let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which are continuous linear functionals
S:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
(S,\varphi)
the action of
S
on
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We say that a distribution
S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is (identified with) a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
if

(S,\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\varphi(x)dx\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of
\mathbb{C}
-valued rapidly decreasing
C^{\infty}
-functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of complex tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which are continuous linear functionals
S:\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{C}
.

The Fourier transform is an isomorphism
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with inverse

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\varphi=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\overline{\mathcal{F}\varphi}
.
The Fourier transform of
S\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a distribution
\mathcal{F}S\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
given by:

(\mathcal{F}S,\varphi)=(S,\mathcal{F}\varphi),\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Definition 2.6
.

We say that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
tempered
if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|x|^{2}}\right)^{\ell}|f(x)|dx<\infty\quad\mbox{for some $\ell>0$}.

A tempered function
f
is locally integrable and induces a distribution
S_{f}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by:

(S_{f},\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\varphi(x)dx\quad\mbox{for all $\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

We identify
f
and
S_{f}
. We say that
\mathcal{F}f=\mathcal{F}S_{f}
is the Fourier transform of
f
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

We introduce the following assumption.

Assumption A1.
Let
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a tempered function such that:

(a) the Fourier transform
h:=\mathcal{F}\kappa
in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a tempered function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(\xi)\phi(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\kappa(x)\mathcal{F}\phi(x)dx\quad\mbox{for all $\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

(b)
|h|^{2}
is a tempered function;

(c) there exists a tempered function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
such that
|h|^{2}=\mathcal{F}f
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h(\xi)|^{2}\phi(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\mathcal{F}\phi(x)dx\quad\mbox{for all $\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

(25)

Part (a) of Assumption A1 can be written also as:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\kappa(x)dx=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)}h(\xi)d\xi\quad\mbox{for all $\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$.}

(26)

This implies that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\varphi*\kappa
is well-defined and is given by:

(\varphi*\kappa)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x-y)\kappa(y)dy=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\,h(\xi)d\xi.

(27)

Moreover,
\varphi*\kappa
is a tempered function, since if
2\ell>d
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|x|^{2}}\right)^{\ell}|(\varphi*\kappa)(x)|dx\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|x|^{2}}\right)^{\ell}dx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)h(\xi)|d\xi<\infty.

Part (c) of Assumption A1 says that
\mathcal{F}(\kappa*\widetilde{\kappa})=\mathcal{F}f
, which is equivalent to

f=\kappa*\widetilde{\kappa},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widetilde{\kappa}(x)=\kappa(-x).

(28)

From relation (
25
), we deduce that:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)f(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\mu(d\xi),\quad\mbox{for any $\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$},

where

\mu(d\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi.

(29)

Replacing
\varphi
by
\varphi*\widetilde{\psi}
, we obtain that for any
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(y)f(x-y)dxdy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(\xi)}\mu(d\xi).

(30)

We have the following result.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be arbitrary. (i) If part (a) of Assumption A1 holds, then

\mathcal{F}(\varphi*\kappa)=\mathcal{F}\varphi\mathcal{F}\kappa
in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. (ii)
If in addition, part (b) also holds, then
\varphi*\kappa\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\mathcal{F}(\varphi*\kappa)=\mathcal{F}\varphi\mathcal{F}\kappa
in
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

(i) We show that
(\varphi*k,\mathcal{F}\phi)=(\mathcal{F}\varphi\,\mathcal{F}k,\phi)
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, or equivalently,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi*k)(x)\psi(x)dx=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(\xi)}d\xi,

(31)

for all
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This follows by (
26
), since

\mbox{LHS of}\ \eqref{cF-phi-k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\widetilde{\varphi}*\psi)(y)\kappa(y)dy=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{\varphi}*\psi)(\xi)}h(\xi)d\xi=\mbox{RHS of}\ \eqref{cF-phi-k}.

(ii) We denote
v:=\mathcal{F}\varphi\mathcal{F}\kappa
. Since
|\mathcal{F}\kappa|^{2}
is tempered, there exists
\ell>0
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-\ell}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi<\infty
. Since
C:=\sup_{\xi}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\ell}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)|^{2}<\infty
, we infer that:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(\xi)|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\ell}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\ell}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}\right)^{\ell}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi<\infty.

This shows that
v\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The conclusion follows by Lemma
A.1
, using part (i).
∎

In view of Lemma
2.7
and definition (
17
) of the Fourier transform
\hat{L}_{t}
of
L_{t}
, we are now ready to introduce the
Lévy colored noise

\{X_{t}(\varphi);t\geq 0,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
, given by:

X_{t}(\varphi):=L_{t}(\varphi*\kappa)=\widehat{L}_{t}(\mathcal{F}\varphi\mathcal{F}\kappa),\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(32)

Using the isometry property (
15
) of
L
, Plancherel’s theorem, Lemma
2.7
.(ii), and relation (
30
), we compute the covariance of the Lévy colored noise:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t}(\varphi)X_{s}(\psi)]

\displaystyle=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\varphi*\kappa)(x)(\psi*\kappa)(x)dx

\displaystyle=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}(\varphi*\kappa)(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}(\psi*\kappa)(\xi)}d\xi

\displaystyle=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(\xi)}\mu(d\xi)

\displaystyle=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(y)f(x-y)dxdy.

Remark 2.8
.

The Lévy colored noise was introduced in
[
2
]
under the sole condition given by part (b) of Assumption A1 (without assuming the existence of the kernel
\kappa
). This condition is satisfied by the example
h(\xi)=C_{H}|\xi|^{1/2-H}
with
H\in(0,1)
in dimension
d=1
,
for which the noise
X
has the same spatial covariance structure as the fractional Brownian motion of index
H
. In this case, part (a) of Assumption A1 holds in the “regular” case
H>1/2
(with
\kappa(x)=C_{H}^{\prime}|x|^{H-3/2}
), but not in the “rough” case
H<1/2
. In the regular case, part (c) of Assumption A1 also holds, with
f(x)=C_{H}^{\prime\prime}|x|^{2H-2}
. Here
C_{H},C_{H}^{\prime},C_{H}^{\prime\prime}
are positive constants depending on
H
.

To define the stochastic integral with respect to
X
, we will use the martingale measure theory developed in
[
14
,
41
]
. For this, we will need the following assumption.

Assumption A2.
(a)
h
is non-negative and
\kappa
is continuous, non-negative, symmetric with
\kappa(x)<\infty
if and only if
x\not=0
.

(b)
f
is continuous, non-negative, symmetric with
f(x)<\infty
if and only if
x\not=0
.

By Lemma 5.6 of
[
28
]
, part (a) of Assumption A2 implies that for any
non-negative

\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\kappa(x-y)dxdy=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)|^{2}h(\xi)d\xi=:\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(\varphi).

(33)

By Lemma A.1 of
[
6
]
, for any
\varphi,\psi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(|\varphi|)<\infty
and
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(|\psi|)<\infty
, we have:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(y)\kappa(x-y)dxdy=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(\xi)}h(\xi)d\xi.

Similarly, part (b) of Assumption A2 implies that for any
non-negative

\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\varphi(y)f(x-y)dxdy=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)|^{2}|h(\xi)|^{2}d\xi:=\mathcal{E}_{f}(\varphi).

(34)

Lemma 2.9
.

Under Assumptions A1 and A2, for any
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

1_{A}*\kappa\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{F}(1_{A}*\kappa)=\mathcal{F}1_{A}\mathcal{F}\kappa\ \mbox{in}\ L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be arbitrary. Since
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(1_{A})<\infty
and

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(|\varphi|)<\infty
, by (
33
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{A}*\kappa)(x)\varphi(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(y)\kappa(x-y)\varphi(x)dxdy=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}1_{A}(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)}\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)d\xi.

This means that
\mathcal{F}(1_{A}*\kappa)=\mathcal{F}1_{A}\mathcal{F}\kappa
in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; see (
26
).
The conclusion will follow by Lemma
A.1
, as long as we show that
v:=\mathcal{F}1_{A}\mathcal{F}\kappa\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is clear, since by (
34
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(\xi)|^{2}d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}1_{A}(\xi)|^{2}|h(\xi)|^{2}d\xi=(2\pi)^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(x)1_{A}(y)f(x-y)dxdy<\infty.

∎

For the remaining part of the article, we suppose that Assumptions A1 and A2 hold.

We include below some examples of kernels
\kappa
which satisfy these assumptions.

Example 2.10
.

(The heat kernel)

Let
\kappa=H_{d,\alpha/2}
for some
\alpha>0
, where

H_{d,\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\alpha)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2\alpha}\right)\quad\mbox{for $\alpha>0$}.

Assumptions A1 and A2 hold with
f=\kappa*\kappa=H_{d,\alpha}
and
|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}=\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\exp(-\alpha|\xi|^{2}/2)
.

Example 2.11
.

(The Riesz kernel)

Let
\kappa=R_{d,\alpha/2}
for some
\alpha\in(0,d)
, where

R_{d,\alpha}(x)=C_{d,\alpha}|x|^{-(d-\alpha)}\quad\mbox{with}\quad C_{d,\alpha}=\pi^{-d/2}2^{-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}.

It is known that
\mathcal{F}R_{d,\alpha}(\xi)=|\xi|^{-\alpha}
and
R_{d,\alpha}*R_{d,\beta}=R_{d,\alpha+\beta}
(see
[
40
]
). Hence, Assumptions A1 and A2 hold with
f=\kappa*\kappa=R_{d,\alpha}
and
|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}=\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=|\xi|^{-\alpha}
.

Example 2.12
.

(The Bessel kernel)

Let
\kappa=B_{d,\alpha/2}
for some
\alpha>0
, where

B_{d,\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{\alpha/2-1}e^{-w}\frac{1}{(4\pi w)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4w}\right)dw.

It is known that
\mathcal{F}B_{d,\alpha}(\xi)=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-\alpha/2}
and
B_{d,\alpha}*B_{d,\beta}=B_{d,\alpha+\beta}
(see
[
40
]
). Hence, Assumptions A1 and A2 hold with
f=\kappa*\kappa=B_{d,\alpha}
and
|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}=\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-\alpha/2}
.

Example 2.13
.

(The Poisson kernel)

Let
\kappa=P_{d,\alpha/2}
for some
\alpha>0
, where

P_{d,\alpha}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\pi^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\alpha\left(\frac{1}{|x|^{2}+\alpha^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}

is the density of a
d
-dimensional Cauchy distribution (see Example 2.12 of
[
39
]
). Since
\mathcal{F}P_{d,\alpha}(\xi)=\exp(-\alpha|\xi|)
and
P_{d,\alpha}*P_{d,\beta}=P_{d,\alpha+\beta}
, Assumptions A1 and A2 hold with
f=\kappa*\kappa=P_{d,\alpha}
and
|h(\xi)|^{2}=\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\exp(-\alpha|\xi|)
.

Example 2.14
.

(Kernels of product-type)

Let
\kappa(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\kappa_{j}(x_{j})
for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, where
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{d}
are kernels on
\mathbb{R}
which satisfy Assumptions A1 and A2, with
f_{j}=\kappa_{j}*\widetilde{\kappa}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
. Then
\kappa
satisfies Assumptions A1 and A2 with
f(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}f_{j}(x_{j})
.

Let
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of
C^{\infty}
-functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support, and
\mathcal{P}_{0,d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the completion of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the inner product

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{0}=m_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\psi(y)f(x-y)dxdy.

(The lower index
d
stands for “deterministic”.) The map
\varphi\to X_{t}(\varphi)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is an isometry which can be extended from
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathcal{P}_{0,d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The space
\mathcal{P}_{0,d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
contains distributions: by Theorem 3.5.(1) of
[
9
]
, we know that
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathcal{P}_{0,d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d}=\{S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\mathcal{F}S\ \mbox{is a function and}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}S(\xi)|^{2}\mu(d\xi)<\infty\}.

On the other hand, for any set
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by approximating the indicator
1_{A}
with functions in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we infer that
1_{A}\in\mathcal{P}_{0,d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

X_{t}(A):=X_{t}(1_{A})=L_{t}(1_{A}*\kappa).

Note that for any
t,s>0
and
A,B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}(A)X_{s}(B)]=m_{2}(t\wedge s)\int_{A}\int_{B}f(x-y)dxdy.

We now proceed to extend the definition of the Lévy colored noise to incorporate integrands which depend also on time.

Lemma 2.15
.

Under Assumption A1,
the map
(t,x)\mapsto\big(\varphi(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.7
,
\varphi(t,\cdot)*\kappa\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{F}(\varphi(t,\cdot)*\kappa)=\mathcal{F}\varphi(t,\cdot)\mathcal{F}\kappa
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We denote
\phi_{\xi}(t)=\mathcal{F}\varphi(t,\cdot)(\xi)
. If
t<0
, then
\varphi(t,\cdot)=0
and
\phi_{\xi}(t)=0
. Note that
\phi_{\xi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
: if
\varphi(t,\cdot)
has compact support
K
, then

|\phi_{\xi}(t)|\leq\int_{K}|\varphi(t,x)|dx\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\phi_{\xi}(t)|^{2}\leq|K|\int_{K}|\varphi(t,x)|^{2}dx.

We apply Plancherel theorem twice, first on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and then on
\mathbb{R}
. We obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\big(\varphi(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)|^{2}dxdt=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(t,\cdot)(\xi)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi dt

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{\xi}(t)|^{2}dt\right)|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|\mathcal{F}\varphi(\tau,\xi)|^{2}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi d\tau<\infty,

where for the second equality we used the fact that
\mathcal{F}\phi_{\xi}(\tau)=\mathcal{F}\varphi(\tau,\xi)
.
∎

In view of Lemma
2.15
, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define

\displaystyle X(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(t,x)X(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\varphi(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)L(dt,dx)

(35)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(t,\cdot)(\xi)\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)\widehat{L}(dt,d\xi).

The covariance of this process is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X(\varphi)X(\psi)]

\displaystyle=m_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\varphi(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)\big(\psi(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)dxdt

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(t,\cdot)(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(t,\cdot)(\xi)}|h(\xi)|^{2}d\xi dt=:\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{0},

for any
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and coincides the covariance of the spatially homogeneous Gaussian noise considered by Dalang in
[
14
]
.

We fix
T>0
. We let
\mathcal{P}_{0,d}
be the completion of
\mathcal{D}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0}
. The map
\varphi\to X(\varphi)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is an isometry which is extended from
\mathcal{D}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathcal{P}_{0,d}
.
This notation is consistent with the discussion above. More precisely,
by approximation with functions in
\mathcal{D}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it can be proved that for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

1_{[0,t]}\varphi\in\mathcal{P}_{0,d}\quad\mbox{and}\quad X(1_{[0,t]}\varphi)=X_{t}(\varphi),

and for any
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

1_{[0,t]\times A}\in\mathcal{P}_{0,d}\quad\mbox{and}\quad X(1_{[0,t]\times A})=X_{t}(A).

The space
\mathcal{P}_{0,d}
contains distributions in the space variable, namely
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d}\subset\mathcal{P}_{0,d}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d}
is the set of maps
S:[0,T]\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mathcal{F}S(t,\cdot)
is a function for all
t\in[0,T]
,
(t,\xi)\mapsto\mathcal{F}S(t,\cdot)(\xi)
is measurable on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\|S\|_{0}^{2}:=m_{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}S(t,\cdot)(\xi)|^{2}\mu(d\xi)<\infty.

For general
S\in\mathcal{P}_{0,d}
, if the convolution
S(t\cdot)*\kappa
is not well-defined, we cannot express
X(S)
in terms of
L
(as in (
35
)). But if
S\in\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{d}
, we still have

X(S)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}S(t,\cdot)(\xi)\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)\widehat{L}(dt,d\xi).

3
Integration with respect to Lévy colored noise

The stochastic integral with respect to the noise
X
is defined exactly as in the case of the spatially homogeneous Gaussian noise considered in
[
14
]
. For the sake of clarity, we review this theory briefly in this section.

The process
\{X_{t}(A);t\geq 0,A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
is a worthy martingale measure, as defined in
[
41
]
.
If
g
is an elementary process of form (
18
), we define its stochastic integral with respect to
X
by:

(g\cdot X)_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(s,x)X(ds,dx)=Y\Big(X_{t\wedge b}(A)-X_{t\wedge a}(A)\Big).

We fix
T>0
. The map
\mathcal{E}\ni g\to(g\cdot X)_{T}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is an isometry:

\mathbb{E}\big[|(g\cdot X)_{T}|^{2}\big]=m_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x)g(t,y)f(x-y)dxdydt\right]=:\|g\|_{0}^{2},

which is extended to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, the completion of
\mathcal{E}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{+}
be the set of predictable processes
g
such that

\|g\|_{+}^{2}:=m_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(t,x)g(t,y)|f(x-y)dxdydt\right]<\infty.

Then
\mathcal{P}_{+}\subset\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\|g\|_{0}\leq\|g\|_{+}
for any
g\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
.
Note that
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is also the completion of
\mathcal{E}_{0}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{0}
, where

\mathcal{E}_{0}
is the set of
g\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
such that
g(\omega,t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
(\omega,t)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The following observation will play an important role. For any
g\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\big|\big(g(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)\big|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,y)g(t,z)\kappa(x-y)\kappa(x-z)dydz,

(36)

and therefore, by (
28
), we have:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\big(g(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)\big|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,y)g(t,z)f(y-z)dydz.

(37)

The following result gives the relation between the stochastic integrals with respect to
X
and
L
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
g\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
, then
\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\big(g(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)\big|^{2}dxdt<\infty
and

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(t,x)X(dt,dx)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(g(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)L(dt,dx).

(38)

Proof.

By (
37
),

0\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\big(g(t,\cdot)*\kappa\big)(x)\big|^{2}dxdt\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(t,y)g(t,z)|f(y-z)dydzdt<\infty.

We know that relation (
38
) holds if
g\in\mathcal{D}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
; see (
35
). The general case follows by approximation, since
\mathcal{D}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is dense in
\mathcal{P}_{+}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\left\{\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|(G_{t-s}*\kappa)(y)\big|^{2}dyds\right)^{p/2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|(G_{t-s}*\kappa)(y)\big|^{p}dyds\right\}.

We denote

J_{p}(t):=\|G_{t}*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Then the previous estimate can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\left\{\left(\int_{0}^{t}J_{2}(s)ds\right)^{p/2}+\int_{0}^{t}(J_{p}(s))^{p/2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{p}\left\{\big(\mathcal{M}_{2}(t)\big)^{p/2}+\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)\right\}.

(54)

Remark 4.2
.

Recall that
\mu(d\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}|\mathcal{F}\kappa(\xi)|^{2}d\xi
. Then, by Plancherel’s theorem,

J_{2}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|(G_{t}*\kappa)(x)\big|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)|^{2}\mu(d\xi).

To estimate
J_{p}(t)
, we consider several examples of kernels
\kappa
. The first class of examples are those that satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption A3.

There exists a function
K_{p}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
such that

\kappa^{p/2}*\widetilde{\kappa}^{p/2}=K_{p}^{p/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{F}K_{p}=\mu_{p}\ \mbox{in $\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$},

where
\mu_{p}
is a non-negative tempered measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Note that Assumption A3 automatically holds for
p=2
, with
K_{2}=f
and
\mu_{2}=\mu
.

Example 4.3
.

(The heat kernel)

Let
\kappa=H_{d,\alpha/2}
be the heat kernel from Example
2.10
. Then
\kappa^{p/2}=H_{d,\alpha/2}^{p/2}=CH_{d,\alpha/p}
, and
\kappa^{p/2}*\kappa^{p/2}=CH_{d,2\alpha/p}=CH_{d,\alpha}^{p/2}
. Hence, Assumption A3 holds for all
p>0
, with
K_{p}=CH_{d,\alpha}
and
\mu_{p}(d\xi)=C\exp(-\alpha|\xi|^{2}/2)
. Here
C>0
is a constant depending on
(d,\alpha,p)
which may be different in each of its appearances.

Theorem 4.4
.

a) If
\kappa
satisfies Assumption A3 for some
p\geq 2
, then

J_{p}(t)\leq J_{p}^{\prime}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)|^{2}\mu_{p}(d\xi),

provided that
J_{p}^{\prime}(t)<\infty
.

b) If
\kappa=R_{d,\alpha/2}
is the Riesz kernel of order
\alpha/2
(given by Example
2.11
) for some
\alpha\in(0,d)
, and
G_{t}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
q>1
, then for all
t>0
,

J_{p}(t)\leq C_{d,\alpha,p}\|G_{t}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},\quad\mbox{with $\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{\alpha}{2d}$},

where

C_{d,\alpha,p}>0
is a constant depending on
(d,\alpha,p)
.

c) If
\kappa=B_{d,\alpha/2}
is the Bessel kernel of order
\alpha/2
(given by Example
2.12
) for some
\alpha>0
, and
G_{t}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
p\geq 1
, then for all
t>0
,

J_{p}(t)\leq C_{d,\alpha,p}\|G_{t}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

where
C_{d,\alpha,p}>0
is a constant depending on
(d,\alpha,p)
.

Proof.

a)
Case 1.
Assume that
G_{t}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We write

|(G_{t}*\kappa)(x)|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}(y)G_{t}(z)\kappa(x-y)\kappa(x-z)dydz.

By Minkowski’s inequality, for all
p\geq 2
,

\displaystyle J_{p}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|(G_{t}*\kappa)(x)|^{p}dx\right)^{2/p}=\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}(y)G_{t}(z)\kappa(\cdot-y)\kappa(\cdot-z)dydz\right\|_{L^{p/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}(y)G_{t}(z)\left\|\kappa(\cdot-y)\kappa(\cdot-z)\right\|_{L^{p/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}dydz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}(y)G_{t}(z)K_{p}(y-z)dydz=J_{p}^{\prime}(t).

Case 2.
In the general case, we consider the approximation
G_{t}^{(n)}=G_{t}*\psi_{n}
, where
\psi_{n}(x)=n^{d}\psi(nx)
for some non-negative function
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with support contained in the unit ball, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)dx=1
. Then
G_{t}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
n\geq 1
. Hence, by Case 1,

\|G_{t}^{(n)}*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t}^{(n)}(\xi)|^{2}\mu_{p}(d\xi)\quad\mbox{for all $n\geq 1$}.

(55)

Note that
G_{t}^{(n)}*\kappa=G_{t}*\kappa*\psi_{n}\to G_{t}*\kappa
pointwise. By Fatou’s lemma and (
55
),

\displaystyle\|G_{t}*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|G_{t}^{(n)}*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t}^{(n)}(\xi)|^{2}\mu_{p}(d\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)|^{2}\mu_{p}(d\xi),

where the last equality is due to the dominated convergence theorem.

b) This follows by Littlewood-Hardy-Sobolev Theorem (Theorem 1, page 119 of
[
40
]
).

c) This estimate can be found on page 134 of
[
40
]
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Theorem
4.4
allows us to find
sufficient
conditions which ensure that the solution

of equation (

) has a finite
p
-th moment, for

4.1
The linear heat equation

Consider the heat equation with additive noise:

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta v(t,x)+\dot{X}(t,x)\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(56)

and zero initial conditions. In this case,

G_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2t}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)=\exp\left(-\frac{t|\xi|^{2}}{2}\right).

The solution of equation (
56
) exists provided that
G_{t}
satisfies (
51
), which is equivalent to Dalang’s condition (
9
), as shown in Section 3 of
[
14
]
.

Remark 4.6
.

Condition (
9
) always holds when
d=1
, and for
d\geq 2
can be expressed in terms of the function
f
. See Remark 10.(b) of
[
14
]
.

We now examine the existence of the higher moments of this solution for the three examples of kernels
\kappa
mentioned above.

Example 4.7
.

(The heat kernel)

Let
k=H_{d,\alpha/2}
be the heat kernel given by Example
2.10
for some
\alpha>0
.
Then
\mu(d\xi)=(2\pi)^{-d}\exp(-\alpha|\xi|^{2}/2)d\xi
and Dalang’s condition holds for all
\alpha>0
. By Example
4.3
, Assumption A3 holds for all
p>0
. By Theorem
4.4
.(a), for all
p\geq 2
and
t>0
,

J_{p}(t)\leq J_{p}^{\prime}(t)=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-t|\xi|^{2}}e^{-\frac{\alpha|\xi|^{2}}{2}}d\xi=C\left(t+\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{-d/2}\leq C\alpha^{-d/2},

and hence
\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(J_{p}(s))^{p/2}ds\leq C
. By
(
54
), for any
d\geq 1
,
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq C\quad\mbox{for any $p\geq 2$ such that $m_{p}<\infty$}.

Example 4.8
.

(The Riesz kernel)

Let
k=R_{d,\alpha/2}
be the Riesz kernel given by Example
2.11
for some
\alpha\in(0,d)
. Then
\mu(d\xi)=(2\pi)^{-d}|\xi|^{-\alpha}d\xi
and Dalang’s condition holds for all
\alpha\in((d-2)\vee 0,d)
. By Theorem
4.4
.(b), for any
p\geq 2
, letting
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\alpha}{2d}
, we have:

J_{p}(t)\leq C\|G_{t}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=Ct^{d(1-q)/q}=Ct^{d(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\alpha}{2d}-1)}.

(The condition
q>1
is equivalent to
p>\frac{2d}{2d-\alpha}
, which holds for any
p\geq 2
.)
Therefore,

\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(J_{p}(s))^{p/2}ds\leq C\int_{0}^{t}s^{\frac{dp}{2}(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\alpha}{2d}-1)}ds=Ct^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+\frac{\alpha p}{4}+1},

provided that
\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+\frac{\alpha p}{4}+1>0
. By (
54
),
for any
d\geq 1
,
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq C\Big(t^{\frac{p}{2}(1-\frac{d-\alpha}{2})}+t^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+\frac{\alpha p}{4}+1}\Big)\quad\mbox{for any $2\leq p<\frac{2d+4}{2d-\alpha}$ such that $m_{p}<\infty$}.

Example 4.9
.

(The Bessel kernel)

Let
k=B_{d,\alpha/2}
be the Bessel kernel given by Example
2.12
for some
\alpha>0
. Then
\mu(d\xi)=(2\pi)^{-d}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-\alpha/2}d\xi
and Dalang’s condition holds for all
\alpha>d-2
. By Theorem
4.4
.(c), for any
p\geq 2

J_{p}(t)\leq C\|G_{t}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=Ct^{d(1-p)/p}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(J_{p}(s))^{p/2}ds\leq C\int_{0}^{t}s^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}ds=Ct^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1},

provided that
p<1+\frac{2}{d}
. Since
p\geq 2
, this forces
d=1
. If
d=1
, for any
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq C\Big(t^{\frac{p}{4}}+t^{\frac{3-p}{2}}\Big)\quad\mbox{for any $2\leq p<3$ such that $m_{p}<\infty$}.

4.2
The linear wave equation

Consider the wave equation with additive noise:

\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\Delta v(t,x)+\dot{X}(t,x)\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(57)

and zero initial conditions.

We recall that the fundamental solution of the wave equation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by:

\displaystyle G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}1_{\{|x|<t\}}\quad\mbox{if}\ d=1,

\displaystyle G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}-|x|^{2}}}1_{\{|x|<t\}}\quad\mbox{if}\ d=2,

\displaystyle G_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi t}\sigma_{t},\quad\mbox{if}\ d=3,

where
\sigma_{t}
is the surface measure on the sphere
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3};|x|=t\}
.
If
d=1
or
d=2
,
G_{t}
is a non-negative function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and if
d=3
,
G_{t}
is a finite measure in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

If
d\geq 4
is even,
G_{t}
is a distribution with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by:

G_{t}=\frac{1}{1\cdot 3\cdot\ldots\cdot(d-1)}\left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{(d-2)/2}(t^{d-1}\Upsilon_{t}),\quad\Upsilon_{t}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{\omega_{d+1}}\int_{B(0,1)}\frac{\varphi(ty)}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}dx,

and if

d\geq 5
is odd,
G_{t}
is a distribution with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by:

G_{t}=\frac{1}{1\cdot 3\cdot\ldots\cdot(d-2)}\left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{(d-3)/2}(t^{d-2}\Sigma_{t}),\quad\Sigma_{t}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{\omega_{d}}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}\varphi(tz)d\sigma(z),

where
\omega_{d}
is the surface area of the unit sphere
\partial B(0,1)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\sigma
is the surface measure on
\partial B(0,1)

(see e.g. Theorem (5.28), page 176 of
[
17
]
).

It is known that for any
d\geq
1, the Fourier transform of
G_{t}
is given by:

\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)=\frac{\sin(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(58)

The solution of equation (
57
) exists provided that
G_{t}
satisfies (
51
), which is again equivalent to Dalang’s condition (
9
).
We examine the existence of the higher moments of this solution for three examples of kernels
\kappa
.

Example 4.10
.

(The heat kernel)

Let
k=H_{d,\alpha/2}
be the heat kernel given by Example
2.10
for some
\alpha>0
.
Dalang’s condition holds for all
\alpha>0
.
By Theorem
4.4
.(a) and the inequality
|\sin(x)|\leq|x|
, for all
p\geq 2
and
t>0
,

J_{p}(t)\leq J_{p}^{\prime}(t)=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\sin^{2}(t|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}e^{-\frac{\alpha|\xi|^{2}}{2}}d\xi\leq Ct^{2},

and
\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(J_{p}(s))^{p/2}ds\leq C\int_{0}^{t}s^{p}ds=Ct^{p+1}
. By (
54
), for any
d\geq 1
,
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq C\Big(t^{\frac{3p}{2}}+t^{p+1}\Big)\quad\mbox{for any $p\geq 2$ such that $m_{p}<\infty$}.

Example 4.11
.

(The Riesz kernel)

Let
k=R_{d,\alpha/2}
be the Riesz kernel given by Example
2.11
for some
\alpha\in(0,d)
. Recall that Dalang’s condition holds for all
\alpha\in((d-2)\vee 0,d)
. But to apply Theorem
4.4
.(b),
G_{t}
has to be a function, so we can only consider
d=1
or
d=2
.

If
d=1
, for any
t>0
and
p\geq 2
, letting
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\alpha}{2}
(and noticing that
q>1
),

J_{p}(t)\leq C\|G_{t}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=Ct^{2/q}=Ct^{\frac{2}{p}+\alpha},

and
\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(J_{p}(s))^{p/2}ds\leq C\int_{0}^{t}s^{\frac{\alpha p}{2}+1}ds=Ct^{\frac{\alpha p}{2}+2}
.
By (
54
), for any
t>0
and

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq\quad C\Big(t^{\frac{p}{2}(\alpha+2)}+t^{\frac{\alpha p}{2}+2}\Big)\quad\mbox{for any $p\geq 2$ such that $m_{p}<\infty$}.

If
d=2
,
\|G_{t}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=Ct^{(2-q)/q}
if

and
\|G_{t}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\infty
if

\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\alpha}{4}

J_{p}(t)\leq C\|G_{t}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}=Ct^{2(2-q)/q}=Ct^{\frac{4}{p}+\alpha-2},

provided that

, which is equivalent to

. In this case,

since

(which is equivalent to

). By (

), for any
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\mathbb{E}|v(t,x)|^{p}\leq C\Big(t^{\frac{p}{2}(\alpha+1)}+t^{3+\frac{\alpha p}{2}-p}\Big)\quad\mbox{for any $2\leq p<\frac{4}{2-\alpha}$ such that $m_{p}<\infty$}.

.

(The Bessel kernel)

Let

. But to apply

5
The non-linear equation

In this section, we study the non-linear equation (
11
) with constant initial condition
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
.

5.1
Existence of solution and moments

The goal of this section is to prove the existence of the solution under very general assumptions on
G_{t}
(similar to those given in
[
14
]
for the Gaussian case), and to present some sufficient conditions under which the solution has a finite
p
-th moment, for
p\geq 2
. We then analyze these conditions for the heat equation and the wave equation, in the case of three examples of kernels
\kappa
: the heat kernel, the Riesz kernel, and the Bessel kernel.

As in Section
4
, we assume that the fundamental solution
G_{t}
of
\mathcal{L}u=0
is a distribution with rapid decrease on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In addition, we impose the following hypothesis, which is listed as Hypothesis C in
[
15
]
.

Hypothesis B.
a) For any
t>0
,
G_{t}
is a non-negative finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}G_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty\quad\mbox{for all $T>0$},

and the distribution induced by this measure (denoted also by
G_{t}
) has rapid decrease and satisfies (
51
);

b)
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

c) there exists a non-negative distribution
k_{t}
with rapid decrease and
\varepsilon>0
such that

|\mathcal{F}G_{t+h}(\xi)-\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)|\leq|\mathcal{F}k_{t}(\xi)|\quad\mbox{for all $h\in[0,\varepsilon],t>0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

and
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}k_{t}(\xi)|^{2}\mu(d\xi)dt<\infty
for any
T>0
.

Remark 5.1
.

Hypothesis B holds is satisfied by the fundamental solution of the heat equation in any dimension
d\geq 1
, and by fundamental solution of the wave equation in dimension
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, provided that
\mu
satisfies Dalang’s condition (
9
).

Theorem 5.2
.

If Hypothesis B holds, then equation (
11
) with constant initial condition
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and globally Lipschitz functions
b
and
\sigma
, has a unique solution
u
. Moreover,
u
is
L^{2}(\Omega)
-continuous and satisfies

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{2}<\infty.

(59)

In particular, this holds for the stochastic heat equation in any dimension
d\geq 1
, and the stochastic wave equation in dimension
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, provided that
\mu
satisfies Dalang’s condition (
9
); if
d=1
, no extra condition is required, since (
9
) always holds.

Proof.

The proof follows using the same argument as in the proof of Theorem 13 of
[
14
]
, using the Picard iteration sequence:
u_{0}(t,x)=\eta
,

u_{n+1}(t,x)=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\sigma\big(u_{n}(s,y)\big)X(ds,dy)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b\big(u_{n}(t-s,x-y)\big)G_{s}(dy)ds.

The only part which requires some new justification is the fact that
Z_{n}(t,x)=\sigma(u(t,x))
satisfies Hypothesis A, i.e.

\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}(t,x)Z_{n}(t,y)]
depends only on
x-y
. This is a consequence of the fact that
u_{n}
has property (S), which is shown in the proof of Lemma
5.5
below.
∎

We recall the following definition from
[
14
]
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
Z=\{Z(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be a process defined on the same probability space as
X
.
We say that
Z

has property (S)
(with respect to
X
) if the finite-dimensional distributions of

\left\{\{Z^{(z)}(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\},\{X_{t}^{(z)}(B);t\geq 0,B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}\right\}

do not depend on
z\in\mathbb{R}
, where
Z^{(z)}(t,x)=Z(t,x+z)
and
X_{t}^{(z)}(B)=X_{t}(B+z)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

The process
X
is spatially homogeneous, in the sense that the finite-dimensional distributions of
\{X_{t}^{(z)}(A);t\geq 0,A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
do not depend on
z
.

Proof.

By Lemma 5.2 of
[
8
]
, the finite-dimensional distributions of
\{L_{t}^{(z)}(A);t\geq 0,A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
do not depend on
z
. Then, for any
t>0
and
A\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle X_{t}^{(z)}(A)

\displaystyle=X_{t}(A+z)=L_{t}(1_{A+z}*\kappa)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{A}*\kappa)(x-z)L(ds,dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{A}*\kappa)(x)L^{(z)}(ds,dx)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{A}*\kappa)(x)L(ds,dx)=X_{t}(A),

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. The same argument shows that
\big(X_{t_{1}}^{(z)}(A_{1}),\ldots,\linebreak X_{t_{k}}^{(z)}(A_{k})\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big(X_{t_{1}}(A_{1}),\ldots,X_{t_{k}}(A_{k})\big)
for any
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

The solution
u=\{u(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
of equation (
11
) has property (S). In particular, for any
(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,(t_{k},x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
and for any

,

\big(u(t_{1},x_{1}+z),\ldots,u(t_{k},x_{k}+z)\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm d}}}{{=}}\big(u(t_{1},x_{1}),\ldots,u(t_{k},x_{k})\big),

(60)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. In particular, the process
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is strictly stationary, for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since
u_{n}(t,x)\to u(t,x)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it suffices to show that

has property (S), for any

.
We will prove this by induction on

For

does not depend on

is spatially homogeneous, by Lemma

[

∎

Theorem 5.6
.

Suppose that Hypothesis B holds. Let
p\geq 2
be such that
m_{p}<\infty
. If
\mathcal{M}_{p}(t)<\infty
for all
t>0

u
of equation (

\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{p}<\infty\quad\mbox{for all}\quad T>0.

(61)

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of Picard iterations defined in the proof of Theorem
5.2
. Note that

\displaystyle u_{n+1}(t,x)-u_{n}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\Big(\sigma\big(u_{n}(s,y)\big)-\sigma\big(u_{n-1}(s,y)\big)\Big)X(ds,dy)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(b\big(u_{n}(t-s,x-y)\big)-b\big(u_{n-1}(t-s,x-y)\big)\Big)G_{s}(dy)ds

\displaystyle=:U_{n+1}(t,x)+V_{n+1}(t,x).

We treat separately the two terms. For the first term, by Theorem
3.6

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|U_{n+1}(t,x)|^{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\big|\sigma\big(u_{n}(s,y)\big)-\sigma\big(u_{n-1}(s,y)\big)\big|^{p}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t-s}(\xi)|^{2}\mu(d\xi)+\|G_{t-s}*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}\right)ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}(t){\rm Lip}^{p}(\sigma)\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\big|u_{n}(s,y)-u_{n-1}(s,y)\big|^{p}\Big(J_{2}(t-s)+\big(J_{p}(t-s)\big)^{p/2}\Big)ds.

For the second term, using Hölder’s inequality, letting

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|V_{n+1}(t,x)|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{t}^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big|b\big(u_{n}(t-s,x-y)\big)-b\big(u_{n-1}(t-s,x-y)\big)\Big|^{p}G_{s}(dy)ds

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{t}^{p-1}{\rm Lip}^{p}(b)\sup_{s\in[0,t]}G_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}|u_{n}(s,y)-u_{n-1}(s,y)|^{p}ds.

By Lemma 15 of

∎

5.2
Exponential bounds for moments

In this section, we consider the non-linear equation without drift:

\mathcal{L}u(t,x)=\sigma(u(t,x))\dot{X}(t,x),\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(62)

with constant initial condition
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and globally Lipschitz function
\sigma
.
The goal of this section is to provide an exponential upper bound for the
p
-th moments of the solution to equation (
62
), for values
p\geq 2
for which these moments exist.
For this, we use the method introduced in
[
18
,
27
]
for the Gaussian case.

For any
\beta>0
and
p\geq 2
, we let
\mathcal{L}^{\beta,p}
be the set of predictable processes
\Phi=\{\Phi(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
such that

\|\Phi\|_{\beta,p}<\infty
, where

\|\Phi\|_{\beta,p}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \Big(e^{-\beta t}\|\Phi(t,x)\|_{p}\Big).

It can be proved that
\mathcal{L}^{\beta,p}
equipped with
\|\cdot\|_{\beta,p}
is a Banach space. (We identify processes
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
, if
\Phi_{1}
is a modification of
\Phi_{2}
.)

For any predictable random field
Z=\{Z(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, we let

(G*Z)(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)Z(s,y)X(ds,dy)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)X^{Z}(ds,dy),

provided that
G_{t-\cdot}(x-\cdot)1_{[0,t]}\in\mathcal{P}_{0,Z}
.

We begin with some preliminary results. Recall that
J_{p}(t)=\|G_{t}*\kappa\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}
.

Proposition 5.9

(Stochastic Young Inequality)
.

Let
p\geq 2
be such that
m_{p}<\infty
. Suppose that
G_{t}
satisfies the following conditions:

a)
G_{t}
is a non-negative distribution with rapid decrease such that (
51
) holds;

b)
(t,x)\mapsto(G_{t}*\kappa)(x)\in L^{p}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
T>0
;

c) there exists
\beta>0
such that
A_{\beta,p}<\infty
, where

A_{\beta,p}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\beta t}J_{2}(t)dt\right)^{p/2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-p\beta t}\big(J_{p}(t)\big)^{p/2}dt.

(63)

Then, for any predictable process
Z=\{Z(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
which satisfies Hypothesis A and (
45
) for all
T>0
, we have:

\|G*Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}A_{\beta,p}\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p},

where
\mathcal{C}_{p}
is the constant from Proposition
2.3
.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of Proposition 3.2 of
[
4
]
(for the Lévy white noise
L
), which was in turn inspired by the
proof of Proposition 5.2 of
[
27
]
(for the space-time Gaussian white noise). Condition a) implies that
G_{t-\cdot}(x-\cdot)1_{[0,t]}\in\mathcal{P}_{0,Z}
, by Theorem
3.5
, and hence
G*Z
is well-defined. Condition b) allows us to apply Theorem
3.7
to the distribution
S(s,\cdot)=G_{t-s}(x-\cdot)1_{[0,t]}(s)
to deduce the following estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|(G*Z)(t,x)|^{p}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\left\{\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|Z(s,y)\|_{p}^{2}J_{2}(t-s)ds\right)^{p/2}+\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}|Z(s,y)|^{p}\big(J_{p}(t-s)\big)^{p/2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\left\{\left(\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\beta s}J_{2}(t-s)ds\right)^{p/2}+\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}e^{p\beta s}\big(J_{p}(t-s)\big)^{p/2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathcal{C}_{p}\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p}\left\{\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\beta(t-s)}J_{2}(s)ds\right)^{p/2}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{p\beta(t-s)}\big(J_{p}(s)\big)^{p/2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathcal{C}_{p}\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p}e^{p\beta t}\left\{\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\beta s}J_{2}(s)ds\right)^{p/2}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-p\beta s}\big(J_{p}(s)\big)^{p/2}ds\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p}e^{p\beta t}A_{\beta,p}.

Hence
e^{-p\beta t}\mathbb{E}|(G*Z)(t,x)|^{p}\leq\mathcal{C}_{p}A_{\beta,p}\|Z\|_{\beta,p}^{p}
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

Theorem 5.10
.

Suppose that Hypothesis B holds. Let
p\geq 2
be such that
m_{p}<\infty
. Suppose that
(t,x)\mapsto(G_{t}*\kappa)(t,x)\in L^{p}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
T>0
, and there exists
\beta>0
such that
A_{\beta,p}<\infty

(64)

where
A_{\beta,p}
is given by (
63
),
\mathcal{C}_{p}
is the constant from Proposition
2.3
, and

is the solution of the linear equation (
50

\frac{1}{p}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{p}\leq\beta^{*}:=\inf\{\beta>0;{\rm Lip}^{p}(\sigma)\mathcal{C}_{p}A_{\beta,p}<1\}.

(65)

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of Picard iterations defined in the proof of Theorem
5.2
with
b=0
. Then
u_{n+1}-u_{n}=G*\big(\sigma(u_{n})-\sigma(u_{n-1})\big)
for any

, where

. By Proposition
5.9
and the Lipschitz property of

\|u_{n+1}-u_{n}\|_{\beta,p}\leq\big(\mathcal{C}_{p}A_{\beta,p}\big)^{1/p}\|\sigma(u_{n})-\sigma(u_{n-1})\|_{\beta,p}\leq\gamma\|u_{n}-u_{n-1}\|_{\beta,p},

where

. Hence,
\|u_{n+1}-u_{n}\|_{\beta,p}\leq\gamma^{n}\|u_{1}-u_{0}\|_{\beta,p}
for any

. Note that

\|u_{1}-u_{0}\|_{\beta,p}=\sigma(\eta)\|v\|_{\beta,p}<\infty
.

Since

,

. The limit of

K_{\beta,p}:=\|u\|_{\beta,p}^{p}=\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(e^{-p\beta t}\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{p}\big)<\infty,

for any

and

. Letting

, we get:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{p}\leq\beta p.

∎

6
Intermittency

In this section, we provide a lower bound for the second moment of the solution of the non-linear equation (
62
) (without drift). We will assume that
\mathcal{L}
is the heat operator in any dimension
d\geq 1
, or the wave operator in dimension
d\leq 2
.
We show that, under some conditions,

\overline{\gamma}(2)>0,

where
\overline{\gamma}(2)
is the upper Lyapounov exponent of order 2, defined by (
14
).
In the next section, in the case when
\sigma(u)=\lambda u
, we will provide an explicit expression for
\overline{\gamma}(2)
, and we will show that in some cases, this is actually a limit.

Our result is similar to Theorem 1.8 of
[
19
]
, which treats a more general parabolic equation:

\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{L}^{*}u+\sigma(u)\dot{W},

(66)

driven by a spatially-homogeneous Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
, which is white in time and has the spatial covariance function
f
. In (
66
),
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the generator of a
d
-dimensional Lévy process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and the fundamental solution
G_{t}^{*}
of the equation
\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{L}^{*}u
is the density of
X_{t}
at time
t
. In particular, if
\mathcal{L}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta
,
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion, and (
66
) becomes the heat equation.

Our method covers simultaneous the heat and wave equations, based on the observation that the Laplace transform:

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}|\mathcal{F}G_{t}(\xi)|^{2}dt,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\beta>0,

(67)

has a similar form for both equations: (see Lemma 2.2 of
[
7
]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta+|\xi|^{2}}\quad\mbox{for the heat equation},

(68)

The parabolic and hyperbolic Anderson models

In this section, we study the equation

with constant initial condition

Using tools from Malliavin calculus, we will provide the chaos expansion of the solution, and under some conditions, an explicit expression for the upper Lyapunov exponent

We recall that the solution of (

u(t,x)=\eta+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)u(s,y)X(ds,dy).

(77)

We will first show that

has an explicit series representation.
For this, we need to recall some basic elements of Malliavin calculus on the Poisson space. We refer the reader to
[
29
]
for more details. We consider the Hilbert space

\mathfrak{H}=L^{2}({\bf Z},\mathcal{Z},\mathfrak{m}),

where
{\bf Z}=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}

\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{B}(Z)

It is known that any random variable

\mathbb{E}|I_{n}(f)|^{2}=n!\|f\|_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}\quad\mbox{for all $f\in\mathfrak{H}^{\odot n}$}.

To identify the chaos expansion of the solution

For the formal argument, on the right hand-side of (

using the same integral form, and we iterate this procedure. We obtain:

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\eta+\eta\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)X(ds,dy)+

\displaystyle\quad\eta\lambda^{2}\int_{0<t_{1}<t_{2}<t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}G_{t-t_{2}}(x-x_{2})G_{t_{2}-t_{1}}(x_{2}-x_{1})X(dt_{1},dx_{1})X(dt_{2},dx_{2})+\ldots

Using (

\displaystyle\int_{0<t_{1}<t_{2}<t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}G_{t-t_{2}}(x-x_{2})G_{t_{2}-t_{1}}(x_{2}-x_{1})X(dt_{1},dx_{1})X(dt_{2},dx_{2})=

To give the rigorous proof for the chaos expansion of the solution, we need to introduce few more facts from Malliavin calculus.

where

.

Proof.

From the chaos expansion
u(t,x)=\eta+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}\big(f_{n}^{*}(\bullet,t,x)\big)

and the orthogonality of the Poisson chaos spaces, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}+\sum_{n\geq 1}n!\|\widetilde{f}_{n}^{*}(\bullet,t,x)\|_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2},

where
\widetilde{f}_{n}^{*}(\bullet,t,x)
is the symmetrization of
f_{n}^{*}(\bullet,t,x)
in the first
n
variables. Since the function
f_{n}(\bullet,t,x)
contains the indicator of the simplex
T_{n}(t)
, we have:

n!\|\widetilde{f}_{n}^{*}(\bullet,t,x)\|_{\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}=m_{2}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}|\mathcal{F}\big(f_{n}(t_{1},\cdot,\ldots,t_{n},\cdot)*\kappa^{\otimes n}\big)(\boldsymbol{x_{n}})|^{2}d\boldsymbol{x_{n}}d\boldsymbol{t_{n}},

where
\boldsymbol{x_{n}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. The conclusion now follows passing to Fourier transforms. For this, we use the same argument as in the Gaussian case (see e.g. Lemma 2.1 of
[
7
]
), and we recall definition (
29
) of the measure
\mu
in our case.
∎

The previous corollary allows us to deduce the following result.

Corollary 7.5
.

Suppose that the measure
\mu
satisfies Dalang’s condition (

). Let

be the solution of equation (

) with constant initial condition

and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\verb 2\2\{0\}

in condition c). Then

Proof.

Recall the definition (

. In the case

\displaystyle H_{\beta}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{2}}{\beta}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{n\geq 1}m_{2}^{n}\lambda^{2n}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(\xi_{1}+\ldots+\xi_{j})\mu(d\xi_{1})\ldots\mu(d\xi_{n}).

This is precisely relation (

) in which
L_{\sigma}^{2}
is replaced by

, and the

∎

More precisely, let

The following result was proved in

for the heat equation, respectively
[
7
]
for the wave equation, in the case

For this result, we need to introduce the following variational constants:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{F}_{d}}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g^{2}(x)dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}dx\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}(f)

\displaystyle=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{F}_{2d}}\left\{\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)g^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{1}dx_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}|\nabla f(x_{1},x_{2})|^{2}dx_{1}dx_{2}\right\},

where
\mathcal{F}_{d}=\{g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g^{2}(x)dx=1\}
and

is the Sobolev space of order 1.

Theorem 7.7
.

Suppose that
\mu
satisfies Dalang’s condition (
9
) and
f
satisfies the scaling property:

f(cx)=c^{-\alpha}f(x)\quad\mbox{for any $c>0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

(85)

for some
\alpha>0
. For any
\theta>0
, let

be the solution of equation (
83

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|u_{\theta}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}2^{-\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}}\mathcal{E}(f)\quad\mbox{for the heat equation},

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|u_{\theta}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{\frac{1}{3-\alpha}}2^{\frac{2-3\alpha}{6-2\alpha}}\big(\mathcal{E}(f)\big)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{6-2\alpha}}\quad\mbox{for the wave equation}.

Proof.

The limit or limsup are not affected by the choice of
\eta
, so we can take
\eta=1
. Let
v_{\theta}
be the solution of equation

\mathcal{L}v=vW_{\theta}
with

,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
initial condition 1, where
W_{\theta}
is a spatially-homogeneous Gaussian noise with covariance (
82
), in which the function
f
is replaced by
\theta f
. Let
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{0,d})

\mathbb{E}|u_{\theta}(t,x)|^{2}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\theta^{2n}n!\|\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}|v_{\theta}(t,x)|^{2}.

For the heat equation, by Theorem 1.3 of
[

]
and Lemma B.2 of
[
7

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|v_{\theta}(t,x)|^{2}=\mathcal{E}_{2}(\theta f)=\theta^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}2^{-\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}}\mathcal{E}(f),

For the wave equation, by Theorem 1.1 of
[
7
]
and Lemma B.2 ibid,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}|v_{\theta}(t,x)|^{2}=2^{\frac{2-3\alpha}{6-2\alpha}}\big(\mathcal{E}(\theta f)\big)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{6-2\alpha}}=2^{\frac{2-3\alpha}{6-2\alpha}}\theta^{\frac{1}{3-\alpha}}\big(\mathcal{E}(f)\big)^{\frac{2-\alpha}{6-2\alpha}}.

∎

Corollary 7.8

Appendix A
Some auxiliary results

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\mathcal{F}f=h
in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some (tempered
\mathbb{C}
-valued) function
h
. If
h\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{F}f=h
in
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

We know that
(f,\mathcal{F}\phi)=(\mathcal{F}f,\phi)=(h,\phi)
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, or equivalently,

(f,\varphi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}(h,\overline{\mathcal{F}\varphi})\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(87)

Since
h\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{F}:L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a one-to-one map, there exists a function
g\in L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mathcal{F}g=h
. This means that there exists a sequence
(g_{n})_{n}
in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
g_{n}\to g
in
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{F}g_{n}\to h
in
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By Plancherel theorem and (
87
),

(g,\varphi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}(\mathcal{F}g,\overline{\mathcal{F}\varphi})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}(h,\overline{\mathcal{F}\varphi})=(f,\varphi),\quad\mbox{for any $\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

It follows that
f=g
a.e.,
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
h=\mathcal{F}f
in
L_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

The following result gives a useful representation of the predictable
\sigma
-field

\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. We include its proof since we could not find it in the literature.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be the predictable
\sigma
-fields on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, respectively
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

We denote
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

(a) We first prove that any process
X\in\mathcal{E}
is
\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-measurable. This will imply that
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\subset\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, since
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
is the minimal
\sigma
-field with respect to which all processes in
\mathcal{E}
are measurable.

Let
X\in\mathcal{E}
be arbitrary. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
X
is of the form (
18
). Since
(\omega,t)\mapsto Y(\omega)1_{(a,b]}(t)
is
\mathcal{P}
-measurable, and
x\mapsto 1_{A}(x)
is
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-measurable, it follows that
(\omega,t,x)\mapsto Y(\omega)1_{(a,b]}(t)1_{A}(x)
is
\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-measurable.

(b) For the reverse inclusion, it is enough to prove that
\mathcal{R}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is the set of rectangles of the form
F\times B
, with
F\in\mathcal{P}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
For this, we will use a
\lambda-\pi
-class argument. More precisely,
let
\mathcal{L}
be the class of all sets
F\in\mathcal{P}
such that
F\times B\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Note that
\mathcal{L}
is a
\sigma
-field.

We need to prove that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{P}
. For this, we observe that
\mathcal{P}=\sigma(\mathcal{A})
where
\mathcal{A}
is the
\pi
-system consisting of sets of the form

F=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}F_{i},\quad\mbox{with}\quad F_{i}=\{(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+};X_{i}(\omega,t)\in A_{i}\},

(88)

where
k\geq 1
,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
are simple processes, and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. It is enough to prove that

\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{L}.

(89)

This will imply that
\mathcal{P}=\sigma(A)\subset\mathcal{L}
, and hence
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{L}
. To prove (
89
), let
F
be a set of the form (
88
). We have to prove that
F\times B\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be arbitrary. Then

F\times B=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}(F_{i}\times B)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)\in A_{i},x\in B\}.

Note that

\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)1_{B}(x)\in A_{i}\}=\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)\in A_{i},x\in B\}\cup\{(\omega,t,x);0\in A_{i},x\in B^{c}\}.

The last set is
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times B^{c}
if
0\in A_{i}
, or
\emptyset
if
0\not\in A_{i}
. Hence

\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)\in A_{i},x\in B\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)1_{B}(x)\in A_{i}\}\verb 2\2(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times B^{c})&\mbox{if $0\in A_{i}$}\\
\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)1_{B}(x)\in A_{i}\}&\mbox{if $0\not\in A_{i}$}\end{array}\right.

In both cases,
\{(\omega,t,x);X_{i}(\omega,t)\in A_{i},x\in B\}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
. Hence
F\times B\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎
