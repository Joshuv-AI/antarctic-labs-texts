Local insights into hepatitis B Virus: genotype distribution and clinical profiles among HIV/HBV co-infected patients in Maputo, Mozambique
Authors: Lucia Mabalane Chambal, Esperança Sevene, Charlotta Nilsson, Corssino Tchavana, Orvalho Augusto, José Luís João, Elias Manjate, Alice Manjate, João Piedade, Ricardo Parreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHepatitis B virus (HBV) coinfection worsens HIV care outcomes and liver disease risk, but genotype-specific data in the World Health Organization (WHO) Africa Region is limited. To address this gap, we assessed HBV genotype distribution and genotype-specific clinical features in a cohort of people living with HIV (PLHIV) in Maputo City, Mozambique.MethodsThis was a sub-analysis of a prospective cohort study that included newly diagnosed, HIV/HBV coinfected patients who were enrolled from May 2021 to November 2023. DNA extraction and partial-genome nested PCR with Sanger sequencing was performed on plasma samples. HBV genotypes were assigned by BLASTn, Geno2pheno, HBVdb, and NCBI-HBV, and phylogeny was inferred with MAFFT-based alignments and maximum likelihood-based phylogenetics. Clinical/laboratory data (Hepatitis B e antigen, HBV viral load, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, CD4+ T cell count, HIV viral load) were recorded. Fibrosis was estimated using the AST-Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) score and WHO thresholds. R was applied for statistical analyses. Group comparisons used Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact and Wilcoxon rank sum tests (complete-case analysis).ResultsOf 1,106 newly diagnosed ART-naïve PLHIV, 81 (7.3%) were hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-positive and genotyping was successful in 55 (68%). Among HBV genotyped patients, the median age was 33.0 years (IQR 30.0, 39.0), 37 (67.3%) were male, 46 (83.6%) had HBV genotype A (subgenotype A1) and 9 (16.4%) genotype E. Median AST, ALT, and APRI scores tended to be higher in genotype E than subgenotype A1 cases, although differences were not statistically significant (AST 71.9 vs. 37.9 U/L; IQR 26.0-118.0 vs. 29.0-98.1; ALT 36.5 vs. 32.6 U/L; IQR 20.4–63.0 vs. 20.2–57.7; APRI 1.3 vs. 0.5; IQR 0.3–1.8 vs. 0.3–1.3). HBV DNA > 2,000 IU/mL occurred in 52.2% of subgenotype A1 and 55.6% of genotype E cases. Most cases were HBeAg-negative (A1: 36/46, 78.3%; E: 6/9, 66.7%).ConclusionHBV subgenotype A1 and genotype E are prevalent among HIV/HBV coinfected patients in Maputo, often with high HBV DNA levels and evidence of liver injury. Routine HBV screening, simple fibrosis assessment and further research are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13454-9.

Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global health problem. In 2023, an estimated 253 million people were living with HBV worldwide (prevalence of 3.2%) and 5.4 million were aged ≤5 years giving an early-childhood HBV prevalence of 0.7% [1]. The global prevalence of chronic hepatitis B (CHB) varies by region. High-endemic regions defined as having a prevalence ≥ 8% include the World Health Organization (WHO) South-East Asia, Western Pacific and Africa Regions [2, 3]. Although 62.7% of people living with HBV reside in the WHO South-East Asia and the Western Pacific Regions, most early-childhood infections and a disproportionate number of new annual infections occur in the WHO African Region (AFR), which accounts for 63% of the 1.1 million new hepatitis B infections globally [1, 2]. Vertical transmission is the predominant route of infection in high-endemic settings and strongly predisposes to chronicity. Approximately 90% of infected neonates develop chronic infection versus only 5% of the infected adults [4–6]. The introduction of the hepatitis B birth-dose vaccine (HepB-BD) in 2004 has reduced the incidence, but coverage remains suboptimal, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2, 5, 7, 8].

HBV (or Orthohepadnavirus hominoidei), a member of the Hepadnaviridae family (genus Orthohepadnavirus; https://ictv.global/taxonomy), is a spherical, enveloped, DNA virus with a diameter of approximately 42 nm [7]. Its genome consists of a relaxed, partially double-stranded circular DNA of about 3.2 kb, containing four overlapping open reading frames (S, C, P and X) that encode seven proteins: preS1, preS2 and S surface antigen (HBsAg), precore and core protein, polymerase protein and X protein [6–8]. The polymerase exhibits reverse transcriptase (RT) and RNase H activities. Still, it lacks a proofreading function during the reverse transcription of the pregenomic RNA, resulting in a relatively high error rate during replication, which contributes to the high genetic heterogeneity of HBV [6, 7, 9]. According to phylogenetic analyses, HBV can be classified into 10 genotypes (A to J) based on an intergroup divergence of more than 8% in the complete genomic nucleotide sequence [3, 6, 7]. Genotypes are further subdivided into at least 35 subgenotypes, defined by more than 4% whole genome divergence [10].

HBV genotypes are distinctly different geographically and exhibit specific individual characteristics. In the WHO AFR region, genotypes A (subgenotypes A1 – A8), D (D1 – D7) and E predominate, while genotypes B (B1 – B10) and C (C - C16) are common in the WHO Western Pacific Asia Region [7, 10]. Genotype D, frequent in parts of Middle East and North Africa, can induce high viral replication yet hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) and HBsAg expression is lower compared to what is seen in other genotypes [11, 12]. Genotypes A, B and C are predominantly described as having vertical transmission as the main mode of transmission [7]. HBV genotypes and subgenotypes affect clinical course and management differently, including prognosis, immune response, HBeAg seroconversion, precore/core promoter mutations, treatment response, and HBsAg seroclearance [6–8]. Previous studies in Mozambique have found that the most prevalent genotypes are A (notably subgenotype A1) and E [13–18]. Subgenotype A1 is associated with greater liver injury, lower levels of HBeAg positivity, an earlier onset of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and a higher risk of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT). In contrast, genotype E has been associated with higher viral load and HBeAg positivity in regional studies [7, 19, 20]. Mozambique has one of the highest HCC burdens globally, with cases presenting at young ages [21]. Routine genotyping can, therefore, inform prognosis, prevention (including MTCT risk), and tailored clinical management in settings with diverse genotypes.

Mozambique is highly endemic for both HIV and HBV, with reported HBV prevalence among people living with HIV (PLHIV) of 7.3% in the south and 9.1% in the north [17, 22]. Progression of HBV-related liver disease can be influenced by HIV status (especially when combined with low CD4+ T cell counts), HBV viral load (HBV DNA), genotype and HBeAg status [23].

In this predefined sub-analysis of HBV coinfection in a prospective cohort of newly diagnosed antiretroviral treatment (ART)-naïve PLHIV in Maputo, we aimed to describe the patients’ HBV genotypes and subgenotypes. In addition, we assessed clinical and laboratory features associated with the identified genotypes.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a sub-analysis of a prospective cohort study that enrolled newly diagnosed, antiretroviral treatment (ART)-naïve PLHIV between May 2021 and November 2023 at Mavalane Health Center (MHC), a primary care peri-urban facility in Maputo City. The cohort study included patients with HIV/HBV coinfection. Exclusion criteria were tuberculosis, pregnancy, known pre-existing liver disease, or inability/unwillingness to provide informed consent. The full study methods and sample-size calculations are described elsewhere [22].

Sample collection and processing

A total of 120 samples were used in the present study. The samples were collected at different timepoints during a 12-month follow-up of 80 HIV/HBV coinfected patients.

The sample collection procedures are described elsewhere [22]. Two mL of blood were collected on site at MHC into EDTA tubes. The blood samples were processed and stored at − 80 °C at the Faculty of Medicine of Eduardo Mondlane University in Maputo. Samples for molecular analyses were thereafter transferred on dry ice to the Instituto de Higiene e Medicina Tropical (IHMT), Universidade NOVA de Lisboa, using a courier service.

DNA extraction and HBV genome amplification

DNA extraction from plasma was done using the Quick-DNA™ Viral Kit (Zymo Research, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the HBV genome amplification, primers were designed from highly conserved overlapping regions of the S and P regions of the HBV genome [24]. A nested PCR was performed. Outer primer pairs were HBPr134 (sense) 5’-TGCTGCTATGCCTCATCTTC-3’ and HBPr135 (antisense) 5’-CAGAGACAAAAGAAAATTGG-3´, and the inner primers were HBPr75 (sense) 5’-CAAGGTTATGTTGCCCGTTTGTCC-3’ and HBPr94 (antisense) 5’-GGTATAAAGGGACTCACGATG-3’ [24]. These primers were originally developed and validated in West African populations and target conserved regions across the main genotypes circulating in Africa (notably genotypes A and E), thereby minimising genotype-related amplification bias and ensuring good sensitivity for African strains [24].

The nested PCR was conducted in two successive steps using the NZYTaq II 2x Green Master Mix (NZYTech, Portugal). The second-round PCR used an aliquot of the first-round amplification product as its template. For each PCR, two negative controls (NC) were included, in which the template DNA was replaced with sterile ultrapure water. A positive control was excluded to reduce the risk of cross-contamination. Both first- and second-round amplification reactions were carried out in 25 µL of total volume, using 0.4 mM of primers HBPr134 and HBPr135 (first round) or HBPr75 and HBPr94 (second round). The PCR reactions were conducted using a Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA), and used a thermal profile that included an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s. A final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 5 min, with an additional step at 4 °C to preserve the reaction products. The success of each PCR amplification was confirmed by analyzing aliquots of the second-round amplification using gel electrophoresis. The amplified DNA were either directly purified and concentrated from the amplification reactions using the kit DNA Clean & Concentrator™ (Zymo Research, USA) or isolated from an agarose gel slice (in case non-specific products were observed after electrophoresis) using the Zymoclean™ Gel DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, USA).

HBV genotyping and phylogenetic analysis

Firstly, sequence homology searches were performed using BLASTn against the GenBank nucleotide sequence database. Thereafter, we applied a combination of approaches that included phylogenetic analysis (see below) and the use of online genotyping resources including Geno2pheno [hbv] 2.0 (https://hbv.geno2pheno.org/index.php), HBVdb (https://hbvdb.lyon.inserm.fr/HBVdb/HBVdbGenotype) and NCBI-HBV (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/genotyping/formpage.cgi) genotyping tools.

Phylogenetic analysis of HBV nucleotide sequences was performed using multiple sequence alignment generated with MAFFT v7 [25], which was subsequently edited with Gblocks [26]. Tree reconstruction was carried out with IQ-TREE [27] using the maximum likelihood optimization criterion based on the suggested evolutionary model (TIM3 + F + I+G4 as indicated by the Akaike information criterion). This model consists of a TIM3 transition model [28] where nucleotide frequencies (F) were calculated from the alignment, non-variable sites are accounted for with the I parameter [29] and where the rate variation among sites is modelled with a four-rate category gamma (G4) distribution [30]. The robustness of the tree topology was assessed using both Shimodaira - Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test [31] and conventional bootstrapping.

Clinical assessment

Liver tests [aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP)], serological markers of hepatitis B [IgM Core hepatitis B antibody (IgM anti-HBc), HBeAg, and hepatitis B e antibody (anti-HBe)], and HBV viral load (HBV DNA) were assessed using standard procedures [22]. For liver disease staging, we used one non-invasive test (NIT), APRI (AST-Platelet Ratio Index), which is calculated using AST and platelet count [32]. According to the 2024 WHO guidelines, evidence of significant fibrosis should be based on an APRI score greater than 0.5, and cirrhosis should be based on clinical criteria or an APRI score greater than 1.0 [32].

Data management and analysis

Data from all study participants were collected using REDCap (© 2024 Vanderbilt University) [33, 34] and then exported to R version 4.3.1 for analysis [35]. Descriptive statistics were used, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means, standard deviations (SDs), and quantiles for quantitative variables. We compared groups using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare medians. Furthermore, we used 2000 bootstrap resamples for the median differences with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). There was a low missingness in the variables collected for this study (less than 1% of observations); therefore, it was plausible that values were missing at random conditional on observed covariates, making complete-case analysis reasonable, and no imputation was performed (given the small number of missing values).

Ethics and approvals

The study was approved by the National Bioethics Committee for Health Research (reference 556/CNBS/18) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonization – Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent and each participant was assured of the confidentiality of their participation.

A material transfer agreement (MTA) was signed between the Faculty of Medicine of Eduardo Mondlane University and the IHMT, Universidade NOVA de Lisboa in Portugal.

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Results

Molecular analyses of hepatitis B virus sequences

We analyzed 120 samples obtained at different visits from 80 patients (one patient’s sample was excluded due to low quality), and amplified a small (approximately 340 bp) fragment of the HBV genome corresponding to an overlapping section of the HBsAg and Pol coding regions using primers and amplification conditions previously described by Oluyinka et al. [24]. From the 120 DNA extracts, we succeeded in obtaining a specific amplicon of the expected size in 61 extracts, corresponding to an amplification success rate of 50.8%. These amplicons were purified and Sanger sequenced, and an unambiguous sequence was obtained from 60 of them. The sequences have been submitted to the DDBJ/ENA/GenBank sequence databases under accession numbers PQ450557-PQ450616. These 60 sequences correspond to 55 patients, of whom 83.6% (n = 46) were infected with genotype A and 16.4% (n = 9) with genotype E (Fig. 1). Of the 120 samples analysed, 58 were HBV PCR – negative. For 42 of these 58 samples, HBV DNA was also undetectable when HBV viral load was later measured on stored aliquots from the same specimens, indicating that low or absent viraemia – particularly in follow-up samples from patients on tenofovir/lamivudine-containing regimens – was the principal reason for amplification failure, rather than issues related to sample storage or transport.

Not surprisingly, due to the short sequences being analyzed, most tree branches were only supported by one of the topology-stability tests used (aLRT). However, reference sequences were found to cluster in separate clusters (with the exception of subtype D, all other were monophyletic) corresponding to the main viral genotypes. Unexpectedly, genotype B sequences formed a robust clade nested within the lineage that grouped a large number of HBV sequences (including references) defined as belonging to genotype A. The isolates in this study partitioned into two distinct monophyletic groups, one representing subgenotype A, and another encompassing genotype E. The latter also incorporated known A/E recombinants. In the phylogenetic tree, the isolates frequently clustered into internally ill-defined clusters as their internal branching pattern was topologically unsupported. Their closest relatives were sequences from the Republic of South Africa (for most of these, a genotype had not been ascertained in their GenBank records, but clearly corresponded to subtype A references) or Mozambique, in both cases corresponding to direct submissions to the sequence databases. One of the sequences obtained in this work (PQ450572) was placed in the tree halfway between the genotype A and G clusters, segregating into an unstable pair with PP123846 from South Africa. No genotype was defined for PP123846 in its GenBank record, but this sequence is over 98% similar to others (e.g. KF922424, AY934774 or AB453987) all classified as genotype A. While genotype assignment to PQ450572 was not entirely clear by phylogenetic analysis, the majority of the online sequences used classified it as genotype A.

HBV genotyping was additionally conducted using the online tool Geno2pheno [hbv] 2.0 (https://hbv.geno2pheno.org/index.php). The analysis categorized the sequences into genotypes A, G, and E, with all genotype A sequences further classified as subgenotype A1.

Demographic, clinical and laboratory characteristics of 55 individuals with an assigned HBV genotype

Among the 55 HIV/HBV coinfected participants included in the genotyping analysis, 46 (83.6%) had HBV subgenotype A1 and 9 (16.4%) had genotype E (Table 1; Fig. 3). Sex and age distributions were similar (male: 65.2% in A1 vs. 77.8% in E; median age 33.0 years [IQR 31.0–39.0] vs. 34.0 [IQR 28.0–36.0]; all p > 0.6). Median platelet count was slightly lower in genotype E than A1 (192 × 109/L [IQR 121.0-216.0] vs. 219 × 109/L [IQR 144.0-296.0]; p = 0.237).

Correlation of HBV genotypes with high APRI and HBV DNA levels

Advanced fibrosis (APRI 0.5-1.0) occurred mainly in subgenotype A1 (8/9 cases). These A1 patients were predominantly male and aged < 45 years, with a median CD4+ count of 265.5 cells/mm³ (IQR 160.5–294.0), and 5/8 had HBV DNA ≥ 2,000 IU/mL. Cirrhosis (APRI > 1.0) also occurred mainly in A1 (14/19 cases). In these, median CD4+ T cell counts were 228.5 cells/mm³ (IQR 79.0-324.0) and 8/14 had HBV DNA ≥ 2,000 IU/mL. Genotype E accounted for 1/9 advanced fibrosis and 5/19 cirrhosis cases, with broadly similar CD4+ T cell levels but fewer cirrhotic patients with high HBV DNA (1/5).

Among the 29 participants with HBV DNA ≥ 2,000 IU/mL, 24 (82.8%) had subgenotype A1 and 5 (17.2%) genotype E. Among the A1 cases, median HBV DNA was 125,844.5 IU/mL (IQR 18,425.0–5,318,353.5) and median CD4+ T cell counts was 160.5 cells/mm³ (IQR 75.0-284.0). Five A1 cases had APRI 0.5-1.0 and eight A1 cases had APRI > 1.0. Among the five genotype E cases, median HBV DNA was 32,700.0 IU/mL (IQR 22,850.0–2,280,501.0) and median CD4+ T cell counts were 165.0 cells/mm³ (IQR 51.0-207.0). One E case had APRI 0.5-1.0 and one E case had APRI > 1.0.

Across all three groups (advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis and high HBV DNA, most affected patients were male and under 45 years of age, consistent with substantial burden of advanced liver fibrosis in younger adults.

Discussion

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) remains a major public health threat in LMICs, where prevention, screening and treatment access are limited. Mozambique has intermediate-high endemicity of hepatitis B. In our study, we observed an HBV prevalence of 7.3% among PLHIV, carrying HBV genotypes A (subgenotype A1) and E, which is consistent with prior Mozambican studies in HIV/HBV coinfected patients and blood donors [13, 16, 17]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 44 African studies reported genotype E as the most common genotype overall and a pooled prevalence of 70.37% (95% CI 55.37–83.39, P < 0.001) [36]. Regional analyses showed genotype A predominating in Eastern and Southern Africa with a pooled prevalence of 72.79% (95% CI 41.7–94.86, P < 0.0001) in 10 studies, and 76.04% (95% CI 64.09–86.20, P < 0.0001) in 11 studies [36]. Genotype A (notably subgenotype A1) predominance has been reported among blood donors, asymptomatic carriers, and patients with cirrhosis, HCC and HIV/HBV coinfection in South Africa, Zimbabwe and Kenya, underscoring regional heterogeneity in genotype distribution and its potential implications [19].

While the HBV genome fragment analyzed was small, which somewhat compromises the stability of the phylogenetic tree, especially in what concerns the internal structure of the main tree branches, phylogenetic analysis appears to indicate that there is no single origin for the HBV sequences. This means that within each genotype there is a large diversity of circulating viral strains, possibly resulting from multiple HBV introductions into Mozambique. On comparative phylogenetic analysis, all of the HBV genotype A sequences obtained in our study clustered randomly with previous isolates obtained from Mozambique and other African countries, including South Africa, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Botswana, which further fortifies the hypothesis of the Southern and Eastern African origin of HBV genotype A [36].

Fibrosis and cirrhosis are key determinants of prognosis and treatment decisions in CHB [32]. The WHO recommends APRI as the preferred non-invasive test to assess the presence of significant fibrosis or cirrhosis among adults in resource-limited settings, and has recently lowered the thresholds to prioritize treatment using APRI ≥0.5 for significant fibrosis and ≥1.0 for cirrhosis (previously 1.5 and 2.0) [32]. Most of the information on the clinical significance of HBV genotypes has been based on studies of patients with chronic HBV infection in Asia with strong evidence for an association between genotype C and a greater fibrosis burden and higher APRI scores than those with genotype B [37]. For African genotypes (A, E, D), associations with APRI are inconsistent, limited by small studies that have been mainly cross-sectional and variably controlled for key confounders such as HIV or hepatitis D virus (HDV) coinfection, alcohol, metabolic disease, and schistosomiasis [38, 39]. In our study, genotype E patients had numerically higher AST, ALT and APRI values than subgenotype A1 patients, though these differences did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution given the small number of genotype E cases (n = 9). Furthermore, 33.3% of genotype E cases were HBeAg-positive compared to 21.7% of A1 cases. This pattern contrasts with a large Zambian study, in which genotype E showed a lower prevalence of HBeAg positivity than genotype A, and while it is directionally consistent with studies in African-descendant populations in Canada and reports from Sudan, which have associated genotype E with higher HBV DNA levels and more frequent HBeAg positivity [7, 19, 40, 41], our data alone do not confirm this association at a statistically significant level. In our study, subgenotype A1 patients were predominantly HBeAg-negative (78.3%), yet 22/46 (47.8%) had APRI > 0.5, consistent with southern African reports that subgenotype A1 may present with substantial fibrosis despite low HBeAg prevalence [19, 20, 42].

A systematic review and meta-analysis performed to inform the 2024 WHO guidelines demonstrated that the HBV DNA levels correlate with increased risks of HCC, cirrhosis, and liver-related mortality [32]. A baseline HBV DNA level > 2000 IU/mL is particularly significant. Dose-response relationship shows that higher strata of HBV DNA are associated with elevated incidences of HCC and cirrhosis, with the risk increasing progressively beyond 2000 IU/mL, whereas persistently low viremia (< 2000 IU/mL) is linked to substantially lower cirrhosis incidence and liver-related mortality, underscoring the importance of monitoring and managing HBV DNA levels above this threshold [32]. HIV/HBV coinfected individuals experience higher liver-related morbidity and mortality and faster progression to cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease and HCC than those with HBV alone, particularly if not on anti-retroviral treatment (ART) [19].

In our study, 24/46 (52.2%) of co-infected individuals with subgenotype A1 and 5 (55.6%) of 9 with genotype E had HBV DNA levels greater than 2000 IU/mL, and nearly 50% of individuals with subgenotype A1 and genotype E presented with CD4+ T cell counts below 200 cells/mm3. These findings are consistent with regional data. Cohort studies from South Africa and other sub-Saharan African settings report high proportions of significant HBV replication and more rapid progression of liver disease among HIV/HBV coinfected patients, and reviews summarising data from Uganda, Zambia and Malawi document similar patterns of elevated HBV viremia in coinfected populations [42–48]. In addition, meta-analyses and regional syntheses estimate HBsAg prevalences of around 7–8% in the general population of the WHO Africa Region and HBV coinfection rates of approximately 7–10% among PLHIV, with a substantial proportion of coinfected individuals meeting the virological thresholds for HBV treatment (≥ 2000–20000 IU/mL) [49–51]. HIV/HBV coinfection, particularly in those with advanced immunosuppression, can enable higher HBV replication and reduce immune control. The late presentation, despite the implementation of Treat-All policies, together with the absence of routine HBV screening in our setting, likely contributed to the high viremia and low CD4+ T cell counts observed.

Our data also showed that men and younger adults were more likely to present with advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis and high HBV DNA levels, a pattern that is concordant with regional evidence linking subgenotype A1 to more aggressive liver disease in younger men [19, 20, 52]. A case-control study from Southern Africa reported a 4.5-fold higher risk of HCC with subgenotype A1 versus other (sub)genotypes, and patients with genotype A developed HCC on average ˜6.5 years earlier than those with non-A genotypes [19, 52]. The heightened hepatocarcinogenicity of subgenotype A1 has been attributed to distinctive sequence features and virological behavior (including HBeAg negativity and variable HBV DNA levels) that may accelerate fibrosis and malignant transformation [52]. These findings underscore the need for targeted screening and early intervention – particularly among men and younger adults – in high subgenotype A1-prevalence settings.

Limitations of our study include the clinic-based sampling frame and small genotype-specific sample sizes, which limit generalizability and warrant cautious interpretation of the results. For this study, patients were newly diagnosed ART-naïve adults at a single peri-urban primary care facility, which likely over-represents younger adults presenting for the first time and under-represents those with advanced disease already on treatment, those in more rural settings, and those who never engaged with care. Furthermore, the study did not include HBV-monoinfected by design, so genotype distribution findings reflect HIV/HBV coinfection specifically, not the broader Mozambican HBV-positive population, and while we can describe genotype and subgenotype patterns among HIV/HBV coinfected patients and compare them with published data from the region, we cannot determine whether HIV infection itself alters HBV genotype distribution at the population level. Not all 81 HBV-positive samples were successfully amplified. We assessed whether PCR amplification failure introduced selection bias, by comparing the 55 successfully genotyped patients with the 26 HBsAg-positive patients who did not yield an amplifiable sequence (supplementary table S1). Most clinical and demographic characteristics (including age, sex, liver enzymes, APRI, FIB-4, and HIV RNA) were similar between groups. The principal differences were virological: the non-genotyped group had markedly lower HBV DNA (median 5.0 vs. 2,676.0 IU/mL; p < 0.001), higher rates of undetectable viraemia (61.5% vs. 18.2%), lower HBeAg positivity (3.8% vs. 23.6%; p = 0.030), and higher CD4 + T cell counts (median 317.0 vs. 205.0 cells/mm³; p = 0.011), consistent with better immune control of HBV replication. These findings suggest that amplification failure was driven by low or absent viremia rather than by differences in liver disease burden, and that the genotyped cohort represents the clinically relevant subset with active HBV replication. Findings should, however, be interpreted with caution in relation to HBsAg-positive PLHIV with fully suppressed HBV DNA. We acknowledge that HCV and HDV co-infection are important contributors to liver disease in African settings. In this cohort, systematic HCV and HDV testing was undertaken, but detailed results and their relationship to liver outcomes will be presented in a separate manuscript focused on viral co-infections. Here, we have restricted our analyses to HBV genotypes, viraemia and their associations with HIV and demographic factors. The present findings on genotype-associated liver injury should therefore be interpreted with this caveat in mind. In addition, the 50.8% amplification success rate for HBV PCR likely reflects low or absent viraemia in many samples, rather than storage or transport problems, which reduced the number of cases available for genotyping and may have limited statistical power for some comparisons. Nonetheless, this is the first detailed genotype-phenotype study in Mozambique with comprehensive clinical and laboratory data, a relatively large overall sample for an HBV genotyping study that included screening of 1,106 PLHIV, of whom 81 were HBsAg-positive, the use of programmatically relevant WHO-endorsed non-invasive fibrosis assessment (APRI) alongside standard virological markers (HBV DNA, HBeAg), and phylogenetic analysis that placed local strains in a regional context [22].

The clinical implications from this study are that significant liver injury and high HBV DNA can occur in relatively young, predominantly HBeAg-negative adults with HIV/HBV coinfection, and that the observed genotype-specific differences in liver injury markers are hypothesis-generating and should not yet be used to guide differential clinical management; both subgenotype A1 and genotype E infections warrant careful monitoring pending larger, well-powered studies. We recommend strengthening HIV/HBV service delivery (diagnosis, linkage to care, and antiviral access), incorporating programmatic non-invasive fibrosis assessment (APRI or, where available, transient elastography) into patient evaluation, and prioritizing prevention of MTCT through screening and treatment during pregnancy and the introduction of the HepB-BD. Finally, there is an urgent need for well-powered longitudinal, genotype-aware studies in African settings that combine standardized genotyping, robust fibrosis measurement and careful adjustment for HIV, HDV and other cofactors. Such evidence will inform genotype-informed risk stratification, surveillance, and treatment policies tailored to high-burden, resource-constrained contexts.

Conclusion

This study highlights the substantial burden of HBV among PLHIV in Maputo, Mozambique and the clinical relevance of determining local genotype diversity. To accelerate progress towards the WHO’s target to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030, we advocate that routine HBV screening is integrated into HIV programs to enable early detection and specific follow-up.
