Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
Mikhail Venchakov

Abstract
Let U U be a maximal unipotent subgroup in a symplectic group over a finite field of sufficiently large characteristic p p . According to the Kirillov’s orbit method, the coadjoint orbits of the group U U play the key role in the description of irreducible complex characters of U U . Almost all important classes of orbits and characters studied to the moment can be uniformly described as the orbits and characters associated with so-called orthogonal rook placements. In the paper, we construct a semi-direct decomposition for the corresponding irreducible characters in the spirit of the Mackey little group method. As a corollary, we present an explicit formula for the character corresponding to an orbit of maximal possible dimension. Keywords: unipotent group, symplectic group, coadjoint orbit, orbit method, irreducible character, Mackey method, polarization, orthogonal rook placement. AMS subject classification: 20C15, 17B08, 20D15.

Let
U
be a unipotent algebraic group over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of sufficiently large characteristic
p
. The main tool in representation theory of
U
is the orbit method created in 1962 by A.A. Kirillov, see
[
Ki62
]
,
[
Ki04
]
,
[
Ka77
]
. The key idea of the orbit method says that the irreducible representations of
U
are in one-to-one correspondence with the coadjoint orbits of this group. Namely, the group
U
acts on its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}
via the adjoint action; the dual action of
U
on the dual space
\mathfrak{u}^{*}
is called coadjoint. It turns out that there is a natural bijection between the set
\mathbin{\mathrm{Irr}}({U})
of all irreducible complex characters of
U
and the set
\mathfrak{u}^{*}/U
of coadjoint orbits, see Theorem
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
below for the precise statement.

Let
U
be a maximal unipotent subgroup (or, equivalently, a Sylow
p
-subgroup) in a simple classical group over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. A complete description of all coadjoint orbits for the group
U
is a wild problem, so a natural question is how to describe certain important classes of orbits and the corresponding irreducible characters. For the case
A_{n-1}
, orbits of maximal possible dimension were classified in the first Kirillov’s work on the orbit method
[
Ki62
]
. All of them are associated with the so-called Kostant cascade, which is in fact an orthogonal rook placement. For other classical root systems, orbits associated with the Kostant cascades also have maximal possible dimension, see
[
Ko12
]
and
[
Ko13
]
. For
A_{n-1}
, orbits of submaximal dimension were classified by A.N. Panov
[
IP09
]
. Such orbits also correspond to orthogonal rook placements (modulo adding simple root covectors to the canonical forms on them). A classification of orbits of maximal dimension in type
C_{n}
follows from C. Andre and A. Neto’s paper
[
AN06
]
. A description of orbits of maximal possible dimension for
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
, as well as of orbits of submaximal dimension for all types
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
and
D_{n}
, will be presented in
[
IV26i
]
. All these orbits are also associated with orthogonal rook placements (modulo adding simple root covectors to the canonical forms on them).

The irreducible characters corresponding to the orbits of maximal dimension
M
in type
A_{n-1}
were computed by C. Andre in 2011
[
An01
]
. The characters of submaximal dimension
M-2
for
A_{n-1}
were calculated by M. Ignatev in
[
Ig09i
]
. A formula for the characters of maximal dimension in type
C_{n}
can be obtained from the results of Andre and Neto
[
AN06
]
. Characters of maximal dimension in the orthogonal case, as well as the characters of submaximal dimension for classical cases, will be computed in
[
IV26i
]
. Andre’s method is based on very nice stratification of
\mathfrak{u}^{*}
established by him, whose strata are numerated by arbitrary (possibly, non-orthogonal) rook placements and maps from them to the set
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
of nonzero numbers.

In this paper, we present another approach to calculating characters based on the Mackey method of little groups. This method reduces the calculation of irreducible characters of a semi-direct product group with abelian normal subgroup to the calculation of irreducible characters of certain subgroups called little groups, see Section
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
for the detail. It turned out that this method allows to compute characters of maximal and submaximal dimension for
A_{n-1}
, see
[
Ig09i
]
. Furthermore, in
[
IV25
]
we applied this method to the calculation of characters of the next possible dimension
M-4
. Here we apply a modification of the Mackey method to the characters associated with arbitrary orthogonal rook placements in the symplectic case. As a corollary, we give an alternative proof of the formula for the characters of maximal dimension given in
[
AN09
]
. We also give an independent classification of orbits of maximal dimension previously obtained in
[
AN06
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
, we briefly recall main definitions and facts about finite symplectic groups and its maximal unipotent subgroups. We also define rook placements in root systems and associated coadjoint orbits. In Section
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
, we recall the orbit method. As the main result, we present a decomposition for an arbitrary character corresponding to an orbit associated with orthogonal rook placement in the spirit of the Mackey method, see Theorem
10
. In particular, to construct the representation corresponding to the orbit of a linear form
f
, and to obtain a decomposition mentioned above, it is needed to present a polarization for
f
. In that section we also established such a polarization for the canonical form on an orbit associated with an orthogonal rook placement.

In the Section
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
, we explore the characters of maximal dimension. At the first part, we provide a classification of orbits of maximal dimension in terms of so-called canonical forms, see Theorem
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
. Next, we describe the support (i.e., the set of elements of group
U
on which the character is not zero) of an arbitrary character of maximal dimension and calculate it’s value. More precisely, we split the support into the union of conjugacy classes and calculate the value of our character on each class, see Theorem
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
.

2. Definitions and notations

The symplectic group has
C_{n}
as it’s corresponding root system. We will denote the root system of type
C_{n}
by
\Phi
. As usual, we will identify it with the following subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

C_{n}=\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j},1\leq i<j\leq n\}\cup\{\pm 2\varepsilon_{i},1\leq i\leq n\},

where
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Pick the set of simple roots
\Delta=\Delta(\Phi)
as in

[
Bou02
]
:

\Delta(C_{n})=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i+1},1\leq i\leq n-1\}\cup\{2\varepsilon_{n}\}.

(1)

The set of positive roots
\Phi^{+}\supset\Delta
looks as follows:

C_{n}^{+}=\{\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j},1\leq i<j\leq n\}\cup\{2\varepsilon_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}.

(2)

Denote by
\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{u}(\Phi)
the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

spanned by the vectors

,

, where

\begin{array}[]{ll}&e_{2\varepsilon_{i}}=e_{-i,i},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\\
&e_{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j}}=e_{j,i}-e_{-i,-j},\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n,\\
&e_{\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j}}=e_{-j,i}+e_{-i,j},\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n.\\
\end{array}

Here,

, and we numerate the rows and the columns of

matrix by the indices

and we denote by

the usual elementary matrix. Of course, it is a maximal nilpotent subalgebra in the corresponding symplectic algebra

Definition 2.2.
Let a subset
D=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t}\}\subset\Phi^{+}
consisting of pairwise orthogonal roots satisfy the following condition:

|D\cap R_{i}|\leq 1\mbox{ and }|D\cap C_{j}|\leq 1\mbox{ for all }i,j.

(4)

We call such a subset

orthogonal rook placement
. (Note than in
[
AN06
]
, such a subset

is called a basic subset of
\Phi
.)

Denote maximal unipotent subgroup
\exp(\mathfrak{u})
in the corresponding symplectic finite group
G
by
U
. In the sequel, we will assume everywhere that
p=\mathrm{char}\,{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\geqslant n
. Under this assumption, the map

\exp\colon\mathfrak{u}\to U\colon x\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}

is well-defined and is in fact a bijection (and also an isomorphism of algebraic varieties over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
);
we denote the inverse map by
\ln
. Furthermore, the
Backer–Campbell–Hausdorff formula claims that, for a Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{u}
and arbitrary
u,v\in\mathfrak{a}
, one has

\exp(u)\exp(v)=\exp(u+v+\tau(u,v)),

(5)

where

\tau(u,v)\in[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}]
(here
[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}]=\langle[x,y],x,y\in\mathfrak{a}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
).

The group
U
acts on its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}
by the adjoint action; the dual action of
U
on the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-dual space
\mathfrak{u}^{*}
is called
coadjoint
. Using the non-degenerate form

\langle A,B\rangle=\mathrm{tr}(AB)

on
\mathfrak{gl}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, one can identify the dual space
\mathfrak{u}^{*}
with the space
\mathfrak{u}^{t}
(in this case,
e_{\alpha}^{*}=e_{\alpha}^{t}
for any

\alpha=2\varepsilon_{i}\in\Phi^{+}
and
e^{*}_{\alpha}=e_{\alpha}^{T}/2
otherwise). Under this identification, the coadjoint action has the following form:

g.x=\mathrm{pr}(gxg^{-1}),~g\in U,~x\in\mathfrak{u}^{*}

(here we denote the projection

\mathfrak{gl}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\to\mathfrak{u}^{*}
along
\mathfrak{u}
by
\mathrm{pr}
). The orbits of the coadjoint action play the key role in the classification of irreducible representations of group
U
.

Definition 2.3.
Let
D=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t}\}\subset\Phi^{+}
be an orthogonal rook placement, and

\xi=(\xi_{\beta})_{\beta\in D}
be a set of nonzero constants from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Put

f=f_{D,\xi}=\sum_{\beta\in D}\xi_{\beta}e_{\beta}^{*}\in\mathfrak{u}^{*}.

We say that the coadjoint orbit
\Omega=\Omega_{D,\xi}\subset\mathfrak{u}^{*}
of the linear form
f
is
associated
with
D
, and
f
is called the

canonical form
on
\Omega
.

Definition 2.4.
For an arbitrary Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and a linear form
f\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
let us consider a set
\mathfrak{p}
which satisfies the following conditions.

1.

For an arbitrary
x,y\in\mathfrak{p}
,
f([x,y])=0
.

2.

The set
\mathfrak{p}
is maximal subspace in
\mathfrak{u}
satisfying property 1.

3.

The set
\mathfrak{p}
is a subalgebra.

Such a subspace is called a
polarization
for the form
f
.

Note that for any linear form a polarization always exists
[
Ve70
]
.
Also, it is easy to see that the dimension of the coadjoint orbit of
f
is connected with the dimension of a polarization of
f
by the simple formula

\dim\Omega_{f}=2\cdot\mathrm{codim}\,\mathfrak{p}

(see, e.g.,
[
Sr74
, page
117]
).

3. Mackey type decomposition for charactersassociated with orthogonal rook placements

First, I want to recall the main idea of the orbit method created by A.A. Kirillov
[
Ki62
]
,
[
Ki04
]
and adapted by D. Kazhdan
[
Ka77
]
to finite groups (see also
[
Ki95
]
), and provide a construction for orbits associated with orthogonal rook placements in symplectic groups. According to the orbit method, the construction of a polarization is a necessary step to obtain the irreducible representation corresponding to the orbit of a given linear form.

Specifically, pick and fix
\theta\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, an arbitrary nontrivial character of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(i.e., a nontrivial homomorphism from the additive group of this field to the multiplicative group of complex numbers). Recall that we called the map inverse to
\exp\colon\mathfrak{u}\to U
by
\ln\colon U\to\mathfrak{u}
.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathfrak{u}^{*}
be an arbitrary coadjoint orbit.
Consider the following function
\chi=\chi_{\Omega}\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
:

\chi(g)=q^{-\frac{1}{2}\dim\Omega}\cdot\sum_{f\in\Omega}\theta(f(\ln(g))),\quad g\in U

(6)

(over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, coadjoint orbits are affine varieties of even dimension in
\mathfrak{u}^{*}
; furthermore,

q^{\dim\Omega}=|\Omega|
).
The main idea of the orbit method can be expressed by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1.
[
Ka77
, Proposition
2]

The map

\Omega\mapsto\chi_{\Omega}
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the set of coadjoint orbits of the group
U
and the set
\mathbin{\mathrm{Irr}}({U})
of finite-dimensional irreducible complex characters of the group
U
. Furthermore
,
the complex dimension of the representation corresponding to
\Omega
equals

q^{\frac{1}{2}\dim\Omega}=\sqrt{|\Omega|}.

\square

Moreover, the construction of the representation corresponding to a given orbit
\Omega
is as follows. One should pick a linear form
f\in\Omega
and a polarization
\mathfrak{p}
for
f
. Next, one should consider the following one-dimensional representation of
\exp(\mathfrak{p})
:

\varphi_{f}(h)=\theta(f(\ln(h))),~h\in\exp(\mathfrak{p}).

The required representation now has the form
\mathrm{Ind}_{\exp(\mathfrak{p})}^{U}{(}\varphi_{f})
.

Now, we aim to provide a construction of a polarization for an arbitrary linear form
f=f_{D,\xi}
associated with an orthogonal rook placement. Let us introduce some more notations.

Definition 3.2.
Let
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
. Roots
\alpha
,
\gamma\in\Phi^{+}
are called

\beta
-
singular
if
\alpha+\gamma=\beta
. The set of all
\beta
-singular roots is denoted by
S(\beta).

It is easy to see that the singular roots look as follows:

\begin{split}S(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j})=&\bigcup_{l=i+1}^{j-1}\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{l}-\varepsilon_{j}\},~1\leq i<j\leq n,\\
S(2\varepsilon_{i})=&\bigcup_{l=i+1}^{n}\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{l}\},~1\leq i\leq n,\\
S(\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j})=&\bigcup_{l=i+1}^{j-1}\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{l}+\varepsilon_{j}\}\cup\bigcup_{l=j+1}^{n}\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{j}+\varepsilon_{l}\}\\
\cup&\bigcup_{l=j+1}^{n}\{\varepsilon_{j}+\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon_{l}\}\cup S_{ij},~1\leq i<j\leq n,\\
\end{split}

where
S_{ij}=\{\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{j},2\varepsilon_{j}\}.

Remark 3.3.
Note that, given
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
, the column of
\beta
contains exactly one of the root from each pair of
\beta
-singular roots whose sum is
\beta
, except for the case
\beta=2\varepsilon_{i}
. Precisely, put

S^{+}(\beta)=\begin{cases}\{\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j},~i<k\leqslant n\},&\text{ if }\beta=2\varepsilon_{i},\\
S(\beta)\cap C_{\text{col}(\beta)}&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

so that
S(\beta)=S^{+}(\beta)\cup S^{-}(\beta)
, where
S^{-}(\beta)=S(\beta)\backslash S^{+}(\beta)
.

Notation 3.4.
Let
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{t}
be the numbers of columns of roots from an orthogonal rook placement
D
. Put
j_{0}=0
and define
M=M_{D}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{t}M_{i}
, where
M_{0}=\varnothing
and, for any
i=1,\ldots,t
, inductively,

M_{j_{i}}=\{\gamma\in S^{-}(\beta)\mid\beta\in D\cap C_{j_{i}}\text{ and }\gamma,\beta-\gamma\notin\bigcup_{l=0}^{i-1}M_{j_{l}}\}.

The next proposition provides a construction of a polarization for
f_{D,\xi}

[
Ig09
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Proposition 3.5.
Let us consider
f=f_{D,\xi}
and put
P=\Phi^{+}\backslash M
,
then

\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}_{D,\xi}=\sum_{\alpha\in P}ke_{\alpha}

is a polarization for the form
f
.
\square

Above we observed a connection between coadjoint orbits and irreducible representations. The orbit method also provides us a formula (
6
) for the irreducible character corresponding to a coadjoint orbit, but this formula is not convinient for calculations in case we want to obtain an explicit formula for character, so we have to use some special methods. More precisely, in the papers
[
Ig09i
]
and
[
IV25
]
we used the Mackey method of little groups to calculate the value of subregular characters and characters of depth 2 for
U
of type
A_{n}
. Furthermore, in the paper
[
IV26
]
for cases when group
U
has type
B_{n}
and
D_{n}
, it was used to obtain so-called Mackey decomposition for characters corresponding to the orbits associated with orthogonal rook placements. Here we modify the Mackey method for type
C_{n}
. The Mackey method states the following.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an arbitrary finite group,
A
,
B
be its subgroups such that
\mathcal{G}
is the semi-direct product of
A
and
B
, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}=AB
,
A\triangleleft\mathcal{G}
and

A\cap B=\{1\}
(we denote
\mathcal{G}=A\rtimes B
). Assume additionally that
A
is an abelian group and that
\psi
is its irreducible character. The
centralizer

of
\psi
in the group
B
(or the
little group
) is the subgroup of the form

B^{\psi}=\{b\in B\mid\psi\circ\tau_{b}=\psi\},

where
\tau_{b}\colon A\to A.~a\mapsto bab^{-1}.

An arbitrary element
g\in\mathcal{G}
can be uniquely represented as

g=ab
, where
a\in A,b\in B
; this defines the maps

\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{A}\colon\mathcal{G}\to A\colon g\mapsto a,~\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{B}\colon\mathcal{G}\to B\colon g\mapsto b,~g\in\mathcal{G}.
Note that
\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{B}
is a group homomorphism, while
\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{A}
, in general, is not. For an arbitrary subgroup
\widetilde{B}
in
B
and arbitrary characters

\psi\colon A\to\mathbb{C}
and
\eta\colon\widetilde{B}\to\mathbb{C}
, one can define the character
\psi_{0}\eta_{0}
of the group

A\rtimes\widetilde{B}=A\widetilde{B}
, where

\psi_{0}=\psi\circ\pi^{A\rtimes\widetilde{B}}_{A},~\eta_{0}=\eta\circ\pi^{A\rtimes\widetilde{B}}_{\widetilde{B}}.

Denote by
\mathbin{\mathrm{Irr}}({\mathcal{G}})
the set of irreducible characters of
\mathcal{G}
. The basic idea of the Mackey method, which allows us
to reduce the study of representations of the group
\mathcal{G}
to the study of representations of
its subgroups
A
and
B
, can be formulated as follows.

Theorem 3.6.
[
Le74
, Proposition
1.2]

Let
\mathcal{G}=A\rtimes B

be a finite group with
A
being an abelian group. Then each irreducible character
\chi
of the group
\mathcal{G}
has the form

\chi=\mathrm{Ind}_{A\rtimes B^{\psi}}^{G}{\psi_{0}\eta_{0}}

(7)

for certain
\psi\in\mathbin{\mathrm{Irr}}({A})
,

\eta\in\mathbin{\mathrm{Irr}}({B})
. Conversely
,
every function of the form
(
7
)
is an irreducible character of
\mathcal{G}
.
\square

However, in the case
C_{n}
we have a problem with finding appropriate groups
A
and
B
such that
U=A\rtimes B
and
A
is abelian, which will be described later. So we can not use the Mackey method directly, but we are going to provide a decomposition of an arbitrary character associated with orthogonal rook placement in the spirit of the Mackey method with minor modifications.

We will consider two separated cases. For an orbit
\Omega_{D,\xi}
associated with an orthogonal rook placement
D=\{\beta_{1}\succ\ldots\succ\beta_{t}\}
in
\Phi^{+}
, let us denote by
\mathfrak{u}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{v}
the subalgebras of
\mathfrak{u}
of the following form:

\mathfrak{u}_{1}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in C^{\prime}}\mathbb{F}_{q}e_{\alpha},~\mathfrak{v}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\setminus C_{1}}\mathbb{F}_{q}e_{\alpha},

where
C^{\prime}=C_{1}\backslash\{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{3},\ldots,\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{n}\}
if
D\cap C_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}
, and
C^{\prime}=C_{1}
otherwise. Note that in the case when
D\cap C_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}
, the algebra
\mathfrak{u}_{1}
is obviously not abelian, and
\mathfrak{u}\not=\mathfrak{u}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{v}
otherwise. Denote
U_{1}=\exp(\mathfrak{u}_{1})
,

V=\exp(\mathfrak{v})
. Also define a function
\psi=\psi_{D,\xi}
on the group
U_{1}
by

\psi(x)=\theta(f(\ln(x)))=\begin{cases}\theta(\xi_{\beta_{1}}x_{i,1}),&\mbox{if }\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})=1,\mathrm{row}(\beta_{1})=i,\\
1,&\mbox{if }\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})>1.\end{cases}

(8)

As usual, we denote the
(i,j)
th element of a matrix
x
by
x_{ij}
.

On the other hand, consider the following subgroup of
V
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&V^{\prime}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{1}^{+}}X_{\alpha},\mbox{ where}\\
&\Phi_{1}^{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Phi^{+}\setminus C_{1},&\mbox{if
}\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})>1,\\
\Phi^{+}\setminus(C_{1}\cup S^{-}(\beta_{1})),&\mbox{if }\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})=1,\end{array}\right.\\
&X_{\alpha}=\{x_{\alpha}(t),t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\},~x_{\alpha}(t)=\exp(te_{\alpha}).\end{array}

In particular, if
\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})>1
or
D\cap C_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}
, then
V^{\prime}=V
. It is clear that
V^{\prime}=\exp(\mathfrak{v}^{\prime})
, where
\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi_{1}^{+}}\mathbb{F}_{q}e_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 3.7.
For an arbitrary orthogonal rook placement

D\subset\Phi^{+}
and an arbitrary
\xi
,
the subgroup
V^{\prime}

coincides with the centralizer of the character
\psi
in the subgroup
V
,
i.e.
,

V^{\prime}=V^{\psi}
.

Proof
.
It is clear if
\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})>1
, because in this case
V^{\prime}=V^{\psi}=V
. Let

\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})=1,\mathrm{row}(\beta_{1})=i.

Here and next, we will write
x=\exp(y)\in V
and

, where

y=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\setminus C_{1}}y_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{v},~z=\sum_{\gamma\in C^{\prime}}z_{\gamma}e_{\gamma}\in\mathfrak{u}_{1},~y_{\alpha},z_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.

Since

, one has

\begin{array}[]{ll}xhx^{-1}&=\exp(y)(1_{m}+z)(\exp(y))^{-1}\\
&=1_{m}+\exp(y)z(\exp(y))^{-1}.\end{array}

But
\exp(y)z(\exp(y))^{-1}=(\exp\mathrm{ad}_{y})(z)
, so we obtain

xhx^{-1}=1_{m}+(\exp\mathrm{ad}_{y})(z).

In particular, by formula (

\psi(xhx^{-1})=\theta(\xi_{\beta_{1}}((\exp\mathrm{ad}_{y})(z))_{i,1}).

Note that
V^{\prime}=V
in case

, so
V^{\psi}\subset V^{\prime}
. It means that we need only to prove that
V^{\prime}\subset V^{\psi}
. It is clear whenever
\mathrm{col}(\beta_{1})=1
. Indeed, by definition,
V^{\prime}
is the product of the subgroups
X_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\Phi_{1}^{+}
, but this subgroups centralize the character
\psi
. Hence, it is left to prove that
V^{\psi}\subset V^{\prime}
in the case when
\beta_{1}\neq 2\varepsilon_{1}
.

Assume that
y\in\mathfrak{v}\backslash\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}
, we claim that there exists
z\in\mathfrak{u}_{1}
such that
z_{i,1}\neq((\exp\mathrm{ad}_{y})(z))_{i,1}
. More precisely, let us rewrite

in the following form:

y=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi_{1}^{+}}y_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}+\sum_{\delta\in S^{-}(\beta_{1})}y_{\delta}e_{\delta}\in\mathfrak{v}\setminus\mathfrak{v}^{\prime},~y_{\alpha},~y_{\delta}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.

Let
\delta_{0}
be the smallest root with respect to the order

(see

for which

. There exists
\gamma_{0}\in\Phi^{+}
such that
\gamma_{0}+\delta_{0}=\beta_{1}
(furthermore,
\gamma_{0}\in C_{1}
). Consider the element

. By definition,
z_{i,1}=0
. At the same time,

(\exp\mathrm{ad}_{y})(z)=z+\mathrm{ad}_{y}z+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{y}^{2}z+\ldots.

At first, let us assume that
\delta_{0}\neq 2\varepsilon_{i}
(the case
\delta_{0}=2\varepsilon_{i}
is little technically different, we will consider it later). It is easy to see that
\mathrm{ad}_{y}z=c_{0}y_{\delta_{0}}e_{\beta_{1}}
where

, so

for all

. Hence,

Now, let us move to the case when

. Define the set

to be the set of roots from

which are bigger than

in sense of the order
\prec
. The roots from

are, in fact, roots from the column

without the root

. Here we see that
\gamma_{0}=\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{i}
. The obvious calculations shows us that

\mathrm{ad}_{y}z=c_{0}y_{\delta_{0}}e_{\beta_{1}}+\sum\limits_{S^{*}_{i}}y_{\delta}e_{\delta+\gamma_{0}}.

Note that all the roots
\delta+\gamma_{0}
where

and even to

S^{*}_{i}

Here

\square

Theorem 3.8.
Let
\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{\widetilde{U}}=\pi^{V^{\prime}}_{\widetilde{U}}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{V^{\prime}}

\chi=\mathrm{Ind}_{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}^{U}{((\psi\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot(\widetilde{\chi}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{\widetilde{U}}))}.

(10)

Proof
. Put
P=\exp(\mathfrak{p})\subset U,\widetilde{P}=\exp(\widetilde{\mathfrak{p}})\subset\widetilde{U}
, then
P=U_{1}\rtimes(V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P})
. Let us denote the function on the right-hand side of
(
10
) by
\eta
. By Theorem

Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
,

\chi=\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)},\quad\widetilde{\chi}=\mathrm{Ind}_{\widetilde{P}}^{\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)}.

We will break down further reasoning into a series of steps.

1. We claim that if
y\in P
then
f(\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y))=f(\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y))
. Indeed, it is evident that

\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y)=(\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}_{V_{1}}\circ\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}})(y)\cdot(\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}_{\widetilde{P}}\circ\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}})(y)=\pi^{P}_{V_{1}}(y)\cdot\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y).

Let
u=\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}}(y)\in\mathfrak{p}
,
v=\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y)\in\mathfrak{p}
, then, from (
5
), one has

\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y)=\exp(u)\exp(v)=\exp(u+v+\tau),\quad\mbox{where }\tau\in[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}],

so

\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y)=\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}}(y)+\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y)+\tau
. But
\mathfrak{p}
is a polarization for
f
, so
f(\tau)=0
. At the same time,
\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}}(y)\in\mathfrak{v}_{1}
and, clearly,

f{\mid}_{\mathfrak{v}_{1}}\equiv 0
(see the definition of
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
in
(
9
)). It follows that

f(\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y))=f(\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}}(y))+f(\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y))+f(\tau)=f(\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y)).

2. We claim that if
y\in P
, then
f(\ln y-\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y)-\ln\pi^{P}_{U_{1}}(y))=0
. The proof is similar to the previous step. Indeed, if
u=\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y)\in\mathfrak{p}
,

v=\ln\pi^{P}_{U_{1}}(y)\in\mathfrak{p}
then, according to
(
5
),

y=\pi^{P}_{U_{1}}(y)\cdot\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y)=\exp(u)\exp(v)=\exp(u+v+\tau),\quad\mbox{where }\tau\in[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}].

Hence,
\ln y=\ln\pi^{P}_{U_{1}}(y)+\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}+\tau
.
But
\mathfrak{p}
is a polarization for
f
, so
f(\tau)=0
and, consequently,

f(\ln\pi^{P}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}(y))=f(\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(y))
.

3. The next claim is as follows. Let
\mathcal{G}=A\rtimes B
be a finite group,
C
be a subgroup in
B
and
\lambda
be a complex representation of the group
C
. Then

\mathrm{Ind}_{A\rtimes C}^{G}{(\lambda\circ\pi^{A\rtimes C}_{C})}=(\mathrm{Ind}_{C}^{B}{\lambda})\circ\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{B}
. This is proved in
[
Le74
, Proposition
1.2]
.

4. Let us apply step 3 to the case
\mathcal{G}=V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}
,
A=V_{1}
,
B=\widetilde{U}
,
C=\widetilde{P}
,
\lambda=\theta\circ f\circ\ln
. We obtain that

\mathrm{Ind}_{\widetilde{P}}^{\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)}\circ\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}}_{\widetilde{U}}=\mathrm{Ind}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}_{\widetilde{P}})}.

Now we apply step 3 to the case of
\mathcal{G}=U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}
,

A=U_{1}
,
B=V^{\prime}=V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}
,
C=V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}
,

\lambda=\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}_{\widetilde{P}}
.
We have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{Ind}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}_{\widetilde{P}})}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}}&=\mathrm{Ind}_{U_{1}\rtimes(V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P})}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}}_{\widetilde{P}}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes(V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P})}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{P}})}=\\
&=\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}})}.\end{array}

Hence, keeping in mind that
V^{\prime}=V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}
, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{Ind}_{\widetilde{P}}^{\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{\widetilde{U}}&=(\mathrm{Ind}_{\widetilde{P}}^{\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)})\circ\pi^{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}}_{\widetilde{U}}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{V_{1}\rtimes\widetilde{U}}=\\
&=\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}})}.\end{array}

5. One can rewrite the right-hand side of (
10
) taking into account

\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta&=\mathrm{Ind}_{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}^{U}{((\psi\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot(\widetilde{\chi}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{\widetilde{U}}))}=\\
&=\mathrm{Ind}_{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}^{U}{((\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot(\mathrm{Ind}_{\widetilde{P}}^{\widetilde{U}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)}\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{\widetilde{U}}))}=\\
&=\mathrm{Ind}_{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}^{U}{((\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}})})}.\end{array}

6. We claim that the following equality holds:

\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln)}=(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}})}.

Indeed, let
H\subset U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}
be an arbitrary complete
representative system of
(U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime})/P
(in fact, one can choose
H
to be a subset of
V^{\prime}
, because
U_{1}\subset P
). Then, for any

,

\begin{array}[]{ll}&((\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}})})(x)=\\
&=\theta(f(\ln\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}}(x)))\cdot\sum_{h\in H\mid h^{-1}xh\in P}\theta(f(\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(h^{-1}xh)))=\\
&=\sum_{h\in H\mid h^{-1}xh\in P}\theta(f(\ln\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}}(x)+\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(h^{-1}xh))).\end{array}

But
h\in B
implies that
h^{-1}\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{A}(x)h=\pi^{\mathcal{G}}_{A}(h^{-1}xh)

for an arbitrary finite group
\mathcal{G}=A\rtimes B
and arbitrary

x\in G
,
h\in B
. In our situation, this means that

\psi(X)=f(\ln\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}}(x))=f(\ln(h^{-1}\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}}(x)h))=f(\ln\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}}(h^{-1}xh)),

because

, see Lemma

Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
.

In addition, for

, we have

. Using step 2,
we obtain

\begin{array}[]{ll}&((\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}_{U_{1}})\cdot\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{U_{1}\rtimes V^{\prime}}{(\theta\circ f\circ\ln\circ\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}})})(x)=\\
&=\sum_{h\in H\mid h^{-1}xh\in P}\theta(f(\ln\pi^{P}_{U_{1}}(x)+\ln\pi^{P}_{\widetilde{P}}(h^{-1}xh)))=\\

7. Combining steps 5 and 6, we conclude that

4. Characters of maximal dimension

In this section, we give an alternative proof of the formula for the characters of maximal dimension given in
[
AN09
]
. We also give an independent classification of orbits of maximal dimension previously obtained in
[
AN06
]
. Firstly, we need to recall some notations from
[
IP25
]
.

Let
X
be a subset of
\Phi^{+}
. Set
\mathfrak{u}_{X}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in X}\mathbb{F}e_{\alpha}
with the (Lie) bracket given by the formula

[e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}]=\begin{cases}[e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}],&{\rm~if~}\alpha+\beta\in X,\\
0,&\text{if }\alpha+\beta\notin X\end{cases}

for all
\alpha,\beta\in X

(note that if
\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
, then
[e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}]
is proportional to
e_{\alpha+\beta})
. Such a bilinear bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
do not always define a Lie algebra but it does define a Lie algebra under the assumption that
X
is a
C
-pattern (a shorthand for “combinatorial counterpart of a pattern subgroup”) or
C
-quattern (a shorthand for “combinatorial counterpart of a quattern subgroup”), see the next definitions.

Definition 4.1.
We will say that
X
is a
C-pattern
if
X
satisfies the following condition:

\text{if }\alpha,\beta\in X\text{ and }\alpha+\beta\in\Phi^{+}\text{ then }\alpha+\beta\in X.

This condition is clearly equivalent to the statement that
\mathfrak{u}_{X}
is a sub-Lie algebra of
\mathfrak{u}
.

Definition 4.2.
We will say that
X
is a
C-quattern
if
X=X_{+}\setminus X_{-}
for two C-patterns
X_{+},X_{-}
such that
X_{-}\subset X_{+}
and

\text{if }\alpha_{-}\in X_{-},\alpha_{+}\in X_{+}~\text{ and }\alpha_{-}+\alpha_{+}\in X_{+}\text{ then }\alpha_{-}+\alpha_{+}\in X_{-}.

This is clearly equivalent to the statement that
\mathfrak{u}_{X_{-}}
is an ideal of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}_{X_{+}}
and in this case we have
\mathfrak{u}_{X}\cong\mathfrak{u}_{X_{+}}/\mathfrak{u}_{X_{-}}
where the right hand side is a quotient of Lie algebra by its ideal and thus the left hand side is a Lie algebra.

From now on we assume that
X
is always a C-quattern. We denote by
U_{X}
the unipotent group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}_{X}
. Every C-pattern
X
is a C-quattern for
X_{+}=X,X_{-}={\varnothing}
and for every C-quattern
X
the data
(\mathfrak{u}_{X},[\cdot,\cdot])
provides a structure of Lie algebra on
\mathfrak{u}_{X}
.

Set

Z(X):=\{\alpha\in X\mid(\alpha+X)\cap X={\varnothing}\}.

Then it is easy to verify that
\mathfrak{n}_{Z(X)}
coincides with the center of
\mathfrak{n}_{X}
. In particular, this implies that
Z(X)\neq\varnothing
for any C-quattern
X
.

Definition 4.3.
Pick
f\in\mathfrak{u}_{X}^{*}
and consider
Z\subset Z(X)
. We will say that
f
is
Z
-saturated
if
f(e_{\alpha})\neq 0
for all
\alpha\in Z
. We will say that
f
is
saturated
if
f
is
Z(X)
-saturated.
We denote the variety of
Z
-saturated linear forms by
\mathfrak{n}^{*}_{X;Z}
.

It is clear that each
\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}
is
U_{X}
-stable and thus is a collection of coadjoint orbits. We map
\mathfrak{u}^{*}_{X;Z}
to
\mathfrak{u}^{*}
by the formula
e_{\alpha}^{*}\mapsto e_{\alpha}^{*}
and denote by
\underline{\mathfrak{n}}^{*}_{X;Z}
the image of this map.
We will frequently identify
\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}
with
\underline{\mathfrak{u}}_{X;Z}^{*}
.

Definition 4.4.
Let
Z\subset Z(X)
be a subset and let
Y\subset\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}
be a subvariety.
We say that
Y
is a
set-section
for
\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}
if
Y
intersects each
U_{X}
-orbit of
\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}
in exactly one point.

It is clear that a set-section is a section of the native quotient map
\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}\to\mathfrak{u}_{X;Z}^{*}/U_{X}
but only in set-theoretic terms.
Also note that all the set-sections which we will consider explicitly will be unions of the form
V(S_{1})\sqcup V(S_{2})\sqcup\ldots
for some subsets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots\subset\Phi^{+}
.

Next, we define the subset
D_{\text{reg}}
of positive roots as follows:

D_{\text{reg}}=\{(2\varepsilon_{1},2\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,2\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n-1}+\varepsilon_{n})\}\in\Phi^{+}

(i.e., the subset
D_{\text{reg}}
consists of the roots corresponding to the squares
(-1,1),\ldots,(-n,n)
and
(-2,1),\ldots,(-n,n-1)
, as in Example
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
). As in Section

Theorem 4.5.
Let

Proof
. The fact that all such orbits have maximal dimension follows directly from the construction of polarizations (note that maximal possible dimension of a coadjoint orbit in

To prove the converse, we firstly want to proof that, given

of the matrix
x
without the first column and the last row); let us denote this matrix by
\widetilde{x}
. The coefficients of the linear part of this form are
(-2x_{-2,1},-2x_{-3,1},\ldots,-2x_{-n,1}
); let us denote this vector by
b
. The set of all centres of this quadratic form is described by the system of linear equations
\widetilde{x}y^{t}=-\frac{b^{t}}{2}
, where
y
is the vector of variables. Applying the Kronecker–Capelli criterion to this system, we obtain the remaining part of equations on the first column, except the conditions on the minor
\Delta_{2\varepsilon_{1}}^{D^{*}}(x)
, what gives us the variety
\widetilde{k}_{D^{*}}(\phi)
where this minor might have any value. It is equal to the condition that the root
\beta_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}
can belong to
D^{*}
or not. Hence,
x\in k_{D^{*}}(\phi)
and
g\in K_{D^{*}}(\phi)
for some
D^{*}\in D^{\prime}
.

Let us move to the case
\beta_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{n-1}
. As in the previous case, we will start from describing a complete set of cosets representatives of
U_{1}V^{\prime}
. One can easily check that set
M
can be chosen in the following way:

M=\{\exp(t)\mid t\in\mathfrak{u}\backslash(\mathfrak{u}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{v}^{\prime})\}.

Next, we need to notice that elements not from
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
and
R_{-1}
,
R_{-2}
in arbitrary
g\in U
do not change by the conjugation with
h\in M
. Furthermore, the projections from
U_{1}V^{\prime}
to
V^{\prime}
and from
V^{\prime}
to
\widetilde{U}
work just by deleting the elements from
C_{1}
,
R_{-1}
and
C_{2}
,
R_{-2}
respectively. So the formula (
14
) can be rewritten as

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\sum\limits_{y=h^{-1}gh\mid y\in U_{1}V^{\prime},h\in M}\theta(f(\ln(y_{U_{1}}))).

Since
\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\neq 0
, we obtain the following equations on the element
x
:

\Delta_{\beta}^{D^{*}}(x)=\Delta_{\beta}^{D^{*}}(x_{D^{*}}(\varphi))\text{ for all }\beta\in R(D^{*})\backslash(C_{1}\cup C_{2})

for certain
D^{*}\in D^{\prime}
. Now, we want to satisfy the condition
y=h^{-1}gh\in U_{1}V^{\prime}
. As in the previous case, this statement is equivalent to the statement that
z=h^{-1}xh\in\mathfrak{u}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}
by arguments as above. From the conditions
z_{i,2}=0
for
j=\overline{3,n}
and the equations on the minors written above, we obtain that
x_{j,2}=0
for
j=\overline{3,n}
(for this places
\Delta_{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{i}}^{D^{*}}(x)=x_{i,2}
). Let us write down the remaining conditions which appear from the condition that
z\in\mathfrak{u}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}
. The conditions
z_{i,2}=0
for
i
from
-n
to
-3
give us that
\widetilde{x}\widetilde{h}^{t}=b^{t}
, where
\widetilde{x}
is
(2n-2)\times(2n-2)
matrix consists of the elements of the matrix
\pi(x)
,
\widetilde{h}=(h_{3,2},h_{4,2},\ldots,h_{n,2})
and
b=(h_{-n,2},h_{-n+1,2},\ldots,h_{-3,2})
. The condition
z_{-2,2}=0
gives us

\sum\limits_{i,j=3}^{n}h_{i,2}h_{j,2}x_{-i,j}-2\sum\limits_{i=3}^{n}h_{i,2}x_{-i,2}+x_{-n,2}=0.

Using equations from the system
\widetilde{x}\widetilde{h}^{t}=b^{t}
we can rewrite latter expressions as follows:

\sum\limits_{i=3}^{n}x_{-i,2}h_{i,2}=x_{-2,2}.

Combining this equation with the system
\widetilde{x}\widetilde{h}^{t}=b^{t}
we have the new system of linear equations
\widetilde{\widetilde{x}}\widetilde{h}^{t}=\widetilde{b}^{t}
, where
\widetilde{\widetilde{x}}
is obtained from
\widetilde{x}
by adding one string
(x_{-3,2},x_{-4,2},\ldots,x_{-n,2})
and the vector
\widetilde{b}
is obtained from
b
by adding
x_{-2,2}
. This system also have to be consistent, so we can use Kronecker–Capelli criterion. It gives us the remaining conditions on the second column, in particular the minor
\Delta_{2\varepsilon_{2}}^{D^{*}}(x)
is always zero (because the rank of the matrix
\widetilde{x}
is not bigger than
n-2
, and the size of the augmented matrix of the system
\widetilde{\widetilde{x}}\widetilde{h}^{t}=\widetilde{b}^{t}
is
(n-1)\times(n-1)
). Hence, now we have the following equations:

\Delta_{\beta}^{D^{*}}(x)=\Delta_{\beta}^{D^{*}}(x_{D^{*}}(\varphi))\text{ for all }\beta\in R(D^{*})\backslash C_{1}

To obtain the equations for the first column we have to look at the expression

\theta(\xi_{\beta_{1}}(\ln(y_{U_{1}})))=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}x_{i,1}h_{-i,1}-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}x_{-i,1}h_{i,1}+x_{-1,1}.

From the system
\widetilde{x}h^{t}=b^{t}
we see that some of the elements
h_{i,2}
(and only them),
i=3,\ldots,n
, can be expressed from the others. As a corollary from the expression above, we immediately obtain that
x_{i,1}=0
for
i=2,\ldots,n
. Hence,

\theta(\xi_{\beta_{1}}(\ln(y_{U_{1}})))=-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}x_{-i,1}h_{i,1}+x_{-1,1}.

(15)

So, we need to solve the system of linear equations
\widetilde{x}h^{t}=b^{t}
. Let us consider two sets of indices sorted with respect to the order
\prec
:

T_{D^{*}}=\{\mathrm{row}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in D^{*},\mathrm{row}(\alpha)<-2\}\cup\{-\mathrm{col}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in D^{*},\mathrm{col}(\alpha)>2\},

T^{D^{*}}=\{-\mathrm{row}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in D^{*},\mathrm{row}(\alpha)<-2\}\cup\{\mathrm{col}(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in D^{*},\mathrm{col}(\alpha)>2\}.

Denote the matrix lying on the intersection of the strings from
T_{D*}
and the columns from
T^{D^{*}}
by
x^{\prime}
. This matrix will be a matrix of maximal order consisting of the elements from
\widetilde{x}
with nonzero determinant, denote this determinant by
\Delta
. After solving the system
\widetilde{x}h^{t}=b^{t}
and substituting the result into the equality (
15
) we obtain that

\theta(\xi_{\beta_{1}}(\ln(y_{U_{1}})))=-\sum\limits_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\backslash T^{D^{*}}}\Delta_{-i,1}h_{i,1}+c,

here the minors
\Delta_{-i,1}
are lying on the strings from
T_{D^{*}}\cup\{-i\}
and the columns
T^{D^{*}}\cup\{1\}
and
c
is a certain constant. Thus, all the minors
\Delta_{-i,1}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\backslash T^{D^{*}}
must be zero as the coefficients before linear summands. It gives us the remaining part of the equations on the first column, except for the minors
\Delta_{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{n-1}}^{D^{*}}(x)
and
\Delta_{2\varepsilon_{1}}^{D^{*}}(x)
, that gives us a variety
\widetilde{k}_{D^{*}}(\phi)
, where these two minors might have any value. It is equivalent to the following: the root
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{n-1}
belongs to
D^{*}
, or
2\varepsilon_{1}
belongs to
D^{*}
and
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{n-1}
does not belong to
D^{*}
, or both of these roots do not belong to
D^{*}
. That concludes the proof of the first statement of the theorem.
\square

Proof of Theorem
Rook placements and coadjoint orbits for maximal unipotent subgroups of finite symplectic groups
, part (
b
)
.

Given
D
,
D^{*}\subset D^{\prime}
,
\phi
and
\xi
as in the theorem. We will consider the two cases. At first, we will consider the case when
\beta_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}\in D
. For convenience, we will write
\phi_{\alpha}=0
if
\phi
is not defined on
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
. We proceed by induction on
n
. The base of induction in this case for
\Phi=C_{2}
can be checked directly by the formula

\chi=\chi_{\Omega_{f}}(g)=q^{-\frac{1}{2}\dim\Omega_{f}}\sum\limits_{f^{\prime}\in\Omega_{f}}\theta(f^{\prime}(\ln(g))),

where
f=f_{D,\xi}
and
g=X_{D^{*}}(\phi)=\exp(x_{D^{*}}(\phi))
. For example, let us consider this calculation for
\beta_{1}=2\varepsilon_{1}
(in fact, this is the most technically complicated case). We have

\chi(g)=q^{-1}\sum\limits_{f^{\prime}\in\Omega_{f}}\theta(f^{\prime}(x_{D^{*}}(\phi)).

According to the formula of the coadjoint action and noticing that
f^{\prime}(x_{D^{*}}(\phi))
is taken
|\mathrm{Stab}(f)|
times we obtain that

\chi(g)=q^{-1}\theta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{2}}\phi_{2\varepsilon_{2}})\theta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}\phi_{2\varepsilon_{1}})\sum\limits_{y\in\mathfrak{u}}\theta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}(y_{2,1}^{2}\phi_{2\varepsilon_{2}}))|\mathrm{Stab}(f)|^{-1}.

Using
[
LN97
, Theorem 5.3.3]

and taking into account that
|\mathrm{Stab}(f)|=|U|/|\Omega_{f}|
we get

\chi(g)=\begin{cases}q\prod\limits_{\alpha\in D\cap D^{*}}\theta(\xi_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}),&\text{ if }\phi_{2\varepsilon_{2}}\notin D^{*},\\
\eta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}\phi_{2\varepsilon_{2}})G(\eta,\theta)\prod\limits_{\alpha\in D\cap D^{*}}\theta(\xi_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha})&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

which agrees with the formula. Thus, the base of induction is proven in that case. Other cases can be checked similarly.

So, let us move to the step of induction. Recall the definitions of
M
,
U_{1}
,
\widetilde{U}
,
V^{\prime}
from the proof of the previous part. Since our character can be obtained as an induced character, we can use the standard formula for an induced character:

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\sum\limits_{y=h^{-1}gh|y\in U_{1}V^{\prime},h\in M}\theta(f(y_{U_{1}})).

Here
g_{\widetilde{U}}
is the element
x_{\widetilde{D}^{*}}(\widetilde{\phi})\in U(C_{n-1})
, where
\widetilde{D}^{*}=\pi(D^{*})
and
\widetilde{\phi}
is the natural restriction of
\phi
to the set
\widetilde{D}^{*}
. Proceeding by the formula of the coadjoint action, we obtain

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\theta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}\phi_{2\varepsilon_{1}})\sum\limits_{y=h^{-1}gh|y\in U_{1}V^{\prime},h\in M}\theta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}(\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\widetilde{D}^{*}_{0}}\phi_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}^{2}+\sum\limits_{\beta\in\widetilde{D}^{*}_{1}}2\phi_{\beta}h_{\beta}h_{\beta^{\prime}})),

where
h_{\alpha}=h_{i,1}
for
\alpha=2\varepsilon_{i}\in\widetilde{D}^{*}_{0}
and
h_{\beta}=h_{k,1}
,
h_{\beta^{\prime}}=h_{l,1}
for
\beta=\varepsilon_{k}+\varepsilon_{l}\in\widetilde{D}^{*}_{1}
. Using the argumentation as above, we see that

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\theta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}\phi_{2\varepsilon_{1}})G^{|\widetilde{D}^{*}_{0}|}(\eta,\theta)q^{(n-1)-|\widetilde{D}^{*}|}\prod\limits_{\beta\in\widetilde{D}^{*}_{0}}\eta(\xi_{2\varepsilon_{1}}\phi_{\beta}).

Taking into account that
D_{0}=\widetilde{D}_{0}\cup\{2\varepsilon_{1}\}
,
D_{1}=\widetilde{D}_{1}
and

t(D,D^{*})=t(\widetilde{D},\widetilde{D}^{*})+|\widetilde{D}^{*}|,\quad t_{0}(D,D^{*})=t_{0}(\widetilde{D},\widetilde{D}^{*})+|\widetilde{D}^{*}_{0}|,\quad t_{1}(D,D^{*})=t_{1}(\widetilde{D},\widetilde{D}^{*}),

and by applying the inductive assumption to
\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})
, we obtain the requested formula.

In fact, the case when
\beta_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
is much simpler. We will skip the proof for the base of induction because it is similar to the previous case. Here we have

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\sum\limits_{y=h^{-1}gh|y\in U_{1}V^{\prime},h\in M}\theta(f(y_{U_{1}})).

Simple calculations with matrices show us that

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})\sum\limits_{y=h^{-1}gh|y\in U_{1}V^{\prime},h\in M}\theta(\xi_{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}\phi_{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}).

The set of
h\in M
such that
y\in U_{1}V^{\prime}
can be described as the set of
h\in M
such that
h_{i,2}=0
for
i\in\{-n,\ldots-2\}\cup T^{D^{*}}
. It is easy to see that
|T^{D^{*}}|=|D^{*}_{0}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})|+2|D^{*}_{1}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})|
. Hence,

\chi(g)=\widetilde{\chi}(g)\theta(\xi_{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}\phi_{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}})q^{n-1+n-2-|D^{*}_{0}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})|+2|D^{*}_{1}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})|}.

It is only left to notice that
\widetilde{D}_{0}=D_{0}
and

t(D,D^{*})=t(\widetilde{D},\widetilde{D}^{*})+|\widetilde{D}^{*}|,\quad t_{0}(D,D^{*})=t_{0}(\widetilde{D},\widetilde{D}^{*}),\quad t_{1}(D,D^{*})=t_{1}(\widetilde{D},\widetilde{D}^{*})+|D_{1}^{*}(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})|.

After applying the inductive assumption to
\widetilde{\chi}(g_{\widetilde{U}})
we obtain the required result.
\square
