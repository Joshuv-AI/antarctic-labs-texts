Spectral focused imaging enables enhanced colorectal adenoma detection: a multicenter, parallel randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qing-Wei Zhang, Juan Liao, Xianhui Zeng, Nan Yi, Na Li, Tao Zhang, Mei Liu, Guoliang Ye, Ping Yao, Jifeng Chou, Chen Hu, Xiao-Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) contributes markedly to global cancer-associated mortality, typically arising through the well-established adenoma–carcinoma sequence [1, 2]. Evidence demonstrates that the early detection and removal of precancerous adenomas via colonoscopy significantly decreases the incidence and mortality of CRC [3–5]. A central metric used to assess the quality and efficacy of colonoscopic screening is the adenoma detection rate (ADR), representing the proportion of procedures identifying a minimum of one adenoma [6]. An ADR below 25% has been strongly correlated with an increased risk of post-colonoscopy CRC and associated mortality [7]. As such, enhancing ADR has become a critical objective in CRC prevention strategies.

While white light imaging (WLI) continues to serve as the conventional modality for adenoma detection during colonoscopy, it has notable limitations, particularly in identifying diminutive (≤ 5 mm) and flat lesions. These limitations are reflected in miss rates reported to be as high as 26% for adenomas ≤ 5 mm, and 13% for those measuring 6–9 mm [4]. These undetected lesions have been linked to interval cancer development. Moreover, lesions with higher malignant potential—such as depressed-type lesions (Paris 0-IIc), which carry an elevated risk of submucosal invasion even at small sizes, and sessile serrated lesions (SSL), which may progress rapidly through the serrated neoplasia pathway within three years—are also recognized contributors to interval CRCs [8, 9]. These considerations further underscore the need for improved imaging technologies capable of detecting a broad spectrum of precancerous lesions. In response to these challenges, a range of image-enhanced endoscopy (IEE) techniques have been developed to improve lesion visibility and diagnostic accuracy. These include chromoendoscopy [10], narrow band imaging [11], linked color imaging (LCI) [12], texture and color enhancement imaging (TXI) [13], and I-scan approaches [14]. Although each of these approaches has shown promise, adenoma detection remains suboptimal in certain scenarios, underscoring the need for continued innovation in endoscopic imaging.

Spectral Focused Imaging (SFI) is an emerging endoscopic technology designed to enhance the detection of colorectal neoplasms by utilizing multiple discrete light wavelengths. By selectively enhancing light absorption by specific tissue chromophores, particularly hemoglobin, SFI amplifies the visual contrast between neoplastic and normal mucosa [15]. This targeted spectral approach theoretically improves the visualization of subtle or flat lesions that may be missed with conventional WLI. However, despite its theoretical advantages, there have been no investigations providing direct comparisons of the effectiveness of SFI and WLI in improving ADR. The present study was thus developed to address this knowledge gap by conducting the first large-scale, multicenter, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) to determine whether SFI significantly improves ADR compared to conventional WLI in both screening and diagnostic colonoscopy procedures.

Methods

This parallel-group, multicenter, RCT was carried out across eight endoscopy centers, with each site receiving approval from its respective institutional review board. The study was coordinated through a lead center (approval ID: LY2024-136-C-AMD-02). Ethics approval was not secured at an additional ten centers prior to study closure, and therefore no participants were recruited from those sites. The investigation is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06603948). The study followed the CONSORT 2025 guidelines for reporting randomized trials [16], and adhered strictly to the recommedations of the Declaration of Helsinki. All authors had full access to the data and contributed to the final review and approval of the manuscript.

Patients

Participants were recruited based on standard indications for colonoscopy, as outlined in current clinical guidelines [17–19]. Between October 2024 and April 2025, 698 participants were enrolled after meeting predefined eligibility criteria. Inclusion criteria were as follows: adults aged 45 to 85 years undergoing their first screening or diagnostic colonoscopy, and those who voluntarily provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: patients unable to tolerate or cooperate with the procedure (e.g., due to suspected intestinal obstruction, perforation, or toxic megacolon); individuals with known inflammatory bowel disease, hereditary polyposis syndromes, CRC, or prior colorectal surgery; patients presenting with gastrointestinal bleeding; individuals with contraindications for biopsy or polypectomy; pregnant or lactating women; those concurrently enrolled in another clinical trial; and individuals who declined to participate.

Bowel preparation

Participants underwent bowel cleansing using split dosages of high-volume polyethylene glycol solution supplemented with simethicone. Bowel cleanliness was assessed using the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS), with scores categorized as “adequate” (total BBPS score 6–9 and all segment score > = 2) or “inadequate” (a total BBPS score < 5 or any segment score < 2).

Randomization

Eligible subjects were randomly assigned (1:1) to undergo colonoscopy with either SFI or high-definition WLI applied during both insertion and withdrawal phases. Randomization was stratified by center, with each participating site performing independent block randomization (blocks of 4) using computer-generated random number sequences. Allocation was managed independently of the endoscopists, who had no involvement in sequence generation or group assignment. Patients were blinded to the imaging modality used during their examination.

Colonoscopy procedures

All procedures were performed with the Sonoscape EC-550 series high-definition video colonoscopes (EC-550, EC-550 L), paired with HD580 processors and DSP-320WA monitors (Additional file 1: Figure S1). Magnification was not employed. SFI technology (Fig. 1, Additional file 2: Video 1, and Additional file 3: Video 2) was integrated into the endoscopy system and could be activated with a single button press on the endoscope control head (Additional file 4: Video 3). The SFI mode provided real-time spectral enhancement and could be toggled on or off instantaneously during the procedure without interrupting the examination flow. Endoscopists were trained on the SFI operation protocol prior to study initiation, which included standardized techniques for optimal image acquisition and lesion characterization using the enhanced spectral contrast.

Each procedure adhered strictly to the randomized imaging modality (either SFI or WLI) throughout both insertion and withdrawal stages. Upon reaching the cecum, a detailed inspection of the colonic mucosa was performed during withdrawal. The duration of withdrawal represented the total time taken for inspection of the mucosa, excluding any time spent on washing, diagnostic biopsies, or therapeutic interventions. Consistent with established colonoscopy quality standards [20], a minimum withdrawal time of 6 min was targeted.

Once a lesion was identified in either group, both WLI and SFI modes were used to observe and photograph the lesion before proceeding with endoscopic resection or biopsy. For every lesion identified, its size, morphology, and anatomical location were recorded. Lesions were partially or completely resected using polypectomy, biopsy, endoscopic resection of the mucosa or dissection of the submucosa, according to the nature and extent of the lesion, to allow histological assessment. A total of 32 endoscopists conducted the procedures; 29 were classified as experienced (having performed ≥ 500 colonoscopies), while 3 were considered less experienced (< 500 colonoscopies).

Histopathology

Resected tissue samples were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin and stored in separate containers for each lesion. Standard histopathologic processing and staining protocols were followed. All tissue specimens were classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumours of the digestive system (5th edition) [21] by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists at each participating center, with blinding to the imaging modality used during the procedure. Lesion with diameters ≥ 10 mm were considered advanced adenomas, as were those with villous architecture or exhibiting high-grade dysplasia.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the ADR), representing the proportion of cases with a minimum of one adenomatous lesion. Secondary endpoints included the polyp detection rate (PDR), representing the proportion of cases with one polyp, the advanced adenoma detection rate (aADR), indicative of the proportion of a minimum of one advanced adenoma, the sessile serrated lesion detection rate (SDR), representing a minimum of one SSL, adenomas per colonoscopy, indicating average adenoma number per procedure, and the total lesions per colonoscopy, representing the overall lesion number identified including adenomas, advanced adenomas, and SSLs per colonoscopy.

Primary analysis was undertaken using the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) and per-protocol (PP) frameworks. The mITT cases included all randomized participants who successfully reached the cecum and had complete histopathological data from biopsies or resections. The PP framework included cases who had undergone full colonoscopy with the allocated imaging modality and had adequate bowel preparation as defined by BBPS.

Sample size

Given the absence of prior data on ADR using SFI, an internal pilot phase involving 200 patients (enrolled as part of this trial before January 2025) was conducted to estimate effect size and inform power calculations. These patients were included in the final analysis. ADRs observed were 33% and 45% in the WLI and SFI groups, respectively, suggesting a 12% absolute improvement with SFI. Assuming a power of 80% and a two-sided alpha of 0.05, at least 520 patients were required. To accommodate an anticipated attrition rate of 20%, the final recruitment target was set at 650 patients to maintain adequate statistical power.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0. Continuous variables including adenomas per colonoscopy and polyps per colonoscopy are shown as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on distribution. Between-group comparisons were performed using t-tests for normal distribution of variables or the Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normal distribution of variables. Categorical variables are given as frequencies and percentages, and were compared using chi-square tests. Effect sizes for continuous outcomes are reported as mean differences (MD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For categorical outcomes, relative risks (RR) and 95% CIs were calculated. The primary analysis was focused on ADR. Secondary endpoints and subgroup analyses were pre-specified but are considered exploratory, as no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. These findings should be interpreted with caution given the inflated risk of type I error. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P ≤ 0.05 was considered indicative of significance.

Results

The participant selection strategy is detailed in Additional file 1: Figure S2. After excluding individuals with bowel obstruction caused by advanced CRC, failure to reach the cecum, or absence of histological samples, 698 cases were enrolled in the final analysis, with 350 in the SFI group and 348 in the WLI group. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between groups regarding age, sex, colonoscopy indication, use of anesthesia, insertion and withdrawal times, BBPS scores, or adequacy of bowel preparation. No procedure-related adverse events, including delayed perforation or clinically significant post-procedural bleeding, were observed in either group during the follow-up period.

Outcomes

Per-patient analysis

A detailed comparison of the ADR and other outcomes is presented in Table 3; Fig. 2A. The ADR was significantly greater in the SFI group relative to the WLI group (38.29% vs. 30.46%; RR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.02–1.55; P = 0.030). Similarly, the PDR (56.29% vs. 47.99%; RR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.02–1.35; P = 0.028), aADR (6.57% vs. 3.16%; RR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.03–4.20; P = 0.036) showed a marked improvement with SFI. Although higher rates of SDR (3.14% vs. 1.15%; RR, 2.73; 95% CI, 0.88–8.50; P = 0.069) were seen in the SFI group, the differences were non-significant. Findings from the PP analysis (Additional file 1: Table S1) were consistent with the mITT results. The ADR remained higher with SFI (38.72% vs. 30.56%; RR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.02–1.57; P = 0.029), as did the PDR (56.40% vs. 48.46%; RR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.00–1.35; P = 0.042) and aADR (7.01% vs. 3.09%; RR, 2.27; 95% CI, 1.10–4.70; P = 0.022), while the SDR did not (3.05% vs. 1.23%; RR, 2.47; 95% CI, 0.78–7.79; P = 0.110).

Per-lesion analysis

Per-lesion outcomes are presented in Table 2; Fig. 2B. The average adenoma number per colonoscopy was markedly greater in the SFI group relative to the WLI group (0.74 vs. 0.51; MD, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.03–0.42; P = 0.021). This trend was also observed for the mean number of polyps per colonoscopy (1.33 vs. 0.98; MD, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.10–0.59; P = 0.006), advanced adenomas per colonoscopy (0.08 vs. 0.03; MD, 0.05; 95% CI, 0.01–0.09; P = 0.025), and SSLs per colonoscopy (0.04 vs. 0.01; MD, 0.03; 95% CI, 0.00–0.05; P = 0.048). The PP analysis confirmed these findings for adenomas, polyps, and advanced adenomas per colonoscopy. However, the difference in SSL detection per colonoscopy did not reach statistical significance in this subset (0.04 vs. 0.01; MD, 0.02; 95% CI, 0.00–0.05; P = 0.074).

Further exploratory subgroup analysis (Fig. 3B and Additional file 1: Table S2) revealed that SFI led to a higher average number of adenomas per colonoscopy in the right colon (0.26 vs. 0.16; MD, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.01–0.20; P = 0.032), diminutive adenomas (0.47 vs. 0.30; MD, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.04–0.31; P = 0.013), and large adenomas (0.07 vs. 0.03; MD, 0.04; 95% CI, 0.00–0.08; P = 0.033). Similar results were noted for IIa or transverse polyp per colonoscopy, with SFI affording a higher detection rate as compared to WLI. For further details, see Additional file 1: Table S2.

Discussion

This multicenter, parallel-group RCT represents the first comparative evaluation of SFI and high-definition WLI in both screening and diagnostic colonoscopy settings. Our findings demonstrate a substantial 25.7% increase in adenoma detection using SFI compared to WLI (ADR: 38.29% vs. 30.46%; P = 0.036) but did not significantly increase withdrawal time, reinforcing ADR as a critical quality metric for endoscopic performance. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggest that SFI is particularly effective in identifying proximal, flat, and diminutive lesions, which are features commonly associated with missed or interval CRC.

In our study, a methodological consideration is the use of the assigned imaging modality during both the insertion and withdrawal phases. While ADR is primarily influenced by the withdrawal phase, lesion identification during insertion is not uncommon, particularly in the left colon. The rationale for applying the assigned modality throughout the entire procedure was twofold: first, to evaluate SFI’s real-world clinical performance as it would be used in routine practice, where endoscopists typically maintain a consistent imaging mode throughout the procedure; and second, to ensure methodological consistency by eliminating potential confounding from switching imaging modes mid-procedure, which could introduce observer adaptation effects. Since both groups used their assigned modality throughout the entire procedure, any detection advantage during insertion applies symmetrically to both arms and does not systematically bias the between-group comparison. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that this design choice may modestly increase the overall detection rate in both groups relative to studies that restrict enhanced imaging to the withdrawal phase alone.

Previous studies, including a recent meta-analysis, have reported that NBI enhances ADR during withdrawal [11]. However, these benefits were largely confined to patients with near-perfect bowel preparation (BBPS score of 9). In cases with only “adequate” preparation (BBPS ≥ 6), NBI’s performance declined, likely due to its reduced brightness and increased susceptibility to obscuration by luminal content. Such limitations compromise its effectiveness in routine clinical practice. From a mechanistic perspective, SFI enhances lesion visibility by utilizing multiple discrete narrow-band wavelengths that are preferentially absorbed by tissue chromophores, particularly hemoglobin. This spectral approach amplifies the color contrast between neoplastic mucosa—characterized by increased microvasculature and altered surface architecture—and adjacent normal tissue (Fig. 1). Importantly, SFI preserves overall brightness levels comparable to WLI, thereby minimizing the image degradation associated with luminal residue that commonly compromises NBI performance. These attributes make SFI more robust in real-world settings where bowel preparation may be less than ideal. Our findings are consistent with emerging evidence from recent RCTs demonstrating that enhanced imaging modalities significantly improve ADR compared to high-definition WLI [12, 22–25]. The collective data suggest that image-enhancement technologies that boost lesion visibility without compromising brightness or introducing visual artifacts from luminal content are more suitable for routine polyp detection than those relying solely on blue-light imaging.

When evaluating polyp subtypes based on size, the present results are consistent with prior tandem colonoscopy studies, which have consistently shown that diminutive polyps (< 5 mm) are more frequently overlooked than their small or large counterparts [26, 27]. The clinical implications of enhanced diminutive adenoma detection merit careful consideration. While the majority of diminutive adenomas (≤ 5 mm) carry low individual malignant potential, their detection is directly linked to ADR—the most widely accepted colonoscopy quality benchmark—and higher ADR is consistently associated with reduced rates of interval CRC and CRC-related mortality [6, 28]. Moreover, the identification of additional diminutive adenomas may reclassify patients into higher-risk surveillance categories according to current post-polypectomy guidelines [29, 30], potentially affecting surveillance colonoscopy intervals. Emerging evidence also suggests that a subset of diminutive adenomas, particularly those harboring high-grade dysplasia or villous architecture, may carry non-negligible progression potential [31–33]. Finally, enhanced detection of diminutive lesions reflects the overall vigilance of the colonoscopic examination and may serve as a surrogate for improved detection of clinically significant but morphologically subtle lesions that might otherwise be missed [28]. These findings reinforce the potential utility of SFI in enhancing the detection of these diminutive lesions. Notably, the improvement with SFI was more apparent in the right colon, as an anatomical region often implicated in the development of interval CRCs [34]. In the cohort of this study, the proximal ADR was 1.5 times greater with SFI compared to WLI, paralleling outcomes reported in studies utilizing other image-enhancement technologies [23, 24, 35].

Flat lesions, which tend to exhibit subtle visual characteristics and blend with adjacent mucosa, are a well-documented challenge in conventional WLI colonoscopy [22, 25, 36]. These morphologies are also frequently associated with post-colonoscopy CRC [37]. When stratifying adenomas by morphological subtype, we observed marked increases in ADR, PDR, and the mean number of polyps detected per colonoscopy for lesions classified as type IIa under SFI. This observation aligns with prior investigations comparing other image-enhancement technologies with WLI for flat lesion detection [12, 13, 22, 24, 25, 38, 39], a finding likely attributable to the shared image-enhancement principles among these technologies. Furthermore, several studies have suggested that other image-enhancement technologies, and by extension, SFI, may outperform WLI in identifying SSLs [12, 38]. In our study, the SDR was 2.7 times higher in the SFI group relative to the WLI group, although this was found to be non-significant, likely reflecting a limited sample size. The relatively low SDR observed in our study compared with some Western cohorts is likely multifactorial. Beyond the limited sample size, SSL diagnosis is highly dependent on pathological expertise and the quality of tissue orientation during sectioning; interobserver agreement for SSL classification is notably lower than for conventional adenomas [40]. Substantial variability has also been documented at both the endoscopy-center and pathology-laboratory levels, with some centers rarely identifying any proximal serrated lesion as an SSL [41, 42]. In our multicenter setting, a proportion of endoscopically suspected SSLs were reclassified as hyperplastic polyps upon local histopathological review, possibly reflecting incomplete capture of characteristic architectural features in sectioned specimens [43]. Future studies specifically designed and powered to evaluate the impact of SFI on sessile serrated lesion detection, with SDR as the primary endpoint and incorporating centralized expert pathology review with serial sectioning protocols, are warranted to confirm these preliminary observations.

Recent advances in AI have transformed computational pathology and oncology imaging, with foundation models such as CHIEF [44] and PROGPATH [45] demonstrating generalizable performance across diverse cancer types, and domain-specific models such as HiCervix reaching expert-level accuracy in cytology [46]. This progress is rapidly translating into gastrointestinal oncology: deep learning digital biopsies now predict postoperative recurrence of gastric cancer from H&E pathology [47], CT radiomics [48], or multimodal fusion [49]; multimodal AI virtual biopsies further enable preoperative detection of peritoneal lavage cytology-positive disease [50] and occult peritoneal metastasis [51]. Within colonoscopy specifically, computer-aided detection (CADe) systems have been evaluated in multiple large randomized controlled trials [52]. AI-based polyp detection systems have shown promise in reducing adenoma miss rates in several recent RCTs [53]. Importantly, SFI and AI-assisted detection represent complementary rather than competing approaches. SFI enhances the visual contrast between neoplastic and normal mucosa at the image acquisition level, effectively providing a higher-quality input signal for both human observers and AI algorithms. Enhanced mucosal contrast may improve the sensitivity and specificity of AI-based polyp recognition, and the integration of SFI with computer-aided detection systems represents a promising avenue for future investigation. We plan to explore this synergistic approach in subsequent studies.

This investigation has several important strengths. It represents the first multicenter RCT specifically designed to assess the effects of SFI on colorectal adenoma detection. Conducted across eight institutions, the study ensured equivalent withdrawal times between groups, a critical factor in maintaining the internal validity of colonoscopy outcomes. By incorporating both tertiary academic hospitals and non-academic institutions, we aimed to reflect a broader range of clinical practice settings. This diversity enhances the external validity of our findings, allowing for more generalizable conclusions regarding SFI’s performance in real-world settings. Moreover, the use of a parallel-group RCT design with ADR as the primary endpoint provides a robust methodological framework well-suited to assessing the efficacy of emerging imaging technologies. Nonetheless, such trials are logistically demanding, requiring pathological confirmation all identified lesions and large sample sizes to achieve adequate statistical power.

An additional methodological consideration is the use of the assigned imaging modality during both the insertion and withdrawal phases. While ADR is primarily influenced by the withdrawal phase, lesion identification during insertion is not uncommon, particularly in the left colon. The rationale for applying the assigned modality throughout the entire procedure was twofold: first, to evaluate SFI’s real-world clinical performance as it would be used in routine practice, where endoscopists typically maintain a consistent imaging mode throughout the procedure; and second, to ensure methodological consistency by eliminating potential confounding from switching imaging modes mid-procedure, which could introduce observer adaptation effects. Since both groups used their assigned modality throughout the entire procedure, any detection advantage during insertion applies symmetrically to both arms and does not systematically bias the between-group comparison. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that this design choice may modestly increase the overall detection rate in both groups relative to studies that restrict enhanced imaging to the withdrawal phase alone.

There are, however, several limitations to consider. First, blinding of the endoscopists was not feasible, as the imaging modality (SFI vs. WLI) was inherently visible during procedures, introducing the potential for detection bias favoring the newer modality. However, several design features mitigated this risk. SFI is activated by a single button press on the endoscope control head and does not alter the fundamental inspection technique; the transition between SFI and WLI is instantaneous, requiring no additional technical expertise beyond standard colonoscopy skills. All participating endoscopists received standardized training on SFI operation prior to study initiation, ensuring comparable familiarity with both modalities. Furthermore, 29 of 32 endoscopists were experienced (≥ 500 colonoscopies), and the involvement of eight centers with diverse practice patterns reduces the likelihood of systematic operator bias. Random allocation of participants was computer-generated with block randomization and managed independently of the endoscopists, who had no involvement in allocation sequence generation. Patients were blinded to the imaging modality. Although we did not perform a formal temporal trend analysis to assess learning effects or performance drift during the trial period, the standardized training protocol and the technical simplicity of SFI activation minimize the likelihood of significant learning curve effects. We recommend that future studies incorporate prospective monitoring of temporal performance trends and learning curves.

Second, although the trial involved multiple centers, all participating institutions were located within China. This geographical limitation may affect the applicability of the results to other populations, as differences in baseline ADR, patient risk profiles, dietary and environmental factors, and colonoscopy practice standards may exist across geographic regions [54, 55]. However, several features of our study support reasonable external validity: (a) the multicenter design incorporating both tertiary academic and non-academic community hospitals represents a broad spectrum of clinical environments; (b) the relative improvement in ADR with SFI (25.7% increase, RR 1.26) is consistent with the effect sizes reported for other image-enhancement technologies across diverse populations, suggesting that the benefit of spectral enhancement is likely generalizable; and (c) SFI’s mechanism of action—spectral contrast enhancement of tissue chromophores—is based on fundamental tissue optical properties that are not population-specific. Validation in geographically diverse, multi-ethnic populations is an important next step.

Third, although all pathologists followed the WHO classification criteria for digestive system tumours [21], the absence of centralized pathology review may have introduced interobserver variability in lesion classification, particularly for advanced adenoma classification and SSL diagnosis [56]. Future trials should incorporate centralized expert review to minimize diagnostic discrepancies.

Fourth, the overall ADR observed in our study was relatively low compared to international studies evaluating advanced imaging modalities, where reported ADRs range from 40.9% to 69.6% [12, 13, 22–24, 35, 36]. This discrepancy is in line with other trials conducted in China [38, 57] and likely reflects multiple contributing factors. First, our study required strict histopathological confirmation for all lesions; if we had included optically diagnosed adenomas, the ADRs would have been 39.72% for the SFI group and 31.28% for the WLI group, suggesting that our strict inclusion criteria contributed to the observed rates. Second, our cohort included both screening and diagnostic indications, and the proportion of average-risk screening patients may differ from Western cohorts that predominantly enroll high-risk or symptomatic individuals, where baseline ADR is inherently higher. Third, well-documented regional variations in colonoscopy practice exist across different geographic regions; studies conducted in East Asian populations have consistently reported ADRs in the range of 14–25% [54, 55], compared with 30–50% in many Western cohorts [28, 58]. These differences may reflect variations in patient selection criteria, dietary and environmental factors, and colonoscopy technique standards. Finally, the mix of screening versus diagnostic indications may also influence ADR, as studies enriched for symptomatic patients tend to report higher detection rates [59]. Notably, the relative improvement in ADR with SFI (25.7% increase) remains clinically meaningful regardless of absolute baseline rates.

Fifth, ASA classification was not prospectively recorded, which precludes direct comparison of comorbidity burden between the two groups. However, standard exclusion criteria were applied uniformly, and colonoscopy indications were balanced between groups (Table 1). Sixth, the study included multiple secondary and subgroup analyses without adjustment for multiple comparisons. These findings are exploratory in nature and carry an elevated risk of type I error. Confirmatory studies with pre-specified primary endpoints for individual subgroup outcomes are warranted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this trial represents the first and most extensive parallel-group RCT to date comparing SFI and WLI for adenoma detection. Our findings demonstrate a clear advantage of SFI over standard high-definition WLI, particularly in identifying adenomas located in the right colon, as well as those that are diminutive or flat in morphology. These results support the incorporation of SFI into routine clinical colonoscopy, where it could offer meaningful improvements in neoplasia detection.
