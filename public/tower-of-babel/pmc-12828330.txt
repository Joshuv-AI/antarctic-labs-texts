The effects of doxorubicin on blood-brain barrier integrity in hCMEC/D3
Authors: Chadni Patel, Christina Glytsou, Mi-Hyeon Jang, Peter D. Cole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current chemotherapy regimens have significantly improved overall survival for children with cancer. However, these treatments are associated with detrimental side effects like chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment (CICI), or “chemobrain.” Measurable deficits in cognitive function persist years after treatment. Specifically, doxorubicin (DOXO), a commonly used chemotherapeutic agent in curative regimens for children with cancer, plays a pivotal role in the development of CICI, even though it doesn’t cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Using a juvenile rat model, we found that DOXO compromises the BBB integrity. To further address the poorly understood mechanism of DOXO-related CICI, we utilized human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) to study the changes induced by DOXO in BBB integrity. RNA sequencing after DOXO exposure demonstrated changes in inflammatory pathways that may play a critical role in BBB integrity. Upon DOXO treatment, there was an increase in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin-6 (IL-6), regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) and granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF). DOXO induced the activation of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK1/2), and cAMP response element binding protein (CREB). Using xCELLigence Real Time Cell Analysis, we found that DOXO doesn’t immediately compromise the barrier in hCMEC/D3. Additionally, we found that DOXO treatment significantly decreases maximal respiration and the spare respiratory capacity in hCMEC/D3 cells, indicating mitochondrial bioenergetic defects. Our findings provide critical insights on how DOXO impacts the BBB and builds a foundation for developing preventative measures that may improve the quality of life for patients.

Introduction

Advancements in treatment have significantly improved overall survival rates in cancer patients, especially pediatric cancer patients, with a survival rate of approximately 80 % in the United States (McEachron and Helman, 2021). However, chemotherapy treatments are associated with side effects such as neurotoxicity, formally known as chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment (CICI) (Argyriou et al., 2011). CICI includes deficits in executive functioning, working memory, information processing speed, calculations, multitasking, fine motor skills, and concentration (Argyriou et al., 2011). These measurable deficits continue years after chemotherapy treatment, significantly impacting the quality of life for patients (Ferguson and Ahles, 2003; Kunin-Batson et al., 2014; Mavrea et al., 2021). For example, among pediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the most common childhood cancer, approximately 40–60 % of pediatric ALL survivors have cognitive deficits that hinder their daily activities, like school performance, due to impaired executive functioning, working memory, attention and processing speed (van der Plas et al., 2015, 2021; Egset et al., 2024). Long-term survivors (5 years post-diagnosis) of childhood ALL who were treated with only chemotherapy (i.e. without cranial radiation) displayed cognitive dysfunction at rates exceeding age-matched controls (Krull et al., 2016). ALL is not the only pediatric cancer that is associated with CICI; survivors of childhood osteosarcoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and neuroblastoma also show cognitive deficits (Krull et al., 2012; Kadan-Lottick et al., 2010; Edelmann et al., 2016; Sleurs et al., 2019). CICI impacts not only the quality of life for children surviving cancer but also that of the parents and families of the children and prompts the need for psychosocial aftercare (Van Dongen-Melman, 2000). Moreover, this cognitive impairment has also been observed in adult cancer patients. A longitudinal study of early-stage breast cancer patients treated with doxorubicin (DOXO) and cyclophosphamide showed motor function, memory, and visuospatial impairments (Jansen et al., 2008). Therefore, uncovering the neurotoxic mechanisms of CICI is essential for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Despite extensive research on the multifactorial causes of CICI, its pathophysiology remains unclear. The diversity of treatment regimens, including the wide range of chemotherapeutic agents utilized, complicates this understanding, highlighting the need to study these therapies individually. Potential mechanisms involved in CICI include, mitochondrial dysfunction, blood-brain barrier (BBB) damage, cytokine dysregulation, inflammation, impaired neurogenesis and myelination loss (Murillo et al., 2023). The first line of defense for the various drugs and harmful toxins to penetrate/diffuse into the brain is the BBB (Daneman and Prat, 2015), which is a selective barrier that plays an important role in maintaining the homeostasis and it consists of endothelial cells amongst other cell types like glial cells and pericytes. Proinflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress are commonly increased upon chemotherapy exposure and have been reported to induce structural damage (tight junction protein changes) and affect BBB permeability (Wardill et al., 2016). Inflammatory cytokines also have individually been known to partly play a role in the BBB disruption and lead to changes in the signaling molecules in the brain (Wang et al., 2014; Geng et al., 2018).

Anthracyclines (like DOXO) are a vital part of treatment protocols for approximately 60 % of childhood cancer malignancies, including ALL, acute myelogenous leukemia, Hodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, osteosarcoma, Wilms tumor, and soft tissue sarcomas (Armenian and Bhatia, 2018). DOXO is administered to patients intravenously and does not cross the BBB. Its impact on neuronal function in the central nervous system (CNS) must, therefore, be indirect. DOXO is known to induce pro-inflammatory mediators in the periphery which can alter the function of the BBB and elicit a neurotoxic effect (Bigotte et al., 1982). DOXO increases pro-inflammatory cytokines that have been associated with BBB damage, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Wardill et al., 2016; Cardoso et al., 2020; Tangpong et al., 2006; Sauter et al., 2011). In breast cancer patients treated with chemotherapy had higher levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, which correlated with structural changes in the hippocampus (Wardill et al., 2016). Insults to the BBB as such can cause mitochondrial dysfunction in brain endothelial cells, induce neuroinflammation within the brain, and possibly allow the entry of neurotoxic factors into the brain that normally cannot penetrate and further compromise the BBB integrity (Doll et al., 2015) and may cause neuronal damage (Wang et al., 2023). What remains unknown, however, is how DOXO treatment impacts the BBB endothelial cells and if its activation becomes a source of inflammatory signaling in close proximity to the brain.

There is a clear unmet need for investigating these mechanisms to understand endothelial toxicity of DOXO, which may help guide interventions to mitigate the debilitating neurotoxic effect. To further investigate the mechanisms by which DOXO alters the function of the BBB and contributes to CICI, we utilized human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3). We evaluated the impact of DOXO on BBB permeability, expression of genes related to BBB integrity, and mitochondrial functioning. In addition, we explored the protective impact of memantine, an FDA approved drug for dementia (Lo and Grossberg, 2011) because recent evidence suggests a rescue in BBB disruption in other pathologies (Chen et al., 2021; Gu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2017). We and others have previously demonstrated that memantine protects against intrathecal methotrexate-induced (Cole et al., 2013), DOXO-induced (Elbeltagy et al., 2021), and paclitaxel-induced (Sung et al., 2021) cognitive deficits. Memantine may rescue chemotherapy-induced BBB damage, yet the protective effects of memantine on brain endothelial cells in the context of DOXO-induced chemobrain has not been studied.

Methods

Rat model of BBB permeability

Four-week-old male and female Long Evans rats were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, Massachusetts) and allowed to acclimate to the vivarium environment for one week. At five weeks of age, the rats were injected intraperitoneally with sterile doxorubicin (DOXO), 20 mg/kg (Doxorubicin HCl 2 mg/mL, Fresenius Kabi, LLC. – 63323010161; Henry Schein, Catalog No. 1164027) or equal volume of saline (control group) (1X PBS (Phosphate Buffered Saline pH 7.4, Gibco, Catalog No. 10010049). Three days post-injection and one hour prior to the dissections, the rats were injected intraperitoneally with 360 μL of sodium fluorescein (Sigma, Catalog No. F6377–100G) at 100 mg/mL dissolved in sterile UltraPure DNase/RNase-Free Distilled Water (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 10977015). There was one rat from this cohort that was not injected with DOXO or saline and was not injected with sodium fluorescein, which was utilized for the standard curve calculations. Blood was collected from the left ventricle of the heart and allowed to coagulate for 20 minutes at room temperature and then immediately put on ice and centrifuged at 4600 rpm (revolutions per minute) (or 2000g) for 10 min at 4°C and transferred supernatant to new tube and stored in −80°C. Following the cardiac blood collection, all rats were perfused with PBS, subsequently the brain was removed, and a sagittal cut was done, where the left side of the brain and cerebellum were snap-frozen separately and stored in −80°C until further processing. All experiments were approved by the Animal Institute and Use Committee of Rutgers University and were conducted following the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” The ARRIVE guidelines were followed for all experiments described here.

Sample processing to analyze the BBB permeability by the ratio of the dye uptake in the brain to the amount present in the serum following an established method from the University of Queensland (Chen et al., 2021). Briefly, using 1:3 of acetonitrile (Sigma, Catalog No. 271004–1 L), the serum samples were deproteinized, followed by centrifugation at 11,600 rpm (or 13,000 g) for 15 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and put into the SpeedVac Concentrator (Thermo Fisher, Model SPD1010) at room temperature until fully evaporated. The samples were reconstituted in equal volume of TRIS buffer (Thermo Scientific Chemicals TRIS, 1.0 M buffer solution, pH 7.4, Catalog No. AAJ60202K2). The left side of the brain tissue without the cerebellum was homogenized in milli Q water at a 1:2 ratio to the tissue weight. Using 1:6 of acetonitrile, the brain samples were deproteinized and subsequently evaporated in a similar to the serum samples. In order to account for background fluorescence a standard curve matrix was created and processed in a similar manner as detailed above. Known quantities of sodium fluorescein were added to serum and brain samples collected from a control rat (not exposed to DOXO or sodium fluorescein). 10 μL of reconstituted serum or brain samples and standards were diluted with 200 μL of TRIS buffer in 96-well black plate. The fluorescence levels were measured with a microplate reader with excitation at 460 nm and emission at 515 nm. The standard curve was calculated using a simple linear regression and values that were below the blank were eliminated for the calculations. Fluorescence uptake into the brain was calculated by taking the ratio of fluorescent marker level in the brain to the fluorescent marker in the serum. Any negative readings were adjusted to 0. The sample ratios of fluorescence uptake were normalized to the saline-treated control to calculate the fold change.

Cell culture

Human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) were purchased from Millipore Sigma (Catalog No. SCC066, RRID: CVCL_U985). The hCMEC/D3 cells were cultured in the endothelial basal media (EBM-2, Lonza Walkersville, Catalog No. CC-3156) supplemented with 5 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Corning Fetal Bovine Serum, Regular, USDA Approved, Catalog No. 35–011-CV), 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Penicillin-Streptomycin 10,000 U/mL Gibco, Catalog No. 15140122), 1.4 μM hydrocortisone (Hydrocortisone, Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. H0135–1MG), 5 μg/mL ascorbic acid (L-Ascorbic acid, Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. A4544–25G), 1 % chemically defined lipid concentrate (Chemically Defined Lipid Concentrate, Gibco, Catalog No. 11905031), 10 mM HEPES (HEPES 1 M Gibco, Catalog No. 15630080), and 1 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (Fibroblast Growth factor basic, human, Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. GF003) on CytoOne TC Flasks (USA Scientific, Catalog No. CC7682–4875) treated with Cultrex rat collagen I (R&D Bio-techne, Catalog No. 3440–100–01) (150 μg/mL concentration of collagen diluted in distilled water used to coat all plates and flasks) in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5 % CO2. The cells were passaged every 3–4 days and used for no more than 10 passages, as suggested by Millipore Sigma, to ensure there was no significant impact on the cell marker expression and functionality.

MTS assay

Approximately 8,000–10,000 cells were seeded per well in a 96-well plate and allowed to grow overnight. The following day, the cells were treated with a range of doses of doxorubicin (DOXO) (Doxorubicin HCl 2 mg/mL, Fresenius Kabi, LLC. – 63323010161; Henry Schein, Catalog No. 1164027), diluted in PBS, for 24 hours. Upon completion of the treatment, the media was replaced with complete media, and the Cell-Titer One solution reagent was added to each well following the Cell-Titer 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation assay protocol as suggested by the manufacturer (Promega, Catalog No. G3580).

Memantine pretreatment experiments

For the memantine pretreatment experiments, the cell culture was conducted as explained above except for a 6-hour pretreatment of 20 μM Memantine hydrochloride (Sigma, Catalog No. M9292–100MG) diluted in sterile UltraPure DNase/RNase-Free Distilled Water (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 10977015) and filtered. For the control samples, an equal volume of distilled water was used, followed by the DOXO treatment.

RNA-sequencing

The hCMEC/D3 were treated 500 nM of doxorubicin (DOXO) or PBS, for 24 hours. RNA was isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini protocol was followed by the manufacturer (Qiagen, Catalog 74104) and shipped to Admera Health. RNA-sequencing was performed by Admera Health (South Plainfield, New Jersey).

Isolated RNA sample quality was assessed by TapeStation 4200 RNA Assay (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA), and quantified by Infinite F Nano+ 200 Pro Tecan® RNA assay (Tecan, Switzerland). Paramagnetic beads coupled with oligo d(T)25 are combined with total RNA to isolate poly(A)+ transcripts based on NEBNext® Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module manual (New England BioLabs Inc., Massachusetts, USA). Prior to first strand synthesis, samples are randomly primed (5 ´d(N6) 3 ´[N = A,C,G,T]) and were fragmented for 10 minutes at 92°C. All remaining steps for library construction were used according to the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England BioLabs Inc., Massachusetts, USA). Final libraries quantity was assessed by Qubit 2.0 (ThermoFisher, Massachusetts, USA) and quality was assessed by TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies Inc., California, USA). Final library size was about 430 bp with an insert size of about 300 bp. Illumina® 8-nt dual-indices were used. Equimolar pooling of libraries was performed based on QC values and sequenced on an Illumina® NovaSeq X Plus 10B (Illumina, California, USA) with a read length configuration of 150 PE for 40 M PE reads per sample (20 M in each direction). Raw RNA-Seq reads were initially assessed for quality using FastQC (version v0.12.1), which provides a comprehensive analysis of the sequence data, including base quality scores, GC content, and adapter contamination. To improve the quality of the reads, Trimmomatic (version v0.39) was employed to remove adapter sequences and trim low-quality bases from both ends of the reads, using the default settings. Reads were aligned to the reference genome using the STAR Aligner (version 2.7.10b), a highly efficient RNA-Seq aligner that performs well with large genomes. The alignment was performed with default parameters to map the reads to the genome, producing a BAM file of aligned reads. To assess the quality of the aligned RNA-Seq data, CollectRnaSeqMetrics (part of Picard Tools, version 3.0.0) was used. This tool generates various RNA-Seq quality metrics, including the proportion of aligned reads, distribution of reads across different genomic features (such as exons and introns), and the overall integrity of the sequencing data, helping to ensure that data quality is suitable for downstream analysis. Aligned RNA-Seq reads were used to calculate transcript abundances using StringTie (version 2.2.1), a software tool for transcript quantification. Specifically, StringTie was employed to calculate Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) and Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values. These metrics provide estimates of transcript expression levels, which are essential for differential expression analysis and the interpretation of gene expression patterns across different conditions. Gene-level read counts were obtained using either HTSeq (version 2.0.3), which are tools for counting mapped reads aligned to genomic features such as genes, exons, promoters, and gene bodies. These counts are used for differential gene expression analysis. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.40.2), a widely used package for RNA-Seq data analysis. DESeq2 normalizes read counts and identifies differentially expressed genes based on statistical models that account for variations between samples.

To conduct functional analysis, the normalized counts provided by Admera were uploaded on Xia Lab ExpressAnalyst (Liu et al., 2023), followed by DESeq2, to determine the upregulated and downregulated pathways from Gene Ontology: Biological Processes (GO:BP) pathway analysis sorted by a −log10 (adjusted p-value) threshold of 1.3 and with a threshold of |log2(Fold Change)|= 0.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted on ExpressAnalyst and the GSEA barcode plots were generated by ExpressAnalyst. Analyzed RNA-sequencing data was uploaded to Qiagen’s Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) platform and the threshold selection for the dataset was |log2(Fold Change)|= 0.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. IPA was utilized to identify canonical pathways and toxicology lists impacted after 24 hours of DOXO.

Quantitation of gene expression

Cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and allowed to grow overnight and reach confluency. The following day, the cells were treated with 500 nM DOXO (unless otherwise noted) or PBS for 24 hours. Cells were washed with cold-PBS, and Buffer RLT was added to the wells, a sterile scraper was used to collect the cells, and the Qiagen RNeasy Mini protocol was followed as per guidelines provided by manufacturer (Qiagen, Catalog 74104). Alternatively, Trizol (Invitrogen™15596018) was used to isolate RNA by following the manufacturer’s protocol. For some experiments with lower doses of DOXO (125 nM or 250 nM), after the 24-hour treatment was completed, the treated media was removed and replaced with untreated complete media and 12 hours post the media change, the cells were washed with cold-PBS buffer and RLT was added to the wells.

cDNA synthesis was performed following the 20 μL reaction first-strand cDNA synthesis protocol from ThermoFisher using 500ng-1μg RNA, Invitrogen SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 18080044), Invitrogen RNaseOUT Recombinant Ribonuclease Inhibitor (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 10777019), Thermo Scientific dNTP Mix (10 mM each, Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. R0191), Thermo Scientific Random Hexamer Primer (Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. SO142), and Thermo Scientific RNase H (5 U/μL) (Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. EN0201).

Next, the cDNA was diluted 1:10 in UltraPure DNase/RNase-Free Distilled Water (Invitrogen, Catalog No. 10977015) (~50–100 ng cDNA). Primers were designed using National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), based on previous references or obtained from OriGene Technologies. All primers used in this study are outlined in Table 1. A 10 μM forward and reverse primer mixture (Integrated DNA Technologies, Morrisville, NC) was made. Using the Applied Biosystems protocol in the standard cycling mode and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No. A25742), qPCR was performed in a 384-well plate. The qPCR was performed with these conditions: Standard Cycling: UDG Activation 50°C for 2 2 minutes, Dual Lock DNA Pol 95°C for 2 minutes, Denature 95°C 15 seconds and Anneal/Extend 60°C 1 minutes for 40 cycles, Dissociation Steps: (McEachron and Helman, 2021) 95°C for 15 seconds (1.6°C/second) (Argyriou et al., 2011) 60°C for 1 minute (1.6°C/sec) (Ferguson and Ahles, 2003) 95°C for 15 seconds (0.15°C/second). Data analyzed using the standard ΔΔCt and was normalized to the housekeeping genes, GAPDH or B2M; the housekeeping gene utilized is specified in the legend of the figure.

Western blot

Cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and grown overnight to confluency. The following day, the cells were treated with 500 nM DOXO or PBS for 24 hours. At the completion of the timepoint, media was removed, cells were washed with ice-cold 1X PBS, and freshly prepared 1X RIPA buffer (RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer, Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. 89901) with protease inhibitors (Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA-Free (100X), Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. 78425) and phosphatase inhibitors (Pierce Phosphatase Inhibitor Mini Tablets, Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. A32957) was added to each well to harvest the cells. The lysates were collected using a rubber policeman scraper and transferred to a tube to sonicate the samples using the sonicator’s probe or the Bioruptor and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C and supernatant was collected in a fresh tube and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits (Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. 23225) and samples were prepared using 8–10 μg of protein lysates, RIPA buffer, 4X Bolt LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. B0007), 10X Bolt Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. B0009) and heated at 95°C for 5–7 minutes. Proteins were separated by Bolt™ Bis-Tris Plus Mini Protein Gels, 4–12 %, 1.0 mm, 15 well (Invitrogen Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. NW04125BOX) with pre-stained protein ladder (Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standards Bio-Rad, Catalog No. 1610374). 1X of Running Buffer was prepared using the Bolt MES Running Buffer, 20X (Invitrogen Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. B0002). Proteins were transferred onto BrightStar™ PVDF Membrane, Pore Size 0.45 μm (Asi Alkali Scientific, Catalog No. XR732) in the BioRad Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System using the standard predefined protocol. Ponceau S. staining confirmed the transfer of proteins onto the membrane. Blots were blocked either in 5 % Milk (Blotting-Grade Blocker, Biorad, Catalog No. 1706404) or 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS-T with 0.1 % Tween or Tris buffered saline with 0.1 % Tween (TBS-T) for 60–120 minutes at room temperature on a shaker/rocker. Followed by primary antibody incubation in 5 % Milk or 5 % BSA at 1:500–1:1000 dilution at 4°C overnight. The following primary antibodies were used: p38 MAPK (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 9212S, RRID:AB_330713), Phospho-p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 9211S, RRID: AB_331641), Phospho-CREB (Ser133) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 9198S, RRID:AB_2561044), CREB (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 9197S, RRID:AB_331277), Phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 9101S, RRID:AB_331646), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 4695S, RRID: AB_390779), Vinculin (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Catalog No. 13901S, RRID:AB_2728768), ZO-1 (1:500–1:1000, Invitrogen, Catalog No. 40–2200, RRID: AB_2533456), Occludin (1:300–1:500, Invitrogen, Catalog No. 71–1500, RRID: AB_2533977). Blots were washed 3 times in PBS-T or TBS-T for 15 minutes each, followed by secondary antibody incubation in 5 % Milk at 1:5000 dilution (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) Secondary Antibody, HRP, Invitrogen, Catalog No. 31460, RRID: AB_228341) or (Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Secondary Antibody, HRP, Invitrogen, Catalog No. 31430, RRID: AB_228307). Blots were washed 3 times in PBS-T or TBS-T for 15 minutes each, and blots were developed in the ChemiDoc Biorad Imaging System or Azure Biosystems Imager or the Invitrogen iBright Imaging system using the SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Scientific, Catalog No. 34096). All images were analyzed using ImageJ. The loading control used for the Western Blots in this manuscript is vinculin.

xCELLigence real time cell analysis (RTCA)

The xCELLigence Real Time Cell Analysis (RTCA) assay was utilized as a label-free automatic alternative to the transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay and other solute permeability assays. There are many advantages in utilizing the RTCA, including, continuous cell monitoring, noninvasive, and higher reproducibility. Following the standard guidelines, the xCELLigence calibration was completed, followed by a media background and subsequently 10,000–20,000 cells were plated on the xCELLigence E-plate (RTCA E-plate Agilent, Catalog No. 300600910). After 3–4 days a plateau in the cell index curve was observed, which represents a complete barrier formation. The cells were treated with 500 nM DOXO (unless otherwise stated) or PBS (control) for 24 hours. At the completion of the time point, the media was removed, a warm PBS wash was done, and complete media was added to all the wells and put back into the xCELLigence incubator and monitored for a few days to see the effects of DOXO treatment post media removal. Subsequently, the analysis was performed by exporting the experimental information on an Excel sheet and calculating the barrier function as suggested by Agilent. First, the normalized cell index was calculated by selecting a timepoint to normalize all the wells, which for our studies was immediately before the completion of 24-hour DOXO treatment. Then, we calculated the barrier function (% of control) by normalizing it to the respective control for each sample well for that specific timepoint of interest. Experiment was repeated 4 independent times.

Mitochondrial respiration

Cells were seeded (40,000 cells/well) in XFe24 cell culture micro-plates and grown overnight to confluency, and four wells with no cells were accounted for the background. Cells were treated with 500 nM of DOXO or PBS for 12 hours. Depending on the experiment, some plates were pretreated with 20 μM Memantine hydrochloride or distilled water for 6 hours, followed by 500 nM of DOXO or PBS for 12 hours. The cartridge was hydrated with the calibration buffer and incubated in a non-CO2 incubator at 37°C for 24 hours before the start of the Seahorse XFe24 Mito Stress Test (Seahorse XFe24 FluxPak Mini Agilent, Catalog No. 102342–100). At the day of the experiment, the media containing the drugs was removed and replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium, pH 7.4 (Agilent, Catalog No. 103575–100) supplemented with 2 g/L glucose (Agilent, Catalog No. 103577–100), 2 mM glutamine (Agilent, Catalog No. 103578–100), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Agilent, Catalog No. 103579–100). The cells were then placed in the non-CO2 incubator at 37°C for at least 30 minutes-1 hour. In the meantime, the cartridge was loaded with the indicated compounds as described below and calibrated in the XFe24 analyzer. Once calibration was completed, the plate with cells was loaded into the machine. The assay consisted of oxygen consumption rate (pmol/min) measurements during time starting with the basal conditions and followed by sequential injections of 1 μM Oligomycin (Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. 75351), 0.5 μM Carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP; Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. C2920), 0.5 μM Rotenone (Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. R8875), 0.5 μM Antimycin A (Sigma-Aldrich Millipore Sigma, Catalog No. A8674). Three measurements were performed after each compound injection. Immediately upon completion, the media was removed, the wells were washed with PBS, and the cells were lysed in RIPA buffer. A BCA protein assay was performed to determine the total protein concentration per well and normalize each well by total protein concentration. Data were analyzed using Wave software (Agilent). Three independent experiments were conducted, each set was normalized to the PBS control, and the fold changes for each independent experiment were combined. For the memantine pretreatment, two independent experiments were combined.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

The tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) dye allows the detection and measurement of mitochondrial transmembrane potential in endothelial cells and many other cell types (Sivandzade et al., 2019). JC-1 dye exhibits green fluorescence naturally. The JC-1 dye will accumulate in the mitochondria and form J-aggregates, and the J-aggregates will be red. The JC-1 dye will accumulate in the mitochondria and form the red fluorescent aggregates for healthy cells. However, in unhealthy (apoptotic) cells, the JC-1 dye will still travel to the mitochondria but not as much, which will cause an increase in the membrane permeability and loss of electrochemical potential. Therefore, the J-aggregates will not be at a high enough concentration to be red, and they will remain green instead. 12,000 cells per well were seeded in a 96-well plate and grown overnight to confluency. Cells were treated with 500 nM of DOXO or PBS for 24 hours. At the completion of the timepoint, JC-1 solution was diluted 1:10 in the media, and 10 μL was added to each well (Cayman Chemical, Catalog No. 10009172). The plate with cells was incubated at 37°C for 15–30 min, followed by centrifugation at 400 g for 5 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and assay buffer was added to each well, followed by centrifugation at 400 g for 5 minutes at room temperature, and this step was repeated for a total of 2 times. Lastly, assay buffer was added to each well, and the plate was read using a BioTek microplate reader for excitation/emission wavelengths of J-aggregates: 535/595 nm and J-monomers: 485/535 nm. For the analysis, wells were subtracted by the background, and the ratio of J-aggregates to J-monomers was calculated. Each set was normalized to the PBS control to combine the 5 independent experiments.

DCFDA

DCFDA assay (enQuire Bioreagents, Catalog No. AB113851300TEST) was conducted using the microplate procedure following the manufacturer’s protocol with slight modifications. 12,000 cells were seeded on a 96-well plate and once confluency was reached, the cells were treated with 500 nM DOXO or PBS for 24 hours. 30–45 minutes prior to the completion of the assay, then 25 μM DCFDA reagent was added to the wells. At the completion of the timepoint, the fluorescence levels were measured with a microplate reader with excitation of 485 nm and emission of 535 nm. Wells with just regular media without DCFDA were used for background. Each independent experiment was normalized to the controls for that experiment, then the fold changes for all 4 independent experiments were combined.

ATP luminescent assay

To measure the ATP levels in the cell, we utilized the Luminescent ATP Detection Assay Kit (abcam, Catalog No. ab113849) following the recommended protocol by the manufacturer. The assay was conducted after 24-hour 500 nM DOXO or PBS treatment. The independent experiments normalized to the controls for that experiment and subsequently the fold changes for the 2 independent experiments were combined.

8-isoprostane

To identify a method that demonstrates if DOXO treatment induces oxidative stress in hCMEC/D3, the 8-isoprostane ELISA kit was utilized (Cayman Chemical, Catalog No. 516351) on freshly collected cell culture supernatant samples following the 24-hour 500 nM DOXO or PBS treatment. The standard protocol for this kit was followed based on the manufacturer’s guidelines. Cell culture media (uncultured) was used for the dilution of the standard curve. Samples and standards were added to the plate with the 8-isoprostane tracer and antiserum for an 18-hour incubation at 4°C. The plate was washed, and Ellman’s Reagent was added for the development of the plate for 2 hours. The absorbance reading of the plate was done at a wavelength between 405 and 420 nm. The analysis was conducted as per the recommendations on the assay’s protocol. Each independent experiment was normalized to the PBS treated samples, and the fold change for each of the 3 experiments was combined.

Statistical analysis

To ensure reproducibility, all the in vitro experiments described are performed with at least 3 biological replicates and also independently repeated. All statistical analysis was performed to compare the different groups through GraphPad Prism 10.4 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). First, all the data was analyzed by the Shapiro Wilk test for normality. For normally distributed data, we used parametric tests for our analysis, like unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For non-normally distributed data, we used non-parametric tests like the Mann Whitney U-test or Kruskal-Wallis Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for our analysis. Results are considered statistically significant when the p-value is less than or equal to 0.05.

Results

The impact of doxorubicin on BBB integrity

Disrupted BBB integrity is known to contribute to cognitive dysfunction observed in neurological and neurodegenerative conditions (Puig-Pijoan et al., 2024; Che et al., 2024). To investigate whether DOXO impairs BBB integrity, we assessed BBB permeability using a clinically relevant dose in 5-week-old Long Evans rats, modeling a pediatric regimen (Nair and Jacob, 2016; Moghrabi et al., 2007). Using sodium fluorescein as an indicator of BBB breakdown, we found that DOXO significantly increased BBB permeability to sodium fluorescein when compared to the PBS-treated control group (Fig. 1A). Moreover, there was a significant increase in monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1) serum levels, an indicator of inflammation and compromised BBB integrity (Yao and Tsirka, 2014), in the DOXO-treated group when compared to the control group (Fig. 1B). Normally, TNF-α serum levels are low in untreated rats, however, 3 days post-DOXO treatment, TNF-α serum levels were also significantly increased (Fig. 1C). There was a considerable increase in IL-1β serum levels (Fig. 1D). Surprisingly, there was no change in the IL-6 serum levels, however, there was one DOXO-treated rat that had considerably high IL-6 serum levels (Fig. 1E). Although DOXO does not penetrate the BBB, our sodium fluorescein results suggest that the BBB is compromised after DOXO treatment. Additionally, our findings show that a clinically relevant treatment of DOXO causes the release of pro-inflammatory mediators that have previously been known to play a critical response in a BBB disruption (Chiang et al., 2019). However, characterizing the cellular and molecular level changes induced by DOXO on the brain endothelial cells is challenging in vivo, making it important to investigate alternative models.

To explore the molecular changes induced by DOXO treatment in brain endothelial cells, we utilized human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3), a well-studied model cell line for BBB studies (Weksler et al., 2013; Cucullo et al., 2008; Qi et al., 2023). This cell line provides translational value and clinically relevant insights to the brain endothelial response after chemotherapeutic agents like DOXO. The DOXO treatment dose we determined for this study was 500 nM DOXO, which does not induce more than 25 % of cell death and achieves levels similar to plasma levels in children with ALL or non-Hodgkin lymphoma (Hempel et al., 2002; Frost et al., 2002). To investigate the changes in the BBB integrity induced by DOXO, we directly measured the impact of DOXO by monitoring the barrier function. Although barrier integrity was not compromised immediately at the completion of 24 hours of treatment (Fig. 2A), we observed a time-dependent decrease in barrier function beginning at 3 hours post the DOXO free media change (Fig. 2B). Additionally, we noted a decrease in mRNA expression of occludin, a key tight junction protein, with 24-hour treatment of 500 nM DOXO, however, memantine did not rescue this decrease (Fig. 2C). No change was observed in the mRNA level expression of tight junction protein zona occludens (ZO-1) (Fig. 2D). DOXO treatment slightly decreased the occludin protein level expression, but there was no protective or preventative effect of memantine (Fig. 2E) and there was no change for protein levels of ZO-1 (Fig. 2F). These barrier function results suggest that DOXO treatment compromises the barrier potentially in response to the proinflammatory changes and/or the activation of key signaling pathways.

To further understand the impact of DOXO on the BBB integrity and the role of cell death, we tested 24-hour exposure to two lower concentrations of DOXO: 250 nM and 125 nM (Supplemental Figure 1A and Supplemental Figure 1B). Twelve hours post the 24-hour treatment of lower-concentration DOXO exposure, there was a significant decrease in occludin and ZO-1 mRNA levels (Supplemental Figure 1 C and Supplemental Figure 1D, respectively) and a significant increase in the mRNA levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and IL-1β (Supplemental Figure 1E and Supplemental Figure 1F, respectively). Both cytokines are known to partly play a role in BBB disruption in different pathological conditions (Blamire et al., 2000; de Vries et al., 1996). We next sought to investigate whether key adhesion molecules are affected by DOXO treatment, focusing on intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), which has also been linked to increased BBB permeability (Muller, 2019). We observed an increase in ICAM-1 mRNA expression (Supplemental Figure 1G). Our results clearly suggest that DOXO treatment does not immediately impact the BBB. Rather DOXO induces a cascade of cellular events to the endothelial cell environment, which appear to trigger mechanisms that lead to barrier dysfunction post the removal of DOXO.

Doxorubicin dysregulates many critical pathways linked with BBB

RNA-sequencing was performed to gain insights regarding the functional pathways either upregulated or downregulated by DOXO treatment in the hCMEC/D3. We found 115 significantly upregulated and 115 significantly downregulated genes after DOXO treatment (Fig. 3A). Gene ontology analysis indicated that DOXO treatment on the BBB causes an upregulation of cytokine mediated signaling pathways and immune responses, both of which play a critical role in inflammation (Fig. 3B). DOXO treatment downregulated the CNS development pathway, providing evidence that the impact of DOXO on the BBB leads to abnormal functioning and may play a role for cognitive decline (Fig. 3C). Additionally, gene ontology analysis indicated a decrease in vasculature development and endothelial cell migration pathways (Fig. 3C). These results clearly imply that the BBB integrity is compromised after DOXO treatment.

Moreover, we highlighted key IPA toxicology lists that were significantly impacted in our RNA-sequencing dataset, including a decrease transmembrane potential of mitochondria and mitochondrial membrane, mechanism of gene regulation by peroxisome proliferators via PPARα, p53 signaling, and Cell Cycle: G2/M DNA Damage Checkpoint Regulation (Fig. 3D). A decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential and an increase in peroxisome may indicate mitochondrial depolarization and damage in DOXO-treated cerebral microvascular endothelial cells. Using IPA, we also observed predicted activation (displayed by the positive z-scores) of several canonical pathways, including interferon alpha/beta signaling, the role of hypercytokinemia/hyperchemokinemia in the pathogenesis of influenza, p53 signaling, cell death, autophagy, cellular senescence, autism-related signaling, p38 MAPK signaling, cGAS-STING signaling, glycation signaling, and cyclophilin signaling (Fig. 3E). The upregulation of genes associated with these disease-related pathways suggests that DOXO treatment induces damage to brain endothelial cells in a similar manner. On the other hand, several different canonical pathways were found downregulated (displayed by the negative z-scores) upon DOXO treatment, including the STAT3 signaling pathway, HIF1α signaling, IL-4 and IL-13 signaling, and the S100 family signaling pathway (Fig. 3E). This data supports the hypothesis that the integrity of BBB is compromised or disrupted by observed dysregulation of some of these pathways. For example, increased cyclophilin A signaling in pericytes has been linked with an increase in BBB breakdown in Apolipoprotein E4 (ApoE4) mice (Bell et al., 2012) possibly responsible for neurodegeneration. Autism increases neuroinflammation, which may induce endothelial injury and play a role in the pathology (Gozal et al., 2021). These results demonstrate that DOXO dysregulates pathways pertinent to BBB damage and may likely play a role in the neurodegenerative state. Additionally, these results support clinical studies that show patients treated with chemotherapy who experienced cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) and anxiety display significant changes in major neurodegenerative pathways, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Huntington disease, Parkinson disease, and prion disease (Oppegaard et al., 2024). Further, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed upregulation in cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (Fig. 3F) and downregulation of numerous pathways including, cell-substrate junction (Fig. 3G) and angiogenesis (Fig. 3H). The factors that are dysregulated within these pathways discussed can play a role in mediating the observed BBB dysfunction. The increase in pro-inflammatory pathways likely suggests that there is endothelial cell activation, which can play a role in the downstream signaling cascade within the brain. Therefore, our pathway analysis results imply that DOXO plays a role in the dysregulation of BBB and alters key cytokines.

Doxorubicin alters key inflammatory factors

Systemic inflammation is known to impair BBB function (Varatharaj and Galea, 2017), and there are implications that an increase in IL-6 secretion is dependent on p38 MAPK activation (Bent et al., 2016). Our RNA-sequencing analysis provided evidence of an increase in the cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, therefore, we next investigated whether DOXO increases inflammation as that plays an important role in the BBB dysfunction. We found that DOXO induces a significant increase in the secretion of granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (Fig. 4A), a cytokine that is linked to pro-inflammatory effects (Lotfi et al., 2019). DOXO exposure also increased the secretion of regulated on activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) (Fig. 4B), a pro-inflammatory chemokine that plays a key role in the body’s inflammatory response (Mikolajczyk et al., 2016). There was a strong trend towards significant increase for the secretion of IL-6 (Fig. 4C) and MCP-1 (Fig. 4D) upon DOXO treatment, suggesting a pro-inflammatory state. Interestingly, there was a decrease in vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion after DOXO treatment, indicating a decrease in angiogenesis (Fig. 4E), further validating the results the results we saw in Fig. 3H. We found no significant differences in IL-1β and TNF-α secretion, amongst many other cytokines that also showed no significant change upon DOXO treatment (Supplemental Figure 2A). DOXO treatment significantly increased IL-6 secretion, however, memantine is only able to slightly reduce this increase (Fig. 4F). DOXO treatment significantly increased the mRNA level expression of IL-6, IL-1β, and ICAM-1 and we found a partial protective effect of memantine on mRNA levels of these key pro-inflammatory mediators/factors (Fig. 4G–I). These results suggest a pro-inflammatory state induced by DOXO treatment and the dysregulation of these cytokines and chemokines potentially create an inflammatory state within the brain and compromising the BBB integrity.

Doxorubicin induces the activation of key MAPK proteins

Transcriptomic analyses presented an upregulation in cytokine mediating signaling and p38 MAPK signaling. Therefore, we next investigated the response of DOXO on the activation of key MAPK signaling proteins to determine the central molecular mediators responsible for this inflammatory response. A MAPK phosphorylation array presented an increase in the phosphorylation of cAMP response element binding protein (CREB) (Fig. 5A), p90 ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK) (Fig. 5B), extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK1/2) (Fig. 5C), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) (Fig. 5D) following 24 hours of DOXO exposure in the hCMEC/D3. We also observed considerable phosphorylation of GSK3α, MEK1, MKK6 and MSK2 (Supplemental Figure 2B and Supplemental Figure 2C). We further confirmed by Western Blot robust activation of p38 MAPK (Fig. 5E, 5F) and ERK1/2 (Fig. 5E, 5G). Interestingly, DOXO exposure significantly increased both phosphorylated CREB (p-CREB) and total CREB protein levels when normalized to vinculin (Fig. 5E, 5H, 5I). However, when evaluating phosphorylated CREB relative to total CREB protein, there was a slight increase in the ratio of p-CREB to total CREB (Fig. 5E, 5J). These transcriptomic and phosphorylation array results suggest a central role for p38 MAPK signaling in DOXO-induced endothelial toxicity and explain a possible link to the resulting inflammatory conditions.

Doxorubicin impairs mitochondrial membrane potential and bioenergetics in brain endothelial cells

Mitochondrial defects in endothelial cells have been implicated in driving BBB disruption observed in stroke, multiple sclerosis and in other neuroinflammatory disorders (Doll et al., 2015; Sweeney et al., 2018). Our transcriptomics analyses suggested that DOXO induce mitochondrial membrane dissipation and mitochondrial dysfunction in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells. To test the effects of DOXO on mitochondrial function, we first assessed mitochondrial respiration utilizing the Seahorse Cell MitoStress Test. DOXO exposure significantly decreased the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) after FCCP addition (Fig. 6A). We observed that exposure to 500 nM of DOXO for 12 hours significantly decreased basal respiration (Fig. 6B), maximal respiration (Fig. 6C), and the spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 6D), indicating oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) reduction. Additionally, we observed a significant increase in the non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption after 12 hours of DOXO exposure (Fig. 6E). We also showed a significant decrease in the ATP production from mitochondria upon DOXO administration (Fig. 6F). Interestingly, we observed a significant decrease in proton leak after 12 hours of DOXO exposure (Fig. 6G). No change was observed in the coupling efficiency following DOXO treatment (Fig. 6H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that DOXO impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics and the ability of mitochondrial to reach their maximal respiratory capacity when forced to, suggesting that they poorly adapt under stress conditions.

Given the evidence of protective effects of memantine against chemobrain, we investigated the impact of a memantine pretreatment on mitochondrial function. We stained the cells with JC-1 to assess changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential. First, we found that 24 hours of DOXO treatment significantly decreases the mitochondrial membrane potential, further validating the transcriptomic analyses and confirming that DOXO treatment leads to mitochondrial defects in hCMEC/D3 cells. Notably, a 6-hour pretreatment of memantine significantly rescued DOXO-induced mitochondrial depolarization (Fig. 6I). Memantine pretreatment followed by 12 hours of DOXO treatment presented no significant changes in spare respiratory capacity, basal respiration, maximal respiration, ATP production, and proton leak (Supplemental Figure 3A). To more broadly evaluate the oxidative status of the hCMEC/D3 cells upon DOXO exposure, we measured reactive oxygen species (ROS) production using DCFDA. No significant differences were observed in the total ROS generation at this exposure of DOXO (Supplemental Figure 3B). Interestingly, 8-isoprostane secretion levels were significantly increased following 24 hours of DOXO treatment, supporting that DOXO induces lipid peroxidation which may result in oxidative stress in the BBB (Supplemental Figure 3C). Unlike earlier timepoints, no significant differences were found in total ATP luminescence after 24 hours of DOXO exposure (Supplemental Figure 3D). This might be explained by potential compensatory mechanisms from glycolysis to maintain ATP production when mitochondrial respiration is impaired. Taken together, our findings indicate that DOXO induces mitochondrial defects and oxidative stress, which may be the main driver for the subsequent pro-inflammatory state we observe.

Discussion

Although DOXO does not cross the BBB to reach the CNS, the deleterious impact of DOXO treatment on cognitive function has been well-described (Krull et al., 2016; Ali et al., 2020; Tong et al., 2020; Alhowail, 2023; Christie et al., 2012; Tonning Olsson et al., 2019). DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, inflammation, impaired neurogenesis and apoptosis are potential mechanisms involved in DOXO-induced neurotoxicity (Du et al., 2021). However, there are currently no drugs to prevent this debilitating side effect, resulting in a clear and urgent need to understand the cellular level changes leading to cognitive impairment. The effect of DOXO on BBB damage is less well-described as a neurobiological mechanism of CICI. Given the extensive use of DOXO in the clinic, investigating its role in CICI is of critical importance (Siebel et al., 2020). To address this gap and shed light on the mechanism behind DOXO-induced cognitive impairment, we illustrated the impact of doxorubicin on the BBB in vivo and studied the impact of DOXO on the endothelial cells that are an essential component of the BBB in vitro. We demonstrated that DOXO induces changes in mitochondrial functionality in hCMEC/D3 cells, altered gene expression clustered in pathways pertinent to mitochondrial dysregulation and BBB disruption, dysregulation of key proinflammatory cytokines and mediators, and activation of key MAPK proteins. We found that DOXO does not immediately impact barrier integrity in vitro, supporting that DOXO induces a cascade of cellular events resulting in the observed compromise of the BBB integrity post-removal of DOXO. Finally, we demonstrated that memantine can induce partial protection against these changes in vitro.

Our study is the first to directly investigate the impact of DOXO treatment on BBB endothelial cells, a model for the first line of defense for the brain against exogenous toxins like chemotherapy. Other in vitro studies investigated the effect of DOXO mostly on neuronal cells (Kaminska and Cudnoch-Jedrzejewska, 2023). These models are not fully representative of DOXO-induced neurotoxicity because DOXO does not penetrate the brain (with the exception of certain cases where there is a pharmacological manipulation of the BBB to use DOXO as a treatment for brain cancer). Inflammatory factors produced in response to DOXO can impact the BBB and cause either direct or indirect neurotoxicity. In our animal and cell model, we provide evidence supporting an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines after DOXO treatment. Our data showed a significant increase in serum levels of MCP-1 and TNF-α after a clinically relevant DOXO regimen in a juvenile rat model. Additionally, we demonstrated an increase IL-6, MCP-1, RANTES, and GM-CSF secretion in hCMEC/D3 in response to the DOXO treatment and a decrease in VEGF. This observation is consistent with other studies showing altered cytokine secretion after 24 hours of DOXO treatment in different cell lines including, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells (Bent et al., 2016). Moreover, there are implications that p38 MAPK is responsible for the inflammatory stress response (IL-6 and IL-1β gene expression levels) after DOXO treatment in macrophages (Elsea et al., 2008). Similarly, in our study here, we also observed a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines and a robust activation of p38 MAPK, which may prove to be a starting point in understanding how the BBB is impacted after DOXO treatment. The activation of p38 MAPK may also play a role in the observed CREB phosphorylation (Tan et al., 1996). Interestingly, CREB activation is involved in memory formation (Kida, 2012). The observed increase in CREB protein expression in our hCMEC/D3 model might play a neuroprotective role, however, further studies are required to validate its role.

The results presented here show interesting parallels with what has been previously published about the impact on the BBB of other classes of chemotherapy, including platinum and taxanes. Oxaliplatin compromises the BBB integrity by the disassembly of tight junctions, which is caused by the entrance of oxaliplatin in the BBB endothelial cells (Branca et al., 2018). Similarly, cisplatin treatment increases BBB permeability, proving BBB disruption may cause early neurotoxicity (Sugimoto et al., 1995). Cisplatin-induced BBB disruption in mice persists two months post-treatment, suggesting it as a plausible mechanism for CICI (Patai et al., 2025a). A recent study showed that paclitaxel induces senescence in cerebromicrovascular endothelial cells, which contributes to CICI (Ahire et al., 2023). Further, transcriptomic profiling of paclitaxel-induced senescent endothelial cells suggests that BBB dysfunction may play a critical role in neurodegeneration and cognitive decline (Patai et al., 2025b). These models and our DOXO results demonstrate precedence for exploring BBB damage as a critical mechanism to further elucidate CICI.

Vascular endothelial cell dysfunction is a plausible consequence for neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease (Yuan et al., 2023). The mitochondria of endothelial cells maintain the BBB integrity (Wang et al., 2023; Oldendorf et al., 1977), however, mitochondrial oxidative stress can disrupt the BBB integrity (Wang et al., 2023). Mitochondria play an important role in the VEGF pathway, which is considered as a key mediator of angiogenesis (Wang et al., 2023); in ischemic rats, VEGF increased angiogenesis and reduced neurological deficits (Zhang et al., 2000). Here, we reported a significant mitochondria depolarization and decrease in the spare respiratory capacity and maximal respiration after DOXO treatment, indicating mitochondrial defects and lack of adapting to stress conditions. This may contribute to our observed downregulation of the angiogenesis signaling pathway in GSEA and a significant decrease in VEGF secretion after DOXO treatment. This reduction in angiogenesis to the BBB, may lead to energy deprivation and play a critical role in compromising the BBB and thus neuronal dysfunction. Our results support the notion that mitochondrial functionality is important and may be the driver of the subsequent dysfunction we observed in our study. Therefore, manipulating mitochondrial functionality by mitochondrial transplantation therapy (Khan et al., 2023) or nasal administration of mesenchymal stem cells (Alexander et al., 2021), may be a probable therapeutic approach to mitigate the neurotoxicity of chemotherapy.

Memantine is an amantadine analog which appears to exert some neuroprotective effect in both animal models (Cole et al., 2013; Sung et al., 2021; Salih and Al-Baggou, 2020) and clinical trials (Nakamura et al.,). Additionally, in vitro models show how memantine increases the mitochondrial membrane potential (Mansoor et al., 2010; Lv et al., 2020) and propose that memantine can rescue a TNF-α induced BBB disruption and increase the expression of adhesion molecules (Wang et al., 2017). In our current study, we provided evidence of a partial rescue of DOXO-induced changes in our cell model with memantine pretreatment, however, memantine’s protective effects should be further characterized relative to the BBB in vivo. Future studies would be beneficial in increasing the memantine dose or extending the pretreatment period which may help rescue the observed BBB damage. In addition, other putative neuroprotective agents, such as metformin (Alhowail and Chigurupati, 2022; Chunchai et al., 2023), anti-inflammatory agents like steroidal glucocorticoids (Che et al., 2024) or flavonoids (Açar et al., 2023; Utpal et al., 2025) and natural or grape polyphenols (Serini and Calviello, 2024; Naomi et al., 2023), can also be tested in this model.

While our studies provide insights into plausible mechanisms of DOXO-induced BBB damage, our model has a few limitations that could be improved. First, any in vitro model using a single cell type does not fully recapitulate multicellular interactions in vivo. Future studies should include a coculture system or an organ-on-chip method to study the BBB integrity after DOXO treatment. Second, our study did not account for recapitulating systemic inflammation for patients. Future experiments may include patient or rat serum after multiple rounds of DOXO to mimic the inflammatory mediators (i.e. cytokines and chemokines) that are commonly increased in patients after direct DOXO exposure. Ongoing studies in our laboratory will include translating this work into an in vivo model to assess phenotypic changes like behavioral assays in rodent models.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings indicate mitochondrial dysfunction, a subsequent dysregulation of genes related to BBB maintenance, and activation of signaling pathways that confirm an inflammatory state in hCMEC/D3 after DOXO treatment. The observed changes in mitochondria could drive the increase in proinflammatory factors. Our findings support that DOXO-induced changes in the BBB may be responsible for the observed neurotoxic effects of DOXO and suggests further investigations to understand how this endothelial dysfunction plays a vital role in the development of CICI. Our study lays the groundwork on how DOXO impacts the BBB and may lead to the development of preventative measures that improve the quality of life for patients.
