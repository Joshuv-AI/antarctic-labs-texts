Procyanidin A2, a polyphenol autophagy-enhancer, ameliorates hyperglycemia through multifaceted insulin-related mechanisms
Authors: Bo-Rim Song, Sung Kyu Yoon, Chang Hyung Lee, Fanar Hamad Alshammari, Chi-Yeol Yoo, Kyu-Shik Jeong, Dong-Sun Lee, Sang-Han Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Procyanidin A2 (PCA2), a flavonoid from cranberries and lingonberries, has shown various bioactivities, but its antidiabetic effects remain largely under-investigated. This study assessed PCA2’s glucose-lowering potential through in silico, in vitro, and in vivo analyses. Molecular docking revealed strong binding of PCA2 to α-glucosidase (PDB: 3TOP), with competitive inhibition (IC₅₀ = 3.62 ± 0.841 µM). PCA2 was non-toxic up to 100 µM in C2C12 and INS-1 cells and enhanced glucose uptake via AMPK/AKT phosphorylation and GLUT4 expression. It also reduced oxidative stress and modulated autophagy-related proteins, increasing Beclin1, LC3-II, and Atg5 while decreasing p62. In diabetic mice, PCA2 improved glucose tolerance, protected β-cells, and restored hepatic and muscular glycogen levels, as confirmed by H&E and PAS staining. Western blotting supported modulation of insulin signaling and autophagy. These findings suggest PCA2 as a promising candidate for functional foods targeting glucose regulation and metabolic dysfunctions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25111-3.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is highly prevalent and shortens life expectancy through costly, life-threatening complications1. Insulin resistance (IR) is a key pathophysiological driver characterized by reduced glucose uptake in insulin-sensitive tissues such as adipose tissue and skeletal muscle. Skeletal muscle accounts for approximately 75% of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal, making it a critical site for glucose use; impairment of this process yields chronic hyperglycemia and accelerates disease progression2,3.

The glucose transporter (GLUT) family (GLUT1–14) shows distinct tissue distributions and functions. Among them, GLUT4 has a central role in glucose homeostasis4,5; it is expressed in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue and is recruited to the plasma membrane by insulin signaling, whereas GLUT2 in pancreatic β-cells and liver functions as a glucose sensor6. Two major inputs promote GLUT4 translocation: the insulin-dependent PI3K–AKT pathway and the energy-sensing AMPK pathway7–10. In the insulin arm, receptor activation leads to IRS1 phosphorylation and PI3K activation; PDK1 then activates AKT, which facilitates GLUT4 trafficking and inhibits GSK3β to support glycogen synthesis7,8. AMPK, activated by metabolic stress and contraction, also promotes GLUT4 translocation and cellular energy homeostasis9,10, helping counteract IR-associated hyperglycemia. Free fatty acids (FFAs) can be lipotoxic: palmitic acid (PA) elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and perturbs insulin signaling11, and in muscle cells excessive ROS lowers AKT activity and glucose uptake, thereby promoting IR12. These ROS-driven defects are tightly coupled to the cell’s quality-control machinery: by clearing dysfunctional mitochondria and oxidized macromolecules, autophagy lowers ROS and stabilizes proximal insulin signaling, whereas impaired autophagy sustains oxidative stress, promotes inhibitory IRS1 serine phosphorylation, and diminishes GLUT4 trafficking. Thus, redox stress and insulin resistance are mechanistically linked through the status of autophagy.

Autophagy is a conserved quality-control pathway that degrades damaged proteins and organelles to maintain cellular homeostasis13. At the signaling level, autophagy is promoted by AMPK-dependent ULK1 activation and suppression of mTOR, whereas AKT–mTOR activity restrains autophagosome formation; accordingly, the AMPK–AKT–mTOR axis couples energy status to insulin responsiveness. Functional autophagy flux is reflected by LC3-II formation together with p62 turnover, and efficient flux dampens ROS to preserve IRS1/AKT signaling and GLUT4 mobilization. Autophagy intersects with insulin action and redox control across metabolic tissues: in skeletal muscle, mitophagy lowers ROS and helps prevent inhibitory IRS1 serine phosphorylation; in liver, lipophagy supports hepatic insulin sensitivity; and in adipose tissue, autophagy restrains inflammation. These effects also align with antioxidant defences such as the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, which mitigates oxidative and inflammatory stress and improves insulin sensitivity14,15. Thus, enhancing autophagy and antioxidant responses provides a mechanistic route to lessen IR and limit ROS-driven damage.

Procyanidin A2 (PCA2) is a dimeric flavanol enriched in cranberries and lingonberries and present in Aesculus hippocastanum and Cinnamomum species16,17. PCA2 exhibits anticancer, antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities18, but its insulin-related mechanisms across tissues and its potential interaction with autophagy remain incompletely defined. Here we investigate the antidiabetic potential of PCA2 using integrated in silico, in vitro, and in vivo approaches. We hypothesize that PCA2 improves glycemic control by attenuating ROS and coordinately engaging AMPK and AKT signaling together with autophagy to interrupt the ROS–IR cycle, thereby enhancing GLUT4 translocation in muscle while supporting islet and hepatic function under hyperglycemic conditions. We focus on skeletal muscle for mechanistic validation and assess pancreatic and hepatic tissues to evaluate cross-organ effects. To mechanistically connect autophagy with insulin action, we also interrogate these nodes with pathway inhibitors (LY294002 for PI3K/AKT, dorsomorphin for AMPK) and autophagy modulators (3-MA, chloroquine) while monitoring flux markers alongside insulin-signalling readouts. This framework positions autophagy as a functional link between redox control and insulin action and guides the experimental design that follows.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

PCA2 and procyanidin B2 (PCB2) were acquired from Chengdu Biopurify Phytochemicals Ltd. (Chengdu, China). The purity of PCA2 (95%–99%) was analyzed using HPLC, with its identification confirmed by MS and NMR. α-Glucosidase (G5003, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG, N1377, Sigma-Aldrich), Acarbose (A8980, Sigma-Aldrich), colorimetric 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT, M2128, Sigma-Aldrich), and 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein-diacetate (DCFH-DA; 35845-5G, Sigma-Aldrich) were also used. RIPA buffer (EBA-1149, Elpis Biotech, Daejeon, Korea), protease inhibitor cocktail I (P8340, Sigma-Aldrich), phosphatase inhibitor cocktail II (P5726, Sigma-Aldrich), skim milk (MB-S16676, MB Cell, Seoul, Korea), and bovine serum albumin (BSA, 160069, MP Biomedicals., LLC, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France) were obtained commercially. Additionally, GLUT4, p-IRS1, AKT, PI3K, p-AMPK, and AMPK (Bioworld Technology, Inc., MN, USA); IRS1, p-AKT, PDK1, GSK3β, p-GSK3β, HO-1, LC3B, Atg5, Beclin1, p62, and α-tubulin (Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Beverly, MA, USA); p-PI3K, Nrf2 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK); β-actin, GPx-1, SOD1, and CAT (Santa Cruz Technology, Inc., Heidelberg, Germany); and anti-mouse, anti-rabbit, and anti-goat IgG HRP-linked antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Beverly, MA, USA) were all commercially available and used in the study. LY294002 (L9908, Sigma-Aldrich) and Dorsomorphin (P5499, Sigma-Aldrich) were also used in the study.

Protein, ligand preparation, and molecular Docking analysis

Molecular docking analyses were performed to investigate the binding interactions of a potential inhibitor with the alpha-1,4-glucosidase model, sourced from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3TOP), thereby providing a reliable structural reference for the study. In vitro studies used the α-glucosidase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Due to the unavailability of the S. cerevisiae α-glucosidase structure, docking was conducted on the homology model of S. cerevisiae α-glucosidase. The homology model of the protein sequence in the FASTA format was retrieved from UniProtKB (P53341) (Fig. S1). The model was developed on the SwissModel server, and CASTp was used to perform binding pocket analysis of the α-glucosidase enzyme (job ID j_66ef8b080542c; Fig. S2). The quality of the homology model was validated using ERRAT (Fig. S3) Using PROCHECK, the Ramachandran plot confirmed the homology model, which contains a total of 587 residues (Fig. S4). The Ramachandran plot indicated that the residues were distributed in the most favoured, additionally allowed, generously allowed, and disallowed regions as follows: 459 (89.3%), 52 (10.1%), 1 (0.2%), and 2 (0.4%). The structural data file was sourced from PubChem. Molecular docking analyses were conducted using two distinct software applications (Suppl. Materials and Methods). Initially, AutoDock Vina was employed through UCSF Chimera to dock the prepared PDB files of the target protein and ligand. The grid box for 3TOP was positioned at coordinates x: -30.60, y: 35.46, and z: 26.61, with a dimension of 25 Å grid. The receptor and ligand settings were enabled, along with advanced configurations for 10 binding modes, an exhaustiveness of 8, and a maximum energy difference of 3 kcal/mol. Subsequently, Molegro Virtual Docker (MolDock) was used to import the generated PDB files of the target protein (receptor) and ligand. All potential cavities in the protein were identified, and the largest cavity, based on surface area and volume, was designated as the active site. Molecular docking simulations were conducted using the crystal structures of the target proteins, specifically those associated with GLUT1 (PDB: 4PYP) and GLUT4 (PDB: 7WSN). The binding sites for each structure were established as follows: for 4PYP, the binding site was defined at (X: 586.499, Y: -29.223, Z: 208.179), while for 7WSN, it was centred at (X: 97.495, Y: 103.717, Z: 103.297). In both cases, the search box was defined with a grid size of 20 Å in all three dimensions, allowing for a thorough investigation of potential ligand binding conformations within the active sites. The docking calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina and MolDock ensured the accuracy of binding energy estimations and the optimal orientation of ligands within the binding pockets of each protein. Following docking, ligands were prioritized based on their minimal binding energy (kcal/mol). The docked complexes were visualized using PyMOL, and their interaction profiles were analysed with Discovery Studio and LigPlot+.

Cell culture

The American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) provided the immortalized mouse myoblast cell line, C2C12. Cells were cultured in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing high glucose (SH30243.01, Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, P4333, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The cells were sub-cultured when they reached 80% confluency. Once the cell growth exceeded 80%, the medium was changed for differentiation. The differentiation medium was refreshed with 2% horse serum (HS, 10650122, Thermo Fisher, Penrose, Auckland, New Zealand) and 1% P/S in DMEM high glucose, and incubated in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C for 5 days. Afterward, the medium was replaced with serum-free medium for experiments involving the differentiated C2C12 myotubes. The INS-1 rat pancreatic β-cell line was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and passaged every 2–3 days upon reaching 80–90% confluence.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the colorimetric MTT assay to measure the cytotoxicity of PCA2. Cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) were plated into a 96-well plate. After a 24-h incubation, the cells were treated with various concentrations of PCA2 (1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 75, and 100 µM) and incubated for an additional 24 h. The MTT solution was diluted to a 10% concentration in medium. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of the 10% MTT solution in the 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the MTT solution was discarded, and the intracellular insoluble formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 100 µL of 100% DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer (Multiskan GO 1510, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, OH, USA) in the Bio-Health Materials Core Facility, Jeju National University. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Measurement of intracellular ROS

The intracellular ROS scavenging activity of PCA2 was assessed using the oxidant-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Cells were cultured in a 96-well plate (1 × 105 cells/mL) in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by treatment with PCA2 (5–20 µM) with or without 250 µM PA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h. Finally, the cells were rinsed with DPBS, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a Synergy HT Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Ex, 485/20 nm / Em, 528/20 nm, BIO-TEK Instruments Inc., Hampton, NH, USA).

Protein extraction and western blotting analysis

In 6-well plates, C2C12 cells were cultivated and incubated for 24 h in DMEM high glucose. After incubation, the medium was changed to HS in DMEM high glucose and incubated for 6 d until differentiation was complete. The differentiation medium was replaced with low-glucose DMEM once the cells were fully differentiated, followed by an incubation of 6 h. Then, 30 µL of PCA2 was added to the cells. After intervals of 0 min to 6 h, the cell proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer, which included the protease inhibitor cocktail I and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail II. Protein extracts were quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. SDS-PAGE was used to conduct electrophoresis, and the proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (0.45 μm) on ice. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk (MB-S16676, MB Cell, Seoul, Korea) or 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The primary antibodies were then applied overnight at 4 °C after rinsing the membranes with 1 X Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST). After additional rinsing, secondary antibodies that were horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated were attached for 2 h using 5% skim milk or 5% BSA solutions. After 2 h, the membranes were treated with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution and rinsed to enable protein detection. Protein bands were detected using a ChemiDoc™ XRS + System with the Image Lab™ Software (1708265, Bio-Rad, Laboratories, Redmond, WA, USA). A loading control was performed using β-actin and α-tubulin. The following primary antibodies were used in this study: GLUT4, p-IRS1, AKT, PI3K, p-AMPK, AMPK, IRS1, p-AKT, PDK1, GSK3β, p-GSK3β, HO-1, LC3B, Atg5, Beclin1, p62, α-tubulin, p-PI3K, Nrf2, and β-actin, GPx-1, SOD1, and CAT. Anti-mouse, anti-rabbit, and anti-goat IgG HRP-linked antibodies were employed as secondary antibodies.

Inhibitor assays of the AKT and AMPK pathways

Western blot analysis was performed to confirm whether the phosphorylation of AKT and AMPK and the expression of GLUT4 were regulated by PCA2. LY294002 (L9908, Sigma-Aldrich), a PI3K/AKY-specific inhibitor, was used to inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathways. Dorsomorphin (P5499, Sigma-Aldrich), an AMPK-specific inhibitor, was used to inhibit the AMPK pathway. Both inhibitors were applied for 30 min before sample treatment. Protein extraction and Western blot analysis were conducted as described in a previous study19.

Insulin resistance induced by PA

To develop a palmitic acid-induced insulin resistance (PA-IR) model, differentiated C2C12 myotubes were treated with 250 µM palmitic acid (PA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h, as described by Song et al.20. Briefly, PA was first dissolved in ethanol, then conjugated with fatty acid-free (> 98%) bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v) to achieve a final concentration of 2%(w/v) BSA. The resulting PA-BSA complex was subsequently diluted 1:10 in DMEM containing 1% FBS and 2% (w/v) BSA for treatment.

Autophagy induction by PCA2

To evaluate the effect of PCA2 on autophagy, INS-1 cells were used. Western blot analysis was performed to confirm whether the expressions of LC3B, Atg5, Beclin1, and p62 were regulated by PCA2. 3-MA (M9281, Sigma-Aldrich), an autophagy inhibitor, was used to inhibit the LC3B and p62 pathways. Chloroquine (C6628, Sigma-Aldrich), a lysosomal degradation inhibitor, was employed to inhibit the lysosomal pathway. Both inhibitors were administered for 30 min prior to sample treatment. Protein extraction and Western blot analysis were conducted as outlined in insulin resistance induced by PA session.

Type 2 diabetes mouse model induced by streptozotocin and nicotinamide

Type 2 diabetes was induced in mice using low doses of streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich) and nicotinamide (NA; Sigma-Aldrich). Six-week-old male ICR mice (28 ± 4 g) were obtained from Samtako (Osan, Korea). The mice were maintained under specified conditions at 22 °C ± 2 °C and 55% ± 5% humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle, and were fed ad libitum a normal pellet diet (NPD, D12450J, Research Diet, Inc., New Brunswick, NJ, USA). After a 1-week adaptation period to the laboratory environment, the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6) as follows: (G1) control group (NT), (G2) STZ/NA-induced diabetic control group (DM), (G3) DM + low-dose PCA2 (5 mg/kg) group, (G4) DM + high-dose PCA2 (10 mg/kg) group, and (G5) DM + glibenclamide (5 mg/kg) group. To establish the T2D mouse model, following a 1-week adaptation period, all STZ/NA groups were intraperitoneally injected with NA (100 mg/kg) dissolved in 0.1 mL normal saline and STZ (65 mg/kg) in a 50 mM citrate buffer (pH 4.5) for three days. A blood glucose level of ≥ 200 mg/dL was deemed to indicate diabetes and was used for the experiment. The samples were treated after the induction of diabetes through daily oral administration for 5 weeks, while the control group received distilled water in the same manner. Glibenclamide served as a positive control. Mice were first anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation until loss of consciousness. Euthanasia was subsequently performed using carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation in a controlled flow-rate chamber, following AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals and the approved protocol. This study was conducted with approval from the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee (KNU-2023-0135) at Kyungpook National University, Daegu, Korea. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

The OGTT is a test designed to determine how quickly glucose is removed from the blood, and it plays a critical role in diagnosing insulin resistance. This study was conducted in week 5 of the treatment period. Initially, the mice were fasted overnight and subsequently treated with a glucose solution (2.0 g/kg), followed by sample collection. Blood samples were taken at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min from the tail vein, and blood glucose levels were measured using the Accu-Chek® active blood glucometer (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland).

Histological analysis

After 6 weeks of treatment, liver, skeletal muscle, and pancreas tissues were collected, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, and paraffin-embedded. Section (5 μm) were cut for staining. H&E staining of pancreatic tissue was performed to assess β-cell morphology. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with Harris hematoxylin, differentiated in acid alcohol, blued in Scott’s solution, and counterstained with eosin Y. After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted. PAS staining was conducted on liver and skeletal muscle sections to evaluate glycogen content. All stained slides were observed under a light microscope (ECLIPSE TE2000-U, Nikon, Japan), and representative images were captured. All procedures were approved by the Biomedical Research Institute of Kyungpook National University Hospital (Daegu, Korea).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance), followed by Dunnett’s test with the SigmaPlot software version 12.5 (Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while p < 0.001 was interpreted as indicating high statistical significance.

Results

Molecular docking, enzyme inhibition assays, and kinetic analysis of α-glucosidase inhibition by PCA2

Molecular docking streamlines virtual screening of extensive compound libraries by reducing time and resources while uncovering novel molecular frameworks. In natural product-based drug research, it helps connect traditional medicinal uses to bioactive candidates and new therapeutic opportunities21. Here, we performed in silico docking to evaluate α-glucosidase inhibition observed in vitro and in vivo. ESI-MS/MS of Nypa fruticans identified phenolic acids and flavonoids; 42 compounds were selected for docking22, and complexes were assessed by binding energy.

All compounds were effectively docked to α-glucosidase, and Suppl. Table 1 shows the binding score for each compound. PCA2 and PCB2 exhibited notable binding affinities in both α-glucosidase protein models. Specifically, PCA2 demonstrated binding energy values of -8.8894 kcal/mol for the 3TOP crystal structure and − 9.3 kcal/mol for the homology model. In contrast, PCB2 displayed even greater affinities, with binding energies of -9.635 kcal/mol and − 9.882 kcal/mol for the respective models (Fig. 1A and B). The molecular docking investigation employed a dual-software approach to enhance the precision and reliability of the ligand-protein interaction analysis. This methodological framework leveraged two sophisticated computational tools, AutoDock and MolDock, to provide a comprehensive and robust characterization of potential molecular interactions23,24. When these results were compared to the positive control, acarbose (ACB), which yielded binding energies of -6.194 kcal/mol and − 7.154 kcal/mol for the 3TOP structure and the homology model (Fig. 1A–D), respectively, it became evident that both PCA2 and PCB2 exhibited significantly enhanced interaction potentials. These findings highlight the strong inhibitory capabilities of PCA2 and PCB2, suggesting their potential as effective candidates for α-glucosidase inhibition.

The α-glucosidase inhibition activity of PCA2 and PCB2 is shown in Fig. 1E. The results indicated that ACB exhibited a 66.09 ± 1.01% inhibition, while PCA2 and PCB2 demonstrated inhibition rates of 93.94 ± 0.12% and 64.57 ± 1.56%, respectively, with PCA2 showing significantly higher inhibitory activity than ACB and PCB2. PCA2 and ACB inhibited α-glucosidase activity in a dose-dependent manner, as shown in Fig. 1F. PCA2 inhibited α-glucosidase activity by approximately 84% at the highest concentration (20 µM), and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was found to be 3.62 ± 0.841 µM. ACB inhibited 78% of α-glucosidase activity with an IC50 value of 197.65 ± 13.3 µM. These results demonstrate that PCA2 (Fig. 1G) has the potential to inhibit α-glucosidase activity.

The inhibitory mechanism of PCA2 was elucidated through kinetic assays using Lineweaver-Burk plots to determine the relevant kinetic parameters. The initial reaction velocity of α-glucosidase-catalyzed hydrolysis was measured across various substrate (pNPG) concentrations to characterize the inhibition type of PCA2. The Michaelis constant (Km) and maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) were calculated to define the inhibition profile. Based on the Lineweaver-Burk plot analysis, it was concluded that PCA2 acts as a competitive inhibitor (Fig. 1H).

Molecular docking analysis of PCA2 interactions with GLUT1 and GLUT4 binding affinity characterization

We evaluated the molecular interactions of PCA2 with GLUT1 and GLUT4 using two computational docking approaches: AutoDock Vina and MolDock. These methods provided complementary insights into the binding mechanisms and affinities of PCA2 for these glucose transporters. Molecular docking studies were conducted on GLUT1 and GLUT4 to assess their roles in antidiabetic mechanisms. Given the high structural similarity between GLUT1 and GLUT4, GLUT1 (PDB 4PYP) was used as the structural reference to define the binding site and docking grid; the same parameters were then applied to both GLUT1 and GLUT4 (PDB 7WSN) (Fig. S5). The close alignment of these transporters’ structures allows for accurate insights into glucose transport mechanisms via GLUT125. To evaluate the binding affinities of PCA2 for GLUT1 and GLUT4, molecular docking was performed using two programs: AutoDock Vina and MolDock. For GLUT1, PCA2 yielded a favourable predicted binding energy in AutoDock Vina (− 9.791 kcal/mol) and a MolDock score of − 168.000 (Fig. 2A; Table 1). In contrast, the PCA2–GLUT4 complex showed a comparatively weaker profile (AutoDock Vina: −6.764 kcal/mol, MolDock score: −165.718, Fig. 2B; Table 1).

Therefore, PCA2 was applied at concentrations up to 100 µM for subsequent experiments. To examine the protective effects of PCA2 against oxidative stress in C2C12 muscle cells and INS-1 β-cells, PA was used to induce oxidative stress. ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA reagent. Both C2C12 muscle cells and INS-1 β-cells were treated with PA to induce ROS production. PA treatment led to a significant increase in ROS levels in both cell lines compared to untreated controls. To evaluate the antioxidant effect of PCA2, each cell type was subsequently treated with PCA2 at three concentrations (5, 10, and 20 µM). These results suggest that PCA2 effectively attenuates PA-induced ROS production in both muscle and β-cells in a concentration-dependent manner, potentially countering oxidative stress-related dysfunction.

Western blot analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression of key antioxidant proteins associated with Nrf2 signaling, including Nrf2, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD1), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase-1 (GPx-1). In both C2C12 and INS-1 cells, PA treatment resulted in a notable decrease in the expression levels of these endogenous antioxidant enzymes, indicating a suppression of the cellular antioxidant defence mechanisms. However, Fig. 3E and F show that treatment with 20 µM of PCA2 significantly restored the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream targets (HO-1, SOD1, CAT, and GPx-1) in both cell lines, effectively reversing the PA-induced suppression of these antioxidant proteins. These findings demonstrate that PCA2 not only reduces ROS levels but also reactivates Nrf2 signaling, restoring the cellular antioxidant capacity and potentially providing protective effects against oxidative stress-induced damage in muscle and β-cells.

GLUT4 expression and the AMPK/AKT pathway of PCA2 treatment in C2C12 cells

GLUT4 can be upregulated through the phosphorylation of AKT and AMPK, leading to enhanced glucose uptake. To examine the effect of PCA2 on GLUT4 expression in C2C12 cells, cells were treated with 20 µM concentrations of PCA2 for 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 6 h. Figure 4A shows that GLUT4 expression peaked 2 h after PCA treatment. Additionally, GLUT4 expression increases in a dose-dependent manner with higher PCA2 concentrations, as illustrated in Fig. 4B. When PCA2 was administered together with insulin, there was a further increase in GLUT4 expression, suggesting that insulin enhances GLUT4 translocation to the cell membrane.

A Western blot analysis was conducted to elucidate the involvement of insulin signaling proteins in the GLUT4 translocation mechanism within C2C12 myotubes. GLUT4 is controlled by AKT and AMPK, we therefore tested time- and dose-responses. To investigate the effects of PCA2, differentiated C2C12 cells were treated with 20 µM PCA2 for various time intervals (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 6 h) and at increasing concentrations to identify the optimal time and dose-dependent response. Results showed that 2 h of PCA2 treatment led to a maximum increase in the phosphorylation of AMPK, IRS1, PI3K, AKT, PDK1, and GSK3β, with expression levels rising proportionally with higher concentrations of PCA2, indicating dose-dependent activation. These findings suggest that PCA2 activates both AKT- and AMPK-mediated signaling pathways in a time-dependent manner, as shown in Fig. 4C and D. This dual pathway modulation underscores PCA2’s potential as a therapeutic mechanism to support glucose homeostasis and mitigate insulin resistance in T2D.

Effects of PCA2 on autophagy activation in INS-1 cells

To ascertain the involvement of autophagy in the observed effects, we conducted western blot analyses to quantify autophagy-related biomarkers critical for autophagosome formation and degradation. Specifically, we investigated PCA2’s potential to modulate autophagic pathways. Treatment with PCA2 induced a dose-dependent increase in LC3B, Atg5, and Beclin1 expression (Fig. 6A), suggesting substantial autophagic activation. In contrast, the expression of p62, a marker for substrate degradation in autophagic processes, was markedly reduced, indicating efficient autophagic flux.

To explore whether autophagy activation by PCA2 impacts oxidative stress modulation, we examined the activities of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), both of which are critical to cellular antioxidant responses. The expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 was notably upregulated following PCA2 treatment, underscoring the association between autophagic initiation and antioxidant response in INS-1 cells (Fig. 6B). These results highlight that PCA2’s induction of autophagy is intricately linked to its antioxidative effects, underscoring a potential mechanism for ROS reduction and cellular resilience under oxidative stress conditions. Further analysis of LC3B expression as an indicator of autophagic flux was conducted under conditions with and without frequently used autophagy inhibitors. Elevated LC3B protein levels were reduced when co-treated with PCA2 or 3-methyladenine (3-MA), a known PI3K inhibitor. Additionally, in cells treated with PCA2 in combination with chloroquine, an inhibitor of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, LC3B conversion was significantly enhanced, effectively impeding lysosomal degradation in INS-1 cells. These findings strongly suggest that PCA2 facilitates both autophagosome formation and lysosomal degradation within the autophagic pathway.

Blood glucose modulation following OGTT, histopathological analysis, and western blot analysis in the mouse model

The glucose-lowering efficacy of PCA was assessed via the area under the curve (AUC) derived from the OGTT results. We modelled T2DM using a low-dose streptozotocin plus nicotinamide protocol, which yields moderate β-cell dysfunction while mitigating overt cytotoxicity and is widely used to approximate insulin-resistant T2DM26. In this model, mice underwent OGTT to determine the glucose-lowering potential of PCA2 treatment, with glibenclamide serving as the positive control. Blood glucose levels were measured at 30-min intervals over a 120-min period as shown in Fig. 7A. The AUC and blood glucose increments were lowest in the non-diabetic (NT) group (G1) following glucose administration, whereas the diabetic (DM) group (G2) exhibited the highest AUC value, indicating significant glucose intolerance. Notably, mice receiving high doses of PCA2 displayed the lowest increases in blood glucose levels and AUC values among the treatment groups (G4). Interestingly, both low and high PCA2 treatment groups showed smaller increases in blood glucose compared to the positive control group (G5), glibenclamide, as represented in Fig. 7B. These findings suggest that PCA2 treatment exerts a dose-dependent glucose-lowering effect in the STZ/NA-induced T2DM mouse model.

To assess differential glycogen storage between the liver and skeletal muscle in PCA2-treated T2DM mice, PAS staining was conducted. In the liver tissue of the non-diabetic (NT) group (G1), extensive purple staining indicated high glycogen content (Fig. 7C). In contrast, the diabetic (DM) group exhibited significantly reduced purple-stained areas, reflecting a marked decrease in glycogen storage (G2). However, treatment with glibenclamide and PCA2 at both low and high doses restored glycogen levels in the liver, displaying staining patterns comparable to the NT group (G5, G3). Similarly, in skeletal muscle tissue, PAS staining revealed abundant glycogen in the NT group (G1), whereas the DM group (G2) showed a notable reduction in glycogen content (Fig. 7D). Remarkably, glycogen levels in the glibenclamide (G5) and PCA2 treatment groups (G3 and G4) were comparable to those in the NT group (G1), indicating that PCA2 treatment effectively restored glycogen content in skeletal muscle as well. These findings demonstrate that glycogen storage, which is compromised in both the liver and skeletal muscle in the diabetic state, can be significantly restored by PCA2 treatment, highlighting its potential role in improving glucose storage in T2DM models.

Next, we examined the glucose absorption mechanism of PCA2 based on GLUT4 expression via the AKT/AMPK signaling pathway in mouse muscle samples. Skeletal muscle samples were taken and submitted to Western blot analysis to demonstrate the signaling pathway involved in this process in vivo. The findings demonstrated that the STZ/NA-induced T2D group had decreased expressions of p-IRS1, p-PI3K, p-AKT, p-GSK3β, p-AMPK, and GLUT4, as shown in Fig. 7E and F. In contrast, the expression of the aforementioned proteins tended to rise in a dose-dependent manner in the PCA2 treatment group (G2). These results imply that PCA2 can enhance GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake through various signaling pathways. Furthermore, PCA2 shows similar effects in both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Discussion

Diabetes is a complex metabolic disorder associated with numerous adverse health outcomes. Recent pharmacological interventions that target glucose metabolism can be categorised into a range of therapeutic modalities. These include agents that modulate insulin sensitivity, stimulated pancreatic insulin secretion, and regulate postprandial glycemic excursions. This is a subject that has been comprehensively documented in the contemporary endocrinological literature27. However, many of these drugs can cause significant side effects, particularly in patients with low tolerance. Therefore, it is essential to explore both natural products and pharmaceuticals extensively to develop treatments that minimize adverse effects28. Although substantial evidence indicates that PCA2 exhibits antidiabetic properties in vitro, its precise mechanisms in vivo remain unclear. Furthermore, the role of PCA2 in enhancing autophagy is intriguing yet uncertain. Thus, our study aimed to achieve two primary objectives: first, to determine whether PCA2 exerts antidiabetic effects in C2C12 and INS-1 cells, as well as STZ/NA-induced diabetic mice; and second, to identify the potential mechanisms underlying these effects, if they exist.

α-Glucosidase is an enzyme in the small intestine that breaks down complex sugars (oligosaccharides) into simple sugars (monosaccharides), enabling their absorption into the bloodstream. Inhibiting this enzyme slows down the breakdown of oligosaccharides and disaccharides into monosaccharides, thereby reducing glucose absorption and mitigating postprandial hyperglycemia. As a result, α-glucosidase inhibition serves as a valuable model for investigating the effects of nutraceuticals on type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Limited research has comprehensively examined α-glucosidase enzymatic inhibition mechanisms. Our investigation revealed that among polyphenolic derivatives, PCA2 competitively inhibits α-glucosidase, demonstrated through Lineweaver-Burk plots and concentration-dependent analysis (Fig. 1H). Molecular docking using S. cerevisiae-derived α-glucosidase homology models indicated robust PCA2 binding, with binding energies of − 9.3 kcal/mol (AutoDock Vina) and − 146.0553 kcal/mol (MolDock), respectively (Fig. 2A and B). Structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis emphasized the critical role of free hydroxyl groups and electron-donating substituents in enzyme inhibition. PCA2’s nine free hydroxyl groups appear central to its α-glucosidase inhibitory potential, suggesting its promising therapeutic application for T2DM management.

A further consideration is the in vivo disposition of procyanidin dimers. Oral bioavailability of parent PCA2 is generally low, and a substantial fraction of unabsorbed compound reaches the colon where the microbiota convert it into smaller phenolics, such as phenyl-γ-valerolactones and 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propionic acid, that are readily absorbed and circulate at higher concentrations than the parent29,30. Accordingly, some of the benefits observed in vivo may be mediated, at least in part, by these microbial metabolites rather than by intact PCA2 alone. In our cellular assays we used purified PCA2 to define pathway engagement; however, future studies should test physiologically relevant microbial metabolites side-by-side with PCA2 and quantify systemic exposure to each. Targeted LC–MS/MS of plasma and tissues, ex vivo colonic fermentation or microbiota-modulation approaches, and dose–response experiments that bracket reported circulating levels will be prioritized to disentangle parent-versus-metabolite contributions.

It is well documented that oxidative stress, caused by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), is a key factor in islet cell death, insulin resistance (IR), and T2DM31. While normal ROS levels are involved in insulin signaling, their overproduction due to excessive food intake causes oxidative damage leading to IR, both in vivo and in vitro32. Antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx-1), and catalase (CAT) neutralize free radicals to prevent cell damage33. Thus, antioxidants are beneficial in diabetes treatment. Data shown in Fig. 3C ~ F indicate that PCA2 significantly reduced ROS and increased antioxidant enzyme activity in C2C12 and INS-1 cells treated with palmitic acid (PA). Prior studies have demonstrated that natural plants and compounds, such as D. indica bark extracts20, lariciresinol from sesame oil27, and gossypol from cottonseed34, can reduce ROS in cells. Other substances like lupeol from mango35, terpenes from Fructus corni36, and D-limonene from citrus oils37 also exhibit strong ROS-scavenging properties.

To further investigate whether GLUT4 is a key biomarker for optimizing the reduction of ROS during the regulation of insulin signaling, we conducted experiments related to the involvement of GLUT4 and Nrf2 expressions by PCA2. Within the GLUT/SLC2A family of 14 facilitative transmembrane hexose transporters, GLUT4 is widely distributed across skeletal muscle, myocardium, adipose tissue, kidney, and brain tissue. Insulin and muscle contraction trigger its translocation from cytoplasmic vesicles to the cell membrane, enabling critical glucose transport. Impaired GLUT4 membrane translocation represents a primary mechanism underlying insulin resistance in T2DM. Our Western blotting analysis examining GLUT4-Nrf2 protein interactions during ROS onset demonstrated that PCA2 significantly enhanced glucose uptake by promoting GLUT4 membrane translocation (Fig. 3A, B), suggesting its potential therapeutic efficacy in mitigating insulin resistance. In Fig. 3C, the administration of palmitic acid (PA) to C2C12 cells resulted in oxidative stress, indicated by a marked increase in ROS production. In contrast, PCA2 demonstrated a concentration-dependent ability to reduce ROS levels, highlighting its significant antioxidant properties. This observation was further supported by Western blot analysis, which revealed upregulation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway, along with an increase in the expression of essential antioxidant enzymes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPx-1). These findings underscore the pivotal role of PCA2 in alleviating oxidative stress through the activation of Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defence mechanisms.

To date, there are no direct reports on the relationship between GLUT4 expression and Nrf2 translocation into the nucleus during the onset of ROS. However, PCA2 increased GLUT4 expression, which in turn induces the translocation of the Nrf2 protein into the nucleus. The investigation revealed PCA2’s potential to enhance glucose metabolism through multifaceted molecular mechanisms. The compound significantly increased GLUT4 expression, suggesting improved glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity. Mechanistic analyses demonstrated PCA2’s impact on critical signaling pathways, specifically the AKT and AMPK pathways (Figs. 4 and 5). By activating AKT, PCA2 facilitates GLUT4 trafficking to the cell membrane, potentially restoring glucose transport in insulin-resistant conditions. Simultaneously, PCA2’s activation of AMPK, a key metabolic regulator, promotes glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation independent of insulin signaling. The concurrent modulation of both AKT and AMPK pathways positions PCA2 as a promising therapeutic approach for managing hyperglycemia in diabetes, highlighting its potential to address metabolic dysregulation through a comprehensive molecular strategy. As previously reported, molecular docking data showed that compound PCA2 exhibited the highest binding energy within the active site of the target protein, indicating its potential as an α-glucosidase inhibitor. In C2C12 and INS-1 cells, PCA2 enhanced glucose uptake and homeostasis. Additionally, PCA2 demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in antioxidant capacity. PCA2 also showed potent α-glucosidase inhibitory effects, and PCA2 further activated key signaling pathways AMPK, IRS1, PI3K, AKT, PDK1, and GSK3 leading to increased GLUT4 expression.

The autophagic response to stressors such as palmitic acid (PA) and streptozotocin (STZ) provides compelling evidence of the pathway’s adaptive capabilities. In experimental models of diabetes, these triggers elevate oxidative stress, prompting a robust autophagic response that helps mitigate potential cellular damage.

Autophagy, a critical cellular degradation and recycling mechanism, significantly influences the pathophysiology of T2DM by maintaining cellular homeostasis and impacting insulin resistance and diabetic complications38,39. Furthermore, autophagy plays a critical role in mitigating insulin resistance and modulating glucolipid metabolism, with emerging potential for utilizing autophagy-modulating natural products in T2DM treatment. This cellular process is instrumental in enhancing insulin sensitivity and regulating metabolic homeostasis. This process is particularly crucial in liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissues, where it enhances insulin sensitivity and regulates glucolipid metabolism. Moreover, autophagy contributes to augmenting pancreatic β-cell mass and functionality, essential for insulin production40. Additionally, it has been observed to augment pancreatic β-cell mass and functionality, which are essential for insulin production. The activation of autophagy-related pathways, such as AMPK and SIRT1, or the inhibition of mTOR, has been shown to facilitate this process. Cellular autophagy is orchestrated through complex signaling pathways, primarily AKT and AMPK, which dynamically influence organismal aging and lifespan. While AMPK promotes autophagy by suppressing mTOR and facilitating autophagosome dynamics, AKT predominantly functions as an autophagy inhibitor through mTOR activation41. Nevertheless, AKT demonstrates contextual versatility, occasionally contributing to autophagy initiation during viral infections and specific cellular stress scenarios42. The AMPK/AKT/mTOR signaling axis emerges as a critical regulatory network implicated in numerous physiological and pathological processes, encompassing cancer, neurodegeneration, and cellular stress responses. AMPK activation can mitigate oxidative stress by autophagy stimulation, while AKT’s regulatory role remains context-dependent41,43,44. Molecular regulators such as Atg5 overexpression promote autophagy and counteract cellular senescence in fibroblasts43, with p62 functioning as an autophagy inhibitor25 and Beclin1 initiating autophagy under nutrient-deprived conditions. The microtubule-associated protein LC3B is a pivotal molecular component in autophagosome biogenesis, critically regulating the formation and maturation of autophagic vesicles45. LC3-I’s transformation to LC3-II represents a key molecular marker and mechanism for autophagy progression46. PCA2 influenced Atg5, p62, Beclin1, and LC3B levels in Fig. 6A–B, implicating autophagy in its antidiabetic effects. Autophagy inhibitors reduced PCA2’s benefits, suggesting PCA2 acts as an autophagy enhancer.

Furthermore, PCA2 mitigated the reduced glucose uptake caused by PA and improved insulin resistance. Treatment with PCA2 impacted the development of glucose tolerance in the T2D mouse model. Histological analyses showed restoration of liver and skeletal‑muscle glycogen (Fig. 7C–D), and pancreatic islet morphology was preserved and approached the control (Fig. 9A). Through the phosphorylation of AMPK, IRS1, PI3K, PDK1, AKT, and GSK3β, PCA2 reduced insulin resistance as shown in Fig. 7E. It also increased skeletal muscle GLUT4 activation, thereby enhancing glucose uptake. By inhibiting PCK2 and G6P expression in the liver, gluconeogenesis was prevented, and GSK3β indicated glycogen production. Moreover, PCA2 induced autophagy by increasing the expression of proteins related to autophagosome formation and maturation, such as LC3B, Beclin1, and Atg5. PCA also stimulated the expression of antioxidant proteins, including Nrf2 and HO-1, while decreasing ROS. Figure 10 depicts the overall antidiabetic mechanism of PCA2.

In summary, procyanidin A2 acts through multi-faced insulin-related mechanisms to ameliorate hyperglycemia. Specifically, in skeletal muscle, it promotes GLUT4 translocation via AMPK/Akt activation; in the liver, it enhances insulin signaling while reducing ROS and inhibiting gluconeogenesis; and in pancreatic β-cells, it protects against oxidative stress-induced dysfunction. These tissue-specific effects suggest that PCA2 exerts both antioxidant and insulin-sensitizing actions in parallel, rather than one being a downstream consequence of the other. We fully acknowledge that direct assessments of insulin sensitivity, such as hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp studies or HOMA-IR calculations, represent gold-standard methodologies in diabetes research and would indeed provide more definitive validation of the proposed mechanisms. In the current study, we primarily evaluated insulin signaling through tissue-specific phosphorylation levels of IRS and Akt, as well as changes in blood glucose and insulin levels, which are widely accepted indirect indicators of insulin sensitivity in preclinical models. While these surrogate markers offer valuable mechanistic insights, we recognize their limitations and agree that they do not replace direct metabolic assessments. Due to technical and logistical constraints, clamp studies were not performed in this work; however, we aim to incorporate such methodologies in future investigations to comprehensively evaluate systemic insulin sensitivity and to substantiate the pathways proposed herein.

Conclusion

In this study, PCA2, a flavonoid found in cranberries and lingonberries, demonstrated potent antidiabetic properties. Molecular docking revealed PCA2’s ability to inhibit α-glucosidase through a competitive binding mechanism. In vitro, PCA2 showed no cytotoxicity and increased glucose uptake by upregulating AKT, AMPK, and GLUT4 while also promoting autophagy via Beclin1, Atg5, and LC3-II. In vivo, PCA2 improved glycemic control in mice with type 2 diabetes, outperforming glibenclamide and enhancing glycogen storage while limiting islet damage. These findings suggest PCA2’s potential as a therapeutic agent for diabetes. Future research should focus on exploring its long-term effects, optimal dosing, broader metabolic impacts, and interactions with other antidiabetic agents.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although molecular docking suggested potential interactions between PCA2 and GLUT1/GLUT4, these interactions were not experimentally validated. Future studies employing biophysical methods such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) are required to confirm direct target engagement. Second, considering the low oral bioavailability of procyanidin dimers, PCA2 may be metabolized into smaller phenolic compounds by gut microbiota prior to systemic absorption. However, the presence and biological activity of PCA2 or its metabolites were not evaluated in vivo. Further investigation using LC–MS/MS and in vitro assays is warranted to clarify their respective contributions.
