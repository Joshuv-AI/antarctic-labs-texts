Initially Cohen–Macaulay Modules
Mohammed Rafiq Namiq

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce initially Cohen–Macaulay modules over a commutative Noetherian local ring R R , a new class of R R -modules that generalizes both Cohen–Macaulay and sequentially Cohen–Macaulay modules. A finitely generated R R -module N N is initially Cohen–Macaulay if its depth is equal to its initial dimension, an invariant defined as the infimum of the coheights of the associated primes of N N . We develop the theory of these modules, providing homological, combinatorial, and topological characterizations and confirming their compatibility with regular sequences, localization, and dimension filtrations. When this theory is applied to simplicial complexes, we establish analogues of Reisner’s criterion, the Eagon–Reiner theorem, and Duval’s characterization of sequentially Cohen–Macaulay complexes. Finally, we classify certain classes of initially Cohen–Macaulay graphs of interest and those whose projective dimension coincides with their maximum vertex degree.

Primary 13C70, 13C15, 13F55; Secondary 05E40, 05C90, 05C07.

1.
Introduction

For nearly a century, Cohen–Macaulay modules have been a central object of study in commutative algebra, following the foundational work of Cohen and Macaulay
[
7
,
17
]
. This concept was later broadened by Stanley and Schenzel, who independently defined sequentially Cohen–Macaulay modules
[
24
,
23
]
. While these generalizations are powerful, they still exclude many modules and rings that possess significant algebraic properties.

This paper introduces a new, broader class of modules, which we term
initially Cohen–Macaulay
. Our approach is to replace the classical Krull dimension in the definition of Cohen-Macaulay modules with a different invariant, which we call the
initial dimension
, given by

\operatorname{indim}N=\inf\{\operatorname{coht}P\mid P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N\}.

A finitely generated module
N
over a local ring
(R,M)
is then defined as
initially Cohen–Macaulay
if
N=0
or if its depth equals this new invariant:

\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{indim}N.

This definition immediately includes sequentially Cohen–Macaulay modules and offers a deeper combinatorial and homological perspective on these modules and their associated invariants. The well-known fact that depth is bounded by the coheight of any associated prime
[
18
, Theorem 17.2]
provides the foundational inequality for this new property. We state this formally:

Theorem 1.1

(Proposition
3.13
; c.f.
[
18
, Theorem 17.2]
)
.

For any finitely generated
R
-module, we have

\operatorname{depth}N\leq\operatorname{indim}N.

Equality holds if and only if
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

This theorem establishes that the initially Cohen–Macaulay condition is a natural weakening of the classical Cohen–Macaulay property. It serves as the starting point for investigating the structural, homological, and combinatorial consequences of this definition.

This paper has two primary goals. We first build the foundational theory of initially Cohen–Macaulay modules, establishing their relationship to both the classical Cohen–Macaulay and sequentially Cohen–Macaulay classes. Our second goal is to demonstrate the utility of this new definition by applying it to modules arising in combinatorics, specifically Stanley–Reisner rings and graph edge ideals. In doing so, we gain new insights into their homological invariants. We begin by establishing a key structural theorem:

Theorem 1.2

(Proposition
3.16
)
.

A finitely generated
R
-module
N
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is initially Cohen–Macaulay and counmixed, meaning all associated primes of
N
have the same coheight.

This theorem shows that the initially Cohen–Macaulay property is precisely the Cohen–Macaulay property without the counmixed condition. Furthermore, we establish that the property is well-behaved; in Propositions
3.22
and
3.23
, we prove that it is preserved under localization and when quotienting by regular elements.

One of the key tools in our analysis is the dimension filtration introduced by Schenzel
[
23
]
. For an
R
-module
N
with
\dim N=d
, this filtration is given by

0=N_{0}\subset N_{1}\subset\dots\subset N_{d}=N,

where
N_{i}
is the largest submodule of
N
with
\dim N_{i}\leq i
. This filtration is used to define sequentially Cohen–Macaulay modules. According to Schenzel
[
23
, Definition 4.1]
and Stanley
[
24
, Section III, 2.9]
(for graded modules), an
R
-module is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if every quotient
N_{i}/N_{i-1}
of this filtration is Cohen–Macaulay (starting from
i=\operatorname{indim}N
, see Lemma
3.24
). We connect our new definition to this concept, showing that the initially Cohen–Macaulay property depends only on the
first
non-zero piece of this filtration.

Theorem 1.3

(Proposition
3.26
)
.

A finitely generated
R
-module
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
N_{\operatorname{indim}N}
is Cohen–Macaulay in the dimension filtration of
N
.

This result immediately shows that every sequentially Cohen–Macaulay module is also initially Cohen–Macaulay. Consequently, many classical properties and results known for Cohen–Macaulay or sequentially Cohen–Macaulay modules extend naturally to this broader class. We also provide a purely dimension-theoretic characterization:

Theorem 1.4

(Proposition
3.28
)
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module with
\operatorname{indim}N=b
. Then
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if there exists a sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{b}\in M
such that
N_{b}/(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})N_{b}

is counmixed of dimension
b-i
for all
1\leq i\leq b
, where
N_{b}
is the largest submodule of
N
of dimension
b
.

The initially Cohen–Macaulay theory extends naturally to the combinatorial setting of Stanley–Reisner rings. We call a simplicial complex
\Delta
initially Cohen–Macaulay if its Stanley–Reisner ring
\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Our main results in this area generalize three classical theorems. Using the concept of a degree resolution
[
21
, Definition 3.1]
, we generalize the Eagon–Reiner theorem
[
9
, Theorem 3]
:

Theorem 1.5

(Proposition
4.4
)
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its Alexander dual ideal has a degree resolution.

This leads to a simple and powerful characterization in terms of the complex’s skeletons:

Theorem 1.6

(Proposition
4.5
)
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its initial dimension skeleton
\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}
is Cohen–Macaulay.

This result refines
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
and provides a simpler computational check. We also refine the characterization of sequentially Cohen-Macaulay complexes:

Theorem 1.7

(Proposition
4.6
)
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\Delta^{[i]}
is Cohen–Macaulay for all
\operatorname{indim}\Delta\leq i\leq\dim\Delta
.

Furthermore, we generalize Reisner’s criterion
[
22
, Theorem 1]
from Cohen–Macaulay to initially Cohen–Macaulay complexes:

Theorem 1.8

(Proposition
4.11
)
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if for every face
F
in
\Delta
, the reduced homology
\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0
for all
i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|
.

In the final sections, we apply these results to graph theory. We show that all path graphs and, more broadly, all chordal graphs are initially Cohen–Macaulay (Proposition
5.2
). We also find that:

Theorem 1.9

(Proposition
5.3
)
.

Cycle graphs
C_{n}
are initially Cohen–Macaulay except when
n\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

We provide criteria for co-chordal graphs:

Theorem 1.10

(Proposition
5.4
)
.

The independence complex of a co-chordal graph is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is weakly connected.

As a corollary, we show that the independence complex of a co-chordal graph is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is stably connected (Corollary
5.6
). Furthermore, we define and classify bi-initially Cohen–Macaulay graphs. These are graphs whose independence complexes are initially Cohen–Macaulay and whose Stanley–Reisner ideals admit a degree resolution (Proposition
5.8
). We conclude by classifying all graphs for which the projective dimension equals the maximum vertex degree, a problem previously studied only in special cases.

Theorem 1.11

(Proposition
5.10
)
.

For a graph
G
, the projective dimension of
\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{G}]
equals its maximum vertex degree if and only if
\Delta_{G}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and has a minimum facet containing a free vertex.

This article is structured as follows. We first review essential background concepts in Section
2
. The core algebraic theory of initially Cohen–Macaulay modules, including their behavior with regular sequences and filtrations, is developed in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we apply this theory to Stanley–Reisner rings, establishing several combinatorial characterizations. Section
5
investigates applications to graph theory, and concludes with our classification of graphs where projective dimension equals maximum vertex degree.

2.
Preliminaries

We recall fundamental concepts from commutative algebra, simplicial complexes, and graph theory. For a comprehensive background, we refer the reader to
[
5
,
18
,
24
,
27
]
and the references therein. All rings
R
are assumed to be commutative and Noetherian, and
N
denotes an
R
-module.

2.1.
Commutative Algebra

We first recall standard concepts from commutative algebra. The
height
of a prime ideal
P
,
\operatorname{ht}P
, is the length
h
of the longest chain of prime ideals
P_{0}\subset\dots\subset P_{h}=P
. The
dimension
of the ring,
\dim R
, is the supremum of all such heights. Dually, the
coheight
,
\operatorname{coht}P=\dim R/P
, is the supremum of lengths of chains starting at
P
. These are related by
\operatorname{ht}P=\dim R_{P}
and
\operatorname{ht}P+\operatorname{coht}P\leq\dim R
.

For an
R
-module
N
, its
dimension
is
\dim N=\dim R/\operatorname{Ann}_{R}N
, where
\operatorname{Ann}_{R}N
is the annihilator of
N
,
\operatorname{Ann}_{R}N=\{r\in R\mid rn=0\text{ for all }n\in N\}
. A prime ideal
P
is
associated
to
N
if
P=\operatorname{Ann}_{R}(n)
for some
n\in N
. The set of
associated primes
of
N
is denoted
\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N
. From this set, we define the
height

\operatorname{ht}N=\inf\{\operatorname{ht}P\mid P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N\}
and
big height

\operatorname{bight}N=\sup\{\operatorname{ht}P\mid P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N\}
. An
R
-module
N
is
unmixed
if all its associated primes have the same height.

An element
r\in R
is
N
-regular
if it is not a zerodivisor on
N
. A sequence
r_{1},\dots,r_{d}\in I
is a maximal
N
-regular sequence in
I
if
r_{i}
is
N/(r_{1},\dots,r_{i-1})N
-regular for all
i
and the sequence cannot be extended. The length of such a sequence is the
I
-depth
of
N
,
\operatorname{depth}_{I}N
. If
(R,M)
is a local ring, we write
\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}_{M}N
.

For a standard graded ring
R
and a finitely generated graded
R
-module
N
, the minimal graded free resolution

\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}R(-j)^{\beta_{i,j}}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j}R(-j)^{\beta_{0,j}}\longrightarrow N\longrightarrow 0

gives the graded Betti numbers
\beta_{i,j}(N)
. From these, we define the
projective dimension

\operatorname{pdim}N=\max\{i\mid\beta_{i,j}(N)\neq 0\}
, the
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity

\operatorname{reg}N=\max\{j-i\mid\beta_{i,j}(N)\neq 0\}
, and the
maximum degree

\operatorname{deg}N=\max\{j\mid\beta_{0,j}(N)\neq 0\}
. The resolution is an
r
-linear resolution
if
\beta_{i,j}(N)\neq 0
only for
j=i+r
, and a
degree resolution
if
\operatorname{reg}N=\operatorname{deg}N
.

2.2.
Simplicial Complexes

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a finite vertex set. A
simplicial complex

\Delta
on
X
is a collection of subsets of
X
, called
faces
, such that
\Delta
is closed under taking subsets. The
dimension
of a face
F
is
\dim F=|F|-1
, and the
dimension of
\Delta
is
\dim\Delta=\max\{\dim F\mid F\in\Delta\}
. A maximal face with respect to inclusion is a
facet
. We denote the set of facets by
\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
. A complex is
pure
if all its facets share the same dimension. A complex
\Delta
is
connected
if any two facets can be joined by a path of facets that overlap. A subset of
X
not in
\Delta
is a
non-face
, and
\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
denotes the set of minimal non-faces.

The
i
-skeleton

\Delta^{i}
consists of all faces with dimension
\leq i
, while the
pure
i
-skeleton

\Delta^{[i]}
is the complex generated by all
i
-dimensional faces.

For a face
F\in\Delta
, its
deletion
is
\operatorname{del}_{\Delta}(F)=\{F^{\prime}\in\Delta\mid F^{\prime}\cap F=\emptyset\}
and its
link
is
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)=\{F^{\prime}\in\Delta\mid F^{\prime}\cap F=\emptyset,F^{\prime}\cup F\in\Delta\}
. The
Alexander dual
of
\Delta
is
\Delta^{\vee}=\{X\setminus F\mid F\notin\Delta\}
.

Let
R=\mathbb{K}[x\mid x\in X]
be a polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{K}
. The
Stanley–Reisner ideal

I_{\Delta}
is the squarefree monomial ideal generated by the minimal non-faces:

I_{\Delta}=\left(\prod_{x_{i}\in F}x_{i}\mid F\in\mathcal{N}(\Delta)\right).

The
Stanley–Reisner ring
is the quotient
\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=R/I_{\Delta}
. The Alexander dual ideal
I^{\vee}
is the Stanley–Reisner ideal
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
of the dual complex.

The
f
-vector

f(\Delta)=(f_{-1},f_{0},\dots,f_{d-1})
records the number of faces in each dimension
i
. The
h
-vector

(h_{0},\dots,h_{d})
and the
h
-polynomial

h_{\mathbb{K}[\Delta]}(t)=\sum h_{i}t^{i}
are defined by the relation
\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i-1}t^{i}(1-t)^{d-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}t^{i}
.
The
reduced simplicial homology

\widetilde{H}_{q}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})
measures the
q
-dimensional topological holes of
\Delta
; in particular,
\widetilde{H}_{0}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})=0
measures connectivity.

2.3.
Graphs and Hypergraphs

A
hypergraph

\mathcal{H}
on a vertex set
X
is a collection of subsets of
X
, called edges, such that no edge properly contains another. A
graph

G
is a hypergraph where all edges have size 2. The
edge ideal

I(\mathcal{H})
is the squarefree monomial ideal generated by the edges:

I(\mathcal{H})=\left(\prod_{x_{i}\in e}x_{i}\mid e\in\mathcal{H}\right).

A subset
F\subseteq X
is an
independent set
of
\mathcal{H}
if
F
contains no edge
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
. The
independence complex

\Delta_{\mathcal{H}}
is the simplicial complex formed by all independent sets of
\mathcal{H}
.

A
vertex cover
is a subset
C\subseteq X
that intersects every edge of
\mathcal{H}
. A
clique
in a graph
G
is a set of vertices that are all pairwise adjacent. The
clique complex

\Delta(G)
is the simplicial complex formed by all cliques of
G
.

We use the following standard graph families:

•

Path graph

P_{n}
: Edges
\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}
for
1\leq i<n
.

•

Cycle graph

C_{n}
: The graph
P_{n}
plus the edge
\{x_{1},x_{n}\}
.

•

Chordal graph
: A graph containing no induced cycles
C_{k}
for
k\geq 4
.

•

Co-chordal graph
: A graph whose complement
\overline{G}
is chordal.

3.
Initial Dimension and Initially Cohen–Macaulay Modules

This section establishes the foundational theory of initially Cohen–Macaulay modules. We begin by defining the
initial dimension
of an
R
-module, an invariant based on the coheights of its associated primes. We then use this to define the initially Cohen–Macaulay property and explore its primary characterizations, connecting it to classical module properties. Throughout,
R
remains a commutative Noetherian ring and
N
an
R
-module.

Definition 3.1
.

The
initial dimension
of
N
, denoted
\mathrm{indim}\ N
, is the infimum of the coheights of its associated primes:

\mathrm{indim}\ N=\inf\{\mathrm{coht}\ P\mid P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N\}.

Remark 3.2
.

(1)

This definition contrasts with the Krull dimension. Since
\operatorname{coht}P=\dim R/P
, we have:

\mathrm{indim}\ N=\inf\{\dim R/P\mid P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N\},\quad\dim N=\sup\{\dim R/P\mid P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N\}.

(2)

For any
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
, the following inequalities hold, as
\mathrm{ht}\ P\geq 0
:

\mathrm{indim}\ R\leq\mathrm{coht}\ P\leq\mathrm{ht}\ P+\mathrm{coht}\ P\leq\dim R.

(3)

The following bounds are immediate:

\mathrm{indim}\ R\leq\mathrm{bight}\ N+\mathrm{indim}\ N\leq\dim R\ \text{ and }\ \mathrm{indim}\ R\leq\mathrm{ht}\ N+\dim N\leq\dim R.

Example 3.3
.

Let
R=\mathbb{K}[x,y]
and
I=(x^{2},xy)
. The associated primes are
\mathrm{Ass}_{R}R/I=\{(x),(x,y)\}
. Their coheights are
\mathrm{coht}(x)=1
and
\mathrm{coht}(x,y)=0
. Therefore,
\mathrm{indim}\ R/I=0
while
\dim R/I=1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
P
be an associated prime of
N
such that
\mathrm{ht}\ P+\mathrm{coht}\ P=\mathrm{indim}\ N
. Then
P
is a minimal prime.

Proof.

The hypothesis is
\mathrm{ht}\ P+\mathrm{coht}\ P=\mathrm{indim}\ N
. By definition,
\mathrm{indim}\ N\leq\mathrm{coht}\ P
. Substituting this into the hypothesis gives
\mathrm{ht}\ P+\mathrm{coht}\ P\leq\mathrm{coht}\ P
, which implies
\mathrm{ht}\ P\leq 0
. Since height is non-negative, so
\mathrm{ht}\ P=0
, and thus
P
is minimal.
∎

Definition 3.5
.

An
R
-module
N
is
counmixed
if all its associated primes have the same coheight (equivalently, the same dimension).

Lemma 3.6
.

An
R
-module
N
is counmixed if and only if
\mathrm{indim}\ N=\dim N
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the definitions, as
\mathrm{indim}\ N
is the infimum of the set
\{\dim R/P\mid P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N\}
and
\dim N
is its supremum. These values are equal if and only if the set contains a single value.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Every integral domain is counmixed.

Proof.

As
R
is a domain,
\mathrm{Ass}\ R=\{(0)\}
. Thus
\mathrm{indim}\ R=\mathrm{coht}(0)=\dim R
. The result follows from Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

If
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{K}
, it is counmixed, and
\mathrm{indim}\ R=n
.

Proof.

The ring
R
is an integral domain of dimension
n
, so Corollary
3.7
applies.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

If
R
is a counmixed ring, then it is unmixed. Furthermore, for every prime
P\subset R
, we have

\mathrm{ht}\ P+\mathrm{coht}\ P=\dim R.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.6
, the hypothesis implies
\mathrm{indim}\ R=\dim R
. For any
P\in\mathrm{Ass}\ R
, the inequality
\dim R=\mathrm{indim}\ R\leq\mathrm{ht}\ P+\mathrm{coht}\ P\leq\dim R
forces equality. Lemma
3.4
then shows
\mathrm{ht}\ P=0
for all
P\in\mathrm{Ass}\ R
, so
R
is unmixed. The second assertion follows from the fact every primes ideal
Q
of
R
contains a minimal (associated) prime
P
with
\mathrm{ht}\ P=0
and
\mathrm{coht}\ P=\dim R
, giving
\mathrm{ht}\ Q+\mathrm{coht}\ Q=\mathrm{coht}\ P=\dim R
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

If
R
is counmixed and
N
is an
R
-module, then:

\mathrm{bight}\ N+\mathrm{indim}\ N=\dim R\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{ht}\ N+\dim N=\dim R.

∎

Example 3.11
.

The converse of Lemma
3.9
is false; unmixedness does not imply counmixedness. Let
S=\mathbb{K}[x,y,z]/(xy,xz)
. The associated primes are
\mathrm{Ass}\ S=\{(\bar{x}),(\bar{y},\bar{z})\}
. Both have height zero, so
S
is unmixed. However, their coheights differ, as
\mathrm{coht}(\bar{x})=2
and
\mathrm{coht}(\bar{y},\bar{z})=1
. Thus,
S
is not counmixed.

We now introduce the central definition of this paper. We restrict our setting to a commutative Noetherian local ring
(R,M)
.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring. A finitely generated
R
-module
N
is
initially Cohen–Macaulay
if
N=0
or

\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{indim}\ N.

If
R
itself satisfies this as an
R
-module,
R
is an
initially Cohen–Macaulay ring
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Then

\mathrm{depth}\ N\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N.

Equality holds if and only if
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

The depth of
N
is bounded by
\dim R/P=\mathrm{coht}\ P
for every
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N

[
18
, Theorem 17.2]
. Taking the infimum over all
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
gives
\mathrm{depth}\ N\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N
. The module
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay precisely when this inequality is an equality.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Then
M\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
if and only if
\mathrm{indim}\ N=0
. In particular, any module with
\mathrm{indim}\ N=0
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

If
M\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
, then
\mathrm{indim}\ N\leq\mathrm{coht}\ M=0
. Conversely, if
\mathrm{indim}\ N=0
, there exists
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
with
\mathrm{coht}\ P=0
, which implies
P=M
. Such modules are initially Cohen–Macaulay because
0\leq\mathrm{depth}\ N\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N=0
.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
N
be a finitely generated
R
-module and
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
. Then the localization
N_{P}
is an initially Cohen–Macaulay
R_{P}
-module, and
\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}=0
.

∎

The next result connects the initially Cohen–Macaulay property to the classical Cohen–Macaulay condition.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Then
N
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and counmixed.

Proof.

A module
N
is Cohen–Macaulay if
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\dim N
. By Lemma
3.6
,
N
is counmixed if and only if
\mathrm{indim}\ N=\dim N
. The proposition follows, as

N
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\dim N

if and only if
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{indim}\ N
and
\mathrm{indim}\ N=\dim N
if and only if
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and counmixed.
∎

Corollary 3.17
.

Every polynomial ring
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

∎

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
R
be a Cohen–Macaulay local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Then

\mathrm{pdim}\ N\geq\mathrm{bight}\ N.

Equality holds if and only if
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

The Auslander–Buchsbaum formula
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
states
\mathrm{pdim}\ N+\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{depth}\ R
. Since
R
is Cohen–Macaulay,
\mathrm{depth}\ R=\dim R
. As a Cohen–Macaulay ring,
R
is also counmixed, so Corollary
3.10
gives
\dim R=\mathrm{bight}\ N+\mathrm{indim}\ N
. Equating these yields
\mathrm{pdim}\ N+\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{bight}\ N+\mathrm{indim}\ N
.
From Proposition
3.13
, we know
\mathrm{depth}\ N\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N
. This implies
\mathrm{pdim}\ N\geq\mathrm{bight}\ N
. Equality holds in this new inequality if and only if
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{indim}\ N
, which is the definition of
N
being initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Corollary 3.19
.

Let
R
be a Cohen–Macaulay local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Then
N
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\mathrm{pdim}\ N=\mathrm{ht}\ N
.

Proof.

This follows by combining Proposition
3.16
(noting
N
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\mathrm{bight}\ N=\mathrm{ht}\ N
and
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay), Proposition
3.18
, and Corollary
3.10
.
∎

We now analyze how the initial dimension and the initially Cohen–Macaulay property behave with respect to regular sequences. The following lemma is standard:

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring,
N
a finitely generated
R
-module, and
a\in M
an
N
-regular element. If
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N
, any prime
P^{\prime}
minimal over
(P,a)
belongs to
\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N/aN
. In particular,

\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N-1\quad\text{and}\quad\dim N/aN=\dim N-1.

This result can be extended inductively to regular sequences of arbitrary length.

Corollary 3.21
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. If
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in M
form an
N
-regular sequence, then

\mathrm{indim}\ N/(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})N\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N-r\quad\text{and}\quad\dim N/(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})N=\dim N-r.

∎

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring,
N
a finitely generated
R
-module, and
a=a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in M
an
N
-regular sequence. Then
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
N/aN
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and
\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN=\mathrm{indim}\ N-r
.

Proof.

Assume
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Then
\mathrm{depth}\ N/aN=\mathrm{depth}\ N-r=\mathrm{indim}\ N-r
.
From Corollary
3.21
, we have
\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N-r
.
Combining these,
\mathrm{depth}\ N/aN\geq\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN
. Proposition
3.13
provides the reverse inequality, so we must have
\mathrm{depth}\ N/aN=\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN
. This shows
N/aN
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and forces
\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN=\mathrm{indim}\ N-r
.

Conversely, assume
N/aN
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and
\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN=\mathrm{indim}\ N-r
. Then
\mathrm{depth}\ N-r=\mathrm{depth}\ N/aN=\mathrm{indim}\ N/aN=\mathrm{indim}\ N-r
. This implies
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{indim}\ N
, so
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

The initially Cohen–Macaulay property is also well-behaved under localization.

Proposition 3.23
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated initially Cohen–Macaulay
R
-module. For any prime ideal
P
of
R
, the localization
N_{P}
is an initially Cohen–Macaulay
R_{P}
-module. Moreover, if
N_{P}\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N=\mathrm{depth}\ N_{P}=\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}
.

Proof.

Assume
N_{P}\neq 0
. The inequalities
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N\leq\mathrm{depth}\ N_{P}\leq\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}
are standard. We prove
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N=\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}
by induction on
t=\mathrm{depth}_{P}N
.

If
t=0
, then
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{P}=0
. This means the maximal ideal
PR_{P}
of
R_{P}
is an associated prime of
N_{P}
. By Corollary
3.14
, this implies
\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}=0
. Thus,
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N=\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}=0
.

Now assume
t>0
and the claim holds for all initially Cohen–Macaulay modules with
P
-depth less than
t
. Choose an
N
-regular element
a\in P
and set
N^{\prime}=N/aN
. By Proposition
3.22
,
N^{\prime}
is also initially Cohen–Macaulay and
\mathrm{indim}\ N^{\prime}=\mathrm{indim}\ N-1
. Since localization preserves exactness, the element
a
remains
N_{P}
-regular in
R_{P}
. By localizing at
P
, we obtain

\mathrm{indim}\ (N_{P}/aN_{P})=\mathrm{indim}\ N^{\prime}_{P}=\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}-1
. We have
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N^{\prime}=\mathrm{depth}_{P}N-1=t-1
. By the induction hypothesis applied to
N^{\prime}
, we get
N^{\prime}_{P}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N^{\prime}=\mathrm{indim}\ N^{\prime}_{P}
. We can now chain the equalities:

\mathrm{depth}_{P}N-1=\mathrm{depth}_{P}N^{\prime}=\mathrm{indim}\ N^{\prime}_{P}=\mathrm{indim}\ (N_{P}/aN_{P})=\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}-1.

Therefore,
\mathrm{depth}_{P}N=\mathrm{indim}\ N_{P}
, and
N_{P}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

We now connect the initially Cohen–Macaulay property to Schenzel’s dimension filtration
[
23
]
.

Lemma 3.24
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Let
N_{i}
be the largest submodule of
N
with
\dim N_{i}\leq i
, and let
b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
,
d=\dim N
. The dimension filtration of
N
starts at index
b
:

0=N_{b-1}\subset N_{b}\subset N_{b+1}\subset\dots\subset N_{d}=N.

Furthermore,
\mathrm{indim}\ N=\dim N_{b}=b
.

Proof.

Schenzel’s filtration
[
23
]
ensures that
\mathrm{Ass}(N_{i}/N_{i-1})=\{P\in\mathrm{Ass}\ N\mid\dim R/P=i\}
. Since
b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
is the minimum dimension of
R/P
for
P\in\mathrm{Ass}\ N
, the set
\mathrm{Ass}(N_{i}/N_{i-1})
is empty for all
i<b
. This means
N_{i}=0
for
i<b
, so
N_{b}
is the first non-zero module in the filtration. It follows that
\dim N_{b}=\dim(N_{b}/N_{b-1})=b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
.
∎

The following result shows that the depth of
N
is controlled entirely by the first non-zero piece of its dimension filtration.

Proposition 3.25
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a nonzero finitely generated
R
-module with dimension filtration

0\subset N_{b}\subset N_{b+1}\subset\cdots\subset N_{d}=N,

where
b=\operatorname{indim}N
and
d=\dim N
. Then
\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}N_{i}
for each
b\leq i\leq d
.

Proof.

We first show by induction on
r
that if
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
is an
N
-regular sequence, then it is also an
N_{i}
-regular sequence for all
b\leq i\leq d
.

For
r=1
, suppose
a_{1}
is
N
-regular. Since
N_{i}\subseteq N
, multiplication by
a_{1}
on each
N_{i}
is injective, hence
a_{1}
is
N_{i}
-regular. By the second isomorphism theorem, we have
N_{i}\cap a_{1}N=a_{1}N_{i}
, which yields

\frac{N_{i}+a_{1}N}{a_{1}N}\cong\frac{N_{i}}{N_{i}\cap a_{1}N}=\frac{N_{i}}{a_{1}N_{i}}.

Thus, up to isomorphism,

\frac{N_{i}}{a_{1}N_{i}}\subseteq\frac{N_{i+1}}{a_{1}N_{i+1}}\quad\text{for }b\leq i\leq d-1.

Furthermore, Lemma
3.20
implies
\dim N_{i}/a_{1}N_{i}=\dim N_{i}-1=i-1
for each
b\leq i\leq d
. Hence the chain

(1)

0\subset\frac{N_{b}+a_{1}N}{a_{1}N}\subset\frac{N_{b+1}+a_{1}N}{a_{1}N}\subset\cdots\subset\frac{N_{d}+a_{1}N}{a_{1}N}=\frac{N}{a_{1}N}

is a part of the dimension filtration of
N/a_{1}N
, which starts at dimension
b-1
.

For
r>1
, suppose the result holds for sequences of length
r-1
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
be an
N
-regular sequence. Then
a_{2},\dots,a_{r}
is an
(N/a_{1}N)
-regular sequence. By the inductive hypothesis applied to the filtration (
1
),
a_{2},\dots,a_{r}
is
(N_{i}/a_{1}N_{i})
-regular for all
i\geq b
. Since
a_{1}
is a nonzerodivisor on every
N_{i}
, it follows that
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}
is an
N_{i}
-regular sequence for all
i
. As
r
can be any integer up to
\operatorname{depth}N
, this implies

\operatorname{depth}N\leq\operatorname{depth}N_{i}\quad\text{for all }b\leq i\leq d.

We now prove
\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}N_{b}
by induction on
b=\operatorname{indim}N
.

If
b=0
, then
\dim N_{b}=0
, so
\operatorname{depth}N_{b}=0
. Since
\operatorname{indim}N=b=0
,
M\in\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N
, which implies
\operatorname{depth}N=0
. Thus
\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}N_{b}=0
. As
\operatorname{depth}N\leq\operatorname{depth}N_{i}
, the equality
\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}N_{i}=0
holds for all
i
, and the assertion holds.

Now suppose
b\geq 1
and the statement holds for all modules with initial dimension less than
b
. Since
b\geq 1
,
\operatorname{indim}N>0
, which implies
M\notin\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N
. Also, if
\operatorname{depth}N=0
, then
M\in\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N
, a contradiction. Thus
\operatorname{depth}N>0
. By prime avoidance, there exists an element

a\in M\setminus\bigcup_{P\in\operatorname{Ass}_{R}N}P,

which is
N
-regular. From the first part of our proof,
a
is
N_{i}
-regular for all
i\geq b
, and the induced filtration of
N/aN
has nonzero layer
(N_{b}+aN)/aN\cong N_{b}/aN_{b}
which has dimension
b-1
by (
1
). Thus,
\operatorname{indim}(N/aN)\leq b-1
. By the induction hypothesis applied to
N/aN
, we obtain

\operatorname{depth}N/aN=\operatorname{depth}N_{i}/aN_{i}\quad\text{for each }b\leq i\leq d.

Since
a
is regular on both
N
and
N_{b}
, we have
\operatorname{depth}N/aN=\operatorname{depth}N-1
and
\operatorname{depth}N_{b}/aN_{b}=\operatorname{depth}N_{b}-1
. Therefore,

\operatorname{depth}N-1=\operatorname{depth}N_{b}-1,

which implies
\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}N_{b}
. Combining with
\operatorname{depth}N\leq\operatorname{depth}N_{i}
for all
i\geq b
, we conclude

\operatorname{depth}N=\operatorname{depth}N_{i}\quad\text{ for all }b\leq i\leq d.

∎

This immediately provides a characterization of initially Cohen–Macaulay modules using the dimension filtration.

Proposition 3.26
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module. Then
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if the first non-zero module
N_{b}
in its dimension filtration is Cohen–Macaulay (where
b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
).

Proof.

We may assume
N\neq 0
. By Lemma
3.24
,
\mathrm{indim}\ N=\dim N_{b}=b
.
Assume
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. By definition,
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{indim}\ N=b
. By Proposition
3.25
,
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}=\mathrm{depth}\ N
. Chaining these equalities,
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}=b=\dim N_{b}
, so
N_{b}
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Conversely, assume
N_{b}
is Cohen–Macaulay. Then
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}=\dim N_{b}=b
. By Proposition
3.25
,
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}
. Thus
\mathrm{depth}\ N=b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
, which means
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Corollary 3.27
.

Every sequentially Cohen–Macaulay module is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

An
R
-module
N
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if every quotient
N_{i}/N_{i-1}
of its dimension filtration (for
i\geq b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
) is Cohen–Macaulay
[
23
, Definition 4.1]
,
[
24
, Section III, 2.9]
. In particular, the first non-zero factor
N_{b}/N_{b-1}=N_{b}
must be Cohen–Macaulay. By Proposition
3.26
, this is a sufficient condition for
N
to be initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Finally, we provide a characterization in purely dimension-theoretic terms.

Proposition 3.28
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module with
\mathrm{indim}\ N=b
. Then
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if there exists a sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{b}\in M
such that for all
1\leq i\leq b
, the module
N_{b}/(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})N_{b}
is counmixed of dimension
b-i
, where
N_{b}
is the largest submodule of
N
of dimension
b
.

Proof.

Suppose
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. By Proposition
3.26
,
N_{b}
is Cohen–Macaulay of dimension
b
. Thus, there exists an
N_{b}
-regular sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{b}\in M
. For any
1\leq i\leq b
, the quotient
N_{b}/(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})N_{b}
is also Cohen–Macaulay of dimension
b-i
. By Proposition
3.16
, any Cohen–Macaulay module is counmixed. This proves the forward direction.

Conversely, suppose such a sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{b}
exists. We claim this sequence is
N_{b}
-regular. We prove this by induction on
i
. Fix
1\leq i\leq b
and set
N^{\prime}:=N_{b}/(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1})N_{b}
. (For
i=1
,
N^{\prime}=N_{b}
.) By hypothesis,
N^{\prime}
is counmixed of dimension
b-(i-1)
, and
\dim N^{\prime}/a_{i}N^{\prime}=b-i=\dim N^{\prime}-1
.
If
a_{i}
were a zerodivisor on
N^{\prime}
, then
a_{i}\in P
for some
P\in\mathrm{Ass}_{R}N^{\prime}
. Since
N^{\prime}
is counmixed,
\dim R/P=\dim N^{\prime}
. This would imply
\dim N^{\prime}/a_{i}N^{\prime}\geq\dim R/P=\dim N^{\prime}
, which contradicts
\dim N^{\prime}/a_{i}N^{\prime}=\dim N^{\prime}-1
. Thus,
a_{i}
must be
N^{\prime}
-regular. This completes the claim.

Since
a_{1},\dots,a_{b}
is an
N_{b}
-regular sequence,
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}\geq b
. Proposition
3.13
gives the reverse inequality
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}\leq\dim N_{b}=b
. Therefore,
\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}=b
. By Proposition
3.25
,
\mathrm{depth}\ N=\mathrm{depth}\ N_{b}=b=\mathrm{indim}\ N
. Hence,
N
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Corollary 3.29
.

Let
(R,M)
be a local ring and
N
a finitely generated
R
-module of dimension
d
. Then
N
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if there exists a sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in M
such that
N/(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})N
is counmixed of dimension
d-i
for every
1\leq i\leq d
.

∎

4.
Characterizations of Initially Cohen–Macaulay Simplicial Complexes

This section extends the theory by developing specific characterizations for initially Cohen–Macaulay Stanley–Reisner rings. The focus is on criteria related to combinatorial skeletons and reduced homology.

We fix
R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
as the polynomial ring with the standard grading
\operatorname{deg}(x_{i})=1
for all
i
. Let
I\subset R
be a squarefree monomial ideal, which is the Stanley–Reisner ideal of a simplicial complex
\Delta
on the vertex set
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, so
I=I_{\Delta}
.
We define a simplicial complex
\Delta
as
initially Cohen–Macaulay
over
\mathbb{K}
if its Stanley–Reisner ring
\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. We may use
\Delta_{I}
to emphasize that its Stanley–Reisner ideal is
I
.

Let
M=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be the unique graded maximal ideal of
R
. Following
[
1
]
, for any integer
k>0
,
I_{k}
denotes the squarefree part of the intersection
I\cap M^{k}
. This ideal
I_{k}
consists of all squarefree monomials in
I
with degree at least
k
. The following lemma is essential for our subsequent analysis.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
I
be a squarefree monomial ideal in
R
. For every integer
k\geq\min\{\operatorname{deg}(u)\mid u\in I\}
,

(\Delta_{I_{k}})^{\vee}=(\Delta_{I}^{\vee})^{n-k-1}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
k
. The base case, where
k=\min\left\{\operatorname{deg}(u)\mid u\in I\right\}
, is trivial. By definition,
I_{k}
contains all squarefree monomials in
I
of degree at least
k
, which implies
I=I_{k}
. Thus,

\left(\Delta_{I_{k}}\right)^{\vee}=\Delta_{I}^{\vee}=\left(\Delta_{I}^{\vee}\right)^{n-k-1},

as the Alexander dual
\Delta_{I}^{\vee}
has dimension
n-k-1
.

Next, we prove the result for
q+1
, assuming
q\geq k
. The Alexander dual of
\Delta_{I_{q+1}}
is, by definition,

\left(\Delta_{I_{q+1}}\right)^{\vee}=\left\langle\overline{e_{u}}\mid u\in\mathcal{G}(I_{q+1})\right\rangle,

where
e_{u}:=\operatorname{supp}(u)=\{x_{i}\mid x_{i}\ \text{ divides }u\}
and
\overline{e_{u}}=X\setminus e_{u}
. The ideal
I_{q+1}
is generated by

I_{q+1}=\left(ux_{i}\mid\operatorname{deg}(u)=q,\ u\in I_{q},\ x_{i}\notin e_{u}\right)+\left(v\in I_{q}\mid\operatorname{deg}(v)>q\right).

The Alexander dual therefore becomes

\left(\Delta_{I_{q+1}}\right)^{\vee}=\left\langle\overline{e_{ux_{i}}}\mid\operatorname{deg}(u)=q,\ u\in I_{q},\ x_{i}\notin e_{u}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle\overline{e_{v}}\mid\operatorname{deg}(v)>q,\ v\in I_{q}\right\rangle.

This can be rewritten as

(\Delta_{I_{q+1}})^{\vee}=\left\langle e_{\overline{ux_{i}}}\mid\dim e_{\overline{u}}=n-q-1,e_{\overline{u}}\in\Delta_{I_{q}}^{\vee},x_{i}\in e_{\overline{u}}\right\rangle\cup\left\langle e_{\overline{v}}\mid\dim e_{\overline{v}}<n-q-1,e_{\overline{v}}\in\Delta_{I_{q}}^{\vee}\right\rangle.

For each
u\in I_{q}
with
\operatorname{deg}(u)=q
, the corresponding face
\overline{e_{u}}\in(\Delta_{I_{q}})^{\vee}
has dimension
n-q-1
. For each
x_{i}\in\overline{e_{u}}
, the face
\overline{e_{ux_{i}}}=\overline{e_{u}}\setminus\{x_{i}\}
has dimension
n-q-2
. All other faces
\overline{e_{v}}
(where
\operatorname{deg}(v)>q
) have dimensions strictly less than
n-q-1
.

Thus, the facets of
\left(\Delta_{I_{q+1}}\right)^{\vee}
are generated by removing a single vertex from the
(n-q-1)
-dimensional facets of
\left(\Delta_{I_{q}}\right)^{\vee}
. This means

(\Delta_{I_{q+1}})^{\vee}=\left((\Delta_{I_{q}})^{\vee}\right)^{n-q-2}.

Applying the induction hypothesis,
(\Delta_{I_{q}})^{\vee}=(\Delta_{I}^{\vee})^{n-q-1}
, we get

(\Delta_{I_{q+1}})^{\vee}=\left((\Delta_{I}^{\vee})^{n-q-1}\right)^{n-q-2}=(\Delta_{I}^{\vee})^{n-(q+1)-1}.

This completes the induction.
∎

This lemma allows us to extend Theorem 3 of Eagon and Reiner
[
9
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
I
be a squarefree monomial ideal and
k\geq\min\{\operatorname{deg}(u)\mid u\in I\}
. Then
I_{k}
has a linear resolution if and only if its Alexander dual complex,
(\Delta_{I}^{\vee})^{n-k-1}
, is Cohen–Macaulay.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

From Lemma
4.2
and
[
1
, Corollary 2.6]
, we can conclude that if
\Delta^{i}
is Cohen–Macaulay, then
\Delta^{i-1}
is also Cohen–Macaulay.

Following
[
21
]
, we say a squarefree monomial ideal
I
has a
degree resolution
if
\operatorname{reg}I=\operatorname{deg}I
, where
\operatorname{deg}I=\max\{\operatorname{deg}(u)\mid u\in\mathcal{G}(I)\}
and
\mathcal{G}(I)
is the set of minimal generators of
I
. We now connect the initially Cohen–Macaulay property to this notion.

Proposition 4.4
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its Alexander dual ideal
I_{\Delta^{\vee}}
has a degree resolution.

Proof.

Let
I=I_{\Delta}
. We use the fact that
\operatorname{bight}I=\operatorname{deg}I^{\vee}
.

For the forward direction, assume
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Applying
[
25
, Theorem 0.2]
along with Proposition
3.18
, we obtain

\operatorname{reg}I^{\vee}=\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\operatorname{bight}I=\operatorname{deg}I^{\vee}.

This shows that
I^{\vee}
has a degree resolution.

Conversely, if
I^{\vee}
has a degree resolution, then

\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\operatorname{reg}I^{\vee}=\operatorname{deg}I^{\vee}=\operatorname{bight}I.

By Proposition
3.18
, this equality implies that
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

The following proposition provides a key characterization of the initially Cohen–Macaulay property in terms of a specific skeleton.

Proposition 4.5
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if its initial-dimension skeleton
\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

Let
I=I_{\Delta}
. From Proposition
4.4
,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay precisely when
I^{\vee}
has a degree resolution. By
[
21
, Proposition 3.3]
, this is equivalent to
(I^{\vee})_{k}
having a linear resolution, where
k=\operatorname{deg}I^{\vee}
.

We relate
k
to the initial dimension of
\Delta
:

k=\operatorname{deg}I^{\vee}=\operatorname{bight}I=n-\operatorname{indim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=n-(\operatorname{indim}\Delta+1)=n-k-1.

Hence,
\operatorname{indim}\Delta=n-k-1
.

By Lemma
4.2
, the ideal
(I^{\vee})_{k}
has a linear resolution if and only if the complex
(\Delta_{I^{\vee}}^{\vee})^{n-k-1}
is Cohen–Macaulay. Using Lemma
4.1
, this complex is
\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}
. Chaining these equivalences gives the desired conclusion.
∎

The next result refines Theorem 3.3 from Duval’s work
[
8
]
. It follows from Proposition
4.5
, Remark
4.3
, and the definition of sequentially Cohen–Macaulay simplicial complexes.

Proposition 4.6
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if the pure skeleton
\Delta^{[i]}
is Cohen–Macaulay for all
\operatorname{indim}\Delta\leq i\leq\dim\Delta
.

∎

The following propositions describe properties of truncated complexes.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on
n
vertices and
I=I_{\Delta}
. For any integer
k>\min\{\operatorname{deg}(u)\mid u\in I\}
, the truncated complex
\Delta_{I_{k}}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and

\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{I_{k}}]=n-k+1.

Proof.

Set
i=n-k-1
. As
i<\dim\Delta^{\vee}
,
[
21
, Corollary 3.7]
implies that the ideal
I_{(\Delta^{\vee})^{i}}
has a degree resolution. By Lemma
4.1
,

(\Delta^{\vee})^{i}=(\Delta^{\vee})^{n-k-1}=(\Delta_{I_{k}})^{\vee}.

Therefore, Proposition
4.4
shows that
\Delta_{I_{k}}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Furthermore,
[
1
, Proposition 2.2]
gives
\operatorname{bight}I_{k}=n-k+1
. Since
\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{I_{k}}]=\operatorname{bight}I_{k}
for initially Cohen–Macaulay complexes by Proposition
3.18
, the result follows.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

For a squarefree monomial ideal
I
, the following inequality holds:

\dim\mathbb{K}[\Delta]-\operatorname{depth}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]\geq\operatorname{bight}I-\operatorname{ht}I.

Equality holds if and only if
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Corollary
3.10
and Proposition
3.13
.
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]
has a unique extremal Betti number and

\operatorname{reg}\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]=\operatorname{deg}h_{\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]}(t).

Proof.

If
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay, Proposition
4.5
states that
\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}
is Cohen–Macaulay. By
[
26
, Corollary B.4.1]
, this implies
\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]
has a unique extremal Betti number. From
[
20
, Proposition 2.5.2]
, we have the relation

\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]+\operatorname{reg}\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]=\operatorname{deg}h_{\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]}(t)+\operatorname{ht}I_{\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}}

The result then follows from applying Corollary
3.19
since
\mathbb{K}[\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}]
is Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

The following well-known lemma provides the topological interpretation necessary to generalize classical Cohen–Macaulay criteria.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex. The depth of its Stanley–Reisner ring is

\operatorname{depth}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\min\{|F|+i+1\mid F\in\Delta,\ \widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})\neq 0\}.

Proof.

This result follows from Grothendieck’s characterization of depth via local cohomology
[
14
, Corollary 3.10]
, combined with Hochster’s formula
[
24
, Theorem 4.1]
which expresses local cohomology modules using the reduced homology of links.
∎

Proposition 4.11

(Generalized Reisner’s Criterion)
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if for every face
F\in\Delta
,

\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0\quad\text{for all }i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|.

Proof.

The equality
\operatorname{depth}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\operatorname{indim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]
holds if and only if the minimum value provided by Lemma
4.10
equals the initial dimension by Proposition
3.13
. This means

\operatorname{indim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\min\left\{|F|+i+1\mid F\in\Delta,\ \widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})\neq 0\,\right\}.

This condition is met if and only if
\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0
for all faces
F
and all integers
i
such that

|F|+i+1<\operatorname{indim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta].

Since
\operatorname{indim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=\operatorname{indim}\Delta+1
, the inequality is equivalent to
i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|
. Therefore, the vanishing condition becomes:

\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0\quad\text{for all }F\in\Delta\text{ and all }i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|.

Thus,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if the reduced homology of
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)
vanishes for all
i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|
, for every face
F\in\Delta
.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0
for all faces
F\in\Delta
with
|F|\leq\operatorname{indim}\Delta
and for all
i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|
.

Proof.

The forward direction is trivial by Proposition
4.11
.

Conversely, assume the vanishing condition holds for all
F
with
|F|\leq\operatorname{indim}\Delta
. The condition is trivially satisfied if
|F|>\operatorname{indim}\Delta
, because the range
i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|
is empty (as
i\geq-1
). Thus, the condition holds for all faces
F\in\Delta
, and by Proposition
4.11
,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

A simplicial complex
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)
is initially Cohen–Macaulay for every face
F
contained in a minimum facet of
\Delta
.

Proof.

First, suppose
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Let
F
be a face in a minimum facet of
\Delta
, and let
F^{\prime}\in\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)
. By Proposition
4.11
,

\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F\cup F^{\prime});\mathbb{K})=0\quad\text{for all }i<\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F\cup F^{\prime}|.

Using the identity
\operatorname{link}_{\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)}(F^{\prime})=\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F\cup F^{\prime})
and the fact that
\operatorname{indim}\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)=\operatorname{indim}\Delta-|F|
, the condition becomes

\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)}(F^{\prime});\mathbb{K})=0\quad\text{for all }i<\operatorname{indim}\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)-|F^{\prime}|.

By Proposition
4.11
, this implies
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Conversely, assume
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)
is initially Cohen–Macaulay for all
F
contained in a minimum facet. Taking the trivial face
F=\emptyset
(which is in every facet), we have
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(\emptyset)=\Delta
. By hypothesis,
\Delta
must be initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

A disconnected complex cannot be initially Cohen–Macaulay unless it has a 0-dimensional facet. Connectedness is therefore crucial, motivating the following definitions.

Definition 4.14
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex. We say that
\Delta
is

•

strongly connected
if for any two facets
F,F^{\prime}
, there exists a sequence of facets
F=F_{0},F_{1},\dots,F_{t}=F^{\prime}
such that
|F_{i}\cap F_{i+1}|=\dim\Delta
for all
i=0,\dots,t-1
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Definition 11.6]
).

•

weakly connected
if its initial-dimension skeleton
\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}
is strongly connected.

•

stably connected
if the pure skeleton
\Delta^{[i]}
is strongly connected for every
-1\leq i\leq\dim\Delta
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex.

(1)

If
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay, then it is weakly connected.

(2)

If
\Delta
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay, then it is stably connected.

Proof.

(1) This follows from Proposition
4.5
and the fact that Cohen–Macaulay complexes are strongly connected
[
4
, Proposition 11.7]
.

(2) This similarly follows from Proposition
4.6
and
[
4
, Proposition 11.7]
.
∎

The converses of these implications are not true in general. For example, the independence complex of the cycle graph
C_{7}
is weakly connected, but Proposition
5.3
shows it is not initially Cohen–Macaulay. However, the converse does hold for co-chordal graphs (see Proposition
5.4
and Corollary
5.6
) and for complexes with
\operatorname{indim}\Delta\leq 1
.

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex with
\operatorname{indim}\Delta\leq 1
. Then
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is weakly connected.

Proof.

The forward implication is given by Lemma
4.15
.

To prove the converse, we analyze the two possible cases for
\operatorname{indim}\Delta
.

Case 1:

\operatorname{indim}\Delta=1
. By Proposition
4.11
,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\widetilde{H}_{i}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0
for all
i<1-|F|=-\dim F
. We examine this homological condition based on the dimension of the face
F
:

(i)

If
F=\emptyset
(
\dim F=-1
), the condition is
i<1
. This requires
\widetilde{H}_{i}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})=0
for
i=-1,0
.
\widetilde{H}_{-1}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})=0
means
\Delta\neq\emptyset
.
\widetilde{H}_{0}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})=0
means
\Delta
is connected.

(ii)

If
F
is a vertex (
\dim F=0
), the condition is
i<0
, so
i=-1
. This requires
\widetilde{H}_{-1}(\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F);\mathbb{K})=0
, meaning
\operatorname{link}_{\Delta}(F)\neq\emptyset
. This is equivalent to
\Delta
having no isolated vertices (every vertex belongs to a facet of dimension
\geq 1
, which must be 1 since
\operatorname{indim}\Delta=1
).

(iii)

If
F
is an edge (
\dim F=1
), the condition is
i<-1
, which is always true as reduced homology vanishes for
i<-1
.

Taken together,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is non-empty, connected, and has no isolated vertices. These are precisely the conditions for
\Delta
(with
\operatorname{indim}\Delta=1
) to be weakly connected (i.e.,
\Delta^{1}
is strongly connected).

Case 2:

\operatorname{indim}\Delta=0
. If
F=\emptyset
, the condition is
i<0
, so
i=-1
. This requires
\widetilde{H}_{-1}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})=0
, which just means
\Delta\neq\emptyset
. For any non-empty face
F
, the condition is
i<-1
, which is vacuous. Thus,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
\Delta\neq\emptyset
, which is equivalent to
\Delta^{0}
being strongly connected (i.e., weakly connected).

In both cases, the converse holds.
∎

5.
Applications in Graph Theory

This part of the paper transitions from the general algebraic theory of initially Cohen–Macaulay modules to their specific applications in graph theory, focusing on the properties of independence complexes and edge ideals.

5.1.
The Initially Cohen–Macaulay Property of Graphs

While many classical results for sequentially Cohen–Macaulay and Cohen–Macaulay complexes extend to the initially Cohen–Macaulay setting, a direct extension to edge ideals is not straightforward. The primary obstruction is that the initial-dimension skeleton,
\Delta^{\operatorname{indim}\Delta}
, of an independence complex
\Delta_{G}
does not necessarily correspond to the independence complex of any graph. This lack of correspondence complicates the translation between algebraic properties and their underlying graph-theoretic interpretations.

We define a graph
G
as
initially Cohen–Macaulay
over
\mathbb{K}
if its Stanley–Reisner ring
\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{G}]
(where
\Delta_{G}
is the independence complex) is initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
P_{n}
be the path graph on
n\geq 2
vertices and
C_{n}
be the cycle graph on
n\geq 3
vertices. Then

\operatorname{indim}R/I(P_{n})=\operatorname{indim}R/I(C_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be the vertex set of
G
. For small
n\leq 5
, the claim is easily verified. For
n>5
, one can construct an explicit maximal independent set
F_{n}
of minimum cardinality based on
n\pmod{3}
, namely

F_{n}=\begin{cases}\{x_{1},x_{4},\dots,x_{n-5},x_{n-2}\},&\text{ if }n=3k\\
\{x_{1},x_{4},\dots,x_{n-3},x_{n-1}\},&\text{ if }n=3k+1\\
\{x_{1},x_{4},\dots,x_{n-4},x_{n-1}\},&\text{ if }n=3k+2.\end{cases}

For
P_{n}
, this set shows
\operatorname{indim}R/I(P_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}\right\rceil
. Since this set
F_{n}
remains a maximal independent set of minimum cardinality for
C_{n}
as well, the same result holds.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Every chordal graph is initially Cohen–Macaulay. In particular, for
n\geq 2
, the path graph
P_{n}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and
\operatorname{depth}R/I(P_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}\right\rceil
.

Proof.

It is known that all chordal graphs are sequentially Cohen–Macaulay
[
10
, Theorem 3.2]
. The proposition follows as a direct consequence of Corollary
3.27
which states sequentially Cohen–Macaulay implies initially Cohen–Macaulay and Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

For
n\geq 3
, the cycle graph
C_{n}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
n\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

This characterization is obtained by comparing the module’s initial dimension with its depth. From Lemma
5.1
, we have
\operatorname{indim}R/I(C_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{n}{3}\right\rceil
. The depth is known to be
\operatorname{depth}R/I(C_{n})=\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{3}\right\rceil

[
6
, Proposition 1.3]
. The module is initially Cohen–Macaulay precisely when these two values are equal, which occurs if and only if
n\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.
∎

We now shift focus to the clique complex
\Delta(G)
of a chordal graph.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
G
be a chordal graph. Then its clique complex
\Delta(G)
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is weakly connected.

Proof.

The forward direction is given by Lemma
4.15
.

Conversely, assume
G
is chordal and
\Delta=\Delta(G)
is weakly connected. By Fröberg’s theorem
[
11
, Theorem 1]
, the ideal
I_{\Delta}
has a linear resolution and
n-\operatorname{indim}\Delta
equals the maximal number of vertices in an induced disconnected subgraph of
G
. This implies
\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta]=n-\operatorname{indim}\Delta-1=\operatorname{bight}I_{\Delta}

[
16
, Corollary 1.2]
. By Proposition
3.18
, this equality is sufficient to show that
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

For a chordal graph
G
, the clique complex
\Delta(G)
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is strongly connected.

Proof.

If
\Delta=\Delta(G)
is strongly connected, it must be pure. This purity ensures that
\Delta
is counmixed (all associated primes have the same coheight). The result then follows from Proposition
3.16
and Proposition
5.4
.
∎

Let
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
be a non-increasing sequence of positive integers. A graph
G
is a
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
-tree
if it is formed by recursively gluing complete graphs
\mathcal{K}_{d_{i}}
to a previously formed graph
H_{i-1}
along a common complete subgraph
\mathcal{K}_{d_{i}-1}
, starting from
H_{1}=\mathcal{K}_{d_{1}}

[
2
, Definition 3.1]
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta(G)
be the clique complex of a chordal graph
G
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

G
is a
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q})
-tree.

(2)

\Delta
is stably connected.

(3)

\Delta
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

We prove the implication (1)
\implies
(2) by induction on
q
. The base case
q=1
is clear, since
G=\mathcal{K}_{d_{1}}
means
\Delta
is a simplex, which is stably connected. For the inductive step, let
G
be formed by gluing
\mathcal{K}_{d_{q}}
to a
(d_{1},\dots,d_{q-1})
-tree
H_{q-1}
along
\mathcal{K}_{d_{q}-1}
. The clique complex
\Delta(G)
decomposes as
\Delta^{\prime}\cup\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, where
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta(H_{q-1})
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}=\Delta(\mathcal{K}_{d_{q}})
. By induction,
\Delta^{\prime}
is stably connected. Since
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
is a simplex of dimension
d_{q}-1
, it is also stably connected. Their intersection,
\Delta^{\prime}\cap\Delta^{\prime\prime}=\Delta(\mathcal{K}_{d_{q}-1})
, is a simplex of dimension
d_{q}-2
and is thus also stably connected. Therefore,
\Delta
is stably connected.

For (2)
\implies
(3), if
\Delta
is stably connected, its pure skeletons
\Delta^{[i]}
are strongly connected. Since
G
is chordal, these skeletons are also clique complexes of chordal graphs. By Corollary
5.5
, they are Cohen–Macaulay. Thus,
\Delta
is sequentially Cohen–Macaulay by Proposition
4.6
.

The implication (3)
\implies
(1) is established in
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
.
∎

Finally, we define a property that combines the initial Cohen–Macaulay condition with a homological property of the ideal itself.

Definition 5.7
.

A graded ideal
I
is
bi-initially Cohen–Macaulay
if
R/I
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and
I
has a degree resolution. In particular, a simplicial complex
\Delta
is bi-initially Cohen–Macaulay if both
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\vee}
are initially Cohen–Macaulay.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta(G)
be the clique complex of a graph
G
. Then
\Delta
is bi-initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
G
is chordal and
\Delta
is weakly connected.

Proof.

This follows by combining three characterizations. First,
I_{\Delta}=I(\overline{G})
has a linear resolution if and only if
G
is chordal
[
11
, Theorem 1]
. Second, by Proposition
4.4
,
I_{\Delta}
has a degree resolution if and only if
\Delta^{\vee}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Third,
\Delta
is initially Cohen–Macaulay if and only if it is weakly connected by Proposition
5.4
.
∎

5.2.
Graphs with Projective Dimension Equal to Maximum Degree

We conclude by addressing the problem of classifying graphs whose projective dimension equals their maximum vertex degree. This extends previous work, which was primarily focused on co-chordal graphs
[
2
,
12
,
13
,
19
]
, to a classification of all graphs with this property.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
G
be a graph. The big height of its edge ideal,
\operatorname{bight}I(G)
, equals the maximum vertex degree of
G
if and only if there exists a free vertex contained in a facet of the independence complex
\Delta_{G}
of minimum cardinality.

Proof.

First, suppose
x
is a free vertex in a minimum facet
F
of
\Delta_{G}
. The set
C:=X(G)\setminus F
is a minimal vertex cover. The freeness of
x
implies it belongs to no other facet, which means
x
must be adjacent to every vertex in
C
. Thus
N_{G}(x)=C
, and
\operatorname{deg}_{G}(x)=|C|=\operatorname{bight}I(G)
. Since
F
is a minimum facet,
x
has minimum degree in
\overline{G}
and thus maximum degree in
G
.

Conversely, suppose
\operatorname{bight}I(G)=\operatorname{deg}_{G}(x)
for a vertex
x
of maximum degree. Let
C=X(G)\setminus F
for a minimum facet
F
. The condition implies
|C|=\operatorname{bight}I(G)=\operatorname{deg}_{G}(x)=|N_{G}(x)|
. It follows that
N_{G}(x)=C
, which means
x
is not adjacent to any vertex in
F
and adjacent to all vertices not in
F
. This implies
F
is the only facet containing
x
, making
x
a free vertex in a minimum facet.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

For any graph
G
,
\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{G}]
equals the maximum vertex degree of
G
if and only if
\Delta_{G}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay and has a minimum facet containing a free vertex.

Proof.

This result is a direct combination of our previous findings. Proposition
3.18
states that
\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{G}]=\operatorname{bight}I(G)
if and only if
\Delta_{G}
is initially Cohen–Macaulay. Proposition
5.9
provides the combinatorial condition for when
\operatorname{bight}I(G)
equals the maximum vertex degree.
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
G
be a co-chordal graph. Then
\operatorname{pdim}\mathbb{K}[\Delta_{G}]
equals its maximum vertex degree if and only if
\Delta_{G}
is weakly connected and contains a minimum facet with a free vertex.

Proof.

This follows by applying Proposition
5.10
and then using Proposition
5.4
to replace the initially Cohen–Macaulay condition with the equivalent weakly connected condition for co-chordal graphs.
∎

Concluding remark.

We conclude by revisiting Hochster’s celebrated remark that “life is really worth living in a Cohen–Macaulay ring”
[
15
, p. 887]
. The results in this paper suggest that life is still very much worth living in the broader class of initially Cohen–Macaulay modules, given their rich and natural algebraic-combinatorial structure.
