Bridging sequence-structure motifs and genetic variants for genome-wide dynamic RNA-protein interaction profiling
Authors: Yubo Wang, Haoran Zhu, Gaoyang Hao, Yanchi Su, Zhuohan Yu, Yuning Yang, Fuzhou Wang, Xingjian Chen, Ka-chun Wong, Xiangtao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA-binding proteins (RBPs) often recognize complex RNA secondary structure motifs, and these interactions can vary between cell lines. However, no current computational model can explain the dynamic behavior of RNA sequence-structure motifs across multiple cell lines and link these dynamics to the functional impact of genetic variation. Here we show BRIDGE, an end-to-end unified model that bridges sequence-structure motifs with the functional effects of noncoding genetic variants, thereby enabling genome-wide analysis of dynamic RNA-protein interaction profiles. Our evaluations demonstrate that BRIDGE outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods in static single cell line predictions. Without any retraining, the framework transfers to unseen cellular contexts, thereby revealing a conserved yet adaptable grammar of RNA recognition. Attention-guided interpretation identified 3,571 integrative motifs, including motifs associated with splicing regulation. Moreover, genome-wide in-silico perturbation provides an interpretable view of RBP-binding disruption across variant classes, including splice-region and pathogenic alleles.Subject terms: Sequence annotation, Sequence annotation

Introduction

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are widely distributed across various subcellular compartments in eukaryotic cells and play an indispensable role in post-transcriptional regulation, profoundly influencing cell fate determination, embryonic development, and the onset and progression of numerous diseases, particularly neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders1,2. As a large and functionally diverse protein family, RBPs bind to both mRNAs and noncoding RNAs, carrying out complex, multi-level regulation that makes them pivotal for illuminating RNA regulatory networks and fundamental biological mechanisms3,4. Although existing in vivo and in vitro experiments (such as RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)5, cross-linking and immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput sequencing (CLIP-seq)6–8, and SELEX9, RNAcompete10, and RNA bind-n-Seq11) have mapped numerous RBP–RNA interactions. However, RBP–RNA interactions are highly context-dependent, with binding patterns that can vary dynamically across different cell types and conditions12. This dynamic regulation enables precise gene expression control but complicates our understanding of how RBP networks contribute to disease. Indeed, dysregulation of RBP–RNA interactions, for example, due to noncoding mutations that disrupt RBP binding sites, has been implicated as a major contributor to complex disease risk.

Over the past decade, numerous computational methods have been developed to predict genome-wide RBP binding sites. Early approaches based on consensus motifs or position weight matrices (PWMs) captured basic sequence preferences but overlooked RNA structural context and cell-specific effects. Recent machine learning models have achieved higher accuracy by learning complex sequence patterns and even incorporating RNA secondary structure features; for instance, GraphProt13 integrates sequence and RNA secondary structure features in a kernel-based framework, while DeBooster14 leverages boosting strategies to enhance predictive accuracy. Deep learning systems, such as DLPRB15, DeepBind16, and DeepCLIP17, can automatically learn discriminative sequence patterns from large-scale transcriptomic data, and DeepRiPe18 employs genomic annotations to improve model generality. However, these models typically assume a static binding landscape in a single cellular condition and are limited in predicting RBP–RNA interactions under other contexts. Some efforts have begun to address the dynamic nature of RBP binding; for instance, PrismNet19 and HDRNet20 take important steps toward context-specific prediction, but their performance still falls short of universal applicability; achieving truly accurate, cross-cell-type predictions remains challenging because it demands simultaneous integration of context-dependent variables, such as cell-specific RNA structures and sequence-structure motifs, into a single predictive framework. Still, current frameworks largely lack the ability to evaluate how genetic variants alter RBP–RNA interactions. Only a few studies have started to tackle variant effects; for example, by using deep learning to predict how mutations dysregulate RBP binding2 and integrating variant impact into RBP binding models remains an open challenge.

In parallel with predictive modeling, significant efforts have focused on deciphering the RNA code of RBP targeting. High-throughput studies and analyses have revealed that, beyond simple linear motifs, many RBPs recognize complex sequence-structure motifs and other contextual features of their RNA targets1. Computational motif-discovery frameworks can leverage CLIP-seq and other data to identify enriched sequence and secondary structure motifs for RBPs21, providing interpretable insights into binding preferences. At the same time, recent advances in RNA language modeling have introduced transformer-based architectures to capture long-range dependencies and contextual cues in RNA sequences. For instance, RNA-FM22 established a transformer encoder-based foundation model on a massive of unannotated RNA sequences to support downstream structural and functional predictions. 3UTRBERT23 pre-trained a language model on large-scale mRNA 3’ UTR regions to construct contextual representations of post-transcriptional regulatory areas, enhancing RBP target site recognition; 5UTRBERT24 extended this paradigm to the 5′ UTRs, learning latent regulatory semantics via semi-supervised pre-training for predicting functional phenotypes, such as translation efficiency, ribosome occupancy, and mRNA expression levels. RNAErnie25 further demonstrated strong performance across diverse RNA analysis tasks through multi-level motif masking strategies with type-guided fine-tuning. However, these methods, ranging from motif scanning to RNA language modeling, typically operate in isolation and fail to unify sequence-structure codes with the effects of genetic variation. Currently, no unified framework exists that links RBP binding motifs and structures to variant-induced regulatory changes and functional outcomes, highlighting a critical gap in our ability to interpret genetic variants.

Meanwhile, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered countless genetic variants associated with diseases, the majority of which reside in noncoding regions26–28. Many of these variants are thought to act by perturbing gene regulation, for example, by altering splicing or RBP binding sites29. However, deciphering how such specific disruptions contribute to disease has been very challenging. At present, most variant annotation approaches provide only limited mechanistic insight into disease causality. They can often predict that a variant might change an RBP’s binding affinity or gene expression level, but they do not connect this molecular perturbation to higher-order cellular effects or clinical phenotypes30. Bridging this gap requires integrative methods capable of tracing how an initial molecular perturbation (such as a lost RBP binding event) cascades through regulatory networks to ultimately influence cellular function and organismal phenotype. Recent studies have provided proof-of-principle that such connections are possible; for instance, deep learning models have successfully linked disruptions in RBP binding to heritable disease risk2. Nevertheless, we still lack a comprehensive, cell context-aware framework to interpret noncoding variants in terms of RBP and directly relate RBP-binding predictions to disease risk.

Here, we present an end-to-end deep learning framework that Bridges sequence-structure motifs and genetic variant effects (BRIDGE) to enable genome-wide dynamic RBP–RNA interaction profiling. BRIDGE integrates RNA sequence and secondary structure information in a graph-based architecture to capture the intrinsic sequence-structure motifs driving RBP binding. Importantly, it simultaneously models the impact of genetic variants on these interactions, thereby unifying motif discovery with variant effect prediction in one framework. In benchmark evaluations, BRIDGE outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods on static single-cell-line binding predictions and, without any retraining, generalizes effectively to unseen cellular contexts, suggesting that it learns a conserved yet context-adaptable grammar of RNA recognition. Meanwhile, we discovered that nearly 40% of RBPs, including non-canonical regulators, such as TIA1 and DDX6, preferentially bind AG- or GU-rich splice-adjacent motifs, suggesting a widespread coupling between RBP binding and downstream post-transcriptional regulation. Moreover, by integrating large-scale human genetic data, BRIDGE enables systematic evaluation of the potential impact of noncoding variants on RBP binding and suggests that splice-region and pathogenic variants are more likely to affect RBP binding. Based on unbiased in-silico perturbation analysis of 10.5 million GWAS variants, BRIDGE identified cell-line dependent perturbations of RBP–RNA interactions at a substantial fraction of ALS risk loci, beyond what is captured by classical eQTL annotations. In particular, the intron SNP rs2985994 within the COG3 gene was predicted and validated to alter TAF15-associated RNA binding and COG3 expression in a cell-context-dependent manner, suggesting a tissue-specific regulatory scenario relevant to ALS-associated variation. In summary, BRIDGE provides a single, interpretable model that bridges RNA sequence-structure code and variant effects, enabling systematic dissection of dynamic RBP regulation and its potential relevance to disease-associated regulation.

Results

Methodology overview of BRIDGE

BRIDGE is a computational framework designed to bridge RNA sequence-structure motifs and genetic variant regulatory effects in genome-wide, dynamic RBP–RNA interaction profiling, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. It takes large-scale RBP binding profiles (e.g., CLIP-seq across various cellular conditions), in vivo RNA secondary structures, and any relevant genetic variants in those regions as input. The overall task is to learn how combined sequence and structural features (motifs) determine RBP binding, and how alterations in these motifs (due to sequence changes or different cellular contexts) affect binding outcomes. BRIDGE addresses these issues by providing an end-to-end pipeline: first integrating sequence and structure information into a unified cross-modal representation, then applying a multi-scale learning architecture to predict binding affinities, and finally linking motif-level predictions to variant-driven regulatory changes. In essence, the method establishes a direct bridge between RNA sequence-structure motifs and the regulatory effects of genetic variants, enabling comprehensive analysis of dynamic RBP binding profiles.

To capture the dualistic interplay between RBP-binding signals and genetic variation, BRIDGE employs a dynamic cross-modal representation module that integrates RNA sequence and RNA secondary structure features into a unified encoding. First, each nucleotide is embedded by combining its sequence identity with structural icSHAPE reactivity and motif prior scores (obtained from sliding-window scans of STREME-derived position frequency matrices (PFMs)), yielding an initial per-residue representation that encodes both conformational flexibility and known motif signals. Next, these embeddings are enriched with residue-level biochemical descriptors (such as hydrogen-bonding potential and stacking energy), so that thermodynamic feasibility directly influences the attention weights learned downstream. Finally, a recurrent cross-attention layer re-evaluates each residue in the context of its neighbors; bases that consistently attract high attention are promoted to nodes in an evolving semantic graph, with context-dependent edges learned during training. These priors steer the co-attention mechanism toward motif-enriched bases while remaining fully integrated with data-driven updates from sequence, structural, and biochemical cues. Because this semantic graph is recomputed at every layer, the structural, biochemical, and long-range sequence cues continually update one another. This enables the network to track variant-sensitive dependencies and deliver richly contextualized features to the subsequent multi-scale architecture. By jointly encoding sequence and structure in this manner, BRIDGE provides a foundation for recognizing key RNA motifs and assessing how changes (in sequence, structure, or context) might alter RBP binding (Fig. 1b).

Built on the cross-modal representations, BRIDGE utilizes a multi-scale learning architecture inspired by the Kolmogorov–Arnold (KA) representation theorem (Fig. 1c). The architecture first applies a cascade of Fast KA blocks that perform layer-wise KA decomposition on the fused embeddings, yielding low-rank basis functions that approximate the nonlinear binding landscape with far fewer parameters than a conventional multi-layer perceptron. These compact basis functions are passed into an ADPNet module, where dilated convolutions adaptively expand the receptive field across entire transcripts while preserving nucleotide-level resolution. The asynchronous convolutional pyramid feeds context back to refine the KA bases, ensuring that local motif signals and global semantic context iteratively inform one another. Overall, this multi-scale, KA-based architecture thus not only enhances predictive power for complex sequence-structure relationships, but also forms a transparent bridge between individual motif elements (and their perturbations by variants) and the model’s output.

BRIDGE’s unified motif-variant modeling enables multiple downstream applications in functional genomics (Fig. 1d). In these experiments, BRIDGE achieved state-of-the-art predictive performance, substantially outperforming previous RBP–RNA interaction models and maintaining robust accuracy even in unseen cellular contexts. BRIDGE can identify enriched sequence-structure motifs that underlie static and dynamic RBP binding. By analyzing the learned graph embeddings and weights, we can extract subgraph patterns corresponding to characteristic RNA motifs recognized by the RBP. Then, BRIDGE quantifies the effect of genetic variants (such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms) on RBP–RNA interactions. For a given variant within a binding site, the model can compare the predicted binding affinity for the reference vs. the alternate allele, producing a variant impact score. By accurately predicting the dysregulation of RBP target sites due to mutations, BRIDGE enables genome-wide prioritization of noncoding variants that are likely to affect post-transcriptional regulation. Finally, BRIDGE provides a powerful tool for interpreting disease-associated variants through the lens of RBP regulation. By bridging motifs, variants, and functional outcomes, the model can link a noncoding genetic variant to a potential disease mechanism by conventional molecular QTL maps and may offer hypotheses regarding ALS risk variants. BRIDGE is freely accessible as an online server at https://bridge.aibio-lab.com/.

BRIDGE models RNA–protein interactions in a cell-type-specific manner

RBPs recognize specific RNA sequence motifs, and this recognition is strongly influenced by local RNA secondary structure conformations4. However, the extent to which an RBP’s binding specificity varies across different cellular contexts remains unclear. To investigate this, we compiled binding sites for 261 RBPs across multiple cell lines from the ENCODE31 and POSTAR32 databases. From each CLIP-seq experiment, we retained the top 5000 peaks (high-confidence binding sites) to reduce noise (Fig. 2a). By incorporating prior knowledge of RNA sequence-structure motifs into a contextualized graph representation, BRIDGE provides a refined view of RNA–protein interactions that capture both sequence-driven and structure-driven determinants of binding specificity.

We systematically evaluated BRIDGE against several state-of-the-art RBP binding prediction methods (Supplementary Note 1), including HDRNet20, PrismNet21, DeepRiPe18, iDeepE33, DeepCLIP17, GraphProt234, and DeepBind16. For a fair comparison, all methods were trained and tested on the same datasets for the 261 RBPs. We adopted four metrics to quantify performance: AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), AUPRC (area under the precision-recall curve), MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient), and ACC (accuracy), with formal definitions given in Supplementary Note 2. Given the dataset’s 1:2 ratio of positive to negative samples, we prioritized the AUPRC and MCC metrics (which are more informative under class imbalance) and treated AUROC and ACC as supplementary indicators. The experimental results showed that BRIDGE achieved the highest overall performance across most RBPs. Notably, it attained an average AUPRC of 0.824, substantially higher than the next-best method (HDRNet, AUPRC = 0.784). Meanwhile, BRIDGE also outperformed DeepRiPe18, with an average AUPRC improvement of +14.0% across the 261 RBP datasets (Fig. 2b, d). This gap may be related to the modeling focus of DeepRiPe, which learns region-aware binding preferences mainly from sequence features together with transcript-region annotations. In contrast, BRIDGE additionally integrates motif-level prior knowledge and biochemical features, providing stronger inductive biases for learning binding patterns from heterogeneous CLIP-seq data.

Furthermore, BRIDGE also showed consistently superior performance on the other metrics (average AUROC, MCC, and ACC), indicating a robust improvement over previous methods (Supplementary Figs. S1a and S2). Consistently, the ROC and precision-recall curves indicate that BRIDGE achieved greater sensitivity and precision in identifying bona fide RBP binding sites compared to the baseline methods (Figs. 2e and S3).

The performance of BRIDGE exhibited some variability across cell lines, which may be attributed to differences in CLIP protocols as well as cell-type-specific RNA contexts that influence RBP binding landscapes. For example, BRIDGE achieved an AUPRC of 0.921 for CPSF6 (an alternative polyadenylation factor35) in K562 cells, whereas the same RBP in HEK293 cells yielded a lower AUPRC of 0.806. Similarly, for CSTF2T (an mRNA 3′ processing factor), the AUPRC was 0.818 in HepG2 vs. 0.786 in HEK293. These cross-cell-line performance differences may be partly attributed to differences in the experimental protocols. Notably, K562 and HepG2 datasets were generated by the eCLIP method6, whereas HEK293 (as well as H9, HeLa, and HEK293T in our data) were profiled using the older HITS-CLIP protocol8. The eCLIP protocol includes a size-matched input control (SMInput) that omits the immunoprecipitation step and thus captures background RNA fragments that non-specifically crosslink to proteins6. This improvement in experimental design allows better background correction and reduces false positives. Consistent with this, BRIDGE’s performance was generally higher on eCLIP-derived data (e.g., K562, HepG2) than on HITS-CLIP data (e.g., HEK293), suggesting that the cleaner eCLIP data enables the model to learn RBP binding patterns more effectively. In addition to protocol differences, cell-type-specific RNA context could also play a role: certain RBPs may bind sites that have different RNA structural contexts or post-transcriptional modifications in different cell types, potentially adding noise when a single model is trained across combined contexts. Despite these challenges, BRIDGE maintained strong predictive performance across all cell lines tested, demonstrating robustness to both experimental artifacts and true biological variation (Fig. 2c and see Supplementary Fig. S1b for performance by cell line).

We next examined how intrinsic RBP properties, specifically their protein domains and molecular functions, impact the model’s predictability (Supplementary Fig. S4). From a domain perspective, RBPs containing classical RNA-recognition motifs (RRMs) showed the highest predictive performance by BRIDGE. RRMs are well-known for binding single-stranded RNA with high affinity and specificity via conserved RNP1 and RNP2 sequence motifs36; therefore, the model likely learns these clear sequence signatures effectively. In contrast, RBPs with double-stranded RNA-binding domains (dsRBDs) or helicase domains tended to have lower prediction performance. From a functional perspective, we found that RBPs involved in translation regulation (for example, those binding in 5′ or 3′ UTRs) were predicted with the highest accuracy. Binding regions for translational regulators are often relatively consistent and structurally accessible37, which may simplify model learning. In contrast, splicing-related RBPs were the most challenging for BRIDGE, showing the lowest performance as a group. Splicing factors bind in highly context-dependent and structurally dynamic regions, such as the 5′ and 3′ splice sites within introns38, which can vary greatly between exons and cellular conditions. This variability likely makes their binding patterns more difficult to learn. Taken together, these findings suggest that the learnability of RNA–protein interactions is significantly shaped by the binding mechanisms encoded in RBP domains, functional roles, and target RNA regions.

To understand how BRIDGE’s learned representations differ from those of previous methods, we compared the latent feature embeddings learned by BRIDGE and HDRNet. Visualizing the correlation matrices of the embedding features (Fig. 2f), we found that BRIDGE’s features exhibited a broader range of both positive and negative correlations with each other, in contrast to the patterns observed for HDRNet. Specifically, BRIDGE’s richer feature relationships likely arise from its architectural design: (1) motif-aware modeling of RNA sequence-structure interactions, (2) topology-enhanced semantic embeddings derived from the RBPformer module, and (3) joint modeling of local and global contextual features. Beyond classification performance, an important advantage of BRIDGE is that its output scores appear to correlate with experimental measures of binding strength. In the HepG2 cell line, the predicted binding probabilities from BRIDGE showed a moderate but highly significant correlation with eCLIP peak intensities for two RBPs: NCBP2 (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.27, P = 8.29e-67) and DDX3X (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.28, P = 1.45e-66), indicating that sequences predicted to be high-probability binding sites by BRIDGE tend to have higher experimentally observed binding signals (Fig. 2g).

Finally, we explored RBP-specific differences in predictive features by focusing on case studies using only eCLIP data (to eliminate protocol differences). We analyzed BRIDGE’s predictions for select RBPs in K562 and HepG2 to identify why certain RBPs were easier or harder for the model to learn (Fig. 2h). Overall, BRIDGE effectively captured the binding determinants of diverse RBPs, but the relative contributions of sequence motifs versus RNA structure varied by protein. For example, BRIDGE accurately predicted the binding specificity of FTO (an RNA demethylase) in K562 cells (Fig. 2i). This strong performance can be attributed to the enrichment of informative 6–9 nucleotide subsequences in the FTO dataset, including the known FTO-binding mini-motif GCG39, as shown by k-mer frequency analysis (Fig. 2j). In these sites, BRIDGE could rely on clear sequence signals; indeed, saliency maps confirmed that the model focused on those motif positions (along with compatible structural context) to distinguish true FTO binding sites from negatives (Fig. 2k). In contrast, for NONO in K562, BRIDGE’s predictions yielded a relatively higher false-positive rate (Fig. 2i). We found that the NONO binding sites in this dataset did not show enrichment for NONO’s known motif UGUGCCG39; instead, a large fraction of sequences contained repetitive, low-complexity tracts like UUUUUUUU that are not informative for specific binding (Fig. 2j). This lack of a strong sequence signature, combined with possibly more reliance on structural context, may have hindered the model’s ability to learn the binding rules of NONO, leading to more spurious predictions. Two additional case studies (TIA1 and TBRG4 in HepG2 cells) are provided in Supplementary Figs. S5 and S6, further illustrating how motif availability and RNA structure influence performance.

In summary, BRIDGE not only achieves high predictive accuracy for RBP binding across cell types but also offers interpretable insights into the binding mechanisms. It remained robust against protocol-specific biases and cell-type variations, and it was able to discern whether the binding of an RBP is governed more by sequence motifs or by the structural context of the RNA.

Dynamic cross-cell-type prediction and interpretation of RBP–RNA interactions

RBPs exhibit highly cell-type-specific binding patterns, highlighting the importance of dynamic prediction for deciphering RBP regulatory mechanisms across different cellular contexts21. To systematically investigate this, we reanalyzed eCLIP/HITS-CLIP datasets from six human cell lines, each representing diverse developmental or pathological states with unique transcriptional regulatory landscapes. After merging technical replicates and consolidating overlapping or adjacent binding sites within each cell line, we found that only about 1–12% of RBP binding sites overlapped between any two cell lines. The highest overlap was 12% (HepG2 vs. K562), and the lowest was 1% (H9 vs. HEK293) (Figs. 3b and S7). This striking lack of cross-cell overlap likely reflects multiple influences. Biologically, different cellular environments can lead to substantial variation in RNA secondary structure stability, which in turn may create or occlude RBP binding sites unique to each context.

To illustrate this, when examining DGCR8 (an essential microprocessor component for pri-miRNA processing40), we found many of its binding sites overlapping regions of pronounced RNA structural variability—particularly in gene loci, such as PLOD1, MFN2, and PPIG (Figs. 3c and S8a). Notably, DGCR8 binding was significantly elevated in regions where the local RNA conformation shifted from single-stranded (icSHAPE score ≥ 0.233) to double-stranded (icSHAPE score < 0.233), consistent with its known preference for double-stranded RNA via its dRBM domain31 (Supplementary Fig. S8b, c). Technically, differences in experimental protocols may also contribute to the limited overlap; for instance, the HepG2 and K562 binding data were generated using eCLIP, whereas data for H9, HEK293, HEK293T, and HeLa came from HITS-CLIP, potentially introducing platform-specific biases. These results highlight the strong dependence of RBP binding profiles on both cellular state and experimental platform, necessitating the need for a predictive framework that generalizes across cell types and profiling techniques. To address the need for cross-context RBP binding predictions, we used BRIDGE to learn an RBP–RNA binding representation in a source cell line and shift it to predict binding in a target cell line, allowing predictions of cross-cell line even when direct peak overlap is minimal (Fig. 3a). Unlike previous models constrained to single cell line training, BRIDGE leverages the underlying shared sequence and structural motifs of RBP binding that tend to be conserved across cell types, while also allowing for context-specific adaptation to account for differences in the target cell line’s environment. Furthermore, BRIDGE has built-in interpretability at the motif and RNA structure level, which means that it can not only predict binding sites but also reveal the sequence-structure features that underpin each prediction.

We evaluated BRIDGE’s performance in dynamic RBP interaction prediction by benchmarking it against seven state-of-the-art algorithms (HDRNet20, PrismNet21, DeepRiPe18, GraphProt234, DeepBind16, DeepCLIP17, and iDeepE33) on 139 RBP profiles across multiple cell lines. BRIDGE outperformed all competing methods, achieving on average 1.7% higher AUC, 2.2% higher ACC, 2.5% higher AUPRC, and 5.4% higher MCC than the next best method (HDRNet) (Figs. 3e and S9a). Compared with DeepRiPe, BRIDGE achieved consistent performance gains, with average improvements of +7.1% in AUC, +7.2% in ACC, +9.3% in AUPRC, and +8.3% in MCC. This difference may be explained by the fact that DeepRiPe is designed to capture region-aware binding preferences primarily from static transcript-derived features, which can be less effective when RBP binding exhibits strong cell-type- or condition-specific dynamics. iDeepE showed the poorest performance and often failed to produce any shiftable predictions for many datasets, highlighting the challenge of the dynamic prediction task. The advantage of BRIDGE became even more pronounced in specific cross-cell shift scenarios. For example, when shifting a model for PTBP1 from HeLa to HepG2, BRIDGE improved the prediction metrics by a wide margin relative to HDRNet (AUC +7.6%, ACC +2.9%, AUPRC +7.9%, MCC +10.0%; Figs. 3f and S10–S13). Similar performance gains were observed for other RBPs, such as SND1 and PRPF8, under various source/target cell shifts. These improvements stem from BRIDGE’s capacity for cross-cell generalization, achieved by modeling context-dependent RBP–RNA interactions through motif-guided sequence-structure encodings and a semantic graph representation.

To further demonstrate the practical applicability of our dynamic cross-cell-type prediction framework in heterogeneous cellular environments, we focused on transcripts with well-established clinical or developmental relevance, mRNAs that frequently remodel RNA structural motifs across cell states, thereby redirecting RBP binding within UTRs or coding regions and, in turn, modulating transcript stability, translation efficiency, and even protein function41. Accurately predicting RBP binding in these regions is therefore crucial for understanding context-dependent gene regulation. As a representative example, we examined FXR2 (a protein critical for hippocampal neurogenesis42) and its binding to the UTR and CDS of the HTT transcript (which encodes huntingtin). In HepG2 cells, eCLIP experiments identified 6 FXR2 binding sites on HTT, whereas in K562 cells, 13 sites were found. Using a model trained on HepG2 and dynamically shifted to K562, BRIDGE correctly predicted 9 of the 13 FXR2 binding sites in K562 (with no false positives), outperforming HDRNet, which identified only 6 of these sites (Fig. 3d). We observed similarly strong shift performance for HNRNPK on the HCG18 transcript: BRIDGE’s dynamic shift model accurately captured context-specific binding on both exonic and intronic regions (Supplementary Fig. S9b), further highlighting the model’s robustness in complex cellular contexts. We further analyzed the performance differences between BRIDGE’s dynamic shift models and cell-specific models (trained and tested within the same cellular context) for each RBP. Although BRIDGE remained the overall top performer, many RBPs exhibited reduced prediction performance under shift conditions. Notably, the degree of performance drop varied substantially across RBPs, reflecting their differential sensitivity to cellular context (Supplementary Fig. S14). This analysis highlights the biological variability in RBP binding landscapes and underscores the importance of incorporating context-awareness in predictive modeling for generalization across cell types.

Accumulating evidence suggests that, for certain RBPs, local RNA secondary structure rather than its underlying primary sequence is the main factor determining target recognition43. However, fine-grained structural preferences and how they shift with cellular context remain poorly understood. To address this gap, we integrated our RBP binding data with computational RNA secondary structure profiles. Specifically, we used RNAplfold44 to compute local base-pairing probabilities and annotate secondary structure features (e.g., hairpins, multiloops) for target transcripts, thus complementing experimental icSHAPE probing data45 with high coverage and resolution. Using these RNAplfold-derived annotations, a peak-centered structural-context analysis revealed clear associations between RBP binding and local RNA conformation. For example, in K562 cells, we found that binding sites of LIN28B and DDX52 were highly enriched in double-stranded (paired) regions of transcripts (Figs. 3g and S15), suggesting that both RBPs preferentially bind to stable secondary structures in this cell context. Notably, LIN28B showed elevated binding in hairpin loop regions as well, consistent with its cold-shock domain’s known affinity for looped RNA hairpins46. These results validate that BRIDGE’s predictions align with known structural preferences and also suggest that some RBPs might generally favor structured RNA elements.

To understand why BRIDGE makes certain predictions and how these may shift between contexts, we applied explainable AI techniques to the model’s outputs. Using SHAP value analysis47 and integrated gradients (IG)48, we generated saliency maps and attribution maps that highlight the most influential sequence and structural features for each predicted binding event (Fig. 3h). This interpretability analysis revealed differential focus on motif and structure by the model under different cell conditions. For instance, when shifting the model for DDX52 from K562 to HepG2, the predicted binding probability at several sites dropped from 0.82 (in K562) to 0.20 (in HepG2). Correspondingly, SHAP/IG analysis indicated a shift in the recognized motif and structural attention for DDX52 between the two cell types, suggesting that DDX52 might alter its binding preference or accessibility depending on cellular context. By contrast, LIN28B’s binding probability remained virtually unchanged (0.90 in K562 vs 0.88 in HepG2), and the model consistently highlighted the GGAGA motif49 for LIN28B in both cell lines. Mapping the high-confidence predicted sites onto RNA secondary structure models (Fig. 3i) further showed that these sites often reside in hairpin loops or stem regions, reinforcing the notion of structure-driven binding for these RBPs. Overall, BRIDGE enables robust cross-cell-type prediction of RBP binding events and, importantly, provides mechanistic interpretation through integrated sequence-structure analysis. By generalizing across cellular contexts, BRIDGE uncovers how RBPs engage with RNA in a dynamic, context-dependent manner.

Modular architecture and interpretability of BRIDGE

To delineate how individual components of BRIDGE shape both predictive accuracy and biological insight, we performed targeted ablation analyses combined with interpretability assessments. We first benchmarked BRIDGE across five positive-to-negative training ratios (1:1, 1:1.5, 1:2, 1.5:1, and 2:1) to mimic the class-imbalance spectrum typical of CLIP datasets. Performance peaked at a 1:2 ratio (Fig. 4c), suggesting that a moderate surplus of negatives mirrors the natural predominance of non-binding regions in the transcriptome and provides the contrast necessary for BRIDGE to discern bona fide RBP–RNA interactions.

To assess the individual contribution of each biological modality to RBP binding prediction, we systematically removed each in turn: (i) icSHAPE reactivities, (ii) the semantic graph, (iii) sequence-structure motif priors, and (iv) biochemical descriptors (Figs. 4a and S16a). Dropping any one modality impaired performance across all six cell lines tested, but the magnitude and pattern of decline were modality-specific. Removing icSHAPE data caused performance reductions in a cell-type-specific manner, mirroring the structural heterogeneity of different transcriptomes. Although icSHAPE reactivities remain crucial for capturing local RNA folding, the sequence-structure motif priors provided the dominant source of biological interpretability, enabling BRIDGE to generalize across cell types and capture dynamic RBP–RNA binding rules. We next evaluated the importance of experimentally derived RNA structure by replacing icSHAPE reactivities with RNAplfold-predicted pairing probabilities. The icSHAPE-driven model consistently outperformed the RNAplfold-based model across all cell lines, and even combining both sources offered no further improvement (Figs. 4d and S16b). This result is consistent with the notion that the thermodynamic forces governing RNA folding in vitro may not fully capture the stable structural states present in vivo50.

We next examined four categories of biochemical descriptors: dinucleotide physicochemical properties (DPCP), k-tuple nucleotide frequency patterns (KNFP), nucleotide chemical properties (NCP), and nucleotide density (ND). When used individually, each descriptor type yielded a mean MCC of approximately 0.52–0.54 (DPCP: 0.5233; KNFP: 0.5294; NCP: 0.5352; ND: 0.5320). However, when BRIDGE incorporated all four descriptors together, the mean MCC climbed to 0.5971 across 261 RBPs (Figs. 4e and S16c). This finding highlights that the combined constraints from these biochemical properties provide a more comprehensive and biologically grounded representation of RBP binding preferences than any single feature alone. To isolate the contribution of BRIDGE’s global-context language model, we replaced the RBPformer backbone with DNABERT51 and Nucleotide Transformer v2 (NT v2)52 and evaluated the effects of k-mer tokenization and model size. At matched size, NT v2 with shorter tokenization (50M; 3-mer) consistently outperformed the 6-mer counterpart, and increasing NT v2 capacity at fixed 6-mer tokenization (50M/100M/500M) led to a monotonic performance decline. In parallel, sweeping DNABERT across k = 3–6 showed only modest, non-monotonic variation, with no consistent benefit from intermediate k values. Overall, BRIDGE with the native 1-mer RBPformer backbone remained best-performing, supporting the importance of preserving nucleotide-level positional detail for RBP binding prediction (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. S17).

To quantify the benefit of task-specific fine-tuning, we compared model performance immediately after pre-training on a general RNA corpus with performance after fine-tuning on each CLIP dataset (Fig. 4g). Across 261 RBP-specific tasks, fine-tuning consistently improved AUROC. The mean rose from 0.8723 after pre-training to 0.8873 after fine-tuning. Pre- and post-fine-tuning performance remained strongly correlated (Pearson r = 0.916), indicating that even models starting from a high pre-trained baseline still benefited from task-specific refinement. These results confirm that fine-tuning reliably boosts performance over the task-agnostic pre-training baseline. In addition, we explore the interpretability of BRIDGE through its semantic attention graph. The correlation patterns within this graph provide a direct readout of how the model integrates local and global contextual cues (Fig. 4h). At single-nucleotide resolution, the BRIDGE attention maps highlight how individual bases can influence each other throughout the transcript. By jointly modeling neighborhood-level and transcript-wide interactions, BRIDGE uncovers long-range correlations and potential intramolecular contacts that underlie post-transcriptional regulation. To assess whether the model’s attention mechanism aligns with biologically meaningful signals, we next examined the co-occurrence between attention hotspots and the predicted dynamic motifs. Taking AGO in HEK293 cells as an example (Fig. 4b), nucleotides adjacent to crosslinking sites showed the highest attention scores within a 101-nucleotide window in positive samples, whereas no such enrichment emerged in negative controls. These data highlight the ability of attention-driven models to discriminate biologically relevant signals from background noise.

Finally, to assess the contribution of each architectural block, we trained four ablated variants of BRIDGE, each missing a key component: (i) without the graph-convolutional network (GCN) that overlays topological context on RBPformer embeddings; (ii) TextCNN-based predictor variants that replace different parts of the KA multi-scale prediction network; (iii) KA-based decomposition ablations, including a KA replacement variant (each KA block replaced by a parameter-matched MLP) and a KA removal variant (removing the cascaded Fast KA module); and (iv) with max pooling replaced by average pooling. Restoring the GCN improved MCC in 199 of 261 RBPs, with gains most pronounced for RBPs with lower baseline performance when using sequence features alone (Figs. 4i and S16d; Pearson r = −0.70, P = 6.54 × 10−31). Specifically, helicase-domain proteins, such as DDX3X (K562), were already well captured without the topology information, showing only a marginal rise in MCC (from 0.812 to 0.815). By contrast, RRM-domain proteins—PTBP1 in HepG2 and LARP4 in K562—showed pronounced gains (MCC, from 0.138 to 0.501 and from 0.311 to 0.671, respectively). These results indicate that the GCN is most valuable when sequence-based predictions alone are insufficient. By aggregating one-hop neighborhood information, the GCN couples local nucleotide signals with long-range dependencies, thereby sharpening predictions. In addition, substituting the KA multi-scale prediction network with TextCNN-based variants reduced performance in both static and cross-cell-type predictions, with the best TextCNN hybrid still trailing BRIDGE across all metrics (Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Fig. S18). For KA-based decomposition, replacing KA blocks with capacity-matched MLPs caused a moderate decrease, whereas removing the entire cascaded fast KA module led to a larger degradation, and feature correlation maps further showed that the full KA cascade yields more structured, low-redundancy representations than either replacement or removal (Fig. 4j, Supplementary Note 5, and Supplementary Fig. S19). Figure 4f compares the use of max pooling versus average pooling within BRIDGE across four evaluation metrics, revealing subtle yet important differences. Averaging base-level binding scores smooths out the high-affinity peaks that define true RBP binding sites, whereas max pooling preserves the strongest motif signals and therefore delivers more precise predictions of biologically relevant binding events.

Together, these ablation experiments and interpretability analyses validate BRIDGE’s superior predictive performance and highlight its unique strengths. The integration of multimodal biological features with neural network modules makes BRIDGE particularly well-suited for dynamic, variant-aware inference of RNA–protein binding sites.

BRIDGE uncovers dynamic sequence-structure motifs that couple RBP binding to splicing regulation

Dynamic identification of sequence-structure motifs across cell types is critical for elucidating the context-specific regulatory functions of RBPs39. Beyond canonical splicing regulators, many RBPs traditionally associated with mRNA transport, stability, or translation also exhibit significant binding enrichment around splicing consensus motifs53. Thus, systematic identifying combined sequence-structure motifs can provide mechanistic insights into how RBPs coordinate splicing decisions with other post-transcriptional events across diverse cellular contexts. To lay the foundation for motif-level analysis, we first performed a large-scale comparative analysis of k-mer enrichment patterns across eCLIP datasets spanning diverse RBPs and human cell lines (Fig. 5b). The results revealed a clear correlation between specific k-mer enrichment and RBP functional categories. For example, splicing regulators (e.g., TRA2A in K562 and HepG2 cells) preferentially enriched AG dinucleotides54, whereas RBPs involved in 3′ UTR processing (e.g., the IGF2BP family in HEK293, K562, and HepG2) favored C-rich sequences55. Unsupervised clustering further showed groups of RBPs sharing highly conserved motif biases dominated by GC-, G-, or U-rich k-mers, consistent with earlier observations that such motifs are frequently overrepresented in eCLIP data relative to in vitro assays31. Notably, over 75% of the most enriched k-mers across all datasets were G-rich, underscoring the universal importance of G content in modulating RBP–RNA interactions.

Despite the known importance of RNA secondary structure for certain RBPs, fine-grained binding preferences to structured RNA have been thoroughly characterized for only a limited subset of proteins56. To address this gap, we used our graph-attention network BRIDGE to extract sequence-structure motifs from high-confidence RBP binding sites (Fig. 5a). Specifically, candidate RNA segments were first selected based on high attention weights, then evaluated for enrichment in positive samples using the hypergeometric test, with multiple testing correction performed via the Benjamini–Hochberg method (adjusted p-value < 0.05). In total, BRIDGE identified 3571 significant motifs across 220 RBP-cell line pairs (Figs. 5d and S20). To facilitate cross-context comparisons, we selected one representative motif per RBP per cell line, choosing the motif with the lowest information entropy. The sequence components of these representative motifs aligned well with known RBP binding preferences documented in databases like ATtRACT57 and mCrossBase39 (Supplementary Figs. S21–S23). For instance, ELAVL1 in HEK293 cells preferentially recognized poly-U motifs, whereas TARDBP in HEK293T and CSTF2 in HEK293 showed strong affinity for UG-rich sequences, all consistent with previously reported motifs. Notably, BRIDGE also captured dynamic motif differences between cellular contexts: for example, the splicing factor HNRNPM was found to bind distinct but related motifs in different cell lines (UGUGGC in K562 vs. UGUUGG in HepG2), highlighting the context-dependence of RBP–RNA interactions. Beyond sequence preferences, each RBP exhibited distinct structural affinities (Supplementary Fig. S24). For example, PUM2 in K562 and LIN28A in H9 both showed strong binding biases toward hairpin loop structures58,59. Compared to models limited to sequence-only motifs or coarse structural features (e.g., treating nucleotides as simply paired vs. unpaired), BRIDGE detected much finer-grained sequence-structure motifs in dynamic cellular environments, significantly reducing the false positive rate (Fig. 5f). Collectively, these results suggest RBP–RNA interactions are jointly governed by a dual-recognition paradigm involving both conserved sequence elements and specific RNA conformations.

In addition to recovering known RBP-binding motifs, BRIDGE uncovered numerous previously unannotated sequence-structure motifs, not found in existing databases (e.g., ATtRACT, mCrossBase). These motifs were frequently enriched at high-confidence binding sites across multiple RBPs and cell types, and they clustered into distinct groups sharing common sequence and structural features (Fig. 5c). For instance, the U2AF splicing factor family (U2AF1, U2AF2/U2AF65) showed a consistent preference for U-rich sequences situated in external or internal loop structures across diverse cell types, aligning with U2AF’s known role in recognizing the poly-pyrimidine tract at 3′ splice sites during pre-mRNA splicing60. To evaluate motif conservation across cell types, we computed pairwise similarity scores between motifs using Frobenius norm-based comparisons of their PWMs (Fig. 5e).

We found that structural motifs were far more conserved across contexts (minimum similarity 0.9) than sequence-only motifs (minimum similarity 0.6), suggesting that conserved RNA structural features provide a more stable regulatory scaffold for RBP binding in dynamic cellular environments. To further validate the dynamic motif extraction capability of BRIDGE, we applied saliency maps to identify regulatory motifs associated with both specific loci and global binding patterns. As an illustrative example, BRIDGE predicted high binding affinity of HNRNPA1 at a representative intronic region within the KMT2E in HepG2, while the signal was nearly absent in K562 (Fig. 5g). Nucleotide-level importance scores revealed that the HepG2-specific binding was driven by a UG-rich hairpin motif at that locus, consistent with known HNRNPA1 binding elements (Fig. 5h). These results demonstrate that BRIDGE effectively learns biologically meaningful dynamic sequence-structure motifs.

Finally, to assess the involvement of these motifs in pre-mRNA splicing regulation, we extended our analysis to a set of 67 RBPs with well-characterized binding profiles. Strikingly, 27 (40%) showed preferential clustering toward AG- or GU-rich motifs (Fig. 5i), consistent with the canonical roles of AG and GU dinucleotides in 3′ and 5′ splice sites61,62. Specifically, 11 RBPs favored AG, 22 favored GU, and 6 displayed dual preferences. Notably, the RBPs enriched at splice-like motifs included not only known splicing factors, such as SRSF7 (which autoregulates its expression via non-productive splicing)63 or ZRANB2 (implicated in drug-induced alternative splicing programs)64, but also RBPs like TIA1 and DDX6 that are traditionally linked to mRNA 3′ end processing or stability65,66. The unexpected enrichment of TIA1, DDX6 and similar RBPs at splice consensus sites suggests a mechanistic interplay between spliceosome assembly and downstream post-transcriptional processes67,68. Indeed, we observed pronounced clustering of RBP binding events immediately adjacent to splice junctions (Figs. 5k and S25), supporting a functional linkage between RBP localization and the splicing machinery. Furthermore, a quantitative distance analysis confirmed that RBP binding peaks lie in close proximity to splice-site motifs at frequencies far above random expectation (Figs. 5j and S26), highlighting coordinated recruitment of these RBPs with core spliceosomal components. These results support the emerging view that many RBPs, beyond classical splicing factors, interface functionally with the spliceosome, offering perspectives on the regulatory complexity of co- and post-transcriptional gene expression.

BRIDGE deciphers and interprets regulatory pathogenic mutations that disrupt dynamic RNA–protein interactions across genomic contexts

RBPs are key post-transcriptional regulators increasingly implicated in human disease, with over 1000 RBPs potentially involved in pathogenic processes and more than 20% carrying disease-associated variants3. Although coding-region mutations in RBPs are well studied, changes within RNA targets, particularly at RBP binding sites, can significantly disrupt RNA metabolism and regulatory networks2. To address this, we used BRIDGE to predict and compare differences in RBP binding affinity between reference and mutant alleles to quantify the effect of sequence variation on RNA–protein interactions, defined as an “impact score” (Fig. 6a), which can be used to identify variants that may be functionally relevant to disease69.

We applied this framework to 553,803 single-nucleotide variants (SNVs)70 from the TCGA71, ClinVar72, and 1000 Genomes Project73 databases to explore whether certain RNA regions, particularly those involved in splicing, are disproportionately affected by disruptive mutations. As shown in Figs. 6b and S27a, BRIDGE assigned significantly higher impact scores to splicing-related SNVs compared with those in UTRs or coding regions, indicating that splicing signals may be more susceptible to sequence perturbations and carry stronger regulatory consequences. To evaluate BRIDGE’s ability to distinguish disease-relevant mutations, we compared its impact scores for pathogenic and benign variants annotated from the 1000 Genomes, ClinVar and TCGA databases. As shown in Figs. 6c and S27b, BRIDGE consistently assigned significantly higher scores to pathogenic variants compared to benign ones across all datasets: 2.8192 vs. 1.0922 (1000 Genomes), 2.7846 vs. 1.0182 (ClinVar), and 1.4491 vs. 1.1470 (TCGA), respectively. These findings support the ability of the model to rank candidate regulatory variants, especially in noncoding regions where mechanistic interpretation is often incomplete.

We also benchmarked BRIDGE with HDRNet to assess their relative ability to detect disease-related variants that alter RNA–protein binding affinity. By comparing predicted scores for variants that either increase or decrease RBP binding affinity (Figs. 6d and S27c), we found that BRIDGE significantly outperformed HDRNet in detecting pathogenic mutations (P < 0.001). To evaluate whether BRIDGE can identify disruptions in regulatory RNA elements, we investigated the elevation of impact scores in both noncoding and coding regions under negative selection pressure. In Figs. 6e and S27d, we categorize mutations by region (3′ UTR, 5′ UTR, splice donor and coding regions) and assess correlations with the PhyloP and PhastCons scores of 100 and 20 vertebrate species. Variants situated in strongly conserved regions consistently exhibit higher impact scores, indicating that even minor perturbations at these essential loci can severely affect RNA–protein binding. We further performed a disease- and tissue-aware analysis of variant-RBP combination events across ClinVar, TCGA, and the 1000 Genomes Project. This analysis indicates that both the counts and RBP composition of variant-RBP combinations can differ across disease contexts, while RBPs across multiple contexts suggest potential shared post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms (Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. S28).

After that, to provide mechanistic insights into how mutations affect RNA-protein interactions, we performed in-depth analysis of specific variants with high impact scores. For each variant, we extracted 100 nt flanking sequences and compared predicted RBP binding probabilities between wild-type and mutant alleles. Variants causing substantial changes in binding score (>0.3) were further investigated using attribution maps to determine their local contribution to binding events. We observed that mutations can influence RBP binding in two primary ways: by disrupting existing binding sites or creating new potential targets, thereby altering RNA–protein interactions either positively or negatively. As illustrated in Fig. 6f (top), we examined a variant in the intronic region of an RNA transcript in K562 cells, where it disrupts the binding site of the RBP AQR. This disruption, in turn, has been associated with familial hypercholesterolemia, highlighting how mutations affecting RBP binding can contribute to disease. Similarly, in HepG2 cells, we analyzed a C-to-G variant (negative strand) in the FANCA gene that lies within both the 3′ UTR and an intronic region and is associated with an increased risk of Fanconi anemia74. Attribution maps for the wild-type and variant sequences (Fig. 6f, bottom) reveal that the variant rs556465 disrupts the canonical PRPF8 binding motif, AGGUAAG39, reducing the predicted binding probability from 0.998 to 0.

We next performed a systematic computational perturbation analysis to examine how sequence variants within or beyond the PRPF8 binding motif affect RNA-protein interactions. Specifically, we analyzed each nucleotide within a 50-nt window around rs556465, yielding 150 variant impact scores that reflect local disruptions (Fig. 6g). Variants residing outside the PRPF8 motif exhibited consistently low impact scores, indicating minimal effects on predicted binding signals. In contrast, those located within the motif displayed substantially higher scores, underscoring their crucial role in destabilizing RNA-protein interactions. Altogether, these results highlight that mutations targeting core regulatory elements are significantly more likely to compromise RBP binding, reinforcing their importance in post-transcriptional gene regulation.

We then focused on RNA structure-disruptive variants (RiboSNitches), defined as special SNV or SNP sites exhibiting allele-specific RNA structural differences75. We systematically identified such variants across 15 distinct cell line pairs and performed intersection analysis with disease-associated variants from the ClinVar database, aiming to explore potential associations between structure-disruptive variant-induced RBP binding alterations and human diseases (Supplementary Note 7). Compared to variants that do not alter RNA structure, structure-disruptive variants were more likely to induce significant changes in binding strength at dynamic RBP–RNA interactions (Figs. 6h and S29a). To further elucidate the functional significance of RBP binding alterations in disease contexts, we quantified the cumulative frequency of disease types associated with significant changes in RBP binding strength (impact score > 0.5) across all cell lines and identified the top 11 disease categories. The results revealed that structure-disruptive variants linked to different diseases exhibit directional effects on RBP binding capacity (Fig. 6i). For instance, variants associated with acute intermittent porphyria tend to enhance RBP binding signals, whereas mutations related to cancer-type diseases, such as pheochromocytoma (PC), are more likely to impair RBP binding, potentially disrupting post-transcriptional regulation76.

To elucidate the regulatory relationship between RNA structure-disruptive variants and disease, we selected an SNP within the KMT2A gene that contributes to Parkinson’s disease for validation. RNA structural profiling data revealed distinct conformations between the T and C alleles in both HepG2 and K562 cell lines (Figs. 6k and S29b, c). We further employed BRIDGE to scan the binding probabilities of each RBP at this locus. Notably, in the context of the T allele, AUH showed minimal binding affinity to the RNA transcripts in both cells (Fig. 6j; binding probabilities: 0.045 in HepG2, 0.244 in K562). In contrast, for the C allele, AUH exhibited markedly higher binding affinity to the RNA in K562 than HepG2 (binding probabilities: 0.071 in HepG2, 0.746 in K562).

To assess whether the RBP–RNA binding events identified by BRIDGE reflect genuine physical interactions, we further performed molecular docking analysis. We selected the crystal structure of the AUH protein (PDB ID: 1HZD), and the secondary structure of the target RNA was constrained using icSHAPE data analyzed with RNAProbe77. The RNA tertiary structure was subsequently modelled using SimRNA by selecting the lowest-energy conformation from multiple simulated trajectories78. Docking was conducted using the HDOCK79, which employs a fast Fourier transform-based global search algorithm to identify shape-complementary binding modes80, followed by root-mean-square deviation-based clustering and AMBER molecular dynamics minimization to optimize docking conformations81. As shown in Fig. 6l, no stable complexes were formed with the T allele in either cell line, while successful docking was achieved exclusively for the C allele in K562, yielding an optimized complex with a docking score of −321.22 after energy minimization. The final complex structure displays the spatial distribution of heavy atoms within 3 Å of RNA, where sphere radii (0–1.5) are proportional to atom-RNA distances. In summary, these analyses are consistent with a model in which RiboSNitches can dynamically modulate RBP binding, potentially affecting downstream post-transcriptional regulation and warranting further investigation of their relevance to disease.

BRIDGE maps the genome-wide landscape of dynamic RNA-binding-protein target-site dysregulation and its association with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis risk

Despite considerable research into the functional consequences of changes in RBP binding, substantial gaps remain in mapping these regulatory disruptions directly to specific disease phenotypes. By systematically investigating how RBP binding is perturbed, it may be possible to precisely link the phenotypic impact of individual RBPs to their respective genetic variants. Leveraging BRIDGE’s robust genome-wide capability to detect disruptions in RBP regulation, we first examined whether the binding sites affected by RBP dysregulation display signatures of selective pressure. Drawing on our earlier work, demonstrating a strong correlation between cross-species evolutionary constraints and RBP regulation, we next explored whether such constraints could also be observed at the human population level by analyzing minor allele frequency (MAF). Previous studies have noted that purifying selection often manifests in allele frequencies, in part because these variants regulate gene expression82. To this end, we used BRIDGE to perform an unbiased in silico perturbation of RBP binding in a wide set of phenotype-linked variants selected in the GWAS catalog. Aggregating binding alterations for noncoding SNPs in RBP interaction sites, we assessed 10,458,782 variants and evaluated their potential effects on RBP binding. Unexpectedly, we found no notable differences between common variants (MAF > 5%) and rare variants (MAF < 0.1%), nor any clear functional stratification among major pre-mRNA regions (Supplementary Fig. S30f). These results suggest that the biological outcomes of RBP binding perturbations may be highly context-dependent, and thus neither variant frequency nor simple annotation-based classification is sufficient to reliably predict functional consequences.

Given the established role of RBPs in evolutionary selection and adaptation, we next probed their contribution to the genetic architecture of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). We performed a systematic evaluation of the functional localization of RBP binding alterations in ALS risk loci and assessed heterogeneity across cell lines by computing z-scores for RBP binding changes in GWAS variants (Fig. 7a). Out of 171,233 analyzed loci, 52.4% (89,726) showed conserved RBP binding changes in at least two cell types, whereas 47.6% (81,507) exhibited cell type-specific regulatory dynamics (Fig. 7c). We then identified the top five genes in each cell line most enriched for dynamic RBP binding changes, identifying several enriched genes with prior links to ALS or neurodegeneration (Figs. 7d and S30d). Further z-score analyses revealed significant variability in the proportion of SNP-induced RBP binding dynamics across different genes and cell types (Fig. 7b). Notably, both HeLa and HEK293T cell lines displayed lower proportions of dynamic variants than the median across all cell types, implying their limited suitability for inferring RBP-mediated disruptions in other contexts.

Earlier studies have shown that molecular quantitative trait loci (QTLs) tend to cluster in disease-associated genomic regions83,84. To explore whether RBP dysregulation similarly contributes to complex-trait genetics, we estimated the effect sizes of individual RBPs on ALS risk variants and jointly analyzed them with molecular QTL data from GTEx v1085. Our distribution analysis of significant expression QTLs (eQTLs) across different cell lines (Fig. 7f) revealed that K562 harbored the largest number of significant eQTLs (n = 1083) with higher statistical significance relative to other cell types, likely reflecting its complex and active transcriptional regulatory landscape. Notably, the estimated RBP-mediated ALS effects showed low correlation with conventional QTL annotations (∣r∣ ≤ 0.1; Figs. 7g and S30a), indicating that RBP-related signals may capture complementary information to classical QTL-based analyses.

A cross-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis uncovered 15 genome-wide significant ALS risk loci, including COG3 as a potential susceptibility gene86 (Supplementary Fig. S30b, c). Focusing on the strongly ALS-associated region in European populations, Fig. 7e highlights the signal strength on chromosome 13 and the linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure across SNPs. By harnessing BRIDGE’s genome-wide capacity for RBP regulatory mapping, we prioritized an intronic SNP (rs2985994) in COG3 that is predicted to enhance TAF15 binding and alter local RNA structure (Figs. 7h, i, and S30e), consistent with TAF15’s reported link to ALS87. Further analysis (Fig. 7j) indicated that TAF15 and its target COG3 are both highly expressed in HepG2 (>15 TPM) but barely expressed in K562 (<1 TPM), revealing a dynamic regulatory paradigm. Moreover, shRNA-mediated TAF15 knockdown substantially decreased the primary COG3 transcript level in both cell lines, with a stronger effect observed in HepG2 (ΔTPM = 14.62) compared to K562 (ΔTPM = 0.61; Fig. 7l, m). Collectively, these results suggest a TAF15-mediated regulation of COG3 that may represent a context-dependent regulatory mechanism relevant to ALS-associated signals.

To further characterize the transcriptional consequences of TAF15 knockdown in HepG2 and K562, we performed disease ontology (DO) and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses on differentially expressed genes before versus after knockdown. As illustrated in Figs. 7n, S30g, and Supplementary Note 8, a community detection algorithm identified multiple functionally coherent modules in each cell line, pointing to distinct disease-associated gene regulatory profiles. In liver-derived HepG2, module M6 was significantly enriched for lysosomal storage disorder terms (Q = 0.0002), suggesting a potential lysosomal dysfunction route to ALS pathogenesis88, whereas in blood-derived K562, modules M3 and M4 were enriched for broader neurodegeneration terms (Q < 0.0005). This suggests that ALS-associated regulatory signals may involve multiple tissue contexts. GO enrichment further revealed distinct pathways with cell type-specific regulation (Fig. 7k). In HepG2, differentially expressed genes centered on neuronal apoptosis (GO:1903377, GO:1903376, GO:0043523, and GO:0036480)89 and DNA damage responses (GO:0006302, GO:0006303)90, whereas in K562, genes predominantly aligned with protein homeostasis (GO:0050821, GO:0031647)91 and mitochondrial function (GO:0006626, GO:0007006)92. Together, these transcriptomic and pathway enrichments suggest tissue-dependent pathway differences relevant to ALS-associated loci.

Integrating BRIDGE’s genome-wide RBP binding map with functional perturbation assays, we provide evidence consistent with a route by which noncoding variants may modulate RBP-centered regulation in a context-dependent manner, with potential implications for disease-associated genetic risk. These results further suggest that RBP-related regulatory effects can be sensitive to cellular environment, and that allele frequency and conventional QTL annotations may not fully capture the complexity of these context-dependent dynamics on their own.

BRIDGE provides a multi-functional and user-friendly web portal for analyzing dynamic RNA–RBP interactions

In recent years, deep learning-based tools for RBP interaction site prediction have proliferated, largely aiming to reduce experimental complexity in wet-lab settings. Nonetheless, three principal limitations remain: first, most methods depend on sequence-derived RNA structures, overlooking the dynamic intracellular environments critical for cell type-specific RBP–RNA interactions; second, current frameworks generally lack the capacity to mechanistically link dynamic RBP dysregulation with genetic variants, thereby limiting their utility in variant interpretation and disease studies; third, many existing models require advanced programming skills and complex software setups, limiting their adoption by non-expert researchers. To address these issues, we developed the BRIDGE webserver, a code-free interactive interface (Fig. 8) that reduces the computational barrier for biologists while integrating a comprehensive pipeline for inferring disease-causing dynamic RBP binding events.

Discussion

Advancing from merely cataloging genetic risk loci to truly understanding disease mechanisms requires examining the earliest post-transcriptional layer of gene regulation. This is the stage where RBPs engage nascent transcripts and exert pervasive control over each RNA’s fate. However, no existing approach has provided a genome-wide, dynamic framework that bridges sequence-structure motifs with genetic variants to pinpoint pathogenic sites of molecular dysfunction. We present BRIDGE as a fully end-to-end model that fills this gap by unifying sequence and structural motif recognition with the functional interpretation of noncoding variants. This framework enables transcriptome-wide profiling of dynamic RNA–protein interactions under both normal and variant-perturbed conditions. BRIDGE’s architecture integrates multiple data modalities into a single predictive model. It employs a dynamic cross-modal encoder that fuses nucleotide sequence identity, icSHAPE-derived structural reactivity profiles, known motif priors, and other biochemical descriptors into a unified latent representation. This latent space is designed to capture dependencies in RBP binding that are sensitive to sequence or structure changes introduced by genetic variants. Building on this foundation, BRIDGE uses a KA-inspired multi-scale architecture: it decomposes the fused embeddings into compact low-rank components and then refines them through dilated adaptive convolutions. This multi-scale design iteratively couples local motif cues with global transcript context, enabling the model to capture hierarchical sequence-structure determinants of RBP binding. As a result, BRIDGE can directly link the perturbation of these determinants by genetic variants to potential functional outcomes.

We evaluated BRIDGE on 261 RBP binding datasets across six human cell lines. The model reconstructs transcriptome-wide RBP binding landscapes with performance exceeding all current state-of-the-art methods. When analyzing performance by RBP domain and function, we found that proteins containing classical RRMs and those mediating translation at 5′ or 3′ UTRs were intrinsically easier for BRIDGE to predict. These RBPs bind well-defined, single-stranded RNA motifs, which likely explains their high predictability. In contrast, RBPs with dsRBDs or helicase domains, as well as splicing factors that recognize intronic splice sites, showed lower predictive scores. Notably, BRIDGE generalizes robustly to new cellular contexts. Without any retraining, the network was able to predict RBP binding in unseen cell types, implying that it has internalized a flexible RNA recognition grammar that transcends cell-specific conditions. Moreover, the model’s attention mechanisms allow us to mine sequence-structure motifs that are important for RBP binding. Using an attention-based motif mining approach, we identified 3571 distinct dynamic sequence-structure motifs across the transcriptome. Approximately 40% of RBPs—including some not traditionally known for sequence-specific binding (e.g., TIA1 and DDX6)—showed a pronounced preference for AG- or GU-rich elements near splice sites, highlighting a pervasive link between RBP binding and downstream post-transcriptional control.

We next leveraged its dynamic predictive power to study the impact of genetic variation on RBP–RNA interactions. We scored over 550,000 noncoding variants from databases, such as 1000 Genomes, ClinVar, and TCGA, directly assessing how each allele change might disrupt sequence-structure motifs and alter RBP binding. To evaluate the evolutionary significance of the predicted variant effects, we compared BRIDGE’s variant impact scores with measures of evolutionary conservation and constraint. Variants located in highly conserved regions (indicated by high PhyloP and PhastCons scores) showed markedly higher predicted disruptions to RBP binding. This trend is consistent with the expectation that mutations in evolutionary conserved RNA elements are likely to have functional consequences. By contrast, the predicted impact scores did not vary systematically with MAF in human populations, suggesting that even common genetic variants can substantially perturb RBP–RNA interactions. Crucially, our analysis revealed that variants in splice-region contexts disproportionately affect RBP binding, suggesting potential relevance to disease. In a genome-wide in silico perturbation screen of 10.5 million variants associated with human traits (e.g., GWAS variants), BRIDGE predicted cell-line-specific RBP–RNA interaction perturbations at nearly half of known ALS-associated loci. These regulatory disturbances may not be detected by conventional eQTL analyses, highlighting that BRIDGE may suggest regulatory mechanisms of disease. Notably, the model predicted that an intronic SNP (rs2985994) in the COG3 gene may disrupt RNA structure, potentially increasing TAF15 binding, consistent with reduced COG3 expression in liver cells. This tissue-restricted RBP binding event was validated and suggests a possible regulatory mechanism linking the noncoding variant to ALS-relevant biology in a context not captured by typical expression quantitative trait locus studies.

Although BRIDGE advances context-aware prediction of RBP binding and variant-associated binding changes, several limitations should be noted. First, while BRIDGE is designed to be efficient under typical CLIP-scale settings and can support large inference via chunked/batched execution, substantially larger workloads may still introduce practical bottlenecks (e.g., memory footprint, IO, and wall-clock time) and may require additional engineering optimizations, such as distributed execution. Second, BRIDGE primarily learns binding determinants from sequence and local structural context within the modeled input and therefore may not fully capture higher-order RNA tertiary (3D) folding effects (e.g., multi-helix junction packing or stabilized conformational states) that can reshape RBP recognition. Third, our current training and evaluation primarily focus on human datasets across multiple cell lines; extending BRIDGE to broader tissue contexts and to multi-species settings would likely require additional high-quality CLIP datasets. Finally, BRIDGE does not explicitly model co-/post-transcriptional processes, such as isoform-resolved alternative splicing and RNA decay, which may influence effective binding context and transcript availability over time.

In the present study, BRIDGE is intended primarily as a computational framework that links context-dependent binding logic to interpretable sequence-structure motifs and predicted variant effects. Accordingly, biological support in this work is primarily scoped to three layers of evidence: (i) extensive computational validation, including held-out benchmarking across diverse RBPs and cell lines, robustness and ablation analyses, and motif-level interpretability via attribution and motif recovery; (ii) indirect biological support through genome-wide analyses, such as enrichment and constraint/disease-relevance evaluations that help contextualize predicted binding changes at scale; and (iii) focused experimental support in a representative case study (COG3/rs2985994), providing evidence consistent with a specific variant-linked TAF15 binding event and its regulatory consequence. Future experimental work that could be particularly informative includes allele-aware binding measurements (e.g., allele-specific CLIP or allele-aware pulldown strategies), reporter assays to validate predicted RBP regulatory sites, and genome editing-based perturbations in the relevant cellular context coupled with binding and expression readouts.

In summary, BRIDGE offers a genome-wide, context-aware framework to connect RNA sequence-structure motifs with dynamic RBP binding and the predicted functional impact of noncoding genetic variants across cellular contexts. By integrating prediction, interpretable motif discovery, and large-scale variant-effect simulation within a single modeling framework, BRIDGE can facilitate systematic interrogation of context-dependent RBP binding programs and can help nominate mechanistic, testable hypotheses for follow-up in the relevant cellular setting. We expect that BRIDGE may be broadly useful as a practical guide for linking dynamic RNA–protein interaction logic to genetic variation and disease-relevant mechanisms, while continued expansion of experimental resources and targeted validations could further refine and extend its biological conclusions.

Methods

Data processing

Predicting RBP binding sites across different cell lines requires a high-quality, consistently processed dataset that allows direct comparisons of cross-conditions. Building on established pipelines from HDRNet20 and PrismNet21, we aggregated RBP binding sites from the ENCODE project (eCLIP)31 and the POSTAR database (HITS-CLIP)32 to create 261 RBP datasets that span six cell lines (172 unique human proteins in total). To minimize cross-laboratory inconsistencies, we avoided using data from the original publications, which often applied heterogeneous preprocessing or parameter choices. Instead, we uniformly processed all eCLIP and HITS-CLIP data to ensure comparability.

Each CLIP peak was designated as a binding site. Because previous large-scale benchmarks showed that extracting a fixed 101-nt window minimizing background noise, we standardized each site to 101 nt centered on the peak. For peaks shorter than 101 nt, we symmetrically extended them from the center; for peaks longer than 101 nt, we truncated inward from both ends. To capture high-confidence RBP binding signals, we retained the top 5000 binding sites (ranked by eCLIP or HITS-CLIP peak intensity) as positive samples for each RBP. Because real biological data contain substantially more non-binding than binding sites, we drew 10,000 random 101-nt regions (nonoverlapping with known peaks) per dataset to form the negative set. Each dataset was then randomly split into 80% for model training and 20% for held-out validation/testing. Notably, instances of the same RBP in different cell lines were treated as separate tasks to capture cell-line-specific binding signals. All curated datasets are available at https://bridge.aibio-lab.com/ to foster reproducibility and further exploration.

Dynamic cross-modality representations: bridging sequence, structure, and context

We developed dynamic cross-modality representations that integrate diverse biochemical, structural, and sequence-derived features of RNA, ensuring context-aware and intrinsically variant-aware modeling of RBP–RNA interactions. Specifically, we combined icSHAPE-based structural scores, positionally enriched sequence-structure motifs, and a transformer-based graph semantic modeling approach to capture both local conformation and long-range dependencies.

Predicting dynamic RBP interactions across diverse cellular contexts and genetic-variant backgrounds requires converting raw RNA sequences into mathematically meaningful descriptors that capture local conformations, biochemical properties, and long-range dependencies. In other words, BRIDGE jointly considers local motif signals and global RNA structure context, allowing it to generalize across cellular contexts and remain sensitive to changes in the RNA (such as mutations) that may affect protein binding.

Sequence-structure motifs

Modeling RBPs across multiple cell lines requires capturing both sequence-driven motif patterns and local conformational cues, especially since RNA secondary and tertiary structures can vary substantially between cellular environments. Although traditional sequence motifs are known to influence the specificity of RBP binding93, relying solely on linear sequence fails to explain dynamic structural changes in diverse cell types. Incorporating secondary-structure motifs (e.g., stem-loops and hairpins, etc.) helps model how local conformations might shift in different cell lines and, in turn, modulate RBP accessibility94.

To complement the icSHAPE45 reactivity profiles, we used RNAfold95 with the -p option to generate consensus secondary structures at 37 °C for each RNA. Transformer-based graph semantic modeling of RNA sequence

Recent attention-based language modeling has reshaped NLP, yet genomic Transformers still face two primary obstacles: (1) coarse k-mer tokenization: most adaptations23,97 segment nucleotides into k-mers (k = 3, 4, 5, or 6), diluting single-base resolution critical for RBP–RNA binding specificity. (2) Long-range dependencies: Transformers have quadratic complexity with sequence length, while regulatory elements often span large genomic distances. Even Enformer97, which uses convolution layers for an expanded receptive field, trades off language model flexibility and can compromise single-base fidelity.

To address the k-mer limitation and preserve global contextual semantics at single-nucleotide granularity, we developed RBPformer, a graph-augmented language model fine-tuned on RNA sequences. Unlike prior approaches, RBPformer treats each nucleotide as an individual token, enhancing RBP binding site detection via finer contextual embeddings. By capturing hidden states and attention weights for every base, it highlights nuances in primary sequence composition, often decisive for functional motifs. The architecture of the multi-head attention of RBPformer is provided in Supplementary Note 11. To further tailor RBPformer for dynamic and variant-aware RBP binding prediction, we fine-tuned RBPformer on our curated RBP datasets for five epochs, applying early stopping to limit overfitting and computational cost. With an Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 10−5, the model converged reliably. We extracted final-layer embeddings and attention weights, discarding [CLS] and [SEP], resulting in a 101 × 512 embedding. By combining single-base resolution with global semantic context, RBPformer provides a robust foundation for capturing dynamic and variant-sensitive RBP–RNA interactions.

Even with single-nucleotide tokens, long-range or structurally distant regulatory elements can remain elusive due to quadratic self-attention scaling. The Enformer97 showed that expanding the model’s receptive field is crucial, but uses convolution layers that can undermine the language model capabilities and the single-base fidelity. Kolmogorov–Arnold multi-scale protein–RNA binding network

To capture key information in dynamic cross-modality representations and improve variant-aware protein–RNA interaction site identification, we propose the KA multi-scale RBP neural network, depicted in Fig. 1. This approach extends the parallelism of traditional deep neural networks by leveraging multiple output sizes to capture correlations among proteins and RNAs at varying depth scales. Additionally, an asynchronous pyramid mechanism is introduced to better extract long-distance feature dependencies from diverse embeddings (e.g., sequence motifs, structural cues). Together, these innovations enable the projection of high-dimensional inputs into multiple low-dimensional potential spaces, after which a deep pyramidal convolutional neural network yields accurate RBP binding and genetic variant predictions.

With this asynchronous updating and feature compression mechanism, ADPNet can efficiently capture complex dependencies while maintaining computational efficiency, ultimately enhancing the accuracy of variant-aware predicting binding site probabilities.

Training of BRIDGE

For each of the 261 RBP datasets covering 172 human proteins across six cell lines, we trained an independent variant-aware BRIDGE model to identify dynamic cellular RNA-binding events. RNA sequences served as the primary input, processed via both the sequence-structure motifs and the Transformer-based graph semantic modeling modules. During training, we randomly allocated 20% of the samples as an independent test set for performance evaluation, while the remaining 80% formed the training set. The hyperparameters of BRIDGE were optimized through an iterative exploration of the parameter space using the training dataset. The architectural details, including the parameters for dynamic cross-modality representations and the KA multi-scale protein–RNA binding network module, are provided in Supplementary Note 12. Remaining parameters were set as follows: BRIDGE is implemented in PyTorch 2.0.1, with Kaiming initialization99 to enhance convergence. To mitigate overfitting, we employ an end-to-end training scheme with the Adam optimizer, with a batch size of 32, an initial learning rate of 0.001, and a weight decay of 1e-6. A 40-epoch linear warm-up stabilizes early optimization, after which an adaptive scheduler triggers exponential learning rate decay if validation loss does not improve for five consecutive epochs. All experiments run in parallel on four NVIDIA A40 GPUs (48 GB RAM each), ensuring efficient resource use for the computationally intensive tasks.

Identification of enriched motifs

To capture sequence-structure motifs relevant to RBP binding, we employ the STREME algorithm for the analysis of the PFM. Specifically, we ran STREME with -pvt 0.01, -minw 4, -maxw 6, and -alph <X>, where <X> is the file path containing the letter definitions for our probe annotations. The -pvt 0.01 threshold restricts motifs to those with P-values below 0.01, ensuring statistical significance, whereas -minw 4 and -maxw 6 limit the motif length to a range of four to six bases, capturing both shorter partial motifs and standard-sized motifs. This approach effectively detects enriched binding motifs in their structural context, offering valuable insights into potential RBP-binding preferences.

BRIDGE server construction

BRIDGE runs on an Ubuntu 24.04 LTS system featuring 2×AMD EPYC 7B12 3.80 GHz CPUs, 128 GB RAM, and 1×NVIDIA L40 48 GB GPU, ensuring ample computational resources for both CPU-intensive bioinformatics pipelines and GPU-accelerated prediction tasks. The frontend utilizes jQuery for dynamic DOM manipulation and event handling, with Apache ECharts generating interactive visualizations and real-time data updates. On the backend, Django orchestrates business logic and stores structured data in a MySQL database, guaranteeing ACID-compliant persistence; all frontend-backend communications proceed through RESTful APIs.

To optimize resource usage, workloads are dispatched asynchronously via Redis-based job queues, while NGINX acts as a reverse proxy, managing SSL termination and mitigating latency under heavy loads. Finally, cross-browser compatibility ensures stable operation in Google Chrome, Mozilla Firefox, Microsoft Edge, and Safari, providing a seamless experience for diverse user environments.
