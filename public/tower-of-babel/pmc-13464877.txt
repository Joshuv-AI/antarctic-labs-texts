Spectrophotometric determination of cefepime and enmetazobactam in pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma using chemometric modeling, metaheuristic wavelength selection, and comprehensive sustainability assessment
Authors: Ahmed Farghaly, Lateefa A. Al-Khateeb, Belal Muneeb Kanaan, Galal Magdy, Moayad M. Khashoqji, Mahmoud A. Tantawy, Ahmed Emad F. Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recently introduced cefepime–enmetazobactam (CEF–ENM) combination requires reliable analytical methods for pharmaceutical quality control and proof-of-concept bioanalytical evaluation. Existing methods are predominantly chromatographic and rely on large volumes of organic solvents, costly instrumentation, and high energy consumption. In this work, a UV spectrophotometric approach integrated with chemometric analysis was established for the simultaneous quantification of CEF and ENM in pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. Calibration samples were prepared according to the Brereton multilevel experimental design, whereas an independent external validation set was constructed using the Maximin Distance Design (MMD) algorithm. The analytical performance of six chemometric models (CLS, PCR, PLS, GA-PLS, FA-PLS, and MCR-ALS) was evaluated to resolve the severe spectral overlap between both analytes. Matrix-specific calibration models were developed for processed plasma samples. The developed models showed excellent analytical performance, with MCR-ALS providing the highest predictive accuracy and robustness. Sustainability was comparatively evaluated using the integrated Multi-color Assessment (MA) Tool together with complementary greenness metrics. The proposed method demonstrated superior overall sustainability compared with previously reported chromatographic methods while maintaining reliable quantitative performance. The proposed approach offers a rapid, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable alternative for routine pharmaceutical quality control and proof-of-concept evaluation in processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. Introduction

The increasing demand for sustainable practices in pharmaceutical sciences has extended beyond manufacturing to include analytical workflows, where method development is now expected to balance analytical quality with environmental responsibility [1–5]. Analytical methodologies are indispensable across the pharmaceutical chain, from drug discovery and formulation development to quality assurance and therapeutic monitoring. Despite their importance, many conventional analytical procedures remain dependent on high solvent volumes, sophisticated instrumentation, and energy-intensive operations, all of which contribute to waste generation and an increased environmental footprint. As a result, Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC) has progressively shifted from a narrow focus on waste minimization toward a broader sustainability-centered philosophy that incorporates environmental safety, operational efficiency, economic feasibility, and social accessibility [6, 7].

Among contemporary antibacterial therapies, β-lactam antibiotics continue to represent one of the most effective strategies against serious bacterial infections. However, the global rise of antimicrobial resistance has accelerated the development of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations as a means of restoring antibacterial efficacy [8, 9]. The recently approved cefepime (CEF)–enmetazobactam (ENM) combination has emerged as an important therapeutic advancement, particularly against multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing strains [10–12]. This combination constitutes the first clinically approved cefepime-based β-lactamase inhibitor system, which highlights the growing need for reliable analytical methodologies capable of monitoring both components simultaneously in pharmaceutical products and biological samples.

To date, analytical investigations of the CEF–ENM combination remain scarce and are largely restricted to chromatographic techniques, including LC–MS/MS for plasma analysis [13] and RP-HPLC method for pharmaceutical dosage forms [14]. Although these approaches provide high sensitivity and selectivity, their dependence on organic solvents, costly instrumentation, and extended analytical runtime reduces their sustainability profile and limits routine applicability in laboratories with restricted resources.

Within sustainable analytical development, isolated improvements in individual greenness criteria do not necessarily guarantee overall sustainability enhancement. In some cases, apparent environmental gains may introduce hidden penalties such as increased operational complexity, resource demand, or lifecycle burden, a phenomenon commonly described as the rebound effect [15–17]. This challenge has emphasized the importance of integrated benchmarking strategies in which newly developed methods are evaluated against existing alternatives using harmonized sustainability assessment tools [18–20]. Such comparative frameworks provide a more realistic assessment of whether a method achieves genuine sustainability advancement rather than superficial optimization.

Chemometric-assisted UV spectrophotometry represents an effective approach for the simultaneous analysis of multicomponent pharmaceutical mixtures exhibiting extensive spectral overlap, where conventional univariate methods are inadequate [21–23]. Compared with chromatographic techniques, it offers lower solvent consumption, simpler instrumentation, reduced cost, and faster analysis. Recent advances in experimental design have further improved multivariate calibration by enabling statistically efficient construction of calibration and validation sets [24, 25]. Among these approaches, the Maximin Distance Design (MMD) generates representative validation samples with broad coverage of the concentration space, improving the reliability of external model evaluation while avoiding redundant experiments [26, 27]. Matrix-specific calibration has also become increasingly important for accurate chemometric analysis of complex pharmaceutical and biological matrices [28].

Based on these considerations, this study developed a sustainable UV spectrophotometric method to simultaneously quantify CEF and ENM in pharmaceutical dosage forms and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. A water–ethanol (1:1, v/v) solvent system was used throughout the analysis, and calibration mixtures were constructed using the Brereton multilevel design with independent MMD-based validation. Six chemometric models (CLS, PCR, PLS, GA-PLS, FA-PLS, and MCR-ALS) were comparatively evaluated to resolve the extensive spectral overlap between the two analytes. The method was validated for both pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma using independently developed calibration models and was comparatively assessed against previously reported methods using the integrated MA Tool. The proposed approach combines reliable quantitative performance with improved sustainability for routine pharmaceutical analysis and proof-of-concept evaluation in processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma.

Experimental

Instrumentation and computational environment

Spectral measurements were obtained with a Shimadzu UV-1800 double-beam UV–visible spectrophotometer (Kyoto, Japan) using matched quartz cuvettes (1 cm path length). Instrument operation and spectral acquisition were controlled through UV-Probe software (version 2.62), employing a spectral bandwidth of 1.0 nm. Sample weighing was performed on an AUX-220 analytical balance (Shimadzu, Japan), while dissolution was facilitated with a USC300TH ultrasonic bath (Julabo, Germany). Data processing and chemometric calculations were completed in MATLAB R2023a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Multivariate regression models (CLS, PCR, and PLS) were generated using PLS_Toolbox v9.1 (Eigenvector Research, USA), whereas bilinear curve resolution was accomplished with MCR-ALS Toolbox v2.1 (Universitat de Barcelona, Spain). Variable selection by the Genetic Algorithm (GA-PLS) and Firefly Algorithm (FA-PLS), together with the (MMD) for validation set generation, was implemented using in-house MATLAB scripts.

Materials and chemicals

Reference standards

The CEF analytical reference standard used throughout this study was obtained from the National Organization for Drug Control and Research (NODCAR, Cairo, Egypt). (ENM) reference standard (Fig. S1) was obtained from Jiangxi Fushine Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Jiangxi, China). The certified purities were 99.74 ± 0.90% for CEF and 99.60 ± 0.80% for ENM. CEF purity was verified according to the official pharmacopeial procedure, whereas ENM purity was confirmed using a previously reported validated method.

Pharmaceutical formulation

Exblifep® powder for concentrate for solution for infusion (Advanz Pharma Ltd., Dublin, Ireland; Marketing Authorization No. EU/1/24/1794/001) was purchased from a local pharmacy. Each vial is labeled to contain 2.0 g CEF and 0.5 g ENM.

Solvents and reagents

Ultrapure water was produced using a Milli-Q IQ 7000 purification system (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Absolute ethanol (≥ 99.8%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). A water–ethanol (1:1, v/v) mixture was used as the working solvent throughout the study.

Biological matrix

Drug-free human plasma was obtained from the Egyptian Holding Company for Biological Products and Vaccines (VACSERA, Giza, Egypt). Plasma samples from healthy donors were collected in accordance with the approved institutional ethical requirements and stored at −80 °C until analysis. All experiments involving human plasma were performed using processed post-extraction-fortified plasma samples prepared as described in Sect. 2.10.2.

Preparation of Standard Solutions

Separate stock solutions of CEF and ENM (1.0 mg mL⁻¹) were prepared by dissolving accurately weighed 50.0 mg portions of each reference standard in 50.0 mL volumetric flasks using a water–ethanol (1:1, v/v) mixture. Dissolution was assisted by ultrasonication for 20 min. Working solutions were prepared by serial dilution with the same solvent system through intermediate solutions (100 µg mL⁻¹). These solutions were used to prepare all calibration and validation mixtures. All volumetric measurements were performed using Class A glassware.

Spectral measurements and definition of the analytical window

Absorption spectra of CEF and ENM were recorded over 200–400 nm using the optimized instrumental conditions described above, with the water–ethanol (1:1, v/v) mixture as the reference blank (Fig. 1). The spectra exhibited extensive overlap throughout the UV region, precluding direct univariate determination. Therefore, the wavelength range used for chemometric analysis was restricted to 210–350 nm, where stable absorbance signals and favorable signal-to-noise characteristics were obtained. This interval provided 141 wavelength variables for multivariate model development. Calibration mixtures covered concentration ranges of 1.0–16.0 µg mL⁻¹ for CEF and 2.0–10.0 µg mL⁻¹ for ENM. These ranges were used for both calibration and external validation, and all spectra were recorded under identical experimental conditions.

Calibration and validation design

Separate calibration and validation schemes were developed for pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma, because the two matrices exhibit different spectral characteristics. Consequently, independent calibration and validation datasets were prepared for each matrix to ensure reliable model development and unbiased performance evaluation. This strategy ensured that model optimization and performance evaluation were conducted within the corresponding sample matrix while avoiding calibration transfer between pharmaceutical and biological systems.

The calibration mixtures were constructed using the Brereton multilevel design, which provides a balanced distribution of calibration samples throughout the multidimensional concentration space. Twenty-five binary mixtures were prepared by combining five concentration levels of each analyte according to the coded levels (−2, − 1, 0, + 1, +2). The investigated concentration ranges were selected according to the established analytical working intervals of 1.0–16.0 µg mL⁻¹ for CEF and 2.0–10.0 µg mL⁻¹ for ENM. This experimental arrangement provided uniform coverage of the calibration space while minimizing extrapolation during concentration prediction.

External model validation was performed using an independent validation dataset generated by the Maximin Distance Design (MMD) algorithm. Chemometric data processing and model development

Prior to chemometric analysis, all spectral datasets were imported into MATLAB for multivariate processing. Only the analytical wavelength interval defined in Sect. 2.4 was considered during model construction. Spectral matrices were subjected to baseline correction to compensate for minor instrumental fluctuations, followed by mean-centering before calibration. Variable scaling was introduced only when required by the corresponding optimization algorithm to ensure balanced contributions of all wavelength variables during model computation. No derivative transformation or spectral smoothing was applied because the acquired spectra exhibited stable baselines and satisfactory signal-to-noise characteristics throughout the investigated concentration range.

Separate chemometric models were constructed for the pharmaceutical and processed post-extraction-fortified plasma datasets, each using its own calibration set. The external validation samples were reserved exclusively for independent model evaluation and were not used during model development, model optimization, wavelength selection, latent-variable selection, or parameter tuning. Instead, they were used solely for independent assessment of predictive performance. Six complementary multivariate approaches representing direct regression, latent-variable modelling, evolutionary wavelength optimization, and bilinear curve resolution were investigated, namely CLS, PCR, PLS, GA-PLS, FA-PLS, and MCR-ALS.

Classical least squares (CLS)

CLS calibration was established according to the Beer–Lambert additive model, in which the absorbance matrix of each mixture was expressed as a linear combination of the spectra of the individual components weighted by their corresponding concentrations. Concentration estimates were obtained through least-squares minimization of the residual error between the experimental spectral matrix and its reconstructed counterpart.

Principal component regression (PCR)

PCR models were generated by decomposing the mean-centered spectral matrix into orthogonal principal components using singular value decomposition. Regression analysis was subsequently performed between the selected principal-component scores and the corresponding analyte concentrations. Candidate models containing increasing numbers of latent variables were evaluated by random five-fold cross-validation. The optimum model complexity was selected according to the minimum root mean square error of cross-validation (RMSECV), thereby minimizing prediction error while preventing overfitting.

Partial least squares (PLS)

PLS regression was employed to establish the relationship between spectral variables and analyte concentrations through simultaneous decomposition of the predictor and response matrices. Latent variables were sequentially introduced into the model and evaluated using random five-fold cross-validation. The final number of latent variables corresponded to the minimum RMSECV while maintaining the simplest statistically adequate model.

Genetic algorithm–partial least squares (GA-PLS)

GA-PLS was applied as an evolutionary wavelength-selection strategy prior to PLS calibration. Each candidate wavelength subset was represented as a binary vector, with individual elements indicating whether a specific wavelength was selected or omitted during the optimization process. Population evolution proceeded through iterative cycles of selection, crossover, and mutation, whereas model fitness was assessed from the prediction error obtained by five-fold random cross-validation. Optimization continued until the predefined convergence criterion was satisfied, after which the optimum wavelength subset was employed to construct the final PLS calibration model. The genetic algorithm parameters, including population size, mutation rate, crossover strategy, convergence threshold, and cross-validation settings, were predetermined before optimization and maintained constant throughout model development.

Firefly algorithm–partial least squares (FA-PLS)

FA-PLS was implemented as an alternative metaheuristic wavelength-selection procedure. Each firefly represented a candidate subset of spectral variables, while the quality of each solution was evaluated according to the prediction error of the corresponding PLS model obtained by five-fold random cross-validation. Firefly movement was controlled by the attractiveness function together with the predefined randomization and absorption coefficients. The optimization process continued iteratively until the stopping criterion was achieved, after which the retained wavelength subset was used for construction of the final regression model. Algorithmic parameters were fixed before optimization to ensure consistent comparison with the remaining chemometric approaches.

Multivariate curve resolution–alternating least squares (MCR-ALS)

MCR-ALS modelling was performed by decomposing the spectral data matrix into concentration and pure spectral profile matrices according to the bilinear model. Initial concentration estimates were generated using Evolving Factor Analysis (EFA), followed by alternating least-squares optimization under non-negativity constraints imposed simultaneously on concentration and spectral profiles. The optimization proceeded until the predefined convergence criterion was satisfied, defined as stabilization of the residual matrix below the predefined convergence tolerance or attainment of the maximum permitted number of optimization cycles. The resolved concentration profiles obtained after convergence were subsequently employed for quantitative prediction.

Chemometric workflow and model selection

All chemometric models were developed using identical calibration datasets and subsequently evaluated using the independent validation mixtures generated through the experimental design. External validation samples were not included during model calibration, wavelength optimization, latent-variable selection, or parameter tuning. Model optimization was performed independently for each analytical matrix to avoid transfer of calibration information between pharmaceutical and plasma datasets.

The investigated chemometric strategies were selected to represent complementary analytical philosophies for resolving highly overlapping spectral signals. CLS provided direct linear regression based on Beer–Lambert additivity, whereas PCR and PLS represented latent-variable methods designed to reduce spectral collinearity. GA-PLS and FA-PLS incorporated metaheuristic optimization to identify the most informative wavelength variables before regression modelling, while MCR-ALS provided bilinear decomposition capable of resolving concentration and spectral profile matrices simultaneously. Application of these complementary modelling strategies enabled systematic evaluation of regression-based, variable-selection-based, and curve-resolution approaches using the same experimental datasets under identical validation conditions.

Method sensitivity was characterized by calculating the limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) using the net analyte signal (NAS) approach. By extracting the analyte-specific spectral contribution from the multicomponent dataset, the NAS methodology provides a reliable measure of sensitivity for systems exhibiting extensive spectral overlap. Accuracy was assessed by recovery studies using independently prepared samples at three concentration levels spanning the validated working range. Six replicate samples were analyzed at each concentration level, and the percentage recovery was calculated by comparing the predicted concentrations with their corresponding nominal values. For the plasma matrix, these studies were performed using processed post-extraction-fortified plasma samples and therefore represent quantitative recovery within the processed matrix rather than extraction recovery.

Method precision was investigated as repeatability (intra-day precision) and intermediate precision (inter-day precision). Repeatability was assessed from six replicate measurements performed during a single analytical session under identical operating conditions. Intermediate precision was evaluated by repeating the complete analytical procedure over three consecutive days using independently prepared solutions.

Robustness was assessed by intentionally applying minor changes to instrumental settings, including spectral bandwidth, scan speed, and cuvette alignment. The influence of these variations on the predicted analyte concentrations was subsequently evaluated.

Method selectivity was verified by examining the capability of the chemometric models to selectively resolve the spectral contributions of CEF and ENM in the presence of pharmaceutical excipients and endogenous plasma constituents. Separate matrix-specific calibration models were employed for pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma to eliminate matrix-induced prediction bias and ensure reliable quantitative determination within each analytical window.

For each chemometric model, simple linear regression was performed by plotting the predicted concentrations against the corresponding reference concentrations obtained from the independent validation samples. The resulting regression coefficients, namely the intercept ( a) and slope ( b), were subsequently subjected to joint confidence region analysis.

Analysis of the commercial pharmaceutical product

The applicability of the proposed spectrophotometric–chemometric procedure was assessed by analyzing the commercially available injectable formulation Exblifep® powder. After reconstitution according to the manufacturer’s instructions, a volume containing approximately 20 mg of CEF together with the corresponding amount of ENM was transferred into a 250 mL volumetric flask. The sample was diluted with a water–ethanol mixture (1:1, v/v), sonicated for 30 min to facilitate complete dissolution, and then brought to volume with the same solvent. The resulting solution was passed through a 0.45 μm membrane filter before further dilution to obtain working solutions covering the validated concentration ranges of both analytes.

UV absorption spectra were collected over the selected analytical interval (210–350 nm) under the optimized experimental conditions described previously. Quantitative determination of CEF and ENM was performed by applying the recorded spectra to the established chemometric calibration models.

The accuracy of the procedure in the pharmaceutical matrix was further verified using the standard addition approach. Known quantities of the reference standards were added to the sample solution at three concentration levels (80, 100, and 120% of the nominal assay concentration). Each level was prepared and analyzed in sextuplicate, and method performance was evaluated from the obtained recoveries together with the corresponding relative standard deviations and analytical bias.

Bioanalytical implementation in plasma samples

Matrix-matched calibration and validation samples

To establish reliable proof-of-concept quantitative analysis in a biological matrix, a dedicated plasma-specific calibration framework was developed using processed blank human plasma as the working matrix. Prior to use, frozen plasma samples were allowed to equilibrate to room temperature and gently homogenized to ensure uniformity. Blank spectral profiles were initially recorded across the selected UV region to evaluate any intrinsic matrix absorbance and to confirm the absence of significant interfering signals within the analytical window.

Unlike the pharmaceutical calibration system, plasma calibration and validation sets were prepared independently to preserve matrix specificity during model construction. Based on the experimental design described in Sect. 2.5, twenty-five calibration mixtures and thirteen external validation samples were generated within the same concentration range. Following the plasma pretreatment procedure described in Sect. 2.10.2, processed blank plasma extracts were fortified (post-extraction) with appropriate aliquots of CEF and ENM working solutions to obtain the required concentration levels. This matrix-matched calibration strategy was adopted to compensate for endogenous plasma background effects and to improve predictive reliability under biological conditions. Accordingly, plasma quantification in this study relied exclusively on these independently established matrix-specific models without applying calibration transfer from neat or pharmaceutical preparations.

Plasma pretreatment protocol

Prior to spectral measurement, plasma samples underwent protein precipitation to minimize interference from endogenous proteins and other macromolecular constituents. For this purpose, 1.0 mL of plasma was mixed with 3.0 mL methanol in centrifuge tubes, followed by vortex agitation for 60 s to promote protein aggregation and phase disruption.

The treated samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to achieve efficient separation of the precipitated proteins. The clear supernatant was then isolated and evaporated under a gentle nitrogen stream at 40 °C until dryness to eliminate the precipitation solvent completely.

The residue was subsequently re-dissolved in 1.0 mL of water–ethanol (1:1, v/v), which served as the working solvent for spectrophotometric acquisition. Before analysis, all prepared solutions were passed through 0.22 μm syringe filters to remove any remaining particulate matter and ensure optical clarity. The resulting processed blank plasma extracts were subsequently used for preparation of the matrix-matched calibration, validation, and quantitative evaluation samples by post-extraction fortification with the appropriate concentrations of CEF and ENM.

Quantitative processing of plasma samples

Processed post-extraction-fortified plasma samples were diluted as necessary to fit within the validated analytical concentration range of the developed models. UV spectra were then collected under the optimized instrumental settings and within the predefined wavelength window.

The acquired spectral datasets were subsequently introduced into the corresponding plasma-specific chemometric models for quantitative prediction of CEF and ENM concentrations. Since the applied models were constructed using processed post-extraction-fortified matrix-matched calibration samples, the obtained predictions inherently accounted for background spectral contributions arising from the plasma matrix.

Because the calibration and evaluation samples were prepared by post-extraction fortification of processed blank plasma, the present plasma investigation represents a proof-of-concept assessment of matrix-specific chemometric quantification rather than a complete regulatory bioanalytical validation. Accordingly, parameters requiring pre-extraction fortification, such as extraction recovery and process efficiency, were beyond the scope of the present study.

Results and discussion

Selection of the working solvent

Sustainability-based solvent screening

Selection of the extraction and dilution medium was guided by both sustainability and analytical performance. Candidate solvents with different physicochemical properties, namely water, ethanol, methanol, acetonitrile, ethyl acetate, hexane, and chloroform, were first assessed using the Green Solvent Selection Tool (GSST) developed by Larsen et al. [31]. The GSST combines health, safety, environmental, and waste-management criteria into a single composite score, allowing objective comparison of potential solvent systems.

The calculated GSST scores (Fig. S2) identified acetonitrile (7.3), water (6.7), ethyl acetate (6.6), ethanol (5.8), and methanol (5.8) as the most sustainable candidates, whereas hexane and particularly chloroform received substantially lower ratings because of their unfavorable environmental and toxicological characteristics. These results excluded chlorinated and hydrocarbon solvents from further consideration and limited subsequent experiments to the greener alternatives.

Qualitative greenness assessment and final solvent selection

The shortlisted solvents were further examined using the Spider Diagram for Assessment of the Greenness Index (SDAGI), which summarizes Safety, Health, and Environmental (SHE) characteristics based on safety data sheet information [32, 33]. The corresponding spider plots are presented in (Fig. 2), while the calculated greenness indices are summarized in (Table S1).Fig. 2Spider diagram attained by SDAGI

Spider diagram attained by SDAGI

Among the evaluated solvents, ethanol and ethyl acetate exhibited the most balanced safety and environmental profiles, with average greenness indices of 1.33 and 1.44, respectively. Methanol and acetonitrile achieved lower ratings because of their greater health hazards despite their acceptable analytical characteristics.

The final solvent selection was based on the combined outcome of the sustainability assessment and experimental spectroscopic evaluation. Although several solvents demonstrated favorable environmental characteristics, ethanol produced the highest absorbance response together with well-defined spectral profiles for both analytes. Accordingly, a water–ethanol (1:1, v/v) mixture was selected as the working medium for all subsequent experiments because it provided an appropriate balance between analytical performance and environmental sustainability.

Development of the chemometric calibration framework

Spectral characteristics of the CEF–ENM binary system

The zero-order UV absorption spectra of CEF and ENM are shown in (Fig. 1). Both compounds exhibit strong absorbance in the ultraviolet region with extensive overlap, particularly between 210 and 250 nm, preventing their direct spectrophotometric determination. ENM displays a dominant absorption band at approximately 210–215 nm, whereas CEF exhibits broader absorption extending from the deep-UV region to approximately 280 nm owing to its aminothiazole and methoxyimino chromophores.

Several conventional spectrophotometric approaches, including direct absorbance measurements, derivative spectrophotometry, ratio spectra, and dual-wavelength methods, were examined during preliminary investigations. None provided adequate selectivity because of the extensive spectral overlap and the absence of suitable isoabsorptive or interference-free wavelengths. Consequently, multivariate calibration was adopted for quantitative analysis.

Spectra were initially collected over the 200–400 nm range. After evaluating baseline quality and signal-to-noise characteristics, only the 210–350 nm region was retained for model development, generating a predictor matrix comprising 141 wavelength variables. Measurements below 210 nm were excluded because of increased solvent absorption and instrumental noise, whereas wavelengths above 350 nm contributed negligible analytical information.

The selected spectral interval retained the characteristic absorption features of both analytes while excluding non-informative regions, providing a stable and information-rich dataset for subsequent chemometric modelling.

Matrix-specific chemometric modeling strategy

The analytical performance of multivariate calibration models depends strongly on the spectral characteristics of the investigated matrix. Because pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma differ substantially in their chemical composition and background absorbance, independent calibration models were developed for each analytical application rather than attempting calibration transfer between matrices. This strategy ensured that each model was optimized under the spectral conditions encountered during its intended application [34, 35].

Pharmaceutical formulations generally produce relatively simple spectral backgrounds because formulation excipients exhibit limited absorption within the selected UV region. In contrast, processed human plasma extracts contains numerous endogenous constituents that contribute additional background absorbance and increase spectral complexity, even after sample preparation. Consequently, matrix-specific calibration minimizes systematic prediction bias arising from differences in spectral background and improves the reliability of quantitative analysis.

To accommodate these differences, two independent chemometric workflows were established, one for pharmaceutical formulations and the other for processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. Each workflow included separate calibration and external validation datasets generated from its corresponding analytical matrix, and all six chemometric algorithms were developed independently for each dataset. Consequently, wavelength optimization, latent-variable selection, and model validation were performed separately for each analytical window without sharing calibration information between matrices.

The experimental design further strengthened model reliability by combining a structured calibration space with an independent validation strategy. A calibration dataset consisting of 25 mixtures was constructed using the Brereton multilevel design to provide balanced representation throughout the studied concentration intervals, whereas thirteen external validation mixtures were selected using the MMD algorithm to maximize their spatial distribution throughout the experimental region. Independent implementation of this design for both pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma ensured unbiased evaluation of predictive performance under matrix-specific conditions.

This calibration strategy offers several practical advantages. Matrix-specific calibration compensates for background spectral contributions associated with each analytical matrix, enables wavelength-selection algorithms to identify the most informative spectral variables under the corresponding experimental conditions, and provides a more realistic assessment of model performance during external validation. Furthermore, because the pharmaceutical and plasma datasets were calibrated and validated independently, the obtained prediction statistics directly reflect the analytical performance within each application rather than the ability to transfer calibration between fundamentally different matrices.

Accordingly, the proposed dual-matrix framework should be regarded as two independently optimized chemometric systems developed using a common analytical methodology. This design improves the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed spectrophotometric platform while ensuring reliable quantification in pharmaceutical quality control and proof-of-concept quantitative evaluation of processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma.

To accomplish this, A calibration set was constructed according to the Brereton multilevel design, which provides an organized distribution of calibration samples throughout the entire concentration range while minimizing undesirable correlations between analyte concentrations. The resulting calibration matrix therefore offers an appropriate foundation for constructing stable regression models from highly overlapping spectral data.

The concentration combinations selected for CEF and ENM uniformly covered the validated analytical ranges, producing twenty-five calibration mixtures that represented low-, intermediate-, and high-concentration regions of the experimental region. As illustrated in (Table 1), the design distributed calibration samples symmetrically around the central concentration region, ensuring that no portion of the analytical space was over- or under-represented. Such balanced coverage is particularly advantageous for spectroscopic calibration because it promotes uniform spectral variability throughout the predictor matrix.

The structured calibration space also contributes to the robustness of subsequent wavelength-selection procedures. Because the spectral variability introduced by both analytes is evenly represented across the experimental space, optimization algorithms such as GA-PLS and FA-PLS can identify informative wavelength variables based on genuine analytical information rather than localized concentration effects. Similarly, latent-variable methods including PCR and PLS benefit from improved variance representation, leading to more reliable model construction and enhanced predictive stability.

Beyond its statistical advantages, the adopted calibration strategy provides practical experimental benefits. The multilevel design achieves comprehensive coverage of the analytical concentration range using only twenty-five calibration mixtures, thereby reducing the number of experimental preparations required compared with larger conventional calibration layouts. Chemometric model development

Classical least squares (CLS)

The CLS model was initially developed as a reference calibration approach to establish the baseline analytical performance for simultaneous determination of CEF and ENM. Owing to its direct implementation of the Beer–Lambert law, CLS provides the simplest multivariate calibration framework and therefore serves as an appropriate benchmark for evaluating the improvements achieved by more advanced chemometric algorithms.

Using the calibration mixtures generated from the Brereton multilevel design, CLS successfully established a linear relationship between the measured spectral responses and analyte concentrations across the selected analytical wavelength region. Since the model utilizes the complete spectral matrix without dimensionality reduction or wavelength optimization, concentration prediction depends on the collective contribution of all measured spectral variables. This characteristic preserves the physical relationship between absorbance and concentration but simultaneously exposes the model to the influence of highly correlated spectral variables.

The extensive spectral overlap observed for the CEF–ENM system presents a challenging environment for CLS because the algorithm assumes that the measured absorbance can be described solely by the additive contribution of the individual components. Under these conditions, covariance among neighboring wavelengths may reduce model stability and increase prediction error, particularly when the informative spectral signals are distributed over broad overlapping absorption bands rather than isolated analytical wavelengths.

Despite these limitations, the CLS model demonstrated satisfactory quantitative performance for both pharmaceutical formulations and plasma samples, confirming that the selected calibration design adequately represented the investigated concentration range. Nevertheless, as expected for highly collinear spectral datasets, CLS produced the largest prediction errors among the investigated chemometric approaches, reflecting its limited ability to compensate for redundant spectral information.

The analytical value of CLS therefore lies primarily in providing a transparent reference model against which the advantages of latent-variable regression and wavelength-optimization strategies can be objectively assessed. Improvements observed for the subsequent PCR, PLS, GA-PLS, FA-PLS, and MCR-ALS models can consequently be interpreted as the result of progressively enhanced management of spectral collinearity and extraction of chemically relevant information from the overlapping UV spectra.

Principal component regression (PCR)

PCR was investigated to overcome the strong spectral collinearity observed in the CEF–ENM system by projecting the original spectral variables into a reduced orthogonal latent-variable space. Model complexity was optimized using random five-fold cross-validation, and the optimum number of latent variables was selected according to the minimum RMSECV criterion.

The cross-validation profiles shown in (Fig. S3A) demonstrate a marked reduction in RMSECV during the incorporation of the first three latent variables for both analytes. The first latent variable captured the dominant variance within the spectral dataset, while the second and third components contributed additional chemically relevant information associated with concentration-dependent spectral changes. Beyond the third latent variable, only negligible improvements in prediction error were observed, indicating that the remaining spectral variance was primarily attributable to noise or redundant information rather than meaningful analytical variation. Consequently, three latent variables were selected as the optimum model complexity for both CEF and ENM.

Compared with the CLS model, PCR provided improved calibration stability by eliminating spectral collinearity through orthogonal decomposition of the predictor matrix. This transformation reduced the influence of redundant wavelength information and produced a more robust calibration model for the highly overlapping UV spectra. Nevertheless, because the principal components are extracted solely from the spectral variance without considering analyte concentrations during decomposition, part of the retained variance may not be directly related to quantitative prediction. This limitation explains why PCR, although superior to CLS, does not necessarily provide the highest predictive performance among latent-variable regression methods.

Partial least squares (PLS)

PLS regression was subsequently evaluated to determine whether incorporation of concentration information during latent-variable extraction could further improve predictive performance. In contrast to PCR, PLS simultaneously decomposes the spectral and concentration matrices, allowing the latent variables to be constructed according to their covariance with the analytical response rather than spectral variance alone.

Optimization of model complexity was again performed using random five-fold cross-validation. As illustrated in (Fig. S3B), the prediction error decreased rapidly during incorporation of the first three latent variables, after which the RMSECV profiles approached a stable plateau. The absence of meaningful error reduction beyond the third latent variable indicates that additional components contributed little useful analytical information while increasing the risk of unnecessary model complexity. Accordingly, three latent variables were retained for the final PLS models for both analytes.

Although PCR and PLS required the same number of latent variables, the PLS models exhibited greater calibration efficiency because the extracted latent variables were directly optimized for quantitative prediction. By maximizing the covariance between spectral responses and analyte concentrations, PLS generated a more informative latent-variable space and therefore improved utilization of the spectral information contained within the highly overlapped absorption profiles. This behaviour establishes PLS as a more effective baseline for subsequent wavelength-optimization strategies based on GA and FA.

Genetic algorithm–PLS (GA-PLS)

To further improve calibration efficiency, PLS regression was combined with a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to identify the subset of wavelength variables containing the highest predictive information while eliminating redundant spectral data. The optimized algorithmic settings employed during wavelength selection are summarized in (Table S2).

Model complexity was optimized using random five-fold cross-validation. As illustrated in (Fig. S3C), the RMSECV decreased markedly upon incorporation of the first two latent variables, whereas additional latent variables produced only marginal improvements in prediction accuracy. Optimization identified two latent variables as sufficient for constructing the final GA-PLS models for both CEF and ENM. The reduction from three latent variables required by conventional PLS to only two latent variables after wavelength optimization indicates that the GA successfully concentrated the chemically relevant information into a more informative predictor matrix.

Besides reducing model complexity, the GA substantially decreased the dimensionality of the spectral dataset. From the original 141 wavelength variables, the optimized model retained 84 variables for CEF and 90 variables for ENM, corresponding to dimensionality reductions of approximately 40.4% and 36.2%, respectively (Fig. S4). Removal of nearly two-fifths of the original variables indicates that a considerable proportion of the recorded spectral information was either redundant or contributed minimally to quantitative prediction.

The selected wavelength subsets remained distributed across the informative spectral region rather than being confined to isolated absorbance maxima, demonstrating that quantitative information was extracted from multiple complementary spectral intervals. This behaviour is consistent with the highly overlapped nature of the CEF–ENM spectra, where reliable quantification depends on the collective contribution of several wavelength regions instead of individual analytical wavelengths.

Compared with the conventional PLS model, GA-assisted wavelength selection generated a more parsimonious calibration model by simultaneously reducing the number of predictor variables and the required latent variables without compromising predictive capability. These findings demonstrate that removing redundant spectral variables, the resulting models become more efficient while maintaining accurate concentration estimates.

Firefly algorithm–optimized PLS (FA-PLS)

To further improve the efficiency of wavelength selection, the Firefly Algorithm (FA) was coupled with PLS regression as an alternative metaheuristic optimization strategy. The optimized algorithmic parameters used during the search process are summarized in (Table S3).

Optimization of the FA-PLS model was performed using random five-fold cross-validation. The corresponding RMSECV profiles presented in (Fig. S3D) show a pronounced decrease in prediction error after incorporation of the second latent variable, whereas inclusion of additional latent variables produced only marginal improvement. Consequently, two latent variables were considered sufficient to describe the concentration-dependent spectral variation of both analytes and were retained for construction of the final FA-PLS models.

Application of the Firefly Algorithm produced a substantial reduction in the dimensionality of the spectral dataset. From the original 141 wavelength variables, the optimized FA-PLS models retained only 71 variables for CEF and 76 variables for ENM, corresponding to reductions of approximately 49.6% and 46.1%, respectively (Fig. S4). This degree of variable compression exceeded that obtained with the GA-PLS approach, indicating that the Firefly Algorithm identified a more compact subset of informative wavelength variables while preserving the spectral information required for accurate quantitative prediction.

The retained wavelength subsets were distributed throughout the analytical spectral region rather than being restricted to isolated wavelengths, confirming that quantitative information originated from multiple complementary spectral intervals within the overlapping UV spectra. Such distributed variable selection is advantageous for multicomponent spectrophotometric analysis because it minimizes the influence of local spectral fluctuations while preserving the overall covariance structure required for robust multivariate calibration.

Comparison with the conventional PLS model further demonstrates the effectiveness of FA-assisted optimization. By simultaneously reducing both the number of predictor variables and the required latent variables, FA-PLS generated a simpler and more computationally efficient calibration model without compromising predictive performance. The greater dimensionality reduction achieved by FA relative to GA also suggests that swarm-intelligence optimization provides an effective strategy for eliminating redundant spectral information in highly overlapped spectroscopic datasets.

Multivariate curve resolution–alternating least squares (MCR-ALS)

Among the investigated chemometric approaches, MCR-ALS provided the highest predictive performance by resolving the overlapped spectral matrix into chemically meaningful concentration and pure spectral profiles through constrained bilinear decomposition. Unlike regression-based models, which establish a direct mathematical relationship between spectral variables and analyte concentrations, MCR-ALS reconstructs the original data matrix while simultaneously estimating the spectral signatures and concentration distributions of the individual components. This characteristic makes the algorithm particularly suitable for highly overlapped spectrophotometric systems such as the CEF–ENM mixture.

The optimization procedure was initiated using concentration estimates generated by EFA, which identified two significant factors corresponding to the two analytes present in the binary system. The optimization protocol employed non-negativity constraints for both concentration and spectral matrices together with closure constraints to preserve the compositional balance of the calibration mixtures. By simultaneously reconstructing concentration and spectral matrices while maintaining physically meaningful constraints, the algorithm achieved the highest predictive accuracy and the greatest robustness among the evaluated chemometric models. Analytical performance of the developed method

The analytical performance of the proposed methodology was assessed following the recommendations of ICH Q2 (R1). The complete validation results for pharmaceutical formulations and processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma are summarized in (Tables S5 and S6).

Method sensitivity was estimated using the NAS approach. For pharmaceutical samples, LOD values ranged from 0.0286 to 0.0827 µg mL⁻¹ for CEF and 0.0264–0.0743 µg mL⁻¹ for ENM, with corresponding LOQ values of 0.0867–0.2506 µg mL⁻¹ and 0.0800–0.2252 µg mL⁻¹, respectively. Slightly higher detection limits were observed in fortified plasma (LOD: 0.0415–0.1043 µg mL⁻¹ for CEF and 0.0386–0.0968 µg mL⁻¹ for ENM), reflecting the additional background contribution from the biological matrix while remaining appropriate for the intended proof-of-concept analytical application. The residual error statistics consistently favored the more advanced chemometric models. Across both matrices, RMSEC, RMSEP, RRMSEP, BCRMSEP, and SEC decreased progressively from CLS to MCR-ALS, indicating improved calibration quality and external prediction performance (Tables S5 and S6). The repeated five-fold cross-validation results (Table S7) showed the same ranking with low standard deviations over 50 repeated runs, while bootstrap analysis (Table S8) produced narrow 95% confidence intervals for RMSEP, confirming that the observed performance differences were reproducible rather than the result of random variation or overfitting.

Recovery studies demonstrated satisfactory trueness for all investigated models. Mean recoveries ranged from 99.63 to 100.02% for CEF and 99.28–99.97% for ENM in pharmaceutical formulations, whereas values of 99.21–100.01% and 98.94–99.96% were obtained in fortified plasma (Tables S5 and S6). The corresponding experimental t-values were well below the critical limits, indicating the absence of significant systematic error.

Precision was also satisfactory. Repeatability, intermediate precision, and robustness remained below 0.61%, 0.65%, and 0.69% RSD, respectively, for pharmaceutical formulations, and below 0.64%, 0.78%, and 0.87% RSD, respectively, for fortified plasma (Tables S5 and S6). Although plasma samples exhibited slightly higher variability because of matrix complexity, all values remained within commonly accepted analytical validation criteria.

Overall, the validation results demonstrate that the proposed spectrophotometric–chemometric procedure provides adequate sensitivity, accuracy, precision, and predictive reliability for the quantitative determination of CEF and ENM in pharmaceutical formulations and fortified human plasma.

Statistical comparison of the proposed models

Evaluation of systematic bias

Systematic bias was examined by comparing the mean recoveries with the theoretical value of 100% using Student’s t-test [37]. The calculated t-values for all chemometric models were lower than the corresponding critical values, indicating that none of the methods exhibited statistically significant systematic error (Tables S5 and S6). Although all models satisfied the acceptance criterion, the MCR-ALS model consistently produced the smallest t-values, reflecting the closest agreement between the predicted and nominal concentrations.

Comparison of analytical performance

The quantitative performance of the six chemometric models was further compared with the reported reference method [14] using one-way ANOVA at the 95% confidence level. The complete statistical results are summarized in Tables S9 and S10.

For pharmaceutical formulations, the calculated F-values were 1.21 for CEF and 1.34 for ENM, with corresponding p-values of 0.33 and 0.27, respectively. Similar results were obtained for fortified plasma, where F-values of 1.36 (CEF) and 1.47 (ENM) were associated with p-values of 0.26 and 0.22, respectively. In every case, the calculated F-values remained below the critical value and the p-values exceeded 0.05, indicating no statistically significant differences among the investigated chemometric models and the reference procedure.

Although the statistical analysis demonstrated equivalent analytical performance, numerical differences in prediction errors and recovery statistics (Tables S5 and S6) consistently favored the advanced models, particularly MCR-ALS. This observation agrees with the lower RMSEP values, improved cross-validation performance (Table S7), and narrower bootstrap confidence intervals (Table S8), indicating enhanced prediction stability while maintaining statistical equivalence with the reference method.

Joint confidence region analysis

The regression performance of the investigated chemometric models was further examined using the Elliptical Joint Confidence Region (EJCR) approach, which simultaneously evaluates the slope and intercept of the calibration relationship. The resulting 95% confidence ellipses for pharmaceutical and fortified plasma datasets are presented in Figs. S6 and S7, respectively, where the ideal analytical response corresponds to a slope of 1 and an intercept of 0. Clear differences in regression uncertainty were observed among the investigated models. CLS and PCR produced the largest confidence ellipses, indicating greater uncertainty in the estimated regression parameters. Incorporation of latent-variable modelling (PLS) and wavelength selection (GA-PLS and FA-PLS) progressively reduced the ellipse dimensions, reflecting improved agreement between predicted and reference concentrations. The smallest confidence regions were obtained with MCR-ALS for both analytes in pharmaceutical formulations and fortified plasma. The ellipse centres were located close to the theoretical coordinate and enclosed the ideal point within the 95% confidence limits, indicating the absence of significant constant or proportional bias. This behaviour is consistent with the lower prediction errors reported in Tables S5 and S6, the repeated cross-validation results (Table S7), and the bootstrap confidence intervals (Table S8), demonstrating stable regression performance across independent validation procedures. Although the one-way ANOVA (Tables S9 and S10) indicated no statistically significant differences among the investigated methods, the EJCR plots provide a graphical comparison of regression uncertainty and show that MCR-ALS yielded the most consistent calibration performance for the CEF–ENM system.

Statistical robustness, model ranking, and practical significance

Although the external validation results demonstrated a clear improvement in predictive performance from CLS to MCR-ALS, additional statistical analyses were performed to verify that the observed ranking was reproducible and not the consequence of a favorable calibration–validation partition or overfitting. Therefore, repeated cross-validation, bootstrap resampling, one-way ANOVA, and MCR-ALS convergence analysis were employed as complementary validation procedures.

The stability of all calibration models was first assessed using repeated five-fold cross-validation performed over fifty independent repetitions. As summarized in (Table S9), the mean RMSECV values progressively decreased from CLS to MCR-ALS for both analytes in pharmaceutical formulations and plasma, while the corresponding standard deviations remained consistently small. The low inter-run variability demonstrates that model performance was highly reproducible across repeated random partitions of the calibration data and confirms that the selected latent-variable structures and optimized wavelength subsets remained stable throughout repeated validation cycles.

To further quantify predictive uncertainty, bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was performed using the independent validation datasets. The resulting 95% confidence intervals for RMSEP are presented in (Table S10). All investigated models produced relatively narrow confidence intervals that remained fully consistent with the ranking obtained from external validation. Although slight overlap between adjacent confidence intervals is expected because the external validation set consisted of only thirteen independent mixtures, the bootstrap analysis confirmed that the progressive reduction in prediction error from CLS through MCR-ALS represents a reproducible analytical trend rather than a random consequence of sample selection. These findings provide additional evidence that the optimized GA-PLS and FA-PLS models did not suffer from detectable overfitting despite the use of evolutionary wavelength-selection algorithms.

The overall statistical comparison was further examined using one-way ANOVA. As shown in (Tables S7 and S8), no statistically significant differences were observed among the investigated chemometric models at the 95% confidence level for either pharmaceutical formulations or plasma samples (P > 0.05). This result indicates that all six calibration approaches satisfy acceptable analytical performance requirements. However, ANOVA evaluates statistical significance rather than analytical superiority. Therefore, the integrated interpretation of prediction errors, recovery studies, residual diagnostics, and multivariate statistical analyses provides a more informative basis for comparing model performance.

Additional support for the superior performance of MCR-ALS is provided by its optimization characteristics summarized in (Table S4). The algorithm converged successfully after only 31 and 35 iterations for pharmaceutical and plasma datasets, respectively, while maintaining very low lack-of-fit values (0.43 and 0.57%), high explained variance (> 99.9%), and no detectable rotational ambiguity. These optimization characteristics confirm that the bilinear decomposition accurately represented the experimental spectral matrices without evidence of numerical instability or over-parameterization.

Considering all analytical and statistical evidence together, the investigated models consistently followed the performance sequence:

The progressive improvement observed across this hierarchy reflects successive reductions in spectral redundancy, improved extraction of chemically relevant information, and increasingly effective treatment of spectral overlap. Conventional regression (CLS) was most susceptible to multicollinearity, whereas latent-variable methods (PCR and PLS) substantially improved predictive capability through dimensionality reduction. The incorporation of intelligent wavelength-selection algorithms further enhanced model efficiency by retaining only informative spectral variables. Finally, MCR-ALS achieved the highest predictive performance because bilinear decomposition resolves the concentration and pure spectral profiles simultaneously before quantitative prediction, thereby minimizing covariance interference in highly overlapping spectral systems.

From a practical perspective, all developed chemometric models are suitable for routine determination of CEF and ENM in pharmaceutical formulations and human plasma. Nevertheless, when maximum prediction accuracy, robustness against matrix interference, and long-term calibration stability are required, MCR-ALS represents the preferred analytical strategy. The combination of structured experimental design, space-filling-assisted wavelength optimization, rigorous statistical validation, and bilinear spectral resolution establishes a transparent, reproducible, and reliable analytical platform that can be readily extended to other multicomponent spectrophotometric applications involving severely overlapping spectra.

Comparative model performance in pharmaceutical matrix

Recovery performance in plasma

The recoveries obtained for CEF ranged from 95.62% to 98.94%, whereas ENM recoveries varied between 95.88% and 98.97% across the investigated concentration levels and chemometric models (Table 4 and S11). Compared with pharmaceutical formulations, the slightly lower recoveries observed in plasma are expected because endogenous plasma constituents introduce additional spectral background and increase the complexity of the analytical system. Precision remained satisfactory throughout the investigated concentration range. Repeatability values did not exceed 1.82% RSD for CEF and 1.76% RSD for ENM, demonstrating acceptable reproducibility despite the increased matrix complexity. As observed during model validation, CLS exhibited the largest variability, whereas latent-variable models (PCR and PLS) provided improved precision through spectral compression. The wavelength-selection strategies incorporated into GA-PLS and FA-PLS further reduced analytical variability by eliminating redundant spectral variables, while MCR-ALS achieved the highest precision, with minimum RSD values of 0.73% for CEF and 0.71% for ENM, reflecting its superior capability for resolving overlapping spectral information in complex matrices.

Precision evaluation in plasma samples

A preliminary evaluation of matrix influence was performed by comparing post-extraction spiked plasma samples with corresponding neat standard solutions using the optimized MCR-ALS model. The results (Table S12) demonstrated matrix factor values ranging from 0.980 to 0.991, corresponding to minor signal suppression between 0.9% and 2.0% for both analytes.

The highest suppression was observed for ENM at the lowest concentration level (−2.0%), whereas matrix effects decreased progressively with increasing concentration, remaining below approximately 2% throughout the investigated working range. These findings indicate that the optimized protein-precipitation procedure effectively minimized endogenous spectral interference and that residual matrix influence was limited under the employed experimental conditions.

It should be emphasized that this evaluation represents a preliminary assessment of matrix influence performed using processed post-extraction-fortified laboratory-prepared plasma samples. Accordingly, the observed matrix factor reflects residual matrix influence after sample preparation rather than the regulatory matrix effect determined using pre-extraction fortified plasma. A comprehensive regulatory bioanalytical evaluation involving multiple plasma lots, extraction recovery, process efficiency, dilution integrity, analyte stability, carryover, and selectivity toward endogenous compounds and co-administered medications was beyond the scope of the present study.

Matrix compensation efficiency and model reliability

The satisfactory analytical performance obtained in fortified plasma demonstrates the effectiveness of constructing an independent matrix-specific calibration model rather than transferring calibration developed from pharmaceutical or neat standard solutions. Matrix-matched calibration compensated for spectral contributions originating from plasma constituents and enabled reliable prediction across the investigated concentration range.

Among the investigated chemometric approaches, FA-PLS and MCR-ALS exhibited the greatest robustness toward matrix-induced spectral variability. The improved performance of FA-PLS is attributed to optimization of the wavelength subset, whereas the MCR-ALS model benefits from bilinear decomposition of the spectral data into concentration and pure spectral profiles, thereby reducing interference from non-informative background variation. These observations are consistent with the lower prediction errors and higher recoveries reported in the validation study (Tables S5 and S6).

Analytical relevance and practical utility

The plasma experiments performed in the present study were based on fortified (spiked) human plasma samples and were intended to evaluate the analytical capability of the proposed spectrophotometric–chemometric platform under biologically relevant laboratory conditions rather than to establish a fully validated clinical bioanalytical method.

The validated plasma working ranges ( CEF: 1–16 µg mL⁻¹ and ENM: 2–10 µg mL⁻¹) were selected to encompass concentrations that may be encountered during sample dilution or in the later phases of drug disposition. Following intravenous administration of the cefepime–enmetazobactam combination, reported maximum plasma concentrations ( Cmax) are approximately 30.7 µg mL⁻¹ for CEF [38], and 10 µg mL⁻¹ for ENM [39], depending on dose and infusion regimen. Consequently, the concentration intervals investigated in this work represent diluted or sub-maximum plasma concentrations rather than peak systemic exposure.

Despite the absence of chromatographic separation, the proposed methodology provided satisfactory recovery, precision, and minimal matrix influence after simple protein precipitation, demonstrating that UV spectrophotometry combined with advanced chemometric calibration can successfully resolve highly overlapping spectra in fortified plasma samples. These characteristics, together with the short analysis time, low solvent consumption, and reduced instrumental cost, make the proposed approach attractive for laboratory investigations requiring rapid quantitative analysis under controlled experimental conditions.

Nevertheless, the present study should be regarded as a proof-of-concept evaluation. Before routine clinical implementation or pharmacokinetic application, additional bioanalytical validation would be required using incurred patient samples together with comprehensive assessment of matrix effects across multiple plasma lots, extraction recovery, process efficiency, dilution integrity, analyte stability, carryover, and selectivity toward endogenous constituents and co-administered medications. Accordingly, the current results demonstrate the feasibility of matrix-specific chemometric analysis in spiked human plasma, while future studies will be necessary to establish full regulatory bioanalytical applicability.

Although the chromatographic methods maintained acceptable practicality, their requirement for specialized infrastructure and longer run times reduced operational feasibility.

In the RAPI module, all methods maintained high analytical quality scores, reflecting acceptable analytical reliability. However, the proposed workflows demonstrated stronger analytical balance by achieving robust quantitative performance without requiring chromatographic separation.

The VIGI module highlighted the innovative advantage of the developed spectrophotometric–chemometric platform. The integration of multivariate spectral resolution, space-filling-guided experimental design, and sustainability-driven workflow optimization contributed to higher innovation scores compared with conventional separation-based analytical approaches.

Rebound effect interpretation and comparative sustainability ranking

The unified MA Tool assessment clearly demonstrates that the sustainability gains achieved by the proposed analytical platform are not superficial or metric-dependent. By incorporating all operational steps—including plasma pretreatment—the present evaluation avoids overstated greenness claims and provides realistic sustainability benchmarking.

Importantly, the proposed plasma workflow remained superior to both chromatographic methods despite the additional analytical burden introduced by biological sample preparation. This observation confirms that the sustainability benefits of the proposed methodology extend beyond pharmaceutical analysis and remain valid under more demanding bioanalytical conditions.

The final comparative sustainability ranking was:

Pharmaceutical workflow > Processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma workflow > RP-HPLC > LC–MS/MS.

This ranking is fully consistent with the operational and environmental characteristics summarized in (Table 5) and confirms that the developed spectrophotometric–chemometric platform represents a genuine multidimensional sustainability advancement for simultaneous determination of CEF and ENM. Beyond analytical sustainability, the broader societal contribution of the proposed methodology was assessed through its alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN-SDGs). The developed platform supports multiple SDGs by promoting safer analytical practices, reducing hazardous solvent consumption, improving energy efficiency, minimizing laboratory waste, and facilitating sustainable pharmaceutical quality control and education (Table S13).

Study limitations and future perspectives

The present study demonstrates the applicability of the proposed spectrophotometric–chemometric platform for the simultaneous determination of CEF and ENM in pharmaceutical formulations and proof-of-concept quantitative analysis of processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged.

The plasma investigation was performed using processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma samples prepared from processed blank plasma extracts rather than pre-extraction fortified samples or incurred clinical specimens. Consequently, the current investigation does not constitute a full bioanalytical method validation according to current regulatory guidance for pharmacokinetic or therapeutic drug monitoring studies.

Although matrix-factor evaluation demonstrated minimal residual matrix influence and the matrix-matched calibration models provided satisfactory recovery and precision, the reported recovery values represent quantitative recovery within the processed plasma matrix and should not be interpreted as extraction recovery. Accordingly, the plasma results should be interpreted within the scope of the adopted proof-of-concept experimental design. Additional investigations using multiple plasma lots, incurred patient samples, extraction recovery, process efficiency, stability studies, dilution integrity, carryover assessment, and selectivity against concomitantly administered medications would be required before routine clinical implementation.

Future work will therefore focus on comprehensive bioanalytical validation and application of the proposed chemometric strategy to authentic clinical plasma samples.

The proposed methodology is primarily intended as a rapid, environmentally sustainable analytical platform for pharmaceutical quality control and proof-of-concept quantitative analysis of processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma, rather than a replacement for validated LC–MS/MS methods used in regulatory pharmacokinetic investigations. Instead, the proposed workflow should be regarded as a complementary analytical platform for rapid laboratory-based investigations in which simplicity, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness are desirable, while LC–MS/MS remains the method of choice for fully validated regulatory bioanalytical applications.

Conclusion

A sustainable UV spectrophotometric–chemometric methodology was successfully developed for the simultaneous determination of CEF and ENM in pharmaceutical formulations and proof-of-concept quantitative analysis of processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. The proposed approach combines UV spectrophotometry with complementary multivariate calibration strategies, enabling accurate quantification of severely overlapping spectra without chromatographic separation. Matrix-specific calibration models developed independently for pharmaceutical and plasma samples demonstrated satisfactory accuracy, precision, predictive ability, and external validation performance, confirming the robustness of the proposed analytical workflow across both analytical matrices.

Comparative evaluation of the investigated chemometric models demonstrated a progressive improvement in predictive performance from CLS to PCR, PLS, GA-PLS, FA-PLS, and MCR-ALS. Among the evaluated approaches, MCR-ALS provided the most favorable overall analytical performance owing to its effective resolution of overlapping spectral information and superior prediction accuracy, while the wavelength-selection strategies further improved model efficiency by eliminating redundant spectral variables.

The sustainability of the proposed methodology was comprehensively evaluated using the MA Tool within the White Analytical Chemistry framework. Independent sustainability assessments were performed for the pharmaceutical and plasma workflows to account for the additional operations required during plasma sample preparation. The pharmaceutical workflow achieved the highest overall sustainability performance, whereas the plasma workflow maintained a more favorable sustainability profile than the reported RP-HPLC and LC–MS/MS methods despite the inclusion of protein precipitation, centrifugation, solvent evaporation, and filtration. The complete MA Tool reports and parameter-level scoring have been provided as supplementary material to ensure transparency and reproducibility of the sustainability assessment.

The present study demonstrates the applicability of the proposed methodology for routine pharmaceutical quality control and proof-of-concept quantitative analysis of processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma. However, the plasma investigation was performed using processed post-extraction-fortified human plasma samples prepared from processed blank plasma extracts rather than pre-extraction fortified samples or incurred clinical specimens. Consequently, the reported recovery values represent quantitative recovery within the processed plasma matrix rather than extraction recovery, and additional bioanalytical validation, including evaluation using multiple plasma lots, incurred patient samples, extraction recovery, process efficiency, stability, dilution integrity, carryover, and selectivity against co-administered medications, will be required before clinical or therapeutic drug-monitoring applications can be considered.

Overall, the proposed methodology provides a rapid, environmentally responsible, and statistically validated alternative for multicomponent UV spectrophotometric analysis. The integration of systematic experimental design, complementary chemometric modeling, rigorous statistical validation, and transparent sustainability assessment provides a practical framework for the future development of sustainable analytical methods for the analysis of complex multicomponent pharmaceutical systems and proof-of-concept matrix-specific bioanalytical applications involving highly overlapping spectroscopic signals.
