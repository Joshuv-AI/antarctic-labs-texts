Factors Influencing Burnout Among Hospice Nurses: Focusing on Humanistic Knowledge, Nursing Workplace Spirituality, and Professional Self-Concept
Authors: Ye Hyun An, Kyoung-Mi Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to identify the factors affecting burnout among hospice nurses and provide the basic data necessary for developing an effective and sustainable burnout management program for hospice ward nurses.MethodsThe participants were 147 nurses who had worked for more than 3 months in hospice wards at hospitals or higher, or at inpatient hospices and palliative care institutions located in G province and B, U, and C cities. The data were collected from January 15, 2025, to March 15, 2025, using a structured questionnaire. They were analyzed using IBM SPSS 25.0 with descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson’s correlation, and stepwise multiple regression.ResultsThe factors affecting participating nurses’ burnout were professional self-concept (β=-0.54, P<0.01), age (β=-0.16, P=0.01), and nursing workplace spirituality (β=-0.19, P=0.01). The total explanatory power of these variables for burnout was 52.9%.ConclusionIt is necessary to develop and implement a burnout management program that includes content that improves professional self-concept and nursing workplace spirituality. Additionally, burnout among hospice ward nurses should be actively managed using a customized approach that considers burnout patterns according to age.

INTRODUCTION

1. Need for the study

With the increase in life expectancy, South Korea entered a super-aged society in 2025, with the proportion of older adults exceeding 20% of the total population. This proportion is projected to exceed 30% by 2036 and 40% by 2050 [1]. Accordingly, social interest in healthy living and quality of life has expanded beyond the entire life course to encompass dignified death and the quality of death at the end-of-life stage [2]. According to the 2023 Hospice Awareness Survey, 91.7% of the respondents recognized the need for hospice care. In line with the growing awareness of hospice care in society, the hospice utilization rate has also continued to increase, reaching 24.2% in 2022, which represents a 6.7% increase compared to 2016 [3]. In response to these societal demands, the Korean government announced through 「The Second Comprehensive Plan for Hospice and Life-Sustaining Treatment (2024~2028)」its plans to expand the number of specialized hospice institutions from 188 in 2023 to approximately 360 by 2028 and to increase the hospice utilization rate to approximately 50% by 2028 [4]. As hospice services continue to expand quantitatively, social interest in improving the quality of hospice care has also increased. To ensure dignified care for patients at the end of life and their families, specific efforts that reflect the experiences and work environments of care providers who deliver such care directly are also needed.

As caring for patients at the end of life and their families requires substantial physical and emotional energy, hospice professionals have been reported to be at a higher risk of burnout than professionals in other healthcare occupations [5]. In particular, nurses are healthcare professionals who have the closest contact with patients and their families and possess the greatest understanding of their circumstances. They are pivotal not only in providing holistic nursing care but also in coordinating the overall activities of hospice team members [6,7]. Nurses’ burnout refers to physical, mental, and emotional exhaustion, which is a form of response to occupational stress that includes negative self-concept, negative attitudes toward work, and a loss of interest in patients [8]. Repeated burnout can lead to emotional exhaustion, loss of motivation, a diminished sense of accomplishment, impaired work-related judgment, indifference toward patients, and depersonalization, thereby negatively affecting not only the quality of nursing care but also organizational performance in hospitals [9]. Therefore, nurses’ burnout may ultimately hinder improvements in the quality of life and the dignified end-of-life experience of hospice patients. Accordingly, burnout should be actively managed to improve the quality of hospice and palliative care services, and this requires a thorough understanding of and close attention to burnout among hospice nurses.

Humanistic knowledge can be defined as an individual’s attitude and mental disposition toward reflecting on and engaging with the conditions of life and human existence that encompass human beings [10]. In the field of nursing, where care is provided to uphold human dignity and maintain a high quality of life, nurses’ humanistic knowledge is increasingly important for promoting a holistic approach to and restoring respect for human beings [11]. Nurses’ humanistic knowledge facilitates an empathic understanding of human beings, thereby enabling them to provide authentic, person-centered nursing care [12]. Furthermore, higher levels of nurses’ humanistic knowledge have been reported to be associated with higher levels of end-of-life care performance, emotional intelligence, and empathy [13,14]. Hospice nurses, meanwhile, are repeatedly exposed to experiences of loss and emotional stress while caring for patients at the end of life and their families, which may lead to burnout. In this context, humanistic knowledge may contribute to alleviating the emotional burden experienced in the course of nursing practice by facilitating the empathic acceptance of patients’ suffering and encouraging reflection on the meaning of care based on an understanding of human beings and life. Therefore, to comprehensively identify the factors underlying burnout among hospice nurses, individual internal resources, such as humanistic knowledge, should be considered.

To explore multifaceted intervention strategies for preventing burnout among hospice nurses, nursing workplace spirituality at the organizational level, along with individual internal resources, can also be considered an important factor. When nursing workplace spirituality is high, nurses can recognize the intrinsic meaning of their nursing practice beyond their occupation and anticipate holistic personal growth through close and meaningful relationships with colleagues [5]. Furthermore, a high level of nursing workplace spirituality enables nurses to provide holistic nursing care [15], which is particularly important for hospice unit nurses who remain with patients through the final moments of life and provide holistic care to patients at the end of life and their families. In fact, nursing workplace spirituality has been reported to improve nurses’ job satisfaction and organizational commitment [16,17] and to be associated with the level of caring behaviors among hospice unit nurses [18]. As noted above, hospice nurses are particularly vulnerable to burnout due to the nature of their work. In this context, nursing workplace spirituality may serve as a positive resource for alleviating burnout by helping nurses find meaning and purpose in their work and strengthening their intrinsic values regarding patient care.

In addition, to effectively understand and manage burnout among hospice nurses, it is necessary to consider professional self-concept, which reflects nurses’ professional identity, along with the aforementioned individual and organizational resources. Nurses with a positive and well-established professional self-concept can collaborate harmoniously with various healthcare professionals to provide high-quality nursing care, experience personal satisfaction through efficient job performance, and establish a positive social status as professionals [19]. In contrast, nurses who have not established a healthy professional self-concept may experience decreased motivation, a lack of a sense of accomplishment, and greater burnout as they encounter feelings of anger, frustration, and inefficacy while caring for patients [20]. Previous studies have identified professional self-concept as the variable with the greatest influence on burnout among oncology unit nurses [21]. In addition, among intensive care unit nurses, higher levels of professional self-concept were associated with lower levels of burnout, a subdomain of professional quality of life [20]. Although the importance of professional self-concept in relation to burnout has been demonstrated among nurses working in various clinical settings, few studies have examined the relationship between professional self-concept and burnout among hospice unit nurses.

Previous studies on burnout among hospice unit nurses have examined the effects of selected variables, such as emotional labor, job stress, resilience, and clinical nursing character, on burnout [22,23]. However, studies investigating the factors influencing burnout by comprehensively incorporating variables at both individual and organizational levels remain limited. In particular, few studies have comprehensively examined the factors influencing burnout among hospice unit nurses by integrating humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, and professional self-concept. Therefore, this study aims to examine the effects of these variables on burnout, focusing on humanistic knowledge and professional self-concept as individual internal resources and nursing workplace spirituality as an organizational resource among hospice unit nurses. Based on the findings, we aim to provide fundamental data to develop and implement effective and sustainable burnout management programs for hospice unit nurses.

2. Purpose of the study

The study aimed to examine the effects of humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, and professional self-concept on burnout among hospice unit nurses. The specific objectives were as follows:

1) To identify the general characteristics of the participants.

2) To assess the levels of humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, professional self-concept, and burnout among the participants.

3) To examine differences in burnout according to the general characteristics of the participants.

4) To examine the relationships among humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, professional self-concept, and burnout among the participants.

5) To identify the factors influencing burnout among the participants.

Participants and Methods

1. Research design

This study employed a descriptive survey design to identify the factors influencing burnout among hospice unit nurses, focusing on the effects of humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, and professional self-concept.

2. Study participants

The participants were nurses working in hospice wards at 12 specialized inpatient hospice and palliative care institutions registered with the Ministry of Health and Welfare who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows:

1) Nurses working in hospice wards at hospital level or higher healthcare institutions among the specialized inpatient hospice and palliative care institutions registered with the Ministry of Health and Welfare

2) Nurses who had been working in a hospice ward for at least 3 months

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. With a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, a medium effect size of 0.15, and 15 predictor variables, the required sample size was 139 participants. Considering the potential dropout rate, the questionnaires were distributed to 155 participants. Of the collected questionnaires, 5 with missing responses and 3 with inadequate responses were excluded, leaving 147 questionnaires for the final analysis.

3. Research instruments

1) Humanistic knowledge

Humanistic knowledge refers to exploring and internalizing humanity and human values through literature, history, philosophy, art, religion, and culture [24]. This study used the Humanistic Knowledge Scale developed by Jeong and Joo [24] for college students after partial modification to suit its purpose, following content validity verification by and consultation with three nursing professors. The scale comprises 22 items across 5 subdomains as follows: literary knowledge, philosophical knowledge, historical knowledge, artistic knowledge, and religious and cultural knowledge. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating a higher level of humanistic knowledge. The reliability of the scale was Cronbach’s α=0.93 in the study by Jeong and Joo [24] and Cronbach’s α=0.93 in the present study.

2) Nursing workplace spirituality

Nursing workplace spirituality refers to experiencing an inner world that enables nurses to overcome difficulties by finding meaning in nursing within the nursing environment, experiencing a sense of engagement and self-actualization through nursing practice, identifying with the values pursued by both the organization and oneself, and experiencing a sense of interconnectedness and community through interactions with colleagues [15]. This study measured nursing workplace spirituality using the Nursing Workplace Spirituality Scale developed by Seok and Go [15] for nurses. The scale comprises 32 items across 6 subdomains as follows: interaction with the workplace environment, meaning of nursing, inner self, connection with colleagues, harmony between the organization and the individual, and transcendence through nursing work. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 7 (“very much”), with higher scores indicating a higher level of nursing workplace spirituality. The reliability of the scale was Cronbach’s α=0.96 in the study by Seok and Go [15] and Cronbach’s α=0.96 in the present study.

3) Professional self-concept

Professional self-concept refers to the feelings and perceptions that professional nurses have toward themselves and their work [25]. Professional self-concept was measured using the Professional Self-Concept Scale originally developed by Arthur [25] for nursing students and nurses, translated by Sohng and Noh [26], and subsequently modified for clinical nurses. The scale comprises 27 items across 3 subdomains as follows: professional practice, satisfaction, and communication. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“disagree”) to 4 (“agree”). Negatively worded items (Items 9, 12, 13, 18, 21, 23, and 25) were reverse-scored, with higher scores indicating a higher level of professional self-concept. The reliability of the scale was Cronbach’s α=0.85 in the study by Sohng and Noh [26] and Cronbach’s α=0.91 in the present study.

4) Burnout

Burnout refers to a syndrome of physical, mental, and emotional exhaustion resulting from the response to work-related stress, characterized by negative self-concept, negative attitudes toward work, and a loss of interest in clients [8]. This study measured burnout using the Burnout Scale developed by Pines et al. [8], translated by Pick [27], and modified and supplemented for nurses. The scale comprises 20 items across 3 subdomains as follows: physical burnout, mental burnout, and emotional burnout. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). Positively worded items (Items 3, 6, 11, 13, and 17~19) were reverse-scored, with higher scores indicating a higher level of burnout. The reliability of the scale was Cronbach’s α=0.86 in the study by Pick [27] and Cronbach’s α=0.92 in the present study.

4. Data collection procedure

Data were collected between January 15 and March 15, 2025, following approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of K University (KUIRB 2024-0049), from one general hospital, two hospitals, and one long-term care hospital in G Province; one tertiary general hospital, two general hospitals, and one long-term care hospital in B City; two hospitals in U City; and two general hospitals in C City. The collection process commenced after the researcher obtained approval from the nursing departments of each hospital through their respective official procedures. Following approval, the researcher personally visited the hospice wards of each hospital and verbally explained the purpose of the study and the data collection procedures to the head nurses, along with providing a detailed information sheet. Only participants who voluntarily expressed their willingness to participate in the study were asked to provide written informed consent and complete the questionnaire. Upon completion, the questionnaires were placed in envelopes, sealed with double-sided tape, and were either collected by mail or retrieved in person by the researcher.

5. Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (SPSS Korea, Data Solution Inc.).

1) The participants’ general characteristics and levels of burnout, humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, and professional self-concept were analyzed using means, standard deviations, minimum values, and maximum values.

2) Differences in the level of burnout according to the participants’ general characteristics were analyzed using t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and Scheffé tests.

3) Correlations among burnout, humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, and professional self-concept were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients.

4) Factors influencing burnout among the participants were analyzed using stepwise multiple regression analysis.

RESULTS

1. General characteristics of the participants

Among the general characteristics of the participants, the largest proportion was in their 30s, accounting for 27.8% (41 participants). The majority of participants were women (93.2%, 137 participants). Regarding religion, the largest proportion reported having no religion (39.5%, 58 participants). In terms of marital status, unmarried participants accounted for 50.3% (74 participants), slightly more than the married participants (49.7%, 73 participants). Regarding the highest educational attainment, those with a bachelor’s degree accounted for the largest proportion (65.3%, 96 participants). Staff nurses constituted the largest proportion by position or job classification (83.0%, 122 participants). Regarding total clinical experience, those with 10 or more years of experience accounted for the largest proportion (54.4%, 80 participants), while those with 1 to less than 3 years of experience in hospice wards accounted for the largest proportion of hospice ward experience (38.1%, 56 participants). Regarding the motivation for choosing to work in a hospice ward, “because I find the work rewarding” was the most common response (31.3%, 46 participants). Most participants worked three-shift schedules (76.2%, 112 participants). Regarding hospital type, hospitals accounted for the largest proportion of respondents (44.9%, 66 participants). Auxiliary personnel were available in 80.3% (118 participants) of cases, compared to 19.7% (29 participants) where they were not available (Table 1).

2. Levels of humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, professional self-concept, and burnout among the participants

The mean score for humanistic knowledge was 77.26±12.38, with a mean item score of 3.49±0.56 (scale range: 1~5), indicating a level above the midpoint. The mean item scores for the subdomains, in descending order, were religious and cultural knowledge (3.70±0.67), literary knowledge (3.59±0.79), philosophical knowledge (3.55±0.63), historical knowledge (3.30±0.86), and artistic knowledge (3.29±0.82) (Table 2).

The mean score for nursing workplace spirituality was 165.16±26.08, with a mean item score of 5.09±0.81 (scale range: 1~7), indicating a level above the midpoint. The mean item scores for the subdomains, in descending order, were meaning of nursing (5.66±0.92), harmony between the organization and the individual (5.44±0.99), connection with colleagues (5.43±0.84), inner self (5.11±1.12), transcendence through nursing work (4.72±1.12), and interaction with the workplace environment (4.19±1.25) (Table 2).

The mean score for professional self-concept was 78.62±10.24, with a mean item score of 2.90±0.39 (scale range: 1~4), indicating a level above the midpoint. The mean item scores for the subdomains, in descending order, were professional practice (2.93±0.40), communication (2.90±0.49), and satisfaction (2.87±0.59) (Table 2).

The mean score for burnout was 49.68±13.22, with a mean item score of 2.50±0.67 (scale range: 1~5), indicating a level below the midpoint. The mean item scores for the subdomains, in descending order, were physical burnout (2.78±0.87), emotional burnout (2.43±0.67), and mental burnout (2.29±0.66) (Table 2).

3. Differences in burnout according to the participants’ general characteristics

Significant differences exist in the level of burnout according to age (F=4.74, P=0.00), religion (t=–2.02, P=0.05), marital status (t=–3.64, P=0.00), motivation for choosing to work in a hospice ward (t=–2.93, P=0.00), and hospital type (F=3.02, P=0.03). Post-hoc analysis showed that the 20s and 30s age groups had higher levels of burnout than the 60-and-older age group. Regarding hospital type, the general hospital group had a higher level of burnout than the hospital group (Table 3).

4. Relationships among humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, professional self-concept, and burnout among the participants

Burnout among the participants had a weak negative correlation with humanistic knowledge (r=–0.22, P=0.01) and moderate negative correlations with nursing workplace spirituality (r=–0.55, P<0.01) and professional self-concept (r=–0.70, P<0.01) (Table 4).

A moderate positive correlation was observed between humanistic knowledge and nursing workplace spirituality (r=0.51, P<0.01). Humanistic knowledge also had a weak positive correlation with professional self-concept (r=0.29, P<0.01) (Table 4).

Nursing workplace spirituality had a moderate positive correlation with professional self-concept (r=0.63, P<0.01) (Table 4).

5. Factors influencing burnout among the participants

To identify the factors influencing burnout among the participants, stepwise multiple regression analysis was conducted with humanistic knowledge, nursing workplace spirituality, professional self-concept, and the general characteristics that showed significant differences in burnout, including age, marital status, motivation for choosing to work in a hospice ward, and hospital type. Marital status, motivation for choosing to work in a hospice ward, and hospital type were entered as dummy variables.

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed. The tolerance values ranged from 0.58 to 0.93, all exceeding 0.1, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.08 to 1.72, all below 10, indicating no multicollinearity among the independent variables. In addition, the Durbin–Watson statistic was 2.05, which was close to 2, indicating no autocorrelation of the errors. The assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity of the residuals were also satisfied. The regression model was statistically significant (F=55.36, P<0.01).

The factors influencing burnout among the participants were professional self-concept (β=–0.54, P<0.01), age (β=–0.16, P=0.01), and nursing workplace spirituality (β=–0.19, P=0.01) in descending order of influence. These variables collectively explained 52.9% of the variance in burnout (see Table 5).

DISCUSSION

This study is significant in that it identified the factors associated with burnout among hospice ward nurses. The findings of this study can serve as a basis for developing intervention strategies to prevent and manage burnout among hospice ward nurses. Based on the findings of this study, the results are discussed as follows.

First, the level of humanistic knowledge among hospice ward nurses in this study was 3.49 out of 5, similar to the finding of 3.40 reported by Woo [11] among clinical nurses. Among the subdomains, “artistic knowledge” had the lowest score. This may be because nursing work primarily focuses on direct patient care, whereas continuing education is largely centered on physical care, resulting in limited opportunities for formal education to cultivate artistic knowledge. Therefore, nurses should be provided with educational opportunities to engage with various fields of the arts. In addition, strategies that integrate such opportunities into burnout management programs could be considered so that nurses can participate without the burden of additional time or costs.

The level of nursing workplace spirituality among hospice ward nurses in this study was 5.09 out of 7, similar to the 4.91 reported by An [18] among hospice ward nurses and higher than the 4.29 reported by Baek and Kim [17] among clinical nurses. Among the subdomains, “interaction with the workplace environment” had the lowest score, suggesting that hospice ward nurses may not experience sufficient care and respect in their workplace. Therefore, to foster an organizational culture based on mutual respect, a horizontal and open communication structure should be established in which nurses can freely express their opinions and participate in organizational decision-making.

The level of professional self-concept among hospice ward nurses in this study was 2.90 out of 4, higher than the 2.51 reported by Kim and Jung [21] among oncology ward nurses. Hospice care is based on a multidisciplinary team approach in which each team member is required to establish their own professional role while also understanding the professional roles of other team members and collaborating with them [28]. This structure may help nurses to develop a clear understanding of their professional expertise, which may be related to the findings of this study. Among the subdomains, “satisfaction” had the lowest score, possibly because hospice ward nurses experience insufficient policy and administrative support in performing their duties. Previous studies [29] have also reported that hospice ward nurses experience a lack of administrative support, facilities, and staffing. Therefore, policies and administrative support measures that reflect the needs of nurses working in the field are required.

In this study, the level of burnout among hospice ward nurses was 2.50 out of 5, lower than the 2.90 reported by Kim [23] among hospice ward nurses using the same instrument. Among the subdomains, “physical burnout” had the highest score, which may be attributed to shift work and the high proportion of direct nursing care provided by hospice ward nurses. However, hospice ward nurses may experience different types of burnout than nurses in other departments, and the factors contributing to burnout may also differ [30]. Therefore, further studies using measurement instruments with more detailed subdomains are required to better characterize the specific features of burnout among hospice ward nurses. In addition, because the characteristics and contributing factors of burnout may vary according to the type of hospice service, including inpatient, home-based, consultative, and pediatric and adolescent hospice care, repeated studies involving nurses across various types of hospice services are warranted.

Second, significant differences in the level of burnout according to general characteristics were found in age, marital status, religion, motivation for choosing to work in a hospice ward, and hospital type. The levels of burnout were higher among the participants in their 20s and 30s than those aged 60 years or older and among the unmarried participants than the married participants. Younger nurses may experience greater psychological burdens because of limited experience with end-of-life situations and caring for bereaved families, as well as less-established values regarding death and dying. In contrast, being married may be associated with a buffering effect against burnout through stable family support from spouses or children. Regarding religion, the participants without a religion had a higher level of burnout than those with a religion. Nurses with a religion may attribute religious meaning to death and loss and may interpret death not simply as an end but as a source of hope in another dimension based on their beliefs about the afterlife. Among the motivations for choosing to work in a hospice ward, the group with “extrinsic motivation” had a higher level of burnout than that with “intrinsic motivation.” This finding differs in part from that of Lee [9], who studied hospice ward nurses. Further research is needed to clarify the relationship between motivation for choosing to work in a hospice ward and burnout. Regarding hospital type, the general hospital group had a higher level of burnout than the hospital group. However, with previous research being insufficient to support this finding, further repeated studies are warranted.

Third, burnout among hospice ward nurses was significantly and negatively correlated with humanistic knowledge. Humanistic knowledge can serve as a foundation for developing a holistic understanding of patients, suggesting that it is a particularly important competency for hospice ward nurses who provide holistic care to terminally ill patients and their families. Therefore, systematic and effective educational programs are needed to help hospice ward nurses cultivate humanistic knowledge across various fields. At the institutional level, such programs could be developed in collaboration with community resources, such as public libraries and cultural programs offered by local governments. However, in this study, humanistic knowledge was not identified as a factor influencing burnout among hospice ward nurses. This finding suggests that humanistic knowledge may not directly affect burnout but may have an indirect effect through other psychological or occupational factors. In addition, because humanistic knowledge is related to understanding and empathy toward human beings, it may share some conceptual similarities with professional self-concept and nursing workplace spirituality. Therefore, future studies should further clarify the role of humanistic knowledge by examining its mediating effects or conducting structural equation modeling. Furthermore, the lack of sufficiently developed instruments for measuring humanistic knowledge among nurses may have limited the extent to which their occupational characteristics were adequately reflected. Accordingly, future research should develop a humanistic knowledge measurement instrument that sufficiently reflects the characteristics of nursing work and further investigate the relationship between humanistic knowledge and burnout.

Fourth, professional self-concept was identified as the most influential factor affecting burnout among hospice ward nurses. This finding is consistent with that of Kim and Jung [21], who also identified professional self-concept as a factor influencing burnout among oncology ward nurses. These findings suggest that professional self-concept can serve as an important psychological resource for hospice ward nurses by helping them develop a positive perception of their professional roles and prevent burnout. Therefore, it is necessary to develop and implement educational programs that incorporate professional practices and communication skills tailored to hospice care characteristics in order to promote the development of a healthy professional self-concept among hospice ward nurses. In addition, continued efforts to raise awareness about hospice care through active public relations and educational activities targeting the general public and hospital staff are required to enhance the professional pride of hospice ward nurses.

Age was the second most influential factor affecting burnout among hospice ward nurses. Similarly, Choi [30], who studied hospice ward nurses, reported age as a factor influencing burnout. In this study, the participants in their 20s and 30s experienced higher levels of burnout, with younger nurses showing higher levels of burnout. This finding suggests that younger nurses may be more vulnerable to the emotional burden experienced during hospice care because they have relatively less clinical experience and experience in caring for patients who are terminally ill. Future studies should examine the differences in burnout patterns and major contributing factors according to age, and tailored burnout management strategies that consider individual characteristics should be developed. Instead of adopting a one-size-fits-all approach, burnout management programs should incorporate content that reflects the actual interests and needs of younger nurses through regular needs assessments and the use of various social networking resources.

Finally, nursing workplace spirituality was identified as a factor influencing burnout among hospice ward nurses. Jin et al. [16] reported a significant correlation between nursing workplace spirituality and burnout among hospital nurses. These findings suggest that nursing workplace spirituality may play an important role in preventing burnout by helping hospice ward nurses find meaning and value in their work and develop positive relationships with their colleagues. Therefore, programs should be developed and implemented to enhance nursing workplace spirituality as part of burnout management for hospice ward nurses. Such efforts may include developing and implementing spirituality training programs for nurses at the ward or hospital level, regularly providing opportunities for staff members to express appreciation and share positive feedback with one another, and offering workshops in which all hospice team members can participate regardless of their professional discipline.

The study findings identified professional self-concept, age, and nursing workplace spirituality as major factors associated with burnout among hospice ward nurses, suggesting that a multifaceted approach that considers both individual characteristics and organizational factors is required to prevent and manage burnout among hospice ward nurses. Based on these findings, future studies should develop and implement burnout management programs aimed at enhancing professional self-concept and nursing workplace spirituality among hospice ward nurses and evaluate their effectiveness. In addition, burnout should be actively managed through tailored approaches that consider differences in burnout patterns according to age. As the participants were conveniently sampled from hospice ward nurses at a limited number of specialized hospice and palliative care institutions in the selected regions, caution should be exercised when generalizing or broadly interpreting the findings. Furthermore, because this study employed a descriptive survey design to examine the relationships among variables, there are limitations in making causal interpretations regarding the relationships among professional self-concept, age, nursing workplace spirituality, and burnout. Future repeated and longitudinal studies involving hospice nurses from diverse regions and institutions are needed to examine the relationships among these variables more clearly. In addition, this study primarily limited its review of previous research to the domestic literature to reflect the unique occupational context of nurses working within Korea’s healthcare system and hospice and palliative care environments. Consequently, a comprehensive, but limited, examination of international research trends was performed, which could restrict the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, future research should broaden the scope to include international literature and conduct comparative analyses of global trends.
