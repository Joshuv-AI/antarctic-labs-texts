Acute malnutrition among children in the Gaza Strip, Palestine: May 2024–November 2024
Authors: Dana Hassneiah, Rana Sobh, Fadi Abed, Abdallah Aqel, Mohammed ElDakroury, Marwa Saleh, Amir M Mohareb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeople in the Gaza Strip have been denied food and medical care through war and displacement since October 2023, introducing the risk of famine in the region. We sought to estimate the proportion of children assessed in nutritional centres with moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in the Gaza Strip during this war.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of nutritional assessments for children up to 5 years old in the Gaza Strip, Palestine, from May 2024 to November 2024. We used de-identified data from humanitarian relief clinics to determine the proportion of MAM and SAM. We defined SAM as weight-for-length Z score <−3. For children above 6 months, we additionally used mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) <11.5 cm to identify SAM. We defined MAM as weight-for-height Z-score between –2 and −3, or MUAC 11.5–12.4 cm. We used logistic regression to determine factors associated with acute malnutrition.ResultsOf 22 901 children (49.6% girls; 12.0% in North Gaza), there were 2042 (8.92%, 95% CI 8.56% to 9.28%) children diagnosed with SAM or MAM during the study period. SAM was higher in North Gaza/Gaza City than in South/Middle Gaza (15.7% vs 0.4%, p<0.001). In North Gaza/ Gaza City, SAM was associated with having a deceased father (adjusted OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.06) and in children <6 months (adjusted OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.96). The prevalence of SAM decreased in North Gaza/Gaza City during this study period.ConclusionA high proportion of children under 5 years old had evidence of SAM or MAM, particularly in North Gaza and Gaza City, which has been subjected to more intense bombings and restrictions in aid. Cessation of hostilities and resumption of humanitarian aid relief is urgently needed for children in Gaza.Keywords: Nutrition, Global Health, Child health, Violence

Introduction

The Gaza Strip is one of the most densely populated regions of the world with more than 2 million people living in a total area of 365 km2.1 Approximately half of the population of Gaza is under the age of 18 years old.1 Since 7 October 2023, Israel’s military has caused widespread destruction of civilian infrastructure in the Gaza Strip, including healthcare facilities, water infrastructure and residential homes.2,4 In this context, clinicians and humanitarian workers have reported the epidemic spread of infections, including diarrhoea, acute jaundice syndrome and the resurgence of poliovirus 25 years after eradication in the region.5 6 Acute food insecurity has been a major factor increasing the susceptibility of the population to infectious diseases, particularly among young children.

The war on the Gaza Strip has been characterised by severe limitations in food importation and availability. In March 2024, the Famine Review Committee, a specialised committee of technical experts on food insecurity, warned that the population in Gaza was facing ‘imminent famine’.7 Data from the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) on the risk of famine in Gaza warned that an estimated 65% of the entire population was in either ‘catastrophic’ or ‘emergency’ conditions of food insecurity as of March 2024.7 More than 1 year later, in May 2025, this forecast worsened as an updated IPC report estimated that 75% of the population was in either ‘catastrophic’ (IPC Phase 5) or ‘emergency’ (IPC Phase 4) conditions, with the remaining in ‘crisis’ (IPC Phase 3).8

Despite the urgency of the public health crisis of food insecurity in the Gaza Strip, there has been limited published data on the prevalence of acute malnutrition in the region since October 2023. One published study of 1200 children between February 2024 and June 2024 estimated that 16.6% had evidence of malnutrition.9 The Gaza Strip has had limited access to healthcare services, nutritional aid and reconstruction, even during a brief de-escalation of hostilities between January 2025 and March 2025. Furthermore, the region is cut-off from access to most humanitarian and medical research teams around the world, which limits the availability of data from the region. Our objective was to use clinical data from humanitarian clinics to estimate the proportion of children with moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in the Gaza Strip for the latter half of 2024.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of nutritional assessments in the Gaza Strip, Palestine, from May 2024 to November 2024. We chose these study dates as there were high-quality data collected for analysis during this time period. We used de-identified data from a humanitarian relief operation in the Gaza Strip to determine the proportion of MAM and SAM in these clinics. We used the available data to determine factors associated with malnutrition during the study period. This project was approved by the institutional review board of MassGeneral Brigham and by the leadership of MedGlobal.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the study design or statistical analysis. The study concept, design and results were formulated with Gaza-based clinical staff and community health workers.

Setting and population

We used data from MedGlobal, an international humanitarian relief organisation working in Gaza since 2018. Clinical sites included nutrition stabilisation centres and an outpatient therapeutic programme for children with malnutrition. We collected data from two clinical nutritional centres in North Gaza and Gaza City affiliated with hospitals. In Middle and South Gaza, data were collected from ten different clinical centres and also included medical sites of screening of displaced persons situated in sheltered encampments. All clinical staff were trained on community-based management of acute malnutrition based on the UNICEF Global Nutrition Cluster guidelines. Training occurred under the supervision of a nutrition specialist. Clinicians at these facilities followed WHO-approved guidelines and clinical procedures, including SAM diagnosis and treatment protocols, paediatric nutritional rehabilitation and medical management of complications.10

Data collection

Trained clinical staff measured height, weight and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) for all patients in the clinics in North Gaza and Gaza City. A rigid length board was used for measuring height for children in this age group. Measurements were performed once. Clinicians in North Gaza and Gaza City also recorded children’s shelter status as being in a school or residential building/tent and the vital status of the child’s parents if that information was available at the time of evaluation. In Middle and South Gaza, data were collected for MUAC measurements but not height and weight.

Outcome definitions

Our primary outcome of interest was SAM, primarily based on adapted WHO definitions.10 For children younger than 6 months of age, SAM was operationally defined as any of (1) Weight-for-length Z score <−3 or (2) The presence of symmetrical pitting oedema. For children aged between 6 months and 59 months, SAM was defined as any of (1) Weight-for-height Z-score <−3, (2) A MUAC <11.5 cm or (3) The presence of symmetric pitting oedema. The outcome variable was dichotomised (SAM vs no SAM) for logistic regression analysis. Our secondary outcome of interest was global acute malnutrition, including SAM and MAM. MAM for ages between 6 months and 5 years was defined according to WHO criteria, which includes weight-for-height Z-score between –2 and −3, or MUAC between 11.5 to 12.4 cm, with the absence of symmetrical pitting oedema.

Statistical analysis

We report categorical variables as number (percentages). We categorised children ages into 0–5 months, 6–23 months and 24–60 months. We combined the limited data available from May 2024 with the full month of June 2024. We compared the proportion of acute malnutrition detected in North Gaza/Gaza City and Middle/South Gaza using χ2 testing. We used multivariable logistic regression to determine factors associated with SAM (model 1) and SAM or MAM (model 2) based on the covariates available for analysis in North Gaza/Gaza City. For this analysis, we divided the study period into three time periods (May 2024 to July 2024; August 2024 to September 2024 and October 2024 to November 2024) based on our hypothesis that increasing aid access between March 2024–July 2024 would manifest as a lower burden of malnutrition in the latter months of our study period in North Gaza and Gaza City. To evaluate the change in malnutrition over time, we used logistic regression to test trend by individual month. We excluded observations with incomplete data. We used R (2024) V.44 for analyses.

Results

Our study population included 22 962 children evaluated during the study period. Data were available on 22 901 (99.7%) children (table 1). There were 11 370 (49.6%) girls and 11 398 (49.8%) boys. Ages of the children were <6 months (143, 0.6%), 6 to 23 months (8162, 35.6%) and 24 to 60 months (14 595, 63.7%). There were 2743 (12.0%) children evaluated in North Gaza/Gaza City and 20 158 (88.0%) children evaluated in Middle/South Gaza. Among those in North Gaza/Gaza City, there were 2105 (76.7%) children sheltering in residential areas or tents while 621 (22.6%) were sheltering in schools. We found that 139 (5.1%) children had a deceased mother and/or father. The likelihood of having a deceased parent remained stable over time during the study period.

There were 2042 (8.92%, 95% CI 8.56% to 9.28%) children diagnosed with acute malnutrition during the study period. SAM was present in 516 (2.25%, 95% CI 2.06% to 2.44%) children while MAM was present in 1526 (6.66%, 95% CI 6.34% to 6.98%) children. SAM and MAM were higher in North Gaza/Gaza City than in Middle/South Gaza: SAM was 15.7% versus 0.4% (p<0.001) and MAM was 44.4% versus 1.53% (p<0.001), respectively.

We analysed factors associated with SAM or MAM in North Gaza/Gaza City, which had a higher prevalence of malnutrition and where more covariates were recorded by the clinical teams during the study period (table 2). SAM was more prevalent in children <6 months (adjusted OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.96) and 6–23 months (adjusted OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.84) compared with 24–60 months. SAM was associated with having a deceased father (adjusted OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.06) and an assessment in May 2024–July 2024 (adjusted OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.55 to 2.53). Both SAM individually and severe or MAM combined were less frequently observed in girls compared with boys (adjusted OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.69 to 1.07 and adjusted OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.99, respectively). There was no statistically significant association between malnutrition and shelter type.

In North Gaza/Gaza City, the highest frequency of SAM occurred in May 2024 and June 2024 (33.1%, 95% CI 25.0% to 41.1%). SAM declined until its nadir in October 2024 (4.5%, 95% CI 0% to 13.2%) and then rose again in November 2024 to 9.2% (95% CI 0% to 18.5%) (figure 1). Temporal analysis by logistic regression showed a decreasing prevalence of SAM over time in North Gaza/Gaza City (OR for each subsequent month 0.69, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.74; p<0.001). However, the combined prevalence of SAM and MAM in North Gaza/Gaza City did not change during the study period (OR for each subsequent month 0.97, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.02; p=0.18). In Middle/South Gaza, the combined prevalence of SAM and MAM was highest in May 2024 and June 2024 (3.6%, 95% CI 0% to 8.45%) and decreased to 1.3%–1.5% between September and November. The OR of combined SAM and MAM for each subsequent month of observation in Middle/South Gaza was 0.83 (95% CI 0.78 to 0.88).

Discussion

In this study of children in the Gaza Strip who were subjected to siege and war since October 2023, we found that 8.92% of children evaluated in clinical nutrition centres throughout the region were diagnosed with severe or MAM. The present study represents one of the few large analyses of malnutrition using clinical data collected during the ongoing war in Gaza, which has been characterised by an unprecedented restriction of nutritional aid and widespread targeting of civilian infrastructure. Malnutrition represents an acute hazard for children in conflict zones as there is a synergistic relationship between acute malnutrition and other adverse health effects in children. Specifically, acute malnutrition can serve as both a risk factor for and a consequence of infections of public health importance, including epidemic infections which have spread throughout the Gaza Strip since 2023.6

Our study leverages data collected under conditions of intense security risk as healthcare workers, clinical centres and patients seeking medical care have all been considered combatants by the Israeli military since the start of the hostilities.11 12 Using these data, we found that 2.23% of children presenting to clinical sites were diagnosed with SAM and 6.66% were diagnosed with MAM. Our findings are consistent with the assessments of the Famine Review Committee throughout the conflict and with other analyses of humanitarian clinic data and modelling projections of acute malnutrition in the Gaza Strip.7 13

A dramatic finding in this study was the high number of severe and MAM in North Gaza/Gaza City (15.68% and 44.40%, respectively) compared with Middle/South Gaza (0.42% and 1.92%, respectively). While this is consistent with other studies of malnutrition in the region during an overlapping time period,13 it may also be a subject of sampling bias as data from the South region included shelter-based nutritional assessments which are likely to capture healthier children, whereas data from the North was restricted to medical and nutritional stabilisation centres. A separate study of 1200 children in Gaza between February 2024 and June 2024 estimated that 6.7% and 9.6% of children below age 5 years had severe and MAM, respectively, also with higher estimates among children in North Gaza.9 The IPC report from the Famine Review Committee also warned against greater food insecurity in North Gaza, which is consistent with our findings in this study.7

The spatiotemporal variation in acute malnutrition found in this analysis suggests a direct link between the intensity of hostilities and the impact on children throughout the war. From October 2023 until February 2024, the Israeli government closed the Erez and Zikim crossings into North Gaza and prevented most aid convoys from passing from South Gaza to North Gaza,14 which likely explains the regional differences we observed in malnutrition. In the first several months of the hostilities, civilian infrastructure in North Gaza sustained a high volume of bombings.3 We found an increased prevalence of malnutrition in May 2024 to July 2024 compared with August 2024 to November 2024, also consistent with estimates from other researchers.13 This temporal observation is associated with brief periods of more aid permitted into North Gaza from March 2024 to June 2024,15 which would have manifested in less malnutrition in the latter months (ie, 60–90 days after aid delivery) of the study period. Another recently published study of acute malnutrition found that the prevalence of acute malnutrition in children rose after November 2024, particularly in Rafah, consistent with the Israeli military further restricting aid delivery and escalating violence in that governorate.16

Importantly, we also found that orphaned children were more likely to be diagnosed with malnutrition, with nearly twice the odds of SAM in children whose fathers were deceased compared with children whose parents were both alive. This crucial finding serves to highlight the impact of war on families and the more specific health impact on children in Palestine, many of whom suffer from physical injuries and emotional trauma.17 18 Following the data period reported in this study, the situation in the Gaza Strip worsened considerably. After a brief pause in hostilities between January 2025 and February 2025, there was a near complete blockade on entry of all food and aid for eleven weeks between March 2025 and May 2025. This resulted in a precipitous rise in malnutrition in infants and young children, and the IPC declared famine in the Gaza Strip in August 2025.16

The most important interventions needed to address the childhood malnutrition crisis in the Gaza Strip are a complete cessation of hostilities, free mobility for the population and unfettered entry of humanitarian aid and medical resources. Given the updated IPC reports of famine in the Gaza Strip, a minimum of 2000 tonnes of food aid per day is needed to stabilise food security, with approximately 1000 tonnes of ready-to-use therapeutic food for cases of SAM.19 20 Integration with water, sanitation, hygiene and infant and young children feeding interventions is essential to prevent relapse and epidemic infection. Sustained humanitarian corridors and sufficient fuel for bakeries are critical to rebuild essential nutrition services. Despite a ceasefire being declared in October 2025, insufficient aid has been permitted to the region according to the UN World Food Programme and civilians in the Gaza Strip have still been subjected to attacks by the Israeli military.20

These findings should be interpreted within the strengths and limitations of the study design. An important strength of the study is the inclusion of high-quality data from North Gaza and Gaza City, which has been limited from other humanitarian entities. Additionally, the association between having a deceased parent and malnutrition is a novel finding and one that warrants further investigation. Regarding limitations, the accuracy of the anthropometric measures used in this study is limited by the resource limitations impacting healthcare providers in the Gaza Strip since October 2023. Specifically, use of MUAC may have limitations in serving as a proxy for weight-for-height measures, though it has been found to be predictive of mortality in children facing health risks in other settings.21 Additionally, sampling for this study is unlikely to be representative of the general paediatric population of the Gaza Strip for a variety of reasons. For example, these data are subject to survivorship bias as families who are injured or missing in the rubble of destroyed buildings would be unrepresented in these data despite being at high risk of being malnourished. Young children whose parents are deceased would be less capable of accessing clinical centres, which would suggest that our results underestimate the true burden of malnutrition in the region. On the other hand, families with children who are not food insecure may be under-sampled in the clinical sites, particularly if accessing a clinical site poses a security risk to displaced families in the context of the Israeli military’s deliberate targeting of healthcare facilities.2 This may partially explain the higher proportion of SAM and MAM observed in North Gaza compared with the South.

In conclusion, we used data from a large humanitarian relief operation in Gaza Strip to estimate the proportion of children 5 years and younger who were diagnosed with acute malnutrition. We found a high prevalence of acute malnutrition overall and a disproportionate prevalence of malnourished children in North Gaza/Gaza City. The spatiotemporal trends outlined in this study suggest a direct impact on children of the Israeli siege of the Gaza Strip, the restriction of aid and the widespread targeting of civilian infrastructure and families, including healthcare facilities. An immediate cessation of hostilities with resumption of humanitarian aid and safe passage for patients and medical personnel is necessary for the health of children in the Gaza Strip.
