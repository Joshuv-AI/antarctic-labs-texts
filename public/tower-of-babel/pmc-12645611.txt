Community-involved care transition interventions to support essential care partners of stroke survivors: a rapid review protocol
Authors: Jaylyn Leighton, Marianne Saragosa, Brina Ludwig-Prout, Evan MacEachern, Michelle L A Nelson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEssential care partners (ECPs), also known as family caregivers, play a critical role in the Canadian healthcare system across the continuum of care, particularly in managing complex conditions like stroke. With the rising number of stroke incidents occurring in Canada each year, there is an increased need for caregiver assistance to help manage the care needs of stroke survivors as they transition to home and community services. Although existing research has highlighted the practical and psychosocial needs of stroke ECPs, these challenges have been mainly overlooked. The lack of integrated intersectoral care services across stroke care pathways places additional significant burdens on caregivers, leading to increased stress, social isolation and a decreased quality of life. Nelson and colleagues’ novel Discharge Assistance and Supports at Home model uses community-based interventions mobilised through intersectoral partnerships and volunteers as human resources to facilitate grassroots solutions to the discharge and transition challenges often faced by stroke survivors. As an extension of this work, this rapid review will investigate and detail community-involved or community-led interventions that have been proven effective in addressing the unmet needs of stroke ECPs during critical care transitions. The findings of this review will identify what works, for whom and in what context regarding community-involved caregiver-centred transition interventions to inform the creation of an actionable Research Agenda—DASH-Caregiver.MethodsThis rapid review will be conducted using the updated guidance on methods used in Cochrane rapid reviews of effectiveness. The search strategy will be refined by the study team with assistance from an information specialist and applied to six databases: Medline, Cochrane, Embase, CINAHL and PubMed. Grey literature will be searched using Google search engines, targeted websites and consultation with knowledge holders. Two research team members will conduct a two-stage screening process to determine study eligibility. Data from eligible studies will be extracted using a piloted charting form and synthesised narratively.Ethics and disseminationThis review protocol does not require ethics approval, as no data have been collected or analysed. The results will be shared with key knowledge holders through publications and presentations and incorporated into the team’s future research.

Introduction

Essential care partners (ECPs) (also known as caregivers) supporting those with complex care needs, like those following a stroke incident, face significant challenges in the post-hospital-to-home care transition period.15 ECPs are unpaid, typically untrained individuals—often middle-aged women caring for ageing parents—who provide medical, practical and psychosocial support to a family member, partner, friend or neighbour, with caregiving roles spanning diverse intersectional demographics that shape access to and experiences of care.6 ECPs face significant task-shifting, where once shared responsibilities like childcare, household duties or elder care become their sole responsibility7 8 alongside added caregiving duties such as transportation to medical appointments and medication assistance.5 8 These increased demands, combined with existing responsibilities, create challenges in managing their caregiving role, leading to physical, emotional and financial strain.5 8 9 Despite the availability of robust data and established best practices for stroke care pathways and rehabilitation, including early supported discharge10 and transitional support for stroke survivors,1115 support for ECPs caring for stroke survivors during critical care transitions to home and community care remains under-researched and under-resourced. In the context of stroke care, critical care transitions refer to high-risk points in a survivor’s journey—such as the shift from acute care to home or community settings—that involve complex medical needs, potential complications and require intensive support and coordination, whereas non-critical transitions involve more stable conditions, less complex care and reduced medical oversight. Furthermore, there is limited knowledge regarding the involvement of community-based organisations and interventions in supporting ECPs during critical transition periods.16 17 Synthesising community-involved or community-led interventions for stroke ECPs during critical transitions will help identify effective strategies, the population they benefit, and the context in which community interventions are most impactful.16 17 This rapid review18 will explore caregiver-centred19 stroke support strategies during critical care transitions.

Background

The rising incidence of stroke globally20 and in Canada21 22 is further complicated by increasingly complex care needs and comorbidities associated with stroke care,23 indicating an increasing number of stroke survivors who require necessary care from ECPs. Following a stroke incident, up to 80% of survivors are discharged home, where ECPs provide most of the daily care required during the transition and beyond.2428 ECPs find themselves in roles for which they are unprepared or inadequately trained,2 with few integrated support strategies—defined as coordinated systems of care, resources and guidance—to help them manage complex care needs during critical transitions, like hospital-to-home.5 11 The most demanding tasks for stroke ECPs, including managing finances, addressing complex behaviours and care needs, and providing emotional support, are linked to poorer caregiver outcomes, such as negative effects on mood and overall well-being.29

Caregiving burdens significantly impact the well-being of ECPs, limiting their time, energy and capacity for self-care9 30 and meaningful social or leisure activities.25 According to the 2024 Ontario Caregiver Organization Spotlight report, 43% of ECPs reported feelings of depression, 45% faced financial difficulties, 52% felt the need for a break and 73% expressed concern about their ability to continue caregiving.31

In the context of stroke care, a meta-synthesis of qualitative studies on stroke survivors and ECPs revealed that caregiving often requires a complete life restructuring, including an alteration to existing relationships, and has a significant effect on psychosocial well-being.9 While participating in meaningful social and leisure activities has been shown to impact the psychosocial well-being of stroke ECPs positively,32 many ECPs continue to face social isolation and loneliness.303336 This ongoing caregiver burden highlights the need for targeted interventions that address the unmet needs of ECPs, especially during critical transitions when ECPs are particularly vulnerable to stress and overwhelming feelings.11 To address these challenges, ECPs require education on care management, as well as practical and psychosocial support, during the transition to help them cope with their emotional and mental health needs.3740

Nelson and colleagues highlight that non-governmental organisations (NGOs) or third-sector organisations, when integrated effectively through intersectoral partnerships, are well-positioned to support both stroke survivors and ECPs during critical care transitions.16 Evidence suggests that ECPs who access community support services, such as counselling or respite, experience better health management and report high satisfaction with these programmes.30 41 42 However, the progress in integrating community-led services to support ECPs during transitions has been limited.16 17 The uptake of community-based services remains low, with the 2024 Canadian Centre for Caregiving Excellence report6 showing that only 21% of ECPs use home and community services, leaving these resources underused.41 42 This is partly due to fragmented partnerships between hospital/acute care and community services, limited awareness or access to available supports, especially in rural or Indigenous communities with fewer resources, and inequities in healthcare access—issues most acutely affecting vulnerable populations.3441 4345 This underscores the need for better integrated community-involved or community-led interventions to support ECPs during critical transitions.

The novel Discharge Assistance and Supports at Home (DASH) model developed by Nelson and colleagues illustrates how intersectoral partnerships and community-based interventions can create compelling, action-oriented solutions for addressing the needs of stroke survivors.16 17 46 In collaboration with an NGO, DASH provides a model for leveraging community assets and partnerships to enhance care transitions for stroke survivors through a volunteer-led intervention. However, a key limitation of the DASH model is the exclusion of stroke ECPs, which hinders a complete understanding of how ECPs’ practical and psychosocial needs can be better supported during critical care transitions. Specifically, despite what is known about the benefits of community-based interventions and intersectoral partnerships,516 17 4749 there is limited understanding of how the DASH model can apply to stroke ECPs. Recent scoping reviews have focused on accessing community-based services50 and care delivered by nurses51 but fail to consider how integrated community-led interventions can support the care transition needs of ECPs. What sets the current review apart is the direct focus on community-involved interventions (concept) for stroke ECPs (population) during care transitions to home and community care (context). Specifically, this rapid review will investigate how components of the DASH model have been used to support ECPs during critical transitions while applying a stroke lens. We will focus our inquiry on the activities implemented, the context of their implementation and the resulting outcomes. The insights from this review will inform the mobilisation of community-involved caregiver-centred interventions to support stroke ECPs and guide future models and practices of care aimed at reducing caregiver burdens.

Methods

This paper presents the protocol for a rapid review18 that will be used to inform the creation of an actionable Research Agenda. A rapid review methodology is particularly well-suited for this research stage, as it facilitates the identification and exploration of studies that have used DASH components to support ECPs in transition through a stroke lens, enabling the timely synthesis of relevant literature for practical application. This review will be guided by the updated guidance on methods used in Cochrane rapid reviews of effectiveness.18 It will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines to ensure procedural rigour52 (see online supplemental material). Our research team used aspects of the population, concept and context (PCC) framework to guide the development of the protocol title, research questions and inclusion criteria. The review is set to begin in June 2025, with the dissemination of results anticipated within 1 year. This rapid review protocol has also been registered in the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/ygsne/).

Step one: topic refinement—setting the research question

This review aims to explore existing literature of the use of community-involved and community-led interventions for stroke ECPs during critical care transitions. The proposed review will address the following RQs:

The research team developed the research questions without the public’s involvement. In alignment with the recommendations put forth by Garritty and colleagues to involve knowledge users,18 one author (BLP) is an ECP with lived experience in stroke caregiving who is positioned involved with a regional caregiving advocacy group, and the other three authors are Ph.D.-trained in qualitative health service research. The findings of this rapid review will be used to identify effective transitional interventions and pinpoint existing gaps for stroke ECPs.

Step two: searches

A preliminary search strategy was developed in collaboration with the Information Specialist, Health Science Librarian (CW), and the primary (JL) and second (MS) author to guide the final search process (see online supplemental file 2).53 We will follow the recommendations from Garritty and colleagues to develop the search strategy, which will be peer-reviewed by all our research team members and approved before execution.18 Relevant peer-reviewed literature will be identified through a search of five key databases (Medline, Cochrane, Embase, CINAHL, PubMed) using subject headings and text words related to the following concepts: ‘community-involved interventions,’ ‘caregivers,’ ‘transitional care’ and ‘stroke.’ Following the initial screening of the study selection process, the research team will collaboratively assess the need for grey literature and additional supplemental searches.18 To identify studies not captured in the initial searches, we will manually review the reference lists of included articles and perform forward screening by examining the citation index of studies that cite the eligible articles included in the review.18

Step three: study selection—title and abstract and full text screening

The results of the searches will be uploaded to Covidence to facilitate the review process, and duplicate studies will be removed.18 Two study authors (JL and MS) will pilot test 50 records to determine authors’ level of agreement and to test and revise the screening form and adapt the eligibility criteria displayed in table 1 below as needed to ensure reviewer consistency, resolving discrepancies through discussion.18 The same two study authors (JL and MS) will screen approximately 20% of the records to assess reviewer agreement. If agreement is high (eg, k is ≥0.8), we will proceed with single screening (JL); if agreement is low, we will proceed with the dual reviewer screening until adequate agreement is reached.18

Publications that do not meet the eligibility criteria will be excluded. If the abstract is unavailable or if there is uncertainty about inclusion, a full-text review of the citation will be conducted. Sources deemed potentially relevant will be retrieved and thoroughly assessed against the eligibility criteria through a full-text review conducted by the same independent reviewers (JL and MS). The rapid review will document and report the reasons for excluding sources at the full-text screening stage that do not meet the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies in the review process will be resolved through discussions with the reviewers until a consensus is reached. A third reviewer (MLAN) will intervene to resolve disagreements if and where consensus cannot be reached. The results of this process will be presented in full using a PRISMA flow diagram.54

Eligibility criteria

To ensure a comprehensive review, community-involved interventions will be broadly defined to include any community-involved or community-led programmes, services or strategies targeted to support ECPs during critical transitions (eg, hospital-to-home and postdischarge planning and support). While the review will prioritise studies focused directly on ECPs as the primary subject, studies involving stroke survivors and ECPs will also be included. No restrictions will be placed on a date or study design to ensure comprehensiveness. We also limited our search to studies published in English.

Exclusion criteria will include articles that focus on populations other than stroke, target stroke survivors rather than ECPs or are not implemented during care transitions (eg, in-hospital or in-patient interventions). Commentaries, conference abstracts or posters that do not offer sufficient details about stroke ECP needs in transition will be excluded. Our research team made these decisions collaboratively to capture studies that specifically implement DASH model components for stroke ECPs. As Levac and colleagues recommended, the inclusion and exclusion criteria may be adjusted iteratively, with regular team meetings held to discuss and finalise the criteria during the piloting phase.55 See table 1 for study selection eligibility criteria in alignment with elements of the PCC framework.

Step four: extracting the data

The data will be extracted and limited only to important data fields that address the review question,18 including author details, publication year, study type, geographical location, ECP context/population, types/modality of community-involved interventions, use of intersectoral partnership, utilisation of volunteers, study findings or results, implications for practice, identified future directions and limitations. The data chart will be co-created by the independent reviewers (JL and MS) using the Covidence software. Using the data chart, the two independent reviewers (JL and MS) will complete a piloting exercise (at least two studies) to help identify misunderstandings and reduce disagreements during the data verification process.18 The data chart will be shared with all members of the study team to incorporate all authors’ perspectives on essential information, and it will be modified as needed during the extraction process from each included source, with any changes detailed in the final rapid review. One independent reviewer (JL) will extract the data, which will then be verified by a second reviewer (MS).18 Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with the same third reviewer (MLAN).

Risk of bias assessment

As recommended by Garritty and colleagues,18 we will use the AMSTAR 2 to assess risk of bias for this rapid review.56

Step five: synthesis

Data will be reported following the PRISMA-P checklist.54 Two study authors (JL and MS) will independently review the charted data and include articles to identify key topic categories for organising the extracted studies. The extracted data will be synthesised using a descriptive content analysis approach,57 guided by a 5W framework (what, where, when, why and how)(RQ1), and assess the extent to which intersectoral partnerships, volunteers as human resources and leisure modalities were mobilised to support the integration of the intervention (RQ1a, b and c). The synthesis will be shared and presented to the study team for review and consultation on dissemination tactics. During consultations with the study team, we will also explore community-based or community-led interventions for stroke ECPs in transition that our findings may not capture (RQ2). The synthesis of the review findings will highlight study limitations, knowledge gaps and opportunities for future research related to community-involved interventions supporting stroke ECPs during transitions.

Patient and public involvement

This manuscript does not include any personal or medical information relating to an identifiable individual. The public was not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this work.

Ethics and dissemination

This review protocol does not require ethics approval, as no data have been collected or analysed. The results will be shared with key knowledge holders through publications and presentations and incorporated into the team’s future research.

Discussion

This rapid review marks the first step in developing an actionable Research Agenda—DASH-Caregivers—to improve stroke ECPs’ experiences during transitions by integrating community-involved and community-led strategies to address their unmet practical and psychosocial needs. These insights will be used to guide a co-design consultation process that will involve stroke stakeholders, including ECPs and community organisations, to collaboratively create a Research Agenda that addresses the unmet needs of stroke ECPs. Once established for stroke ECPs, this Research Agenda will be adapted for other chronic and complex care needs, ensuring better support for ECPs across Canada in their homes and communities. This work is critical, as ECPs are vital to the Canadian healthcare system. Without timely support, the growing burdens on their health and well-being will remain a looming crisis, affecting both stroke caregivers and those they care for.
