AI-Powered Conflict Management in Open RAN: Detection, Classification, and Mitigation Corresponding author: Abdul Wadud (email: abdul.wadud@ucdconnect.ie).
Abdul Wadud 1 2 , Fatemeh Golpayegani 1 , and, Nima Afraz 1

Abstract
Open RAN ( RAN ) was designed with native AI ( AI ) as a core pillar, enabling AI -driven xApp and rApp to dynamically optimize network performance. However, the independent ICP ( ICP ) adjustments made by these applications can inadvertently create conflicts– direct, indirect, and implicit, which lead to network instability and KPI ( KPI ) degradation. Traditional rule-based conflict management becomes increasingly impractical as Open RAN scales in terms of xApp , associated ICP , and relevant KPI , struggling to handle the complexity of multi- xApp interactions. This highlights the necessity for AI-driven solutions that can efficiently detect, classify, and mitigate conflicts in real-time. This paper proposes an AI -powered framework for conflict detection, classification, and mitigation in Open RAN . We introduce GenC, a synthetic conflict generation framework for large-scale labeled datasets with controlled parameter sharing and realistic class imbalance, enabling robust training and evaluation of AI models. Our classification pipeline leverages GNN , Bi-LSTM, and SMOTE-enhanced GNNs, with results demonstrating SMOTE-GNN’s superior robustness in handling imbalanced data. Experimental validation using both synthetic datasets (5-50 xApps) and realistic ns3-oran simulations with OpenCellID-derived Dublin topology shows that AI-based methods achieve 3.2 × 3.2\times faster classification than rule-based approaches while maintaining near-perfect accuracy. Our framework successfully addresses ES ( ES )/ MRO ( MRO ) conflict scenarios using realistic ns3-oran and scales efficiently to large-scale xApp environments. By embedding this workflow into Open RAN ’s AI -driven architecture, our solution ensures autonomous and self-optimizing conflict management, paving the way for resilient, ultra-low-latency, and energy-efficient 6G networks.

Introduction

Open
RAN
is increasingly recognized as a promising framework for next-generation communication systems, as it decouples software and hardware while enabling a broader and more flexible ecosystem. A key concept in Open
RAN
is the native integration of
AI
, where intelligent functionalities are embedded directly into the Open
RAN
architecture rather than added as external modules in the form of
xApp
,
rApp
, and
dApp
. In this vein, the O-RAN Alliance has recently released a comprehensive research report outlining a vision for
Native AI in O-RAN

[
21
]
. This document details how
AI
-based
xApp
can be employed to control and optimize network
ICP
, particularly in the
RIC
(
RIC
)-thus unlocking new levels of automation and performance in 6G networks. However, in 6G’s
AI
-native
RAN
, where dozens of vendor
xApp
will run concurrently over shared control parameters. Unmanaged conflicts in such environment can directly threaten reliability, automation, and SLA guarantees, making scalable conflict management a foundational requirement for 6G.

Despite these advances, the scope of the O-RAN Alliance report focuses primarily on leveraging
AI
to improve network control and optimization with minimal attention to the inherently multi-dimensional challenges of conflict management, stating briefly about conflict mitigation in Section 6.2.3 of the technical specification in
[
7
]
. In other words, while Open
RAN
’s native
AI
functionality may prevent
xApp
from issuing conflicting commands in theory, there remains a critical gap in addressing how to detect, classify, and resolve these conflicts, especially when a large number of
xApp
from various vendors simultaneously interact with thousands of
ICP
and
KPI
. This gap becomes even more pronounced as networks transition to 6G and the operational complexity of Open
RAN
continues to grow. We dive deep into this matter in Section
IV-B
.

Managing conflicts among different
xApp
is crucial for ensuring smooth and efficient communication
[
30
,
2
]
. Therefore, this research focuses on
xApp
conflict detection and classification in Open
RAN
, where different software applications called
xApp
control aspects like power distribution, resource allocation, and mobility management. However, as these
xApp
operate on shared
ICP

KPI

[
31
,
12
,
15
]
.

O-RAN Alliance’s technical specification has classified

conflicts into direct, indirect, and implicit categories
[
7
]
. Previous studies often relied on manual detection or rule-based heuristics for conflict resolution
[
2
,
1
,
31
]
. While these methods are effective for small-scale Open
RAN
deployments, they become impractical in large-scale Open
RAN
networks comprising a large number of
xApp

ICP
. In such scenarios, real-time conflict detection, classification, and mitigation must scale efficiently to meet strict latency constraints, which makes
AI
-driven classification essential.

This work builds upon our previous works
[
30
,
29

, which are focused on conflict mitigation strategies and introduced a

xApp

AI

This paper advances conflict management in Open
RAN
by addressing detection, classification, and mitigation of

-driven approaches. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We develop a stochastic simulation framework, GenC, to generate large-scale, labeled conflict datasets across various

We implement and evaluate GNNs, Bi-LSTM, and SMOTE-enhanced GNNs for scalable conflict classification. We analyze their ability to handle imbalanced conflict datasets and demonstrate that SMOTE-GNN significantly enhances classification robustness compared to standard

We compare manual, rule-based conflict classification against

We validate our AI-powered conflict management framework using realistic ns3-oran simulations with OpenCellID-derived Dublin topology, demonstrating
3.2\times
speed improvement over rule-based methods while maintaining near perfect accuracy in practical

This work provides a comprehensive and future-proof framework for conflict management in Open

by integrating synthetic conflict data,
AI
-driven classification, and scalable mitigation strategies, paving the way for autonomous,
AI
-native 6G networks.

II
Understanding Conflicts among xApps

The definitions of
xApp
conflicts provided by O-RAN Alliance’s technical specification
[
7
]
are not comprehensive, not defined and explained clearly with proper example. Specifically, the subtle difference between indirect and implicit conflict is hard to understand. Therefore, we provide a concise but comprehensive definitions of these conflicts with real-
xApp
examples in this section based on our recent work in
[
31
]
.

Fig. 2
:
Various
xApp
conflicts in Open RAN.

•

Direct Conflict:
This occurs when multiple
xApp
explicitly control the same parameter that affects a network performance indicator. For example, if two applications try to adjust the same frequency band, their conflicting instructions may degrade signal quality. To explain this, we considered two
xApp

MRO
and
CCO
(
CCO
) in Fig.
2
. Both shares a common parameter
TXP
(
TXP
), which may create a direct conflict between these two
xApp
if conflicting setting for
TXP
is instructed from them in subsequent request.

•

Indirect Conflict:
This happens when an
xApp
modifies a parameter that is not directly controlled by another
xApp
but still affects a
KPI
managed by that other
xApp
. Imagine adjusting the transmission power of a base station, which in turn influences another
xApp
that manages user connectivity. To explain this, we considered the
MRO

xApp
in Fig.
2
, where its
KPI

CDR
(
CDR
) is degraded when changes are made to the
RET
(
RET
) parameter. Notably,
RET
is not an
ICP
for the
MRO

xApp
; instead, it is an
ICP
associated with the
CCO

xApp
. This demonstrates how modifications by one
xApp
can indirectly impact the performance of another.

•

Implicit Conflict:
An implicit conflict arises when an
xApp
changes a parameter that is not explicitly linked to the affected
KPI
but still causes unexpected network behavior. This could be due to hidden dependencies between the changed parameter and the affected
KPI
or something else within the system that are not immediately visible. To explain this, we considered the
xApp

ES
and
MLB
(
MLB
) in Fig.
2
, where
MLB
detects high
TL
(
TL
) on a particular cell and attempts to offload users to neighboring cells by lowering
CIO
(
CIO
), making those neighboring cells more attractive. On the other hand,
ES
autonomously reduces
TXP
in the neighboring cells to conserve power, unaware that
MLB
is actively trying to push users into those cells. As a result, many offloaded users experience poor signal strength and higher handover failures, leading to degraded
QoS
, causing an implicit conflict. Here,
TL
is the key metric affected by the implicit interaction between
ES
and
MLB
, illustrating how such conflicts can degrade network performance.

A real-world simulation environment that captures all the aforementioned types of conflicts is extremely difficult to construct due to several practical limitations. First, Open RAN deployments require the integration of multiple independently developed
xApp
, each designed to control specific aspects of the network such as mobility, power, or scheduling. Since most of these
xApp
are not open source, developing them for large scale simulation are time consuming and not feasible. Even when multiple
xApp
are deployed in the
Near-RT-RIC
, the manifestation of actual conflicts–especially diverse types like indirect or implicit conflicts–is not guaranteed. Moreover, inducing all possible conflict types in a real system would require exhaustive and carefully timed parameter manipulations under varying network loads and topologies, which is neither scalable nor feasible in practice. Safety, reliability, and operational policies also limit the scope for experimentation in live networks, making it nearly impossible to observe edge-case conflicts or rare cascading failures.

To overcome these challenges, our previous work
[
29
,

IV
System Model and Problem Formulation

IV-A

System Model

We consider an Open
RAN
environment comprising:

•

A set of
m
xApps
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\}
deployed on the RIC, each potentially from different vendors;

•

A set of
n
controllable
ICP

\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}\}
, where typically
n\gg m
;

•

A set of
k

KPI

\mathcal{K}=\{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{k}\}
reflecting network objectives and
SLA
(
SLA
)s.

Each xApp
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
manipulates a subset of parameters
\mathcal{P}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and targets a subset of KPIs
\mathcal{K}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
. The global control state at time
t
can be represented as a vector
\mathbf{p}(t)=[p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t),\dots,p_{n}(t)]
. The system’s observed performance is measured by
\mathbf{k}(t)=[k_{1}(t),k_{2}(t),\dots,k_{k}(t)]
.

Let
A_{i,j}=1
if
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
act on overlapping parameter(s), i.e.,
\mathcal{P}_{i}\cap\mathcal{P}_{j}\neq\varnothing
, indicating potential for conflict. The system dynamics can be expressed as:

\mathbf{k}(t+1)=f\hbox{$\left(\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$}\mathbf{p}(t),\mathbf{w}(t)\hbox{$\left)\vbox to0.0pt{}\right.$},

where
f(\cdot)
models the RAN evolution and
\mathbf{w}(t)
represents exogenous factors (e.g., traffic, channel variation).

The control objective is to coordinate all
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
such that the aggregate KPIs meet or exceed their respective SLAs:

k_{\ell}(t)\geq\mathrm{SLA}_{\ell},\quad\forall k_{\ell}\in\mathcal{K}.

This abstract model captures the high-dimensional, multi-agent, and multi-objective nature of Open RAN control.

IV-B

Problem Formulation

In the context of Open
RAN
systems, the optimization problem grows significantly more complex as the number of
xApp
,
ICP
, and
KPI
increases. Specifically, when a large number of
xApp
from various vendors simultaneously interact with thousands of
ICP
and
KPI
, the solution space expands exponentially due to the combinatorial explosion of possible parameter configurations. Each
xApp
introduces its own set of
ICP
and optimizes for specific
KPI
(e.g., throughput, latency, energy efficiency), which may conflict with the objectives of other
xApp
. This creates a high-dimensional optimization problem characterized by intricate inter-dependencies and potential conflicts among
xApp
, which requires sophisticated coordination to achieve
SLA
goals. Furthermore, the computational complexity of evaluating and optimizing thousands of
KPI
across heterogeneous
xApp
poses significant scalability challenges, necessitating advanced algorithms to navigate the vast solution space efficiently.

To dive deep into the technicalities of the scaling, we pinpoint three specific reasons:

•

Combinatorial interaction space:
A modern
gNB
(
gNB
) already exposes hundreds of tunable configuration leaves in each 3GPP-NR object (e.g., gnbDuFunction, cellCarrier, beamformingGroup), and the O-RAN YANG tree augments these with dozens more per leaf class; across all RAN functions, this easily exceeds
2000
ICPs per cell
[
22
,
23
]
. If
m
xApps each touch even
n<<2000
overlapping parameters, the potential pair-wise collision space grows as
O(m^{2}\times n^{2})
. For only
20
xApps tweaking 5 shared parameters, the conflict matrix already has
\approx 10000
entries to check every control interval.

•

High-dimensional cause/effect ambiguity:

KPI
are rarely mapped one-to-one to a single parameter. With hundreds of correlated counters (e.g., BLER, CQI variance, PRB utilisation), a small adjustment in power-control might surface seconds later as mobility failures or slice
SLA
violations. Classifying which parameter triggered the drift, therefore, becomes an under-determined inverse-problem in a
KPI
space, something that adds huge computational overhead to a simple rule-based “conflict detection” method to resolve.

•

Multi-timescale instability:

Near-RT-RIC

xApp
loop every 10ms to 1s, while non-RT
rApp
may reconfigure hourly. The larger the parameter set, the more likely two loops will touch the same parameter at different cadences. This is a classic control-theory “integrator wind-up”. Recent sandbox studies show that even two benign xApps (throughput-maximisation vs. energy-saving) can drive throughput down by 50% once their decisions start oscillating
[
12
]
.

By addressing these challenges, our proposed AI-driven approach enables effective management of large-scale, multi-vendor xApp interactions, thereby enhancing system performance and scalability.

2

VI-D

Relation to O-RAN WG2 and WG3 Conflict-Mitigation Efforts

Recent O-RAN Alliance activities have begun formalizing conflict-management principles across different layers of the architecture. WG2 focuses on
policy- and configuration-level
conflicts that arise before actions reach the Near-RT RIC. The WG2 Study on A1 Policy Conflict Mitigation
[
6
]
specifies how overlapping or incompatible rApp policies can be detected and arbitrated using priorities or pre-emption. A complementary WG2 effort, the R1 Service-related Conflict Mitigation report
[
5

potential
conflicts before actions are applied to the network.

WG3, in contrast, addresses
runtime
conflicts that occur when multiple xApps concurrently issue E2-related control actions. The WG3 Conflict Mitigation technical reports
[
9
,
8
]
formalize direct, indirect, and implicit conflict types and define high-level procedures for conflict detection, resolution, and avoidance. These reports specify architectural behaviour and “solution requirements” but intentionally do not prescribe concrete machine-learning algorithms or provide evaluation methodologies.

Our CMS complements both WG2 and WG3 by providing the missing algorithmic layer for scalable runtime conflict handling. Using GenC-generated datasets and ns3-oran traces, we implement and evaluate GNN, Bi-LSTM-, and SMOTE-GNN-based classifiers capable of identifying conflicts at KPI/ICP level when dozens of xApps operate simultaneously. While WG2 and WG3 define
what
conflicts must be handled within the O-RAN architecture, our CMS demonstrates
how
such conflicts can be efficiently detected and classified in practice. Thus, the methods developed here can serve as candidate algorithmic realizations for the conflict-management capabilities anticipated, but not yet specified, in the current WG2 and WG3 outputs.

VII-B

Online Learning and Continuous Adaptation.

Open
RAN
’s

In our architecture, continuous adaptation is realized via periodic or drift-triggered retraining in the Non-RT RIC. Newly collected KPI/EI samples are buffered in the Training Cache; once a retraining threshold (e.g., time-based or volume-based) is met, the conflict-classification model is re-trained, packaged as a new inference bundle, and registered in the AI Model Catalog. After passing governance checks, this updated model version is re-deployed to the Near-RT RIC, replacing the previous one without changing the real-time control logic.

VIII
Synthetic Dataset Generation with
GenC

To train and benchmark the AI classifiers we need a large,
labeled
stream of xApp interactions. Live testbeds rarely exhibit all conflict modes, so we build the dataset synthetically with our
GenC
generator.
4
4

4

GenC extends the stochastic model proposed in
[
29
,
30
]
; meta-datasets are available at
https://github.com/dewanwadud1/GenC
.
Below we give a high-level view of the synthetic data generation process.

VIII-A

Entity synthesis and relationship maps

1.

Enumerate ICPs and KPIs.

For
M
xApps we create

\displaystyle P=2M+\!\left\lfloor M/2\right\rfloor
ICPs and

K=P/2
KPIs, matching the “
\sim 2000
parameters per gNB” scale
reported in
[
23
]
. Each symbol is named

p_{i}
or
k_{j}
to keep the feature vectors compact.

2.

Unique seeding.

Every xApp
x_{m}
is first given one exclusive ICP and one exclusive
KPI; this guarantees
direct
influences exist.

3.

Controlled sharing (
p=0.3
).

Remaining parameters and KPIs are re-drawn and assigned with
probability
p
to
two
distinct xApps, creating realistic

shared-control
surfaces without flooding the graph.
The result is the mapping tables P2X and K2X.

4.

KPI-parameter groups.

For every KPI we union the ICPs of all xApps that manage it,
obtaining the set

\text{P}_{k_{j}}\!\subseteq\!\{p_{1},\dots,p_{P}\}
and thus P2K.

5.

Indirect-conflict injection.

To emulate latent couplings, one parameter from a
different

xApp is randomly added to each

\text{P}_{k_{j}}
. This produces
indirect-conflict tuples without breaking exclusivity.

6.

Implicit parameters.

At least one ICP is kept unassigned; its random tweaks later
degrade KPIs via the Gaussian channel, yielding implicit
conflicts.

These six steps give the static overlays P2X, P2K, K2X, the

shared-parameter graph

G_{\text{XP}}
and the

KPI-coupling graph

G_{\text{KP}}
(Algorithms

generate_xp_graph
and
generate_kp_graph

[
31
]
). They
are stored once and reused during simulation.

VIII-B

Time-series simulation loop

For each second
t\in[1,T]
we:

(i) pick an ICP
from three buckets (i.e., shared, unassigned, or indirect) with specific probability on each parameter to mirror field-log statistics
[
32
]
;

(ii) update its value
by a random drift

\Delta p\!\sim\!\mathcal{U}(-70,70)
;

(iii) recompute affected KPIs
via a Gaussian response

k_{j}(t)\;=\;\exp\!\left[-\frac{(p_{i}+\xi)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right],\qquad\xi\!\sim\!\mathcal{U}(-20,20),

which captures the local, non-linear sensitivity of a KPI to its parameters that mimics their real-world properties
RAN

[
11
]
;

(iv) compare

k_{j}(t)
with its SLA threshold

\tau_{j}\!\sim\!\mathcal{U}(0.6,0.8)
and store only the
new

violations, mimicking the near-RT alarm stream; we use different SLA bounds for different conflicts intensities.

(v) label the row
with the rule engine of
Alg.
1
, producing the ground-truth class

No/Direct/Indirect/Implicit Conflict
.

All features, including the RCP string, parameter/KPI snapshots, mapping columns
and conflict labels are appended in a single CSV line.

VIII-C

Realism and class balance

GenC reproduces three key properties observed in operator logs

[
32
,
27
]
:

•

Rare events.

Only
<10\,\%
of rows carry a conflict label, matching the
“sub-1% KPI-breach” rate in live RANs
[
4
]
.

•

Skewed taxonomy.

Direct:Indirect: Implicit ratios are
roughly
3:5:2
. This is because parameter changes made by one
xApp frequently affects KPIs governed by other xApps through shared
dependencies, whereas direct conflicts arise only when both the parameter
and the violated KPI belong to the same xApp, and implicit conflicts are
triggered only through secondary effects. A recent study tests models on a synthetic dataset where only 10-40% of the instances involve actual conflicts, and the rest of the time (90-60%), everything runs smoothly
[
4
]
.

•

Heterogeneous influence graph.

Only a handful of ICPs are shared by many xApps, while the vast
majority are unique to one or two controllers, resulting in a

heavy-tailed
degree distribution for
G_{\text{XP}}
.
This “few-parameters-highly-shared” pattern is qualitatively consistent with the parameter-sharing observations reported in the PACIFISTA open-RAN test cluster
[
12
]
.

Consequently, the datasets drive the classifier under the same class-
imbalance and structural sparsity it will face in production, while
remaining fully reproducible and parameterizations for ablation studies.

X-A

ns3-oran Simulation Setup and KPI Data Collection

Our ns3-oran simulation was configured with the OpenCelliD-derived Dublin topology comprising 13 eNodeBs and 117 UEs to generate realistic conflict scenarios between ES and MRO xApps. The simulation focused on collecting comprehensive network
KPI
data while systematically varying
ICP
values, particularly transmission power (TXP), to induce authentic conflicts between energy optimization and mobility management objectives.

The data collection process spanned a total simulation time of 540 seconds. During this period, the simulation monitored six critical
KPI
: Energy Efficiency, Power Consumption, Throughput, Handover Rate, Call Drop Rate, and Handover Failure Rate. Simultaneously, six key
ICP
were tracked: Transmission Power (TXP), Time to Trigger (TTT), Cell Individual Offset (CIO), Neighbor List (NL), Hysteresis (HYS), and Radio Electrical Tilt (RET).

Each timestamped entry in the collected dataset represents a complete snapshot of the network state, including parameter values, KPI measurements, Service Level Agreement (SLA) thresholds, and the resulting conflict classification. The ES xApp controlled energy-related parameters to minimize power consumption, while the MRO xApp optimized mobility parameters to reduce handover failures and call drops. This created realistic conflict scenarios where energy-saving actions (e.g., reducing TXP) conflicted with mobility optimization requirements (e.g., maintaining adequate signal strength for seamless handovers).

The resulting dataset contains detailed conflict annotations generated using our rule-based classification algorithm (Algorithm
1
). Each row includes the conflict type classification (Direct or No Conflict), the instructing xApp, the violated KPIs, and comprehensive relationship mappings (P2X, P2K, K2X), which are essential for both rule-based and AI-based conflict detection methods. X-D

Scalability Analysis for Multi-Vendor Open RAN

The ns3-oran results provide important insights for scaling to multi-vendor Open RAN environments. While our simulation focused on the two-xApp ES/MRO scenario, the performance characteristics align with our synthetic dataset analysis across 5-50 xApps. The consistent sub-millisecond detection times for AI methods suggest that the proposed framework can handle the complexity of modern Open RAN deployments where dozens of xApps from different vendors simultaneously optimize network parameters.

The
3.2\times
speed improvement over rule-based methods becomes increasingly critical as the number of xApps grows. Based on our complexity analysis in Section
VI-B
, rule-based classification time scales as
O(n×v×a)
, while AI methods maintain O(1) per-sample complexity through batched tensor operations. This fundamental difference ensures that AI-based classification remains viable even as Open RAN networks scale to hundreds of xApps and thousands of ICPs, as envisioned for 6G deployments. The small error bars and stable macro-F1 scores across all xApp counts and conflict intensities further support that this scalability is not only theoretical but also reflected in robust empirical behaviour.

XI
Conclusion

Conventional conflict management in Open
RAN
often detects issues too late
and struggles to adapt to the increasing complexity of
xApp
interactions.
In contrast,
AI
-based methods provide a proactive approach by enabling the
network to anticipate conflicts, classify them with high accuracy, and rapidly
mitigate their impact. Our work demonstrates that AI-based conflict
classification achieves
3.2\times
speed improvement over rule-based methods
while maintaining near perfect accuracy in realistic Open RAN scenarios using ns3-oran. The GenC framework successfully generates training datasets that reflect real-world conflict distributions, enabling robust AI model development.

We acknowledge that the present study evaluates scenarios with up to 50 xApps, which is far smaller than the thousands of ICPs and KPIs envisioned for large-scale O-RAN and future 6G deployments. Scaling to such sizes is currently constrained by the computational cost of GenC dataset generation and the near-quadratic growth of GNN neighborhood sampling as controllable parameters increase. Addressing massive-scale deployments therefore remains an open challenge. Future work will explore distributed and multi-node training, hierarchical and clustered graph representations, and adaptive learning mechanisms to enable scalable CMS operation under the diverse, dynamic, and unpredictable conditions expected in 6G networks.
