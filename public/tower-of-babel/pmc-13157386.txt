Transcriptome analysis and characterization of cathepsin B-like protein in Melanaphis sorghi in response to resistant sorghum
Authors: Kang Lei, Xiao Sun, Yongbang Li, Zhiqiang Hu, Yating Guo, Mingshu Wei, Yaping Yang, Kunpu Zhang, Huihui Bi, Guangwei Li, Daowen Wang, Di Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sorghum aphid (Melanaphis sorghi), a phloem-feeding insect, is a major pest affecting sorghum production. Despite advances in understanding plant resistance mechanisms, the molecular responses of aphids to resistant host plants remain poorly characterized. Here, we aimed to elucidate transcriptional changes in sorghum aphids feeding on the resistant sorghum variety HN16 and to identify key aphid regulatory genes involved in host adaptation. RNA-seq analysis identified 1,388 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in aphids feeding on HN16. Expression profiling revealed coordinated regulation of genes involved in apoptosis and detoxification. Through weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), 10 candidate response genes were identified. Notably, knockdown of the DEG MsCathB1, encoding a cathepsin B-like protease, significantly impaired aphid fitness on resistant plants. Functionally, MsCathB1 also suppressed cryptogein-induced plant cell death and hydrogen peroxide accumulation. These findings suggest that sorghum aphid responses to host resistance are closely linked to apoptosis-related pathways, and that MsCathB1 may function as a virulence effector modulating both aphid performance and plant immunity. This work provides new insights into aphid-host interactions and supports the development of RNAi-based strategies for aphid control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11032-026-01670-9.

Introduction

Over approximately 400 million years of coevolution, herbivorous insects and their host plants have developed a wide array of defense and counterdefense strategies (War et al. 2012; Shih et al. 2023). Unlike chewing insects, piercing-sucking insects such as aphids cause minimal tissue damage by inserting slender stylets to access phloem sap. During feeding, they secrete salivary components capable of manipulating host defenses (Powell and Hardie 2000; Tjallingii 2006). In plants, well-characterized resistance mechanisms include phytohormone signaling, production of toxic secondary metabolites, and phloem occlusion (Züst and Agrawal 2016). Recognition of herbivore-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs) or secreted effectors can trigger plant immune signaling cascades, leading to calcium influx, activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and subsequent defense responses such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, callose production, and upregulation of defense-related genes (Rossi et al. 1998; Ngou et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2021; Shih et al. 2023). While plant responses have been extensively investigated, much less is known about how phloem-feeding insects respond to resistant hosts.

The sorghum aphid, Melanaphis sorghi, as named Melanaphis sacchari during the period 2013 to 2020 (Nibouche et al. 2014, 2018, 2021), is now recognized as one of the most devastating pests of sorghum. Aphids damage sorghum directly by extracting phloem sap throughout all developmental stages (Souza and Davis 2021). Infestations can reach densities of 10,000 individuals per plant, with honeydew secretion impairing photosynthesis and nutrient absorption, ultimately leading to significant yield losses or complete crop failure (Singh et al. 2004; Bowling et al. 2016). Additionally, aphids transmit numerous plant viruses, compounding their economic impact (Rassaby et al. 2003). Current control measures rely heavily on synthetic insecticides, which have led to the emergence of resistant aphid populations (Bass and Nauen 2023). To counter this, multi-omics approaches—spanning genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—have been employed to uncover resistance loci and dissect plant-insect interactions (Almeida-Silva and Venancio 2023; Wang et al. 2023; VanGessel et al. 2024). A major breakthrough was the identification of RMES1, a dominant resistance gene isolated from the grain sorghum variety Henong 16 (HN16), which promotes hydrogen peroxide accumulation in response to aphid attack (Wang et al. 2013; Shao et al. 2019).

Transcriptome profiling via RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) is a powerful tool for deciphering aphid molecular responses to host defenses. For example, salivary gland transcriptomics of Sitobion avenae identified calcium-binding proteins and enzymes involved in digestion, hydrolysis, and detoxification, suggesting roles in plant interaction (Zhang et al. 2017). In Macrosiphum euphorbiae, host plant defenses modulated aphid expression of genes involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, xenobiotic processing, oxidative stress, immunity, and hormone biosynthesis (Planelló et al. 2022). Importantly, transcriptome screening has revealed numerous putative effector genes, some of which have been functionally characterized. For instance, the AcE4 protein from Aphis citricidus suppresses INF1- and BAX-induced cell death in plants, indicating a role in immune evasion (Shangguan et al. 2024). Similarly, a combined transcriptomic and proteomic analysis identified Sg2204, an effector from Schizaphis graminum that enhances aphid fitness by inhibiting plant cell death and promoting phloem feeding (Zhang et al. 2022a). Another salivary protein, Sm9723, from Sitobion miscanthi, plays a vital role in suppressing host immunity and facilitating aphid virulence (Zhang et al. 2022b). However, information on HAMPs or effectors specific to M. sorghi feeding on resistant plants remains scarce.

To address this gap, we conducted aphid feeding experiments on the resistant sorghum variety HN16, which carries the RMES1 gene. We performed transcriptome analysis to identify DEGs and key pathways involved in aphid adaptation to host resistance. Among the DEGs, we identified MsCathB1, encoding a cathepsin B-like protease, through WGCNA. Functional analysis demonstrated that MsCathB1 promotes aphid survival and fecundity on resistant plants while suppressing plant immune responses. These findings reveal a novel aphid effector candidate that facilitates host adaptation and offer a potential target for RNAi-mediated aphid control strategies to enhance sorghum yield.

Materials and methods

Plants and insects

Sorghum genotypes HN16 and BTx623 were cultivated in a greenhouse under controlled conditions (28 °C, 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod, 60% relative humidity). HN16 carries the RMES1 gene conferring resistance to sorghum aphids, while BTx623 is a susceptible line widely used in sorghum genomic studies (Paterson et al. 2009; Chang et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2013). N. benthamiana plants were grown under similar conditions at 25 °C.

Sorghum aphids (M. sorghi) were maintained on BTx623 seedlings under the same environmental parameters (28 °C, 16-hour light/8-hour dark, 60% RH) to ensure consistent rearing conditions.

Aphid performance on sorghum genotypes

HN16 and BTx623 seeds were germinated and grown in a growth chamber under the aforementioned conditions. At the two-leaf stage, fifteen vigorous seedlings (five per pot) were selected per genotype. Aphid performance was evaluated by measuring survival rate, host preference, and honeydew secretion. For survival assays, ten apterous adult aphids were placed on each plant and confined within transparent mesh enclosures. Aphids were counted at 3, 6, 24, and 48 h post-infestation. In host choice assays, seeds of each genotype were intercropped in the pots and kept at equal distances from each other (3 plants per genotype, 6 plants total). Five adult aphids per plant were released. The number of insects on each plant were recorded at 3, 6, 12, 24 and 48 h after release (Guo et al. 2018). Honeydew production was assessed as previously described (Shangguan et al. 2018), with minor modifications. Two freshly cut sorghum stems were placed on filter paper within a Petri dish (2.5 cm diameter, 1 cm height). Three aphids, starved for 8 h, were allowed to feed on the stems for 36 h. The filter paper was then stained with 0.1% (w/v) ninhydrin in acetone to visualize secreted honeydew. The area of purple-stained sediment was quantified using ImageJ software.

Sample collection and RNA isolation

Aphids feeding on HN16 were collected at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. For each time point, 300 aphids were sampled in triplicate (100 individuals per replicate) and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions and stored at − 80 °C. RNA concentration and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (USA), and agarose gel electrophoresis (Lonza, Switzerland). Samples with OD260/280 between 1.8 and 2.2 and RNA integrity number (RIN) > 8 were selected for sequencing.

Illumina library construction and RNA sequencing

cDNA libraries were constructed using a TruSeq™ RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Poly-A mRNA was isolated using oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads and fragmented (~ 300 bp) by divalent cations. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random primers and reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand synthesis using a dUTP mix. Sticky ends were converted to blunt ends, and an ‘A’ base was added at the 3′ termini to facilitate adapter ligation. Adapter-ligated fragments were purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads and PCR-amplified to generate the final libraries.

Cluster generation was performed on a cBot system, and libraries were sequenced using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (Wefindbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Wuhan, China). Raw reads were filtered using Fastp (Chen et al. 2018) to remove adapters, ambiguous bases, and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the M. sorghi reference genome (NCBI RefSeq: GCF_002803265.2) using HISAT2, and transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie (Pertea et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2019). Expression levels were normalized as TPM (transcripts per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads). PCA was conducted using R to evaluate variance across time points. RNA-seq data have been deposited in the NCBI SRA under BioProject accession number PRJNA1306752.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with criteria of false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05 and |fold change| > 2. Dynamic expression patterns were clustered using the Mfuzz package based on TPM values. GO enrichment analysis was carried out using GOATOOLS (https://github.com/tanghaibao/GOatools), and significant GO terms were identified using ClusterProfiler and visualized as bubble plots (Yu et al. 2012). KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted using KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do) (Xie et al. 2011), with P ≤ 0.05 considered significant after Fisher’s exact test and Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Regulatory network construction by WGCNA

We use WGCNA package (v1.69) to explore gene regulatory networks (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). The imported data were the TPM values of all 13,690 genes, and all low-expression genes were removed (TPM < 1 in each treatment) according to Zeng et al. 2022; and the top 5000 genes were screened based on the median absolute deviation (MAD) value. Genes were clustered according to the topological overlap similarity. Modules were defined using the Dynamic Tree Cut algorithm (Zhan et al. 2015), with parameters: soft-thresholding power = 14, TOM type = unsigned, minimum module size = 50, and merge cut height = 0.25. Module–trait relationships were determined by Pearson correlation of module eigengenes. Genes with module membership (KME) > 0.8 were defined as hub genes (Yuan et al. 2024). Co-expression networks were visualized using Cytoscape (Shannon et al. 2003).

qRT-PCR validation

To validate RNA-seq results, six highly connected DEGs from the cathepsin B gene family were selected for qRT-PCR. Aphids feeding on HN16 were sampled at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. Total RNA was isolated by RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Japan) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, USA) per the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed using gene-specific primers (Table S4) and SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, Japan) on a Roche LightCycler 480 system. The M. sorghi Actin gene (NCBI accession: XM_025350371) served as the internal control. All assays were conducted in triplicate. Relative expression levels were calculated using 2−△△CT (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

dsRNA artificial diet assay

To synthesize double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) targeting MsCathB1, bioinformatics analysis was firstly performed to check the homologs of MsCathB1 in the M. sorghi genome and no highly similar sequences were identified, which excluded potential off-target silencing. DNA templates were amplified using gene-specific primers dsMsCathB1-F and dsMsCathB1-R, which included T7 promoter sequences. Following PCR fragments purification, MsCathB1-dsRNA (559 bp) was synthesized using a MEGAscript RNAi Kit (Ambion, USA) according to previously described (Jacques et al. 2020; Dong et al. 2022; Cui et al. 2023). A GFP (Green fluorescent protein) -derived dsRNA (500 bp) served as a negative control. The purity, size, and integrity of the dsRNAs were confirmed via agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentrations were quantified by absorbance at 260 nm. Primer sequences used for dsRNA synthesis are listed in Table S4.

The dsRNA artificial diet assay was adapted from previously established protocols with modifications (Whyard et al. 2009; Zhang et al. 2013). In brief, loading 20 µl artificial diet (20% w/v sucrose supplemented with 125 ng/µL dsRNA) between parafilm sheets allowed for aphids to puncture the stretched membrane with its stylet and to feed on the provided diet. Third-instar nymphs were collected from sorghum and starved for 8 h to ensure that each aphid can subsequently consume the artificial food (Hao et al. 2021). These aphids were then transferred with a fine paintbrush and held in place with a sealed and breathable cage to prevent aphids from escaping (Jacques et al. 2020). After 72 h of feeding, 10 surviving aphids were collected for RNA extraction, and MsCathB1 transcript levels were quantified by RT-qPCR to evaluate RNAi efficiency with three replicates. To assess the physiological effects of MsCathB1 knockdown, aphids from each treatment group (mock, GFP-dsRNA, MsCathB1-dsRNA) were transferred onto both resistant (HN16) and susceptible (BTx623) sorghum genotypes. Aphid performance was evaluated by counting adult survivors and newly produced nymphs at 24-hour intervals. Honeydew excretion was also measured on filter paper as previously described. The entire experiment was repeated three times independently.

Cell death assay

To investigate the cell death-inducing potential of MsCathB1, its coding sequence was cloned into a modified pCAMBIA1300 vector, generating a MYC-tagged construct (MsCathB1-MYC). The construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. A GFP-tagged construct was used as a negative control, while a FLAG-tagged cryptogein construct served as a positive control for eliciting programmed cell death (Amelot et al. 2011; Ptáčková et al. 2015). Agrobacterial cultures were grown overnight in LB medium supplied with 50 mg/mL rifampicin and 25 mg/mL kanamycin (28 °C). Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6, 150 µM acetosyringone), adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 0.8, and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The bacterial suspensions were infiltrated into the leaves of four-week-old N. benthamiana plants. Symptoms were visually monitored daily from 2 to 7 days post-infiltration (dpi). Cell death was photographed at 3 dpi. Expression of the recombinant proteins was validated via immunoblotting using anti-MYC, anti-FLAG, and anti-GFP antibodies. All experiments were conducted with three biological replicates, yielding consistent results.

Immunoblot analysis

To detect MsCathB1 protein expression in N. benthamiana, recombinant plasmids (pCAMBIA1300-MsCathB1-MYC, pCAMBIA1300-Cryptogein-FLAG, and pCAMBIA1300-GFP) were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 and infiltrated into tobacco leaves. At 48 h post-infiltration, 400 mg of leaf tissue was harvested and ground in liquid nitrogen. Total protein was extracted using 1 mL of buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM KCl, 20 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM DTT, and 1 mM PMSF. Extracts were boiled at 100 °C, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatants were subjected to 12% SDS-PAGE followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. Immunodetection was performed using mouse monoclonal antibodies: anti-MYC (M192-3; MBL), anti-FLAG (M185-7; MBL), and anti-GFP (M20004; ABmart). Secondary detection was carried out with goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (A0216; Beyotime) at a 1:10,000 dilution.

DAB staining and H2O2 quantification

To assess hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation, N. benthamiana leaves expressing MsCathB1 or cryptogein were subjected to DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) staining 48 h post-infiltration. Leaves were incubated for 10–12 h in DAB-HCl solution (1 mg/mL, pH 3.8), then decolorized with boiling 95% ethanol at 36 h post-infiltration, as previously described (Daudi and O’Brien 2012). Quantitative measurement of H₂O₂ was performed using a commercial assay kit (Invitrogen, A22188) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates. Data were statistically analyzed using Student’s t-test or Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test, with significance set at P ≤ 0.05. Survival analysis of the aphids was performed using the Kaplan-Meier log-rank test.

Results

Aphid performance on a resistant sorghum genotype

To assess the aphid resistance of sorghum genotypes, seedling-stage plants of HN16 (resistant) and BTx623 (susceptible) were inoculated with ten adult aphids each. Fourteen days post-inoculation (dpi), BTx623 plants were completely dead, while HN16 plants remained healthy and exhibited strong resistance (Fig. 1A). Aphid population dynamics differed significantly between the two genotypes. After 24 h, aphid numbers on HN16 were already lower than those on BTx623, with statistically significant differences emerging at 48 h (Fig. 1B), indicating reduced fecundity on the resistant host. In a dual-choice assay, the number of aphids settling on HN16 declined progressively between 3 and 48 h post-inoculation, suggesting active avoidance of the resistant genotype (Fig. 1C). As a proxy for feeding activity, honeydew excretion was also quantified. Aphids feeding on HN16 for 48 h produced significantly less honeydew compared to those on BTx623, indicating impaired sieve element sap ingestion on the resistant host (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that sorghum aphids experience considerable resistance pressure when feeding on HN16.

Transcriptome profiling reveals differentially expressed genes

To uncover the transcriptional responses of sorghum aphids to host plant resistance, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on aphids fed on HN16 at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-inoculation, with three biological replicates per time point. After quality filtering and removal of low-quality reads, each library yielded an average of 69.60 million clean reads, of which 65.17 million (93.68%) were uniquely mapped to the M. sorghi reference genome (Table S1). Correlation analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and gene expression distribution confirmed high consistency among biological replicates (Fig. S1).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified at each time point compared to the 0-hour control using a threshold of fold change (FC) ≥ 2 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 2). A total of 1,388 DEGs were detected across five comparisons (3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h vs. 0 h) (Fig. 2A, Table S2). In all comparisons, the number of upregulated genes significantly exceeded that of downregulated genes (Fig. 2B). A heatmap of DEG expression revealed distinct temporal expression patterns as aphids fed on the resistant host over time (Fig. 2C), reflecting dynamic changes in metabolic and regulatory processes in response to resistance stress.

Temporal dynamics of aphid DEGs during resistant host feeding

Among the 1,388 DEGs, 1,372 annotated genes were clustered into four main expression patterns (excluding 16 unknown new genes). Cluster A contained 316 genes consistently upregulated across all time points. Cluster B included 271 genes that were predominantly downregulated at 24 h. Cluster C comprised 359 genes strongly upregulated at 24 and 48 h, while Cluster D contained 426 genes that peaked in expression at 12 h (Fig. 3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was used to infer the biological roles of these clusters. Cluster A was enriched in genes associated with membrane transport, signal transduction, and molecular binding. Cluster B was enriched for genes linked to extracellular processes, proteolysis, and protein catabolism. Cluster C was primarily associated with microtubule-related movement, while Cluster D included genes involved in transcriptional regulation and tissue development (Fig. 3).

Gene co-expression network identifies candidate effectors

To identify regulatory genes involved in aphid responses to plant resistance, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using transcriptome data. The coexpression network was constructed based on the gene expression of all samples. After filtering raw data in materials and methods, the top 5,000 genes were selected to construct a co-expression network. Based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM), eight major co-expression modules were identified and labeled with distinct colors (Fig. 4A, B).

To characterize module-specific temporal expression, module eigengenes were correlated with six feeding time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h). The turquoise module exhibited an expression pattern closely resembling Cluster B from the earlier time-course analysis (Fig. 4C). As previously described, genes in Cluster B were enriched in stress response-related functions, including apoptosis and detoxification. This led us to focus further on the turquoise module.

Using eigengene-based connectivity (KME ≥ 0.8), 966 hub genes were identified from the 1,584 genes in the turquoise module, suggesting their potential regulatory importance (Fig. 4D). To refine the network, we intersected these hub genes with those from Cluster B, yielding a core set of 120 genes. The top ten most highly connected genes in this network were designated as candidate stress-response regulators (Fig. 4E). The heatmap shows the expression of these ten candidate genes across all time points (Fig. 4F).

Excluding four genes without functional annotation and low expression (LOC112601392, LOC112597948, LOC112601173, and LOC112602245), the remaining six annotated cathepsin B family members (LOC112603908, LOC112602421, LOC112596806, LOC112590854, LOC112593023, and LOC112602405), which are known to be involved in apoptotic processes, were examined by qRT-PCR. Consistent with transcriptome data, these genes appeared a trend for reduced expression upon aphid feeding on HN16 (Fig. 4F, S3). These results indicate that genes associated with apoptosis are strongly repressed in M. sorghi in response to resistant host plants, highlighting their potential roles in mediating resistance stress.

MsCathB1 knockdown reduces aphid fitness

Cathepsins belong to a group of papain-like cysteine proteases, serving as central regulator for insect growth, survival and immunity (Zhao et al. 2005; Saikhedkar et al. 2015; Yu et al. 2019; Ferrara et al. 2020). Among the candidate hub genes, LOC112603908—hereafter named M. sorghi Cathepsin B1 (MsCathB1)—was the most highly connected node in the co-expression network. MsCathB1 was predicted to encode a 322-amino acid protein with N-terminal signal secretion peptide (amino acids 1–20), C-terminal papain-like cysteine protease domain and no transmembrane domain (Fig. S4A). Homologs of MsCathB1 were identified across diverse aphid species, indicating evolutionary conservation and potential functional importance (Fig. S4B).

We then investigated whether the altered aphid behaviour observed in aphids fed on resistant sorghum is mediated by MsCathB1, as this information is crucial to understand the aphids responses to resistant plants. A double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) mediated RNA interference in aphids was used to specifically silence MsCathB1 gene to determine its role in M. sorghi. qRT-PCR demonstrated that MsCathB1 expression was effectively reduced in MsCathB1-dsRNA treated aphids in comparison with the GFP-dsRNA control (Fig. 5A). Silenced aphids showed no apparent morphological differences (Fig. 5A), but their survival on resistant HN16 was significantly reduced (Fig. 5B). The number of nymphs produced by MsCathB1-dsRNA aphids was also significantly less than that produced by aphids in the control groups (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, we performed the ninhydrin chromogenic method to measure the insects honeydew secretion, which indicate the amount of food intake by sap-sucking insects. The results showed that the MsCathB1-dsRNA aphids fed on resistant plants excreted less honeydew than Mock treated or GFP-dsRNA insects fed on the same genotype (Fig. 5D). The reduction in the feeding of MsCathB1-dsRNA aphids was also observed on susceptible sorghum BTx623 (Fig. S5). Overall, these results suggested that MsCathB1 positively affects the feeding fitness of M. sorghi on sorghum leaves.

MsCathB1 suppresses plant defense in tobacco

Papain-like cysteine proteases have been implicated in the suppression of plant pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) in various pathogens (López-Solanilla et al. 2004; Zhu et al. 2004). To assess the potential immunosuppressive role of MsCathB1, Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression was performed in N. benthamiana leaves. The oomycete-derived elicitor cryptogein, a 10-kDa protein secreted by Phytophthora cryptogea, was used as a positive control to induce defense-related gene expression and cell death (Lecourieux et al. 2002). GFP served as the negative control. Expression constructs of MsCathB1-MYC, GFP, and cryptogein-FLAG were driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter and infiltrated into tobacco leaves for 48 h. Cryptogein alone induced pronounced necrosis and chlorosis. In contrast, co-expression of MsCathB1 with cryptogein suppressed necrosis, indicating that MsCathB1 interferes with cryptogein-triggered cell death (Fig. 6A). Immunoblot analysis confirmed successful expression of all transgenes, with equal loading verified by Ponceau staining (Fig. 6B).

Given that ROS accumulation is a hallmark of plant immunity (Lee et al. 2020; Weralupitiya et al. 2024; Gahlowt et al. 2025), we investigated whether MsCathB1 also suppresses ROS production. DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) staining revealed brown deposits in cryptogein-expressing regions, indicative of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation. This response was abolished when MsCathB1 was co-expressed with cryptogein (Fig. 6C). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that MsCathB1 significantly reduced H₂O₂ accumulation induced by cryptogein, compared to GFP control (Fig. 6D). Notably, expression of MsCathB1 alone did not induce ROS accumulation or cell death, indicating it specifically suppresses immune responses rather than triggering them (Fig. 6). These results support a role for MsCathB1 in suppressing host PTI.

Discussion

Herbivorous insects are responsible for an estimated 20% annual loss in global crop yields (Van der Meijden et al., 2014). Among these, M. sorghi is a major pest of sorghum, threatening food security and crop productivity. To address this challenge, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying sorghum defense against aphid infestation and how aphids adapt to resistant genotypes. By integrating RNA-seq with RNA interference (RNAi), we identified a novel role for an aphid-derived cathepsin-type protease in modulating plant defense responses, providing insights that may inform the development of targeted pest control strategies for piercing-sucking insects.

During prolonged evolutionary interactions, sorghum has developed resistance mechanisms that vary in strength and include physical barriers, defense-related phytohormones, and metabolic enzymes (Wang et al. 2013, 2023; Thudi et al. 2024). Among these, the RMES1 locus—comprising two tandem resistance genes, RMES1A and RMES1B—encodes atypical resistance proteins that regulate immune signaling and key defense responses. Prior work has shown that aphid infestation induces a ROS burst and callose deposition in RMES1-carrying plants (Lei et al. 2024). Consistent with this, our study confirmed again that aphids feeding on resistant RMES1 plants exhibited significantly reduced survival and feeding activity compared to those on susceptible wild-type plants. Additionally, the transcriptome comparisons between resistant and susceptible sorghum plants have revealed that several NBS-LRR and disease resistance proteins in the resistant line may confer both antixenosis and antibiosis for M. sorghi, and the genes related to cell wall modification, photosynthesis and phytohormone biosynthesis may also affect the aphid feeding behavior (Tetreault et al. 2019).

Plants defend against herbivorous insects through the production of toxic compounds that disrupt insect physiology and metabolism, thereby limiting damage (War et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2024). In aphids feeding on resistant sorghum, we detected transcriptional changes linked to apoptotic signaling pathways, which plays a critical role in stress-induced tissue remodeling and homeostasis (Wu et al. 2013; Ren et al. 2021). Cathepsins, previously implicated in insect apoptosis across several taxa (Stoka et al. 2007), may play a central role. In our study, six cathepsin B family genes were significantly downregulated in aphids feeding on resistant plants. Notably, silencing MsCathB1—a cathepsin B gene—led to substantial reductions in aphid survival and fecundity, suggesting that apoptosis-related genes are suppressed in response to host resistance. These findings support a model in which aphid responses to resistant sorghum involve apoptosis modulation.

Additionally, aphids feeding on RMES1 sorghum exhibited transcriptional changes in genes also linked to metabolism and development. DEGs were enriched in xenobiotic and glutathione metabolism pathways—key detoxification processes thought to counteract plant chemical defenses. These findings align with the known roles of cytochrome P450s and glutathione S-transferases in detoxification among hemiptera (Vontas et al. 2002; Li et al. 2007; Koirala et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025). Energy metabolism pathways—including pentose and glucuronate interconversion, starch and sucrose metabolism, and arachidonic acid metabolism—were significantly enriched in Cluster B. These are consistent with previous reports that carbohydrate, glycan, and fatty acid metabolism provide energy for insect maintenance and growth on resistant hosts (Arrese and Soulages 2010; Dolezal et al. 2019). These findings indicate that aphid responses to sorghum resistance are mediated in part by stress-responsive gene networks that regulate cell survival and detoxification. Nevertheless, the exact functions of these metabolic genes warrant further exploration.

Cathepsin B genes are massively expanded in aphids, and some exhibit abundant expression in the guts. As important proteases with digestive and hydrolytic functions, cathepsin B proteins play a significant role in the process of aphids extracting nutrients from plant phloem sap (Cristofoletti et al. 2003; Liang et al. 2025). Furthermore, cathepsin B proteins are also expressed in the aphid salivary gland and secreted into host cells during the feeding process (Ramsey et al. 2007; Cui et al. 2012). The present study demonstrated that MsCathB1 protein contains signal peptide as secretory sequence, suggesting that MsCathB1 may be secreted into plant tissues during feeding.

Aphids also secrete effector proteins into host plant cells to manipulate immunity and facilitate feeding. For example, salivary proteins such as Mp1, Mp2, Mp55, and MpMIF from Myzus persicae suppress host defenses by reducing callose deposition, hydrogen peroxide accumulation, or hypersensitive cell death (Pitino and Hogenhout 2013; Elzinga et al. 2014; Naessens et al. 2015; Rodriguez et al. 2017). Similarly, S. miscanthi effectors Sm10 and SmC002 enhance wheat susceptibility by modulating defense signaling (Fu et al. 2023). Cathepsin B family proteins In M. persicae have been identified as major venom components injected into plant tissues, where they are thought to influence host-pathogen interactions (Mathers et al. 2017; Guo et al. 2020). It is tempting to speculate that the MsCathB1 protein may have the same biological function. In this study, MsCathB1 facilitates sorghum aphid feeding and manipulate plant defense, providing evidence supporting that the MsCathB1 could be a conserved effector protein modulating host immunity to benefit aphid performance.

All herbivorous hemipteran insects use their specialized stylets to pierce the plant tissue and reach the phloem to access the nutrient-rich sap (Jiang et al. 2019). Once attacked by insects or pathogens, ROS as a defensive messenger, including H2O2, are rapidly produced and extensively accumulated in plants, thereby inducing programmed cell death (Besson-Bard et al. 2008; Mignolet-Spruyt et al. 2016; Tetreault et al. 2019). Several studies suggest ROS is detected in the vascular bundles of different plant species under biotic and abiotic stresses (Tanou et al. 2009; Gaupels et al. 2017). Loss-of-function experiments of RbohD-silenced tobacco plants shows the ROS accumulation impairs phloem feeding by Myzus persicae (Guo et al. 2019). The apoplastic H2O2 production of vascular tissue has a role in triggering extracellular lignin formation and cell wall metabolism, which is one of the most effective way to limit aphid infestation (Laitinen et al. 2017; Rasool et al. 2017). Thus, it appears that the M. sorghi has the ability to secrete MsCathB1 to inhibit the ROS production in plants, thereby facilitating their feeding in the phloem.

Conclusion

In summary, comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed that aphid responses to resistant (HN16) versus susceptible (BTx623) sorghum genotypes are associated with apoptosis-related signaling. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified ten candidate DEGs, including MsCathB1, which significantly influences M. sorghi feeding behavior and suppresses cryptogein-triggered immune responses in plants. These findings not only uncover apoptosis-related transcriptional responses in M. sorghi but also provide a new candidate effector for RNAi-based pest control strategies. Future studies should focus on identifying the specific targets of MsCathB1 in sorghum and elucidating the mechanisms by which it suppresses ROS production and other plant defenses.
