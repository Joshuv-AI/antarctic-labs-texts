Clonal hematopoiesis and thrombosis risk—what are research and clinical gaps?
Authors: Cornelia Englisch, Cihan Ay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) is characterized by the clonal expansion of hematopoietic stem cells driven by somatic mutations that confer a survival or proliferative advantage. In recent years, numerous studies have identified an association between clonal hematopoiesis and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, particularly thromboembolic events. This has led to in-depth research efforts to uncover a potential underlying causal link and to use this knowledge to find preventive strategies and treatments. Although these efforts have provided important insights into underlying biological pathways and possible clinical implications, significant gaps in both research and clinical practice remain. This article aims to summarize the current evidence and critically evaluate the existing knowledge gaps.

Introduction

Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) refers to the expansion of hematopoietic stem cells carrying acquired somatic mutations that confer a proliferative or survival advantage. In 2015, a common definition of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) was proposed by Steensma et al. [1]. This definition includes the following criteria: (1) presence of CH due to a somatic mutation in a known driver gene with a variant allelic frequency (VAF) of at least 2% or other nondisease-defining clonal cytogenetic alterations; (2) no dysplasia/blasts in the bone marrow; (3) no blasts in the peripheral blood; and (4) paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, and monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis are excluded [1]. Most common are mutations in the genes DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 as well as JAK2, TP53, PPM1D, and SF3B1, which are driving clonal expansion.

It is important to distinguish CHIP from related entities. Age-related CH also describes detectable clonal expansion but does not apply a VAF threshold, thus including smaller clones (<2%) [2]. In contrast, clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance is characterized by the presence of somatic mutations alongside unexplained cytopenia and carries a substantially higher (∼10-fold) risk of progression to hematologic malignancy [3].

Early research on CH aimed to identify a premalignant state that could help detect individuals at increased risk of hematological cancers. However, the absolute risk of progression to malignancy proved to be relatively low (∼1%-2% per year), limiting the usefulness of CH as an early detection tool.

Instead, a different and unexpected signal was uncovered: an increased risk of all-cause mortality, driven in large part by cardiovascular-related mortality in individuals with CH [4]. This observation, initially identified by chance, sparked growing interest in the cardiovascular implications of CH. As evidence accumulated linking CH to cardiovascular events, the field rapidly gained momentum, prompting researchers to investigate underlying mechanisms and the potential causal role of CH in cardiovascular, and especially in thrombotic, diseases.

Overview of Available Evidence

Clinical evidence

CH has been consistently associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, particularly coronary artery disease in diverse clinical studies [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Early studies demonstrated that individuals with CH have a roughly 2-fold higher risk of coronary artery disease and markedly increased odds of early-onset myocardial infarction [4,5]. The magnitude of risk appears to depend on the specific mutated gene: mutations in DNMT3A, TET2, or ASXL1 confer modest risk increases, whereas JAK2 mutations are associated with substantially higher risk [9]. CH has also been linked to higher coronary artery calcification [5] and recurrent cardiovascular events, although some recent randomized trial analyses found weaker or inconsistent associations, particularly for recurrent myocardial infarction [10].

Beyond coronary disease, CH has also been linked to other vascular disorders, including stroke or peripheral artery disease [4]. Several studies and meta-analyses report a modestly increased risk of ischemic stroke, particularly small-vessel disease and cases associated with TET2 mutations, with CH appearing more prevalent among younger stroke patients [11,12]. CH has further been associated with peripheral artery disease and broader pan-arteriosclerosis [13].

Evidence for venous thromboembolism (VTE) is more limited and inconsistent overall; however, JAK2 mutations appear to confer a substantially increased risk, while other CH mutations show weaker or unclear associations [14]. Furthermore, it was reported that in patients at low baseline risk, CH might represent a risk factor [15].

Thus, evidence quality and quantity differ between the arterial and venous systems. Therefore, the association between CH and atherosclerosis and subsequent thrombotic events in the context of accelerated plaque development seems likely. However, if it is also associated with the thrombogenesis in the venous system, which has a different underlying pathomechanism, could not be conclusively shown in studies so far.

Preclinical evidence

CH mutations were proposed to influence atherosclerosis and thrombosis through gene-specific mechanisms, largely driven by inflammation and immune cell dysfunction. TET2 and DNMT3A mutations promote atherosclerosis by increasing inflammatory cytokine production—particularly interleukin (IL)-1β (TET2) and IL-6 (DNMT3A)—mainly from macrophages, leading to larger plaque formation [5,16,17]. These effects can be mitigated in preclinical models by targeting inflammatory pathways, such as the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-6 signaling, or using anti-inflammatory agents like colchicine [16,18]. ASXL1 mutations show less consistent effects in vivo, although they may still enhance inflammasome activation in macrophages [9].

In contrast, TP53 and especially JAK2 mutations act through distinct mechanisms. TP53 mutations promote plaque growth via expansion of mutant macrophages without strong inflammatory signaling [13]. JAK2 mutations have the most pronounced effects, driving both atherosclerosis and thrombosis through multiple pathways, including increased neutrophil extracellular trap formation, enhanced platelet and endothelial activation, and elevated cytokine release [8,[19], [20], [21]]. These changes result in larger, more unstable plaques and higher thrombotic risk. Importantly, targeted interventions—such as JAK-STAT inhibitors, integrin or P-selectin blockade, and IL-1 inhibition—have shown potential in reversing these pathological effects in experimental models [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]].

Translational evidence

In cohort or biobank studies, it was noted that individuals with CH show elevated inflammatory markers, including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, IL-6, and IL-8, with mutation-specific patterns such as increased IL-1β in TET2 and IL-18 in JAK2 mutations [5,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Supporting a causal role, post hoc analyses of the Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) trial demonstrated that anti-inflammatory therapy with canakinumab, a human monoclonal antibody that acts as a potent inhibitor of IL-1β, reduced cardiovascular risk specifically in patients with TET2-mutated CH but not in those without CH or with other mutations [30]. Similarly, colchicine may offer benefit, although results are conflicting so far [27]. Moreover, IL-6 pathway modulation may be a promising treatment approach, as individuals with CH and IL-6 receptor mutation experienced less cardiovascular events than those with CH but without IL-6 receptor mutation [7,31].

Research and Clinical Gaps

Pitfalls—definition, VAF, reporting, and methodology

Although a standardized definition of CH was introduced to improve consistency and enable comparisons across studies, such uniformity is still lacking in the current research landscape. Many studies apply their own definitions of CH, and key diagnostic criteria, such as bone marrow examination, are often unavailable, particularly in large biobank cohorts of otherwise healthy individuals. Even basic clinical data, such as blood count parameters, are frequently missing. While requiring bone marrow data in these settings may be impractical, the routine inclusion of blood count values would be both feasible and valuable.

Furthermore, greater consensus on VAF thresholds would enhance comparability between studies, despite differences in sequencing technologies. The lack of standardization in definitions and methodologies likely contributes to variability in findings and may explain the conflicting results reported across studies.

Mouse models and relevance to humans

Similarly, in cardiovascular research, a wide range of mouse models is used, each reflecting certain aspects of the human condition to varying degrees. If CH influences only specific components of the disease pathophysiology, its effects may or may not be detectable depending on whether a given model adequately captures that aspect. Moreover, most mouse models rely on complete gene knockouts to mimic CH, which does not accurately reflect the human situation, where typically only a subset of hematopoietic cells carries a mutation. In addition, individual mouse studies usually focus on a single gene knockout, thereby representing only gene-specific CH rather than the broader heterogeneity observed in humans.

Gene-specific effects

The strength of the association between CH and cardiovascular outcomes appears to depend largely on the specific mutated gene driving clonal expansion. For example, JAK2-driven CH shows a particularly strong association with early-onset myocardial infarction and VTE, whereas other mutations, such as DNMT3A, have shown weaker or inconsistent associations across studies. These observations raise the question of whether CH should be considered a single entity or rather a group of biologically distinct conditions. A more granular, gene-specific approach to CH research may therefore be necessary. However, this would require substantially larger study populations to ensure sufficient statistical power for meaningful subgroup analyses.

Chicken-or-egg dilemma

Another unresolved issue is the direction of causality. It has been shown that the proliferation of CH clones is enhanced in proinflammatory environments. This aligns with the observation that CH becomes more prevalent with age, a process that is also associated with increasing systemic inflammation, also referred to as inflammaging. However, this raises the possibility that atherosclerotic plaque formation and the associated inflammatory milieu may themselves promote clonal expansion. This bidirectional relationship complicates the interpretation of causality and may have important implications for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Venous vs arterial thromboembolism

The quantity and quality of available data on the role of CH differ substantially between VTE and arterial thromboembolism. While VTE is less common than arterial thromboembolism, it remains a clinically significant cardiovascular event. To date, evidence linking CH to arterial events is more robust, whereas data on VTE are limited and, in part, inconsistent. Addressing this gap will be an important focus of future research.

Clinical consequences

Ultimately, the key question is how these findings translate into clinical practice. CH, particularly in the form of CHIP, is a premalignant condition associated with an ∼14-fold increased relative risk of hematologic malignancy. However, the absolute risk of progression remains low, with an estimated transformation rate of 1% to 2% per year. Importantly, the type of mutation plays a critical role in risk stratification. For example, TP53-driven CH has been associated with a higher risk of therapy-related secondary malignancies, while mutations in genes such as IDH1, IDH2, SRSF2, SF3B1, and RUNX1 are also strongly linked to progression to myeloid malignancies [2]. Despite this knowledge, no preventive or targeted therapeutic strategies are currently available for these patients.

Similarly, no CH-specific treatments exist to reduce cardiovascular risk. In some countries, specialized CHIP clinics have been established to support the management of affected individuals. Recently published clinical guidance [2] recommends focusing on aggressive management of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including systematic assessment using the 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk score, consideration of coronary artery calcium scoring where appropriate, and the use of statins and aspirin according to established indications for primary or secondary prevention.

The question of whether targeted medical interventions can reduce CH-associated risks remains open. Ongoing clinical trials are investigating anti-inflammatory approaches, including inhibitors of IL-1β, IL-18, and the NLRP3 inflammasome, as well as colchicine. The results of these studies will be crucial for determining whether CH represents a modifiable risk factor and how it should be integrated into future clinical practice.

Conclusion

CH represents an interesting research topic, with various implications in diverse diseases and disease entities. While the association between CH and thrombotic cardiovascular events has been described in many clinical studies and preclinical models indicate the presence of causal links, open questions remain (Figure). Research gaps exist as results are limited by substantial heterogeneity in definitions, methodologies, and data reporting, as well as by the limited translatability of existing mouse models to human disease. Importantly, CH appears to have strong gene-specific effects, and the causal relationship of every driver gene and thrombotic outcomes remains unclear. Clinically, although CH is associated with increased risks of hematologic malignancy and cardiovascular disease, no targeted therapies exist yet, and management currently focuses on conventional risk reduction while ongoing trials explore anti-inflammatory strategies. Therefore, further efforts will be necessary to close both—research and clinical—gaps regarding CH and thrombotic risk.FigureAssociation of clonal hematopoiesis and thrombosis risk.
