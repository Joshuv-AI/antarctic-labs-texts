Towards hepatitis C elimination with integrated risk-based screening and decentralized care in Taiwan
Authors: Yen-Po Yeh, Abbie Ting-Yu Lin, Wei-Wen Su, Chih-Chao Yang, Sheng-Lei Yan, Yu-Chin Yao, Hsiao-Chi Wang, Ming-Ling Hsieh, Li-Chen Shih, Hui-Chun Liu, Hui-Hua Chen, Amy Ming-Fang Yen, Sam Li-Sheng Chen, Chen-Yang Hsu, Chao-Chih Lai, Dih-Ling Luh, Rong-Nan Chien, Chien-Jen Chen, Philippa Easterbrook, John W Ward, Tsung-Hui Hu, Tony Hsiu-Hsi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains a major cause of liver disease worldwide and requires effective strategies to achieve elimination targets. Taiwan has a substantial HCV burden, with prevalence varying by region; in Changhua County, prevalence was 4.3% before large-scale intervention. In 2019, Changhua launched a county-wide micro-elimination program—the Changhua Integrated Program to Stop Hepatitis C Infection (CHIPS-C)—which combined existing population screening with targeted outreach to higher-risk groups and decentralized delivery of curative direct-acting antiviral (DAA) treatment. Using individual-level data from a population-based cohort, we compared care cascade performance before and after CHIPS-C implementation. The program was associated with increased screening coverage (53.7% to 90.2%) and fewer individuals screened to achieve similar numbers of diagnosed infections. Improvements were driven by streamlined confirmatory testing and coordinated care delivery. Here we show that CHIPS-C substantially increased the adjusted treatment completion rate (83.9% vs 38.7% before implementation). Model-based projections indicate that these gains could reduce HCV-related liver cancer and mortality to levels meeting World Health Organization elimination targets by 2030. These findings demonstrate that integrating risk-based targeting with existing health systems can markedly improve efficiency and outcomes, providing a scalable model for accelerating HCV elimination in diverse settings.Subject terms: Health care, Hepatitis C

Introduction

The global scale-up of oral direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) has fundamentally reshaped the control of chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. In response to these advances, the World Health Organization (WHO) has articulated an ambitious goal to eliminate HCV as a public health threat by 2030, targeting a 90% reduction in new infections, a 65% reduction in HCV-related mortality, and diagnostic and treatment coverage of 90% and 80%, respectively1. Taiwan is well positioned to meet these targets. Since 2019, its National Health Insurance program has provided full reimbursement for all oral HCV therapies2, enabling broad and equitable access to curative treatment. Nevertheless, HCV remains a persistent public health challenge. Nationwide serosurveys report age-adjusted seroprevalence ranging from 0.8 to 7.6%3,4, with a prevalence of 4.3% observed in Changhua County prior to the implementation of county-wide micro-elimination efforts5.

Globally, several high-burden regions have adopted large-scale screening and treatment initiatives to accelerate progress toward WHO elimination goals. Egypt demonstrated that a community-based test-and-treat strategy, supported by strong community engagement and infection control measures, achieved coverage exceeding 85% and reduced HCV incidence from 2.4 to 0.37 per 1000 person-years6,7. Modeling studies from Georgia8–10 and Pakistan11–13 further projected substantial declines in liver cancer mortality following national elimination programs. Despite these successes, few studies have generated locally grounded, risk-stratified estimates of HCV infection across diverse target populations—evidence that is essential for designing efficient risk-based micro-elimination strategies. This gap is particularly salient in Asian settings, such as Taiwan, where marked geographic and population-level heterogeneity persists3,4. Moreover, existing frameworks seldom account for the complex, hierarchical structure of real-world care cascades required to evaluate the long-term effects of risk-based interventions on downstream outcomes, including HCV-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver-related mortality.

To address these gaps, Changhua County in central Taiwan launched the Changhua Integrated Program to Stop Hepatitis C Infection (CHIPS-C) in 201914. Building on a longstanding county-wide cancer and chronic disease screening infrastructure5,15,16, CHIPS-C implemented a comprehensive, risk-based micro-elimination strategy targeting populations at elevated risk—including people who inject drugs17–20, incarcerated individuals aged ≥12 years20–23, persons living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) or acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)20,24, patients receiving dialysis or living with chronic kidney disease25, and individuals with diabetes26—while simultaneously extending coverage to the underlying population. Screening was delivered through two complementary portals: a community-based integrated screening program for eligible adults and opportunistic screening for individuals of all age with abnormal liver function or those aged 40 years or older attending routine subsidized health check-ups5,15,16. In this study, we integrate real-world, individual-level data with a Bayesian hierarchical cascade-of-care model linked to a Markov process describing the natural history of chronic HCV infection, progression to cirrhosis, HCC, and liver-related mortality (Fig. 2). This approach provides empirical evidence on the long-term impact of HCV micro-elimination in an Asian setting. The study addresses two objectives aligned with the WHO elimination agenda. First, we evaluated the effectiveness of a county-wide micro-elimination model that integrates risk-based screening with multidisciplinary, decentralized care to enhance screening uptake, improve viremia confirmation through reflex ribonucleic acid (RNA) testing, and achieve high DAA treatment coverage. To capture heterogeneity across age- and sex-specific risk groups, we developed a Bayesian hierarchical cascade-of-care model that explicitly accommodates the nested structure of real-world screening, diagnostic, and treatment pathways.

Second, we estimated the long-term population-level impact of CHIPS-C through 2030 by incorporating the observed performance of DAA-based therapy, quantified through the cascade-of-care model, into a cohort-based Markov framework. This integration enabled evaluation of how CHIPS-C modified the natural history of HCV infection—from chronic infection through cirrhosis and HCV-related HCC to liver-related mortality—relative to a counterfactual scenario in which DAA-based care was absent. By coupling care-cascade performance with modeled changes in disease progression, CHIPS-C yields robust, evidence-based projections of sustained reductions in HCV incidence and HCC burden. The success of the Changhua model has subsequently informed national strategic planning and supports Taiwan’s progress toward achieving the WHO HCV elimination targets.

Results

Comparisons of cascade care performance between Pre-CHIPS-C and CHIPS-C periods

As shown in Fig. 1, the HCV care cascade in Changhua County underwent a major transformation with the introduction of the county-wide, risk-based micro-elimination program (CHIPS-C). The program targeted the entire underlying population listed in the population registry (n = 1.28 million) and, importantly, hard-to-reach groups, such as people who inject drugs (PWID) in correctional facilities.

Before CHIPS-C, the HCV care cascade (2005-2018) relied on interferon-based therapy delivered through two parallel pathways: (1) the Changhua community-based integrated screening (CHCIS) platform for anti-HCV testing among adults aged ≥30 years as part of cancer and chronic disease screening, and (2) an opportunistic anti-HCV testing through gastroenterology visits across all ages or the national adult health check-up for individuals aged ≥40 years in primary care clinics and hospitals. National policy required abnormal liver function as the eligibility criterion for the opportunistic anti-HCV testing. As a result, the proportion of the population eligible for anti-HCV screening was effectively narrowed from the entire population (1.28 million) to 595,097 individuals, all of whom entered the interferon-era care cascade without reflex confirmatory testing.

Since 2019, CHIPS-C has implemented a DAA-based care cascade aligned with national policy, incorporating reflex RNA testing to streamline RNA diagnosis. Building on the same underlying eligible population, the program adopted a more efficient risk-based strategy, proactively engaging multidisciplinary chronic disease care networks, including dialysis, pre-end-stage renal disease (ESRD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and diabetes mellitus (DM) clinics, to uncover previously undiagnosed chronic HCV infections. CHIPS-C also extended systematic outreach to traditionally underserved groups, such as PWID, methadone substitution treatment (MST) patients, and people living with HIV/AIDS.

Figure 1 illustrates how CHIPS-C integrated and reorganized both the CHCIS community screening platform and the opportunistic screening portal into a unified, risk-stratified framework. Notably, despite reducing the number of individuals undergoing screening by approximately half compared with the pre-CHIPS-C period, the program achieved nearly identical numbers of confirmed viremia cases (7925 vs. 7785) and slightly higher numbers of successfully treated individuals (7261 vs. 6187). This finding demonstrates a substantial improvement in the efficiency, equity, and yield of HCV case finding under the risk-based micro-elimination strategy.

Cascade performance in targeted risk-based groups

Table 3 details the care cascade for the risk-based targeted groups in the CHIPS-C period (corresponding to the third column of Table 2). As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the demographic profiles of CHIPS-C participants differed markedly across risk groups. Extremely high-risk groups, namely the marginal populations (MST recipients, people living with HIV/AIDS, and incarcerated individuals), were predominantly male and concentrated in younger to middle-aged adults, whereas patients in renal- and metabolic-related groups (dialysis, pre-ESRD, CKD, and diabetes shared-care programs) were older, with a substantial proportion aged ≥60 years and a more balanced sex distribution. Between 2019 and 2022, 214,053 residents were screened, of whom 184,419 (86.2%) underwent anti-HCV testing. Screening rates varied by risk group, ranging from 80.9% (28,911/35,727) among diabetes shared care patients to 100% (5700/5700) among dialysis patients.

Among the 184,419 individuals tested for anti- HCV, 12,098 (6.6%) were anti-HCV positive. A clear risk gradient was observed, with prevalence ranging from 5.1% (4198/81,681) among individuals identified through opportunistic screening to 90.2% (321/356) among those receiving MST.

Among anti-HCV-positive individuals, 86.3% (10,437/12,098) underwent HCV RNA testing. RNA diagnostic uptake ranged from 81.1% (1515/1852) in the diabetes shared-care group to 98.8% (317/321) among MST recipients. HCV RNA positivity also demonstrated a risk gradient, increasing from 59.4% (1918/3230) among individuals with CKD to 77.4% (374/484) among incarcerated individuals.

Following RNA confirmation, treatment initiation rates were uniformly high, ranging from 88.8% (317/357) among patients receiving dialysis to 95.5% (234/245) among MST recipients. SVR12 was exceptionally high across subgroups, reaching 99.8% among incarcerated individuals, 97.6% among patients receiving dialysis or diabetes shared care, and 100% among individuals with pre-ESRD or CKD, consistent with prior studies (Supplementary Table 2).

HCV incidence reduction

Table 1 (the bottom panel) shows that the annual incidence of HCV infection in the pre-CHIPS-C period was 510 per 100,000. After CHIPS-C implementation, repeat anti-HCV testing revealed a markedly reduced incidence of 2.59 per 100,000, achieving the WHO incidence target for HCV elimination.

Despite county-wide micro-elimination efforts, it remains uncertain whether all viremic patients eligible for treatment were identified. Among high-risk groups, such as PWID, HCV incidence declined from 9.7% in the pre-CHIPS-C period to 1.0% among 203 incarcerated individuals with available repeated testing data.

Following CHIPS-C implementation, the total number of HCC cases was further reduced to 38, reflecting a 76% reduction (95% CI: 66-83%) compared with no treatment.

Incremental effectiveness beyond background ultrasound screening

Using the Markov model to simulate the disease natural history of HCC in the absence of ultrasound screening (Supplement 1), we estimated that the CHCIS alone reduced HCC deaths from 112 to 82 (26.8% effectiveness, 95% CI: 3–45%). After allowing for the ultrasound screening effect, CHIPS-C implementation further reduced HCC deaths from 82 to 19, corresponding to an additional 77% reduction in mortality (95% CI: 62–86%).

Regarding HCV-related deaths from cirrhosis, the Markov model estimated that CHIPS-C reduced cirrhosis-related deaths from 9.4 to 2.3, representing a 76% reduction (Table 5).

Effectiveness across targeted risk-based groups

Because RNA positivity and disease progression affect outcomes, we evaluated risk-group-specific reductions in HCC (Supplementary Table 6). Among MST recipients, incarcerated persons, and people living with HIV/AIDS groups, the expected numbers of HCC cases (1.6, 3.4, and 1.2, respectively) declined to 0.4, 1.0, and 0.4, yielding effectiveness rates of 73.9%, 70.6%, and 64.3%. For dialysis, pre-ESRD, and CKD groups, HCC cases declined from 4.2, 3.7, and 35.2 to 1.1, 0.9, and 12.1, corresponding to reductions of 72.9%, 76.8%, and 65.6%, respectively. Diabetes shared care, opportunistic screening, and community care groups showed reductions from 17.1, 34.7, and 14.8 to 6.3, 9.7, and 3.3, with effectiveness of 63.2%, 72.2%, and 77.5%, respectively.

Discussion

This study provides a programmatic evaluation of a county-wide, risk-based HCV micro-elimination strategy in Changhua County, Taiwan, using complementary empirical and model-based evidence. Using a before-after comparison of two care-cascade porgrams (pre-CHIPS-C, 2005-2018 vs CHIPS-C, 2019–2022), we quantified changes in screening uptake, confirmatory RNA completion, and treatment initiation following the introduction of a decentralized DAA-era cascade with reflex RNA testing. We then linked empirically estimated cascade performance to a locally parameterized Markov natural history framework to project downstream effects on HCV-attributable cirrhosis mortality and HCC incidence and mortality through 2030, while explicitly accounting for the background ultrasound screening program initiated in 2005. Together, these analyses show that CHIPS-C was associated with a step-change in cascade performance and—under plausible assumptions supported by local data—a substantial reduction in HCV-attributable liver outcomes consistent with WHO elimination endpoints.

The principal empirical finding is that the post-2019 cascade achieved markedly higher completion across key cascade stages, particularly at points where attrition historically occurs. Compared with the pre-CHIPS-C period, the anti-HCV testing screening rate increased, RNA completion improved substantially, and treatment initiation among viremic patients increased. Importantly, these improvements were not restricted to community screening participants. Among targeted risk-based groups—previously reached largely through opportunistic testing conditioned on abnormal liver function—screening rates increased and RNA completion improved. These results indicate that the evaluation is not simply documenting “more treatment in the DAA era,” but rather quantifying where in the cascade the program reduced leakage, with reflex RNA testing and coordinated outreach plausibly explaining the large improvement in confirmatory diagnosis. The underlying factors contributing to improvements in screening uptake and RNA diagnosis included the multidisciplinary, collaborative, decentralized, and accessible delivery of the HCV care cascade across diverse healthcare settings under CHIPS-C. Specialist physicians managed 58% of care in targeted risk-based groups, predominantly among MST recipients, people living with HIV/AIDS, incarcerated individuals, and patients receiving dialysis or with pre-ESRD, whereas non-specialist providers accounted for the remaining 42%, primarily delivering care to individuals with CKD, DM, and the general population through the CHCIS program and the opportunistic portal. Notably, SVR-12 was high in both the pre-CHIPS-C period (91.8%) treated with interferon-based therapy and CHIPS-C period (99.0%) with DAA-based therapy. The relatively high SVR rate observed with interferon-based therapy in Taiwan, compared with Western countries, is attributable to a lower prevalence of genotype 1 (40–50%) and a high prevalence of genotype 2 (around 40%) of HCV infection in Taiwan27.

The Bayesian hierarchical cascade-of-care regression offers three key advantages. First, it enables adjustment for age, sex, and age-sex interactions with the marginal populations, while explicitly accounting for heterogeneity across marginalized groups. Second, flexible specification of the follow-up parameter (π) within the Bayesian framework allows robust handling of incomplete observations, including loss to follow-up among anti-HCV-positive individuals prior to RNA confirmation, as well as accommodation of efficient sampling schemes used to estimate viremia rates through selective RNA testing. Third, by explicitly modeling the nested structure of the care cascade, the Bayesian approach yields more reliable uncertainty quantification, producing credible intervals that are more accurate than crude proportions derived from unadjusted analyses. Consequently, reporting adjusted treatment completion rates derived from the hierarchical model provides a more informative and policy-relevant assessment of program performance than crude treatment rates that do not account for underlying population structure or missing data mechanisms. This evaluation strengthens causal interpretability by separating observed cascade performance from demographic imbalance and heterogeneity in care settings. After adjustment, the complete treatment rate increased from 39% in the pre-CHIPS-C to 84% under CHIPS-C, and all risk strata still exceeded the WHO micro-elimination treatment-coverage threshold (72%). Notably, adjusted complete treatment rates remained high across groups requiring different delivery settings and provider types, supporting the inference that performance gains were not confined to specialist-led hospital pathways. The observed provider-mix findings (specialists leading care in extreme-risk and renal pathways with non-specialists contributing substantially in CKD, diabetes, and general population care) further aligns with a decentralized model of care that is operationally scalable.

A second outcome domain concerns population-level transmission dynamics. Using repeat anti-HCV testing within the community-based integrated screening cohort, the estimated annual incidence decreased dramatically following CHIPS-C implementation (from 510 per 100,000 in the pre-CHIPS-C to 2.59 per 100,000 post-CHIPS-C), achieving the WHO incidence benchmark. In extreme-risk groups, incidence also declined (e.g., PWID and incarcerated settings), although these estimates depend on the availability of repeat-testing and should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive measures of ongoing transmission. This finding is nevertheless supported by programmatic indicators, including the distribution of 725 syringes and needles distributed per PWID per year14, which meets WHO target. Consistent with this evidence, the markedly lower viremia prevalence among first-time community screening attendees in the CHIPS-C era suggests that a large fraction of prevalent infections in the general population had already been identified, although continued surveillance is required to quantify reinfection and residual transmission in hard-to-reach groups.

To translate cascade improvements into long-term clinical endpoints, we linked empirically derived cascade parameters and locally estimated natural history transitions to a Markov model spanning chronic infection, cirrhosis, preclinical and clinical HCC, and liver-related mortality. This component addresses a common gap in elimination evaluations, where program reporting often ends at SVR and does not quantify downstream HCC and mortality. Long-term projections suggest that by 2030, HCV-related HCC incidence and mortality, as well as overall HCV-related mortality would fall below WHO endpoint targets (≤5 per 100,000 for HCC incidence and ≤2 per 100,000 for mortality), recognizing that these estimates are model-based and contingent on sustained cascade performance and stable transition rates.

In the context of international elimination initiatives (e.g., Egypt’s mass test-and-treat program and treatment scale-up and modeling efforts reported from Georgia and Pakistan), the contribution of this study is not to restate that micro-elimination is beneficial, but to demonstrate a practical evaluation framework for a heterogeneous, real-world care cascade. Specifically, this work integrates (i) a before-after cascade analysis that identifies where attrition was reduced, (ii) Bayesian hierarchical adjustment accommodating nested risk-stratified delivery and demographic imbalance, and (iii) a locally calibrated Markov framework that translates cascade performance into projected HCC and mortality outcomes while accounting for a pre-existing ultrasound screening environment. This integrated approach may be particularly relevant for Asian settings characterized by geographically clustered endemicity and mixed pathways of care, where local risk stratification and decentralized delivery are essential but robust outcome evaluation is rarely conducted.

Because the CHIPS-C represents a county-wide HCV elimination program with a decentralized care model, it remained fully operational during the COVID-19 pandemic, supported by teleconsultation and staggered clinic schedules. National screening data showed no decline in HCV testing volumes during this period28. More broadly, Taiwan’s success in controlling the COVID-19 pandemic without full national lockdowns reflected an integrated zero-COVID strategy implemented during the first two years of the pandemic. Healthcare system stability was maintained, and public adherence to public health and social measures (PHSMs) remained high. Strict PHSMs, including school closures and bans on social gatherings, were enforced only during two short intervals, each lasting less than three months, after which HCV screening activities resumed rapidly.

As of 2024, the CHIPS-C model has expanded across Taiwan, leading to an expected treatment coverage of over 92% among viremic individuals. Its adoption under the Ministry of Health and Welfare demonstrates national-level scalability and cross-regional policy integration29. Our findings support the value of risk-guided micro-elimination, integrated with community-based general screening and opportunistic screening, as a highly efficient and equitable model. Embedding HCV testing into existing communicable and non-communicable disease programs leveraged established service platforms, reducing costs and expanding reach without building parallel systems. However, our results reflect improved program efficiency under a risk-based micro-elimination strategy rather than causal effects from identical populations. By shifting from opportunistic screening to proactive risk-based enrollment, CHIPS-C achieved comparable or higher numbers of viremic detections and treatment completions while screening substantially fewer individuals. These findings highlight the efficiency gains of risk-stratified case finding for population-level HCV elimination and underscore the importance of analytic frameworks that explicitly address cohort heterogeneity when evaluating program performance.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational pre-post evaluation, it cannot fully exclude secular trends associated with the availability of DAA-based cascade care and other contemporaneous policy changes and also precluded us from using difference in difference (DID) design for the two-arm study. However, the magnitude and location of cascade improvements—especially RNA completion coinciding with reflex testing—support a program-associated mechanism. Importantly, the secular trend influences may be minimized when leveraging the counterfactual CHIPS-C cohort in the absence of DAA-based therapy as the comparator for long-term effectiveness evaluation. The DID design and analysis may be a good suggestion for other countries where HCV care cascade for elimination is in planning. However, as the majority of chronic HCV persistent infections in Taiwan scenario have been acquired in childhood and early adult in the old days the comparisons between the pre-intervention period (before 2018) and the post-intervention period (2019–2023) are not supposed to be substantially affected by environmental changes over time. Second, incidence estimates in high-risk groups relied on available repeat-testing subsets and may underestimate ongoing transmission or reinfection; routine reinfection monitoring, particularly among PWID and correctional settings, is an important next step. An additional limitation relates to the restricted inclusion of the general population aged 20–29 years. In Taiwan, chronic HCV infection is predominantly concentrated in older cohorts as a result of historical iatrogenic transmission, with very low community prevalence among young adults. Importantly, the CHIPS-C program included younger individuals meeting predefined risk criteria, such as people who inject drugs and incarcerated populations. Nevertheless, the exclusion of low-risk young adults from general population screening may lead to underestimation of recent transmission, underscoring the need for continued targeted surveillance among younger cohorts. Third, the Markov model focuses on disease progression among prevalent chronic HCV infections and does not explicitly incorporate transmission dynamics or incident infections. Incidence estimates derived from CHCIS may underrepresent younger adults, and exclusion of age-specific incidence and reinfection may result in conservative estimates of long-term benefit. Consequently, the model primarily captures reductions in HCC and mortality rather than prevention of new infections. Future work incorporating age-stratified incidence and transmission processes would provide a more comprehensive assessment of population-level elimination impact. Furthermore, the Markov projections depend on transition rate estimates. Although this concern can be ameliorated by utilizing the proposed calibrated Markov process with our local data and the already well-established parameters from literature and the consistency between predicted and observed HCC incidence/mortality prior to 2022, the uncertainty in parameters in projection to 2030 still remains and should be continuously validated and monitored. Fourth, the CHIPS-C cohorts were not random samples of the general population but reflected individuals engaged with public health screening and care infrastructures. Compared with the pre-CHIPS-C period—which likely over-represented individuals with better healthcare access—CHIPS-C incorporated proactive outreach to marginalized populations and integrated screening into routine chronic disease care, reducing access-related selection bias. Accordingly, our findings are most generalizable to settings with integrated public health screening systems and universal health coverage, and extrapolation to healthcare systems with fragmented access should be interpreted with caution. Finally, generalizability beyond Changhua and Taiwan will require calibration to local epidemiology, care structures, and financing, although the evaluation framework itself is transferable. More importantly, “risk-based groups” in this study were defined according to programmatic yield and operational efficiency for HCV case finding rather than entirely etiologic risk; accordingly, these categories should not be extrapolated as universal “high-risk groups” without local epidemiologic validation.

In conclusion, this paper evaluates a county-wide HCV micro-elimination program using a design that links observed care-cascade improvements to projected long-term reductions in cirrhosis mortality and HCC burden. The findings indicate that a risk-based, decentralized cascade—with high RNA completion and high treatment initiation—can plausibly achieve WHO-aligned elimination endpoints when implemented at scale and sustained through 2030. Beyond describing CHIPS-C as an intervention, this study provides an evaluation template that other regions can adapt to quantify both cascade performance and downstream health impact using locally grounded data.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with all relevant ethical regulations and guidelines. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the TMU-Joint Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University (TMU-JIRB approval number: N201912093). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the TMU-JIRB, as this study involved secondary analysis of administrative program records collected as part of a public health initiative, for which obtaining individual consent was impracticable.

Study setting and eligible populations for the HCV care cascade

Changhua County, located in central Taiwan, comprises 26 townships and has a population of approximately 1.28 million. The county ranks second nationally in both the incidence and mortality of HCC with geographical difference in prevalence of HCV infeciton5, underscoring the urgent need for comprehensive liver disease prevention strategies30. In 2005, an abdominal ultrasound screening program was introduced and integrated into the CHCIS program, a county-wide platform designed for the simultaneous detection of cancers and chronic diseases for people aged 30 years or older31. The CHCIS program subsequently evolved into a primary mechanism for identifying individuals with HCV infection and linking them to the HCV care cascade as we found 4.3% prevalence of HCV infection via this CHCIS platform5.

In parallel with the CHCIS program, opportunistic screening functioned as a complementary pathway for identifying previously undiagnosed HCV infection across diverse risk groups, with an HCV prevalence of 5.7% reported in a prior study5. This approach encompassed individuals of all ages presenting with abnormal liver function test results during gastroenterology visits, as well as adults aged 40 years or older undergoing routine, nationally subsidized health check-ups provided by primary care clinics and hospitals. Together, these two platforms constituted the principal infrastructure for delivering the HCV care cascade before implementation of the county-wide HCV micro-elimination program (Fig. 1).

Before the introduction of the CHIPS-C, the majority of chronic HCV infections reflected long-standing exposure, often acquired during early childhood or young adulthood among birth cohorts prior to 1975. These individuals increasingly presented with comorbid chronic conditions—such as DM, CKD, pre-ESRD, or ESRD—as they aged. In contrast, hard-to-reach populations, including PWID, patients administered with MST, and individuals living with HIV/AIDS, represented smaller but extremely high-risk groups with disproportionate transmission potential.

Following the launch of CHIPS-C in 2019, a risk-based strategy was adopted to optimize enrollment efficiency while continuing to leverage the two existing screening platforms (Fig. 1). In addition, targeted outreach was expanded to correctional facilities and to primary care clinics providing MST or HIV/AIDS services, recognizing the challenges inherent in identifying and engaging hard-to-reach populations.

Before and after risk-based HCV micro-elimination

Figure 1 illustrates the enrollment framework for the HCV care cascade before and after implementation of the CHIPS-C program, both targeting the same underlying Changhua population as defined by the population registry. In the pre-CHIPS-C period (2005–2018), the eligible population for the HCV care cascade comprised 595,097 individuals, including 124,548 identified through the CHCIS platform and 470,549 detected via opportunistic screening, all managed under an interferon-based treatment paradigm (Fig. 1; Table 1, right panel).

With the introduction of the CHIPS-C program in 2019, eligibility criteria were refined through a re-identified priority risk approach for both the CHCIS platform and the opportunistic screening platform, in which individuals assigned according to anti-HCV-positive status in the presence of overlapping risk groups. This strategy reduced the eligible population to 303,285 individuals, consisting of 89,232 identified through CHCIS and 214,053 drawn from targeted risk-based groups (Fig. 1; Table 1, left panel). Multidisciplinary and collaborative care models were employed to prioritize enrollment beginning with extremely high-risk groups and extending to moderate-risk populations. Individuals at average risk continued to be identified through the parallel CHCIS and opportunistic screening pathways. Following implementation of CHIPS-C, participant enrollment was organized using a hierarchical risk-based classification strategy. Individuals meeting criteria for predefined high-risk groups (e.g., diabetes, chronic kidney disease, dialysis, PWID) were prioritized and classified accordingly, even if they also met traditional indications for opportunistic screening, such as abnormal liver function. As a result, the residual opportunistic screening category consisted only of individuals who did not fall into any specific high-risk group. This reclassification applied across all enrollment pathways, including CHICS, and reflects intentional prioritization rather than changes in screening eligibility.

This study evaluated two alternative HCV case-finding and care cascade strategies implemented within the same county population, rather than performing a causal comparison of identical cohorts. Both pre-CHIPS-C and CHIPS-C programs were derived from the Changhua population registry; however, eligibility criteria were deliberately refined after 2019 to prioritize predefined high-risk strata. Accordingly, observed differences reflect changes in case-finding strategy—opportunistic versus risk-based—rather than differences in the source population. To account for heterogeneity, cascade indicators were stratified by screening pathway and risk group, and adjusted treatment completion rates were estimated using a Bayesian hierarchical cascade-of-care model incorporating age, sex, and risk-group effects.

At the national level, CHIPS-C was implemented within the institutional framework of the Ministry of Health and Welfare and Taiwan’s National Health Insurance system, ensuring full reimbursement for both diagnostic testing and antiviral therapy. This alignment embedded the program within Taiwan’s national commitment to achieving the WHO’s HCV elimination targets, while allowing collaborative, decentralized delivery of the HCV care cascade to be effectively implemented at the community level through subsidized funding mechanisms.

Risk-based strategy and decentralized HCV care cascade

As mentioned earlier, the CHIPS-C program integrated population-wide screening with risk-based targeted group approach. Program components included harm reduction services for PWID, HCV screening in correctional facilities, HIV-HCV co-management20, CKD and ESRD-associated screening25, diabetes mellitus shared-care programs16,26, and community-based outreach screening through the CHCIS platform. Screening priority was determined by disease burden and transmission risk.

Care delivery was decentralized through 27 public health centers and collaborative multidisciplinary healthcare institutions organized and coordinated by the Changhua County Public Health Bureau (Supplementary Fig. 1), ensuring accessibility across both rural and urban settings to tack the geographical differences in the prevalence of HCV infection5. These centers and healthcare institutions were staffed by physicians and nurses and supported by a multidisciplinary care teams comprising general practitioners, specialists, and allied health professionals (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Real-time monitoring of patient status and care cascade outcomes was facilitated through an integrated information system maintained by the Public Health Bureau.

Empirical data sources for modeling and long-term effectiveness evaluation

The pre-CHIPS-C and CHIPS-C analyses for care-cascade performance evaluation and long-term effectiveness projection of the CHIPS-C has followed a retrospective cohort based on individual-level primary data from the CHCIS screening and opportunistic screening platform. Each participant was uniquely identified and followed from anti-HCV testing through RNA confirmation, treatment initiation, and SVR12 verification. Supplementary Fig. 2b summarizes the empirical datasets used to estimate model parameters and evaluate the long-term effectiveness of the CHIPS-C program. Hepatocellular carcinoma screening protocols, introduced in 2005, remained consistent across both the CHCIS and CHIPS-C periods. These protocols included abdominal ultrasound screening for the general population, with risk stratification ranging from high-risk groups (including individuals with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or HCV infection and elevated alpha-fetoprotein levels) to lower-risk populations.

Concurrently, HBV control efforts—including universal infant vaccination, routine surveillance of HBsAg-positive individuals, and reimbursement for nucleos(t)ide analog therapy since 2003—were ongoing throughout the study period. Given these overlapping public health interventions, reductions in liver-related mortality required careful attribution. Accordingly, this study separately modeled HCV-attributable cirrhosis and HCC mortality using a Markov framework that integrated the natural history of HCV infection with the subsequent progression to cirrhosis, preclinical and clinical HCC, and HCV-related death (Fig. 2).

Transition rates and liver-related mortality were estimated using CHIPS-C data to characterize the natural course of chronic HCV infection until the disease progression to HCC, alongside selected pre-CHIPS-C and CHIPS-C periods with ultrasound surveillance to estimate progression from chronic HCV infection to cirrhosis or HCC. To isolate HCV-specific mortality, CHCIS datasets containing both HCV and HBV serostatus were analyzed. As 38% of HCC deaths were attributable to HCV infection, this proportion was applied to county-level cancer registry and mortality data. Cirrhosis-related mortality rates were derived from national death registries. To minimize bias arising from ultrasound-based case detection, screen-detected HCC cases were approximated using preclinical disease states and subtracted from annual HCC incidence estimates.

Laboratory methods and antiviral treatment

Under CHIPS-C, reflex HCV RNA testing was performed immediately following a positive anti-HCV antibody result, thereby minimizing diagnostic delays. In contrast, the pre-CHIPS-C program required separate visits for confirmatory testing. Anti-HCV antibodies were detected using the AxSYM HCV 3.0 assay (Abbott), HBsAg was measured with the Elecsys HBsAg II Quant assay (Roche), and HCV RNA was quantified using either the COBAS TaqMan assay (Roche) or the Abbott RealTime HCV assay. Genotyping was performed using the Inno-LiPA™ system or the RealTime HCV Genotype II RUO assay (Abbott).

Patients with confirmed viremia received 12-week treatment regimens of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir, prescribed by either general practitioners or hospital-based physicians. Details of treatment eligibility before and after the CHIPS-C program and the evolution of HCV management are provided in Supplements 2 and 3. SVR12 was verified in representative CHIPS-C subgroups (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Adjusted complete treatment rate analysis

Descriptive analyses of the HCV care cascade followed the framework established in Egypt’s national elimination program32, examining sequential stages from anti-HCV screening to RNA confirmation, treatment initiation, treatment completion, and SVR12. To account for differences in RNA diagnosis rate with and without using reflex testing practices, demographic composition between risk groups, and baseline risks across groups, a Bayesian hierarchical regression model was applied to estimate anti-HCV positivity (λ), RNA diagnosis (π), and RNA positivity (p), leading to completed treatment rates, adjusting for age, sex, and interactions between age and the extremely high-risk marginal group. This model accounts for nested data (individuals within risk strata), allows joint estimation of correlated cascade probabilities, and reduces bias from sparse sub-groups. Adjusted complete treatment was calculated as the number of individuals who completed DAA therapy divided by the expected number of viremic cases, defined as the anti-HCV positivity rate × RNA positivity rate (after adjustment for π)× the number of anti-HCV screening attendees. (Supplement 1). Non-informative priors were used.

To assess the yield of identifying HCV infection and RNA positive cases, the NND was defined as an efficiency metric representing the number of individuals who needed to be screened to identify one additional anti-HCV-positive or viremic case under the CHIPS-C program with the formula: NND = 1 / (difference in prevalence of anti-HCV positivity or RNA positivity before and after CHIPS-C). All the statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc.).

Long-term projection to 2030

Figure 2 illustrates the integration of the Bayesian hierarchical cascade-of-care model with a Markov process describing the natural history of chronic HCV infection, progression to HCC, and liver-related mortality, thereby providing empirical evidence for the long-term impact of HCV micro-elimination. Detailed computational procedures are provided in Supplement 1.

Briefly, we first simulated the natural history of disease from chronic HCV infection to cirrhosis, preclinical and clinical HCC, and liver-related mortality under a counterfactual scenario representing the CHIPS-C population in the absence of a DAA-based care cascade, using a cohort-based Markov model. The counterfactual CHIPS-C scenario models the same eligible cohort aging under the risk-based framework but without DAA treatment, representing the projected natural history of DAA-untreated HCV. Model parameters governing the pre-cirrhotic phase of chronic HCV infection were calibrated using local empirical data from the CHIPS-C cohort, including anti-HCV positivity and RNA positivity rates of 5.1% and 64.7%, respectively. The annual incidence of cirrhosis among individuals with chronic HCV infection was calibrated at 0.012 per year using data from an ultrasound screening cohort within the KCIS program (Supplement 1, Supplementary Fig. 2b). Parameters governing subsequent disease progression—from cirrhosis or non-cirrhosis to preclinical HCC and then to clinical HCC—were derived from a previously published Markov models with a comparable structure33–35.

Parameters of the HCV care cascade, estimated using a Bayesian hierarchical model, were then incorporated to quantify the projected long-term effectiveness of the CHIPS-C program in reducing HCV-associated HCC incidence and mortality through modification of the underlying disease natural history attributable to DAA-based treatment. Model validity was assessed by comparing predicted HCC incidence and mortality prior to 2022 with observed data from the Changhua Cancer Registry and national mortality records (see observed versus projected trends in Fig. 3a, b).

From the 2019 Changhua population (1,277,686 individuals), 303,285 high-risk individuals were included in the initial model. To project outcomes through 2030, the modeled population was expanded to 417,675 individuals to account for cohort aging. The model was used to estimate future trends in HCV-related HCC incidence and mortality and to evaluate the extent to which the CHIPS-C program aligns with WHO elimination targets.
