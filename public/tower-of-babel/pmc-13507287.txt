Evaluation and selection of superior snap melon (Cucumis melo var. momordica Duth. & Full.) genotypes using morphological, yield, and selected fruit quality traits
Authors: Sanjay Kumar, Lalu Prasad Yadav, Om Prakash Yadav, Yazgan Tunç, Ali Khadivi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The snap melon (Cucumis melo var. momordica Duth. & Full.), recognized for its characteristic fruit splitting at maturity, is a nutritious and resilient cucurbit with considerable potential for crop improvement. However, systematic breeding efforts remain limited due to low genetic variability present in available germplasm and the relationships among yield and selected fruit quality traits. This study evaluated 30 snap melon genotypes to quantify genetic variability, heritability, trait correlations, and genetic divergence for the identification of superior breeding materials. Substantial phenotypic variation was observed for key traits, including vine length (185.13–535.54 cm), fruit length (6.59–22.00 cm), fruit weight (168.13–1065.40 g), and fruit yield per plant (0.49–4.68 kg). High heritability coupled with high genetic advance for average fruit weight, fruit yield per plant, and titratable acidity indicated the predominance of additive gene action and the effectiveness of direct selection. Fruit yield per plant exhibited strong positive correlations with fruit weight, fruit length, and fruiting period, suggesting that these traits can serve as reliable selection criteria for yield improvement. Cluster analysis grouped the genotypes into five distinct clusters, with the greatest genetic divergence observed between Clusters 1 and 2, indicating their potential use as parents in hybridization programs. Principal component analysis revealed that the first six principal components explained 88.12% of the total variation, with fruit weight, vine length, and fruit yield contributing most to genotypic differentiation. Based on their overall performance and comparison with the released check variety AHS-82, genotypes HSM-13, HSM-19, and HSM-9 were identified as promising breeding materials for developing high-yielding snap melon cultivars.

Introduction

The snap melon (Cucumis melo var. momordica Duth. & Full.), commonly known as “phut” in India due to its characteristic splitting upon ripening, is a versatile and nutrient-rich fruit native to the country. Belonging to the family Cucurbitaceae, it has a chromosome number of 2n = 24 and is recognized as a hardy crop with tolerance to several biotic and abiotic stresses1. The evolutionary development of the “Momordica” group is associated with several morphological traits such as fruit shape, reduced bitterness, fewer seeds, and larger fruit size, contributing to substantial genetic variability within and among species2–4. Its area, production, and productivity are not reported separately in official horticultural statistics but are included under the muskmelon category (National Horticulture Board, 2024–25; Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare, 2024–25). During 2024–25, muskmelon occupied 3,288 ha in Haryana, with a production of 59,340 metric tonnes and an average productivity of 18.05 t ha⁻¹ (Department of Horticulture, Haryana). At the national level, muskmelon is cultivated on approximately 68,000 ha, producing about 1.49 million tonnes with an average productivity of 22.0 t ha⁻¹ (FAOSTAT, 2023; Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare, 2024–25). Requiring minimal inputs, the crop thrives across diverse climates and soil types. Snap melon is valued for its richness in vitamin C and essential minerals, and its wide culinary and functional uses as it can be consumed raw or cooked, incorporated into salads and desserts, used in skincare formulations, and even applied as a natural cooling toner. Beyond its significance locally, snap melon is becoming more well-known worldwide as a useful genetic resource for melon enhancement. It is native to the Indian subcontinent, has a wide genetic background, and is a significant gene donor for improving the resistance, fruit quality, and production of cultivated melons (Dhillon et al. 5). The crop is a prospective resource for climate-resilient breeding since it is naturally adapted to dry and semi-arid conditions and shows tolerance to drought, high temperatures, and salinity5,6.

The significant genetic variety found in native snap melon germplasm has been emphasized in earlier research7. For Indian snap melon germplasm, however, thorough assessments of morphological, yield, and quality attributes with genetic variability, heritability, trait association, and principal component analyses are still scarce. Moreover, there has not been much focus on identifying superior genotypes by combining these complementing methods. Because of this, breeders still do not know enough about the amount of genetic variability, the connections between economically significant traits, and the degree of genetic divergence needed for effective cultivar development and parent selection. In order to find suitable breeding materials, make it easier to choose genetically divergent parents, and aid in the production of high-yielding, improved-quality, and climate-resilient cultivars, a methodical assessment of various snap melon genotypes is crucial.

While a number of studies have employed morphological descriptors to report genetic variability in snap melon, the majority have mainly concentrated on characterizing local collections or assessing diversity. There are still few thorough studies that combine estimates of genetic variability, heritability, correlation, principal component analysis, and Mahalanobis D² statistics with morphological, yield, and fruit quality traits to find superior breeding parents, especially for germplasm adapted to the northwest semi-arid region of India. Divergence analysis helps explain the degree of variability among the characters. D2 statistic is an effective tool to estimate the genetic divergence among the genotypes8. It classifies the genotypes into relatively homogeneous groups in such a way that within clusters, diversity is minimized, and between clusters, diversity is maximized. The breeding programs can utilize the respective genotypes from diverse clusters9. Genetic divergence existing in the population helps in the selection of suitable parents for utilization in any crop breeding program, leading to a reduction in the number of crosses10. By leveraging the genetic diversity of snap melon, breeders can develop high-yielding cultivars with improved fruit quality. The current study aims to identify superior snap melon genotypes based on morphological, yield, and selected fruit quality traits among 30 evaluated genotypes including indigenous breeding lines and the released variety AHS-82 as a standard check, representing valuable genetic resources maintained at CCS Haryana Agricultural University through comprehensive evaluation of morphological and selected fruit quality traits, and to employ PCA for determining major contributing components governing variability, genotype discrimination, and trait-based selection for use in future breeding programs. These findings provide practical breeding insights that extend beyond conventional diversity assessment and contribute to the effective utilization of snap melon germplasm in crop improvement programs.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The present study was conducted at the Research Farm of the Department of Vegetable Science, CCS HAU, Hisar, Haryana, India, to investigate 30 snap melon genotypes. Among the 30 evaluated genotypes, the released variety AHS-82 (developed by ICAR-CIAH, Bikaner) was included as a standard check to facilitate comparative assessment of yield and other morphological and selected fruit quality traits. The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replications to minimize environmental variability. The experiment material was laid out with a plot size of 6 × 2.5 m, maintaining a crop spacing of 2.5 × 0.6 m. The crop was raised as per the standard package of practices recommended by CCS HAU, Hisar. The experiment was conducted in Hisar, which is situated at 29.10° N latitude and 75.46° E longitude, with an elevation of 215.2 m above mean sea level. Hisar city of Haryana lies on the outer margins of the southwest (SW) monsoon region. It has a semi-arid tropical climate, characterized by hot summers, cool winters, low and erratic annual rainfall, and high evaporative demand. Most of the annual rainfall (455 mm) is received during the southwest monsoon season (July–September), while the remaining period is generally dry. The soil exhibits a mixed pattern of Aeolian and Alluvial deposits. The mean weekly weather condition during the spring season 2023-24 is depicted in Fig. 1. Characters measured

Observations were recorded on five randomly selected and tagged plants per genotype per replication for a comprehensive set of morphological, flowering, yield, and selected fruit quality traits. Morphological traits included leaf blade length and width, measured at 30–35 days after sowing (DAS) using a measuring scale; internodal length, taken as the distance between two consecutive mid-vine nodes; number of primary and secondary branches, counted at 45–50 DAS when branching was fully expressed; and total vine length, measured from the base to the terminal growing tip at physiological maturity. Flowering traits comprised days to the appearance of the first staminate flower, days to first pistillate flower, and node number bearing the first pistillate flower. Yield-related traits included fruit length and circumference recorded on marketable fruits at harvest; average fruit weight derived from five representative fruits using a digital balance; number of fruits per vine; fruit yield per plant calculated as the cumulative fruit weight from each plant; days to first harvest; and fruiting period determined as the interval between first and last harvests as per the muskmelon DUS guidelines, PPVFRA, New Delhi. In the present investigation, only total soluble solids (TSS) and titratable acidity were analyzed as quality characters11. This selection was based on the original research design, which focused primarily on assessing morphological and agronomic divergence among snap melon genotypes. Among the current study to identify high-yielding and agronomically superior snap melon genotypes through comprehensive evaluation of morphological and selected fruit quality traits, and to employ PCA for determining major contributing components governing variability, genotype discrimination, and trait-based selection for used future breeding program.

Data analysis

Data on characters were recorded on a whole-plot basis, and yield characters were recorded at the time of harvest on five randomly selected plants. The data recorded on five plants from each genotype were averaged and used for statistical analysis. The data and plot analysis were carried out using various packages of RStudio12. Analysis of variance in a randomized block design (RBD) for each quantitative character was statistically analyzed as described by Panse and Sukhatme13. The genetic variability parameters, such as genotypic and phenotypic coefficients of variation were calculated according to Burton and De Vane14, and heritability (broad sense) and genetic advance formula were calculated as suggested by Johnson et al.15. The genetic diversity D2 analysis was done using the method suggested by Mahalanobis16 and elaborated by Murty and Arunachalam17. Tocher’s Method18 was the primary clustering method used to define the five genotype clusters. The average intra- and inter-cluster average D2 values were calculated using the formula given by Singh and Chaudhary19 and Nadarajan and Gunasekaran20.

Correlation between the measured traits

The genotypic correlation matrix shows significant correlations (p < 0.01) between several growth and fruit characters (Table 4). Better-performing genotypes typically produce more fruit, as seen by the strong positive connection (p < 0.01) between genotype performance. The genotypic correlation showed that average fruit yield per plant had positive and significant correlations with germination percentage (0.708), leaf blade length (0.228), leaf blade width (0.278), no. of primary branches (0.299), fruit length (0.845), fruit circumference (0.611), average fruit length (0.885), days to first fruit harvest (0.561), no. of fruits per plant (0.249), fruiting period (0.921), and acidity (0.274), and had significant negative correlations with TSS (− 0.289) and internodal length (− 0.233). The number of fruits per plant exhibited significant positive correlation with germination percentage (0.469), leaf blade length (0.206), leaf blade width (0.241), no. of primary branches (0.891), no. of secondary branches (0.795), days to emergence of 1st pistillate flower (0.249), average fruit yield per plant (0.249) and significant negative correlated vine length (− 0.714) and internodal length (− 0.785), suggesting that shorter vines might improve overall performance by more effectively directing resources toward fruit production rather than vine growth. The correlation findings in snap melon genotypes are similar to those observed in Coccinia grandis23. The genotypic correlation coefficients for the majority of factors are larger than the phenotypic ones, indicating that there are substantial intrinsic correlations between features in both species. The TSS, which is linked to fruit sweetness, exhibits a weakly negative connection with fruit production and size, indicating that there is no significant relationship between higher sugar content and larger fruits or higher yields. Fruit TA and AFYPP exhibit a weakly positive correlation (0.274), suggesting that fruit acidity may have some bearing on total yield; however, the association is not very robust.

Principal components analysis (PCA)

PCA revealed that out of nineteen principal components, only six had eigenvalues greater than one (> 1) and accounted for 88.12% of the analyzed traits’ variability, while the remaining twelve principal components contributed just 11.88% (Table 5). A scree plot (Fig. 3) depicted the proportion of variance linked with eigenvalues and principal components for each graphed principal component (PC).

The first three principal components explained 68% of the total phenotypic variation, and therefore they were considered sufficient for interpreting the multivariate structure of the genotypes. PC1, accounting for 31.30% of the variation, was primarily influenced by vine length, fruit weight, and fruit yield, indicating that these traits contributed most to overall size and productivity differences among genotypes. PC2, explaining 22.41% of the variation, was associated mainly with fruit length, fruit circumference, and days to first harvest, reflecting variation in fruit shape and earliness. PC3, contributing 14.34% of the variation, was characterized by high loadings for number of fruits per vine, days to first pistillate flower, and total soluble solids, representing traits related to reproductive behaviour and internal fruit quality. Notably, distinct sets of traits dominated each principal component, confirming that different biological dimensions-plant vigour, fruit morphology, and reproductive/quality attributes—collectively structured the diversity among snap melon genotypes.

Discussion

Genetic variability and breeding strategy

Genetic variability is the cornerstone of crop improvement as it determines the scope for selecting superior genotypes and developing improved cultivars. The wide genetic foundation of the germplasm, which has probably been sculpted by long-term adaptation to the various agro-ecological environments of northwestern India, is reflected in the significant heterogeneity found across the assessed snap melon genotypes. Natural selection and farmer-mediated selection have favored genotypes with varied plant architecture, fruit qualities, and tolerance to moisture-limited settings in snap melon, which is usually grown under rainfed and low-input production methods. Significant genetic and phenotypic diversity is maintained as a result of these evolutionary and selection forces, offering important resources for crop development.

Average fruit weight had a very high genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) among the assessed variables, suggesting significant heritable variability that may be selected for242. As fruit weight is a primary driver of commercial output in snap melon, the observed diversity offers considerable opportunity for selecting improved breeding materials. High genetic advancement and high heritability further imply that this feature is mostly governed by additive gene effects, which makes direct phenotypic selection useful for attaining genetic improvement. Similar findings have been documented by Muthuselvi et al.21, who found that fruit-related characteristics significantly increased snap melon output.

From a breeding perspective, the availability of wide genetic variation for economically important traits provides opportunities to combine favourable alleles through hybridization and selection. The superior-performing genotypes found in this study are important parental resources for creating improved cultivars suited to semi-arid production conditions, especially those that combine higher fruit weight with desired yield performance. Additionally, the genetic base of snap melon breeding programs will be expanded by the conservation and use of this native germplasm, increasing genetic gain while lowering the hazards associated with a small breeding pool.

Phenotypic and genotypic variations

The richness of the available germplasm and its potential for crop improvement are highlighted by the significant phenotypic and genotypic variety found among the assessed snap melon genotypes. This variety, which results in significant variation in economically significant features, is probably the consequence of long-term adaptation to various agroclimatic conditions and farmer-mediated selection under low-input production systems. Sulochana et al.25 and Ahmed et al.26 observed similar patterns of high PCV and GCV for growth, phenological, and yield-related parameters in cucurbits, demonstrating that snap melon has enough genetic variety for efficient selection and breeding. As a result, features with substantial heritable variation offer chances to create superior cultivars and achieve long-term genetic gain.

Leaf length (LL) and leaf width (LW) exhibited high heritability coupled with low genetic advance as a percentage of the mean, indicating the predominance of non-additive gene effects and limited scope for improvement through direct phenotypic selection4,23,27–32. Consequently, hybridization may be a more efficient way to take advantage of these characteristics in order to capture heterosis. Additionally, the identification of genetically varied parents was made easier by the estimation of genetic divergence using Mahalanobis D² statistics16,18, which offered a thorough evaluation of genetic relationships among genotypes. In snap melon breeding programs, these divergent genotypes are useful for producing large segregating populations and increasing the likelihood of recovering superior recombinants.

Correlation and genetic associations

Correlation analysis revealed biologically meaningful associations between growth traits and fruit quality parameters, highlighting their collective influence on yield formation. The weak positive correlation between fruit acidity and average fruit yield per plant suggests that although acidity contributes positively to yield, the association is weak. Similar multi-trait contributions to yield have been reported in cucumber26, supporting the consistency of yield architecture across cucurbits. Strong positive genotypic correlations indicate that several yield components can be improved simultaneously through indirect selection. On the other hand, the negative correlations with vine length point to a trade-off between reproductive efficiency and vegetative growth. Overgrowth can reduce reproductive efficiency and production potential by diverting assimilates and photosynthates from fruit initiation and filling to vine elongation and canopy development. In cucurbits, similar trade-offs between vegetative vigor and reproductive performance have been documented; robust vine growth was linked to lower assimilate partitioning to developing fruits, delayed blooming, and decreased fruit set21,26. Therefore, to maximize fruit yield, there should be an ideal balance between vegetative growth and reproductive development. From a breeding standpoint, choosing genotypes with increased reproductive efficiency and moderate vine development may increase production potential while preserving desired plant design.

Genetic diversity, cluster analysis, and principal component analysis

Snap melon genetic diversity has been extensively documented in the Middle East and South Asia, where traditional landraces maintain substantial agronomic variation. The high heritability observed for fruit weight and vine length in the present study aligns with findings from Egyptian melon landraces reported by Ahmed et al.,26, confirming the genetic stability of these traits. Their strong contribution to principal component variation (PC1, 31.30%) highlights their central role in determining yield architecture. Similar associations between fruit size and productivity were reported in Iranian genotypes33, suggesting a conserved biological relationship across geographic regions. The agreement among studies reinforces that fruit-related traits are reliable selection targets. Exploiting this diversity can accelerate breeding programs aimed at improving yield and adaptation.

A physiological trade-off between fruit size and sweetness is indicated by the substantial negative correlation between fruit weight and total soluble solids (r = − 0.256, p < 0.05), which suggests that higher assimilate allocation to fruit growth may prevent sugar buildup. Therefore, balanced selection tactics will be needed to simultaneously improve fruit quality and productivity. A strong framework for detecting genetically different parents was provided by the Mahalanobis D2 analysis, which divided the genotypes into discrete groups. The efficacy of this method for evaluating genetic diversity in cucurbits is supported by reports of similar clustering patterns in bottle gourd, pumpkin, and snap melon34–36. The maximum inter-cluster distance between Clusters 1 and 2 indicates high genetic divergence. Larger distances highlight promising combinations for heterosis breeding. The observed clustering reflects underlying genetic divergence rather than geographic origin, a pattern supported by earlier studies. Although only five clusters were formed, cluster structure depends primarily on genetic distances rather than genotype number. Expanding the germplasm base could further reveal hidden diversity and strengthen breeding opportunities. Crosses between genotypes belonging to Clusters 1 and 2, which exhibited the maximum inter-cluster distance, are expected to generate broad genetic recombination because of their high level of genetic divergence. Important agronomic and fruit characteristics varied among the genotypes from these clusters, suggesting the existence of complementing alleles that can be hybridized. Similarly, genetically distant genotypes with desired fruit quality or earliness can be mated with the superior-performing genotype HSM-13, which was discovered for fruit yield, to create transgressive segregants that combine several advantageous features. It is anticipated that these hybrid combinations will increase genetic variability, increase the effectiveness of selection in separating generations, and aid in the creation of snap melon cultivars that are extensively adaptable, high-yielding, and of better quality.

Principal component analysis (PCA) identified the key traits responsible for most of the observed variability, with components having eigenvalues greater than one representing biologically meaningful variation. The concentration of variability in a few components suggests that yield structure is governed by a limited number of major traits, which simplifies selection strategies. Similar PCA patterns in traditional melon groups37 indicate conserved trait architecture across Cucumis melo. Molecular studies further support the presence of intermediate landraces bridging snap and snake melon types38, highlighting the evolutionary flexibility of the species. Crossability with related taxa offers opportunities to expand the genetic base39. Integrating multivariate and taxonomic insights strengthens germplasm utilization in breeding programs.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed structured trait associations, helping to distinguish major growth and yield components. Vine length and internode length were strongly aligned with PC1, whereas total soluble solids showed positive association with both principal components, indicating its broad contribution to variability. In contrast, fruit-related traits such as days to first harvest, yield per plant, fruit weight, and fruit length displayed inverse relationships with the first two PCs, suggesting the presence of distinct yield-oriented trait groups. Reproductive and vegetative traits, including flowering time, branching pattern, and leaf dimensions, showed contrasting loadings across PCs, reflecting complex developmental interactions. These multivariate patterns highlight the coordinated nature of snap melon growth and productivity. PCA therefore provides a useful framework for identifying trait combinations that can be strategically targeted in breeding programs.

Implications for breeding and conservation

This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of snap melon, revealing substantial morphological and genetic diversity among the examined genotypes. Variation in fruit size, shape, and rind texture highlights a broad adaptive base, which is essential for resilience and breeding progress. Comparable diversity patterns reported in other Cucumis melo groups21,26 and snap melon landraces36,40 support the consistency of these findings. Mahalanobis D² statistics further confirmed significant genetic differentiation, validating the effectiveness of multivariate approaches in diversity assessment. Similar applications in soybean and urd bean demonstrate the broader relevance of this methodology. Collectively, the results emphasize the strategic value of conserving and exploiting snap melon diversity for future improvement programs.

Although the study successfully captured substantial morphological and agronomic diversity, the limited assessment of quality traits remains a notable limitation. Important biochemical attributes such as total sugars, β-carotene, and other nutritional components were not evaluated, restricting a full understanding of fruit quality in relation to genetic variability. Incorporating biochemical profiling would allow more precise trait characterization and improve selection efficiency41,42. Environmental factors may also have influenced the expression of traits such as vine length and yield, which are sensitive to temperature and soil moisture fluctuations. Therefore, multi-environment trials across diverse agroclimatic zones are necessary to validate genetic stability. Integrating molecular marker approaches alongside morphological evaluation would further strengthen breeding decisions. Advanced genomic tools such as genome-wide association studies (GWAS) can help identify key genes controlling fruit-related traits23,43–45. Such integration of phenotypic, biochemical, and genomic data would accelerate marker-assisted selection. Collectively, these future directions would enhance breeding accuracy and long-term cultivar improvement.

Conclusions

The study revealed substantial genetic variability among 30 snap melon genotypes across 19 morphological, agronomic, and selected fruit quality traits. The presence of high genotypic and phenotypic variability, particularly for average fruit weight, vine length, and average fruit yield, indicates strong potential for effective selection and genetic improvement. Based on qualitative characteristics, genotypes HSM-13, HSM-19, and HSM-9 emerged as promising candidates for use in future breeding programs. The considerable inter-cluster distances (D² values) further confirm the usefulness of these diverse genotypes for hybridization and heterosis exploitation. Correlation analysis identified meaningful associations among key growth and fruit traits, highlighting opportunities for selecting genotypes with desirable trait combinations to enhance fruit yield. Additionally, PCA effectively identified the major traits contributing to overall variation, providing valuable guidance for future selection strategies.

Overall, the findings underscore the importance of targeted selection based on genetic diversity to develop superior snap melon varieties. To strengthen and complement the morphological and agronomic insights obtained in this study, future research should incorporate broader biochemical and nutritional profiling, along with molecular marker–based diversity evaluations. The integration of these approaches will support more precise selection decisions and enable a comprehensive evaluation of snap melon germplasm.
