A superelastic titanium alloy showing nearly temperature-independent high strength and elastic modulus
Authors: Boer Chen, Honghong Su, Shuanglei Li, Zhonghan Li, Ying Yang, Honghui Wu, Lishan Cui, Kaiyuan Yu, Daqiang Jiang, Yang Ren, Zhihui Zhang, Tae-hyun Nam, Jiaming Zhu, Shengcheng Mao, Shijie Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLightweight superelastic alloys with temperature-insensitive properties are critical for deep-space exploration devices, yet their development is hindered by the challenge of simultaneously balancing strength, recoverable strain, and temperature sensitivity. Here, we overcome these limitations in a Ti50.5Zr40Nb6Sn2O1.5 alloy through Zr/O co-doping to induce local chemical orders (LCOs). This strategy yields an unprecedented combination of large recovery strain (εr > 4.5%), high superelastic stress (σc > 700 MPa), nearly temperature-independent strength (dσc/dT ≈ 0.59 MPa·K⁻¹), and stable elastic modulus over a wide temperature range from 123 K to 298 K. These properties arise from the elastic confinement imposed on the transformable matrix induced by the LCOs, which enhances resistance to dislocation slip and suppresses martensitic transformation, while the modulus hardening caused by LCOs due to anharmonic atomic vibrations compensates for matrix softening. This work provides a feasible and effective route for developing high-performance superelastic alloys with wide-temperature stability through LCO engineering.Subject terms: Metals and alloys, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Ongoing advances in deep-space exploration place stringent performance requirements on precision elastic devices. These devices are required to withstand alternating loads over a wide temperature range1,2 (e.g., the Martian surface temperature variation from 153 to 298 K) while maintaining stable elastic performance to ensure precise operation of instruments3–5. Applications such as suspension damping components and precision instrument springs require elastic materials to simultaneously exhibit (i) large recoverable strain (εr) to accommodate extreme deformations6, (ii) high superelastic stress (σc, the critical stress for inducing martensitic transformation) for lightweight design4,7,8, and (iii) temperature-independent elastic modulus (E) and σc to ensure stable performance over wide operating temperature ranges5,9–11.

The elastic strain limit of bulk metals typically remains below 1% due to dislocation initiation and/or premature failure induced by structural defects. Concurrently, the E exhibits anharmonic hardening with decreasing temperature12, rendering it inadequate for advanced applications. Shape memory alloys (SMAs), although capable of achieving 5–8% superelastic strain via stress-induced martensitic transformation (SIMT)6,13,14, typically exhibit either low superelastic stress or pronounced temperature sensitivity in both the σc and E. For example, Ti-Ni alloys exhibit a σc temperature sensitivity of 5–7 MPa/K, resulting in a constrained superelastic temperature window (<100 K) and performance instability13. Fe-Mn-Al-Ni alloys possess a σc below 600 MPa and a high density of 6.78 g/cm³, failing to meet the requirements for lightweight design11. Lightweight Mg-Sc alloys exhibit a σc of only 200 MPa, with a recovery strain of merely 1%5. To date, developing lightweight superelastic alloys that simultaneously possess high σc, large εr, and temperature-independent E and σc over wide operating temperature ranges remains a significant challenge.

Metastable β-type Ti-Nb superelastic alloys, renowned for their low density, high specific strength, superior corrosion resistance, and manufacturability, have emerged as promising candidates for aerospace elastic structural devices. However, their inherent properties and conventional microstructural design strategies present significant challenges in achieving the desired performance targets. Constrained by the β → α″ phase transformation strain, these alloys typically exhibit superelastic strains of less than 3%15, with critical stress for dislocation slip generally below 500 MPa16–18. Moreover, the high temperature sensitivity (dσc/dT > 3 MPa K⁻¹) leads to a narrow superelastic temperature range and unstable performance19. Although various alloying and microstructural regulation strategies have been explored to address these limitations, their effectiveness remains insufficient. For instance, the nanoscale Nb concentration modulation structure constructed through spinodal decomposition can broaden the superelastic temperature range20, but the elastic contribution entropy (ΔSE) of Nb-rich domains increases significantly as the temperature decreases, thereby exacerbating the temperature dependence of E (ΔE/E₀ΔT ≈ 2.7 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹) and σc (dσc/dT ≈ −4.2 MPa K⁻¹). Zr doping (substituting for Nb) can alter the lattice parameters of both β and α″ phases to enhance superelastic strain, but the strain increment is limited (<5%) and compromises the stability of the β-phase, thus narrowing the superelastic temperature window21,22. Oxygen doping, while beneficial for increasing the critical stress for dislocation slip and introducing local stress fields that suppress martensitic transformation23, excessive addition (>1 at%) causes significant lattice distortions at microstructural sites24, leading to brittleness and limiting potential performance improvements.

To overcome the challenge of simultaneously achieving large εr, high σc, and temperature-independent E and σc, this study proposes a Zr/O co-doping strategy in Ti-Nb alloys. This strategy facilitates the formation of local chemical orders (LCOs), developing a Ti50.5Zr40Nb6Sn2O1.5 superelastic alloy. The alloy demonstrates an exceptional combination of large recovery strain (εr > 4.5%), high superelastic stress (σc > 700 MPa), nearly temperature-independent strength (dσc/dT ≈ 0.59 MPa K⁻¹), and stable elastic modulus over a wide temperature range of 123–298 K. This work provides a feasible and effective route for designing high-performance superelastic alloys with wide-temperature stability via LCO engineering.

Figure 1c presents the correlation between elastic modulus and temperature for the O1.5 alloy over a temperature range of 123–298 K. The modulus was determined from the corresponding tensile loading-unloading curves at 2% strain, as shown in the inset. In contrast to the typical elastic hardening of Ti alloys such as TC4, in which the modulus increases with decreasing temperature, and the modulus softening observed in TiNb superelastic alloys, the O1.5 alloy exhibits a nearly temperature-independent Young’s modulus. This behavior is further corroborated by the storage modulus-temperature relationship shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Figure 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2 display the superelastic response of the O1.5 and O1.0 alloys under 4% tensile strain in the temperature range 123–298 K. As summarized in Fig. 1e, the superelastic stress (σc) of the O1.5 alloy remains above 700 MPa across this temperature range, with a nearly temperature insensitivity (dσc/dT) of 0.59 MPa/K. Compared to the reported polycrystalline Ti-based superelastic alloys17,20,22,25–29, the O1.5 alloy simultaneously exhibits the highest σc and the lowest temperature sensitivity. Figure 1f shows the σc and recoverable strain (εr) of the O1.5 and O1.0 alloys under 8% cyclic tensile strain from 123 to 298 K (Supplementary Fig. 3), demonstrating that the O1.5 alloy possesses the synergistic advantages of high σc and large εr. Furthermore, compared to other types of superelastic alloys such as commercial NiTi13, Mg-based5,7, and Fe-based shape memory alloys6,11, the O1.5 alloy combines high superelastic stress with a moderate density of 5.5 g/cm³, resulting in a competitive specific strength, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Figure 2d presents an aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope annular bright-field (STEM-ABF) image of the O1.5 alloy viewed along the [110]bcc zone axis, highlighting a region containing bright clusters. Figure 2d1 and 2d2 provide magnified views of the blue and yellow boxed regions, respectively. In Figure 2d1, additional bright spots (marked by white arrows) are observed in lattice interstices, a feature absent in Figure 2d2. This contrast variation indicates localized inhomogeneity. Given the intentional oxygen addition and the high sensitivity of ABF imaging to light elements, the interstitial bright contrast is primarily attributed to the inhomogeneous distribution of oxygen atoms. Specifically, these oxygen atoms occupy the octahedral interstitial sites in the BCC lattice31,32. The intensity line profile from the corresponding STEM-HAADF image (Fig. 2f1, green arrow in Fig. 2e) identifies a region with brighter atomic columns, signifying a local enrichment of heavier atoms, predominantly Zr. Conversely, the line profile from the STEM-ABF image (Fig. 2f2, blue arrow in Fig. 2d) reveals interstitial features indicative of oxygen enrichment. Specifically, these two regions exhibit precise spatial overlap. This direct correlation demonstrates that oxygen atoms preferentially occupy the octahedral interstitial sites adjacent to the Zr-rich lattice sites, thereby forming the oxygen-enriched LCOs.

To quantitatively verify the distribution of LCOs inferred from STEM contrast and to elucidate the associated elemental partitioning, we performed atom probe tomography (APT). The APT analysis shows the iso-composition surfaces with an oxygen content of 3.5 at% (Fig. 2g), which correspond to the oxygen-enriched LCOs observed in STEM images. Compositional profiles across the matrix/LCOs interfaces (Fig. 2h) demonstrate a significant depletion of Ti alongside a marked co-enrichment of O and Zr within the LCOs, accompanied by a slight enrichment of Nb and Sn. This quantitative partitioning behavior confirms the co-segregation trend of oxygen with heavy elements (particularly Zr) suggested by the spatial overlap in STEM analysis. A schematic model summarizing the LCO structure is presented in Fig. 2i. For comparison, the O0.5 alloy also forms similar oxygen-rich LCOs, but both the oxygen concentration and the number density of these ordered structures are significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Figure 3a illustrates the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of the O1.5 alloy taken along the [100] zone axis at room temperature. The intensity line profile extracted from the squared region (inset) reveals clear diffuse scattering between the fundamental β-phase diffraction spots, indicating the presence of nanodomains. Imaging this diffuse signal using dark-field transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 3b, reflections marked by yellow arrows in Fig. 3a) reveals a high density of fine, uniformly distributed nanodomains within the β-phase matrix, with an average size of 1.25 nm (Supplementary Fig. 9). Comparable nanodomains are also observed in the O0.5 and O1.0 alloys (Supplementary Fig. 10), though their average sizes are larger (2.26 nm and 1.74 nm, respectively). To further characterize these features at atomic resolution, aberration-corrected STEM-HAADF imaging was performed (Fig. 3c). The corresponding Fast Fourier Transform (FFT, inset) again shows diffuse scattering around the main reflections. An inverse FFT (IFFT) reconstruction filtered using the diffuse signal (cyan circle in FFT) highlights the real-space distribution of the nanodomains (Fig. 3d). Statistical analysis of over 100 such IFFT regions yields an average domain diameter of 1.35 nm (Supplementary Fig. 9), consistent with the dark-field results.

To resolve the crystal structure of the nanodomains, the IFFT image corresponding to the BCC lattice was superimposed onto the HAADF image in Fig. 3c, resulting in Fig. 3e. An enlarged view of the nanodomain within the white square (inset) reveals a characteristic shuffling of adjacent (011)β planes along the [011¯] and [01¯1] directions, respectively. Figures 3f1 and 3f2 display magnified views of atomic arrangements in the regions marked by cyan and yellow squares in Fig. 3c. By comparing the atomic column intensity peak positions between the green and blue reference regions (Figures 3f3 and 3f4), the plane shuffle magnitude in the nanodomain was quantified to be only 4.9% of the interplanar spacing of d(011)β. Furthermore, the shear magnitude was evaluated by comparing d(011)β in the BCC lattice (Fig. 3f3) with the lattice parameter, b, of the orthorhombic nanodomain Fig. 3f4. The calculated resulting shear magnitude is nearly zero (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting that the nanodomains are the O′ phase formed via shuffling of the β phase23.

Figure 3g shows a contrast-enhanced view of the HAADF image (Fig. 3c), which clearly distinguishes regions enriched in heavy atoms (bright) from those enriched in light atoms (dark). A direct spatial correspondence is observed: the nanodomains identified in the IFFT map (Fig. 3d) coincide with the light-atom-rich regions (encircled in white in Fig. 3g). This is corroborated by the line intensity profile across a representative area (yellow square, inset), confirming the local depletion of heavy elements. Given the alloy composition, these light-atom-rich regions are predominantly Ti-rich. Conversely, regions enriched with heavy atoms, primarily Zr, with contributions from Nb and Sn, tend to retain the β-phase structure. This stability is attributed to the local variations in transformation temperatures induced by LCOs33,34. To further demonstrate the effect of compositional heterogeneity on phase stability, we performed phase-field simulations. The simulated Zr concentration map for the O1.5 alloy (Fig. 3h) confirms that O′ nanodomains preferentially nucleate in Zr-lean (i.e., Ti-rich) regions (Fig. 3i), effectively capturing the experimentally observed correlation between local compositional variations and nanodomain formation. A schematic diagram of the O′ nanodomains is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 12. Moreover, in contrast to the O0.5 alloy, geometric phase analysis (GPA) of atomic lattice strain reveals significant nanoscale strain fluctuations in the O1.5 alloy, characterized by alternating tensile and compressive strains (Supplementary Fig. 13). This feature arises from the strong lattice distortion jointly induced by oxygen-enriched LCOs and nanodomains.

To explore the microstructural evolution of the O1.5 alloy upon cooling, In-situ characterization methods were employed. In-situ cooling XRD (Fig. 4a) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) results (Fig. 4b) demonstrate that the O1.5 alloy retains a single β phase structure throughout cooling, without any thermally induced martensitic transformation. However, the electrical resistivity curve (Fig. 4c) shows a monotonic increase in resistivity upon cooling, indicating the occurrence of a nanodomain transformation4. This behavior is consistent across other composition alloys (Supplementary Fig. 14). Direct structural evidence for this transformation is provided by in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) during cooling. The SAED patterns (Fig. 4d) reveal a progressive intensification of the additional diffuse scattering spots (highlighted by yellow arrows in the corresponding intensity profiles, Fig. 4e). Dark-field images obtained using these diffuse scattering spots (Fig. 4f) show that both the size and the volume fraction of the O′ nanodomains increase continuously as temperature decreases, exhibiting the characteristics of constrained continuous growth.

The temperature-dependent microstructural evolution of O’ nanodomains in the O1.5 alloy is further evidenced by phase-field simulation as shown in Fig. 4g. At 298 K, the β-phase matrix contains a small number of O′ nanodomains with limited size. As the temperature decreases, the β → O′ transformation proceeds continuously, where multiple crystallographic variants of O′ nanodomain nucleate and grow, leading to a gradual increase in their volume fraction (Fig. 4h). These simulation results are in excellent agreement with the in-situ TEM observations.

As the oxygen content increases from 0.5 to 1.5 at%, the relative intensity of the (110)β peak of the retained β phase increases from 0.26 to 0.68, indicating a decreased volume fraction of SIMT (β → α″). This trend demonstrates that higher oxygen levels significantly enhance the stability of the β phase. After unloading, the recovery of the (110)β peak intensity, quantified by increases of 0.03, 0.17, and 0.28 for the O0.5, O1.0, and O1.5 alloys, respectively, confirms a reverse α″ → β martensitic transformation. Figure 5c shows the 1D WAXRD patterns obtained by integrating the scattering intensity along the loading direction (φ = 0° ± 5°). Figure 5d quantitatively compares the interplanar spacing of (110)β planes perpendicular to the loading direction in the initial and 5%-strained states to evaluate the lattice strain. The derived lattice strain of the (110)β plane increases from 0.25% to 1.11% as oxygen content increases from 0.5 to 1.5 at%, suggesting that oxygen significantly enhances the elastic deformability of the β phase. Phase-field simulations were conducted to further elucidate the martensitic transformation mechanism. Figure 5e shows the simulated Zr concentration distribution field in the O1.5 alloy, while Fig. 5f depicts the corresponding stress-induced transformation sequence under increasing load. The results indicate that the martensitic variants (α″) preferentially nucleate in Zr-lean regions and gradually extend into Zr-rich regions as the external load increases. When the external stress reaches 1000 MPa, approximately 55% of the computational cells undergo martensitic transformation (Fig. 5g). Notably, these martensite variants remain confined to the nanoscale, with Zr-rich LCO regions effectively pinning the microstructure and retaining the β phase. These simulation results are consistent with the in-situ XRD observations, confirming the role of oxygen-induced LCOs in mediating a nanoscale-constrained martensitic transformation.

Discussion

Based on the microstructural and computational evidence presented, we elucidate why oxygen-enriched LCOs are capable of achieving high superelastic stress, large recovery strain, and temperature-insensitivity synergistically over a wide temperature range.

First, within the Ti–Zr–Nb–Sn–O system, enthalpy-driven interactions among constituent elements lead to stronger chemical affinity between certain atomic pairings (e.g., Zr–Sn, ΔHmix = −55 kJ/mol), while repulsive tendencies occur between others (e.g., Ti–Nb, ΔHmix = +2 kJ/mol)35. This promotes the formation of local light/heavy-atom-rich regions within the alloy and induces nanoscale compositional fluctuations36. Furthermore, the high binding energy between O and Zr atoms37, supported by their significant differences in atomic size and electronegativity (Supplementary Table 2), drives oxygen to preferentially segregate into these Zr-rich, heavy-atom regions. This promotes the formation of oxygen-enriched LCOs (Fig. 2f, h).

Consequently, the microstructure separates into two distinct nanoscale regions: O-enriched LCOs (predominantly Zr, along with minor amounts of Nb and Sn) and an O-lean, Ti-rich matrix. Previous studies have indicated that the martensitic transformation start temperature (Ms) of metastable β-phase Ti alloys is highly sensitive to composition. For instance, the Ms decreases sharply by 38, 160, and 81 K per 1 at% increase in Zr, O, and Sn contents22,24,38, respectively. Thus, the inherent compositional fluctuations create a broad, continuous distribution of local Ms temperatures spanning several hundred Kelvin. While the transformation becomes thermodynamically favorable in matrix regions where the local Ms is reached, its progression is strongly constrained. The surrounding oxygen-enriched LCOs, which possess a much lower local Ms, act as potent elastic barriers. This confinement effectively suppresses long-range martensitic transformation, instead promoting the formation of fine, dispersed O′ nanodomains within the matrix (Fig. 3b). Besides, increasing the overall oxygen content amplifies both the density and oxygen concentration of LCOs and makes their distribution more dispersed in the matrix (Supplementary Fig. 8), intensifying this spatial confinement, which in turn suppresses the spatial continuity of nanodomain growth, limiting them to a smaller scale. This results in a corresponding refinement and reduction in both the size and volume fraction of the O′ nanodomains (Supplementary Fig. 10). In contrast, when no additional oxygen is doped, the lack of LCOs makes it difficult to suppress the martensitic transformation during quenching, as observed in the O0 alloy (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Second, oxygen doping establishes a multi-mechanism strengthening effect. On the one hand, it increases the activation energy required for grain growth, resulting in grain refinement (Supplementary Fig. 6)39. On the other hand, the solid solution strengthening effect induced by co-doping with O and Zr (atomic size mismatch of ~7.8%), combined with the local lattice distortion from the oxygen-enriched LCOs (Supplementary Fig. 13), leads to a large strain field and enhances the lattice friction stress, consequently increasing the resistance to dislocation glide3,31. These synergistic actions collectively enhance the yield strength, thereby ensuring the exhibition of a high superelastic stress.

From the perspective of phase transformation regulation, the SIMT in the O-lean, Ti-rich regions consistently encounters resistance from the surrounding oxygen-enriched LCO regions with high β-phase stability during loading. This nanoscale elastic confinement acts as an effective barrier, effectively suppressing the autocatalytic growth of martensite plates and delaying the martensitic transformation (MT) process34,40. As the oxygen content increases, the proportion of oxygen-enriched LCO regions increases correspondingly, thereby enhancing this constraining effect. Simultaneously, the increased oxygen content significantly decreases the martensitic transformation temperature24. The combined effect of these factors raises the critical stress for phase transformation.

Third, the large recoverable strain of the O1.5 alloy arises from the synergistic contribution of the high elastic deformability of the β phase and the highly reversible stress-induced martensitic transformation. In-situ XRD analysis during loading (Fig. 5a) shows that the lattice parameter of the BCC β phase is a = 3.42 Å, while the α″ martensite phase exhibits lattice parameters of a = 3.14 Å, b = 5.24 Å, and c = 4.88 Å, under an applied macroscopic strain of ~5%. Based on the lattice deformation theory of β → α″ SIMT15, the theoretical maximum transformation strain for the most favorable stress-induced α″ martensite variant along the [011]β direction is calculated to be 8.2% in the O1.5 alloy, which significantly exceeds that of TiNb superelastic alloys (~3%). This is attributed to the high Zr content substituting for Nb21, which effectively tunes the lattice parameters of both phases and thereby provides a theoretical basis for achieving large recoverable strain.

Moreover, the recrystallization texture of the alloy shows a preferred orientation of <110>β ∥ rolling direction (Supplementary Fig. 15), which promotes cooperative deformation among grains and effectively increases the macroscopic phase transformation strain output41. Additionally, with increasing oxygen content, both the lattice strain of the β phase and the volume fraction of the reversible martensitic transformation increase (Fig. 5b, d). This behavior is closely related to the increased critical stress for dislocation slip, which raises the elastic strain limit of the material and suppresses dislocation nucleation and motion3, thereby endowing the SIMT with superior reversibility and facilitating strain recovery under high stress conditions.

Fourth, the weak temperature dependence of superelastic stress can be understood from both thermodynamic principles and microstructural perspectives. Thermodynamically, the critical stress σc follows the Clausius–Clapeyron relationship42, which is expressed as:

where ∆S is the entropy change during martensitic transformation, ε is the phase transformation strain, and Vm is the molar volume. For the binary TiNb26 alloy, the temperature sensitivity of σc is about 4.4 MPa/K19. Previous studies have shown that higher configurational entropy leads to lower entropy change during phase transformation43. Specifically, the configurational entropy of TiNb26 alloy is 0.57 R, while that of TiZr40Nb6Sn2O1.5 alloy is 1.02 R44. The O′ nanodomains dispersed within the matrix act as martensite embryos (Fig. 3b), which directly grow under applied stress without the nucleation process, leading to a further reduction in the effective entropy change. In addition, the maximum phase transformation strain (εmax) of this alloy system is approximately 8.2%, more than double that of TiNb26 alloy (~3%). The introduction of Zr with a larger atomic radius increases the Vm of this system to 12.04 cm³/mol, which is higher than the 10.62 cm³/mol of TiNb26 alloy45. As a result, the present alloy has a larger ε and Vm, and a smaller absolute value of ΔSMT. According to the Clausius-Clapeyron relationship, these characteristics endow the alloy with an intrinsic weak temperature dependence of σc.

Furthermore, from the perspective of microstructure, as the temperature decreases, the lattice strain fields in the oxygen-enriched LCO regions, caused by the atomic size difference between Zr and O, become increasingly difficult to relax or release due to diminished thermal vibrations40. The accumulated elastic strain energy further enhances the confinement effect on the surrounding lattices. This enhanced confinement effect acts as a stronger kinetic barrier for SIMT, thereby compensating for the thermodynamic trend of decreasing superelastic stress. Meanwhile, the prerequisite for martensitic transformation in the O-lean, Ti-rich regions is overcoming the elastic resistance of the oxygen-enriched LCO regions. This strong confinement effect effectively reduces the amount of SIMT (β → α″) under high stress conditions, resulting in a substantial fraction of the untransformed β phase (Fig. 5b). The persistent presence of the oxygen-enriched LCO regions enables the alloy to retain its elastic deformation capacity over a wide temperature range, while the temperature insensitivity of the apparent elastic deformation stress further diminishes the dσc/dT value46.

Fifth, the near-constant elastic modulus arises from the suppression of thermally induced first-order (discontinuous) martensitic transformation during cooling (Fig. 4a, b). This suppression promotes the evolution of nanodomains in a confined continuous growth mode (Fig. 4f), resulting in reduced lattice instability of the matrix. This weaker elastic softening effect exactly offsets the typical elastic hardening trend in the oxygen-enriched LCO regions associated with anharmonic atomic vibrations5. As a result, the alloy exhibits a nearly temperature-independent elastic modulus.

In summary, we report a Ti50.5Zr40Nb6Sn2O1.5 superelastic alloy that simultaneously exhibits a large recovery strain while maintaining near-constant high superelastic stress and elastic modulus over a broad temperature range. This exceptional performance originates from the confinement effect of LCO regions on the transformable matrix. The LCO regions act as elastic constraint barriers, suppressing stress- and thermally-induced martensitic transformation, thereby strengthening the alloy by impeding dislocation motion and enhancing transformation reversibility. Furthermore, the kinetic compensation effect and anharmonic hardening behavior of the LCO regions counterbalance the decrease in critical transformation stress and the elastic modulus softening of the matrix. Our findings provide a class of elastic materials for applications in extreme temperature environments, and offer a feasible and effective route for developing high-performance superelastic alloys via LCO engineering.

Methods

Sample preparation

The ingots with nominal compositions of Ti–40Zr–6Nb–2Sn–xO (x = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 at%) were prepared by arc-melting furnace under pure argon atmosphere, using high-purity Ti, Zr, Nb, Sn (more than 99.95 wt%) and TiO₂ powder (99.99 wt%). The TiO₂ powder was wrapped in thin pure Ti sheets. Each ingot was flipped and remelted at least six times to ensure chemical homogeneity. Oxygen contents in the as-cast alloys were measured by inert gas fusion analysis (LECO-ONH836), and the corresponding data are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The as-cast ingots were subsequently sealed in quartz tubes under vacuum, homogenized at 1273 K for 7.2 ks, and water-quenched. Following homogenization, the ingots were cold-rolled to a final thickness of 0.15 mm, corresponding to a total thickness reduction of 98%. Specimens for tensile tests were then cut from the rolled sheet via electrical discharge machining along the rolling direction. The samples were annealed in evacuated quartz tubes (5 × 10−4 torr). A final annealing treatment was conducted at 873 K for 300 s, followed by water quenching. As the O0 alloy exhibits a martensite phase at room temperature, it is not discussed in detail in the main text. The corresponding mechanical properties and phase transformation behavior are presented in the Supplementary Information.

Mechanical characterization

The stress-strain curves were recorded using a Shimadzu AGS-X tensile machine and a WDT II-20 Instron test machine, both equipped with an environmental temperature control box, at a strain rate of 6 × 10−4 s−1. The dimensions of the samples for the tensile test were 50 mm in length, 0.15 mm in thickness, and 2.2 mm in width, the gage length was 30 mm. The loading axis was parallel to the rolling direction. Young’s modulus was calculated from the elastic regime in the tensile stress–strain curves by the secant modulus method, and superelastic stress (σc) was determined by using 0.2% offset strain method.

Phase transformation behavior

Phase transformation behavior was analyzed using a Q20 differential scanning calorimetry instrument from TA Instruments. Electrical resistance was measured via the four-probe method on an RTL550 instrument equipped with a temperature control system. The test sample dimensions were 20 mm (length) × 2.2 mm (width) × 0.15 mm (thickness), with a heating/cooling rate of 10 K/min. A dynamical mechanical analyzer (TA, Q800) was employed to measure the storage modulus as a function of temperature at different frequencies (0.2, 1, 5, 20 Hz) with a cooling/heating rate of 10 K/min under thin film stretching mode.

Structural determination

The phase constitution at different temperatures/mechanical deformation was determined by transmission wide-angle X-ray diffraction NANOPIX-WE system with Mo Kα radiation (wavelength = 0.7093 Å). The samples had dimensions of 2.2 × 0.15 × 50 mm3, with a heating/cooling rate of 10 K/min and an applied tensile strain rate of 6 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. Electron back-scattered diffraction (EBSD) characterization was conducted by an FEI Scios 2 Hivac focused ion beam field-emission scanning electron microscope equipped with a Bruker eFlash FS electron backscatter diffraction system, operated at an acceleration voltage of 30 kV with a step size of 30 nm and an exposure time of 12 ms. The EBSD specimens were mechanically polished and argon ion polished to obtain a flat surface and remove strain layers, and the data were analyzed using MTEX software (version 5.04)47.

Atom probe tomography measurement

Needle sample for APT testing was prepared by focused ion beam milling on a dual-beam FEI Helios G4 UX instrument. The needle sample was analyzed using a CAMECA LEAP 4000X HR atom probe by laser mode with a pulse repetition rate of 250 kHz and laser energy of 60 pJ. The base temperature of the sample was kept at 50 K, and the evaporation rate was kept at 1%. Quantitative compositional analyses of the samples and the associated 3D reconstructions of atom maps were conducted by the IVAS 3.6.8 software.

TEM characterization

FEI Tecnai G2 F20 was used for bright-field and dark-field imaging, as well as SAED. STEM-HAADF was performed with a double aberration-corrected FEI Titan-Themis-Z instrument containing a superXG2 EDS addon to analyse the atomic-resolution structure and the chemical distribution. An aberration-corrected Spectra 300 S/TEM was used to analyse the atomic-resolution chemical distribution. The microstructural observations at different temperatures were conducted with an aberration-corrected JEM-ARM200F instrument equipped with a Gatan 915 double-tilt sample holder.

The STEM (FEI Titan-Themis-Z) was performed at 300 kV with a convergence semiangle of 25.2 mrad. The apertures of the optics were C1 = 2 mm, C2 = 70 μm, and C3 = 2 mm. The spot size was nine. The gun lens was 783, and the beam current was set between 25 and 30 pA. The full EDS mapping process took roughly 0.5 h to achieve a reasonably high signal-to-noise ratio. The TEM specimens were first mechanically ground to 50 μm thickness and then twin-jet electropolished using 93% methanol + 5% H2SO4 + 2% HF (vol%) solution at about 233 K. High-resolution TEM images were analyzed using Digital Micrograph software (3.51 version, Gatan). Atomic strain maps were obtained by using the geometric phase analysis method48.
