Exploratory insights into salivary E-cadherin as a non-invasive biomarker in a rat model of diabetes
Authors: Sarah Binhassan, Hajera Tabassum, Abdullah M. Alsoghier, Manal Abudawood, Reem Alrashoudi, Islam A. Elredah, Hassan H. Kaabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
E-cadherin, an adhesion molecule critical for epithelial integrity, may be downregulated by oxidative stress (OS) in diabetes. While serum E-cadherin has been investigated in various conditions, its presence in saliva and potential as a non-invasive biomarker for diabetes remain poorly understood. This study aimed to evaluate salivary E-cadherin levels in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rats and examine their relationship with OS markers, assessing the potential of E-cadherin as a non-invasive early diagnostic biomarker for diabetes. Twenty male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to control and diabetic groups. The diabetic model was established by administering a single intraperitoneal injection of 65 mg/kg STZ. Saliva was collected via pilocarpine stimulation, and blood samples were obtained for serum analysis. Salivary and serum E-cadherin levels, alongside serum OS markers including malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were measured. Pearson’s correlation assessed relationships between E-cadherin and OS markers. Diabetic rats exhibited significantly lower salivary E-cadherin levels (0.60 ± 0.15 ng/ml) compared to controls (1.21 ± 0.45 ng/ml; p < 0.05). Serum E-cadherin levels were higher than salivary levels in both groups (Control: 18.17 ± 2.23 vs. 1.21 ± 0.45 ng/ml, p < 0.001; Diabetic: 15.64 ± 3.62 vs. 0.60 ± 0.15 ng/ml, p < 0.001), but serum E-cadherin was also reduced in diabetic rats (15.64 ± 3.62 ng/ml) compared to controls (18.17 ± 2.23 ng/ml; p < 0.05). Diabetic rats showed elevated MDA (1.32 ± 0.03 nmol/ml vs. 0.50 ± 0.04 nmol/ml in controls; p < 0.001) and decreased antioxidant markers: GSH (0.76 ± 0.06 vs. 2.55 ± 0.04 µg/ml; p < 0.001), SOD (0.11 ± 0.02 vs. 0.18 ± 0.02 inhibition rate; p < 0.001), and CAT (2.02 ± 0.17 vs. 4.84 ± 0.56 mU/ml; p < 0.001). Furthermore, significant negative correlations were observed between E-cadherin and MDA levels (p < 0.05), while salivary E-cadherin positively correlated with GSH (p < 0.01) and SOD (p < 0.05). This preclinical investigation is the first to show that salivary E-cadherin levels are reduced in diabetic rats and correlate with serum levels, indicating that saliva may reflect systemic changes. Associations with oxidative stress markers suggest that oxidative imbalance contributes to E-cadherin downregulation. These findings provide preliminary evidence for salivary E-cadherin as a promising non-invasive biomarker for early detection and monitoring of diabetes-related epithelial dysfunction. Its translational potential lies in enabling simple, stress-free, and accessible screening approaches that could aid in early diagnosis and disease management in clinical settings.

Introduction

With the rising prevalence of metabolic diseases, diabetes mellitus (DM) has become a global health concern, affecting over half a billion people1. This chronic disorder is characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, oxidative stress (OS), and systemic inflammation, contributing to widespread cellular and tissue dysfunction2. Researchers have recently intensified efforts to identify sensitive and reliable biomarkers for early DM detection and monitoring. While blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) remain the gold standard for diagnosis, these methods rely on invasive procedures like venous puncture, often causing discomfort and distress and making frequent monitoring challenging for patients3. In response to this challenge, salivary biomarkers have emerged as promising non-invasive alternatives owing to their proteomic similarity to blood4,5. Evidence from research highlights robust associations between salivary and blood glucose markers, with salivary glucose, HbA1c, and fructosamine exhibiting significant positive correlations with blood glucose and HbA1c levels3. Immunoglobulin A (IgA) biomarkers have been linked to DM and may reflect oral manifestations, particularly in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)6. Given its ease of collection, saliva serves as a useful adjunct to blood-based diagnostics, especially for elderly, critically ill, and debilitated patients7. Increasing efforts to identify novel salivary markers are not only enhancing the potential of saliva in diabetes monitoring and detection but are also driving advances in biomarker discovery by providing a readily accessible medium for early disease detection.

One of the key complications of diabetes is epithelial barrier dysfunction, which extends to various organs, including the oral cavity and salivary tissues7. Cadherins are glycoproteins involved in cell-cell adhesion, and among them, Epithelial cadherin (E-Cadherin) is a critical component of adherens junctions. As a 120 kDa transmembrane glycoprotein, E-Cadherin facilitates calcium-dependent adhesion in epithelial cells and is essential for maintaining epithelial integrity, including in the endocrine pancreas8. In pancreatic islets, the disruption of E-cadherin-mediated cell adhesion has been shown to impair glucose-stimulated calcium signaling and insulin secretion, establishing a direct link between E-cadherin loss in β-cells and defective insulin release9. Studies in type 2 diabetes models have further demonstrated reduced E-cadherin expression, often accompanied by disorganized islet architecture10. Similarly, a decline in E-cadherin expression within the gingival epithelium has been associated with periodontal disease, while its altered expression has been implicated in various diabetes-related complications, including nephropathy and retinopathy11. Diabetes induction by STZ in a rat model demonstrated that hyperglycemia significantly reduced E-cadherin expression in the dorsal tongue epithelium and was associated with decreased epithelial thickness12. This finding suggests that chronic hyperglycemia may compromise epithelial integrity by altering E-cadherin-mediated cell–cell adhesion, potentially contributing to the oral epithelial alterations observed in diabetic patients. Beyond epithelial tissue changes, serum and urinary E-cadherin levels have been reported in diabetic nephropathy (DN), supporting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for kidney complications and indicating that E-cadherin alterations in diabetes may involve both local and systemic processes13.

Recent research highlights the critical role of OS in the development of metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders, including DM14. While changes in E-cadherin expression have been linked to various pathological conditions, including diabetes-related tissue damage, the relationship between diabetes and E-cadherin in the context of OS remains inadequately understood, especially concerning its role in saliva and systemic circulation. Elevated glucose in diabetes drives ROS-mediated oxidative stress, promoting tissue injury and diabetes-related complications15. However, the precise mechanisms underlying OS, particularly in the interplay between saliva and systemic circulation, remain inadequately understood. Given that saliva is a non-invasive biofluid reflecting systemic physiological changes, analyzing salivary E-cadherin may offer valuable insights into the impact of diabetes. Although prior investigations have examined the impact of hyperglycemia on OS markers across various tissues, direct comparisons of salivary and serum E-cadherin in diabetic models remain scarce15. To address this gap, the present study evaluates salivary E-cadherin levels in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rats and examines their relationship with serum E-cadherin and oxidative stress markers. The aim is to assess the potential of salivary E-cadherin as an early and non-invasive diagnostic marker for diabetes.

Result

At baseline, body weights were comparable between groups (275.77 ± 17.11 g in controls vs. 292.88 ± 17.12 g in diabetic rats). By the end of the experiment, control rats gained weight substantially (453.0 ± 29.95 g), while diabetic rats showed impaired growth with significantly lower final body weights (322.80 ± 97.50 g; p < 0.05). FBG was markedly elevated in the diabetic group (440.0 ± 75.10 mg/dl) compared with normal levels in controls (96.44 ± 2.88 mg/dl; p < 0.05). In addition, diabetic rats exhibited higher serum creatinine and urea, along with significantly elevated ALT and AST activities, relative to controls (p < 0.01) (Table 1).

Compared to controls, diabetic rats demonstrated significantly lower E-cadherin concentrations in serum and saliva (p < 0.05), as summarized in Table 2. When the two biofluids were compared directly, serum E-cadherin levels were consistently higher than salivary levels in both groups, and this difference was highly significant (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the OS markers showed significant alterations between diabetic and control groups (Fig. 1). MDA levels were markedly higher in diabetic rats (1.32 ± 0.03 nmol/ml) than in controls (0.50 ± 0.04 nmol/ml; p < 0.001), indicating increased lipid peroxidation. In contrast, antioxidant markers were significantly lower in the diabetic group compared to controls. GSH levels were reduced in diabetic rats (0.76 ± 0.06 µg/ml) relative to controls (2.55 ± 0.04 µg/ml; p < 0.001). Likewise, SOD activity was lower in diabetic rats (0.11 ± 0.02 inhibition rate) compared to controls (0.18 ± 0.02 inhibition rate, expressed in % in Fig. 1; p < 0.001), and CAT activity was also reduced (2.02 ± 0.17 mU/ml vs. 4.84 ± 0.56 mU/ml; p < 0.001).

Table 3 presents the correlations between E-cadherin and OS markers. Both salivary and serum E-cadherin were negatively associated with MDA (r = -0.66 and − 0.65, p < 0.05). Salivary E-cadherin showed a positive association with GSH (r = 0.78, p < 0.01) and SOD (r = 0.63, p < 0.05), with similar trends observed for serum E-cadherin. Decreased levels of GSH and SOD were positively correlated with diminished serum E-cadherin levels in diabetic rats. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between salivary and serum E-cadherin levels (r = 0.65, p < 0.05). Discussion

This study provides an integrated view of the systemic and local effects of STZ-induced diabetes on E-cadherin expression and its relationship with OS markers. While E-cadherin has been previously studied in serum, urine, and tissues in diabetes, our study is among the first to assess salivary E-cadherin, offering a minimally invasive approach that may complement existing systemic measurements. Although OS in diabetes has been extensively described, the combined assessment of salivary E-cadherin with OS markers provides novel insights into the interplay between epithelial integrity and redox imbalance in a non-invasive biofluid, which has not been previously explored.

Our primary findings reveal significant decreases in E-cadherin levels in both serum and saliva of diabetic rats, which were strongly correlated with OS markers. Reduced E-cadherin was associated with elevated MDA and decreased antioxidants (GSH, SOD, and CAT). Importantly, a significant positive correlation was observed between salivary and serum E-cadherin levels, providing direct evidence that salivary E-cadherin can mirror systemic changes. These results highlight the central role of OS in diabetes pathophysiology and its direct influence on cell adhesion proteins such as E-cadherin. The consistency of E-cadherin reduction in both serum and saliva, along with their strong correlation, suggests that salivary measurements could reliably mirror systemic changes, offering a practical, non-invasive alternative to blood tests for assessing epithelial integrity and monitoring OS-related alterations in diabetes. Beyond E-cadherin, diabetic rats also exhibited hyperglycemia and elevated renal and liver biomarkers, reflecting multi-organ involvement.

E-cadherin is fundamental to maintaining epithelial integrity, particularly in the oral mucosa, by regulating cell-to-cell adhesion and epithelial barrier function16. In the oral gingival epithelium (OGE), E-cadherin provides structural stability and protection against external stressors17. Disruptions in its expression compromise epithelial homeostasis, increasing tissue vulnerability to inflammation and disease. Previous research supports this, showing significantly reduced E-cadherin in gingival epithelium during periodontal disease18 and altered salivary E-cadherin levels in patients with advanced oral squamous cell carcinoma. Our findings align with clinical studies reporting variable serum E-cadherin levels in diabetic patients, often correlating with increased disease severity and complications like nephropathy and cardiovascular disorders10,13. Critically, this study demonstrates that reductions in E-cadherin are also detectable in saliva, a less explored biofluid, highlighting its potential for non-invasive monitoring. Saliva testing offers several advantages, being non-invasive, simple, and easy to collect19, which may enhance its utility for early detection and monitoring, particularly when blood sampling is challenging. Recent studies have shown that salivary biomarkers including E-cadherin, OS markers, and inflammatory cytokines accurately reflect systemic changes in diabetes20, supporting further exploration of salivary E-cadherin as a complementary marker.

The decrease in serum and salivary E-cadherin in diabetic rats suggests a link between impaired E-cadherin expression and diabetes-induced oxidative damage to epithelial integrity. Our results align with previous findings in diabetic patients10,21 and diabetic rat studies reporting decreased gene and protein expression of E-cadherin in renal fibrosis21, as well as weakened E-cadherin-mediated adhesion in the lingua epithelia12. Persistent hyperglycemia may disrupt epithelial junctions through OS and altered cadherin regulation. However, data on cadherin levels in diabetes are conflicting. While some studies report decreased serum E-cadherin in DN, others indicate elevated levels that progressively rise with disease advancement10,13. For instance, a Chinese study found significantly elevated urinary E-cadherin levels in early-stage T2DM patients, with levels continuing to increase as DN progressed22. Similarly, Koziolek et al.., 2020 demonstrated that urinary E-cadherin serves as an early indicator of kidney damage in individuals with diabetes showing elevated levels in those with nephropathy and effectively distinguishing between different stages of diabetic nephropathy13. These discrepancies suggest that E-cadherin expression patterns may vary based on disease stage and subtype. Despite these variations, cadherins, particularly E-cadherin, undeniably play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications.

Systemic OS is a well-established factor driving diabetes pathogenesis23. It originates in pancreatic cells, initiating inflammation, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and ultimately, pancreatic beta-cell death15. This beta-cell loss leads to hypoinsulinemia and hyperglycemia, creating a vicious cycle of OS that propagates throughout the body, contributing to structural and functional changes in various organs and promoting diabetes-related complications. Modern perspectives recognize diabetes as a multifactorial disorder, where chronic hyperglycemia, glucose variability, and hypoglycemic episodes all generate OS24,25. This amplifies cellular damage and dysfunction across multiple organs, perpetuating tissue injury and worsening disease progression. In line with this, our study revealed elevated MDA levels and decreased antioxidant markers in diabetic rats, reflecting a critical imbalance between pro-oxidants and antioxidants. These findings align with previous research26,27 and highlights the importance of GSH, a key non-enzymatic antioxidant, whose depletion contributes to increased MDA and may further impair enzymatic antioxidants like SOD and CAT28,29.

E-cadherin, is highly vulnerable to OS in diabetes30. The decrease in E-cadherin observed in both serum and saliva of diabetic rats suggests that reactive oxygen species (ROS) may contribute to its reduction via several pathways. One pathway involves activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9), which cleave the extracellular portion of E-cadherin, resulting in increased shedding from the cell membrane31. In addition, OS can trigger the transcriptional repressor Snail, which downregulates E-cadherin expression by promoting protein degradation and inhibiting transcription32. OS may also stimulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, causing β-catenin to move into the nucleus and further suppress E-cadherin transcription33. Collectively, these mechanisms can drive epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), characterized by the loss of epithelial markers like E-cadherin and the acquisition of mesenchymal features, promoting tissue remodeling and fibrosis. The strong correlation between reduced E-cadherin and OS markers supports the idea that oxidative imbalance disrupts epithelial integrity. Consequently, the lowered salivary E-cadherin likely reflects a combination of reduced expression and enhanced shedding, highlighting its potential as a non-invasive biomarker for early detection and monitoring of diabetes-related epithelial dysfunction. Importantly, salivary E-cadherin reflects systemic alterations, highlighting its potential as a non-invasive biomarker. Further studies are needed to clarify its molecular regulation, explore strategies to restore expression, and validate its clinical utility in larger cohorts, including human subjects. Such investigations could pave the way for stress-free, accessible approaches for early detection and longitudinal monitoring of diabetes-related epithelial dysfunction.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that diabetes-induced oxidative stress is associated with reduced E-cadherin levels, with moderate to strong correlations between salivary and serum E-cadherin and oxidative stress markers. Our findings highlight the potential of salivary E-cadherin as a non-invasive biomarker for early detection and monitoring of diabetes-related epithelial dysfunction. Although preliminary and based on a rat model, these results provide a foundation for future studies in larger cohorts, including human subjects, to validate its clinical utility. Salivary E-cadherin could offer a practical, stress-free alternative to blood-based assessments, facilitating accessible screening and longitudinal monitoring in clinical and community settings.

Study limitations

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size. As the correlations are based on a small number of animals, the observed associations may be inflated and should be interpreted cautiously. While the findings suggest a relationship between salivary E-cadherin and oxidative stress in diabetes, further validation in larger preclinical and clinical cohorts is needed. Additionally, the study did not include validation in human samples, and mechanistic experiments to confirm the causal relationship between E-cadherin and oxidative stress were not performed.

Methodology

Ethical statement

The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee at King Saud University (KSU-SE-23-72). All experiments were carried out at the Central Research Laboratory (CRL), King Saud University, Saudi Arabia, with animals housed and handled in accordance with established guidelines for laboratory animal care. Compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines34, was ensured, and the ARRIVE checklist was utilized to provide comprehensive information on the experimental design, determination of sample size, randomization and blinding procedures, as well as steps taken to protect animal welfare.

Animal model and study design

Twenty male Wistar rats (8–10 weeks old, 250–320 g) were included in this study, with the number of animals (n = 20) determined by power analysis using G*Power (v3.1.9.4) to achieve 95% statistical power, an effect size of 0.8, and a significance level of 0.05. Male rats were selected to avoid hormonal fluctuations associated with the estrous cycle in females, which can influence glucose metabolism and oxidative stress markers. This choice ensures more consistent and reproducible results in the diabetic model. To minimize variability, rats with similar age, body mass, and physiological condition were included and all procedures were carefully standardized in terms of housing, diet, and handling. To allow for proper acclimation, rats were individually housed in clear-sided cages for a 10-day period prior to the experiment. Environmental conditions were strictly controlled, with a room temperature of 22 °C, relative humidity of 55%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Rats had unrestricted access to food and water throughout this period. After an adjustment period, animals were randomly assigned to two groups of ten. To reduce bias, samples were coded, and data collection and analysis were performed by investigators blinded to group assignments. For anesthesia, animals received a single intraperitoneal injection containing a sterile solution of ketamine (95 mg/kg) and xylazine (14 mg/kg), which was administered with a 25-gauge needle. To induce diabetes, the diabetic model group (Group II) was administered freshly prepared streptozotocin (STZ; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 65 mg/kg body weight (2.0 ml/kg), dissolved at 32.5 mg/ml in sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5). The control group (Group I) received a volume-matched intraperitoneal injection of sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5) as vehicle. The induction of diabetes in this study was performed based on well-established protocols in rodent models35,36. Specifically, Furman (2021) provides a detailed overview of STZ-induced diabetic models in mice and rats, including recommended dosages, preparation, and administration procedures35. Rehman et al.., (2023) further support this methodology by demonstrating the impact of STZ at similar doses on glucose homeostasis in rats36. All injections were performed intraperitoneally into the caudal abdominal cavity using a sterile 25-gauge needle.

Prior to injection, each rat was positioned dorsally, and the injection site was sterilized with povidone-iodine. Following STZ administration, control animals were maintained on regular drinking water, while the STZ-treated group received a 10% sucrose solution for the initial two days to mitigate the risk of hypoglycemia. They were then returned to normal water. The success of diabetes induction was verified on day 5 by measuring fasting blood glucose from tail vein samples after a 6–8 h fast, using a Contour Next ONE glucometer. A blood glucose level greater than 250 mg/dl was considered confirmation of diabetes. Body weight and blood glucose were monitored at both the baseline and end of the study. All rats were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation ten weeks after the initial injection, in accordance with the AVMA (2020) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals37.Prior to the procedure, the CO₂ cylinder, regulator, and flow meter were inspected to ensure proper function, and all steps were conducted in a well-ventilated area with appropriate personal protective equipment. Each rat was placed in a chamber of suitable size, and CO₂ was introduced gradually at a rate of 10–30% of the chamber volume per minute from the top or side to minimize distress. Loss of consciousness was confirmed by the absence of the righting reflex and response to a toe pinch, after which CO₂ flow was maintained for at least one minute following the cessation of respiration. Death was verified by the absence of heartbeat, respiratory movement, and corneal reflex.

Sample collection

Saliva collection: Saliva samples were obtained using pilocarpine stimulation following a previously described protocol by Bagavant (2018)38. Pilocarpine solution (0.006% w/v) was prepared by dissolving 3 mg of pilocarpine hydrochloride (Catalogue number SIALP6503-5G, Sigma) in 50 mL of sterile saline. Anesthesia was subsequently induced in the rats by a single intraperitoneal (IP) injection of the anesthetic mixture at a dose of 200 mg/kg body weight (BW). A period of 3–4 min was allowed to ensure complete sedation. Once anesthetized, pilocarpine was administered via IP injection at 0.375 mg/kg BW, taking care to follow precise dosing to prevent toxicity. A sterile, non-toxic polyurethane swab was carefully inserted into the rat’s oral cavity using forceps, ensuring stability without causing obstruction. After 15 min, the sponge was removed and placed in a perforated 0.5 ml tube inside a 2 ml tube. After eliminating all sponges, the tubes were centrifuged at 8000 g for 3 min with the 2 mL tube lid open. After centrifugation, the saliva was collected from the bottom of the tube. Each rat yielded approximately 150 µL of saliva during a single collection period. Although saliva was collected only once per animal, the collection procedure was performed under strictly controlled and standardized conditions (same pilocarpine dose, anesthesia protocol, collection duration, and environmental conditions) across all animals, ensuring consistency of the measurements. Previous studies using this method have demonstrated that a single pilocarpine-stimulated collection provides reliable and physiologically meaningful assessment of salivary gland function in rodents38. Saliva samples were immediately stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Blood collection and serum preparation: Blood samples were obtained by cardiac puncture under anesthesia11. Serum was separated from the collected blood by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Basic biochemical investigation

Serum creatinine and urea levels, along with liver function enzymes including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), were quantitatively determined using commercially available colorimetric enzymatic assay kits (Quimica Clinica Aplicada (QCA), Spain). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and absorbance readings were obtained using a spectrophotometer (Jenway 6405, UK). Results were expressed in mg/dl for creatinine and urea and in U/L for ALT and AST.

Quantification of E-cadherin in saliva and serum

E-cadherin levels in saliva and serum were measured using Rat Cadherin, epithelial ELISA kit (MBS458273, Mybiosource, San Diego, USA). Assays were conducted as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting absorbance was measured on a microplate reader (Tecan, Sunrise, Austria). A 100 µl volume of either the standard or the sample was pipetted into each well and all samples were analyzed in duplicates for accuracy. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for a period of 60 min. After washing, 100 µl of detection solutions A and B were sequentially added, each kept for 45 min in an incubator maintained at 37 °C, with washes in between. Substrate (90 µl) was incubated for 20 min, followed by 50 µl stop solution. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Sunrise, Austria). The assay demonstrates high sensitivity with a lower limit of detection (LOD) of 0.079 ng/ml, and a detection range of 0.312–20 ng/ml. It is specific for rat Epithelial cadherin and exhibits excellent specificity, and is validated for use in all biological fluids, including serum, plasma, and saliva, ensuring reliable and accurate detection across these sample types; standard curves were concurrently run with all samples to verify proper quantification in saliva.

Evaluation of oxidative stress

All ELISA kits used in this study are validated by the manufacturers for use in all biological fluids, including serum, plasma, and saliva. Standard curves were run in parallel with all samples to confirm accurate quantification in the matrices used.

Lipid peroxidation (Malondialdehyde-MDA) assay

The extent of lipid peroxidation was assessed by quantitatively determining malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a commercially available MDA Assay Kit (ab118970, Abcam, UK). Briefly, serum samples were combined with a thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution and incubated at 95 °C for 60 min to facilitate the formation of MDA-TBA adducts. The reaction was stopped by cooling the samples on ice, followed by centrifugation to remove precipitates. The resulting supernatant was then collected for measurement. Absorbance was recorded at 532 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Sunrise, Austria). A standard curve was generated using MDA concentrations between 4 and 20 nmol/well (20–100 µM), with the assay sensitivity > 0.1 nmol/well. MDA concentration was expressed as nmol/ml.

Analysis of antioxidant markers

Glutathione assay

Glutathione (GSH) levels were quantified using the Glutathione Assay Kit (CS0260-1KT, Sigma, USA). Serum samples were treated with 5,5’-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) in the presence of glutathione reductase and NADPH. The assay monitors the conversion of DTNB to 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB). This conversion is driven by the recycling of oxidized glutathione by glutathione reductase and NADPH, and the formation of TNB was measured colorimetrically at 412 nm using a Tecan Sunrise microplate reader and GSH concentration was expressed in µg/ml, with the assay detecting concentrations as low as 0.0001 mg/L.

Antioxidant enzymes: superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT)

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity was quantified using SOD Assay Kit-WST (19160, Sigma). The assay is based on the ability of SOD to inhibit the reduction of the water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST-1) by superoxide anions, producing a colored formazan dye. Serum samples were incubated with the WST-1 reagent and xanthine oxidase, and the rate of formazan formation was measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The activity of superoxide SOD was presented in inhibition rate. The kit has an LOD of 0.001 U/ml and a detection range of 0.001–200 U/ml.

Catalase Activity Assay Kit (ab83464, Abcam, UK) was used to quantify catalase activity. Serum samples and standards were incubated with hydrogen peroxide at 25 °C, followed by stop solution and developer addition. Catalase decomposed H₂O₂, and residual H₂O₂ reacted with a probe to produce a colorimetric signal. The intensity of the color was read at 570 nm using a Tecan Sunrise microplate reader, and activity was expressed in mU/ml. The kit has LOD of 1 µU and a detection range of 1–100 µU.

Statistical analysis

Sigma Plot software (version 14) was used to analyze the data. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Student’s t-test was then used to compare continuous variables, including body weight, blood glucose, serum and salivary E-cadherin levels, and serum oxidative stress markers, between the diabetic and control groups. The comparison of salivary and serum cadherin levels between the groups was conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by the Holm-Sidak post hoc test. Pearson’s correlation was applied to assess the relationship between E-cadherin levels and OS markers in saliva and serum. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, the correlations between salivary and serum E-cadherins with OS markers were illustrated by scatter plots.
