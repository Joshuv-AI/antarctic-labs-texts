Sampling Polynomial Rational Remainders with SP ℚ \mathbb{Q} R: A new Package for Polynomial Division and Elimination
Vsevolod Chestnov, Giulio Crisanti

Abstract
We introduce SP ℚ \mathbb{Q} R, a new Mathematica package for the division and elimination of variables from polynomial systems. SP ℚ \mathbb{Q} R works by sampling and reconstructing results over finite fields, in an analogous manner to many state of the art Integration by Parts algorithms for Feynman integrals. This allows SP ℚ \mathbb{Q} R to effectively overcome expression swell during the construction of Gröbner bases, which in many cases is the major bottleneck in such computations. Benchmarks on state of the art Macaulay resultants show that SP ℚ \mathbb{Q} R can deliver substantial gains over symbolic computer algebra workflows — reducing both runtime and memory footprint by multiple orders of magnitude. Likewise when applied to study Feynman integrals, we show how SP ℚ \mathbb{Q} R can be used to find previously unknown Landau singularities.

Finally this strategy is massively parallelisable, as numerical evaluations of sample points are fully independent calculations.
This allows for effective scaling against available computer resources.

Theoretical Background

As already anticipated, the core functionality behind SP
\mathbb{Q}
R revolves around understanding the structure and eliminating variables from systems of equations. This is primarily achieved through the use of polynomial division as well as related operations, which are performed over finite fields in SP
\mathbb{Q}

2.1
Review of Polynomial Division

2.1.1
Univariate Polynomial Division

Given two univariate polynomials
f(x)
and

f(x)=q(x)\,p(x)+r(x)\,,

(1)

where crucially

The polynomials

The fact that the form in

As an example, if

\displaystyle f(x)=x^{3}+ax^{2}-(4+2a)x+1\>,\quad p(x)=x^{2}-2x-1\>,

(2)

The second equation can be rearranged as
x^{2}=p(x)+2x+1

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=x\,(p(x)+2x+1)+a\,(p(x)+2x+1)-(4+2a)x+1\,,

(3)

\displaystyle=p(x)(x+a)+2x^{2}+a-3x+1\,,

\displaystyle=p(x)(x+a)+2(p(x)+2x+1)+a-3x+1\,,

\displaystyle=p(x)(x+a+2)+x+a+3\,.

Thus, for this specific example

It is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=\{m_{1},\>m_{2}\}=\{x,\>1\}\,,\qquad r(x)=m_{1}+(a+3)\,m_{2}\,.

(4)

Indeed for a generic univariate polynomial

In many applications, only the remainder of the polynomial division is of interest.

Univariate polynomial division as linear system solving

It is also possible to recast the problem of finding polynomial remainders as solving a linear system of equations.

2.1.5

2.1.6
Gröbner Bases

The uniqueness of polynomial divisions in the multivariate setting can be restored by introducing Gröbner bases

2.5.1
Review of Finite Field Sampling and Reconstruction

One of the major challenges in computer algebra is
expression swell
: as symbolic manipulations proceed, intermediate expressions often become dramatically larger than the final simplified result.

A particularly effective way to circumvent this problem is through
black-box rational reconstruction
combined with
finite-field sampling

Kauers:2008zz
;
Kant:2013vta
;
vonManteuffel:2014ixa
;
Peraro:2016wsq
;
Peraro:2019svx
;
Smirnov:2019qkx
;
Klappert:2019emp
.

First introduced to high energy physics via integration-by-parts reductions, this method has since become a central component of many modern computational pipelines in perturbative Quantum Field Theory.
The strategy is to reformulate the problem so that the desired quantities are represented as multivariate rational functions.
One then sets up an Ansatz with unknown coefficients and fixes the them by probing the system numerically as a black box at multiple sampling points. The exact rational result is subsequently reconstructed from several modular evaluations using the Chinese remainder theorem in combination with the Wang algorithm
Wang:1981
;
Wang:1982
.

This approach presents three important advantages. Firstly, instead of working with exact rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
during sampling, one evaluates the problem in the more efficient arithmetic setting of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, with
p
a large prime. Such finite-field arithmetic naturally caps the maximum expression size, avoiding expression swell even on numerical slices. Secondly, any complicated cancellations in the algorithm’s output happen numerically, and before any interpolation/reconstruction. This effectively avoids the need for heavy intermediate manipulations required in a fully algebraic approach. Finally this strategy lends well to paralellisation, as numerical evaluations of sample points are fully independent calculations.

2.5.4
Advantages and Disadvantages of SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s Approach

By design SP
\mathbb{Q}
R performs polynomial divisions in a different manner to most computer algebra systems. Whilst in many cases this can provide large computational benefit, there are also many examples where a more traditional computer algebra approach is more advantageous. Which algorithm performs best in each situation depends almost entirely by the structure of the ideal being considered.

Crudely speaking, the complexity of a polynomial ideal can be divided into two categories: “variable complexity” and “parameter complexity”. Whilst seemingly similar, this distinction can enormously impact the efficiency of the chosen algorithm.

If a given ideal has many variables raised to high powers, then this will result in a difficult Gröbner basis computation with many intermediate steps. In SP
\mathbb{Q}
R, this translates to having to generate a large Macaulay system to high degree. By its nature, the Gaußian elimination performed by SP
\mathbb{Q}
R is a cruder operation when compared to fine tuned Gröbner basis algorithms
FAUGERE199961
. Thus, for ideals with complicated variable dependence (and no or few parameters to reconstruct) it is more likely that traditional polynomial division strategies will outperform SP
\mathbb{Q}
R.

Conversely, there exist
many ideals where the variable complexity is contained, but there are many additional parameters in the polynomial system. In these cases, even if the Gröbner basis requires relatively few operations to calculate, symbolic computer algebra approaches may suffer greatly due to intermediate expression swell. Due to its finite field back end, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R does not suffer from this problem and thus can efficiently handle ideals with very complicated parameter dependence.

In summary, the worst case scenario for SP
\mathbb{Q}
R is an ideal in many variables with high powers and no parameters. The best case scenario instead is an ideal that is relatively simple on a numerical slice, but has many parameters creating intermediate expression swell, hindering the efficiency of algebraic algorithms when trying to work in the full parameter space.

Fortunately, many problems in high energy physics and beyond fall into this latter category, where (physical) parameters are almost universal. For such systems, we find SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s approach to enormously beneficial compared to algebraic approaches. A more quantitative analysis of specific relevant examples is provided in
section

4
.

In practice, for problems where SP
\mathbb{Q}
R is useful, the computation of a numerical Gröbner basis is not a bottleneck, even with

’s built in tools. Indeed as outlined in

, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R is most effective when the variable complexity of the ideal is contained. This is equivalent to Gröbner bases on numerical slices being accessible.
. From this information, the set of irreducible monomials can be extracted and compared against those found by the Gaußian elimination strategy. If a match is found, then the seeding weight was sufficient. If not, larger systems can be iteratively generated until a sufficient weight is reached.

Finally, in most cases, the seeding strategy described above produces an overdetermined linear system, containing many more equations than are strictly necessary to determine all unknowns.
Consequently, it is highly desirable to find equivalent but smaller reformulations that preserve the algebraic content of the problem while substantially reducing computational cost.
Within SP
\mathbb{Q}
R, this simplification is achieved through
FiniteFlow
’s Mark-and-Sweep algorithm, which automatically discards the redundant relations after solving the system numerically once.
This strategy is conceptually equivalent to the
tracing
algorithms used by modern Gröbner basis implementations

.

Expression parsing

As discussed in
section

2.3
, the reduction of complicated rational functions
f(\mathbf{x})
relies on the usage of companion matrices.
In SP
\mathbb{Q}
R, this process is handled by a recursive parser that automatically converts any rational function of arbitrary nested depth into its corresponding companion matrix representation within
FiniteFlow
.

Each function
f(\mathbf{x})
is represented as an
expression tree
, whose leaves correspond to variables
\mathbf{x}
and constants, while its internal nodes represent basic algebraic operations of addition, multiplication, and exponentiation
\{\text{{+}},\text{{*}},\text{{\textasciicircum}}\}
.
The parser traverses this tree recursively, replacing each algebraic operation with its matrix analogue and each leaf with the companion matrix representation of the corresponding variable or constant.

Whilst some of these required operations to parse rational functions are already present in
FiniteFlow
, others are instead built from more basic operations. Two such cases are the recursive implementation of matrix powers, as well as the computation of matrix inverses, as illustrated in
fig.

5
.

An example of the automatically generated internal SP
\mathbb{Q}
R representation of the rational function from
eq.

68
is shown in
fig.

Characteristic polynomial algorithm

To build the characteristic polynomials of companion matrices as discussed in
section

2.4
, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R implements the Faddeev–LeVerrier algorithm. For a given
|\mathbf{m}|\times|\mathbf{m}|
matrix this approach computes each coefficient of the characteristic polynomial

\det(M_{f}-\lambda\mathbb{1})=c_{0}+c_{1}\lambda+\cdots+c_{|\mathbf{m}|-1}\lambda^{|\mathbf{m}|-1}+\lambda^{|\mathbf{m}|}\,,

(64)

without ever needing to explicitly introduce the eigenvalue parameter
\lambda
. The coefficients
c_{|\mathbf{m}|-i}
are computed recursively by introducing an auxiliary set of matrices
B_{i}
. The induction begins as

B_{0}=0\,,\qquad\qquad c_{|\mathbf{m}|}=1\,,

(65)

and all following coefficients are computed with

B_{k}=M_{f}\,B_{k-1}+c_{|\mathbf{m}|-k+1}\mathbbm{1}\,,\qquad\qquad c_{|\mathbf{m}|-k}=-\frac{1}{k}\mathrm{tr}(M_{f}\,B_{k})\,.

(66)

The operations required to run this recursion are matrix traces and multiplications, which are both supported inside
FiniteFlow
. In practice, we find this this algorithm to be very efficient, adding negligible computation time when compared to the (very often) more expensive previous row reduction steps. A schematic overview of the SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s implementation showing the relevant
FiniteFlow
functions is given in
fig.

4.1
Macaulay Resultants

4.1.1
Background

Consider a polynomial ideal
\mathcal{I}
comprised of
v
variables (unknowns),
n=v+1
equations, as well as various parameters. In general, such a system is overdetermined and thus has no solutions.

For special values of the parameters of the system however, the equations may no longer become overdetermined and a solution can exist. Such information is encoded in the
Macaulay resultant

\mathcal{R}

Sturmfels:2002
;
CoxLittleOshea:2005
;
Cox:2015ode
. This object is a new polynomial which depends
only
on the coefficients of
\mathcal{I}
, which is defined to vanish precisely on the parameter configurations which allow for
\mathcal{I}
to have roots. For example, suppose

\mathcal{I}=\langle x-a,a\,x-1\rangle\,,

(117)

where
x
is the single variable and
a
is a parameter.
V(\mathcal{I})\neq\emptyset
only when
a=\pm 1
. Thus, we have

\mathcal{R}(a)=(a-1)(a+1)\,.

(118)

4.1.2
Implementation in SP
\mathbb{Q}
R

By their nature, the computation of Macaulay resultants inevitably involves dealing with ideals with multiple variables and parameters. Thus, as discussed in
section

2.5.4
, such computations often lend themselves well to SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s finite fields approach.

Suppose
\mathcal{I}
is comprised of
n=v+1
equations,
v

\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\}

4.2
Landau Analysis

The specific example considered in
sections

4.1.2
and
4.1.3
was purely illustrative and of limited practical interest. In this section we instead focus on how SP
\mathbb{Q}
R can be used to tackle a class of polynomial ideals motivated by high energy physics.

4.2.1
Background

Feynman integrals are ubiquitous in modern high energy physics calculations. Through the use of a parametric representation (see
Weinzierl:2022eaz
for a review), mathematically they can be interpreted as
twisted period
or
Euler
integrals, which can be chosen to take the form
Lee:2013hzt
,

I(\mathbf{s})\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})\right)^{-d/2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}}{x_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{n}}{x_{n}}\,,

(126)

where an irrelevant prefactor for this discussion has been omitted.
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{F}
are polynomials depending both on a set of integration (Schwinger) variables
\mathbf{x}
and a set of (Mandelstam/kinematic) parameters
\mathbf{s}
, on which
I
ultimately depends. The parameter
d
is taken to be generic, and thus
\mathcal{G}^{-d/2}
is a multivalued function (twist) with branch points at the roots of
\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{F}
.

In general
I(\mathbf{s})
is a complicated expression with an involved branch cut structure. When attempting to evaluate Feynman integrals, knowledge of the branch points can be of great help, in particular for the construction of the differential equation systems they obey
Gehrmann:1999as
;
Remiddi:1997ny
. The methods and algorithms to obtain this information are collectively known as
Landau Analysis

Bjorken:1959fd
;
Landau:1959fi
;
Nakanishi:1959jzx
;
Cutkosky:1960sp
.

Specifically, given a specific
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{x})\,,\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})
the goal of most Landau algorithms is to compute the
Landau variety

l(\mathbf{s})
. Similar to a resultant, this is a polynomial in the kinematics
\mathbf{s}
, such that for
l(\mathbf{s}^{*})=0
,
\mathbf{s}^{*}
is a branch point of
I
. In practice
l(\mathbf{s})
often factors into smaller polynomials
l(\mathbf{s})=l_{1}(\mathbf{s})\cdots l_{k}(\mathbf{s})
. Each irreducible polynomial
l_{i}(\mathbf{s})
is known as a
Landau singularity
17
17

17

For reviews of Landau analysis we refer the reader to
Weinzierl:2022eaz
;
Badger:2023eqz
.
.

In recent years there has been an enormous growth of interest and progress in computing Landau singularities, spurred by both theoretical and technological breakthroughs
Dlapa:2023cvx
;
Brown:2009ta
;
Panzer:2014caa
;
Klausen:2021yrt
;
Hannesdottir:2022xki
;
Correia:2021etg
;
Mizera:2021icv
;
Berghoff:2022mqu
;
Fevola:2023kaw
;
Fevola:2023fzn
;
Helmer:2024wax
;
Correia:2025yao
;
Caron-Huot:2024brh
.

The traditional and most used approach to computing Landau singularities is via the
Landau equations

Landau:1959fi
: one formulation seeks values of
\mathbf{s}
such that the equation system

\mathcal{F}=0\,,\quad x_{i}\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial x_{i}}=0\quad\forall\,i\,.

(127)

has solutions. In practice to find
all
the Landau singularities for a Feynman diagram, different equation systems along with blow ups may need to be considered
Fevola:2023fzn
. Nevertheless even after such manipulations the end result is always a set of polynomial equations which need to be solved for.

4.2.2
Implementation in SP
\mathbb{Q}
R

Solving
eq.

127
amounts to eliminating variables from polynomial systems with many parameters, and thus lends itself well to SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s approach. One approach to finding Landau singularities with SP
\mathbb{Q}
R is as follows: we consider the ideal

\mathcal{I}=\left\langle\mathcal{F},\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial x_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial x_{v}},1-x_{0}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})\right\rangle\,,

(128)

where compared to
eq.

127
the simpler solutions involving
x_{i}=0
are excluded, as they result in simpler (subsector) subsystems which can be solved separately.

As in
section

4.1.2
,
\mathcal{I}
is overdetermined, as there are
v+2
equations and
v+1
unknowns. Thus finding a set of Landau singularities can be translated to finding the multivariate resultant of
\mathcal{I}
. In SP
\mathbb{Q}
R one thus proceeds by again “promoting” one parameter in
\mathbf{s}
, say
s_{1}
to a variable, and eliminating the
v+1
variables
\{x_{0},\cdots x_{v}\}
from the resulting
v+2
variable system.

As before this approach will miss singularities that depend only on
\{s_{2},\cdots\}
. Exactly as in
section

4.1.3
, this can once again be checked for by comparing against standard computer algebra procedures on a numerical slice. If any factors are missing these can in turn be reconstructed by promoting the relevant parameter to become a variable instead, before a simpler reconstruction on a partial numerical slice.

5
Conclusions and Outlook

The study of polynomial systems is ubiquitous in mathematics, physics and beyond. In this work we presented SP
\mathbb{Q}
R, a new
Mathematica
package for tackling division and elimination problems in systems of polynomial equations.

SP
\mathbb{Q}
R processes polynomial systems differently to many other programs: all algorithms are systematically recast as solving linear systems of equations and matrix algebra. The key innovation is that these operations are then in turn implemented in terms of finite field sampling and black box reconstruction pipelines.

Essentially SP
\mathbb{Q}
R thus borrows many of the techniques that have pushed the state of the art in scattering amplitude computations, and repackages them to problems in polynomial algebra. This allows SP
\mathbb{Q}
R to effectively avoid large intermediate expressions, which can significantly impact analytic computer algebra approaches.

Indeed we argue that expression swell can often be the bottleneck in processing polynomial systems, in many cases more so than the efficiency of Gröbner basis algorithm itself. SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s approach is thus best suited to ideals with complicated parameter (coefficient domain) dependency, but moderate variable complexity. For systems of this kind we find SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s approach to be extremely effective, significantly pushing the state of the art when compared to other publicly available implementations.

To this end we benchmarked the package’s elimination tools on state of the art Macaulay resultant computations. Across these tests, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R delivered marked improvements over other computer algebra systems, reducing both runtime and memory by at least 5-6 and 3-4 orders of magnitude respectively, when compared to
Singular
,
Macaulay2
and
msolve
. We also tested SP
\mathbb{Q}
R on ideals motivated by theoretical physics: when applied to previously studied state of the art Feynman integrals, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R was able to find new previously missed Landau singularities without requiring a specialised solver specific to this task.

Despite its extensive usage of finite field sampling and reconstruction, the package has been designed to require no knowledge from the end user of its inner workings. At the same time however it remains flexible enough to fit into a wide range of algorithmic pipelines.

The ideas behind SP
\mathbb{Q}
R open several directions for future exploration. SP
\mathbb{Q}
R as of current builds (numerical) Gröbner bases via solving Macaulay systems of equations. Whilst this is similar to modern dedicated algorithms such as
F4
, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s approach is not as fine tuned. Integrating a fast numerical Gröbner basis implementation such as
msolve
into SP
\mathbb{Q}
R would help push the package’s scope even further.

Another potential improvement could be made in the reconstruction phase of SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s pipeline. In particular, SP
\mathbb{Q}
R’s back end
FiniteFlow
reconstructs all expressions fully expanded. An algorithm that attempts to understand the factorisation structure of the output could in many cases save several orders of magnitude in sample points. We expect such a procedure to be particularly beneficial for the reconstruction of Landau singularities, as we have often observed strong factorisation of the output in such cases.

Finally, we hope that the ideas behind SP
\mathbb{Q}
R will enjoy broad application in other scientific fields, beyond just the physics and mathematics motivated examples primarily presented in this work.
