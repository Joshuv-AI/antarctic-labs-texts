Manual and automated capture of analgesic-responsive facial pain behaviors of unrestrained mice in a trigeminal neuralgia model
Authors: Ramiro Colon-Vilella, Darian Mohsenin, Isabella M.G. Triana, Biafra Ahanonu, Allan I. Basbaum, Xiaobing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

1. Introduction

A quarter of the adult population worldwide suffers from orofacial pain.31 Among orofacial pain, trigeminal neuralgia (TN), which is characterized by paroxysmal lancinating or electric shock pain in the trigeminal nerve distribution, is considered the most devastating neuropathic pain condition that profoundly affects human physical and psychological well-being.2,12 The incidence of TN is estimated to be 12.6 to 28.9 per 100,000 person-years46 and rapidly increases with age.55 Despite an initial response to carbamazepine (CBZ), many patients become refractory and require invasive procedures,54 such as microvascular decompression (MVD), stereotactic radiosurgery, and trigeminal ganglion ablation. Unfortunately, pain can still return.40

To better elucidate the underlying mechanisms of TN and enhance pain therapies, several preclinical trigeminal neuropathic pain models have been developed. Although extracranial chronic constriction injury (CCI) of the trigeminal nerve reliably induces mechanical hypersensitivity of the face, unfortunately the response to CBZ treatment has not been convincingly demonstrated. However, recent reports suggest that CBZ-responsive evoked facial pain behavior can be established by intracranial CCI of the trigeminal nerve, through the foramen rotundum (FR)34,50 or the foramen lacerum.13 These mimic the intracranial compression injury of the trigeminal nerve observed in classical TN, which MVD attempts to address.12 Long-term assessment of pain, nevertheless, has been difficult.39,51

Innocuous stimuli-evoked pain is a mandatory criterion for the clinical diagnosis of TN.6,12 In rodents, withdrawal threshold in response to von Frey (vF) stimulation applied is commonly used to represent orofacial pain. However, reliably recapitulating the facial pain phenotype characteristic of patients in an alert rodent is extremely challenging. Typically head fixation,7,29 hand holding13,30,34 or small cage confinement,26 are required. However, significant stress could be triggered by restraint and lead to unpredictable and inconsistent behavioral outcomes that are not necessarily pain associated.39 A significant concern is that acute stress can activate disinhibitory circuits22,48 and trigger robust endogenous analgesia,10 thereby undermining the clinical translatability of behavioral outcomes. A recent introduction of a sheltering tube method for orofacial sensory testing21 avoids restraint, but identifying analgesic responsive, long-term pain-relevant behaviors is still challenging.39

Video recording allows in-depth, frame-by-frame analysis of injury-induced spontaneous behaviors and evoked responses,27,47 and an important advance was the development of a quantitative facial coding of pain method using the grimace scale (GS).27 Furthermore, recent advances in machine learning have accelerated automated monitoring of body responses in preclinical models.19,23,32 Here, to assess facial pain in a preclinical model of TN, namely FRCIT-ION model,34 we used manual and automated analyses of high-frame rate videography to recapitulate spontaneous and evoked pain-triggered behaviors in unrestrained mice after intracranial FRICT-ION injury. The relevance of these behavioral measures to TN was validated with a cohort of nonopioid analgesics, including CBZ, L-Baclofen, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) and a Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) receptor antagonist, that either succeeded or failed in managing TN clinically.5,18,41 With improved accuracy and increased reliability, we have demonstrated new methodology to assess analgesic-responsive facial pain behaviors of unrestrained mice in a preclinical TN model.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice of both sexes (6-8 weeks old; The Jackson Laboratory, Sacramento, CA) were used in all experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at UCSF and were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory animals.

2.2. Surgery

After an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (9 mg/mL) and xylazine (1 mg/mL) (200 μL/20-30 g), anesthetized mice were placed in a supine position with limbs extended and secured using adhesive tape. To expose the intraoral buccal space, the superior and inferior incisors were retracted with the surgical silk sutures (Ethicon, Raritan, NJ 6.0). A small incision was made along either the left or right palatal-buccal mucosa to expose the foramen rotundum where the infraorbital nerve (ION), a trigeminal V2 branch, exits. We modified the original FRICT-ION model,34 by inserting 4 rather than 1, chrome-gut sutures (Ethicon, 4.0) of 3 to 4 mm in length and a piece of poorly absorbable surgifoam along the nerve through the foramen rotundum with forceps with 45°C angled tips (ie, Dumont 5/45 forceps [FST, #11251-35], or BRI #10-1725 forceps). This modification significantly enhanced intracranial compression of the trigeminal V2 nerve. We found that the pain phenotype, when assessed by a new DeepLabCut (DLC)-based method in an unrestrained mouse, was significantly prolonged, for at least 5 months and was CBZ responsive. Finally, the incision was closed with 3M Vetbond (#1469SB). As expected, the procedure negatively affected postsurgical chewing: We recorded a significant postoperative weight loss (Fig. S1, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). Switching from hard to soft food did prevent the weight loss (Fig. S1, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553).

2.3. Unrestrained behavioral setup

Using the sheltering tube method,21 we placed mice individually in a custom-made elevated acrylic clear tube (TAP Plastic, SKU#23353-CTS: 1-3/4″OD/1-1/2″ID × 2.25″ L) with a laser-cut bottom that allowed for free movement. A monochrome camera (The Imagingsource, DMK 37BUX252) with a mounted infrared C-Mount lens (Tamron 4-12 mm F/1.2; BH #TA41212ML; MFR #12VM412ASIR) was used for recording. Camera was synchronized with a frame counter, and the size of von Frey filament and number of stimulations were registered at real time. We tested recordings at various frame rates ranging from 30 to 120 fps and selected 60 fps for the final study. We also created a python script which allows the users to choose the pre- and post-stimulation time frame to extract videos of each evoked trial for analysis. For example, a 10-second video includes 5 seconds before stimulation and 5 seconds after stimulation.

2.4. Behavioral testing of unrestrained mice

For all behavioral tests, the experimenter was always blind to treatment. If the experimenter was no longer blind during DLC analysis, the identical code was used for all assessments.

2.4.1. Spontaneous and evoked facial behavioral testing

Training session took place over 2 consecutive days, with each daily habituation lasting 30 minutes. During experimental session, after 10 minutes of habituation in the elevated tube, the mouse was recorded for 20 minutes to assess spontaneous behaviors. Then, the maxillary facial region (V2) was stimulated with vF filaments of various forces in an ascending order. In brief, the mouse was mechanically stimulated 5 times on each side of the face with a single strength vF filament, 30 to 60 seconds apart. Both evoked and spontaneous facial behaviors were recorded and analyzed by 2 independent observers. Grooming42 is defined as bilateral sweeping of both front paws across the face; this movement may be symmetrical or asymmetrical. Wiping42 is defined as a single motion of one front paw making contact and moving rostrally in a sweeping motion across the ipsilateral side of the face. Rubbing is defined as the combined total of grooming and wiping behaviors.

2.4.2. Grimace score

Following the published protocol,27 the habituated mouse in the PainFace chamber (Hypothesis to Hardware, LLC) was recorded for 20 minutes. The videos were then scored manually. In brief, 10 frames for each side of the face were extracted by a blinded experimenter and then scored manually by 2 experimenters blind to the treatment. As we trimmed whiskers, we only analyzed 4 facial action units: orbital tightening, nose bulge, cheek bulge, and ear position. These action units were scored on a 0 to 2 scale, with “0” as not present and “2” as clearly present. The mean grimace score was calculated as an average of the grimace features for each side of face. To generate automated grimace scores,33 we also analyzed the videos with a cloud-based PainFace platform (http://painface.net).

2.4.3. Orofacial pain assessment device assay

Overnight food-deprived mice were placed in orofacial pain assessment device (OPAD) cage (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL) and tested at neutral temperature every other day for 2 to 3 weeks. Only mice that consistently achieved with at least 800 licks during a 12-minute session were recruited for further study. Ensure Original nutrition shake (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL) was used as reward. The change of ratio of licks over contacts during 12-minute test was used to reflect the severity of orofacial pain.37

2.4.4. Rotarod test

As previously reported,49 we tested motor coordination using an accelerating rotarod (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Varese, Italy Model #7650) at a maximum speed of 33 rpm. The duration that the mouse spent on the rotarod before fall was recorded, with a cutoff at 300 seconds. Mice were first trained on an accelerating rotarod for three 5-minute trials before testing 2 hours later.

2.4.5. Elevated Plus-Maze for anxiety

As we previously described,49 the mouse was placed at the junction of the open and closed arms of the Plus-Maze (Kinder Scientific, Chula Vista, CA) and allowed to explore for 5 minutes. The time spent on the closed and open arms was monitored with tracking software. Longer time spent in the closed arms was considered to represent a higher anxiety state.

2.4.6. DeepLabCut

Seventy-five mp4 video files captured at 60-fps showing unrestrained mice performing a range of behaviors were loaded into DeepLabCut (DLC, v3.0). To train DLC's machine learning process, we manually labeled 20 frames from each video with key points for the nose, paws, whisker pads, and superior/inferior eyelids. Within DLC, we used a convolutional neural network architecture, namely RestNet-50, that is known for its balance between learning speed and memory efficiency in the laboratory setting. To subset the labeled frames for model training, we used k-means clustering to ensure the model is exposed to diverse animal poses. The model underwent 20 rounds of training with 1000 epochs each and a batch size of 16. The model's labeling accuracy was further validated using previously unseen behavioral videos. A python script was developed to filter the tracking data by removing low-confidence values. To further reduce tracking inaccuracy, a rolling median filter was also applied to unfiltered DLC tracking outputs. Kinematic information including total distance, acceleration, and velocity across each frame was retrieved for analysis. Python and R scripts for behavior analysis are available on Github (ucsf-yu-lab/EVOKE: Automated kinematic analysis of evoked orofacial behavior in rodents).

2.5. Pharmacological characterization of chronic facial pain of unrestrained mice

Mice received a nonsedative dose of one of the following compounds intraperitoneally: 60 mg/kg of CBZ, 2 mg/kg of L-Baclofen, 5 mg/kg of PS75, 5 mg/kg of meloxicam, and 5 mg/kg of BIBN 4096. Each drug was paired with its corresponding vehicle control for comparison: CBZ, BIBN 4096 or PS75 was resuspended in vehicle comprising 20% of 2-hydroxypropyl β-cyclodextrin in pH 4.5 citrate buffer; L-Baclofen and meloxicam were formulated in NaCl 0.9%. Behavioral testing was conducted after 30 minutes following administration.

2.6. Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with 2.5% Avertin and perfused transcardially with 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 4% formaldehyde. Dissected tissues were first postfixed in the same fixative for 3 hours, then preserved overnight in 30% sucrose in PBS before cryostat sectioning. The following antibodies were used to immunostain TG, caudalis, and peripheral nerve sections: rabbit anti-PU1 (1:500, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA #2266), rabbit anti-IBA1 (1:2000; Wako, Santa Ana, CA #019-19741), mouse anti-NF200 (1:1000, Sigma, St. Louis, MO #N5389), rabbit anti-ATF3 (1:500, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO #HPA001562), and fluorophore-coupled secondary antibodies (1:1000, Alexa Fluor 488, 555, 594, 647, ThermoFisher Scientific, South San Francisco, CA). Images were captured with a Carl Zeiss LSM 700 microscope and processed with Fiji/ImageJ (NIH).

2.7. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 11 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Behavioral data are expressed as mean ± SEM, where n represents the number of mice. Student t test, 1-way, or 2-way repeated-measures ANOVA were used to analyze the behavioral results; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. The translational challenge to assess facial pain long term in unrestrained mice

A PubMed search between January 1, 2020, and November 12, 2025, with the key words “(behavior) AND (orofacial pain) AND (mouse OR rat),” resulted in 135 publications in which orofacial behavioral testing was conducted (Fig. S2, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). To define facial mechanical allodynia in rodents, Von Frey (vF) withdrawal threshold47 was monitored in 82.3% (111/135) of the publications (Fig. 1A). By contrast, nonwithdrawal behavioral measures, such as facial rubbing (wiping or grooming)47 or use of the OPAD37 only appeared in 13.3% (18/135) and 4.4% (6/135) of the studies, respectively. Which of these approaches can produce results that best translate to clinical orofacial neuropathic pain assessment, including TN, continues to be debated.39

Westlund et al. originally developed the CBZ-sensitive FRICT-ION model and reported that vF-measured mechanical allodynia in hand-held restrained mice lasted for at least 10 weeks.34,50 The model also reproduced sustained anxiety-like behaviors after surgery,34 which were evident for at least 8 weeks (Fig. S3, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). To minimize the additional stress inevitably induced by restraining, we focused on unrestrained mice using the FRICT-ION model (Fig. 1B) to better capture spontaneous and evoked pain behaviors. By adopting the sheltering tube method,21 we established a facial pain behavioral assessment system that allows high-frame rate videography analysis (Fig. 1C) that captured responses of an unrestrained mouse experiencing ongoing, spontaneous pain and mechanical allodynia evoked by innocuous facial (V2) stimulation. Unexpectedly, in an unrestrained setting, facial stimulation of uninjured, naive mice with a filament force as low as 0.02 g already triggered a withdrawal response rate of 60 to 80% (Fig. 1D), making it difficult to assess the changes induced by injury. Therefore, we concluded that withdrawal threshold is not a sufficient pain measure in an unrestrained setting.

We next tested the human-annotated GS27 in unrestrained mice. As noted above, because the mouse whiskers were trimmed, we only analyzed 4 grimace features, orbital tightening, nose bulge, cheek bulge, and ear position (Fig. S4A, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). As expected, compared with the contralateral, uninjured side (Fig. 1E), we recorded a significant increase of mean GS of the ipsilateral face within the first week after FRICT-ION surgery. Unfortunately, this increase was short-lived. At 2 weeks after surgery, the mean GS of the ipsilateral, injured and contralateral, uninjured side no longer differed (Fig. 1E). Moreover, the GS did not differ between the vehicle (VEH) and CBZ treated mice, when examined 2 weeks after surgery (Fig. S4B, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). To mitigate potential experimenter bias, we also evaluated a cloud-based automated GS system, namely the PainFace neural network.33 However, despite improvements to the hardware (Fig. S4C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553), the algorithm was unable to detect the injury-induced changes in facial expression annotated by human observers, even at postoperative day 1 (Fig. S4D, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). Based on these limitations, we conclude that GS is not sufficiently sensitive to assess chronic facial pain provoked in our TN model.

We also evaluated the OPAD, another unrestrained approach to measure how much stimulus-evoked pain a freely moving mouse is willing to bear to receive a reward.37 To gain access to a liquid award, the mouse is trained to press its face against 2 metal bars (Fig. 1F). In our setup, to increase the noxious stimulus during face contact, we replaced the smooth metal bars with serrated bars (Fig. 1G). Facial mechanical hyperalgesia is calculated by dividing the total number of licks by the total number of facial contacts with the bars, before and after surgery.37 Within 1 week of the surgery, the mice did exhibit a significantly reduced ratio of licks/contacts compared with uninjured mice (Fig. 1H), indicating that facial mechanical allodynia was acutely induced. However, within 10 days after surgery, this drop in licks/contacts ratio disappeared (Fig. 1H), even when surgery was performed bilaterally. We believe that the mice modified their head position to minimize the contact of the sensitized V2 region to the serrated bars, which made it possible to avoid triggering pain while accessing the reward. Together, we concluded that these commonly used facial pain measures, when used in unrestrained settings, are limited in their ability to capture trigeminal neuropathic pain long term.

3.2. Capturing long-term persistent facial pain in unrestrained mice after FRICT-ION injury

To capture persistent facial pain in the unrestrained mice reliably, we reviewed the video recording, frame-by-frame, for pain phenotypes that could be missed by live monitoring of the mice.1,8 In fact, compared with naïve, uninjured mice, we recorded a significant increase of injury-induced asymmetric grooming, favoring the injured side of the face (Fig. 2A, Supplemental Video 1, Video 2, Video 3) that persisted for at least 4 weeks postinjury (Fig. 2B). As the presence of innocuous stimulus-evoked pain is mandatory for a diagnosis of TN, we also applied an innocuous vF filament (0.02 or 0.04 g) to the affected ipsilateral V2 region. We recorded that this stimulus triggered a significant increase in facial rubbing, including both grooming and ipsilateral wiping, which also lasted for at least 4 weeks (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Video 4, Video 5). However, compared with the preinjury values, the detected injury-induced changes became less significant when reexamined 6 to 7 weeks after FRICT-ION injury (Figs. 2B and C).

To reduce the limitations of human observations and to increase throughput, next, we introduced DLC tracking across each video frame, which generated kinematic data sets of both head and paw movement after facial stimulation (Supplementary Video 6). Figure 2D shows, not surprisingly, that head movement is followed by paw movement. Notably, the speed of head movement (mm/s), calculated from the distance that the head travelled (mm) within 15 seconds after the vF stimulus, significantly increased after surgery, compared with the preinjury baseline values (Fig. 2E, Fig. S5, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). Most notably, the injury-induced increase in head movement speed persisted through a 5-month study (Fig. 2F), even after the injury-induced increase in asymmetric spontaneous grooming or evoked facial rubbing was no longer detectable by manually scoring measures (Figs. 2B and C). Furthermore, this sustained increase in head movement speed was also observed in female mice and remained evident chronically for at least 10 weeks after FRICT-ION injury (Fig. 2G). Interestingly, female mice exhibited lower speed than their age-matched male counterparts. Taken together, we suggest that this new, computer-assisted measure provides a reliable correlate of the persistent facial pain triggered by the FRICT-ION injury in the unrestrained setting.

3.3. Pharmacological characterization of facial pain behaviors in unrestrained mice after FRICT-ION injury

Do these injury-induced behavioral measures truly model the pain experienced in patients with TN? To address this question, we tested whether these behavioral measures could be regulated by CBZ, the first-line pharmacotherapy for TN. The mice were treated either with a nonsedating dose of CBZ (60 mg/kg) or VEH (Fig. 3A) and then we recorded spontaneous behavioral changes. As expected, CBZ significantly suppressed both spontaneous asymmetric grooming (Fig. 3B) and evoked rubbing (Fig. 3C), spontaneous behavioral phenotypes indicative of ongoing trigeminal neuropathic pain in this TN model. Notably, CBZ had no effect on the mouse evoked rubbing behavior when the contralateral, uninjured face was stimulated (Fig, S6A, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). The efficacy of CBZ in attenuating injury-induced spontaneous asymmetric grooming was further validated in female mice after FRICT-ION injury (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, in naïve, uninjured mice, CBZ did not alter stimulus-induced speed of head movement (Fig. 3E). Post–FRICT-ION injury, however, the increased speed of head movement was readily reversed by CBZ in both male (Fig. 3F) and female mice (Fig. 3G).

Racemic baclofen is often recommended for the patients with TN who fail to respond to CBZ.5 Interestingly, a small cohort double-blinded crossover trial revealed that L-Baclofen was at least 5 times more effective than racemic baclofen.18 We chose to validate the efficacy of L-Baclofen at a nonsedating dosing (Fig. S6B, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553) in the FRICT-ION model. Notably, L-Baclofen did significantly reduce both spontaneous asymmetric grooming (Fig. 3H) and evoked ipsilateral rubbing (Fig. 3I). Moreover, it also markedly reduced the injury-induced increase in head-movement speed (Fig. 3J). Our results support a future large trial to reassess the efficacy of L-Baclofen in the TN treatment.

We also tested the analgesic efficacy of a novel nonopioid analgesic, namely an α2A adrenergic receptor agonist PS75, that we recently demonstrated has a nonsedating analgesic action in the spared nerve injury mouse model of neuropathic pain.15 Figure 3K shows that the nonsedating dose of PS7515 significantly inhibited injury-induced spontaneous asymmetric grooming as well as stimulus-evoked rubbing of the face (Fig. 3L, Supplementary Video 7, Video 8). Moreover, PS75 further reversed the increase in speed of stimulus-induced head movement captured after FRICT-ION injury (Fig. 3M). It will clearly be of interest to assess the action of P75 and related compounds in patients with TN.

Importantly, we also tested with an NSAID that is rarely effective in managing neuropathic pain conditions,16 including TN.54 Figure 3N shows that meloxicam, which is effective in managing inflammatory pain,17 had no impact on the speed of head movement in the FRICT-ION model. Speed of head movement was also unresponsive to BIBN 4096, a selective CGRP receptor antagonist (Fig. 3O) that is effective in acutely managing migraine.38 As expected, neither meloxicam nor BIBN 4096 had any significant impact on evoked rubbing in those mice 5 months post–FRICT-ION surgery (Fig. S6C, http://links.lww.com/PAIN/C553). This result is consistent with a recent randomized placebo-controlled trial that failed to demonstrate efficacy of blocking CGRP receptor in patients with TN.41

In summary, we have identified long-term analgesic-responsive, trigeminal neuropathic pain behaviors, including spontaneous grooming, evoked facial rubbing and speed of head movement measured with DLC in an unrestrained setting. Importantly, the speed of head movement in the unrestrained mouse can capture facial pain behavior 5 months after FRICT-ION injury. Moreover, the speed of head movement also accurately recapitulated the success and failure of specific analgesics in the clinical management of TN.

3.4. Chronic neuroimmune activation parallels the chronic facial pain produced in the FRICT-ION model

The prolonged behavioral phenotype observed after peripheral nerve injury is paralleled by persistent neuroimmune changes, including peripheral macrophage expansion and dorsal horn microglia activation.20,24,25,53 We next examined whether comparable neuroimmune changes occur in the FRICT-ION model and the extent to which they persist. Figure 4A illustrates a significant infiltration of PU.1+ (a myeloid-specific transcription factor) macrophages in the ipsilateral infraorbital nerve, a V2 branch, that persisted for at least 5 months postinjury. There was no significant monocytic infiltration in the contralateral uninjured nerve (Fig. 4B). In the ipsilateral trigeminal ganglion, 5 months postsurgery, we also recorded significant ATF3 expression (Figs. 4C–E), a stress marker readily observed in injured sensory neurons.9 And in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC), which receives nociceptive trigeminal input, we recorded a significant increase of IBA1-immunoreactive microglia that coexpressed PU.1 in the ipsilateral, injured side 1 month postinjury (Fig. 4F), with no change in the contralateral TNC. These results demonstrate that a long-lasting, injury-activated neuroimmune signature indeed parallels the chronic facial pain after FRICT-ION injury.

4. Discussion

Here, we used high-frame rate videography analysis to capture long-term chronic facial pain behaviors in unrestrained mice exhibiting persistent neuroinflammation after intracranial FRICT-ION injury. Most importantly, these behavioral measures were readily regulated by CBZ, the first-line pharmacotherapy for TN. We also identified a novel analgesic that significantly reduced pain behavior and confirmed that specific drugs that fail to treat TN clinically are without effect in this model.

Facial withdrawal threshold to mechanical stimulation remains the most common behavioral assessment in orofacial pain research. However, this approach is particularly challenging in vigilant rodents. The readout during live animal monitoring is often heavily dependent on the experimenter's real-time judgement, which can be easily influenced by random factors that are not pain- or injury-related. With video assistance, we observed that even a very low-force vF filament often induces facial withdrawal in naïve, unrestrained mice, making the measure of facial mechanical withdrawal threshold unreliable. We also tested several well-established spontaneous or evoked orofacial pain measures, including the grimace scale and OPAD assessment, both of which can be introduced in the unrestrained setting. Unfortunately, none of these measures detected behavioral changes for long term, suggesting that more sensitive measures are needed for subtle behavioral indices of chronic facial pain.

For this reason, we introduced high-frame rate video recording.28,44 Among the injury-induced facial pain behaviors, we captured asymmetric spontaneous grooming and increased evoked rubbing. Comparable behaviors were previously observed in rats after acute extracranial trigeminal CCI47 and in mice carrying a CACNA1A R192Q mutation that is associated with human familial hemiplegic migraine type 1.11 Here, we determined the extent to which these behaviors persist and respond to specific nonopioid analgesics in the FRICT-ION model in an unrestrained setting. We not only observed a prolonged pain phenotype, but using CBZ, the first-line treatment for TN, we now demonstrate that these 2 injury-induced behaviors are indeed relevant to the chronic trigeminal neuropathic pain generated in our TN model.

To capture the persistent, unbiased behavioral signatures induced by FRICT-ION injury in an unrestrained setting, we used a DLC algorithm that monitors labeled body parts to track mouse movement longitudinally. We conclude that the vF filament-evoked, head-movement speed is a reliable and sensitive measure of injury-induced facial mechanical allodynia throughout the 5-month postsurgical study period. By contrast, the sensitivity of spontaneous pain measures, including grimacing and asymmetric grooming, declined markedly by 1 month post-injury. This temporal divergence is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that mechanical allodynia persists long after spontaneous pain behaviors have resolved in multiple nerve injury-induced neuropathic pain models.3,35,36 Importantly, we validated the specificity of this machine-learning based measure with a cohort of nonopioid analgesics, including CBZ, L-Baclofen, an NSAID, and a CGRP receptor antagonist, whose efficacy in treating TN has been studied clinically. As expected, this speed measurement accurately detected the therapeutic benefit of CBZ and L-Baclofen. Consistent with clinical evidence,2,41 we found no therapeutic effect of an NSAID or a selective CGRP receptor antagonist in our TN model. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that a DLC-based orofacial signature can reliably phenotype a treatment-responsive persistent TN-associated pain. We also suggest that this methodology can be adopted in other preclinical models of trigeminal neuropathic pain, including that characteristic of patients with painful temporomandibular disorders (TMD),4,14,43,52 another common orofacial pain condition, with limited therapy options. Interestingly, as mechanical hypersensitivity of the V1 trigeminal region is commonly used to assess pain in preclinical models of migraine,45 this novel method should have a broad impact by strengthening the translational relevance of orofacial pain research.

Taken together, through manual and automated analysis in a CBZ-responsive TN model, we can capture reliable, and long-term, chronic trigeminal neuropathic pain behaviors in unrestrained mice. Importantly, these behavioral measures accurately recapitulated the observed clinical outcomes—both success and failure—of specific nonopioid analgesics that have previously been tested in the clinical management of TN. Our findings will enable more effective preclinical screening of novel analgesics for TN and lay the groundwork for a deeper mechanistic investigation linking trigeminal neuronal activity to both ongoing and evoked pain behaviors in mice.
