CFD-based investigation of the aerodynamic characteristics of an aircraft emergency vertical tail
Authors: Zeyou Zhou, Zehang Zhao, Dongfeng Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents an exploratory two-dimensional CFD investigation of a backup vertical-tail concept for rudder-jam scenarios. The proposed configuration introduces retractable auxiliary vertical tails to the primary vertical stabilizer, enabling the generation of additional aerodynamic moment after deployment. Both the primary and backup vertical tails employ the NACA0012 airfoil, and the flow field is simulated using the Spalart–Allmaras turbulence model with a dynamic-mesh approach under a freestream condition of Mach 0.6. The analysis focuses on the evolution of the flow field and the resulting aerodynamic-moment behavior of the tail system under jammed-rudder conditions within an idealized two-dimensional framework. The results indicate that deployment and deflection of the backup vertical tail alter the pressure distribution and aerodynamic interactions between the tail components, resulting in significant variations in the computed aerodynamic moment. Under certain deflection ranges, the total aerodynamic moment of the modeled tail system exhibits zero crossings, indicating a compensatory aerodynamic-moment trend opposing the jam-induced moment. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how a backup vertical-tail configuration modifies the aerodynamic-moment characteristics of the vertical-tail system under rudder-jam conditions. Because the present study is conducted within an idealized two-dimensional framework, the results reflect qualitative aerodynamic trends and mechanisms and may serve as a basis for future three-dimensional investigations.

Introduction

In recent years, the continuous advancement of aeronautical technology has led to increasingly complex aircraft designs, which impose more stringent demands on flight control systems. The rudder, which is one of the primary control surfaces of an aircraft, is crucial to the governance of the yaw motion of the aircraft because it ensures directional stability under the action of lateral forces. Specifically, the aerodynamic characteristics of the vertical-tail rudder directly determine the capacity of an aircraft to counteract disturbances, such as crosswinds and asymmetric thrust. When airflow passes over the vertical-tail rudder, it generates a lateral force and a yawing moment, which are fundamental aerodynamic properties that are essential to the maintenance of directional equilibrium. Moreover, changes in the aircraft heading are achieved by deflecting the vertical-tail rudder; such a deflection alters the effective camber of the vertical stabilizer, which subsequently modifies the pressure distribution and produces the lateral force and yawing moment required for turning maneuvers1. However, rudder jam-induced abrupt changes in heading pose significant threats to flight safety. From an aerodynamic perspective, such a failure condition imposes a sustained yawing moment on the aircraft, which could potentially cause the aircraft to enter a hazardous sideslip–yaw flight state. The resulting aerodynamic coupling can generate substantial yawing moments, thereby compromising the controllability of the aircraft. USAir Flight 4272 crashed into a forest due to a failure in the rudder system. Tragically, this crash resulted in the loss of all 127 passengers aboard. Dongfeng Airlines Flight 5173 encountered a similar incident. Fortunately, the aircraft was able to land safely, and there were no casualties. Subsequent investigations revealed that this incident was also caused by a failure in the rudder system. The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB)4 of the United States reported that several airlines that operate Boeing aircraft may be utilizing faulty rudder actuators, which pose significant safety hazards. This information emphasizes that the obstruction of the rudder of an aircraft can cause a potential safety hazard; thus, it is necessary to address this issue.

In response to this flight-safety challenge, scholars worldwide have undertaken numerous studies and explorations. Syed et al.5 designed a reference controller for a rudder-jam scenario. They utilized the Lyapunov principle to address the reduced maneuverability caused by the failure of the aircraft control surface. Ismail et al.6 improved the fault tolerance of the traditional neural-assisted sliding-mode controller for automatic landing in scenarios that included actuator failures and strong winds. They achieved this improvement by extending the scheme used for proportional–derivative–integral controllers to a cascaded trajectory method in their designed controller. This approach can be used for a variety of actuator blockage failures, including issues with the ailerons and rudder of an aircraft. He Deyu et al.7 proposed a hybrid fault-diagnosis method based on virtual prototype simulation knowledge. They used this method to study the types of failures related to blocked rudders and steady-state errors on ships. Their results provide valuable references for other researchers that subsequently address the rudder-blockage issue in aircraft. Cui S M et al.8 proposed a hypothesis in which the rudder actuator of an aircraft experienced a failure, and they conducted a simulation analysis of the control performance for this scenario. The results provide an important theoretical basis for fault-tolerant control in aircraft during failure situations and offer theoretical support for improvements to flight safety and reliability. Li A et al.9 proposed a rapid orientation fault-tolerant control scheme for over-driven hypersonic vehicles, specifically addressing controller failures (such as blockages and failure faults). This approach provides an effective solution for control faults in hypersonic vehicles. It is evident that researchers have extensively studied the potential risks of aircraft rudder blockages and have designed various solutions to address this issue.

Scholars have used numerical simulation techniques to conduct in-depth analyses of academic research10, and these analyses have led to significant achievements in the field of aviation. Khan F et al.11 employed the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) technique to investigate the lift and drag coefficients of the NACA2415 airfoil. Their goal was to optimize the wing design and thereby enhance the aircraft performance. Their results demonstrated that, from the perspective of wing design, a planar wing generates the greatest drag and a hybrid wingtip is the most advantageous design. These findings emphasize the crucial role of the CFD technique to advancements in aerospace design. Ejeh C et al.12 utilized the CFD technique to conduct numerical simulations and computationally model aircraft wings while focusing on the unsteady aerodynamic characteristics of the aircraft. Their results indicated that variations in the air density can induce detrimental aerodynamic behaviors that can potentially lead to aircraft stalls. This finding further validates the applicability of CFD techniques in the aviation sector and emphasizes its research potential. Nazri A et al.13 conducted a comprehensive CFD analysis of the aerodynamic performance of wings with high aspect ratios using the ANSYS software. This study not only provided a valuable case for researchers that utilize CFD techniques to investigate wing aerodynamics but also serves as a detailed guide for new researchers who wish to learn the precise steps that are involved in CFD investigations. Thus, the successful application of CFD techniques in various fields fully validates its value for aerospace engineering operations and demonstrates the high level of credibility it has shown in academic research.

It is evident from the discussion above that various methods of addressing rudder failures are already in place in current technological fields. However, the research regarding the optimization of vertical-tail configurations remains insufficient. Therefore, this paper proposes an improvement to the vertical-tail structure that can address the rudder-jam issue. The improvement scheme involved the design of a retractable backup vertical-tail device within the traditional vertical-tail structure. Given that this study conducts a two-dimensional analysis of the vertical tail, all improved vertical tail configurations are presented in the form of planar line contours to prevent readers from misunderstanding the research dimension due to three-dimensional rendering details, as depicted in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2. These figures include the normal vertical-tail orientation mode (Fig. 1) and the emergency vertical-tail orientation mode (Fig. 2). When a sudden rudder jam occurs, the retractable backup vertical tail extends to generate additional compensatory moment by means of its deflection. This moment serves a dual purpose. First, it counteracts the abnormal yawing moment caused by the rudder failure. Second, after compensating for the abnormal yawing moment, it utilizes the residual moment to establish the necessary control moment thereby modifying the aerodynamic-moment trend of the vertical-tail system.

During this study, numerical CFD simulation was used to conduct a multi-condition simulation analysis of the aerodynamic characteristics of the improved vertical-tail system. This analysis focused on the evolution of the flow-field structure in the tail region for different modes. Key parameters, such as flow-field pressure contours, vortex changes, and separation-zone variations, were compared for the normal and emergency operation modes. Additionally, the aerodynamic response of the vertical-tail system and the associated moment trends were systematically evaluated from both aerodynamic and dynamic perspectives. Therefore, the flow-field characteristics and moment variation dimensions were both used to provide preliminary aerodynamic evidence for the proposed concept.

Model and methods

Geometric models

In this study, the CFD technique was used to create a two-dimensional model of the vertical-tail wing of an aircraft. The model represents the vertical-tail wing from a top-down view. The NACA0012 airfoil was chosen for the vertical-tail wing. This airfoil choice was based on the extensive experimental validation of the airfoil by researchers from various countries. Its numerical simulations and simulation data have been thoroughly refined; thus, they provide a solid foundation, as well as reliable and accurate support, for subsequent research. Ahmed T et al.14 investigated the aerodynamic characteristics of the conventional NACA0012 airfoil for a variety of flap angles and Mach numbers. Their results indicated that, within certain Mach number constraints, the lift coefficient increased as the Mach number rose, while the drag coefficient remained relatively constant. These findings provide a valuable reference for subsequent research. Shabur A et al.15 used the CFD technique to compare the lift and drag coefficients of the NACA0012 and NACA0018 airfoils at low Reynolds numbers. It was found that the NACA0012 airfoil generated greater lift than the NACA0018 airfoil. Kazim G et al.16 studied the characteristics of the flows over the NACA0012, NACA4412, and NACA2412 airfoil profiles. Their research emphasized the importance of maintaining the optimal angle of attack if an aircraft is to avoid turbulence and optimize its performance. Molaa A and Abdulwahid M17 used computational models and low-speed wind-tunnel experiments to study the aerodynamic characteristics of the NACA0012 airfoil for an angle-of-attack range of 0–18°. The findings of the abovementioned study were supported by validation with wind-tunnel experiments. Given the abundance of research data regarding the NACA0012 airfoil and its excellent performance, this airfoil was selected as the model for this study.

The discrete-point coordinate dataset of the airfoil was exported using the Profili2 software. Then, a geometric model of the NACA0012 reference airfoil was constructed in the CATIA 3D modeling platform. For the conventional vertical-tail system, the primary geometric parameters were determined using a scaling approach based on the design data of the Cessna 172 aircraft: the chord length of the vertical stabilizer and rudder combination, c1, was 25 cm, and the rudder segment occupied 33% of the main vertical-tail chord length. This geometry was then imported into the ANSYS ICEM software to create a 2D top-down view of the system.

The dual-side backup vertical-tail system, which also utilized the NACA0012 airfoil, was constructed as a fully movable tail structure. Its geometric parameters were set to include a chord length, c2, of 20 cm. It actuated around the center of the airfoil, and its rotation center was located at 50% of the airfoil chord length. The distances between the main vertical tail and each of the two backup vertical tails were both equal to c1, and three airfoil leading edges were arranged according to the principles of a collinear layout. Turbulence model

In this study, the Spalart–Allmaras (SA) turbulence model was utilized. This model, introduced by Spalart and Allmaras in 1992, was specifically designed for the analysis of subsonic flows over airfoils18. In addition to its computational efficiency and reasonable cost, the SA model also incorporates all the sources of turbulence generation and dissipation19. The SA model is a robust turbulence model that provides highly accurate initial estimates for aerodynamic flows with low Reynolds numbers. It only needs to solve one equation; thus, it converges extremely rapidly. Moreover, it demonstrates a remarkable grid-quality tolerance, particularly in the wall region20. The SA model is extensively employed in aerospace-engineering and rotating-machinery operations, and it has demonstrated superior performance in reverse pressure gradient problems. It has also exhibited excellent applicability to boundary-layer issues with certain degrees of complexity21.

By accounting for both the rotation and strain tensors, a more accurate depiction of the complex mechanisms by which the rotation influences the turbulence can be achieved.

Mesh and boundary conditions of the computational domain

Structured quadrilateral meshes have been proven to be effective in CFD studies, in which coarse mesh distributions are utilized to solve complex flow fields and solution domains. Structured quadrilateral meshes have demonstrated outstanding accuracy when used to solve such problems23. Unstructured meshes are also widely recognized to be effective tools for academic research; they are capable of achieving very precise solutions for complex flow fields24. Due to the high accuracy of both approaches, a hybrid mesh model that combines structured and unstructured meshes was used for the analysis of this study. Park S et al.25 employed a hybrid mesh-generation method for various tests with complex geometries, and the results showed that the simulation accuracy was satisfactory.

Based on the aforementioned statement, the modeling process for the NACA0012 airfoil was conducted using a hybrid mesh. To mitigate the occurrence of reflection phenomena at the far-field pressure boundary, an unstructured mesh with a diameter of 9c2 was used within the computational domain, and triangular mesh elements were utilized for the discretization. Simultaneously, to reduce the computational load and alleviate potential disturbances caused by dynamic meshes on large-scale grids, a structured mesh with an external diameter of 30c2 was applied in the external domain. Details of the grid layout are depicted in Fig. 5 and Fig. 6. The boundary conditions were set such that the atmospheric pressure was 101,325 Pa, the turbulent viscosity ratio was 10, the temperature was 300 K, and the air density was calculated using the ideal gas law. The inlet was given a far-field pressure boundary condition. The velocity was equal to Mach 0.6; such a value is typically observed during the subsonic flight phases of various aircraft, particularly during cruising or climbing operations.Fig. 5Grid division around the vertical tail.Fig. 6Local meshes around the vertical tails in various modes.

Because this study investigated the effects of a backup vertical-tail system on the empennage system for various deflection angles, it was essential to precisely replicate the rotational motion of the auxiliary tail structure. To achieve this replication, a dynamic mesh technique was used to conduct the necessary simulations. The theoretical foundation of dynamic mesh techniques has been well established, and their core principles, algorithmic formulations, and validation processes have been thoroughly discussed in the literature30. Therefore, detailed derivations are not provided in this paper.

Dynamic meshing is a fundamental CFD technique that is used to address problems that involve boundary motion or structural deformation. Allowing the computational mesh to be adjusted in real time along with the moving components ensures that the flow simulations remain accurately aligned with the actual geometry. Thus, precise capture of the unsteady flow phenomena is enabled.

Accordingly, mesh-smoothing and mesh-reconstruction methods were utilized within the dynamic mesh framework during this study to conduct a systematic analysis of the flow characteristics near the vertical tail. In the mesh-smoothing method31–33, diffusion equations are solved to determine the displacements of mesh nodes. By the simulation of a diffusion process, the node positions are adjusted such that local distortions are smoothed out. The original topological structure is simultaneously maintained, however, to accommodate mesh deformation induced by boundary motion.

When smoothing alone could not prevent significant mesh distortion, local mesh regeneration was performed in regions near the airfoil with large deformations. This regeneration ensured that the mesh quality in these critical regions met the accuracy requirements of the simulation, while the mesh in the undeformed regions remained unchanged. Thus, computational precision was balanced with efficiency. This approach is referred to as mesh reconstruction.

In addition, the aerodynamic coupling effects of the triple-vertical-tail configuration were considered in order to further thoroughly investigate the fluid dynamics evolution mechanisms.

In this study, the two backup vertical tails rotate uniformly around their respective central points with an angular velocity of 1.39626 rad/s, a total deflection time of 0.5 s and a time step size of 1 × 10–5 s. For the dynamic mesh technique, the mesh smoothing and reconstruction methods are adopted, with the minimum mesh length set to 0.00001 m, the maximum mesh length to 0.05 m, the maximum cell skewness to 0.7, and the size reconstruction interval set to 5.

In this study, the finite volume method was used to discretize the two-dimensional Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations. The solver was a double-precision solver that was based on pressure in ANSYS FLUENT. The turbulent flow was modeled using the Spalart–Allmaras model. Pressure–velocity coupling was achieved by means of the Coupled algorithm.

The transient simulations were performed with a physical time-step size of 1 × 10⁻5 s, and a maximum of 20 inner iterations was applied within each time step. Convergence at each physical time step was assessed primarily based on the stabilization of the monitored aerodynamic quantities, including the aerodynamic moment of the vertical-tail system. A time step was considered converged when these quantities no longer exhibited noticeable changes with further inner iterations. The residuals of the governing equations were monitored throughout the calculations. The residuals of the momentum and turbulence-model equations were reduced below 1 × 10⁻5 at each time step, whereas the continuity residual typically remained on the order of 1 × 10⁻3 due to the unsteady dynamic-mesh nature of the simulation. Therefore, the final convergence assessment was based mainly on the stabilization of the integral aerodynamic quantities rather than on a single residual threshold.

Verification of the effectiveness of the simulation method with wind-tunnel experiments

The numerical framework employed in this study has been assessed for accuracy and general applicability using the benchmark case of the NACA0012 airfoil. The computed results show good agreement with experimental data available in the open literature, supporting the reliability of the present numerical approach.

It should be noted that no dedicated wind-tunnel experiments have been conducted for the specific backup vertical-tail configuration or the exact flow conditions considered in this study. The present work is primarily intended as an exploratory analysis under a simplified two-dimensional framework, focusing on the underlying flow mechanisms associated with aerodynamic-moment compensation in rudder-jam conditions, rather than on high-fidelity quantitative prediction.

Furthermore, the numerical analysis is limited to subsonic flow conditions (Ma = 0.6), and does not account for compressibility effects associated with shock–boundary-layer interactions that may arise in transonic or supersonic regimes. Therefore, the conclusions drawn are applicable only within the subsonic flow regime considered.

Rudder jammed with a right deflection of 20°

To address the issue of continuous right yawing that is caused by a rudder jam with a 20° rightward deflection, the flow-field characteristics around the vertical tail were compared and analyzed for the normal and emergency modes. Figure 11 shows that, in the normal mode, significant flow stagnation zones were formed at the leading edges of the vertical tail and the rudder, and this flow stagnation resulted in high-pressure regions at both locations. The airflow velocity on the right side was relatively lower, however, which resulted in a higher pressure. The two-dimensional simulation clearly captured the formation of high-speed jets in this gap; thus, the pressure reduction in this region was significant and a deep low-pressure groove was formed, which significantly enhanced the right suction peak of the left backup vertical tail itself. The passage between the right backup vertical tail and the leading edge of the primary vertical tail widened. The streamlines in this region became less dense and the airflow velocity decreased. Therefore, the pressure increase in this region was large.

A comparison of Fig. 12(b) and Fig. 12(c) revealed that, as the deflection angle of the backup vertical tail continued to increase, the sensitivity of the left backup vertical tail to the deflection angle was much greater than that of the right backup vertical tail. The pressure difference between the leading edge of the left backup vertical tail and the primary vertical tail increased relative to previous conditions. For both backup vertical-tail wings, the pressure at the leading edge of the right side of the wing increased, while the pressure at the trailing edge decreased. The pressure on the left side continued to rise, however. This phenomenon extended toward the trailing edge, at which point the suction peak disappeared and the pressure on the right side became smaller than that on the left side. For the primary vertical-tail wing, the pressure at the leading edge of the left side increased, while the pressure at the trailing edge decreased. This behavior, which is shown inFig. 13(b) and Fig. 13(c), was caused by the effects of the coupling between the vertical-tail wings. As the backup vertical tail continued to deflect to the right, the physical occlusion effect of the left backup vertical tail on the main incoming flow dominated. A large-scale airflow-stagnation region was formed upstream of it, and this stagnation resulted in a decrease in the airflow velocity. Therefore, the overall pressure on the left side and the front end of the right side of the left backup vertical tail increased. Meanwhile, according to the continuity theorem and the law of conservation of mass, it was concluded that the airflow velocity between the middle portion of the left backup vertical tail and the primary vertical tail increased and formed a high-speed and low-pressure shear layer that extended toward the rear edge. This result is consistent with the phenomenon that can be observed in Fig. 13(c), where, according to Bernoulli’s principle, the pressure decreased. During this process, the two-dimensional simulation clearly captured the formation of a large-scale and stable reflux vortex on the right side of the left backup vertical tail. As the deflection angle increased, this reflux vortex induced a new reflux vortex, which is referred to as the induced vortex. This vortex system not only directly led to the region of extremely low pressure at the rear right side of the left backup vertical tail, but more importantly, the intense vortex–wall interaction promoted the premature separation of the boundary layer, which resulted in a further reduction of the pressure on the right side. Meanwhile, the passage between the leading edge of the right backup vertical tail and the primary vertical tail further increased. According to the continuity theorem and Bernoulli’s law, the velocity in this region decreased and the pressure increased. However, the narrow channel between the trailing edge of the right backup vertical tail and the main vertical tail generated a “Venturi effect,” which resulted in a local high-speed jet and caused a low-pressure region to form at the trailing edge. Outside the right backup vertical tail, the two-dimensional flow field exhibited a large region of unstable flow separation, which was accompanied by the initial signs of vortex shedding. These phenomena caused a wide low-pressure separation region to form, which resulted in a further decrease in the pressure on the right side of the right backup vertical tail.

Preliminary analysis of limitations associated with three-dimensional effects

This study investigates the aerodynamic characteristics of the backup vertical tail using two-dimensional (2D) computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. The use of a 2D framework reduces grid size and computational cost by neglecting spanwise effects, enabling rapid evaluation of aerodynamic-moment characteristics and providing a basis for conceptual design. However, due to the geometric simplification, 2D simulations cannot capture spanwise flow features present in real three-dimensional (3D) configurations, which may lead to discrepancies between the computed results and actual flow behavior. Accordingly, the conclusions of this study are limited to qualitative and mechanistic insights under simplified 2D conditions and should not be interpreted as quantitative aerodynamic performance for a full-scale 3D configuration. The main limitations are summarized as follows.

In a three-dimensional flow, the finite span of the vertical tail induces a pressure-driven spanwise flow from the high-pressure side to the low-pressure side around the tip, forming a spanwise velocity component. The interaction between this flow and the freestream results in the formation of tip vortices, which are not captured in two-dimensional simulations.

Despite these limitations, 2D simulations remain valuable for engineering analysis. They enable efficient estimation of aerodynamic characteristics and support the preliminary assessment of the backup vertical-tail concept, providing a practical reference for subsequent three-dimensional studies and engineering development.

Based on the airfoil geometry parameters that were analyzed using CFD techniques in this study, physical models that conform to wind-tunnel testing standards will be fabricated. Using the same computational conditions, key scenarios that involve rudder jams will be replicated. Aquantitative comparison between wind-tunnel experimental data and CFD results will be conducted, and particular focus will be placed on verifications of the deviations in the total moment generated by the vertical tail in the emergency mode.

Regarding future challenges, the introduction of the backup vertical tail inevitably increases structural weight, which may affect the overall mass distribution of the configuration and slightly influence the center-of-gravity location, thereby introducing a trade-off with the aerodynamic-moment response. For subsequent engineering development, the results of this study, including characteristic dimensions and aerodynamic-moment magnitudes, can serve as a reference for the design of lightweight structures and actuation systems, with the aim of minimizing the adverse effects associated with additional weight while maintaining the required aerodynamic performance.

To ensure reliable stowage and deployment of the backup vertical tail, an actuation mechanism compatible with flight conditions is required, which places specific demands on response stability and structural reliability.
