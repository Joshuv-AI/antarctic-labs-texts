Registered and Specialist Nurses' Perspectives on Organisational Changes in Swedish Municipal Home Care: A Qualitative Study Using Shadowing
Authors: Elzana Odzakovic, Agneta Kullberg, Therése Bielsten, Jan Marcusson, Ingrid Hellström
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore and characterise organisational changes related to clinical competencies, responsibilities, and roles from the perspectives of registered and specialist nurses in municipal home care settings.DesignA qualitative exploratory study was conducted.MethodsInductive thematic analysis was used to analyse field notes from shadowing 18 participants (12 registered nurses and six specialist nurses) from the beginning of their working day until the end of their shift.ResultsThe analysis revealed four main themes: the black box in the health care system; workarounds in the workplace; a profession on the brink of change; and the transfer of tasks towards the neighbourhood with linked subthemes.ConclusionThis study indicates that registered and specialist nurses in municipal home care need to be more visible in the health care system, collaborating with others and as part of broader society, to meet the needs of the increasing number of older people living at home.Patient or Public ContributionThe results can be used when developing home care in communities by acknowledging the accountability that registered and specialist nurses exercise, despite limited resources and support, as well as recognising the importance of advanced practice nurses in coordinating home care. This study also highlights potential suggestions for developing municipal home care by acknowledging the accountability of registered and specialist nurses.

Introduction

In recent decades, health and social care policies have shifted from institutional care to home care, making people's homes and communities the primary workplaces for registered and specialist nurses (World Health Organization 2022). This shift is driven by an aging population (≥ 65 years), which is expected to grow in the coming years due to increased life expectancy and healthier lifestyles (World Health Organization 2025). These structural and policy changes have led to organisational transformations in municipal home care, affecting nurses' roles and responsibilities and the use of their clinical competencies. Nurses continue to perform the same core tasks, but in new contexts—primarily in patients' homes rather than in hospitals or institutional settings. In addition, specific tasks are increasingly undertaken by less‐qualified healthcare staff, creating new dynamics and responsibilities for registered and specialist nurses. This has resulted in evolving professional roles, mismatched skill utilisation, and expanded responsibilities, which nurses perceive as challenging and, at times, beyond their formal qualifications. Understanding these changes is crucial for identifying how nurses navigate complex care environments and adapt to shifting organisational expectations. Research in Europe shows that a significant proportion of municipal home care staff have limited formal healthcare education, highlighting the growing challenges associated with these organisational changes (Lillsjö et al. 2023; Rosengren and Friberg 2024; Anita et al. 2025). Despite these developments, limited awareness and sparse research remain on how organisational changes in municipal home care affect registered and specialist nurses' clinical competencies, responsibilities, and roles (Ethier et al. 2024; Ranta and Kaunonen 2024; Vaismoradi et al. 2024).

Background

Ensuring high‐quality, evidence‐based healthcare outside hospital settings has become an increasingly central challenge for healthcare systems. Across Europe, the organisation of home care for older people varies widely, with differences in policies, regulations, and funding structures (Mobasseri et al. 2023). Home care is often described as a complex organisational setting because of the diversity of welfare models and governance structures across countries; however, at its core, it is professional care delivered to individuals living in their own homes (Peterson and Brodin 2022). Older people constitute a significant group in home care services due to age‐related health needs and frequent multimorbidity, including conditions such as cardiovascular disease and dementia (Pilotto et al. 2020). Demographic projections indicate that by 2050, one in five individuals in Iceland and one in three individuals in Germany and Sweden will be aged 65 years or older (United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division 2020). These demographic changes have contributed to political agendas across Europe that emphasise care delivery outside hospitals, increased reliance on informal care, and greater involvement of private service providers alongside public provision (Börsch‐Supan 2019). In Sweden, these developments have led to significant organisational changes in the provision of care for older people. Responsibility for healthcare and social services is divided among national, regional, and local levels (Hälso‐och sjukvårdslag [HSL] 2017). While the national government oversees legislation and policy, regional authorities manage hospitals and primary healthcare, and municipalities are legally responsible for providing home care and social services to older people living in ordinary housing, that is, their own homes rather than nursing homes. Municipalities are also responsible for healthcare services within the registered nurse's scope of practice, including medication administration and medical procedures (Hälso‐och sjukvårdslag [HSL] 2017).

Major organisational restructuring occurred in Sweden in 1992 through the Ädel Reform (Ädelreformen), regulated by the Social Services Act (Socialtjänstlagen 2001), which transferred responsibility for home care for older people from county councils to municipalities. This reform significantly altered the professional roles and responsibilities of registered nurses (National Board of Health and Welfare 1996). A subsequent reform in 2014, the Hemsjukvårdsreformen, further consolidated municipal responsibility for home healthcare and home visits, bringing additional patient groups and care activities under municipal governance. These reforms led to substantial changes in nurses' working conditions. Nurses transitioned from employment within county councils to municipal home care, requiring municipalities to develop new leadership structures and care management systems for medically trained nursing staff (National Board of Health and Welfare 1996). Registered and specialist nurses in Swedish municipal home care increasingly work independently, coordinating care across multiple providers and services. The nursing workforce includes several roles that differ in education, responsibilities, and scope of practice. An overview of these roles is provided in Table 1.

Registered nurses (RNs) hold a bachelor's degree (180 ECTS) and are licensed to independently assess patients, plan and deliver care, administer medications, and supervise assistant staff. Specialist nurses have advanced education, typically a 1‐year master's degree (60–75 ECTS), providing enhanced competence to manage complex care situations, including preventive care, patient education, and care coordination (Taneva et al. 2023). Assistant nurses have vocational training and provide basic nursing and personal care under supervision, performing routine tasks delegated by licensed nurses. Organisational changes in municipal home care have expanded nurses' responsibilities beyond direct patient care to include supervision, coordination, and accountability within increasingly complex care arrangements (Oldland et al. 2020; World Health Organization 2020). Despite these regulatory frameworks, previous research indicates that organisational changes in municipal home care have led to unclear role boundaries, increased time pressure, and challenges in effectively utilising nurses' clinical competencies. Studies have shown that registered nurses in municipal home care experience organisational ambiguity and high workloads (Lillsjö et al. 2023), ethical challenges in home‐based end‐of‐life care (Heggestad et al. 2021), and uncertainty regarding professional responsibilities (Munkeby et al. 2023). Nurses have also described situations in which they feel overqualified for specific routine tasks while simultaneously being responsible for complex clinical decision‐making without adequate organisational support. Clinical competence—defined as a combination of skills, knowledge, attitudes, and abilities required to deliver appropriate patient care in specific contexts (Fjørtoft et al. 2021)—is essential for maintaining healthcare quality. However, organisational constraints in municipal home care may limit nurses' opportunities to fully apply or further develop these competencies, contributing to more task‐oriented approaches to care (Rasmussen et al. 2021). This is concerning given evidence that advanced nursing competence is critical for safe, effective, and person‐centred care in community settings (Cao et al. 2023). Despite the central role of municipal home care in contemporary healthcare systems, there remains limited research exploring how organisational changes influence registered and specialist nurses' clinical competencies, responsibilities, and professional roles. Existing research has focused mainly on hospitals or primary care, leaving municipal home care underexamined. Greater insight into nurses' experiences is therefore needed to understand how system‐level transformations shape professional practice and to inform organisational strategies that support nurses working in increasingly complex home care environments. Therefore, this study aims to explore and characterise organisational changes related to clinical competencies, responsibilities, and roles from the perspectives of registered and specialist nurses in municipal home care settings.

Methods

Study Design

A qualitative, exploratory design (Braun and Clarke 2006) was chosen, with shadowing as the data collection method, in the often‐unnoticed context of municipal home care. Shadowing is a research method developed by organisation and management scholars from social science literature, in which everyday events or the dynamics of practice are explored as they unfold over time and across the spaces of organising (McDonald 2005). Earlier research has demonstrated the value of shadowing in health care settings, although it has often been used in hospital settings (Husebø and Olsen 2019; Monahan et al. 2018). There is little reference to shadowing as a data collection method in the nursing field, reflecting the ongoing organisational changes involving nurses in municipal home care after the 1992 reform. Thus, shadowing was chosen to capture the unexpected and episodic nature of municipal home care, during which organisational changes occurred as registered and specialist nurses practiced.

Setting

This study is part of a larger project, entitled ‘Proactive health care for older people’, a 3‐year initiative involving a proactive primary‐care intervention aiming to predict risks for the population of older people in the County of Östergötland (see Marcusson et al. 2019 for further details). In this study, we report on one of the sub‐projects, which explores the organisational changes from the perspectives of registered and specialist nurses who care for older people living at home. The municipal home care under study is located in southeast Sweden, in the County of Östergötland, with a population of 461,583. Municipal home care in Östergötland is provided for 2294 people; registered nurses and assistant nurses made 17,137 home care visits during 2016 (Davidsson and Wiman 2017). Municipal home care accounts for 25% of all health care provided in Sweden in 2018 (National Board of Health and Welfare 2019). Shadowing took place in the two largest municipalities in Sweden to observe the broader organisational context and how the living conditions of inhabitants affect the distribution of municipal home care.

These two municipalities differ in socio‐economic status within their respective county councils. The rate of cardiovascular disease is significantly higher in the municipality of the eastern part of Sweden (Wennerholm et al. 2011). Thus, the two municipalities were selected to capture the versatility of daily work situations, conditions, and the organisation of registered and specialist nurses.

Shadowing

In recent years, shadowing has been widely used in professional education among health care professionals to gain an introduction into the role of a nurse (Monahan et al. 2018) or to explore the organisation of emergency care (Husebø and Olsen 2019). However, so far, few studies have used data collection methods such as shadowing nurses in municipal home care. The shadowing data collection method involves a researcher closely following a member of the organisation over time (McDonald 2005). Some commentators have argued that the benefit of shadowing as a method lies in its mobility, which allows the researcher to follow participants and dynamics as they unfold (Czarniawska 2014; McDonald 2005). In this study, the actions and surroundings of municipal home care were shadowed from the participants' perspective (Gill et al. 2014), not from the researchers' or shadow's perspective. According to McDonald (2005), the aspects of organisational life are the hardest to research but can make a significant contribution. In line with this, to capture the unexpected and interrupted nature of organisational changes in municipal home care, the activities and actions of the shadowed nurses were central to understanding the organisational process within an invisible health care organisation.

Recruitment of Participants

The care managers of the two municipalities were contacted between November 2017 and October 2018. They were asked to help select registered and specialist nurses who met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were that the nurse must be employed in one of the geographic areas of the main municipal home care project (Marcusson et al. 2019), have at least 1 year of work experience as a registered nurse, and be employed at least half‐time. All the care managers agreed to send information about the study to their registered and specialist nurses by e‐mail. A purposive sampling method was used to contact the nurses. All 12 registered nurses and six specialist nurses selected by the care managers agreed to participate, and none withdrew their consent during the study. In this study, district nurses are defined under an overall definition of specialist nurses. The relevant details of the nurses are presented in Table 2.

Data Collection

All shadowing was conducted by the first author (E.O.), a district nurse with prior experience in ethnographic fieldwork and qualitative research methods. It was beneficial to have experience in organisational work to generate rich data while shadowing (McDonald 2005). The care managers of one of the municipal home care organisations met with the first author (E.O.) before emailing dates for shadowing and the names of the nurses who wanted to participate in the study. In another municipality in southeastern Sweden, the care manager sent out contact information, and appointments were made personally with the nurses via email. The 23 instances (spanning a considerable period from November 2017 to October 2018) of shadowing the 18 participants occurred during the daytime, from the beginning of the working day until the end of their shift. Some participants were shadowed multiple times across 23 instances to capture various situations and touchpoints during a nurse's workday, as well as the organisational process on a typical day in municipal home care (Gallan et al. 2021). The day began with brief information about the study, and participants were informed they could withdraw at any time without explanation. A written consent form was provided and was signed by all participants. All patients who received municipal home care visits during shadowing gave verbal consent before the researcher entered their home. Patients were informed about the study in both verbal and written form, and a presentation (by E.O.) was given on the role of the first author as a researcher and observer, not as a district nurse in direct communication with patients or participants. An information letter about the study, with the names of the first (E.O.) and last author (I.H., project leader) and a picture of the first author (E.O.), was given to all patients. During shadowing, no personal information about the patients was collected. On 21 shadowing occasions, the first author wore municipal home care working clothes without a nameplate. On the other two occasions, participants who requested no nameplate wore personal clothing.

The participants were informed that the first author would serve as their shadow and follow their work in the field from their perspective, rather than that of the researchers. All 23 shadowing occasions (155 h; median, 5 h) were documented using handwritten field notes, photographs (only of materials and spaces, not of any person), and other documentation (e.g., medication regulations). The photographs were used as triggers for dialogue with participants and for data analysis. Field notes were recorded in a notebook during and after shadowing sessions. Rather than following a detailed interview guide, questions were asked spontaneously as relevant topics emerged. The notes were structured around three main components: (1) Descriptive observations—Chronological accounts of events, interactions, and routines observed during the day. (2) Analytical memos—Initial interpretations and emerging themes noted alongside or shortly after observations. (3) Reflexive commentary—Reflections on the researcher's positionality, assumptions, and potential influence, documented at the end of each session. To deepen understanding of organisational changes, questions were posed during informal moments—such as in the car or at the office—but never inside patients' homes. These conversations and insights were incorporated into the field notes. To encourage participants to share more information and experiences, follow‐up questions were asked, such as: ‘How did you feel in that situation?’ and ‘How did you experience that moment in the room?’ or ‘What are your suggestions for the future of home care?’ All handwritten field notes were transcribed into 274 pages of Word documents (by E.O.) in detail.

Data Analysis

During data collection and analysis, the research team met several times to discuss and use member‐checking processes to verify data accuracy (Braun and Clarke 2006). Thematic analysis using an inductive approach was performed on the transcribed field notes, incorporating dialogue. The analysis followed the six phases of data analysis and was manually conducted by the first author. In the first phase of the analysis, the data were read several times, and notes were written in the margins of the transcripts to gain an overall view. All authors read and discussed the data to ensure a consistent perspective. Our central purpose in the analysis was to provide a straightforward picture of the day‐to‐day work participants do for patients living at home. In the second phase of the analysis, an inductive approach was adopted to code the data from the bottom up, capturing when different interactions and activities occurred; for example, when nurses were delegated the task of administering medication. Here, organisational changes were underway, with specific tasks being moved to assistant nurses with minimal training and fewer qualifications. Participant age and years of experience were noted and considered during this second phase; however, no clear patterns or differences in these factors emerged regarding organisational changes or work practices. While these variables were acknowledged, they did not significantly influence the thematic development in this study. In the third phase, the codes were grouped to identify initial subthemes and ensure relevance to the dataset. An example of the coding process is presented in Figure 1. In the fourth phase, the subthemes from the previous phase were revised and further developed across the entire dataset to ensure the trustworthiness of the themes and achieve validity (Nowell et al. 2017). In the fifth phase, the subthemes were listed on a single sheet of paper to gain a sense of the overall data, then clustered into main themes. A thematic map was then created based on the coding to provide information on the themes. The themes were then developed, and the findings were shared with the entire research team until total agreement was reached. In the sixth phase, the analysis was presented according to the themes in the Results section.

Trustworthiness

This study followed the four criteria of scientific trustworthiness, which included credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Lincoln and Guba 1985). Credibility and confirmability were established by recruiting only those participants who met the inclusion criteria and by using direct quotations from participants' statements. A comprehensive description of all methodological processes used in this study, along with the study's context, has been provided, which enhances the dependability and transferability of the research.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Linköping, Sweden (dnr: 2017/369‐3). The fieldwork ethics of shadowing differ from those of other data collection methods because unexpected events can occur. The researcher is not placed in a single study setting with only participants who have agreed to participate (Johnson 2014). During shadowing, other people interact with the participants, for example, those at the primary health care centre, who were not involved and were unaware that a research project was underway. To avoid harm to others who were not participating in the study, the care managers sent an information letter about the research and the researcher's contact information to everyone who worked in the two municipal home care settings. The patients received information, and all allowed the first author (E.O.) into their homes. They could contact the first author with any questions in advance before agreeing to participate. The first author (E.O.) continuously reflected on ethical decisions throughout the shadowing (Johnson 2014) and conducted the study in line with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2013). No sensitive or confidential information about participants or patients was collected. All data were treated confidentially, and only the research team had access via encrypted, sensitive files. The notebook with the fieldnotes and the camera with the photos were treated with respect and never opened to the public during the shadowing. During lunch breaks, the notebook and camera were locked in a locker accessible only to the researcher. Informed consent was obtained both verbally and in writing from all participants, again, if secondary shadowing was performed. In some situations, the first author (E.O.), together with the participant, decided not to attend staff meetings where workplace crises were discussed to avoid any harm to the registered and specialist nurses. At the end of each shadowing session, the first author (E.O.) asked the participant how they had experienced being shadowed, to give them an opportunity to reflect on ethical issues (Johnson 2014). Some participants said it was reassuring to have someone with them to discuss situations, as they felt few people within the organisation had shown interest in them as nursing professionals. Participants provided only positive reflections at the end of shadowing, but the care managers at one municipality reported that one participant experienced shadowing as a form of control over their work. This was not mentioned during the reflection with the participants. Although the first author (E.O.) offered to revisit that participant, they declined, saying it was not necessary.

Results

The organisational changes in municipal home care were characterised and explored in many forms during shadowing and were connected to competencies, responsibilities, and roles. The process towards organisational changes was based on four main themes: the black box in the health care system; workarounds in the workplace; a profession on the brink of change; and transfer of tasks towards the neighbourhood. These, along with the subthemes (Figure 2), are presented throughout the findings.

The Black Box in the Health Care System

Municipal home care offices were often located in an old apartment within a nursing home or in rented spaces that were not initially designed as workplaces for health care professionals. For example, a former apartment in a nursing home did not indicate that it served as the office of a health care organisation (Figure 2A). Lisa and Ted reported that community‐dwelling citizens and professionals in hospitals and/or primary health care did not know their actual function within the health care system. Participants struggled with workplace neglect, which they described as a sense of invisibility and a feeling of being inside a black box. The nurses explained that the focus is on hospital and primary health care, and that municipal home care does not seem to be part of the health care system:Municipal home care isn't even included on the map of health care organisations where the actual care is provided, as hospital care takes up all that space. We're not present anywhere, even though we're working close to the patients in their home[s]. (David, personal communication, October 2018)

Municipal home care isn't even included on the map of health care organisations where the actual care is provided, as hospital care takes up all that space. We're not present anywhere, even though we're working close to the patients in their home[s]. (David, personal communication, October 2018)

This challenge highlights the experience of being an employee in an organisation that is placed in a black box, an invisible room. This is likely to have a long‐term effect on the process of organisational changes, including shifting boundaries between patients' homes and hospitals, serving as a service provider to other organisations regardless of resources, and maintaining information sources.

Shifting Boundaries Between Patients' Homes and Hospitals

During the shadowing, there was not a single day when the nurses did not discuss the fluid boundaries between hospitals and municipal home care with each other. It was observed that the participants cared for patients who had been in the hospital for only a few days and were then sent home, even though they were not formally (medically) ready. For example, patients were sent home with wounds on their backs or arms that had not healed. There were patients from the psychiatric clinic who had no help when they returned home, and others who needed advanced treatment, such as assistance with a tracheostomy or central venous catheter. There were no clear guidelines for who would assume responsibility when patients came home. The participants took on the role of coordinator between patients and hospitals to ensure the best possible care. This was illustrated by Pernilla, who discussed with her colleague how home care had become more of a form of advanced care, and that the focus on person‐centred care was not the reality they encountered daily:The demand for advanced care has increased, and at the hospital, they don't understand how we [municipal home care] work … The talk about seeing the whole person and person‐centred care is a misunderstanding. There's no one caring for the patients; they [hospital] just let them out. (Pernilla, personal communication, April 2018)

The demand for advanced care has increased, and at the hospital, they don't understand how we [municipal home care] work … The talk about seeing the whole person and person‐centred care is a misunderstanding. There's no one caring for the patients; they [hospital] just let them out. (Pernilla, personal communication, April 2018)

This observation, shared by Pernilla, Elsa and Ted, highlights that hospital treatment times have become much shorter, creating new challenges for registered and specialist nurses. They had to reorganise care for patients living at home, which sometimes extended beyond their competencies and role in municipal home care. Often, they helped organise home care services for patients who had just been discharged from the hospital and had no support at home. The participants explained that hospital professionals often did not ask patients about their home situation or contact municipal home care services to learn about it. This uncertainty from the hospitals and their limited awareness of the organisation of municipal home care affected participants' working situations. Their tasks have become more complex because they have to handle these issues on their own, given the fluid boundaries between municipal home care and the hospital. The need for a better understanding, greater awareness, and closer collaboration with the hospitals was evident during shadowing.

Being a Service Provider for Other Organisations Regardless of Resources

One of the main observations during shadowing was the ambiguity among municipal home care, primary health care, and hospitals about the eligibility criteria for receiving municipal home care. Everyone interpreted these criteria differently, so municipal home care became a service provider to other organisations. These situations often arose because general practitioners in primary health care felt uncertain about the patient's home environment. Therefore, they wanted municipal home care for the patient as a checkup, or the palliative care unit wanted to transfer more of its tasks to the participants. These tasks were often unnecessary under the eligibility criteria because the patients could travel to primary health care facilities. One of these moments was captured while shadowing Alma, answering the speakerphone in her car:We [one department at the hospital] will send home one of our patients with cancer tomorrow, as the palliative care unit can't take them until next week. The patient needs intravenous antibiotics and will receive a central venous catheter tomorrow before going home. (Alma, personal communication, August 2018)

We [one department at the hospital] will send home one of our patients with cancer tomorrow, as the palliative care unit can't take them until next week. The patient needs intravenous antibiotics and will receive a central venous catheter tomorrow before going home. (Alma, personal communication, August 2018)

Alma mentioned on the same call that it would be better if they called her colleague instead, as she did not have access to paper or a pen at that moment. After the call, Alma expressed her discontent with always having to be available and never being able to say ‘no’, unlike other organisations. Other essential tasks performed by participants in the home environment without appropriate resources include blood transfusions and antibiotic infusions. During shadowing, it was observed that one of the participants, Amanda, did her best to find the medication that the palliative care unit had prescribed verbally for a cancer patient. Amanda looked everywhere, even in the nursing home, to see the medication. At last, she found it at the nursing home, but it was not the correct dosage. She brought the medication from one patient at the nursing home and drove to the other patient for whom it was prescribed. When she arrived at the patient's home, a physician from the palliative care unit was seated at the kitchen table. Amanda informed the physician that the medication was missing and that no prescription had been written. The physician explained that they had assumed this medication was available for home care. The physician then decided that the medication was no longer needed. These examples demonstrate that participants do everything for the patients despite limited resources and a lack of understanding from others. The participants wanted to see the palliative care unit and other organisations reorganised to develop a more unified municipal home care system in which participants worked together as one unit.

Keeping Track of Information Sources

The nurses not only had a responsibility to care for patients but also had to keep track of up to eight electronic resources, each requiring a password for daily logins. Most contact between organisations occurred electronically. Contact with the general practitioner or assistant nurses was often established through a specific online programme for all health care organisations or by fax. Other information sources included parts of the medical records, a weekly planner, and phone applications that could open the patient's door by knocking on it with the phone, which increased stress because some of these sources had to be checked twice a day.

In both municipalities, participants had no access to the county council's medical record system (hospitals or primary health care). Camilla stated: ‘The fact that we don't have the same medical record system as primary health care [centers] and the hospitals contributes much more work for us to keep the patient safe’. Thus, at the end of shadowing, all participants stated that they wanted a single medical record for all organisations and fewer electronic systems to ensure patient safety and communicate appropriately.

Workarounds in the Workplace

This theme emerged due to a lack of basic health care supplies for nursing tasks. A review by Debono et al. (2013) identified ‘Workarounds as observed or described behaviors that may differ from organizationally prescribed or intended procedures. They circumvent or temporarily “fix” an evident or perceived workflow hindrance to meet a goal or to achieve it more readily’. We found that the nurses' workplace was not equipped to provide medical care because there was a constant shortage of essential medical devices for blood sampling, and the available supplies would last only a few days. No fume hoods, as ventilation devices for preparing antibiotics or other toxic medications, were provided in any of the units for municipal home care. Figure 2B illustrates how Alma and Amanda prepared injections on the washing machine in the staff bathroom because they could not find a better place. Generally, the nurses did not have the typical medical equipment or facilities corresponding to their responsibilities or the work they had to do, and the care managers were aware of this. This was observed with Victoria, who was showing the staff refrigerator in the kitchen where some medicines were kept:Since we don't have a refrigerator for medicines, we've solved it by ourselves and placed them here instead, where we have our food boxes …. (Victoria, personal communication, May 2018)

Since we don't have a refrigerator for medicines, we've solved it by ourselves and placed them here instead, where we have our food boxes …. (Victoria, personal communication, May 2018)

During the shadowing, ad hoc solutions were the norm, such as bringing an empty backpack from home to carry their work equipment and using their private contacts at primary health care centres to borrow medical equipment for capillary blood samples. Materials were often reused. Participants frequently checked their own backpacks or their colleagues' backpacks for medical equipment to take to the patient. Blood sample tubes were usually borrowed from primary health care units, and two municipal home care units lacked a machine to print patient labels for blood samples. One participant stated that she had to use a thread instead of an intravenous stand to hang the blood bag because the municipal home care units did not have any stands. The stands were at a nursing home, and it would take about half an hour to bring them back to the patient's house (the workplace). Furthermore, most participants had no assigned space for taking breaks with their colleagues; therefore, they took coffee breaks at local venues. This was observed in the car when Iris was talking on the phone with a colleague. Iris discussed with the person on the phone where they should eat breakfast. They decided to meet at a McDonald's.We usually do that because we don't have time to go back [to the office]. (Iris, personal communication, March 2018)

We usually do that because we don't have time to go back [to the office]. (Iris, personal communication, March 2018)

The participants prioritised patient care over their own working conditions to keep the cycle of municipal home care moving within the Swedish welfare system.

A Profession on the Brink of Change

Previous research has shown that registered nurses serve as substitutes for doctors in primary health care (Maier and Aiken 2016). This study found that the professions of registered and specialist nursing in municipal home care were on the brink of change. The participants were doing all the work, and other professions expected them to do so (Figure 2C; a desk full of pending tasks). During the 23 instances of shadowing, assistant nurses were present. Connected to every neighbourhood area, assistant nurses, as licensed care providers, worked closely with participants, performing tasks delegated by the registered or specialist nurse, such as administering medication and performing advanced tasks, including stoma care. The assistant nurses were delegated many tasks beyond providing enteral nutrition and managing narcotics. In one of the municipalities, one assistant nurse helped with drugs and had further specialist educational qualifications in dementia care. The assistant nurse working in home care services was assigned responsibility for drug delivery. Shadowing Nora indicated that the nurses also faced demands from others to educate and approve the delegation of medication:The care managers sometimes require us to approve the staff they've sent, but I have talked to them many times about it. We can't approve someone who can't read or write. They simply didn't understand instructions and didn't have Swedish as their first language. (Nora, personal communication, March 2018)

These situations affected the type of care the assistant nurse could provide, and when s/he was not approved for delegation, the participants had more tasks to perform. On other occasions, registered and specialist nurses asked assistant nurses to order special wound equipment for some patients. The assistant nurses were reasonable about ordering wounds and other equipment in the two municipalities where shadowing took place. Participants discussed the assistant nurses' desire to do more and be assigned more advanced tasks. There were tensions between the participants (registered and specialist nurses) and assistant nurses because more of the participants' tasks had been shifted to them, leaving them dissatisfied. During shadowing, the registered and specialist nurses expressed that they often questioned the nursing profession and that there were no limits to their mission.

Questioning the Nursing Profession

The shadowing underlined that the participants were questioning whether there were any differences between working (or being employed) as registered or specialist nurses:I really don't know if there's any difference between district nurses and us. We do the same things here in municipal home care. (Karin, personal communication, March 2018)

The same phenomenon was observed among the specialist nurses, who questioned their identity as specialised nurses while trying to hold onto their role as much as possible. Mona noted in March 2018 that she tried to maintain her specialist competence as a district nurse by providing health‐promoting care, working with the assistant nurses around the patient. Rosa expressed her colleagues' view of her role as a district nurse:My role as a district nurse hasn't changed, but I do the same tasks as a registered nurse here. Many don't know the difference between us. I get a lot of questions from my colleagues, but I don't know if they see me as a district nurse. (Rosa, personal communication, April 2018)

The participants were, in a sense, downgraded in their professional roles, though a few expressed appreciations for their roles.

No Limits to Our Mission

In addition to being leaders in municipal home care, the participants helped patients with basic nursing tasks, such as changing sheets. There was no limit to the functions that supported patients. The care managers were often in the background or absent during the difficult decisions participants had to make. Shadowing showed that the participants took responsibility for everything beyond nursing care. During a meeting, a care manager stated that all staff should regularly wash patients' cars and change the tyres twice a year, in response to a query from one participant who wondered whether their tasks really included washing patients' cars.

Transfer of Tasks to the Neighbourhood

The municipalities under investigation differed in the socio‐economic level of the inhabitants, which affected the functions of the registered and specialist nurses. Some participants noted that the patient's background was essential and that neighbourhood characteristics mattered. In one municipality, where education levels and socio‐economic resources were higher than in the others, participants reported greater social trust among neighbourhood residents. They were all part of society and offered help when needed, as Frida had done the previous summer:Last summer, I called the housekeeper because there were spikes on the street. If someone came with a walker or wheelchair, it could break their wheels. (Frida, personal communication, May 2018)

Last summer, I called the housekeeper because there were spikes on the street. If someone came with a walker or wheelchair, it could break their wheels. (Frida, personal communication, May 2018)

Although there were differences among the workplaces across municipalities, the participants reported feeling part of the neighbourhood and noted that the environment around the patients affected them and others working nearby. The geographic neighbourhood influenced how many tasks the participants could delegate to the patient's neighbours or family members. In neighbourhoods with lower socioeconomic levels, the participants had to assist more with simple tasks, such as cleaning an apartment with the patient's adult children. The process of organisational changes was not only based on the organisational level but also on the participants' level, where neighbours and family members are responsible for health care tasks, as shown in Figure 2D, which illustrates an infusion hanging over an intravenous stand that the patient and his wife took care of after Zahra had set it up. The neighbourhood has to be considered to a great extent as part of the nurses' professional role.

Neighbours and Family Members Are Responsible for Health Care Tasks

Neighbours and families were part of municipal home care. Neighbours often called or emailed the participants for advice on medication. They helped patients with their medication and other necessary tasks. While she was being shadowed, Zahra, who worked mostly in rural areas, described how neighbours and families helped each other more in the countryside than in the city:Out here in the countryside, families and/or neighbours usually helped if patients were unable to go out by themselves to buy their medicines from the pharmacy, so that differs from the city. (Zahra, personal communication, May 2018)

The participants had many more tasks and blamed themselves for leaving patients who were living alone at home. Therefore, neighbours were valued, and they wanted to work together to provide proper health care and adopt a more health‐promoting approach towards the patients. The families also helped with any tasks the patients needed. In many situations, the participants called the children when they knew they had a close relationship or lived nearby to ask if they could run errands. In suburban areas, the children were present more often because their parents could not speak Swedish and needed a translator. The participants were part of the neighbourhood where nursing took place daily.

Discussion

This study employs a qualitative approach to explore and describe how registered and specialist nurses experience organisational changes in municipal home care, regardless of their competence and responsibilities, while working from an invisible workplace (i.e., locked in a black box). The registered and specialist nurses led their workplace and cared for their patients despite a lack of resources, providing the best possible quality of health care based on their clinical competence. Using shadowing (McDonald 2005), an understanding of the organisation at the municipal level was established. As Czarniawska (2014) reports, shadowing offers unique access, allowing the researcher to become involved in the organisation. Nonetheless, using shadowing as a data collection method, the organisational changes among registered and specialist nurses could be observed in municipal home care.

The participants found that there were stressful moments during shadowing due to a lack of resources and equipment to perform tasks for which they were responsible and competent. A stressful working environment has been discussed in previous research (Anshasi et al. 2020), but not in line with the lack of fundamental resources. In our study, registered and specialist nurses had to find new solutions to provide high‐quality home care for patients. There was insufficient space at the office for the registered and specialist nurses to prepare injections, which is part of their professional role. The care managers were aware of this, according to the nurses, but no actions were taken to change or improve the situation. This is in line with other studies that have shown how care managers lack understanding of the registered nurse's work situation (Pejner and Kihlgren 2019), and that a positive relationship with one's manager is essential for experiencing satisfaction at work (Persson et al. 2018). The working situation of registered and specialist nurses highlights a lack of awareness of their roles and competencies, not only among care managers but also within the health care system. Thapa et al. (2022) found that nurses working in hospitals were not acknowledged by other medical professionals, despite their increased workload. Jackson et al. (2022) argue that recent challenges in the health care system and in society have affected nurses' working conditions, making them more complex, and that new challenges have emerged. Studies of hospital environments (Godsey et al. 2020; Thapa et al. 2022) imply that the historical view of nurses having low status within the health care professional hierarchy is still perpetuated. We found that registered and specialist nurses were being downgraded in their roles, and more of their tasks were being delegated to assistant nurses. This shift was creating a competence gap in nursing, where nurses' tasks became invisible and their central role became something other than their original professional role. Similar studies have indicated that district nurses perceive their responsibilities as becoming more complex, as they must lead and supervise home care staff in their role as leaders (Lillsjö et al. 2023; Gautun et al. 2020). We found that it was unclear what a registered or specialist nurse was expected to do in municipal home care, and that they had no opportunity to develop competence. The environment within municipal home care does not support the nurses in their work (or provide nursing care). Our results are in line with previous studies showing that registered nurses experienced a lack of competence development in home care (Sabetsarvestani et al. 2022; Rusli et al. 2023). This development is clearly connected to previous studies where it was found that less‐educated nurses who do not have a bachelor's degree and who work in a more pressured environment correlate with an increase in premature mortality of patients (Aiken et al. 2014). Our results clearly demonstrate that municipal home care, as an employer, must take responsibility for acknowledging the profession of registered and specialised nursing. Further, our findings also reflect organisational changes in home care and evolving nurse roles. We interpret these developments as part of organisational changes in which clinical responsibilities are increasingly transferred to staff with varying levels of nursing education and training. The policy makers responsible for municipal home care also need to provide an overview of the organisation to assess whether organisational changes in home care affect patient safety. The registered and specialist nurses also need more resources in municipal home care to perform their jobs in line with their competencies and responsibilities. The professional role of registered and specialist nurses needs to be acknowledged by the nurses themselves. Otherwise, if this trend continues, municipal home care will be organised by health care workers with insufficient education, as seen in developing countries, which could affect patient safety and the role of nurses.

We have found limited studies focusing on the organisational changes in this area, despite an extensive literature search. The registered and specialist nurses wanted to be included more in decisions and research involving them, and they expressed positive sentiments about the research that was undertaken during shadowing. A clearer picture of their clinical competence and role as registered and specialist nurses could be developed from this study. This aligns with a Swedish Government Official Report on how good local care could be created within the health care system, which focuses on primary health care and municipal home care (Government Official Report 2018). This is in line with our results, which show that municipal home care must be more visible as an actor in the healthcare system, collaborating with others and as part of broader society to meet the needs of the growing number of older people living at home.

Our findings showed that the participants' tasks were being delegated to assistant nurses, neighbours, and family members. We found differences between municipalities and neighbourhoods, depending on whether they were in the countryside or the city, or in a municipality with a higher socio‐economic status, where neighbours and family members helped more. In this situation, the registered and specialist nurses helped with simple tasks, such as changing sheets, for people with limited resources. From our findings, we conclude that neighbourhood nursing (Cumberlege 1986) must be stipulated in municipal home care, and that registered and specialist nurses are responsible for one area with geographic boundaries and have the possibility to interact with neighbourhood residents, as do primary health care practitioners. Neighbourhood nursing and municipal home care should be further explored in future studies.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

The study has highlighted an area of nursing practice that has been under‐researched and invisible within the health care system, which is a strength. Much of the research over the years has focused on hospitals; the new area of municipal home care has not been included in health care organisations. The choice of an exploratory design with thematic analysis was appropriate for addressing the research aim and establishing trustworthiness (Braun and Clarke 2006). The study was based in Sweden, so our findings present only one country's dimension of organisational changes in home care. According to the Tarricone and Tsouros (2008), the perspective on home care is best explored in one's own country because political differences in how to organise home care exist across countries. Other limitations include participant recruitment. In our case, the care managers helped with the process, which could affect participation compared with a voluntary approach. All participants gave informed consent without any influence from their care managers. Our use of shadowing provided valuable insights into municipal home care and nursing practices (Gill et al. 2014). Nevertheless, having only one researcher involved may have led to interpretive bias. To mitigate this, we implemented regular peer debriefings and maintained reflexive documentation throughout data collection. Despite the benefits of shadowing, it has inherent limitations: it offers only a partial view of roles, limits hands‐on experience, and may result in misinterpretations. Furthermore, logistical challenges such as scheduling conflicts and potential disruptions to the professional being shadowed's workflow present additional constraints. Despite these challenges, shadowing remains a powerful method for understanding work environments, organisational structures, and professional practices (McDonald 2005). The thematic analysis was conducted by the same researcher who carried out the shadowing and who has professional experience as a nurse. While this insider perspective enriched the understanding of nursing practices, it also introduced the potential for interpretive bias. To address this, the researcher engaged in continuous reflexive journaling to critically examine personal assumptions and to maintain awareness of their influence on data interpretation. Furthermore, regular peer debriefing sessions were held to provide independent viewpoints, challenge interpretations, and enhance the rigour and credibility of the analysis. Despite these efforts, the dual role of the researcher remains a limitation that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Lastly, a limitation of this study is the timing of data collection, which occurred in 2017–2018. While the data are now over 7 (Ljungbeck et al. 2023), years old, the findings remain relevant because the organisational challenges, role ambiguity, and evolving responsibilities of nurses in municipal home care persist. Furthermore, insights into coordination, supervision, and skill utilisation in home care are still applicable in today's healthcare context, particularly as COVID‐19 (Eklund Saksberg et al. 2024) has further highlighted the critical role of community‐based nursing and intensified workforce pressures. Although some specific practices may have changed, the organisational issues identified in this study continue to inform the development of municipal home care and the support of nurses working in these settings.

Further Research and Implications for the Patient and/or Public Contribution

This study has several implications for nurses and other professionals working in municipal home care. Our results outline the accountability that registered and specialist nurses exercise, despite limited resources and support. The community and policymakers must acknowledge the importance of advanced practice nurses who coordinate care in the home. Municipality home care has to be developed with those nurses working in the home care organisation. Unfortunately, the voices of advanced practice nurses working at home are often not heard by other clinical professionals working in primary health care or in hospitals. Registered and specialist nurses, clinicians, and policymakers should contribute to developing interventions and policies that acknowledge the role of registered and specialist nurses working in municipal home care and the process of organisational changes.

Our study sends several messages to policymakers. First, there is a need for a paradigm shift in the health care system to see beyond hospital care towards municipal home care. Second, the roles, responsibilities, and competencies of registered and specialist nurses in municipal home care need to be more carefully described for these professionals. There is a lack of information about municipal home care, which is necessary because much of this care is shifting from hospitals to ordinary housing. Interest in working in municipal home care must be nurtured in future generations of newly graduated nurses to support that registered and specialist nurses start working in this area before there is a deficiency of such professionals. Registered and specialist nurses in municipal home care take professional risks due to a lack of understanding of the various roles they have and a desire to do the right thing to secure patient safety. Often, this entails nurses going beyond their scope of responsibility and jurisdiction of duty. The importance of advanced practice nurses who coordinate care in patients' homes must be recognised by the global healthcare system. Thus, this paper contributes to the international clinical community by advancing the field of study and paving the way for future research.

Conclusion

Our findings show that municipal home care, as an organisation, is placed in a black box within the health care system. This has led to the tasks and clinical competence of registered and specialist nurses being shifted to assistant nurses, neighbours, and family members, as their roles and workplaces differ across organisations. Existing district nurses are associated with primary health care and not municipal home care. Consequently, it is time to consider these findings and the existing literature, and to establish a new profession with a specialist degree in municipal home care to meet the needs of all countries with an aging population living at home and in a neighbourhood setting. The inspiration for neighbourhood nursing could be applied to this evaluation, along with nurses working in municipal home care. There is a need for more research into municipal home care as a workplace within a neighbourhood and to further explore the patients', carers', and assistant nurses' experiences of municipal home care.
