MulFeRL: Enhancing Reinforcement Learning with Verbal Feedback in a Multi-turn Loop
Xuancheng Li, Haitao Li, Yujia Zhou, Yiqun Liu, Qingyao Ai

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is widely used to improve reasoning in multiple domains, yet outcome-only scalar rewards are often sparse and uninformative—especially on failed samples, where they merely indicate failure and provide no insight on why the reasoning fails. In this paper, we investigate how to leverage richer verbal feedback to guide RLVR training on failed samples, and study how to convert such feedback into a trainable learning signal. Specifically, we propose MulFeRL ( Mul ti-turn Fe edback-guided R einforcement L earning). Concretely, MulFeRL builds on three mechanisms: (1) dynamic multi-turn regeneration guided by feedback, triggered only on failed samples, (2) two complementary learning signals for within-turn and cross-turn optimization, and (3) structured feedback injection into the model’s reasoning process. Trained on sampled OpenR1-Math, MulFeRL outperforms supervised finetuning and RLVR methods on in-domain and generalizes well out-of-domain. 1 1 1 We release our code at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/MulFeRL .

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has become a popular approach for improving reasoning capabilities in domains such as mathematics and code generation
(
27
;
5
;
3
)
. After a model produces an answer, a verifier provides a verifiable scalar signal (e.g., correct/incorrect), which is then used to optimize the policy
(
30
;
7
;
29
)
. However, as model performance increases, training that relies solely on final outcome correctness yields diminishing gains and eventually struggle to further improve the model
(
43
;
42
)
.

We argue that this bottleneck stems from the sparsity and uninformative content of scalar rewards
(
41
;
34
)
. In early training stages or on harder problems, models often generate a large fraction of entirely incorrect solutions; under standard RLVR, failed solutions typically receive near-zero or negative rewards that merely indicate incorrectness, but provide little information about why the reasoning failed or how to improve it
(
1
;
16
)
. Consequently, the resulting learning signal suffers from poor credit assignment: gradient estimates are noisy, training becomes sample-inefficient, and progress slows or even plateaus, ultimately limiting final performance
(
45
;
33
;
18
)
.

In contrast, when errors occur, humans typically leverage verbal feedback to localize mistakes and obtain fine-grained guidance, helping learners reduce the gap between current performance and desired outcomes
(
9
;
13
)
. Inspired by this, we incorporate verbal feedback into model training: beyond indicating whether an answer is correct, we use natural language to explicitly identify key errors, missing steps, and actionable suggestions for improvement, providing a substantially more informative, fine-grained learning signal for unsuccessful trajectories. This enables the model to learn from failed trajectories that would otherwise be treated as uniformly uninformative under outcome-only rewards.

Recent work has begun exploring learning from verbal feedback, demonstrating that it can improve reasoning. However, it remains non-trivial to convert verbal feedback into a consistent and learnable signal.
Some methods convert verbal feedback into scalar rewards for RL
(
37
;
20
;
12
)
.
This makes optimization straightforward, but it distills the rich information in feedback into a much simpler signal, weakening fine-grained guidance about errors and fixes. Moreover, studies like
(
43
)
generate feedback broadly across sampled trajectories rather than explicitly targeting failures, so a substantial fraction of feedback and the resulting updates focus on already-correct trajectories, leaving less learning capacity for hard failure cases per update and reducing sample efficiency.
Another line of work uses verbal feedback to generate revised solutions and then applies supervised finetuning on these revisions
(
8
;
17
)
. While often effective, this setup may not explicitly train the model to use and internalize feedback, limiting how effectively it can learn from external feedback during training.

Building on these observations, we focus on a central question:
how can verbal feedback be transformed into a stable learning signal that continues to drive RL training progress on failed samples?
Our key idea is that, during training, when a sample receives uniformly zero scalar reward and thus provides no informative learning signal, we introduce verbal feedback to guide regeneration and measure the resulting feedback-conditioned improvements, using them to construct an optimizable training objective, thereby converting verbal feedback into gradient signals.

To this end, we propose
MulFeRL
(
Mul
ti-turn
Fe
edback-guided
R
einforcement
L
earning), a dynamic multi-turn framework that strengthens RLVR by learning from verbal feedback on failed samples. MulFeRL builds on three key mechanisms:
dynamic multi-turn feedback-driven regeneration
that triggers targeted feedback only when all candidates fail and uses it to guide iterative regeneration;
two complementary learning signals
, with an in-turn GRPO
(
7
)
objective that learns from relative advantages among candidates within the same turn, and a cross-turn DPO
(
23
)
objective that learns a preference between the previous-turn attempt and its feedback-guided revision; and
structured feedback injection
that integrates feedback into the model’s reasoning process to improve its ability to use and learn from feedback.

We train MulFeRL on sampled OpenR1-Math
(
39
)
and evaluate it on five challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks. We further assess generalization on three out-of-distribution scientific and general reasoning tasks. Extensive experiments show that MulFeRL consistently outperforms prior supervised and RL-based finetuning baselines, and analyses of its dynamics and internal mechanisms further support its effectiveness.

Related Work

Enhancing LLM Reasoning with Reinforcement Learning.

In verifiable domains such as mathematics and code, RLVR typically trains LLMs with rule-based outcome signals and updates the policy through online optimization
(
27
;
19
)
.
A common foundation is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
26
)
, whose clipped policy-gradient objective supports stable on-policy updates.
Building on PPO, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
7
)
is a variant tailored for efficient, large-scale reasoning post-training. Subsequent GRPO variants, such as Dr.GRPO
(
19
)
, DAPO
(
42
)
, and GSPO
(
44
)
, further improve training stability and efficiency.
Meanwhile, methods like RAGEN
(
36
)
extend GRPO from single-shot rollouts to multi-turn interactive agent training with environment feedback.
Despite algorithmic progress, RLVR remains limited by sparse scalar rewards: when verification fails, the signal offers little guidance on what went wrong or how to fix it, often leading to training plateaus
(
33
;
18
)
. We address this by injecting verbal feedback during training to provide richer guidance and improve performance.

Learning from Verbal Feedback.

Verbal feedback can pinpoint errors and provide actionable correction cues, offering a richer signal than outcome-only supervision on failed trajectories
(
21
;
6
)
. Prior work mainly uses feedback in two ways. One line incorporates feedback into RL-style updates, either by converting it into scalar rewards or using critique to guide one-shot revisions
(
37
;
20
;
12
;
43
)
. While easy to optimize, this often compresses rich feedback into a coarse, single-step signal; moreover, when feedback is generated broadly rather than triggered by failures, updates can be wasted on already-correct trajectories, reducing sample efficiency
(
43
)
. Another line uses feedback to produce revised solutions and then applies supervised finetuning on these revisions
(
17
;
8
;
25
)
, which may not explicitly train models to internalize and exploit feedback during training. In contrast, we target failed samples in RLVR and use multi-turn feedback-guided regeneration to turn feedback-driven improvements into stable learning signals.

3.1
Problem Formulation

In GRPO-style RLVR, we sample an input prompt
x
from the training dataset
\mathcal{D}
. The policy
\pi_{\theta}
then generates
K
rollouts
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
with
y_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
, and a verifier
\mathcal{V}
assigns a binary reward
R_{\mathcal{V}}(x,y_{i})\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether each rollout is correct. GRPO updates rely on within-group relative advantages computed from these verifier rewards. A key difficulty arises in the failed-sample situation, where all
K
rollouts receive zero verifier reward, i.e.,
R_{\mathcal{V}}(x,y_{i})=0
for all
i
.
In this case, the verifier provides no discrimination among candidates, causing the within-group advantage signal to collapse and providing little policy-gradient learning signal, which leads to diminishing returns and training plateaus.

Inspired by how humans use verbal feedback to localize errors and guide corrections, we introduce verbal feedback into GRPO training to provide learning signal on failed samples, where the scalar verifier offers little discrimination. This raises a core question:

How can we convert verbal feedback into a stable learning signal that continues to improve the policy on failed samples?

Formally, our goal is to design a feedback-derived surrogate objective
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}
that provides informative gradients and supports stable optimization:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)^{K}}\Big[\,\mathcal{J}_{\theta}\!\big(x,\mathcal{T}(x)\big)\,\Big],

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x)

\displaystyle=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K},\hskip 9.24994pty_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=F_{\psi}\!\big(x,\mathcal{G}(x)\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x)

\displaystyle=\{(y_{i},R_{\mathcal{V}}(x,y_{i}),f(x))\}_{i=1}^{K}.

Here,
\psi
denotes the feedback simulator (e.g., a user or an LLM), and
F_{\psi}(x,\mathcal{G}(x))
denotes the verbal feedback generated for the rollout group
\mathcal{G}(x)
given
x
.

3.2
Overview of MulFeRL

To tackle this challenge, we propose
MulFeRL
(
Mul
ti-turn
Fe
edback-guided
R
einforcement
L
earning), an online RL algorithm that turns verbal feedback into a trainable signal by leveraging reward gains from feedback-guided regeneration.

The training pipeline of MulFeRL is as follows (as illustrated in Figure
1
):

1.

Initial group rollout and verification.
For each prompt
x
, sample a group of
K
rollouts
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
from the current policy and obtain verifier rewards
\{R_{\mathcal{V}}(x,y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{K}
from the verifier
\mathcal{V}
.

2.

Branching optimization.

(a)

All-positive group: skip.
We skip updates for initial-turn all-positive groups, as they indicate the current policy already solves the prompt reliably; these groups offer little learning signal.

(b)

Mixed group: apply GRPO.
If the group contains both positive and negative rollouts, update the model with a GRPO loss on this group.

(c)

All-failed group: feedback-guided multi-turn regeneration.
If all rollouts fail, iteratively:
(i) query the feedback simulator
\psi
on the current failed group to obtain verbal feedback,
(ii) regenerate a new group of
K
rollouts conditioned on the feedback,
(iii) verify the regenerated group with
\mathcal{V}
.
After each regeneration turn:

i.

If the group becomes
all-positive
, apply a cross-turn DPO loss between the regenerated responses and the previous-turn responses, then defer to the per-sample termination step.

ii.

If the group becomes
mixed
, apply a GRPO loss on the regenerated group, then defer to the per-sample termination step.

iii.

If the group remains
all-failed
, we request feedback again and perform a new feedback-guided regeneration turn. We repeat this process until reaching the maximum number of turns.

3.

Per-sample termination.
For the current prompt
x
, we terminate the procedure once a learning signal (GRPO or DPO) is obtained or the maximum number of turns is reached, and then proceed to the next training sample.

At inference time, the MulFeRL-trained model can be used with standard single-pass decoding, or optionally with multi-turn refinement assisted by an external feedback simulator.
MulFeRL builds on three key mechanisms: (1)
dynamic multi-turn feedback-guided regeneration
to enable learning on failed samples,
(2)
two complementary learning signals
(within-turn GRPO and cross-turn DPO), and
(3)
structured feedback injection
that integrates feedback into the model’s reasoning process.
We describe each mechanism next.

3.3
Dynamic Multi-turn Feedback-driven Regeneration

As discussed above, the key challenge in incorporating verbal feedback into RL training is to turn it into a learnable gradient signal.
Our key intuition is that effective verbal feedback should guide the model to turn an incorrect attempt into a correct one.
Accordingly, we leverage the feedback-induced improvement magnitude: for the same prompt, we measure how much verifiable outcomes improve when generation is conditioned on feedback.
When such feedback-conditioned improvements occur consistently, they can be converted into a natural learning signal for RLVR by quantifying the resulting reward gains.

MulFeRL explicitly operates over multiple turns. At each turn
t
, we sample a group of
K
candidates and query the verifier.
MulFeRL triggers verbal feedback
only
when the group is all-failed, and uses it to guide regeneration in the next turn.
Concretely, when all sampled outputs fail at turn
t
, we generate feedback with the feedback simulator
\psi
:

\small f^{(t)}\sim F_{\psi}\!\left(\,\cdot\,\middle|\,x,\;\{y_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{K},\;\{R_{\mathcal{V}}(x,y_{i}^{(t)})\}_{i=1}^{K}\right),

(2)

where the feedback is a group-level, actionable summary of the current rollouts, identifying the key failure mode and providing concrete guidance for revision
2
2

2

Since rollouts are typically long, we use a two-step aggregation: generate
g
subgroup feedbacks and then summarize them into a single feedback (details in Appendix
A
).
.
We then regenerate the next-turn candidates by conditioning the policy on the feedback:

\small y_{i}^{(t+1)}\sim\pi_{\theta}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,x,\;f^{(t)}\right),\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\dots,K.

(3)

We repeat this feedback–regeneration loop for up to a maximum number of turns, stopping early once the group is no longer all-failed.
By triggering feedback and regeneration exclusively on all-failed turns, our multi-turn design concentrates training on hard, low-signal cases where learning progress would otherwise be limited.
Across turns, external feedback progressively refines the model’s attempts, increasing the likelihood of converting failures into verifiable successes.
Moreover, the resulting feedback-conditioned improvements expose an optimizable signal, which we later leverage to construct stable training objectives in failure-dominated regimes.

3.4
Two Complementary Learning Signals

Building on dynamic multi-turn feedback-driven regeneration, a single prompt can exhibit different reward patterns across turns. When the regenerated group is
mixed
(containing both successes and failures), GRPO can be applied directly by exploiting within-group contrast. However, feedback quality varies: in some cases, feedback is sufficiently effective that the next-turn group becomes
all-positive
after regeneration. This poses a challenge for GRPO, whose signal relies on relative differences within a sampled group; when all candidates succeed, within-group contrast vanishes and the resulting advantages become uninformative.

To stay informative in this regime, MulFeRL additionally relies on
cross-turn
comparisons, contrasting a previous attempt with its feedback-guided revision for the same prompt. Even if all candidates in the current turn succeed, such comparisons still yield a well-defined preference signal by contrasting a previous attempt with its feedback-guided revision. Concretely, MulFeRL uses two complementary objectives: an
in-turn
GRPO signal when the regenerated group is mixed, and a
cross-turn
DPO signal when the regenerated group is all-positive.

In-turn signal (GRPO).

For a prompt
x
, consider a turn
t
where the sampled group
\mathcal{G}^{(t)}=\{y_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{K}
exhibits within-group discrimination (i.e., it contains both verifier successes and failures under
R_{\mathcal{V}}
).
We compute group-relative advantages by normalizing rewards within the group:

\displaystyle r_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\;R_{\mathcal{V}}(x,y_{i}^{(t)}),\hskip 18.49988pt\bar{r}^{(t)}\;=\;\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}r_{j}^{(t)},

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\big(r_{j}^{(t)}-\bar{r}^{(t)}\big)^{2}},

\displaystyle A_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{r_{i}^{(t)}-\bar{r}^{(t)}}{\sigma_{r}^{(t)}+\delta}.

We compute the GRPO objective:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}^{(t)}\mid c^{(t)})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i}^{(t)}\mid c^{(t)})}

\displaystyle,\hskip 18.49988pt\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(t)}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\rho_{i}^{(t)},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right),

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(x;\mathcal{G}^{(t)})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\min\!\Big(\rho_{i}^{(t)}A_{i}^{(t)},\,\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(t)}A_{i}^{(t)}\Big)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\,\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c^{(t)})\,\big\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid c^{(t)})\Big).

Here,
c^{(t)}
denotes the turn-specific conditioning context:
c^{(0)}=x
for the first turn, and
c^{(t)}=(x,f^{(t-1)})
for feedback-guided turns.
\pi_{\text{old}}
denotes the behavior policy used to sample the rollouts at turn
t
, and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a fixed reference policy for KL regularization. During training, external feedback tokens
f^{(t-1)}
are masked out from the loss computation, and we only apply the policy-gradient loss to the response tokens in
y_{i}^{(t)}
.

Cross-turn signal (DPO).

When strong feedback makes the regenerated group all-positive, the within-group GRPO signal becomes uninformative. In this case, we form cross-turn preference pairs between the regenerated responses at turn
t
and the responses from the previous turn
t\!-\!1
for the same prompt.
Let
{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)}=\{{y}_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{K}
denote the group generated under context
c^{(t)}
and verified as all-positive, and let
\mathcal{G}^{(t-1)}=\{y_{i}^{(t-1)}\}_{i=1}^{K}
denote the previous-turn group.
We construct
K
preference pairs by matching each regenerated sample with the previous-turn sample at the same index, treat
{y}_{i}^{(t)}
as preferred over
y_{i}^{(t-1)}
, and optimize the following DPO objective:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\theta}({y}_{i}^{(t)},c^{(t)})-\Delta_{\theta}(y_{i}^{(t-1)},c^{(t)}),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\sigma\!\big(\beta\,\delta_{i}^{(t)}\big).

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the logistic sigmoid,
\beta
is the DPO temperature, and

\small\Delta_{\theta}(y,c)\;=\;\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid c)\;-\;\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid c).

(7)

We aim to learn a preference for the improved solution given the feedback, so we define the preference under the same feedback-augmented context
c^{(t)}
. This cross-turn signal remains informative even when the current group is uniformly positive, because it relies on improvement relative to the previous turn rather than within-group contrast.

Mini-batch objective.

For a training mini-batch
\mathcal{B}
of prompts, MulFeRL assigns each prompt a loss according to the reward pattern of the selected update group after feedback-conditioned regeneration. Let
\textsc{Mixed}(x)
denote that the selected group is mixed (containing both successes and failures),
\textsc{AllPos}(x)
denote that the selected group is all-positive, and
\textsc{AllFail}(x)
denote that the rollout terminates with all-failed groups up to the turn limit. To balance the scale of the two objectives, we weight the cross-turn DPO loss by a coefficient
\lambda
. Concretely, let
\mathcal{G}^{(t)}
be the selected group at the updating turn
t
, and let
\mathcal{G}^{(t-1)}
be the previous-turn group used to form cross-turn pairs when
\textsc{AllPos}(x)
holds. We define the per-prompt loss as:

\small\mathcal{L}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(x;\mathcal{G}^{(t)})&\text{if }\textsc{Mixed}(x),\\
\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(x;\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(t)},\mathcal{G}^{(t-1)})&\text{if }\textsc{AllPos}(x),\\
0&\text{if }\textsc{AllFail}(x).\end{cases}

(8)

We optimize the mini-batch objective by aggregating
\mathcal{L}(x)
over prompts
x\in\mathcal{B}
.

3.5
Structured Feedback Injection

Another key design in MulFeRL is structured feedback injection. Inspired by passage injection in retrieval-augmented generation
(
31
)
, we incorporate verbal feedback into the reasoning context via a fixed slot, rather than appending text to the prompt. Integrating feedback via this fixed slot more tightly embeds it into the model’s reasoning context, enabling the model to leverage it more effectively.

Output format

<thinking>

<feedback>(verbal feedback)</feedback>

(rest of reasoning)

</thinking>(final answer)

Figure 2
:
The output format used by MulFeRL.

We train the policy to follow the output schema in Figure
2
. In the first attempt, the model fills the
<feedback>
slot with self-produced feedback and then continues its reasoning. In subsequent regeneration turns, the feedback simulator
\psi
produces feedback, which is injected into the
<feedback>
slot and continues the remaining reasoning. This ensures the feedback is always presented at a fixed location with explicit delimiters, so next-token prediction naturally conditions the reasoning on the injected feedback.
During MulFeRL training, the verifier
\mathcal{V}
enforces answer correctness and schema compliance; we assign reward
1
iff both hold, else
0
.
To mimic effective human feedback, we prompt
\psi
to produce two components:
Issue
(pinpointing the root mistake and concrete errors) and
Fix steps
(a step-by-step revision plan). This combines what failed with how to fix it, improving the reliability of feedback-guided regeneration and the resulting feedback-induced improvements used for training.

Notably, although MulFeRL often uses feedback from external LLMs, it is fundamentally different from distillation. Traditional distillation trains a student to match the teacher’s outputs using teacher-generated reasoning traces, implicitly assuming answer-level supervision. In contrast, MulFeRL leverages verbal feedback to improve the policy’s own reasoning process.
Compared with the distillation paradigm, MulFeRL (1) enhances the model’s reasoning capability via external feedback during training rather than imitating a teacher model, thus preserving the model’s intrinsic behaviors and inductive biases; (2) allows more flexible feedback sources, including simulators or real users; and (3) the multi-turn regeneration training scheme employed can also be transferred to the inference reasoning procedure (see §
5.2
). Moreover, as shown in §
5.1
, MulFeRL still delivers significant gains over GRPO even without any external feedback simulator, using only self-feedback from the backbone.

4.3
Ablation Study

As shown in Table
2
, we ablate three core components of MulFeRL: (1) Multi-turn regeneration (disabling regeneration so no additional turns are triggered, reducing training to single-turn GRPO), (2) Cross-turn DPO loss (removing the DPO objective and relying on GRPO updates only), and (3) Feedback injection (replacing structured injection with a plain prompting variant that provides the same feedback content but without the injection structure).
Performance degrades in all ablations, indicating that each component contributes materially to MulFeRL. Disabling multi-turn regeneration leads to the largest drop, consistent with the fact that single-turn scalar rewards provide weak learning signal on failed samples. Removing the cross-turn DPO objective while keeping regeneration also results in a clear decline, suggesting that regeneration alone does not reliably translate feedback-induced improvements into stable training signal: when feedback is effective, the regenerated group often becomes uniformly correct, making within-turn contrast signals sparse, and a cross-turn preference constraint helps consolidate the improvement direction. Finally, ablating feedback injection consistently hurts performance, implying that structured injection conditions the model’s reasoning on feedback more faithfully than plain prompting, improving feedback utilization and stabilizing training.

5
Analysis

5.1
Impact of Feedback Simulators

Table
3
compares MulFeRL trained with feedback from different simulators. We observe a consistent trend that higher-quality simulators lead to better downstream performance. Using the base backbone itself as the feedback simulator already yields a strong MulFeRL model that substantially outperforms GRPO (Table
9
for full results). This suggests that even without guidance from a stronger external simulator, MulFeRL can improve reasoning via self-feedback, in contrast to distillation, which typically depends on a strong external teacher model. Moreover, stronger simulators provide larger gains, with GPT-4o-mini improving over self-feedback and GPT-4o further boosting performance. We hypothesize that stronger simulators provide more accurate error diagnoses and more actionable feedback, which supports better regeneration trajectories and denser learning signals during training, ultimately improving generalization on challenging math reasoning tasks.

5.2
Test-time Multi-turn Feedback

We evaluate test-time multi-turn feedback on TheoremQA to assess whether MulFeRL training improves a model’s ability to use external natural-language feedback for inference-time revision. At inference time, GPT-4o provides feedback using the same feedback request/application prompts as in training, and the feedback is restricted to identifying errors and suggesting repair steps without revealing the answer. Figure
3
shows that more feedback turns improve all methods, while MulFeRL consistently achieves the best performance across turns and a higher final plateau than GRPO and Critique-GRPO. This indicates that MulFeRL can exploit external feedback more effectively to refine its solutions and improve performance under multi-turn feedback. Performance also tends to level off in later turns, suggesting diminishing returns as the feedback simulator becomes the dominant limiting factor.

6
Conclusion

In RLVR, outcome-only scalar rewards are often sparse and uninformative on failed samples, providing little guidance and leading to training plateaus. We address this by introducing MulFeRL, a multi-turn feedback-guided RLVR framework that applies verbal feedback to failed groups and turns feedback-driven improvements into effective learning signals, thereby allowing the model to learn from verbal feedback during RL training. Experiments show consistent gains over strong supervised and RL baselines, and ablations confirm the contribution of each component. Future work will focus on improving efficiency, robustness to noisy feedback, and extending the approach to partially verifiable or open-ended settings.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning by improving reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards for reasoning tasks, through incorporating natural-language feedback in a multi-turn training loop. Such techniques may enable more capable and sample-efficient models for domains where verification is available (e.g., mathematics, scientific reasoning, and other structured problem solving), which could benefit applications like education, tutoring, and reliable assistance for technical workflows.

At the same time, stronger reasoning and iterative self-correction can amplify existing risks of large language models, including misuse for generating convincing but incorrect explanations, facilitating academic dishonesty, or producing overconfident outputs in high-stakes settings. Additionally, multi-turn training and feedback generation can increase computational cost and associated environmental impact, and feedback sources (human or model-based) may introduce biases that propagate into the learned policy. Our design partially mitigates some concerns by using answer-free feedback (focused on error localization and repair suggestions rather than revealing solutions) and relying on verifiable outcome checks where available; nonetheless, careful deployment, access control, and task-appropriate safeguards remain important when applying these methods beyond research contexts.

Appendix E
Additional Experiments

E.1
Output Format

To control for confounding effects caused by differences in output formatting, we align the prompts and output constraints of GRPO and Critique-GRPO to exactly match those used by MulFeRL. Specifically, both baselines use the same input prompt (Figure
6
) as MulFeRL; for Critique-GRPO, the prompts for getting feedback (Figure
7
) and for feedback injection (Figure
9
) are also identical to MulFeRL. Finally, we enforce the same structured output-format constraint as in Figure
2
for all methods. The corresponding results are reported in Table
7
.

Under this controlled setting, GRPO shows a slight performance drop compared to the main-table results. A plausible explanation is that the structured format introduces additional generation overhead (consuming token budget) and partially limits the model’s freedom to organize reasoning and intermediate steps, reducing solution flexibility on harder instances. In addition, strict formatting can increase the risk of format violations or truncation, which lowers the fraction of valid outputs.

Critique-GRPO also underperforms its original numbers. Although it employs feedback injection, the amount of injectable feedback is relatively limited in this setting, preventing the model from fully benefiting from critique signals. Meanwhile, the structured template and strict formatting further constrain how the model organizes reasoning and intermediate steps, and can increase the likelihood of format violations/truncation. Together, these effects can shrink the effective solution space on complex problems and reduce the valid-output rate, thereby offsetting and potentially outweighing the gains from feedback. Overall, this alignment study suggests that a unified output format alone does not account for our improvements; MulFeRL’s gains are more fundamentally driven by feedback-guided iterative refinement and its associated optimization objectives.

E.2
Ablation Study

The complete ablation results are reported in Table
8
. The conclusions are consistent with the main experiments. Performance degrades across all ablations, indicating that each component contributes materially to MulFeRL. Disabling multi-turn regeneration yields the largest drop, consistent with the observation that single-turn scalar rewards provide a weak learning signal on failed samples. Removing the cross-turn DPO objective while keeping regeneration also leads to a clear decline, suggesting that regeneration alone does not reliably convert feedback-induced improvements into a stable training signal: when feedback is effective, the regenerated group often becomes uniformly correct, making within-turn contrast signals sparse, and a cross-turn preference constraint helps consolidate the improvement direction. Finally, ablating feedback injection consistently hurts performance, implying that structured injection conditions the model’s reasoning on feedback more faithfully than plain prompting, improving feedback utilization and stabilizing training. We omit Qwen2.5-7B-Base as a simulator because, without instruction tuning, it frequently violates the required output schema, making feedback extraction difficult.

E.3
Simulators

The complete results are given in Table
9
. The same takeaway as in the main paper holds: simulator quality strongly correlates with the final performance of MulFeRL. As shown in Table
9
, replacing the feedback source with a more capable simulator consistently improves downstream accuracy. Notably, even using the training backbone itself as the simulator is competitive and surpasses a weaker small model, indicating that MulFeRL can effectively leverage reasonably good self-generated critiques and is not tied to a specific simulator. Moving to stronger external simulators yields additional gains: GPT-4o-mini provides a clear improvement over self-feedback, and GPT-4o further increases performance. A plausible explanation is that higher-quality simulators produce more precise failure localization and more targeted, executable suggestions, which in turn guide regeneration toward better candidates and provide cleaner learning signals, leading to better generalization on difficult mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

E.4
Convergence and Validation Curves

Figure
13
plots the average performance on five math benchmarks as training progresses. We observe that MulFeRL improves more rapidly in the early stage and stabilizes at a strong level in substantially fewer optimization steps than GRPO and Critique-GRPO across both backbones. We attribute this faster convergence to two design choices. First, feedback-guided regeneration can often refine otherwise unsuccessful rollouts into higher-quality candidates before parameter updates, thereby reducing the extent to which the learning signal is dominated by low-quality samples. Second, cross-turn comparative learning extracts additional supervision from within-trajectory comparisons across turns, providing denser and more informative learning signals per prompt.

Appendix F
Limitations and Future Work

Limitations.

(1)
Higher training/inference cost.
Multi-turn regeneration with feedback introduces extra decoding and verification overhead, making MulFeRL slower and more expensive than single-turn RLVR baselines, especially when failures are frequent.
(2)
Dependence on verifier and simulator quality.
Performance relies on reliable outcome verification and the usefulness of the feedback simulator; stronger simulators improve results but add cost and reproducibility constraints, while weaker/noisy feedback can limit gains.
(3)
Limited scope of triggering and pairing.
Feedback is only triggered in ALLFAIL groups and cross-turn preference pairing is relatively simple, which may miss near-miss improvements and may not always extract the most informative learning signal.

Future Work.

(1)
Efficiency improvements.
Explore adaptive triggering/turn budgets (e.g., confidence-based), early stopping, and distilled/cheaper feedback models to reduce overhead while preserving gains.
(2)
Robustness to imperfect feedback.
Study training under noisy/biased/inconsistent feedback via filtering, uncertainty weighting, multi-simulator consensus, and verifier-guided feedback validation.
(3)
Beyond strictly verifiable tasks.
Extend MulFeRL to partially verifiable or open-ended settings by combining weak verifiers with learned rewards/process supervision, and by leveraging intermediate checks for richer signals.
