1.
Introduction

The systematic study of the solvable and non-solvable graphs of finite groups began in 2013, when Hai-Reuven introduced the non-solvable graph
\mathcal{S}_{G}
of a group
G
and proved that, for any non-solvable finite group, the induced subgraph on
G\setminus\mathrm{sol}(G)
is never regular and has diameter at most
2

[
11
]
. Six years later, Bhowal, Nongsiang, and Nath investigated the complementary solvable graph
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}(G)
, showing that it is neither a tree nor
n
-partite, and can never be planar, toroidal, or projective. They also established a lower bound on its clique number in terms of the group’s
solvability degree

[
3
]
. In 2020, Akbari
et al.
proved that every finite simple group yields a
split
solvable graph and that the degree pattern of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}(G)
can distinguish various classical groups
[
1
]
. A comprehensive monograph by Yadav and collaborators followed in 2021, elucidating the interplay between
\mathcal{S}_{G}
, the solvable radical
R(G)
, and invariably generating sets
[
15
]
. Most recently, Akbari embedded both
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}(G)
and
\mathcal{S}_{G}
into a broader “supergraph” framework, improving the universal diameter bound to
2
for all non-solvable groups
[
2
]
.

These developments highlight a unifying theme: algebraic structure can often be encoded in the combinatorial properties of graphs attached to groups. In this spirit, it is natural to ask whether similar constructions are possible for Lie algebras. Although finite groups and finite-dimensional Lie algebras differ substantially in structure, both possess solvable radicals, centralizers, and natural generating subsets, suggesting that their corresponding “solvable graphs” may display analogous phenomena.

More recently, attention has also turned to the Lie-theoretic counterpart of these constructions. In
[
14
]
, the nilpotent graph of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra was introduced and systematically studied, establishing a close parallel with the solvable and non-solvable graphs of groups. It was shown there that the nilpotentizer behaves well with respect to direct sums, that the graph encodes structural properties such as connectivity and isolation of vertices, and that explicit computations for triangular matrix algebras reveal highly regular decompositions. This complementary perspective highlights the robustness of the graphical approach to solvability and nilpotency across different algebraic settings.

The present work initiates a systematic investigation of the
solvable graph
of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
L
, where each vertex represents an element of
L
and adjacency encodes the failure of the subalgebra generated by two elements to be solvable. We establish several basic structural properties of these graphs, including divisibility conditions on the sizes of solvabilizers, constraints on degrees, and non-connectedness phenomena. Along the way, we demonstrate explicit examples for small Lie algebras such as
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, showing how their solvable graphs decompose into multiple components, and we provide algorithmic tools for computing such graphs in practice.

Beyond these foundational results, we also prove that in characteristic zero a simple
S
-Lie algebra must be abelian of dimension one, and we formulate a strong divisibility conjecture supported by extensive computational evidence. Taken together, these results reveal both striking parallels and crucial differences with the group-theoretic case, and open the door to further exploration of solvable and non-solvable graphs in the Lie algebraic setting.

The present article should be viewed as complementary to our earlier work on the
nilpotent graph
of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
[
14
]
. In both settings, one attaches a combinatorial structure to an algebra by considering two-generated subalgebras and by testing for structural properties such as nilpotency or solvability. The analogy is striking: in the nilpotent graph, connectivity phenomena and coset decompositions play a central role, while in the solvable graph, we observe parallel divisibility conditions and degree constraints. At the same time, there are key differences: for instance, solvable graphs may fail to be connected even in very small examples such as
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, and the hierarchy of divisibility relations for solvabilizers has no direct analogue in the nilpotent case. This illustrates that the two graph perspectives are genuinely complementary and that the solvable graph captures new aspects of Lie algebra structure.

In Section 2, we introduce the basic definitions of solvabilizers and the solvable graph of a Lie algebra, and we establish their first structural properties, such as coset decompositions and elementary divisibility results.

Section 3 develops the general theory of
S
-Lie algebras, including constraints on centralizers and solvabilizers, and culminates with the proof that in characteristic zero every simple
S
-Lie algebra is one-dimensional abelian.

In Section 4 we investigate the global properties of solvable graphs, including degree bounds, connectivity issues, and examples computed for small Lie algebras such as
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
.

Finally, Section 5 outlines a strong divisibility conjecture, supported by computational evidence, and discusses directions for further research on the interplay between Lie algebra structure and solvable graphs.

Preliminaries

Basics on Lie Algebras.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field
F
. A
Lie algebra
is a vector space
L
over
F
, equipped with a bilinear operation
[\cdot,\cdot]:L\times L\to L
, called the
Lie bracket
, satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

Alternating property:

[x,x]=0

\forall x\in L
;

(2)

Jacobi identity:

[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+[z,[x,y]]=0

\forall x,y,z\in L
.

These imply
anticommutativity
:
[x,y]=-[y,x]
for all
x,y\in L
.

A Lie algebra is said to be
abelian
if
[x,y]=0
for all
x,y\in L
. Every one-dimensional Lie algebra is abelian. The
dimension
of a Lie algebra is its dimension as a vector space over
F
. A
subalgebra
of
L
is a subspace closed under the Lie bracket. If
A,B
are subspaces of
L
, then
[A,B]
denotes the subspace spanned by all
[a,b]
with
a\in A
,
b\in B
.

Let
F
be a field. Standard examples include:

•

\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(F)
, the Lie algebra of all
n\times n
matrices over
F
with bracket
[x,y]=xy-yx
;

•

\mathfrak{t}_{n}(F)
, the subalgebra of upper triangular matrices;

•

\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(F)
, the subalgebra of traceless
n\times n
matrices over
F
.

•

\mathfrak{so}_{n}(F)
, the subalgebra of skew-symmetric
n\times n
matrices relative to the standard quadratic form.

•

\mathfrak{sp}_{2m}(F)
, the symplectic Lie algebra over
F
.

A subspace
I
of
L
is called an
ideal
if
[x,y]\in I
for all
x\in I
,
y\in L
, that is,
[I,L]\subseteq I
. Intersections, sums, and Lie products of ideals are again ideals.

The
derived series
of
L
is defined recursively by

\displaystyle L^{(0)}

\displaystyle=L,

\displaystyle L^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=[L^{(k)},L^{(k)}].

The
lower central series
is given by

\displaystyle L^{1}

\displaystyle=L,

\displaystyle L^{k+1}

\displaystyle=[L^{k},L].

A Lie algebra

is called

if
L^{(r)}=0
for some
r
, and

if
L^{k}=0
for some
k
. Every nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable, but the converse is not true.

Examples: Let
F
be a field.

•

If
\mathrm{char}(F)\neq 2
then
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(F)
is non solvable;

•

If
\mathrm{char}(F)\neq 2
then
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(F)
is non solvable;

•

\mathfrak{t}_{n}(F)
is solvable but not nilpotent;

•

\mathfrak{so}_{n}(F)
is non solvable;

•

\mathfrak{sp}_{2m}(F)
is non solvable.

For
x\in L
, define the
adjoint map

\mathrm{ad}\,x:L\to L
by
\mathrm{ad}\,x(y)=[x,y]
. The map
\mathrm{ad}:L\longrightarrow\mathrm{Der}(L)
, where
\mathrm{Der}(L)
is the set of derivations of
L
, is called the
adjoint representation
of
L
. The
centralizer
of
x
in
L
, denoted as
C_{L}(x)
or simply

when

is clear from context, is defined as:

C_{L}(x)=\{y\in L\mid[x,y]=0\}

where
[x,y]
denotes the Lie bracket of
x
and
y
.

The
center
of
L
is
Z(L)=\{x\in L\mid[x,L]=0\}
. Define the upper central series recursively as

\displaystyle Z_{0}(L)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle Z_{i}(L)/Z_{i-1}(L)

\displaystyle=Z(L/Z_{i-1}(L)),\quad i\geq 1.

The union
\bigcup_{i\geq 0}Z_{i}(L)
is called the
hypercentre
, denoted
Z^{*}(L)
. For finite-dimensional
L
, this series terminates.

The solvabilizer of a Lie algebra

In this paper,

Definition 3.1
.

Let

be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field

(1)

For

of

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(h):=\{x\in L\mid\langle h,x\rangle\ \text{is a solvable Lie subalgebra of}\ L\}.

(2)

The solvabilizer of
L
is defined by:

\mathrm{sol}(L):=\{x\in L\mid\langle h,x\rangle\ \text{is solvable for all}\ h\in L\}.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra defined over a field
F
. We denote with
R(L)
the maximal solvable ideal
of
L
, and we call it
the solvable radical of
L
.

Example 3.1
.

(1)

\mathrm{sol}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(F_{2}))=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(F_{2})
.

(2)

\mathrm{sol}(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(F_{2}))=\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(F_{2})
.

(3)

If

, then
\mathrm{sol}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(F))=0
.

(4)

If
\mathrm{char}(F)\neq 2

.

Remark 3.2
.

Some immediate properties:

(1)

\langle h\rangle\subseteq\mathrm{sol}_{L}(h)
,

(2)

\mathrm{sol}(L)=\bigcap_{h\in L}\mathrm{sol}_{L}(h)
,

(3)

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(h)
is in general not a subalgebra of

. Let

e\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad f\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Their Lie brackets are
[h,e]=2e,\;[h,f]=-2f,\;[e,f]=h

and

Then

Properties of solvabilizers

If
x\in L
, then
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
is the union of all maximal solvable subalgebras containing

We will need the following result from linear algebra.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
be subspaces of a vector space

such that
B_{i}\not\subseteq\cup_{j\neq i}B_{j}
. If
|F|>n-1
, then
\cup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}
is not a subspace

Proof.

Suppose that
B=\cup_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}
is a subspace. Let
x\in B_{1},x\notin\cup_{j>1}B_{j},y\notin B_{1}
and put

|C\cap B_{1}|=0
. Also,
|C\cap B_{j}|\leq 1

|C|\leq n-1
.

Let
\theta:F\rightarrow C:\alpha\mapsto y+\alpha x

\theta
is injective, whence
|F|=|C|\leq n-1
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
L
be a Lie algebra of dimension

over a field

with

. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
is a

of

(ii)

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
is a

of

(iii)

Proof.

First, we claim that

(1)

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)=\bigcup\big\{M\leq L\mid M\text{ maximal solvable and }x\in M\big\}.

In fact, let

Conversely, if

(ii)
\Rightarrow

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii). If there is a unique maximal solvable subalgebra

\Rightarrow

(i)
\Rightarrow

Lemma 3.9
.

Let

be a Lie algebra,

\mathrm{sol}(L)\subseteq\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)

(iii)

In particular, (iii) and (iv) explain how passing to quotients with solvable radical preserves the subalgebra structure of solvabilizers, which will be useful later when analysing

Definition 3.3
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field

,

These are straightforward.

Definition 3.4

Let

Lemma 3.14
.

Let

be a finite–dimensional S-Lie algebra. Then, for any Lie subalgebra

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
L
be a Lie algebra over any field
F
with radical
R
. Then
R\subseteq\mathrm{sol}(L)
and
\mathrm{sol}(L/R)=\mathrm{sol}(L)/R
.

Proof.

Let

and

. Then
\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle\cap R+Fy
, so
\langle x,y\rangle
has a solvable ideal of codimension at most one and thus is solvable. Hence

.

Let
x+R\in\mathrm{sol}((L/R)
,
y\in L
. Then
\langle x,y\rangle+R=\langle x+R,y+R\rangle
is solvable. But then
\langle x,y\rangle
is solvable, so

. The reverse inclusion is clear.

The next theorem shows that, outside characteristic

, the only ideals that can lie inside the solvabilizer of

are those already contained in the solvable radical. In other words, the solvabilizer does not introduce new solvable Ideals beyond the radical, which reinforces the structural role of

.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
L
be a Lie algebra over an infinite field
F
of characteristic greater than
2
with radical
R
. If
N
is an ideal of
L
with
N\subseteq\mathrm{sol}(L)
then
N\subseteq R
.

Proof.

Since the set of elements which generate a solvable subalgebra is closed in the Zariski topology and
L
is dense in some algebraic closure of
F
, we may assume that
F
is algebraically closed. Suppose that
N\not\subseteq R
. Then
(N+R)/R
is an ideal of
L/R
inside
\mathrm{sol}(L/R)
, so it suffices to assume that
L
is semisimple and to show that
N=0
. If
N\neq 0
there will be a minimal ideal
I
of
L
inside
\mathrm{sol}(L)
and
I
contains a unique maximal ideal
J
, by
[
5
, Theorem 9.3]
, so
I/J
is simple. Moreover,
I/J
has a subalgebra
S/J
with quotient
\frac{S/J}{K/J}\cong S/K\cong sl_{2}(F)
, by
[
9
, Lemma 3.2]
. Let
x
be such that
x+K\in S/K
. Then there exists
y+K
such that
\langle x,y\rangle+K=\langle x+K,y+K\rangle=S/T
. But
\langle x,y\rangle
cannot be solvable, so
x\notin\mathrm{sol}(L)
, a contradiction. Hence

as required.
∎

Next Lemma shows that
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
depends only on the one-dimensional subalgebra generated by
x
; in particular, it is unchanged when
x
is rescaled.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
L
be a finite–dimensional Lie algebra over a field
F
.
Let
x,y\in L
, let
N
be an ideal of
L
with
N\subseteq\mathrm{sol}(L)
, and let
\varphi\in\operatorname{aut}(L)
be any Lie–algebra automorphism of
L
.

(1)

If
\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle
, then
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)=\mathrm{sol}_{L}(y)
.

(2)

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(\varphi(x))=\varphi(\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x))
.

(3)

Let
\pi:L\longrightarrow L/N
be the canonical projection. Then

\mathrm{sol}_{L/N}(\pi(x))=\pi(\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x))=\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)/N.

Proof.

(1)  Suppose
\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle
. Then for every

a\in L
we have
\langle a,x\rangle\subseteq\langle a,y\rangle
and
\langle a,y\rangle\subseteq\langle a,x\rangle
. That is,
\langle a,x\rangle=\langle a,y\rangle
for all
a\in L
. Therefore,
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)=\mathrm{sol}_{L}(y)
.

(2)  Let
z\in L
. Then

\displaystyle z\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(\varphi(x))

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\langle z,\varphi(x)\rangle\text{ is solvable}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\langle\varphi^{-1}(z),x\rangle\text{ is solvable}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\varphi^{-1}(z)\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;z\in\varphi(\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)).

(3)  If
a\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
then
\langle a,x\rangle
is solvable; and its image
\pi(\langle a,x\rangle)=\langle\pi(a),\pi(x)\rangle
is solvable because
\pi
is a homomorphism.
Thus
\pi(a)\in\mathrm{sol}_{\,L/N}\bigl(\pi(x)\bigr)
and
\pi(\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x))\subseteq\mathrm{sol}_{L/N}(\pi(x))
.

Conversely, let
b+N\in\mathrm{sol}_{L/N}(\pi(x))
. Then the subalgebra
\langle b+N,x+N\rangle
of
L/N
is solvable, so
\langle b,x\rangle+N
is solvable; hence
\langle b,x\rangle
itself is solvable. Therefore
b\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
and
b+N\in\pi(\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x))
. So
\mathrm{sol}_{L/N}(\pi(x))=\pi(\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x))
, which is the same as
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)/N
.

This result shows that solvabilizers only depend on the one-dimensional subalgebra
\langle x\rangle
, and are stable under automorphisms and quotients. Hence, the notion of solvabilizer is robust under standard structural operations
on Lie algebras.

∎

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
L
be a finite–dimensional Lie algebra over a field
F
. Then, for every
x\in L
,
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
is a disjoint union of some cosets of
Fx
. More precisely, for every
a\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
one has
a+Fx\subseteq\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
, and hence

\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)=\bigsqcup_{a\in\mathcal{R}}\,(a+Fx)

for some set
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
of representatives of the
cosets of
Fx
contained in
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in L
and
a\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
. For any
t\in F
we have
\langle a+tx,x\rangle=\langle a,x\rangle
.
As
\langle a,x\rangle
is solvable by the choice of
a
, the same holds for
\langle a+tx,x\rangle
, so
a+tx\in\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
. Thus
a+Fx\subseteq\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
.

Cosets
a+Fx
in the additive group
L
are either equal or disjoint, hence
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)
is a disjoint union of those cosets that it contains; choose one representative
a
from each such coset to form
\mathcal{R}
.
∎

.

Let
L

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
L
be a finite–dimensional S-Lie algebra over a field

Proposition 3.24
.

Let
L
be a finite–dimensional Lie algebra over a field
F
. Then

(1)

If
L
is solvable, then
L
is an
S
–Lie algebra; in fact
\mathrm{sol}_{L}(x)=L

x\in L

(2)

Conversely, assume that
F
is algebraically closed of characteristic zero. If
L
is an
S
–Lie algebra, then
L
is solvable.

In particular, under the standing hypothesis in
(2)

L
is solvable if and only if
L
is an
S
–Lie algebra.

Proof.

(1) is clear, so assume that
F
is algebraically closed of characteristic zero and that
L
is an
S
-Lie algebra. Suppose first that
L
is semisimple. By general theory, it is clear that
L
has more than one Borel subalgebra. Let
B

be two different Borel subalgebras. A standard Borel subalgebra has dimension greater than
\frac{1}{2}\dim L
and all Borel subalgebras are conjugate. So, since

B\cap B^{\prime}\neq 0

Suppose now that
L
is not semisimple nor solvable, so

is the radical and

is a Levi subalgebra. Then, if
x\in R
, any subalgebra of the form

, where

is a maximal solvable subalgebra containing

L
is not an

-Lie algebra.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

For
q>2
the degree sequence of
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{S}}\,(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is:

(\underbrace{2q^{2}-q-2}_{\frac{q(q^{2}-1)}{2}-\text{times}},\ \underbrace{q^{2}-2}_{(q^{2}-1)},\ \underbrace{q-2}_{\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{2}}),

and the graph is non-connected for
q>2
.

Proof.

Consider the algebra acting on itself. A typical element of
L=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
can be represented by a matrix of the form
\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&-a\end{matrix}\right)
, where
(a,b,c)\neq(0,0,0)
. This matrix has characteristic polynomial
\lambda^{2}-a^{2}-bc
. There are three possibilities for eigenvalues: there can be none, one or two. We’ll consider each of these possibilities in turn.

No eigenvalues:
This occurs if
a^{2}+bc
is not a square. Each of
a,b,c
can take q values, except that each of
b,c
must be non-zero, so there are
q(q-1)^{2}
possibilities for
a^{2}+bc
. However, in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, precisely half of them will be quadratic residues, so there are
\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{2}
vertices represented by elements of this kind.

If
x
is such a vertex, it can’t belong to a two-dimensional subalgebra. Hence, it can only be attached to
\alpha x
where
\alpha\neq 0,1
. This leaves
q-2
possibilities.

One eigenvalue:
This occurs when
a^{2}+bc=0
. If
c=0
, then
a=0
and there are
q-1
choices for
b
. Similarly, if
b=0
, there are
q-1
choices for
c
. If
b,c\neq 0
, there are
(q-1)^{2}
choices for
bc
, half of which are quadratic residues. For each quadratic residue, there are two choices for
a
, so there are
(q-1)^{2}
possibilities here. The total number of such vertices, therefore, is
(q-1)^{2}+2(q-1)=q^{2}-1
.

If
x
is such a vertex, it belongs to precisely one two-dimensional subalgebra, spanned by
x,y
, say. Then
x
can only be attached to
\alpha x+\beta y
where
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0),(1,0)
. This leaves
q^{2}-2
possibilities.

Two eigenvalues:
This total number of vertices in the graph is
q^{3}-1
, so the number remaining for this case is

q^{3}-1-\frac{q(q-1)^{2}}{2}-(q^{2}-1)=\frac{(q-1)(q^{2}+q)}{2}=\frac{q(q^{2}-1)}{2}.

If
x
is such a vertex, it belongs to precisely two two-dimensional subalgebras
M_{1},M_{2}
spanned by
x,y
and
x,w
, respectively. It can be attached to any vertex in
M_{1}
,
\alpha x+\beta y
, other than those for which
(\alpha,\beta)=(0,0),(1,0)
, and there are
q^{2}-2
of these, as in the previous case. It can also be attached to any vertex in
M_{2}
,
\gamma x+\delta w
, other than
0
and those already counted; that is, for which
\beta=0
, and there are
q^{2}-q
of these. Hence, there are
q^{2}-2+q^{2}-q=2q^{2}-q-2
possibilities.

None of the vertices corresponding to the first case can be connected to any of the vertices in the other two cases, so the graph is non-connected.
∎

This result shows that the solvable graph of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
breaks into multiple components, with vertices grouped according to the number of eigenvalues of the corresponding matrix (none, one, or two). In particular, vertices with no eigenvalues are only linked to scalar multiples of themselves, producing isolated components. This contrasts with the group-theoretic setting, where solvable graphs are always connected, and highlights a genuinely new Lie-theoretic phenomenon.

Theorem 4.4
.

For
q>2
the degree sequence of
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{S}}\,(\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is

(\underbrace{2q^{3}-q^{2}-q-1}_{q^{2}(q^{2}-1)/2-\text{times}},\underbrace{q^{3}-q-1}_{q^{3}-q},\underbrace{q^{2}-q-1}_{q^{2}(q-1)^{2}/2})

Proof.

Write
L=\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\oplus Z(L)
, where
Z(L)
is the centre of
L
, that is, the scalar matrices in
L
. Then every element of
x\in L
belongs to a solvable subalgebra of dimension at least two, namely
\mathbb{F}x+Z(L)
.

Suppose first that
x
belongs to a solvable subalgebra of dimension two, but not three. Then it is of the form
y+z
where
y
belongs to a solvable subalgebra of dimension at most one in
sl_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
z\in Z(L)
. Using Table 2 we have that there are
q^{2}(q-1)^{2}/2
such
x
. Such an
x
can only be connected to any vertex of the form
\alpha x+\beta z
provided that
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,\beta),(1,0)
and thus to
q^{2}-q-1
other vertices.

Next, suppose that
x
belongs to just one solvable subalgebra of dimension three. Then it is of the form
y+z
where
y
belongs to a solvable subalgebra of dimension at most two in
sl_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
z\in Z(L)
. Using Table 2, we have that there are
q(q^{2}-1)
such
x
. Such an
x
can only be connected to any vertex of the form
\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z
provided that
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\neq(0,0,\beta),(1,0,0)
and thus to
q^{3}-q-1
other vertices.

Finally, suppose that
x
belongs to more than one solvable subalgebra of dimension three. Then it is of the form
y+z
where
y
belongs to more than one solvable subalgebra of dimension at most two in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
z\in Z(L)
.
Using Table 2, we have that there are
q^{2}(q^{2}-1)/2
such
x
. If
x
is such a vertex, it belongs to two three-dimensional subalgebras
M_{1},M_{2}
spanned by
x,y,z
and
x,w,z
, respectively. It can be attached to any vertex in
M_{1}
,
\alpha x+\beta y+\gamma z
, other than those for which
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0,0,\gamma),(1,0,0)
, and there are
q^{3}-q-1
of these. It can also be attached to any vertex in
M_{2}
,
\delta x+\epsilon w+\zeta z
, other than those already counted; that is, for which
\epsilon=0
, and there are
q^{3}-q^{2}
of these. Hence, there are
q^{3}-q-1+q^{3}-q^{2}=2q^{3}-q^{2}-q-1
possibilities.
∎

This formula reveals that the solvable graph of
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
naturally separates into three classes of vertices, according to the dimension of the smallest solvable subalgebra they belong to. The three different degree values reflect the different “spectral types” that appear inside
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, shifted by the presence of the one-dimensional centre of
\mathfrak{gl}_{2}(F_{q})
.
Thus, degree distributions provide a combinatorial fingerprint of the underlying Lie algebra structure.

Finally, we present the complement of the soluble graph and an example of it. This complement condition immediately implies, for example, that the non-soluble graph of
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{S}}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is connected.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra. The non-solvable graph of
L
, denoted by

L\setminus\mathrm{sol}(L)
, and two vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent if and only if
\langle x,y\rangle
is a non-solvable subalgebra of
L
.
