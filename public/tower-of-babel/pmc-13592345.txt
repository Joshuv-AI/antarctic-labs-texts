Cabozantinib After Immunotherapy in Advanced Renal Cell Carcinoma. The CARE Study by the Hellenic Gu Cancer Group
Authors: Aristotelis Bamias, Michael Liontos, Ioannis Binas, Dimitra Stefanou, Kimon Tzannis, Roubini Zakopoulou, Nikolaos Dedes, Dimitrios Deligiannis, Ioanna Katsiana, Areti Mamali, Meropi Galari, Konstantina Kakogianni, Vasiliki Nikolaidou, Dimitrios C Ziogas, Napoleon Moulavasilis, Vasiliki Magoula, Efstathia Giannopoulou, Christos Christodoulou, Meletios Athanasios Dimopoulos, Michael Chrisofos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread use of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)–based combinations as first-line therapy for metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) has created an unmet need for effective treatment options at disease progression. Cabozantinib is recommended after ICI failure; however, real-world evidence regarding its efficacy and safety in this setting remains limited, particularly following contemporary ICI-containing regimens.MethodsCARE was a retrospective, multicenter, non-interventional study conducted in Greece. Patients with locally advanced or metastatic RCC who received cabozantinib monotherapy after progression on at least one prior ICI-containing regimen were included. The primary endpoint was objective response rate (ORR). Secondary endpoints included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and safety. Exploratory analyses evaluated outcomes according to prior therapies and timing of cabozantinib administration.ResultsThirty patients were included. Prior immunotherapy consisted mainly of nivolumab monotherapy or nivolumab plus ipilimumab, while cabozantinib was administered predominantly as second-line treatment. No complete responses were observed; twelve patients achieved partial response, resulting in an ORR of 40% (95% CI: 22.7–59.4). Stable disease was observed in 56.7% of patients. Median follow-up was 20 months (95% CI 15.2–24.6). Median PFS was 21 months (95% CI: 12.5–NR), and median OS was 29.7 months (95% CI: 25.5–NR). Prior exposure to tyrosine kinase inhibitors was associated with shorter PFS, while ORR and OS were not significantly affected. Cabozantinib demonstrated a manageable safety profile, with fatigue and diarrhea being the most common adverse events. Grade III–IV toxicities occurred in 26.7% of patients.ConclusionIn this real-world Greek cohort, cabozantinib demonstrated meaningful clinical activity and an acceptable safety profile in mRCC patients progressing after ICI-based therapy. These findings support cabozantinib as an effective treatment option in the post-immunotherapy setting and contribute valuable real-world evidence to inform therapeutic sequencing in mRCC.Keywords: metastatic renal cancer, cabozantinib, immunotherapy

Introduction

During the last twenty years the treatment paradigm of metastatic renal-cell carcinoma (mRCC) has been dramatically changed, mainly due to our advancement in the understanding of the molecular biology of RCC.1 Agents targeting the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway, the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and the interaction between the T lymphocyte programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) receptor with its ligand (PD-L1) (immune checkpoint inhibitors-ICIs) have been developed for the treatment of mRCC. Consequently, since 2005, when the first VEGF receptor (VEGFR) tyrosine-kinase inhibitor (TKI), sorafenib, gained approval, the armamentarium of mRCC treatment has been enriched by more than 10 different approved therapies.2,3

Recently, the ICI combination of Nivolumab plus Ipilimumab4 and the combinations of TKIs plus ICIs, specifically Pembrolizumab plus Axitinib,5 Cabozantinib plus Nivolumab,6 Pembrolizumab plus Lenvatinib,7 all became recommended 1st-line therapies for all mRCC patients. In spite of the magnitude of success in 1st-line therapy, most patients will experience disease progression. Therefore, effective treatment for relapse is necessary. Since 2009, the mTOR inhibitor everolimus,8 the anti-VEGFR TKIs cabozatinib9 and axitinib,10 the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab11 and the combination of TKI Lenvatinib plus everolimus12 have all been approved for this setting, albeit for different 1st-line therapies. The optimal sequence of available agents in relapsed disease is not yet completely defined. International guidelines recommend that the use of nivolumab and cabozantinib should predate that of everolimus, while the timing of axitinib and the combination Lenvatinib/Everolimus is undetermined.

As a consequence of the above developments, the majority of mRCC patients will receive immunotherapy (IO) in first line. More importantly, for patients who receive the two most efficient classes of agents, i.e VEGFR TKI inhibition and ICI in the initial treatment line, the development of effective options at relapse represents an urgent medical need. Thus, the activity of TKIs in the post ICI/ICI or ICI/TKI setting becomes critically important. In addition, data on the tolerability of TKIs in this setting are only available in the context of clinical trials, which may not accurately reflect everyday practice. Therefore, generating “real-world” evidence (RWE) is necessary in order to confirm the efficacy and safety of modern treatments in this setting. Nadal et al studied seventy patients who received axitinib, pazopanib, sunitinib or sorafenib after immunotherapy and found that anti-VEGF TKIs remains effective and safe after PD-1 inhibition resulting in 28% ORR and 6.4 months median PFS.13

Cabozantinib is a multi-targeted TKI that targets a range of receptor kinases involved in tumor pathogenesis, including AXL, MET, and VEGF receptors.14 It is proposed by international guidelines as a valid option after failure of current 1st-line standards.15 It can also be offered as 1st-line therapy in patients who are not candidates for immunotherapy.16 Nevertheless, real-world data on its efficacy and safety in this setting is largely anecdotal. Until now, relevant reports are limited to mixed TKI populations, with only few patients receiving cabozantinib. The generation of such data in Greek patients is, therefore, particularly relevant and could serve as a benchmark for future larger “real-world” series or clinical trials. We, therefore, designed a retrospective study (CARE) aiming to collect data on the efficacy and tolerability of cabozantinib in mRCC, after progression on 1st-line ICI-containing therapy.

Patients and Methods

Patients and Study Objectives

This was a retrospective, non-interventional, multicenter study conducted in three Oncology referral centers in Attika Area: ATTIKON University Hospital, Chaidari; ALEXANDRA Hospital, Athens; LAIKON Hospital, Athens. The study was conducted according to the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines (GCP) of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to starting the study, ethical approval of study protocol was obtained from the local institutional review board (IRBs) of the participating centers. Respective reference numbers of approvals’ decisions are: ATTIKON Hospital IRB meeting no. 1/11-1-2022, ALEXANDRA Hospital IRB meeting no. 8/23-12-2021, LAIKON Hospital IRB meeting no. 12/21-11-2021. Subjects provided written informed consent for the use of their medical data. For deceased patients a waiver of consent was granted by the institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of the participating centers.

To be included in the study patients should have: 1. Histologically or cytologically confirmed diagnosis of RCC; 2. Measurable locally advanced or metastatic disease; 3. Treatment with cabozantinib monotherapy for mRCC previously treated with at least one line of anti-PD1 or anti-PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor. Combinations of anti-PD1 or anti-PD-L1 agents with other agents were allowed. Cabozantinib could have been administered at any line after immunotherapy; 4. Baseline and at least one tumor evaluation during therapy with cabozantinib should be available unless death due to disease occurred before first tumor evaluation. Patients who received cabozantinib in first-line were excluded.

Data collection started in January 2022 and study database was closed in January 2024. Data were retrieved from patient medical records and were entered anonymously in the study database. All histology and laboratory results were retrieved retrospectively from patients’ visits performed in the context of their treatment or routine follow up. No biological samples were obtained for the purposes of this study.

Primary objective was to study the efficacy of cabozantinib in mRCC Greek patients who were previously treated with ICIs. Secondary objectives were to study the safety of cabozantinib in mRCC patients who were previously treated with ICIs. Exploratory analyses included the efficacy of cabozantinib in mRCC patients who were previously treated with ICIs according to the timing of ICI treatment and the identification of predictive factors for efficacy of cabozantinib in this real-world setting.

Statistical Analysis and Sample Calculation

Primary outcome was investigator-assessed objective response rate (ORR), while secondary outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS) and safety. Exploratory analyses included efficacy outcomes stratified by 1st-line agent and by the timing of cabozantinib therapy (2nd-line vs subsequent lines).

Continuous data were summarized using descriptive statistics, e.g median and interquartile range or mean and standard deviation, as appropriate. Categorical data would be summarized using frequency counts and percentages. Standard tests would be used to check univariate associations between categorical and categorical (Fisher’s exact tests or chi-squared tests) or categorical and continuous (Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test, t-test) variables. For continuous variables categorized versions were also explored.

Responses to cabozantinib were assessed according to RECIST v1.1 criteria in all three centers participating in the study. PFS and OS were calculated from the time of cabozantinib initiation. Patients without an event at the time of analysis were censored at the date of last known follow‑up. Patients who died without documented progression were censored at the date of death, unless death was considered as progression‑related, in which case it was counted as a PFS event. For time-to-event endpoints, Kaplan–Meier estimates were used to describe and visualize the effect of categorical variables. Log rank tests were used to explore the prognostic value of binary variables on clinical outcomes, while Cox models were used to explore the prognostic value of covariables. All statistical tests would be two-sided and would be performed at a 0.05 significance level.

At the time of study design, no data to base a reliable statistical hypothesis for sample size calculation existed. We, therefore, based our assumptions on the only paper reporting a 36% ORR for cabozantinib after previous ICI (16 Graham J et al, 2021) and on the METEOR trial (17 Choueiri TK et al, 2016), which reported a 21% ORR after TKI. Using Non-Inferiority Tests for One Proportion, we estimated that a sample size of 57 patients would achieve 80% power to detect a minimum proportion (P0) of 0.25 using a one-sided exact test with a significance level (alpha) of 0.05. These calculations assumed that the actual proportion (P1) was 0.40. Data collection was anticipated for 12 months. Due to slow enrollment, initially an extension to 18 months was applied. At the end of this period only 30 patients had been enrolled and study enrollment was suspended. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata. (StataCorp. 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

Thirty patients from three Oncology centers were included. Their baseline characteristics at the time of cabozantinib initiation are shown in Table 1. Two patients received adjuvant treatment following nephrectomy: pembrolizumab (n=1) and pazopanib (n=1). Prior Immunotherapy consisted mainly of nivolumab monotherapy (n=11, 36.7%) or nivolumab combined with ipilimumab (n=11, 37.9%). Most patients (n=17, 56.7%) received cabozantinib as 2nd line treatment for metastatic disease following 1st line ICI/ICI (n=11), ICI/TKI (n=4) or ICI monotherapy (n=2). Efficacy Analyses

No complete responses to cabozantinib treatment were reported. Twelve patients achieved partial response (PR) as best tumor response, resulting in ORR of 40% (95% CI: 22.7–59.4). Remaining best responses were: stable disease (SD) in 17 cases (56.7%) and progressive disease (PD) in 1 case (3.3%).

Median follow up after initiation of cabozantinib was 20 months (95% CI 15.2–24.6). During follow up 7 patients died, all due to mRCC progression. PFS and OS estimations are shown in Table 2. Median PFS was 21 months (95% CI: 12.5-NR). Median OS was 29.7 months (95% CI: 25.5-NR). 1-year and 2-year PFS rates were 72% (95% CI: 51.7–84.9) and 33.2% (95% CI: 10.9–57.7), respectively. 1-year and 2-year OS rates were 89.2% (95% CI: 70.1–96.4) and 77.9% (95% CI: 52.8–90.7), respectively (Figure 1A and B).

Prior immunotherapy was stratified by prior exposure to TKI and by combination (ICI/ICI or ICI/TKI) vs ICI monotherapy (Table 3). Prior therapy with TKI had been administered to 15 patients. Prior TKI included sunitinib, pazopanib and axitinib. ORR did not differ between the two groups, but median PFS was shorter among patients with prior TKI exposure, with a borderline statistically significant difference (p=0.045) (Table 3 and Figure 2A). OS appeared unaffected by prior TKI exposure. (Figure 2B). In contrast, prior combination therapy was not correlated with ORR (p=0.98), PFS (p=0.18) or OS (p=0.80). Timing of cabozantinib therapy also did not affect efficacy of this agent. There was no association of ORR, PFS and OS with line of therapy for metastatic disease which cabozantinib was used for (p=0.47, p=0.22 and p=0.55, respectively, for 1–2 vs >2 lines of previous therapy) (Supplementary Figures 1A and 1B). Similar results were also observed for analyses according to PFS after previous immunotherapy (Table 3).

Characteristics of Cabozantinib Treatment

Median exposure to cabozantinib was 10 months (IQR: 4.8–15.4), while mean exposure was 10.2 months (SD: 6.7). Cabozantinib was started at full dose (60 mg daily) in 19 patients (63.3%), while starting dose was at 40 mg in 6 patients (20%). There was no association between ORR and starting dose (p=0.63).

Toxicity

Reported toxicities are shown in Table 4. The most frequent AEs were fatigue (11 patients, 36.7%), diarrhea (8 patients, 26.7%), hepatic (5 patients, 16.7%) and anemia (4 patients, 13.3%). Nine cases of grades III or IV toxicities were reported in 8 patients (26.7%): hepatic, diarrhea, fatigue (n=2), fatigue (n=1) mucositis (n=2), renal (hyponatremia) and skin dryness.

Information on treatment interruption, dose reduction and premature discontinuation was available for 25 patients. Treatment interruption due to toxicity was reported in 16 patients (64%): fatigue (n=4), diarrhea (n=2), stomatitis (n=2), skin toxicity (n=2), mucositis (n=1), mucositis+liver toxicity (n=1), proteinuria (n=1), thrombocytopenia (n=1), postular hypotension (n=1), acoustic hallucinations (n=1). In all these cases toxicity resulted in dose reductions. Premature discontinuation of cabozantinib was reported in 6 patients (24%): fatigue was the reason in 3 cases and diarrhea, gastrointestinal bleeding and transient ischaemic attack in one case each.

Discussion

Cabozantinib is recommended for the treatment of mRCC following 1st-line ICI/ICI or ICI/TKI. This recommendation comes with Level of Evidence II. Although two randomized trials used cabozantinib in this setting, they both investigated the potential benefit of adding another agent to cabozantinib. CONTACT 03,17 was designed to address the question of ICI rechallenging after IO failure. In CONTACT 03 patients were randomized to cabozantinib vs cabozantinib/atezolizumab. No difference in efficacy was found, while cabozantinib monotherapy was associated with an impressive ORR (40.9%) and median PFS (10.8 months). OS data have not been reported yet. In the CANTATA trial,18 telaglenastat, a selective, oral glutaminase inhibitor was added to cabozantinib. Preclinically, telaglenastat synergized with cabozantinib in RCC models.19 No benefit by telaglenastat was found, while Cabozantinib plus placebo produced RR of 28% and median PFS and OS 9.3 and 24.8 months, respectively. Importantly, 38% of enrolled patients had not received IO prior to cabozantinib. In a subanalysis, patients who received placebo+cabozantinib after ipilimumab/nivolumab and no prior anti-angiogenic agents achieved 48% ORR and median PFS 10.9 months. Taking into consideration the lack of level I evidence to support the use of cabozantinib in 2nd-line, RWE are necessary to confirm efficacy and safety of this agent in everyday practice.

Few real-world studies investigating cabozantinib in patients who received the current standards in 1st-line (ICI/ICI or ICI/TKI) exist (Table 5).20–24 All studies confirm the feasibility of cabozantinib as the standard treatment of mRCC in 2nd-line. We reported a toxicity profile similar to that observed in previous studies. Some toxicities, such as diarrhea and skin toxicity, were more frequently observed in other RW studies.20,21 A degree of underreporting cannot be excluded, since adverse events documented through chart review may underestimate the true incidence of lower-grade toxicities. Racial differences in the tolerability of the drug could also account for the lower frequency of side effects in our population. Nevertheless, the rate of treatment interruption, dose reduction and permanent discontinuation due to adverse events was similar to that of previous RW studies or clinical trials.20,21,23,25,26

Regarding efficacy, ORR and OS have been the main endpoints in previous RW studies, while PFS has been less frequently reported. ORR were similar across all studies and also similar to those reported in CONTACT-03. Median OS ranged from 13 to our 29.6 months, while Ishihara also reported a similar to ours median OS of 25.5 months. Differences in the characteristics of patients, timing of cabozantinib treatment, type of prior therapies and availability of subsequent therapeutic options could account for the variability in OS observed across these different studies. It is interesting that similar median OS to ours and Ishihara’s was reported in clinical trials studying cabozantinib after ICI: 26.8 months in a subanalysis of the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial among 60 patients who received cabozantinib after 1st-line avelumab/axitinib combination27 and 24.1 in the Phase II CaboPoint trial.26 We reported a median PFS of 21 months, whereas 4 of the other 5 RW studies reported respective medians up to 10 months.20,22,23 The reason for this discrepancy is unclear, but our unusually long PFS should be viewed with caution due to our small sample size resulting in a very broad 95% CI and a lower value at 12.5 months. Factors which were suggested to explain the differences in OS are also plausible for the differences in PFS. It is also interesting that such variability in PFS has also been observed across clinical trials. A median PFS of 10.8 months was reported in CONTACT-03, while a median of 18.2 months was reported among 60 patients who received cabozantinib after 1st-line avelumab/axitinib in the JAVELIN Renal 101 study.27

Since many agents are recommended for treatment of mRCC following ICI, it would be useful to identify factors to select patients for each different option. We found that prior treatment with TKI was associated with reduced PFS. Nadal et al,13 also found lower ORR in patients who previously received IO/TKI, while respective median PFS was numerically but not statistically shorter, in a study which included various VEGFR-TKIs after ICI. Other RW studies have found similar results, albeit mainly in reference to ORR,27 while no association with prior treatment has also been reported.22 Although these findings resulted from exploratory analyses, they are intuitive. Cross resistance between TKIs jas long been described in preclinical models,28 albeit such evidence for novel TKIs is lacking. Regarding their clinical relevance, these data can only be viewed as question generating and should be confirmed in properly designed prospective trials.

Our study has several limitations, mainly related to its retrospective nature and the small sample size. As a consequence, our results may have been affected by selection bias and nonstandardized response assessment, while the power of the primary endpoint and subgroup analyses is substantially limited. In addition, missing data (although minimal) also exist, which may bias study results if the data are not missing completely at random. In spite of these limitations, this is the first report on post IO cabozantinib in Greek patients with mRCC with a long follow up and we believe that it is a useful RWD contribution in this setting. Our study adds to the existing RW evidence that cabozantinib is a safe and effective option following failure of prior IO. The median OS in excess of 2 years reported by us and others underline the significant progress in systemic therapy of mRCC and the development of many effective options in this disease.

Finally, our results should be viewed in the context of a landscape which is constantly evolving. Recent data question the position of cabozantinib in the treatment sequencing in metastatic renal cell carcinoma. In a recent randomized phase II study (LenCabo), the combination of lenvatinib/everolimus showed to prolong PFS after 1st-line immunotherapy compared to cabozantinib.29 In a single-arm phase II study, the combination of Cabozantinib with the HIF-2a inhibitor Belzutifan, showed promising efficacy in patients treated with IO/IO or IO/TKI combination in 1st-line.30 Furthermore, ongoing studies explore the combinations of Belzutifan with other TKIs, such as Lenvatinib, in patients progressing after 1st-line standard combinations.31 For these reasons RWD reported here and by other investigators, showing efficacy of cabozantinib in later lines, support the notion that cabozantinib remains a valid option for relapsed mRCC. Further refinement could be achieved by the use of prospectively validated biomarkers. This area has not yet been exploited in mRCC as in other cancers. Recent data on KIM-1 and ct DNA suggest that personalized sequencing of availble options may become possible for patients with mRCC.32
