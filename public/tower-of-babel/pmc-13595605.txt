Overcoming the limitation: energy-gap tailoring of thermally coupled levels from Er3+ in fiber microspheres for optical thermometry over 213–823 K
Authors: Xuanhao Zhang, Guichen Bao, Shuai Hu, Jinshu Huang, Chao Lin, Xin Guo, Maohui Yuan, Kai Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of a lanthanide-based ratiometric optical thermometer is a highly promising non-invasive method for detecting temperature. However, due to inherent bandgap limitations, the optimal temperature measurement range for specific elements is restricted to a specific range. In this work, we have developed a novel optical fiber microsphere fabricated by doping an Er3+/Yb3+ solution into an SiO2 optical fiber. With this microcavity, stable emissions corresponding to small, medium and large energy gaps between thermally coupled levels resulting from the splitting of the Er3+ energy levels (2H11/2 and 4S3/2) have been achieved. Owing to these tailored energy gaps, the effective temperature sensing range based on the fluorescence intensity ratio of Er3+ has been successfully extended from 295–503 K to 213–823 K. In terms of relative sensitivity (Sr), a maximum value of 1.71% K−1 was obtained at 213 K for the small energy gap, while a considerable sensitivity of 0.20% K−1 was maintained even at 823 K for the large energy gap. The temperature uncertainty (δT) of the thermometer remains below 0.35 K within the commonly employed temperature measurement range of 200–500 K. Given its wide temperature measurement range, high relative sensitivity, and low temperature uncertainty, the easily fabricated Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped microsphere exhibits outstanding temperature sensing performance, showing great potential for applications in microscale thermometry.

Introduction

As a fundamental physical quantity, temperature critically influences a wide array of technological processes and fields of scientific research, driving the need for its precise measurement. To achieve this at the micro- and even nanoscales, considerable efforts have been devoted to developing ratiometric luminescent thermometry.1–8 Among various approaches, the use of lanthanide-doped upconversion materials has emerged as a prominent strategy due to their high photochemical stability,9–11 low toxicity,12–14 diverse working mechanisms,15–17 and excellent thermometric performance.18–20 Consequently, ratiometric luminescent thermometers based on lanthanide ions have found broad applications in the fields of catalytic reactions,21,22 remote temperature monitoring,23,24 integrated electronics and optoelectronics,25–27in situ analysis of battery performance,16 biomedical diagnosis,28–32 and wearable health monitoring systems.33

In recent years, numerous material designs have been explored to enhance the performance of these thermometers. Notable examples include two-dimensional nanomaterials,34,35 organic–inorganic hybrids,36 silica-shell-encapsulated upconversion nanoclusters,37 hybrid nanocomposites,38 and fluorescent-powder-based polymer optical fibers.16 Among these, ratiometric luminescent fibers are particularly promising. They leverage the flexibility, light-guiding capability, and stability of optical fibers, enabling real-time temperature monitoring inside lithium-ion batteries with an accuracy of 0.12 °C,16 which highlights their potential for use in complex environments. Nevertheless, research on fiber microcavities tailored for specific high- and low-temperature regimes remains limited. It is worth noting that the working principle of most ratiometric luminescent thermometers relies on thermal coupling between two closely spaced thermal energy levels following the Boltzmann distribution. Although this dual-level Boltzmann thermometry approach is straightforward and enables accurate temperature sensing, its effective range is inherently constrained:39 at low temperatures, non-radiative transitions required to populate the higher excited state are hindered; while at high temperatures, the populations of the two excited states tend to equilibrate, leading to diminished relative thermal sensitivity.

To expand the effective temperature measurement range of Boltzmann thermometry, Markus Suta et al. pioneered its expansion to systems with more excitation energy levels.40 They established a quantitative framework that elucidates how the selection of energy gaps between these excited states dictates performance across different temperature regimes. This approach enables the maintenance of high relative sensitivity and measurement accuracy across a wide temperature range within a single system.

As shown in Fig. 1, the two principal emitting energy levels (level 1 and level 2) of a rare-earth ion are split into sublevels, giving rise to four distinct emission peaks and creating six possible energy gaps between them. Three representative gaps are defined as follows: small ΔE corresponds to the gap between sublevel II of level 1 and sublevel I of level 2; medium ΔE to that between sublevel I of level 1 and sublevel I of level 2; and large ΔE to the gap between sublevel I of level 1 and sublevel II of level 2. According to formula (1), these energy gaps dictate the optimal temperature measurement range: small ΔE for lower temperatures, medium ΔE for intermediate temperatures, and large ΔE for higher temperatures, which is consistent with the spectral features shown on the right-hand side of Fig. 1. Consequently, by selectively utilizing specific energy gaps of the same ion, each associated with a unique optimal temperature sub-range, the overall effective measurement range is substantially expanded beyond the limitation of using a single gap. This strategy exploits the multi-gap nature of a single ion, where each gap serves as the basis for thermometry over a specific sub-range, thereby collectively achieving a significant extension to the overall optimal temperature measurement range.

By applying the extended thermometric theory to the classic thermally coupled levels 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 of Er3+ ions, this study achieves a major extension to the temperature measurement range. Through the creation of an Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped fiber microsphere, we obtained thermally stable Stark-split spectra at temperatures up to 873 K. Subsequent analysis of the relative sensitivity and temperature uncertainty for the small, medium and large energy gaps demonstrated outstanding performance, confirming the successful expansion of the optimal range from 295–503 K to 213–823 K.

Experimental section

Materials

All the chemicals used, including Yb(NO3)3·5H2O (99.99%), Er(NO3)3·5H2O (99.99%), polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) and acetone, were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation. None of the chemicals were purified further.

Fabrication of Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped SiO2 fiber microspheres

In this work, we employed a solution doping and laser melting method to fabricate the rare-earth-doped fiber microspheres with various doping schemes. The preparation process is illustrated in Fig. 2. First, a precursor gel was prepared by dissolving specific proportions of erbium nitrate, ytterbium nitrate, and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) in acetone. The mixture was stirred in an 80 °C water bath until a homogeneous, viscous gel was obtained. The molar ratio of Er3+ to Yb3+ was 1 : 2. An appropriate amount of this gel was then applied to a glass-fiber endface. Finally, a doped microsphere was formed by rotating the fiber and melting the gel-coated endface with a CO2 laser. Owing to the size of the laser spot being larger than that of the fiber microsphere, the ions within the microsphere exhibit free motion and tend to distribute uniformly (Fig. S1–S4). Mapping of the elements revealed a relatively homogeneous distribution of Yb and Er elements in the microsphere (Fig. S5). After being subjected to spectral temperature dependency tests, this microsphere can function as a temperature sensor.

Schematic of the fabrication process for Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped SiO2 fiber microspheres via a solution doping and laser melting method. The scale bar is 10 µm.

Upconversion luminescence measurement

For the upconversion luminescence measurement, a 980 nm laser beam was directed into an inverted microscope (BX43, Olympus) and focused onto the microspheres with a 50× objective lens. The upconversion signals emitted from the microspheres were collected by the same objective lens and subsequently transmitted to a spectrometer (SR-500I-B1, Andor) equipped with a charge-coupled device camera (DU970N, Andor).

Temperature-dependent upconversion luminescence measurement

For temperature-dependent upconversion luminescence measurements, the doped microspheres were placed into a temperature controller (HCS621GXY, Instec, resolution of 0.001 K), which operated within a temperature range of 213–823 K, with 25 K increments above 273 K and 10 K increments below 273 K. The spectra were collected at different temperatures to establish the correspondence between temperature and spectral emission ratio. The temperature controller was mounted on the microscope stage, and the focused laser from the objective lens was directed onto the microspheres. The same objective lens collected the PL signals for data analysis. Specifically, by choosing the critical laser power density at which the spectral shape remained invariant with laser power during the process of continuously reducing the laser power to zero, the impact of laser self-heating was effectively eliminated.

Results and discussion

Fig. 3a shows the upconversion spectrum under 980 nm excitation at 323 K, with broad peaks in the green (525 nm, 545 nm) and red (658 nm) regions, each showing a doublet profile. To extract quantifiable signals for ratiometric thermometry, the green emission band was deconvoluted via multi-peak fitting, revealing four constituent peaks at 525 nm, 534 nm, 547 nm, and 558 nm, as shown in Fig. 3b. Notably, different fiber microspheres and different positions for the same microsphere exhibit similar upconversion emission properties (Fig. S6 and S7). The origin of these peaks was elucidated by examining the energy level diagrams (Fig. 3c). The 980 nm pump light is absorbed by Yb3+ ions, which then transfer their energy to Er3+ ions, populating the thermally coupled 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 levels. The four fitted peaks are assigned to radiative transitions from the Stark sub-levels of these two excited states down to the 4I15/2 ground state. Accordingly, this set of four peaks was selected for subsequent temperature measurement. The red emission at ∼658 nm arises from the transition 4F9/2 → 4I15/2. Furthermore, we conducted a study on UC intensity as a function of laser pump power (Fig. S8). The calculated slopes of n were 1.72, 1.74, 1.73 and 1.77, which correspond to the 526 nm, 534 nm, 549 nm and 560 nm UC emissions of Er3+, respectively. This indicates that the 2H11/2 → 4I15/2 and 4S3/2 → 4I15/2 electronic transitions occurred via two-photon processes in the Yb3+/Er3+ co-doped fiber microsphere.

The evolution of the upconversion spectra with temperature ranging from 83 K to 873 K is shown in Fig. 4a. As the temperature increases, a general quenching effect leads to an overall reduction in emission intensity. In particular, the relative intensities of the short-wavelength peaks near 525 nm and 545 nm exhibit a dramatic and systematic shift. The spectrum is initially dominated by the 545 nm peak at low temperature. A crossover occurs around 450 K, where the two peaks are of equal intensity. Beyond this point, the 525 nm peak becomes stronger, and its dominance increases with temperature. This behavior originates from the changing thermal population of the thermally coupled 2H11/2 and 4S3/2 levels of Er3+. Generally, the performance of a luminescent thermometer is quantitatively evaluated by its relative sensitivity (Sr). This parameter allows for a direct comparison of temperature resolution across different materials and sensing schemes. The Sr for the three energy gaps were calculated and are plotted as a function of temperature in Fig. 5a–c. A maximum Sr of 1.71% K−1 was achieved at 213 K for small ΔE, with sensitivity gradually decreasing as the temperature increased across all gaps. Notably, even at 823 K, the temperature sensor maintained a significant Sr of 0.2% K−1, demonstrating its capability of high-temperature detection. Furthermore, the observed variation in Sr within the overlapping temperature regions of the different gaps is consistent with theoretical expectation. As predicted by eqn (3), a larger energy gap yields a higher Sr at a given temperature, which is precisely what our data confirms. Therefore, when multiple sub-ranges overlap, priority should be given to the measurement readout from the larger energy gap.

We demonstrate the application of the fiber microsphere thermometer in everyday scenarios involving both hot and cold environments. As an important material in the fresh and frozen logistics industry, blue ice is widely employed due to its ability to maintain a low temperature of several tens of degrees below zero for extended periods. Meanwhile, in high-temperature scenarios, the heat continuously generated by electronic components during operation—causing elevated temperatures that impact their performance—has also garnered significant attention. In this experiment, common household items such as blue ice and circuit boards were used to confirm the temperature measurement performance of doped fiber microspheres. As shown in Fig. 6a, after placing 200 mL of blue ice in a refrigerator at 263 K for 12 hours, it is taken out and placed on a stage. Then, the Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped fiber microsphere is placed on the surface of the blue ice and excited by a 980 nm laser. Multiple measurements of the upconversion luminescence spectra were performed to calculate and determine the temperature of the blue ice, which is approximately 265 K, as illustrated in Fig. 6b. Furthermore, the unpowered circuit board is placed on the stage, and a temperature probe is positioned on the central non-metallic surface. Once collection begins, the circuit board is powered up and set into operation. The variation of ΔLIR with the operating time of the circuit board is shown in Fig. 6d, displaying an overall increasing trend. At the start of operation, the temperature was 307 K and rose rapidly. After 8 s, the rate of temperature increase slowed. By 32 s, the temperature had reached 326 K. The results illustrate the temperature evolution of a circuit board following power activation. Our findings highlight the potential of the thermometer for applications in both high- and low-temperature environments.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped fiber microspheres were fabricated using a solution doping and laser melting method, and their temperature-dependent upconversion performances were systematically investigated. Utilizing the thermally-coupled Stark splitting levels of Er3+, effectively extended the optimal measurement range from 295–503 K to 213–823 K. Among the small, medium, and large energy gaps studied, the highest Sr reached 1.71% K−1 at 213 K for the small energy gap—surpassing previously reported values. Moreover, it maintained a considerable Sr of 0.20% K−1 even at 823 K, confirming its potential for reliable high-temperature sensing. For the small energy gap of Er3+, the optimal temperature operating range was 213–348 K, with δT below 0.35 K. The medium energy gap provided the best temperature range between 348 K and 548 K, while the large energy gap covered 498–823 K with δT remaining below 3.1 K. These results demonstrate that, by predefining the optimal temperature range for each energy gap, both high Sr and low δT can be achieved simultaneously. This work establishes Er3+/Yb3+ co-doped fiber microspheres as high-performance temperature sensors and provides a key foundation for enhancing micro-scale thermometry based on Er3+ spectral ratio conversion across both low- and high-temperature environments. Furthermore, this energy-gap tailoring strategy can be extended to other host materials and lanthanides, provided that the relevant energy levels satisfy the condition of a Boltzmann distribution.
