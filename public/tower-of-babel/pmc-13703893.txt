Step‐Associated Cu‐Ceria Interfaces Enhance Catalytic Activity and Selectivity
Authors: Shuxuan Feng, Shan Jiang, Chengyu Song, Chaochao Dun, Yang Deng, Hao Xu, Zihao Zhang, Xiong He, Temitayo Ojuetimi Ikuerowo, Mark R. Hoffmann, Jeffrey T. Miller, Yong Wang, Weixin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrecise control of metal–support interactions is essential for understanding and steering catalytic processes. Here, we demonstrate that the surface location of metal nanoparticles (NPs) on oxide supports can strongly influence catalytic performance. Using atom‐trapped Cu catalysts as precursors enabled the formation of Cu NPs with distinct Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments compared with catalysts prepared by impregnation or colloidal methods. These resulting catalysts exhibit enhanced CO2 hydrogenation toward methanol formation by promoting formate activation. Enhanced reactivity arises from these Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments that render the methanol formation pathway thermodynamically more favorable, as revealed by theoretical calculations. These findings provide mechanistic insights into how NP anchoring environments influence catalytic activity and selectivity, offering a rational design strategy for oxide‐supported metal catalysts.

Introduction

Metal–support interaction is a fundamental factor that governs the catalytic behavior of both metal nanoparticles (NPs) and their oxide supports in heterogeneous catalysis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Achieving precise control over these interactions has emerged as an effective approach to tailor catalytic activity and selectivity [6, 7, 8]. Oxide supports, with diverse surface structures, create a heterogeneous environment that profoundly influences the structural and electronic properties of anchored metal NPs [9, 10, 11]. This intrinsic heterogeneity governs the local chemical structure, which affects metal dispersion [12, 13, 14], and the formation of encapsulating oxide layers [15, 16, 17, 18], and ultimately determines reaction pathways, especially in reactions involving the active participation of lattice oxygen [19, 20, 21]. Despite growing recognition of metal–support interactions, fundamental questions remain: how metal NPs can be preferentially anchored at step sites, defect sites, or other undercoordinated regions on oxide surfaces, and whether the resulting distinct interfacial environments affect catalytic performance. Addressing these questions could guide the design of catalysts that leverage the unique properties of these sites in combination with metal NPs.

In this study, we demonstrate that the catalytic performance of copper (Cu) on cerium oxide (CeO2) is highly sensitive to the spatial location of Cu NPs on the support. It has been reported that atom trapping (AT) can anchor isolated metal atoms at undercoordinated CeO2(111) step environments on CeO2 [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Building on this concept, we designed an atom‐trapping strategy to guide Cu NP formation toward these specific environments. Theoretical calculations suggest that atom‐trapped Cu species may provide favorable stabilization environments for subsequent Cu NP formation during reduction treatment. Cu NPs formed by this method exhibit a methanol space‐time yield (STY) about four times higher than that of catalysts prepared by wet impregnation or colloidal methods. The enhanced reactivity arises from distinct Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments, which facilitate formate‐to‐methanol conversion. These findings deepen our understanding of metal–support interactions and offer a general strategy for catalyst design through control of NP anchoring environments.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 Samples

A Cu catalyst with approximately 1 wt% Cu loading was synthesized via the AT method [22, 28] and is referred to as CuATCeO2‐p, where “p” indicates the pristine, unreduced state. Following H2 reduction, which may promote the formation of Cu NPs, the catalyst is designated as CuATCeO2. For comparison, a reference catalyst was prepared by conventional impregnation, labeled Cu/CeO2‐p and Cu/CeO2 before and after H2 reduction.

Cu K‐edge X‐ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra (Figure 1a) show that, relative to Cu and CuO references, the absorption edges of CuATCeO2‐p and Cu/CeO2‐p shift to higher energies, closely matching those of CuO. A Cu2+ pre‐edge peak is observed at ∼8.9775 keV, which is assigned to the dipole‐forbidden 1s to 3d transition. This indicates that the Cu species in both samples exhibit an average Cu valence similar to CuO. The corresponding Cu K‐edge extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra (Figure 1b) display a single dominant peak attributed to the first‐shell Cu–O coordination. Notably, no spectral signatures attributable either to Cu–Cu coordination in metallic Cu foil or to the extended Cu–O–Cu network in CuO are observed. In both samples, there is a small higher shell peak, which fits well with a Cu–O–Ce scattering path, which strongly supports the atomic dispersion of Cu in both catalysts. The corresponding x‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) fitting results are provided in Table S1.

To induce the formation of metallic NPs from single‐atom Cu species, the catalysts were subjected to a H2 reduction treatment. In situ XAS was then conducted immediately after H2 treatment, with the samples protected under Helium atmosphere to avoid changes caused by air exposure (Figure 1c,d). Under these experimental conditions, both catalysts exhibit a substantial shift in XANES edge energies to lower values, closely matching that of a metallic Cu foil reference. Notably, the XANES spectra show slight deviations from the Cu foil reference, consistent with known NP size effects that induce bond contraction and characteristic changes in XANES features, as previously observed in Pt and Au systems [28]. The corresponding EXAFS spectra show the emergence of a distinct Cu–Cu scattering feature at ∼2.50 Å, closely matching the Cu–Cu distance in metallic Cu, providing direct evidence for metallic Cu formation.

Notably, unlike the above in situ characterization, ex situ analyses did not confirm the extensive formation of metallic Cu NPs (Figure S1). Instead, XANES indicates that a significant fraction of the Cu centers remains in an oxidized state (Figure S1a). This can be inferred from the Cu K‐edge near‐edge structure, which exhibits spectral features characteristic of Cu(II) species. A dominant Cu–O scattering peak in the extended fine structure further supports the persistence of oxidized Cu environments (Figure S1b). These results indicate that metallic Cu formed during H2 treatment is readily reoxidized upon air exposure, causing the ex situ measurements to reflect an oxidized CuO‐like state rather than the intrinsic reduced structures present immediately after reduction.

CO2 Hydrogenation

The catalytic performance of CO2 hydrogenation to methanol was systematically evaluated for Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 catalysts within a temperature range of 180°C–250°C and under a pressure of 3 MPa. As shown in Figure 2a, the CuATCeO2 catalyst exhibits notably higher methanol selectivity and space‐time yield (STY) than the Cu/CeO2 catalyst throughout the temperature range. At 200°C, the methanol STY over CuATCeO2 was nearly four times greater than that of Cu/CeO2. Moreover, Figure 2b illustrates methanol selectivity as a function of CO2 conversion for both catalysts, revealing that CuATCeO2 consistently maintains higher selectivity at comparable conversion levels. Kinetic analysis further demonstrated that the apparent activation energy for methanol formation over CuATCeO2 was 73.0 kJ mol−1, significantly lower than the 86.8 kJ mol−1 measured for Cu/CeO2 (Figure 2c). These differences in catalytic behavior suggest that Cu NPs may occupy distinct surface sites on CeO2, which critically influence methanol production. Beyond its enhanced activity, CuATCeO2 also displays excellent stability, retaining ∼90% of its methanol STY after approximately 100 h of continuous reaction (Figure 2d).

Reaction Mechanisms

To elucidate the reaction intermediates and underlying mechanism, in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted at 200°C and 3 MPa. Vibrational band assignments are detailed in Table S2. Upon exposure to a CO2–H2 mixture, both CuATCeO2 and Cu/CeO2 catalysts exhibited characteristic absorption bands assigned to formate species (HCOO*, 1330, 1380, 1550, 1587, and 2851 cm−1) and methoxy species (CH3O*, 2928 cm−1) [29, 30, 31, 32], as shown in Figures 3a,b, S2, and S3. The most notable difference lies in the significantly stronger methoxy signal on CuATCeO2. To capture the dynamic evolution of intermediates, the intensity ratio of methoxy to formate bands (CH3O*/HCOO*) was monitored over time (Figure 3c). This ratio consistently remained higher on CuATCeO2, indicating faster formate consumption coupled with accelerated methoxy formation. These differences in intermediate evolution reflect variations in the local environment of Cu NPs, which modulate the binding affinity and reactivity of surface species.

Following in situ DRIFTS measurements of CO2 hydrogenation, the catalysts were exposed to an H2/Ar atmosphere to assess the reactivity of surface‐adsorbed intermediates. As shown in Figure 3e, after the CO2 feed was stopped, the HCOO* signal on CuATCeO2 initially increased rather than decreased, which is attributed to residual CO2 remaining in the high‐pressure reactor and continuing to react on the surface. As this residual CO2 was gradually consumed, the intensities of both the HCOO* and CH3O* bands subsequently decreased, indicating their stepwise conversion from HCOO* to CH3O* and ultimately to methanol. In contrast, the HCOO* band on the Cu/CeO2 catalyst initially increased and then stabilized at a steady‐state intensity with no discernible decrease following CO2 removal, and the CH3O* species exhibited a similar trend but with a substantially smaller change in intensity (Figure 3d). These observations were further supported by the temporal intensity profiles of HCOO* and CH3O* (Figures 3f and S4). On CuATCeO2, the HCOO* signal (red curve) began to decline sharply after 170 min (Figure 3f), reflecting conversion to CH3O* intermediates. This step is recognized as the rate‐limiting process in CO2‐to‐methanol conversion [33, 34]. In contrast, on Cu/CeO2, the HCOO* signal (blue curve) showed no decline over the same period, indicating slower conversion to CH3O* under identical H2 exposure. A similar trend was observed for the CH3O* signal (Figure S4). On CuATCeO2, CH3O* decreases substantially over time, consistent with rapid conversion to methanol, whereas on Cu/CeO2, the signal declined only slightly, indicative of slower methanol formation. Overall, these results show that CuATCeO2 enables more efficient turnover of HCOO* and CH3O* intermediates, thereby accelerating the overall CO2‐to‐methanol conversion.

Several factors may explain the observed differences in catalytic activity between the CeO2‐supported Cu NP catalysts. Among these, surface oxygen vacancies are widely recognized as a key influence on CO2 adsorption and activation, as well as the stabilization of reaction intermediates. To examine whether variations in oxygen‐vacancy concentration contribute to the observed reactivity, we quantified the surface defect content using XPS, as shown in Figure 4a. Ce 3d spectra show similar surface Ce3+ fractions for Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 (23.6% vs. 25.5%), but CuATCeO2 exhibits an STY nearly four times higher. This disconnect indicates that the activity enhancement is not governed by differences in Ce3+ abundance. This finding is further supported by Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 4b, both catalysts display a prominent F 2g vibrational mode characteristic of fluorite‐structured CeO2 at approximately 460 cm−1. Additionally, the broad Raman band centered around 600 cm−1, commonly associated with oxygen‐vacancy‐related defects in CeO2, shows nearly identical intensity in both samples. The consistent spectral features observed in XPS and Raman analyses indicate that the atomic trapping synthesis does not significantly alter the concentration of surface oxygen vacancies.

The size of the metal NPs is a critical factor governing catalytic performance, as variations in particle size change both the fraction of reactive surface sites and the coordination environment of surface atoms, thereby modulating chemical reactivity [35, 36]. In this work, Cu NP sizes were estimated from coordination numbers derived by EXAFS analysis. As shown in Figure 4c, the Fourier transforms (FT) of the k 2‐weighted Cu K‐edge EXAFS spectra show clear distinctions between CuATCeO2 and bulk Cu foil. The supported catalysts display markedly lower FT magnitudes than metallic Cu, consistent with the presence of small Cu NPs. In addition, the imaginary components of the FT for both catalysts shift to lower R relative to the Cu foil, signifying contracted Cu‐Cu bond distances, a feature commonly associated with nanoscale Cu clusters [28]. A similar trend is evident in the k‐space spectra, where the oscillations show reduced amplitude and altered periodicity relative to Cu foil (Figure S5).

Quantitative EXAFS analysis further reveals differences in the local atomic environments of the reduced catalysts. As shown in Table S1, the CuATCeO2 catalyst shows an average Cu‐Cu coordination number (CN) of ∼4.5 at 2.48 Å, while the conventional Cu/CeO2 catalyst exhibits a higher CN of ∼6.7 at 2.52 Å. For comparison, bulk metallic Cu has a CN of 12 at a Cu–Cu distance of 2.55 Å, indicating that both catalysts contain much smaller Cu domains characterized by a high fraction of under‐coordinated surface atoms. These reduced coordination numbers and contracted Cu‐Cu bond distances confirm the formation of small metallic Cu NPs with a large proportion of surface atoms, in agreement with the size‐dependent spectral features observed in XANES. The Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 catalysts exhibit average Cu–Cu coordination numbers of approximately 6.7 and 4.5, corresponding to mean particle diameters close to 2 and 1 nm, respectively. This pronounced size difference raises the question of whether the exceptionally small Cu NPs on CuATCeO2 drive its enhanced catalytic activity.

To evaluate this point, CuATCeO2 and conventional Cu/CeO2 catalysts with similar Cu NP sizes were examined. A CuATCeO2 sample with an increased Cu loading of 1.5 wt% was synthesized, denoted 1.5CuATCeO2, in which the higher loading is expected to favor the formation of larger Cu NPs. As shown in Figure 4c, the FT of the k 2‐weighted Cu K‐edge EXAFS spectra show that increasing the Cu loading from 1.0 to 1.5 wt% leads to higher Fourier transform magnitudes, indicating larger Cu NP sizes at higher metal loadings. EXAFS and XANES analyses confirmed that the electronic states and local coordination environments of Cu atoms in 1.5CuATCeO2 remained consistent with those of CuATCeO2, both before and after H2 reduction (Figure S6). In contrast, the 1.5CuAT CeO2 sample exhibited an increased average number of Cu–Cu bonds (7.4 at 2.52 Å), indicative of larger Cu NPs approximately 3 nm in size. Catalytic testing demonstrated that despite the larger NP size in 1.5CuATCeO2, its catalytic activity surpassed that of the smaller 2 nm Cu NPs in Cu/CeO2 (Figure S7). This observation challenges the prevailing assumption that smaller NPs intrinsically lead to higher catalytic activity.

To further probe the influence of particle size, Cu NPs with a uniform diameter of ∼3.1 nm were synthesized via a colloidal method (Figure S8) and subsequently deposited onto the CeO2 surface, yielding the Cu NPs/CeO2 catalyst. Despite having a comparable NP size to those in 1.5CuATCeO2 (∼3 nm), the Cu NPs/CeO2 catalyst exhibited significantly lower activity than 1.5CuATCeO2 under identical reaction conditions (Figures 4d and S9). This comparative analysis demonstrates that particle size does not account for the enhanced performance of CuATCeO2.

With the above factors ruled out, the enhanced catalytic reactivity in CuATCeO2 likely originates from alternative causes. In this context, the metal–support interface appears to be the most plausible contributor. Previous studies have shown that catalysts prepared via the AT method preferentially stabilize isolated metal species at undercoordinated CeO2(111) step‐associated environments [8, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. In atom‐trapped Pt‐CeO2 systems, electron microscopy observations revealed atomically dispersed Pt species associated with ceria step‐edge structures [23, 39]. The formation of these structures was proposed to arise from interactions between mobile Pt–O species and undercoordinated Ce3+‐associated surface environments, leading to stabilization of interfacial Pt–O–Ce motifs during AT process [23]. Similar observations have also been reported for atom‐trapped Cu‐CeO2 systems, in which step‐associated trapping structures for isolated single‐atom Cu species were proposed based on the agreement between local coordination environments obtained from XAS and computational step‐defect structural models [42]. The polyhedral CeO2 support used in the present study was synthesized following procedures reported in previous atom‐trapping studies and is known to predominantly expose CeO2(111) facets together with surface step structures [22, 24, 26, 42]. By analogy with previous atom‐trapping studies, we propose that undercoordinated Ce3+‐associated environments may stabilize atomically dispersed Cu through Cu–O–Ce interactions and influence subsequent Cu NPs formation.

To test this hypothesis, DFT calculations were performed to compare the migration of a single Cu atom toward two types of Cu3 clusters: clusters anchored at step sites and clusters located on terrace sites. This comparison was designed to determine whether isolated Cu atoms preferentially migrate and aggregate at step‐bound Cu3 clusters rather than at terrace‐bound Cu3 clusters. As illustrated in Figure 4e, the computed migration energy barrier is lower for migration toward the step‐site Cu3 cluster (ΔE ‡ = 1.34 eV) than for migration toward the terrace‐site Cu3 cluster (ΔE ‡ = 2.87 eV), indicating that step sites facilitate Cu aggregation by reducing the kinetic barrier. Consistent with this trend, the step‐site pathway is also thermodynamically more favorable, with a smaller reaction energy (ΔE = 0.55 eV) compared with the terrace‐site pathway (ΔE = 0.98 eV). These results suggest that step sites serve as preferential trapping and nucleation centers, where a subset of Cu species is captured and initiates early‐stage cluster growth during reduction.

Atomic‐resolution HAADF‐STEM provides direct structural evidence for Cu‐rich nanoscale features associated with CeO2 surface steps in CuATCeO2 (Figure 5). The ceria particles predominantly expose (111) facets containing surface steps, highlighted by the red dashed lines in Figure 5a,b. Distinct low‐contrast nanoscale features are observed along the CeO2 surface‐step regions and can be distinguished from the surrounding ceria lattice. The corresponding EELS elemental maps show spatially coincident Cu signals, identifying these features as Cu‐rich regions. The projected thickness of the Cu‐rich features normal to the CeO2 surface is approximately 1 nm, consistent with the nanoscale Cu domains inferred from XAS. Similar step‐associated Cu‐rich features are consistently observed in additional CuATCeO2 images (Figure S10). In contrast, such Cu features are not observed in most examined regions of Cu/CeO2, with only sparse Cu species occasionally observed near CeO2 step edges (Figures S11 and S12). These observations support a preferential association of Cu species with step‐related environments in CuATCeO2 (Figure 6a). This structural assignment is consistent with the calculated energetics, which favor Cu aggregation at step‐associated sites (Figure 4e). The resulting step‐associated Cu–CeO2 interfaces therefore likely contribute substantially to the enhanced catalytic activity.

To further probe the local environments of Cu species in CuATCeO2 and Cu/CeO2, CO‐DRIFTS measurements were performed. As shown in Figure S13, although the CO‐DRIFTS spectra display similar CO adsorption peak positions for the two samples, their CO desorption behaviors differ markedly. After reaching saturated CO adsorption, the gas atmosphere was switched from CO to Ar, and the evolution of the CO–Cu bands was monitored over time. Following the Ar purge, the CO–Cu band intensity decreased substantially faster on Cu/CeO2 than on CuATCeO2. To quantitatively compare the CO desorption kinetics, the integrated CO–Cu peak intensities were plotted as a function of time, and the corresponding decay rates were extracted. As shown in Figure S13c,d, the CO–Cu band intensity decreases approximately three times more slowly on CuATCeO2 than on Cu/CeO2, indicating substantially stronger CO binding on CuATCeO2. These results provide evidence for distinct local Cu environments in the two samples.

To examine the local environments associated with Cu stabilization in CuATCeO2, DFT calculations of CO adsorption energies were performed on representative CeO2 surface models, including CeO2(111) terrace sites and low‐coordinated CeO2(111) step sites. The CeO2(111) terrace model was selected based on high‐resolution STEM analysis (Figures S14 and S15, Table S3), which revealed that CeO2(111) is the dominant exposed facet in both samples (∼65%–87%), while CeO2(110) and CeO2(100) are present in lower fractions. Because CeO2(111) is the dominant exposed surface in both samples, low‐coordinated CeO2(111) step environments were further considered as representative local coordination environments in the DFT calculations. The calculated Gibbs free energies for CO adsorption follow the order: Cu/CeO2(111) terrace < Cu/CeO2(111) step (Figure S16), indicating substantially stronger CO binding at low‐coordinated CeO2(111) step environments than at terrace environments. The markedly slower CO desorption observed experimentally for CuATCeO2 is consistent with stronger CO binding at step‐associated environments. Collectively, these experimental and computational results support that Cu species in CuATCeO2 are preferentially stabilized at low‐coordinated CeO2(111) step‐associated environments.

To elucidate the role of metal–support interfaces in CO2 hydrogenation, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using representative Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 interfacial models. The analysis focuses exclusively on the formate pathway, CO2* → HCOO* → HCOOH* → CH2O* → CH3O* → CH3OH*, which is widely regarded as the primary route for methanol synthesis during CO2 hydrogenation [43, 44]. Computed energy profiles and reaction pathways are presented in Figures 6b and S17–S20, with corresponding reaction energies summarized in Table S4. The Cu13 cluster model employed in this work represents a simplified structural model intended to capture the essential local Cu–CeO2 interfacial interactions. Similar Cu cluster models have been employed in prior DFT studies of supported Cu catalysts [45, 46, 47, 48]. Although the experimentally observed Cu NPs are larger (∼1–3 nm), the present calculations primarily focus on comparing the relative thermodynamic trends associated with different Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments. The interfacial structures considered here are representative computational models used to compare relative energetic trends.

Comparison of the catalysts indicates that CuATCeO2 provides a more favorable thermodynamic pathway for methanol formation. The free energy diagram suggests that the initial CO2 adsorption and activation steps are similar on both catalysts, as reflected by the small difference in CO2 adsorption energy and the modest stabilization of HCOO* on CuATCeO2. The major distinction instead appears in the subsequent transformation of HCOOH*‐derived intermediates to CH3OH*. On CuATCeO2, the reaction pathway following HCOOH* formation is thermodynamically more favorable, with lower free energies observed for the CH2O*, CH3O*, and CH3OH* intermediates. These results indicate that the primary role of CuATCeO2 is to facilitate the subsequent conversion of oxygenated intermediates and promote methanol formation through a more favorable reduction pathway.

To further evaluate whether the modeled Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments are representative of experimentally relevant structures, additional DFT calculations were performed on CeO2(110) and CeO2(100) surfaces under both stoichiometric and oxygen‐deficient conditions (Figures S22 and S23). Although the reaction energetics vary across different surface structures, similar energetic trends were observed, with low‐coordinated CeO2(111) steps exhibiting the most favorable thermodynamics for methanol formation among the examined models. CeO2(110) surfaces also exhibited competitive energetics under stoichiometric conditions; however, STEM analysis showed that the exposed fraction of CeO2(110) facets remained relatively low after H2 reduction in both samples (4%–9%). These results support the relevance of step‐associated Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments in CuATCeO2.

As discussed above, the two catalysts contained oxygen vacancies at comparable levels; however, oxygen vacancies may still modulate the reaction energetics and pathways. Our previous work demonstrated that oxygen vacancies on reducible oxides such as TiO2 and In2O3 promote CO2 activation and enhance methanol selectivity [21]. To extend this understanding to CeO2‐supported systems, Gibbs free‐energy profiles were further calculated using representative oxygen‐deficient Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 interfacial models (Figures 6b, S24, and S25). As shown in Figure 6b, Introducing oxygen vacancies lowers the Gibbs free energies of several intermediates along the CO2‐to‐methanol pathway, including CO2*, HCOO*, CH2O*, and CH3O*, indicating that oxygen vacancies thermodynamically facilitate methanol formation through stabilization of adsorbed intermediates. Similar energetic trends are observed for both terrace‐associated and step‐associated Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments (Figures S24 and S25), although the step‐associated models generally remained more favorable for methanol formation. To further examine the electronic nature of oxygen vacancies, projected density of states (PDOS) analyses were performed on pristine and oxygen‐deficient Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2 models with adsorbed HCOOH* intermediates (Figures S26 and S27). The PDOS analyses reveal that oxygen‐vacancy formation shifts the Ce 4f states closer to the Fermi level, indicating increased Ce3+ character and enhanced surface reducibility. The step‐associated CuATCeO2 models also exhibit stronger energetic overlap between Ce 4f states and the O 2p states of adsorbed HCOOH* near the Fermi level, suggesting enhanced electronic coupling relevant to methanol formation. However, because the experimentally measured oxygen‐vacancy concentrations are comparable for Cu/CeO2 and CuATCeO2, differences in oxygen‐vacancy concentration are unlikely to represent the dominant origin of the enhanced methanol formation observed for CuATCeO2. Collectively, these results suggest that both oxygen vacancies and distinct Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments contribute to the reaction thermodynamics relevant to methanol formation.

Conclusion

In this work, we synthesized a series of Cu NPs supported on CeO2 and evaluated their performance in CO2 hydrogenation to methanol. Among these catalysts, CuATCeO2 exhibited markedly enhanced activity compared to conventional Cu/CeO2 and colloidal Cu NP counterparts, achieving higher methanol selectivity and STY. In situ DRIFTS measurements revealed that CuATCeO2 promoted the conversion of critical reaction intermediates, specifically facilitating the transformation of adsorbed formate (HCOO*) toward methoxy species (CH3O*). This conversion was less favorable on the Cu/CeO2 catalyst. Combined experimental and computational analyses attributed the catalytic differences to distinct Cu–CeO2 interfacial environments associated with atom‐trapping‐derived Cu species. DFT calculations suggest thermodynamically favorable Cu aggregation at step‐associated environments, while STEM‐EELS shows that Cu‐rich nanoscale features in CuATCeO2 are preferentially associated with CeO2 surface steps. These step‐site Cu–CeO2 interfaces offer a more favorable pathway for CO2‐to‐methanol conversion than terrace sites. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic insight into how NP anchoring environments influence catalytic behavior in oxide‐supported metal catalysts.
