RNA sequencing in ovarian cancer research: a comprehensive review
Authors: Fang Shen, Chan Li, Yue Li, Ying Shen, Fengjuan Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a highly fatal and heterogeneous disease, ovarian cancer (OC) lacks sufficient treatment choices. Over the past decade, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has profoundly changed research methods in molecular biology, enabling comprehensive analysis of transcriptomes, somatic mutations, and tumour heterogeneity. In OC research, the application of RNA-seq technologies has significantly enhanced scientists’ understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying the disease, providing deeper insights into the intrinsic mechanisms of key processes such as tumour initiation, metastasis progression, drug resistance formation, and immune evasion. This breakthrough not only expands our understanding of OC but also holds promise for the discovery of novel diagnostic biomarkers, the development of innovative therapies, and the establishment of precise prognostic assessment systems. This review summarizes RNA-seq technologies and their applications in OC research, while also discussing the challenges and opportunities for their broader implementation in cancer studies.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) has the worst prognosis and the highest mortality rate among gynecological cancers. It is critical to distinguish between early- and advanced-stage disease, as their clinical management and prognosis differ vastly. Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), its most common subtype, is the eighth leading cause of cancer death among women worldwide. Due to the absence of specific symptoms at early stages and the lack of approved population screening tools, 75% of patients with EOC are diagnosed at an advanced stage(FIGO stages IIIC-IV) [1]. The standard treatment for advanced EOC consists of multivisceral debulking surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy. Despite these interventions, advanced disease commonly relapses due to emerging drug resistance, resulting in a five-year survival rate of only around 30% [2]. This high recurrence rate underscores the urgent need for new treatment options and, crucially, for a deeper biological understanding that spans the entire disease spectrum, from initial pathogenesis to late-stage therapy resistance.

The poor prognosis of advanced EOC is a direct consequence of its complex and adaptive tumor biology. Several interconnected mechanisms underpin its therapeutic resistance. A key feature is the formation of ascites and new blood vessels, which creates a pro-metastatic environment in the peritoneum and shelters tumor cells [3]. EOC cells also exhibit remarkable metabolic flexibility, allowing them to survive stress and resist chemotherapy [4]. Furthermore, cancer stem cells are widely believed to drive tumor diversity, initiate metastases, and cause relapse by surviving initial therapy [5]. Finally, EOC shapes a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that shields the tumor by disabling immune attacks [6]. Many of these traits, while dominant in late-stage disease, originate from earlier biological events. This evolving complexity is why EOC remains a formidable clinical problem.

To unravel the multifaceted biology of EOC across its entire continuum, robust and comprehensive molecular tools are required. In this context, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has transformed molecular biology by providing unprecedented insights into genome function. This technology offers multidimensional solutions for RNA research, covering gene expression analysis at the single-cell level, whole-transcriptome sequencing, and in-depth analysis of RNA molecular structure and function [7, 8]. Its unparalleled capacity to generate holistic transcriptomic profiles makes it an indispensable tool for dissecting not only the hallmark complexities of advanced EOC but also the foundational events of tumor origin and early progression.

Given its broad utility, RNA-seq has found transformative applications in human disease, profoundly changing our understanding of tumor biology by enabling systematic analysis of gene expression, mutations, and heterogeneity [9] (Fig. 1). However, the power of RNA-seq is tempered by significant interpretational challenges. Technical variability, batch effects, and decisions in the computational pipeline can significantly shape the resulting biological conclusions. Particularly in the field of OC research, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has gained significant prominence in recent years. This technology provides unprecedented resolution in dissecting the molecular landscape of OC, revealing critical mechanisms underlying tumor initiation, progression, acquisition of chemoresistance, and immune evasion strategies. However, this high-resolution approach is not without limitations. The central objective of these investigations is to translate molecular insights into clinical advances. RNA-seq-derived data are paving the way for discovering new therapeutic targets, identifying diagnostic biomarkers, and refining prognostic models. By characterizing tumor-specific gene expression profiles with high precision, RNA-seq serves as a cornerstone for the future of precision medicine in OC. It has not only advanced our fundamental knowledge of cancer pathogenesis but also opened new avenues for personalized treatment.

In this review, we first provide an overview of key RNA-seq technologies. We then systematically summarize their transformative applications across the entire disease spectrum of OC, including its pathological origins, tumorigenesis and progression, metastasis, tumor heterogeneity, drug resistance mechanisms, and the immunosuppressive TME, as well as their implications for biomarker discovery and immunotherapy.

RNA-seq technologies

The advancement of transcriptomics research is closely tied to the progress of sequencing technologies. Each innovation and improvement in sequencing technology has profoundly influenced the research paradigms of transcriptomics, providing significant impetus to its development.

Bulk RNA-seq: foundational role

As an economical and high-throughput technology, bulk RNA-seq can detect gene expression across the entire transcriptome in tissue or cell populations, with results representing the combined expression profiles of all cell types in the sample. Despite rapid advancements in emerging technologies such as scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq remains indispensable in current cancer research due to its unique advantages in large-scale analyses, clinical applicability, and cost-effectiveness [10]. Although bulk RNA-seq is useful, its ability to provide an average transcriptome profile is a fundamental limitation. This conceals the crucial cellular heterogeneity and may also mask biologically significant expression changes in rare but functionally important cell subpopulations, such as cancer stem cells or specific immune cell subtypes [11]. This makes it more challenging to evaluate molecular subtypes and candidate biomarkers from a biological perspective, as the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) discovered in bulk tissue tissues may not accurately identify their cellular origin.

By analysing bulk RNA-seq data from large-scale cancer cohorts such as the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), researchers can identify molecular subtypes, prognostic biomarkers, and oncogenic driver genes [12, 13]. For instance, a study analyzing bulk RNA-seq data from 489 high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) cases classified HGSOC into four molecular subtypes: immunoreactive (characterized by high immune cell infiltration and better prognosis), mesenchymal (associated with stromal activation and chemotherapy resistance), differentiated (marked by epithelial differentiation), and proliferative (exhibiting high cell-cycle activity and poor prognosis) [14]. This classification laid the foundation for personalized therapeutic strategies in OC. Additionally, bulk RNA-seq studies have suggested the potential of genes such as CXCL11 [15] and B3GALT1 [16] as prognostic biomarkers, and provided preliminary evidence for correlations between tumor-promoting pathways (e.g., SOX6-FBXO2-SUN2 axis) and patient survival [17].

Apart from cancer research, bulk RNA-seq has also become an important fundamental approach for studying various diseases due to its several key advantages. Firstly, it possesses outstanding technical performance in detecting low-abundance transcripts and alternative splicing isoforms, thereby providing higher sensitivity for pathway analysis and differential gene expression studies [18]. Second, bulk RNA-seq demonstrates greater compatibility with low-quality or limited-quantity samples (e.g., frozen or FFPE clinical biopsies), thereby enhancing its utility in retrospective clinical investigations [19]. Third, it exhibits high complementarity with single-cell technologies. For example, in a ‘global-to-focal’ research paradigm, bulk RNA-seq first identifies transcriptome-wide alterations such as DEGs between tumor and normal tissues, followed by scRNA-seq to dissect specific cell subpopulations [20]. Lastly, bulk RNA-seq retains integrative potential with cutting-edge multi-omics approaches. In combined analyses, it remains a core component alongside whole-exome/genome sequencing and epigenomic data, forming the mainstream framework for cancer multi-omics studies [21].

Single-cell RNA sequencing

First demonstrated by Tang et al. in 2009 [22], scRNA-seq has rapidly advanced to become a crucial tool in cancer research. The application of scRNA-seq technology has transformed transcriptome research, allowing us to gain insights into complex biological systems such as ovarian tumors that were previously difficult to access. Unlike traditional bulk RNA-seq, which can only provide population averages of gene expression, scRNA-seq offers single-cell resolution and can help researchers elucidate the complex cellular composition in OC [23] (Table 1). All scRNA-seq techniques involve tissue dissociation, single-cell isolation, RNA labeling, and amplification before sequencing. The standard scRNA-seq workflow typically involves: (1) single-cell isolation, (2) RNA reverse transcription, (3) cDNA amplification, (4) library preparation, (5) sequencing, and (6) data analysis [25] (Fig. 2). A critical challenge in step (1) is preserving cell viability during dissociation, while step (3) requires careful optimization of amplification to minimize technical noise. Isolation of single cells primarily involves laser capture microdissection (LCM), fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), micromanipulation, and microfluidics [26]. Each method for isolating single cells has unique strengths. Micromanipulators allow for precise single-cell extraction under microscopic observation, including the handling of fragile cells. LCM enables the isolation of single cells while preserving spatial information from solid tissue samples. Microfluidic techniques offer economical advantages by requiring minimal cellular samples and reagent quantities. FACS technology exhibits exceptional adaptability, enabling its flexible application in isolating various rare cell subpopulations across a wide range of cell types. ScRNA methods primarily fall into two main categories: (1) droplet-based platforms like InDrop, Drop-Seq, and 10 × Genomics (Pleasanton, CA, USA), with the capacity to process cell counts in the thousands to hundreds of thousands; and (2) plate-based systems such as Smart-Seq2, which analyze smaller cell numbers but achieve greater transcriptome depth [27]. Different scRNA-seq systems can be evaluated in two dimensions—breadth and depth. Typically, droplet-based methods (e.g., 10 × Genomics) excel in scalability, whereas plate-based methods (e.g., Smart-Seq2) provide superior sensitivity for detecting low-abundance transcripts [7]. This internal trade-off implies that the research results regarding cell composition and gene expression may vary depending on the experimental platform. However, the analysis of scRNA-seq data still faces numerous technical and analytical challenges. The first step of tissue dissociation can introduce substantial biases, as it may selectively damage sensitive cell types or alter their transcriptomes. This necessitates that researchers carefully consider whether the “rare cell populations” identified are a genuine biological entity or are merely an artifact caused by the dissociation process. On the other hand, systematic differences between different experimental batches and between different single-cell sequencing platforms also pose difficulties in integrating and replicating the research results. During the data analysis stage, the clustering algorithms used to identify cell populations are susceptible to parameters such as resolution settings and feature selection, leading to potential instability in cluster definitions [28]. The high dimensionality and inherent sparsity (dropout events) of scRNA-seq data further increase the difficulty in distinguishing true biological variations from technical noise [29, 30]. Therefore, only through rigorous validation can the biological discoveries obtained be ensured to be reliable, rather than artifacts caused by technical confounders.

Despite challenges, the advent of scRNA-seq technology has fundamentally transformed the research paradigm in oncology. This technique not only enables precise characterisation of rare cell subpopulations, cancer stem cells, and unique molecular expression profiles, but has also made significant contributions to elucidating core mechanisms underpinning tumour initiation and progression, metastasis, clonal dynamics, disease recurrence, and therapeutic resistance [31]. By enabling multi-omics analysis at the single-cell level—integrating transcriptomic, genomic, epigenomic, and metabolic features—this technology provides a valuable new approach to investigating tumor heterogeneity.

By offering single-cell resolution, this method can be used to investigate rare cell subsets, explore intricate regulatory networks, and infer developmental pathways among diverse cell types [32]. In addition to developmental biology, scRNA-seq and its derived technologies may help identify new markers [20, 33]. ScRNA-seq technology allows for high-resolution characterization of the cellular composition of the TME, thereby illuminating the functional heterogeneity of tumor-infiltrating immune cells [34]. The molecular information it provides—including copy number variations (CNVs), single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), and structural variants (SVs)—provides a valuable resource for elucidating tumor heterogeneity, particularly in OC [35]. These discoveries enhance our comprehension of cancer development, disease advancement, and treatment resistance, thereby laying the groundwork for more precise therapeutic approaches and individualized patient care [36].

Spatially resolved transcriptomics

Through scRNA-seq, researchers have gained unprecedented access to the molecular profiles of individual cancer cells. However, merely investigating differences between cancerous and normal tissues in terms of gene replication, transcription, and translation proves insufficient. The lack of spatial information in conventional transcriptomic data poses a significant challenge for investigating cellular communication networks and complicates the comprehensive analysis of tumor heterogeneity. To address these limitations, spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT) has emerged as a highly influential technological advancement. The most critical feature of SRT lies in its ability to characterize gene expression profiles while preserving spatial information, enabling multidimensional analysis of the TME within intact tissue architecture [37]. Early SRT techniques were constrained by low throughput (e.g., single-gene localization methods in the 2000 s) [38]. However, since 2016—when Sweden’s Lundeberg team published the first spatial barcode-based in situ capture technology in Science [39]—SRT has entered the high-throughput era, rapidly advancing in resolution and scalability.

According to their detection strategies, SRT technologies can be broadly classified into two categories [40]. The first category, imaging-based methods (e.g., FISH-based approaches), enables in situ transcriptome readout via microscopy but is often limited by throughput. In contrast, the second category, in situ barcoding (e.g., 10 × Genomics Visium), decouples spatial barcoding from sequencing, allowing high-throughput profiling while preserving tissue architecture. Commercial platforms, such as 10 × Genomics’ Visium and NanoString’s GeoMx, dominate current SRT applications due to their standardized protocols, high reproducibility, and vendor-supported experimental pipelines.

However, SRT exhibits significant limitations. Its resolution typically falls below that of scRNA-seq, with each detection point capturing multiple cells, thereby obscuring single-cell signals and rare cell types. Data analysis faces challenges, including difficulties in normalisation, imprecise cell segmentation, and a lack of standardised benchmarking tools. The high experimental costs and substantial data storage requirements further constrain the technology’s widespread adoption. More critically, the effective integration of spatial and single-cell data to achieve precise cell-type localisation remains an urgent analytical challenge requiring resolution [41–43].

Recently, SRT has emerged as a powerful and widely adopted tool in biomedical research, with broad applications across key disciplines such as developmental biology, oncology, immunology, and neuroscience, where spatial organization is crucial for understanding functional mechanisms [44]. Cellular spatial positioning is increasingly recognized as providing valuable insights into intercellular communication and is understood to play a vital role in both normal physiology and disease processes [45]. This approach can provide a detailed molecular map, thereby enhancing insights into how cellular gene expression correlates with microenvironmental interactions [46]. As a discovery platform and validation tool, SRT helps to bridge the gap between single-cell transcriptomics and tissue morphology, thereby advancing our understanding of tissue biology and disease pathology.

NGS-based SRT technologies have been widely adopted in the life sciences, particularly in cancer research [37]. Cells in cancer tissues actively interact with the surrounding TME, thereby bypassing immune defence mechanisms and promoting proliferation [47]. Therefore, analysing the composition characteristics, spatial distribution patterns, and interaction networks of cell subpopulations in tissues is of great significance for elucidating the mechanisms of tumourigenesis and discovering novel therapeutic targets. SRT and its associated methodologies enable the profiling of hundreds of transcriptomes while preserving their spatial context in tissue sections, supporting the study of cellular diversity, functional states, and intercellular dynamics in tumors and their surrounding microenvironments [46]. Because of this, SRT is increasingly seen as an essential tool for sifting through the TME [48]. By uncovering transcriptome signals at a particular location, researchers seek to elucidate the TME better. This approach holds the potential to identify potential therapeutic targets and provide insights into the mechanisms driving tumor growth and progression [49].

In OC, studies using SRT have begun to uncover tumor heterogeneity, with distinct spatial distributions of malignant and stromal cell populations that were associated with treatment response and clinical outcomes in specific cohorts [50]. Examining the geographic patterns of gene activity within ovarian tumor tissues suggests that it may reveal distinct characteristics that impact patient prognosis and therapeutic efficacy [51]. These preliminary findings highlight a promising direction for research, which, if confirmed through larger studies, could eventually help in the design of targeted treatment strategies and the screening of prognostic biomarkers, thereby advancing the field of precision medicine for OC.

Emerging and specialized technologies

Advances in RNA-seq technologies have provided new methodological approaches for OC research. Cutting-edge techniques, including long-read sequencing (LRS) and single-cell multi-omics sequencing, have enabled researchers to gain deeper insights into transcriptional complexity, identify rare cell subpopulations, and facilitate the clinical translation of findings.

Although second-generation sequencing (SGS) has brought remarkable progress to transcriptome research, its widespread application is hindered by inherent technical constraints such as limited read lengths. In recent years, the emergence of third-generation sequencing (TGS), or LRS, has largely overcome these technical barriers, providing a critical foundation for transcriptome studies at the full-length transcript level. The defining feature of TGS, in contrast to earlier sequencing platforms, is its ability to perform single-molecule real-time sequencing (SMRT) without requiring PCR amplification, while simultaneously achieving ultra-long read lengths. Currently, the field is primarily led by two leading LRS technologies: (1) SMRT, introduced by Pacific Biosciences (PacBio), and (2) Nanopore-based sequencing, developed by Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) [52]. With average read lengths exceeding 15 kb, TGS provides unprecedented capabilities for characterizing complete gene structures at the full-length transcript level. For instance, a recent study leveraging long-read RNA-seq achieved a sequencing depth of 12,000 reads per cell, which facilitated the identification of novel isoforms in OC and provided new insights into the intricate transcriptomic complexity of this malignancy [53]. Furthermore, LRS offers innovative methodological approaches for elucidating the architectural complexity and functional implications of fusion genes. For instance, a comprehensive investigation of fusion genes and SVs in breast cancer utilized LRS technology, which demonstrated the potential value of this methodology in both clinical diagnostics and oncological research [54]. However, it should be noted that the per-base error rate of LRS technology currently remains higher than that of SGS. This shortcoming, compounded by higher reagent costs and lower single-cell throughput, hinders its widespread adoption—particularly in clinical applications where detection accuracy and cost-effectiveness are paramount considerations [55, 56].

Single-cell multi-omics technologies aim to decode cellular states by integrating multi-layered data from genomes, transcriptomes, epigenomes, and proteomes [57]. These approaches enable high-resolution characterization of tumor heterogeneity [58], microenvironmental crosstalk [59], and epigenetic dynamics [60] during development, aging, and disease. By providing high-resolution molecular portraits, they are profoundly enhancing our understanding of cellular biology in both physiological and pathological contexts. Complementing these advances, spatial multi-omics techniques further incorporate spatial dimensions, mapping cellular distributions and their associated omics profiles within tissues to uncover spatially resolved molecular networks that contribute to disease mechanisms [61]. Among single-cell omics methods, single-cell transcriptomics currently stands as the most mature and widely adopted platform, frequently combined with other modalities to interrogate genotype–phenotype linkages. Single-cell multi-omics has so far been extensively used in a variety of research domains, including cell lineage tracing, the construction of organ cell atlases, as well as tumor immunology and cancer genetics [62]. The power of these approaches is tempered by the immense analytical complexity of integrating disparate data types. Technical noise, drop-out events, and the challenge of biologically meaningful data integration can lead to ambiguous interpretations. The field is still developing robust statistical frameworks and computational tools to fully leverage these rich, multi-layered datasets without introducing integration artifacts [62, 63].

These emerging technologies have not only deepened our understanding of complex genetic structures and functions but also provided novel avenues for further exploration of the regulatory mechanisms underlying human complex diseases and the development of effective translational medicine strategies.

Bioinformatics analysis pipelines and data resource

The aforementioned powerful RNA-seq technologies generate vast amounts of raw data, and transforming this into biologically meaningful discoveries relies entirely on robust computational workflows and bioinformatics resources. The deep integration of bioinformatics has fundamentally transformed the practice of precision oncology—by extracting actionable insights from complex high-throughput datasets to identify molecular targets for personalised therapies [64]. This section outlines the analytical tools, databases, and analytical approaches that play a pivotal role in OC RNA-seq analysis.

Data resources and repositories

The advancement of this field relies on large-scale public databases, which constitute valuable assets for scientific exploration and validation. Collaborative initiatives such as the TCGA have mapped comprehensive molecular profiles for HGSOC, establishing a robust foundation for defining molecular subtypes and prognostic markers [65]. The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository consolidates thousands of independent RNA-seq datasets, enabling integrated analyses and independent validation. Platforms such as cBioPortal empower researchers to intuitively interpret these complex multi-omics datasets, advancing the discipline through open-access resources and standardized methodologies [64].

Standard analysis pipelines

The standard bioinformatics analysis workflow typically commences with quality control of raw sequencing reads, followed by adapter trimming. Sequences are then aligned to a reference genome using splice-aware aligners such as STAR or HISAT2, which can recognise splice sites. When processing scRNA-seq data, the standard analysis toolkit Cell Ranger is commonly employed to perform sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and sequence alignment. Following alignment, mapping sequencing reads to corresponding genomic features yields a count matrix [66]. For conventional RNA-seq data, such count matrices serve as input for specialised analysis tools like DESeq2 or edgeR, enabling the identification of statistically significant gene expression changes across different experimental conditions [67].

Advanced downstream analysis and discovery

The core strength of bioinformatics lies in its highly efficient downstream analytical capabilities. Within scRNA-seq, integrated analysis tools such as Seurat (R environment) and Scanpy (Python environment) have become established standard methods within the academic community. Through comprehensive analytical workflows—encompassing data dimensionality reduction, cell clustering, and biomarker gene identification—they effectively empower researchers to interpret cellular heterogeneity. Furthermore, trajectory inference algorithms can model dynamic processes, and tools for inferring cell–cell communication (e.g., CellChat) predict ligand-receptor interactions within the TME [64].

At present, computational methods are emerging as a pivotal driver of significant advances in OC research. Specifically, researchers have employed integrated bioinformatics approaches to conduct analyses of TCGA data, establishing a novel prognostic assessment system encompassing nine immune-related genes, including LRP1. These genes ultimately influence patient clinical outcomes by regulating the state of the TME [65]. The study employed univariate Cox regression, LASSO analysis, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to validate the model’s predictive efficacy. This demonstrates the advantages of bioinformatics workflows in precisely identifying potential therapeutic targets. Similarly, another study integrated multiple databases, including TISIDB and LinkedOmics, alongside network analysis tools such as STRING and Cytoscape. This research revealed a cooperative role between TFF3 and PVRL2 in shaping the TME, providing novel theoretical foundations for developing combined targeted therapies against tumours [68].

Looking ahead, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) with multi-omics data will undoubtedly accelerate the discovery of biomarkers. These technologies not only automate the processing of complex data and uncover underlying patterns but also enhance the precision of predicting drug responses and resistance mechanisms [64]. With the continuous advancement of computational capabilities, they will become a core force in optimising precision medicine strategies, providing crucial support for developing more effective personalised treatment plans for OC.

Applications

Understanding origin and early pathogenesis

A profound understanding of cellular origins is fundamental to developing early detection and intervention strategies. For the most common and lethal HGSOC, the hypothesis of its origin in fallopian tube epithelium (FTE) has gained widespread support [69, 70]. RNA-seq technologies—particularly at the single-cell level—have provided high-resolution molecular evidence for this paradigm shift, revealing the initial transcriptional events driving tumorigenesis that play a pivotal role in this process.

Elucidating cellular origins through transcriptomic profiling

scRNA-seq technology can precisely identify progenitor cell populations within FTE that may progress to HGSOC. In a key study, Dinh and colleagues sequenced approximately 53,000 human fallopian tube cells and identified a distinct early secretory cell subpopulation. Crucially, the transcriptional signature of this specific cell group remained enriched in advanced HGSOC tumor tissues, providing compelling evidence that these early secretory endothelial cells (ECs) may represent the origin cells of the disease [71].

Another scRNA-seq study identified a distinct type of malignant ECs in OC that strongly retain ciliated cell markers. This finding further supports the “FTE origin hypothesis,” suggesting that some tumors may preserve or reacquire their ciliated progenitor characteristics during carcinogenesis [72].

However, the pathogenesis of OC may not be singular. A mouse model study utilizing scRNA-seq technology proposed a dual origin hypothesis for EOC—involving both FTE cells and ovarian surface epithelial (OSE) cells. This analysis revealed stem cell/progenitor cell populations in both sites, whose distinct gene expression profiles and niche structures may drive divergent carcinogenic pathways [73]. Collectively, these RNA-seq studies reveal a complex cellular landscape of OC origin, moving beyond a single-source model.

Uncovering early transcriptional programs in tumorigenesis

Beyond identifying the origins of cancer cells, RNA-seq also plays a crucial role in deciphering the molecular programs activated during the early stages of malignant transformation. This technology enables analysis of these initial transcriptional changes, with scRNA-seq offering unprecedented insights into tumor heterogeneity and cellular states.

Transcriptomic analysis showed key similarities between cancer growth and embryonic development. In a landmark study, Zhao et al. built single-cell atlases of OC and embryonic tissues. Their work showed that the gene expression patterns of several OC subpopulations closely resemble those of embryonic tissues. This similarity suggests that ovarian carcinogenesis may co-opt fundamental biological pathways active during early development, providing crucial insights into the disease’s pathogenesis [74].

Conclusion

In summary, RNA-seq has fundamentally advanced our understanding of OC, yielding direct molecular evidence for its cellular origins (primarily the fallopian tube) and revealing early transcriptional activities consistent with developmental biology. These discoveries serve as cornerstones upon which future risk assessment, early screening, and preventive treatment strategies will be built.

However, it remains a challenge to translate these findings into clinically applicable detection methods. The complexity of cellular origins implies that a single detection method may not be suitable for all types of OC. Moreover, how to develop a sufficiently sensitive early cancer signal detection technology using easily accessible samples such as blood or secretions remains a significant challenge at present.

Overcoming diagnostic challenges: the search for biomarkers

OC is often referred to as the “silent killer”—due to the lack of reliable early diagnostic indicators, about 70% of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [75]. Currently, there is no reliable screening technology for OC. Although the serum cancer antigen 125 (CA125) test is the most widely used, it has obvious limitations, especially with low specificity in premenopausal women [76]. This severe diagnostic situation highlights the urgent need for novel and reliable biomarkers. RNA-seq technology plays a central role in this exploratory effort and holds promise for revolutionizing OC diagnosis. This is accomplished through the identification of molecular features, including DEGs, non-coding RNAs, and specific cell subpopulations.

Elucidating tumorigenic pathways to uncover diagnostic targets

RNA-seq plays a key role in unraveling the molecular pathways involved in OC development, which can help uncover biomarkers for diagnosis. Research into long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), for example, has detected specific molecules that show dysregulation during the initial phases of tumorigenesis. A case in point is OIN1—a long intergenic non-coding RNA situated on chromosome 10q21. According to RNA-seq data, OIN1 expression is markedly elevated in OC samples. It facilitates tumor growth by helping cells avoid programmed death, underscoring its promise as a cancer-specific indicator and supporting the need for additional diagnostic studies [77].

Similarly, the hypoxic microenvironment of OC triggers unique transcriptional programs. Through RNA-seq technology, researchers discovered that HIF1A-AS3 is a novel hypoxia-induced lncRNA, whose expression is directly activated by the HIF1A-α. This finding, based on comprehensive transcriptome analysis, not only reveals a facet of the pathological mechanism of OC but also elevates HIF1A-AS3 to a potential biomarker that can reflect the hypoxic characteristics of the tumor [78].

Although the discovery of abnormal transcripts regulated by OIN1, HIF1A-AS3, etc., demonstrates the ability of RNA-seq technology to screen candidate biomarkers, these biomarkers still face significant challenges for clinical translation. The primary problem is the development of detection methods: how to convert the findings based on research-grade batch RNA-seq into robust, cost-effective, and clinically certified diagnostic detection methods suitable for routine pathology laboratories. Additionally, the validation of these tissue biomarkers requires large-scale, independent, and prospective cohort studies to confirm that their diagnostic sensitivity/specificity has a significant and reproducible improvement compared to current standards, and ultimately can improve patient prognosis.

Leveraging transcriptomic signatures from tissues and blood

The exploration process of diagnostic markers has gone through several stages, and each breakthrough has been supported by RNA-seq technology. Early studies mainly relied on tissue analysis and were conducted by integrating large-scale RNA-seq data from databases such as TCGA and GEO. One study found that the expression level of fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) in OC tissues was significantly lower than that in normal samples, which might become a potential diagnostic marker. When performing the receiver operating characteristic curve analysis in an independent GEO dataset, although it was a preliminary result, the area under the curve (AUC) value reached 0.8256, suggesting that FGFR1 can distinguish between malignant and non-malignant tissues [79].

Driven by the clinical demand for minimally invasive tests, this field has decisively shifted towards liquid biopsy. A landmark multi-center diagnostic study demonstrated that by conducting RNA-seq analysis of the mRNA signatures of platelets in “tumor-educated” blood cells, it is possible to distinguish OC patients from healthy individuals effectively. This discovery makes platelets a potential medium for OC detection based on blood [80].

The findings above mainly verify the technical and biological feasibility. To truly promote clinical application, these biomarkers still need to undergo strict prospective validation in the target population. The ultimate test lies in clinical practicability; that is, compared with traditional diagnostic pathways, using this biomarker to guide clinical decision-making can indeed improve patient outcomes. Additionally, the complexity of biomarkers based on RNA-seq is relatively high, which also poses challenges for the development of detection methods.

Discovering novel cellular subpopulations with diagnostic potential

The high-resolution capability of scRNA-seq reveals that rare cell populations can serve as particular diagnostic markers. For instance, one study identified a distinct S100A9 + malignant cell subpopulation present in both primary and metastatic lesions, which correlates with reduced patient survival. This aggressive subpopulation exhibits distinct gene expression patterns (including S100A8, S100A9, and ADGRF1), and the resulting multi-gene signature may serve as a novel biomarker for disease diagnosis and prognosis assessment [81].

Another promising cellular discovery concerns the identification of tip-like ECs. scRNA-seq revealed that these cells are virtually absent in normal ovarian tissue, yet are highly abundant within OC tumours. A key finding is their specific expression of prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA, encoded by the FOLH1 gene), a cell surface protein rarely observed on other ovarian cell types. This characteristic renders PSMA a potential target for developing particular blood-based diagnostic assays or imaging technologies for OC. Bioinformatics analysis further corroborated the clinical relevance of this finding: elevated levels of tip-like ECs in tumour samples correlated with poor patient prognosis [82].

These results underscore the distinct value of scRNA-seq for detecting rare tumour-specific cell subpopulations. However, this high resolution also reveals a major obstacle for clinical translation: tumour heterogeneity. These cells are sparse and unevenly distributed within tissues, meaning a single tissue biopsy or liquid biopsy may fail to capture them effectively, leading to false negative results. Relying solely on such cells for diagnosis could undermine the reliability of test outcomes. Successful translation hinges on developing effective strategies to overcome this sampling bias.

Conclusion and path forward

Overall, RNA-seq facilitates OC diagnosis through multiple strategies, aiding in the discovery of potential biomarkers. However, as early studies have demonstrated, translating promising scientific discoveries into widely adopted diagnostic tools remains a challenge. Whether these markers come from tissue samples, liquid biopsies, or rare cell types, they encounter similar obstacles in clinical translation: the need for thorough validation in large-scale prospective studies; converting complex sequencing data into simple, reliable, and cost-effective testing methods; the impact of tumour heterogeneity on biomarker accuracy; and, crucially, proving they offer real advantages over existing standards to benefit patients meaningfully.

Therefore, future work must prioritise practical application alongside exploratory discovery. This necessitates systematically translating complex RNA detection metrics into practical clinical testing protocols, conducting rigorous efficacy validation studies, and establishing novel methods for assessing spatio-temporal heterogeneity. Only by overcoming these critical challenges can RNA detection technology realise its clinical value, providing reliable and precise diagnostic tools for OC.

Predicting patient outcomes: prognostic stratification

The marked heterogeneity of OC leads to substantial variation in patient outcomes—even when disease staging and treatment regimens are similar, results remain inconsistent. In clinical practice, accurately stratifying patient risk remains a significant challenge. RNA-seq technology offers a powerful complement to traditional pathological assessment by revealing the molecular basis of prognosis. This section will explore how to use transcriptome analysis to identify key biological features, including the composition of the TME, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic status, which will provide important evidence for the construction of prognostic models.

Decoding the prognostic signals of the tumor microenvironment

The cellular composition and spatial distribution of the TME are key factors influencing patient survival [6]. Single-cell and spatial RNA-seq technologies provide ideal tools for analyzing this complexity and identifying stromal cell populations with opposing prognostic impacts.

For instance, a scRNA-seq study of treatment-naïve HGSOC tumors identified six cell subpopulations correlated with clinical outcomes. Notably, five of these subpopulations—including specific populations of mesothelial cells, myofibroblasts, transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)-activated cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), a tumor-specific subpopulation, and lymphatic ECs—were associated with poorer survival. Conversely, a plasma cell population was identified as a favourable prognostic indicator [83]. Spatial transcriptomics research further deepened this understanding, demonstrating that specific CAF-tumor crosstalk signaling networks correlate with long-term patient survival (exceeding 10 years). At the same time, the absence of certain cancer-associated fibroblast subpopulations characteristically defines these long-term survivors [51].

The TME exerts a significant influence on tumour recurrence and metastasis. Integrated analysis combining spatial transcriptomics with CT imaging showed distinct molecular signatures between recurrent and non-recurrent patient cohorts: TNF-α signalling pathways and oxidative phosphorylation indicated poor prognosis, whereas chemotaxis and immune regulation markers predicted favourable outcomes [84]. Separate research utilised scRNA-seq of omental metastatic lesions to construct a prognostic model based on the cellular states unique to metastatic sites. This fully demonstrates the advantages of RNA-seq technology in capturing the molecular drivers of aggressive disease [23].

Translating these complex cellular characteristics into reliable clinical diagnostic tools remains subject to critical hurdles: repeated validation across diverse populations and independent patient cohorts is essential. Furthermore, the TME exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity, meaning prognostic models derived from single-site biopsies may not accurately represent the entire tumor’s biology. Future multi-region sampling strategies may prove instrumental in addressing this limitation.

Leveraging tumor heterogeneity for risk stratification

OC exhibits significant heterogeneity at the genetic, cellular, and metabolic levels, with this high complexity directly contributing to poor treatment outcomes and divergent patient prognosis [85]. RNA-seq technologies enable precise quantification of this complexity and extraction of clinically actionable prognostic information. At the genetic level, scRNA-seq studies revealed that HGSOC tumours comprise multiple subclones distinguished by unique CNAs and transcriptomic profiles [86]. The presence and proportional abundance of these subclones, often masked in bulk sequencing data, are critical determinants of disease progression and patient survival.

Apart from genetic factors, metabolic reprogramming also constitutes a significant cause of functional disparities, directly influencing disease prognosis. For instance, classifying patients based on glutamine metabolism-related genes can divide them into subtypes with significantly different survival rates and immune infiltration patterns. The resulting glutamine metabolism prognostic index (GMPI) can identify high-risk patients with poorer overall survival and a diminished response to immunotherapy [87]. Beyond glutamine metabolism, dysregulation of core energy pathways similarly holds predictive value. Glycolysis-related gene signatures can delineate a high-risk tumor subcluster, with multi-omics integration pinpointing IGF2 as a key regulator that links glycolytic activation to proliferative, migratory, and chemoresistant phenotypes [88].

Moreover, dysregulation of pathways such as copper metabolism impacts prognosis, as shown by a multi-gene prognostic model comprising copper death-associated genes (e.g., WASF2, CLDN4). This model exhibits potential in predicting patients’ overall survival and tumor immune profiles [89]. Overall, by translating the complex manifestations of tumour heterogeneity at the genetic, cellular, and metabolic levels into quantifiable risk scores, RNA-seq holds promise for advancing the establishment of more precise, biologically grounded prognostic stratification systems.

The above prognostic index, like the GMPI, exemplifies the potential of using RNA-seq technology to quantify heterogeneity and thereby achieve risk stratification. However, its clinical application still faces two fundamental challenges. The first is how to convert complex signatures into routine clinical assays; moreover, due to the tumor evolution, static prognostic scores may become ineffective over time, and dynamic assessment models may be a direction for future research.

Harnessing epigenetic and transcriptional dysregulation

The transcriptional landscape of OC is significantly influenced by epigenetic and transcriptional dysregulation, and this influence in turn provides a rich source of prognostic insight. Epigenetic modifications usually require specialized techniques for investigation, while RNA-seq can capture functional transcriptional outputs triggered by these changes, thereby helping to discover prognostic biomarkers and mechanisms. For instance, integrated multi-omics analyses have revealed clinically relevant epigenetic drivers. One study combining RNA-seq with ChIP-seq demonstrated that EZH2, beyond its canonical role in PRC2-mediated gene silencing, promotes tumor growth through a non-canonical mechanism—transcriptionally activating IDH2 to enhance TCA cycle activity. This EZH2-IDH2 axis represents not only a mechanistic driver of tumor progression but also a potential prognostic indicator and therapeutic target [90]. Similarly, epitranscriptomic regulation mediated by RNA modifications such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) has been linked to aggressive disease behavior. Integrated mRNA sequencing and m6A profiling identified KREMEN2 as a key target stabilized by the m6A demethylase FTO and the reader protein IGF2BP1, forming a regulatory loop that promotes metastasis [91]. Such findings illustrate how RNA-seq, when coupled with epigenetic data, can uncover post-transcriptional regulatory networks with prognostic significance.

Advancing further, single-cell multi-omics techniques (such as concurrent scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq are now capable of depicting epigenetic heterogeneity and its association with transcriptional programs driving poor outcomes. These methods have begun to identify cancer-specific distal regulatory elements (dREs) that are activated in subclones of tumor cells, which are associated with resistance pathways and aggressive phenotypes. Studies have found that key transcription factors such as YY1, CEBPD, and KLF6 are involved in regulating these epigenetic alterations, exhibiting differential activity across tumor subpopulations [92]. This integrative framework not only elucidates the epigenetic basis of intratumoral heterogeneity but also provides a systematic method for identifying druggable epigenetic dependencies. In a translational example, combined RNA-seq and ATAC-seq analyses revealed a novel epigenetic mechanism of PARP inhibitor (PARPi) resistance mediated by the CYP1B1/histone H1.4 axis, which remodeled chromatin accessibility independently of canonical DNA repair pathways [93]. Collectively, this work underscores that epigenetic dysregulation leaves a discernible mark on gene expression profiles. By analyzing these profiles with RNA-seq, we can extract a critical layer of prognostic information, yielding insights into the disease’s biological drivers and revealing potential biomarkers for risk stratification in OC.

Combining RNA-seq with epigenetic data can reveal a deep layer of prognostic biology. However, applying these findings to clinical practice still faces significant challenges. Technologies such as scATAC-seq, which involve complex operations and high costs, are currently challenging to use for routine clinical prognostication. Incorporating them into patient management requires overcoming the clinical applicability of these intricate detection techniques and achieving independent validation of their effectiveness.

Synthesis: from biology to prognostic models

Overall, RNA-seq can integrate multiple types of information, enabling us to transform simple biological descriptions into prognostic assessments that can be applied in clinical settings. This technology functions as a unified observation window, enabling the precise quantification of complex characteristics of malignant diseases and integrating these features into a comprehensive risk model.

The stromal compartment, for instance, yields critical prognostic signals through the identification of specific favorable and unfavorable non-malignant cell populations. Concurrently, the technology deciphers variations within cancer cells—including genetic subclones, cellular states, and metabolically reprogrammed subpopulations defined by glycolysis, glutamine, or copper metabolism—thereby revealing drivers of cancer metastasis and treatment resistance. Furthermore, by identifying regulatory factors such as EZH2 and FTO—which orchestrate pro-tumorigenic networks—it offers novel insights for prognostic assessment of the regulatory layer of the transcriptome.

Although the prospects are promising, the clinical application of these sophisticated models still faces several recognized challenges. Validating these RNA-seq-based signatures in large, prospective, and independent clinical trials is still necessary, and their clinical utility must be proven. Moreover, practical issues such as tumor heterogeneity, as well as the difficulties in converting complex multi-omics data into routine clinical tests, are all obstacles to the transformation process. Overcoming these challenges is essential to bridge the gap between prognostic discovery and clinical practice.

Deciphering and overcoming therapy resistance

Platinum-based chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of systemic treatment for OC. However, up to 75% of patients with advanced HGSOC develop resistance—particularly to platinum agents—a situation that frequently leads to disease recurrence and shortens overall survival to just 12–14 months [94, 95], representing a devastating setback. Overcoming this challenge is paramount. RNA-seq technology is playing a crucial role: it can deeply analyze the complex mechanisms of drug resistance, identify predictive biomarkers, and provide an important basis for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Uncovering cancer cell-intrinsic resistance mechanisms

RNA-seq has been instrumental in identifying gene expression programs and cellular states within cancer cells that confer resistance. Comparative transcriptomic analyses of pre- and post-chemotherapy tumor samples have revealed that chemotherapy itself induces adaptive changes. One study in HGSOC patients identified chemotherapy-induced overexpression of the AP-1 transcription factor family (e.g., FOS, FOSB) and recurrent genomic amplification at 11q23.1 (containing the SIK2 gene) in resistant tissues. The functional relevance of these findings was confirmed in vitro, where inhibition of AP-1 or SIK2 synergized with chemotherapy [96]. Similarly, other studies have implicated aberrant expression of genes like FBN1 and OSMR in resistance [97, 98].

Beyond specific genes, scRNA-seq has revealed that pre-existing or therapy-induced cellular states are a key determinant of resistance. A prospective study of paired HGSOC samples identified a stress-related cellular phenotype enriched after chemotherapy, which was associated with poorer outcomes [99]. Another scRNA-seq study suggested that platinum-resistant tumors may harbor a higher proportion of cancer cells with decreased G2/M phase activity, hinting at a chemotherapy-induced quiescent state as a survival mechanism [100]. Furthermore, scRNA-seq has identified specific malignant cell subpopulations linked to treatment response. For instance, a highly proliferative TOP2A + subpopulation was associated with sensitivity to NACT [101]. In contrast, a recurrent epithelial subpopulation (S1 subtype) characterized by interferon-responsive genes (e.g., IFI6) was linked to platinum resistance via NF-κB-mediated suppression of apoptosis [102]. These findings underscore that intrinsic resistance is not a monolithic trait but a heterogeneous landscape of cellular phenotypes.

Although these findings have shown potential biomarkers, their path to clinical application faces two significant challenges. Firstly, tumor heterogeneity means that a single biopsy may miss pre-existing resistant cell groups. Secondly, tumors change over time, and repeated tissue biopsies to monitor these changes are impractical. Therefore, simultaneously seeking more non-invasive methods (such as liquid biopsy) to track these resistance mechanisms continuously may be where our focus should lie.

The role of the tumor microenvironment in fostering resistance

The TME is a key factor driving therapy resistance. scRNA-seq studies consistently show that chemotherapy profoundly reshapes the composition of stromal and immune cells. A key finding is the significant expansion and persistent presence of CAFs following chemotherapy [100]. These therapy-resistant fibroblasts (such as ANTXR1 + myofibroblasts) suppress anti-tumour immunity via signalling pathways including YAP1, thereby constructing a protective niche for tumour cells [103].

CAFs also induce treatment resistance by directly suppressing immune mechanisms. Among these, specific subtypes such as the pro-tumour CAF_c1 express immunomodulatory factors (e.g., STAT1, IRF1) and interact with immune cells via ligand-receptor pairs (e.g., CD55-ADGRE5). These characteristics correlate closely with poorer patient prognosis and reduced response to ICIs [104]. Multi-omics studies further reveal that CAF subsets promote an immunosuppressive milieu through secretory factors (e.g., IL-8) and non-canonical immune checkpoints (e.g., VSIG4), which impair T cell activity [105].

The immune cells themselves constitute the root cause of treatment resistance. T cells in OC exhibit severe functional exhaustion and spatial segregation. Exhausted T cells, characterized by high SPP1 expression, aggregate in immunosuppressive niches and are linked to poor prognosis [106]. Moreover, dysfunctional T cells are often sequestered in the primary lesion, while metastatic sites are enriched with unresponsive bystander T cells, forming a dual “immune cold zone”, which resists immunotherapy [107]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) further strengthen this immunosuppressive state. M2-type macrophages in ascites express a series of co-inhibitory receptors (TIGIT, TIM-3, LAG-3, PD-1), and in vitro studies have shown that targeting TIGIT can reprogram them and enhance their phagocytic function, providing a combined strategy to overcome treatment resistance [108].

However, the clinical application of these studies also faces practical challenges. It is necessary to consider how to transform the complex research findings into standardized tests suitable for clinical use and prove through large-scale clinical trials that the combined therapy guided by these biomarkers can improve patient outcomes compared to standard treatments alone, thereby proving their value.

Genomic drivers and their influence on therapy response

The genomic landscape of tumors plays a dominant role in the TME and treatment sensitivity. RNA-seq analysis has elucidated how distinct gene mutation patterns give rise to various immune escape and resistance mechanisms. For instance, tumors with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) (including those with BRCA1/2 gene mutations) typically exhibit an inflamed but dysfunctional TME, characterized by T-cell exhaustion and HLA loss, resulting in resistance to single ICIs [109]. This understanding has paved the way for the development of PARPis, which utilize HRD vulnerability through a “synthetic lethality” mechanism. However, the development of PARPi resistance—often through the restoration of HR function—remains a significant challenge, spurring the exploration of combination therapies that include anti-angiogenics, ICIs, and other targeted agents [110–112].

In contrast, mutations in genes such as ARID1A may enhance tumour immunogenicity. ARID1A loss triggers the cGAS–STING signalling pathway, inducing a type I interferon signature and recruitment of CD8 + T cells. scRNA-seq studies of ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC) tissues revealed that ARID1A mutations shape a tumour microenvironment highly infiltrated by immune cells, notably featuring abundant CXCL13⁺, CTLA4⁺, and CD8⁺ T cells. This characteristic correlates strongly with enhanced response to ICIs [113, 114]. This positions ARID1A mutational status as a promising predictive biomarker for immunotherapy.

The analysis of the impact of genetic variations on treatment responses represents a key breakthrough in the field of OC research, particularly evident in the application of PARPis to HRD-positive tumors. However, significant challenges remain in translating these discoveries into practical diagnostic tools. Biomarkers identified through RNA-seq must be thoroughly validated against existing standard methods, and the predictive value of mutations like ARID1A needs to be strictly confirmed through large-scale prospective clinical trials to ensure their stability and reliability.

Towards clinical translation: biomarkers and combination therapies

The above research has shown that the mechanistic insights gained through RNA-seq have the potential to be translated into clinically actionable strategies for predicting and overcoming drug resistance issues. These advances manifest primarily in two areas: the discovery of predictive biomarkers and the rational design of combination therapies.

Transcriptome analysis has facilitated the development of a new generation of biomarkers. One of the breakthroughs is the upgrade from single genes to multi-gene signatures, which enables researchers to stratify patients based on their likelihood of responding to treatment. For instance, the integration of scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data can yield differential gene expression features that model and predict sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy [115]. Furthermore, risk scores derived from specific malignant cell subpopulations—such as those based on the S1 subtype marker IFI6—can effectively identify patients with innate or acquired platinum resistance [102].

Beyond chemotherapy, multi-omics approaches have established signatures, including those based on glycosylation-related genes and CAF-derived gene sets, that may stratify patients into potential responders and non-responders to ICIs [105, 116]. These mechanistic findings are driving a transformation in the clinical therapeutic paradigm, shifting from empirical single-agent trials to biomarker-driven combination strategies.

The core objective is to co-target complementary resistance pathways simultaneously: for instance, reversing CAF-mediated immunosuppression to restore tumour sensitivity to chemotherapy/ICIs; employing dual checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti-PD-1 + anti-LAG-3) to mitigate T-cell exhaustion; combining ICIs with macrophage-reprogramming agents (e.g., anti-TIGIT + anti-CD47); or genomically matched combinations—pairing PARPis with ICIs in HRD tumours and prioritizing immunotherapy for ARID1A-mutated cancers [104, 108, 109, 114, 117].

Ultimately, by providing a systematic overview of resistance mechanisms, RNA-seq has the potential to advance a multi-pronged precision treatment framework for OC, thereby offering a new strategy to overcome the challenge of treatment resistance.

Engineering the immune response: insights for immunotherapy

OC remains particularly difficult to treat with immunotherapy, mainly because of its low mutational burden, varied infiltration of immune cells, and dense stromal structure [118]. Recent progress in RNA-seq, especially scRNA-seq, has begun to change this. These methods enable researchers to create far more detailed “cellular maps,” uncovering new aspects of how immune responses can be enhanced. Beyond simply describing cell types and states, scRNA-seq reveals diverse cellular functions, heterogeneity, and communication networks—information that is increasingly being utilized to inform the next wave of immunotherapies. Still, translating these intricate cellular insights into effective clinical treatments remains a considerable challenge.

The project begins with a detailed census of TIME. scRNA-seq can reveal the complex cellular landscape of OC and identify potential therapeutic vulnerabilities within specific cell populations. For example, a detailed analysis of T cells delineated a dysfunctional lineage transitioning from an effector state to a severely exhausted state. Spatial transcriptomics further confirmed that exhausted T cells predominantly accumulate in immunosuppressive niches, while dysfunctional T cells and non-reactive bystander cells are preferentially located in primary sites and metastases, respectively [106, 107]. Beyond adaptive immunity, the myeloid compartment exhibits marked plasticity. Notably, immunosuppressive M2-like TAMs express multiple co-inhibitory receptors (e.g., TIGIT, TIM-3, LAG-3, PD-1) and have been established as key contributors to immune evasion [85, 108]. Furthermore, scRNA-seq has identified distinct subpopulations of CAFs. Among these, the pro-tumour CAF_c1 population suppresses T-cell function via immunoregulatory ligands, and its presence predicts poor response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [104, 105]. This cellular landscape provides a reference framework for targeted interventions. The primary challenge currently lies in analyzing, verifying, and clinically validating these complex cell characteristics, converting research-level scRNA-seq discoveries into reliable clinical biomarkers, and verifying their reproducibility across different patient groups and clinical laboratories.

Guided by this blueprint, researchers are devising novel strategies to correct defective adaptive immunity. The academic community recognises that T-cell exhaustion is accompanied by the co-expression of multiple inhibitory receptors, driving the advancement of combined checkpoint blockade therapies—from monotherapy with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 to dual-targeted treatments such as concurrent inhibition of PD-1 and LAG-3 [119, 120]. Within the field of cell therapy, novel CAR-T designs are actively addressing limitations of conventional approaches—including susceptibility to exhaustion and poor persistence in suppressive TME [121]. These measures encompass selecting less differentiated T cell subsets as starting material and engineering anti-exhaustion properties through genetic modification [122]. Furthermore, rare tumour-reactive T cell clones identified in ARID1A-mutated tumours—such as the subtype rich in CXCL13 + CTLA4 + CD8 + T cells—have opened new avenues for developing personalised TCR therapies [114].

Although these engineering strategies hold great promise, their application in clinical practice still faces challenges. For instance, the combination of checkpoint blockade therapy requires considering how to balance efficacy and immune-related adverse reactions [123]; moreover, there is an urgent need to identify predictive biomarkers that can precisely screen out patients who will benefit. For advanced cell therapies, such as CAR-T and TCR-T, the processes are complex and costly, which presents a significant obstacle to their widespread application [124]. Tumor heterogeneity is also a major problem, as overcoming individual differences in responses and preventing disease recurrence remains a significant challenge to this day.

Effective response strategies require reprogramming the innate immune and stromal compartments within the TME. The discovery of immunosuppressive M2-TAMs has spurred efforts to polarize them towards an anti-tumor M1 phenotype or enhance their phagocytic capacity. Preclinical studies indicate that targeting receptors such as TIGIT not only promotes M2-TAMs reprogramming but also synergises with pro-phagocytic signalling pathways like anti-CD47 therapies [108]. At the same time, the key role of specific CAF subsets in fostering an immunosuppressive niche makes them important therapeutic targets [125]. However, the clinical translation of these stromal and innate immune-targeting strategies faces major challenges. For many agents in early-phase trials, a central question is whether modulating these non-cancer cell populations can ultimately improve patient survival – a challenge common to stroma-targeting approaches [126].

Integrating genomic data with transcriptomic information is propelling precision immunotherapy towards a new era guided by genomic insights. Multi-omics studies have revealed that although HRD tumours exhibit an inflamed phenotype, they frequently undergo immune editing (such as HLA loss), leading to T-cell dysfunction. This explains why ICIs alone yield limited efficacy [109]. This finding provides a rationale for combination therapy with PARPis and ICIs, targeting cancer cell vulnerabilities while reviving anti-tumor immunity. Conversely, studies have shown that mutations in the ARID1A gene activate the cGAS-STING pathway, creating a TME rich in immune cells and recruiting tumor-reactive T cells for infiltration. This genetic characteristic thus becomes an effective biomarker for predicting the efficacy of ICIs, allowing for better patient selection [113, 114].

However, translating such promising multi-omics discoveries into clinical applications remains a significant challenge. First, although the mechanism of action for combining PARPis with ICIs is understood, the inconsistent clinical trial results indicate that its therapeutic efficacy requires verification in larger-scale, prospective studies [127]. Second, for biomarkers such as ARID1A mutations to achieve clinical utility, current identification techniques must be translated into standardized, straightforward, reliable, and cost-effective testing protocols. Only then will large-scale implementation become feasible.

Conclusions and perspectives

Transcriptome sequencing technology has significantly advanced OC research and is now a key component of precision medicine. As illustrated in Fig. 3, RNA-seq technology encompasses all facets of OC research: it not only enables in-depth analysis of the pathological origins and mechanisms of tumourigenesis, characterising the complex TME, but also reveals epigenetic dysregulation, identifies potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets, and elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying drug resistance. Nevertheless, we are also aware that this review has certain inherent limitations. As a narrative comprehensive review, the viewpoints and conclusions presented in the article are largely constrained by the scope and quality level of the existing original research literature. Given that RNA-seq technology is in a rapid development stage, this review essentially only reflects the research progress in this field up to the time of literature retrieval completion, and more innovative research methods may emerge subsequently. Although we have strived to cover multiple application directions extensively, the focus on certain influential research results may still unintentionally introduce a certain degree of selection bias.

However, translating molecular discoveries into clinical applications presents significant interpretative challenges. A primary concern is the rigorous validation of transcriptomic findings. While scRNA-seq has remarkably revealed cellular heterogeneity, it is susceptible to technical artifacts—including tissue dissociation-induced stress, batch effects, and variability in clustering and differential expression [28]. Therefore, confirming whether rare cell populations or specific cellular states possess genuine biological significance necessitates cross-validation through complementary techniques such as spatial transcriptomics, multiplex fluorescence imaging, or in situ detection [128]. Ultimately, such findings must be further corroborated in large-scale, multicentre, prospective cohort studies.

In OC research, advances in transcriptomics increasingly rely on the integration of multidimensional data. To comprehensively map disease profiles, RNA-seq data must be analyzed in synergy with genomic, epigenetic, proteomic, and digital pathology information [128]. Against this backdrop, AI and ML technologies are progressively becoming indispensable analytical tools. These tools can provide a deep exploration of the complex correlations within and across these massive datasets, enabling the prediction of patient prognosis, evaluation of drug responses, identification of new biomarker spectra, and the revelation of potential patterns when integrating transcriptomic and histopathological images. This AI-driven, multi-omics analysis will serve as the core pathway for converting massive amounts of data into clinically applicable knowledge [18, 129, 130].

As a pivotal technology, SRT bridges the critical gap between single-cell resolution and tissue-level context. Future advancements must focus on enhancing its spatial resolution and detection sensitivity, incorporating three-dimensional tissue structures, and establishing a robust framework for spatial multi-omics. From a computational perspective, significant challenges exist; the development of novel bioinformatic approaches is essential for interpreting complex spatial data and accurately distinguishing biological signals from background noise [131].

To achieve effective integration with clinical practice, the key lies in the joint efforts from multiple dimensions. Technically, it is not only necessary to improve and enhance scRNA-seq technology to increase its detection sensitivity and throughput, but also to build a computational analysis system capable of integrating multi-omics data and spatial location information. At the same time, it is crucial to enhance the scientific understanding of the spatial regulatory mechanisms within the TME. Ultimately, all research progress must be integrated into clinical practice, systematically transforming scattered discoveries into validated diagnostic and prognostic systems, thereby promoting the realization of patient stratification treatment strategies based on comprehensive molecular characteristics.

In conclusion, promoting the development of RNA-seq in the field of OC requires the joint efforts of technological innovation and algorithm optimization. It is even more inseparable from the in-depth cross-border integration of basic research, computational biology, and clinical medicine. With the integration of advanced analytical methods powered by AI, transcriptome sequencing may evolve from a research tool into a reliable pillar of clinical practice, thereby improving patient outcomes and enhancing their quality of life.
