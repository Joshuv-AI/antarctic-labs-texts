Significance of eGFR and proteinuria for cardiovascular disease in individuals beyond 85 years of age
Authors: Tatsuhiko Azegami, Hidehiro Kaneko, Akira Okada, Yuta Suzuki, Katsuhito Fujiu, Hiroyuki Morita, Norifumi Takeda, Norihiko Takeda, Akira Fukui, Takashi Yokoo, Koichi Node, Hideo Yasunaga, Masaomi Nangaku, Kaori Hayashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThere are limited data on how advancing age influences prediction of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and proteinuria, especially in older adults, including those aged ≥85 years old. This study aimed to clarify the association of eGFR and proteinuria with CVD outcomes and the impact of age on this association.MethodsThe distribution of eGFR and urine protein in Japan was assessed retrospectively using real-world administrative claims and health checkup data collected between April 2014 and November 2022. We investigated the associations of these two parameters with the incidence of CVD, with an emphasis on the impact of aging.ResultsWe assessed 1 829 020 individuals for distribution of eGFR and proteinuria; after excluding those with known CVD, their association with CVD risk was examined in 1 040 101 individuals aged ≥40 years. The prevalence of impaired kidney function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) increased with age, being 0.7%, 9.2%, 21.9%, 40.2% and 60.2% at the ages of 18–39, 40–64, 65–74, 75–84 and ≥85 years, respectively (P for trend <.001); similarly, the proportion with positive proteinuria increased with age, being 2.7%, 4.3%, 5.6%, 9.2% and 15.8%, respectively (P for trend <.001). Both eGFR and urine protein were identified to be independent risk factors for CVD. Hazard ratios for CVD increased significantly when eGFR was <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 at the ages of 40–64, 65–74 and 75–84 years and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 at ≥85 years, while proteinuria remained significantly associated with a high CVD risk regardless of age. These findings were consistent even when analyzed separately by sex.ConclusionsThis study identified eGFR and urine dipstick proteinuria to be independent risk factors for CVD, even among individuals aged ≥85 years. However, the contribution of eGFR to the CVD risk was attenuated by aging, whereas proteinuria remained less affected by advancing age.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, estimated glomerular filtration rate, older adults, proteinuria

INTRODUCTION

Individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are at high risk of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1–3]. The global prevalence of CKD is increasing as a result of population growth and aging and is estimated to be 9.1% [2]. Therefore, CKD is considered one of the most significant diseases impacting global health.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is calculated from age and the serum creatinine level, with the cutoff value for impaired kidney function set at below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [4]. However, this threshold does not account for normal kidney aging. Therefore, whether an eGFR of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 is an appropriate threshold for predicting the risk of CVD remains controversial. In the past decade, several studies have addressed this controversy and examined the association between eGFR values and outcomes in older adults [5–7]. However, data for individuals aged ≥85 years are scarce.

On the other hand, proteinuria is considered a reliable marker of kidney damage at any age because it does not occur solely because of normal kidney aging. In older adults with diabetes, the association between albuminuria category and the risk of end-stage kidney disease has been found to be consistent across all age groups [8]. However, no study has assessed the association between proteinuria and CVD in the general population according to age group.

Both CKD and CVD are common in older adults [9, 10] and impose a significant economic burden [11, 12]. Global disability-adjusted life years attributable to CVD have been increasing [13], emphasizing the importance of strengthening primary prevention. Therefore, it could be beneficial to stratify the risk of CVD based on eGFR and proteinuria, particularly in older adults. The aims of this study were to clarify the association of eGFR and proteinuria with CVD outcomes in the general population and to assess the impact of age on this association using real-world data, with the ultimate goal of contributing to primary prevention of CVD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study had a retrospective observational design and analyzed data from DeSC Healthcare Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The DeSC database contains individual health insurance claims for both inpatients and outpatients based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) coding for employees of large companies (Kempo), the National Health Insurance for nonemployees (Kokuho) and the Advanced Elderly Medical Service System for people aged ≥75 years (Koki Koreisha Iryo Seido). Primary and secondary prevention programs, including annual health checkups, are mandatory for the general population in Japan, where more than 70% of men aged 45–54 years undergo health checkups at least once a year [14]. In addition to health insurance claims data, the DeSC database includes annual health checkup records, including blood pressure (BP) measurements, fasting laboratory data and responses to a questionnaire regarding medical history, lifestyle and current medications.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tokyo (approval number 2021010NI) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because all data included in the dataset were de-identified.

Study participants

We analyzed data for 1 829 020 individuals who were enrolled in the DeSC database between April 2014 and November 2022 and had data available on serum creatinine and urine dipstick protein to determine the distribution of eGFR and urine protein across age groups. Data for individuals with a history of CVD [myocardial infarction (MI), angina pectoris (AP), heart failure (HF) and stroke], those with missing data on cigarette smoking and physical activity, and those under the age of 40 years were excluded. We then assessed the association of eGFR and urine protein with the incidence of CVD in 1 040 101 individuals stratified by age group (Fig. 1).

Flowchart showing the participant selection process. We extracted data for 1 829 020 individuals enrolled in the DeSC database between April 2014 and November 2022 with data available on serum creatinine and urine dipstick protein and assessed the distribution of eGFR and proteinuria. Subsequently, we excluded individuals with a history of myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, heart failure or stroke, those with missing data on cigarette smoking or physical activity, and those aged under 40 years. Finally, we analyzed 1 040 101 individuals to assess the association of eGFR and proteinuria with CVD.

Measurements and definitions

We collected baseline data on age, sex, body mass index, serum creatinine, urine dipstick protein, systolic and diastolic BP, low-density and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides and HbA1c. We obtained information on cigarette smoking (current or non-current), alcohol consumption (every day or not every day) and physical activity (not engaging in either 30 min of exercise two or more times a week or ≥1 h of walking per day, or engaging in either of these activities) from a self-reported questionnaire completed during the health checkup. We also retrieved data on replacement therapy (dialysis and kidney transplantation) and CVD (MI, AP, HF and stroke) from the administrative claims records.

We defined hypertension as a systolic BP ≥140 mmHg, a diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg or use of BP-lowering medications, diabetes as an HbA1c ≥6.5% or use of blood glucose-lowering medications, and dyslipidemia as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥140 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL or use of lipid-lowering medications. Proteinuria was categorized as negative, trace or positive (1+, 2+ or 3+) based on a urine dipstick test. We calculated the eGFR using the following formula: eGFR = 194 × serum creatinine − 1.094 × age − 0.287 (×0.739 for women) [15]. The eGFR was categorized into six groups: ≥90, 60–89, 45–59, 30–44, 15–29 and <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 (including those receiving renal replacement therapy).

Outcome

The primary outcome was a composite cardiovascular endpoint that included MI (ICD-10 codes I210–I214 and I219), AP (ICD-10 codes I200, I201, I208 and I209), HF (ICD-10 codes I500, I501, I509 and I110) and stroke (ICD-10 codes I630, I631–I636, I638, I639, I600–I611, I613–I616, I619, I629 and G459), which were assessed for each of four age categories (40–64, 65–74, 75–84 and ≥85 years).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as the number (percentage). The distribution of eGFR categories and proteinuria categories is presented as the proportion for each age group, as well as the distribution of eGFR categories by age group in the entire population and in individuals with negative proteinuria. We assessed the prevalence of impaired kidney function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and positive proteinuria in each age group using the Cochran–Armitage trend test. We also evaluated the correlation between age and eGFR, as well as proteinuria, using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient test in seemingly healthy individuals without comorbidities.

We conducted Cox regression analyses to assess the association of eGFR and proteinuria with subsequent risk of CVD. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the composite CVD endpoint were calculated for each eGFR value using a reference value of ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 and for each proteinuria value using negative proteinuria as the reference value. We calculated HRs after adjusting for covariates, including age, sex, body mass index, cigarette smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes and physical activity. To validate the robustness of our primary findings, we conducted subgroup analyses by sex and performed sensitivity analyses for each outcome, redefining them as ischemic heart disease (MI and AP), HF or stroke. In addition, considering the possibility of death as a competing risk, we extracted a subset with available data about life and death (n = 725 104). We then performed the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model as a competing risks analysis. For the analysis using age-adjusted eGFR reference values, HRs for composite CVD endpoint were also evaluated using a reference value of eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 for age 40–64 years and eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2 for age ≥65 years. Using the same reference value, HRs for composite CVD endpoint based on eGFR calculated by the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation were evaluated [16]. We also stratified the composite risk of CVD using a combination of eGFR and proteinuria for each age group.

All analyses were performed using STATA version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), and the figures were created using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Distribution of eGFR and proteinuria by age

The prevalence of impaired kidney function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) increased with age, reaching 0.7%, 9.2%, 21.9%, 40.2% and 60.2% at the ages of 18–39, 40–64, 65–74, 75–84 and ≥85 years, respectively (P for trend <.001) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the proportion of individuals with eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 also increased with age, being 0.1%, 0.8%, 2.5%, 8.3% and 22.7% (P for trend <.001) (Supplementary data, Table S2). The proportion that was positive for proteinuria also increased with age, being 2.7%, 4.3%, 5.6%, 9.2% and 15.8%, respectively (P for trend <.001) (Fig. 2B). In individuals without comorbidities, the correlation between age and proteinuria was smaller (Spearman's rank correlation coefficient 0.0135; P < .001) compared with the correlation between age and either eGFR values (coefficient –0.4305; P < .001) or eGFR categories (coefficient 0.3550; P < .001) (Supplementary data, Table S3).

Distribution of eGFR (A) and proteinuria (B) according to age.

Risk of CVD according to eGFR and age

Overall, a lower eGFR was associated with a higher risk of CVD (Figs 3 and 4A). Among participants with an eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, using an eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 as a reference, the HRs (95% CIs) for CVD were 3.72 (2.82–4.92), 2.92 (2.22–3.82), 3.74 (2.87–4.87) and 1.91 (1.26–2.91) at the ages of 40–64, 65–74, 75–84 and ≥85 years, respectively. The trend of decreasing HR with age was consistently observed in both males and females (Supplementary data, Fig. S1). The HR for CVD increased significantly when the eGFR was <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 at the ages of 40–64, 65–74 and 75–84 years, and when it was <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 at the age of ≥85 years. The impact of eGFR on the risk of CVD diminished with increasing age (Fig. 4A). We also conducted sensitivity analyses for the risks of ischemic heart disease, HF and stroke. Similar to the composite CVD outcome, a lower eGFR was associated with an increased risk of each of these outcomes (Supplementary data, Figs S2–S4). HRs for composite CVD outcome that were evaluated using a reference value of eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 for age 40–64 years, and eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2 for age ≥65 years exhibited a similar trend to the results obtained using a fixed reference value (Supplementary data, Fig. S5). Using the same reference value, consistent results were obtained when evaluating HRs for composite CVD outcome based on eGFR calculated by the CKD-EPI equation (Supplementary data, Fig. S6). In the competing risks analysis as well, we observed results consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary data, Fig. S7).

Risk of CVD according to proteinuria and age

When stratified by proteinuria, the risk of CVD increased significantly with trace proteinuria compared with negative proteinuria in all age groups, and even further with positive proteinuria (Figs 4B and 5). Among participants with positive proteinuria, the HRs (95% CIs) for CVD were 1.50 (1.43–1.57), 1.43 (1.38–1.47), 1.31 (1.26–1.36) and 1.27 (1.19–1.35), respectively, at the ages of 40–64, 65–74, 75–84 and ≥85 years. The results were consistently observed in both males and females (Supplementary data, Fig. S8). The impact of trace or positive proteinuria on the risk of CVD did not diminish with increasing age (Fig. 4B). In the analyses of the risks for ischemic heart disease, HF and stroke, as with the composite CVD outcome, there was a tendency for the risk to increase with the degree of proteinuria (Supplementary data, Figs S9–S11). In the competing risks analysis, proteinuria was associated with an increase in CVD risk across all age groups, in a manner dependent on its severity (Supplementary data, Fig. S12).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide real-world study, we used data from 1 829 020 participants to assess the distribution of eGFR and proteinuria and examined the association between these factors and the subsequent risk of CVD in 1 040 101 participants, with an emphasis on evaluating the impact of aging on their association. At any age, including individuals aged ≥85 years, a low eGFR and proteinuria were associated with a higher risk of the composite CVD outcome. However, the association between eGFR and the risk of CVD weakened with advancing age while the association between proteinuria and the risk of CVD remained significant regardless of age.

The prevalence of impaired kidney function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and positive proteinuria increased with age and peaked at 60.2% and 15.8%, respectively, in the oldest age group (i.e. those aged ≥85 years). Although data on the prevalence of CKD in older adults aged ≥75 years are limited, the prevalence has been reported to be 56.1% in the US population aged ≥75 years [17], 35.9% in Japanese men and 26.3% in Japanese women aged ≥75 years [18], and 62.3% and 60.5%, respectively, in Icelandic men and women aged ≥85 years [19]. The diagnosis of CKD is primarily based on proteinuria in younger individuals, whereas in the older population it is largely determined by a decline in eGFR [18]. These findings are generally consistent with the results of our present study.

The clinical importance of decreased eGFR in older adults is still controversial, and whether it is a pathological condition or a physiological change attributable to aging is unclear. There have been proposals to establish age-adapted eGFR cutoff values for the definition of CKD; suggestions include an eGFR of <75 mL/min/1.73 m2 for younger persons and <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 for older persons [20]. The fixed eGFR threshold (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2) irrespective of age has resulted in a 60% higher incidence of CKD in comparison with an age-adapted eGFR cutoff value [5]. Furthermore, in comparison with use of an age-adapted threshold, use of a fixed eGFR threshold is associated with lower risks of kidney failure (1.7% vs 3.0%) and death (21.9% vs 25.4%) at 5 years [5], suggesting that use of a fixed eGFR threshold may lead to overdiagnosis of CKD. In the older population, when kidney failure and death were considered as outcomes, there was no significant difference in the prognosis between individuals with an eGFR of 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m2 and those without CKD (i.e. an eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2) [5]. Based on the assessment of the association between eGFR and kidney failure or death in that study [5], our present research focused specifically on the relationship between eGFR and CVD. The risk of CVD increased significantly when the eGFR decreased to <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 in those aged 40–84 years and to <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 in those aged ≥85 years. This finding was consistent even when analyzed separately by sex, confirming the robustness of the results. From the perspective of risk of CVD, these findings raise concerns that using an eGFR of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 as a threshold may overestimate the risk of CVD in the older population.

In this study, the correlation between age and proteinuria was small in individuals without comorbidities, suggesting that normal aging per se may not significantly contribute to the development of proteinuria. This is because, unlike eGFR, which is influenced by aging, proteinuria serves as a direct marker of glomerular damage. The urine dipstick protein measurement is associated with the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [21]. Furthermore, in the older population, the presence of trace or positive urine protein on dipstick testing is significantly associated with a higher mortality risk [22]. However, it has not yet been confirmed whether the prognostic impact of proteinuria is modified by aging. We addressed this issue and found that the association between proteinuria and the risk of CVD remained significant regardless of age. The urine albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR), used as a diagnostic criterion for CKD, relies on urinary creatinine for its calculation. However, in older individuals with reduced muscle mass and creatinine production, there is a potential for overestimation. On the other hand, qualitative assessment of urine protein using dipstick does not rely on urinary creatinine, making it potentially less influenced by aging compared with UACR. From a primary prevention standpoint, urinalysis with dipsticks, being minimally invasive and cost-effective, appears to be meaningful for assessment of the risk of CVD in older adults.

The present study has several clinical implications. In the context of stratifying the risk of CVD for primary prevention purposes, both eGFR and proteinuria are considered valuable tools. However, setting an eGFR cutoff value of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 may classify many older adults as being at higher risk than they actually are. It is important to note that even if classified as CKD G3a, individuals aged ≥65 years with negative proteinuria may not necessarily have a high risk of CVD. Treating all of them as candidates for preventive care could pose a burden on healthcare and raise pharmacoeconomic concerns. In view of our present results, it might be reasonable to lower the eGFR cutoff for stratification of the risk of CVD in older adults to 45 or even 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. On the other hand, assessment of proteinuria using a urine dipstick, which can be easily employed in routine clinical practice, appears to be cost-effective for stratifying the risk of CVD and less susceptible to the impact of advancing age. However, aging is a potent risk factor for CVD, and in the older population where the absolute risk of CVD is high, it is essential to be aware that even if the relative risk increase is small, it may still have a significant impact on public health. In addition, further investigation is needed to determine the effectiveness of management and interventions for prevention of CVD in individuals identified as high-risk based on stratification of the risk of CVD using eGFR and proteinuria.

This study has several limitations. First, although we conducted multivariate Cox regression analyses to assess the associations of eGFR and proteinuria with the risk of CVD, it was not possible to fully eliminate the possibility of unmeasured confounders, such as the duration of impaired kidney function and proteinuria, family history of CVD and socioeconomic status. These factors may have influenced our results but no relevant data were available in the DeSC database. Second, CVD was diagnosed based on ICD-10 codes for MI, AP, HF and stroke, raising concerns about diagnostic accuracy. Therefore, we conducted separate sensitivity analyses for ischemic heart disease (MI and AP), HF and stroke. Third, although the DeSC database broadly encompasses health insurance information for employees (Kempo), nonemployees (Kokuho) and individuals aged ≥75 years (Koki Koreisha Iryo Seido), it is unclear whether our findings can be generalized to other populations. Fourth, since data on cystatin C were not available in this study, the findings obtained using creatinine-based eGFR may weaken the conclusion of the study. Grams et al. have clearly shown that eGFR calculated using both creatinine and cystatin C reflects cardiovascular outcomes more sharply than eGFR based solely on creatinine [23]. Fifth, quantitative assessments such as UACR or protein–creatinine ratio data were not available in this study because urine testing using dipstick is commonly performed in health checkups due to its cost-effectiveness and simplicity in Japan. While dipstick urine protein testing is useful as a simple screening method for primary prevention of CVD, it has low sensitivity and positive predictive value for detecting A2-level protein–creatinine ratio in quantitative tests [24].

This large-scale study of real-world data identified both eGFR and urine dipstick protein to be independent risk factors for CVD, even among the older adults, including those aged ≥85 years. However, the contribution of eGFR to the risk of CVD was attenuated by aging, while proteinuria remained significantly associated with a high risk of CVD regardless of age.
