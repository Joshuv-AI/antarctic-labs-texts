Deformable foot orthoses shift the functional roles of foot and ankle joints during walking gait
Authors: Adrienne Henderson, Dustin Bruening, Elisa Arch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon fiber deformable foot orthoses (DFOs) with customizable stiffness may benefit clinical populations by allowing an individual approach to enhancing gait energetics. Prior research suggests that increasing DFO stiffness can decrease energy loss while enhancing energy return at the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, making it more spring-like. However, it remains unclear whether increasing DFO stiffness enhances the mechanics of the midtarsal and ankle joints or only the MTP joint, especially during walking. This study investigated whether walking with increasingly stiff DFOs impacted mechanics or functional roles (damper, motor, spring, strut) of the MTP, midtarsal, and ankle joints. When walking with increased DFO stiffness, the MTP joint showed decreased peak negative power and increased positive work/peak power resulting in more spring- and strut-like behavior. The midtarsal joint had increased peak negative work and decreased positive work resulting in more damper-like and less motor-like behavior. The ankle joint only decreased peak positive power but not work resulting in minimal changes in functional roles. Overall, stiffness-driven energetic gains at the MTP joint were offset by proximal compensations at the midtarsal, suggesting that in healthy gait, DFO-induced energetic benefits may be limited to the MTP joint rather than positively impacting the entire ankle–foot system.

Introduction

Deformable foot orthoses (DFOs) are commonly used in clinical populations (e.g. stroke, cerebral palsy, spinal cord injuries, etc.), typically as part of ankle foot orthoses (AFOs), with the goals of controlling ankle motion and stabilizing gait. The DFO is often thought of simply acting as an interface with footwear but has the potential for modulating ankle and foot energetics1,2, either by itself or in combination with AFOs. DFO stiffness, or its ability to resist deformation under load, impacts how much mechanical energy can be stored and returned during gait and can readily be customized during the fabrication process. Because DFO stiffness can directly influence the foot’s overall mechanical properties (foot + footwear), customizing DFO stiffness could allow for a targeted approach to addressing individual patients’ needs and improve overall gait function. However, since few past DFO studies have directly examined foot joints, the joint-level adaptations resulting from different DFO stiffness levels remain poorly understood.

Efficient gait depends on coordination among the ankle and foot joints. The metatarsophalangeal (MTP) and midtarsal joints are strongly coupled kinematically and kinetically in terminal stance3. The MTP joint shows substantial deformation (i.e. toe extension) and negative work earning it the title of the foot’s primary energy sink1. This large amount of negative work is partially offset by the midtarsal joint (combination of talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints), which produces a substantial amount of positive work during terminal stance3. The midtarsal joint’s positive work may come from active muscle contractions4,5, elastic energy storage and return in the plantar fascia and other ligaments in early stance6, additional elastic contributions from the tension placed on the plantar fascia in late stance from toe extension7, or a combination of these mechanisms. The ankle joint also contributes substantial amounts of positive work in terminal stance1, working closely with the midtarsal joint to achieve sufficient power for push-off3. However, the ankle joint’s contribution to late-stance positive work can be overestimated when the midtarsal and ankle joints are not considered separately8.

To date, evidence of DFO’s energetic benefits is derived primarily from studies focused on running gait (e.g.9–13,) with limited studies on walking and clinical applications. Studies on running performance show that increasing stiffness under the foot through DFO use decreases the amount of negative work at the MTP joint by decreasing its dorsiflexion angular velocity and peak dorsiflexion angle14,15. In walking, this same mechanism could decrease the need for positive work via active muscular contraction at the midfoot and ankle, therefore providing a direct energetic benefit to patients. Additionally, some MTP joint deformation during this portion of gait also deforms the DFO, storing mechanical energy which can be returned to the foot during propulsion in a spring-like action. Thus, DFOs may have a dual energetic benefit to its users: a decreased need for ankle and midtarsal positive work either by decreasing the amount of energy lost via a stiffer MTP joint, and by adding positive work from the stored spring energy in the DFO14,15. Similarly, two walking gait studies demonstrated a significant reduction in negative work and an increase in positive work when walking with DFOs when compared to walking with no DFOs and when calculated from the power curve for all structures distal to the shank (encompassing all ankle and foot joints), though the individual foot joints were not analyzed2,16. To determine whether patient populations, who are commonly confined to walking speeds, can obtain similar energetic benefits, it is first necessary to determine how DFOs alter the energetics and functions of the foot and ankle joints during walking.

The purpose of this study was to quantify how using DFOs with various stiffness levels during healthy walking impacts foot and ankle joint energetics and function. We hypothesized that late-stance MTP joint positive work would significantly increase while negative work would significantly decrease with stiffer DFOs, allowing the MTP joint to significantly decrease its damping role and shift its function to be more spring-like (H1). We also hypothesized that late-stance midtarsal joint positive work would significantly increase while negative work significantly decreased resulting in a similar increase in spring function with stiffer DFOs (H2). Last, we hypothesized that there would be no significant change in ankle joint positive and negative work resulting in the ankle continuing to function primarily as a strut and secondarily as a spring (H3)1. The results from this study have the potential to inform our understanding of how assistive devices influence natural foot mechanics and the functional roles of the ankle, midtarsal and MTP joints, ultimately leading to more effective approaches for optimizing lower extremity and ankle–foot assistive device design and prescription.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to quantify how using DFOs with various stiffness levels during healthy walking impacts foot and ankle joint energetics and function during stance. Our results show that increasing DFO forefoot stiffness decreased MTP joint negative work while increasing its positive work, making it more spring and strut-like, but less damper-like. Conversely, the midtarsal joint increased its negative work making it more damper-like and less motor-like. Last, adding stiffness showed no significant differences in negative or positive work at the ankle joint.

MTP

The MTP joint is the main damper of the foot, dissipating large amounts of energy (negative work) while generating minimal amounts of energy (positive work)1,15. Because this energy loss is thought to contribute to reduced overall ankle–foot energetic efficiency, DFOs are designed in part to target and decrease MTP joint energy loss, with the goal of enhancing overall ankle–foot energetics12. The typical damping role of the MTP joint was confirmed in the present study, especially when considering the MS condition, which is analogous to walking barefoot16. Each increase in DFO forefoot stiffness significantly reduced the MTP joint’s damper-like role during stance by reducing the amount of negative work (supports H1). Unsurprisingly, as DFO forefoot stiffness was added, the MTP joint acted as a stiffer joint overall, as seen by its increased strut index. This increase in strut-like behavior comes from a combination of decreased peak dorsiflexion angle with a significantly increased plantarflexion moment (primarily a result of a more anterior COP2). In the stiffest DFO condition (9L), this shift in negative work and plantarflexion moment was large enough to shift the MTP joint’s primary functional role from a damper to a strut.

Increasing DFO forefoot stiffness also resulted in increased positive work and to a less extent, decreased negative work. This increased positive work likely indicates an increased proportion of energy at the MTP joint is being stored in the DFOs (as opposed to dissipated) then later returned as positive work. This likely was the primary influence for the increased spring index (supports H1). Although the magnitude of increase in spring index is somewhat less than the increase in the strut index, it nevertheless confirms that DFOs are capable of storing and returning some energy to the MTP joint12,16. This alteration in mechanics suggests DFOs help the MTP joint use energy more efficiently, which could positively influence gait energetics of clinical populations who use DFOs or other assistive devices.

Midtarsal

In contrast to the MTP joint, the midtarsal joint’s roles did not change with DFO forefoot stiffness. The midtarsal joint primarily acted as a strut during stance with a much smaller secondary spring role. As DFO forefoot stiffness increased, the midtarsal joint’s limited ROM was further constrained as the arch was prevented from rising during terminal stance, reducing the midtarsal joint’s potential to produce positive work. Considering these changes, it is surprising that increasing DFO forefoot stiffness did not act to increase the midtarsal joint’s function as a strut similar to the MTP joint. This may be due to a possible longer time to heel rise as seen with DFO use16–18 and its associated negative work with increased footwear stiffness.

Changes in midtarsal joint mechanics primarily resulted in a trade-off between damper- and motor-like behavior at this joint. Although these changes were small in magnitude, they are likely impactful to midtarsal function, as added stiffness appeared to suppress active energy generation (i.e., extrinsic and intrinsic foot muscles) while increasing energy dissipation (partially supporting H2). This shift was driven primarily by greater negative work across conditions and reduced positive work with any added stiffness. The balance of positive and negative work was further impacted by a delayed peak negative power, likely resulting from delayed peak dorsiflexion angle (i.e., peak arch drop). This delay suggests that increased DFO forefoot stiffness may prolong the eccentric demand on intrinsic and extrinsic arch-supporting musculature. The extended eccentric phase could limit the time available for active energy generation during terminal stance, thereby reducing midtarsal positive work and its motor contribution to gait. Additional work with electromyography (EMG) and musculoskeletal modeling are needed to confirm this proposed mechanism. Existing evidence is limited and potentially contradictory. One prior study using EMG shows greater muscle activation (abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis) while running in conventional shoes compared to barefoot5; whereas, studies on extended minimalist footwear usage show greater muscle size and strength adaptations (abductor hallucis, flexor hallucis brevis, abductor digiti minimi, flexor digitorum brevis, quadratus plantae)19,20. The increased damper role and decreased motor role at the midtarsal reported in the present study add to the mixed evidence of intrinsic foot muscle function in shod gait.

Consistent with previous studies describing the midtarsal joint as spring-like6,21,22, our findings quantitatively demonstrate a substantial spring index which remained relatively unchanged across all conditions. Interestingly, despite the DFOs’ ability to store and return energy at the MTP joint, this effect did not extend to the midtarsal joint. The absence of increased spring-like behavior may be a result of the strong kinematic coupling between the midtarsal and MTP joints23, where reduced MTP motion under stiffer conditions may act to limit midtarsal joint rise. Alternatively, although we increased the MTP joint’s spring-like role during stance, a compensatory decrease in midtarsal or ankle spring function to maintain overall foot–ankle system compliance (e.g.5,) did not occur due to a timing mismatch. Specifically, the increased MTP spring index was primarily driven by greater late-stance positive power, while the proposed compliance-regulation mechanism operates during early stance through loading the foot’s arch and shoe foam. A third option is the spring-like function at the midtarsal joint may come from the natural energy storage and return from passive soft tissue crossing this joint such as titin (an elastic protein within muscle), tendons, ligaments, or the plantar fascia which may not change with footwear stiffness. Thus, changing DFO forefoot stiffness may not meaningfully alter these tissues’ functional demands, resulting in a consistent spring index across conditions.

Ankle

In the MS condition, the ankle joint behaved primarily as a strut and secondarily as a spring, consistent with findings from our previous work using a multi-segment foot model1. However, these findings are in contrast with past studies modeling the foot as a single rigid segment, which reported reversed role weightings. Assigning all foot motion to the ankle joint, as happens in single-segment foot models, likely inflates the ankle joint’s positive and negative work estimates24, thereby lowering the calculated strut index enough for it to no longer appear as the primary role of the ankle. Adding stiffness did not alter the ankle joint’s primary or secondary roles in the present study, supporting H3, and suggesting that footwear stiffness may confine most of its effects within the foot itself. The only notable deviation was in the 5L condition, where a small reduction in the strut role and a corresponding increase in the motor role mirrored changes in the midtarsal joint. This may reflect a compensatory ankle response to functional shifts in the more distal midtarsal joint in order to preserve overall gait mechanics, though the absence of this response in the stiffer DFO conditions remains unclear.

The most pronounced change in ankle joint mechanics was an increase in dorsiflexion angle with the stiffer DFO conditions. This likely reflects a compensatory mechanism used by participants in order to sufficiently load the DFOs, thereby allowing a more typical MTP dorsiflexion angle. The greater ankle dorsiflexion appears to be a key factor underlying the previously reported anterior shift in the COP with DFO usage, which act to lengthen the external moment arms of the ankle, midtarsal, and MTP joints2. While we did not focus on COP position in the present study, the differences seen in MTP and midtarsal peak moments are likely driven by small moment arm length increases (more anterior COP) with increasing stiffness12. However, because the ankle typically experiences larger forces than the midtarsal or MTP joints, a small shift in COP may not be sufficient to meaningfully alter the ankle’s plantarflexion moment or its overall functional role12.

Limitations

This study used previously published equations to calculate the functional indices for each joint for stance phase. While this method provides for a more accessible interpretation of data and direct clinical relevance, there are important considerations to the equations especially in regard to the spring index, which relies on the assumption that there is energy being stored and returned as opposed to energy being fully dissipated and then fully generated. Interpretation of the functional indices should be done with this limitation in mind. Additionally, we opted to use a standardized walking velocity as this provides the most direct comparison between participants. However, allowing participants to self-select their walking velocity could unveil additional energetic benefits from DFO use. Including EMG of some key muscles in the leg and foot could further the interpretation of future studies by helping to confirm muscle contribution to functional indices especially for motor and damper roles. Last, while six foot strikes per condition is a relatively small sample, this is somewhat moderated by a within-subjects experimental design and the use of a young, healthy population who typically walk with low gait variability25; however practical constraints related to overground data collection limited the feasible number of trials.

Conclusion

Past studies which have aimed to enhance gait function through the use of DFOs, theorized the decreased energy loss at the MTP joint would increase the overall mechanical efficiency of the foot–ankle complex26,27. While our results clearly show enhanced MTP joint energetics, this benefit seemed to be washed out or possibly even negated by the midtarsal and ankle joint’s response to the DFOs, resulting in no clear energetic advantage to the foot–ankle-complex during healthy walking gait. Specifically, enhanced energetics would be reflected by an increased spring index (more passive energy storage and return) or a decreased damper index (less energy loss) at the midtarsal or ankle joints. However, neither joint demonstrated these changes. Instead, the midtarsal joint showed a significant increase in damper function, opposite of what would be considered an advantage.

This lack of enhanced overall ankle–foot energetics with increasingly stiff DFOs may be due to the fact that walking mechanics in healthy individuals are already likely optimized, especially at a typical walking speed, and changing one aspect of these mechanics (e.g., decreased MTP energy dissipation) will result in another area of the body substituting for that change (e.g., increased midtarsal joint energy dissipation)28. It is still possible that patients who have decreased ankle–foot energetic capabilities could show an enhancement in gait function with energetic assistance at the MTP joint provided by the DFO29. This concept has been proposed in the past as the Work Substitution/Enhancement Paradigm, where use of an assistive device does not meaningfully improve overall gait in healthy individuals, as any biomechanical benefits are typically offset by compensatory changes at other joints (substitution30). However, customizing the same device for individual patients’ gait deficits can enhance their overall gait function (enhancement29). Accordingly, future studies should investigate the extent to which customizing DFO forefoot stiffness to an individual patient’s deficits can provide targeted energetic assistance and improve ankle–foot function.

Methods

Fifteen participants (7 male/ 9 female, 29.1 ± 5.6 yrs, 1.69 ± 0.1 m, 72.3 ± 13.4 kg) were recruited. Individuals were eligible for participation if they were aged 18–40 years, had a body mass index less than 40, and had no history of lower-extremity injury/disease within the previous six months. Individuals were excluded if they had a history of systemic or lower-extremity pathology that could alter their gait or if they had an unsafe or unsteady gait pattern. All participants gave written informed consent before participating. All experimental protocols were approved by the University of Delaware Institutional Review Board Approval No. 1535908-10 and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

DFO fabrication

Bilateral individualized DFOs were fabricated at the University of Delaware’s Center for Composite Materials. To fabricate the DFOs, thirteen layers (thickness: 2.3 mm) of pre-impregnated carbon fiber cloth were layered for the stiffer, posterior region of all DFOs. Three ply drops were incorporated in the midfoot region of the DFO such that the additional plies were terminated proximal to the metatarsal head axis. The more flexible anterior region contained either five, seven, or nine layers of carbon fiber (thickness 5L: 0.9 mm, 7L: 1.3 mm, 9L: 1.7 mm, respectively) for the three DFO conditions evaluated in this study (Fig. 5B). This tapered design was selected to more closely replicate the foot component of an AFO, where the posterior region transitions from the relatively thick ankle strut and must gradually decrease in thickness toward the forefoot to avoid impeding forefoot function. Layers were cured for one hour at 250° Fahrenheit with a 45-min heat up and cooldown period. Once the DFOs were manufactured, they were trimmed based on each participant’s shoe size and the location of their metatarsal head axis. A cantilever mechanical testing protocol determined the stiffness of two sets of representative DFOs (two tested DFOs per stiffness level) using an Instron (3345 1kip Mini Tensile Tester, 500N load cell, Instron, Norwood, MA, USA). Each DFO was clamped at the metatarsal head axis, and a vertical load was applied in the toe region at 5 cm distal to the clamp. Three cycles per DFO were completed at 0.25 mm/s to a total displacement of 2 mm. Bending stiffness was quantified as the slope of the force–displacement curve and averaged across the loading cycles per insole, then averaged across the DFOs within each stiffness level.

Visit 2

Participants returned to the laboratory once all the three pairs of DFOs were fabricated. A researcher applied a six-degree-of-freedom marker set to the participants’ legs and pelvis31. This set included markers placed on left/right anterior superior iliac spines, right/left posterior superior iliac spines, medial/lateral femoral epicondyles, medial/lateral malleoli, as well as marker clusters on each thigh and shank. A three-segment foot model was also used, which was composed of three separate anatomical segments (hindfoot, forefoot, and hallux) separated by two joints (midtarsal and MTP), and one near-massless connector segment spanning from the posterior heel to the ankle joint center24. The following markers were used to define and track the foot model: three heel markers (medial, lateral, and posterior), the navicular tuberosity, cuboid bone, medial aspect of the first MTP joint, lateral aspect of the fifth MTP joint, dorsum of the foot, and dorsum of the distal hallux (Fig. 5A).

After a static trial, participants walked across the laboratory floor at a height-normalized comfortable walking velocity (0.8 statures/second32) under four randomly presented footwear conditions: minimalist shoes (MS), minimalist shoes with the 5-layered DFOs placed inside (5L), with the 7-layered DFOs (7L), and with the 9-layered DFOs (9L). Kinematic and kinetic data were collected (motion capture: 200 Hz, force: 1200 Hz) until at least three full stance phases were recorded for both left and right feet for each condition. A LabView (National Instruments, Austin, TX) real-time feedback program monitored participants’ walking velocity to ensure participants remained within the desired threshold (± 0.05 stat/sec)33. Participants’ starting position was adjusted to prevent targeted foot placement and to ensure a full stance phase naturally occurred on the force plates. Foot markers were removed and reapplied between each footwear condition, and a new static trial was collected.

Data and statistical analysis

All motion capture and force data were imported into Visual 3D (HAS Motion, Kingston, ON, Canada), where an 11-segment model was created and applied. This model included segments for the pelvis, thighs, and shanks with a flexion/extension axis defined as parallel to the frontal plane in anatomical position. The longitudinal axes for the foot segments were parallel to the laboratory floor. The hindfoot segment extended from the posterior heel to the midtarsal joint center (midpoint between the cuboid and navicular markers). The forefoot extended from the midtarsal joint center to the MTP joint center (the point 25% along the metatarsal head axis from the 1st MTP). Last, the hallux segment extended from the MTP joint center to the distal hallux marker.

Data were filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 6 Hz for motion data and 25 Hz for force data. Heel strike and toe-off events were created using a 20N minimum force threshold. Forces were assigned to individual foot segments using the previously established center-of-pressure-cross method34. This method assigns forces to foot segments based on the anterior–posterior position of the center of pressure (COP).

Joint angle, moment, and power were calculated using standard inverse dynamics for the ankle, midtarsal, and MTP joints. These variables were time-normalized to 100% stance. Additionally, moment and power were normalized to participant mass. Analyzed metrics include peak positive/negative power, positive/negative work, peak dorsiflexion angle, total joint range of motion (ROM), and peak plantar flexion moment. Positive and negative work were calculated by integrating the area under the positive and negative power, respectively.

Functional indices were calculated for each joint for each condition. This method identifies and quantifies the mechanical role a joint assumes (damper, motor, spring, and/or strut) based on its power and moment profiles during stance, excluding any initial collision work35,36. The resultant moment, calculated as the vector sum of the joint moments in the three anatomical planes, was used for the ankle and midtarsal calculations while sagittal plane moment was used for the one-degree-of-freedom MTP joint. The damper index represents the amount that a joint behaves as a mechanical damper, dissipating energy whereas the motor index represents the amount it behaves as a mechanical motor, generating energy. The spring index represents the amount a joint behaves as a mechanical spring, storing then returning energy at a later time. Last, the strut index represents the amount a joint behaves as a mechanical strut, defined as a rigid connector based on the ratio of work to the joints’ impulse (e.g., small amounts of work with large joint moments results in a higher strut index).

All metrics and functional indices were statistically analyzed between the four footwear conditions using a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS (SPSS version 29 IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). Sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity. If the assumption of sphericity was violated, a Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. When the main effects were significant, a Holm correction was used in pairwise comparisons to test for significance. A Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to account for the multiple comparisons. Significance was set to ⍺ = 0.05. Figures, including line graphs and violin plots, were generated using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
