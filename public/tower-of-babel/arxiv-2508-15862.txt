Parameter Estimation of Magnetised Kerr Black Holes Using Their Shadows
Heena Ali, Sushant G. Ghosh

Abstract
We investigate the shadow features of magnetized Kerr black holes (MKBHs) using a deviation parameter B B that captures the effect of an external magnetic field on spacetime geometry. These spacetimes, of Petrov type D D and are asymptotically non-flat. We use the Hamilton-Jacobi equation’s separability to generate null geodesics and examine the crucial impact parameters for unstable photon orbits that define the black hole shadow. We carefully study how the magnetic field intensity B B and spin parameter a a influence shadow morphology, discovering that increasing B B enlarges the shadow while also introducing additional distortions, especially at high spins. We compute the shadow observables—area A A and oblateness D D —and create contour plots in the parameter space ( a / M , B ​ M ) (a/M,BM) to facilitate parameter estimation. We also investigate the dependence of the shadow on observer position, specifically altering the radial coordinate r O r_{O} and inclination angle θ \theta . The results reveal that as B B increases, so does the shadow size, and the distortion is affected by both spin and observer orientation. For far viewers, the shadow approaches its asymptotic shape, but finite-distance observers perceive substantial deviations. Our findings demonstrate that MKBH shadows encode clear imprints of magnetic deviations, thereby offering a potential avenue to distinguish Kerr from non-Kerr spacetimes and to probe magnetic effects in the strong-gravity regime.

IV
Shadow of Magnetized Kerr Black Holes

Here, we explore in detail the black hole shadow of MKBHs and analyze whether the presence of an external magnetic field introduces notable observational features. MKBHs are exact rotating solutions to the Einstein–Maxwell equations with an external magnetic field parameter
B
. These spacetimes are of Petrov type
D
and are asymptotically non-flat due to the presence of the magnetic field
Podolsky and Ovcharenko 2025
. A black hole shadow offers a direct way to probe the geometry of spacetime and estimate the physical parameters of black holes.

The unstable circular photon orbits define the shadow silhouettes around a black hole, appearing as a sharp contrast in the dark and bright regions in the observer’s sky. The shape and size of this shadow, under ideal circumstances, is determined by the black hole parameters and the underlying geometry
Falcke et al. 2000
;
Chandrasekhar 1985
. However, these characteristics can be significantly influenced by including the magnetic fields or modified gravity effects. In a rotating geometry, assuming the direction of rotation to be counter-clockwise, the prograde orbit photons (left side of the shadow) are closer to the event horizon as compared to the retrograde photons (right side)
Hioki and Maeda 2009
. This assyemetry arises due to the fact that prograde and retrograde orbit photons do not experience the same effective potential
Bambi and Freese 2009
. With an increase in the rotation parameter
a
, the prograde photon orbit shrinks rapidly as compared to the retrograde one resulting in an assymetrical and distorted shadow. At large spins (
a\gtrsim 0.5
), the shadows deviate from the circular symmetry of a non-rotating black hole due to a squeeze in the perpendicular direction to the spin axis
Bardeen 1973b
;
Chandrasekhar 1985
.

Additional distortions are introduced to MKBHs by the presence of the magnetic field parameter
B
resulting in the modification of shadow shape, shift in the centroid and compression of shadow boundary. We cannot place the observer at spatial infinity as the spacetime is not asymptotically flat, hence a static observer is considered at a large finite radial distance
r_{O}
in a way that
r_{O}\gg r_{+}
. The observer is considered to be in the equatorial plane (
\theta_{O}=\pi/2
) so that the spin-induced effects are maximum.

We exploit the standard technique of ray tracing from the observer backwards in time to study photon trajectories. These photons either fall into the black hole or go to the background source. The boundary between these two scenarios define the shadow. An orthonormal tetrad is created at the observer’s location, according to Grenzebach
Grenzebach et al. 2014
, as follows:

\displaystyle e_{0}=\frac{(r^{2}+a^{2})\partial_{t}+a\partial_{\phi}}{\sqrt{\Sigma\Delta}},\;\;\;\;e_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Sigma}}\partial_{\theta},

(35)

\displaystyle e_{2}=-\frac{\partial_{\phi}+a\sin^{2}{\theta}\partial_{t}}{\sqrt{\Sigma}\sin\theta},\;\;\;\;e_{3}=-\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\Sigma}}\partial_{r}.

(36)

A tangent vector to a light ray
\lambda(\tau)
, with affine parameter
\tau
, reads

\dot{\lambda}(\tau)=\dot{t}\partial_{t}+\dot{r}\partial_{r}+\dot{\theta}\partial_{\theta}+\dot{\phi}\partial_{\phi},

(37)

and it can be expressed at the observer’s position as

\dot{\lambda}(\tau)=\alpha(-e_{0}+\sin\Phi\cos\Psi e_{1}+\sin\Phi\sin\Psi e_{2}+\cos\Phi e_{3}).

(38)

Using the conserved quantities
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{L}
, the scalar factor
\alpha
is calculated as

\alpha=\frac{a\mathcal{L}-(r^{2}+a^{2})\mathcal{E}}{\sqrt{\Sigma\Delta}}.

(39)

Corresponding to the photon’s direction in the observer’s sky, the celestial coordinates
\Phi
and
\Psi
are given by
Grenzebach et al. 2014

\displaystyle\sin\Psi(r_{O},r_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\left(\frac{\xi-a}{\sqrt{(a-\xi)^{2}+\eta}}\right)\right|_{r=r_{O}},

(40)

\displaystyle\sin\Phi(r_{O},r_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Delta[(a-\xi)^{2}+\eta]}}{(r^{2}+a^{2}-a\xi)}\right)\right|_{r=r_{O}},

(41)

where
\xi
and
\eta
are the conserved quantities corresponding to the impact parameters. We introduce Cartesian coordinates
X
and
Y

\displaystyle X(r_{O},r_{p})=-2\tan\left(\frac{\Phi(r_{O},r_{p})}{2}\right)\sin(\Psi(r_{O},r_{p})),

(42)

\displaystyle Y(r_{O},r_{p})=-2\tan\left(\frac{\Phi(r_{O},r_{p})}{2}\right)\cos(\Psi(r_{O},r_{p})).

(43)

Plotting

B

B

Akiyama et al. 2019a
;
Akiyama et al. 2022a

Because the MKBH spacetime is not asymptotically flat, we place the observer at a large but finite radius
r_{O}

V
Parameter Estimation Using Shadow Observables: Kumar-Ghosh Method

The parameters associated with the MKBHs are expected to be constrained from EHT observations. The supermassive black holes M87* and Sgr A*’s observed images match the GR’s Kerr black hole. Nevertheless, black holes in MTG were not mentioned in the EHT observation
(
Akiyama et al. 2019a
;
Akiyama et al. 2019b
;
Akiyama et al. 2019c
)
, which is what we plan to investigate using the calculated parameters of our MKBHs. We assume the observer is in the equatorial plane (
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
) for parameter estimation.
A black hole’s shadow’s shape and size can furnish necessary information about the black hole’s properties and the underlying geometry of spacetime. Hioki and Maeda
(
Hioki and Maeda 2009
)
proposed two shadow observables: the shadow radius
R_{s}
, which defines the approximate size, and the distortion parameter
\delta_{s}
, which quantifies the shadow’s deviation from circularity. These observables allowed numerical estimations of black hole parameters, particularly the inclination angle and spin value
a
. This method was then expanded to include analytical estimations, increasing its application (
Tsupko 2017
).

In addition to that, it was highlighted by
Tsukamoto et al. 2014
that variations caused due to general relativity could be reflected in the shadow asymmetries, hence suggested a method by which shadows of Kerr black holes can be distinguished from those resulting from modified theories of gravity.
These methods, however, rely primarily on the assumptions like certain symmetries in the shape of shadow (near-circularity or equatorial reflection symmetry) when in reality the shadows may likely become irregular and asymmetric, specifically in the presence of deviations due to modified gravity, surrounding matter, or magnetic fields
(
Schee and Stuchlik 2009
;
Johannsen 2013
;
Abdujabbarov et al. 2015
;
Younsi et al. 2016
)
.

Also, significant noise, instrumental resolution limits, and image reconstruction artifacts are present in the observational data from the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT), which can cause the shadow to distort considerably from the idealized symmetric form making it more challenging to use standard circularity-based observables accurately
(
Abdujabbarov et al. 2015
;
Kumar and Ghosh 2020b
;
Akiyama et al. 2019a
;
Akiyama et al. 2022a
)
. To overcome this, a more reliable collection of observables was proposed by
Kumar and Ghosh 2020b
. These observables can accurately describe non-circular and asymmetric shadow boundaries containing
the total shadow area
A
and oblateness
D
and, which are, respectively, defined as follows:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int{Y(r_{p})\,dX(r_{p})}=2\int_{r_{p}^{-}}^{r_{p}^{+}}\left(Y(r_{p})\frac{dX(r_{p})}{dr_{p}}\right)dr_{p},

(44)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{X_{r}-X_{l}}{Y_{t}-Y_{b}},

(45)

where
(X_{l},X_{r})
and
(Y_{b},Y_{t})
represent the extremal coordinates of the shadow in the image plane—specifically, the leftmost, rightmost, bottommost, and topmost points of the shadow boundary.

For an equatorial observer, the oblateness
D
serves as a direct measure of deviation from circularity. It ranges from
D=1
for a perfectly circular shadow, such as in the non-rotating Schwarzschild black hole (
a=0
), down to

for the extremal Kerr case (
a=M
)
(
Tsupko 2017
)
. These observables are particularly advantageous for studying Magnetized Kerr Black Holes (MKBHs), where a magnetic field parameter
B
introduces further deviations from axial symmetry.

Here, we estimate the spin parameter
a
and the magnetic field strength
B
of the MKBH spacetime using the shadow observables
A
and
D
. We get a strong tool for parameter estimation by creating contour maps of constant
A
and
D
in the (
B
–
a

A
and
D
. The intersection points of the contours corresponding to observationally inferred values of
A
and
D

(B,a)

a
and
B

The magnetic parameter
B
introduces degeneracies with the black hole spin
a
and the observer’s inclination angle
\theta_{0}

a

B
can grow and distort the shadow in comparable ways, while the inclination angle also determines the degree of asymmetry

;
Luminet 1979

;

Ayzenberg et al. 2025

VI
Conclusions

There is a pressing need to test deviations from the Kerr geometry using high-resolution EHT observations of black hole shadows. While the Kerr solution remains the foundation of astrophysical black hole modeling in general relativity, it ignores true astrophysical factors like magnetic fields that commonly occur in accretion environments. The MKBH, with its additional magnetic deviation parameter
B
, provides a theoretically consistent and observationally relevant extension of the Kerr solution. Given that surrounding matter can introduce subtle but measurable modifications to black hole shadows, we aimed to explore whether MKBHs can serve as viable candidates for the supermassive black holes. By estimating the MKBH parameters using shadow observables, this work contributes to the broader goal of probing gravity in the strong-field regime and assessing the viability of non-Kerr metrics as descriptions of real astrophysical black holes.

Motivated by this, we have conducted a comprehensive investigation into the shadows of MKBHs, characterized by a deviation parameter
B
, and explored their potential as viable astrophysical black holes in light of recent EHT observations. By analyzing null geodesics and computing shadow observables, we demonstrated how an external magnetic field influences the horizon structure and the shadow morphology of rotating black holes.

We estimate the parameters of MKBHs, which are rotating solutions to the Einstein–Maxwell equations with an external magnetic field parameter
B
. The spacetimes are of Petrov type
D
and are asymptotically non-flat due to the presence of the magnetic field. In contrast to the standard Kerr solution, this external magnetic field alters the geometry. We investigate how the MKBH spacetime influences photon orbits and shadows.

We summarise our main findings as follows:

•

Adding a magnetic field,
B
, changes the size and shape of a black hole’s shadow. The stronger the magnetic field, the larger the shadow becomes, and its shape gets more distorted, especially for black holes that are spinning very fast.

•

Using the separability of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation in the MKBH spacetime, we derived null geodesics and critical impact parameters for unstable photon orbits. These were used to compute the shadow contour for various values of spin
a
and magnetic field strength
B

•

We employed the Kumar–Ghosh method for shadow characterization, using the shadow observables—
area

A
and
oblateness

D
—which are robust against asymmetries and observational artifacts. Contour plots of constant
A
and
D
in the
(a,B)
parameter space enabled precise parameter estimation.

•

The MKBH shadow solutions strongly agree with the existing observations, implying that MKBHs are observationally indistinguishable from Kerr black holes in certain parameter regions. MKBHs appear as feasible alternatives to Kerr black holes.

Thus, MKBHs emerge as strong candidates for astrophysical black holes and viable alternatives incorporating an external magnetic field parameter
B
. Our findings emphasise the necessity of high-resolution measurements in discriminating between general relativity and modified gravity scenarios. The next-generation Event Horizon Telescope (ngEHT) and space-based VLBI missions will help to refine the MKBH parameters. Lastly, the MKBH framework is a powerful tool for exploring black hole physics that goes beyond what Einstein’s general relativity explains. To understand gravity in its most extreme form and test how quantum mechanics works near a black hole, we need to keep studying these phenomena with both new observations and continued theoretical work. To evaluate MKBH scenarios and establish quantitative limitations on near-horizon magnetic fields in SMBHs such as M87* and SgrA*, the ngEHT provides an efficient approach.
