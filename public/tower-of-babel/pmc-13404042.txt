Patient Selection for Supersaturated Oxygen Therapy in Anterior STEMI
Authors: Oludamilola Akinmolayemi, Gregg W Stone
Published: 2026

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) restores epicardial blood flow in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), but microvascular blood flow and microvascular salvage are often suboptimal, leading to large infarctions and adverse left ventricular (LV) remodeling. While prompt PPCI has lowered mortality and heart failure hospitalizations (HFH), these improvements have plateaued,1 underscoring the need for new therapeutic strategies targeting other pathways in STEMI.

Coronary microvascular obstruction (MVO) and dysfunction (CMD) are common after PPCI and are major drivers of increased infarct size.2 MVO and CMD are independent predictors of mortality in STEMI, even after successful PPCI.3 Their pathogenesis involves ischemia-related injury (eg, causing endothelial cell swelling), endogenous or PCI-induced distal embolization of atherothrombotic debris, red blood cell rouleaux formation and white blood cell aggregates, vasoconstriction, extensive myonecrosis with destruction of the vascular bed, and reperfusion injury. While these mechanisms have informed investigation of novel approaches, the only adjunctive therapy to have been proven to reduce infarct size beyond PPCI is supersaturated oxygen (SSO2).4, 5, 6

SSO2 therapy involves hyperoxygenating the patient’s own blood to a PaO2 of 760-1000 mm Hg and reinfusing it for 60 minutes via the left main coronary artery to ischemic yet viable myocardial tissue in the anterior wall of the LV following reperfusion of an occluded left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. While the beneficial mechanisms of actions of SSO2 are multifactorial, by reducing endothelial cell swelling, SSO2 therapy may reduce MVO and enhance regional myocardial blood flow in anterior STEMI. This has been investigated previously. Using cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging within 10 days after PPCI in 874 patients with anterior STEMI (90 of whom were treated with SSO2), Falah and colleagues demonstrated that the extent of MVO was reduced in SSO2-treated patients.7 In a meta-analysis of 6 studies with 1660 patients, SSO2 after PPCI reduced MVO compared with PCI alone (standardized mean difference −0.72; 95% CI −1.11 to −0.34; P < .01), a reduction that was associated with reduced infarct size with SSO2 therapy (mean difference [% of LV mass] −4.3; 95% CI −6.7 to −1.9; P < .01).8

If one of the major mechanisms of action of SSO2 is in reducing MVO, perhaps this therapy would be most effective in patients who have high MVO immediately after PPCI. MVO can be assessed in the cardiac catheterization laboratory with a pressure wire measuring the index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR), a thermodilution-based technique requiring hyperemia, typically induced with adenosine. Although not yet in widespread clinical use, an IMR >40 has been associated with greater MVO based on CMR, infarct size, adverse LV remodeling, and death or HFH.9,10 Wire-based IMR may be emulated with varying angiographic techniques that assess contrast wash-in rates and use computational fluid dynamics or simplified mathematical models to determine resting IMR and to virtually model hyperemic IMR. Several studies have shown good correlation between angiographic IMR and wire-based IMR,11 and an angiographic IMR >40 has been correlated with MVO and infarct size,12 as well as with death and HFH.13 However, the accuracy of angiographic IMR compared with wire-based IMR has been challenged, and different angiographic IMR systems may yield different results.14,15

In this issue of JSCAI, Caullery et al16 present findings from a small prospective pilot study evaluating the effects of SSO2 therapy on CMD assessed by angiographic IMR using the Medis QAngio XA 3D software (QFR version 2.1) following PPCI in 12 patients with anterior STEMI. The IMR after PPCI but prior to SSO2 was reduced from a median of 44.5 [32.5-52.5] to 23 [20-30.5] after SSO2 (P = .003). IMR was reduced to a greater extent in the 8 patients in whom the baseline post-PPCI angiographic IMR was >40 (median IMR reduced from 49.5 to 24.0) vs the 4 patients in whom the baseline IMR was ≤40 (median IMR reduced from 25.0 to 23.0). Although reported, the study was too small to meaningfully examine differences in exercise performance, infarct size, LV function, or clinical outcomes.

The results of this study are broadly consistent with those from a retrospective analysis of 46 patients from the IC-HOT (Evaluation of Intracoronary Hyperoxemic Oxygen Therapy in Anterior Acute Myocardial Infarction Patients) and Optimized SSO2 trials, in whom SSO2 therapy after PPCI reduced the angiography-derived IMR (using a different software system) in those with baseline IMR >40 but not in those with baseline IMR ≤40.17

As the only therapy proven to reduce infarct size, SSO2 use is increasing in patients with large anterior STEMI after PPCI; however, SSO2 therapy requires device preparation and a 60-minute infusion in the catheterization laboratory after PPCI in STEMI, a time commitment that impacts patient throughput and is inconvenient for STEMIs that occur in the early morning hours. While the therapy is very safe, the concept of reserving SSO2 therapy for patients in whom it will benefit the most (or conversely, withholding its use in patients not likely to benefit) is attractive. Should such decisions be based on angiography-derived IMR?

Unfortunately, this is a step (perhaps many steps) too far based on the current data. Only a small number of patients have been studied (with different angiographic IMR systems), and outcomes have not been adequately correlated with infarct size or clinical outcomes. The lack of a control group in the present study is a major limitation, and regression to the mean in the patient group with a high baseline IMR is a real concern. Most importantly, SSO2 may have benefits beyond reducing MVO and CMD, including decreasing the generation of free oxygen radicals and inflammatory cytokines, decreasing white cell adhesion and local coagulation, improving myocardial metabolism, and reducing reperfusion injury and apoptosis.18,19 Thus, a large-scale randomized trial of SSO2 vs placebo, stratified by post-PCI IMR, is required to address this issue. Alternatively, SSO2 could be provided to all patients with IMR >40, and only patients with IMR ≤40 would be randomized. Whether angiographic IMR or wire-based IMR should be used for such a study will be debated. The primary outcome could be infarct size measured at days 3 to 5 by CMR imaging, or better yet, death and HFH measured at 1 year. Until such a trial is performed, SSO2 should be administered in all patients with anterior STEMI without cardiogenic shock presenting within ∼6 hours of infarct onset after successful PPCI of the proximal or mid LAD. The 60-minute delay is well worth the time and effort, given the substantial reduction in infarct size with this therapy, a benefit that has been strongly associated with substantially reduced death and HFH.20
