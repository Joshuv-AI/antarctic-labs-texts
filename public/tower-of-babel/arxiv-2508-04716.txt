Structural approximation and a Minkowski space-time lattice with Lorentzian invariance
Boris Zilber

Abstract
We introduce a framework of structural approximation to represent Lorentz-invariant Minkowski space-time as the limit of finite cyclic lattices, each equipped with the action of a finite quasi-Lorentz group. This construction provides a discrete model preserving Lorentz symmetry and offers new insights into the algebraic and geometric structure of space-time.



1
Introduction

1.1

A physical theory is an approximation to reality. But what is an
approximation? In

[
Zilber(2014)
]
we discussed this problem from the perspective of model theory. One of the necessary properties of a physical theory is that the model preserves the same structural properties that is being assumed for reality. Such properties can often be formulated in formal languages studied by logicians and we used well-studied model-theoretic tools to introduce in

[
Zilber(2014)
]
the definition of
structural approximation
and respective notion of limit, which
the current paper heavily relies upon. An earlier application of this notion in context of quantum and statistical mechanics is in
the recent paper
[
Zilber(2025)
]
.

Among the fundamental properties that physics may ascribe to the universe is the property of being symmetric with regard to the action of a certain group of transformations. On the other hand we might allow
the possibility of the universe of being discrete or even finite (consisting of enormous number of points) while the present theory treats it as continuous.
In such a setting structural approximation considers a sequence of

{\bf M}_{i},

i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, of finite structures each equipped with a finite group
G_{i}
acting on
{\bf M}_{i}
in a certain prescribed way. The definition
in section
2
, following
[
Zilber(2014)
]
, makes precise the statement that
({\bf M}_{i},G_{i}),

i\in{\mathbb{N}},

approximates
a continuous model
{\bf M}
with an action of continuous group
G
along an ultrailter
\mathcal{D}
. This can be written as

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathcal{D}}({\bf M}_{i},G_{i})=({\bf M},G).

However, such a straightforward form of structural approximation
gives intuitively expected outcomes
only in simple cases, basically when
G
is abelian and compact. In more interesting cases, it turns out that both
{\bf M}
and
G
have to be both representable as
compact complex manifolds
, see
2.3
. As a result
G
is a
compactified group
of transformations, that is a group extended by
infinitary
transformations which send elements of
{\bf M}
to a connected components of its boundary (obrained as a result of approximation) rather than a single point. For this reason we call the approximation as defined in
[
Zilber(2014)
]

global
.

This is, in effect, due to the fact that in the discrete setting the algebra of the meaningful
{\bf M}_{i}
and
G_{i}
is based on rings of the form
{\mathbb{Z}}/n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
(residue ring modulo
n_{i}
) with
n_{i}\to\infty.
It turns out that in the limit of structural approximation such rings have to be identified as the compactified field of complex numbers
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
that is

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathcal{D}}\,{\mathbb{Z}}/n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}=\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}

along any non-rpincipal ultafilter.

1.2

There is another, more standard and intuitive form of approximation, which we refer to as
local approximation
. This corresponds to “an observer located inside
{\bf M}
with a limited observation span”. Namely,
suppose a part of
{\bf M}_{i}
, has a form of a 1-dim lattice of a large size
l_{i}
which still is much smaller than the size of
{\bf M}_{i}.
It is convenient in this context to use the language and the setting of non-standard numbers (see e.g.
[
Albeverio1986
]
for definitions and examples) and assume that we work in some pseudo-finite (equivalently, hyper-finite) structure
{{}^{*}{\bf M}}
with a lattice of pseudo-finite size
\mathfrak{l}.

Let us call
\mathfrak{u}
the spacing of the lattice.
Set a non-standard distance on the lattice so that the shift by
k\cdot\mathfrak{u}
corresponds to the distance
\frac{k}{\mathfrak{l}}.
The latter
by definition is an element of the set
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Q}}
of non-standard rational numbers.
Now use the standard part map (of non-standard analysis)

\mathrm{st}:{{}^{*}\mathbb{Q}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty,-\infty\}

into compactified reals to convert the interval
\{k\mathfrak{u}:\ 0\leq k\leq\mathfrak{l}\}
in the lattice into the interval
[0,1]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and more generally, the union of increasing intervals

\mathrm{st}:\ \bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\{k\mathfrak{u}:\ -m\mathfrak{l}\leq k\leq m\mathfrak{l}\}\ \twoheadrightarrow\ {\mathbb{R}}

where
{\mathbb{R}}
is the real line as a metric space. In case that the
{\bf M}_{i}
have an algebraic structure of rings or fields, as e.g. in the case of
{\mathbb{Z}}/p_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}\,(=\mathrm{F}_{p_{i}})

p_{i}
prime, one can arrange that map
\mathrm{st}
preserves also relations of the structure, see
[
Zilber(2025)
]
, Theorem 4.11. The proof of this theorem in
[
Zilber(2025)
]
furnishes a construction of
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathcal{D}}
so that the limit map coinsides with the standard part map on the subset of non-standard rational numbers embedded in the pseudo-finite field. Thus this construction

locally approximates
{\mathbb{R}}
while globally approximates
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}
by a sequience of finite fields.

1.3

In the paper we apply these techniques and construct a sequence of
finite groups acting on finite 4-dim lattices which
locally approximates
the Lorentz group
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(1,3)
acting on the Minkowski space-time
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}})
endowed with the Minkowski metric.

The same sequence of finite structures
globally approximates
a compact complexification
\overline{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{C}})
of Minkowski space acted upon by a compactification of the group
\overline{\mathrm{SO}}(4,{\mathbb{C}}).
As it happens the structure is almost identical with Penrose’s twistor space, see
[
PenroseRindler(1984)
]
. The same approximation map considered
locally
induces a unique compactification
\overline{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}})
of
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}})
which turns out to be a conformal compactification. More precisely,

\overline{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}})\subset\overline{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{C}})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}{\bf P}^{5},

with embedding into
{\mathbb{C}}{\bf P}^{5}
as the Grassmanian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,4),
and

\overline{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{R}})\cong{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{3}

(note that its double cover is
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
).

Unlike Penrose’s
this compactification is truly cyclic: null rays are closed, in fact isomorphic to the projective line
{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}.
This agrees well with the cyclic structure of the discrete lattices which structurally approximate Minkowski spacetime in our construction.

1.4

Finally we would like to mention without going here into details that the 4-dimensional lattice modelling Minkowski space and the structure around it fits well with constructions in
[
Zilber(2025)
]
. Essentially, the 4-dimensional universe would be representable as
\mathbb{U}^{4}
where
\mathbb{U}
is the 1-dimensional universe of the earlier paper.

2
Structural approximation by rings

In this section we give precise definitions and quote main theorems related to approximation, in particular, in the class of rings.

2.1

Ultraproducts and pseudo-finite structures.

A structure
{\bf M}
is a set
M
with a collection
\Sigma
of
n
-ary relations
S\subset M^{n},
for some
n,
called the language (or the vocabulary) of
{\bf M}=(M;\Sigma).

Suppose we are given a sequence
\{{\bf M}_{i}:i\in{\mathbb{N}}\},

{\bf M}_{i}=(M_{i},\Sigma)
, of structures in language
\Sigma.
One can choose a Fréchet ultrafilter
D
on
{\mathbb{N}}
and construct the ultraproduct

{{}^{*}{\bf M}}:=\prod_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\bf M}_{i}/D

which is a structure in language
\Sigma
with the
key property (the Loś theorem): given a first-order sentence
\sigma
in the language
\Sigma,

\sigma\mbox{ is true in }{{}^{*}{\bf M}}\mbox{ if and only if }\sigma\mbox{ is true in }{\bf M}_{i}\mbox{ along }\mathcal{D}

(1)

{{}^{*}{\bf M}}
is often referred to as
the model-theoretic limit of
{\bf M}_{i}
along
D
(not a structural approximation yet).

In case the
{\bf M}_{i}
’s are finite,
{{}^{*}{\bf M}}
is said to be a
pseudo-finite
structure (or hyper-finite in the terminology of
[
Albeverio1986
]
).

In particular, if
{\bf M}_{i}=\mathrm{F}_{p_{i}},

p_{i}
prime numbers, structure
{{}^{*}{\bf M}}
is a pseudo-finite field of characteristic zero.

2.2

Structural approximation.

It is convenient to consider the system
\mathcal{T}_{n}
of topologies on
{{\bf M}}^{n},
all
n
, the basic closed sets of which are realisations
S({\bf M})\subset{{\bf M}}^{n}
of the
n
-ary
S\in\Sigma
.
In geometric/physics setting the closed sets could be introduced as the zero sets of appropriate systems of equations.

Such a system is said to be quasi-compact (or complete) if the projection maps

{\rm pr}_{n+1,n}:{{\bf M}}^{n+1}\to{{\bf M}}^{n}
preserve closed subsets, that is

{\rm pr}_{n+1,n}(S)
closed, for
S
closed.

This definition makes sense for the
{\bf M}_{i},

i\in I,
and indeed for any structure in language
\Sigma.

A
structural approximation
of
{\bf M}
by
\{{\bf M}_{i}:i\in I\}
along
D

is determined by a surjective map (
limit map
)

\mathsf{lm}:{{}^{*}{\bf M}}\twoheadrightarrow{\bf M}

(2)

which has the property, for any
n
-ary closed
S\in\Sigma
and
s\in{{}^{*}{\bf M}}
:

s\in S({{}^{*}{\bf M}})\mbox{ closed}\Rightarrow\mathsf{lm}(s)\in S({\bf M})

As it happens, below, most of the time
{\bf M}_{i},

{\bf M}
and
{{}^{*}{\bf M}}
are rings in the language
\{x+y=z,\ x\cdot y=z\}

or groups in the language
x*y=z,
and
closed
for
S\subset{\bf M}^{n}
means
S
is the set of solutions of a system of algebraic equations in
n
-variables with parameters in
{\bf M},
or equivalently, closed in
Zariski topology
.

Note that despite the coarse topology we still are able to use the intuition of infinitesimals: two elements

a,a^{\prime}\in{{}^{*}{\bf M}}^{n}
are seen to be “infinitesimally close” if

\mathsf{lm}\,a=\mathsf{lm}\,a^{\prime}.

Thus, in view of (
1
) and (
2
) structural approximation is a formalisation of the statement
a very large structure
{\bf M}_{i}
looks like
{\bf M}
from afar.

2.3

Approximation and compactness.

It was established in
[
Zilber(2014)
]
that in main cases
{\bf M}
has to be quasi-compact in the formal topology (complete in Zariski topology)
in order for it to be approximated by a non-trivial sequence
{\bf M}_{i}
as in (
2
).

For fields it takes the following form (Theorem 5.2 of
[
Zilber(2014)
]
):

The compactification
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}:={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}={\mathbb{C}}{\bf P}^{1}
of the field of complex numbers can be approximated by a sequence of finite fields
\mathrm{F}_{p_{i}}
along an ultrafilter
D
on prime numbers
p_{i}.

Moreover,
for any zero-characteristic pseudo-finite field
\mathrm{E}

there exists a structural approximation

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}:\mathrm{E}\twoheadrightarrow\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}.

(3)

The field of complex numbers is the only locally compact field that can be structurally approximated by a sequence of finite fields.

The limit map in (
3
) is far of being unique but we can pick ones with some specific and useful properties as in
[
Zilber(2025)
]
, which allows to mimic complex analysis in the pseudo-finite field
\mathrm{E}
.

2.4

Scales and scale-dependendence of approximation.

The interplay between the domain and the range of the approximation map
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}
as in (
3
) brings in some features not encountered in the limit construction with inherent metrics. By its nature field
\mathrm{E}
is of a pseudo-finite (non-standard) characteristic
\mathfrak{p}
(“the limit” of
p_{i}
) while
{\mathbb{C}}
is characteristic zero field with a natural metric.

More precisely, as in
[
Zilber(2025)
]
let
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
be an
\aleph_{0}
-saturated model of arithmetic,
\mathfrak{p}\in{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
an infinite prime number
and

\mathrm{E}={{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}/\mathfrak{p}{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}.

Note that in
\mathrm{E}
usual integers
1,2,3\ldots
are represented, and the sequence continuous so far as it does not reach the infinite pseudo-finite number
\mathfrak{p},
which is bigger than any standard number.

It is clear by algebraic considerations that
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}
sends standard integers
1,2,3\ldots
of
\mathrm{E}
to respective integers
1,2,3\ldots
of

{\mathbb{C}}.
Moreover, on the domain of standard integers
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}
acts as an isomorphism of subrings. The same is true
for rational numbers, where we represent rational numbers
\frac{k}{m}
as pairs
(k,m)
of integer numbers satisfying the identity

(k_{1},m_{1})=(k_{2},m_{2})\Leftrightarrow k_{1}m_{2}=k_{2}m_{1}.

In
[
Zilber(2025)
]
, 3.8-3.9 we defined a convex subring
\mathrm{O}
(denoted

\mathrm{O}(\mathcal{F})
therein) of the ring
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
of
small scale
non-standard integers. Namely,

-\mathfrak{p}<<\mathrm{O}<<\mathfrak{p}

and
\mathrm{O}
is closed under applications of all arithmetic functions. In fact

\mathrm{O}\prec{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}},

(4)

is an elementary submodel of
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
containing an infinite integers
\mathfrak{l}
.

Note that the field of fractions
\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O})
of
\mathrm{O}
can exactly be represented as

\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O})=\{(k,m):\ k,m\in\mathrm{O},\ m\neq 0\}

and by construction

\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O})\subset{{}^{*}\mathbb{Q}},

(5)

the non-standard rational numbers, i.e. the fraction field of
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}.

We also use the field of
finite
rational numbers, that is

\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O})=\{(k,m)\in\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O}),\ \exists N\in{\mathbb{N}}:\ |k|<mN\}.

Note that by assumptions the natural map

k\mapsto k\,\mathrm{mod}\,p;\ \mathrm{O}\to\mathrm{E}

is an embedding of rings, which extends to the embedding of fields, and we will assume for convenience of notation the inclusions
\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O})\subseteq\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O})\subseteq\mathrm{E}
.

Define

\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}:=\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O})

The construction in
[
Zilber(2025)
]
, section 4, defines
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}
on a subfield
\mathrm{Fr}(\mathcal{F})
of a pseudofinite field to be
the standard part map

\mathrm{st},
that is

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}(x):=\mathrm{st}(x),\mbox{ for }x\in\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}

(6)

while the values
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}(x)
for generic points of
\mathrm{E}
have no explicit definition.

Note that (
6
) is well-defined because the standard part map is well-defined on
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Q}}.

We will call an approximation (limit) map
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}
satisfying (
6
) as
locally canonical
. In the paper all our limit maps are locally canonical by default.

It is clear that (
6
) along with the fact that
\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}
contains all finite rational numbers of a non-standard model of arithmetic implies

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}:\ \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}.

(7)

In other words, an observer which has only access to small scale elements of
\mathrm{E}
can think of
\mathrm{E}
as being
{\mathbb{R}}.

Locally field
\mathrm{E}
approximates the field of reals.

However, as we continue along the natural order
1,2,3,\ldots

of
\mathrm{E}
, inevitably, (by 5.2(ii) of
[
Zilber(2014)
]
)
we will encounter elements of
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}
which are not in
{\mathbb{R}},
in particular an element
\mathbf{i}\in\mathrm{E}
such that

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}:\mathbf{i}\mapsto i=\sqrt{-1};\ \ \ \mathbf{i}\cdot\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}\twoheadrightarrow i\cdot{\mathbb{R}}.

Again by (
3
), we will also have a non-empty domain

\mathrm{E}_{\infty}=\{x\in\mathrm{E}:\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}(x)=\infty\}.

So, an observer which has tools to explore the global characteristics of
\mathrm{E}
has to think of
\mathrm{E}
as a Riemann sphere
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}.

\mathrm{E}
locally looks like
{\mathbb{R}}
while globally looks like
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}
.

2.5

Approximation by rings.
Consider a more general case of a ring of nonstandard integers
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
modulo
\mathcal{N}

{\rm K}={{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}/\mathcal{N}{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}

where
\mathcal{N}\in{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
divisible by all standard integers, the condition used in
[
Zilber(2025)
]
. (In the current setting it suffices to assume that
\mathcal{N}
is divisible by unboundedly large standard prime numbers).

Lemma.

There is a surjective ring-homomorphism

\mathsf{lm}_{\rm K}:\ {\rm K}\to\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}.

\mathsf{lm}_{\rm K}
is the composition of two Zariski homomorphisms

\mathrm{res}_{\mathfrak{p}}:{\rm K}\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{E}\mbox{ and }\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}:\mathrm{E}\twoheadrightarrow\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}

where
\mathrm{E}=\mathrm{E}_{\mathfrak{p}}={{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}/\mathfrak{p}{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
for some infinite prime, a pseudo-finite field.

There is an embedding
\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O})\subset{\rm K}
such that

\mathsf{lm}_{\rm K}(x):=\mathrm{st}(x)\mbox{ for }x\in\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O})

and, setting
{\rm K}_{\mathrm{Loc}}:=\mathrm{Fr}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{F}),

\mathsf{lm}_{\rm K}:\ {\rm K}_{\mathrm{Loc}}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

(8)

that is
\mathsf{lm}_{\rm K}
is locally canonical.

Proof.
Since
\mathcal{N}
, the order of
{\rm K}
, is divisible by every standard prime
p,
there is a residue map, ring-homomorphism
\mathrm{res}_{\mathfrak{p}}:\ {\rm K}\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{E}_{\mathfrak{p}},
for an infinite non-standard
\mathfrak{p}.
As in
2.4

\mathrm{E}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathrm{E}
contains the non-standard rational numbers
\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O})
and the subring
\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}
which can be naturally lifted to
{\rm K}_{\mathrm{Loc}}.

Now, consider a large enough non-standard model
{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}
of complex numbers so that it contains
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
and thus embeds
\mathrm{O},
that is we may assume
\mathrm{O}\subset{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}.

Since
{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}
is algebraically closed of large enough transcendence degree
there is an embedding

\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{E}}:\mathrm{E}\hookrightarrow{{}^{*}\mathbb{C}}

which is an identity on
\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O}).

Define
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}=\mathrm{st}\circ\mathrm{I},
the composite of two maps. This is a Zariski homomorphism.
Note that
\mathrm{st}(\mathrm{Fr}(\mathrm{O}))={\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\},
that is
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathrm{E})\supseteq{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}.
However
\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathrm{E})\neq{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
by Theorem 5.2(ii) of
[
Zilber(2014)
]
. It follows

\mathsf{lm}_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathrm{E})=\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}.

\Box

2.6

Approximation of algebraic varieties.
Let
V\subset\mathrm{A}^{n}
be an affine variety over
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
V({\rm K})
its
{\rm K}
points. By definition
V
is given by a system of polynomial equations
P_{V}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
and

V({\rm K})=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in{\rm K}^{n}:\ P_{V}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=0\}.

We consider
V
as a Zariski structure, that is a structure with universe
V({\rm K})
and basic
k
-ary relations given as Zariski closed subsets
S\subseteq V({\rm K})^{m}
defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}.

Along with
V({\rm K})
consider the complex variety
V({\mathbb{C}}),
also a Zariski structure by the same definition. There exists a projective variety
{\bf P}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
(not unique) such that

V
can be embedded over
{\mathbb{Q}}
into
{\bf P}
as a quasi-projective subvariety. Let

e:V\hookrightarrow{\bf P}

be the embedding.

Set
V^{\bf P}\subseteq{\bf P}
be the Zariski closure of
e(V)
in
{\bf P}.
It follows that
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
is a complex projective variety, and so compact (complete).
So
e(V({\mathbb{R}}))\subset e(V({\mathbb{C}}))\subset V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
and
e(V({\mathbb{R}}))
is a real algebraic variety isomorphic to
V({\mathbb{R}})
over
{\mathbb{Q}}.

Note that
V({\mathbb{C}})
is a Zariski substructure of
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
since every Zariski predicate
S\subset(V({\mathbb{C}}))^{m}
is a restriction of
S^{\bf P}\subset(V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}}))^{m},
the closure of
S
in
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
, to
V({\mathbb{C}}).

2.7

Theorem.

Suppose
V({\mathbb{Q}})
is dense in
V({\mathbb{C}})
in the metric topology.
There exists a surjective Zariski homomorphism

\mathsf{lm}_{V({\rm K})}:\ V({\rm K})\twoheadrightarrow V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})

such that the restriction of

\mathsf{lm}_{V({\rm K})}
to
V({\rm K}_{\mathrm{Loc}})
is coordinate-wise the standard part map and

\mathsf{lm}_{V({\rm K})}:\ V({\rm K}_{\mathrm{Loc}})\twoheadrightarrow e(V({\mathbb{R}}))\subset V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}}).

Proof.
First consider the Zariski homomorphism

\mathrm{res}_{\mathfrak{p}}:{\rm K}\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{E};\ \ k\mapsto k\,\mbox{mod}\,\mathfrak{p}

for the prime
\mathfrak{p}
and field
\mathrm{E}=\mathrm{E}_{\mathfrak{p}}
described in
2.5
.

Extend
\mathrm{res}_{\mathfrak{p}}
to
V({\rm K})
coordinate-wise to get the Zariski homomorphism

\mathrm{res}_{\mathfrak{p}}:V({\rm K})\twoheadrightarrow V(\mathrm{E}).

This reduces the problem of constructing
\mathsf{lm}_{V({\rm K})}
to the problem of constructing

\mathsf{lm}_{V(\mathrm{E})}:\ V(\mathrm{E})\twoheadrightarrow V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}}).

We carry out the construction below.

Define

\mathsf{lm}_{0}:V(\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}})\to V({\mathbb{R}});\ \ \mathsf{lm}_{0}(\bar{a})=\mathrm{st}(\bar{a}),

where
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{E}^{n}
and
\mathrm{st}(\bar{a})=(\mathrm{st}(a_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{st}(a_{n})).

By definition
\mathrm{st}
is the map that preserves the order relation on rationals of
\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}
and
preserves polynomial equations. It follows that
\mathsf{lm}_{0}
is a Zariski homomorphism and tt is surjective due to (
7
) and the assumption on density of rational points in
V({\mathbb{R}}).

At the next stage we require a slightly stronger version of (
4
).

Claim.

There exist a model
{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
of the ring of integers, prime
\mathfrak{p}\in{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
and a convex elementary submodel
\mathrm{O}\prec{{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}
such that
{\mathbb{Z}}\neq\mathrm{O}
and

{\rm tr.d.}(\mathrm{E}/\mathrm{O})\geq 2^{\aleph_{0}}.

(9)

Proof. First consider an
\aleph_{0}
-saturated model
\mathcal{Z}
of the ring of integers and note that
{\mathbb{Z}}\prec\mathcal{Z},
by definition. It follows that there exists a prime
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that the field
\mathrm{E}_{0}=\mathcal{Z}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{Z}
contains an infinite subset
A

of algebraically independent elements, that is
{\rm tr.d.}(\mathrm{E}/{\mathbb{Z}})\geq|A|\geq\aleph_{0}.

Now let
D
be a non-rpincipal ultrafilter over
{\mathbb{N}}
and set the ultrapowers

\mathrm{O}:={\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{N}}/D,\ \ {{}^{*}\mathbb{Z}}:=\mathcal{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}/D,\ \mathrm{E}:=\mathrm{E}_{0}^{\mathbb{N}}/D,\ {{}^{*}A}:=A^{\mathbb{N}}/D.

We then get

{\rm tr.d.}(\mathrm{E}/\mathrm{O})\geq|{{}^{*}A}|\geq 2^{\aleph_{0}}

which proves the claim.

As a corollary we get:
There exists a subset of
n
-tuples
B\subset V(\mathrm{E})\subset\mathrm{E}^{n}
such that
|B|\geq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
and any
n
-tuples

(b_{11}\ldots b_{1n}),\ldots,(b_{m1}\ldots b_{mn})\in B

with distinct
b_{ij}
are algebraically independent over
\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}},
that is
for any non-zero polynomial
P(x_{11}\ldots x_{1n},\ldots,x_{m1}\ldots x_{mn})
over
\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}},

P(b_{11}\ldots b_{1n},\ldots,b_{m1}\ldots b_{mn})\neq 0.

This implies that any map
\mathsf{lm}_{B}:\mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{Loc}}\cup B\to{\mathbf{V}}^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
extending
\mathsf{lm}_{0}
is a Zariski homomorphism. We define the extension
\mathsf{lm}_{B}
so that

\mathsf{lm}_{B}(B)=V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})

(10)

It remains to extend
\mathsf{lm}_{B}
to
A=V(\mathrm{E})\setminus B.

We start by enumerating
A
by ordinal numbers
\alpha<\kappa
where
\kappa=|A|,
the cardinality of
A,

A=\{a_{\alpha}:\ a<\kappa\}.

We also extend the Zariski topologies on cartesian powers of
V(\mathrm{E})
and
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
by adding, for every Zariski closed relation
S(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
on
m
th cartesian power of the variety the new
k-1
-ary relation
\exists x_{m-k}\ldots x_{m}S(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
(this procedure was called in
[
Zilber(2014)
]

the formal completion of the topology
). Note that each relation
\exists x_{m}S(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
on

V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
defines a Zariski closed subset since
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
is
projective and thus complete.

Let

A_{\beta}:=\{a_{\alpha}\in A:\ \alpha<\beta\}.

Assume
that for
\beta<\kappa,
there is

\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta}:\ B\cup A_{\beta}\to V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})

such that,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mbox{for any }c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1}\in B\cup A_{\beta},\\
V(\mathrm{E})\vDash\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m}S(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},x_{k}\ldots x_{m})\Rightarrow\\
\ \ \ \ \ V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})\vDash\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m}S(c_{1}^{\mathsf{lm}},\ldots,c_{k-1}^{\mathsf{lm}},x_{k}\ldots x_{m})\end{array}

(11)

where
c^{\mathsf{lm}}
stands for
\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta}(c).

This is the case for
\beta=0
since

\mathsf{lm}_{B,0}:=\mathsf{lm}_{B}.

Now we construct
\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta+1}
extending
\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta}.
We need to find an element
a_{\beta}^{\mathsf{lm}}\in V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
such that

\begin{array}[]{ll}V(\mathrm{E})\vDash\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1}S(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1},a_{\beta})\Rightarrow\\
\ \ \ \ \ V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})\vDash\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1}S(c_{1}^{\mathsf{lm}},\ldots,c_{k-1}^{\mathsf{lm}},x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1},a_{\beta}^{\mathsf{lm}})\end{array}

Consider the type over
A_{\beta},
collection of formulas with parameters
A_{\beta},

p(y)=\begin{array}[]{ll}\{\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1}S(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1},y):\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ V(\mathrm{E})\vDash\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1}S(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1},a_{\beta})\}\end{array}

We claim that
p(y)
can be realised in
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}}).

Indeed, each formula of
p(y)
defines a Zariski closed subset of
V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
and the type corresponds to the intersection of all the Zariski closed subsets. By the descending chain condition of Zariski topologies
p(y)
is equaivalent to the intersection of finitely many sets, equivalently to a single set defined by a formula
\exists x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1}S(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1},y).

Thus we need to see that

V^{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})\vDash\exists y,x_{k},\ldots,x_{m-1}\,S(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k-1},x_{k}\ldots x_{m-1},y)

which just follows from the indection assumption (
11
).

We have proved the induction step from
\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta}
to
\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta+1}.
For a limit ordinal
\gamma\leq\kappa
define
\mathsf{lm}_{B,\gamma}=\bigcup_{\beta<\gamma}\mathsf{lm}_{B,\beta}.
This determines all the steps of the construction by induction and we set

\mathsf{lm}_{V(\mathrm{E})}:=\mathsf{lm}_{B,\kappa}.

This finishes the proof of the theorem.
\Box

3
Pseudo-finite Minkowski space structure and its limit

3.1

It is well-known that
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is isomorphic to the double cover of the
Lorentz group
\mathrm{SO}^{+}(1,3)
and it acts in agreement with this on the Minkowski space .

More precisely (see e.g.
[
Klinker(2015)
]
),
one represents a vector with components
(t,x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
(Minkowski space) as a
2\times 2
matrix

X:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}t+z\ \ x-iy\\
x+iy\ \ t-z\end{array}\right)

with
X^{\dagger}=X
and
\det(X)=t^{2}-x^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2},
and considers

X\mapsto MXM^{\dagger}\mbox{ with }M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}).

(12)

This preserves
\det X
and thus the Minkowski metric, which leads to the proof that (
12
) is a Lorentz transformation and all Lorentz transformations can be expressed in this way.
The fact that
\pm M
both give the same transformation of
X
corresponds to the fact that
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is the double cover of the Lorentz group, that is

\mathrm{SO}^{+}(1,3)\cong\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})/{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}

(13)

We denote

\left(\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}),\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})/{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}\right)

the structure which consists of
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear Minkowski space
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}})
with metric given by
X\mapsto\det X
along with the group
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})/{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}
acting on the space as describe in (
12
).

We note that the isomorphism of groups induces the isomorphism of structures

\left(\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}),\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})/{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}\right)\cong\left(\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}),\mathrm{SO}^{+}(1,3)\right)

(14)

Let
\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
which we will treat as a Zariski structure, that is the set with Zariski closed relations
R\subset\bar{{\mathbb{C}}}^{n}
on it.

3.2

Complexification of a ring.

Let
A
be a commutative unitary ring. Define

to be the unitary ring obtained from the ring

as follows:

A^{(2)}:=\{(a,b)\in A\times A\};

(a_{1},b_{1})+(a_{2},b_{2}):=(a_{1}+a_{2},b_{1}+b_{2}),\ \ (a_{1},b_{1})\cdot(a_{2},b_{2}):=(a_{1}a_{2}-b_{1}b_{2},a_{1}b_{2}+a_{2}b_{1}).

Clearly,

is an embedding of

into
A^{(2)}
as a subring
(A,0)
and

(a,b)\mapsto(a,-b)\mbox{ an automorphism of }A^{(2)}.

3.3

Let

be the set of

matrices over

which we treat as an
8
-dim

We have

For the general

(c_{1},c_{2})\ \ \ \ (d_{1},d_{2})\end{array}\right)

(b_{1},-b_{2})\ \ \ \ (d_{1},-d_{2})\end{array}\right)

Clearly,

In particular,

Thus we have established:

4
Algebraic compactification of Minkowski space

In this section we construct the complex projective variety
{\bf P}
in which

\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{C}})
can be suitably embedded according to
3.6
. Since we at the same time consider
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}),
we carry out the construction over arbitrary field of characteristic
0
to substitute
{\mathbb{C}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}
later.

The construction, which is usually applied to
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}),
leading to a conformal compactification,
is well known, although some small variations are possible. We found the algebraic presentation in
[
Yadczyk(1984)
]
useful for our purposes. The author is grateful to M.Kabenyuk for the help with the algebra involved.

4.1

Let
\mathrm{E}
be a field of characteristic
0,

\mathcal{M}(\mathrm{E})=\mathrm{E}\times\mathrm{E}^{3}
be the Minkowski space with the quadratic form
encoding Minkowski metric

Q(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{3})=-t^{2}+x^{2}_{1}+\ldots+x^{2}_{3}.

We define an embedding
\Phi
of
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(\mathrm{E})
into a
higher-dimensional space
V=\mathrm{E}^{2}\times\mathrm{E}^{4}
as

\Phi:\ (t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{3})\mapsto(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{q}{2},t,x_{1},...,x_{3},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{q}{2})

where
q=Q(t,x_{1},...,x_{3}).

The equation

\bar{Q}(s,t,x_{1},...,x_{3},x_{4})=-s^{2}-t^{2}+x^{2}_{1}+,...,x^{2}_{3}+x^{2}_{4}=0

defines a hypersurface
L
in
V
. It is easy to verify that
\Phi(\mathcal{M})\subseteq L.

Let
\pi(V)
be the projective space obtained by projectivisation

\pi:V\to\pi(V);\ (y_{0},\ldots,y_{5})\mapsto[y_{0}:\ldots:y_{5}].

We have dim
\dim\pi(V)=5,

\dim\pi(L)=4
and

\pi(\mathcal{M})\subset\pi(L)\subset\pi(V).

Moreover,
\pi
bijectively maps
\Phi(\mathcal{M})
onto
\pi(\Phi(\mathcal{M})),
(because
\Phi(\mathcal{M})
lies in
s+x_{4}=1
)
so
\dim\pi(\Phi(\mathcal{M}))=4=\dim\pi(L).

4.2

Thus the map

\pi\circ\Phi:(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{3})\mapsto[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{q}{2}:t:x_{1}:...,x_{3}:\frac{1}{2}+\frac{q}{2}]

\mathcal{M}\twoheadrightarrow\pi(\Phi(\mathcal{M}))\subset\pi(L)\subset\mathrm{E}{\bf P}^{5}

is bijective onto
\pi(\Phi(\mathcal{M}))
and
\pi(\Phi(\mathcal{M}))
is in the open subset of the projective variety
\pi(L)
defined by equation
s+x_{4}=1.

Moreover, by the dimensional equalities
\pi(\Phi(\mathcal{M}))
is dense in
\pi(L).
Thus we may identify the closure in Zariski topology

\bar{\mathcal{M}}:=\pi(L)=\{[s:t:x_{1}:...,x_{3}:x_{4}]\ :\ s^{2}+t^{2}=x^{2}_{1}+,...,x^{2}_{3}+x^{2}_{4}=0\}

a smooth quadric in
\mathrm{E}{\bf P}^{5}.

4.3

The complex case.
In case
\mathrm{E}={\mathbb{C}},
the field of complex numbers,
\bar{\mathcal{M}}({\mathbb{C}})
by the above formula is a Klein quadric in
{\mathbb{C}}{\bf P}^{5}
and is easily identifiable with the complex Grassmanian
\mathrm{Gr}(2,4)\subset{\mathbb{C}}{\bf P}^{5}.

This identifies
{\bf P}({\mathbb{C}})
of
3.7
as
{\mathbb{C}}{\bf P}^{5},

4.4

The real case and conformal compactification.
In case
\mathrm{E}={\mathbb{R}}
it is possible to define the embedding

\psi:[s:t:x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}:x_{4}]\mapsto([s:t],[x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}:x_{4}]);\ \ \pi(L)\to{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{3}

Indeed, given
(s,t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in L,
non-zero, we have

s^{2}+t^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+...+x_{4}^{2}.
Thus one of the affine coordinates

has to be nonzero because otherwise

and so

For the same reason one of the coordinates of

has to be non-zero. Thus

[s:t]\in{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}
and
[x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}:x_{4}]\in{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{3}
which defines
\psi
.

The embedding is surjective. Indeed, let
[s:t]\in{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}
and
[x_{1}:...,x_{3}:x_{4}]\in{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{3}.
Let

\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{x_{1}^{2}+...+x_{4}^{2}}{s^{2}+t^{2}}}

Then
[\lambda s:\lambda t]=[s:t]
and
[\lambda s:\lambda t:x_{1}:...,x_{3}:x_{4}]\in\pi(L).
By definition

\psi:\ [\lambda s:\lambda t:x_{1}:...,x_{3}:x_{4}]\mapsto([s:t],[x_{1}:...,x_{3}:x_{4}]).

Now note that
{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{3}
is homeomorphic to the quotient
(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3})/{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
of products of the spheres, where
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
acts diagonally. In fact the map

\psi^{\prime}:\left(\frac{(s,t)}{\sqrt{s^{2}+t^{2}}},\frac{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}}}\right)\mapsto([s:t],[x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}:x_{4}])

is a 2-1 map
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}{\bf P}^{3}.

\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
is a well-known conformal compactification of
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}).
Since
\psi^{\prime}
preserves conformal metric, our algebraic construction provides another known conformal compactification of
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}).

4.5

Set the boundary of
\mathcal{M}
to be the difference

\mathcal{F}=\pi(L)\setminus\pi\Phi(\mathcal{M})=\bar{\mathcal{M}}\setminus\mathcal{M}.

The entire boundary is described by the single condition
s+x_{4}=0
together
with the quadratic equation
\bar{Q}=0
(as in
4.1
). That cuts out exactly one quadric
in the hyperplane
\{s+x_{4}=0\}
in
\mathrm{E}{\bf P}^{5},

The hyperplane
\{s+x_{4}=0\}
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{E}{\bf P}^{4}
and does not intersect with
\mathcal{M}
(that is with
\pi\Phi(\mathcal{M})
).
Restricting
\bar{Q}
to this hyperplane yields a
nondegenerate quadric

Q^{3}:\ -t^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=0

of
dimension 3 inside
\mathrm{E}{\bf P}^{4}.
Thus,

\mathcal{F}\cong Q^{3}.

which, for
\mathrm{E}={\mathbb{R}}
is the
light cone at infinity
in the terminology of R.Penrose.
