Bowel Obstruction/Ileus Associated With the Use of Glucagon‐Like Peptide‐1 Receptor Agonists: A Nationwide Database Study
Authors: Yuichiro Matsuo, Akira Okada, Takashi Sakamoto, Hideo Yasunaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe association between glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1RA) use and the development of bowel obstruction/ileus remains unclear, and evidence for specific bowel obstruction/ileus subtypes remains limited. We examined the association between GLP‐1RA use and the incidence of bowel obstruction/ileus subtypes across different subgroups of individuals.MethodsUsing the commercially available Japanese administrative claims DeSC database, we identified individuals aged ≥ 18 years with type 2 diabetes who newly initiated either GLP‐1RAs or sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) between April 2016 and February 2024. An active‐comparator new‐user design was adopted, designating GLP‐1RA users as the exposure group and SGLT2i users as the control group. The primary outcome was bowel obstruction/ileus requiring tube decompression or surgery within 1 year. Secondary outcomes included specified small bowel obstruction, small bowel obstruction requiring surgery, paralytic ileus and colonic volvulus. Inverse probability weighting with propensity scores was used to adjust for confounding. Adjusted cumulative incidence was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods; hazard ratios were estimated using Cox regression.ResultsAmong 298 124 eligible individuals, 25 779 and 272 345 were classified into the GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i groups, respectively. The adjusted 1‐year incidence proportion of the primary outcome did not significantly differ between groups (0.14% vs. 0.17%; difference −0.03%, 95% confidence interval (CI), −0.10% to 0.04%; hazard ratio 0.80, 95% CI, 0.52–1.24). No significant between‐group differences were observed for the secondary outcomes.ConclusionsGLP‐1RA initiation in individuals with type 2 diabetes was not associated with an increased risk of bowel obstruction/ileus compared with SGLT2i initiation.Keywords: antidiabetic drug, cohort study, database research, GLP‐1 analogue, pharmaco‐epidemiology, SGLT2 inhibitor

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) are widely used in the management of type 2 diabetes because of their beneficial effects on cardiovascular outcomes, all‐cause mortality, glycemic control and weight loss [1, 2]. Since their early clinical use, concerns have been raised regarding the potentially elevated risk of bowel obstruction or ileus (i.e., impaired propulsive bowel activity in the absence of mechanical obstruction) associated with GLP‐1RAs. Drug regulatory authorities in Japan, Europe and the United States have issued safety signals regarding this potential adverse reaction [3, 4, 5].

Previous studies investigating the association between GLP‐1RA use and the development of bowel obstruction/ileus have produced inconsistent findings [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Moreover, evidence on specific subtypes of bowel obstruction/ileus (e.g., small bowel obstruction or ileus without mechanical obstruction [paralytic ileus]) remains limited. These conditions differ in pathophysiology and risk factors. For example, the most common cause of small bowel obstruction is intra‐abdominal adhesions, typically occurring in individuals with a history of abdominal surgery [11, 12]. On the other hand, diabetes affects gastrointestinal motility [13], and individuals with advanced diabetes and microvascular complications may be more susceptible to paralytic ileus [14]. Therefore, the effect of GLP‐1RAs on different subtypes of bowel obstruction/ileus may vary across subgroups of individuals, and it is clinically important to assess their safety in individuals at higher risk for specific subtypes.

The present study aimed to comprehensively examine the association between GLP‐1RA use and the incidence of different subtypes of bowel obstruction/ileus across different subgroups of individuals, using sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) as active comparators [15, 16]. We chose SGLT2is as active comparators because they have clinical indications generally similar to those of GLP‐1RAs, including use as second‐line or subsequent agents in individuals with increased cardiovascular disease risk [17, 18], and because no clear association with bowel obstruction/ileus has been reported.

Methods

Terminology

The term ‘ileus’ has sometimes been used to describe clinical entities encompassing impaired intestinal flow due to mechanical obstruction (e.g., ‘mechanical ileus’) [19, 20]. To avoid confusion, we use the term ‘bowel obstruction/ileus’ to refer to the overall clinical entity characterised by impaired intestinal flow irrespective of the presence of mechanical obstruction, and ‘paralytic ileus’ to refer specifically to impaired intestinal flow in the absence of mechanical obstruction.

Study Design and Data Source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the DeSC database (DeSC Healthcare Inc., Tokyo, Japan) [21]. This commercially available insurer‐based database contains outpatient, inpatient and dispensing claims data obtained from multiple health insurance systems (Health Insurance for salaried employees, National Health Insurance and Advanced Elderly Medical Service System for individuals aged ≥ 75 years) between April 2014 and August 2024, covering approximately 12.5 million individuals in Japan. Medical encounters are comprehensively captured while individuals are enrolled in participating health insurance systems. Observation may end prematurely when a change in health insurer occurs or the insurer ceases to provide data. Individuals who change insurers cannot be linked across insurance systems as the same person, and may be included as different individuals at different time points. The median observation period among all individuals in the database was 36 months (interquartile range, 12–64 months). Diagnoses are recorded using International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD‐10) codes, and medications are coded according to the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system. The database also contains annual health check‐up data for approximately 30% of individuals, including measurements such as height, weight, blood and urine test results and self‐reported lifestyle questionnaires.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the authors' institution. The requirement for written informed consent was waived, owing to the anonymised nature of the data.

Participants and Exposures

We identified the first prescription record for either GLP‐1RAs or SGLT2is for each individual in the database, with no previous prescription for either drug class before that record, among individuals aged ≥ 18 years with type 2 diabetes between April 2016 and February 2024. Type 2 diabetes was identified by GLP‐1RA or SGLT2i prescription accompanied by ICD‐10 codes for type 2 diabetes (E11, E12, E13 and E14) recorded in the same month at the same medical institution. We only enrolled individuals whose first prescription was provided in an outpatient setting and excluded those who were prescribed both GLP‐1RAs and SGLT2is on the same day. The date of the first prescription was designated as the index date.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes (ICD‐10 code E10); (2) end‐stage renal disease (ICD‐10 code N185) or dialysis within 3 months before the index date; (3) pregnancy (any ICD‐10 code starting with O) within 3 months before the index date; (4) < 1 year of database enrollment before the index date and (5) a history of hospitalisation for bowel obstruction/ileus (ICD‐10 codes K560, K562, K565, K566, K567 and K913) within 1 year before the index date. The final cohort included individuals who initiated either GLP‐1RAs or SGLT2is after at least 1 year without a recorded prescription for either drug class, ensuring an active‐comparator new‐user design.

Eligible individuals were classified as new users of GLP‐1RAs or SGLT2is based on dispensing records of either drug. A graphical description of the study design is provided in Figure S1.

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

The primary outcome was bowel obstruction/ileus requiring tube decompression or surgery. First, we identified hospitalisation records with ICD‐10 codes K562, K565, K566, K567 or K913, accompanied by radiography or computed tomography performed on the day before admission, day of admission or day after admission. We further restricted the outcome to hospitalisations during which nasogastric tube decompression, long‐tube decompression or small bowel obstruction‐specific surgery (intestinal adhesiolysis or small bowel resection) was performed. This definition was intended to primarily capture small bowel obstruction events, because these procedures are standard treatments for small bowel obstruction [22]. We evaluated two additional secondary outcomes related to small bowel obstruction: specified small bowel obstruction and small bowel obstruction requiring surgery. The other secondary outcomes included paralytic ileus and colonic volvulus. The outcome definitions are summarised in Table S1.

The outcomes were captured for up to 1 year from the index date. Individuals were followed‐up from the index date until the outcome, completion of 1‐year of follow‐up, death or the end of database recording (e.g., change in health insurer, cessation of data provision by an insurer or August 2024), whichever occurred first.

Adjustment Variables

Covariates for confounding adjustment included age, sex, calendar year of the index date, the number of months with administrative claims records in the previous 12 months (proxy for healthcare utilisation) and the number of hospitalisations within the previous 12 months. A history of 44 conditions was collected from the entire period before the index date using ICD‐10 codes, supplemented by health check‐up data when available. A history of 33 medications was defined as any prescription within 120 days before the index date, including the index date. We also assessed antidiabetic treatments before the index date, categorised as no treatment, 1 class of oral antidiabetic medication without insulin, 2 or more classes of oral antidiabetic medications without insulin and insulin therapy (based on prescriptions within 120 days before the index date, excluding the index date). A history of abdominal surgery within 1 year before the index date was categorised as high (colonic or gynaecological surgery) or low risk (other abdominal surgery) for bowel obstruction [23]. The full list and definitions of the covariates are provided in Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

First, we summarised the participants' baseline characteristics. We also calculated the duration of assigned‐drug continuation and adherence to the assigned treatment during follow‐up. The duration of assigned‐drug continuation was calculated by assuming treatment continuation for 120 days from each dispensing date. Adherence to the assigned treatment was defined as continuation of the assigned drug without initiation of the comparator drug.

For crude analyses of the primary outcome, we calculated the incidence rates and estimated hazard ratios (HRs) using Cox regression. Adjusted analyses were performed using inverse probability weighting with propensity scores [24]. The probability of receiving GLP‐1RAs (propensity score) was estimated using logistic regression incorporating the above‐mentioned covariates, and stabilised weights were constructed. Covariate balance was assessed using standardised mean differences (SMDs), with SMD ≤ 0.1 denoting acceptable balance [25]. In the weighted population, we constructed adjusted cumulative incidence curves using Kaplan–Meier methods and estimated the incidence proportions and between‐group differences at 1 year. We used nonparametric bootstrapping with 500 iterations to estimate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the between‐group difference [26]. Weighted Cox regression with robust standard errors was used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs. Two‐tailed p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using the intention‐to‐treat approach, and the same methods were applied to the secondary outcomes.

Variable information was collected and cleaned at a daily resolution. For the analyses, time was discretised into weekly intervals to reduce computational burden.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA/MP, version 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Subgroup Analyses

We evaluated the outcomes in predefined subgroups: younger adults (age 18–64 years), older adults (age ≥ 65 years), individuals with microvascular diabetic complications and individuals with a history of abdominal surgery. Microvascular complications were defined as diabetic neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy or chronic kidney disease or diabetic kidney disease, using the definitions applied when extracting these variables for confounding adjustment. A history of abdominal surgery was defined as any prior low or high risk abdominal surgery recorded in the database before the index date.

Primary outcome analyses were conducted in the same manner as for the overall population. Secondary outcome analyses were also conducted, except that colonic volvulus was not evaluated within subgroups because of the small event counts, and 95% CIs for between‐group differences in incidence proportions at 1 year were not calculated because of the computational burden of bootstrapping.

Additional Analyses of Individual GLP‐1RAs

We performed additional analyses comparing the risk of the primary outcome between individual GLP‐1RAs and SGLT2is, focusing on the commonly used GLP‐1RAs: dulaglutide, semaglutide and liraglutide. In each analysis, GLP‐1RA users were restricted to those who initiated the specific GLP‐1RA on the index date, and those who initiated more than one GLP‐1RA on that date were excluded. The analyses were conducted using the same methods as in the main analysis. However, in the analysis of liraglutide, the variable representing baseline antidiabetic treatment was excluded from the adjustment set because it strongly predicted treatment assignment and resulted in instability of the propensity model.

Sensitivity Analyses

We performed eight sensitivity analyses for the primary outcome in the overall population. First, we restricted bowel obstruction/ileus hospitalisations to events in which radiography or computed tomography was performed on the admission day (instead of allowing a 1‐day lag). Second, we shortened the look‐back window for baseline medication use to 90 days before the index date. Third, we redefined the adjustment variable for a history of abdominal surgery to include surgery performed at any time before the index date, rather than limiting ascertainment to 1 year before the index date. Fourth, we additionally adjusted for a proxy measure of diabetes duration. Although the database does not contain a direct measure of diabetes duration, the date of initiation of medical care for each diagnosis at each medical institution is recorded. We calculated the interval between the earliest recorded date of initiation of medical care for type 2 diabetes and the index date, categorised the interval as < 5, 5–10, 10–15 and ≥ 15 years, and included this variable as an additional adjustment variable. Fifth, we treated death as a competing event and estimated the adjusted subdistribution HRs with 95% CIs and p values using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models [27, 28]. Sixth, we performed a per‐protocol analysis, censoring individuals at treatment discontinuation (no prescription for the assigned drug within 120 days from the last prescription) or initiation of the comparator drug. We created two separate time‐varying censoring weights for treatment discontinuation and initiation of the comparator drug using the treatment indicator, flexible function of time, their product terms and the baseline covariates as explanatory variables in pooled logistic models. Thereafter, we applied the treatment weights and both censoring weights to create a pseudopopulation without censoring due to treatment deviation. Seventh, we repeated the analysis using metformin as an active comparator. Metformin is associated with adverse gastrointestinal symptoms [29] but has not been shown to increase the risk of bowel obstruction/ileus; it may also be avoided in individuals perceived to be susceptible to abdominal adverse events to a similar extent as GLP‐1RAs, thereby serving as an alternative active comparator. Eighth, we repeated the analysis using dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4is) as an active comparator. Like GLP‐1RAs, DPP‐4is act on the incretin pathway, and concerns have similarly been raised regarding their potential association with bowel obstruction/ileus [30]. Previous studies of this association have reported inconsistent findings [6, 7].

Results

Participants

We initially identified 618 489 first prescription records for either GLP‐1RAs or SGLT2is among individuals aged ≥ 18 years with type 2 diabetes between April 2016 and February 2024. After applying the exclusion criteria, 298 124 individuals remained for the analysis. Of these, 25 779 and 272 345 individuals were assigned to the GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i groups, respectively (Figure 1).

The mean age of the study cohort was 73.2 years, and 42.2% of individuals were male. Overall, approximately 80% of the study population had hypertension, and 35% had heart failure. Outcomes

The mean follow‐up period was 44.8 weeks (standard deviation [SD], 14.7), and the median follow‐up period was 53 weeks (interquartile range [IQR], 43–53) in the GLP‐1RA group. In the SGLT2i group, the corresponding values were 45.1 weeks (SD, 14.6) and 53 weeks (IQR, 45–53). During follow‐up, 1039 (4.0%) individuals in the GLP‐1RA group and 7235 (2.7%) individuals in the SGLT2i group died. The mean duration of assigned‐drug continuation during follow‐up was 38.6 weeks in the GLP‐1RA group and 41.3 weeks in the SGLT2i group. The mean duration of adherence to the assigned treatment was 36.4 weeks in the GLP‐1RA group and 40.8 weeks in the SGLT2i group. A total of 17 077 (66.2%) individuals in the GLP‐1RA group and 222 138 (81.6%) individuals in the SGLT2i group remained adherent to the assigned treatment until the end of follow‐up.

The primary outcome occurred in 34 (0.13%) and 390 (0.14%) individuals in the GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i groups, respectively, corresponding to crude incidence rates of 1.53 and 1.65 per 1000 person‐years. After adjustment, no significant difference in the incidence of the primary outcome was observed between GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i users. The adjusted 1‐year incidence proportion for GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i users was 0.14% vs. 0.17% (difference −0.03%; 95% CI, −0.10% to 0.04%), respectively, and the adjusted HR was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.52–1.24; p = 0.321) (Table 2, Figure 2). No significant between‐group differences were observed for the secondary outcomes (Table 2, Figure 3).

Subgroup Analyses

The incidence of the primary outcome did not differ significantly between the GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i groups across the prespecified subgroups (Table 3, Figure 4). Moreover, the incidence of secondary outcomes did not differ significantly across subgroups (Tables S4–S7, Figures S2–S5). The risks of small bowel obstruction‐related outcomes did not increase among individuals with a history of abdominal surgery (adjusted HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.26–2.76] for specified small bowel obstruction; adjusted HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 0.20–6.74] for small bowel obstruction requiring surgery), and neither was the risk of paralytic ileus increased among individuals with microvascular diabetic complications (adjusted HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.28–1.69).

Additional Analyses of Individual GLP‐1RAs

The most commonly used GLP‐1RAs among the study participants were dulaglutide (n = 11 735, 45.5%), semaglutide (n = 9025, 35.0%) and liraglutide (n = 4188, 16.3%) (Table S8); separate analyses were therefore conducted for these agents. No significant difference in the primary outcome was observed in any of the analyses. The adjusted HRs were 0.80 (95% CI, 0.30–2.13) for dulaglutide, 0.81 (95% CI, 0.27–2.41) for semaglutide and 1.19 (95% CI, 0.45–3.15) for liraglutide (Table S9, Figure S6).

Sensitivity Analysis

The findings of most sensitivity analyses were consistent with those of the primary analysis. No significant difference in the incidence of the primary outcome was observed between the GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i groups (Table S10, Figures S7–S13), except in the analysis using DPP‐4is as an active comparator. Compared with DPP‐4i users, GLP‐1RA users had a lower risk of the primary outcome in both the crude and adjusted analyses (crude HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.22–1.00; adjusted HR, 0.40; 95% CI, 0.17–0.96) (Table S10, Figure S14). Compared with metformin users, GLP‐1RA users generally had more comorbidities and greater medication use at baseline. Compared with DPP‐4i users, GLP‐1RA users were younger, more likely to be obese or overweight and to have diabetic complications, and had greater use of antidiabetic medications at baseline. Covariate balance was achieved between the groups after adjustment (Tables S11 and S12).

Discussion

This study included relatively older individuals with type 2 diabetes (mean age, 73.2 years). GLP‐1RA users were more likely to have chronic kidney disease or diabetic kidney disease, dementia, microvascular diabetic complications and receive insulin therapy, whereas they were less likely to have heart failure or be obese or overweight. No significant difference was observed in the incidence of the primary outcome (bowel obstruction/ileus requiring tube decompression or surgery) between GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i users. This finding was consistent across younger and older age groups, individuals with microvascular diabetic complications and those with a history of abdominal surgery. No significant between‐group differences were observed for other types of bowel obstruction/ileus in the overall population or across subgroups. Notably, the risks of small bowel obstruction‐related outcomes were not increased among individuals with a history of abdominal surgery, and neither was the risk of paralytic ileus increased among individuals with microvascular diabetic complications.

The relatively advanced age of the study population likely reflected the aging population in Japan. The higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease or diabetic kidney disease and microvascular diabetic complications may reflect the tendency for GLP‐1RAs to be prescribed later in the course of diabetes than SGLT2is in Japan [31]. Conversely, GLP‐1RA users were less likely to be obese or overweight. Although this appears counterintuitive, obesity may not have been a strong driver of GLP‐1RA prescribing in the present study population, given the relatively low prevalence of obesity or overweight among older individuals with diabetes in Japan [32] and general concerns regarding weight loss or appetite loss in older adults [33].

Previous real‐world database studies comparing the risk of bowel obstruction/ileus between GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i users have reported inconsistent results. A study from the United Kingdom reported that the risk of bowel obstruction/ileus was increased among GLP‐1RA users (HR 1.69; 95% CI, 1.04–2.74) [6], whereas more recent studies have reported no significant differences [7, 8, 9]. These studies typically defined outcomes using ICD‐10 codes for bowel obstruction/ileus of any type, while some also evaluated paralytic ileus separately, reporting no significant results [6, 7, 8]. Similarly, a recent meta‐analysis of randomised controlled studies similarly found no difference in intestinal obstruction or paralytic ileus between the GLP‐1RA and placebo groups [10].

GLP‐1RA use has been associated with adverse gastrointestinal events such as nausea, vomiting and gastroesophageal reflux disease [10, 34]. These adverse effects are biologically plausible because GLP‐1RAs induce delayed gastric emptying [35]. In contrast, the direct biological link between GLP‐1RA use and small bowel obstruction is less clear. Most small bowel obstructions are attributable to pre‐existing intra‐abdominal adhesions [11, 12], which are not expected to be influenced by GLP‐1RA use. Therefore, it is plausible that the incidence of the primary and small bowel obstruction‐related outcomes did not differ between groups. Furthermore, although some evidence suggests that GLP‐1RAs may reduce small and large bowel motility [35, 36], the clinical relevance of these effects is unclear. Accordingly, it is plausible that GLP‐1RA use was not associated with an increased risk of paralytic ileus. The lower risk of the primary outcome among GLP‐1RA users in the sensitivity analysis using DPP‐4is as the comparator warrants cautious interpretation. Although it may reflect a potential causal relationship between DPP‐4i use and bowel obstruction, it may also reflect unmeasured confounding. DPP‐4is have long been, and continue to be, the most frequently prescribed antidiabetic medications in Japan, typically as a first‐line treatment, likely because of their perceived safety among prescribing physicians [31, 37]. This may have led to an imbalance in unmeasured factors between groups, such as frailty. Nevertheless, this result would at least support the hypothesis that GLP‐1RAs may not increase the risk of bowel obstruction.

Clinically, our results suggest that even among individuals assumed to be at a higher risk of bowel obstruction/ileus, such as those with microvascular diabetic complications or a history of abdominal surgery, decisions regarding GLP‐1RA initiation can be primarily guided by standard considerations, including cardiovascular disease risk, need for weight reduction, cost and the treated individual's preference [38].

Our study has some limitations. First, as with any observational studies, residual confounding may remain. Some potentially important variables, including HbA1c, body mass index (BMI) and diabetes duration, were unavailable for most individuals and therefore could not be directly included as adjustment variables. However, we adjusted for related proxy measures, including concomitant antidiabetic medication use and microvascular diabetic complications as proxies for glycemic control, obesity or overweight defined using ICD‐10 codes combined with BMI from health check‐up data when available, and microvascular diabetic complications as a proxy for diabetes duration. In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis with additional adjustment using an indirect measure of diabetes duration available in the database. Second, we could not perform analyses extending the follow‐up period beyond 1 year. This was due to the relatively limited observation period for each individual in the overall database (median, 36 months), frequent deviation from the assigned treatment and the lack of detailed time‐updated determinants of treatment deviation. Therefore, although one previous study suggested a more pronounced increase in the risk of bowel obstruction/ileus beyond 1 year after GLP‐1RA initiation, we could not assess outcomes beyond 1 year [6]. Third, the numbers of outcome events were relatively small, particularly for some secondary outcomes and within some subgroups, which may have reduced the statistical power necessary to detect modest differences. Consequently, the absence of statistical significance does not necessarily exclude clinically meaningful associations. Fourth, outcome definitions for bowel obstruction/ileus have not been formally validated, potentially resulting in misclassification of events. However, we restricted outcomes to events requiring hospitalisation and with accompanying imaging to improve specificity and focus on clinically meaningful events.

In conclusion, no significant difference in the incidence of bowel obstruction/ileus requiring tube decompression or surgery was observed between GLP‐1RA and SGLT2i users.
