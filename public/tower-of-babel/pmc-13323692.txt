Determination of glycemic index and load of commercially available non-pigmented and pigmented rice varieties in the Philippines
Authors: Cecile Leah T. Bayaga, Cecile Klaudine C. Cabigas, Isa-Terese D. Santos, Marietoni B. Pico, Elisa Marie M. Andaya, Rosaly V. Manaois, Riza G. Abilgos-Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rice is a key commodity in the Philippines, with the majority of Filipinos considering it their staple food. Concerns have arisen about the potential link between the long-term consumption of carbohydrate-rich foods, particularly those with a high glycemic index (GI), and the development of chronic illnesses such as Type 2 diabetes. This study characterized locally grown pigmented and non-pigmented rice varieties in vitro (amylose content, proximate composition, dietary fiber, antioxidant capacity) and assessed their in vivo glycemic index and glycemic load in healthy adults.All rice samples were provided by the Philippine Rice Research Institute sourced from the local farmers. The three rice varieties (Ominio, Red Dinorado, and NSIC 222) were selected based on their differences in pigmentation and expected starch digestibility, while physicochemical properties including lipid, protein, dietary fiber, amylose content, and antioxidant capacity were subsequently evaluated as part of the study. The study enrolled 10 adult participants (aged 29–59 years) and was conducted over an 11-week period. Following the international glycemic index testing standards (ISO 26642:2010), capillary blood glucose measurements were obtained postconsumption of test foods standardized to provide 25 g of available carbohydrate. Ten healthy adult participants (n = 10), aged 29–59 years, with mean BMI 26.2 ± 2.2 kg/m² and HbA1c 5.6 ± 0.3% at baseline, were recruited. Fasting and postprandial capillary blood glucose samples were collected via finger-prick using the HemoCue® Glucose 201 System. The fasting sample was taken at − 5 min relative to test food intake, followed by postprandial measurements at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min. The incremental area under the glucose response curve (iAUC₀–₁₂₀min), excluding the baseline fasting value and considering only the positive incremental area, was calculated to derive the glycemic index (GI) of each rice variety. The corresponding glycemic load (GL) values were subsequently determined.The glycemic index (GI) values of the test foods varied across rice types. Black rice had the lowest GI at 49, which fell within the low category. Red rice had a GI of 69, placing it in the medium category, while white rice registered the highest GI at 71, classifying it as high.In terms of glycemic load (GL), all rice varieties were consistently classified within the medium range. Black rice had a GL of 12.25, while both red rice and white rice recorded a GL of 17.75.These findings provide valuable insights for consumers seeking to manage weight and lower the risk of non-communicable diseases by choosing locally grown pigmented rice varieties. Further studies should examine how rice processing, harvesting, and storage affect glycemic responses, and should also include a broader range of pigmented rice varieties to strengthen dietary guidelines.

Introduction

Rice serves as the primary dietary staple for approximately 80% of the Filipino population, with consumption occurring three to five times daily1. Despite its role as a fundamental energy source, the high intake of carbohydrate-dense foods such as rice has raised concerns regarding long-term health risks, particularly the development of chronic conditions such as type 2 diabetes. Milled or polished white rice is typically classified as a high-glycemic index (GI) food. The GI measures the overall glycemic excursion—quantified as the incremental area under the blood glucose response curve (iAUC)—relative to a reference standard, providing a more relevant metric for postprandial glycemia management than peak glucose levels alone. The incorporation of low-GI rice varieties into the diet may improve glycemic control and mitigate risks associated with cardiovascular disease, obesity, and type 2 diabetes2,3.

The glycemic response to rice is modulated by several intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including amylose content, the structural integrity of dietary fiber, and the presence of bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and anthocyanins4–6. Glycemic load (GL) further refines this assessment by integrating both carbohydrate quality (GI) and quantity per serving, offering a superior predictor of glycemic impact in typical dietary patterns. Evaluation of both metrics is critical in the Philippine context, where type 2 diabetes prevalence among adults is approximately 7.5% and cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality7,8. Higher dietary GI and GL have been associated with increased cardiovascular disease events and mortality in large prospective analyses9,10.

Despite the ubiquity of rice in the Philippines, data on the GI and GL of local cultivars remain scarce. Existing literature often relies on international data, which may not accurately reflect the unique physicochemical properties and cultivation conditions of Philippine-grown rice.

To address this gap, this study integrated in vitro characterization (amylose content, proximate composition, dietary fiber, and antioxidant capacity) with in vivo GI and GL testing of three prominent local varieties: black, red, and white rice. It was hypothesized that the bioactive-rich pigmented varieties (black and red) would exhibit significantly lower GI and GL values compared to polished white rice, thereby serving as healthier staple alternatives for consumers.

Materials and methods

Selection, Preparation, and Physicochemical Testing of Types of Rice

Samples and Reagents

Three varieties of locally grown rice were used as test foods: black rice (Ominio), red rice (Red Dinorado), and white rice (NSIC 222). These were obtained from local farmers in San Mateo, Isabela; Midsayap, South Cotabato; and Science City of Muñoz, Nueva Ecija, respectively. Chemicals including 2,2-diphenylpicrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,4,6-trispyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ), and Trolox (6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethyl-chroman-2-carboxylic acid) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich for antioxidant capacity assays. All other chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Test Food Processing

The rice samples were processed at the Philippine Rice Research Institute following standard procedures. The varieties were dehulled, and the white rice was further polished. A portion of the grains was ground to a fine powder and stored at −20 °C for physicochemical analysis. The remaining whole grains were cooked following standardized procedures; the specific cooking time, warming time, water-to-rice ratios, and soaking times (for unpolished samples) were optimized for each variety (Table 1). Each serving was standardized to provide 25 g of total available carbohydrates (TAC). After cooking, samples for proximate analysis were lyophilized, ground, and stored at − 20 °C.

Physicochemical Characterization of Rice Samples

The amylose and amylopectin contents were determined using the Megazyme Assay Kit. Proximate composition, including moisture, crude ash, crude protein, and crude fat, was analyzed using standard AOAC International methods. Total dietary fiber was determined via the enzymatic-gravimetric method, and available carbohydrates were calculated by subtracting total dietary fiber from total carbohydrates. Antioxidant capacity was measured using DPPH radical scavenging and FRAP assays, expressed as Trolox equivalent (TE)/g.

Test Sessions

Study design and randomization

This study utilized a randomized, repeated-measures crossover design over an 11-week period. Each participant attended nine test visits, testing each of the three rice varieties twice and the glucose reference three times. The sequence was randomized to minimize carry-over effects, and a minimum one-week washout period separated each session. While blinding participants was not feasible due to the visible differences in the rice, investigators processing the blood glucose data were blinded.

Subject Selection and Recruitment

Ten healthy adults aged 29–59 years were recruited and enrolled in two cohorts (batches) due to clinic capacity and logistical constraints. Both cohorts adhered to the exact same study protocol without modifications. Eligibility was assessed based on the following criteria:

Trial Procedures and Blood Glucose Measurement

Prior to each feeding session, baseline vital signs were assessed by the study physician to ensure participants were within acceptable ranges. Following a 10–12 h overnight fast, participants consumed either a rice test food (standardized to 25 g TAC) or the glucose reference drink (Gluco Plus 50® orange-flavored beverage containing 50 g TAC). Capillary blood samples were collected via finger-prick using the HemoCue® Glucose 201 System at 0 (fasting baseline), 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min post-consumption. Participants remained seated during the 2-hour test to reduce physical activity variability.

Statistical and Data Analyses

The incremental area under the curve (iAUC₀–₁₂₀ min) for blood glucose was calculated using the trapezoidal method, utilizing only the positive area above the fasting baseline value in accordance with international GI testing standards. The mean iAUC from the three reference glucose tests served as the denominator for each participant’s GI calculation. In this study, test foods contained 25 g of available carbohydrate, while the reference food contained a standard 50 g dose to mitigate participant burden from the volume of whole grain rice consumed. To facilitate standard calculation and align the results with the 50 g glucose reference, the iAUC values of the 25 g rice test foods were doubled. Results of GI and GL Testing

The postprandial glucose response curves for the three rice varieties and the glucose reference are presented in Fig. 2. The minimal error bars observed in the postprandial curves reflect low variability in glucose responses within participants, indicating good reproducibility of the measurements. All calculated SEM values were below 4 mg/dL. Given that the y-axis of the graph is scaled in 50 mg/dL increments, these SEM values may appear visually minimal or less prominent in the figure, although these values are present and accounted for in the analysis. Peak postprandial glucose responses occurred at approximately 30–45 min across all test foods, followed by a gradual decline toward baseline levels by 120 min. The glycemic index (GI) values varied across the rice types. Black rice demonstrated the lowest GI at 49, placing it in the low category. Red rice recorded a GI of 69 (medium category), and white rice registered the highest GI at 71 (high category). Despite the variations in GI, the glycemic load (GL) for all three rice varieties fell consistently within the medium range. Specifically, black rice had a GL of 12.25, while both red and white rice recorded a GL of 17.75.

Discussion

Interpretation of Glycemic Outcomes and Physicochemical Influences

The present study evaluated the glycemic index (GI) and glycemic load (GL) of three commercially available rice varieties in the Philippines. Black rice exhibited a low GI of 49, which is lower than global averages for both white and brown rice. The distinctly lower glycemic response of black rice is consistent with its specific physicochemical profile, notably its higher dietary fiber content compared to polished white rice, as well as its rich anthocyanin and polyphenol content. Endogenous phenolic compounds in pigmented rice are known to inhibit starch-hydrolyzing enzymes, thereby slowing digestion and glucose absorption11,12.

The GI of unpolished red rice (69) was found to be statistically similar to that of polished white rice (71), despite red rice possessing higher amylose (23.54 g/100 g) and greater antioxidant capacities. While elevated amylose and polyphenol contents typically attenuate starch digestibility by promoting resistant starch formation13,14, this effect may have been counteracted by the physical preparation required for the red rice. Specifically, the red rice required a significantly longer cooking time (56 min) compared to the white rice (30 min) due to its harder texture and thicker bran layer. Prolonged thermal treatment and greater water absorption enhance starch gelatinization, which may counteract the potential GI-lowering effects of the grain’s intrinsic macronutrient profile15. This shows the impact of culinary practices and cooking conditions on postprandial glycemic responses.

This study compares unpolished pigmented varieties (black and red rice) with polished non-pigmented white rice. While this comparison introduces variables such as fiber matrix integrity and bran thickness, the design prioritizes ecological validity by evaluating the cultivars in the forms most commonly commercially available and consumed. Current nutritional guidelines consistently recommend the substitution of refined cereals with whole grains to improve cardiometabolic outcomes16,17. The findings provide region-specific empirical support for these guidelines, demonstrating that the commercially available unpolished black rice elicited a substantially lower glycemic response compared to the traditional polished white staple.

Comparison with Existing Literature

The GI values observed in this study align with existing literature, which reports wide variability in the GI of rice products worldwide. The GI of the white rice tested (71) is highly comparable to international averages for polished white rice (mean GI of 73)18, confirming its classification as a high-GI food. The GI of the red rice (69) falls within previously reported ranges of 55 to 73 for red varieties19. Furthermore, the low GI of the black rice (49) is highly consistent with comparable findings in Southeast Asia, where GI values ranging from 42 to 53 have been reported for black rice cultivated in Indonesia20.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides valuable, locally relevant data by combining comprehensive in vitro characterization with in vivo clinical testing. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, to mitigate participant burden, the test foods were administered at a 25 g available carbohydrate dose rather than the standard 50 g dose and mathematically doubled to align with the 50 g glucose reference. This proportional scaling assumes an approximate linear relationship between carbohydrate load and glycemic excursion21,22, which may not fully capture physiological responses. Consequently, absolute values should be interpreted with caution when directly compared to isoglycemic 50 g reference studies18,23,24.

Second, the participant pool predominantly consisted of overweight individuals with normal fasting glucose but some borderline HbA1c values. Excess adiposity and prediabetic tendencies can influence insulin sensitivity and postprandial glucose disposal, potentially affecting GI outcomes25,26. Third, normal day-to-day biological variability in fasting glucose was observed, likely influenced by the absence of standardized evening meals prior to the test sessions. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts and standardized pre-test diets are warranted to further investigate these interactions.

Lastly, given the study’s focus on glycemic index, only total dietary fiber was determined. It is recommended that future studies should profile soluble and insoluble fiber fractions to better understand their distinct effects on the glycemic matrix.

Conclusion

The present study confirms that pigmented rice varieties, particularly unpolished black rice, exhibit lower glycemic indices, likely driven by their higher fiber, protein, and antioxidant content. While red rice also demonstrated strong antioxidant properties, its GI was statistically similar to that of polished white rice, which recorded the highest GI. This highlights that extended cooking times and increased water absorption, required for varieties with thicker bran layers, can enhance starch gelatinization and counteract the intrinsic GI-lowering benefits of amylose and polyphenols. These findings underscore the complex interplay between starch composition, macronutrients, fiber, polyphenols, and culinary preparation in determining postprandial glucose responses.

From a methodological standpoint, future GI testing should prioritize standardizing the total available carbohydrate (TAC) content between test and reference foods to 50 g to improve direct comparability and eliminate the need for linear mathematical adjustments. Additionally, future research should investigate the effects of blended rice consumption, various cooking methods, and post-harvest handling on the glycemic index. Further characterization of specific polyphenolic compounds—such as cyanidin-3-glucoside, ferulic acid, and p-coumaric acid, which inhibit α-amylase and α-glucosidase activity to slow starch digestion—is also highly recommended27,28.

Ultimately, from a public health perspective, integrating unpolished pigmented rice, such as black rice, into Filipino dietary guidelines offers a culturally appropriate, evidence-based strategy to enhance population-level glycemic control and mitigate the rising risk of diet-related chronic diseases.
