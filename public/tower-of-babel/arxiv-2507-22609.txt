A Structure-Preserving Rational Integrator for the Replicator Dynamics on the Probability Simplex
Mario Pezzella

Abstract
In this work, we introduce a quadratically convergent and dynamically consistent integrator specifically designed for the replicator dynamics. The proposed scheme combines a two-stage rational approximation with a normalization step to ensure confinement to the probability simplex and unconditional preservation of non-negativity, invariant sets and equilibria. A rigorous convergence analysis is provided to establish the scheme’s second-order accuracy, and an embedded auxiliary method is devised for adaptive time-stepping based on local error estimation. Furthermore, a discrete analogue of the quotient rule, which governs the evolution of component ratios, is shown to hold. Numerical experiments validate the theoretical results, illustrating the method’s ability to reproduce complex dynamics and to outperform well-established solvers in particularly challenging scenarios.



Keywords.
Rational approximation, replicator system, structure-preserving scheme, high order, dynamical consistency.

Introduction

The replicator equation emerges in a wide spectrum of scientific disciplines, where it models the competitive dynamics among interacting species or strategies shaped by frequency-dependent selection. In its classical continuous-time formulation, the replicator system is given by

\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}(t)=x_{i}(t)\left(f_{i}(\bm{x}(t))-\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t)f_{j}(\bm{x}(t))\right),\quad i=1,\dots,N,

(1)

where
\bm{x}(t)=[x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{N}(t)]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denotes the state vector and
f_{i}(\bm{x}(t))
,
i=1,\dots,N
, represents the fitness associated with the
i
-th component. The primary objective of this work is to construct a high-order, positivity-preserving numerical method for system (
1
), which unconditionally retains key structural features of the continuous dynamics constrained to the probability simplex.

Originally introduced by Taylor and Jonker
[
68
]
and later named by Schuster and Sigmund
[
62
]
, the replicator framework was formalized in a biological setting by Maynard Smith and Price
[
66
,
48
]
to describe natural selection independently of genetic mechanisms. It also laid the foundations of evolutionary game theory, where replicator equations describe how populations adjust their strategic behavior in response to payoff differences. In this setting, the equilibria of the system correspond to the Nash equilibria of the underlying game, offering a dynamical perspective on classical notions of game-theoretic stability
[
60
,
36
,
11
,
4
]
.
Since then, it has become a cornerstone of theoretical biology, with applications ranging from adaptation theory to ecological and genetic modeling
[
37
,
54
]
. In theoretical ecology, the replicator equation can be derived from generalized Lotka–Volterra systems reformulated in one higher dimension
[
37
,
38
]
, a connection that has motivated studies on polymorphic populations and phenotype distributions
[
69
]
. In population genetics, it provides a deterministic approximation of allele frequency dynamics in diploid, randomly mating populations under selection
[
38
,
25
]
. More recently, replicator dynamics have gained renewed relevance in the modeling of pathogen competition and infectious disease transmission, particularly in scenarios involving multiple co-circulating strains
[
26
,
46
]
.

Beyond biological contexts, equation (
1
) has found increasing application in computational sciences. It has been employed to model strategic behavior on complex networks
[
44
,
59
]
, to identify dominant sets in clustering and pattern recognition
[
23
,
56
]
and to tackle problems in combinatorial optimization
[

]
, multi-agent reinforcement learning
[

]
and evolutionary finance
[

]
.

Due to this diversity of applications, the efficient numerical integration of system (
1
) presents practical challenges. In particular, standard numerical schemes often fail to retain the structural properties and invariants of the continuous system, unless the time step is adequately refined. As a result, numerical simulations may lose long-term reliability and interpretability in applied contexts. Such considerations, combined with the lack of structure-preserving schemes specifically devised for the replicator system, motivate the present study and the development of a second-order, dynamically consistent integrator for model (
1

Several contributions in the literature have addressed similar goals in different settings. Particular emphasis has been placed on the numerical preservation of positivity, a fundamental requirement in many dynamical systems arising in real-world applications. In this regard, NonStandard Finite Difference (NSFD) schemes

,

2
The continuous-time model

We consider the replicator model (
1
) compactly reformulated as follows

\dfrac{d\bm{x}}{dt}(t)=\bm{x}(t)\odot\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}(t)\right)\,\bm{e}\right)

(2)

where
\bm{x}(t)=[x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{N}(t)]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the non-negative state vector,
\bm{f}(\bm{x})=[f_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,f_{N}(\bm{x})]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the vector of fitness functions,
\bm{e}=[1,\dots,1]^{\mathsf{T}}
is the vector of all ones and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard (component-wise) product. There is ample literature devoted to the study and analysis of models of the form (
2
), with foundational contributions in
[
65
,
62
]
. In what follows, we briefly recall the main structural features of the model, which will guide the construction of dynamics-preserving numerical schemes.

•

The
N-1
dimensional probability simplex
\Delta^{N-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(see, for instance,
[
14
, Section 2.2.4]
), defined as the set

\Delta^{N-1}=\left\{\bm{\xi}=[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ :\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}=1\text{ and }\xi_{i}\geq 0,\ \forall\,i=1,\dots,N\right\},

(3)

is positively invariant under the flow of the replicator dynamics, that is,

\bm{x}(0)\in\Delta^{N-1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bm{x}(t)\in\Delta^{N-1},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(4)

Equivalently, if
\bm{x}(0)\in\Delta^{N-1}
, then

\bm{x}(t)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}(t)=1,\quad\forall t\geq 0,

(5)

where here, and throughout this work, vector inequalities are to be intended component-wise. Moreover, every face of the simplex, i.e. each subsimplex of the form

\Delta_{\mathcal{I}}^{N-1}=\left\{\bm{\xi}=[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\Delta^{N-1}\ :\ \xi_{i}=0\ \ \forall i\in\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\dots,N\}\right\},

(6)

is positively invariant as well.

•

The invariance of the probability simplex naturally leads to a classification of the equilibria of (
1
) according to their location within
\Delta^{N-1}
, as detailed below.
Let
\bm{e}^{(k)}=[e_{1}^{(k)},\dots,e_{N}^{(k)}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\Delta^{N-1}
, for
k=1,\dots,N
, denote the
k
-th canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, that is,

e_{k}^{(k)}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{l}^{(k)}=0,\quad\text{for all }l\in\{1,\dots,N\}\setminus\{k\}.

(7)

Each of these vectors
\bm{e}^{(k)}
is an equilibrium of the replicator system (
1
), in the sense that

\bm{x}(0)=\bm{e}^{(k)}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\bm{x}(t)=\bm{e}^{(k)},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(8)

Such equilibria are referred to as corner equilibria, since they lie at the vertices of the simplex.
In addition, there may exist other equilibria within the interior or on the faces of
\Delta^{N-1}
. These are the so-called internal equilibria, given by points
\bm{x}^{*}\in\Delta^{N-1}
satisfying

\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{*}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{j}(\bm{x}^{*})=f^{*},\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,N,

(9)

for some constant
f^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

A distinctive analytical property of the replicator system is the
quotient rule
, which describes the evolution of the ratio between any two components. For all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
with
x_{j}(t)>0
, it holds that

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\right)=\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\left(f_{i}(\bm{x}(t))-f_{j}(\bm{x}(t))\right).

(10)

This shows that the relative growth rate between components
i
and
j
depends solely on their fitness difference.

The structural features outlined above often reflect fundamental physical or biological principles inherent in replicator systems. For this reason, particular emphasis will be placed, in Section
3
, on the development of a numerical scheme that unconditionally preserve such properties in the discrete-time setting.

3
The normalized rational integrator

Let
\{t_{n},\ n=0,\dots,M\},
with
M\in\mathbb{N}
, denote a (possibly nonuniform) discretization of the integration interval
[0,T]
, satisfying

t_{0}=0,\quad t_{M}=T\quad\text{and}\quad t_{n+1}=t_{n}+h_{n},\quad\text{with }h_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\quad n=0,\dots,M-1.

(11)

To approximate the solution of the replicator system (
1
), we introduce the following numerical scheme

\begin{split}&\varphi_{i}^{n}=\dfrac{h_{n}}{2}\left(f_{i}(\bm{x}^{n})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n}f_{j}(\bm{x}^{n})\right),\qquad\qquad\qquad\rho_{i}^{n}=\dfrac{(\varphi_{i}^{n})^{2}+6\varphi_{i}^{n}+12}{(\varphi_{i}^{n})^{2}-6\varphi_{i}^{n}+12},\\
&x_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(x_{i}^{n}\,\rho_{i}^{n})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n}\,\rho_{j}^{n}\right)^{-1},\\
&\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=h_{n}\left(f_{i}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}f_{j}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right),\quad\rho_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\dfrac{(\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{2}+6\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+12}{(\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{2}-6\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+12},\\
&x_{i}^{n+1}=(x_{i}^{n}\,\rho_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n}\,\rho_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-1},\\
\end{split}

(12)

where
\bm{x}^{0}=\bm{x}(0)
is given and
\bm{x}^{n}=[x_{1}^{n},\dots,x_{N}^{n}]^{\mathsf{T}}\approx\bm{x}(t_{n})
for each
n=1,\dots,M
.

Throughout this paper we refer, when needed, to the following equivalent compact reformulation of (
12
)

\begin{split}&\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\dfrac{\bm{x}^{n}\odot\bm{R}\left(\dfrac{h_{n}}{2}\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n}\right)\bm{e}\right)\right)}{\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\bm{x}^{n}\odot\bm{R}\left(\dfrac{h_{n}}{2}\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n}\right)\bm{e}\right)\right)\right)},\\[5.16663pt]
&\bm{x}^{n+1}=\dfrac{\bm{x}^{n}\odot\bm{R}\left(h_{n}\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{e}\right)\right)}{\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\bm{x}^{n}\odot\bm{R}\left(h_{n}\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{e}\right)\right)\right)},\end{split}

(13)

where
\odot
denotes the component-wise Hadamard product and

\bm{f}(\bm{x})=[f_{1}(\bm{x}),\dots,f_{N}(\bm{x})]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{R}(\bm{x})=[\rho(x_{1}),\dots,\rho(x_{N})]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

with
\rho(z)=(z^{2}+6z+12)(z^{2}-6z+12)^{-1}
for
z\in\mathbb{R}.
The two-stage formulation (
13
) is fully explicit, yielding an efficient and easy-to-implement algorithm. The next sections are devoted to its numerical and dynamical analysis.

3.1
Structure-preserving properties

The normalized rational scheme (
12
) is specifically designed to preserve, with no limitations on the steplength, the fundamental structural features of the replicator dynamics. The forthcoming results establish the dynamical consistency, as defined in
[
1
,
22
]
, of the proposed discretization.

Theorem 3.1

(
Simplex-confined discrete dynamics
)
.

Let the initial value
\bm{x}^{0}
belong to the probability simplex
\Delta^{N-1}
defined in (
3
) and let
\{\bm{x}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},

\{\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the sequences generated by the numerical method (
13
). Then, for any choices of positive discretization steps
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},

x_{i}^{n},x_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{n}=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=1,\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Furthermore, if
\bm{x}^{0}
lies on a face of the simplex
\Delta^{N-1}_{\mathcal{I}},
as defined in (
6
), then
\bm{x}^{n},\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\in\Delta^{N-1}_{\mathcal{I}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Proof.

We preliminarily remark that the polynomials
p_{\pm}(z)=z^{2}\pm 6z+12
satisfy

p_{\pm}(z)=(z\pm 3)^{2}+3,

which implies that
p_{\pm}(z)>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the rational function
\rho(z)=p_{+}(z)/p_{-}(z)
and the vector-valued mapping
R(\bm{x})=[\rho(x_{1}),\dots,\rho(x_{N})]^{\mathsf{T}}
are well defined and strictly positive on the real line and on
\mathbb{R}^{N},
respectively.

We proceed by mathematical induction to prove that the statements
\bm{x}^{n},\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0
and
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{n}=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=1
hold true for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.
The base case
n=0
naturally follows from the assumptions and the positivity of
\bm{R}
. Consider
n\geq 0
and assume that the properties are verified for each
0\leq m\leq n.
Then, from (
13
),

\bm{R}(\bm{x}^{n})\geq 0\,\implies\,\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0\quad\wedge\quad\bm{R}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})\geq 0\,\implies\,\bm{x}^{n+1}\geq 0,

that yields the non-negativity result. Furthermore, straightforward manipulations of (
13
) lead to
\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n}=\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=1.
Analogously, if we assume that
\bm{x}^{m}\in\Delta^{N-1}_{\mathcal{I}}
for each
0\leq m\leq n,
then from the second and the last equations in (
12
) it follows that
x_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=x_{i}^{n+1}=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I},
which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
3.1
shows that the rational integrator (
12
) preserves the structure of the probability simplex, ensuring the validity of (
4
) at the discrete-time level, regardless of the chosen stepsizes. Equivalently, in compliance with (
5
), the scheme (
12
) is unconditionally non-negative and for the numerical solution the linear invariant

\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n}=1,\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(14)

holds true. In addition, all faces of the simplex are invariant under the discrete dynamics, thereby preserving the geometric structure of the continuous system.

Beyond geometric invariance, another crucial aspect of dynamical consistency lies in the preservation of equilibrium configurations. The following results show that the rational integrator unconditionally reproduces both corner and internal equilibria of the continuous-time system (
1
).

Theorem 3.2

(
Corner equilibria preservation
)
.

Assume that the initial value satisfies
\bm{x}^{0}=\bm{e}^{(k)},
for any
k\in\{1,\dots,N\},
with
\bm{e}^{(k)}
defined in (
7
). Then, for any choices of positive steplenghts
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},
the properties

x_{k}^{n}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad x_{l}^{n}=0,\quad\text{for each}\quad l\in\{1,\dots,N\}\setminus\{k\},\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

hold true. Equivalently,
\bm{x}^{n}=\bm{x}^{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and the simplex corner
\bm{e}^{(k)}
is a fixed point for the discrete equation (
12
).

Proof.

Standard induction arguments yields the result. As a matter of fact, assuming that the statement holds true for an integer
n\geq 0
leads to

\begin{split}&x_{l}^{n}=0,\quad l\in\{1,\dots,N\}\setminus\{k\}\implies x_{l}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=x_{l}^{n+1}=0,\quad l\in\{1,\dots,N\}\setminus\{k\},\\
&x_{k}^{n}=1\implies\varphi^{n}_{k}=0\implies\rho_{k}^{n}=1\implies\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n}\rho_{j}^{n}=\rho_{k}^{n}=1\implies x_{k}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=x_{k}^{n}=1\end{split}

and therefore to

\varphi^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{k}=0\implies\rho_{k}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=1\implies\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\rho_{k}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=1\implies x_{k}^{n+1}=x_{k}^{n}=1,

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(
Internal equilibria preservation
)
.

Assume that the initial value
\bm{x}^{0}=\bm{x}^{*}
exhibits the properties in (
9
). Then, independently of the positive discretization steps
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},

x_{i}^{n}=x_{i}^{*},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

and therefore
\bm{x}^{*}
is a fixed point for the numerical method (
12
).

Proof.

We proceed by mathematical induction. The base case, i.e.
n=0,
follows from the the assumptions. Consider now an integer
n\geq 0
and assume that
\bm{x}^{n}=\bm{x}^{*}.
It then follows that
\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})=f^{*}\bm{e}
and, from (
9
) and (
13
),

\begin{split}\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n}=f^{*}&\implies\bm{R}\left(\dfrac{h_{n}}{2}\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n}\right)\bm{e}\right)\right)=\bm{R}(0)=\bm{e}\\
&\implies\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{x}^{n}=\bm{x}^{*}.\end{split}

Taking advantage of the last equality, similar arguments yield the relation
\bm{R}\left(h_{n}\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\bm{e}\right)\right)=\bm{e},
which then results in
\bm{x}^{n+1}=\bm{x}^{n}=\bm{x}^{*}.

∎

Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
ensure that the equilibria of the continuous-time system (
1
) are preserved by the numerical scheme (
12
), regardless of the discretization stepsizes. Combined with Theorem
3.1
, these outcomes confirm the structure-preserving nature of the proposed discretization and thus render it well suited for the approximation of replicator dynamics on the probability simplex.

4
Error Analysis and Convergence

Here, we perform an analysis of the approximation error yielded by the two-stage discretization (
12
) and prove the second-order convergence of the corresponding numerical solution. As a first step, we address the numerical consistency through the local truncation error, here denoted by

\begin{split}&\delta_{i}(h_{n};t_{n+1})\!=\!x_{i}(t_{n+1})-\dfrac{x_{i}(t_{n})\ \rho\!\left(\!h_{n}\!\left(\!f_{i}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))f_{j}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\right)\!\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})\rho\!\left(\!h_{n}\!\!\left(\!f_{j}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\!-\!\!\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))f_{k}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\!\right)\!\!\right)},\end{split}

(15)

i=1,\dots,N,
where the components of
\eta(\bm{x}(t_{n}))=[\eta_{1}(\bm{x}(t_{n})),\dots,\eta_{N}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))]^{\mathsf{T}}
are defined as

\eta_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))=\dfrac{x_{i}(t_{n})\ \rho\!\left(\!\dfrac{h_{n}}{2}\!\left(\!f_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})f_{j}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\right)\!\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})\ \rho\!\left(\!\dfrac{h_{n}}{2}\!\left(\!f_{j}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}(t_{n})f_{k}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\right)\!\right)}.

(16)

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that the given functions describing problem (
1
) are twice continuously differentiable on the probability simplex
\Delta^{N-1}
defined in (
3
). Then, the normalized rational integrator (
12
) is consistent with (
13
), of order
2.

Proof.

For each
i=1,\dots,N
, define the scalar functions

F_{i}(\bm{\xi})=f_{i}(\bm{\xi})-\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}f_{j}(\bm{\xi}),\qquad\qquad\bm{\xi}\in\Delta^{N-1}.

(17)

Since
\rho(z)=(z^{2}+6z+12)(z^{2}-6z+12)^{-1}
for
z\in\mathbb{R}
, a straightforward Taylor expansion yields

\rho\left(\frac{h_{n}}{2}F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\right)=1+\frac{h_{n}}{2}F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{8}F_{i}^{2}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3}),

for each
i=1,\dots,N.
Moreover, from the identities
\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})=1
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})F_{j}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))=0
, we deduce

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})\,\rho\left(\frac{h_{n}}{2}F_{j}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\right)\right)^{-1}=1-\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{N}F_{j}^{2}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\,x_{j}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3}).

Hence, recalling the definition in (
16
), it follows that

\begin{split}\eta_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))=&x_{i}(t_{n})\left(1+\frac{h_{n}}{2}F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{8}\left(F_{i}^{2}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))-\sum_{j=1}^{N}F_{j}^{2}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))x_{j}(t_{n})\right)\right)\\
&+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3}),\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad i=1,\dots,N.\end{split}

(18)

In particular, one obtains

\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))-\bm{x}(t_{n})=\frac{h_{n}}{2}\,\dfrac{d\bm{x}}{dt}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2}),

which in turn implies

\begin{split}\rho\left(h_{n}F_{i}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\right)&=1+h_{n}F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\frac{h_{n}^{2}}{2}\left(\nabla F_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\cdot\dfrac{d\bm{x}}{dt}(t_{n})+F_{i}^{2}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\right)\\
&\quad+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3}),\end{split}

and, recalling
\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}(t_{n})=x_{i}(t_{n})F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n})),

x_{i}(t_{n})\,\rho\left(h_{n}F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))\right)=x_{i}(t_{n+1})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3}),

(19)

for all
i=1,\dots,N
. Therefore, we conclude

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})\,\rho\left(h_{n}F_{j}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\right)\right)^{-1}=1+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3}),

(20)

so that
\delta_{i}(h_{n};t_{n+1})=\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{3})
for all
i=1,\dots,N
, which yields the result.
∎

The straightforward extension of well-established results assures the quadratic convergence of the scheme (
12
).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\bm{x}(t)
be the continuous-time solution to (
1
) on the interval
[0,T]
and let
\{\bm{x}^{n}\}_{0\leq n\leq M}
be its approximation computed by the normalized rational integrator (
13
), with stepsizes
\{h_{n}\}_{0\leq n<M}
satisfying (
11
). Suppose that the known functions defining (
13
) are twice continuously differentiable on the probability simplex
\Delta^{N-1}.
Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\max_{n=0,\dots,M}\|\bm{x}(t_{n})-\bm{x}^{n}\|\leq Ch^{2},\qquad\qquad h=\max_{i=0,\dots,M-1}h_{i},

and hence the method (
13
) is second-order convergent.

Proof.

Since
\bm{R}\in C^{\infty}(\Delta^{N-1}),
from the assumptions, the increment functions at the right-hand sides of (
13
) are twice continuously differentiable. Moreover, by Theorem
4.1
, the method is consistent of order 2. Therefore, the result follows from
[
30
, Theorem 3.6.]
.
∎

The numerical method (
12
) is constructed as a composition of a two-stage rational approximation with a normalization step. The latter is introduced to ensure that the discrete-time dynamics remains confined to the probability simplex for any stepsize (cf. Theorem
3.1
). On the other hand, equation (
20
) reveals that the normalization procedure corresponds to a third-order perturbation of the identity operator, which does not alter the second-order accuracy of the underlying rational approximation established by (
19
). As a result, the structure-preserving scheme (
12
) exhibits quadratic convergence.

Remark 4.1
.

Positivity-preserving methods of second order are rare, especially in the context of nonlinear systems. The NSFD schemes recently introduced in
[
27
,
41
]
attain second-order accuracy and preserve local asymptotic stability of equilibria for scalar autonomous ODEs, yet they do not guarantee positivity. A quadratically convergent positivity-preserving method is proposed in
[
35
]
, although still restricted to scalar problems. In contrast, our approach achieves second-order accuracy while ensuring positivity and dynamical consistency for the multidimensional replicator dynamics.

4.1
The discrete quotient rule

The continuous-time
quotient rule
(
10
), which governs the evolution of the relative growth
x_{i}(t)/x_{j}(t)
through the fitness difference
\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}):=f_{i}(\bm{x})-f_{j}(\bm{x})
, can be reformulated in integral form as follows

\frac{x_{i}(t_{n+1})}{x_{j}(t_{n+1})}=\frac{x_{i}(t_{n})}{x_{j}(t_{n})}\exp\left\{\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}(\tau))\,d\tau\right\}.

(21)

This identity suggests that the discrete-time ratio
x_{i}^{n+1}/x_{j}^{n+1}
should replicate the exponential structure on the right-hand side of (
21
). The result below shows that the rational integrator (
12
) achieves this up to second-order accuracy in the stepsize.

Theorem 4.3

(
Discrete quotient rule
)
.

Assume that the functions defining the replicator system (
1
) are twice continuously differentiable on the probability simplex
\Delta^{N-1}
, defined in (
3
). Let
\{\bm{x}^{n}\}
be the sequence generated by the numerical scheme (
12
) with stepsizes
\{h_{n}\}
. Then, for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
with
x_{j}^{0}>0
, the following relation holds

\frac{x_{i}^{n+1}}{x_{j}^{n+1}}=\frac{x_{i}^{n}}{x_{j}^{n}}\left(\,\exp\left\{h_{n}\,\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}^{n})\right\}+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2})\,\right),\qquad\text{ for all }n\geq 0,

(22)

where
\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}):=f_{i}(\bm{x})-f_{j}(\bm{x})
denotes the fitness difference.

Proof.

Theorem
3.1
guarantees that
\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\in\Delta^{N-1}
. As a consequence,
\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\mathcal{O}(h_{n})
. Recalling that the rational function
\rho(z)
coincides with the
[2/2]
Padé approximant of the exponential function (see
[
10
, Sections 1.2 and 1.4]
), we have

\rho(z)=\frac{1+\frac{1}{2}z+\frac{1}{12}z^{2}}{1-\frac{1}{2}z+\frac{1}{12}z^{2}}=\exp(z)+\mathcal{O}(z^{5}),

and thus, for each
i=1,\dots,N
,

\rho_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\rho\left(\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\exp\left(\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{5}).

Now, assuming
x_{j}^{0}>0
(which ensures
x_{j}^{n}>0
for all
n
), we obtain from (
12
)

\begin{split}\frac{x_{i}^{n+1}}{x_{j}^{n+1}}&=\frac{x_{i}^{n}}{x_{j}^{n}}\left(\,\exp\left\{\varphi_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\varphi_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{5})\,\right)\\
&=\frac{x_{i}^{n}}{x_{j}^{n}}\left(\,\exp\left\{h_{n}\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}^{n})+h_{n}(\nabla\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}^{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\bm{x}^{n})\right\}+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2})\,\right),\end{split}

for all
i=1,\dots,N.
Finally, from Theorem
4.2
,
\bm{x}^{n}=\bm{x}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2})
and from (
18
)

\begin{split}x_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{i}^{n}=x_{i}^{n}\left(\rho_{i}^{n}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n}\,\rho_{j}^{n}\right)^{-1}-1\right)=\mathcal{O}(h_{n}),\qquad\qquad i=1,\dots,N,\end{split}

which implies
\bm{x}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\bm{x}^{n}=\mathcal{O}(h_{n})
, yielding (
22
).
∎

We remark that the relation (
22
) constitutes a discrete analogue of the continuous identity (
21
). As a matter of fact, under the aforementioned smoothness assumptions, the integral in (
21
) admits the approximation

\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}(\tau))\,d\tau=h_{n}\,\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2})=h_{n}\,\hat{f}_{ij}(\bm{x}^{n})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2}),

which corresponds to the left-point rectangular quadrature rule.

4.2
Adaptive time-stepping via local error estimation

To improve the efficiency of the rational integrator (
12
), we develop an adaptive time-stepping strategy based on a local error Proportional Integral (PI) controller
[
28
,
67
]
. The structure-preserving properties of the integrator guarantee that, regardless of the time-step variation, the discrete dynamics remain consistent with the qualitative features of the continuous system. This allows us to focus on accuracy and cost-efficiency through step refinement, without compromising the underlying dynamical integrity. To this end, we introduce the auxiliary scheme

\chi_{i}^{n+1}=(x_{i}^{n}\,\tilde{\varrho}_{i}^{n})\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}^{n}\,\tilde{\varrho}_{j}^{n}\right)^{-1},\qquad\tilde{\varrho}_{i}^{n}=\dfrac{(2\varphi_{i}^{n}+3)^{2}+3}{(2\varphi_{i}^{n}-3)^{2}+3},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(23)

for
n=0,\dots,M-1
, with
\varphi_{i}^{n}
defined in (
12
). The structure-preserving properties of (
23
) follow from the same arguments developed in Section
3.1
. Here, we prove that the ancillary scheme (
23
) provides a first-order approximation of the solution to (
1
) and that the quantity
|\chi_{i}^{n+1}-x_{i}^{n+1}|
provides a computable surrogate for the local truncation error
\delta_{i}(h_{n};t_{n+1})
associated with the normalized rational integrator (
13
).

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that the given functions describing problem (
1
) are continuously differentiable on the probability simplex
\Delta^{N-1}
defined in (
3
). Then, the auxiliary method is linearly convergent. Moreover, it asymptotically reproduces the local truncation error of the normalized rational integrator (
13
).

Proof.

Let
\bm{\xi}\in\Delta^{N-1}
and define
\bm{\chi}(\bm{\xi})=[\chi_{1}(\bm{\xi}),\dots,\chi_{N}(\bm{\xi})]^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
F_{i}(\bm{\xi})
is given by (
17
) and

\chi_{i}(\bm{\xi})=\dfrac{\xi_{i}\ \rho\left(h_{n}F_{i}(\bm{\xi})\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\rho\left(h_{n}F_{j}(\bm{\xi})\right)},\qquad\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

Proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we obtain
\chi_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))=x_{i}(t_{n})\cdot(1+h_{n}F_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2}))\cdot(1+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2})),

i=1,\dots,N
and therefore

\bm{\chi}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))=\bm{x}(t_{n})+\frac{h_{n}}{2}\,\dfrac{d\bm{x}}{dt}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2})=\bm{x}(t_{n+1})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2}),

which implies that (
23
) is consistent and convergent of order one. Furthermore, it directly follows from the definition (
15
) that

\begin{split}&\chi_{i}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))-\dfrac{x_{i}(t_{n})\ \rho\!\left(\!h_{n}\!\left(\!f_{i}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{j}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))f_{j}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\right)\!\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}(t_{n})\rho\!\left(\!h_{n}\!\!\left(\!f_{j}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\!-\!\!\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}(\bm{x}(t_{n}))f_{k}(\bm{\eta}(\bm{x}(t_{n})))\!\right)\!\!\right)}\\
&=\delta_{i}(h_{n};t_{n+1})+\mathcal{O}(h_{n}^{2}),\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad i=1,\dots,N,\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎

Having established the approximation and structural properties of the auxiliary scheme (
23
), we now incorporate it into an adaptive time-stepping strategy aimed at controlling the integration accuracy. In the spirit of embedded methods
[
30
, Section II.4]
, schemes (
12
) and (
23
) are interpreted as a primary and embedded pair, with the latter furnishing a practical estimate of the local error. To this end, we introduce two vectors of component-wise tolerances, denoted by
\bm{\tau}^{\text{abs}}
and
\bm{\tau}^{\text{rel}}
, prescribing absolute and relative error bounds. At each time-step
n
, given the tentative approximations
\bm{x}^{n}
and
\bm{\xi}^{n}
computed by the rational and auxiliary schemes with stepsize
h_{n}
, the admissibility threshold for the
i
-th component is defined as

\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}^{\text{abs}}+\tau_{i}^{\text{rel}}\cdot\max\{x_{i}^{n},\,\xi_{i}^{n}\},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

The normalized error estimate associated with the current step is then given by

\varepsilon_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{x_{i}^{n}-\xi_{i}^{n}}{\tau_{i}}\right)^{2}}.

If the condition
\varepsilon_{n}\leq 1
is satisfied, the step is accepted; otherwise, it is rejected and recomputed with a smaller stepsize. Independently of acceptance, the stepsize is subsequently updated by means of a PI controller, following the principles outlined in
[
31
, Section IV.2]
. Specifically, we set

h_{n+1}=h_{n}\cdot\min\left\{\gamma_{\texttt{max}},\ \max\left\{\gamma_{\texttt{min}},\ \gamma\left(\frac{\min_{i}\tau_{i}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)^{\alpha-\beta}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{n-1}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right)^{\beta}\right\}\right\},\qquad n\geq 0

(24)

where the controller parameters are chosen as follows

\alpha=0.7/r,\quad\beta=0.4/r,\quad\gamma=(0.25)^{1/r},\quad r=2,\quad\gamma_{\texttt{min}}=0.1,\quad\gamma_{\texttt{max}}=5.

If no prior information is available regarding a suitable initial stepsize
h_{0}
, the procedure proposed in
[
30
, p.140]
may be employed. Furthermore, in order to mitigate possible instabilities following step rejections, we additionally impose
\gamma_{\texttt{max}}=1
in the iteration immediately succeeding a rejected step.

The adaptive mechanism described above then provides a robust and efficient control of the time-step selection, ensuring that the prescribed accuracy is maintained throughout the integration process. Moreover, since (
23
) employs the same internal function evaluations as the main integrator (
12
) and requires only elementary operations along with an additional normalization step, it introduces no significant computational efforts.

Conclusions

In this work, we developed a structure-preserving rational integrator designed for the numerical approximation of the replicator equation on the probability simplex. The method combines a two-stage rational discretization with a normalization step, yielding an explicit scheme that is straightforward to implement and unconditionally preserves fundamental geometric and dynamical properties of the continuous model. In particular, the integrator guarantees non-negativity, mass conservation, invariance of all simplex faces and exact preservation of both boundary and internal equilibria. We proved second-order convergence and constructed an embedded first-order approximation to enable reliable local error estimation and an efficient adaptive time-stepping strategy based on PI control. Additionally, the adherence to the
quotient rule
governing the reciprocal evolution of component ratios is demonstrated. Extensive numerical tests confirm the theoretical results and highlight the method’s superior performance in preserving qualitative features of the replicator dynamics, even in challenging regimes involving periodic and oscillatory solutions. Comparisons with standard variable-step solvers highlight the advantages of the proposed structure-preserving approach.

The present work opens several directions for further investigation. First, the extension of the proposed structure-preserving integrator to replicator equation variants incorporating mutation effects, namely replicator-mutator systems, which are widely studied in evolutionary dynamics
[
6
,
7
]
. Another natural extension concerns non-local and integro-differential replicator models, as analyzed in
[
5
,
2
,
3
]
and related literature. Additional research topics include delayed replicator dynamics, stochastic formulations and heterogeneous multi-population systems, where preserving structural properties remains a key challenge. Finally, exploring the application of rational approximation based integrators to other differential models with inherent structural properties, such as production-destruction
[
40
,
72
,
16
,
39
]
systems, is a promising area for further study.
