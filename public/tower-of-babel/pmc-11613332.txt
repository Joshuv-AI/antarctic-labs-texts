Visions for the Future of Physical Chemistry
Authors: Gemma C Solomon, Jin
Zhong Zhang, Tanja Cuk
Published: 2024

It is tempting to look back at history, romanticizing a slower pace of life, and imagine that visionary thinking came more naturally to the leaders that brought us to the world we live in today. We cannot say for sure whether this was the case, but we can say that there are visionary thinkers who manage to take the long view even when faced with our busy modern world. In this virtual special issue, we celebrate 14 individuals and teams, who enthusiastically answered our call to present their “vision for the future of physical chemistry in 2050”.

Our primary motivation with this virtual special issue was to invite researchers to share their views on the possible future of physical chemistry, purely based on their vision, without reference to geographic location, career stage, research output, or funding. We also wanted to highlight the many dimensions of our field in terms of research areas, as well as teaching and the human experience of our work. We are incredibly grateful to all the researchers who took time to provide their visions here. To introduce you to these outstanding scientists and their work, short biographies and links to their contributions featured in this virtual special issue are highlighted below.

Ryan C. Fortenberry

We are entering a “golden age” of astrochemistry, and some of the greatest questions in astrochemistry will be answered in the next 25 years.

Ryan C. Fortenberry is Associate Professor and Graduate Program Coordinator of Chemistry at the University of Mississippi. He was previously Assistant Professor of Chemistry at Georgia Southern University beginning in 2013, earning tenure in 2018 before departure. Ryan earned a BS in Mathematics working in chemistry research with Professor David H. Magers in 2006 and a MS in Communication from Mississippi College working with Professor Cliff Fortenberry in 2007. He received a Ph.D. at Virginia Tech in 2012 in Theoretical Chemistry working with Prof. T. Daniel Crawford, and was a NASA Postdoctoral Program Fellow from 2012 to 2013 at the NASA Ames Research Center in Mountain View, California working under the supervision of the late Dr. Timothy J. Lee. Ryan has previously been Chair of the ACS Astrochemistry Subdivision and the Ole Miss Campus Coordinator for the Mississippi Space Grant Consortium. Ryan has been published in Scientific American as well as featured in C&E News, and is the author of “Complete Science Communication”, a text on how to write and talk about science both to expert and nonexpert audiences alike. More information about his research can be found on this webpage: https://r410berry.com/

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Fortenberry’s Perspective is titled “A Vision for the Future of Astrochemistry in the Interstellar Medium by 2050” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00043).

Shubin Liu

Artificial intelligence and quantum computers will provide ample opportunities to harness better understanding of chemical concepts, both old and new, in the next few decades.

Shubin Liu is a Senior Computational Scientist in the Research Computing Center and Adjunct Professor in the Department of Chemistry, both at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC–CH). He obtained his B.Sc. degree from Hunan Normal University (HNU) in 1985 and M.Sc. degree from Lanzhou University, China, in 1988, majoring in physical chemistry. After serving as a lecturer in the Department of Chemistry at HNU for five years, he was admitted to a Ph.D. degree in 1994 at UNC–CH with the late Robert G. Parr in the Department of Chemistry as his adviser. He earned his Ph.D. degree in 1996 after less than three years of study, and then continued postdoctoral research with Parr and the late Jon Hermans of the Department of Biophysics and Biochemistry at UNC–CH as advisors. From September 1998 to August 2000, he conducted postdoctoral research with Weitao Yang of Duke University as his advisor. He has been an independent researcher since September 2000 at UNC–CH, focusing on developing the conceptual framework for chemical reactivity in the language of density functional theory. He edited the book ″Conceptual Density Functional Theory″ (published by Wiley-VCH in April 2022) and is editing a new book with the title of ″Exploring Chemical Concepts from Theory and Computation″, to be published by Wiley-VCH in early 2024. More information about his research interests can be found on this webpage: https://shubin.web.unc.edu.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Liu’s Perspective is titled “Harvesting Chemical Understanding with Machine Learning and Quantum Computers” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00067).

Sason Shaik

Oriented electric field (OEF) is a universal catalyst, which, by 2050, will change the art of chemical synthesis, and impact chemical education.

Sason Shaik graduated from Bar-Ilan University in Chemistry, and earned his B.Sc. and M.Sc. degrees in 1973. He subsequently did his Ph.D. during 1974–1978 (“Spin Inversion in Triplet Reactions“), with Nicholas D. Epiotis, at the University of Washington (Seattle Wa.). He spent a postdoctoral year (1978–1979) with Roald Hoffmann, at Cornell University. Currently, Shaik is a Saerree K. and Louis P. Fiedler Professor of Chemistry at the Insitute of Chemistry at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. In the field of valence bond (VB) theory, he developed general diagrammatic models for chemical reactivity (called VBSCD and VBCMD). In addition, he used VB theory to generalize chemical bonding. Together with Philippe Hiberty he discovered in 1991 a new family of the chemical bonds, so-called charge-shift bonding (CSB). He generalized triplet-bond-pairs (TBP) in high-spin clusters of metallic atoms. He has made major contributions to the field of structure and reactivity of Cytochrome P450 enzymes, and to nonheme enzymes and their models. Additionally, Shaik developed, together with Helmut Schwarz, the Two-State-Reactivity (TSR) paradigm, which applies from the tiny FeO+ diatomic all the way to metalloenzymes. Recently, he has developed the principles of oriented-external-electric fields (OEEFs) on structure and reactivity, including the decomposition of Senile Plaques. Finally, one of his major interest these days, is the modelling of enzymes as Soft-Robots. More information about his research can be found on this webpage: http://yfaat.ch.huji.ac.il/sason/

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Shaik’s Perspective is titled as “My Vision of Electric-Field-Aided Chemistry in 2050” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00064).

Juliana Bonilla and Sarah Wolf

Left to Right: Juliana Bonilla and Sara Wolf.

This vision is really about making physical chemistry more welcoming and accommodating to future scientists.

Juliana Bonilla graduated with a B.S. in Chemistry and a minor in Mathematics from the University of Puerto Rico, Río Piedras campus. She is now in her third year of Ph.D. studies at the University of Pennsylvania. As a member of Dr. Fakhraai’s research group, she studies the molecules in stable glasses using X-ray absorption techniques. As a service dog- and wheelchair-user, she can ascertain that her passion for chemistry has encouraged her through almost every challenge she has had to face in the preparation of her becoming a scientist. Apart from obtaining a Ph.D., her efforts lie in raising awareness about how the chemistry community can be less narrow-minded and more inclusive.

Sarah Wolf graduated with a Ph.D. in Chemistry from the University of Pennsylvania under the guidance of Zahra Fakhraai in 2021, after receiving her B.S. in Chemistry from Arcadia University (2014) and M.S. in Forensic Science from Arcadia University (2015). She is now an Assistant Professor at SUNY Cortland. Her research there focuses on the physical chemistry of organic small molecule glasses. For more information, see https://faculty.cortland.edu/wolf/. Throughout her training, Sarah faced significant challenges as a direct result of highly stigmatized mental health struggles, and is now determined to see her own students supported through barriers to their academic and personal success.

In this virtual special issue, their Editorial is titled “A Vision for Physical Chemistry: An Inclusive Future for all Abilities” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00069).

Josep M. Ribó

Methods and models for applied chemistry will be developed from the theoretically well known, but rarely used, fundamentals of irreversible thermodynamics.

Josep M. Ribó was born in Barcelona in 1940. His academic career has taken place entirely at the University of Barcelona, where he earned a Ph.D. in Chemistry (1968). After working in industry positions, he joined (1974) the Organic Chemistry Department of the University of Barcelona, where he was Full Professor of Organic Chemistry since 1989 and Emeritus since 2011. His research interests in chronological order from 1970 have been natural products, oligopyrrole chemistry (reactivity and structure), polypyrroles as organic conducting polymers, self-assembly of amphiphilic porphyrins, supramolecular chirality and spontaneous mirror symmetry breaking. He is now centered in the origin and causes of biological homochirality and in chemical selectivity, related to abiotic evolution: collaboration with the late David Hochberg leads to a thermodynamic description of this in the framework of irreversible thermodynamics. The points discussed in the present Perspective arise from this scientific collaboration and friendly relationship.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Ribó’s Perspective is titled “Physical Chemistry Models for Chemical Research in the XXth and XXIst Centuries” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00057).

Ronnie Kosloff

Ultracold molecular devices will power the quantum revolution as the steam engine powered the industrial revolution.

Ronnie Kosloff carried out all his studies at the Hebrew University Jerusalem. He completed B.Sc. in 1972 and Ph.D. in 1978 under the supervision of Professor Raphael D. Levine, studying the relation between the second law of thermodynamics and quantum measurement. His postdoctoral research was carried out at the University of Chicago (1979–1981) with Professor Stuart A. Rice, studying quantum chaos and open quantum systems. In 1981, Ronnie Kosloff returned to the Hebrew University where he has been ever since. His research was devoted to quantum thermodynamics, quantum molecular dynamics, ultrafast spectroscopy and ultracold molecules. His current research combines quantum control and quantum thermodynamics applied to quantum gates under environmental noise and quantum refrigerators. In addition, quantum interference in retinal/opsin as the mechanism for birds’ magnetic navigation. More information about his research can be found on this webpage: https://scholars.huji.ac.il/ronniekosloff/home

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Kosloff’s Perspective is titled “Quantum Molecular Devices” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00077).

Mirko Vanzan

Considering the emergence of new computer-based technologies, we will experience a massive growth of the use of computation in the study physical-chemical phenomena.

Mirko Vanzan received his Master’s Degree in Materials Science from University of Padova in 2017, with a dissertation on the optical and dynamical properties of gold-based nanostructures explored through computational chemistry techniques. He pursued his Ph.D. in Molecular Sciences at University of Padova in 2022 under the supervision of Professor Stefano Corni, successfully defending his thesis entitled “Investigating energy and charge transfers in hybrid molecule-nanoparticle systems through atomistic computational methods”. After completing his Ph.D., Mirko undertook a one-year postdoctoral fellowship at University of Padova in the group of Professor Stefano Corni and then in 2023 he moved to the Department of Physics of University of Milan, where he currently serves as Postdoctoral Researcher in the group of Professor Francesca Baletto. During his Ph.D., Mirko was a Visiting Scholar at the University of Basque Country in the group of Professor Roberto D’Agosta, with whom he started a prolific and ongoing collaboration. Mirko’s primary research focuses on the application of computational methods to investigate structural, optical and photocatalytic features of nanoscale materials. In particular, he uses multiscale approaches combining first-principle, molecular dynamics and classical continuum electrodynamics calculations to explore the relation between morphological, optical and photocatalytic features of metallic-based nanomaterials composed of more than one atomic species.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Vanzan’s Perspective is titled “A Vision for the Future of Multiscale Modeling” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00080).

Tim Duignan

Physical chemistry will undergo a profound transformation, driven predominantly by the combination of recent advances in quantum chemistry and machine learning.

Tim T. Duignan is a Lecturer in Applied Maths and Physics at Griffith University. He is working on the modeling and simulation of electrolyte solutions combining continuum solvent models, ab initio molecular dynamics and deep learning. He is focused on developing these techniques for application to improving electrochemical energy storage and CO2 capture and conversion. He received his undergraduate degree in Physics and Maths in 2009 from Victoria University Wellington and Ph.D. in Applied Mathematics in 2015 from the Australian National University working with Drew Parsons and Barry Ninham. He worked as a postdoctoral researcher with Chris Mundy and Gregory Schenter from 2015 to 2017 and at UQ from 2018 to 2022, where he received a DECRA Research Fellowship. More information about his research can be found on this webpage: https://researchers.uq.edu.au/researcher/20490

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Duignan’s Perspective is titled “The Potential of Neural Network Potentials” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.4c00004).

Benjamin Moss

Participation in physical chemistry can be enhanced by freely disseminating knowledge of cutting-edge experiments, analysis techniques and instrumentation.

Benjamin Moss is Schmidt AI in Science Fellow at the iX institute at Imperial College London. Ben received his bachelor’s degree in chemistry from Edinburgh University in 2014 and was awarded his Ph.D. in 2020, working in the group of James Durrant, studying photocatalytic materials for water splitting using transient diffuse reflectance at Imperial College London. Following postdoctoral work developing automated optical spectroscopies for electrochemistry with Professor Ifan Stephens, Ben was awarded a Schmidt AI in Science Fellow at the iX institute at Imperial College London, working with Professor Aron Walsh to develop new algorithms to analyze operando spectroscopies. His current research focuses on developing interpretable machine learning algorithms and custom hardware to drive forward the development of high throughput operando spectroscopies. Outside of this research, Ben is committed to the development of open-source hardware for spectroscopies, providing his designs at www.opensourcespectroscopy.com.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Moss’ Perspective is titled “Physical Chemistry Education and Research in an Open-Sourced Future” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00078).

Denys Biriukov

We believe that molecular dynamics and artificial intelligence will be fully integrated by 2050 that will make computational physical chemistry more intuitive, expansive, and synergistic.

Denys Biriukov obtained his B.Sc. and M.Sc. degrees in biophysics from Kharkiv National University in Ukraine. Then, he moved to the Czech Republic, where he received a Ph.D. degree in biophysics at the University of South Bohemia in České Budějovice in 2020. Following a postdoctoral stay at the Institute of Organic Chemistry and Biochemistry in Prague from 2020 to 2023, he pursued further postdoctoral endeavors at CEITEC Masaryk University in Brno. There, he joined the group of Robert Vácha and currently works on computer modeling of bacterial membranes and their interactions, including the design of antimicrobial peptides. Beginning in 2024, he also took on the role of project investigator at the Faculty of Science. His primary research interests encompass molecular simulations of soft matter and solid/liquid interfaces, force field and methodological development, and, more recently, delving into the “sweet” side of things by modeling glycans and their interactions with ions and biomolecules. More information about this research can be found on this webpage: https://www.ncbr.muni.cz/en/research/researchers/denys-biriukov

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Biriukov’s Perspective is titled “Pathways to a Shiny Future: Building the Foundation for Computational Physical Chemistry and Biophysics in 2050” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.4c00003).

Karina Kwapiszewska

From biochemical reactions to life processes, physical factors play a crucial role in driving biological heterogeneity.

Karina Kwapiszewska is an Assistant Professor at the Institute of Physical Chemistry, Polish Academy of Sciences. She completed her Master’s degree in Biotechnology from the Warsaw University of Technology, Faculty of Chemistry in 2009, and later earned her Ph.D. in Biotechnology from the same University in 2014, under the supervision of Professor Zbigniew Brzózka. Her doctoral research primarily focused on the development of microfluidic-based 3D cell models for drug evaluation. Currently, her research is focused on single-cell biophysics, where she investigates the physical properties of cells. She studies the diffusion of biomolecules inside living cells to understand drug uptake, drug-target interactions, and early physical markers of cellular stress and death. She describes herself as passionate about breaking interdisciplinary barriers, especially at the edge of chemistry, biology, and physics. Before her current position, she worked as a postdoctoral researcher in Professor Robert Hołyst’s group at the Institute of Physical Chemistry, Polish Academy of Sciences from 2014 to 2018.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Kwapiszewska’s Perspective is titled “Physicochemical Perspective of Biological Heterogeneity” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.3c00079).

Rajesh Kumar

Raman based analytical tools have not been used to their fullest potential. New tools like AI/ML will make it possible with technological miniaturization.

Rajesh Kumar is a Professor in the Department of Physics at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT), Indore, India. He received his Bachelor’s degree in Physics in 2002 from Ramjas College (University of Delhi), India and Master’s and Ph.D. from the Department of Physics of the Indian Institute of Technology, Delhi in 2004 and 2009, respectively, under the supervision of Professors A. K. Shukla and V. D. Vankar. His research work revolves around Raman spectromicroscopic studies to understand subtle scale physics at the nanoregime and leveraging Raman microscopy to understand interdisciplinary research areas of physical chemistry, biophysics, and civil engineering. He also works in the areas of herbal electronics, electrochromic sensing, and energy storage device fabrication with a long-term goal of understanding the role of organic and inorganic materials in increasing device efficiency, using spectroscopic and electrochemical methods. More information about his research can be found on this webpage: https://sites.google.com/view/madlabrk/

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Kumar’s Perspective is titled “Developments in Raman Spectromicroscopy for Strengthening Materials and Natural Science Research: Shaping the Future of Physical Chemistry” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.4c00017).

Lorenz J. Falling

This is the EMMA approach–fast-tracking materials innovation with measurement and software services.

Lorenz J. Falling is working in the field of energy materials with a focus on spectroscopic characterization of electrocatalysts. After finishing his Bachelors in (Bio-)Chemistry at the Ludwig Maximilans University in 2014, and Physics/Materials Science at Lund University in 2016, he received his doctorate at the Fritz Haber Institute of the Max Planck Society in Berlin under Professor Schlögl in 2020. In his thesis, he investigated how surface dynamics of iridium catalysts influence their activity and stability. He continued similar work as a Feodor Lynen Fellow at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory under Professor Salmeron until 2023, shifting the focus to the electrical double layer. Inspired by measurement services and standardization in Berlin and involvement in multimodal analysis in Berkeley, he now orients himself toward integrating theory and experiments. He currently does that for a battery-electrocatalyst crossover project in the group of Professor Stein at the Technical University Munich.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Falling’s Perspective is titled “A Vision for the Future of Materials Innovation and How to Fast-Track It with Services” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.4c00009).

Stewart Parker

The unique properties of neutron scattering and muon spectroscopy mean that they are indispensable for studying solids and liquids, both now and in 2050.

Stewart Frederick Parker is the ISIS Catalysis Scientist at the ISIS Neutron and Muon Source (Chilton, UK). His research is centered around the use of neutron scattering to investigate catalysts and catalytic processes in collaboration with both academic and industrial partners. Recent work has been the study of zeolites (H-ZSM5) in the methanol-to-hydrocarbons reaction and the isomerization of meta-xylene to para-xylene using neutron vibrational spectroscopy and diffusion studies. The most exciting development is the use of neutron imaging for operando studies of a working catalyst. He obtained his B.Sc. in Chemistry, including one year of industrial training, in 1978 from the University of Surrey (Guildford, UK). He completed his Ph.D. in Physical Chemistry under the joint supervision of Professor Ralph G. Pearson and Professor Paul H. Barrett and was awarded the degree in 1983 by the University of California at Santa Barbara, (Santa Barbara, USA). He was a postdoctoral researcher under the supervision of Dr Michael A. Chesters at the University of East Anglia (Norwich, UK), 1983–1985.

In this virtual special issue, Dr. Parker’s Perspective is titled “A Vision for the Future of Neutron Scattering and Muon Spectroscopy in the 2050s” (DOI: 10.1021/acsphyschemau.4c00026).

We hope that this virtual special issue is a gift to our readers and the field. We hope these visions can challenge and inspire you to dream a little more in your research, your teaching and the workplaces that we are all shaping. We hope that reading these visions gives you a chance to pause, take a deep breath and lift your head up from the demands of modern life. Only time will tell whether the visions presented here represent the way history pans out, but we believe that the more we can dream and take a long view, the better the chances are that we build the most promising future for physical chemistry. Thank you once again to all our visionaries for taking the time to answer our call, thank you to the referees who reviewed their papers, and thank you for reading along.
