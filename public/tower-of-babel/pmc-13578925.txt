Expanding the Genetic Toolkit of Pichia kudriavzevii E1 for Enhanced Mycoprotein Production
Authors: Yupeng Wang, Shenghan Gao, Chenchen Wu, Liangchen Guo, Peijie Han, Limin Wang, Justyna Ruchała, Bo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Pichia kudriavzevii is gaining attention as a non‐conventional yeast with significant industrial potential, owing to its robust tolerance to low pH and diverse process‐related stresses. However, the lack of a reliable episomal expression system and the scarcity of well‐characterized promoters currently impede predictable gene expression and modular metabolic engineering in this workhorse. Here, we report development of the stable episomal expression vector for P. kudriavzevii E1. This was achieved through identification of a functional autonomously replicating sequence from the endogenous genome, guided by the established host‐vector GC compatibility workflow. Leveraging this episomal platform, we then constructed an endogenous promoter library for P. kudriavzevii E1 via transcriptomic sequencing data analysis. Among the characterized promoters, the endogenous PkTDH3 promoter exhibited the highest activity. Notably, PkTDH3 demonstrated more stable and sustained expression compared to the strong promoter ScTDH3 from Saccharomyces cerevisiae , underscoring the value of establishing native promoter resources for P. kudriavzevii. Furthermore, we expanded the strength range of the promoter library by engineering the upstream activating sequence of PkTDH3 through random mutagenesis. As a proof of concept, the developed promoter library was employed to construct P. kudriavzevii mutants with elevated protein content, demonstrating the utility of this integrated genetic toolkit for strain engineering in P. kudriavzevii.Keywords: autonomously replicating sequence, episomal expression system, mycoprotein content, Pichia kudriavzevii, promoter library

Introduction

Non‐conventional yeasts have emerged as promising cell factories in metabolic engineering and synthetic biology, owing to unique physiological and metabolic characteristics that distinguish them from conventional model hosts, including Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Wang et al. 2025). Among them, Pichia kudriavzevii is increasingly recognized as a non‐conventional yeast of industrial interest, owing to its robust tolerance to low pH and other process‐associated stresses, efficient nutrient utilization, and capacity to accumulate protein‐rich biomass (Du et al. 2022; Wang, Cui, et al. 2026). These attributes establish P. kudriavzevii as a versatile host for both traditional food and beverage fermentations (Chu et al. 2023) and emerging biomanufacturing processes, including low‐pH organic acid production (Xi et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023), single‐cell protein generation, and feed‐related applications (Agpoon et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2026). However, in contrast to the well‐established genetic toolkits available for S. cerevisiae , genetic manipulation of P. kudriavzevii remains challenging due to the limited availability of efficient genetic tools, including plasmid‐based expression systems and promoter libraries with diverse strengths. This deficiency critically constrains predictable gene expression and modular metabolic engineering in this organism (Löbs et al. 2017; Moon et al. 2025; Wagner and Alper 2016).

The development of a stable episomal vector requires two core cis‐acting elements: an autonomously replicating sequence (ARS) and a centromere (CEN). The ARS functions as a replication origin recognized by the host's initiation complex to enable plasmid propagation (Méchali 2010; Yoshida et al. 2013). Many replication origins in model hosts, such as S. cerevisiae , are well characterized (Tan et al. 2021; Foss et al. 2024). By contrast, studies on ARSs in non‐conventional yeasts remain limited. In Kluyveromyces marxianus, ARSs have been identified through genomic library screening (Abdel‐Banat et al. 2021), whereas in Yarrowia lipolytica, a functional mitochondrial origin of replication (mtORI) has been characterized via fragment‐based screening (Cui et al. 2021). Owing to the absence of a common ARS consensus in non‐conventional yeasts, functional validation of heterologous ARSs is often used to avoid laborious screening (Dong et al. 2023). Heterologous ARSs such as S. cerevisiae 2 μ/ScARS and Kluyveromyces lactis panARS have been shown to function in diverse yeast species, including Lachancea, Kluyveromyces and Pichia (Cui et al. 2021). Nevertheless, their efficacy remains highly host‐dependent. Consequently, tailored ARS screening strategies are essential for expanding the range of genetically tractable strains and elucidate species‐specific replication mechanisms. The CEN mediates stable plasmid inheritance by functioning as a partitioning element that recruits the kinetochore complex. This multiprotein assembly ensures faithful segregation during mitosis through the attachment of sister chromatids to spindle microtubules (Cao et al. 2017; Tran et al. 2019). Similarly, CENs are highly species‐specific and usually lack common conserved sequence features. Recent studies in non‐conventional yeasts have demonstrated that host‐derived centromeric or centromere‐like sequences can enhance plasmid stability and expression uniformity, yet their performance remains contingent on local sequence organization and host genetic background (Cao et al. 2017, 2020). Collectively, these observations underscore that the development of stable episomal systems in non‐conventional yeasts depends on the integration of host‐compatible replication elements with effective plasmid partitioning mechanisms.

In addition to stable episomal tools, well‐characterized promoter resources are also essential for metabolic engineering, as they govern transcriptional output and thereby influence pathway flux (Ye et al. 2024). Promoter library‐based fine‐tuning has proven to be an effective strategy for balancing pathway gene expression and improving product formation (Gao et al. 2020). Constitutive promoters are particularly useful for pathway engineering because they provide relatively stable transcriptional activity with limited dependence on environmental or intracellular signals (Arjmand 2024). Studies involving the characterization of promoters from S. cerevisiae (Li et al. 2022) and non‐conventional yeasts like Ogataea polymorpha (Ye et al. 2024) have shown that precise tuning of constitutive promoter strength can enhance pathway performance and increase product titers. Beyond native promoter utilization, engineering of upstream activating sequences (UASs) can further enhance promoter strength and expand the dynamic range available for transcriptional regulation, thereby providing additional flexibility for precise control of gene expression (Li et al. 2022).

In this study, we established a GC‐guided screening framework to identify and optimize endogenous ARS elements in P. kudriavzevii, based on the observed association between plasmid establishment and host‐vector GC compatibility. Candidate CEN elements were independently predicted through bioinformatic analysis and subsequently evaluated for their contributions to plasmid stability. The resulting episomal expression system was then leveraged to construct and characterize an endogenous promoter library in P. kudriavzevii, which was applied to regulate mycoprotein production.

Recombinant Plasmid and Strain Construction

All primers used for plasmid construction, genomic integration, strain verification, sequencing, and qPCR are listed in Table S1. All plasmids used in this study were constructed from a previously established ScARS‐based CRISPR‐Cas9 plasmid in P. kudriavzevii E1 (Zhang et al. 2023). An sfGFP reporter plasmid was generated by replacing the cas9 expression cassette and was used as the common backbone for all episomal assays. Candidate ARS and CEN fragments, endogenous promoter sequences, TDH3 truncation variants, and mutagenized UAS fragments were amplified by PCR and assembled into the corresponding plasmid backbone using Gibson assembly. All constructs were verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing after propagation in E. coli DH5α. Phanta Max Superfidelity DNA polymerase was purchased from Vazyme Co. Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Gel Extraction Kit and Plasmid Mini Kit I were purchased from Omega (Bio‐Tek Inc. USA). Engineered P. kudriavzevii strains were obtained either by episomal transformation or by genomic integration using the previously established CRISPR‐Cas9 system (Zhang et al. 2023). For promoter validation, promoter‐sfGFP expression cassettes were integrated as single copies at the same genomic locus.

GC‐Guided Computational Screening of Candidate Endogenous ARS Sequences

Candidate ARS regions were identified from the genome of P. kudriavzevii E1 using a custom Python‐based scanning procedure implemented in Python 3.11.9. The genome FASTA file was used as input, and each contig was analysed independently. Candidate fragments were generated using a sliding‐window strategy by moving the fragment start position in 10 bp increments. At each start position, fragment length was initially set at 50 bp and subsequently increased in 10 bp steps up to 5000 bp. Before sequence extraction, a boundary check was performed to ensure that the fragment end position did not exceed the length of the corresponding contig. If the fragment extended beyond the contig boundary, length iteration at that start position was terminated. For each valid fragment, both actualGC content and a targetGC metric were calculated. Fragments were retained only when the calculated TargetGC value fell within the predefined range of 38%–40%, which was defined based on the desired GC content range (39% ± 1%) of the final plasmid containing the candidate ARS fragment. For each qualifying fragment, the contig identifier, genomic coordinates, fragment length, actualGC content, targetGC value, and nucleotide sequence were recorded in a tab‐separated output file. To reduce redundancy and improve computational efficiency, once a fragment meeting the GC criterion was identified at a given start position, further length extension from that position was discontinued.

Prediction and Characterization of Candidate Endogenous CEN Sequences

Tandem repeat regions in the strain P. kudriavzevii E1 genome were predicted using TBtools, yielding a total of 2832 repetitive sequences. Based on their repeat characteristics, candidate fragments were selected for functional testing. These candidate fragments were amplified from E1 genomic DNA and individually cloned into the sfGFP reporter plasmid. The resulting constructs were transformed into strain P. kudriavzevii E101 and initially screened by transformant formation on selective medium. Positive candidates were then analysed by flow cytometry, fluorescence microscopy, and serial passage under non‐selective conditions. Genomic regions flanking CEN‐4 and CEN‐6 were further screened using the same GC‐guided workflow to identify CEN‐containing fragments shorter than 20 kb that would yield an overall plasmid GC content between 36% and 42%.

Determination of Plasmid Copy Numbers

Plasmid copy number was estimated by qPCR using total DNA extracted from P. kudriavzevii E101 transformants. The yeast cells were harvested by centrifuging at 6000 rpm for 10 min and the total DNA was extracted as previously described (Gu et al. 2019). The plasmid harbouring the sfGFP gene was used as the target, and the single‐copy genomic gene ACT1 was used as the internal control. A genome‐integrated single‐copy sfGFP strain was used as the calibrator. The relative plasmid copy number was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with ΔC t = C t (sfGFP) − C t (ACT1). Since the calibrator strain contained one genomic copy of sfGFP, the resulting 2−ΔΔCt value was used to represent the estimated plasmid copy number per cell.

Construction and Evaluation of the Endogenous Promoter Library

Promoter candidates were selected from the P. kudriavzevii E1 transcriptome dataset according to the transcription levels, encompassing a broad range of expression strengths (Wang, Cui, et al. 2026). The corresponding upstream regions were amplified from P. kudriavzevii E101 genomic DNA and placed immediately upstream of the sfGFP coding sequence in the ARS‐3‐2‐CEN‐6 episomal reporter vector. The resulting plasmids were verified by PCR and sequencing before transformation into P. kudriavzevii E101. Transformants were cultivated under identical conditions, and promoter activity was assessed by measuring sfGFP fluorescence. To validate promoter activities in a genomic background, the corresponding promoter‐sfGFP expression cassettes were integrated into the P. kudriavzevii E101 genome as single copies at the same chromosomal locus. Correct genomic integration was confirmed by PCR and sequencing. The engineered strains were cultured under the same conditions as the plasmid‐bearing strains, and sfGFP fluorescence was measured using the same procedure. For comparison of the endogenous and heterologous TDH3 promoters, the TDH3 promoter from P. kudriavzevii E1 and that from S. cerevisiae S288C were cloned and tested in the same reporter plasmid. In the genomic assay, both promoters were compared with the same copy number, reporter gene, terminator, integration locus, and cultivation conditions.

Error‐Prone PCR Mutagenesis and Screening of PkTDH3 Promoter Variants

The region from −300 to −200 bp of the PkTDH3 promoter, together with its immediate flanking sequences, was mutagenized using the QuickMutation Random Mutagenesis Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The mutagenized fragments were purified and assembled into the ARS‐CEN‐based sfGFP reporter plasmid to replace the corresponding wild‐type promoter region. The resulting plasmid library was propagated in E. coli DH5α and subsequently transformed into P. kudriavzevii E101. Fluorescence‐based screening of promoter variants was performed using a microplate reader as previously described, with minor modifications (Li et al. 2022). In brief, individual transformants were picked into 96‐well plates containing SD‐URA medium and cultured under identical conditions. The fluorescence intensity and cell density (OD600) of each culture were measured using a microplate reader, and promoter activity was represented by the normalized fluorescence value. Clones showing higher normalized fluorescence than the wild‐type PkTDH3 promoter were selected for repeated screening and sequence verification. The mutation‐positive rate was defined as the proportion of sequenced clones carrying at least one mutation, while mutation frequency was calculated as the number of mutations per kilobase of sequenced promoter region.

Determination of Crude Protein Content and Amino Acid Composition

P. kudriavzevii strains were cultivated in minimal medium containing 20 g L−1 glucose, 3 g L−1 KH2PO₄, 1 g L−1 (NH₄)2SO₄, 0.5 g L−1 MgSO₄·7H2O, 1 mL L−1 vitamin solution, and 2 mL L−1 trace‐metal solution (Gao et al. 2026). Uracil was supplemented to a final concentration of 40 mg L−1 when required by the auxotrophic background. Cultures were incubated at 30°C and 220 rpm for 24 h. For crude protein determination, 300 mL of culture was harvested at the indicated time point and washed three times with sterile water. The cell pellets were dried at 105°C to constant weight to determine dry cell weight (DCW). Total nitrogen content was determined using the Kjeldahl method. Crude protein content was calculated by multiplying the total nitrogen content by a conversion factor of 6.25 and was expressed as a percentage of DCW. The amino acid composition was determined as follows. Briefly, 0.1 g of dried cells was hydrolyzed with 10 mL of 6 mol L−1 HCl at 105°C for 24 h for the analysis of 17 amino acids. Tryptophan was analysed separately after alkaline hydrolysis with 10 mL of 4 mol L−1 NaOH at 105°C for 18 h. After hydrolysis, samples were cooled to room temperature, neutralized with NaOH or HCl as appropriate, and filtered through 0.22 μm nylon membranes. Norvaline and sarcosine were added as internal standards.

Analytical Methods

The GFP intensity was measured by flow cytometry. The transformed P. kudriavzevii E101 cells were cultured in SD‐URA medium for 48 h and then centrifuged for 5 min at 4000× g to remove the supernatant. The cell pellets were resuspended in 10 mM phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and then analysed by flow cytometry at 488 nm on a forsetta flow cytometer analyser. Similarly, for promoter characterization, the plasmids were transformed into P. kudriavzevii E101. Single transformants were selected on SD‐URA plates, individually inoculated into SD‐URA liquid medium, and cultivated for 48 h. Samples were taken after 48 h for GFP fluorescence measurement. Amino acids were derivatized online with o‐phthalaldehyde and 9‐fluorenylmethyl chloroformate and quantified by HPLC using a C18 column (3 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm). All quantitative experiments were performed using at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a t‐test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

The GC Content of ARS Sequence Is a Key Factor to Maintain Plasmid Replication in P. kudriavzevii E1

In our previous work, a CRISPR‐Cas9 editing system was established in P. kudriavzevii E1 using a plasmid carrying the well‐characterized S. cerevisiae ARS (ScARS) (Zhang et al. 2023). To examine whether this plasmid framework could be adapted for episomal reporter expression in P. kudriavzevii E1, the cas9 gene cassette was replaced with GFP expression cassettes of sfGFP, eGFP (Pyne et al. 2023), or yeGFP (Wang, Li, et al. 2026) reporter cassettes. However, no transformants were obtained from any of these constructs (Figure 1A). We then retained the sfGFP cassette and replaced ScARS with the broad‐host‐range panARS (Liachko and Dunham 2014), while an ARS‐free plasmid was included as a control. Neither construct yielded transformants in P. kudriavzevii E101 (Figure 1B). Even though the initial ScARS‐based plasmid facilitated genome editing in P. kudriavzevii E1, substituting the Cas9 expression cassette with GFP expression cassettes led to no transformants, indicating that ScARS might not effectively support plasmids with functional genes in P. kudriavzevii E1, emphasizing the necessity to find suitable replicative elements.

Workflow for genome‐wide identification of candidate ARS elements in P. kudriavzevii E1. (A) Plasmid backbone used for ARS screening, (B) schematic of plasmid constructs used for ARS screening, and (C) workflow for candidate ARS identification.

Plasmid replication relies on origins of replication (ORIs), which in most budding and fission yeasts are A/T‐rich sequences, whereas metazoan origins are generally G/C‐rich and often associated with promoters (Liachko et al. 2014). Notably, the genomic GC content of P. kudriavzevii E1 and that of the previously functional genome‐editing plasmid were both approximately 39%. However, replacement of the large cas9 cassette with the sfGFP expression cassette increased the overall plasmid GC content to 44%. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that plasmid establishment in P. kudriavzevii E1 might be associated with host‐vector GC compatibility. To support this hypothesis, we further analysed the GC content of plasmids used in other non‐conventional yeasts, including Yarrowia lipolytica and Kluyveromyces marxianus. In Y. lipolytica, whose genomic GC content is approximately 49%, the ARS68‐derived episomal vector pYL15 exhibited an overall GC content of approximately 52% (Bredeweg et al. 2017), while the YaliCraft plasmid set mainly ranged from 46% to 51% (Yuzbashev et al. 2023). These values were comparable to the genomic GC background of Y. lipolytica. Similarly, in K. marxianus, the genomic GC content is approximately 40%, and the representative pMTU‐series episomal expression vector showed a relatively low plasmid GC content of approximately 43% (Rajkumar and Morrissey 2020). Collectively, these representative examples suggest that episomal plasmids successfully maintained in non‐conventional yeasts tend to exhibit whole‐plasmid GC contents comparable to those of their respective host genomes.

Establishing a Host‐Vector GC Compatibility‐Guided Workflow for Endogenous ARS Discovery in P. kudriavzevii E1

Based on the above rationale, we used host‐vector GC compatibility as a practical screening criterion rather than as a direct predictor of ARS activity. Traditional ARS screening methods are generally laborious and inefficient, particularly in non‐conventional yeasts where functional sequence signatures remain poorly defined (Schwarzhans et al. 2017). Given the above proposed GC compatibility feature for ARSs, we therefore developed a GC‐guided genome‐wide screening strategy to identify endogenous ARS candidates in P. kudriavzevii E1 (Figure 1C). To identify candidate sequences, the P. kudriavzevii E1 genome was searched by a sliding‐window strategy. For every fragment, the GC content of the reconstructed plasmid after insertion into the ARS‐free backbone was calculated, and fragments predicted to give a plasmid GC content of 39% ± 1% were retained. No qualifying fragment was recovered when the upper length limit was 1000 bp. This limit was therefore increased stepwise to 5000 bp, at which point matching fragments were obtained. To improve computational efficiency, once a qualifying fragment was identified at a given start position, further length extension at that position was terminated. Using this workflow, we ultimately identified 171 candidate sequences from the P. kudriavzevii E1 genome, substantially narrowing the search space for downstream functional validation (Figure 1C).

To validate the candidate sequences identified by the above GC‐guided screening workflow, the 171 identified candidates were first grouped according to their genomic locations because many candidates represented overlapping fragments from the same genomic regions generated by the sliding‐window strategy. Representative candidate regions were then selected for functional validation, and the shortest fragments within these regions that satisfied the GC compatibility criterion were chosen. This strategy reduced redundant testing of overlapping sequences and minimized the inserted fragment size while maintaining the required host‐vector GC compatibility. The detailed screening process, including the candidate fragments, grouped genomic regions, and selected ARS sequences, is summarized in Table S2. Each candidate was individually inserted into the sfGFP reporter plasmid and transformed into P. kudriavzevii E101 for functional characterization. Among the five candidates tested, plasmids carrying ARS‐3 or ARS‐4 produced abundant transformants, whereas the other three candidates, like the empty‐vector control, failed to yield transformants (Figure 2A). Therefore, ARS‐3 and ARS‐4 were selected for further characterization. To evaluate the performance of these two functional ARS elements, plasmid copy number, transformation efficiency, and reporter fluorescence intensity were measured. Plasmid copy number was estimated by qPCR relative to a genome‐integrated single‐copy sfGFP reference strain, revealing that ARS‐3‐ and ARS‐4‐containing plasmids were maintained at approximately 2.6 and 2.4 copies per cell, respectively (Figure 2B). The plasmids could be recovered in E. coli from total DNA extracted from the corresponding yeast transformants, and were progressively lost during passaging under non‐selective conditions, indicating that reporter expression was supported by autonomously replicating plasmids rather than genomic integration. Consistent with their ability to support plasmid maintenance, ARS‐3 and ARS‐4 yielded transformation efficiencies of approximately 1.6 × 104 and 1.5 × 104 CFU μg−1 DNA, respectively, whereas no transformants were obtained from the ARS‐free negative control (Figure 2B). To determine whether ARS‐3 and ARS‐4 could support plasmid‐based sfGFP expression in P. kudriavzevii E101, the corresponding transformants were analysed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. Flow cytometry detected GFP‐positive populations in both transformants, indicating that both plasmids supported sfGFP expression in P. kudriavzevii E101 (Figure S1A). Quantification of median fluorescence intensity showed that both transformants exhibited higher fluorescence than the negative control, with ARS‐3 displaying significantly higher fluorescence intensity than ARS‐4 (Figure 2B). Consistent with this result, fluorescence microscopy also detected fluorescent cells in both strains (Figure S1B). Collectively, these results indicate that ARS‐3 and ARS‐4 could support plasmid‐based sfGFP expression in P. kudriavzevii E101.

ARS‐3 was then selected for further optimization since it gave the higher fluorescence signal compared with others. To reduce vector size and delineate the functional boundary of this sequence, the original 4508‐bp ARS‐3 fragment was truncated to 3123 bp (ARS‐3‐1), 1443 bp (ARS‐3‐2), and 930 bp (ARS‐3‐3), resulting in the constructed plasmids with overall GC contents of 40%, 41%, and 42%, respectively (Figure 2C). Transformation assays revealed that plasmids carrying the 3123 bp and 1443 bp fragments still yielded transformants on selective medium, whereas the 930 bp construct failed to produce transformants (Figure 2C). The functional truncated variants were then compared with the full‐length ARS‐3 plasmid in terms of plasmid copy number, transformation efficiency, and reporter fluorescence intensity. Quantitative characterization showed that ARS‐3‐1 and ARS‐3‐2 exhibited plasmid copy numbers and transformation efficiencies comparable to those of the full‐length ARS‐3 plasmid, with no significant differences observed among the three constructs (Figure 2D). Flow cytometry showed GFP‐positive populations in transformants carrying the 3123 and 1443 bp fragments, indicating that these two truncated variants still supported plasmid establishment and sfGFP expression in P. kudriavzevii E101. Therefore, ARS‐3‐2 was selected for further research due to its shorter sequence.

Functional Validation of CEN Elements for Enhancing Plasmid Stability in P. kudriavzevii E1

To further evaluate sequences that might improve plasmid stability in P. kudriavzevii E1, tandem repeat regions were predicted from the genome. A total of 2832 repetitive sequences were predicted genome‐wide by TBtools based on their repeat characteristics. These candidates were further screened according to their genomic locations and repeat structures. Repetitive regions associated with telomeres and sequences lacking clear tandem repeat organization were excluded. The remaining candidates were examined in their corresponding genomic regions, and sequences showing CEN‐like repeat features, including relatively long repeat arrays, multiple repeat units, and regular repeat‐unit organization, were selected for functional validation. Finally, seven candidate CEN sequences were identified (Table S3). Each candidate CEN was cloned into the sfGFP reporter vector and transformed into P. kudriavzevii E101. Among the seven candidates tested, only CEN‐4 and CEN‐6 gave rise to transformants, with four colonies recovered from CEN‐4 and just one colony from CEN‐6. However, no colonies formed from the remaining constructs or the CEN empty vector (negative control). Accordingly, transformation efficiency analysis showed that both CEN‐4 and CEN‐6 exhibited extremely low plasmid establishment efficiencies when used alone (Figure 3A). The resulting transformants were subsequently evaluated in terms of plasmid copy number and reporter fluorescence intensity. qPCR‐based copy number analysis showed that both CEN‐4 and CEN‐6 constructs displayed markedly higher copy numbers than the single‐copy genomic reference, with CEN‐6 showing a significantly higher copy number than CEN‐4 (Figure 3A). The resulting transformants were subsequently analysed by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. GFP‐positive populations were detected in both transformants by flow cytometry, indicating that plasmids containing CEN‐4 or CEN‐6 could support stable episomal expression of sfGFP in P. kudriavzevii E101 (Figure S2A). Quantification of median fluorescence intensity revealed that both transformants exhibited significantly higher fluorescence than the negative control, with CEN‐6 producing a stronger signal than CEN‐4 (Figure 3B). Consistent with the flow cytometry results, fluorescence microscopy also revealed bright and relatively uniform fluorescent cells in both the CEN‐4 and CEN‐6 transformants (Figure S2B), indicating that these two constructs supported stable episomal expression in P. kudriavzevii E101. Given that the CEN‐6 transformant exhibited a stronger fluorescence signal than the CEN‐4 transformant, CEN‐6 was selected for further stability analysis. Under non‐selective conditions, the CEN‐6‐containing plasmid remained detectable after at least ten successive generations of cultivation, indicating that CEN‐6 contributed to sustained plasmid maintenance in P. kudriavzevii E101 (Figure S3). Despite this additional screening, no CEN‐containing fragment shorter than 20 kb that satisfied the 36%–42% GC criterion was identified. In summary, these results demonstrated that CEN elements can support prolonged plasmid‐based expression in P. kudriavzevii E1. Functional validation of candidate CEN elements in P. kudriavzevii E1. (A) Plasmid copy number, transformation efficiency, and fluorescence intensity of CEN‐4‐ and CEN‐6‐containing plasmids. (B) Plasmid copy number, transformation efficiency, and fluorescence intensity of CEN‐6, ARS‐3‐2, and ARS‐3‐2‐CEN‐6 constructs.

After confirming the role of CEN elements in enhancing plasmid stability, we combined the optimized ARS element ARS‐3‐2 with the selected CEN‐6 element to evaluate the resulting plasmid in P. kudriavzevii E101. Compared with the plasmid carrying the CEN element alone, the combined plasmid showed a lower copy number and lower fluorescence intensity, but its transformation efficiency was substantially increased (Figure 3B). In contrast, compared with the ARS‐3‐2 plasmid, the ARS‐3‐2‐CEN‐6 construct displayed a higher copy number and stronger reporter fluorescence. These results indicate that ARS‐3‐2 mainly contributed to efficient plasmid establishment, whereas CEN‐6 enhanced plasmid maintenance and reporter output. Therefore, the ARS‐3‐2 and CEN‐6 combination provided a balanced episomal expression platform with improved expression performance compared with ARS‐3‐2 alone and markedly better establishment efficiency than CEN‐6 alone, indicating that the ARS‐CEN combination had clear potential for plasmid‐based expression in strain P. kudriavzevii E1 (Figure 3B). The plasmid was retrieved from the recombinant P. kudriavzevii strains to verify its episomal status. Sequencing the entire plasmids revealed that their sequences were identical to the original plasmids (data not shown), indicating that they were preserved in an extrachromosomal form instead of integrating into the genome.

Characterization of an Endogenous Promoter Library in P. kudriavzevii E1

Following the successful creation of a stable episomal expression system using ARS‐CEN sequences in P. kudriavzevii E1, we proceeded to utilize this plasmid framework to analyse promoter strengths in this organism. Based on the transcriptome dataset previously generated from the P. kudriavzevii E1‐based strain cultivated in industrial medium (Wang, Cui, et al. 2026), a total of 28 genes spanning a broad range of transcription levels were selected according to their RPKM values (reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads), and their corresponding upstream regions were chosen as promoter candidates for functional characterization (Table S4). Subsequently, the endogenous promoter candidates were individually inserted upstream of sfGFP within the same plasmid backbone and transformed into P. kudriavzevii E101. As shown in Figure 4A, the fluorescence intensity of the promoter library spanned a wide dynamic range, with a maximal fold change of approximately 157‐fold between the strongest and weakest promoters. Strong promoters (> 2500 AU, e.g., PkTDH3, PGK1) exhibited the highest median fluorescence, whereas medium promoters (1500–2500 AU, e.g., ATPA, CCG6) showed intermediate fluorescence. Weak promoters (< 1500 AU, e.g., PIR1, AIM3) produced low or near‐background sfGFP levels (< 200 AU). The signal distribution showed that the episomal system provided sufficient resolution to distinguish promoters with markedly different strengths, while also capturing variation among promoters within the intermediate range. To further test the promoter activity, we integrated the corresponding promoter‐sfGFP cassettes as single copies at the same genomic location and assessed their activities under the same conditions (Figure 4B). Although the dynamic range of fluorescence signals narrowed in the genomic background and slight rank shifts were observed among promoters with comparable strengths, the overall trend remained consistent between the two systems. Notably, PkTDH3 consistently exhibited the highest activity among all tested promoters, underscoring its suitability for high‐level expression in P. kudriavzevii E101. Although ScTDH3 drives robust expression in its native host S. cerevisiae (Li et al. 2022), its activity in P. kudriavzevii E101 was substantially lower than that of PkTDH3 (Figure S4). These observations illustrated both interspecies variability and the strain‐specific nature of promoter activity.

Engineering Endogenous TDH3 Promoter to Expand Strength Range of the Library

To further characterize sequence features of the PkTDH3 promoter, GC content was calculated for distinct upstream segments of the 600‐bp region (Figure 5A). The analysis revealed a heterogeneous composition, with the −300 bp to −200 bp segment exhibiting the highest GC content (~57%), whereas sequences proximal to the transcription start site (+1 bp) were GC‐poor (~23%). This segmental analysis provided a comprehensive overview of promoter architecture, laying the groundwork for subsequent functional experiments. To identify the sequence region responsible for the high activity of PkTDH3, a series of 5′ truncations were constructed and characterized. Promoter length was found to be closely correlated with transcriptional output (Kang et al. 2025). As shown in Figure 5B, progressive truncation of the promoter from −600 bp to −300 bp had only a limited effect on promoter activity, whereas further truncation to −200 bp caused a sharp decrease in fluorescence intensity. The same trend was observed in the single‐copy genomic validation, in which no detectable fluorescence signal was observed when the promoter was truncated to −200 bp. These results indicate that the region between −300 bp and −200 bp is critical for PkTDH3 promoter activity and therefore likely represents the key upstream activating sequence (UAS) of this promoter in P. kudriavzevii E1. The −300 to −200 bp region, together with its immediate flanking sequences, was therefore subjected to error‐prone PCR to generate promoter variants with enhanced activity and to further broaden the dynamic range of expression (Figure 5C). Sequencing analysis of the resulting mutant library showed an average mutation frequency of 7.1 mutations per kb. Mutant promoters were cloned into the ARS‐CEN episomal plasmid and transformed into P. kudriavzevii E101 for high‐throughput fluorescence screening. The top‐performing variants, Mutant‐2 and Mutant‐3, exhibited median fluorescence increases of 2.9‐fold and 2.6‐fold relative to the wild‐type PkTDH3 promoter, respectively, whereas Mutant‐1 showed a moderate 1.3‐fold enhancement (Figure 5D).

Application of the Promoter Library to Improve Protein Content of P. kudriavzevii E1

P. kudriavzevii has shown promise as a platform for single‐cell protein production owing to its high cellular protein content (Rachamontree et al. 2015), broad substrate adaptability (Hashem et al. 2014), environmental robustness (Chu et al. 2023), and favourable nutritional profile (Agpoon et al. 2024). Compared with S. cerevisiae , P. kudriavzevii has been reported to show faster growth, greater cell accumulation, and higher biomass production on different carbon sources (Vajdič and Erjavec 2025). P. kudriavzevii E1 exhibits a favourable amino acid profile of 44.5% total protein content, which showed a modest enhancement in crude protein levels when compared to S. cerevisiae S288C (Figure 6A). The difference was more pronounced at the amino acid level. Total amino acids and essential amino acids were higher in P. kudriavzevii E1 by 35.3% and 38.3%, respectively. These results indicate that P. kudriavzevii E1 accumulated more total and essential amino acids than S. cerevisiae S288C. Significantly, the L‐methionine level in P. kudriavzevii E1 was 135.6% greater than in S. cerevisiae S288C, making it more appropriate for feed protein use. Additionally, L‐glutamate content in E1 was approximately 24% higher than that in S. cerevisiae S288C (Table S5). Given the central role of glutamate in cellular nitrogen assimilation, genes involved in nitrogen uptake and glutamate/glutamine‐mediated nitrogen assimilation were selected for promoter‐mediated expression tuning. MEP2, encoding a high‐affinity ammonium transporter, was used to modulate inorganic nitrogen uptake, whereas GLN1, encoding glutamine synthetase, was selected to regulate ammonium assimilation through the glutamate‐glutamine node (Valderrama‐Martín et al. 2022; Dawood 2024). The promoter library was utilized to adjust the expression levels of crucial genes affecting protein content. Each gene was individually adjusted using weak, medium, and strong promoters to examine their impact on protein levels.

Values for S288C were set to 100%. (B) Crude protein contents of strains carrying GLN1 or MEP2 expression cassettes driven by endogenous promoters with strong, medium, or weak activity. (C) Crude protein contents of strains carrying the nine promoter combinations for GLN1 and MEP2. The lower matrix indicates the promoter combination corresponding to each strain. (D) Crude protein contents of the parental E101 strain, the strain carrying medium‐promoter‐driven GLN1 and MEP2 expression cassettes, and derivatives in which the native PDC promoter was replaced with the CCG6, IMB1, or AIM3 promoter. Cellular protein content varied with promoter strength, although the response differed between the two genes (Figure 6B). Higher GLN1 expression was associated with increased protein content, whereas the highest protein level in the MEP2 series was obtained with the weak promoter. These results show that adjustment of promoter activity can alter cellular protein accumulation in P. kudriavzevii E101. To further examine the combined effects of GLN1 and MEP2 expression, nine promoter combinations were constructed using strong, medium, and weak promoters for each gene (Figure 6C). Cellular protein content differed among the resulting strains. The strain carrying medium‐strength promoters for both GLN1 and MEP2 showed the highest protein content and was significantly higher than the parental P. kudriavzevii E101. By contrast, simultaneous use of the strongest promoters did not produce the highest protein level. These results indicate that protein accumulation depended on the promoter combination used, rather than on maximal expression of either gene alone. To reduce carbon loss during fungal protein production, pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC) expression was further attenuated in the combined‐regulation strain by replacing its native promoter with endogenous promoters of medium or weak activity (Hong et al. 2024). In the strain with combined GLN1 and MEP2 regulation, the native PDC promoter was replaced with promoters of CCG6, IMB1, or AIM3, whose activities decreased in this order. The resulting strains exhibited different crude protein contents (Figure 6D). Replacement with promoter CCG6 or IMB1 did not further increase crude protein content relative to the combined GLN1/MEP2 regulation strain. In contrast, the AIM3 promoter replacement strain (AIM3pro) showed the highest crude protein content, reaching 43.73% DCW. This value was significantly higher than that of P. kudriavzevii E101 and the strain with combined GLN1 and MEP2 regulation. It represented a 23.9% increase relative to P. kudriavzevii E101. These findings showed that using a promoter library is feasible for precisely adjusting key gene expression to enhance strain performance in P. kudriavzevii E1. To determine whether the increase in crude protein content was accompanied by enhanced amino acid accumulation, we quantified the amino acid profiles of E101, the medium‐GLN1/medium‐MEP2 strain, and the AIM3pro strain (Table S5). The summed content of the 18 detected amino acids increased from 29.12% ± 0.82% of DCW in E101 to 31.80% ± 1.32% and 34.47% ± 0.98% of DCW in the medium‐GLN1/medium‐MEP2 and AIM3pro strains, respectively, corresponding to increases of 9.2% and 18.4% relative to E101. The summed content of the nine essential amino acids likewise increased from 13.09% ± 0.35% of DCW in E101 to 13.96% ± 0.18% and 15.46% ± 0.47% of DCW in the two engineered strains, respectively. Eight of the nine essential amino acids were higher in the AIM3pro strain than in E101; however, methionine content was lower. The EAA/TAA ratio remained comparable among the three strains (43.9%–44.9%), indicating that promoter‐based regulation enhanced overall amino acid accumulation, although it did not uniformly increase every individual amino acid. Together, these results demonstrate that the promoter library enabled coordinated tuning of nitrogen assimilation and carbon flux, thereby improving both crude protein content and overall amino acid accumulation in P. kudriavzevii E101.

Discussion

P. kudriavzevii, a non‐conventional yeast renowned for its robust stress tolerance and metabolic versatility, has emerged as a promising chassis for industrial biotechnology. However, the limited availability of efficient genetic tools remains a major challenge for expanding its application in metabolic engineering. CRISPR/Cas 9‐based genome editing has enabled targeted genetic modifications in this species, while episomal plasmids provide a complementary strategy for rapid genetic manipulation and heterologous expression without chromosomal integration (Tran et al. 2019). In this study, we established a stable episomal plasmid system in P. kudriavzevii E1 through the de novo identification and functional validation of native ARS and CEN elements.

ARS serve as replicator elements to which the origin recognition complex (ORC) directly binds to unwind the DNA double helix and recruit additional factors to initiate DNA replication (Stillman et al. 2025). In S. cerevisiae , most ARS sequences exhibit high sequence similarity, reflecting strong conservation of replication origin motifs (Wang and Gao 2019). However, in K. marxianus, functional ARSs capable of sustaining plasmid replication do not share a common consensus sequence (Abdel‐Banat et al. 2021), indicating that ARS function in eukaryotes is not solely determined by conserved motifs, but rather by the overall sequence and structural context. In addition, genomic composition, chromatin state, and transcriptional activity collectively shape ARS functionality over evolutionary timescales, and exogenous sequences with GC content resembling the host genome are more likely to become transcriptionally active and spatially intermingled with transcriptionally active yeast chromosomes (Meneu et al. 2025). Therefore, ARS activity is highly host‐specific and cannot be reliably predicted from conserved motif features alone. This suggests that, in addition to local ARS activity, the overall sequence composition of an introduced plasmid may influence its compatibility with the host nuclear environment.

Building on these insights, we developed a systematic workflow to identify functional ARS elements in P. kudriavzevii E1 by integrating genome‐wide sequence analysis with experimental validation. By screening candidate genomic fragments based on GC content, we were able to pinpoint sequences capable of supporting episomal replication. This GC‐guided approach not only highlights the importance of host‐vector sequence compatibility for plasmid maintenance in non‐conventional yeasts, but also provides a practical framework for identifying endogenous replicators in species where canonical motifs are insufficient to predict ARS activity. Through functional validation, ARS‐3 was identified as an effective element for episomal plasmid maintenance in P. kudriavzevii E1.

CENs are specialized DNA sequences responsible for kinetochore assembly and faithful chromosome segregation during cell division (Cao et al. 2020). In S. cerevisiae , the CEN‐ARS endowed plasmids with much higher stability than just ARS sequence alone (Cao et al. 2017). In this study, we identified a functional CEN sequence from the P. kudriavzevii E1 genome and demonstrated the feasibility of combining endogenous ARS and CEN elements for episomal plasmid construction. However, its fluorescence distribution remained uneven and was overall more like that of the ARS‐3‐2 plasmid, suggesting that ARS‐3‐2 exerted the dominant effect in this construct. These results suggest that the ARS‐CEN combination can provide favourable plasmid establishment and expression output, although the uneven fluorescence distribution indicates that further optimization of the ARS component is warranted. Recent work in Ogataea polymorpha showed that endogenous centromeric sequences can enable stable multigene expression plasmid tools, further supporting the utility of native CEN elements for episomal vector development in non‐conventional yeasts (Cheng et al. 2025).

Systematic characterization of constitutive promoters is essential for precise transcriptional control because the rational optimization of biosynthetic pathways for chemical production requires meticulous tuning of individual gene expression levels. Excessive gene expression does not necessarily improve production and may instead impose additional stress on the host (Shi et al. 2026). Promoters in P. kudriavzevii were categorized into strong, medium, and weak expression groups through integrative analysis of transcriptomic data and reporter assays; notably, promoter PkTDH3 demonstrated consistently robust activity and TEF1 was designated as a strong promoter (Cao et al. 2020). In P. kudriavzevii E1, PkTDH3 remained among the most active promoters (Figure 4), whereas TEF1 exhibited only intermediate activity, reflecting species‐specific modulation of promoter strength likely shaped by chromatin context, transcription factor availability, and the local regulatory environment. Promoter libraries in S. cerevisiae (Li et al. 2022), O. polymorpha (Ye et al. 2024), and Y. lipolytica (Kang et al. 2025) have demonstrated that modular UAS architectures and combinatorial designs can generate extensive dynamic ranges. The promoter library developed in this study provided a comparable range of activities and was further validated using single‐copy genomic integration. Finally, yeast‐derived protein is recognized as one of the promising applications of microbial platforms. In this study, the established episomal system and promoter toolbox expand the available strategies for gene engineering to improve mycoprotein production in strain P. kudriavzevii. These genetic resources will facilitate future applications of this yeast chassis in biotechnology.

Conclusion

In this study, we established an episomal expression system for the Pichia kudriavzevii strain E1 through functional characterization of endogenous ARS and CEN elements using a host‐vector GC compatibility‐guided screening strategy. Additionally, we developed and characterized an endogenous promoter library covering a broad range of expression strengths. Application of these genetic tools to regulate genes involved in nitrogen assimilation and carbon metabolism increased cellular protein content in P. kudriavzevii E1. Overall, the episomal system and promoter library expand the genetic toolkit available for metabolic engineering of P. kudriavzevii and support its further development as a biotechnological production host.
