Dynamic interplay of atherogenic index and body roundness in cardio-renal-metabolic disease: a multi-state analysis
Authors: Chen-Xi Jin, Le-Rong Liu, Qi Zhong, Xiao-Yan Wang, Jing-Jing Liang, Xiao-Meng Wang, Yi-Ning Xu, Jun-Yan Huang, Mei-Xuan Li, Lu Liu, Hui Huang, An-Ying Liang, Jing Wang, Xian-Bo Wu, Meng-Chen Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are closely linked at epidemiological, pathophysiological, and molecular levels, forming cardio-renal-metabolic (CRM) disease. Their multimorbidity leads to multi-organ dysfunction and increased cardiovascular risk, making prevention and management crucial in clinical and public health practice.MethodsWe included 398,689 participants from UK Biobank free of CRM diseases at baseline. We used both traditional and multi-state regression model to assess the relationships between AIP-BRI —defined as the product of the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and the body roundness index (BRI) —and CRM diseases. Besides, we employed the restricted cubic spline (RCS) approach to visualize the dose-response relationship between AIP-BRI values and three main transition stages (baseline→first CRM diseases (FCRM), FCRM disease→ double CRM (DCRM) diseases, and DCRM diseases→triple CRM (TCRM) disease).ResultsOver a a median follow-up period of 12.7 years, 61,539 individuals developed FCRM disease. Among these, 10,714 further developed DCRM diseases, while 1,332 advanced to TCRM diseases. According to our study, AIP-BRI was significantly associated with the progression of CRM diseases in fully adjusted model (HR [95% CI]: 1.248 [1.241–1.255] for FCRM; 1.180 [1.167–1.193] for DCRM; 1.120 [1.086–1.155] for TCRM).ConclusionOur findings highlight AIP-BRI as a potentially useful composite biomarker for predicting and monitoring CRM disease progression. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) demonstrate profound epidemiolgical, pathophysiological, and molecular interrelationships—forming cardio-renal-metabolic (CRM) disease [1]. In recent years, the rising prevalence of chronic metabolic diseases has led to increasing recognition of the pathophysiological interconnections among metabolic risk factors such as diabetes, obesity, CKD and CVD [2]. Growing evidence suggests that as individuals accumulate diagnoses over time, multimorbidity often progresses in a sequential manner, ultimately leading to multi-organ dysfunction and an increased incidence of adverse cardiovascular events [3]. Consequently, the prevention and management of these interconnected comorbidities have become critically important in clinical practice and public health.

Atherogenic index of plasma (AIP)-a logarithmic transition of the ratio of triglyceride (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), is an established lipid-based metric [4]. It serves as a marker of dyslipidemia and has been associated with cardiovascular disease risk and insulin resistance [4]. Previous studies have confirmed that AIP combined with obesity-related indicators such as waist circumference (WC), body mass index (BMI) and waist-to-height ratio(WHtR) can effectively predict the risk of some metabolic-associated diseases [5, 6]. The Body Roundness Index (BRI) is an anthropometric index derived from height and WC [7]. It was developed to quantify body shape and has been associated with visceral fat distribution and overall adiposity [7]. Both AIP and BRI have been demonstrated to exhibit robust predictive power for CVD, T2DM and CKD [4, 6, 8–14].

However, prior studies mainly focus on the influence of AIP and BRI on the risk of a single CRM disease in participants free of any CRM disease [4, 8, 10, 12–14] or the incidence of related diseases across cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) stages 0–3 [6, 11]. There is still a lack of research on the impact of AIP combined BRI (AIP-BRI) on the longitudinal progression of CRM disease and corresponding effective interventions to delay or prevent this progression. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the association between them.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large-scale biomedical research resource comprising approximately 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years recruited nationwide between 2006 and 2010 [15]. All participants completed comprehensive baseline assessments including touchscreen questionnaires, nurse-conducted interviews, and standardized physical measurements [16]. The study obtained written informed consent for data collection, analysis, and record linkage, with ethical approval granted by the NHS National Research Ethics Service (16/NW/0274) [16, 17]. This research was conducted under application number 55,794. Further methodological details of the UKB have been previously published [17].

In our analysis, we excluded participants with cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus, or chronic kidney disease in baseline (n = 35,065), with missing data on AIP (n = 42,731) or BRI (n = 26,022), which resulted in a final sample of 398,689 (Fig. 1a).

Ascertainment of outcomes

Participants were prospectively followed from enrollment until death, loss to follow-up, or the study’s end on 31 October 2022. The primary endpoint of interest was the incidence of newly diagnosed CRM disease, with case definitions based on previously published criteria [18]. First cardio-renal-metabolic (FCRM) disease was defined as the initial occurrence of any of the following during follow-up: CVD, T2DM, or CKD [3]. Double CRM (DCRM) diseases referred to the co-occurrence of two CRM conditions following an FCRM diagnosis, while triple CRM (TCRM) diseases denoted the simultaneous presence of CVD, T2DM, and CKD. Endpoint data were sourced from the UKB’s first-occurrence dataset. In the UKB, all outcomes were classified based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition (ICD-10), with the following diagnostic codes: CVD ([I20-I25, I50, I60-I69]), T2DM (E11) and CKD (N18) (Table S1).

Calculation of AIP-BRI

AIP was defined as the logarithmic transition of the ratio of TG to HDL-C, calculated as: AIP = log10​(HDL-C (mmol/L)/TG (mmol/L)​) [9]. BRI is a geometric index that quantifies body shape and adiposity by incorporating both height and waist circumference (WC). It is calculated using the following formula: BRI = 364.2 − 365.5×√[1 − (WC/2π)²/(0.5 × height)²], where both WC and height are measured in meters [13]. AIP-BRI was defined as the product of AIP and BRI [10].

Covariates

The following covariates were used in the analyses according to the priori knowledge (Table S1): age (< 60 years or ≥ 60 years), sex (male or female), ethnic (white or non-white), residential area (urban or rural), smoking status (never, previous or current), drinking status (never, previous or current), physical activity (low, moderate or high), history of hypertension (yes or no), history of cancer (yes or no), family history of T2DM (yes or no), family history of heart disease (yes or no) and family history of stroke (yes or no); all were analyzed as categorical variables. Hypertension history was determined based on either: (1) self-reported clinical diagnosis, (2) active antihypertensive treatment, or (3) sustained elevation in blood pressure (average systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg across multiple measurements). Cancer history, along with family history of stroke, heart disease, and T2DM, were defined based on self-reported medical history.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were categorized by AIP-BRI tertile groups. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with corresponding proportions (%) and continuous variables were summarized using means (SDs) or medians (IQRs). Between-group comparisons were performed using ANOVA/Kruskal-Wallis tests and chi-square tests, as appropriate.

In the initial analysis, we employed standard cox proportional hazards models to evaluate relationships between AIP-BRI (modeled per standard deviation increment) and: (1) first, double and triple CRM diseases and (2) individual CRM entities (CVD, T2DM, CKD). Models were adjusted for covariates mentioned above. Discriminative ability was quantified using Harrell’s C-index.

In main analyses, we employed a multi-state model to assess the influence of AIP-BRI on longitudinal progression of CRM disease. Specifically, the Markov multi-state model was adopted, implying that the transition probability between states depends only on the current state rather than the historical trajectory of the disease course. This approach is a widely used methodology in chronic disease epidemiology [19–23]. The multi-state approach extends conventional competing risk analysis, enabling detailed examination of how exposures influence transitional probabilities between distinct clinical states (e.g., disease progression or recovery trajectories). This analytical framework has been well-characterized in existing epidemiological literature [24]. This investigation employed multi-state modeling to examine the temporal relationship between AIP-BRI and the development of CRM multimorbidity, capturing transitions from disease-free status through sequential development of single, dual, and triple CRM conditions. Our model mainly focused on elucidating the transitions between non-fatal disease states. All-cause mortality was not explicitly included as a competing event in the main analysis. This approach was chosen to maintain a clear focus on the interrelationships among the living states of CRM diseases. This analytical strategy is consistent with that employed in previous studies [25, 26]. The analytical framework, illustrated in Fig. 1b, incorporated three clinically relevant transitions: (1) initial development of any CRM condition (transition1), (2) progression to two concurrent conditions (transition2), and (3) advancement to three conditions (transition3). The proportional hazards assumption for each transition in the multi-state model was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and no significant departures from the proportionality assumption were found. To address simultaneous transitions, we followed established methods by assigning a 0.5-day interval between theoretically concurrent events, consistent with prior research methodologies [27]. In further analyses, we evaluated AIP-BRI’s associations with CRM disease progression according to initial subtype presentation (CVD, T2DM, or CKD).

Using a multi-state cox regression model, we computed the hazard ratio (HR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values to assess the associations between AIP-BRI and CRM disease progression. Three models were developed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, ethnic, residential area, physical activity, smoking and drinking status, hypertension history, cancer history, family history of heart disease, family history of stroke and family history of T2DM. The additive effects of AIP and BRI on progress of CRM disease were assessed. We assessed by calculating three established metrics with 95%CI: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI), employing validated variance estimation methods from prior studies [28].

For the three main transition stages in multi-state model as mentioned above, we further employed the Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) approach to visualize the dose-response relationship between AIP-BRI values and three transition stages. The determination of the quantity and positioning of knots within the RCS was informed by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), aiming to achieve a harmonious equilibrium between achieving an excellent fit and avoiding overfitting. In addition, to compare the cumulative incidence of the three transition states over time, we constructed cumulative risk curves stratified by AIP-BRI tertiles.

For the mediation analysis, we selected several inflammatory and glycemic biomarkers closely related to the pathogenesis of CRM disease as potential mediators, examining their role in the association between AIP-BRI and the three transition states. Potential key confounding factors that may affect the relationships among the AIP–BRI (independent variable), the mediating variable, and the course of CRM disease (dependent variable) have been fully adjusted. Specifically, all the covariates mentioned above were included in the mediation analysis. The total effects were decomposed into the average direct effect (ADE) and the average causal mediation effect (ACME). The mediation proportion, calculated as ACME/(ACME + ADE), was evaluated for statistical significance using a non-parametric bootstrap method with 1000 iterations to generate bias-corrected confidence intervals.

To ensure robustness, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses: (1) trimming extreme AIP-BRI values (top and bottom 1%); (2) excluding participants with < 2 years of follow-up; (3) removing individuals with incident cancer. Subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by age (< 60 years or ≥ 60 years), sex (male or female), smoking status (never, previous or current), drinking status (never, previous or current), history of hypertension (yes or no), history of cancer (yes or no), ethnic (white or non-white), residential area (urban or rural), physical activity (low, moderate or high), family history of T2DM (yes or no), family history of heart disease (yes or no) and family history of stroke (yes or no).

The C-statistic analyses and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were performed to compare the predictive performance of AIP-BRI with that of other conventional clinical measurements for CRM diseases.

Statistical analysis was performed with R (version 4.4.1) and Stata software. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Our study included 398,689 eligible participants, with 41.66% over 60 years old, 55.35% being female and 85.89% resided in urban areas. During a median follow-up period of 12.7 years among 398,689 participants, 61,539 individuals (15.43%) developed first CRM disease. Of these cases, 34,460 (8.64%) were diagnosed with CVD, 10,127 (2.54%) with CKD, and 16,952 (4.25%) with T2DM. Among those with at least one CRM disease, 10,714 (17.41%) further developed double CRM diseases: CVD and T2DM (n = 4,920), CVD and CKD (n = 4,074), and CKD and T2DM (n = 1,720). A total of 1,332(12.43%) participants developed triple CRM diseases during follow-up (Fig. 1b). The baseline characteristics by tertiles of AIP-BRI are shown in Table 1. Compared to participants in the lowest AIP-BRI tertile, those in the middle and highest tertile were more likely to be older, male, smokers, exercise less, have a history of hypertension, and have family history of stroke, T2DM and heart diseases.

Association of AIP-BRI with CRM disease

As presented in Table 2, the crude model showed that each standard deviation increase in the AIP-BRI score was associated with a significantly increased risk of developing first CRM disease (HR = 1.339, 95% CI: 1.332–1.345), double CRM diseases (HR = 1.482, 95% CI: 1.467–1.497), and triple CRM diseases (HR = 1.576, 95% CI: 1534–1.619). In adjusted model, the relationship between AIP-BRI and the incidence of CRM diseases remained significant, and the HRs (95% CI) were 1.245 (1.239,1.252), 1.376 (1.361,1.392), 1.455 (1.412,1.499), 1.121 (1.113,1.128), 1.211 (1.196,1.225), 1.491(1.480,1.502), 1.223(1.198,1.248), 1.435(1.412,1.458) and 1.478(1.439,1.517). Notably, there was a marked increase in the C-statistics, which were 0.706 (0.704,0.708), 0.778 (0.776,0.780), 0.831 (0.821,0.841), 0.707 (0.705,0.709), 0.730 (0.726,0.734), 0.766 (0.762,0.770), 0.780 (0.774,0.786), 0.786 (0.780,0.792) and 0.797 (0.787,0.807) respectively.

Association between AIP-BRI and the trajectory of CRM diseases

RCS were employed in multi-state cox regression models and showed that an increased AIP-BRI value was associated with increased risk of dynamic progressions of CRM diseases in all three transition stages (p < 0.05 for all nonlinear results) (Fig. 2). Results of the multi-state analysis are shown in Table 3. By using multi-state models, we could observe that AIP-BRI plays an important role in each transition stage of the CRM diseases trajectories. In the crude model, the HRs (95% CI) associated with AIP-BRI elevation for the three main transition states were 1.342(1.335,1.349), 1.194(1.181,1.207) and 1.129(1.096,1.163), respectively (all p < 0.001). Further secondary analysis revealed that AIP-BRI was closely related to the risk of developing any type of CRM disease and further progressing to double CRM disease and triple CRM diseases (all p < 0.001), except for the route from T2DM to double CRM diseases. After adjustment, the association remained consistent. The interaction analyses showed that AIP and BRI has synergistic effects on the dynamic progress of CRM diseases (Table S2).

Mediating effects of blood biomarkers

To further explore the potential biological mechanisms through which AIP-BRI may influence the progression of CRM diseases, we conducted mediation analyses. Given the established relevance of glycemic and inflammatory pathways in disease pathogenesis, we selected the following mediators: hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting glucose, and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], monocyte-to-HDL ratio [MHR] [29], monocyte count, neutrophil count, and white blood cell count). Our analysis demonstrated that HbA1c and fasting glucose may play a mediating role between AIP-BRI and CRM disease progression at all three transition stages (all proportion of mediation > 10% and p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Inflammatory markers showed weaker mediation than glycemic markers, with MHR mediating 12.76% (baseline→FCRM) and WBC mediating 9.33% (DCRM→TCRM) of AIP-BRI’s effects (both p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding: patients with prevalent cancer; individuals with extreme AIP-BRI values (top and bottom 1%), and participants with less than two years of follow-up. Tables S3-S5 demonstrate robust associations between AIP-BRI and progression through all stages of CRM disease (Baseline→FCRM→DCRM→TCRM), with results remaining stable across multiple sensitivity analyses. These findings align consistently with our primary results. To further validate our findings, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses examining the association between AIP-BRI and first, double and triple CRM diseases in different subgroups (Figures S1-S3). Notably, these associations were consistently stronger in the following populations: male participants, individuals aged over 60 years, urban residents, those with pre-existing hypertension and subjects with a family history of T2DM.

Comparative performance of prediction models

To fairly compare AIP-BRI with existing concepts of dyslipidemia and obesity for their predictive performance for CRM diseases, we conducted comprehensive benchmarking of our proposed AIP-BRI model against established clinical prediction approaches. Table S6 presents the C-index values with 95% CI for all models. The Empty Model, containing only baseline covariates, served as the reference standard with C-index values of 0.686 (95% CI: 0.684–0.688) in multi-state analysis. All enhanced models demonstrated statistically significant improvements over this baseline. The Full Reference Model, incorporating all available anthropometric and lipid measurements, demonstrated the highest overall discriminative performance, with C-index values of 0.724 (95% CI: 0.722–0.726) in multi-state model. Our proposed AIP-BRI model showed competitive performance, with C-index values of 0.704 (95% CI: 0.702–0.706) in the multi-state analysis. Figure S4 demonstrates ROC curves comparing the discriminative performance of key models for CRM diseases. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while the AIP-BRI score provides statistically significant predictive value compared to the empty model (ΔC-index = + 0.018), its discriminative capacity remains modest and does not surpass established clinical measures.

Discussion

In this large prospective study, we employed both traditional cox regression and multi-state models to comprehensively evaluate the association between AIP-BRI and CRM diseases. Our analyses demonstrated that AIP-BRI was significantly associated not only with the incidence of CRM diseases, but also with their dynamic progression (baseline→FCRM diseases, FCRM diseases→DCRM diseases, and DCRM diseases→TCRM diseases). Moreover, AIP-BRI showed obvious associations with progression from baseline to each CRM disease (CVD, T2DM or CKD) as well as each first CRM disease to double CRM diseases (all p < 0.001), except in the T2DM-to-DCRM transition. Mediation analysis identified HbA1c and fasting glucose as significant mediators in the association between AIP-BRI and CRM disease progression. Besides, subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.

The detrimental impacts of AIP-BRI on certain transitions of CRM diseases observed in the present investigation align with previous findings that documented the relationships between either AIP or BRI and individual CRM diseases. A prospective study involving 7,745 Chinese participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) revealed that higher baseline AIP levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of stroke in individuals at CKM syndrome stages 0–3 (Q4 vs. reference: HR = 1.414, 95% CI: 1.052–1.900) [30]. Besides, it has been demonstrated that in individuals with early-to-moderate CKM syndrome (stages 0–3), inadequate AIP control significantly increased the risk of CVD events [11, 31]. For AIP and its modified indices such as AIP-WC, AIP-WHtR and AIP-BMI, a large cohort study also utilizing participants from CHARLS has found that they are significantly associated with the incidence of stroke in CKM stages 0–3 [6]. Concerning BRI, longitudinal analysis demonstrated that elevated BRI trajectories were significantly associated with a 55% higher risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) (multivariable-adjusted HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.26–1.90) [12]. Moreover, a cross-sectional study of 24,162 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2009–2018 reported the significant associations between BRI and the risk of CKD [32]. Both AIP and BRI have been found to have close relationship with T2DM [4, 8, 13, 33, 34].

Potential underlying mechanisms of the association between AIP-BRI and CRM diseases may include endothelial dysfunction, lipid metabolism disorder and inflammatory response [3]. For example, it has been revealed that AIP was closely related to asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) and fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) [9]. ADMA is a biomarker and mediator of endothelial dysfunction [35]. In recent years, elevated circulating ADMA levels have been scientifically demonstrated to contribute to the pathogenesis of T2DM [36], kidney disease [37], and CVD [38]. FABP4, a key regulator of lipid transport and metabolism [39], exhibits elevated circulating levels in obesity [40], T2DM [41], and CVD [35, 42], implicating its involvement in metabolic and cardiovascular pathophysiology. BRI, as an effective metric for assessing the proportion of visceral fat relative to total body fat, reflects an increase in visceral adipose tissue when elevated [7, 43]. This elevation may contribute to heightened production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [43], increased oxidative stress [44], and potential structural and functional impairments to the heart due to obesity [43, 45]. Furthermore, our interaction analyses revealed a synergistic effect between AIP and BRI. A potential explanation may be that visceral adipose tissue, quantified by BRI, secretes pro-inflammatory adipokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, as well as free fatty acids, which exacerbate hepatic very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) production [46]. This lipid overflow directly amplifies the atherogenic lipid profile represented by AIP [47]. The multiplicative interaction likely reflects an amplifying feedback loop between dysfunctional adiposity and impaired lipid metabolism, which constitutes a core mechanism in CRM disease progression [48].

Our mediation analysis demonstrated that HbA1c and blood glucose play a potential mediating role in the association between AIP-BRI and progression of CRM diseases. According to previous research, both AIP and BRI can influence the elevation of glycemic monitoring indicators and contribute to diabetes development [8, 14, 34, 49]. For example, a prospective study involving 15,453 participants from NAGALA Cohort demonstrated that AIP was independently associated with an increased risk of diabetes [49]. Furthermore, combining AIP with HbA1c significantly improved predictive accuracy for diabetes incidence [49]. Moreover, it has been revealed that hyperglycemic states induce activation of the polyol and hexosamine pathways, stimulate protein kinase C (PKC) signaling, and promote the formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [3, 50]. AGEs exert direct pathogenic effects on cardiovascular and renal tissues, thereby accelerating the progression of CRM disorders [3, 51]. These findings indicate that AIP/BRI elevation may influence CRM development through glucose metabolism disruption. The subgroup analysis of our study showed that the association between AIP-BRI and first, double and triple CRM disease were more pronounced in male participants and individuals aged over 60 years, which is consistent with previous research. For instance, a study of 33,868 US adults (aged ≥ 20 years) demonstrated that women exhibited a lower prevalence of CKM stage 3 compared with men (male: 22.4%; female: 15.2%), which may due to sex-based variations in hormonal profiles and lifestyle behaviors [52]. Moreover, a study utilizing data from the CHARLS cohort revealed that the prevalence of advanced CKM syndrome was significantly higher among middle-aged and older adults aged 65–85 years (21.0%) compared to their younger counterparts (12.1%) [53].Additionally, our findings demonstrated that certain inflammatory indices also served as significant mediators, potentially through mechanisms involving the associated inflammatory responses as discussed in the preceding Sect [3].

Our benchmarking analysis provides essential context for interpreting the findings. It is clear that the AIP-BRI score, while a statistically significant predictor, does not outperform established models utilizing similar clinical measurements. This objective assessment is crucial for preventing overinterpretation and aligns with the methodological rigor required for risk score development. Subsequent studies should employ more refined experimental designs to further explore the clinical application scenarios of these indicators.

Our study offers important insights for the intervention and management of chronic diseases. Through multi-state modeling, we establish AIP-BRI as a key factor in CRM diseases development and progression. Specifically, the integration of atherogenic dyslipidemia and central obesity provides refined and detailed guidance for predicting CRM multimorbidity. Besides, we identified potential mediators through which AIP-BRI influences the progression of CRM, providing mechanistic insights into its pathophysiological role-an aspect that has been largely unexplored in prior studies. Furthermore, in our analyses, we adjusted for multiple potential confounders and conducted stratified analyses to verify both the robustness of our findings and potential heterogeneity across subgroups.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, our study utilized baseline measurements of AIP-BRI exposure, and therefore could not account for potential longitudinal variations, particularly those occurring following disease diagnosis. Besides, we acknowledge potential misclassification bias in our outcome definitions. The broad CVD definition may encompass conditions with varying pathophysiology, while the ICD-based CKD definition likely under-ascertains early-stage disease. Future studies with more granular phenotypic data are needed to further investigate the associations explored in our research. Additionally, given the predominantly European-ancestry composition of our cohort, the generalizability of these results to more diverse populations may be limited and requires further investigation. It’s noteworthy that despite employing objective endpoint definitions and statistical adjustments, residual detection bias may persist in this comorbidity study, as intensified medical surveillance following initial diagnoses could inflate observed progression rates to multimorbidity. In addition, this study examines CVD, T2D and CKD comorbidities rather than the comprehensive CKM syndrome (per AHA criteria). While this simplified approach offers clinical practicality and aligns with prior research, it may sacrifice some precision [25]. What’s more, a methodological limitation of our study is that our multi-state model did not treat death as an absorbing state. Consequently, our estimates of transition probabilities and sojourn times should be interpreted as conditional on survival. Future studies employing multi-state models would benefit from the explicit inclusion of mortality to provide a more comprehensive view of the entire disease process. Moreover, we cannot rule out the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors in this cohort study. Notably, the interpretability of our mediation analysis is subject to important limitations inherent to its observational nature. Despite adjusting for key confounders, residual bias from unmeasured factors remains possible and the assumed causal direction, while biologically plausible, is not definitive, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Future causal inference studies are needed to validate these pathways. Furthermore, as our benchmarking analysis revealed, the predictive performance of the AIP-BRI score is modest and does not exceed that of simpler, conventional clinical models. This underscores that AIP-BRI should not be considered a replacement for established metrics but rather as a complementary approach that conceptually integrates two core pathophysiological domains of CRM diseases. Its potential utility may lie in providing a consolidated view of metabolic risk, but this requires validation in specific clinical scenarios.

Conclusions

Our study identifies AIP-BRI as a potentially useful biomarker for CRM disease development and progression. This sensitive indicator enables early risk stratification and targeted prevention, suggesting its clinical utility for reducing disease burden. Further research should validate its applicability across populations and guide integration into healthcare policies and practice.
