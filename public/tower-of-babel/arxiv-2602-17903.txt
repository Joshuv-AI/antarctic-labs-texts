Let

F_{0}=0

F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_{n}

Methods

2.1
Preliminaries on Fibonacci and Lucas numbers

First, we collect some facts on Fibonacci numbers. Let us start with the Binet-formula for the Fibonacci numbers given by

F_{n}=\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\sqrt{5}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\alpha=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2},

(2.1)

are the roots of the characteristic polynomial
X^{2}-X-1
of the Fibonacci sequence. Note that
\beta=-\alpha^{-1}
. The Binet formula for the Lucas numbers is given by

L_{n}=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}\qquad{\text{\rm for~all}}\qquad n\geq 0.

(2.2)

Both the Fibonacci sequence
(F_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and the Lucas sequence
(L_{n})_{n\geq 0}
can be extended to negative integers either by using their recurrence relation or by
plugging negative numbers
n
into the Binet formulas (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). In both cases one checks that
F_{-n}=(-1)^{n-1}F_{n}
and
L_{-n}=(-1)^{n}L_{n}

hold for all integers
n\geq 0
. Furthermore, all formulas such as (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) hold when
n,~m
range through all the integers, positive, zero or negative ones.

Let
n
be a positive integer. A prime factor
r
of
F_{n}
is called primitive if
r\nmid F_{m}
for any positive integer
m<n
. Similarly, a prime factor
r
of
L_{n}
is primitive if

r
does not divide
L_{m}
for any positive integer
m<n
. Since
L_{n}=F_{2n}/F_{n}
, it follows that every primitive prime factor of
L_{n}
is a prime factor of
F_{2n}
. The following is the celebrated primitive divisor theorem of Carmichael
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Carmichael)
.

The number
F_{n}
has a primitive prime factor for all
n\geq 13
.

Listing
F_{n}
for
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,12\}
one notices that
F_{n}
has a primitive prime factor for all
n
except for
n=1,2,6,12
, for which
F_{1}=F_{2}=1
,
F_{6}=8=F_{3}^{3}
and
F_{12}=144=2^{4}3^{2}=F_{3}^{4}F_{4}^{3}
, respectively. Further,
L_{n}
has a primitive prime factor for all
n=1,6
for which
L_{1}=1,~L_{6}=18=2\cdot 3^{2}
, while
L_{3}=2^{2},~L_{4}=3
.

Next we discuss greatest common divisors of Fibonacci and Lucas numbers. The next result is from the paper
[
11
]
of McDaniel.

Lemma 2.1

(McDaniel,
[
11
]
)
.

Let
m,n
be positive integers. Then:

(i)

\gcd(F_{m},F_{n})=F_{\gcd(m,n)}
;

(ii)

\gcd(L_{m},L_{n})=L_{\gcd(m,n)}
if both
m/\gcd(m,n)
and
n/\gcd(m,n)
are odd. Otherwise, the above
\gcd
is
2
or
1
according to whether both
m,n
are multiples of
3
or not;

(iii)

\gcd(L_{m},F_{n})=L_{\gcd(m,n)}
if
n/\gcd(m,n)
is even and
m/\gcd(m,n)
is odd. Otherwise, the above
\gcd
is
2
or
1
according to whether both
m,n
are multiples of
3
or not.

From the above lemma it follows, in particular, that if
a\mid F_{b}
where
a
and
b
are both odd, then
a
is coprime to
L_{n}
for any
n\geq 1
. In particular,
L_{n}
is never a multiple of
5(=F_{5})
or
13(=F_{7})
or
17
(which divides
F_{9}=34
), etc. This fact will be useful later.

We will also need the following result about perfect powers in the Fibonacci and Lucas numbers
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Bugeaud, Mignotte, Siksek)
.

If
F_{n}=y^{a}
for some integers
y>1
and
a>1
, then
n\in\{6,12\}
for which
F_{6}=2^{3},~F_{12}=12^{2}
. If
L_{n}=y^{a}
for some integers

and
a>1
, then
n=3
for which
L_{3}=2^{2}
.

In the interest of this paper, we need to discuss the multiplicative properties of numbers of the form

\delta_{\ell}:=\frac{\alpha^{-\ell}+1}{\sqrt{5}}

for
\ell\geq 2
. Throughout the paper
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

N_{{\mathbb{K}}/{\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\frac{1+\alpha^{-\ell}}{{\sqrt{5}}}\right)=\left\{\begin{matrix}-L_{\ell}/5&{\text{\rm if}}&\ell\equiv 1\pmod{2};\\
F_{\ell/2}^{2}&{\text{\rm if}}&\ell\equiv 2\pmod{4};\\
L_{\ell/2}^{2}/5&{\text{\rm if}}&\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}.\end{matrix}\right.

Proof.

A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle N_{{\mathbb{K}}/{\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\frac{1+\alpha^{-\ell}}{\sqrt{5}}\right)=\frac{(1+\alpha^{-\ell})(1+\beta^{-\ell})}{5}=\frac{L_{-\ell}+1+(-1)^{\ell}}{5}.

If

\ell
is odd, the above is
L_{-\ell}/5=-L_{\ell}/5
. If
\ell
is even, then the above has the value
(L_{\ell}+2)/5
. This is
F_{\ell/2}^{2}
or
L_{\ell/2}^{2}/5
according to whether
\ell\equiv 2\pmod{4}

or
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
k\geq 2
and that
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{k}
are distinct integers
>1
such that

\frac{1+\alpha^{-\ell_{i}}}{\sqrt{5}}\qquad i=1,\ldots,k

are multiplicatively dependent up to units (
\pm
powers of
\alpha
). Then

\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k}\}\subseteq\{2,6,10,12\}.

Proof.

We write a relation of the form

\alpha^{a}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha^{-\ell_{i}}}{{\sqrt{5}}}\right)^{b_{i}}=\pm 1,

for some integers
a,~b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
, where not all
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
are zero. We take norms and apply Lemma
2.2
. We get

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{\ell_{i}}^{b_{i}}=\pm 1,

where
\delta_{\ell}=L_{\ell}/5
for
\ell
odd,
F_{\ell/2}^{2}
for
\ell\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and
L_{\ell/2}^{2}/5
for
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Recall that a primitive prime factor
r
of
F_{n}
is a prime number which does not divide
F_{m}
for any positive integer
m<n
. Furthermore,
F_{n}
has a primitive prime factor
r
for all
n>12
by Theorem
2.1
. Since
L_{n}=F_{2n}/F_{n}
, it follows that
if
n
is even then the primitive prime factor of
F_{2n}
when it exists is a factor of
L_{n}
. In particular,
L_{\ell}
contains the primitive prime factors of
F_{2\ell}
(for
\ell
odd so
2\ell\equiv 2\pmod{4}
) when they exist,
L_{\ell/2}
contains the primitive prime factors of
F_{\ell}
(for
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}
) when they exist, and
F_{\ell/2}
contains the primitive prime factors of
F_{\ell/2}
(for
\ell\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, so
\ell/2
is odd), when they exist. In particular, if
\ell_{j}:=\max\{\ell_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}>12
, then
\delta_{\ell_{j}}
contains a primitive prime factor which does not divide
\delta_{\ell_{s}}
for any
s\neq j
.
Listing
\delta_{\ell}
for
\ell=2,\ldots,12
, we get the numbers

1,~-\frac{2^{2}}{5},~\frac{3^{2}}{5},~-\frac{11}{5},~2^{2},~-\frac{29}{5},~\frac{7^{2}}{5},~-\frac{2^{2}\cdot 19}{5},~5^{2},~-\frac{199}{5},~\frac{2^{2}3^{4}}{5},\ldots,

from where we deduce the desired conclusion.
∎

2.2
Generalities on the equation
F_{n}+F_{m}=p^{x}q^{y}

Assume next that
n\geq 1
. Then the Binet formula (
2.1
) immediately yields the inequalities

0.38\alpha^{n}<\alpha^{n}\left(\frac{1-\alpha^{-4}}{\sqrt{5}}\right)\leq F_{n}=\alpha^{n}\frac{1-(-1)^{n}\alpha^{-2n}}{\sqrt{5}}\leq\alpha^{n}\left(\frac{1+\alpha^{-4}}{\sqrt{5}}\right)<0.52\alpha^{n}.

(2.3)

Next, assume without loss of generality that
n\geq m\geq 1
, and that
F_{n}+F_{m}=p^{x}q^{y}
for some distinct primes
p,q
. Then we have by (
2.3
) that

p^{x}q^{y}=F_{n}+F_{m}<0.52\alpha^{n}+0.52\alpha^{m}\leq 0.52\alpha^{n}+0.52\alpha^{n}<1.1\alpha^{n}.

(2.4)

Taking logarithms on both sides of (
2.4
), we get

\displaystyle x\log p+y\log q<n\left(\dfrac{\log 1.1}{n}+\log\alpha\right)<0.6n.

Since
p\geq 2,q\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle x<\dfrac{0.6n}{\log p}<n\qquad\text{and}\qquad y<\dfrac{0.6n}{\log q}<n.

(2.5)

Lastly here, we recall one additional simple fact from calculus. If
x\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
|x|<1/2
, then

\displaystyle|\log(1+x)|

\displaystyle<|x-x^{2}/2+-\dots|<|x|+\frac{|x|^{2}+|x|^{3}+\dots}{2}<|x|\left(1+\frac{|x|}{2(1-|x|)}\right)<\frac{3}{2}|x|.

(2.6)

In a similar way, we obtain the lower bound
|\log(1+x)|>\frac{1}{2}|x|
provided that
|x|<1/2
. We use these inequalities frequently throughout the paper.

2.3
Linear forms in logarithms

We use several times Baker-type lower bounds for nonzero linear forms in two or more logarithms of algebraic numbers. There are many such bounds mentioned in the literature like that of Baker and Wüstholz from
[
2
]
or Matveev from
[
10
]
. Before we can formulate such inequalities we need the notion of height of an algebraic number recalled below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\lambda
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. The logarithmic height of
\lambda
is given by

h(\lambda):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\lambda^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\lambda
is a rational number represented as
\lambda:=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\lambda)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\lambda_{1}\pm\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda_{1})+h(\lambda_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\lambda_{1})+h(\lambda_{2});

\displaystyle h(\lambda^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\lambda)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\lambda_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\lambda_{t},

(2.7)

where for us
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are nonzero integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{L}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{L}}
.
We start with the general form due to Matveev presented as Theorem 9.4 in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Matveev, Theorem 9.4 in
[
4
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\lambda_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Assume
\Gamma\neq 0
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\lambda_{i}),|\log\lambda_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
. Furthermore, in this case also

\log|\Lambda|>-1.5\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
\Lambda
is shown in (
2.7
).

The inequality concerning
\log|\Gamma|
is from
[
4
]
. The inequality concerning
\log|\Lambda|
follows from the one concerning
\log|\Gamma|
by noting that
for
|\Lambda|<1/2
, we have that
|\Lambda|>|\Gamma|/2
(see (
2.6
)).

2.4
Reduction methods

2.4.1
Continued fractions

After applying Theorem
2.3
, we get upper bounds on our variables. However, such upper bounds are too large, thus there is need to reduce them. In this paper, we use the following result related with continued fractions (see Theorem 8.2.4 in
[
12
]
).

Lemma 2.4

(Legendre)
.

Let
\mu
be an irrational number,
[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
be the continued fraction expansion of
\mu
. Let
p_{i}/q_{i}=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i}]
, for all
i\geq 0
, be all the convergents of the continued fraction of
\mu
, and
M
be a positive integer. Let
N
be a non–negative integer such that

q_{N}>M
.
Then putting
a(M):=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
, the inequality

\bigg|\mu-\frac{r}{s}\bigg|>\dfrac{1}{(a(M)+2)s^{2}},

holds for all pairs
(r,s)
of positive integers with
0<s<M
.

However, since there are no methods based on continued fractions to find a lower bound for linear forms in more than two variables with bounded integer coefficients, we use at some point a method based on the LLL–algorithm. We next explain this method in Subsection
2.4.2
.

2.4.2
Reduced Bases for Lattices and LLL–reduction methods

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
–dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

In this situation we say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
6
]
Sect 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
\frac{3}{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
\mathcal{P}
with
\frac{1}{4}<\mathcal{P}<1
,
(see
[
9
]
Sect 1).

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},v\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{u\in\mathcal{L}}||u-v||\quad;~~v\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq u\in\mathcal{L}}||u||\quad;~~v\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL-algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},v\right)\geq c_{2}
(see
[
13
]
, Sect. V.4).

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z=B^{-1}v
with
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})^{T}
. Furthermore,

(i)

if
v\not\in\mathcal{L}
, let
i_{0}
be the largest index such that
z_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and put
\sigma:=\{z_{i_{0}}\}
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer.

(ii)

if
v\in\mathcal{L}
, put
\sigma:=1
.

Now, define

c_{1}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{\dfrac{\|b_{1}\|}{\|b_{j}^{*}\|}\right\}.

Then,
l(\mathcal{L},y)\geq c_{2}=\lambda\|b_{1}\|c_{1}^{-1}
.

In our application, we are given real numbers
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle|\eta_{0}+a_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+a_{k}\eta_{k}|\leq c_{3}\exp(-c_{4}H),

(2.8)

where the integers
a_{i}
are bounded as
|a_{i}|\leq A_{i}
with
A_{i}
given upper bounds for
1\leq i\leq k
. We write
A_{0}:=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}\{A_{i}\}
.

The basic idea in such a situation, from
[
7
]
, is to approximate the linear form (
2.8
) by an approximation lattice. So, we consider the lattice
\mathcal{L}
generated by the columns of the matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
\lfloor M\eta_{1}\rfloor&\lfloor M\eta_{2}\rfloor&\ldots&\lfloor M\eta_{k-1}\rfloor&\lfloor M\eta_{k}\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

where
M
is a large constant usually of the size of about
A_{0}^{k}
. Let us assume that we have an LLL–reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
of
\mathcal{L}
and that we have a lower bound
l\left(\mathcal{L},v\right)\geq c_{2}
with
v:=(0,0,\ldots,-\lfloor M\eta_{0}\rfloor)
. Note that
c_{2}
can be computed by using the results of Lemma
2.5
. Then, with these notations the following result is Lemma VI.1 in
[
13
]
.

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma VI.1 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
S:=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}A_{i}^{2}
and
T:=\dfrac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}}{2}
. If
c_{2}^{2}\geq T^{2}+S
, then inequality (
2.8
) implies that we either have
a_{1}=a_{2}=\cdots=a_{k-1}=0
and
a_{k}=-\dfrac{\lfloor M\eta_{0}\rfloor}{\lfloor M\eta_{k}\rfloor}
, or

H\leq\dfrac{1}{c_{4}}\left(\log(Mc_{3})-\log\left(\sqrt{c_{2}^{2}-S}-T\right)\right).

Finally, we present an analytic argument which is Lemma 7 from
[
8
]
. It is useful when obtaining upper bounds on some positive real variable involving powers of the logarithm of the variable itself.

Lemma 2.7

(Gúzman & Luca,
[
8
]
)
.

If
s\geq 1
,
T>(4s^{2})^{s}
and
T>\displaystyle\frac{z}{(\log z)^{s}}
, then

z<2^{s}T(\log T)^{s}.

SageMath 10.6 is used to perform all the computations in this work.

3
Proof of Theorem
1.1

3.1
Reduction to Zeckendorf representations

We start with a discussion of
n
versus
m
.

Since we assume that
n\geq m
, we might have
n=m
, in which case

F_{n}+F_{m}=2F_{n}=F_{n+1}+F_{n-2},

or
m=n-1
, in which case we have

F_{n}+F_{m}=F_{n}+F_{n-1}=F_{n+1}+F_{0}.

In all other cases
m\leq n-2
. We always work with the representations appearing in the right-hand side above. Thus, for us,
d:=n-m\geq 2
. This is called the Zeckendorf representation and is unique up to identifying

F_{1}
with
F_{2}
when
m\in\{1,2\}
. Also, when
m\in\{1,2\}
we will always work with the value of
m
such that
n\equiv m\pmod{2}
.

3.2
An absolute upper bound on
n
knowing
p
and
q

For the rest of the paper, we note that since
p,q\geq 2
are distinct primes, then we can assume without loss of generality that
p>q\geq 2
. We first prove the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n,m,x,y
be nonnegative integer solutions to (
1.4
) for fixed primes
p>q\geq 2
with
n\geq m+2
, then

n-m<2\cdot 10^{15}(\log p)(\log q)\log n.

Proof.

We start by rewriting (
1.4
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\sqrt{5}}+\frac{\alpha^{m}-\beta^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}

\displaystyle=p^{x}q^{y},

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{5}}-p^{x}q^{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha^{m}+\beta^{n}+\beta^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}.

Dividing through the above relation by
p^{x}q^{y}=F_{n}+F_{m}>0.38\alpha^{n}
and estimating the right-hand side using the fact that
\beta=-\alpha^{-1}
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\alpha^{n}}{p^{x}q^{y}\sqrt{5}}-1\right|

\displaystyle<\frac{\alpha^{m}+\alpha^{-n}+\alpha^{-m}}{0.38\alpha^{n}\sqrt{5}}=\alpha^{m-n}\left(\frac{1+\alpha^{-(n+m)}+\alpha^{-2m}}{0.38\sqrt{5}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{m-n}\left(\frac{1+\alpha^{-(1+0)}+\alpha^{-2\cdot 0}}{0.38\sqrt{5}}\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{4}{\alpha^{d}},

where
d:=n-m
. So, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|p^{-x}q^{-y}\alpha^{n}(\sqrt{5})^{-1}-1\right|<\frac{4}{\alpha^{d}}.

(3.1)

We now apply Theorem
2.3
to the left-hand side of (
3.1
). Let
\Gamma_{1}:=p^{-x}q^{-y}\alpha^{n}(\sqrt{5})^{-1}-1=e^{\Lambda_{1}}-1
.
Notice that
\Gamma_{1}\neq 0
, otherwise we would have
\alpha^{n}/\sqrt{5}=p^{x}q^{y}
. This implies that
\alpha^{2n}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, a contradiction for
n\geq 1
. We use the field
{\mathbb{K}}
of
degree
D:=2
. Here,
t:=4
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle:=p,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle:=q,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle:=\alpha,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{4}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{5},

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle:=-x,

\displaystyle\quad b_{2}

\displaystyle:=-y,

\displaystyle\quad b_{3}

\displaystyle:=n,

\displaystyle\quad b_{4}

\displaystyle:=-1.

Now, we can write
\max\{|b_{1}|,|b_{2}|,|b_{3}|,|b_{4}|\}=\max\{x,y,n,1\}=n
by (
2.5
), so that we take
B:=n
. Also,

A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\lambda_{i}),|\log\lambda_{i}|,0.16\}\qquad\text{for all}\qquad i=1,2,3,4.

So,

A_{1}:=Dh(\lambda_{1})=2\log p,\quad A_{2}:=Dh(\lambda_{2})=2\log q,\quad A_{3}:=Dh(\lambda_{3})=2\cdot\frac{1}{2}\log\alpha=\log\alpha,

and

A_{4}:=Dh(\lambda_{4})=2\log\sqrt{5}=\log 5.

Then, by Theorem
2.3
,

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma_{1}|

\displaystyle>-1.4\cdot 30^{7}\cdot 4^{4.5}\cdot 2^{2}(1+\log 2)(1+\log n)(2\log p)(2\log q)(\log\alpha)(\log 5)

\displaystyle>-8.1\cdot 10^{14}(\log p)(\log q)\log n,

(3.2)

where we have used the fact
n\geq 2
.
Comparing (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle d:=n-m<2\cdot 10^{15}(\log p)(\log q)\log n.

This proves Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Next, we prove the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be nonnegative integer solutions to (
1.4
) for fixed primes
p>q\geq 2
with

, then

Proof.

We go back to (

) and rewrite it as

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{n}+\alpha^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}-p^{x}q^{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{n}+\beta^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}.

Like before, we divide through the above relation by
p^{x}q^{y}=F_{n}+F_{m}>0.38\alpha^{n}
and estimate the right-hand side using the fact that

. We get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\alpha^{n}\left(1+\alpha^{-d}\right)}{p^{x}q^{y}\sqrt{5}}-1\right|

\displaystyle<\frac{\alpha^{-n}+\alpha^{-m}}{0.38\alpha^{n}\sqrt{5}}\leq\alpha^{-n}\left(\frac{\alpha^{-1}+\alpha^{-0}}{0.38\sqrt{5}}\right)<\frac{2}{\alpha^{n}}.

(3.3)

So, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|p^{-x}q^{-y}\alpha^{n}\left(\dfrac{1+\alpha^{-d}}{\sqrt{5}}\right)-1\right|<\frac{2}{\alpha^{n}}.

We now apply Theorem
2.3
to the left-hand side of (

). Let

\Gamma_{2}:=p^{-x}q^{-y}\alpha^{n}\left(1+\alpha^{-d}\right)(\sqrt{5})^{-1}-1=e^{\Lambda_{2}}-1.

Clearly
\Gamma_{2}\neq 0

\frac{\alpha^{n}+\alpha^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}=p^{x}q^{y}.

Conjugating the above in

The absolute value of the right-hand side above is
<2/{\sqrt{5}}
, which shows that
\alpha^{n}<\alpha^{n}+\alpha^{m}<2

.
We again use the field
{\mathbb{K}}
with
D=2
,

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle:=p,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle:=q,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle:=\alpha,

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{4}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1+\alpha^{-d}}{\sqrt{5}},

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle:=-x,

\displaystyle\quad b_{2}

\displaystyle:=-y,

\displaystyle\quad b_{3}

\displaystyle:=n,

\displaystyle\quad b_{4}

\displaystyle:=1.

Moreover, we still take

,

,

and

. For the case of

, we compute

\displaystyle Dh(\lambda_{4})=2h\left(\dfrac{1+\alpha^{-d}}{\sqrt{5}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2d\log\alpha+2\log\sqrt{5}+2\log 2

\displaystyle<2\left(2\cdot 10^{15}(\log p)(\log q)\log n\right)\log\alpha+2\log\sqrt{5}+2\log 2

We thus define

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma_{1}|

\displaystyle>-1.4\cdot 30^{7}\cdot 4^{4.5}\cdot 2^{2}(1+\log 2)(1+\log n)(2\log p)(2\log q)(\log\alpha)\cdot 2.1\cdot 10^{15}(\log p)(\log q)\log n

where we have used the fact that

.
Comparing (

), we get

\displaystyle n<1.3\cdot 10^{30}(\log p)^{2}(\log q)^{2}(\log n)^{2}.

We apply Lemma

,

Since

∎

def solve_fib_5_adic_lifting():
print("Starting 5-adic lifting search using successive 5th powering...")

# Bounds from theory
MAX_N_BOUND = 10**53
MAX_M = 2074
A = matrix(ZZ, [[1, 1], [1, 0]])
max_val = 0

# Precompute lifting matrices: M[j] = A^(4 * 5^j)
# This ensures O(log n) complexity
M = [A^20]
for j in range(80):
M.append(M[-1]^5)

for m in range(1, MAX_M + 1):
if m % 500 == 0: print(f"Checking m = {m}...")
Fm = fibonacci(m)

# Base Step: n modulo 20
seeds = []
for n0 in range(20):
Fn0 = (A^n0)[0,1]
if (Fn0 + Fm) % 5 == 0 and (n0 % 2) != (m % 2):
seeds.append((n0, A^n0))

# Lifting Step
for n_val, mat_n in seeds:
curr_period = 20
v = 1
j_level = 0

while curr_period < MAX_N_BOUND:
# Find current valuation v
while (mat_n[0,1] + Fm) % 5^(v+1) == 0:
v += 1
max_val = max(max_val, v)

# Lift to next level using precomputed M[j_level]
found = False
mod_next = 5^(v+1)
for k in range(5):
# mat_next = (A^(curr_period))^k * mat_curr
mat_cand = (M[j_level]^k) * mat_n
if (mat_cand[0,1] + Fm) % mod_next == 0:
n_val += k * curr_period
mat_n = mat_cand
curr_period *= 5
j_level += 1
found = True
break

if not found or n_val > MAX_N_BOUND: break

print(f"\nSearch complete. Maximum 5-adic valuation y = {max_val}")
