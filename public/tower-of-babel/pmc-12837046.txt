Methylmalonic acid modulates neutrophil function to promote tumor progression in colorectal cancer
Authors: Fang Hu, Xinyu Shi, Xinxiang Cheng, Huihui Yao, Xiuwei Mi, Baoan Wu, Pei Peng, Yang Lu, Jinmiao Chen, Songbing He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with its progression driven by metabolic reprogramming and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Methylmalonic acid (MMA), a metabolic byproduct accumulated in CRC, participates in modulating neutrophil function through mechanisms that remain poorly defined.MethodsWe integrated metabolomics, bioinformatics, and multi-omics analyses with in vitro and in vivo experiments. The effects of MMA on neutrophil polarization, proliferation, and invasion were assessed using HL-60-induced and human-derived neutrophils, along with colorectal cancer cell lines (HCT116 and HT29). Functional assays included ELISA, immunofluorescence, EdU proliferation, and invasion assays, while xenograft models assessed tumor growth. Multi-omics interrogation of TCGA, GEO, and CPTAC datasets identified MMA-associated genes and pathways, with hub gene analysis pinpointing key regulators. Plasma MMA levels were quantified in CRC patients via LC–MS/MS, and tumor CXCL8 expression was examined by immunofluorescence. Serum vitamin B12 levels were measured by chemiluminescence immunoassay.ResultsMMA induced dose-dependent N2 neutrophil polarization, evidenced by elevated IL-10, TGF-β, MPO secretion, and CD206 expression, alongside reduced iNOS. MMA-conditioned neutrophils enhanced CRC proliferation and invasion in vitro and promoted tumor growth in xenograft models. Multi-omics analysis revealed 50 MMA-associated differentially expressed genes enriched in SMAD signaling, fatty acid metabolism, and mitochondrial pathways, with CXCL8 emerging as a central hub. Mechanistically, MMA upregulated CXCL8 while suppressing p53 and activating TLR4, thereby driving N2 polarization; CXCL8 silencing abrogated these effects. Clinically, CRC patients exhibited elevated plasma MMA and tumor-specific CXCL8 overexpression, without significant alterations in vitamin B12 status.ConclusionsMMA functions as a metabolic driver of CRC progression, likely by promoting CXCL8-mediated N2 neutrophil polarization through the p53/TLR4 axis. Elevated plasma MMA and tumor CXCL8 expression from CRC patients highlight their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, providing important insights for clinical diagnosis and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15489-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, accounting for approximately 10% of all newly diagnosed cancers and cancer-associated deaths annually [1]. According to the 2020 GLOBOCAN statistics, colon cancer alone represents ~ 1.1 million new cases (6% of all cancers) and over 550,000 deaths each year, ranking it as the third leading cause of cancer mortality globally [2]. Despite advances in early diagnosis and therapeutic strategies, the treatment of CRC continues to face multiple challenges, including recurrence, metastasis, and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. There is an urgent need to better understand the interplay between metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation in CRC.

CRC progression is closely associated with profound metabolic reprogramming. Tumor cells exhibit enhanced glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and de novo lipogenesis to sustain rapid proliferation and meet elevated biosynthetic demands. These altered pathways not only fuel tumor growth but also generate metabolites that promote cancer—such as lactate and ketone bodies—that reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) into an immunosuppressive niche, facilitating immune evasion [3–5]. The immune system plays a pivotal role in cancer immunosurveillance and therapeutic responsiveness. Increasing evidence shows that both the abundance and spatial organization of tumor-infiltrating immune cells—including B and T lymphocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, tumor-associated macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells—hold prognostic and predictive value in CRC [6, 7]. Neutrophils, the most abundant immune cell type in the CRC TME, display functional plasticity between anti-tumorigenic (N1) and pro-tumorigenic (N2) phenotypes [8]. While N1 neutrophils exert cytotoxic effects against tumor cells, N2 neutrophils promote angiogenesis, immune suppression, and metastasis through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [9].

Recent studies have uncovered a complex and dynamic interplay between tumor metabolic reprogramming and the immune microenvironment in CRC [10, 11]. Our previous work demonstrated that a ketogenic diet (KD) suppresses CRC progression by modulating tumor-associated neutrophil polarization through the AMOT–YAP/TAZ axis [12]. Among the metabolites altered by KD, methylmalonic acid (MMA)—a mitochondrial byproduct generated during the catabolism of propionate, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), and cholesterol side chains—emerged as a candidate immunometabolic regulator [13]. MMA has been implicated as an oncogenic metabolite in multiple cancer types. Elevated serum MMA levels have been reported in CRC patients, where it may promote tumor progression by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and driving epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [14, 15]. In our metabolomics analysis, KD-fed CRC mice exhibited markedly reduced fecal MMA levels alongside diminished N2 neutrophil polarization, suggesting potential crosstalk between MMA and neutrophil function. However, its role in shaping the immune landscape of CRC, particularly neutrophil polarization, remains poorly understood.

This study aims to investigate the mechanism by which MMA regulates neutrophil reprogramming in CRC through multi-level experiments and systematic analysis. Using integrated multi-omics analyses and functional validation, we reveal that MMA promotes N2 neutrophil polarization and regulates the CXCL8–p53/TLR4 signaling axis, thereby enhancing tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastatic potential. Bioinformatics analysis, coupled with experimental evidence, identifies CXCL8 as a central molecular hub linking MMA-driven metabolic reprogramming to p53 pathway suppression and TLR4-mediated pro-tumorigenic signaling. Furthermore, we demonstrate that plasma MMA levels serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker and confirm tumor-specific CXCL8 overexpression in CRC. Collectively, our findings identify the MMA–CXCL8 axis as a metabolic checkpoint governing neutrophil polarization and highlight novel therapeutic opportunities for targeting metabolic–immune crosstalk in CRC.

Materials and methods

Clinical colorectal cancer (CRC) samples

Blood samples, tumor tissues, and adjacent normal tissues were collected from 10 patients with histologically confirmed colon adenocarcinoma who were admitted to the Hospital of Sihong. Additionally, blood samples were collected from ten healthy volunteers. All patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) histologically confirmed colon adenocarcinoma; (2) age between 18 and 75 years; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1; (4) presence of measurable lesions; (5) expected survival of at least 3 months; (6) adequate organ function; and (7) provision of written informed consent. Additionally, blood samples were collected from ten healthy volunteers. Blood samples were processed for MMA and vitamin B12 quantification, while tumor and adjacent normal tissues were used for CXCL8 expression analysis.

All procedures performed in study involving human participants were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Sihong in Jiangsu (Approve Number: 2023 − 0158). The informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Cell culture

Human acute promyelocytic leukemia cells HL-60, human primary bone marrow-derived neutrophils, and human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and HT-29 were purchased from the Cellverse Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HL-60 cells were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). Human-derived neutrophils were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS, Gibco, USA). HCT116 and HT-29 were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% PS. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37℃ in 5% CO2 and passaged at least every 3 days to maintain cells at 1 × 106 cells/mL.

HL-60 cell differentiation and MMA stimulation

For the differentiation process, HL-60 cells were differentiated to become neutrophils by IMDM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 1.3% Hybri-max DMSO, and 100 ng/mL carrier-free Granulocyte Colony Stimulating Factor (Biolegend, USA) [16]. Then, HL-60-derived neutrophils were treated with 0, 50, 100 and 200 µM of MMA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h, respectively.

siRNA-Mediated gene Silencing and transfection

For transfection, HCT116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 10⁵ cells/well) 24 h prior to reaching 70–80% confluency. CXCL8 siRNA and Scrambled siRNA (as the control) purchased from Santa Cruz (sc-69241, USA) was transfected into cells with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection efficiency was validated at 48 h post-transfection through qPCR and Western blot.

siRNA sequences: Scrambled siRNA: 5′-AAUUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3′, CXCL8 siRNA-1: 5′-GGAAGAAACCACCGGAAGAUU-3′, CXCL8 siRNA-2: 5’-CCAGACAGAGUCACAGAAAdTdT-3’, CXCL8 siRNA-3: 5’-GUAAGAGAGUGUGUGAAAUdTdT-3’.

Co-culture system and cell invasion assay

For the co-cultured system, 1 × 105 HCT116/HT29 cells resuspended in 200 µL basal medium and seeded into the 24‐well chamber insert (8 μm, Corning, USA). Next, 2.5 × 105 DMSO-induced neutrophils were pretreated with MMA (100 µM), washed with PBS, and then seeded into the lower chamber of a 24-well plate. Then 800 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber.

For the cell invasion assay, Transwell inserts (Corning, USA) were pre-coated with 50 µL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel matrix (1:8 dilution in serum-free medium; BD Biosciences, USA) and allowed to polymerize for 30 min at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁵ cells suspended in 200 µL of serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) were seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with 800 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. Following 48 h incubation, non-invaded cells on the upper membrane surface were removed with cotton swabs and the transmembrane cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 1% crystalline violet (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 20 min, and visualized under a light microscope using the Image J analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The cell culture supernatant was collected, and the secretion levels of TGF-β1, IL-10, and MPO in each group were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Cat. No.: E-EL-0162, E-EL-M0046, E-UNEL-H0048). OD values were measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. The sample concentration was determined using a standard curve derived from known concentrations. All samples were assayed in triplicate with three independent experimental replicates, and blank controls were included in each assay.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cell slides or tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature (RT), followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% BSA (Gibco, USA) for 1 h at RT. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with primary antibody against CD16b, iNOS, CXCL8 and CD206 (Servicebio, China, 1:200 dilution). After three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco, USA), fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488/594 (Servicebio, China, 1:500 dilution) were applied and incubated for 1 h at RT under light-protected conditions. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Servicebio, China, 1 µg/mL) for 5 min prior to mounting with antifade reagent. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope, with 5 random fields captured per sample. The experiment included negative controls and was independently repeated three times.

EdU proliferation assay

Following the aforementioned co-culture system, N2-polarized neutrophils induced by MMA were co-cultured with HCT116/HT29 cells. Upon reaching 90% confluency, the cells were transfected. The transfected cells were processed for EdU staining following the manufacturer’s instructions of the EdU Kit (Servicebio, China). Cells were labeled with EdU (final concentration 10 µM) for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized, and incubated with Click reaction buffer (Servicebio, China). After counterstaining with Hoechst 33,342 solution (Servicebio, China), samples were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope, imaged and analyzed by cell counting. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software. The experiment included negative controls and was independently repeated three times.

Cell-derived xenograft (CDX) mouse model

Six- to eight-week-old BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). Mice were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (5 mice per group): HCT116, HCT116 + MMA, HCT116 + HL60, and HCT116 + HL60 + MMA group. The HCT116 CDX model was generated by subcutaneously injecting 1 × 106 logarithmically growing cells (in 100 µL PBS) into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice under aseptic conditions. Prior to inoculation, cell viability was confirmed to be ≥ 95% by Trypan Blue (Servicebio, China) exclusion. On day 7 post-modeling establishment, mice in the HCT116 + MMA, HCT116 + HL60, and HCT116 + HL60 + MMA groups received tail vein injections of MMA (100µM, 0.1 mL/10 g body weight), neutrophils (1 × 106 cells), or N2-polarized neutrophils induced by MMA (1 × 106 cells), respectively. Mice in the HCT116 group were injected with an equal volume of saline as the control. Tumor length, width, and height were measured every 2 days using a digital caliper, with volume calculated as 0.52 × length × width × height. Mice were euthanized and tumors were extracted when tumor volume reached 1000 mm3 (cubic millimeter). In brief, anesthesia was induced using an induction chamber containing 5% isoflurane and maintained at 2–3% via a nose cone to ensure complete loss of consciousness in the animals. Anesthetic depth was verified by the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex and corneal reflex. Euthanasia was then performed by cardiac exsanguination to ensure a humane endpoint.

The animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Sihong in Jiangsu (Approve Number: 2023127).

Bioinformatics and Multi-Omics analyses

To comprehensively investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying our experimental findings, a multi-layered bioinformatics approach was employed, integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and publicly available cancer database analyses. We began by retrieving RNA-seq expression profiles from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://www.cancer.gov/tcga) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) repositories to compare target gene expression patterns between tumor and normal tissues across relevant cancer subtypes. Quality assessment of raw sequencing reads was performed using FastQC. The quality-controlled reads were then aligned to the reference genome using STAR, and the gene count matrix was generated by featureCounts.

The MMA‑associated gene set was obtained from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). Using “methylmalonic acidemia” as the search term, we extracted all genes annotated with “Relevance score” ≥ 10 (as defined by GeneCards).

Differential expression analysis was performed using the “limma” and “edgeR” packages in R. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using stringent statistical thresholds (|log₂FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05). Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize expression landscapes, while principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to assess sample clustering and global transcriptomic variance. Functional enrichment of DEGs was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses via the “clusterProfiler” package, enabling the identification of key biological processes, molecular functions, and signaling pathways potentially regulated by the gene of interest.

To explore protein-level alterations, we integrated proteomic data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) and correlated it with transcriptomic patterns to identify consistent molecular changes across omics layers. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org), followed by hub gene identification through Cytoscape’s CytoHubba plugin, which allowed us to pinpoint central nodes potentially driving tumor progression.

Survival and prognostic relevance were evaluated through Kaplan–Meier analysis and univariate/multivariate Cox regression using TCGA clinical datasets (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga). Additionally, immune infiltration patterns were profiled with the CIBERSORT and TIMER algorithms, assessing the relationship between gene expression and the abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and somatic mutation landscapes were examined using “maftools” to investigate potential associations with genomic instability and immunogenicity.

Multi-omics integration was performed by overlaying transcriptomic, proteomic, and immunogenomic findings, revealing convergent molecular signatures and candidate regulatory networks. This holistic strategy provided a systems-level understanding of the biological functions and clinical significance of the target molecule, guiding further mechanistic exploration and therapeutic hypothesis generation.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse transcribed to cDNA using SweScript RT II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Servicebio, China). qPCR was performed using ArtiCanATM SYBR qPCR Mix (Servicebio, China) on a QuantReady K9600 real-time PCR system (Hangzhou Suizhen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). The gene expression levels were normalized by using β-actin as an internal reference and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Primer sequences: CXCL8-F: ACTGAGAGTGATTGAGAGTGGAC, CXCL8-R: AACCCTCTGCACCCAGTTTTC; actin-F: ACCCAGATCATGTTTGAGACC, actin-R: GAGGGCATACCCCTCGTAGA.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were collected from cells using RIPA lysate containing PMSF (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Protein samples were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Servicebio, China). The membranes were closed in 5% defatted milk for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies p-p53, p53, CXCR1, TLR4, and ACTIN (GenuIN Biotech, China, Cat. No.: 3085, 71136, 51021, 50693 and 2855) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody’s goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Servicebio, China, Cat. No.: GB23303) for 1 h. Finally, the indicated proteins were visualized using Ultra-Sensitive ECL Chemiluminescence Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, Cat. No.: P0018AS) and imaged with a Multifunctional Ultra-sensitive Imaging System (SHST, China, SH-Advance523).

LC-MS/MS quantification of plasma MMA

MMA concentrations were determined by LC-MS/MS with deuterated internal standard (MMA-d3). Briefly, 50 µL plasma proteins were precipitated with 150 µL ice-cold methanol containing 10 ng/mL MMA-d3, followed by derivatization with 3-nitrophenylhydrazine (10 mM in 50% acetonitrile/0.1% HCl) (Servicebio, China) at 40℃ for 30 min. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) using a gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile at 0.3 mL/min flow rate. The mass spectrometer operated in negative electrospray ionization mode with multiple reaction monitoring of m/z 117→73 for MMA and m/z 120→76 for MMA-d3 (collision energy: 12 eV). The method was validated over the 0.1–50 µmol/L range (R2 > 0.99) with limit of quantification at 0.1 µM. Quality control samples demonstrated intra-day and inter-day coefficients of variation < 5% and < 8%, respectively, with recoveries of 92–105%.

Serum vitamin B12 measurement

Serum vitamin B12 levels were quantified using chemiluminescence immunoassay. The specific procedures were as follows: Fasting venous blood samples were collected, and serum was separated by centrifugation. Measurements were performed using a fully automated chemiluminescence immunoanalyzer with matched reagent kits. The experiment strictly adhered to standardized protocols: a standard curve was initially established, followed by co-incubation of samples with vitamin B12-specific antibodies labeled with chemiluminescent markers. Quantification was achieved by measuring the intensity of the luminescent signals. All samples were assayed in duplicate, and quality controls were included to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and SPSS 21. Each experiment was repeated at least 3 times and the data is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, an independent samples t-test was employed. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MMA promotes neutrophil polarization toward the Pro-tumorigenic N2 phenotype

To determine whether methylmalonic acid (MMA) modulates neutrophil phenotype, HL-60–derived neutrophils were treated with increasing MMA concentrations (0–200 µM). ELISA analysis revealed a concentration-dependent elevation in IL-10, TGF-β, and MPO secretion (Fig. 1A), indicating enhanced immunosuppressive activity.

N1‑polarized neutrophils represent a pro-inflammatory phenotype, whereas N2‑polarized neutrophils exhibit an anti‑inflammatory or pro‑repair phenotype. Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated a progressive reduction in inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS; an N1 neutrophil marker) with increasing MMA doses (Fig. 1B), whereas CD206 expression (an N2 neutrophil marker) increased correspondingly (Fig. 1C). Notably, colocalization analysis revealed stronger yellow merge signals between CD206⁺ and CD16b⁺ cells, indicating that MMA treatment enriches for N2-polarized neutrophils. Moreover, human bone marrow-derived neutrophils exhibited consistent results (Supplementary Fig. 1). Collectively, these findings suggest that MMA simultaneously suppresses anti-tumorigenic N1 polarization and promotes the acquisition of a pro-tumorigenic N2 phenotype.

MMA-Induced N2 neutrophils enhance CRC cell proliferation and invasion in vitro

To assess the functional consequences of MMA-induced N2 polarization, CRC cell lines (HT29 and HCT116) were co-cultured with MMA-treated HL-60 neutrophils. EdU incorporation assays showed significantly increased proliferation in the MMA + HL-60 co-culture group compared to untreated controls (Fig. 2A–B). Similarly, Matrigel invasion assays revealed a marked increase in the number of invading CRC cells in the presence of MMA-treated neutrophils (Fig. 2C–F). Furthermore, when co-cultured with MMA-treated human bone marrow-derived neutrophils, CRC cells also exhibited a significant enhancement in invasive capacity (Supplementary Fig. 2). These data indicate that MMA-driven N2 polarization confers a pro-tumorigenic phenotype on neutrophils, which in turn stimulates CRC cell proliferation and invasiveness.

MMA-Induced neutrophils accelerate tumor growth in vivo

In HCT116 xenograft models, mice were injected with either MMA, DMSO-treated neutrophils, or MMA-induced N2 neutrophils. Control tumors maintained an ellipsoid morphology with smooth surfaces and uniform gray-white parenchyma. In contrast, MMA-treated tumors exhibited significantly larger volumes (p < 0.05), pronounced surface neovascularization, and multifocal intratumoral hemorrhage (Fig. 3A, C).

Transcriptomic profiling identifies MMA-Associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Limma differential analysis method in R was used to analyze DEGs. DEGs were filtered using the following criteria: upregulated DEGs were selected with a log fold change (FC) > 2 and P < 0.05, while downregulated DEGs were identified with FC < 0.5 and P < 0.05. Through analysis of TCGA data, we identified 2,623 downregulated and 963 upregulated genes (Fig. 4A), while GSE9348 dataset revealed 1,544 downregulated and 1,375 upregulated genes (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, 1,009 MMA-associated genes were obtained from the GeneCards database. Intersection of these DEGs with MMA-associated genes yielded 30 upregulated and 20 downregulated MMA-related genes exhibiting differential expression in colorectal cancer (Fig. 4C, D).

Functional enrichment highlights SMAD signaling and metabolic pathways

Functional enrichment analysis of the 50 MMA-associated DEGs revealed a strong convergence on signaling and metabolic processes. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis in the Biological Process category pointed to a pronounced involvement in SMAD protein signal transduction (Fig. 5A), suggesting that perturbations in TGF-β/SMAD-mediated regulatory cascades may be a key feature of MMA pathology. This signaling axis is known to orchestrate cell growth, differentiation, and extracellular matrix remodeling, indicating its potential relevance in disease progression. In parallel, enrichment in fatty acid biosynthesis hinted at a metabolic dimension to the molecular alterations (Fig. 5A), linking MMA-associated genes to lipid homeostasis and mitochondrial energy metabolism. From the Molecular Function perspective, the overrepresentation of terms such as identical protein binding and protein homodimerization suggests that protein–protein interaction networks, particularly those involving homodimeric transcription factors and signaling proteins, may underpin the observed biological effects (Fig. 5B). The Cellular Component analysis localized many of these genes to the mitochondrial inner membrane and other mitochondrial compartments (Fig. 5C), reinforcing the notion that mitochondrial function may be central to the pathophysiology.

KEGG pathway analysis provided further context, highlighting metabolic pathways and the TGF-β signaling pathway as dominant functional clusters (Fig. 5D). The convergence of these pathways underscores a potential interplay between metabolic reprogramming and SMAD-dependent signaling in MMA, offering a mechanistic framework that bridges mitochondrial activity, lipid biosynthesis, and growth factor-mediated transcriptional regulation.

PPI network reveals eight key hub genes

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed from the 50 MMA-associated DEGs using STRING and visualized in Cytoscape (confidence score > 0.4) (Fig. 6A). Topological analysis via cytoHubba identified eight hub genes — ACLY, ACO2, CXCL8, G6PD, GPX3, HSP90AB1, MYC, and SHMT2 — with high connectivity (Fig. 6B–E), indicating central regulatory roles in CRC metabolic and immune pathways.

CRC patients exhibit elevated plasma MMA and Tumor-Specific CXCL8 expression

Quantitative LC–MS/MS analysis revealed a clear metabolic distinction between CRC patients and healthy individuals (Fig. 10A). Plasma levels of MMA were markedly elevated in the CRC cohort, averaging 671.76 ± 561.93 µg/L (approximately 5.80 ± 4.85 µM), compared with 354.12 ± 55.31 µg/L (approximately 3.06 ± 0.48 µM) in healthy controls (p = 0.019, Table 1). We also measured serum vitamin B12 levels and found no statistically significant differences, indicating that the elevated MMA levels were not attributable to vitamin B12 deficiency (Fig. 10B). Notably, there was considerable inter-patient variability within the CRC group, suggesting possible heterogeneity in tumor metabolism or patient-specific metabolic states.

In parallel, immunofluorescence staining of paired tumor and adjacent normal mucosal tissues uncovered a striking difference in CXCL8 localization. Tumor regions displayed intense and widespread CXCL8 fluorescence signals, whereas adjacent non-tumor mucosa exhibited only faint or negligible staining (Fig. 10C-D). This spatially restricted expression pattern underscores CXCL8’s potential as a tumor-specific biomarker, linking it to localized inflammatory signaling within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, the survival curve demonstrated that high CXCL8 expression was associated with poorer prognosis, further supporting the significant clinical prognostic value of CXCL8 (p = 0.0032, Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these findings connect elevated systemic MMA levels with intratumoral chemokine activity, hinting at a metabolic–inflammatory axis that may hold diagnostic and therapeutic significance in CRC.

Discussion

MMA is a key intermediate in the propionate metabolic pathway [17]. Under normal physiological conditions, circulating MMA remains at low levels. Pathological accumulation of MMA can result from vitamin B12 deficiency, genetic defects in propionate pathway enzymes, alterations in gut microbiota, and metabolic reprogramming in aggressive cancers [18]. Our previous metabolomic analysis revealed a positive correlation between MMA levels and neutrophil N2 polarization in CRC-bearing mice, although the underlying mechanism remained unclear.

This study demonstrates that MMA exerts a clear pro-tumorigenic effect by driving neutrophil polarization toward an N2 phenotype. In vitro, MMA induced a concentration-dependent increase in IL-10, TGF-β, and MPO secretion, downregulation of the N1 marker iNOS, and elevated CD206 expression with CD206/CD16b co-localization. Functionally, MMA-conditioned N2 neutrophils enhanced CRC cell proliferation and invasion. In vivo, MMA-primed neutrophils accelerated tumor growth, accompanied by increased angiogenesis and necrotic core formation. This reflects the complex regulatory mechanisms involved in cancer development, whereby metabolic reprogramming may enhance inflammatory responses and tumor cell aggressiveness by modulating the immune microenvironment and immune cell activity, ultimately promoting tumor growth [19–21]. Together, these data establish a “metabolite–immune cell–tumor” axis in which MMA shapes an immunosuppressive niche that favors CRC progression through immune cell metabolic reprogramming, tumor-permissive inflammation, and direct support of malignant cell growth.

Multi-omics analysis provided a systems-level view of MMA’s influence. Integration of TCGA and GSE9348 datasets identified 50 MMA-associated DEGs enriched in SMAD signaling, fatty acid biosynthesis, and mitochondrial processes. KEGG pathways highlighted metabolic and TGF-β signaling clusters, suggesting coordinated effects on tumor metabolism and immune regulation. Network analysis pinpointed seven hub genes—ACLY, CXCL8, G6PD, GPX3, HSP90AB1, MYC, and SHMT2—spanning metabolic regulation (ACLY, G6PD, SHMT2) [22–24], inflammatory signaling (CXCL8) [25], and transcriptional/epigenetic control (MYC, HSP90AB1) [26, 27]. TIMER 2.0 correlations linked CXCL8 to neutrophil infiltration and GPX3 to macrophage infiltration, consistent with MMA fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Among these, CXCL8 emerged as a pivotal mediator connecting MMA accumulation to neutrophil polarization. GSEA indicated enrichment in NOD-like receptor, p53, Toll-like receptor, and chemokine signaling pathways. Prior work showed that p53 suppression promotes tumor survival [28] and that TLR4/NF-κB–CXCL8/CXCR1 signaling drives inflammatory metastasis [29, 30]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that inhibition of p53 activity promotes chemoresistance and tumor growth in colorectal cancer, whereas activation of p53 signaling induces tumor cell apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [31, 32]. Therefore, we hypothesized that MMA influence the progression of colorectal cancer by regulating p53 and TLR4-CXCL8 signaling pathways.

Mechanistic assays confirmed that MMA upregulated CXCL8 expression while suppressing p53 signaling (decreased p53 and phospho-p53 levels). Silencing CXCL8 partially restored p53 activation and reduced N2 polarization, identifying CXCL8 as a molecular switch that links MMA-driven metabolic signaling to suppression of the p53 tumor suppressor pathway. The established role of oncogenic regulators in tumors is known to function through ubiquitin-dependent stabilization [33]. Ubiquitination-mediated degradation effectively promotes CRC progression and enhances chemotherapy resistance by disrupting p53 protein homeostasis [31], which may represent a potential mechanism underlying MMA-mediated regulation of CRC. We also observed MMA-induced hyperactivation of CXCR1 and TLR4, both implicated in N2 polarization. TLR4 deficiency induces phenotypic reprogramming of neutrophils toward a protective ischemia-associated phenotype and reduces their generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during inflammatory processes [34]. In contrast, IL-8 signaling promotes neutrophil recruitment, chronic inflammation, angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and immune escape through the CXCR1 and CXCR2 receptors [35]. CXCL8 knockdown reduced this activation, reinforcing the central role of CXCL8-driven chemokine signaling in MMA-mediated immune reprogramming [36]. The incomplete reversal of p53 suppression and N2 polarization after CXCL8 silencing suggests additional MMA-responsive pathways contribute to CRC progression. These findings suggest a dual oncogenic mechanism: (i) MMA promotes immune evasion via the CXCL8–CXCR1/TLR4 axis, and (ii) MMA attenuates p53-mediated tumor surveillance, enabling unchecked tumor growth.

Clinical validation supports the translational relevance. Plasma MMA levels were significantly higher in CRC patients than in healthy controls, with substantial inter-individual variation that may reflect disease stage, tumor burden, or therapy response. In contrast, serum B12 levels showed no significant difference, ruling out vitamin B12 deficiency as a contributing factor to MMA elevation. Additionally, CXCL8 was specifically overexpressed in tumor tissues, and survival analysis revealed that high CXCL8 expression was associated with poorer prognosis, further highlighting its biomarker and a target for immunotherapy [37]. These findings suggest that MMA accumulation in the tumor microenvironment stimulates CXCL8 production, thereby fostering a pro-tumor inflammatory microenvironment.

This study has several limitations. First, this study primarily focuses on the regulatory role of MMA in HL-60-derived neutrophil polarization and has not yet thoroughly investigated the effects of MMA on tumor cells or other immune cells (such as macrophages and T cells) and their crosstalk with neutrophils. This limitation restricts a comprehensive understanding of the MMA-mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment. Second, our characterization of neutrophil phenotypes was conducted exclusively in vitro and not in vivo, resulting in a lack of validation of their baseline anti-tumor functions. Moreover, whether CXCL8 directly interacts with the upstream TLR4 in CRC remains unclear and requires further investigation. Additionally, this study is limited by a small sample size of CRC patients and may be influenced by population‑specific susceptibility to colorectal cancer, which can be attributed to genetic factors, environmental exposures, and other variables [38, 39]. Notably, substantial inter‑individual variability was observed in patients’ MMA levels, precluding the establishment of a quantitative correlation between MMA concentration and clinical staging. As these conclusions are derived from CRC models, the generalizability of the MMA-CXCL8 axis to other cancer types (such as gastric or pancreatic cancer) requires further validation. Finally, although targeted therapeutic strategies against the CXCL8/CXCR1 axis show potential in mechanistic studies, their in vivo specificity and off-target effects require systematic evaluation through preclinical pharmacological research. Future interventional strategies targeting this pathway (such as CXCL8/CXCR1 axis inhibition combined with metabolic modulation) may break through current bottlenecks in CRC treatment [40].

In summary, the data from this study suggest that MMA may influence neutrophil phenotype through the correlated modulation of molecules such as CXCL8, TLR4, and p53. Based on this, we propose a working model: MMA may promote the polarization of neutrophils toward an anti-inflammatory/repair phenotype by upregulating CXCL8, activating TLR4, and inhibiting p53, collectively forming a regulatory axis. This model requires further experimental validation to establish its causality and temporal relationships. Additionally, these insights suggest two translational avenues: plasma MMA measurement as a non-invasive biomarker for CRC risk stratification, and therapeutic targeting of the CXCL8/CXCR1 axis—potentially with agents such as reparixin—as part of a metabolic–immune combination strategy. This integrated perspective systematically linking metabolism, immunity, and oncogenic pathways provides crucial theoretical support for the development of targeted therapies and construction of prognostic evaluation systems in CRC.
