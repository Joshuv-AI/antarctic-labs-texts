NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase mediates P450-dependent metabolic resistance to deltamethrin in Aedes albopictus
Authors: Hui-Juan Yang, Jun-Yu Fu, Zhi-Wen Yi, Chen-Yu Han, Si-Yu Zhao, Xiang-Feng Zhu, Han-Ren Liu, Xiao-Guang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cytochrome P450 monooxygenase system is a key detoxification pathway involved in insecticide metabolism in insects and plays a central role in the evolution of insecticide resistance. NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR), a crucial electron transfer partner in cytochrome P450-mediated detoxification of xenobiotics, is an essential component of this system. However, its role in insecticide resistance in Aedes albopictus remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, two CPR gene isoforms in Aedes albopictus, AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2, were cloned and characterized. qPCR was used to determine their expression levels and spatiotemporal expression patterns in laboratory-resistant strains. The effects of deltamethrin exposure on AalCPR expression were then examined. RNA interference (RNAi) was employed to silence the CPR gene, and the susceptibility of Aedes albopictus to deltamethrin following gene knockdown was evaluated. In addition, AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were heterologously expressed in Sf9 cells to assess cytochrome c reductase activity, and cytotoxicity assays were performed to compare the survival of cells overexpressing the two isoforms.ResultsSequence analysis showed that AalCPR-X1 contained all conserved domains of CPR, whereas AalCPR-X2 lacked the N-terminal membrane anchor and had a shortened FMN-binding domain. qPCR analysis indicated that both isoforms were significantly overexpressed in resistant strains. Spatiotemporal expression profiling revealed that AalCPR-X1 was highly expressed in third- and fourth-instar larvae, adults, and detoxification tissues, whereas AalCPR-X2 was predominantly expressed in adults and malpighian tubules. In addition, deltamethrin exposure significantly upregulated AalCPR-X1 mRNA levels in resistant strains. RNAi-mediated silencing of the CPR gene significantly increased the susceptibility of Aedes albopictus to deltamethrin. Heterologous expression assays confirmed that both AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 exhibited cytochrome c reductase activity, and cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that the survival rate of cells overexpressing AalCPR-X1 was significantly higher than that of cells overexpressing AalCPR-X2.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that AalCPR plays an important role in P450-mediated metabolic resistance in Aedes albopictus and suggests that CPR may serve as a potential candidate marker for insecticide resistance management strategies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13071-026-07593-w.

Introduction

Aedes albopictus (Ae. albopictus) is a highly invasive vector mosquito capable of transmitting multiple arboviruses, such as the dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever, and Zika viruses, through blood feeding [1–4]. It has a broad host range encompassing humans, other mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians [5]. Pyrethroid insecticides have been widely used globally for mosquito control owing to their high efficacy and low toxicity [6, 7]. However, long-term and extensive application has inevitably driven the continuous evolution of insecticide resistance in mosquito populations, leading to reduced control efficacy and posing increasingly severe challenges to the prevention and control of mosquito-borne diseases [8–11].

Insecticide resistance is mainly mediated by two mechanisms: target-site resistance and metabolic resistance [8, 9, 12]. Studies by Ishak et al. and Guo et al. have shown that metabolic resistance serves as the dominant mechanism in populations lacking target-site resistance [9, 13]. Metabolic resistance primarily involves three major detoxifying enzyme families: cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (P450s), glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and carboxylesterases (CCEs), which can detoxify or sequester insecticides before they reach their target sites [14]. Among these, P450s constitute the most important metabolic system in insects. P450s, a heme-containing superfamily of enzymes, are closely associated with insecticide resistance in diverse insect species through their overexpression [15–18]. For instance, overexpression of CYP6M2, CYP6Z3, and CYP6P3 is linked to pyrethroid metabolic resistance in Anopheles gambiae [19]. CYP6FV12 in Bradysia odoriphaga plays a crucial role in mediating imidacloprid resistance [20]. However, the detoxification function of P450s critically depends on the synergistic action of NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR), and these two components jointly catalyze the detoxification of xenobiotics [21, 22]. Thus, CPR may act as a key rate-limiting factor regulating metabolic resistance and may play a critical role in insecticide detoxification.

CPR is a key flavoprotein present in all eukaryotes, containing several major conserved domains, including flavin mononucleotide (FMN), flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) binding domains. It provides electrons for P450-catalyzed reactions through a multistep redox process [23]. Additionally, CPR can supply electrons for the catalytic reactions of various other oxidases [24, 25]. To date, the CPR gene has been identified and characterized in a wide range of insect species, including vector insects such as Anopheles gambiae [26] and Anopheles minimus [27], agricultural pests such as Helicoverpa armigera [28] and Nilaparvata lugens [29], as well as model insects like Drosophila melanogaster [30]. Extensive studies have been conducted on the mechanisms of insecticide resistance involving CPR in these insects. Despite this, the specific role of Ae. albopictus CPR (AalCPR) in insecticide resistance remains largely unclear.

Although the AalCPR gene has been annotated in the genome [31], its specific function in insecticide resistance has not been experimentally validated. In this study, we cloned and characterized two AalCPR isoforms (AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2), and systematically analyzed their molecular characteristics, spatiotemporal expression profiles and induction patterns upon insecticide exposure. Furthermore, RNA interference (RNAi), heterologous expression and cytotoxicity assays were applied to elucidate the role of AalCPR in insecticide detoxification metabolism.

Materials and methods

Mosquito strains and breeding

SH-S: A laboratory-susceptible strain of Ae. albopictus provided by the Shanghai Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention, maintained in the laboratory without exposure to any insecticides.

Four deltamethrin-resistant Ae. albopictus strains, namely R30, R30-M, R30-1532T and R30-1534S, were previously established in our laboratory [9]. The R30 strain was generated through successive selection with deltamethrin for 30 generations from the susceptible SH-S strain as the parental line, with an adult mortality rate of 68.6%. Three sub-strains with distinct resistance mechanisms were subsequently derived from the R30 population: R30-M, which exhibits metabolic resistance only (adult mortality = 38.6%); R30-1532T, which carries homozygous mutations at the 1532 site of the voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) gene in combination with metabolic resistance (adult mortality = 40.0%); and R30-1534S, which carries homozygous mutations at the 1534 site of the VGSC gene in combination with metabolic resistance (adult mortality = 36.2%) [9]. The four resistant strains were then reared for 13 consecutive generations without insecticide pressure to establish DER30, DER30-M, DER30-1532T, and DER30-1534S, respectively. Adult bioassays and CDC bottle bioassays confirmed that P450-mediated metabolic resistance was present in all strains (Additional file 1).

GD-SZ, GD-SG, and GD-GZ: Field-collected Ae. albopictus strains from Shenzhen, Shaoguan, and Guangzhou in Guangdong Province, respectively, provided by the Guangdong Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention.

All mosquito strains were reared in a controlled environment at 27 ± 1 °C, 70 ± 10% relative humidity (RH), and a 12:12 h light:dark cycle. Larvae were fed with turtle food. Adults were maintained in 20 cm × 20 cm × 35 cm cages and supplied with 10% glucose solution. Three to seven days prior to oviposition, female mosquitoes were blood-fed using defibrinated sheep blood via a Hemotek membrane feeding system.

Adult bioassay

Adult insecticide resistance bioassays were performed following the WHO tube bioassay protocol [32, 33] using 0.05% deltamethrin-impregnated filter papers (Universiti Sains Malaysia, Penang, Malaysia). For each bioassay, 25 non-blood-fed female mosquitoes aged 3–5 days were used per tube, with five biological replicates and two control replicates for each group. After 1 h of exposure, mosquitoes were transferred to clean tubes and provided with 10% glucose solution. Mortality was recorded 24 h post-exposure. Resistance status was classified based on mortality according to standard criteria: resistant (< 90% mortality), possible resistance (90–98% mortality), and susceptible (> 98% mortality).

CDC bottle bioassay and effect of synergists

The CDC bottle bioassay was employed to assess the involvement of metabolic enzymes in insecticide resistance [34]. Adult mosquitoes were pre-exposed to the synergist piperonyl butoxide (PBO, 400 μg per bottle) following the standard CDC protocol to evaluate the role of cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (P450s) in resistance [34]. Detailed procedures have been described previously by Guo et al. [8, 9].

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, AalCPR cloning and qPCR analysis.

Total RNA was extracted from the following mosquito samples: (1) tissues (thorax, head, fat body, midgut, malpighian tubules) and developmental stages (eggs, L1–L2, L3–L4, pupae, adults) from four resistant strains (DER30, DER30-M, DER30-1532 T, DER30-1534S); tissue expression was normalized to thorax, and developmental expression normalized to eggs within each strain; (2) mosquitoes surviving at 12, 24, 48, 72 h after exposure to 0.05% deltamethrin-impregnated papers, with acetone-treated papers used as controls; (3) mosquitoes collected at 24, 48, 72 h after dsCPR injection, with dsGFP injection as the control; and (4) adult mosquitoes from the susceptible strain SH-S and three field strains (GD-SZ, GD-SG, GD-GZ).

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the ABScript Neo RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover (Abclonal, Hubei, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene-specific primers targeting AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were designed with Primer5 based on the Ae. albopictus genome database. PCR was performed using Q5® Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. PCR products were purified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, cloned into the pEASY®-Blunt Cloning Vector (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The inserted sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.​

RT-qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using cDNA as template. All reactions were carried out in a final volume of 20 μL. The thermal cycling program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 40 s. Relative expression levels were analyzed using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method with rpS7 (ribosomal protein 7) as the internal reference gene. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates and four technical replicates. Primer sequences and amplification efficiencies are listed in Tables S1 and S3, respectively.

Sequence analysis of AalCPR

The isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (MW) of amino acid sequence were predicted using ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Signal peptides and transmembrane domains were predicted using SignalP 6.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-6.0/) and TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/), respectively. Protein domain analysis was performed using InterProScan 4 tool provided by EMBL-EBI (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/about/interproscan/) to search the protein signature databases integrated in InterPro. Amino acid sequences of CPR from different insect species used for phylogenetic analysis were obtained from the GenBank database (Table S2). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA X [35], with 1, 000 bootstrap replicates to assess tree robustness.

RNAi-mediated silencing of AalCPR and bioassays

The green fluorescent protein (GFP) and AalCPR genes were amplified using primers containing the T7 RNA polymerase promoter (Table S1), and the gel-purified PCR products were used as templates for dsRNA synthesis with the T7 RiboMAX™ Express RNAi System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Adult mosquitoes were cold-anesthetized and injected into the thorax under a stereomicroscope. Approximately 0.5 μL of dsRNA was injected per mosquito using a glass microcapillary needle following our previously established laboratory protocol [4]. Mosquitoes injected with dsGFP served as negative controls. All injected adult mosquitoes were housed individually in paper cups and provided with 10% glucose solution. Fifty female mosquitoes were injected per group, with three biological replicates and technical replicates for both treatment and control groups.

Detection of P450 activity after AalCPR knockdown

Mosquitoes were harvested at the time point with maximal gene silencing efficiency for P450 activity assays to evaluate the effect of dsAalCPR on P450 activity. Samples were homogenized in 0.1 M phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was collected as crude enzyme solution. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. P450 activity was determined according to the method described by Adesanya et al. [36–38]. Each reaction well contained 50 μL enzyme solution, 0.8 mM 7-ethoxycoumarin, and 0.1 mM NADPH, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 30 min. Spontaneous fluorescence of NADPH was quenched by adding oxidized glutathione and glutathione reductase to the reaction system. Phosphate homogenization buffer was used instead of enzyme extract in the control group. The fluorescence intensity of produced 7-hydroxycoumarin was detected with an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and an emission wavelength of 465 nm. A standard curve of 7-hydroxycoumarin was generated according to Adesanya et al. [36–38], which was used to quantify product formation. P450 activities were expressed as pmol of 7-OH/min/mg protein.

Heterologous expression of AalCPR and western blot analysis

The full-length open reading frame (ORF) sequences of AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were subcloned into the pFastBac HTA vector. Recombinant baculoviruses were generated using the Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus expression system and transfected into Sf9 cells. At 72 h post-infection, cells were collected via centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min and resuspended in PBS (pH 7.8). The cell suspension was subjected to sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the recombinant protein sample for measuring CPR enzyme activity. An empty vector was used as the control and expressed using the same procedure. SDS-PAGE was performed using an Omni-Easy™ One-Step PAGE Gel Fast Preparation Kit (10%) (Epizyme, Shanghai, China), and the separated proteins were subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking at room temperature, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with a His-tag primary antibody (1:5000; Mouse anti His-Tag mAb, Abclonal, Hubei, China). Incubation was then performed with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000; HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG, Abclonal, Hubei, China). Finally, the membrane was incubated with BeyoECL Star Ultra-Sensitive ECL Chemiluminescence Kit and imaged using a Bio-Rad imaging system.

CPR activity measurement and cytotoxicity assays

According to previous studies [39, 40], CPR activity was determined by measuring NADPH-cytochrome c reductase activity. Briefly, the reaction mixture contained 25 μL protein sample, 20 μL cytochrome c solution, 20 μL NADPH, and 135 μL potassium phosphate buffer. The absorbance at 550 nm was monitored for 5 min. Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. First, 90 μL of infected cells was seeded into 96-well plates and cultured at 28 °C for 24 h. Next, 10 μL deltamethrin at serial concentrations (1, 5, 10, 20, 40 mg/L) was added to each well and incubated for 24 h. Finally, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 4 h before measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21 (IBM SPSS Statistics). The normality of all data was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.05). Adult mortality was analyzed using the chi-square test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among three or more groups were carried out via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sequence identification and molecular evolutionary analysis of AalCPR

Two isoforms of the CPR gene (LOC109424773) from Ae. albopictus, designated AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2, were cloned (Additional file 2). Their predicted molecular weights and isoelectric points (pI) were 77.47 kDa/5.37 and 67.41 kDa/7.35, respectively. No signal peptides were identified in either AalCPR-X1 or AalCPR-X2. AalCPR-X1 contains a typical N-terminal membrane-anchoring domain, whereas this domain is absent in AalCPR-X2 (Fig. 1). Both isoforms contain three conserved binding domains for FAD, FMN and NADPH; however, the FMN-binding domain of AalCPR-X2 is truncated (Fig. 1). In addition, four conserved catalytic residues (Ser, Cys, Asp, and Trp) critical for hydrogen transfer were identified within the active sites (Additional file 2).Fig. 1Sequence analysis of AalCPR. Sequence analysis of AalCPR. Conserved domains are underlined as follows: transmembrane region (purple), FMN-binding domain (blue), FAD-binding domain (green), and NADPH-binding domain (orange).

To clarify the evolutionary relationships of CPR genes among insect species, phylogenetic analysis was performed using amino acid sequences from Lepidoptera, Hemiptera, Coleoptera, Hymenoptera and Diptera retrieved from the NCBI database. CPR sequences from different insect orders were clearly separated, while sequences from the same taxonomic group clustered within a single clade. Both AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were grouped within the Culicidae clade, showing the closest similarity to Ae. aegypti CPR (Additional file 3), followed by Culex and Anopheles sequences, consistent with established taxonomic classification.

AalCPR expression is induced by deltamethrin exposure

To investigate the transcriptional response of AalCPR to insecticide stress, the relative expression levels of AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were measured at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-deltamethrin exposure in four resistant strains of Ae. albopictus (DER30, DER30-M, DER30-1532T, DER30-1534S). Compared with the acetone-treated control group, deltamethrin exposure significantly increased AalCPR-X1 expression in all strains. A significant induction was observed at 12 h post-treatment (P < 0.01), with fold changes of 1.51 ± 0.07, 2.38 ± 0.16, 1.56 ± 0.06 and 1.43 ± 0.08 in DER30, DER30-M, DER30-1532T and DER30-1534S, respectively (Fig. 4A–D). The induction persisted until 24 h in DER30-M and DER30-1532T, where expression remained significantly elevated (2.22 ± 0.19 and 1.44 ± 0.04-fold, respectively; P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B, C). Knockdown of AalCPR increases susceptibility to deltamethrin

To investigate the functional role of CPR in deltamethrin resistance in Ae. albopictus, RNAi-mediated knockdown was performed in four resistant strains. The two transcripts, AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2, share 92.8% sequence identity, which precluded the design of transcript-specific dsRNAs due to the lack of unique target regions. Therefore, a conserved region shared by both transcripts was targeted to achieve simultaneous knockdown of AalCPR. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR at 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection. Compared with the dsGFP control, AalCPR expression was significantly reduced by more than 50% in all four resistant strains (DER30, DER30-M, DER30-1532T, DER30-1534S) at 48 h (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A–D). Silencing was most effective at 72 h in the DER30 strain, with expression reduced by 74.9% (P < 0.001), whereas the other three strains showed maximal knockdown at 48 h, with reductions of 71.0%, 68.0%, and 69.1%, respectively (P < 0.01). Knockdown of AalCPR markedly increased susceptibility to deltamethrin in all four resistant strains (P < 0.05), resulting in mortality increases of 13.8%, 21.1%, 20.3%, and 20.5% (Fig. 5E–H). AalCPR heterologous expression and cytotoxicity assay

AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were further heterologously expressed in Sf9 cells, and western blot analysis using an anti-his antibody confirmed the successful expression of the target proteins (Additional file 4). CPR activity assays showed that the reductase activity of AalCPR-X1 was significantly higher than that of AalCPR-X2 and the empty vector control (Fig. 6A). Expression characteristics of AalCPR in field strains

The resistance levels and metabolic resistance characteristics of three field-collected strains (GD-SZ, GD-SG, and GD-GZ) were further investigated to compare with laboratory-resistant populations. Bioassay results showed that mortality was < 90% in all three field strains (Additional file 4), indicating high resistance levels to deltamethrin in these field populations. Synergist assays further showed that mortality significantly increased following inhibition of P450 activity, suggesting the involvement of P450-mediated metabolic resistance in all field strains (Additional file 5). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the transcript levels of AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 were significantly higher in all field strains than in the laboratory susceptible strain (SH-S), with AalCPR-X1 consistently showing higher expression than AalCPR-X2 in each population (Additional file 5).

Discussion

Insect P450s mediate the metabolic detoxification of insecticides, a process regulated by the membrane-bound protein CPR, and subtle alterations in CPR can drastically alter P450 enzymatic activity [41–45]. Systematic identification and characterization of insect CPR genes are therefore important for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying P450-mediated detoxification and insecticide resistance, and for providing theoretical support for the development of resistance management strategies. Given the essential role of CPR in P450 function and the limited information available on AalCPR, this study cloned and characterized two transcripts of AalCPR (AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2).

Phylogenetic analysis indicated that CPR-X1/X2 isoforms from the same species preferentially clustered together, suggesting the evolutionary conservation of alternative splicing in insect CPR genes. In addition, the primary amino acid sequences of CPR are highly conserved across diverse insect taxa, indicating that this gene plays an essential physiological role during evolution [46]. AalCPR-X1 contains all conserved structural domains, including the N-terminal membrane anchor, FMN-binding, FAD-binding, and NADP-binding domain. In the cytochrome P450 redox system, the binding interface between the FMN domain and FAD/NADP domain is critical for regulating redox potential and electron transfer efficiency [47–49]. Similarly, the hydrophobic N-terminal membrane anchor ensures proper spatial positioning between CPR and P450, which is essential for participation in the P450 catalytic cycle and normal enzymatic function [50, 51]. By contrast, AalCPR-X2 lacks the N-terminal membrane anchor region and possesses a truncated FMN-binding domain, which may impair its electron transfer capacity. In addition, both isoforms contain tryptophan residues at the C-terminus within the nicotinamide-binding site, which have been shown to be involved in regulating the binding and release of NADPH [41].

Metabolic detoxification and insecticide resistance are primarily associated with the overexpression and induction of P450 genes, and the elevated metabolic activity of P450 enzymes depends on electron transfer mediated by CPR [29]. This study showed that the basal expression level of AalCPR-X1 was significantly higher than that of AalCPR-X2 in all laboratory-resistant strains. Similar patterns have also been reported in Spodoptera litura [40]. Moreover, deltamethrin exposure significantly upregulated AalCPR-X1 in all laboratory-resistant strains, while induced expression of AalCPR-X2 was only detectable in the DER30-M strain. The tissue and developmental distribution patterns of proteins are generally closely associated with their physiological functions [52]. Spatiotemporal expression profiles of CPR have been characterized in various insect species. For example, Drosophila melanogaster CPR is highly abundant in embryos and antennae [30]; Spodoptera litura CPR is predominantly expressed in fifth-instar larvae, midguts and fat bodies [40]; and Anopheles gambiae CPR is detected in both midguts and malpighian tubules [26]. This study found high expression of AalCPR-X1 in 3rd–4th instar larvae and adults, whereas AalCPR-X2 was highly expressed only in adults. Further tissue-specific expression analysis revealed that AalCPR-X1 was significantly highly expressed in major detoxification organs, including the midgut, fat body, and malpighian tubules. In contrast, the elevated expression of AalCPR-X2 was restricted primarily to the malpighian tubules. These tissues are key sites for xenobiotic metabolism in most vector insects [53]. Collectively, these findings suggest a potential role for AalCPR in the metabolic detoxification of deltamethrin.

RNAi-mediated knockdown of CPR has been shown to increase insect susceptibility to insecticides in multiple species [54–57]. In the present study, RNAi was used to silence CPR expression in four laboratory-resistant strains (DER30, DER30-M, DER30-1532T, and DER30-1534S). The results showed significantly reduced P450 enzymatic activity and increased deltamethrin susceptibility in all strains. Similarly, CPR downregulation has been reported to increase deltamethrin susceptibility in Anopheles gambiae, suggesting that CPR may contribute to deltamethrin resistance in mosquitoes.

Heterologous expression systems represent efficient approaches for characterizing gene function and have been widely applied in mechanistic studies of insecticide resistance [40, 52]. In the present study, in vitro functional assays revealed that AalCPR-X1 exhibited significantly higher cytochrome c reductase activity than AalCPR-X2. After deltamethrin exposure, cells expressing AalCPR-X1 also showed markedly higher viability than those expressing AalCPR-X2. Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of Bactrocera dorsalis CPR[51], Aphis citricidus CPR[23], Bemisia tabaci Q CPR[52], and Spodoptera litura CPR [40] in Sf9 cells can enhance cell survival under malathion, abamectin, imidacloprid, and indoxacarb treatments, respectively. In addition, CPR expression levels were markedly higher in field strains relative to the laboratory susceptible strain in this study. A similar trend has been documented in Spodoptera litura [40] and Bemisia tabaci Q [52], suggesting that upregulated CPR expression may be broadly linked to insecticide resistance. Collectively, these findings suggest that CPR overexpression could potentially serve as one of several molecular markers for monitoring deltamethrin resistance in natural mosquito populations and may provide a theoretical basis for chemical vector control strategies. This study has several limitations. First, the functional differences between AalCPR-X1 and AalCPR-X2 in metabolic resistance remain unclear. Second, only a limited number of field populations were included, which may limit the generalizability of CPR-based resistance assessment across diverse ecological settings.

Conclusions

This study preliminarily elucidates the role of AalCPR in P450-mediated deltamethrin resistance, which may provide candidate molecular targets for the development of novel insecticides and molecular markers for field mosquito resistance surveillance.
