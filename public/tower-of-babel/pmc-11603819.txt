Codesign with citizens to prevent cardiometabolic diseases in disadvantaged neighbourhoods: an interview study on needs and priorities among stakeholders in Sweden
Authors: Hedda Ottesen, Helle Mölsted Alvesson, Jonas Idevall Hagren, Mats Martinell, Laran Matta, Pia McAleenan, Meena Daivadanam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCardiometabolic diseases are a global health concern, affecting socioeconomically disadvantaged groups more adversely. Effective public health interventions targeting preventable risk factors like physical inactivity and unhealthy diets are needed. Codesign with citizens presents a promising opportunity for developing context-relevant and population-relevant interventions, with high chances of adoption by the target population. This study aimed to understand the needs and priorities of key stakeholders to engage in codesign with citizens for the prevention of cardiometabolic diseases, focusing on socioeconomically disadvantaged groups in Uppsala, Sweden.DesignA qualitative case study was conducted using semistructured interviews with stakeholders working with prevention and and promotion of healthy and active living in public, private and civil society sectors. The interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.SettingUppsala, Sweden.ParticipantsA total of 24 participants were purposively sampled.ResultsWhile participants described the critical value of participatory processes to achieve goals in their work, they struggled to prioritise prevention efforts over time. This adversely affected the continuity of public health interventions and collaborations with other stakeholders. They also described resistance towards inclusive processes from within their organisations, as they were seen as time-consuming and generating unpredictable outcomes. Additionally, the persistence of a siloed system structure of organisations and the risks associated with accessing target populations through local intermediaries added to the challenges.ConclusionThis study highlights the lack of integration of organisational values and functions within and across organisations, hindering cross-sector collaboration and citizen engagement in prevention and health promotion activities. Additionally, the low priority of prevention and ineffective use of codesign hampers continuity and long-term perspectives of prevention and health promotion. To create more conducive conditions for codesign, facilitating intersectoral activities and more inclusive processes is needed and may be enabled by explicit and systematic support.

Background

Cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) remain a major public health issue in Sweden and globally, contributing to increased all-cause mortality, disability, low quality of life and high healthcare costs.1 Although there are variations in the definition of CMD, they usually include diseases such as diabetes, stroke and heart disease.2 CMDs share common preventable risk factors such as smoking, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity and anthropometric and biochemical factors like central obesity, hypertension, dysglycaemia and dyslipidaemia.1 Socioeconomically disadvantaged (SED) groups are more adversely affected, with higher prevalence of CMDs and related risk factors than the general population.35

Interventions focusing on promoting healthy and active living (HAL) are central to preventing CMDs.1 Health behaviours such as physical activity and diet are influenced by several factors, including the social and physical environments in which people live.6 It is well known that public health interventions only targeting individuals’ behaviours tend not to sustain behaviour change over time,7 8 and a growing body of evidence points to the need to engage stakeholders across sectors to address multiple community-level health determinants and create environments that support HAL.9 10

It is crucial to recognise the target group as a central stakeholder in the design and implementation of health interventions, such as citizens in a public health intervention. Disregarding the needs and priorities of the citizens and using a predominantly top-down approach in the design and implementation of public health interventions, seem to reduce the chances of successful implementation due to lack of alignment to local social norms and priorities.11 12 Public health interventions, tailored to citizens’ needs and priorities that engage citizens and multiple stakeholders across sectors, present a promising opportunity to sustainably prevent CMDs in SED neighbourhoods.13

Codesign can strengthen the success of public health initiatives

Involving the target group in the development and implementation of public health initiatives increases their potential of successful outcomes and have been proposed as a way of overcoming the knowledge to practice gap,14 developing contextually appropriate health interventions, enhancing innovation and implementation and increasing the probabilities of high levels of end-user engagement.11 15 16 In contrast, failing to include key stakeholders may lead to failed implementation efforts.17 Participatory research engages community stakeholders in research processes,18 and ‘participatory action research’ emphasises the involvement of the target group in developing and implementing initiatives.19 A group of participatory action research methods that has gained increasing popularity for aligning research and service development is the ‘co’ approaches, which include cocreation, coproduction and codesign.14 The methods have emerged from different fields but are similar in their emphasis on the involvement of citizens or end-users in the design and/or delivery.14 20 According to Greenhalgh et al, principles connected to a successful use of ‘co’ approaches ‘include a systems perspective, a creative approach to research focused on improving human experience, and careful attention to governance and process’.21 The term ‘co’ usually refers to the involvement of stakeholders throughout a process, from defining a problem to developing and evaluating the solution, such as service development or prioritisation, policy changes, implementation of community programmes or intervention assessment.14 22 Depending on the type of ‘co’ approach used, there is a varying degree of ownership by the target group in terms of initiative in the process. Cocreation involves the target group at all stages from problem definition and boundary setting, codesign often has a clear outcome in mind but the target group is involved to form boundaries and approaches, and in coproduction the result or product is designed prior to engagement with the end-user.14 Even though a large body of studies use the terms cocreation, coproduction or codesign, the literature lacks consistency in the definitions of the methods.14

As described in an overview article of definitions and practices of ‘co’ approaches by Vargas et al, codesign in particular has the capacity to improve design outcomes and enhance social inclusion.14 By focusing on citizens’ insights to establish new priorities, plans and strategies for improvement,14 it has the potential to improve health equity by reaching and empowering vulnerable populations.23 24 This makes codesign a useful method for development and implementation of public health initiatives with SED populations.

Nevertheless, criticism has been raised against tokenistic uses of ‘codesign’, where codesign is said to have been used but has failed in core parts such as shared decision-making or addressing power imbalances between stakeholders.24 Moreover, there is a lack of empirical and experimental evaluations of codesign on the effectiveness and impact on equity.25

For the purpose of this paper, codesign refers to a process where stakeholders across sectors and organisations including citizens actively engage in a process of developing and implementing a public health initiative. The stakeholders shoulde be involved throughout the process to define boundaries and approaches, design the implementation of and evaluate the public health initiative.14 In the process, should be made to minimise power imbalances and improve shared decision-making.22 25

Increasing demand of participatory approaches

In recent years, the demand for inclusive and participatory approaches in research and decision-making processes in public institutions has increased globally.2629 In Sweden, citizen-focused approaches and multistakeholder collaborations are increasingly demanded by public bodies and are outlined in guidelines and assignments at national, regional and local levels.3033 Moreover, the Swedish health system is undergoing a restructuring and reorientation towards a more primary care focused health system and an increased emphasis on prevention and health service integration.34 All Swedish regions have been assigned developing their own programme for this transition. In Region Uppsala’s strategy ‘Effective and Close Care 2030’ (Effektiv och Nära Vård 2030), participatory approaches and codesign that involve patients and citizens have been declared central in the new healthcare organisation.30 To succeed with the implementation of codesign and effective prevention efforts, knowledge about the local context and conditions are needed.35

Aim of this study

This study aimed to understand key stakeholders’ perceptions of roles and responsibilities in the primary prevention of CMD and promotion of HAL in particular, as well as their experiences, needs and priorities for engaging in codesign, with a focus on SED populations in Uppsala, Sweden.

Methods

The PREVENT research project

This study is part of the formative studies of the PREVENT project and informs a public health intervention in SED neighbourhoods in Uppsala. The goal of the PREVENT project is to codesign and implement a community outreach programme combined with tele-health coaching in selected SED neighbourhoods in Uppsala, for the prevention of CMDs and promotion of HAL.36 The PREVENT research team contributing to this study is interdisciplinary, with expertise in research and practice in medicine, public health, medical anthropology, implementation science and codesign.

Study setting

The Uppsala region, the fifth largest region in Sweden, has approximately 400 000 inhabitants, with over half residing in the regional capital, Uppsala.37 In 2023, the municipality of Uppsala mapped unhealthy rates and socioeconomic factors at neighbourhood level in Uppsala, resulting in the report the Social Compass.38 In the report, five neighbourhood clusters were identified (red, orange, purple, blue and yellow). The study sites of the PREVENT project and the SED neighbourhoods focused on in this study were selected from two of these clusters (red and orange), which are the most disadvantaged clusters. These neighbourhoods are characterised by a high disease burden (sickness days), low educational levels, high unemployment rates, low household incomes, a high proportion of financial aid recipients and a large share of children aged 0–19 years living in low-income households. The civil society organisations (CSOs) (for example community-based organisations, ethnic associations, women’s rights groups, co-operatives, professional associations and independent research institutes) participating in this study primarily operated in these neighbourhoods and had a majority of their members living in red or orange neighbourhood clusters.

Public health governance and stakeholders in Sweden

Public health governance in Sweden operates on three different levels: national, regional and local. At the national level, the Public Health Agency is primarily responsible for issues relating to public health, with the mission to promote good and equal health, prevent diseases, control diseases, with focus on high-risk of disease groups.39 They also have a specific mission to promote physical activity. The National Swedish Food Agency works to ensure food security and promote healthy eating on a societal level.40 The National Board of Health and Welfare’s mission is to ensure good health, social welfare and high-quality health and social care in equal terms for the Swedish population, including developing standards of care and procedures.41 Each county in Sweden has a County Administrative Board, a government authority involved in public health and equality.42 The national government regulates the responsibilities and activities of governmental bodies through instructions and appropriation directives.

At the regional level, the regional office oversees healthcare, prevention, infrastructure and regional development.43 The main governing documents are the regional plan and budget. Locally, the municipality office handles services such as city planning, care services and schools.44 The municipality’s main regulatory document is the municipality plan and budget. Non-governmental organisations operating at different levels and trade organisations also influence policy and delivery of public health initiatives and services.

In this article, ‘stakeholder’ refers to any public, private or civil society sector organisation or institution working with public health, rather than specific individuals.

Study design

This qualitative study used semistructured interviews with public health stakeholders. The study adopts a methodological approach within a social constructivist paradigm, viewing knowledge as being developed through the experiences and practices of the participants, including their own and their organisation’s interpretations of their visions and mandates. The study followed the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ, online supplemental appendix 1).45

Patient and public involvement

The participants were not involved in the design of this study. However, several participants in this study represented partners to the PREVENT project and help in disseminating the study’s findings.

Participant selection and recruitment

Participants were purposively sampled. Sampling was guided by the socioecological model, in order to recruit participants from different structural levels and functions.46 In this paper, the structural levels used are macro, meso and micro. The macro level refers to the stakeholder level responsible for governance, guidelines and societal issues; the meso level refers to bridging stakeholders in private sector and CSOs advocating for the interests of their members or organisational mission; and the micro level refers to the stakeholders that work closest to citizens in their daily activities.46 See table 1.

Eligible public sector stakeholders worked directly with chronic disease prevention or health promotion, while CSOs and private sector stakeholders worked indirectly with health promotion or directly with citizens. Data collection

Interviews were conducted between October 2022 and December 2023 by the first author (HO). HO had no previous relationship with any of the interviewees. All were individual interviews, except for one natural group interview with three participants who requested to be interviewed together. The interviews were conducted in Swedish and lasted between 41 and 80 minutes. One pilot interview was conducted online, using a digital meeting platform, while the other 21 were conducted face-to-face in locations chosen by the participants. Only the first author and the interviewee(s) were present in the room, except for one interview where the interviewee requested for a trainee colleague to join as a non-participant observer.

A semistructured interview guide with open-ended and probing questions was developed and piloted with the first participant. Questions were inspired by gaps identified in the literature and focused on the interviewees’ perceptions of their organisation’s role in prevention of CMD and promotion of HAL with focus on SED groups, as well as their experiences of codesign or similar participatory approaches. Additionally, before each interview, the first author reviewed relevant documents from the participant’s organisation to formulate additional questions. Examples of documents were instructions, appropriation directives, budgets and guidelines of the organisations.

After the pilot, minor adjustments were made to the interview guide. Furthermore, the need of facilitating conversation, about several different stakeholders, projects and relationships between stakeholders, was identified during the evaluation of the pilot interview. Visual tools have been shown useful to facilitate interviews on complex topics and generate the rich yet specific data needed for this study,50 51 and were therefore considered appropriate to use in this study. A codesign expert (PM) from the research team was involved to develop additional visual data collection tools to use with the interview guides. The tools that were added were two charts:

Chart 1: a rectangular chart for healthy diet and physical activity promotion activities, represented by one-half of the chart each, and a central circle for citizens in SED neighbourhoods. Participants were encouraged to use the chart and Duplo figures to visualise their responses (online supplemental appendix 2).

The updated data collection method was piloted on research team members and with the pilot interviewee. Only the first pilot interview was included in the analysis since the interview guide only underwent minor revisions. No repeat interviews were carried out.

All the interviews were audiorecorded and transcribed verbatim. Photos of the charts were taken with a cell phone at the end of each interview. Additionally, the data collector took notes after each interview to describe and reflect on the interview setting, dialogue and reactions, to support the reflexive process in the data analysis. All interview data were securely stored in a digital storage space at Uppsala University.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis methodology.52 53 The photographs of the charts and notes from the interviews were used to support the interpretation and analysis of the interviews.

To examine differences in needs and priorities among the various stakeholders based on their structural level, the interviews were grouped into three sets after data familiarisation. Coding was data-driven and performed in three sets using QualCoder and NVivo V.14, resulting in three code sets. Codes were compared to identify similarities and disparities within and between sets. Eventually, all codes were combined and abstracted into categories, subthemes and themes.

The first author (HO) was the primary coder, meeting regularly with the last author (MD) to sense-check codes, explore interpretations and discuss emerging categories and themes.53 Preliminary results and interpretations were discussed with all coauthors at regular meetings. To strengthen credibility, member checking was done by presenting preliminary results in stakeholder workshops in December 2023 and June 2024. Workshop participants recognised their own experiences within the narratives, and confirmed that their experiences were well reflected in the results.54

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The research team in this study brought together a mix of insider–outsider and interdisciplinary perspectives, including anthropology, primary care, public health and implementation research. The first author (HO) is a female medical doctor and doctoral candidate with previous experience in conducting interviews. During this study, HO was elected as group lead of her political party in the city council in her city of residence (outside Uppsala county). To mitigate potential discomfort for the participants, HO disclosed her background and political role to the participants before initiation of each interview, with the option for another team member to take over if needed, though this was never necessary. HO maintained a reflexive journal to track thoughts, reactions and internal processes during data collection and analysis55 and several meetings were held with the coauthors during the course of this study, to engage in collaborative reflexivity.

Ethical considerations

This study has ethical approval from the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (ID 2022-0403401) and permission from Primary Care and Health (Nära vård och hälsa) at Region Uppsala.

The ethical approval for this study was covered by the approval for several formative studies (including the current study) in the PREVENT project. Informed, written consent was obtained from each participant before participation in the study. The participants were given time to read through the consent form and were given the opportunity to ask questions before signing. All participants were informed about their rights to withdraw their consent at any time.

Overview of the themes and subthemes

Theme 1: struggling to keep health promotion on the agenda

The participants described challenges to working with prevention and promotion of HAL in their own roles and with other stakeholders, including difficulties in keeping promotion of HAL prioritised over time financially and mission-wise.

Prevention is generally low in priority

Participants acknowledged the importance of multisectoral collaboration and action to achieve effective prevention and promotion of HAL. However, functional collaborations and satisfactory outcomes in terms of effectiveness in prevention or promotion of HAL were often hampered by differences in organisations’ fundamental values and clashes between them, leading to disparities in the priority of prevention and promotion of HAL between stakeholders:

Sometimes it’s an advantage to be several stakeholders, but sometimes it just leads to compromises that, well, compromises can be both good and bad, but in the end, it can become so eviscerated when everyone has to be able to sign off on something.

(national public stakeholder, IDI 1)

The participants described that projects and programmes for prevention and promotion of HAL were vulnerable to budget cuts, leading to lack of continuity in efforts and a dependency on volunteers and passionate individuals to maintain the work.

Being flexible is a must

Participants stressed that continuity and long-term perspectives were essential to achieve results in prevention and promotion of HAL and to build and maintain fruitful relationships with other stakeholders and citizens. However, the year-to-year basis on which missions and resources were allocated to macro and micro level organisations, challenged the continuity of activities and partnerships.

They [activities and initiatives] are constantly changing depending on the available staff, scheduling and time.

(local CSO, IDI 2)

Micro level actors reported engaging in short-term projects with other stakeholders to tailor activities to the specific needs and requests of their target group and adapting to what the funding that year was focused on. They described the need to be flexible in their tasks and taking individual responsibility for sustaining partnerships and initiatives related to prevention or promotion of HAL. They often needed to be creative in the interpretation of the description of their work or mission to stick with projects over time and prioritise prevention or promotion of HAL. For example, one participant described arranging a cycling course as a labour market initiative, which resulted in that three women learnt to bike and became employed in domestic service.

However, the short-termism characterising the field of prevention and promotion of HAL was described as contributing to project fatigue among stakeholders, and finding ways to ensure long-term financing was of high priority for several micro level stakeholders.

Important actions are left out in a siloed system

Participants from various sectors emphasised the importance of respecting the boundaries of other stakeholders’ responsibilities to maintain good relationships. They noted that macro level stakeholders often adhered strictly to their mandates, which sometimes led to inaction on related needs not explicitly stated in their directives. This lack of a clear mandate, particularly in outreach work related to the promotion of HAL, caused frustration as it allowed social media actors to exploit gaps by promoting non-evidence-based alternatives.

There are some who try to sort of manage things on social media, but […] if a dietitian is going to address false claims in the media, it often takes a long time to find the correct references and to formulate it in the concise way social media demands, and to do so in a professional manner. It takes time, and no one is employed to do that today.

(national CSO, IDI 15)

Participants from public sector at national, regional and local levels declared that their main long-term responsibility in terms of engaging in prevention and promotion of HAL was their role as organisers of inspiration networks and platforms for the exchange of best practices between different stakeholders. The siloed system where stakeholders had differently formulated goals and used different terminologies led to cancelled collaborations and action, despite potential co-benefits of collaborating.

Additionally, participants perceived a shift in focus of public health initiatives.

Targeted efforts for vulnerable and particularly at-risk groups are made, but the universal ones tend to be forgotten.

(regional public stakeholder, IDI 17)

The participants perceived a risk that this change would lead to reduced chances of improved population health, and that the focus over the last years had become more and more on crime prevention, narrowing the target group of interventions significantly.

Micro level stakeholders highlighted the competitive nature of the siloed system, which pushed them to carve out unique niches to survive against larger entities, often resulting in short-term, specific collaborations rather than long-term partnerships between organisations. Despite these challenges, participants acknowledged that successful collaborations, where synergies were identified without trespassing on each other’s territories, led to amplified impacts and were seen as highly beneficial.

Challenging to make inclusivity feasible

Despite positive experiences of initiatives with active and meaningful involvement of other stakeholders and target groups, the participants described several difficulties in using codesign consequently in their organisations.

Risks of limited citizen representation when going through local intermediaries

Due to lack of trust in public institutions among residents in SED neighbourhoods, participants from public sector described that they went through local intermediaries to reach SED for initiatives. This reduced the citizen contact further, but the local rootedness of intermediaries and their close contact with the local population were described as critical in ensuring that interventions were accepted in these neighbourhoods, and that people would show up to activities.

It is good to be in touch with someone who has a good relationship with the target group, who can assist in getting more participants [to activities].

(local public stakeholder, IDI 4)

Identifying the most important local stakeholders was sometimes difficult for local authorities, and admitting the need to collaborate with certain CSOs that they did not particularly align with could be a challenge:

I also notice that politics [local politicians and the political administration] can be quite challenging, that a, for example [name of local CSO] have more legitimacy among the residents than what the elected politicians have. That is very hard to admit, and it’s challenging to deal with.

(national public stakeholder, IDI 12)

Some participants brought up the risks of going through intermediaries, as opposed to direct contact and collaboration with people in the communities. They acknowledged that these intermediaries did not represent everyone and that the approach did not reach nor include perspectives of those who were not represented by them.

Resistance to involving citizens

Participation of citizens was predominantly used during the initial phase of a process where citizens were involved to brainstorm ideas or to give input on an already existing plan. Participants representing public sector stakeholders at different levels explained that they tried to use participatory approaches in their organisation and outlined experiences of how codesign with citizens and shared decision-making had led to better-fitting solutions. Nevertheless, they encountered challenges in using codesign in their organisations on a regular basis and described that there were a resistance to participatory methods like codesign within their organisations, especially from people ‘higher up’ as one participant expressed it.

First of all, we need knowledge about the process, its advantages and disadvantages, in the management and governance. They do not know what it is.

They think it takes a lot of time and is, well, a bit unpredictable.

(local public stakeholder, IDI 16)

The structure of codesign was also described as clashing with the politicians’ demand on quick results and certain and often predefined outcomes.

In contrast, micro level stakeholders working close to SED groups expressed that listening to and actively including the people they were working for was a fundamental part of how they operated. Nevertheless, they highlighted the need to make activities related to prevention and promotion of HAL relevant for the population, since issues like social exclusion and poverty were more pressing for the most disadvantaged people.

That is probably the biggest concern for the target group that I work with, that many of them face extreme difficulties where you hardly have any money at all to live on so that’s what it’s about. And then it’s like, um, yeah, they themselves are unable to take the, like, health responsibility in that way.

(local CSO, IDI 21)

They explained that understanding the real needs of people required repeated conversations, as did building trust and creating possibilities to help the individual. Micro level stakeholders also stressed the need for effort to make the target group to feel seen, heard, welcomed, and safe, in a new organisation or other types of initiatives.

Discussion

The findings of this study show that siloed structures, driven by differing values and priorities in different sectors, create siloed mindsets and pose significant barriers to codesign and prevention work across all stakeholder levels, from macro to micro. Additionally, the short-term planning conditions for finances and activities undermined sustained prevention and HAL efforts, hampering continuity of prevention activities as well as collaboration. Finally, this study found limited use of codesign in macro and meso level organisations, partly caused by resistance to processes with shared decision-making as they might lead to unpredictable outcomes.

Lack of normative and functional integration for prevention

The barriers to codesign identified in this study are reflected in Dijk et al’s Integrated Care Model.56 Dijk et al emphasise the importance of normative and functional integration among stakeholders from different sectors and levels as essential for delivering holistic and comprehensive health services to citizen. The findings from our study demonstrate significant differences in values between stakeholders and sectors, hindering alignment of priorities and negatively impacting the prioritisation of preventive efforts and the promotion of HAL. This was especially highlighted at macro and meso levels, and seemed to have a cascade effect on the stakeholders at the micro level. Paralleling the concept of functional integration in the Integrated Care Model, the lack of normative integration across stakeholders contributed to lack of cohesion in and fragmentation and discontinuity of prevention and HAL promoting services available to citizens, as stakeholders faced challenges in balancing short-term funding and the shift of focus during different funding cycles.56 In a Danish nationwide study by Prior et al, fragmentation of health services has been linked to poorer health outcomes, health inequalities and economic inefficiency.57 Our study’s findings adds to the arguments for addressing fragmentation in preventive health services by highlighting the critical importance of continuity of public health initiatives in SED neighbourhoods for understanding citizens’ real needs and to build trust between stakeholders including citizens.

The siloed structures of organisations led to gaps in action for promotion of HAL and prevention and was identified as a significant barrier to codesign. One public sector participant pointed out the absence of mandate for outreach work, which resulted in critical needs falling between the cracks, creating openings for opportunistic actors to fill the void. This has also been found by Bosdijk et al,23 who observed that siloed organisations and lack of collaboration led to action gaps. However, unlike our study, Bosdijk et al also observed health services overlaps, leading to duplicated efforts.23

The inefficiencies caused by siloed budgeting and fragmented assignments to public and civilsociety stakeholder have been widely recognised.57 Other papers have indicated that the Sustainable Development Goals will not be achieved with the current sectoral siloed financing system, systems of planning, budgeting and delivering services that do not prioritise development synergies.58 This recognition has led to several calls for ‘health in all policies’ and ‘intersectoral action for health’,59 and even calls for reforming global institutions to enable effective action for sustainable health.60 Despite this, not much has changed in governmental structures, in the way governance is executed and in the conditions for multisectoral collaborations. The siloed structures remain, with many recognising the need for governments to provide incentives for multisectoral action as well as more long-term budgetary commitments and sustainable mechanisms to support collaborations.61

In this study, participants at both micro and meso levels provided examples of successful, yet limited availability of, strategies that facilitate long-term commitments and efforts, such as social investments and collaboration contracts between civil society and public institutions. In our study, participants highlighted that budget cuts had negative impacts on long-term efforts to promote HAL. One strategy identified as having potential to overcome the fragmentation and inefficiencies of silo budgeting and enable multisectoral, long-term action by focusing on achieving sectors’ shared goals, is intersectoral cofinancing.62 63 Additionally, a systematic review showed that resource constraints might actually promote collaboration through cofinancing, if the collaboration aligns with political priorities.62

Partial implementation of codesign may be a risk

Participants in this study faced difficulties in ensuring true inclusivity in codesign processes. While some had been trained in methods codesign or similar methods, the involvement of citizens was often limited to one-off dialogues on preplanned changes or activities. This partial use of codesign risks creating false sense of impact64 and leading to frustration among citizens diminish their willingness to participate in future codesign processes.65 A recent interview study with residents in a SED neighbourhood in Uppsala revealed frustration and mistrust among citizen towards citizen dialogues with local authorities, as the dialogues rarely had any influence on the preplanned changes.12 The importance of specifying where and how decisions are taken throughout a participatory process, especially when working with disadvantaged populations, has been underscored by Inge et al.66

Trischler et al have discussed the challenge of designing ‘with’ rather than ‘for’ service users, emphasising that the essence of codesign is to shift the focus from the experts to the target group.67 In the light of this, along with Greenhalgh et al’s principles of codesign—such as adopting a systems perspective and prioritising the process over results—the findings of this study underscore the need to implement several core components of codesign.21

Methodological considerations

This study has several strengths and limitations. The interview method generated rich, specific data with relevant descriptions and examples.68 While the visual tools used in the interviews facilitated discussion on complex topics, the method may have been limited by participants’ varying comfort with and comprehension of the visual tools.50 51 To mitigate this risk, the voluntary use of the visual tools was emphasised.

The sample, drawn from multiple sectors and levels, enhances the study’s potential transferability to similar settings and contexts.69 Techniques such as triangulation including review of regulatory documents before the interviews, reflexivity, team involvement in data analysis, and member checking, have been used to enhance data depth, credibility and validity.70 The member-checking workshops also provided opportunities for error reduction in the interpretation of the data, enhancing trustworthiness.70 Limitations include the absence of representation from religious institutions. Representatives from these organisations were invited, but could not participate due to time constraints. Similarly, citizen voices are lacking in this study, although another study focusing on resident perspectives was conducted in parallel to this study.

The transferability of the results of this study can be considered for areas with comparable demographics in Sweden and in other European countries with similar demographic and governance structures.

Implications for policy and further research

The findings of this study highlight several needs that may be addressed to facilitate codesign with citizens. This includes the need to increase integration of organisational values and priorities across domains, search for synergies across sectors, address the siloed nature of sectors and coordinate public health efforts without leaving gaps in important action. Additionally, this study underscores the need to find alternative financing models that allow stakeholders to work more long-term in partnerships and activities, as well as broaden the health agenda to include prevention and health promotion.

Health service delivery benefits from integration57 71 and should focus on the real needs of citizens. This study highlights an existing emphasis on prevention and health promotion within SED groups, but shows limited engagement these groups. To create more conducive conditions for codesign, facilitating intersectoral activities and inclusive processes is needed, and may be enabled by explicit and systematic support for structural and functional aspects pertaining to prevention. Future research should evaluate the implementation of codesign in terms of public health and equity outcomes, and explore effective models for long-term collaboration for prevention and promotion of HAL.

Conclusion

This study emphasises low integration of values and services focused on prevention and health promotion within and between organisations, which hampers effective prevention efforts, cross-sectoral collaborations and inclusion of citizens in the design of prevention and health promotion services. This fragmentation affects microlevel stakeholders who grapple with short-term allocation of resources and financial constraints, challenging sustained efforts in prevention and promotion of HAL. Furthermore, siloed structures lead to inefficiencies and gaps in health promotion actions. Despite calls for intersectoral action and long-term budgetary commitments, governmental structures have shown limited reform with respect to the integration of finance flows between sectors. While some strategies such as social investments and collaboration contracts show promise, systematic support for intersectoral codesign is needed to enable sustainable health interventions and avoid partial implementation of participatory methods, which risks frustration and mistrust among stakeholders, particularly citizens. Future research should focus on practical models for long-term and meaningful collaborations between sectors and actors, which could enable effective implementation and improved outcomes of codesign in prevention and health promotion activities.
