Ordering groups and the Identity Problem
Corentin Bodart, Laura Ciobanu, George Metcalfe

Abstract
. In this paper, the Identity Problem for certain groups, which asks if the subsemigroup generated by a given finite set of elements contains the identity element, is related to problems regarding ordered groups. Notably, the Identity Problem for a torsion-free nilpotent group corresponds to the problem asking if a given finite set of elements extends to the positive cone of a left-order on the group, and thereby also to the Word Problem for a related lattice-ordered group. A new (independent) proof is given showing that the Identity and Subgroup Problems are decidable for every finitely presented nilpotent group, establishing also the decidability of the Word Problem for a family of lattice-ordered groups. A related problem, the Fixed-Target Submonoid Membership Problem, is shown to be undecidable in nilpotent groups. Decidability of the Normal Identity Problem (with ‘subsemigroup’ replaced by ‘normal subsemigroup’) for free nilpotent groups is established using the (known) decidability of the Word Problem for certain lattice-ordered groups. Connections between orderability and the Identity Problem for a class of torsion-free metabelian groups are also explored.

1.
Introduction

This paper showcases the interplay between topics of different flavours in group theory — notably, orderability of groups, membership problems in groups, and word problems for lattice-ordered groups — and uses a range of tools, from convex geometry to Lie algebras, to establish these connections.

The
Identity Problem
for a group
G
asks if the subsemigroup generated by a given finite set of elements of
G
contains the identity element
e_{G}
. This problem has been studied in
[
4
,
3
,
12
,
15
]
for various matrix groups, and for certain classes of nilpotent and metabelian groups in
[
14
,
17
,
18
]
; see
[
16
,
28
]
for comprehensive surveys.
The Subgroup Problem
, which asks if the subsemigroup generated by a given finite set of elements of
G
is a subgroup, has been studied alongside the Identity Problem for a similar range of groups.

Our first goal in this paper is to explain how the Identity Problem for
G
relates (in some settings) to both the
Left-Order Extension Problem
for
G
, which asks if a given finite set of elements of
G
extends to the positive cone of a left-order on
G
, and the
Word Problem
for a corresponding lattice-ordered group. Left-orders on groups — in particular, their dynamical realizations and associated toplogical spaces — are an active research area (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
11
]
), while word problems for lattice-ordered groups have been studied widely in the ordered groups literature (see, e.g.,
[
25
,
31
,
27
,
5
]
). We also explain how the
Normal Identity Problem
for
G
, with ‘subsemigroup’ replaced by ‘normal subsemigroup’, relates (in some cases) to both the
Bi-Order Extension Problem
for
G
, with ‘left-order’ replaced by ‘bi-order’, and the decidability of the equational theory of a corresponding variety of lattice-ordered groups.

Our second goal is to address these problems in the setting of
nilpotent groups
. In particular, we provide a new (independent) proof that the Identity and Subgroup Problems are decidable for every finitely generated nilpotent group, concluding:

Theorem 1
.

(a)

The Identity Problem is decidable for every finitely generated nilpotent group
G
. That is, given a finite set
S
of elements in
G
, it is possible to decide whether the subsemigroup generated by
S
contains the identity element
e_{G}
.

(b)

The Subgroup Problem is decidable for any finitely generated nilpotent group
G
. That is, given a finite set
S
of elements in
G
, it is possible to decide whether the subsemigroup generated by
S
is a subgroup of
G
.

Although decidability of the Subgroup Problem implies decidability of the Identity Problem, we state them separately in the theorem, as an alternative way of solving the Identity Problem (but not the Subgroup Problem) is possible (see Section
5
). Theorem
1
relies on Proposition
19
, our main technical tool, which establishes the equivalence of the required algebraic statements about a nilpotent group and convex geometry properties of the group. In particular, Proposition
19
allows us to detect finite-index subgroups among subsemigroups. An equivalent result was independently proven by Shafrir
[
38
, Theorem 2]
. During the writing of this paper Theorem
1
has been generalized by the first author and Dong to virtually solvable matrix groups over algebraic numbers
[
6
]
.

As consequences of Theorem
1
, we obtain:

Corollary
11
.

Let
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
be a finite presentation of a torsion-free nilpotent group. Then the
\ell
-group with finite presentation
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
has a decidable Word Problem.

Corollary
15
.

The Normal Identity Problem and Bi-Order Extension Problem are decidable for any finitely generated free nilpotent group of class
c
.

Considering the strong links between orderability and membership problems in nilpotent groups exhibited above, a natural question is whether similar results hold for other groups. We show that the class of groups
G_{\lambda}\leq\mathrm{Aff}_{+}(\mathbb{Q})
consisting of orientation-preserving affine transformations of the rationals (which are torsion-free metabelian) are fully left-orderable (Corollary
30
), and so the question of extending a finite set to a left-order is equivalent to the Identity Problem. While the Identity Problem is known to be decidable in metabelian groups, this equivalence gives a much faster algorithm for the Identity Problem. Section
8
is a starting point to exploring metabelian groups more generally, and finding further groups that are fully (left-)orderable.

Related membership problems.

Theorem
1
stands in contrast to the undecidability of the
Submonoid Membership Problem
in finitely generated nilpotent groups: for certain nilpotent groups
G
, it is undecidable for an arbitrary finite set
S\subset G
and element
g\in G
, whether the submonoid generated by
S
contains
g
(
[
37
]
).

Theorem
1
also stands in contrast to the undecidability of the
Fixed-Target Submonoid Membership Problem
in finitely generated nilpotent groups, proved in Proposition
26
. This asks whether given a nilpotent group
G
together with an element
g_{0}\in G
, it is possible to decide if
g_{0}
is in the submonoid generated by
S
, where
S\subset G
is an arbitrary finite set. This is an intermediate problem between the Identity Problem on one hand, where
g_{0}=e_{G}
and we ask about membership in a subsemigroup, and the Submonoid Membership Problem on the other hand, where both
S
and
g_{0}
are arbitrary.

Complexity of our algorithms.

We establish polynomial time complexity for the Identity and Subgroup Problems for a fixed nilpotent group
G
in Proposition
24
. Although this has already been claimed in
[
38
]
and
[
28
]
, no details were given in those references. We also discuss in Section
6
why the
uniform
versions of these problems, when a group presentation for some group
G
is part of the input, are not polynomial with the algorithms currently available.

Structure of the paper

We give the background on orderability and connections of orderability to the Identity Problem in nilpotent groups in Section
2
, connecting these with word problems for
\ell
-groups in Section
3
. Section
4
contains the main technical results, linking convex geometry to subsemigroups in nilpotent groups, results that are used in Section
5
to establish Theorem
1
. In Section
6
we discuss the computational complexity of the Identity and Subgroup Problems.

Section
7
is concerned with the Fixed-Target Submonoid Membership Problem, which is proved to be undecidable in nilpotent groups. Finally, Section
8
considers an additional class of groups for which full orderability holds, groups of orientation-preserving affine transformations of the rationals, and connects this to the Identity Problem, shifting the focus from nilpotent groups in earlier sections to metabelian ones.
We finish the paper with a few open questions arising from our results on orderability for metabelian groups.

2.
Extending partial orders on groups and the Identity Problem

Let
G
be any group with identity element
e_{G}
. A partial order
\leq
on
G
is called a
partial left-order
on
G
if it is left-invariant, i.e.,
a\leq b
implies
ca\leq cb
for all
a,b,c\in G
; if
\leq
is also total, it is called a
left-order
. A (partial) left-order on
G
is called a
(partial) bi-order
if it is both left- and right-invariant, i.e.,
a\leq b
implies
cad\leq cbd
for all
a,b,c,d\in G
.

The
positive cone

P:=\{a\in G\mid e_{G}<a\}
of any partial left-order
\leq
on
G
is a subsemigroup of
G
that omits
e_{G}
, i.e.,
a,b\in P
implies
ab\in P
, and
e_{G}\not\in P
. Conversely, given any subsemigroup
P
of
G
that omits
e_{G}
, the binary relation
\leq
on
G
defined by
a\leq b\,:\Longleftrightarrow\,{a}^{-1}b\in P\cup\{e_{G}\}
is a partial left-order on
G
with positive cone
P
. Because of this equivalence we will use the term ‘order’ to refer to either an order
\leq
or its positive cone.

Partial left-orders on
G
can be identified with subsemigroups of
G
that omit
e_{G}
, and a subset
S
of
G
extends to a partial left-order on
G
if and only if
{S}^{+}
, the subsemigroup generated by
S
, omits
e_{G}
.

A partial left-order
P
on
G
is a partial bi-order if and only if it is
normal
, i.e.,
a\in P
and
b\in G
implies
ba{b}^{-1}\in P
. Partial bi-orders on
G
can therefore be identified with normal subsemigroups of
G
that omit
e_{G}
, and a subset
S
of
G
extends to a partial bi-order on
G
if and only if
{S}^{\circ}
, the normal subsemigroup generated by
S
, omits
e_{G}
.

If there exists a left-order (bi-order) on
G
, then
G
is said to be
left-orderable
(
bi-orderable
). We call
G

fully left-orderable
(
fully bi-orderable
) if every partial left-order (bi-order) on
G
extends to a left-order (bi-order) on
G
. Let us refer to the problem of deciding if a given finite subset of
G
extends to a left-order (bi-order) on
G
as the
Left-Order (Bi-Order) Extension Problem
. The following results are direct consequences of the preceding definitions and remarks.

Proposition 2
.

Let
G
be a group.

(a)

If
G
is fully left-orderable, then the Identity and Left-Order Extension Problems for
G
are complements: a finite subset
S
of
G
extends to a left-order on
G
if and only if
e_{G}\not\in{S}^{+}
.

(b)

If
G
is fully bi-orderable, then the Normal Identity and Bi-Order Extension Problems for
G
are complements: a finite subset
S
of
G
extends to a bi-order on
G
if and only if
e_{G}\not\in{S}^{\circ}
.

In order to apply these results in the setting of nilpotent groups, we make use of the following theorems from the literature on ordered groups:

Theorem 3

(Malcev
[
30
]
)
.

Every torsion-free nilpotent group is fully bi-orderable.

Theorem 4

(Rhemtulla
[
34
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Every torsion-free nilpotent group is fully left-orderable.

Combining these two theorems with Proposition
2
yields:

Corollary 5
.

Let
G
be any torsion-free nilpotent group.

(a)

The Identity and Left-Order Extension Problems are complements for
G
: a finite subset
S
of
G
extends to a left-order on
G
if and only if
e_{G}\not\in{S}^{+}
.

(b)

The Normal Identity and Bi-Order Extension Problems are complements for
G
: a finite subset
S
of
G
extends to a bi-order on
G
if and only if
e_{G}\not\in{S}^{\circ}
.

Theorem
1
establishes that the Identity Problem, and hence also the Left-Order Extension Problem, are decidable for any finitely generated nilpotent group, noting that the latter is trivial for any group with torsion. To prove this theorem, we will use the fact that every left-order on a torsion-free nilpotent group possesses a key property. A left-order
P
on a group
G
is said to be
Conradian
if
a,b\in P
implies
{b}^{-1}ab^{n}\in P
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Theorem 6

(Rhemtulla
[
34
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Every left-order on a torsion-free nilpotent group is Conradian.

The following fundamental property of Conradian orders is known as Conrad’s theorem; for a modern treatment of the property, see
[
11
, Corollary 3.2.28]
.

Theorem 7

(Conrad
[
10
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Let
G
be any finitely generated group and let
P
be a Conradian order on
G
. Then there exists a homomorphism
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
a\in P
implies
f(a)\geq 0
.

Corollary 8
.

Let
G
be any finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent group and let
S\subseteq G
. If
e_{G}\notin{S}^{+}
, then there exists a non-zero homomorphism
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(a)\geq 0
for all
a\in S
.

Proof.

Suppose that
e_{G}\notin{S}^{+}
. Then
{S}^{+}
is a partial left-order on
G
that extends to a left-order
P
on
G
, by Theorem
4
. But
P
is Conradian, by Theorem
6
, so the existence of a suitable homomorphism
f
follows from Theorem
7
.
∎

3.
Word problems for
\ell
-groups and the Identity Problem

A
lattice-ordered group
(
\ell
-group
) is an algebraic structure
\langle{L,\wedge,\vee,\cdot,{}^{-1},e}\rangle
such that
\langle{L,\cdot,{}^{-1},e}\rangle
is a group,
\langle{L,\wedge,\vee}\rangle
is a lattice with a lattice-order defined by
a\leq b\,:\Longleftrightarrow\,a\wedge b=a
, and
a\leq b
implies
cad\leq cbd
for all
a,b,c,d\in L
. The class
\mathsf{LG}
of
\ell
-groups forms a variety (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
for details). Key examples of
\ell
-groups are provided by sets
Aut(\langle{C,\preceq}\rangle)
of order-preserving permutations of a totally ordered set
\langle{C,\preceq}\rangle
, with the group operation given by composition of functions and the lattice-order defined by setting
f\leq g\,:\Longleftrightarrow\,f(a)\preceq g(a)
for all
a\in C
. In particular,
Aut(\langle{\mathbb{Z},\leq}\rangle)
is isomorphic to the abelian
\ell
-group
\langle{\mathbb{Z},\min,\max,+,-,0}\rangle
. A fundamental result of Holland states that every
\ell
-group embeds into
Aut(\langle{C,\preceq}\rangle)
, for some totally ordered set
\langle{C,\preceq}\rangle

[
24
]
.

For any class
\mathsf{K}
of
\ell
-groups and set of
\ell
-group equations
\mathrm{\Sigma}\cup\{s\approx t\}
, let
\mathrm{\Sigma}\models_{\mathrm{\mathsf{K}}}s\approx t
denote that for any homomorphism
h
from the algebra of
\ell
-group terms to some
L\in\mathsf{K}
, if
h(s^{\prime})=h(t^{\prime})
for all
s^{\prime}\approx t^{\prime}\in\mathrm{\Sigma}
, then
h(s)=h(t)
.

The Word Problem for an
\ell
-group with presentation
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
is decidable if and only if there exists an algorithm to decide if
\{r\approx e\mid r\in R\}\models_{\mathsf{LG}}s\approx t
for any
\ell
-group equation
s\approx t
(see, e.g.,
[
32
]
). Let us note also that deciding such consequences in
\mathsf{LG}
can be effectively reduced to deciding in
\mathsf{LG}
finitely many consequences of the form
\{r\approx e:r\in R\}\models_{\mathsf{LG}}e\leq t_{1}\vee\dots\vee t_{n}
, where
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
are group terms.

For a left-orderable group with a fixed presentation, the existence of a left-order containing a given finite set of elements corresponds to a consequence in
\mathsf{LG}
.

Theorem 9

(
[
9
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
G
be a left-orderable group with presentation
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
. For any elements
[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]
in
G
corresponding to elements
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
of the free group over
X
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\{[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]\}
does not extend to a left-order on
G
;

(2)

\{r\approx e:r\in R\}\models_{\mathsf{LG}}e\leq t_{1}\vee\dots\vee t_{n}
.

Combining this result with Proposition
2
yields the following relationship between the Identity Problem for a fully left-orderable group with a fixed presentation and the Word Problem for a corresponding
\ell
-group:

Corollary 10
.

Let
G
be a fully left-orderable group with presentation
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
.

(a)

For any elements
[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]
in
G
corresponding to elements
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
of the free group over
X
,

\displaystyle\qquad\quad e_{G}\in{\{[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]\}}^{+}\iff\{r\approx e:r\in R\}\models_{\mathsf{LG}}e\leq t_{1}\vee\dots\vee t_{n}.

(b)

The Identity Problem for
G
is decidable if and only if the Word Problem for the
\ell
-group with presentation
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
is decidable.

Since finitely generated torsion-free nilpotent groups are fully left-orderable (Theorem
4
) and have a decidable Identity Problem (Theorem
1
), we obtain:

Corollary 11
.

Let
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
be a finite presentation of a torsion-free nilpotent group. Then the
\ell
-group with finite presentation
\langle{X\mid R}\rangle
has a decidable Word Problem.

Let us turn our attention next to
o-groups
—
\ell
-groups with an underlying total order — and subdirect products of o-groups, referred to as
representable

\ell
-groups. The class of representable
\ell
-groups forms a variety of
\ell
-groups that is axiomatized relative to
\mathsf{LG}
by the equation
e\leq x\vee y{x}^{-1}{y}^{-1}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
for details).

Now let
\mathrm{\Sigma}
be any set of group equations. We denote by
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
the relatively free group over a set
X
of the variety of groups satisfying
\mathrm{\Sigma}
, and let
\mathsf{Rep}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
denote the variety of representable
\ell
-groups satisfying
\mathrm{\Sigma}
. In the case where
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
is bi-orderable, the existence of a bi-order containing a given finite set of elements corresponds to the validity of an equation in
\mathsf{Rep}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
.

Theorem 12

(
[
9
, Theorem 8]
)
.

Let
\mathrm{\Sigma}
be a set of group equations and let
X
be a set such that
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
is bi-orderable. For any
[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]\in F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
corresponding to
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in F(X)
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\{[t_{1}],\ldots,[t_{n}]\}
does not extend to a bi-order on
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
;

(2)

\mathsf{Rep}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\models e\leq t_{1}\lor\cdots\lor t_{n}
.

Combining this result with Proposition
2
yields the following:

Corollary 13
.

Let
\mathrm{\Sigma}
be a set of group equations.

(a)

For any set
X
such that
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
is fully bi-orderable and elements
[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]
in
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(X)
corresponding to
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in F(X)
,

\displaystyle e\in{\{[t_{1}],\dots,[t_{n}]\}}^{\circ}\iff\mathsf{Rep}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\models e\leq t_{1}\lor\cdots\lor t_{n}.

(b)

If
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\omega)
is fully bi-orderable, then the Normal Identity Problem for
F_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}(\omega)
is decidable if and only if
\mathsf{Rep}_{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
has a decidable equational theory.

We apply these results again in the setting of nilpotent groups, where
\mathrm{\Sigma}
is taken to be a set of group equations that defines the variety of nilpotent groups of class
c
. An
\ell
-group is said to be
nilpotent of class
c
if its group reduct is nilpotent of class
c
, and we let
\mathsf{NLG}_{c}
denote the variety of all such
\ell
-groups. We make use of the following result:

Theorem 14

(Koptytov
[
27
]
)
.

Any nilpotent
\ell
-group of class
c
that is finitely presented in
\mathsf{NLG}_{c}
has a decidable Word Problem.

In particular, every finitely generated free nilpotent
\ell
-group of class
c
is finitely presented in
\mathsf{NLG}_{c}
, so the equational theory of
\mathsf{NLG}_{c}
is decidable. Since nilpotent
\ell
-groups are representable (see
[
27
]
), and free nilpotent groups of class
c
are fully bi-orderable, Corollary
13
and Theorem
14
yield:

Corollary 15
.

The Normal Identity and Bi-Order Extension Problems are decidable for any finitely generated free nilpotent group of class
c
.

4.
Convex geometry and subsemigroups of nilpotent groups

The proof of Theorem
1
will rely on Proposition
19
, which combines algebraic characterizations of subsemigroups in nilpotent groups with convex geometry. In order to state and prove Proposition
19
, we first give some background on convex geometry and polytopes.

4.1.
Preliminaries

A
linear form
(or functional)
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
has the form
f(\mathbf{x})=\textbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}
, where
\textbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
hyperplane
is the set
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid f(\mathbf{x})=b\}
given by some non-zero linear form
f
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
.

A subset
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
convex
if
\lambda\mathbf{x}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{y}\in C
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in C
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Given a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its
convex hull
is the smallest convex set containing
S
. Equivalently, the convex hull is the set of
convex combinations
of elements of
S
:

\mathrm{Conv}(S):=\left\{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{s}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}\mathbf{s}_{k}\;\middle|\;k\in\mathbb{N},\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\text{ s.t.\ }\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1,\mathbf{s}_{i}\in S\right\}.

Interesting examples are polytopes, they can be defined in two equivalent ways:

1.

A
convex polytope
is the convex hull of finitely many points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

A
convex polytope
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that can be written as the intersection of finitely many half-spaces
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq a_{i}\}
, where
f_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are non-trivial linear forms and
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Thus any polytope
P
can be described in two ways:

1.

A
V-representation
for
P
is a finite set of points whose convex hull is
P
.

2.

A
H-representation
for
P
is a finite set of half-spaces
\{f_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq a_{i}\}
whose intersection is
P
.

It is algorithmically feasible to produce H-representations from V-representations (and vice versa). This is a classical problem, the
Facet Enumeration Problem
, solved in general using Fourier-Motzkin elimination. See for instance
[
40
, §1.2]
.

A
face
of
P
is the set of the form
\{\mathbf{x}\in P\mid f(\mathbf{x})=b\}
, where
P
is included in the half-space
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(\mathbf{x})\leq b\}
. A
facet
is a face of dimension
d-1
.

Theorem 16

(Carathéodory, see
[
40
, Prop. 1.15]
)
.

Fix
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Conv}(S)
, there exist
d+1
elements
\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{d}\in S
such that

\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Conv}(\{\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{d}\}).

Theorem 17

(Hahn-Banach Separation Theorem)
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex set and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C
. There exists a non-zero linear form
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\mathbf{x})\geq f(\mathbf{p})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in C
.

Finally, a result of relevance for later proofs is the following.

Lemma 18
.

A subgroup
H\leq\mathbb{Z}^{r}
,
r\geq 1
, has infinite-index if and only if there exists a non-zero homomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}
such that
H\leq\ker(f)
. In particular, this is equivalent to
H
belonging to a hyperplane.

Proof.

For the right-to-left direction, if
H\leq\ker(f)
for some non-zero homomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}
, then
[\mathbb{Z}^{r}:H]\geq[\mathbb{Z}^{r}:\ker(f)]=|\mathrm{Im}(f)|=\infty
.

For the left-to-right direction, if
H

Main technical result (Proposition

Given a group

5.
The Identity and Subgroup Problems in Nilpotent Groups (Proof of Theorem
1
)

In this section, we give an algorithm establishing Theorem
1
, which can be restated as:

Theorem
.

The Identity Problem and the Subgroup Problem, stated below, are uniformly decidable in the class of finitely generated nilpotent groups.

The Uniform Identity Problem is the following decision problem:

Input:

A finite presentation
G=\langle X\mid R\rangle
and a finite set

S\subset(X\cup X^{-1})^{*}
.

Output:

YES or NO to the question
‘Is the identity element
e_{G}
in
S^{+}
?’

The Uniform Subgroup Problem is the following decision problem:

Input:

A finite presentation
G=\langle X\mid R\rangle
and a finite set

S\subset(X\cup X^{-1})^{*}
.

Output:

YES or NO to the question
‘Is the subsemigroup
S^{+}
a subgroup of
G
?’
.

The proof of Theorem
1
consists of an algorithm, solving both problems, that relies on the equivalence of parts (1) and (4) of Proposition
19
.
Although decidability of the Subgroup Problem implies decidability of the Identity Problem (the subsemigroup generated by a set
S
contains the identity if and only if the subsemigroup generated by some non-empty finite subset of
S
is a subgroup, so we can apply the Subgroup Problem algorithm for all subsets of
S
), we mention both in this section separately, as an alternative way of solving the Identity Problem (but not the Subgroup Problem) is to appeal to Corollary
8
in step 4(i) of the algorithm, without any mention of Proposition
19
anywhere in the algorithm.

For convenience of exposition, we postpone the proofs of two key facts (Lemmas
20
and
21
) appearing in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We give an algorithm below that solves the Identity and the Subgroup Problems for the finitely presented nilpotent group

(0)

6.
Complexity of the Identity and Subgroups Problems and an alternative approach

The complexity of the algorithm for the Identity and Subgroup Problems (cf. Proof of Theorem
1
) is not always polynomial, since in Step (1) the basis for the torsion-free abelianisation of
G_{t}
, at each step
t
, can be exponential in the size of
X_{t}
(see for example
[
23
]
). This leads to the representations of words in
S_{t}
being potentially exponential over the presentation of the input group; hence, although our algorithm has a polynomial number of steps, in many of the steps we may deal with operands with very large values. Step (1) is the main culprit for an exponential explosion, as in Step (3) we can find an
H
-representation for
\mathrm{Conv}(\pi(S_{t}))
in
O(r_{t}nf)
operations, where
n=\left|S_{t}\right|
is the number of vertices and
f
is the number of facets
[
2
]
. Moreover,
f=O(n^{\lfloor r_{t}/2\rfloor})
by the McMullen Upper Bound Theorem (see
[
40
, §6.4]
).

We therefore suggest another algorithm, based on Dong’s
[
17
, Algorithm 1]
, which can run in polynomial time if a unitary matrix representation for the largest torsion-free quotient of the nilpotent group is given. We assume that for a fixed
G
, finding a unitary matrix representation is part of the preprocessing, so we treat this as an additive constant (albeit a significant one).

6.1.

We start with some preliminaries on nilpotent groups and Lie algebras (see
[
8
, Chapter 6]
and
[

]
for background).

For any torsion-free nilpotent group

, and (3) for all

. Furthermore, we can associate to every torsion-free nilpotent group

the corresponding Lie algebra

via the bijective map

For a set

Otherwise

Return NO to the Identity Problem.

The correctness of this algorithm relies on the chain of equivalences

e\notin S^{+}\;\iff\;C_{S}\neq\emptyset\;\iff\;(L_{S}\neq\emptyset\text{ or }R_{S}\neq\emptyset),

where
C_{S}
is the set of left-orders
\left\{\succ\;\in\mathrm{LO}(G_{\lambda})\;\big|\;\forall s\in S,\,s\succ e\right\}
(and
\mathrm{LO}(G_{\lambda})
denotes the set of all left-orders). Corollary
30
justifies the first equivalence.

We prove the second equivalence:

For the right-to-left direction, if
p\in R_{S}
(resp.
p\in L_{S}
), then type (2) orders defined with reference point
p
and
\sigma_{1}=+1
(resp.
\sigma_{1}=-1
) belong to
C_{S}
.

For the left-to-right direction, we suppose that
C_{S}\neq\emptyset
. From our classification of left-orders or from
[
36
]
, we see that
\mathrm{LO}(G_{\lambda})
is a Cantor set. The set
C_{S}
is open and non-empty, and therefore is uncountable. In particular, there exists an order
\succ
of type (2) in
C_{S}
with reference point
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. We deduce that
p\in R_{S}
if
\sigma_{1}=+1
, or
p\in L_{S}
if
\sigma_{1}=-1
.
∎

We end with a few remarks and directions for future research.

Question 32
.

Is
\mathrm{Aff}_{+}(\mathbb{Q})
fully left-orderable?

Note that
\mathrm{Aff}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
is not fully left-orderable, as the subgroup generated by
a\colon x\mapsto\pi x
and
b\colon x\mapsto x+1
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, which is not fully left-orderable (take
S=\{a^{2},b,(aba)^{-1}\}
). In this case, we may however wonder if each partial left-order
S^{+}
can always be extended to a left-order on
\langle S\rangle
.

Another interesting class of groups is
\mathrm{SOL}_{T}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes_{T}\mathbb{Z}
with
T\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
a hyperbolic matrix (that is, with trace
>2
). Left-orders on
\mathrm{SOL}
have been classified
[
36
, Section 3]
. It would be interesting to see if these groups are fully left-orderable and our tools can be used here and turned into an efficient algorithm for the Identity Problem, as this is a non-trivial case in
[
15
]
.

In order to answer these questions, it is important to note that one can restrict to finitely generated left-orders of finitely generated subgroups.

Proposition 33
.

Let
G
be a group. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

G
is fully left-orderable.

(2)

Every finitely generated subgroup of
G
is fully left-orderable.

(3)

For every finitely generated subsemigroup
S^{+}
of
G
, if
S^{+}
is a partial left-order (i.e.,
e\notin S^{+}
), then
S^{+}
extends to a left-order.

Proof.

Using Zorn’s lemma, every positive cone is included inside a maximal one. If
G
is not fully left-orderable, then there exists a maximal positive cone
P
such that
P\sqcup\{e\}\sqcup P^{-1}\neq G
. In particular, there exists
g\in G\setminus(P\sqcup\{e\}\sqcup P^{-1})
. By maximality, we have
e\in(P\cup\{g\})^{+}
and
e\in(P\cup\{g^{-1}\})^{+}
. In particular, there exists a finite subset
S\subseteq P
such that
e\in(S\cup\{g\})^{+}
and
e\in(S\cup\{g^{-1}\})^{+}
. Therefore
S^{+}
cannot be extended to a total order, despite
e\notin S^{+}
(since
S^{+}\subseteq P
). Moreover, the finitely generated subgroup
\langle S,g\rangle
is not fully left-orderable.
∎
