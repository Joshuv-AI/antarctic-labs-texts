Atherogenic lipid indices and metabolic syndrome in polycystic ovary syndrome: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Sujata Baidya, Pratibha Kandel, Smrity Rajkarnikar, Sagar Parajuli, Apeksha Niraula, Eans Tara Tuladhar, Raju Kumar Dubey, Mithileshwer Raut, Aseem Bhattarai, Poonam Koirala, Vijay Kumar Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is one of the causes of infertility in females. It is also associated with hyperandrogenism and hyperinsulinemia. With these hormonal imbalances, there can be an equally important risk of cardiometabolic consequences as a result of altered atherogenic lipid indices and metabolic syndrome which has not been much explored in context of Nepal. The purpose of this study was to delve into different lipid profile parameters to calculate atherogenic lipid indices to identify the metabolic syndrome (MetS) and cardiovascular risk in PCOS.MethodsWe enrolled 70 PCOS and 60 age-matched non-PCOS females visiting the Infertility Clinic at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital. After written informed consent, anthropometric measurements as well as a blood samples were taken and biochemical analysis of glucose, total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol and triglycerides were measured in Abbott ARCHITECT ci4100 autoanalyzer. Based on the examined variables, various formulas to assess atherogenic risk and metabolic syndrome [Castelli’s risk index I & II (CRI-I, CRI-II), atherogenic coefficient (AC), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), lipoprotein combined index (LCI), lipid accumulation product (LAP)] were applied. Descriptive and inferential analysis was carried in SPSS version 23.0. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered as significant.ResultsThe PCOS group had higher atherogenic lipid indices (CRI-I, CRI-II, AIP, AC, LCI and LAP) compared to their non-PCOS counterparts. The prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) was also higher in the PCOS (81.4%, n = 57) compared to non-PCOS (30%, n = 18). The LAP score was found to be an efficient indicator of MetS in the PCOS patients with an AUC of 0.829 (p < 0.001), with the sensitivity and specificity of 80% and 94.5% respectively.ConclusionsOur study showed a higher frequency of MetS and cardiovascular risk in PCOS as determined by higher atherogenic lipid indices than non PCOS. LAP score was the most efficient indicator of MetS.

Background

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine-metabolic disorder among reproductive age group females, which is characterized by hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovarian morphology, ovarian dysfunction, and hyperinsulinemia [1, 2]. Irrespective of body mass index (BMI), PCOS is frequently linked to atherogenic dyslipidemia with elevated LDL-cholesterol and non-HDL cholesterol [3]. Women with PCOS are more likely to have increased cardiovascular risk factors; demonstrated as an increase in BMI, insulin, triglyceride, total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, waist/hip ratio and systolic blood pressure while, a decrease in total HDL and HDL2-cholesterol levels [4]. These risk factors of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) constitute many elements of metabolic syndrome. There may be a vicious mesh of impaired glucose tolerance/type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, abdominal obesity, and hypertension [5].

About 38 to 88% of the women with PCOS are obese [6]. Insulin resistance with compensatory hyperinsulinemia is a cardinal feature of PCOS, which simulates metabolic syndrome. In a study, metabolic syndrome was reported in 46% of the PCOS-afflicted women [7]. Metabolic syndrome is the harbinger of Coronary Artery Disease (CAD), which manifests at an earlier age in PCOS [8]. Detection of insulin resistance beforehand can ameliorate the anticipated complications. Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) correlates well with the gold standard hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp (HEC) technique in the evaluation of insulin resistance [9]. However, the cut-offs for insulin resistance is lower in Asian population [10, 11] and even occurs at lower BMI with less obesity [12] which still needs to be worked on. Many methods used in the evaluation of insulin resistance are complex and expensive [13].

In a prospective cohort study by Inglesson et al. [14], with a 15-year follow-up results, it is reported that the apoB/apoA-I ratio predicted the incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD) well (HR,1.39; 1.23–1.58) with the similar risk ratios for total cholesterol/HDL-C (HR,1.39; 1.22–1.58 in men and HR,1.39;1.17–1.66 in women) and for LDL-C/HDL-C (HR,1.35; 1.18–1.54 for men and HR,1.36; 1.14–1.63 in women).

It is illustrated in various other studies that lipid ratios outperform the measurements of individual lipids in the prediction of cardiovascular risk as well as occurrence of metabolic syndrome [15–17]. In addition to that, a study by Wang et al. indicated that the discrepancies of misclassification of individuals with high and low levels of HDL-C and LDL-C for the lipid management might be overcome by using the ratio of total cholesterol to HDL-C as the preliminary screening tool [18].

On the other hand, with a combination of two factors; waist circumference (a measure of trunk fat) and fasting triglyceride, the lipid accumulation product (LAP) index is a useful parameter to determine the presence of insulin resistance [19]. The LAP index correlates well with HOMA-IR index in PCOS with a high positive predictive value of Insulin Resistance (IR) when LAP score surpasses 34.5 [20, 21]. The future cardiovascular risks as well as insulin resistance in PCOS can also be predicted by other lipid indices [13, 22].

Increasing sedentary lifestyles and food habits have briskly increased the rate of PCOS with co-existing CVDs. Unopposed elevated androgens in the post-menopausal state which simulates PCOS, precipitates an increased risk of cardiovascular disease in this cohort [23, 24]. With timely screening of PCOS and its complications, the risk of cardio-metabolic disorders can be reduced to a large extent [25].

Therefore, this study intended to determine the association of conveniently available atherogenic lipid indices with the status of metabolic syndrome in women with PCOS.

The minimum sample size calculated was 68. A total of 70 cases and 60 control samples were processed during the study period.

Inclusion criteria

Samples were collected from the women of reproductive age group visiting the infertility clinic at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital (TUTH), with primary or secondary infertility and were diagnosed with PCOS based on Rotterdam’s criteria 2004 [28]; with any two of the three features: (i) hyperandrogenism (clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism); (ii) ovulatory dysfunction; (iii) polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasonographical examination ≥ 20 follicles per ovary in either ovary and/or ovarian volume > 10 ml in at least one ovary), with exclusion of other relevant disorders. On the other hand, control samples were selected from the patients visiting the same department without PCOS-like features.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded patients presenting with thyroid disorders, Cushing’s syndrome, non-classical adrenal hyperplasia, drug-induced androgen excess, habit of smoking and those unwilling to participate.

Study variables

Blood pressure was measured manually in the upper arm using a calibrated aneroid sphygmomanometer by auscultation technique. Anthropometric measurements were recorded following CDC’s recommendation published in anthropometry procedures manual 2007 by the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [29]. The anthropometric components measured were; weight, height, and waist circumference of all the subjects. For the waist circumference, the hip area was palpated to locate the right ilium of the pelvis and marked. The measuring tape was extended around the waist and positioned in a horizontal plane and the measurement was recorded in centimeter (cm) scale. Body mass index (BMI) i.e. Quetelet Index [30] was measured as: [Body weight in kg/(Height in meter)2]. The WHO criteria was adopted to classify the individuals according to BMI [31]; Underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), Normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), Overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2), Obese (≥ 30 kg/m2), Obese Type 1(30–40 kg/m2), Obese Type 2 (40.1–50 kg/m2) and Obese Type 3(> 50 kg/m2).

Laboratory testing

Sample collection and preparation

Following standard laboratory procedure, venipuncture was performed to obtain five ml of blood in a gel vial after an overnight fast (8–12 h) between 8:00 and 10:00 h. Serum samples were separated within half an hour by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min. Routine biochemical investigations were performed on the same day, including serum glucose, lipid profiles [total cholesterol (TC), HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-cholesterol (LDL-C), triglyceride (Tg)]. To correlate with the follicular phase, samples for gonadal parameters; luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (E2) and total testosterone (T) were collected on the second day of menstrual cycle. In amenorrhoeic women, recent ovulation was excluded with progesterone measurement (> 5 ng/ml).

All the biochemical analytes were measured in Abbott ARCHITECT ci4100 Integrated System Abbott Park, IL 60,064 USA. The blood glucose was measured by hexokinase/G-6-PDH method. Total cholesterol was measured using the enzymatic cholesterol oxidase/peroxidase method, while LDL-C was assayed using a homogenous method using liquid selective detergent for direct LDL without the requirement for off-line treatment or centrifugation steps. Serum triglyceride was assayed using glycerol phosphate oxidase/peroxidase method. VLDL-cholesterol was computed as: Triglyceride in mmol/L ÷ 2.2. The ultra-HDL homogeneous assay was used to directly measure HDL-C in serum. Serum LH and FSH were measured using CMIA (a two-step immunoassay). Serum estradiol and Total Testosterone were measured using a delayed one-step immunoassay based on CMIA technology. For the validation of the results, internal quality control sera, two control levels (2 and 3) provided by RANDOX (1393UN, 1104UE, 2179EC and 2228EC), were run for each lot.

Statistical analysis

The data was entered in Microsoft Excel 2015 and SPSS version 23.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences for Windows version; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL) was used for the statistical analyses. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of the data. Based on data distribution, descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range. The chi-square test was employed to analyze the differences in dichotomous variables. The Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test and independent sample T-test were used to compare the study variables between cases and controls. The spearman’s correlation coefficients were computed. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05 as 5% error was allowable.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) were calculated. The area under ROC ≥ 0.8 was considered excellent.

Discussion

The pathophysiology of PCOS is augmented by insulin resistance and ensuing hyperinsulinemia [37, 38]. The sequential pathologies simulate metabolic syndrome indicating risk towards debilitating cardiovascular consequences. The efficient monitoring of the atherogenic lipid changes can aid in early management of symptoms for the better prognosis of PCOS patients. In this hospital-based cross-sectional study, the levels of atherogenic lipid indices in PCOS population were compared with non-PCOS controls on account of its association with the metabolic syndrome. A considerable proportion of the population in our study had metabolic syndrome accounting to 81.4% among PCOS cohorts. This is almost two times the statistics reported in other studies, i.e. 43% in a study by Apridonidze et al. [39] and 46% in a study by Glueck et al. [7] A high prevalence of MetS in our study may be attributed to the genetic predisposition of the South Asian population, body composition (increased visceral fat accumulation), and socioeconomic transition with decreased recreational exercises.

In our study, a decrease in HDL-cholesterol was observed in the majority of participants with MetS in both cases and controls (Table 4), while an elevated waist circumference and plasma glucose were the parameters significantly associated. The insulin resistance in PCOS adds up the burden in metabolic health in PCOS patients having concurrent obesity [40].

The mean BMI and waist circumference of PCOS women was significantly higher as compared to the control groups (Table 1). The higher mean value of the waist circumference in PCOS cohort indicates increased visceral fat accumulation in this population. Triglyceride, which is an indicator of visceral obesity, is also significantly higher in PCOS group as compared to the control population (Table 1). However, the mean values of lipid profile individually were only marginally high and not very high beyond the desirable range. Meanwhile, the mean value of lipid indices/ratios was comparatively higher in PCOS group. These findings align with those of Xiang et al. [13] On the contrary to their study, we could not establish any lipid ratios other than the LAP score as a predictive measure in identifying metabolic syndrome in PCOS.

Our findings illustrate that the LAP score supports the prediction of MetS in PCOS in accord with the findings of Gui et al. [41], which depicted good predictive correlation of the LAP score in predicting MetS conducted among 1672 women. The LAP index showed good predictive accuracy in the insulin resistance risk assessment comparable to the HOMA-IR, QUICKI and FG-IR in other studies [42]. Besides, the use of simple lipid indices is more convenient in early diagnosis of MetS [43].

In addition to the LAP score, triglyceride glucose index (TyG-index) and their other correlation indices (TyG-BMI, TyG-WC and TyG-WHtR) were also good predictive markers of metabolic syndrome in their study. We identified an optimal cut-off value 32.1 for the LAP score in tracing MetS which is higher as compared to Gui et al. [41] establishing a cut-off value of 23.72 in overall female population.

On the other hand, the study by Gasevic et al. [15] postulated that Triglyceride/HDL-C is a superior marker in identification of MetS as compared to CRI-I (TC/HDL-C), CRI-II (LDL-C/HDL-C) and AC (non-HDL-C/HDL-C). The Triglyceride/HDL-C ratio is also held as an accurate measure for the assessment of MetS in the study by Cordero et al. with a cutoff of 1.65 for women and sensitivity and specificity of 80% and 78%, respectively [17]. Here, we have used AIP (log10(Tg/HDL)) which showed sensitivity and specificity of 56.1% and 69.2% respectively at cutoff of 0.22.

We observed that with optimal cut-off of 3.6, the performance of atherogenic coefficient (Non-HDL-C/HDL-C) had sensitivity and specificity of 61.4% and 61.5% respectively. This finding was slightly lower than the study report by Kim et al. [44] where a cut-off of 3.39 in males and 2.89 in females, the sensitivity and specificity ranged from 66.7% to 71.8 and 75.7% to 78.1%, respectively. Their findings postulated AC as a good marker in identifying IR and MetS.

AIP rendered as a surrogate marker of small LDL particle size reflects obesity, hyperinsulinemia, high CRP status and a strong predictor of CHD [45]. We observed that AIP was significantly higher in individuals with PCOS compared to the control population.

In this study, we could not assess the gold standard measures of insulin resistance which could have added essence to the existing data; however, we attempted to compare the common lipoprotein analytes that can be routinely assayed in a limited resource setting and at a lower cost. The correlation of HOMA-IR to validate the LAP scores is recommended in further studies in the present population. With the prevalence of metabolic syndrome galloping in parallel with the incidences of obesity, we have tried to report the cardiometabolic aspect in one of the vulnerable groups in females, the PCOS. PCOS is aligned with long term cardiovascular complications associated with pregnancy as well. The precipitation of these complications could not be documented in our study.

Conclusion

We conclude that atherogenic lipid indices are higher in PCOS compared to non-PCOS. MetS was predominant in PCOS than non-PCOS. The lipid indices can also predict MetS. LAP score was found be the best identifier of metabolic syndrome. Higher atherogenic lipid indices and predominant MetS in PCOS make this group vulnerable to cardiovascular risk if not timely treated.
