Universal Fourier Neural Operators for Micromechanics
Binh Huy Nguyen, Matti Schneider

Abstract
Solving cell problems in homogenization is hard, and available deep-learning frameworks fail to match the speed and generality of traditional computational frameworks. More to the point, it is generally unclear what to expect of machine-learning approaches, let alone single out which approaches are promising. In the work at hand, we advocate Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) for micromechanics, empowering them by insights from computational micromechanics methods based on the fast Fourier transform (FFT). We construct an FNO surrogate mimicking the basic scheme foundational for FFT-based methods and show that the resulting operator predicts solutions to cell problems with arbitrary stiffness distribution only subject to a material-contrast constraint up to a desired accuracy. In particular, there are no restrictions on the material symmetry like isotropy, on the number of phases and on the geometry of the interfaces between materials. Also, the provided fidelity is sharp and uniform, providing explicit guarantees leveraging our physical empowerment of FNOs. To show the desired universal approximation property, we construct an FNO explicitly that requires no training to begin with. Still, the obtained neural operator complies with the same memory requirements as the basic scheme and comes with runtimes proportional to classical FFT solvers. In particular, large-scale problems with more than 100 million voxels are readily handled. The goal of this work is to underline the potential of FNOs for solving micromechanical problems, linking FFT-based methods to FNOs. This connection is expected to provide a fruitful exchange between both worlds. Keywords: Fourier Neural Operator; Lippmann-Schwinger Solvers; FFT-based Computational Micromechanics; Universal Approximation; Deep Learning

Introduction

1.1
State of the art

To design components and structures made of microstructured materials, in particular in case of long-term use or when operating in a nonlinear regime, computational approaches based on the mathematical theory of homogenization
[
1
]
form an integral part. Such techniques split the physical problem into a macroscropic problem which involves a
homogenized
model that is computationally tractable and a microscopic model which resolves the microstructure and permits to identify the parameters of the homogenized model appropriately. This splitting approach hides a significant "amount" of difficulty, encoded by the complexity of the microstructure, in the resolution of the microscopic problem. With solid mechanics applications in mind, these challenges include:

1.

Discontinuous materials, leading to solution fields with (weak) discontinuities across interfaces,

2.

Complex arrangement of the microstructure phases, in particular for materials used in industry,

3.

Randomness of the microstructure.

Computational multiscale methods have a long history, and we refer to the review article
[
2
]
for an in-depth overview. We just summarize some key findings. Due to challenges one and two, traditional interface-conforming finite elements may not be the best choice when optimizing runtime vs. accuracy. Rather, regular-grid based methods
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
lead the way. When restricted to regular grid, traditional finite element (FE) discretizations and solvers lose their edge
[
6
]
, and spectral
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
or finite-difference discretizations
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
combined with solvers based on the fast Fourier transform (FFT)
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
give rise to highly efficient options for dealing with micromechanics problems. To handle item 3, the representative volume element (RVE) method is used
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, which requires solving
multiple
microscopic cell problems on domains of varying size
[
20
,
21
]
.

This work is concerned with neural techniques for solving homogenization problems. The advantages of approaches based on neural networks were demonstrated for high-dimensional and parametric PDEs
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
where they may overcome the "curse of dimensionality"
[
25
]
. However, for low to moderate dimensions, traditional discretizations and solvers typically outperform neural-network approaches. Rather, deep learning may be used to improve the associated discretizations and solvers
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

In the context of homogenization problems, the use of neural networks was reported. Due to the considerable size of the domains to be treated, convolutional neural networks are favored and applied to metal-matrix composites
[
29
]
, porous materials
[
30
]
and random checkerboards
[
31
]
. However, the trained networks use a fixed resolution, require excessive training and are unlikely to compete with dedicated solvers
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. Only under very restrictive circumstances, such techniques may shine. For example, Zheng et al.
[
35
]
consider the inverse design problem for truss-based cellular metamaterials, and report a significant speed up compared to full-field simulations.

To address the shortcomings of traditional deep-learning approaches, the attention shifted from neural networks – which approximate
functions
– to neural operators which should approximate
operators
, i.e., mappings between function spaces. From an abstract point of view, such a strategy appears to be more natural when solving ordinary and partial differential equation, as typically the solution is sought in dependence of the loading and the boundary conditions. Such operator-learning frameworks include the Deep-O-Net
[
36
]
and neural operators
[
37
,
38
]
. Whereas the former represents operators between infinite-dimensional spaces via collocation, i.e., point evaluation of functions, the latter augments the weights and biases traditionally used in neural network architectures by an integral operator. This non-local term complements the action of the activation function and the weights/biases which is applied locally, i.e., at every continuum point. For reasons of efficiency and practical applicability of neural operator, instead of a general integral operator, a
convolution operator
may be used, whose action is readily computed by the FFT. The so-called Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs)
[
39
]

appear to balance simplicity with sufficient expressivity, as demonstrated by mathematical approximation results
[
40
]
. Also, such operator-learning techniques may be infused with physical knowledge, giving rise to so-called physics-informed operator learning
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.

Recently, FNOs were applied to homogenization problems in conductivity and linear elasticity. Bhattacharya et al.
[
44
]
consider the task of learning the temperature field solving the cell problem as a function of the microscopic conductivity field. They provide both a theoretical framework and computational examples to demonstrate their findings. Their approximation result ensures that the temperature field may be learned either uniformly on a compact set of coefficients or on average for an ensemble of coefficients. Wang et al.
[
45
]
train an FNO to provide an initial guess for micromechanical cell problems with linear elastic materials to be solved by the finite-element method.
Harandi et al.
[
46
]
use the FFT to train a conventional neural network with a Lippmann-Schwinger based loss function. Contributing to physics-informed neural operators, Zhang-Guilleminot
[
47
]
devised a label-free FNO for homogenizing hyperelastic materials with the stored elastic energy as the loss function.

1.2
Contributions

The work at hand targets the principal difficulties encountered when using conventional deep-learning frameworks for micromechanics problems in elasticity:

1.

Severe restrictions on the local elastic stiffness tensors, e.g., isotropy.

2.

Grid dependence of the trained network or the ability to solve rather small grid sizes only.

3.

Excessive resources required for the training – both in terms of time and energy usage – in particular compared to the online use.

4.

Restrictions to specific microstructures materials, e.g., porous materials or matrix-inclusion composites with specific type of reinforcements.

To address these issues, we consider one of the most efficient frameworks currently available for computational homogenization, FFT-based micromechanics
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
, and transfer insights from their construction to the design of an FNO. The main message of the article is the following:

What can be done with FFT solvers, can also be done with FNOs.

As FFT-based solvers serve as the current gold standard for computational homogenization, the framework of FNOs has the potential to serve as a real improvement to current computational practice. As will become clear below, we take the simplest FFT solver, the basic scheme
[
7
,
8
,
48
]
, and construct a corresponding FNO. However, also alternative linear and nonlinear solvers like polarization schemes
[
13
,
14
,
49
]
, conjugate gradient methods
[
15
,
50
,
51
]
or Quasi-Newton approaches
[
52
,
53
,
16
]
could be re-cast as FNOs.

As our main result, we establish the following: We fix a material contrast
\kappa
a priori and construct, for pre-defined error margin
\delta
, an FNO
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
with the following properties:

1.

For every (unit) macroscopic strain
\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}
and
every
stiffness distribution
\mathds{C}
with a contrast not greater than
\kappa
, the output
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C})
provides a displacement field which is
\delta
-close to the exact solution. In particular, there are no restrictions other than the contrast bound on the form of the elasticity tensor, the arrangement and the number of phases and the geometry of the interface.

2.

The FNO
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
may be applied to any voxel grid.

3.

There is no training involved in setting up the operator
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
.

The first item provides, in particular, a
uniform
approximation results for the solutions of micromechanical problems for general coefficients. This result differs strongly from Bhattacharya et al.
[
44
]
who provide a result for a compact subset of coefficients only. The third item is not a direct feature, but rather a byproduct of our construction: As the homogenization-learning problem is complicated and non-convex, showing approximation results requires constructing a competitor more or less explicitly. It is expected that actual training would significantly enhance the capabilities of the framework.

Our main idea is to interpret the basic scheme of Moulinec-Suquet
[
7
,
8
]
– which has the form

\bm{\varepsilon}^{k+1}=\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\mathbb{\Gamma}^{0}:(\mathds{C}-\mathds{C}^{0}):\bm{\varepsilon}^{k}

(1.1)

for a reference material
\mathds{C}^{0}
and the Eshelby-Green operator
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{0}
– as a single layer of an FNO. As the basic scheme (
1.1
) converges for
k\rightarrow\infty
, stacking such layers should give rise to approximations with increasing fidelity. For the convenience of the reader, the properties of the basic scheme (
1.1
) are recalled in section
2
.

It appears natural to interpret the action of the basic scheme (
1.1
) as an FNO - the operation is affine linear in the strain
\bm{\varepsilon}^{k}
, and a single convolution operator is evaluated per iteration (
1.1
). However, there is a caveat. The goal of the sought FNO is to take the local field of stiffness tensors
\mathds{C}
as the input and provide the solution of the micromechanics problem as the output. Storing the stiffness
\mathds{C}
in a register gives rise to the update

\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{\varepsilon}^{k+1}\\
\mathds{C}^{k+1}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\mathbb{\Gamma}^{0}:(\mathds{C}^{k}-\mathds{C}^{0}):\bm{\varepsilon}^{k}\\
\mathds{C}^{k}\end{array}\right],

(1.2)

which is nonlinear due to the double contraction

(\mathds{C}^{k},\bm{\varepsilon}^{k})\mapsto\mathds{C}^{k}:\bm{\varepsilon}^{k}.

(1.3)

Therefore, the sought FNO needs to have learned this nonlinearity. In section
3
, we provide conditions on the neural-network approximation to the function (
1.3
) which permit us to conclude the desired statement on the constructed FNO:

Theorem 1.1
.

For every periodic unit cell
Y
, every pair of lower and upper bounds
0<\alpha_{-}<\alpha_{+}
on the material stiffness, every strain bound
\eps_{0}>0
and every error margin
\delta^{\texttt{target}}>0
, there is an FNO

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}:\textrm{Sym}(d)\times\mathcal{M}_{Y}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})\rightarrow H^{1}_{\#}(Y)^{d},

(1.4)

s.t. the estimate

\left\|\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C})-\mathcal{S}(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C})\right\|_{H^{1}_{\#}(Y)^{d}}\leq\delta^{\texttt{target}}

(1.5)

holds for all macroscopic strains
\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
obeying the bound

\|\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|\leq\eps_{0}

(1.6)

and all stiffness distributions

\mathds{C}\in\mathcal{M}_{Y}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+}),

(1.7)

and where
\mathcal{S}
refers to the exact solution operator, i.e., for all macroscopic strains
\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
and all stiffnesses (
1.7
), the displacement field
\bm{u}=\mathcal{S}(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C})\in H^{1}_{\#}(Y)^{d}
solves the corrector equation

\textrm{div }\mathds{C}:\left(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{s}\bm{u}\right)=\bm{0}.

(1.8)

The main technical tools here are perturbation estimates which turn the constructed FNO into a contraction. The principal difficulty is that neural networks are typically only learned on compact domains. The operation (
1.3
) may be confronted with strains of arbitrary magnitude, for instance due to a singularity at a material interface with a corner or a kink. Thus, the error made by disregarding such large strain values needs to be quantified with care.

We discuss a specific approximation to the double contraction (
1.3
) in section
4
, where we make use of the rectified linear unit (ReLU) as a reasonably simple and explicit activation function. In fact, conventional sigmoid activations may be used for learning the mapping (
1.3
) via conventional training, as well, but we chose to remain fully explicit. More precisely, our construction is based on the approximation of the multiplication of two real numbers by a ReLU-network introduced by Yarotsky
[
54
]
.

We showcase our construction in section
5
via dedicated computational examples. We demonstrate that the construction – which was designed for theoretical purposes in the first place – gives rise to an FNO which comes with an execution time that is on the same order of magnitude as FFT-based solvers, the current gold standard for computational homogenization.

2
Homogenization in linear elasticity

2.1
What is given and what is sought

We consider a fixed periodic cell

Y=[0,L_{1}]\times[0,L_{2}]\times\cdots\times[0,L_{d}]

(2.1)

in
d
spatial dimensions. We denote by
\textrm{Sym}(d)
the vector space of symmetric
d\times d
tensors, and we let
\textrm{Sym}(\textrm{Sym}(d))
refer to the vector space of linear mappings on the vector space
\textrm{Sym}(d)
which are furthermore self-adjoint w.r.t. the Frobenius inner product

\langle\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\rangle=\textrm{tr}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}),\quad\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(2.2)

where
tr
refers to the trace. The central object of study is the following. For positive constants
\alpha_{\pm}
, we denote by

\mathcal{M}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})=\left\{\mathds{C}\in L^{\infty}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(\textrm{Sym}(d)))\,\middle|\,\alpha_{-}\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\leq\bm{\varepsilon}:\mathds{C}(\bm{x}):\bm{\varepsilon}\leq\alpha_{+}\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|^{2},\quad\text{a.e. }\bm{x}\in Y,\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)\right\}

(2.3)

the set of periodic fields of elasticity tensors with a material contrast

\kappa=\frac{\alpha_{+}}{\alpha_{-}}.

(2.4)

As the set (
2.3
) is empty unless the inequality
\alpha_{-}\leq\alpha_{+}
is satisfied, the material contrast (
2.4
) is always greater or equal to unity.

For every macroscopic strain
\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
and fixed stiffness
\mathds{C}\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})
, we seek the periodic displacement field

\bm{u}\in H^{1}_{\#}(Y)^{d}\equiv\left\{\bm{u}\in H^{1}(Y)\text{ periodic}\,\middle|\,\int_{Y}\bm{u}(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}=\bm{0}\right\}

(2.5)

with vanishing mean, s.t. the balance equation

\textrm{div }\mathds{C}:\left[\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{s}\bm{u}\right]=\bm{0}

(2.6)

is satisfied in
H^{-1}_{\#}(Y)^{d}
, where
\nabla^{s}
stands for the symmetrized gradient. By standard arguments, it may be shown the the solution (
2.5
) is unique, and satisfies the a-priori estimate

\|\nabla^{s}\bm{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{+}}{\alpha_{-}}}\|\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\|

(2.7)

with the
L^{2}
-norm

\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\frac{1}{\text{vol}(Y)}\int_{Y}\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\|^{2}\,d\bm{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d)).

(2.8)

2.2
The Lippmann-Schwinger framework

Since its inception by Zeller-Dederichs
[
55

and Kröner
[

[
57

[
7

8
]

[
58

59
]
, where we restrict to a reference material proportional to the identity
[
48
]
.

For any prescribed strain
\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
, stiffness field
\mathds{C}\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})
and positive constant
\alpha_{0}>0
, we consider the associated Lippmann-Schwinger operator

\mathcal{L}(\cdot;\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C},\alpha_{0})\in L(L^{2}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d))),

(2.9)

acting via

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\varepsilon};\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C},\alpha_{0})=\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\mathbb{\Gamma}:(\mathds{C}-\mathds{C}^{0}):\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d)),

(2.10)

with the reference material
\mathds{C}^{0}\in\textrm{Sym}(\textrm{Sym}(d))
with action

\mathds{C}^{0}:\bm{\varepsilon}=\alpha_{0}\,\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in\textrm{Sym}(d),

(2.11)

and the Eshelby-Green operator
\mathbb{\Gamma}
is defined via

\mathbb{\Gamma}:\bm{\tau}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\hat{\mathbb{\Gamma}}(\tilde{\bm{\xi}}):\hat{\bm{\tau}}(\bm{\xi})e^{\texttt{i}\,\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\bm{x}},\quad\bm{x}\in Y,\quad\bm{\tau}\in L^{2}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d)),

(2.12)

with the complex unit
i
, the Fourier coefficients

\hat{\bm{\tau}}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{1}{\textrm{vol}(Y)}\int_{Y}\bm{\tau}(\bm{x})e^{-\texttt{i}\,\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\bm{x}}\,d\bm{x},

(2.13)

the scaled frequencies

\tilde{\xi}_{j}=\frac{2\pi}{L_{j}}\,\xi_{j},\quad j=1,2,\ldots,d,

(2.14)

and the tensor-valued function

\hat{\mathbb{\Gamma}}:\mathds{R}^{d}\rightarrow L(\textrm{Sym}(d)),

(2.15)

defined by
\hat{\mathbb{\Gamma}}(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
for vanishing vector
\bm{d}
and by

\hat{\mathbb{\Gamma}}(\bm{d}):\bm{\tau}=\frac{\bm{d}\otimes\left(\bm{\tau}\bm{d}\right)}{\|\bm{d}\|^{2}}+\frac{\left(\bm{\tau}\bm{d}\right)\otimes\bm{d}}{\|\bm{d}\|^{2}}-\frac{(\bm{d}\cdot\bm{\tau})\bm{d}\,\bm{d}\otimes\bm{d}}{\|\bm{d}\|^{4}}

(2.16)

for non-zero
\bm{d}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
.

2.10

2.10

. The third part about the
L^{p}
-estimates, where we use the following
L^{p}
-norm on strain fields

\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{p}}=\left(\frac{1}{\text{vol}(Y)}\int_{Y}\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})\|^{p}\,d\bm{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in L^{p}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d)),\quad 1\leq p<\infty,

(2.17)

2.2
) inside the

.

3
Fourier neural operators via Lippmann-Schwinger solvers

3.1
Fourier neural operators

Figure 1
:
Schematic representation of the FNO topology (
3.2
).

For a given domain
D\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
, a Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
[
39
,
40
,
44
]
is a mapping

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{a}})\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{u}}),\quad d_{a},d_{u}\in\mathds{N}_{+},

(3.1)

between Banach spaces of vector-valued functions on the domain
D
which has the form of a composition

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\texttt{P}_{\theta}\circ\texttt{L}_{\theta,L}\circ\cdots\texttt{L}_{\theta,2}\circ\texttt{L}_{\theta,1}\circ\texttt{E}_{\theta},\quad L\in\mathds{N}_{+},

(3.2)

of a lifting/extension operator

\texttt{E}_{\theta}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{a}})\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}})

(3.3)

with
d_{v}\in\mathds{N}_{+}
,
d_{v}\geq d_{u}
, neural-operator layers

\texttt{L}_{\theta,\ell}:\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}})\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}})

(3.4)

of the form

\texttt{L}_{\theta,\ell}(\bm{v})=\sigma(\bm{W}_{\ell}\,\bm{v}+\bm{b}_{\ell}+\bm{K}_{\ell}\star\bm{v}),\quad\bm{v}\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}}),

(3.5)

with an activation function
\sigma
, and a projection operator

\texttt{P}_{\theta}:\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}})\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{u}}).

(3.6)

Here, the weights
\bm{W}_{\ell}\in\mathds{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
are matrices, the biases
\bm{b}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}})
are functions and the kernel operator

\left(\bm{K}_{\ell}\star\bm{v}\right)(\bm{x})=\int_{D}\bm{K}_{\ell}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\,\bm{v}(\bm{y})\,d\bm{y}

(3.7)

acts via convolution with a
\mathds{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
-matrix valued function
\bm{K}_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,2,\ldots,L
. The input- and output layers (
3.3
) and (
3.6
) are typically taken as locally acting neural networks.

The name FNOs stems from the fact that the convolution (
3.5
) with the kernel
\bm{K}_{\ell}
may be equivalently expressed via a multiplication in Fourier space with a
\mathds{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
-matrix valued function Fourier multipliers
\hat{\bm{K}}_{\ell}
. Fig.
1
depicts a schematic of the FNO construction.

A few remarks are at hand.

1.

We will always consider a rectangular domain
Y
, see eq. (
2.1
), and consider periodic boundary conditions when evaluating the convolution (
3.7
).

2.

For the application at hand, we will consider a few simplifications to reduce the complexity of the FNO. For a start, we will assume that the activation function
\sigma
is applied to
some
of the components in the Fourier layer (
3.5
) only. This assumption does not restrict generality, as the identity mapping may be approximated to arbitrary precision by a neural network. Secondly, we will use a neural network for the input layer (
3.3
) instead of the local lifting operation originally proposed. As discussed in Kovachki et al.
[

, such an operation may be represented by a deeper FNO at the expense of simplicity. Similarly, we will consider an output layer (

) of the form

\texttt{E}_{\theta}(\bm{v})=\bm{W}_{L+1}\,\bm{v}+\bm{b}_{L+1}+\bm{K}_{L+1}\star\bm{v},\quad\bm{v}\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}}),

(3.8)

with a matrix
\bm{W}_{L+1}\in\mathds{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}
, a function
\bm{b}_{L+1}\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{u}})
and a
\mathds{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}
-matrix valued function
\bm{K}_{L+1}
. Such a layer could also be approximated by a deeper FNO with only local and affine input as well as output layers.

3.

Expanding a general function
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathds{R}^{d_{v}})
into a Fourier series

\bm{v}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\hat{\bm{v}}(\tilde{\bm{\xi}})\,e^{\texttt{i}\,\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\bm{x}},\quad\bm{x}\in Y

(3.9)

with Fourier coefficients

\hat{\bm{v}}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{1}{\textrm{vol}(Y)}\int_{Y}\bm{v}(\bm{x})e^{-\texttt{i}\,\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\bm{x}}\,d\bm{x},

(3.10)

and the scaled frequencies (
2.14
),
the action of the convolution operator (
3.7
) in Fourier space reads

\left(\bm{K}_{\ell}\star\bm{v}\right)(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\hat{\bm{K}_{\ell}}(\bm{\xi})\hat{\bm{v}}(\bm{\xi})e^{\texttt{i}\,\tilde{\bm{\xi}}\cdot\bm{x}},\quad\bm{x}\in Y,

(3.11)

with suitable Fourier coefficients

\hat{\bm{K}_{\ell}}:\mathds{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathds{C}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}

(3.12)

which satisfy the condition

\hat{\bm{K}_{\ell}}(-\bm{\xi})=\overline{\hat{\bm{K}_{\ell}}(\bm{\xi})},\quad\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d},

(3.13)

ensuring that the function
\bm{K}_{\ell}
has real-valued components, and where we use the complex conjugation
z\mapsto\bar{z}
.

4.

We follow Kovachki
[
40
]
and do not assume a compact support of the Fourier coefficients (
3.12
), in line with the original neural operator definition
[
37
]
in contrast to other treatments
[
39
,
44
]
. A restriction

\hat{\bm{K}_{\ell}}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{0}\text{ for all frequencies $\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}$ with }|\xi_{j}|\leq\frac{N}{2},\quad j=1,2,\ldots,d,

(3.14)

is not able to handle all input stiffnesses
\mathds{C}
in the set
\mathcal{M}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})
, for trivial reasons. Suppose that a particular inhomogeneous stiffness
\mathds{C}\in\mathcal{M}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})
is fixed, and let
\bm{\varepsilon}=\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{s}\bm{u}
denote the unique solution to the Lippmann-Schwinger equation, i.e., a fixed point of the Lippmann-Schwinger operator (
2.10
). For
K\in\mathds{N}
, consider the rescaled stiffness

\tilde{\mathds{C}}(\bm{x})=\mathds{C}(2^{K}\,\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in Y.

(3.15)

The stiffness
\tilde{\mathds{C}}
is also periodic and defines an element of the space
\mathcal{M}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})
. The corresponding (unique) solution
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}
has the form

\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{x})=\bm{\varepsilon}(2^{K}\,\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in Y.

(3.16)

In particular, the Fourier coefficients of the field
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}
are non-zero only in the case

\widehat{\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}}(2^{K}\bm{\xi})=\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}(\bm{\xi}),\quad\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}.

(3.17)

As a result, in case the power
2^{K}
is chosen to exceed
N
, we have

\widehat{\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})=\bm{0}\text{ for all frequencies $\bm{\xi}\in\mathds{Z}^{d}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}$ with }|\xi_{j}|\leq\frac{N}{2},\quad j=1,2,\ldots,d.

(3.18)

In particular, the solution
\widehat{\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}}
cannot be represented on the restricted lattice (
3.14
), no matter how large the parameter
N
is chosen in advance! To sum up: Restricting the lattice (
3.14
) to be compactly supported in the FNO layer (
3.5
) implies a resolution constraint on the input stiffnesses (
2.3
) to be treated. In case all stiffnesses (
2.3
) are to be treated, such a constraint must not be enforced!

3.2
A neural fixed-point method

To construct an expressive FNO, we take the Lippmann-Schwinger operator (
2.10
)

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\varepsilon};\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C},\alpha_{0})=\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\mathbb{\Gamma}:\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}(\mathds{C}-\mathds{C}^{0}):\bm{\varepsilon},\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d)),

(3.19)

as our blueprint. More precisely, we approximate the double-contraction operator

\bm{\tau}:L(\textrm{Sym}(d))\times\textrm{Sym}(d)\rightarrow\textrm{Sym}(d),\quad(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon})\mapsto\mathds{T}:\bm{\varepsilon},

(3.20)

by a neural network, i.e., work with a more or less abstract (finite-dimensional) operator

\bm{\tau}_{\theta}:L(\textrm{Sym}(d))\times\textrm{Sym}(d)\rightarrow\textrm{Sym}(d).

(3.21)

Then, we set up the Fourier neural operator

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon};\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C},\alpha_{0})=\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}-\mathbb{\Gamma}:\bm{\tau}_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}(\mathds{C}-\mathds{C}^{0}),\bm{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\bm{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(Y;\textrm{Sym}(d)).

(3.22)

To proceed, we consider a few assumptions satisfied by the approximation (
3.21
).

Assumption 1
.

For any positive constant
M\geq 1
and every error margin
\delta>0
, there is a positive constant
C>0
, independent of the parameters
M
and
\delta
, and a neural network (
3.21
), s.t.,

(i)

The inequality

\left\|\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon})-\mathds{T}:\bm{\varepsilon}\right\|\leq\delta\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|

(3.23)

holds for all tensors
\mathds{T}\in L(\textrm{Sym}(d))
and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
satisfying the constraints

\|\mathds{T}\|\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|\leq M.

(3.24)

(ii)

The bound

\left\|\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon})-\mathds{T}:\bm{\varepsilon}\right\|\leq C\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|

(3.25)

is valid for all tensors
\mathds{T}\in L(\textrm{Sym}(d))
and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
satisfying the constraints

\|\mathds{T}\|\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|\geq M.

(3.26)

(iii)

The Lipschitz estimate

\left\|\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon}_{1})-\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2})\right\|\leq\left(\|\mathds{T}\|+\delta\right)\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\|

(3.27)

holds for all strains
\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
and all tensors
\mathds{T}\in L(\textrm{Sym}(d))
obeying the constraint

\|\mathds{T}\|\leq 1.

(3.28)

For the work at hand, we use the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function to construct a multiplication operator (
3.21
) which satisfies the assumption
1
, see section
4
. However, there are numerous other activation functions used to construct neural networks (
3.21
). Thus, for the work at hand, we separated the assumption
1
from the concrete activation function to show that our construction of the neural fixed-point operator (
3.22
) depends on the stated estimates only, and does not involve a specific activation function. In particular, in case the bounds (
3.23
), (
3.25
) and (
3.27
) are established for alternative activation functions would lead to the same conclusions about the expressivity of the involved FNOs for these scenarios, as well.

Under assumption
1
, we establish the following result.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose assumption
1
is satisfied, and we fix a field

\mathds{C}\in\mathcal{M}_{Y}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})

(3.29)

of linear elastic stiffness tensors and a macroscopic strain
\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
. Then, the following holds:

(i)

The neural operator (
3.22
) is well-defined and satisfies the estimate

\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1};\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C},\alpha_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{2};\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C},\alpha_{0})\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq(\gamma_{0}+\delta)\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\|_{L^{2}}

(3.30)

in case the quantity

\gamma_{0}=\max\left(\left|\frac{\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}}\right|,\left|\frac{\alpha_{-}-\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}}\right|\right)

(3.31)

does not exceed unity.

(ii)

In case the quantity

\gamma_{\theta}=\gamma_{0}+\delta

(3.32)

is less than unity, the unique fixed point
\bm{\varepsilon}_{\theta}^{*}
of the contractive neural operator (
3.22
) satisfies the proximity estimate

\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}_{\theta}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{\theta}}\left[\delta\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}\|_{L^{2}}+C\,\frac{\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}\|_{L^{p}}^{\frac{p}{2}}}{M^{\frac{p-2}{2}}}\right]

(3.33)

to the (unique) fixed point
\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}
of the Lippmann-Schwinger operator (
2.10
) which resides in an
L^{p}
space with an exponent
p>2
that depends only on the material contrast
\kappa=\alpha_{+}/\alpha_{-}
, see the statement (
2.22
).

\left\|\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon})-\mathds{T}:\bm{\varepsilon}\right\|\leq 2\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|

(4.23)

is valid for all tensors
\mathds{T}\in L(\textrm{Sym}(d))
and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
satisfying the constraints

\|\mathds{T}\|\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|\geq M.

(4.24)

(iii)

The Lipschitz estimate

\left\|\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon}_{1})-\bm{\tau}_{\theta}(\mathds{T},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2})\right\|\leq\left(\|\mathds{T}\|+\delta_{0}\right)\,\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\|

(4.25)

holds for all strains
\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
and all tensors
\mathds{T}\in L(\textrm{Sym}(d))
obeying the constraint

\|\mathds{T}\|\leq 1.

(4.26)

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, ineq. (
4.15
), the Lipschitz estimate (
4.25
) is satisfied in case the inequality

M\delta d(d+1)\leq\delta_{0}

(4.27)

holds in view of the bound

\max_{i,j}|T_{ij}|\leq\|\mathds{T}\|.

(4.28)

By Lemma
4.2
, ineq. (
4.17
), the small-input estimate (
4.21
) holds provided the inequality

M\delta d(d+1)\leq\delta_{0}

(4.29)

is satisfied. For the estimate (
4.23
) to hold, a necessary condition reads

\delta Md(d+1)\leq 1

(4.30)

via Lemma
4.2
, ineq. (
4.19
). Assuming
\delta_{0}\leq 1
it is thus sufficient to select the parameter
\delta
to satisfy the constraint

\delta\leq\frac{\delta_{0}}{Md(d+1)}.

(4.31)

5.1
Setup

The fixed point
\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}
of the Lippmann-Schwinger FNO (
3.22
) may be interpreted as a compatible field

\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}_{\theta}=\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{s}\bm{u}^{*}_{\theta},

(5.1)

which solves the balance of linear momentum

\textrm{div }\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}\left(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}}+\nabla^{s}\bm{u}^{*}_{\theta};\mathds{C}\right)=\bm{0}

(5.2)

with the neural stress operator

\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}:\textrm{Sym}(d)\times\mathcal{M}_{Y}(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})\rightarrow\textrm{Sym}(d),\quad\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon};\mathds{C})=\alpha_{0}\bm{\tau}_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}(\mathds{C}-\mathds{C}^{0}),\bm{\varepsilon}\right)+\mathds{C}^{0}:\bm{\varepsilon},

(5.3)

involving the reference constant
\alpha_{0}
which we select as in eq. (
2.19
). In case the parameter
\delta
is selected to be small enough, the stress operator (
5.3
) is strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous in the strain variable, as can be seen from the elementary estimates

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1};\mathds{C})-\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{2};\mathds{C})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{0}(1+\|\mathds{T}_{0}\|+\delta)\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\|,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1};\mathds{C})-\bm{\sigma}_{\theta}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{2};\mathds{C})\right]:(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{0}(1-\|\mathds{T}_{0}\|-\delta)\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\|^{2},

(5.5)

valid for all strains
\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\bm{\varepsilon}_{2}\in\textrm{Sym}(d)
, implied by the condition (
3.27
) and where we use the abbreviation

\mathds{T}_{0}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}(\mathds{C}-\mathds{C}^{0}).

(5.6)

In particular, the balance of linear momentum (
5.2
) admits a unique solution. Also, for any discretization on a regular grid compatible to the FFT framework, like the Moulinec-Suquet discretization
[
7
,
8
,
59
,
65
]
, the finite-difference discretizations
[
66
,
11
]
or the finite-element discretizations
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
, the corresponding form of the balance equation (
5.2
) admits a unique solution. From a mechanical point of view, the constitutive law (
5.3
) is nonlinear elastic, but not hyperelastic, and approximates a linear elastic material model. We study the effects of the approximation (
5.2
) to the proper model (
2.6
) in the section at hand.

For this purpose, we integrated the constitutive law (
5.3
) into an in-house FFT-based computational micromechanics code
[

53
]
. This allows us to benchmark the proposed FNO model with the FFT-based framework. In both methods, the volume element is discretized by the rotated staggered grid
[

and solved by the basic scheme

]
with a termination error of

6
Conclusions

This work was concerned with the use of Fourier neural operators (FNOs) for computational micromechanics. Although deep learning and operator-learning frameworks are available for homogenization tasks, these do not appear to outperform the most efficient discretization-solver combinations available via traditional (non-neural) methods. We attribute this shortcoming to the desire of creating a "new" tool, repeating mistakes that may be avoided and falling into traps that are unnecessary.

Instead, we recommend to generalize the available framework
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
of solvers based on the fast Fourier transform (FFT) to include FNOs. The work at hand emulated the basic scheme
[
7
,
8
]
, the simplest FFT solver, by an FNO. By this simple trick, we were able to remove most of the limitations of the approximation result
[
44
]
by Bhattacharya and coworkers on FNOs in homogenization. Rather than relying upon continuity properties of the solution mapping
(\bar{\bm{\varepsilon}},\mathds{C})\mapsto\bm{u}
, we emulate the constructive approximation of the solution to design a recurrent FNO (
3.22
), see also You et al.
[
70
]
and Kelly-Kalidindi
[
71
]
. The obtained approximation result stated in Theorem
1.1
is astonishingly general. It appears that the traditional supervised training procedure serves as the most severe restriction for FNOs in homogenization.

Interestingly, the link that we established between FFT solvers and FNO came with a dictionary that permits us to translate between terminology available for both methods. Most importantly, "depth" of the operator network corresponds to the iteration count required by the FFT solver. Apart from this remark, we made a number of observations:

1.

Traditionally, FFT methods encounter two different kinds of errors
[
72
]
: the resolution error, a consequence of inaccurate discretization, and the iteration error, i.e., the "distance" to discrete equilibrium. In our construction of the FNO discussed in section
3.3
, both errors are mingled. The iteration error is reduced by increasing the depth of the FNO. There is no "classical" discretization error because we permit infinitely many Fourier coefficients to appear in the Fourier layer (
3.11
). There is no alternative, however, if general microstructures are to be treated, see the discussion surrounding eqns. (
3.15
)–(
3.18
).

2.

Instead of the basic scheme
[
7
,
8
]
, more advanced solution schemes developed for FFT schemes could be used: polarization schemes
[
13
,
14
,
49
]
, fast gradient
[
73
,
74
]
and conjugate gradient methods
[
15
,
50
,
51
]
or Quasi-Newton strategies
[
52
,
53
,
16
]
. Some of these methods require much less than the
O(\kappa)
iterations necessary for the basic scheme, where
\kappa=\alpha_{+}/\alpha_{-}
stands for the material contrast. However, no solver requires less than
O(\sqrt{\kappa})
iterations. This fact is a consequence of general results on the minimum number of gradient evaluations required by iterative methods for approximating the minimizer of a strongly convex function with Lipschitz gradient, see Nesterov’s book
[
75
, Thm. 2.1.13]
. It would be interesting to see whether FFT solvers could be improved by deep learning techniques, in particular whether the
O(\sqrt{\kappa})
bound could be overcome. Indeed, FNOs do not require to compute the gradient of the objective function exactly, i.e., there is room for improvement.

3.

The FNO which we constructed was used for a fixed discretization scheme in section
5
. However, a variety of discretization methods is available for FFT methods, including spectral
[
7
,
8
,
76
]
, finite difference
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
, finite element
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
, finite volume
[
77
,
78
]
and more exotic discretizations
[
79
,
80
,
81
]
. The FFT solvers were designed under certain conditions on the discretization, i.e., that the symbol of the Eshelby-Green operator (
2.12
) is self-adjoint and non-expansive. Therefore, deep learning could be employed to learn better discretizations, as well.

4.

Composite-voxel methods
[
82
,
83
]
serve as an indispensable tool for practical computational micromechanics. These associate an appropriate "mixing rule" to voxels comprising more than one microstructure phase. It is conceivable that FNOs might profit from such constructions, as well. Conversely, deep-learning techniques could help constructing more advanced "mixing rules" than the ones currently used.

5.

Our construction (
3.39
) was purely qualitative, but could be made
quantitative
with some effort. More precisely, the number of Fourier layers and the number of neural-network layers for the ReLU network could be estimated as a function of the input parameters
\kappa
,
\eps_{0}
and
\delta
. However, it is not clear whether this estimate would be optimal. The principal technical difficulty here is the optimality of the power
p
in the a-priori
L^{p}
-bound (
2.22
).

6.

It is known that FFT solvers may be applied to solve unit-cell problems involving porous materials as long as the microstructure is mechanically stable and a numerically robust discretization is used
[
48
]
. Thus, FNOs may be constructed to solve such problems, as well. However, the fidelity of such operators depends strongly on specific quantities related to the interface, see Schneider
[
48
]
for the porous case, and Sab et al.
[
84
]
for microstructures involving both pores and rigid inclusions. In particular, problems with "infinite material contrast" may be treated.

7.

The "F"-part in FNO is responsible for obtaining a uniform bound on the depth, a.k.a. iteration count. It is hard to imagine a uniform approximation result for homogenization problems using the Deep-O-Net
[
36
]
similar to ours which was obtained for FNOs. It thus appears that FNOs are more suited to homogenization problems with their low-regularity solutions.

8.

Traditionally, FNOs were used to solve linear homogenization problems. Of course, the primary problem here is the dependence of the solution to the homogenization problem on the coefficients, which is strongly nonlinear. Still, some of the most interesting questions in computational mechanics involve nonlinear and inelastic constitutive laws. These requirements were taken into consideration when designing FFT methods
[
7
,
8
]
. Via the established link between FFT schemes and FNOs, the latter may be infused with knowledge how to handle inelasticity in an efficient manner.

Appendix A
Elementary estimates for fixed-point methods

This appendix collects two rather elementary a-priori estimates valid for general fixed-point methods. We state them in this generality because of their inherent simplicity.

We start with a simple a-priori estimate for fixed-point methods

Lemma A.1
.

For a contraction mapping
F
on a Banach space
X
with unique fixed point
x^{*}\in X
and contraction constant
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the estimate

\|x^{*}-x^{0}\|_{X}\leq\frac{\|F(x^{0})-x^{0}\|_{X}}{1-\gamma}

(A.1)

holds for any element
x^{0}\in X
.

Proof.

By the proof of the contraction mapping theorem, the inductively defined sequence

x^{k+1}=F(x^{k}),\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(A.2)

converges to the unique fixed point
x^{*}
. Re-writing the difference

x^{m+1}-x^{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\quad m>0,

(A.3)

as a telescopic sum, we estimate

\|x^{m}-x^{0}\|_{X}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|_{X}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\gamma^{k}\|x^{1}-x^{0}\|_{X}=\frac{1-\gamma^{m}}{1-\gamma}\|F(x^{0})-x^{0}\|_{X}.

(A.4)

Thus, we obtain the bound

\begin{split}\|x^{*}-x^{0}\|_{X}&\leq\|x^{*}-x^{m}\|_{X}+\|x^{m}-x^{0}\|_{X}\\
&\leq\|x^{*}-x^{m}\|_{X}+\frac{1-\gamma^{m}}{1-\gamma}\|F(x^{0})-x^{0}\|_{X}\\
&\rightarrow\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\|F(x^{0})-x^{0}\|_{X}\end{split}

(A.5)

for
m\rightarrow\infty
, which was to be shown.
∎

We turn our attention to an estimate for the fixed points associated to a perturbed fixed-point mapping.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach space. Suppose that two Lipschitz mappings
F
and
F_{\theta}
are given with respective contraction constants
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\gamma_{\theta}\in(0,1)
. Then the estimate

\|x^{*}-x^{*}_{\theta}\|_{X}\leq\frac{\|F(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*})\|_{X}}{1-\gamma_{\theta}}

(A.6)

holds for the (unique) fixed points

x^{*}=F(x^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad x^{*}_{\theta}=F(x^{*}_{\theta}).

(A.7)

Proof.

We write

x^{*}-x^{*}_{\theta}=F(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*}_{\theta})=F(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*})+F_{\theta}(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*}_{\theta})

(A.8)

and estimate

\|x^{*}-x^{*}_{\theta}\|_{X}\leq\|F(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*})\|_{X}+\|F_{\theta}(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*}_{\theta})\|_{X}\leq\|F(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*})\|_{X}+\gamma_{\theta}\|x^{*}-x^{*}_{\theta}\|_{X}.

(A.9)

Thus, the inequality

(1-\gamma_{\theta})\|x^{*}-x^{*}_{\theta}\|_{X}\leq\|F(x^{*})-F_{\theta}(x^{*})\|_{X}

(A.10)

holds, which was to be shown.
∎

The goal of this Appendix is to provide the mathematical arguments for the validity of Lemma

Lemma E.1
.

For all numbers
x,y,c\in[-M,M]
, the following estimate holds:

\left|m_{\theta}(c,x)-m_{\theta}(c,y)-c(x-y)\right|\leq 2M\,\delta|x-y|.

(E.1)

Proof.

The exact multiplication of two real numbers is represented by the operator

m:\mathds{R}\times\mathds{R}\rightarrow\mathds{R},\quad m(a,b)=2M^{2}\left[q\left(\frac{a+b}{2M}\right)-q\left(\frac{a}{2M}\right)-q\left(\frac{b}{2M}\right)\right].

(E.2)

Both functions
q
and
q_{\theta}
, defined in eq. (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), are absolutely continuous, more precisely admit the representations

q_{\theta}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}q_{\theta}^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi\quad\text{and}\quad q(x)=\int_{0}^{x}q^{\prime}(\xi)\,d\xi

(E.3)

with piece-wise constant functions
q^{\prime},q_{\theta}^{\prime}:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathds{R}
.
To obtain the estimate (
E.1
), we re-write the terms as follows

\begin{split}m_{\theta}(c,x)-m_{\theta}(c,y)-m(c,x)-m(c,y)&=2M^{2}\left[q_{\theta}\left(\frac{x+c}{2M}\right)-q_{\theta}\left(\frac{x}{2M}\right)-q_{\theta}\left(\frac{c}{2M}\right)\right.\\
&-q_{\theta}\left(\frac{y+c}{2M}\right)+q_{\theta}\left(\frac{y}{2M}\right)+q_{\theta}\left(\frac{c}{2M}\right)\\
&-q\left(\frac{x+c}{2M}\right)+q\left(\frac{x}{2M}\right)+q\left(\frac{c}{2M}\right)\\
&+\left.q\left(\frac{y+c}{2M}\right)-q\left(\frac{y}{2M}\right)-q\left(\frac{c}{2M}\right)\right]\\
&=2M^{2}\left[q_{\theta}\left(\frac{x+c}{2M}\right)-q_{\theta}\left(\frac{y+c}{2M}\right)\right.\\
&+q_{\theta}\left(\frac{y}{2M}\right)-q_{\theta}\left(\frac{x}{2M}\right)\\
&-q\left(\frac{x+c}{2M}\right)+q\left(\frac{y+c}{2M}\right)\\
&-\left.q\left(\frac{y}{2M}\right)+q\left(\frac{x}{2M}\right)\right].\end{split}

(E.4)

By the absolutely continuous representations (
E.3
), we notice the identities

\displaystyle q\left(\frac{x+c}{2M}\right)-q\left(\frac{y+c}{2M}\right)
