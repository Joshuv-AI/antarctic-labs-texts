Single-Cell Transcriptomics of Pyrimidine Salvage in HBV-Related HCC Reveals Prognostic and Immune Infiltration Features
Authors: Liping Xie, Shaoyang Wang, Taofa Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is primarily caused by hepatitis B virus (HBV). The pyrimidine-salvage pathway (PSP) is crucial for tumor proliferation, but its prognostic and immunomodulatory roles in HBV-HCC are unclear. By using single-cell transcriptomics, we investigated PSP signatures and their link to clinical outcomes and immune modulation in HBV-HCC. Data from XENA and GEO databases were analyzed. Malignant cell subpopulations were identified, and single-cell PSP activity was quantified. HBV-specific malignant subpopulations were characterized using the Ro/e metric. Cell-cell communication was analyzed. A prognostic model was developed and validated using a training cohort, and a nomogram was constructed. Immune infiltration, drug sensitivity, and the functional role of ferritin light chain (FTL) were examined experimentally. Malignant cells were classified into HBV⁻ and HBV⁺ subpopulations. The HBV⁺ group showed low PSP gene (PSPG) expression. The six-gene prognostic model demonstrated high predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival. High-risk patients had shorter survival and increased infiltration of macrophages, Tregs, and activated dendritic cells. Drug sensitivity analysis identified potential agents. In vitro, FTL silencing suppressed HBV-HCC cell proliferation and migration. This investigation clarified the prognostic significance of PSPGs and their immunomodulatory roles in HBV-HCC, and established a prognostic model with predictive power. FTL, identified as a key gene, represented a potential therapeutic target for HBV-HCC. These findings provided a novel theoretical foundation for the treatment of HBV-HCC.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for approximately 90% of liver cancer subtypes (Hwang et al., 2025). Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is the dominant etiological driver of HCC (Shoraka et al., 2023), triggering persistent necro-inflammation, fibrosis, and ultimately cirrhosis that predisposes to malignant transformation (Songtanin et al., 2024). Despite advances in HBV monitoring and therapy, a substantial proportion of patients with HCC are diagnosed at unresectable or advanced stages, resulting in dismal outcomes (Ito and Nguyen, 2023). Elucidating the molecular underpinnings of HBV-HCC and identifying new prognostic biomarkers as well as therapeutic targets are therefore meaningful in improving prognosis.

Pyrimidines are synthesized via both de novo and salvage pathways, which converge to produce the common intermediate uridine monophosphate (Fu et al., 2022). The pyrimidine-salvage pathway (PSP) is a key pathway of nucleotide synthesis, which can sustain DNA repair and replication, thereby fueling tumor proliferation (Fu et al., 2022; Nwosu et al., 2023; Skinner et al., 2023). Dysregulated pyrimidine metabolism has been documented across multiple tumor types, and PSP genes (PSPGs) have been linked to oncogenic functions and adverse prognosis (Heo et al., 2022; Walter et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). A recent study reported that, under glucose-limited conditions, PSP sustains nucleotide supply in HCC cells by a GART-mediated mechanism that stabilizes UCK2, ultimately promoting tumor growth (Sha et al., 2025). Despite the discussion on PSP’s role in tumorigenesis, the contribution of the PSP to HBV-HCC and its interplay with the tumor microenvironment (TME) remain incompletely defined.

In recent years, the advancement of single-cell transcriptomics has provided a powerful tool for dissecting tumor heterogeneity and TME. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables high-resolution mapping of genetic expression of tumor cells and their immune and stromal compartments, thereby revealing the potential mechanism of tumor initiation and progression. Although recent scRNA-seq studies have interrogated the impact of HBV on HCC biology and its prognosis (Li et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2025a), a systematic evaluation of the roles of PSP in HBV-HCC and its interplay with the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is lacking. Therefore, it is of significant scientific and clinical importance to explore the prognostic value and immunoregulatory role of PSP-associated signatures in HBV-HCC by integrating single-cell transcriptomics.

This work, utilizing scRNA-seq and the construction of a risk prognostic model, aims to systematically elucidate the molecular characteristics of the PSP in HBV-HCC and its interaction with the TIME. It provides novel molecular biomarkers for prognostic assessment of HBV-HCC and can offer a theoretical basis for therapeutic strategies targeting pyrimidine metabolism.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data acquisition

Genetic expression profiles and clinical data (age, gender, tumor grade, TNM stage) were retrieved from the UCSC Xena Functional Genomics Explorer (https://xena.ucsc.edu). HBV-HCC samples were identified on the basis of documented HBV infection. Patients with an overall survival (OS) ≥30 days were included, yielding a final discovery cohort of 97 cases. The single-cell dataset GSE282701 was downloaded from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The series comprises 12 samples with six HBV+ (three tumor biopsies and three matched normal peritumoral tissues) and six HBV− (three tumor and three matched normal peritumoral tissues). For external validation, we downloaded GSE14520 from GEO. GPL3921 was chosen as the validation data to validate the prognostic model. 212 patients remained after removing samples with OS <30 days. The PSPGs were adopted from a previous study (Li et al., 2024b).

Quality control of scRNA-seq

The raw gene-expression matrix was imported into a Seurat object using the “Seurat” R package (Tan et al., 2023) and subjected to quality control. Cells were retained if mitochondrial gene content ≤20% and detected gene counts (nFeature_RNA) between 200 and 6,000. Single-cell data were normalized with “NormalizeData” “FindVariableFeatures” and “ScaleData” functions to identify the top 2000 most highly variable genes. Doublets were estimated with data from 10× Genomics (https://www.10xgenomics.com/) using the “DoubletFinder” package (Zhu et al., 2024) to generate a simulated doublet dataset, which was then integrated with the original data to identify and filter out potential doublets. Multiple sample-objects were merged with the “merge” function from the “Seurat” package. The integrated dataset was then normalized with the “NormalizeData” “FindVariableFeatures” and “ScaleData” functions, and the 2,000 most highly variable genes were selected. Lastly, principal-component analysis (PCA) was executed with the “RunPCA” function, and the “harmony” package (Cao et al., 2023) was used to remove multiple-sample batch effects. We then constructed the SNN graph by selecting 35 harmony dimensions and using the “FindNeighbors” function according to an elbow plot. Dimensionality reduction was then performed with the “RunUMAP” function, after which the “FindClusters” function was run to assign cells to clusters. The optimal resolution parameter was determined to be 0.7 through evaluation with the “clustree” package (Zappia and Oshlack, 2018), resulting in the identification of 30 distinct clusters.

Single-cell annotation

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across cell clusters were identified with the “FindAllMarkers” function (logfc.threshold=0.585, min.pct=0.3, only.pos=TRUE). Cells in each cluster were annotated by cross-referencing marker genes in the published scRNA-seq literature of human HCC, the CellMarker database (a manually curated resource of cell markers in human and mouse, http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/), and automated labels generated by the “singleR” package (Zhang et al., 2022). The expression of top-ranking markers was visualized as dot plots and violin plots. The 10 most specific genes ranked by average logFC per cluster were displayed in a heat map.

Sub-clustering of epithelial cells

Firstly, epithelial cells were isolated with the “subset” function. Single-cell data were normalized with functions “NormalizeData” “FindVariableFeatures” and “ScaleData” to identify the top 2000 most highly variable genes. PCA was executed with the “RunPCA” function, and the “harmony” package (Cao et al., 2023) was used to remove multiple-sample batch effects. A SNN graph was constructed on the 35-harmony-corrected PCs by using FindNeighbors function, followed by dimensionality reduction with the “RunUMAP” function. Cells were then re-clustered with the “FindClusters” function at resolution=0.1, yielding refined epithelial subtypes.

Identification of copy number variation (CNV) signatures with inferCNV

Large-scale chromosomal CNV in tumor cells was inferred with inferCNV. Hepatocytes and macrophage/monocyte cells from normal tissue served as the reference population. By comparing differences in gene expression intensity across genomic regions between tumor cells and control populations, chromosomal CNV events were systematically identified.

Identification of malignant cells

The inferCNV output was visualized and integrated with the clustering dendrogram to determine malignant cell subpopulations bearing significant CNV signals. Next, ssGSEA was performed on the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway gene set from the Hallmark Gene Set Collection (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/). Enrichment scores between the identified malignant and non-malignant cell subpopulations were compared. Inter-group differences were compared by the Wilcoxon test.

Single-cell scoring

Enrichment analyses were performed on the single-cell data of PSPGs with the “irGSEA” package (Fan et al., 2024). Enrichment activity scores of each cell in each pathway were calculated with eight independent methods: AUCell, UCell, singscore, ssGSEA, JASMINE, VIPER, AddModuleScore, and scSE. The scoring matrices generated by each method were Z-score normalized, and the arithmetic mean of the eight normalized scores was taken as the final integrated score (Scoring) (Liu et al., 2024). Cells were then classified into high- and low-scoring groups based on the median scoring value. The distribution of scores across cell types was compared between normal and tumor samples, as well as between HBV+ and HBV− samples, with inter-group differences assessed by the Wilcoxon test.

Comparative analysis of cellular composition between HBV⁺ and HBV⁻ samples

Ro/e (ratio of observed to expected cell numbers) (Zhang et al., 2018) was used to quantify the preference of each cell subpopulation in HBV⁺ and HBV⁻ samples. An Ro/e greater than 1 indicates enrichment, meaning the cell cluster is over-represented relative to random expectation, while an Ro/e less than 1 indicates depletion, meaning it is under-represented. We applied this metric to quantify compositional differences between HBV⁺ and HBV⁻ samples and to identify significantly enriched cell subpopulations. Malignant cells were selected for downstream analysis and, using the same median-scoring cutoff described earlier, partitioned into high- and low-scoring subsets. The distribution of these two malignant high-/low-scoring subsets across HBV⁺ and HBV⁻ samples was then compared. Cell-cell communication analysis with CellCall

Based on the single-cell scoring and HBV-associated malignant cell subpopulation analyses described above, malignant cells were divided into three groups: (1) HBV⁺ SP-low-PSP, (2) HBV⁺ SP-high-PSP, and (3) all other malignant cells. Cell-cell communication was inferred with the “CellCall” package (Lin et al., 2024) as follows: First, data of ligand-receptor-transcription-factor (L-R-TF) axis were compiled from KEGG pathways. Second, based on how L-R-TF interacts, CellCall integrates ligand/receptor expression in some L-R pairs with downstream TF activity to estimate cell-cell communication. Third, CellCall incorporates a pathway-activity analysis method that identifies the key signaling pathways mediating communication between specific cell types.

Construction of a prognostic model

To construct a prognostic model, the TCGA-HBV⁺ LIHC cohort served as the training set. DEGs between HBV⁻ SP-high and HBV⁺ SP-low malignant cells were subjected to univariable Cox regression to identify OS-associated genes (p<0.05). To avoid over-fitting, LASSO penalized regression was applied to narrow down the range of OS-related genes. The optimal λ was determined by 10-fold cross-validation with the λ yielding the minimal mean error. The selected genes were subjected to multivariate Cox regression with stepwise bidirectional selection to establish the final prognostic model. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves were plotted to compare survival rates. Time-dependent receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) analyses were drawn to assess sensitivity and specificity in both TCGA training and validation sets.

Construction and validation of a prognostic nomogram

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression were applied to test the independent prognostic value of RiskScore. Time-dependent ROC curves were generated and AUCs were calculated to quantify the predictive ability of clinical information, such as age and TNM stage, and the model. Integrating the prognostic model and the clinical variables determined by multivariate Cox regression, a composite nomogram was constructed. Calibration curves were drawn to visualize the agreement between the nomogram and the ideal model. The predictive ability of the model was evaluated through the ROC, decision-curve analysis (DCA), and calibration curves.

Immune microenvironment characterization of high- versus low-risk groups

The abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIIC) across the risk groups was quantified by ssGSEA with the “GSVA” package (Hanzelmann et al., 2013). Gene expression signature matrices of TIIC were downloaded from the GSEA platform (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). Expression levels of HLA-related genes (genes prefixed “HLA-”) and key immune-checkpoint molecules (Zhang et al., 2023) were compared across risk groups. Inter-group differences were evaluated with the Wilcoxon test.

Identification and analysis of DEGs between high- and low-risk groups

The “edgeR” package (Li et al., 2022) was used to identify DEGs between high- and low-risk groups in the TCGA-HBV⁺ LIHC dataset (FDR<0.05 and |log₂FC|>1). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the DEGs using the “clusterProfiler” (Wu et al., 2021) and “enrichplot” (Xu et al., 2018) packages to explore potential biological processes involving these DEGs.

Drug Sensitivity Prediction

The “oncoPredict” package (Maeser et al., 2021) was employed to predict drug responses in the high- and low-risk groups of the TCGA-HBV⁺ LIHC dataset. The GDSC2 database was used as the training set to screen for differential drugs and compare drug responses between the two groups. Correlation plots illustrating the relationship between predicted IC50 values and gene expression levels were generated. Group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon test.

Cell culture

Human normal hepatocyte THLE-2 (CL-0833) was purchased from Wuhan Yichuang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Human HCC cell line HepG2 (HB-8065) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Human hepatoblastoma cell line HepG2.2.15 (GT1108LUC), derived from HepG2 and stably expressing HBV DNA, serving as an HBV+ cell line, was acquired from Shanghai Purplestone Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). All cells were cultivated in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher, USA). Cells were maintained in a thermostatic incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa, Japan) on a QuantStudio 5 system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative expression was calculated with the 2-ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB)

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). 30 μg of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 3% non-fat milk (Beyotime, China) for 2 h at room temperature (RT) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against FTL (Proteintech, USA) and GAPDH (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, USA). After three TBST washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (P0948, Beyotime, China) was applied for 1 h at RT. After a final wash, signals were visualized with SuperSignal ECL substrate (Invitrogen, USA) on a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Cell transfection

FTL-targeting siRNAs were synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma (China). HepG2.2.15 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 6×105 per well and incubated for 24 h until grown to 80% confluence. 100 pmol of siRNA per well and 4 µL Lipofectamine™ 8000 (Beyotime, China) were mixed, added to cells and incubated at RT for 20 min. Then, the culture continued to incubate at 37°C and was harvested after 48 h for downstream assays.

Cell-proliferation assays

CCK-8 and EdU were applied to assess the proliferation of HepG2.2.15. For the CCK-8 assay, 1×10³ HepG2.2.15 cells were seeded per well of a 96-well plate in 100 µL DMEM and 10 µL CCK-8 reagent. The plates were incubated for 2 h in an incubator. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured at 0, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h.

1×105 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates, cultured overnight, and incubated with 10 µmol/L EdU (BD Biosciences, USA). Cells were fixed, permeabilized, stained with azide-dye working solution, and incubated at RT protected from light. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and images were acquired on a 40× objective.

Transwell assay

2×104 HepG2.2.15 cells suspended in 200 µL serum-free DMEM were placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell inserts (Corning, USA). The lower chamber contained 700 µL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, the upper chamber was carefully removed. Non-migrated cells were gently washed away using serum-free medium. The migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at RT for 10 min, followed by staining with crystal violet (Shenzhen Kaigen Technology Co., Ltd., China) for 15 min. After PBS washes, the cells were observed under an optical microscope.

Wound-healing assay

HepG2.2.15 cells were seeded at 5×105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured to approximately 80% confluence. A linear scratch was made with a 20 µL pipette tip. After debris was removed by PBS washing, fresh medium was added and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Migration was monitored at 0 h and 24 h under an inverted microscope and wound width was measured.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, USA). Quantitative data were presented as mean ± SD. Each experiment was performed independently at least three times. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated by t-tests. Multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. The criterion for determining statistical significance of the difference was set at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Single-cell classification and proportion analysis of HBV-HCC

We first performed scRNA-seq on HBV-HCC. Cells were partitioned into 30 clusters and the top five DEGs of each cluster were identified (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1). Guided by marker genes, single cells were annotated and assigned to eight major types: endothelial cells (VWF, ENG, CAV1), fibroblasts (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1), macrophages/monocytes (C1QA, CD68, LYZ), neutrophils (S100A8, S100A9, FCGR3B), hepatocytes (ALB, APOA2, CYP2E1), NK cells (NKG7, GNLY, GZMB), B cells (MS4A1, CD79A, CD79B) and T cells (CD3E, CD3D, CD2) (Fig. 1B-1D). The 10 most specific genes in each type were identified, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Across the GSE282701 dataset, T cells constituted the largest fraction, followed by endothelial cells and hepatocytes (Fig. 1E). Compositional analysis revealed that hepatocytes were significantly expanded in tumor tissues, irrespective of HBV+ or HBV⁻, relative to peritumoral normal tissues (Fig. 1F). Therefore, hepatocytes were selected for downstream in-depth characterization.

Sub-clustering of hepatocytes and validation of malignant cells

Hepatocytes were re-clustered into nine distinct subtypes (Fig. 2A). We then inferred CNVs in the tumor cells. By using inferCNV and taking normal hepatocytes and macrophage/monocyte cells as the reference, we compared their gene expression intensities with those of the target hepatocyte tumor cells to identify the CNV in the chromosomal region. We found that compared with the control, tumor samples exhibited extensive chromosomal gains and losses, indicating widespread genomic instability in the cancer environment (Fig. 2B). To differentiate normal and malignant hepatocytes, we visualized the results of inferCNV and compared the CNV scores of each hepatocyte subtype and their control. Results showed that Cluster 4 and Cluster 8 carried lower CNA scores than their controls (Fig. 2C). Therefore, Cluster 4 and Cluster 8 were designated as non-malignant hepatocytes. All remaining clusters were classified as malignant hepatocytes and their distribution in all cell types was observed (Fig. 2D, 2E). Further comparison of EMT pathway activity between malignant and normal hepatocytes revealed a significantly elevated level in malignant cells compared to normal hepatocytes (p<0.0001) (Fig. 2F). This suggested that EMT pathway activation could be a core characteristic of malignant cells and drive tumor invasion and metastasis.

Single-cell irGSEA scoring and sub-clustering of malignant cells

The “irGSEA” package was used to quantify PSP activity at the single-cell level. Eight independent gene-set enrichment methods (AUCell, UCell, singscore, ssGSEA, JASMINE, viper, AddModuleScore and scSE) were integrated, and the normalized enrichment scores were calculated (Fig. 3A). We next compared scoring values across cell types between normal and tumor samples, as well as between HBV+ and HBV⁻ specimens. Malignant cells exhibited a significant decrease in scoring in HBV+ tumors (p<0.0001; Fig. 3B, 3C). Although hepatocytes displayed a modestly lower score in the HBV+ group, their scoring was still higher in tumors than in normal tissue. We therefore focused subsequent analyses on malignant hepatocytes.

Re-clustering of malignant cells in HCC yielded eight subtypes. UMAP visualized their spatial distribution that differed between HBV+ and HBV− samples (Fig. 3D, 3E). Ro/e analysis revealed that Clusters 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 were selectively enriched in HBV+ tumors, whereas Clusters 0, 1 and 6 were preferentially expanded in HBV⁻ tumors (Fig. 3F). We consequently designated Clusters 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 “HBV+ SP” and Clusters 0, 1 and 6 “HBV⁻ SP”. Further examination of PSPG expression in HBV-HCC showed that tumors with high PSPG levels were concentrated in the HBV⁻ cohort, while those with low PSPG levels were predominantly found in the HBV+ cohort (Fig. 3G). This observation suggested that down-regulation of PSPGs was linked to HBV-HCC. Comparing the enrichment or depletion patterns of high- and low-PSPG subsets between HBV⁺ and HBV⁻ SP malignant cells, we found that low-PSPG cells were selectively enriched in the HBV⁺ SP group, whereas high-PSPG cells accumulated predominantly in the HBV⁻ SP group (Fig. 3H). Malignant cells in the HBV+ group exhibited lower expression levels of PSPGs. Therefore, subsequent functional studies were designed to contrast HBV⁺ SP-low PSPG expression with HBV⁻ SP-high PSPG expression cells.

Decoding cell-cell communication with CellCall at single-cell resolution

To dissect how HBV infection reshapes the HCC microenvironment, we constructed a communication network based on L-R interactions across all identified cell types at single-cell resolution (Fig. 4A). When malignant hepatocytes were considered as recipients in L-R interactions, JAM2-F11R and CLDN5-CLDN3 formed the strongest interaction pairs when endothelial cells transferred signals (Fig. 4B). When cells in the HBV⁻ SP-high PSPG expression group served as signal recipients, NK cells as sender cells showed a particularly strong interaction via the EFNA5-EPHA1 L-R pair (Fig. 4C). When cells in the HBV⁺ SP-low PSPG expression group acted as receivers, L-R pairs with endothelial cells and macrophages/monocytes as senders strongly engaged with the HBEGF-EGFR axis (Fig. 4D). We also investigated the communication strength from malignant cells, HBV⁺ SP low-PSPG expression cells, and HBV⁻ SP high-PSPG expression cells to other cells (Fig. 5A-5C). When malignant cells sent signals to endothelial cells, the most prominent interactions occurred through EFNA1-EPHA4, F11R-JAM2, and CLDN3-CLDN5 pairs. When HBV⁻ SP high-PSPG expression cells sent signals to endothelial cells, they preferentially used the INHBE-ACVR2A interaction. When HBV⁺ SP low-PSPG expression cells acted as senders to endothelial cells, the F11R-JAM2 pair demonstrated the strongest interaction.

Construction and validation of PSP-activity-associated prognostic signature

Based on the DEGs between HBV⁻ SP high-PSPG expression and HBV+ SP low-PSPG expression cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A), a prognostic model was constructed using the TCGA-HBV+ LIHC dataset as the training set. Univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to screen the DEGs of HBV⁻ SP high/HBV+ SP low PSPG groups, identifying 28 genes (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, LASSO Cox regression analysis was used to reduce the number of OS-related genes to 10, with the optimal parameter λ (0.0423) adjusted through 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 6B). Multivariate Cox regression analysis and stepwise bidirectional selection were performed on the filtered genes, resulting in 6 signature genes (SAT1, AKR7A3, AGT, FTL, SERPINF2, SLC2A2) and the prognostic model (Fig. 6C). According to the coefficients and expression levels of these signature genes, we established a risk score formula:

Risk Score = 11.7021 × SAT1 - 1.3081 × AKR7A3 – 9.5243 × AGT + 6.8106 × FTL - 3.6413 × SERPINF2 - 2.7176 × SLC2A2

ROC curves then validated strong predictive values of the model: AUCs at 1-, 3-, and 5-year were 0.93, 0.85, and 0.86 in the TCGA cohort, and 0.67, 0.7, and 0.7 in GSE14520 (Fig. 6D, 6E). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups by the cohort-specific median RiskScore. K-M curves compared survival rates across risk groups, showing significantly worse OS in the high-risk group in both TCGA and GSE14520 (p<0.001; Fig. 6F, 6G). These results indicated that the risk model demonstrated stable risk stratification capabilities in both the training set and the external dataset, and had prognostic evaluation value.

Construction and validation of a prognostic nomogram

Integrating the clinical characteristics with risk scores of patients with HBV-HCC, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed that the risk score was significantly associated with patient prognosis (p<0.01, HR>1), indicating its potential as an independent predictive factor (Fig. 7A, 7B). A nomogram was developed to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities for these patients (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, the AUC for the nomogram was 0.88 (Fig. 7D). DCA demonstrated that the f-5 model yielded the highest net benefit across a range of low-risk thresholds, suggesting its superior clinical utility within this interval (Fig. 7E). The calibration curves showed good agreement between the predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities and the actual observed survival rates (Fig. 7F). These results collectively indicated that the developed nomogram achieved high accuracy in predicting survival.

Tumor immune infiltration analysis across risk groups

To investigate the TIME in HBV-HCC, we compared the degrees of immune cell infiltration, alterations in immune checkpoint gene expression, and HLA gene expression across risk groups using ssGSEA scores. The high-risk group exhibited significantly higher levels of immune infiltration by macrophages, Tregs, and activated dendritic cells (aDCs) compared to the low-risk group (p<0.05), suggesting the potential presence of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in the high-risk group (Fig. 8A). Analysis of immune checkpoint gene expression showed that TNFRSF25 was expressed at a significantly higher level in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group (p<0.05) (Fig. 8B). Furthermore, in the high-risk group, the expression levels of HLA-related genes such as DRB5, DMA, DPB1, HLA-DQB1-AS1 and DMB were significantly elevated compared to those in the low-risk group (Fig. 8C). Correlation analysis among different immune cells demonstrated significant positive correlations between most immune cell types, indicating potential cooperative regulation or shared response mechanisms within the TME of HBV-HCC. However, the specific roles of these interactions require further in-depth investigation (Fig. 8D).

Correlations of signature genes

We analyzed correlations among the signature genes and found significant inverse relationships for SERPINF2 versus FTL and for AGT versus AKR7A3, whereas AGT showed a strong positive association with SLC2A2 and SAT1 with AKR7A3 (Fig. 8E). We next examined the association of each signature gene with immune pathways to reveal their roles in shaping the functional state of immune cells (Fig. 9A-9F). SERPINF2 and SLC2A2 exhibited the broadest connectivity with immune cells. SERPINF2 expression correlated significantly and positively with CD8⁺ T cells, Th1 cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and type-II IFN-response. SLC2A2 displayed negative associations with Tregs, Th2 cells, and macrophages.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses across risk groups

Differential expression analysis of TCGA-HBV⁺ LIHC samples identified DEGs (Fig. 10A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs revealed significant enrichment in the GO terms “signal release” “immunoglobulin complex” and “symporter” as well as in the KEGG pathways “neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction” “allograft rejection” and “type I diabetes mellitus” (Fig. 10B-10E).

Drug-sensitivity profiling across risk groups

To identify therapeutics tailored to HBV-HCC, we predicted drug responses in high- versus low-risk patients. Low-risk tumors exhibited higher sensitivity to acetalax, doramapimod, uprosertib, UMI.77, afuresertib and ipatasertib, whereas high-risk cases were more vulnerable to dabrafenib, savolitinib, AZD7762, teniposide, gemcitabine and LJ1308 (Fig. 11). These findings nominated potential treatment options for HBV-HCC.

FTL depletion suppresses proliferation and migration of HBV-HCC

Our prognostic model identified a total of 6 signature genes. FTL was significantly overexpressed in HCC patients, especially in the high-risk group. Its high expression was significantly correlated with poor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 3B-3D). The GSEA enrichment analysis revealed that FTL not only participates in iron uptake and transport, but is also related to purine metabolism, indicating it as a key role in tumor cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Correlation analysis further showed that FTL expression was significantly positively correlated with the PSP score (p<0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 3F), suggesting that FTL was possible to support the growth and survival of tumor cells through synergistically supporting PSP activity. When we used bioinformatics to predict changes in the signature genes between HBV⁺ and HBV⁻ samples, FTL expression was significantly higher in HBV⁺ than in HBV⁻ tumors (p<0.01; Fig. 12A). Thus, we further analyzed FTL’s influence on HBV-HCC. To experimentally verify the bioinformatic prediction, we compared FTL expression at both the mRNA and protein levels across normal human hepatocytes THLE-2, HBV⁻ HepG2 cells, and HBV⁺ HepG2.2.15 cells. Consistent with the bioinformatic result, mRNA and protein levels of FTL were significantly up-regulated in HepG2.2.15 cells relative to THLE-2 and were also higher than those of HepG2 cells (p<0.0001; Fig. 12B, 12C). To verify the impact of FTL in HBV-HCC, we knocked down FTL in HepG2.2.15 cells. qRT-PCR and WB confirmed efficient silencing (p<0.0001; Fig. 12D, 12E), validating the cells for subsequent functional assays. CCK-8 and EdU assays revealed that FTL knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation (p<0.01; Fig. 12F, 12G). Transwell and wound-healing assays showed a significant reduction in the migratory capacity of HepG2.2.15 cells (p<0.001; Fig. 12H, 12I). In conclusion, FTL is highly expressed in HBV-related HCC and can promote tumor proliferation and migration by regulating iron and nucleotide metabolism. On the contrary, inhibiting the expression of FTL can suppress these tumorigenic processes.

DISCUSSION

HBV infection is the most common cause of HCC. Unlike most oncogenic viruses, the persistent replication of HBV in non-proliferative liver cells relies on its ability to hijack the host’s nucleotide metabolic network in a non-cell cycle-dependent manner. Current studies have clearly demonstrated that HBV activates the DNA damage response pathway, specifically inducing the expression of the key rate-limiting enzyme ribonucleotide reductase (RNR), which is essential for nucleotide de novo synthesis, thus providing precursors for its replication (Ricardo-Lax et al., 2015). This discovery reveals the core strategy of viral metabolic hijacking. However, the regulation of the virus on the host metabolic pathways seems to be global. As the main pathway for maintaining nucleotide pool homeostasis in the quiescent state, changes in the pyrimidine salvage synthesis pathway under this metabolic background and their biological significance remain unclear. This study is the first to conduct an in-depth exploration of the prognostic value and immune infiltration characteristics of PSPGs in HBV-related liver cancer through single-cell transcriptomics technology, revealing their significant role in TME. We constructed a six-gene signature prognostic model and validated its efficacy for predicting survival in HBV-HCC. In addition, immune-infiltration microenvironments were compared between high- and low-risk groups, and candidate therapeutics were predicted for each. We further demonstrated the tumor-promoting activity of FTL in HBV-HCC, offering a potential target for future precision therapy.

scRNA-seq successfully dissected the heterogeneity of the microenvironment composition in HBV-HCC. Clustering identified malignant hepatocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, NK cells and macrophages/monocytes. Notably, the proportion of hepatocytes was markedly higher in tumors than in adjacent non-tumor tissues, implying that aberrant proliferation of tumor cells possibly reshape local cellular composition.

Sub-clustering epithelial cells combined with InferCNV successfully determined malignant cells with significant CNV features. These malignant cells exhibited significant up-regulation of the EMT pathway, consistent with enhanced invasiveness and metastatic potential (Ge et al., 2025). Notably, based on the PSP activity score, we found that the expression of PSPGs in HBV+ malignant cells was significantly lower than that in HBV- malignant cells. This discovery provides direct evidence for the reprogramming of nucleotide metabolism in the context of HBV infection. Given the unique biological characteristic of HBV efficient replication in quiescent liver cells, we propose a comprehensive metabolic hijacking model. To achieve the optimal allocation of replication resources, HBV may adopt a coordinated regulation metabolic strategy. On one hand, HBV activates the host’s de novo nucleotide synthesis pathway through previously reported mechanisms, such as inducing the expression of RNR, thereby ensuring the supply of dNTPs required for viral replication (Ricardo-Lax et al., 2015). On the other hand, the virus is likely to inhibit the pyrimidine salvage synthesis pathway that competes with it for substrates. This bidirectional metabolic regulatory pattern facilitates the more efficient allocation of limited metabolic precursors (such as phosphoribose) toward the de novo synthesis pathway required by HBV, thereby creating a competitive advantage for viral replication within the host metabolic network. Previous studies have confirmed that the key protein HBx of HBV can extensively restructure the host’s nucleotide metabolism and DNA repair processes (Chou et al., 2018; Qadri et al., 2011). This provides a potential molecular basis for the aforementioned coordinated regulatory mechanism. Additionally, viral replication depends on the host’s pyrimidine de novo synthesis pathway, such as enhanced DHODH activity can promote viral genome replication (Zheng et al., 2022). Therefore, the downregulation of PSPGs observed in this study is not an isolated event, but is more likely to reflect the result of HBV systematically remodeling the host metabolic system, which drives cell transformation. This process is able to constitute an important basis for the metabolic abnormalities in HBV-related liver cancer, providing a new theoretical perspective for understanding its metabolic carcinogenic mechanism.

Based on these findings, we further classified the malignant cells into the HBV- SP group and the HBV+ SP group. Through a systematic regression analysis of the differentially expressed genes in these two groups, a risk model for predicting the prognosis of HBV-related liver cancer patients was constructed. SAT1, AKR7A3, AGT, FTL, SERPINF2, and SLC2A2 were the important model signatures to predict the survival rate of HBV-related liver cancer patients. These signature genes warrant in-depth analysis. Inflammatory macrophages can upregulate SAT1 expression in HCC, thereby activating an immunosuppressive TME (Liu et al., 2025b), which indirectly supports our findings. Although no study has yet focused on SAT1 in HBV-HCC, its large risk coefficient in our prognostic model suggested that high SAT1 expression correlated with poor prognosis in HBV-HCC, indicating that SAT1 possibly serve as an important biomarker for this disease. AKR7A3 is down-regulated in HCC patients with HBV infection and concurrent aflatoxin B1 exposure, and this down-regulation correlates with worse prognosis (Xiang et al., 2017). Additional work has demonstrated that reduced AKR7A3 expression in HCC is linked to poor outcome and participates in tumor progression through DNA methylation and TF regulatory networks (Dai et al., 2021). In contrast, our risk-model formula assigned AKR7A3 a negative coefficient (-1.3081), implying that its expression may predict a better prognosis in HBV-HCC. This apparent discrepancy with previously published literature reflects etiology-specific differences, underscoring the need to investigate HBV-HCC in depth. AGT, a key component of the renin-angiotensin system, has been implicated in the progression of various diseases, including tumors, yet its role in HBV-HCC remains largely unknown (Su et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). In a novel prognostic model based on complement- and coagulation-cascade genes, SERPINF2 correlates with the TIME and drug sensitivity in HCC, positioning it as a potential prognostic indicator and therapeutic target (Su et al., 2024). However, its specific role in HBV-HCC has not been reported. SLC2A2, which encodes GLUT2, the main glucose transporter in normal liver, is down-regulated in HCC. Low SLC2A2 expression has an impact on tumor immune infiltration, whereas its high expression is associated with better prognosis in HCC patients (Peng et al., 2022). High FTL expression in HCC is associated with poor prognosis. FTL can promote tumor progression by inhibiting ferroptosis. In this study, GSEA results indicated that FTL not only participates in the iron uptake and transport pathways but is also enriched in the pyrimidine metabolic process; moreover, its expression level was significantly positively correlated with the PSP score. Therefore, FTL is crucial for proliferation and maintenance of malignant HCC cells by regulating cellular iron homeostasis, enhancing nucleotide synthesis requirements, and mediating metabolic reprogramming. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that FTL was significantly upregulated in HBV-related HCC, indicating its association with HBV-HCC pathogenesis and potential as a molecular marker (Yoon et al., 2006). Our study confirmed this result: In vitro, FTL knockdown inhibited the proliferation and migration of HepG2.2.15 cells. These results offer concrete proof that FTL is a viable treatment target for HBV-related HCC. Mechanistically, reduced PSP activity might be one of the upstream driving factors for the upregulation of FTL. HBV infection can simultaneously reprogram nucleotide metabolism and iron homeostasis (Alfano et al., 2025; Chou et al., 2018). Suppressed PSP will force tumor cells to rely more on de novo pyrimidine synthesis (Evans and Guy, 2004), thereby increasing metabolic load and elevating mitochondrial oxidative stress (Okesli et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2022). Oxidative stress is an important trigger of ferroptosis (Yu et al., 2022). Under enhanced oxidative stress, tumor cells may need to upregulate iron storage proteins such as FTL to reduce free iron content and enhance tolerance to ferroptosis (Zheng et al., 2025). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the HBx protein can simultaneously regulate nucleotide metabolism and iron metabolism (Gu et al., 2008; Qadri et al., 2011), suggesting that the PSP activity is coordinated with FTL expression or is regulated by the HBV-related transcriptional network. Based on the above clues, we propose a speculative model: nucleotide metabolism stress and the decrease in PSP activity caused by HBV infection lead to increased oxidative stress; tumor cells enhance their iron storage capacity to evade ferroptosis, thereby upregulating FTL expression and promoting the proliferation and migration of HBV-related HCC. Although this model provides a reasonable biological framework for the association between PSP and FTL, further functional experiments are needed to verify its molecular mechanism.

Our results reveal significantly higher infiltration of macrophages, Tregs and aDCs in the high-risk group, implying the presence of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Tregs are suppressor cells that inhibit immune responses through the release of inhibitory cytokines or direct interaction with effector T cells (Goldmann et al., 2024). In HBV-HCC, Tregs promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment and drive tumor progression by suppressing CD8+ T-cell activity, and their abundance correlates with poor prognosis (He et al., 2024). Macrophages polarize into either M1 or immunosuppressive M2 phenotypes. M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines that foster angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2021). In HBV-HCC, M2 polarization is regulated by miR-155, which modulates macrophage phenotype to enhance HCC cell invasion, proliferation and migration (Fei et al., 2023). Under normal physiological conditions, aDCs function as professional antigen-presenting cells that initiate T-cell-mediated immunity (Yin et al., 2021). However, in certain TME, dendritic cells often exhibit functional impairment, with their antigen presentation and T cell activation capabilities being suppressed, thereby facilitating the formation of immune tolerance (Xiao et al., 2023). During chronic HBV infection, the frequency and absolute number of dendritic cells decline markedly, accompanied by defective maturation and impaired function, resulting in inadequate antiviral immune responses (Li et al., 2015).

Cellular phenotype and function are governed by cell-cell communication, which is mediated primarily by L-R interactions (Almet et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023). Our cell-cell communication analysis revealed that when endothelial cells signaled to malignant cells, the JAM2-F11R and CLDN5-CLDN3 L-R pairs exhibited the strongest interaction. CLDN3 and CLDN5 were aberrantly expressed in HCC and drive invasion, migration and tumor progression by disrupting tight-junction integrity and downstream signaling and fostering epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (Wang et al., 2024). When HBV-SP cells with high PSPG expression acted as signal receivers, the EFNA5-EPHA1 pair originating from NK cells displayed robust interaction. EFNA5 is down-regulated in HCC, and its contribution to HCC remains to be elucidated (Huang et al., 2022). EPHA1 promotes HCC cell proliferation, invasion and migration through modulation of the miR-323a-3p/EBLN3P axis (Li et al., 2025). When HBV+ SP cells with low PSPG expression served as receivers, the HBEGF-EGFR axis, delivered by endothelial cells or macrophages/monocytes, showed the strongest interaction. HBEGF is markedly up-regulated in HCC and inversely correlates with miR-98-5p, which restrains tumor progression and improves prognosis by targeting HBEGF (Ji and Wang, 2023). Additionally, the EGFR rs884225 polymorphism reduces HBV-HCC risk in the Chinese population of Han ethnicity by enhancing miR-3196 binding and thereby down-regulating EGFR expression and cell proliferation (Zhang et al., 2021). When HBV+ SP-low PSPG expression cells transmitted signals to endothelial cells, the INHBE-ACVR2A pair displayed the strongest interaction. ACVR2A is negatively regulated by miR-424-5p and miR-223-3p in HBV-HCC. Its up-regulation activates the BMP-SMAD/ERK pathway to enhance the stemness, chemoresistance and metastasis of tumor cells, while lncRNA-KCNQ10T1 sponges these miRNAs to further amplify ACVR2A’s oncogenic effects (Majumdar et al., 2024). No study has yet addressed INHBE in HCC, so the impact of the INHBE-ACVR2A axis on HBV-HCC warrants investigation. Regarding the above signaling pathways, most current research has focused on HCC. There are relatively few studies on HBV-HCC. Therefore, investigating the role of cell-cell communication is of great significance for understanding the development of HBV-HCC.

Leveraging single-cell transcriptomics, we systematically delineated the pivotal role of the PSP in HBV-HCC and constructed a six-gene prognostic model based on SAT1, AKR7A3, AGT, FTL, SERPINF2, SLC2A2 and experimentally validated the oncogenic activity of FTL in HBV-HCC, providing both new biomarkers for risk stratification and a theoretical rationale for targeting pyrimidine metabolism. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, the scRNA-seq cohort was modest in size and the clinical annotations relied on public repositories, which might not fully capture the heterogeneity of HBV-HCC. Second, although in vitro assays confirmed FTL as an oncogenic driver, the precise molecular mechanisms through which the PSP and the other signature genes drove HBV-HCC remain incompletely defined. The drug-sensitivity predictions were purely computational and awaited experimental corroboration. Third, immune-microenvironment analyses were confined to bioinformatic approaches. Spatial transcriptomics or experimental techniques such as flow cytometry were not integrated to further examine the functional status of immune cells. Future work should therefore expand sample size, launch prospective trials, combine multi-omic layers and complementary technologies, and perform rigorous experimental validation to accelerate clinical translation.
