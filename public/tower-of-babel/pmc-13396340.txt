Neuroligin1 in CCK-interneurons gates social memory formation via a disinhibitory microcircuit in the hippocampal CA2 in male mice
Authors: Miao Wu, Zixu Wang, Kun Cao, Chengyan Zhu, Zixuan Wang, Meng Li, Qiaoyun Ren, Ziyue Yi, Xiaojing Yang, Su Wang, Nannan Chen, Wei Xie, An Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Social memory is essential for daily life, and its impairment is associated with psychiatric disorders. The dorsal CA2 (dCA2) region of the hippocampus is known to support social memory formation, yet how it processes social information remains unclear. Here we show that, in male mice, a specific group of dCA2 neurons—cholecystokinin-positive interneurons (CCK-INs)—acts as a hub that receives social cues and activates dCA2 pyramidal neurons (PyNs). Upon social encounters, CCK-INs are activated and suppress parvalbumin-positive interneurons (PV-INs) via GABAB receptors, thereby releasing PyNs from inhibition. The removal of Neuroligin 1, an autism-associated protein, from CCK-INs disrupts their function and leads to social memory deficits. Importantly, dCA2 CCK-INs receive direct glutamatergic inputs from the medial septum, and chemogenetic activation of these CCK-INs rescues memory deficits caused by septal inhibition. These findings delineate a important circuit mechanism for social memory and highlight potential therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Social memory, or social recognition memory, refers to the memory that humans and animals form regarding conspecifics with whom they have previously interacted. Impairments in social memory have been associated with several neuropsychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and schizophrenia1–3. The formation of social memory is believed to depend on a neural circuit centered in the dorsal hippocampal CA2 (dCA2) region. These dCA2 neurons are located in a narrow region between the CA1 and CA3 areas and exhibit significant differences from CA1 and CA3 neurons in terms of molecular composition and afferent/efferent projections4,5. Studies have shown that dCA2 pyramidal neurons (PyNs) can be activated by inputs related to social information and social odors6,7. Inhibition or damage to dCA2 PyNs blocks the formation of social memory8,9. Similarly, knockout or inhibition of certain critical receptors in dCA2 PyNs, such as oxytocin and vasopressin receptors, impairs neuronal activity and suppresses social memory10–12.

The dCA2 region serves as a crucial hub for both cortical and subcortical inputs related to social information, with its activity modulated by complex neural inputs. Studies have revealed that dCA2 neurons receive projections from numerous extra-hippocampal areas, including the supramammillary nucleus (SUM), the lateral entorhinal cortex (LEC), the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) and the medial septum (MS)13–18. These inputs play key roles in the formation of social memory. In addition, compared to its neighboring regions dCA1 and dCA3, the dCA2 region exhibits a significantly higher density of inhibitory interneurons, including parvalbumin-expressing (PV), somatostatin-expressing (SOM), vasoactive intestinal peptide-expressing (VIP) and calbindin-expressing (Calb) interneurons19,20. Among these interneurons, VIP interneurons (VIP-INs) can release the neuropeptide enkephalin (ENK), which acts on delta-opioid receptors (DOR) at the axonal terminals of PV interneurons (PV-INs), inducing activity-dependent long-term depression of GABAergic transmission (iLTD)21. This process weakens the GABAergic inhibition of dCA2 PyNs by PV-INs, and inhibition of DOR suppresses social recognition memory formation22. Conversely, cholecystokinin-expressing interneurons (CCK-INs) have been reported to mediate iLTD through their endogenous cannabinoid receptor CB1, and inhibition of CB1 receptors also impedes the formation of social recognition memory23. These studies indicate that multiple information inputs collectively influence the activity of CA2 PyNs. However, the hierarchical relationships and underlying molecular mechanisms among different types of dCA2 neurons during social information transmission remain unclear.

Neuroligin 1 (NLG1) is a synaptic adhesion molecule associated with ASD24,25. It is well known for its role at glutamatergic postsynapses, where it forms trans-synaptic complexes with presynaptic Neurexins (NRXNs) to mediate synaptic connectivity25,26. Studies in NLG1 knockout (KO) mice have shown that the loss of NLG1 leads to increased repetitive stereotyped behaviors and deficits in social memory formation27,28. We previously demonstrated that NLG1 modulates repetitive behavior by regulating the activity of D2-expressing medium spiny neurons in the dorsal striatum29. However, the neural circuits and molecular mechanisms underlying NLG1-related social memory impairments remain unclear.

In this study, we found that NLG1 in CCK-INs within the dCA2 region is essential for the formation of social recognition memory. In male mice, dCA2 CCK-INs are activated by social interaction with an unfamiliar conspecific, which leads to the suppression of PV-INs and the activation of PyNs in the dCA2 region. Activation of CCK-INs is a prerequisite for social activity-induced PyNs activation and memory encoding. Loss of NLG1 causes a depolarization block in CCK-INs and reduces their inhibitory control over PV-INs due to elevated expression of KCNIP1 and CB1, ultimately impairing social memory formation. Furthermore, we found that dCA2 CCK-INs receive direct inputs from the MS glutamatergic neurons. Chemogenetic activation of CCK-INs rescues the memory formation deficit caused by inhibition of MS neurons.

Results

NLG1 KO mice exhibit reduced excitability in dCA2 PyNs

Previous studies have reported that NLG1 KO mice exhibit impairment in social memory formation27,28, but the underlying circuit and molecular mechanisms remain unclear. The dCA2 region is considered a critical gateway for social information processing within the hippocampus, and its PyNs have been shown to play a vital role in social information encoding and social memory formation4,5. We hypothesized that NLG1 deficiency may impair the responsiveness of dCA2 PyNs to social interaction. Therefore, we generated NLG1 KO; Amigo2-Cre male mice by crossing Amigo2-Cre mice (which exhibit specific expression in dCA2 PyNs) with NLG1 KO mice and performed in vivo fiber photometry recordings to detect calcium (Ca2+) signals during their interaction with unfamiliar conspecifics of the same gender (Fig. 1a, b). The results showed that dCA2 PyNs in WT (Amigo2-Cre) mice were significantly activated following social interaction, whereas those in NLG1 KO; Amigo2-Cre mice failed to respond (Fig. 1c–f). To investigate the cellular mechanisms underlying the impaired response of PyNs to social interaction in NLG1 KO mice, we examined the in vitro electrophysiological activity of dCA2 PyNs in acute hippocampal slices from both WT and NLG1 KO mice (Fig. 1g). The results showed that action potentials (APs) in NLG1 KO PyNs exhibited kinetic properties, including dV/dt curve, peak amplitude, rise tau, decay tau and half-width, similar to those of WT neurons (Supplementary Fig. 1a–g). However, upon injection of the same amount of current, dCA2 PyNs from NLG1 KO mice fired fewer APs (Fig. 1h, i). They also displayed a lower resting membrane potential (RMP) and a higher rheobase current compared to WT dCA2 PyNs (Fig. 1j–l), collectively indicating reduced intrinsic excitability. Further investigation of synaptic inputs revealed that dCA2 PyNs in NLG1 KO mice had a significantly higher frequency of spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs) (Fig. 1m, o) and also increased γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor antagonist picrotoxin (PTX) sensitive tonic current (Supplementary Fig. 1h, i). In contrast, sIPSC amplitude (Fig. 1n) and spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) remained normal (Supplementary Fig. 1j–l), suggesting enhanced inhibitory synaptic transmission in NLG1 KO PyNs.

Subsequently, to investigate whether social interaction is accompanied by alterations in inhibitory synaptic inputs onto PyNs, we injected an AAV encoding the GABA sensor (rAAV-EF1α-DIO-iGABASnFR) into the dCA2 region of Amigo2-Cre and NLG1 KO; Amigo2-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 1o, p). Fiber photometry recordings during social exposure revealed a significant decrease in GABAergic signals onto PyNs following social contact in control Amigo2-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 1q, r). In contrast, this reduction was absent in NLG1 KO; Amigo2-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 1q, s). These results suggest that social behavior may induce disinhibition of dCA2 PyNs, which is abolished in NLG1 KO mice.

We proceeded to block GABA receptors to test whether the elevated inhibitory input underlies the reduced PyN excitability and impaired social memory formation in NLG1 KO mice. First, when inhibitory inputs were blocked using the GABAA receptor antagonist PTX and the GABAB receptor antagonist CGP52432 (CGP), the differences in both excitability and RMP between NLG1 KO and WT dCA2 PyNs were abolished (Fig. 1p–r). In contrast, blocking glutamatergic transmission with NBQX and AP5 had less effect on the reduced intrinsic excitability (Supplementary Fig. 1m, n). Next, to further determine whether enhanced inhibitory synaptic transmission in the dCA2 region underlies the social memory deficits in NLG1 KO mice, we locally administered PTX and CGP into the dCA2 via cannula implantation and conducted social memory tests (Fig. 1s–u). Results showed that both experimental groups displayed prolonged sniffing duration toward the unfamiliar mouse (S1) (Fig. 1v), indicating intact social preference. In the social memory test, saline-injected NLG1 KO mice spent comparable exploration time with both S1 and a second unfamiliar mouse (S2), reflecting an inability to distinguish familiar from unfamiliar social partners (social memory loss). In contrast, KO mice injected with PTX and CGP showed a significant preference for S2 (Fig. 1w), demonstrating that the social memory deficit was effectively rescued by blocking GABAergic neurotransmission in the dCA2 region.

NLG1 in CCK-INs is essential for social memory formation

The above experiments indicate that excessive inhibitory synaptic input is a key factor leading to reduced dCA2 PyN activity and impaired social memory formation in NLG1 KO mice. To determine whether the specific loss of NLG1 in dCA2 PyNs impairs social memory, we employed a knockdown (KD) strategy using the rAAV-DIO-EGFP-shRNA(NLG1) virus to identify the critical cell type in which NLG1 regulates social memory formation. First, we bilaterally injected the KD virus into the dCA2 region of Amigo2-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c), but found that these mice exhibited normal social preference and social memory (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). In contrast, when NLG1 was knocked down broadly in dCA2 neurons using a Syn promoter-driven AAV (Supplementary Fig. 2f–j), or specifically in inhibitory neurons using a GAD67 promoter-driven AAV (Fig. 2a–c), the mice showed significant social memory deficits (Supplementary Fig. 2k, l; Fig. 2d, e). We next sought to identify which types of neurons in the dCA2 region, particularly interneurons, respond to social activity. Immediate early genes (IEGs), such as c-fos, NPAS4 and Arc, which are activated by neuronal activity, are widely utilized to identify brain regions and cells activated following learning30–32. In this study, we conducted c-fos immunostaining to detect activated cells in the dorsal hippocampus of mice that had engaged in social interactions with an unfamiliar mouse (Fig. 2f). The results indicated that, after social interaction, the number of c-fos-positive (c-fos+) cells in the dCA2 region significantly increased (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Further analysis of the identity of c-fos+ cells in the dCA2 region revealed that almost all c-fos+ cells were GABA-positive but very rarely (3 out of 129) co-localized with the dCA2 PyN marker Regulator of G-protein Signaling 14 (RGS14) (Fig. 2g–i).

To investigate the identities of c-fos+ GABAergic cells, we performed immunostaining using antibodies for PV, SST and CCK. The results revealed that only 14.3% and 8.49% of c-fos+ cells were positive for PV and SST, respectively, while 66.11% of c-fos+ cells were CCK-positive (2.889 ± 0.309 among the 4.333 ± 0.333 c-fos+ cells in dCA2 region per slice, Fig. 2j–l and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). Given that CCK-containing basket cells in the hippocampus wrap around the soma of PyNs and other interneurons in the stratum pyramidale (SP) layer33, we observed co-localization of CCK and c-fos double-positive cells primarily in the stratum oriens (SO) and stratum radiatum (SR), but not in the SP layer of the dCA2 region.

Subsequently, we sought to determine whether NLG1 regulates social memory formation through CCK-INs. We co-injected a CCK-promoter-driven Cre virus (rAAV-CCK-Cre) and an NLG1 KD virus into the dCA2 region of WT mice to specifically inhibit NLG1 in CCK-INs (Fig. 2m, n and Supplementary Fig. 2m, n). Behavioral tests showed that the mice exhibited impaired social memory formation (Fig. 2o, p). A similar result was observed when we injected the CCK-Cre virus into the NLG1f/f mice (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). Immunostaining for glutamic acid decarboxylase 67 (GAD67) revealed that over 90% of the fluorescent protein driven by the CCK promoter was expressed in GABAergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i). In contrast, when we specifically suppressed NLG1 expression in PV-INs using PV-Cre mice, the mice retained the ability to form normal social memory (Supplementary Fig. 3j–l). These results suggest that NLG1 may specifically participate in social memory formation through dCA2 CCK-INs.

Suppression of CCK-INs impairs social memory formation

The above results indicate that CCK-INs in the dCA2 region are activated by social interaction, and that suppressing NLG1 within these neurons impairs social memory formation. This raises several questions: First, how does the activity of CCK-INs influence social memory and regulate the function of PyNs? Second, what is the impact of NLG1 deficiency on this process, and what are the underlying molecular mechanisms? Third, from which upstream sources do CCK-INs receive social information?

To address these questions, we first needed to determine whether manipulating CCK-IN activity affects social memory formation in mice. We utilized chemogenetic modulation by bilaterally injecting rAAV-CCK-hM3Dq-mCherry and rAAV-CCK-hM4Di-EGFP viruses into the dCA2 regions of the left and right hemispheres. Two weeks after viral expression, clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) was administered to either activate or inhibit CCK-INs, followed by a social behavior test (Fig. 3a–d). As shown in Fig. 3e, all three groups of mice exhibited intact social preference. In the social recognition memory test, mice in the CCK-mCherry and CCK-hM3Dq-mCherry groups, but not in the CCK-hM4Di-EGFP group, displayed a significant preference for S2 (Fig. 3f). We next employed in vivo fiber photometry using rAAV-αCaMKII-GCaMP6f and rAAV-CCK-hM4Di viruses to monitor Ca²⁺ activity in dCA2 PyN of WT mice, investigating whether this activity is modulated by CCK-INs (Fig. 3g, h). Our recordings demonstrated that inhibiting CCK-INs blocked the social interaction-induced activation of PyN Ca²⁺ signals (Fig. 3i–l). These findings indicate that activation of CCK-INs positively contributes to social memory formation, whereas their suppression impairs PyN activity and memory formation.

To further investigate how CCK-IN activity influences PyN function, we expressed the light-sensitive channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in CCK-INs of WT mice via AAV injection and performed in vitro electrophysiological recordings (Fig. 3m). We found that both 1 Hz and 20 Hz (10 mW, 473 nm blue light) optical stimulation reliably elicited action potentials in CCK-INs (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Subsequent recordings from dCA2 PyNs revealed that 1 Hz light stimulation did not induce fast synaptic currents, regardless of whether PyNs were voltage-clamped at 0 mV or − 70 mV (Supplementary Fig. 4d). However, sustained 20 Hz optical stimulation evoked significant depolarization in PyNs under current-clamp conditions (Fig. 3n). These results suggest the absence of direct ionotropic receptor-mediated synaptic connections between CCK-INs and PyNs, indicating instead potential indirect synaptic modulation or metabotropic neurotransmitter receptor-mediated mechanisms. We then repeated the same ChR2 labeling and electrophysiological recordings in NLG1 KO mice. While 20 Hz stimulation also induced PyN depolarization, the amplitude was significantly smaller compared to WT mice (Fig. 3o, p). When GABAergic transmission was blocked with PTX and CGP, the difference in depolarization amplitude between WT and NLG1 KO PyNs was abolished (Fig. 3q).

Given the co-release of GABA and CCK by CCK-INs and the abundant expression of the CCK2 receptor (CCK2R) in hippocampal PyNs34,35, we confirmed CCK2R expression in RGS14+ dCA2 PyNs through immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Accordingly, bath applying the CCK2R antagonist NAS together with PTX and CGP completely abolished the optically evoked depolarization (Supplementary Fig. 4g–i). To more directly assess the impact of CCK-INs on PyNs during social behavior, we expressed the CCK sensor (rAAV-EF1α-DIO-CCK 1.0) in dCA2 PyNs and monitored signal changes during social interaction (Supplementary Fig. 4j). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4k, l, we observed a significant increase in the CCK sensor signal following social contact, consistent with activation of CCK-INs (Fig. 2l). Next, we investigated the roles of CCK2 and GABA receptors in social memory via cannula-targeted infusion of inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 4m, n). Inhibition of the CCK2 receptor using NAS had no effect on social memory, while inhibition of the GABAB receptor with CGP only partially blocked the formation of social memory. However, simultaneous administration of NAS and CGP completely blocked social memory (Supplementary Fig. 4o, p), indicating that activation of CCK2 and GABAB receptors cooperatively contributes to the formation of social memory.

These findings demonstrate that CCK-INs activate PyNs through the combined actions of GABA receptor-mediated disinhibition and CCK2 receptor-mediated excitation, both of which contribute to social memory formation. In addition, the loss of NLG1 results in a more specific deficit in GABAergic transmission.

CCK-INs suppress PV-INs through GABAB receptor

The above results indicate that the formation of social memory requires the activation of CCK-INs in the dCA2 region. However, in vivo fiber photometry recordings of GABA signals revealed a decrease in GABAergic synaptic input to PyNs during social interaction, suggesting that CCK-INs may mediate the disinhibition of dCA2 PyNs by recruiting another type of GABAergic inhibitory cell.

PV-INs, which are highly expressed in the dCA2 region, exert strong inhibitory control over dCA2 pyramidal neurons36,37. Previous studies in the dCA1 region have demonstrated that PV-INs receive inhibitory input from CCK-INs38. Therefore, we injected Cre-dependent chemogenetic activation and inhibition viruses into the dCA2 region of PV-Cre mice and performed a three-chamber social behavior test (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). We found that activation, but not inhibition, of PV-INs impaired social recognition memory (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e). These findings suggest that excessive PV-IN activity disrupts the encoding of social recognition memory. Subsequently, we investigated whether CCK-INs exert an inhibitory effect on PV-INs. To this end, we expressed ChR2 in CCK-INs and performed in vitro whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in PV-INs (Fig. 4f). The results showed that in WT mice, 1 Hz blue light stimulation (10 mW, 473 nm) failed to elicit fast inhibitory currents in PV-INs (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). However, sustained 20 Hz optical stimulation significantly suppressed action potential generation in PV-INs, indicating tonic inhibition (Fig. 4g). This reduction was largely attenuated by the GABAB receptor antagonist CGP (Fig. 4g, h). We also examined the effects of sustained 20 Hz light stimulation on sIPSCs in PV-INs, and the results showed no significant differences in either amplitude or frequency (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f).

To further elucidate whether activation of CCK-INs induces disinhibition of PyNs, we conducted sIPSC recordings in PyNs during optogenetic activation of CCK-INs (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Quantitative analysis revealed that both the frequency of sIPSCs and the number of large IPSCs (>50 pA), potentially induced by action potentials, were significantly reduced following CCK-IN activation, while the sIPSC amplitude remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6h–J). To investigate the potential involvement of PV-INs in mediating CCK-IN-induced disinhibition of PyNs, we employed a dual-viral strategy by co-injecting rAAV-CCK-hM3Dq-mCherry and rAAV-EF1α-DIO-ChR2-EGFP into the dCA2 region of PV-Cre mice (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 6k). Electrophysiological recordings confirmed that a single 473 nm light stimulation at 10 mW reliably elicited an AP in PV-INs (Supplementary Fig. 6l) and evoked fast, PTX-sensitive IPSCs in dCA2 PyNs (Supplementary Fig. 6m). Subsequently, we examined the impact of chemogenetic activation of CCK-INs (Supplementary Fig. 6n, o) on the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) of PV-IN-evoked IPSCs with interstimulus intervals of 50, 100 and 200 ms (Fig. 4i, j). As illustrated in Fig. 4k, paired light pulses at each interval consistently elicited significant paired-pulse inhibition (PPI) in both groups. Notably, administration of CNO resulted in a more pronounced reduction in the amplitude of the second IPSC, especially at 50 and 100 ms interstimulus intervals, reflecting enhanced PPI and consequently reduced PV-IN-mediated inhibition of PyNs (Fig. 4j, k). We subsequently found that the enhanced PPI was abolished by the GABAB receptor antagonist CGP (Fig. 4l, m). These findings provide evidence that activation of CCK-INs weakens the inhibitory efficacy of PV-INs on PyNs through a GABAB receptor-mediated mechanism.

To further investigate the relationship between dCA2 CCK-INs and PV-INs during social interaction, we performed in vivo fiber photometry recordings of Ca2+ signals in PV-INs. First, we injected the rAAV-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6f virus into the dCA2 region of PV-Cre mice and conducted fiber photometry recordings three weeks later (Supplementary Fig. 6p, q). The results revealed that Ca2+ signals in dCA2 PV-INs decreased after the onset of social interaction (Supplementary Fig. 6r, s), suggesting inhibition of PV-IN activity during social memory formation. Next, we co-injected rAAV-CCK-hM4Di-mCherry and rAAV-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6f viruses into the dCA2 region of PV-Cre mice (Fig. 4n, o). Fiber photometry recordings following CNO administration demonstrated that social stimuli no longer elicited suppression of Ca2+ activity in PV-INs (Fig. 4p–s), indicating that dCA2 CCK-INs serve as a prerequisite for the inactivation of PV-INs in the transmission of social information.

Given that CCK-INs exert GABAergic synaptic inhibition on PV-INs, we assessed the effect of blocking GABAergic synaptic transmission on PV-IN activity. The results showed that application of the GABA receptor antagonists PTX and CGP abolished the hyperexcitability of PV-INs in NLG1 KO mice (Fig. 5g, h), confirming that their excessive excitation is linked to reduced GABAergic inhibition. In addition, we observed that the attenuation of AP generation in PV-INs elicited by high-frequency stimulation of CCK-INs was also blocked (Fig. 5i–k). Taken together, these results suggest that the loss of NLG1 compromises the inhibitory effect of CCK-INs on PV-INs.

To investigate whether the reduced inhibition from CCK-INs onto PV-INs in NLG1 KO mice results from altered innervation, we assessed the colocalization of CCK-IN axons with PV-INs in the dCA2 region using EGFP labeling and PV immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 7m). Quantification of the colocalization area revealed no significant differences between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 7n), indicating that the connectivity between CCK-INs and PV-INs is structurally intact in NLG1 KO mice. Subsequently, we assessed the activation of dCA2 PV-INs in WT and NLG1 KO mice using c-fos staining. Among littermates that had not undergone novel social interaction, the number of c-fos-positive PV-INs, but not the total number of PV-INs, was significantly higher in NLG1 KO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 5l–n). After social stimulation, the number of c-fos-positive PV-INs did not differ significantly between the groups. These findings further support the hyperactivation of dCA2 PV-INs in NLG1 KO mice.

Next, we investigated whether the social memory deficits in NLG1 KO mice are associated with hyperactivation of PV-INs. We injected rAAV-PV-Cre and rAAV-EF1α-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry viruses into the dCA2 region of NLG1 KO mice and conducted a social memory test two weeks later (Fig. 5o). The results demonstrated that inhibiting PV-INs rescued the social memory deficits in NLG1 KO mice (Fig. 5p, q), indicating that excessive PV-IN activity underlies the social memory impairments observed in these mice.

Compromised activity in NLG1 KO CCK-INs

These results indicate that the hyperexcitability of PV-INs in NLG1 KO mice is a key factor contributing to impaired social memory formation. However, since inhibiting NLG1 in PV-INs had a lesser effect on memory formation, and the suppression of PV-INs by CCK-INs is disrupted in NLG1 KO mice, we hypothesize that NLG1 deficiency may impair the ability of CCK-INs to process social information and inhibit PV-IN activity.

First, we examined the response of CCK-INs to social stimuli in NLG1 KO mice using c-fos staining and fiber photometry. We found that after social stimulation, the number of c-fos-positive CCK-INs in the dCA2 region significantly increased in both WT and NLG1 KO mice, with no difference between the two groups (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 8a). However, the peak amplitude and total intensity (area under the curve, AUC) of the social interaction induced Ca2+ events were lower in NLG1 KO mice (Fig. 6g, h). KCNIP1 functions as a Kv4-associated protein that enhances Kv4-mediated A-type potassium currents and may contribute to depolarization block40–42. To confirm the elevated expression of KCNIP1 in NLG1 KO hippocampus, we performed immunostaining using an anti-KCNIP1 antibody, which revealed a significant increase in KCNIP1 fluorescence intensity in the dCA2 region (Fig. 7e, f). We also examined ion channel currents in CCK-INs from both WT and NLG1 KO mice. Because A-type potassium channels rapidly activate and inactivate upon depolarization and are fully activated at positive membrane potentials41, neurons were held at a hyperpolarized potential (−100 mV) to ensure complete closure of Kv4 channels, followed by a step depolarization to +;40 mV (Fig. 7g). The transient peak current, isolated from the sustained delayed rectifier potassium current (IK), was used to quantify the Kv4-mediated A-type potassium current (IA). Our results demonstrated that IA was significantly larger in CCK-INs from NLG1 KO mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 7h). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in voltage-dependent sodium currents (INa) or IK between WT and NLG1 KO CCK-INs (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d).

CB1, the primary receptor of the endocannabinoid system, is abundantly expressed in hippocampal CCK-INs and is well known for its role as a presynaptic receptor that inhibits neurotransmitter release43,44. Further immunostaining with a CB1 antibody confirmed the RNA sequencing results (Fig. 7i, j). To investigate whether elevated CB1 levels affect synaptic regulation of PV-INs, we performed sIPSC recordings. Our results showed that administration of the CB1 receptor antagonist AM251 abolished the reduced sIPSC frequency observed in PV-INs of NLG1 KO mice (Fig. 7k–m). Moreover, intra-dCA2 infusion of the CB1 agonist WIN-55 partially impaired social memory formation (Supplementary Fig. 9e–g), suggesting that the increased CB1 expression in CCK-INs of NLG1 KO mice may contribute, at least in part, to the observed memory deficits. Conversely, a previous study showed that inhibiting CB1 in the dCA2 region impairs social recognition memory formation without affecting social preference23. Similarly, we found that injection of the CB1 antagonist AM251 into the dCA2 region via cannula also inhibited social recognition memory in mice, and this effect could be reversed by chemogenetic inhibition of PV-INs (Supplementary Fig. 9h–k). These findings indicate that the social memory loss caused by CB1 inhibition is dependent on PV-IN activity.

In summary, our snRNA-seq and protein validation data collectively demonstrate that NLG1 deficiency leads to the concurrent upregulation of KCNIP1 and CB1 in CCK-INs. These molecular changes result in an enhanced A-type potassium current and reduced inhibitory synaptic transmission, which together likely underlie the impaired inhibition of PV-INs and the consequent deficits in social memory formation.

Activation of dCA2 CCK-INs restores social memory deficit caused by MS glutamatergic neuron inhibition

We next investigated the upstream source of CCK-INs during social memory formation. Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibiting the activity of MS neurons projecting to the dCA2 region impairs social recognition memory in mice18. However, the mechanism by which MS neurons influence the dCA2 region remains unclear. First, we injected the rAAV-Syn-hM4Di-EGFP virus into the MS and examined the effect of MS neuronal inhibition on social interaction-induced c-fos expression in the dCA2 region (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The results demonstrated that suppression of MS neurons abolished the social stimulation-induced increase in the number of c-fos-positive neurons in the dCA2 region (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Since CCK-INs constitute the primary neuronal population expressing c-fos in dCA2 following social stimulation, these findings suggest that the activity of CCK-INs is modulated by MS neurons.

Next, given that CCK-IN is a prerequisite for PyNs activation during social interaction, we hypothesized that CCK-INs might serve as a critical hub for upstream social information entering the hippocampus, and that activating CCK neurons could potentially mimic social information input. To test this, we injected the rAAV-Syn-hM4Di-EGFP virus into the MS region and the rAAV-CCK-hM3Dq-mCherry virus into the dCA2 region (Fig. 8a–d). We observed that the axons of MS neurons intersected with the soma and processes of CCK-INs (Fig. 8c). To investigate whether a direct functional connection exists between MS neurons and dCA2 CCK-INs, we injected rAAV-Syn-ChR2-EGFP virus into the MS and CCK-mCherry virus into the dCA2 region of WT mice (Fig. 8g). Electrophysiological recordings were performed two weeks later. When CCK-INs were voltage-clamped at − 70 mV and subjected to a single 10 mW, 473 nm blue-light stimulation in brain slices, an outward current was recorded in 61.54% of CCK-INs (8/13 neurons, 3 mice). This current was completely abolished by application of the AMPA receptor (AMPAR) blocker NBQX (Fig. 8h), indicating the presence of glutamatergic synaptic transmission from MS neurons to CCK-INs. Moreover, application of tetrodotoxin (TTX) blocked the light-evoked postsynaptic current, and subsequent addition of the potassium channel blocker 4-aminopyridine (4-AP) restored the current (Fig. 8i), confirming a direct monosynaptic connection between MS glutamatergic neurons and CCK-INs. To investigate whether glutamatergic inputs from the MS to dCA2 CCK-INs were affected by NLG1 deletion, we injected rAAV-Syn-ChR2-EGFP into the MS of NLG1 KO mice and recorded light-evoked responses in dCA2 CCK-INs. The evoked currents in NLG1 KO mice were comparable to those in WT mice (Fig. 8j), suggesting that NLG1 deficiency does not alter these glutamatergic inputs.

To further elucidate the functional roles of MS glutamatergic neurons and CCK-INs in social memory formation, we injected the rAAV-αCaMKII-hM4Di-EGFP virus into the MS region to inhibit MS glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 8k, l) and either CCK-mCherry or CCK-hM3Dq-mCherry virus into the dCA2 region (Fig. 8m, n). Three weeks later, we administered CNO and conducted social memory tests. The results showed that social memory formation was impaired in mice with inhibited MS glutamatergic neurons. In contrast, the impaired memory was rescued in mice whose dCA2 CCK-INs expressed hM3Dq-mCherry (Fig. 8o, p).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically identified that CCK-INs in the dCA2 region serve as a critical hub for encoding social information by mapping the cellular mechanisms underlying NLG1 deficiency-induced impairments in social memory formation. Our results propose a hierarchical model of information processing within the neural microcircuit between interneurons and pyramidal neurons in the dorsal CA2 region, while also revealing a mechanism by which the autism-associated molecule NLG1 regulates social behavior.

First, we found that KD or KO of NLG1 specifically in dCA2 CCK-INs disrupted social memory formation. Although both PyNs and PV-INs neurons are involved in social memory, suppression of NLG1 expression in these neurons had a lesser effect on social memory formation. This phenomenon can be attributed not only to the distinct compositional characteristics of different neuronal subtypes but also to the specific role of CCK-INs within the neuronal microcircuit. Through IEG staining, we found that social interaction with a novel mouse induced c-Fos expression in dCA2 inhibitory neurons, but not in PyNs (Fig. 2g–i). The absence of c-Fos expression in dCA2 PyNs is consistent with previous reports indicating that high expression of RGS14 inhibits Ca2+-dependent synaptic plasticity and transcription-related signaling pathways45,46. Next, we discovered that over 60% of the activated inhibitory neurons were CCK-positive (Fig. 2j–l), suggesting that CCK-INs serve as the primary interneurons responsive to social information input. In addition, by employing chemogenetic approaches to inhibit, rather than activate, CCK-INs, we observed a significant impairment in the formation of social recognition memory (Fig. 3a–f), indicating that activation of CCK-INs is necessary. Furthermore, fiber photometry recordings revealed a significant increase in Ca2+ signals in CCK-INs and CCK probe signals in PyNs during social interaction (Fig. 6e–g and Supplementary Fig. 4j–l), indicating that CCK-INs are activated by social interactions. In summary, these data indicate that CCK-INs are activated by social interaction, and this increase in activity is essential for social memory formation.

We also uncovered the detrimental role of PV-INs in the formation of social recognition memory. Specifically, the activation, rather than inhibition, of dCA2 PV-INs was found to disrupt memory formation (Supplementary Fig. 5a–e). Studies have demonstrated that the dCA2 region contains a high density of PV-INs, which exert strong inhibitory effects on PyNs36,37. Inhibition of DOR-iLTD in the dCA2 region mitigates the inhibitory effects of PV-INs on PyNs, while activation of excitatory projections from the SuM to PV-INs in the dCA2 region also suppresses social memory formation22,47. Through fiber photometry recordings, we further observed reductions in Ca2+ signals in PV-INs and GABA probe signals in PyNs in the dCA2 region of WT mice during social interactions (Supplementary Fig. 1o–r; Fig. 4n–r and Supplementary Fig. 6p–s), indicating that social information input suppresses PV-IN activity. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that PV-IN activity negatively affects the formation of social recognition memory.

A pivotal finding of our study is the elucidation of the hierarchical regulatory relationship among CCK-INs, PV-INs and PyNs. First, regarding the mechanism of interaction between CCK-INs and PV-INs, we observed that activation of CCK-INs does not induce fast currents in PV-INs but instead elicits tonic inhibition mediated by GABAB receptors (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c and Fig. 4g, h). Simultaneously, it attenuates synaptic inhibition from PV-INs onto PyNs (Fig. 4i–m) and reduces the frequency of sIPSCs in PyNs (Supplementary Fig. 6g–j). We propose that this may represent an axo-axonic presynaptic inhibitory mechanism. However, the specific underlying processes require further elucidation using higher-resolution imaging techniques. Next, given that PV-INs directly suppress PyNs via GABAA receptor-mediated currents (Supplementary Fig. 6k–m), these findings collectively suggest a disinhibitory microcircuit mediated through a three-level neuronal hierarchy. In addition, the other transmitter released by CCK-INs, the CCK neuropeptide, which has been implicated in feeding behavior, anxiety and cognition48–50, is also involved in social behavior. Our results show that the depolarization of PyNs induced by CCK-IN activation involves the activity of CCK2 receptors (Supplementary Fig. 4g–i). Moreover, we found that CCK2 receptor activation contributes to social memory formation (Supplementary Fig. 4m–p). Based on these findings, we conclude that the depolarization of PyNs induced by CCK-INs is mediated through dual mechanisms: an indirect disinhibition mechanism via GABAergic signaling and a direct metabotropic signaling pathway mediated by CCK2 receptors.

The in vitro slice recordings demonstrate that CCK-INs disinhibit PyNs and suppresses PV-INs. In vivo fiber photometry recordings and behavioral experiments further confirm this neuronal hierarchy. Fiber photometry results in PyNs show that during social interaction, Ca2+ signals in PyNs increase (Fig. 1a–e), accompanied by a decrease in received GABA neurotransmitter (Supplementary Fig. 1o–r) and an increase in CCK peptide levels (Supplementary Fig. 4j–l). These findings indicate that social interaction activates both PyNs and CCK-INs. However, recordings from PV-INs reveal a significant decrease in Ca2+ activity during social interaction (Supplementary Fig. 6p–s), suggesting that PV-INs exhibit reduced cellular activity during social information transmission. Furthermore, inhibition of CCK-INs prevents the inactivation of PV-INs and the activation of PyNs induced by social interaction (Figs. 3i–l,4n–s). The social memory deficits resulting from CCK-IN inhibition can be rescued by suppressing PV-IN activity (Fig. 4a–e). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that activation of CCK-INs is a prerequisite for dCA2 PyN activation and social information encoding during social behavior, thereby extending and refining the existing theoretical framework of social memory formation5,8,23. Prior studies have described a role for CCK-INs in modulating inhibitory synaptic transmission within the dCA2 region using indirect pharmacological manipulations51,52. In contrast, our study provides direct evidence revealing their critical involvement in social memory formation and the hierarchical circuit organization of the dCA2 region, thereby offering a complementary perspective to previous work.

In addition, we demonstrated that activation of CCK-INs is sufficient to restore social cognitive memory impaired by MS inhibition. Previous studies have shown that cholinergic, glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons in the MS regulate social memory formation through the dCA2 region16,18,53,54. Moreover, glutamatergic neurons in the MS directly influence dCA2 PyNs18. In this study, our data indicate that MS glutamatergic neurons also directly target dCA2 CCK-INs and transmit social information. First, our experiments revealed that social stimulation-evoked c-fos+ neurons in the dCA2 region were predominantly CCK+ (Fig. 2l), and the increase in c-fos+ neuron numbers was abolished by inhibiting MS neurons (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). Second, we demonstrated that dCA2 CCK-INs receive direct glutamatergic input from MS neurons (Fig. 8g–i), and social recognition memory deficits induced by inhibition of MS neurons or excitatory neurons could be rescued through chemogenetic activation of dCA2 CCK-INs (Fig. 8f, p), suggesting that CCK-INs act as a critical gateway for social information flow from the MS to the hippocampus. Furthermore, on a broader level, research has indicated that MS neuron projections to the hippocampus regulate hippocampal theta oscillations55–57. During learning tasks, CCK-INs exhibit pronounced theta-phase locking and may serve as potential theta-driving neurons56,58,59. Collectively, these findings suggest that dCA2 CCK-INs may serve as a hub for social information transmission and neural oscillations dependent on MS neurons.

How do deficits in NLG1 affect CCK-IN-mediated social information transmission? Our research reveals that NLG1 deficiency impairs the GABAergic output of CCK-INs in the dCA2 region. First, using c-fos staining, in vivo fiber photometry Ca2+ imaging, and sEPSC recordings, we observed that CCK-INs in NLG1 KO mice receive upstream glutamatergic synaptic inputs normally and are activated by social interaction (Figs. 6b, e,8j and Supplementary Fig. 9b–d), indicating that social information is properly transmitted to CCK-INs. However, further analysis of the Ca2+ imaging data showed that the amplitude of Ca2+ activity in CCK-INs of NLG1 KO mice during social interaction is lower than that in WT CCK-INs, suggesting compromised processing of social information. Subsequently, patch-clamp recordings revealed that NLG1-deficient CCK-INs exhibit a more pronounced depolarization block under highly depolarized potentials (Fig. 6l). Furthermore, single-nucleus RNA sequencing and immunostaining identified upregulated expression of the Kcnip1 and Cnr1 genes and proteins in CCK-INs of NLG1 KO mice (Fig. 7d, f, j), which may result in excessively high Kv4-mediated A-type potassium currents (Fig. 7g, h) and presynaptic neurotransmitter release inhibition (Fig. 7k–m), collectively impairing the output efficiency of CCK-INs. In addition, administration of the CB1 agonist WIN-55 in the dCA2 region demonstrated that excessive activation of CB1 receptors can partially block social memory formation. The mechanism by which NLG1 deletion alters transcription in CCK-INs remains unclear. We speculate that full-length NLG1 or its soluble intracellular fragment NLG1-CTD29,60 may participate in this process via direct or indirect retrograde signaling, but further investigation is required. Consequently, we propose that in NLG1 KO mice, weakened output from CCK-INs leads to hyperexcitability of PV-INs, which in turn prevents the formation of social memory (Supplementary Fig. 10d).

In summary, our study reveals the essential roles of dCA2 CCK-INs and NLG1 in promoting PyN activity and social recognition memory formation. We present a clear network illustrating how dCA2 interneurons and PyNs process social information and encode social recognition memory, as well as how NLG1 in CCK-INs modulates the function of this microcircuit. This work enhances the understanding of the mechanisms underlying social recognition memory encoding and provides potential therapeutic targets for mental disorders associated with social memory loss.

Methods

Animals

All experiments utilized adult male C57BL/6 mice aged 8 to 14 weeks. Amigo2-Cre transgenic mice (#030215) and NLG1 knockout mice (#008136) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Pv-Cre mice (#T006841), Nlg1-floxed (Nlg1f/f) (#T057608) and wild-type mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). The mice were maintained under standard housing conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on: 7:00 a.m. − 7:00 p.m.), group-housed (4-5 per cage), and provided with ad libitum access to food and water. Bedding and cages were replaced weekly to ensure hygiene. The care and use of all mice adhered to protocols approved by the Animal Care Committee of Southeast University, Nanjing, China (experiment number: 20211101004).

Stereotaxic injection

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol at a dosage of 20 μl/g (M2920, EasyCheck). Following anesthesia, the mice were positioned on a stereotaxic apparatus obtained from Shenzhen RWD Life Science Co., Ltd. The scalp of each mouse was meticulously incised longitudinally. Once a stable condition was established, a 0.5 mm skull drill was employed to perform a craniotomy. The targeted brain region for injection was the dorsal CA2 area, with the following coordinates: anteroposterior (AP): − 1.6 mm, mediolateral (ML): ± 1.6 mm, and dorsoventral (DV): − 1.7 mm. Behavioral tests

All behavioral assessments were performed during the diurnal cycle (7:00 a.m. − 7:00 p.m.) under low-intensity illumination. The subject mice underwent a minimum acclimatization period of two hours in the testing environment prior to experimentation. Inter-trial decontamination was systematically performed using 75% ethanol solution followed by distilled water rinsing.

For the social recognition memory test, a tripartite chamber apparatus (60 × 40 × 20 cm) with interconnecting corridors was utilized for the testing. Age-matched wild-type stranger mice, unfamiliar to the experimental subjects, were confined within perforated cylindrical enclosures. There are three phases: Habituation (Phase I): 10 min unrestricted exploration of all compartments. Social approach (Phase II): 10 min introduction of Stranger 1 (S1) into a lateral chamber. Novelty preference (Phase III): 10 mine concurrent presentation of Stranger 2 (S2) in the contralateral chamber. Proximal olfactory investigation directed toward S1, S2, and neutral enclosures was quantified via an automated video-tracking system (EthoVision XT, Noldus). Social preference index = (tS1−tempty)/(tS1 + tempty); Social recognition index = (tS2 − tS1)/(tS2 + tS1).

Electrophysiological recording

All electrophysiological recordings were performed in the hippocampal dCA2 region of male mice aged 5–7 weeks. Similar electrophysiological procedures have been described previously29. The animals were first transcardially perfused with an ice-cold solution containing (in mM): 10 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 25 glucose, 30 NaHCO₃, 110 sucrose, 20 HEPES, 1.25 NaH₂PO₄, 2 thiourea, 5 Na ascorbate, 3 Na pyruvate, 0.5 CaCl₂, and 10 MgCl₂. Brains were then quickly removed, and sagittal slices of 300 μm thickness were prepared using a vibratome (Leica, VT1000S) in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) composed of (in mM): 120 NaCl, 3.0 KCl, 1.0 NaH₂PO₄, 26 NaHCO₃, 11 D‑glucose, 1.2 MgSO₄ and 2.0 CaCl₂, bubbled with 95% O₂/5% CO₂. The slices were transferred to a recording chamber after recovery at 28 °C for at least 2 hours. Recordings were performed at room temperature (~25 °C). Interneurons expressing mCherry or EGFP were identified by their fluorescent signal under a microscope (Olympus BX51WI, Japan) equipped with appropriate filters and LED light engines (CoolLED‑pE100, 470 nm or 550 nm, UK). The following drugs were used: AP5 (50 μM, Cat# 0106, Tocris), NBQX (20 μM, Cat# N171, Sigma‑Aldrich), Biocytin (10 mM, Cat# B4261, Sigma‑Aldrich), picrotoxin (PTX, 100 μM, Cat# R284556, Sigma‑Aldrich), CGP 52432 (5 μM, Cat# B6655, APExBIO), nastorazepide (5 μM, Cat# S6939, Selleckchem), and TTX (0.2 μM, Cat# 554412, Sigma‑Aldrich). For membrane potential testing and current injection experiments, neurons were held in current‑clamp mode with whole-cell recording using glass pipettes (4–7 MΩ) filled with an intracellular solution containing (in mM): 130.0 C₆H₁₁KO₇, 5.0 NaCl, 1.0 MgCl₂, 10.0 HEPES, 0.05 EGTA, 3.0 Mg‑ATP and 0.3 Na₃GTP (pH 7.25, 280–300 mOsm). For EPSC recording, cells were clamped at −70 mV in voltage‑clamp mode and bathed in ACSF containing 100 μM PTX, with an internal solution composed of (in mM): 130.0 CsMeSO₄, 5.0 NaCl, 1.0 MgCl₂, 10.0 HEPES, 0.05 EGTA, 3.0 Mg‑ATP, 0.3 Na₃GTP, and 5.0 QX‑314 (pH 7.25, 280–300 mOsm). For IPSC recording, 50 μM AP5 and 20 μM NBQX were added to the ACSF, neurons were clamped at 0 mV with the above intracellular solution. Throughout recordings, series resistance was monitored by applying a − 3 mV step at the end of each sweep. The experiments were discarded if resistance changed by more than 20%. For tonic current recording, normal ACSF without any inhibitors was first perfused while cells were voltage‑clamped at − 70 mV. After 5 min of stabilization, 100 μM PTX was added, and recording continued until the holding current stabilized. A 473 nm blue laser (10 mW, Aurora‑220, Newdoon, Hangzhou, China) was used to activate ChR2‑expressing neurons. For voltage‑dependent potassium current recording, 5 mM QX‑314 (Cat# 5369‑03‑9, Sigma‑Aldrich) was added to the above C₆H₁₁KO₇-containing intracellular solution. Neurons were held at − 80 mV for 500 ms to inactivate all voltage‑dependent potassium channels, then stepped from − 80 mV to + 40 mV in 10 mV increments (1 s each). For voltage‑dependent sodium current recording, the intracellular solution contained (in mM): 145.0 CsCl, 2.0 TEA‑Cl, 0.2 EGTA, 10.0 HEPES, 2.0 MgCl₂, and 2.0 Mg‑ATP (pH 7.25, 280–300 mOsm), and the extracellular ACSF contained 3.0 mM 4‑AP and 0.1 mM CdCl₂. Sodium currents were elicited by depolarizing steps from − 80 mV to + 40 mV (10 mV steps, 40 ms duration) after a prepulse to − 100 mV. Activation curves were generated from peak currents and conductance values. All recordings were made with a Multiclamp 700B patch‑clamp amplifier (Molecular Devices, USA), and data acquisition and analysis were performed using pCLAMP 10.6 (Molecular Devices, USA) and MiniAnalysis software (Mini60, Synaptosoft, USA). In all electrophysiological experiments, n represents the number of cells, with typically 3–4 neurons taken per animal.

Fiber photometry

Following the administration of the AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6f, AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-GRAB_CCK1.0 and AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-iGABASnFR viral vectors, an optical fiber (200 μm, diameter, 0.37NA, 2.0 mm length, fixed on 1.25 mm outer diameter ceramic ferrules) (Inper, HangZhou, China) was implanted at a consistent location within the dorsal CA2 region. The optical fiber was secured to the skull with dental cement. After a three-week period to allow for virus expression, fluorescence signals were recorded during behavioral assessments. DualColorMultichannel_TP_OG_470_580 fiber-optic photometry system (ThinkerTech, Nanjing, China) was used to record the fluorescence signals at the dorsal CA2 site in the mouse brain. The power of the 488-nm laser was restricted to 20 mW at the fiber tip to reduce the risk of fluorescence bleaching. The change in fluorescence value (z-score) was calculated using the formula (F - F0)/Δ, where F0 represents the baseline fluorescence signal, and Δ represents the baseline standard deviation. For social events, the mean fluorescence value was calculated from the pre-event 5-second time window and the post-event 3-second time window, respectively; whereas for control events, it was determined from the free movement period during the mouse habituation phase. The experimental mice were placed in a square behavioral box (40 × 40 × 20 cm) equipped with a pen holder in one corner, allowing for 5 minutes of free movement and recording. Subsequently, an unfamiliar mouse was introduced into the pen holder, enabling the experimental mouse to move freely and explore for an additional 5 min. Each social interaction was required to last a minimum of 3 s. The initiation of a social event was defined as the moment the tip of the experimental mouse’s nose approached within a 5 cm radius centered on the pen holder, and the end of the social event was marked by the mouse’s tail exiting this designated range.

Cannula Implantation

NLG1 KO mice or WT mice were anesthetized with 1.25% tribromoethanol solution via intraperitoneal injection. Subsequently, they underwent stereotaxic implantation of bilateral guide cannulas (RWD, ShenZhen, China, 62003) aimed at the dorsal CA2 region, with the following coordinates: AP: − 2.3 mm, ML: ± 2.75 mm, DV: − 2.00 mm. A stainless-steel anchoring screw was affixed to the skull, and the cannulas were secured with dental cement. Cannula caps (RWD, ShenZhen, China, 62102) were employed to protect the implants post-surgery. The mice were allowed to recover on a heating pad until they were fully ambulatory. Behavioral testing was initiated after a recovery period of seven days.

Intracerebral drug microinjection

For drug delivery purposes, mice were initially anesthetized via induction with 4% isoflurane, and then the anesthesia was maintained using 1% isoflurane. AM251 (200 μM, Cat# S2819, Selleckchem), Nastorazepide (50 μM, Cat# S6939, Selleckchem), WIN-55 (200 μM, Cat# S8017, Selleckchem), Picrotoxin (500 μM, Cat# R284556, Sigma-Aldrich), CGP 52432 (100 μM, Cat# B6655, APExBIO) or vehicle was bilaterally infused at a volume of 200 nl per side, at a rate of 100 nl/min, through internal cannulas which were connected to a microsyringe pump. After the infusion was completed, the mice were permitted to recover in their home cages for 30 min before the behavioral assays were commenced.

Chemogenetic experiments

Following a minimum expression period of two weeks for either inhibitory (hM4Di) or excitatory (hM3Dq) designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs), as well as the control fluorophore, the subjects received a systemic administration of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 1 mg/kg, Cat# C0832, Sigma-Aldrich) via intraperitoneal injection. This pharmacological intervention was consistently applied to both experimental and control groups 30 min preceding behavioral assessment to ensure temporal standardization of chemogenetic activation across experimental conditions.

Immunohistochemistry

In this experimental procedure, mice were subjected to transcardial perfusion. Initially, 30 ml of phosphate - buffered saline (PBS) was perfused through the mice, which was then followed by 20 ml of paraformaldehyde (PFA, 4% wt/vol in PBS). Subsequently, the excised brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for a period of 6–8 hours at a temperature of 4 °C. After post-fixation, the brains were cryoprotected by immersion in 30% sucrose solution until the tissues sank, indicating osmotic equilibrium. Next, using a cryostat microtome (Leica, CM1950), coronal brain sections with a thickness of 30 μm were prepared. These brain sections were first permeabilized in a solution of PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (PBST) and then blocked with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in PBST at room temperature for 1 h. Following the permeabilization and blocking steps, the brain sections were incubated with a panel of primary antibodies for 72 h at 4 °C. The primary antibodies included rabbit anti-parvalbumin (1:500, Cat# A2791, Abclonal), rabbit anti-somatostatin (1:500, Cat# A9274, Abclonal), rabbit anti-γ-aminobutyric acid (1:200, Cat# A2052, Sigma-Aldrich), rabbit anti-cholecystokinin (1:200, Cat# ab27441, Abcam), rat anti-c-Fos (1:1000, Cat# 226017, Synaptic Systems), mouse anti-GAD67 (1:200, Cat# MAB5406, Sigma-Aldrich), mouse anti-KCNIP1 (1:200, Cat# 66439-1-Ig, Proteintech), rabbit anti-CB1 (1:200, Cat# ab259323, Abcam), mouse anti-CCK2R (1:200, Cat# sc53522, Santa). After the primary antibody incubation, the brain sections were washed three times for 5 min each in 0.1% PBST. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h at 37 °C. The secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rat IgG (1:500, Cat# A-11006, Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Cat# A-21422, Invitrogen), Cy3 - conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Cat# SA00009-2, Proteintech), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Cat# A-11008, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Cat# ab150113, Abcam). Thereafter, the brain sections were washed 5 times for 5 min each in PBS. Finally, the sections were counterstained with 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime, P0131) to visualize the cell nuclei and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope to capture high-resolution fluorescent images.

Western blot analysis

Brain regions were dissected based on viral-conferred fluorescence after acute sacrifice. Tissue was homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5% NP-40, 2.5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM Na₃VO₄, 20 mM NaF) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cat# A32961, Thermo). After centrifugation at 12,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Following secondary antibody incubation, blots were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Cat# 34577, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the TanonImage 1.0 system (Tanon, China) and then analyzed with AlphaEaseFC software. Protein levels were normalized to GAPDH.

RNAscope

The procedure for PFA fixation of brain tissue was the same as described above. After post-fixation, the brains were cryoprotected by gradient immersion in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions until the tissues sank, indicating osmotic equilibrium. Next, using a cryostat microtome (CM1950, Leica), coronal brain sections with a thickness of 30 μm were prepared. All sections were processed according to the instructions provided with the RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex Kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Cat# 323100). The following probes in this study from Advanced Cell Diagnostics were used: Mm-Nlgn1 (Cat# 25363 A).

Single nuclei suspension preparation

All procedures for preparing nucleus suspensions were conducted on ice. Frozen tissue samples were minced in 1 mL of nuclear lysis buffer (NST) containing 0.1% NP-40, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 146 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl₂, 21 mM MgCl₂, and 1 U/μL RNase inhibitor (Cat# EO0382, Thermo Scientific). Lysis was carried out for 7 min. Complete cell lysis was verified by trypan blue staining and microscopic examination. Subsequently, 1 mL of ST Wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 146 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl₂, 21 mM MgCl₂, 0.01% BSA (Cat# 1000076, MACS)), and 40 U/mL RNase inhibitor was added. The lysate was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Cat# 352340, Falcon), and the filtrate was collected into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. The strainer was rinsed with additional ST Wash buffer, and the rinse was pooled with the nuclear filtrate. The mixture was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C using a horizontal rotor. The resulting nuclear pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of PBS containing 1% BSA and centrifuged again. Finally, nuclei were resuspended in 100 μL of PBS with 1% BSA. Nuclear viability and concentration were determined by trypan blue staining and microscopic counting.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing library contruction

The nuclear suspension was diluted to a concentration of 700–1200 nuclei/μL using PBS with 1% BSA. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the 10 × Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kit v3.1 (Cat# PN-1000268). The constructed libraries were sequenced on the BGI DNBSEQ-T7 PE100 platform for high-throughput sequencing.

SnRNA-seq data processing

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing data from two sample types were processed and analyzed using the following bioinformatics pipeline. Raw sequencing data were aligned, demultiplexed, and quantified using the Cell Ranger software pipeline (v7.0.1, 10x Genomics) with the integrated STAR aligner against the GRCm39 reference transcriptome61.

Subsequent downstream analysis was performed in the R programming environment (v4.2.3) using the Seurat package (v4.0.0)62. Initial quality control and filtering were applied to remove low-quality cells and multiplets. We retained cells that met the following criteria: > 200 detected genes, > 1000 UMIs, a log10 (Genes per UMI) ratio > 0.7, a mitochondrial UMI ratio < 5%, and erythrocyte gene expression < 5%. Potential doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3)63.

Following quality control, the data from the two samples comprised 6725 to 7365 high-quality cells per sample, with median metrics ranging from 7314 to 8739 UMIs, 2564 to 2810 genes, and mitochondrial UMI ratios of 0.004 to 0.007. The filtered datasets were then normalized, scaled, and integrated (if applicable) using standard Seurat workflows. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction, followed by graph-based clustering and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization. This analysis identified six distinct cell populations, which were annotated based on established marker genes as neurons, microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte precursor cells.

A subpopulation of CCK-expressing inhibitory neurons was defined by the co-expression of canonical markers Gad1, Gad2, Cck, and Syt1. To identify statistically significant gene expression changes, differential expression analysis was conducted using the FindMarkers function in Seurat with the MAST test64, applying a threshold of a p-value < 0.05 and a |log2 fold change| > 0.75. The sequencing service and bioinformatics analysis were provided by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Statistics and reproducibility

All data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± s.e.m.), and statistically analyzed using two-tailed unpaired t test, two-tailed paired t test, two-tailed nest t test, ANOVA (nest one-way ANOVA, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA or repeated measures-RM, wherever appropriate) followed by Fisher’s LSD post hoc test. The p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001). Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Graph drawings were performed by GraphPad Prism 9 and Adobe Illustrator 2022. All schematic diagrams in the main figures and Supplementary Figs. were created entirely by the authors using Adobe Illustrator 2022. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results. Representative micrographs are from one of these replicates.
