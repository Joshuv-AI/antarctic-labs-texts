Association between 25-hydroxy vitamin D levels and risk of frailty and pre-frailty in elderly adults: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of epidemiologic studies with GRADE assessment
Authors: Maryam Khakbaz, Zahra Moradmand, Rahele Ziaei, Mohammadreza Moradi Baniasadi, Parvane Saneei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA growing number of studies investigated vitamin D and frailty have led to conflicting results. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to summarize and update the current evidence surrounding the association between vitamin D and risk of frailty and pre-frailty in elderly adults.MethodsMEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, Institute for Scientific Information (ISI) Web of Science databases, and Google Scholar were searched for all published articles up to January 2025. All observational studies that reported odds ratios (ORs) or relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between 25-hydroxy vitamin D (as independent variable) and frailty (as dependent variable) were included. Maximally adjusted effect sizes were pooled. Certainty of evidence was assessed by the GRADE approach.ResultsA total of 41 studies (6 cohorts, 31 cross-sectionals and 4 studies with information of baseline and longitudinal) were included. Highest (vs. lowest) level of vitamin D in prospective cohort studies (with 14,153 participants and 2,137 cases with frailty) was significantly related to a 29% reduction in risk of frailty (pooled RR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.60, 0.84). Also, a significant inverse association was found in cross-sectional studies with 42,936 cases out of 500,826 participants (OR: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.44, 0.56). Based on linear dose-response analysis, each 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L) increase in serum vitamin D level was inversely related to risk of frailty in cohort (RR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.77, 0.91) and cross-sectional (OR: 0.73; 95%CI: 0.66, 0.81) studies. Also, non-linear dose-response analysis on eligible studies was significant in both cross-sectional and cohort studies (Pnon-linearity < 0.001). Combining effect sizes of 15 studies (463,103 participants and 259,673 cases) indicated that the highest level of vitamin D compared to the lowest level was significantly related to a 40% reduction in risk of pre-frailty (OR/RR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.49, 0.73).ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis documented inverse associations between vitamin D and frailty, as well as prefrailty, and supported vitamin D as a potential cost-effective screening tool for identifying individuals at risk of frailty.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07415-0.

Introduction

Population aging as a worldwide phenomenon is considered one of the most serious challenges in healthcare [1, 2]. Today, the ongoing growth of elder populations is leading to a greater prevalence of various age-related chronic diseases and geriatric issues [3]. Among several geriatric syndromes, frailty is a clinical state that makes an individual more vulnerable to stressors and is associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes [4]. Approximately 10% of community-dwelling individuals aged ≥65 years is estimated to be frail, with prevalence ranging from 4.0 to 59.1% depending on the criteria used to define frailty [5]. Frailty represents a form of pre-disability that leads to fatigue, falling, fragility fractures, hospitalization, mortality, and other health costs [6, 7]. People with frailty have a decreased ability to recover from illnesses or injuries. This could significantly impact both life expectancy and quality of life. Thus, frailty is an important predictor and health indicator for older adults [4]. Two main instruments have been identified to diagnose frailty. The “frailty index” measures an individual’s number of impairments and conditions as cumulative deficits. According to “Fried criteria”, frailty is defined by investigating 5 possible components including weight loss, slowness, exhaustion, weakness, and reduced physical activity [8].

Development of frailty is complex and is a part of a multi-factorial dynamic process. Genes, environment, and lifestyle, all appear as pathways involved in frailty; nutritional status is an important factor in both its onset and treatment [6]. Among nutrients, vitamin D is generally associated with musculoskeletal health and is a key factor contributing to the mechanism of frailty [9]. Low serum concentrations of vitamin D might aggravate frailty via affecting mostly two ways, neuromuscular function and bone formation [10]. So, vitamin D can be expected to be involved in prevalence and treatment of frailty in aging societies.

Several epidemiological evidences have investigated the link between vitamin D status and frailty, but the results remain inconsistent. Some studies believed that the optimal levels reduce the risk of frailty [11–13]. However, some others did not confirm this association [14, 15]. Numerous previous meta-analyses have examined the association between vitamin D and frailty status [16–18]. The last meta-analysis published in 2020 included 38,162 participants and found significant differences in serum vitamin D between frail and pre-frail individuals compared to non-frail individuals. The mentioned meta-analysis reported standardized mean difference (SMD) for 25-hydroxy vitamin D for frail and pre-frail groups vs. non-frail subjects. Also, only studies with cross-sectional design that defined frailty by the Fried criteria were included in this analysis [17]. Furthermore, other mentioned meta-analyses published in 2016 and 2018 were limited to small number of studies (n = 7–8), and consequently a relatively small population [16, 18]. Additionally, several new studies have been published from the time of the last meta-analysis in 2020. So, there is still no clear conclusion about the association between vitamin D and frailty. Taking this background into account, the purpose of this study was to summarize the earlier investigations and to provide an update on the association between vitamin D and risk of frailty and pre-frailty in aged individuals, and to quantify the relation via a dose-response meta-analysis.

Methods and materials

The current review was performed by following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA), as shown in Supplementary Table 1. The study protocol was registered at http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/Prospero (registration no. CRD42024590874).

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search in MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, and Institute for Scientific Information (ISI) (Web of Science) databases, as well as Google Scholar, up to January 2025. Language, study location, and publishing year were not restricted. The search strategy consisted of MeSH and non-MeSH keywords relating to vitamin D (“Vitamin D” OR “cholecalciferol”) and frailty (frailty or “frail elderly”). All systematic search terms are provided in detail in Supplementary Table 2. Two investigators (MK and MMB) screened the published articles, and any disagreement was resolved by the principal investigator (PS). Furthermore, manual searches of the bibliographies of all finally included studies were performed to avoid missing any publications.

Inclusion criteria

Observational studies with either cohort or cross-sectional design and the following criteria were considered eligible to be included in the present meta-analysis: 1) conducted in elderly people (aged ≥60); 2) considered 25(OH)D levels as the exposure and frailty/pre-frailty as the outcome; 3) reported effect sizes [odds ratios (ORs), relative risks (RRs) or hazard ratios (HRs)] and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for frailty/pre-frailty.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded studies if they: 1) did not assess frailty (assessed only a single symptom of frailty); 2) did not report RR or OR; 3) were conducted on frail populations at baseline and considered mortality as the outcome of interest. When studies had the same population, only one study was included; there were similar publications from Women’s Health and Aging Studies (WHAS) I and II [19, 20], the KORA-Age study [21, 22], and the Third National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES III) [23, 24]. Among these pairs of studies, we included the reports with the largest sample sizes in the analysis [19, 22, 23]. More details of the investigations that were excluded are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Data extraction

Two investigators (MK and ZM) independently reviewed and extracted the following information: 1) the first author’s name; 2) year of publication; 3) country of investigation; 4) study design and name of study; 5) follow-up period; 6) health status of participants; 7) sample size and the number of participants with frailty/pre-frailty; 8) sex; 9) age (range or mean); 10) method of vitamin D assessment; 11) sample type of 25(OH)D (serum vs. plasma) 12) circulating 25(OH)D concentrations; 13) method of defining frailty; 14) relevant effect sizes for each category; 15) adjustments in the analyses. Data of circulating 25(OH)D concentration in nanomole per liter (nmol/L) were converted to nanogram per milliliter (ng/mL), multiplied by 2.5. A third investigator (RZ) reviewed the data extraction, and any disagreement was resolved through consensus.

Quality assessment

The overall quality of each eligible study was assessed using the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [25], by three independent reviewers (MK, ZM, and RZ). This tool consists of seven domains (confounding, participant selection, misclassification during follow-up, exposure assessment, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results). Any disagreements were discussed and resolved. Detailed of the quality assessment of cohort and cross-sectional studies are shown in Supplementary Tables 4 and 1. Additionally, the certainty of evidence was evaluated by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [26], as shown in Table 5.

Statistical analysis

We computed log RRs/ORs and their standard error (SEs) using the reported RRs or ORs and 95% CI for highest vs. lowest category of vitamin D. When the studies reported an OR for lowest vs. highest level of vitamin D, we reversed the reported OR and its lower and upper limits to calculate an OR for highest vs. lowest level. We used the approach described by Danesh et al. [27] to determine an OR for T3 vs. T1 of vitamin D based on the linear relationship reported between vitamin D and frailty in 10 studies. In short, for each study that reported OR of frailty for one unit increase in serum vitamin D levels, by considering normal distribution, we assumed a log OR and then multiplied log OR to the factors of 2.18 and standard deviation (SD) to have an OR for the third vs. first category of serum vitamin D. The related SE was calculated in the same way. To measure heterogeneity, the Q2 statistic and I2 tests have been used and were considered significant if the p value of the Q test was < 0.05. About I2 values of < 50, 50–75, and > 75% were respectively referred to as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity. The overall effect size was determined through a fixed-effect model when heterogeneity was low (I2 < 50%) and using a random-effect model when heterogeneity was moderate or high (I2 > 50%). In case of significant between-study heterogeneity, subgroup analysis based on categorical variables was performed to find possible sources of heterogeneity. When there was low heterogeneity, we also utilized subgroup analysis to understand the differences between subgroups. A fixed effect model has been used to evaluate heterogeneity between subgroups. Sensitivity analysis was used to examine whether the pooled estimate might depend on a specific study. Assessment of publication bias was performed by visual inspection of funnel plots, and Begg’s and Egger’s tests. We also used the method introduced by Greenland and Longnecker [28] and Orsini et al. [29] to calculate a dose-response analysis. In this method, we required the number of all participants and participants with frailty, and vitamin D levels for each category. Natural logs of the ORs, RRs, and 95% CIs across categories of serum vitamin D were used to compute study-specific slopes (linear trends) and 95% CIs for each 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L). The value of 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L) could represent the differences between thresholds for severe deficient ( < 10 ng/mL), deficient (10–20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–30 ng/mL), and sufficient levels o f vitamin D ( > 30 ng/mL). For nonlinear trends, data of at least three quantitative categories of exposure were required and restricted cubic splines (3 knots at fixed percentiles of 10, 50, and 90% of the distribution) were used to examine potential non-linear dose-response associations between serum vitamin D and risk of frailty. Average level of serum 25(OH)D in each category was allocated to the corresponding OR/RR for each study. In case of studies that reported serum 25(OH)D levels as ranges, the midpoint in each category was computed by taking the average of lower and upper bounds. When the category of the highest level of vitamin D was open-ended, we assumed the length of this interval was similar to the adjacent interval. When the lowest category was open-ended, the lower boundary for 25(OH)D was set to zero. Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA software version 14. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature search

Flow diagram of the study selection procedure is summarized in Fig. 1. A total of 2,902 papers from different databases were identified for screening. After removing duplicates and irrelevant papers, 305 full texts underwent full-text screening. Finally, 41 studies were eligible for inclusion in this meta-analysis. Finally, 6 studies reported data for prospective cohort studies, 31 investigations reported cross-sectional studies, and 4 studies were reported both baseline (as cross-sectional studies) and longitudinal data. Study characteristics

Details of 10 eligible cohort studies are summarized in Table 1. In brief, the included studies were published from October 2005 to February 2022 with 14,153 participants aged 65 years or older. A total of 2,137 of these participants were cases of frailty; except for one study that did not provide the number of cases [30]. Duration of follow-up in eligible studies ranged from 2 to 12.6 years. Out of 10 included studies, 6 studies were performed in European countries including Netherlands, Germany, Italy, and France [15, 30–34], 2 studies in American countries [35, 36], one study in Australia [37], and the last study in Asia [12]. One investigation was conducted only on women [35], two studies were conducted on men [36, 37], and seven others were performed on both genders. The reported risk estimates were primarily categorized by tertiles, quartiles, or classifications of deficient, insufficient, and sufficient levels of circulating 25(OH)D; while one study analyzed circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D as a continuous variable [32]. Among 10 included studies, the definition for 25-hydroxyvitamin D deficiency varied: one study set the threshold as < 30 ng/mL [31], one as < 26 ng/mL [37], four as < 20 ng/mL [15, 30, 35, 36], one as < 18 ng/mL [12], one as < 12 ng/mL [34], one as < 10 ng/mL [33], and the final study did not specify a cut-off level [32]. On the other hand, a sufficient level of 25-hydroxy vitamin D was defined as ≥ 30 ng/mL in five studies [15, 31, 35–37], > 22 ng/mL in one study [12], > 20 ng/mL in 3 others [30, 33, 34], and the last To evaluate circulating 25(OH)D concentrations different methods were used, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (n = 1) [12], radioimmunoassay (RIA) (n = 2) [32, 35], chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) (n = 2) [34, 37], electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) (n = 2) [15, 30], solid-phase extraction liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (SPE-HPLC-MS/MS) (n = 1) [31], liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (n = 1) [36], competitive protein binding assay (CPBA) (n = 1) [33]. Most studies used Fried criteria to assess frailty status of participants (n = 6) [15, 30–32, 35, 36]; however, frailty index (n = 2) [12, 34], FRAIL scale (n = 1) [37], and frailty indicator (n = 1) [33] were applied in the others. Most eligible studies considered adjustments for age (n = 9) [12, 15, 30–32, 34–37], sex (n = 6) [12, 15, 30–32, 34], body mass index (BMI) (n = 6) [12, 15, 32, 34–36], and physical activity (n = 4) [12, 33, 34, 37]. Also, season of blood draw was considered in two studies [30, 34], and vitamin D supplementation was taken into account in one of them [37]. In case of quality, according to the ROBINS-I tool, 60% of cohort studies had moderate risk of bias [12, 15, 32, 35–37], while 40% others had serious risk of bias [30, 31, 33, 34].

Main characteristics of 35 eligible cross-sectional studies are shown in Table 2. Eligible articles were published from October 2005 to June 2024 and involved a total of 500,826 participants, with 42,936 cases of frailty among individuals aged 60 and older. Five studies involved only on men, three focused on women, while others included participants of both genders. Findings from meta-analysis of highest vs. lowest concentration of vitamin D and risk of frailty in prospective cohort studies

As clarified in Fig. 2, combining 10 effect sizes from 10 prospective cohorts (included 14,153 participants and 2,137 cases with frailty) indicated that the highest level of vitamin D compared to the lowest level was significantly related to a 29% reduction in risk of frailty (pooled RR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.60, 0.84); low heterogeneity was observed between studies (I2 = 28.3%, PQ-Test = 0.18). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on variables [including participants’ age, frailty definition, study location, study quality, method of 25 (OH)D assessment, duration of follow-up, adjustment for sex, physical activity, season of blood draw, and BMI]. Results of these subgroup analyses are shown in Table 3. In most of these subgroups, inverse relations were observed. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the overall pooled effect was not substantially influenced by any single study. Findings from linear and non-linear dose-response meta-analysis of vitamin D concentration and frailty risk in prospective cohort studies

Based on linear dose-response analysis on effect sizes from 7 prospective cohorts with a total of 2,837 participants and 389 cases, we found that each 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L) increase in circulating vitamin D was associated with a 17% significant reduction in risk of frailty (pooled RR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.77, 0.91) (Fig. 3). Also, a non-linear dose-response analysis on 7 eligible studies with 2,837 participants revealed a significant relation (Pnon-linearity < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Findings from linear and non-linear dose-response meta-analysis of vitamin D concentration and frailty odds in cross-sectional studies

Linear dose-response analysis of 28 eligible studies with 50,163 participants and 6,188 cases indicated that each 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L) increase in serum level of vitamin D was associated with a 27% significant reduction in likelihood of having frailty (pooled OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.66, 0.81) (Fig. 6). Findings from meta-analysis of highest versus lowest concentration of vitamin D and pre-frailty

Pooling 15 effect sizes from 15 studies (with 259,673 pre-frailty cases and 463,103 participants) revealed that highest vs. lowest level of vitamin D was significantly related to a 40% reduction in risk of pre-frailty (pooled OR/RR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.49, 0.73) (Supplementary Figure 2).

Discussion

In this comprehensive systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis, 41 observational studies, including 10 prospective cohort studies and 35 cross-sectional studies, were included (4 studies provided information of both baseline and longitudinal). We observed that highest serum levels of 25(OH)D were associated with a 29% risk reduction in frailty, based on cohort studies, and 50% decreased odds of frailty based on cross-sectional studies, compared to the lowest levels. Also, it was found that each 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L) increment in the serum concentration of vitamin D was associated with a 17% and 27% reduction in risk of frailty in cohorts and cross-sectionals, respectively. Additionally, meta-analysis provided a reverse link between vitamin D and pre-frailty. In GRADE assessment, the certainty of the evidence was high for frailty in both cohort and cross-sectional studies.

Vitamin D serum concentration of approximately 25 ng/mL was related to the greatest decrease in risk of frailty in cohort investigations. Clinically, it may be considered as the specific cutoff level of serum 25(OH)D that decreases the risk of developing frailty. This threshold was yielded by aggregating studies from various regions, involving diverse populations. However, due to the insufficient or limited number of studies in different subgroups, dose-response analysis across different subpopulations was not possible for us. Therefore, this issue might be taken into account that this threshold might slightly vary among subpopulations. However, in accordance with our findings, a recent review published in 2025 reported that the range of 20–40 ng/mL for serum vitamin D might be associated with a reduced risk of fractures and falls [38]. Considering that observational studies preclude definitive causal inference, the observed associations and potential thresholds should be interpreted cautiously, as their generalizability remains uncertain. Further large-scale and well-designed studies and comprehensive analysis are required to confirm these findings.

The present study was updated evidence of the association between vitamin D status and frailty. Three systematic reviews and meta-analyses were previously done in this field with results consistent with ours [16–18]. Zhou et al. in 2016 examined seven cohort studies (with 17,815 participants) and suggested that the lowest level of vitamin D was directly associated with risk of frailty. However, the mentioned study lacked a dose-response approach, and the definition of the lowest level of 25(OH)D varied across the included studies. Also, subgroup analysis was only performed for sex and measurement method of frailty with limited number of studies which might affect the accuracy of their findings [16]. In a dose-response meta-analysis by Ju and colleagues in 2018 including 8 observational studies, a 25-nmol/L increase in 25(OH)D level was associated with a 11% and 12% decrease in the incidence of frailty in prospective cohort and cross-sectional studies, respectively [17]. The last meta-analysis performed by Marcos-Pérez et al. in 2020 on 13 cross-sectional studies reported that frail and pre-frail individuals had lower serum 25(OH)D levels than non-frail subjects [18]; however, in order to reduce the sources of between-study heterogeneity, their analysis was restricted to cross-sectional studies that considered only Fried criteria as the frailty diagnostic criteria. Therefore, the generalizability of its results might negatively have been affected. The present meta-analysis included a larger dataset (41 studies), in comparison to the previous meta-analyses, which included 7, 10, and 26 studies reported by Zhou et al. (2016), Ju et al. (2018), and Marcos-Pérez et al. (2020), respectively [16–18]. The larger dataset of the current analysis has integrated studies from the earlier meta-analysis, along with some other eligible studies, and newly available evidence and comprehensive analyses, which enhanced methodological rigor and improved population diversity.

A high level of heterogeneity was observed in the findings of cross-sectional studies (I2 = 81.8%), which might be attributed to diversity in vitamin D assessment, geographical areas, and applied frailty criteria. To assess vitamin D concentration, two basic types of assays were applied, automated immunoassays (such as RIA and CLIA) and chromatography-based assays (such as HPLC and LC-MS/MS), which could lead to variability in results and heterogeneity between studies [39, 40]. This difference and heterogeneity in results, due to different vitamin D assessment methods was also observed in our findings, where subgroup analysis showed the lowest heterogeneity in the assessment method of RIA (I2 = 14.4%). Furthermore, included studies in the analysis were conducted in various geographical regions and on diverse populations, but subgroup analyses and meta-regression based on latitude and longitude did not adequately explain the observed heterogeneity. In addition, frailty was assessed by various approaches. Most investigations applied the Fried criteria (Fried frailty phenotype), which evaluated five domains: unintentional weight loss, low physical activity, weakness, exhaustion, and slow gait speed. However, investigators applied different tools or cut-off thresholds for these domains depending on their population and facilities. Of the five domains, weakness and slow gait speed were generally assessed by objective measures, whereas the other domains were often evaluated through questionnaires. It is worth mentioning that the FRAIL scale and the frailty index were completely evaluated by questionnaires. The SOF and osteoporotic fracture indices assessed 3 domains. Nevertheless, the subgroup analysis based on these assessment methods in the present meta-analysis could not explain the observed heterogeneity, and results were somewhat similar in subgroups.

Vitamin D plays a key role in skeletal muscle structure, function, strength, and repair across a broad range of age groups [41, 42]. Vitamin D deficiency and insufficiency were associated with muscle atrophy and mitigated muscle strength in clinical studies, especially in elderly subjects [43, 44]. Therefore, serum 25-hydroxy-vitamin D optimization could delay progression from pre-frailty to frailty. Several in vitro studies have shown that the presence of vitamin D receptors (VDRs) in both human and animals skeletal muscles is decreased with age [45, 46]. Relating the molecular mechanisms of vitamin D in muscle cells, improving myoblast growth and differentiation, angiogenesis, mitochondrial activity and vitamin D signaling components such as CYP24A1 and CYP27B1 were highlighted in these studies [45, 46]. Also, 1,25(OH)2D3 may promote protein synthesis in muscle cells by enhancing the effects of insulin and leucine [45]. It was suggested that vitamin D supplementation in deficient animal models may improve mitochondrial density and function as well as the balance between muscle protein synthesis and breakdown [47]. Vitamin D receptors and CYP27B1 expression are significantly up-regulated following initial muscle damage in pre-frailty, indicating the important role of vitamin D in muscle regeneration and repair, as well as shifting from pre-frailty to frailty [48]. It was suggested that vitamin D may decrease the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and improve mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation capacity in injured muscle [49]. Mounting evidence exists on the critical role of dysregulated immune responses and age-related chronic inflammation in the pathogenesis of frailty [50]. Increased concentrations of inflammatory cytokines such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) have been associated with pre-frailty and frailty status in older adults [50–52]. The effects of pro-inflammatory cytokines on frailty include their direct promoting effect on protein breakdown and their indirect influence on important metabolic pathways [50]. Vitamin D plays a critical role in immune homeostasis and modulation of inflammatory responses [53]. Although, vitamin D deficiency has been associated with an increased risk of frailty and sarcopenia in aged people, current evidence, along with previous evidence, does not support the effect of routine vitamin D supplementation for prevention of frailty in these groups [54, 55].

The present study has several strengths. The association between serum 25(OH)D concentration and risk of frailty has been previously proposed; however, this meta-analysis provides an updated and thorough assessment of all observational studies conducted in this field. We also performed a comprehensive assessment of study quality and biases. Moreover, according to the GRADE assessment, there was high certainty evidence supporting associations between circulating 25(OH)D levels and frailty in both cross-sectional and cohort studies. Some potential limitations of this study should also be noted. First, the included studies employed different criteria to define frailty including Fried criteria, frailty index, FRAIL scale, modified Fried criteria, SOF index and nine frailty indicators; therefore, we performed subgroup analysis based on the frailty definition to attenuate the confounding effect. Second, although heterogeneity among prospective cohort studies was low, we observed a high heterogeneity among cross-sectional studies. Despite performing comprehensive subgroup analyses, the effect of residual confounders on the pooled estimate in cross-sectional investigations should be considered. Third, different confounding variables were adjusted among the included studies. The majority of eligible studies considered some main confounders, such as age, sex, BMI, and sampling season for vitamin D; however, only a minority of them accounted for physical activity, and missing comorbidities, dietary intake, renal function, and socioeconomic status. Because physical activity level was considered as one of the indicators assessed in frailty evaluation, and therefore was not treated as a confounder. Moreover, ignoring other confounders, such as chronic conditions or depression, could result in an overestimate or underestimate of the association; imbalanced nutrient intake and renal dysfunction might impair nutrient balance, musculoskeletal health, and metabolism. Neglecting socioeconomic status could additionally affect the interpretation of population differences. Overall, excluding these factors from study design or analysis can create gaps that distort causal interpretation of the relationships between predictors and frailty. These differences in adjustments could lead to heterogeneity across studies and bias in observed associations. Fourth, some of the eligible studies provided no data regarding the season of blood sampling and vitamin D supplementation. Fifth, most of eligible cohort studies measured vitamin D levels only at baseline, while it might change in the years of follow-up. Also, different methods were applied to assess vitamin D status among included cross-sectional studies, and this diversity in measurement approaches could be involved in the observed between-study heterogeneity. Sixth, the majority of included studies were conducted in North American and European countries, having different UV radiation patterns and latitudes, compared to other countries. Seventh, vitamin D levels for severe deficiency, deficiency, insufficiency, and sufficiency might vary across subpopulations due to factors like age, ethnicity, co-morbidities, sunlight exposure, and clinical context. However, the thresholds defining these categories and the differences between them were considered almost the same in most previous studies, usually around 10 ng/mL (or 25 nmol/L). Finally, pooled estimate of cross-sectional studies was much stronger than pooled effect size of cohort studies; this exaggerated association in cross-sectional studies could be originated from potential biases.

Our comprehensive meta-analysis demonstrates the significant association between high serum levels of vitamin D and decreased risk of frailty and pre-frailty among elderly population in a dose-response manner. It was also observed that serum vitamin D concentration of approximately 25 ng/mL was related to the greatest decrease in risk of frailty. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed associations may not be applied universally. Further observational studies in countries other than North America and Europe, considering vitamin D intake, including vitamin D supplements, and repeated measurement of vitamin D concentration in prospective studies, are warranted to better explore the association. Also, future investigations should be conducted on vitamin D supplementation in older adults, which requires specific doses, longer follow-up, and considering interactions with other treatments, to optimize vitamin D levels. Future epidemiologic investigations should also focus on different ages, health conditions, as well as considering key confounders such as the season of blood drawn, to provide robust and reliable evidence.
