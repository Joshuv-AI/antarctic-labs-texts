Chimera games emerging from coevolutionary dynamics with endogenous feedbacks
Federico Maria Quetti, Andrea Civilini, Giacomo Frigerio, Silvia Figini, Giacomo Livan, Vito Latora

Abstract
From climate change to financial bubbles and wars, the rapid pace of change in our world clearly shows that the commonly adopted assumption of a static environment in evolutionary game-theoretic models is often unrealistic. In this work, we introduce a coevolutionary game model where the game environment and the strategy dynamics are mutually coupled. In particular, the game played at each time is endogenously defined as a convex combination of two social dilemmas, weighted by the current abundance of the cooperative individuals in the population. Depending on the nature of the underlying social dilemmas, emergent scenarios - denoted as Chimera games - can arise. In such games, the stable cooperation level reached at equilibrium would be unstable under standard evolutionary principles, despite identical game settings. Conversely, under different feedback specifications the opposite behavior can also occur, and the stable evolutionary equilibrium be disrupted. Our results open fundamental theoretical questions regarding the possibility of predicting and driving the dynamics of complex social systems through external policymaking interventions designed solely on local observations of the system’s current state.



Introduction.

Evolutionary game theory (EGT) is the paradigmatic framework that combines game theory with natural selection principles. Its rich mathematical framework has been successfully applied over the years to study behavioral dynamics across a wide range of contexts, from biological to socio-political environments
Axelrod and Hamilton 1981
;
Maynard Smith 1976
;
Taylor and Jonker 1978
;
Nowak 2006a
.
EGT models how behavioral traits propagate in a population of agents driven by self interest
Smith and Price 1973
;
Smith 1982
;
Nowak and May 1992
;
Weibull 2004
.
Each agent in the population is associated with a strategy and earns a payoff depending on the strategies of other players and the specific game setting
Von Neumann and Morgenstern 2007
;
Myerson 2013
.
Strategies propagate through the population based on their fitness, with more successful strategies - i.e., those that yield a higher average payoff - being more likely to spread.
In biological systems, this mimics the Darwinian principle of survival of the fittest, whereas in social contexts draws from the phenomenon of imitation of the most successful agent
Livan 2019
;
De Domenico et al. 2024
.
A game is therefore completely defined in terms of its payoff structure, which associates the payoff to the strategy profile of the players.
Games of particular relevance, especially in modeling social environments, are social dilemmas, which capture the tension between individual benefit and collective interest. In a social dilemma, agents choose between two strategies: cooperation, which benefits the group at a personal cost, and defection, which involves no contribution to the common good and yields a higher payoff, provided there are cooperators in the population to exploit
Axelrod and Dion 1988
;
Doebeli et al. 2004
;
Nowak 2006b
;
Perc et al. 2017
. Paradigmatic examples are the
Prisoner’s Dilemma
and the
Snowdrift Game

Axelrod and Hamilton 1981
;
Maynard Smith 1976
.

The game’s payoff structure defines the specific strategic interactions and, more broadly, the environment in which these interactions take place.
In EGT the time scales for the evolution of the game environment and the strategic decision process are usually very different:
the environment is considered fixed, and only the evolution of strategies within this static setting is considered.
However, during their evolution, real world systems are typically subject to different environmental conditions which influence the strategic decisions of interacting individuals
Levin et al. 2013
.
Building on this observation,
recent research has begun to extend the evolutionary paradigm to account for mutable environments, by modeling game settings that change in time with payoffs no longer considered fixed
Akçay 2020
. For instance, the framework of stochastic games has been applied to model fluctuations in the strategy adoption through the payoffs
Shapley 1953
;
Wang et al. 2014
;
Hilbe et al. 2018
;
Kleshnina et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
.
Yet, these models do not underscore the direct and often deterministic effects of the population behavior on the environment itself
Peck and Feldman 1986
;
Bowles et al. 2003
;
Tilman et al. 2018
;
Estrela et al. 2019
;
Cortez et al. 2020
.
Following this line of reasoning, several co-evolution models couple an external environmental dynamics to the abundance of strategies in the population
Weitz et al. 2016
;
Tilman et al. 2020
;
Ito and Yamamichi 2024
.
On the one hand a resource-driven environment favors a specific strategy, while on the other hand the resource and thus the environment themselves are influenced by the strategic composition of the population, in a feedback loop.
Such models are suitable for describing the dynamics of ecological or biological systems driven by reciprocal influence between evolving species and changing environment, e.g. a rich or depleted resource depending on its self-renewal dynamics as well as on the cooperation that agents put up in order to preserve it.
However, in many real world scenarios, the evolution of the environment does not depend on external drivers, but is instead a direct consequence of the behavior of the individuals in the population. That is, the evolution of the game is not driven by exogenous variables, but may endogenously arise from strategy-payoff feedback. Such situations can be representative of two opposite scenarios occurring for instance in social and financial settings: growing pro-social behavior or freeriding promoted by increasing cooperation.
For example, online communities benefit from becoming more welcoming, as every time users cooperate by positively interacting with each other, they slightly impact the next user’s behavior thus encouraging more cooperation
González-Bailón et al. 2011
;
Kang et al. 2015
.
Conversely, those who buy in a Ponzi scheme or in a speculative bubble amplify the illusion of profitability for other prospective buyers, thereby increasing the incentive to defect, i.e., to cash out, for those selling overvalued assets
Kaizoji 2000
. In both examples, strategic interactions themselves reshape their own payoff structures.

In the present work, we propose a novel co-evolutionary game theoretic framework for pairwise symmetric games. In particular, we put forward a parsimonious framework for games (i.e., environments) whose evolution is endogenously driven by the strategic abundance of the population in time,
without assuming a specific external variable describing the game (i.e. environmental) dynamics.
In the context of social dilemmas, the effects of our co-evolutionary model on the cooperation levels are compared with standard (i.e., in a static setting) evolutionary game theory (EGT) results. Results show that even linear feedbacks produce complex transitions from one social dilemma class to another. In particular, we demonstrate the emergence of game settings endowed with apparently irrational dynamical equilibria, which we refer to as
Chimera games
. Such scenarios reveal how cooperation can be sustained (or suppressed) by the very incentives it collectively generates.

Model.

We consider pairwise symmetric games where each agent can choose between two pure strategies.
We focus on the so-called social dilemmas games, where the two available strategies are cooperation (C) and defection (D).
A social dilemma game is completely defined by its normal representation, detailed by the following payoff matrix:

\bordermatrix{&C&D\cr C\ \ &R&S\cr D\ \ &T&P\cr}

(1)

where
R
is the
reward
for mutual cooperation,
S
is the
sucker’s payoff
for cooperating against a defector,
T
is the
temptation to defect
against a cooperator,
P
is the
penalty
for mutual defection.
Being the game symmetric we report only the payoff for the row player.

We adopt a convenient reparameterization of the above matrix, defined by two parameters
Tanimoto and Sagara 2007
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Ito and Tanimoto 2018
:

G(D_{g},D_{r})=\begin{pmatrix}1&-D_{r}\\
1+D_{g}&0\end{pmatrix}

(2)

where
D_{g}:=T-R,\ D_{r}:=P-S
, identified as
gambling intending

and
risk adverting

dilemma strengths
, respectively.
In particular, higher values of
D_{g}
promote defection by enhancing the temptation to defect
T
; higher values of
D_{r}
promote defection penalizing betrayed cooperators by lowering
S
.
Different combinations of the parameters
D_{g},\ D_{r}\in[-1,1]
encompass different games, represented in the space of dilemma strengths as a square where each quadrant defines a different social dilemma
Peña et al. 2016
;
Sadekar et al. 2024
. Classical EGT results determine the equilibrium states in the well mixed scenario and unstructured populations, for each social dilemma: calling
\rho^{*}_{B}
the fraction of cooperators at equilibrium for a baseline game
G_{B}
, we have the following picture:

•

Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD):

D_{g}>0,\ D_{r}>0
, full defection
\rightarrow\rho^{*}_{B}=0

•

Snowdrift Game (SD):

D_{g}>0,\ D_{r}<0
, mixed equilibrium
\rightarrow\rho^{*}_{B}\in(0,1)

•

Stag Hunt (SH):

D_{g}<0,\ D_{r}>0
, bistability
\rightarrow\rho^{*}_{B}=0\ \mathrm{or}\ 1

•

Harmony Game (HG):

D_{g}<0,\ D_{r}<0
, full cooperation
\rightarrow\rho^{*}_{B}=1

We follow a co-evolutionary approach in which games are considered evolving: in our framework, both the strategies of the population and the game representing the environment change over time with reciprocal influence. Differently from previous co-evolutionary models, we simplify the assumption of the environment being driven by an external dynamical variable, instead describing the evolution of the game (representing the environmental setting) by introducing a direct dependence between the payoffs for different strategies and the level of cooperation
\rho
. Consequently, the population itself shapes the environment entirely through a simple co-evolution, in a way interpretable as a closed system with self-feedback.

The general game dynamics of the model involves fixed baseline and target games, and the level of cooperation
\rho=\rho(t)
which changes in time through the evolution of strategies in the population.
The full strategy-game co-dependence reads (omitting the implicit time dependence):

\begin{split}&G(\rho,D_{g},D_{r},D^{T}_{g},D^{T}_{r})=[1-f(\rho)]G_{B}+f(\rho)G_{T}\\
&=[1-f(\rho)]\begin{pmatrix}1&-D_{r}\\
1+D_{g}&0\end{pmatrix}+f(\rho)\begin{pmatrix}1&-D^{T}_{r}\\
1+D^{T}_{g}&0\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3)

where
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
can be a general mapping of the unit interval in itself, describing the strategy-payoff feedback. Natural choices of the functional form should be continuous, monotonic (for consistent feedback for increasing
\rho
), and account for synergistic effects
Hauert et al. 2006
: the simplest specification is
f(\rho)=\rho^{\lambda},\ \lambda\in(0,+\infty)
. Henceforth, the identical mapping
f(\rho)=\rho
, is considered, while the role of
\lambda
is discussed in the Supplemental Material.
The
feedback-driven
game structure is thus defined as a convex combination mediated by
f(\rho)
between the baseline game
G_{B}=G(D_{g},D_{r})
and a target game
G_{T}=G(D^{T}_{g},D^{T}_{r}))
, defined by the baseline and target dilemma strengths respectively. As a consequence, the possible games lie between two extremes: the
G_{B}
game, corresponding to
\rho=0
, and the
G_{T}
game, corresponding to
\rho=1
.
The interpretation of the strategy dynamics in our co-evolutionary model is made clear by considering two extreme choices of parameters
D^{T}_{g}
and
D^{T}_{r}
defining the target game: if
D^{T}_{r}=D^{T}_{g}=1
, the feedback effectively acts as a driver for defection, pushing the game parameters to the most defective case of PD for higher levels of cooperation; conversely, if
D^{T}_{r}=D^{T}_{g}=-1
, the feedback is a cooperation incentive
Akçay and Roughgarden 2011
that drives the game to the most cooperative case of HG.

The interaction between agents in the model is well mixed, and the thermodynamic limit (i.e. infinite sized population) is assumed.
In this setting, the evolutionary dynamics of the level of cooperation
\rho
of the system is given by the mean field replicator equation
Hofbauer and Sigmund 2003
;
Hofbauer and Sigmund 1998
;
Taylor and Jonker 1978
,

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\rho(1-\rho)\Delta\Pi(\rho)\ ,

(4)

where
\Delta\Pi(\rho)=\Pi_{C}(\rho)-\Pi_{D}(\rho)
is the expected payoff difference between cooperators and defectors. Note that in EGT scenarios, where the fitness of agents depends on the abundance of strategies, the payoff term of the replicator equation does as well
Hofbauer and Sigmund 1998
;
Doebeli et al. 2004
. If nontrivial evolutionary stable states (i.e. rest points of eq.
4
) exist, it is due to the condition
\Delta\Pi(\rho)=0
holding for specific values of
\rho
.
The explicit expression for our model can be computed directly from eq.
3
, and due to the mapping
f(\rho)=\rho
we have:

\Delta\Pi=\rho^{2}((D^{T}_{r}-D_{r})+(D_{g}-D^{T}_{g}))+\rho(2D_{r}-D^{T}_{r}-D_{g})-D_{r}\ .

(5)

Upon specifying the four game parameters, the behavior of
\rho
in time and therefore the existence of equilibria for the system is completely described by plugging eq.
5
into eq.
4
.
For the two different target scenarios at fixed
D^{T}_{g},D^{T}_{r}
, stationary solutions
\rho^{*}
of equation eq.
4
are to be studied in the four different social dilemma classes, in the space of
D_{g},D_{r}
.
The stability of the solutions of eq.
4
is analyzed by studying its jacobian.

Conclusions.

Understanding the emergence of evolutionary stable states of cooperation, when populations evolve by interacting through a social dilemma, is a fundamental problem in evolutionary game theory
Axelrod and Hamilton 1981
;
Nowak 2006b
;
Hofbauer and Sigmund 1998
;
Nowak 2006a
. In real-world systems, the evolution of the environment is subject to influence from the cooperation level of the population, and several models have emerged in this direction
Weitz et al. 2016
;
Tilman et al. 2020
;
Betz et al. 2024
;
Perc and Szolnoki 2010
;
Gross and Blasius 2008
.
Our work contributes to this growing field by detailing a minimal framework for systems endowed with an endogenous feedback between population strategies and the payoff structure of the game, without assuming any external dynamics
Tilman et al. 2020
;
Weitz et al. 2016
;
Ito and Yamamichi 2024
or stochasticity
Delton et al. 2011
;
Hilbe et al. 2018
;
Sadekar et al. 2024
;
Civilini et al. 2024
.
Results show that even deterministic endogenous feedbacks of simple specification can profoundly alter the evolutionary outcome, resulting in levels of cooperation driven by the feedback-induced change in game. In particular, a cooperative feedback may enable the population to transition from its initial cooperation level to full cooperation and a Harmony Game setting, from any baseline social dilemma class. Due to the opposite feedback favoring defection, EGT mixed equilibria are only slightly disrupted.
Most notably, a stability switching phenomenon is found when cooperation/defection is promoted in the SD/SH region, respectively. We referred to such scenarios as Chimera games.

These results, with no classical game theory analogue, have concrete implications for real-world systems. Policymakers must consider that dynamic responses to incentives may not just shift the behavior of agents, but also disrupt the stability of cooperation by reshaping the very structure of strategic interactions, leading to hardly foreseeable outcomes through static payoff-based models. Incentive designs as dynamic feedback must account for these findings, recognizing that in social systems the environment is not a fixed backdrop but a malleable and co-evolving entity
Weitz et al. 2016
;
Tilman et al. 2020
;
Ito and Yamamichi 2024
;
Sadekar et al. 2024
;
Gross and Blasius 2008
;
Civilini et al. 2024
;
Civilini et al. 2021
;
Bowles et al. 2003
;
Sadekar et al. 2025
.

SUPPLEMENTAL MATERIAL

I
Simulations

Here we detail the Monte Carlo simulation protocol performed to validate the analytical results.
The standard evolutionary framework adopted for simulations relies on the well mixed assumption and draws from the replicator equation
Szabó and Tőke 1998
;
Traulsen et al. 2006
.
Consider a finite but sufficiently large population of agents, sharing a baseline game scenario determined by the payoff matrix
G_{B}
. The population is initialized to a cooperation level
\rho_{0}
, and each agent equipped with an initial strategy at random. This leads in our model to an initial feedback-driven game
\rho_{0}G_{B}+(1-\rho_{0})G_{T}
.
Upon specifying the initial game dilemma strengths and the target dilemma strengths, the payoff matrix is completely defined. Note that populations endowed with different
G_{B},\rho_{0}
but same
\rho_{0}G_{B}+(1-\rho_{0})G_{T}
, despite starting from the same game, can exhibit drastically different dynamics.

The system is evolved by agents updating their strategies from their interactions through an asynchronous process. In each round, a focal player
f
and a model player
m
are selected randomly. Each plays its strategy against
k
randomly chosen players of the population collecting a total payoff
\Pi_{f}
,
\Pi_{m}
respectively. A stochastic update process depending on the payoff difference drives the strategy update of the focal player: with probability given by

p=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\omega(\Pi_{m}-\Pi_{f}))}

(S1)

where
\omega
is the selection strength (chosen as
\omega=0.1
), the focal player adopts the strategy of the model player. After each round the cooperation level is updated and thus the following round payoff matrix is defined via eq.
3
.
The simulation steps are repeated until the system stabilizes and reaches equilibrium, either by collapsing into an absorbing state of full defection/cooperation, or by reaching a stable nontrivial level of cooperation.
The code used for simulation is available at
https://github.com/GiacomoFrigerio/CoevChimeraGames
.

III
Chimera game characterization

In the main text we discussed the existence of peculiar scenarios in which the dynamics brings the system towards an equilibrium for
\rho^{*}
, and as such for the game played
G_{eq}
, but
\rho^{*}\neq\rho_{eq}

G_{T}
is fixed to
Prisoner’s Dilemma

IV
Role of
\lambda
in the Feedback Function

In the co-evolutionary games introduced in the main text, the level of cooperation
\rho
defines the game played through the convex combination of a baseline game
G_{B}
and a target game
G_{T}
, following:

G(\rho,D_{g},D_{r},D^{T}_{g},D^{T}_{r})=[1-f(\rho)]G_{B}+f(\rho)G_{T}\ .

(S8)

The analysis highlighted emergent scenarios in the linear case, however the model can be extended to consider more general game-strategy dependencies.
Accordingly, the mapping
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
, governs the intensity of the feedback coupling in eq.
S8
.
While in principle the mapping can be general, we aim for the model to plausibly reflect real-world scenarios; therefore, we restrict ourselves to continuous mappings. Furthermore, to account for either synergistic or saturation effects in a consistent manner across different levels of cooperation, it is reasonable to require the mapping to be monotonic.

A simple albeit natural specification of
f
is the monomial form
f(\rho)=\rho^{\lambda}
, where the parameter
\lambda>0
tunes the concavity of the mapping
f
. For
\lambda<1
, the feedback is faster, depicting synergistic effects of cooperation; on the contrary,
\lambda>1
describes a slower feedback due to saturation effects.

Here, we discuss results obtained with the introduction of
\lambda
in the mapping.
The payoff difference
\Delta\Pi(\rho)
appearing in the replicator equation
4
is given,
substituting
f(\rho)=\rho^{\lambda}
, by

\Delta\Pi(\rho)=\rho^{\lambda+1}(D_{r}^{T}-D_{r}+D_{g}-D_{g}^{T})+\rho^{\lambda}(D_{r}-D_{r}^{T})+\rho(D_{r}-D_{g})-D_{r}.

(S9)

The introduction of
\lambda
reshapes the functional form of
\Delta\Pi(\rho)
, thus modifying the fixed points (solutions to
\Delta\Pi(\rho)=0
) and their stability properties.
The behavior of the solutions in the two paradigmatic cases is now analyzed, choosing
\lambda=2
and
\lambda=0.5
for simplicity.

Slow feedback:
\lambda=2

When the feedback function takes the form
f(\rho)=\rho^{2}
, the dynamics qualitatively resembles that of the linear case
\lambda=1
in most game parameter regions. However, a new and unexpected feature emerges when the baseline game lies in the
Stag Hunt
(SH) region and the target game is a
Prisoner’s Dilemma
(PD). In the linear case, this setup yields only full defection as a stable outcome, effectively mimicking the behavior of a PD: cooperation is not sustainable, and no internal equilibrium exists.

In contrast, with
\lambda=2
, the nonlinear synergy introduced by the feedback mechanism enables the appearance of two internal fixed points
\rho_{1}^{*}
and
\rho_{2}^{*}
, with
\rho_{1}^{*}<\rho_{2}^{*}
, within the interval
(0,1)
. These two equilibria correspond to stationary games
G_{eq}
that both remain in the SH region of the dilemma space. Nonetheless, their strategic dynamics diverge from the standard bistability of the SH game in classical EGT, where only full cooperation or full defection are absorbing states. Here, the population may stabilize at intermediate levels of cooperation, unattainable in the absence of feedback, and not explainable by the equilibrium structure of the effective game alone.

This bistable regime is not limited to the SH baseline–PD target case. A similar phenomenon appears when the baseline game lies in the
Snowdrift Game
(SD) region and the target is a Harmony Game (HG). While the linear case
\lambda=1
would lead to a full-cooperation outcome, reflecting the influence of the highly cooperative HG target, the introduction of synergy with
\lambda=2
allows the system to support two interior equilibria. Both correspond to effective games lying in the SD region, yet they support different cooperation levels, again with one stable and one unstable. Thus, slow nonlinear feedbacks may not allow convergence to cooperation even when the target is the most favorable.

In both cases, the feedback not only shifts the outcome but enables an entire dynamical structure absent in the linear setting. These equilibria illustrate a form of dynamical ambiguity where the class of the game at equilibrium does not predict the population’s actual behavior—a hallmark of what we refer to as Chimera games.

Discussion

These observations emphasize that the parameter
\lambda
in the feedback function
f(\rho)=\rho^{\lambda}
plays a pivotal role in shaping the landscape of the game settings dynamically. While
\lambda=1
provides a baseline model for linear coupling between cooperation and environmental response, increasing
\lambda
beyond unity introduces nonlinear effects capable of generating qualitatively new behaviors by dampening the feedback, including multiple internal equilibria and bistable cooperation levels. Conversely, for
\lambda<1
, the system becomes more sensitive to changes in cooperation, leading to strengthened feedback effects and reduced cooperation overall, without altering the fundamental structure of equilibria.

The emergence of bistability in unexpected regions of the dilemma space, especially under synergistic feedback, signals a broader implication for models of coevolutionary dynamics: the interplay between strategy and environment can lead to equilibrium outcomes that are not only quantitatively different but also qualitatively inconsistent with what would be predicted based on EGT or linearly coupled models.
