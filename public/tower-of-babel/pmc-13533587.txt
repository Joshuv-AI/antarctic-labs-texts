Finite element analysis in foot and ankle arthrodesis
Authors: Jitendra Mangwani, Hengshuo Zhang, Simin Li
Published: 2026

­

Arthrodesis, or joint fusion, is a surgical option for patients with end-stage arthritis. It aims to alleviate pain while correcting deformities to restore alignment and the weightbearing function. Arthrodesis is commonly performed in smaller joints, such as the foot and ankle region, especially when arthroplasty options are limited. Common sites include tibiotalar (ankle), subtalar, tibiotalocalcaneal (TTCA), first tarsometatarsal (Lapidus), and first metatarsophalangeal and various mid- and hindfoot arthrodesis.1-6 Previous reviews have reported nonunion rates of 8% to 40% following foot and ankle arthrodesis.7,8 In a retrospective cohort study including 19,120 patients who underwent tibiotalar arthrodesis, the overall postoperative complication rate was 24.9%, and amputation occurred in 0.3% of patients.9 Conventional imaging can depict bony morphology and the apparent status of fusion with reasonable clarity, but it does not allow direct quantification of key mechanical parameters such as intraosseous stress and strain, particularly under different fixation constructs and complex loading conditions. This may partly explain why a satisfactory radiological fusion is sometimes accompanied by suboptimal functional recovery and a persistent burden of complications.10 Cadaveric experiments are constrained by specimen availability, the need for repeated use, and the requirements of experimental apparatus, making it difficult to obtain comprehensive, multidimensional mechanical data from the same specimen, especially in complex cases involving multijoint fusion. 11 Finite element analysis (FEA), by contrast, enables the construction of 3D, patient-specific models from imaging data and the reconstruction of internal stress and strain fields under well-controlled loading and boundary conditions, providing an important computational platform for understanding and optimizing the internal biomechanical environment of the foot and ankle, particularly in different arthrodesis configurations.

Finite element modelling of foot and ankle arthrodesis is generally built on standard foot-ankle workflows, with targeted refinement of three key components: bone, implant, and fusion interface.1,4,12 In most cases, 3D bony geometry is reconstructed from CT or MRI.13 Cartilage is removed according to the planned surgical technique using either planar cuts or contour-preserving preparation, and the bones are translated to bring the intended fusion surfaces into apposition.12,14 Screws, plates, or intramedullary nails are then created with their native geometry and dimensions and assigned appropriate metallic properties, most commonly Ti-6Al-4V titanium alloy, 316 L stainless steel, and nickel-titanium (NiTi) alloys.4,15,16 Locking screw-plate or screw-nail constructs are typically represented using tie constraints, with low-friction or frictional contact used to model the bone-implant interface.3 In some models, initial compression across the fusion site has been simulated by applying bolt preloads or uniaxial thermal contraction to represent compressive screws and nails.17 Mesh density is sometimes increased at the fusion interface, along screw trajectories, and in the distal tibial and calcaneal cortices to better capture micromotion and local stress concentrations, with mesh convergence analyses used to balance accuracy against computational cost.18 Ligaments are most often represented by one-dimensional truss or spring elements,19,20 although more detailed ankle models have reconstructed key ligaments as 3D solid structures with subject-specific geometry and explicit bone-ligament interfaces to permit analysis of ligament-level stress and deformation. 21,22

Loading conditions range from simplified axial bodyweight compression, through combined axial-shear-torsional loading, to multidirectional, gait-derived loading to assess fusion stability and bone-implant load sharing during standing and key gait phases.3,14,18,23 Although a broadly standardized framework for arthrodesis FE modelling has emerged, substantial heterogeneity persists in joint preparation, representation of bone quality, bone-implant contact definitions, and loading scenarios. In this context, it is essential to state explicitly how modelling assumptions relate to the clinical question of interest, and to justify which simplifications are acceptable for the intended application.13

Validation directly targeting the fusion scenario itself remains limited, and current strategies largely follow frameworks developed for native foot-ankle models. Typically, model outputs such as plantar pressure, joint contact pressure, or global stiffness are first compared with in vitro or cadaveric data in the unfused condition, after which arthrodesis is imposed on the same model and the results analyzed.13 This two-step approach is methodologically reasonable, but it only establishes that the unfused foot-ankle response is credible, and does not directly test whether predictions of clinically relevant metrics under specific fusion constructs are reliable. A small number of studies have attempted fusion-specific validation: in a Charcot medial column arthrodesis model, Simonik et al4 compared FE-predicted medial column stiffness with three-point bending tests on the same specimens (ρ = 0.83), and regions of high implant stress coincided with experimentally observed failure sites. Zhang et al3 compared a TTCA model, both pre- and postoperatively, against previously published cadaveric and FE data to support model credibility. Overall, validation of foot and ankle arthrodesis FEA should be guided by a core principle: output measures, the extent of validation, and the intended clinical application must be logically aligned if model results are to be regarded as both technically robust and clinically meaningful.

Many studies remain essentially time-zero simulations, in which the fusion interface is assigned a single set of material or contact properties, and temporal changes in interfacial tissue composition and stiffness are not explicitly represented.13,24 This matters because arthrodesis is a maturation process rather than a single postoperative state. In Lapidus modelling, Wong et al5 assigned the graft the same stiffness as the adjacent bone, which is suitable for construct comparison but implicitly approximates a mechanically consolidated interface rather than explicitly modelling early postoperative healing and biological evolution. Future work should address the temporal healing process, for example by linking fixation stability to interfacial micromotion or by using mechanoregulation-based iterative approaches to simulate the progression of granulation tissue, cartilage, and immature bone over time. These approaches remain uncommon in foot and ankle arthrodesis FEA, and lack robust validation against serial CT or other temporally resolved indicators of bone healing.

Across different types of foot and ankle arthrodesis, FEA has been used mainly to assess initial stability, load sharing, and stress concentration. In ankle arthrodesis, adding crossed screws to an anterior plate improves control of joint micromotion, although this may increase peak stress at the screws and plate junctions.1 In subtalar arthrodesis, screw trajectory affects stress distribution and construct stiffness, with implications for fusion and function.2 In TTCA, intramedullary nails and lateral locking plates can both achieve adequate stability but generate different load-transfer patterns and high-stress regions, suggesting distinct mechanical failure modes.3 Forefoot arthrodesis models, including Lapidus and first metatarsophalangeal fusion, together with related forefoot FEA in hallux valgus correction, similarly show that mechanical optimization is not simply a matter of maximizing stiffness. In Lapidus fusion, restoration of first-ray load bearing may still be accompanied by increased metatarsal stress, adjacent-joint pressure, or tensile stress at the graft or fusion interface, while first metatarsophalangeal models suggest that fixation configuration also affects interfacial motion and the local mechanical environment for union.5,24 In Charcot medial column fusion, patient-specific modelling has further shown value in identifying implant stress and potential failure regions.4 Overall, these studies support a consistent design principle across joint levels and fixation strategies: the optimal construct is not necessarily the stiffest, but the one that balances load transfer, interface motion, and stress concentration.

Most foot and ankle arthrodesis FEAs remain at an exploratory stage, typically single-centre, small-sample, and focused on comparisons of construct configuration. The next step is not simply to build more isolated models or to pursue ever finer geometrical and material detail, but to embed these tools within a cumulative, testable body of evidence. This will require dedicated modelling and reporting standards for foot and ankle FEA, specifying geometry sources, material models, contact and loading definitions, validation procedures,13 and, in fusion applications, harmonized definitions of interface micromotion and time-dependent healing variables, so that immediate postoperative stability can be interpreted in relation to the subsequent maturation of the fusion mass rather than as an isolated endpoint. Where feasible, geometries and code should also be made available to enhance comparability and reproducibility between studies. At the modelling level, finite element libraries have already been developed for the knee,25,26 but comparable resources are lacking for the foot and ankle. A shared geometrical and loading framework would allow different arthrodesis configurations to be implemented on a common platform, substantially reducing duplicated effort and enabling robust cross-comparison of fixation strategies. 25,26

At the clinical research level, key finite element metrics should be calculated using harmonized workflows in multicentre prospective cohorts, and their associations with outcomes such as fusion quality, implant survival, and adjacent joint degeneration should be systematically evaluated.27,28 Only when such cross-model and cross-centre integration and standardization are achieved can foot and ankle arthrodesis FEA realistically serve, on the one hand, as a routine preclinical screening tool for novel implants and dynamic fixation concepts, and, on the other hand, as a robust clinical decision-support tool.
