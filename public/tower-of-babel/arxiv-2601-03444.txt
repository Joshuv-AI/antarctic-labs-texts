Grading Scale Impact on LLM-as-a-Judge: Human-LLM Alignment Is Highest on 0-5 Grading Scale
Weiyue Li Minda Zhao Weixuan Dong Jiahui Cai Yuze Wei

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as automated evaluators, yet prior works demonstrate that these LLM judges often lack consistency in scoring when the prompt is altered. However, the effect of the grading scale itself remains underexplored. We study the LLM-as-a-judge problem by comparing two kinds of raters: humans and LLMs. We collect ratings from both groups on three scales and across six benchmarks that include objective, open-ended subjective, and mixed tasks. Using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to measure absolute agreement, we find that LLM judgments are not perfectly consistent across scales on subjective benchmarks, and that the choice of scale substantially shifts human-LLM agreement, even when within-group panel reliability is high. Aggregated over tasks, the grading scale of 0-5 yields the strongest human-LLM alignment. We further demonstrate that pooled reliability can mask benchmark heterogeneity and reveal systematic subgroup differences in alignment across gender groups, strengthening the importance of scale design and sub-level diagnostics as essential components of LLM-as-a-judge protocols.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as pervasive automated evaluators across diverse NLP tasks
(
9
;
40
;
15
;
63
)
. This “LLM-as-a-judge” approach offers fast, low-cost, and easy reconfigurability via prompts or rubrics, and it is now a default for evaluating everything from translation to dialogue.
However, LLM-based evaluations often lack consistency. The same model can yield different scores if the prompt format or context is altered, and different LLMs used as judges may disagree on the same input
(
31
;
20
)
. Such inconsistency weakens the repeatability of results, raising concerns about the reliability of the LLM judges.

Most prior works validate LLM judges through aggregate correlations against human preferences, or by proposing new judge prompting or training strategies
(
40
;
15
;
5
;
55
)
. Recent analyses highlight consistency as a distinct dimension of judge quality, examining whether an LLM gives stable evaluations under resampling or when using alternative formats
(
31
;
59
)
. However, the grading scale itself remains a largely unexplored factor. In practice, human evaluators use different rating scales (e.g., 0-5 vs. 0-10) and may adjust their judgments differently depending on the scale. Yet, aside from a recent effort that compared scores across scales using Pearson correlations
(
31
)
, little is known about the effect of scale changes on LLM judge consistency under the intraclass correlation coefficient: a more appropriate metric for evaluating grading agreement than Pearson correlation. On the other hand, decades of psychometric research show that the choice of rating scale (number of points, continuous vs. discrete, etc.) can significantly affect reliability and rater behavior
(
37
;
45
;
11
;
42
)
. This suggests that the grading scale may be an essential but often overlooked parameter in LLM-as-a-judge evaluations.

In this work, we fill this gap by investigating the inter-scale consistency and its impact on human-LLM agreement. We collect both human and LLM-generated ratings on multiple scales for prompts drawn from six diverse benchmarks, including both objective questions and open-ended, subjective judgments. Treating human and LLM raters in a fully crossed design, we evaluate score reliability using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC)
(
48
;
29
)
. We then benchmark LLM judge performance across these datasets and scales, and identify which rating scale leads to the highest alignment between LLM scores and human judgments.

In summary, our work contributes the following. We show that the scoring scale is a significant choice for LLM-as-a-judge. Across a diverse suite of tasks, 0-5 maximizes average human-LLM absolute agreement, and 0-10 is consistently the weakest choice, even when within-population reliability remains high. However, the best scale can be benchmark-dependent (e.g., MT-Bench), motivating per-task diagnostics rather than one-size-fits-all. In particular, on subjective open-ended quality benchmarks, model-model agreement drops sharply. Finally, we observe cross-gender agreement where both males and females align more on 0-5 in our annotator pool. We hope our results will motivate the community to treat scale design as a controllable component of LLM-as-a-judge protocols rather than a fixed convention.

Related Work

LLMs as automated evaluators.

The application of LLMs as automatic evaluators has transitioned from reference-based metrics to comprehensive “LLM-as-a-judge” where prompted or lightly tuned models approximate human judgments across natural language generation (NLG), machine translation (MT), code evaluation, and multi-turn dialogue benchmarks
(
9
;
17
;
40
;
15
;
27
;
66
;
63
)
. A variety of specialized LLM-based evaluators have been proposed, often fine-tuned to follow detailed rubrics and produce reasoned scores
(
26
;
64
;
34
;
6
;
32
)
. Multimodal extensions further extend LLM judging capabilities
(
8
;
57
)
. In parallel, researchers have explored improved prompting and training strategies to enhance LLM evaluator performance
(
58
;
52
;
54
)
. Multi-agent or checklist-based evaluation frameworks
(
5
;
55
)
have also been introduced to reduce bias and improve robustness, and new agentic or representation-based judge architectures have been proposed
(
65
;
30
)
. Although these judging frameworks demonstrate impressive scalability and often show high overall correlation with human assessment, notable issues exist. LLM judges frequently display systematic biases. They tend to favor longer, more elaborate responses or later options in a list
(
53
;
28
;
46
;
63
)
, prioritize stylistic fluency over factual accuracy
(
56
)
, and assign unnaturally high scores to content generated by the same model
(
44
)
. Furthermore, minor variations in evaluation setups can influence scores, leading to efforts to debias evaluations
(
35
;
12
)
. When the quality of outputs is ambiguous, using a discrete Likert scale for LLM evaluator may introduce rating indeterminacy and lower consistency
(
20
)
, and these challenges motivate closer examination of LLM evaluator design and parameters.

Grading consistency of LLM judges.

Beyond accuracy or bias, a growing body of work has analyzed judges’ self-consistency, whether an LLM’s evaluations remain stable when scoring is repeated or slightly perturbed, and found worrying patterns such as non-transitive preference cycles in pairwise comparisons
(
31
;
59
)
. A stronger proprietary model may not be more self-consistent than a smaller open-source model when evaluation prompts are resampled, or the rating format is altered
(
31
)
. Meanwhile, fine-tuned open judges still sometimes fail to apply the intended grading criteria consistently
(
1
;
23
)
. From a cognitive perspective, investigations into LLMs’ reasoning capabilities suggest these consistency issues may stem from some higher-order reasoning limitations
(
49
;
3
)
. On the other hand,
14
reveals significant reliability problems on top-tier LLM judges under repeated pairwise scoring and logical transitivity checks, where they fail to maintain consistent preferences in roughly 25% of challenging cases. This suggests that we need to rigorously measure and improve the consistency of LLM-as-a-judge performance. One underexplored variable in this context is the scoring scale itself.
31
examines inter-scale consistency by reporting Pearson correlations between an LLM’s scores on different scales. However, Pearson correlation is not an ideal indicator of reliability under scale transformations, as it reflects only relative ranking and ignores shifts in calibration. A more robust approach is to use statistical reliability measures like the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)
(
48
;
29
)
, which directly assesses absolute agreement and accounts for rater and item variance. On the other hand, prior research has long demonstrated that rating scale design can influence reliability, where continuous or fine-grained scales tend to yield higher inter-rater consistency than coarse Likert or binary scales in human evaluations of language output
(
43
;
51
)
. Some recent studies have started to adopt finer 0-100 scales and report ICC values to gauge evaluator agreement
(
2
;
62
)
, complementing traditional agreement metrics like Krippendorff’s
\alpha

(
22
)
. Still, prior work has not directly addressed how changing the grading scale might alter an LLM judge’s consistency or its agreement with human judges.

Human-LLM agreement.

A core assumption behind LLM-as-a-judge is that model-based evaluations align well with human judgments. Many prior studies validate new LLM evaluators by measuring their aggregate correlation with human preference labels
(
40
;
15
)
. Others focus on improving this alignment through multi-agent debate or checklist approaches that aim to refine LLM judging so that it better matches human opinions
(
6
;
55
)
. Another line of comprehensive benchmarks has also been developed to test LLM judges against human standards
(
61
;
41
)
. However, none of the above studies compare human-LLM agreement across different rating scales. Past evaluations typically fix a single scoring scale and implicitly assume alignment is scale-invariant
(
36
;
33
;
19
;
7
;
16
)
. As a result, it remains an open question whether an LLM judge might agree better with human evaluators on one scale versus another. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to assess how the choice of score scale affects human-LLM alignment, providing empirical evidence to inform the design of evaluation protocols that maximize agreement with human judgment.

Methodology

Figure 1:
Overview of our experimental pipeline for assessing LLM-as-a-judge reliability. The framework consists of four main stages: (1) Data & Task Selection: collecting 5,497 items across six diverse datasets spanning objective and subjective judgments. (2) Inter-scale Consistency (LLM-only): evaluating six LLM judges across 0-5, 0-10, and 0-100 scales to construct consistency matrices. (3) Human-LLM Agreement: collecting fully crossed ratings from 12 human annotators (stratified by gender) and LLMs on 150 pooled items. (4) Reliability & Scale Analysis: employing two-way random-effects ANOVA to calculate Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (
\mathrm{ICC(A,1)}
and
\mathrm{ICC(A,k)}
) for quantifying how scale design influences rater calibration.

3.1
Metrics

Intraclass correlation coefficient.

To quantify reliability, we adopt the ICC, following guidelines from reliability research and annotation studies
(
29
;
22
)
. ICC is appropriate in our setting because the same set of raters evaluates the same set of items on a given scale, and we are interested in absolute agreement in numeric scores rather than mere rank correlation.

For a fixed scale, let
Y_{ij}
denote the score assigned to item
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
by rater
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. We model these scores using a two-way random-effects ANOVA with random item and rater effects:

Y_{ij}=\mu+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}+\varepsilon_{ij},

(1)

where
\mu
is the grand mean,
\alpha_{i}
is the random effect of item
i
,
\beta_{j}
is the random effect of rater
j
, and
\varepsilon_{ij}
is the residual error. Conceptually, both items and raters (humans or LLMs) are treated as samples from larger populations: questions from a broader task distribution and raters from a broader population of human annotators or model instances.

Following the notation of
29
, we report two absolute-agreement ICCs derived from this model. The first,
\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)
, measures the reliability of a single rater drawn from the population. The second,
\mathrm{ICC}(A,k)
, measures the reliability of the mean score obtained by averaging across
k
raters. Both are computed from the ANOVA mean squares for items (
MS_{I}
), raters (
MS_{R}
), and residual error (
MS_{E}
). For
n
items and
k
raters, the formulas are:

\mathrm{ICC(A,1)}=\frac{MS_{I}-MS_{E}}{MS_{I}+(k-1)\,MS_{E}+\frac{k}{n}(MS_{R}-MS_{E})},

(2)

and

\mathrm{ICC(A,}k\mathrm{)}=\frac{MS_{I}-MS_{E}}{MS_{I}+\frac{1}{n}(MS_{R}-MS_{E})}.

(3)

These statistics take values in
(-\infty,1]
, where higher values indicate greater reliability.

Normalized mean absolute error.

To quantify the absolute disagreement between LLM and human judgments, we report the normalized mean absolute error (nMAE). This metric measures the average absolute deviation between model scores and the corresponding human reference scores.

Let
\bar{y}_{i}^{(m)}
denote the mean score assigned by LLMs to data sample
i
, averaged across all evaluated models. Similarly, let
\bar{y}_{i}^{(h)}
denote the mean score assigned by human raters to the same data sample. The mean absolute error (MAE) is defined as

\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bar{y}_{i}^{(m)}-\bar{y}_{i}^{(h)}\right|,

(4)

To ensure comparability across tasks with different scoring ranges, we normalize MAE by the scale range
R
, defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum possible scores on the rating scale. The resulting normalized MAE is

\mathrm{nMAE}=\frac{1}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bar{y}_{i}^{(m)}-\bar{y}_{i}^{(h)}\right|.

(5)

By construction, nMAE takes values in
[0,1]
when model predictions are constrained to the same rating scale as human judgments, with lower values indicating closer agreement between the LLM and the human consensus.

3.2
Human and LLM Reliability

For each grading scale, we compute ICCs separately for different rater groups and then compare these values across scales and benchmarks. Throughout, we use the generic ICC notation above and instantiate
n
and
k
according to the item set and rater pool being analyzed.

Human reliability.

To establish a human reliability baseline, we form an
S\times R
rating matrix containing all human scores for a given scale, where
S
is the number of items (questions) and
R
is the number of human raters. We then compute
\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)
and
\mathrm{ICC}(A,R)
. This quantifies both the reliability of a single human annotator and the reliability of the averaged human panel. We also compute ICCs separately for the female raters and the male raters to examine potential subgroup differences in reliability. In addition, we repeat the same ICC computations on each benchmark subset, yielding human reliability estimates both on the pooled item set and on each individual benchmark.

LLM reliability.

For inter-rater reliability among LLM judges, we construct an
N\times L
rating matrix of LLM scores for a given scale, where
N
is the number of items scored by the LLMs (e.g., all items in a benchmark) and
L
is the number of LLM judges. We compute
\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)
and
\mathrm{ICC}(A,L)
, which measure, respectively, how consistent a single LLM judge is with the broader pool of LLM judges and how reliable an ensemble average over
L
judges would be. As with humans, we also recompute ICCs on each benchmark subset to understand how model-model agreement varies across domains.

Human-LLM agreement.

We assess the alignment between model predictions and human judgments by using the human consensus. Rather than comparing LLMs to individual human raters, we operationalize human judgment as the mean score of the annotator panel for each item, denoted as
\bar{y}^{(h)}_{i}
. This approach mitigates individual annotator variance and focuses on the model’s ability to capture the collective human signal. We conduct this analysis at two levels using the
\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)
. First, to evaluate the alignment of the LLM panel with humans, we form an
S\times 2
matrix for each scale, where the first column is the mean human score and the second is the LLM
ensemble
mean across the
L
LLM judges (models),
\bar{y}^{(m)}_{i}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}y^{(m)}_{i\ell}
. We then compute
\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)
on this two-column matrix to measure the absolute agreement between the human consensus and the model consensus. Second, to evaluate individual models, we analogously form an
S\times 2
matrix consisting of the mean human score and the specific model’s score, and compute
\mathrm{ICC}(A,1)
for each LLM. This measures how closely a specific model aligns with the human consensus.

4
Experiments

We conduct two sets of experiments: (1) LLM inter-scale agreement and (2) human-LLM agreement. Figure
1
summarizes our experimental pipeline.

4.1
Data

Our study uses items drawn from six established benchmarks: MT-Bench
(
63
)
, MoralChoice
(
47
)
, STS-B
(
4
)
, SummEval
(
13
)
, ToxiGen
(
21
)
, and TruthfulQA
(
38
)
. For LLM inter-scale agreement, we use all data points from each benchmark. For the human-LLM agreement, we sample 150 items total, with 25 items sampled from each benchmark. We treat these items as a sample from a broader distribution of tasks within our target domain and analyze reliability both on the pooled set of 150 items and on each benchmark subset of 25 items. Refer to Appendix
B
for detailed dataset overview.

4.2
Raters and scales

Twelve graduate student annotators (six female and six male) participate as human raters. Each annotator scores every item on every scale, yielding a fully crossed item-rater design for the human group. In parallel, we include six LLMs from diverse model families:
gpt-4o

(
24
)
,
gemini-2.5-flash

(
10
)
,
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct

(
18
)
,
Qwen3-32B

(
60
)
,
DeepSeek-v3.2

(
39
)
, and
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3

(
25
)
. We use GPT, Gemini, Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Mistral as abbreviations throughout the paper, referring to the above models. Each model acts as an automatic judge and scores items on all scales. For both human and LLM raters, we treat scores as real-valued quantities. Raters are instructed to stay within the specified scale bounds but are allowed to use fractional values. This reflects common expert-annotation practice, where intermediate values are used to distinguish between different levels of certainty.

We use three grading scales: 0-5, 0-10, and 0-100. Each scale is applied separately.
Fractional scoring is allowed on all scales, which isolates the effect of numeric range independent of discretizations. Human raters complete three rating blocks (one per scale) in randomized order, and within each block, item order is shuffled to reduce anchoring and fatigue effects. LLMs are prompted with a standardized judging template that specifies the rubric (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness) and the target scale, and they are instructed to output a single numeric score within the requested range. Refer to Appendix
D
for detailed human rater instructions.

4.3
Executions

For all LLM judges, we set the default temperature to 1. We also use
non-thinking
mode for all models. We run all experiments independently for each of the three grading scales. For LLM inter-scale agreement, we compute per-benchmark and per-judge ICC-based consistency estimates across scales. We linearly map all scores to [0,1] before computing inter-scale ICC for interpretability. For human-LLM agreement, we compute: (i) human-only ICCs at the pooled and per-benchmark levels, (ii) LLM-only ICCs at the pooled and per-benchmark levels, and (iii) pooled human-LLM ICCs, per-model human-LLM ICCs, and per-gender human-LLM ICCs at each scale. Comparing these quantities across scales allows us to answer three questions: (1) how human and LLM reliability change as the score range becomes finer, (2) whether the scale that yields the most self-consistent LLM judgments also maximizes agreement with human panels, and (3) how these patterns vary across benchmarks, judge models, and genders.

We first examine whether the same LLM assigns consistent scores to the same items when the only protocol change is the grading scale. We would expect near-perfect agreement across all scales if scale choices are unimportant (e.g., a linear rescaling of an internal latent score).

Table
1
shows that this expectation does not hold in general. On benchmarks with comparatively objective or mixed criteria such as STS-B, ToxiGen, and MoralChoice, inter-scale agreement is high across models. In contrast, on more subjective, open-ended quality benchmarks (MT-Bench and SummEval), inter-scale agreement drops substantially, especially for smaller or less capable judges. This indicates that on ambiguous tasks, scale choice can change how an LLM follows and executes the rubric, not just where it places scores within a range. Moreover, these findings have two implications. First, results from LLM-as-a-judge studies using different grading scales are not necessarily comparable without explicit cross-scale examination. Second, because inter-scale self-consistency is imperfect and benchmark-dependent, it becomes important to ask: if scales are not interchangeable, under which scale do LLM scores align most closely with human judgments?

Benchmark heterogeneity and the reliability illusion.

Aggregate ICCs can conceal sharp differences across benchmarks. Table

As a result, pooled LLM reliability can appear excellent largely because it is dominated by benchmarks where the underlying signal-to-noise ratio is high. This “reliability illusion” emphasizes that LLM judges’ reliability should be interpreted at more granular levels (such as by benchmarks, judges, and genders) rather than only in aggregate. 5.4
Analysis

Error analysis.

We use an nMAE threshold of 0.1 to distinguish well-aligned and poorly aligned judgments, corresponding to a 10% deviation relative to the scoring range (e.g., 0.5 points on a 5-point scale). Poorly aligned cases (
\mathrm{nMAE}>0.1
) typically depend on a small number of highly specific elements (e.g., a single factual claim or a niche detail in a question); when the model fails to capture this key point, there is little additional context to offset the error, leading to large disagreements with human judgments. In contrast, well-aligned cases (
\mathrm{nMAE}\leq 0.1
) draw on information that is spread across multiple parts of the input (e.g., several related cues or supporting statements), allowing contextual evidence to jointly support the evaluation and reduce sensitivity to individual misinterpretations. This pattern is consistent across all three score scales (0-5, 0-10, and 0-100). Representative examples are provided in the Appendix
G
.

Metrics validation.

Figure
2
provides empirical evidence of (1) why ICC and nMAE can disagree, (2) why reporting both is informative, and (3) why ICC is preferred over Pearson correlations. nMAE measures the average absolute distance between the LLM consensus score and the human consensus score, normalized by the scale range, so it can remain small even when the judge fails to capture item-to-item variability. ICC, on the other hand, reflects absolute agreement relative to the variance structure of the items and penalizes systematic offsets and variance mismatches. Therefore, this is why we have the case of SummEval, where correlation is relatively high but ICC is much lower, as well as the case of STS-B, where both correlation and ICC are high.

6
Discussions

Across six benchmarks and multiple judge models, our results show that the numeric score range is not a simple prompt choice in LLM-as-a-judge. On subjective, open-ended evaluation tasks, LLM scores are not perfectly self-consistent across scales, and human-LLM alignment shifts materially with the grading interface even when within-population panel reliability remains high. Aggregated across tasks, a 0-5 scale yields the strongest absolute agreement between LLM judges and human consensus (highest ICC and lowest normalized MAE), while 0-10 is consistently the weakest choice, and this ordering is stable under temperature perturbations for representative models. At the same time, pooled reliability can mask substantial benchmark heterogeneity. LLM panels appear highly reliable largely because objective-like tasks dominate aggregate variance, strengthening the need to report reliability and agreement at the benchmark (and rater-subgroup) level. Taken together, these findings motivate treating scale design and diagnostic reporting as standard components of LLM evaluation design protocols, rather than fixed conventions, especially when judging ambiguous, high-variance generations.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that constrain generality. The human raters are all graduate students and thus do not represent the broader populations that produce many large-scale annotations used in modern NLP pipelines. Their educational background, familiarity with LLM systems, and cultural context may influence both calibration and reliability, especially on subjective benchmarks. Moreover, some items, particularly in open-ended and truthfulness-focused benchmarks, are inherently difficult even for humans. When questions are under-specified or require specialized knowledge, human scores may reflect uncertainty or differing interpretations of the rubric; such uncertainty can lower agreement with LLMs and may also make ICC estimates sensitive to benchmark composition.

Ethical considerations

Our study involves ethical considerations related to the interpretation of evaluative judgments, particularly for benchmarks that require normative or subjective assessment. Scores produced by LLM-based judges on tasks such as toxicity detection and moral evaluation should not be interpreted as objective ground truth, as these judgments reflect context-dependent standards embedded in the models. Similar considerations apply to human annotations: human ratings in such benchmarks reflect the perspectives of a specific annotator population and may not generalize to broader moral or social norms. Moreover, both automated and human evaluators may be exposed to sensitive, offensive, or potentially harmful content in certain benchmarks, which calls for caution in the interpretation and downstream use of evaluation results.

(

)

Appendix E
Potential Risks

Complementing the ethical considerations in the main text, we note additional risks. Benchmarks such as ToxiGen contain potentially offensive content; to mitigate harm, all human annotators participate voluntarily with informed consent and guaranteed anonymity. Moreover, the reliability of LLM-as-a-judge evaluations varies substantially across tasks and scoring scales, and automated scores should therefore be interpreted as supporting signals rather than definitive judgments, particularly in high-stakes settings. Finally, observed subgroup differences should be understood as interactions between annotator populations and evaluation scales rather than as inherent group-level traits, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation when deploying automated evaluation systems in normative domains.
