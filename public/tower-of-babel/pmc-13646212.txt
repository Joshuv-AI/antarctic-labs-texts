Optimization of PET acquisition time for thoracic lesions in 18F-FDG PET/MRI: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Haifeng Gu, Yue Jiang, Jun Cai, Gang Yang, Jianrui Li, Sui Chen, Song Luo, Longjiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to define the minimum positron emission tomography (PET) acquisition time for chest PET/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), providing image quality and lesion detectability comparable to a 20-min reference.Materials and methodsIn this retrospective study, list-mode data from 101 patients undergoing chest 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/MRI on a 3-T PET/MRI system were reconstructed to simulate 10 acquisition times (0.5–20 min). Background

The incidence of thoracic diseases, particularly lung cancer, has been increasing [1]. Computed tomography (CT) is widely used for lesion detection but has limited specificity, necessitating a more robust diagnostic approach. Given the relatively low specificity in lung lesion based on CT morphological standards, there has been an urgent demand for a more robust diagnostic standard. Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT serves as a valuable tool for further precise differential diagnosis, confirming the presence of lymph node metastases and effectively distinguishing between benign and malignant chest lesions [2]. However, PET/CT has limited sensitivity for subtle thoracic lesions [3] and exposes patients to ionizing radiation [4].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides a radiation-free alternative for thoracic imaging [5, 6]. However, it cannot capture metabolic activity, limiting its ability to differentiate benign from malignant lesions. PET/MRI overcomes this limitation by adding metabolic information while avoiding CT-related radiation [7].

The integrated PET/MRI systems enable simultaneous data by integrating functional information from PET and anatomic/functional information from MRI, which results in more precise image fusion [8]. Due to the inherently slower nature of MRI, PET scan durations can be correspondingly prolonged. It has been established that extending the PET acquisition time per bed enhances image quality and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) [9, 10]. However, this benefit is counterbalanced by the drawback of patient tolerance, as overly long protocols risk discomfort and motion artifacts, which can compromise image quality. A solution is to optimize scan protocols by either reducing PET acquisition time or streamlining MRI sequences to better balance the duration of both modalities. For example, a recent study by Lütje et al demonstrated that the optimal acquisition time of 68Ga-prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)-ligand PET/MRI in patients with prostate cancer was identified to be 4 min per bed position, balancing imaging quality and lesion detectability [11]. Meanwhile, Noto et al reported that reduction of acquisition duration (if < 3 min per bed position) results in an increase in likelihood of halo artifacts [12]. To date, given the limited existing research on this topic and the relative scarcity of studies on 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/MRI for thoracic examinations, it is crucial to evaluate the impact of different PET acquisition times on the quality of thoracic PET images.

In this study, we used the 20-min acquisition as a reference and retrospectively reconstructed shorter acquisition times, enabling systematic evaluation of image quality and lesion detectability. Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to identify the shortest PET acquisition time that is non-inferior to the 20-min reference for image quality and lesion detectability in thoracic ¹⁸F-FDG PET/MRI, thereby enabling workflow optimization.

Materials and methods

Patients

The study followed institutional guidelines and was approved by the local Ethics Committee (2025DZKY-047-01). As it was a retrospective study, written informed consent from participants was waived. We reviewed 126 patients retrospectively who underwent 18F-FDG Chest PET/MRI between March 2022 and April 2023. Exclusion criteria included contraindications to MRI/gadolinium, lack of 18F-FDG-avid chest lesions, recent antitumor therapy, substantial artifacts, scan duration < 20 min, or incomplete datasets.

Chest PET/MRI imaging protocol

Prior to PET/MRI examinations, all patients were required to fast for at least 6 h, maintain fasting blood glucose levels < 8.0 mmol/L, and refrain from vigorous physical activity. An appropriate dose of 18F-FDG based on the patient’s body weight was intravenously administered before imaging, with a mean activity of 4.11 ± 0.91 MBq/kg (range 2.19–5.88 MBq/kg). Following a standardized tracer uptake period of 66.31 ± 12.21 min, patients underwent PET/MRI acquisition in supine position with arms down by an integrated 3.0-T time-of-flight PET/MRI system (Signa, GE Healthcare) [13].

First, a rapid whole-body PET/MRI acquisition was performed, spanning from the vertex of the skull to mid-thigh (4–5 bed positions, based on the patient’s height, 2 min per bed). Subsequently, a dedicated chest PET/MRI acquisition was conducted at a single bed, with the scanning position determined based on the whole-body PET/MRI. Considering the time required for routine chest MRI protocols, the PET acquisition time was set at 20 min to match the total MRI duration (approximately 20 min; see Table 1). This ensured complete temporal synchronization between modalities. PET data were acquired as list-mode files, allowing for retrospective reconstruction of various acquisition durations. Attenuation correction was performed using a Dixon-based volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination sequence. PET imaging reconstruction

In order to determine the optimal PET acquisition time, we retrospectively reconstructed the raw 20-min PET list-mode data from all patients with 10 simulated time intervals emulating reduced PET acquisition times [14]. Finally, 10 groups of data with different PET acquisition time intervals (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, and 20 min) were obtained. Reconstructions used time-of-flight, point spread function correction, ordered subset expectation maximization (2 iterations, 28 subsets), 5.0 mm Gaussian filter, 600 × 250 mm FOV, 256 × 256 matrix, and 2.8 mm slice thickness.

Image analysis: subjective image quality evaluation

Two readers (16 and 14 years of experience) independently assessed images in random order using PET Volume Computed Assisted Reading (PET VCAR; GE Healthcare) software, blinded to acquisition parameters and clinical information. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

A 5-point Likert scale was employed for assessment [15]. 1 = non-diagnostic (unable to discriminate lesions from background); 2 = limited (slightly discriminate lesions from background); 3 = acceptable (ability to discriminate lesions with significant image noise); 4 = good (ability to discriminate lesions with low image noise); 5 = excellent (ability to discriminate lesions with no image noise).

Image analysis: objective image quality evaluation

Following the above subjective evaluation of image quality review, one investigator (the nuclear medicine physician) reviewed each patient’s PET images in conjunction with their medical history and all other available records to identify lesions. According to the principles of PET imaging, a lesion was deemed to be present in an area of abnormal radionuclide concentration (excluding normal physiological uptake).

The PET images were analyzed starting from 20 min down to 0.5 min to avoid bias in lesion detectability. All measurements were performed by PET VCAR. For each lesion, a volume of interest was defined on the 20-min reference image using the iterative adaptive algorithm [16] and verified by the reader. This volume of interest was then propagated to all other reconstructed time points for the same patient, and L_SUVmax, L_SUVmean, L_SUVsd, and MTV were automatically recorded from each time point. For background measurement, a circular region of interest of approximately 2–3 cm in diameter was manually placed in the lumen of the ascending aorta (avoiding wall and calcification) at the level of the tracheal bifurcation on the 20-min reference image [17, 18]. This region of interest was then copied to the identical positions in all other reconstructed time points, and B_SUVmean and B_SUVsd were recorded accordingly. Lesion detectability evaluation

Lesion detectability was evaluated by comparing each simulated acquisition time to the 20-min reference. For each patient, the total number of lesions visible on the 20-min images was first recorded by two readers in consensus. For each of the other nine time points, the number of lesions that were missed (i.e., not identified) was recorded. The miss rate was calculated as the proportion of lesions visible on the 20-min reference that were missed at each shorter acquisition time.

Subgroup analysis

To further evaluate the robustness of the findings across different clinical scenarios, subgroup analyses were performed stratified by 18F-FDG uptake period and by lesion size. 18F-FDG uptake period was categorized into two groups (≤ 80 min and > 80 min) based on the distribution of uptake times. Lesions were divided into three size groups (< 15 mm, 15–20 mm, > 20 mm) based on MTV-derived equivalent spherical diameter.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software (IBM Corp.). Subjective image quality scores were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR). Interobserver agreement was assessed using weighted Cohen κ with quadratic weights [21]. Subjective scores were compared using Friedman’s test with Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon post hoc tests. Objective metrics were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (acquisition time as fixed effect, random intercepts for patients and lesions), with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. Subgroup analyses used the same model structure. The p-values lower than 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Patient population

After exclusion of 25 patients (Fig. 1), a total of 101 patients (77 males, 24 females; average age 63.8 ± 9.6 years; range 39–84 years) were included in the final analysis. Of these, 89 (88.1%) were pathologically confirmed. The most common primary malignancy was lung cancer (n = 80), followed by esophageal cancer (n = 15). Discussion

In this retrospective simulation study, the minimal PET acquisition time required to match a 20-min reference depends on the clinical goal. Quantitative parameters (L_SUVmax, L_SUVmean, L_SUVsd, LBR) stabilized from 3 min onward, subjective image quality plateaued at 7 min, and INR required 10 min. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for tailoring scan duration to specific diagnostic tasks.

Among these quantitative parameters, the negative correlation between L_SUVmax and acquisition time deserves attention. Although longer acquisitions improve image quality, L_SUVmax paradoxically decreased. This phenomenon is explained by noise-induced bias. Longer acquisitions reduce noise, mitigating SUVmax inflation [22]. Conversely, shorter acquisitions artificially elevate L_SUVmax [23, 24]. As acquisition time extends, noise is reduced and bias is eliminated. Consequently, the decrease in L_SUVmax reflects improved accuracy, not deteriorated quantitation. This relationship has been consistently reported in PET/MRI studies [10, 25]. Our data confirm that bias is eliminated by 3 min, after which L_SUVmax remains stable.

In contrast to the early stabilization of quantitative parameters, subjective image quality plateaued later. Scores at 5 min remained significantly lower than at 20 min, whereas from 7 min onward, no differences were observed. This delay likely reflects the higher SNR required for human observers. Visual detection in noisy PET images depends critically on SNR. As acquisition time increases, noise is reduced and observer performance improves until a plateau [26]. Previous studies found similarly that subjective quality declines at short acquisitions and stabilizes after a minimum duration [12, 27]. Thus, 7 min is the earliest time point at which subjective quality matches the full protocol, supporting its use for routine diagnostic imaging. The reliability of these subjective assessments is further supported by inter‑observer agreement analysis. Weighted kappa values improved from substantial at shorter acquisition times to almost perfect at longer times. At 7 min, the kappa value was 0.751, indicating good agreement between readers. The lower agreement at very short acquisitions reflects increased image noise, but such extreme scan durations are not clinically relevant. The substantial agreement at 7 min thus further reinforces the 7 min recommendation.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that patients with a shorter ¹⁸F-FDG uptake period (≤ 80 min) or larger lesions (≥ 15 mm) achieved stable SUV parameters as early as 2 min, whereas those with a longer uptake period (> 80 min) or smaller lesions (< 15 mm) required 3 min. This finding is physiologically plausible. A shorter uptake period likely occurs during the phase of rapid ¹⁸F-FDG accumulation in tumors, where image contrast is already high and signal-to-noise ratio improves quickly with longer acquisition times [28]. Conversely, with longer uptake periods, ¹⁸F-FDG clearance may have begun, and further extending the scan duration yields diminishing returns in noise reduction, thus requiring more time to match the reference standard [29]. For lesion size, smaller lesions are more susceptible to partial volume effects and noise, necessitating longer acquisitions to achieve SUV stability [30]. These observations underscore the potential for individualizing PET acquisition protocols based on patient- and lesion-specific factors.

Lesion detectability further supports the 7 min protocol. The miss rate at 7 min was low (1.4%, 95% CI 0.6–3.6%), and only 4% of patients had any missed lesions (none missed two or more). In contrast, at 5 min, the miss rate nearly doubled to 2.5% and affected 7% of patients. Missed lesions at 5–10 min were predominantly small nodules near the PET resolution limit, a finding consistent with the known limitations of partial volume effects and count statistics for sub-centimeter lesions [30]. At very short acquisitions (0.5–3 min), high background noise was the dominant masking factor, reflecting the insufficient signal-to-noise ratio for lesion detection [31]. These observations align with our quantitative and subjective findings: small lesions and high noise both delay the achievement of stable image quality. Collectively, these data confirm the clinical safety of a 7 min acquisition, as the miss rate and patient-level impact are minimal, and the causes of missed lesions are well understood.

Our findings align with previous studies that demonstrated the feasibility of reducing PET acquisition times. Murthy et al reported that 7 min acquisition improved image quality in ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 PET/MRI for prostate cancer [10]. Jannusch et al found that 5 min was sufficient for breast cancer staging with ¹⁸F-FDG PET/MRI [31], and Lindemann et al reduced whole-body PET/MRI to 2 min per bed position [32]. Building on these observations, the present study provides several methodological advances. By applying linear mixed-effects models that account for the hierarchical structure of lesion-level data, and by performing subgroup analyses based on ¹⁸F-FDG uptake period and lesion size, we offer more robust estimates and individualized time recommendations than previously available.

Our results have direct implications for clinical practice. For routine diagnostic imaging, a 7-min acquisition is recommended, as it provides the earliest plateau in subjective quality and a low miss rate. For quantitative assessments, 3–5 min suffices, which could increase scanner throughput and reduce motion artifacts. Patients with shorter ¹⁸F-FDG uptake periods or larger lesions might tolerate even 2-min acquisitions, enabling individualized protocols.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center, single-scanner (GE Healthcare Signa) design limits generalizability to other platforms (e.g., Siemens Healthineers, Philips Healthcare). However, the fundamental physical principles suggest that similar trends should apply. Second, the data‑driven thresholds (80-min uptake period, lesion size) and the use of a 20-min reference (not an external gold standard) require validation. Third, the small number of missed lesions precluded robust correlation analyses, and their descriptive characterization lacked quantitative thresholds. Fourth, only standard tracer activity and a 20-min reference were tested. Other dose regimens or longer acquisitions need further study. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted.

In conclusion, the minimal PET acquisition time for chest ¹⁸F‑FDG PET/MRI depends on the clinical objective. A 7-min acquisition provides subjective image quality and lesion detectability comparable to a 20-min protocol, making it suitable for routine diagnostic imaging. For quantitative tasks, 3–5 min is sufficient, and patients with shorter ¹⁸F-FDG uptake periods or larger lesions may tolerate even 2 min. Adoption of a 7-min protocol can improve patient comfort and increase scanner throughput. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these time thresholds and explore further individualization.
