Methotrexate toxicity due to a medication dispensing error compounded by an interaction with proton pump inhibitors
Authors: Ankita Chakrabarti, Murlidhar Pamarthi, Raman Bansal, Sankha Shubhra Chakrabarti, Upinder Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methotrexate at a low weekly dose is used for immunomodulation in autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis. With an elimination half-life of a few hours, the drug is primarily cleared through the renal route. Elimination of the drug is delayed in the presence of polypharmacy or deranged renal function. Here, we present a case of a 55-year-old lady who was on chronic once-weekly methotrexate therapy for rheumatoid arthritis. A recent change in the branded generic formulation of methotrexate by her pharmacist led to confusion with another prescription medication she was receiving (duloxetine), as the packaging resembled each other. As a result, she accidentally consumed methotrexate tablets (5 mg) for 6 consecutive days. This resulted in severe mucositis and moderate hematological changes, necessitating hospitalization. The patient recovered significantly with the use of intravenous fluid support, folinic acid, and alkaline diuresis. However, measurable higher levels of methotrexate were detectable in her serum for ten days after her last dose. In retrospect, the possibility of a pharmacokinetic interaction with a concurrent proton pump inhibitor was suspected. Our case also highlights the complications resulting from dispensing errors for drugs with a narrow margin of safety, especially in a less educated population.

Introduction

Methotrexate is an inhibitor of dihydrofolate reductase used to treat several malignancies, including osteosarcoma and acute lymphoid leukemias. The drug is administered at high doses (>500 mg/m2) as an anti-cancer chemotherapeutic agent. [1] At a low weekly dose (5–20 mg), the drug is an immunomodulator that is utilized as a disease-modifying agent for autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis. With an elimination half-life of a few hours, methotrexate is primarily cleared in urine in unchanged form, with a small fraction excreted as the hydroxy metabolite. [2] The clearance of methotrexate is delayed in the presence of polypharmacy and deranged renal or liver function. [3] Here, we present a case of delayed methotrexate elimination manifesting as multiorgan toxicity in the setting of a self-medication error that was aggravated by concomitant use of a proton pump inhibitor.

Case Report

A 55-year-old lady with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and neuropathic pain presented to our center with complaints of dysphagia and multiple oral ulcers for around a week. For the past 1.5 years, she was on oral methotrexate (5 mg once weekly, leflunomide 20 mg daily, and folic acid 5 mg weekly). Around 2 months back, she had a flare of RA and was prescribed daily oral prednisolone 5 mg and rabeprazole 20 mg. She was also receiving oral duloxetine 20 mg/d for neuropathic pain. On detailed review of the medication history, it was revealed that the patient had confused the methotrexate oral formulation (tablet) with duloxetine after a recent change in the branded generic methotrexate by her pharmacist (Fig. 1). She had inadvertently consumed 5 mg tablets of methotrexate once daily for six days, with the last dose being taken five days before presenting to us. Following this, she had had two episodes of vomiting. For the past 5 days, she was taking rabeprazole and leflunomide and had left other medicines. On examination, the patient had multiple oral ulcers distributed over the palates, inner aspects of the lips, and the visible oropharynx, with clean bases and minimal erythema. Laboratory investigations revealed bicytopenia with reduced total leucocyte and platelet counts (Table 1). Renal function was relatively preserved with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 67 mL/min. Leflunomide was stopped. Serum methotrexate level was estimated on Day 1 of admission (5 days after the last oral methotrexate dose) using the cloned enzyme donor immunoassay (CEDIA) technique (lower limit of detection: 0.04 µmol/L) and was 0.1 µmol/L. Given the methotrexate elimination half-life of 5–8 h, the levels should have been undetectable by this time. The patient was started on intravenous leucovorin (folinic acid) at a dose of 10 mg/m2 of body surface area every 6 h, along with intravenous fluid support. On repeat testing (Day 3 of admission), the serum methotrexate level remained 0.1 µmol/L. Leucovorin administration was continued, and forced alkaline diuresis with intravenous sodium bicarbonate (100 meq) and intravenous furosemide (10 mg) was initiated. Moreover, fluid support was intensified to 3–4 L/day. Detectable methotrexate in the serum persisted till Day 6 (10 days after the last methotrexate dose) and finally, on Day 8 of admission (12 days after the last methotrexate dose), serum methotrexate became undetectable, indicating a delayed clearance of the drug from the body despite preserved renal function (Fig. 2). Concomitant drugs given during the hospital stay were intravenous pantoprazole during the first five days in view of severe gastritis, followed by intravenous omeprazole from Day 6 to Day 8. Oral ulcers were managed conservatively with topical formulations containing benzalkonium chloride, choline salicylate, and benzocaine. The patient was counseled about the dosing error and apprised of the potential risks of incorrect dosing to restore confidence and ensure safe long-term adherence. Discussion

Our case presents a scenario in which methotrexate clearance was delayed in an older adult female, possibly due to a pharmacokinetic drug interaction with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), resulting in significant toxicity. According to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), the patient presented with grade 3 (severe) mucositis and grade 2 (moderate) hematologic toxicity (neutrophil count decreased and thrombocytopenia). [4] Gastrointestinal disturbances are the common mild manifestations of methotrexate toxicity in ambulatory patients. Hospitalized patients, on the other hand, often display mucositis, hematological disturbances, and pneumonitis. [5], [6] Our patient had been on low-dose methotrexate and had normal renal function, with no obvious signs of third-space loss, prompting us to rethink the possible causes of delayed clearance. The interaction with PPIs was suspected only a day before discharge, by which time methotrexate had already cleared from the body due to intensified elimination measures. Methotrexate-proton pump inhibitor rechallenge was not performed due to ethical reasons.

The elimination t1/2 of methotrexate at the weekly dose used in rheumatoid arthritis ranges from 5 to 8 h. The drug, hence, is undetectable after 48–72 h of the last dose, but was present in detectable levels in our patient for up to 10 days after the last dose. The distribution of methotrexate into cells depends on transporters such as the reduced folate carrier, the proton-coupled folate transporter, and the organic anion-transporting polypeptide 1A2 (OATP1A2). The drug is partly metabolized in the liver to a 7-hydroxy metabolite and is mainly excreted in urine by glomerular filtration and tubular secretion, the latter mediated by organic anion transporters such as OAT1 and OAT3, and the multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP). [2], [7] Cotrimoxazole, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and probenecid are medicines commonly implicated in impairing the renal clearance of methotrexate. [2]

The delayed elimination of low-dose methotrexate in the present case, in the absence of renal dysfunction, liver dysfunction, and apparent third-space loss, suggests the possibility of drug interactions at the level of transporters. The patient was on concomitant leflunomide and rabeprazole, and though leflunomide was discontinued at the time of admission, proton pump inhibitors were continued due to severe mucositis. Leflunomide, administered to mice, has been shown to increase methotrexate accumulation in the liver by decreasing the expression of MRP-2 efflux transporter and increasing MRP-3 expression on the basolateral membrane. Cumulatively, these mechanisms result in increased methotrexate retention in the liver and kidney and decreased elimination, resulting in increased blood levels of methotrexate. [8] However, since leflunomide was discontinued on the day of admission, the possibility of interaction was rather suspected with the ongoing PPIs. PPIs such as omeprazole, lansoprazole, rabeprazole, and pantoprazole inhibit the BCRP-mediated efflux of methotrexate. PPIs are associated with a 2.65 times increased risk of delayed elimination of methotrexate given at high doses (>500 mg/m2) in patients with malignancies, as observed in pharmacokinetic studies. [9] Clinical evidence also exists for such an interaction. Delayed methotrexate elimination has been observed with simultaneous use of omeprazole and high-dose methotrexate in patients with malignancies. [10] Pantoprazole given with low-dose methotrexate (15 mg intramuscularly, once a week) has been reported to cause severe bone pain and myalgia in a patient with cutaneous lymphoma, and this was associated with an increased concentration of the -hydroxy metabolite of methotrexate. [11] In a retrospective case series of patients on low-dose methotrexate hospitalized due to drug toxicity, concomitant use of PPIs was proposed as a risk factor. [5] Similarly, our case emphasizes the possibility of an inadvertent drug interaction between PPIs and methotrexate used at low doses in non-malignant conditions, in the setting of accidental over-consumption of methotrexate. The score for the interaction in the current case was calculated as 7 (Probable) using the Drug Interaction Probability Scale. [12]

Aggressive hydration, sodium bicarbonate infusion, and a brief course of furosemide were adopted in the patient to hasten the urinary elimination of methotrexate. While hydration and alkalinization of urine are standard approaches for removing methotrexate, the use of high-ceiling diuretics is not preferred because of the risk of dehydration and associated risk of nephrotoxicity. [3], [13] Diuretics may also compete with methotrexate for renal clearance and reduce its renal elimination. [3] We used low-dose diuretics only for a short while in view of the high fluid therapy being administered.

Apart from the pharmacokinetic drug interaction, the case also highlights the complications that can arise in older patients with poor literacy due to dispensing errors. The common branded generic methotrexate formulations in India come with either bright yellow packaging or in elongated or square strip packs. In the current case, however, both methotrexate and duloxetine were dispensed in blister packs of almost similar dimensions. Despite the two tablets being of different colors, inadequate instructions by the pharmacist and similar outer packaging of the medicines led to confusion for the patient, who was otherwise adherent to proper drug intake for the last 1.5 years. Such drugs, which are nearly Look-Alike/Sound-Alike (LASA) drugs, warrant strict measures at the regulatory level, awareness at the dispensing level, and continued monitoring at the prescribing level. Unnecessary pharmacist replacements of medications should be discouraged, particularly for drugs with narrow therapeutic margins. This is especially true for large developing countries such as India, with several unregulated pharmacies and easy access to medications.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of prolonged measurable methotrexate levels in serum, attributable to a pharmacokinetic interaction with proton-pump inhibitors, in the setting of accidental overdosing while on low-dose therapy. Since acid-lowering drugs might be required in such scenarios due to mucositis, histamine H2 receptor blockers can be employed as alternatives. Unnecessary replacement of a medication formulation should be avoided for drugs with a narrow margin of safety. Educating older patients on key aspects of medication use and safety should be a continuous process, even in those on chronic therapy.
