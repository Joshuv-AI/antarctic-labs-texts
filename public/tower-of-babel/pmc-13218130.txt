Predictors of mortality among non-traumatic critically ill patients with electrolyte imbalances attending a Ugandan regional referral hospital Emergency Department
Authors: Ambrose Okello, Daniel Olinga, Justine Athieno Odakha, Musoki Bonita Kaija, Jonathan Byasi, Evelyn Mwende Musau, Prisca Mary Kizito, Aggrey Lubikire, Kenneth Bagonza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrolyte imbalances are common among critically ill patients and strongly influence outcomes, and yet data from sub-Saharan Africa remain limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, severity of electrolyte imbalances and predictors of 14-day mortality among non-traumatic critically ill patients in a Ugandan tertiary hospital Emergency Department (ED).MethodologyWe conducted a four-month prospective cohort study in 2023 on non-trauma patients at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital ED. Consecutive non-traumatic adults with a Modified Early Warning Score ≥5 or a single parameter score of 3 were enrolled. Serum sodium, potassium and chloride were measured at admission. Patients were followed for 14-day mortality. Multivariate Cox regression was used to identify independent predictors of mortality using Stata version 17.0.ResultsAmong the 400 patients enrolled, 45% (median age 59 years; 64% male) had electrolyte imbalances. The 14-day mortality was 59.9%, with most deaths occurring within the first three days. More than three-quarters of patients had multiple electrolyte imbalances, of which hyponatraemia, hyperchloraemia, and hypokalaemia was the most common. After adjustment, independent predictors of increased mortality were referral from other facilities (AHR 2.4, 95% CI: 1.1–5.3) in addition to heart failure and a history of alcohol use. Specialist involvement (AHR 0.5, 95% CI: 0.3–0.9) was found to be protective in addition to pre-referral electrolyte correction and ward admission from the ED.ConclusionElectrolyte imbalances affect over half of critically ill non-trauma ED patients in Uganda and are associated with high early mortality. Heart failure, alcohol use, and inter-facility referral predict worse outcomes, while targeted pre-referral electrolyte correction, early specialist input and prompt ED disposition are strongly protective. These findings highlight actionable priorities for improving emergency care in similar low-resource settings.Keywords: Patient transport, Critical illness, Electrolyte imbalance, Emergency Department, Uganda, mortality

Introduction

Electrolytes are vital for cellular, neurological, and myocardial activity [1]. Electrolyte imbalances can cause severe complications and are linked to increased mortality [2,3], especially in critically ill patients with renal failure, heart failure, and sepsis, and those with a Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) ≥5 [4]. Early detection and correction are crucial to improving outcomes. Globally, electrolyte imbalances are recognised predictors of mortality in Emergency Department (ED) patients [3,5,6]. Hyponatraemia alone carries hospital mortality rates of up to 40% and ICU-specific mortality of 28% [7,8]. Severe hyperkalaemia accounts for 30.7% in-hospital deaths, especially among diabetics with heart or kidney disease [9,10].In high-income countries, prevalence among critically ill patients ranges from 18 to 23% [4,6]. A Norwegian study reported 9.4% prevalence in general ED patients [5], while studies from Iran and the U.S. found hyponatraemia and hypernatraemia rates of 22% and 17%, with mortality between 7.5% and 39.1% [4,6,11].

In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of electrolyte imbalances among critically ill patients is substantially higher than in high-income settings, ranging from 50% to 72%. This increased burden is compounded by limited intensive care capacity, delayed diagnostic capabilities, and broader resource constraints, contributing to worse clinical outcomes. For example, in Ethiopia, 60.7% of cancer patients presented with electrolyte disturbances, significantly increasing mortality risk [11,12]. In Libya, 72% of elderly patients had electrolyte abnormalities, with hyponatraemia as a major death predictor [13]. In Uganda, research has largely focused on paediatric malnutrition, with sparse data on adult non-trauma critically ill patients. A recent study in elderly heart failure patients found hyponatraemia to be the most common disturbance (24.2%) and a significant mortality contributor [14]. The burden, severity, and mortality risk of electrolyte imbalances in the broader adult ED population remain poorly understood.

This study aimed to determine the prevalence and severity of electrolyte imbalances and identify predictors of 14-day mortality among non-trauma critically ill patients presenting to the ED of a tertiary hospital in southwestern Uganda.

Study methods

A prospective cohort study was conducted at the Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH) ED in Southwestern Uganda over four months (February 1, 2023 – May 31, 2023). The ED, with a 30-bed capacity, is staffed by emergency medicine residents, ED staff, and intern doctors who provide acute care for critically ill patients. We consecutively enrolled critically ill adults (≥18 years) with a Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) ≥5 or a single MEWS parameter score of 3. Including every patient who met the eligibility criteria was intended to eliminate the risk of selective enrolment based on patient characteristics, time of day, or researcher availability. Electrolyte testing (Sodium, Potassium, Chloride) was performed once, at the time of ED admission, for all eligible patients. Hyponatremia was defined as Sodium <135 mmol/L, and Hypernatremia as >145 mmol/L. Hypokalaemia was defined as Potassium 〈3.5 mmol/L, and Hyperkalaemia > 5.5 mmol/L. Hypochloraemia was Chloride <96 mmol/L, while hyperchloremia was>106 mmol/L. Patients with traumatic injuries were excluded. Those with electrolyte imbalances were followed up for 14 days to assess mortality outcomes, and those lost to follow-up were excluded from the final analysis. The research team did not interfere with patient stabilisation. Screening included medical histories and clinical assessment, documented using structured questionnaires. Informed consent was obtained from participants or their caregivers before data collection.

Data analysis

The primary outcome was 14-day mortality. Exposure variables included electrolyte imbalances, demographics, lifestyle factors, clinical and laboratory findings, referral status, MEWS score, kidney function, comorbidities, cadre of the admitting doctor and Patient disposition. Data were entered into EPI Info, cleaned, and analysed in STATA version 17(Stata Corp, Texas, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic and clinical characteristics. The relationship between independent and dependent variables was assessed using crude and adjusted hazard ratios. Predictors of 14-day mortality were first analysed using bivariate analysis, and factors with p < 0.2 were subjected to multivariable Cox regression. A p-value of ≤0.05 at a 95% CI was considered statistically significant.

Quality control

Research assistants were trained by the Principal Investigator (PI) to ensure data uniformity. Questionnaires were pretested and standardised before the study began. The PI reviewed data for errors and completeness before entry. Every 25th sample was cross-validated at an independent laboratory (Lancet Laboratory) for quality assurance.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Emergency Department Faculty Research Committee and the Research Ethics Committee (REC) of Mbarara University of Science and Technology (IRB Number MUST-2022–663), Uganda. The Hospital Director of Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital, Uganda, granted administrative clearance. Written informed consent was obtained from participants or caregivers after explaining the study’s purpose, risks, and benefits. Patient data were anonymised using unique study ID numbers.

Results

Over four months, we screened nearly one thousand non-traumatic patients presenting to the ED (Fig. 1). From these, 400 met our eligibility criteria. Fewer than half of those tested had an electrolyte imbalance and were enrolled in a 14-day follow-up. Only three participants were lost to follow-up, leaving a final analytic cohort of 177 participants.

The overall mortality rate was 59.9%. The survival probability decreased with the number of electrolyte imbalances (Fig. 2). Patients with no electrolyte imbalance had the highest survival probability, with approximately 70% surviving by day 14. Those with a single electrolyte imbalance showed moderate survival, with around half surviving to the end of the follow-up. In contrast, patients with more than one electrolyte imbalance had the lowest survival probability, with only about 30% surviving by day 14. Most deaths occurred early in the course, with over 40% of patients dying within the first three days and more than half by day five.

Kaplan-Meier survival estimates for non-traumatic critically ill patients with electrolyte imbalances.

After adjusting for potential confounders, including age and sex, several factors emerged as independent predictors of 14-day mortality (Table 3). Patients with a history of alcohol consumption had twice the hazard of death. Those referred from other health facilities faced a 2.4-fold increased risk, while the presence of heart failure was associated with a more than fourfold higher mortality hazard. Conversely, several factors were associated with reduced mortality. Involvement of a specialist in patient care, pre-referral administration of diuretics, and admission to the wards from the ED were all protective factors.

Discussion

The majority of patients in this cohort came from rural areas, a finding that underscores the geographic and health system barriers that often delay presentation until illness is advanced. This delay likely contributes to the high prevalence of electrolyte disturbances observed in this setting. Common presenting symptoms, including difficulty breathing, altered mental status, and vomiting, point to underlying infections or metabolic derangements frequently seen in critically ill patients [15]. Hypertension and diabetes were the most frequently documented comorbidities, though their prevalence was notably lower than figures reported from higher-resource settings such as Saudi Arabia [16]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in diagnostic capacity, health-seeking behaviour, and true epidemiological variation.

Electrolyte imbalances were identified in nearly half of the critically ill patients in this cohort, a figure substantially higher than the 28.1% reported among Swiss emergency department patients [17]. The disparity likely reflects the advanced stage of illness at presentation in our setting, where patients often seek care late in their disease course. Hyponatraemia was the most frequent imbalance, affecting over half of the patients. This exceeds the global estimates of 25–45% reported by Rowe [18] and is nearly double the rate observed in a Swiss cohort of COVID-19 patients [17]. In our context, this likely stems from common precipitants such as diarrhoeal illness, inadequate oral intake, and delayed or insufficient fluid resuscitation before arrival.

Hyperchloraemia was present in one-third of patients, a rate lower than that reported in studies from the United States, India, and Pakistan, where prevalence ranged from 50% to nearly 80% [19]. The lower figure in our study may be an artefact of point-of-care testing, rather than a true epidemiological difference, highlighting the need for standardised diagnostic approaches across settings. Hypokalaemia occurred in just over a quarter of participants, a rate comparable to an Ethiopian study (20%) but markedly higher than the 5.6% reported in the United States [20], reflecting differences in dietary patterns, diagnostic thresholds, and timing of presentation. These findings highlight the need for affordable point-of-care testing and access to balanced fluids and electrolyte therapies to improve outcomes in resource-limited settings.

Nearly six in every ten patients died within 14-days which is three-times higher than 20.7% reported in the African Critical Illness Outcomes Study [21]. This reflecting a severe mortality burden in our setting. Access to electrolyte testing is often delayed or unaffordable for patients, meaning imbalances may go uncorrected until irreversible deterioration has occurred. Furthermore, intensive care capacity at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital is severely limited, with only eight ICU beds serving a large catchment population. Consequently, most deaths occurred in the ED while patients awaited transfer, a reminder that in many low-resource settings, the ED functions as both the point of entry and, too often, the endpoint of care for the critically ill. Strengthening critical care capabilities within the ED itself, alongside efforts to expand ICU capacity, could help avert a substantial proportion of these early deaths.

Alcohol use was associated with a twofold increase in the likelyhood of death, consistent with previous research from both Uganda and the United States, where alcohol consumption conferred a 1.4-fold and 1.2-fold increased mortality risk, respectively [15]. This robust association across diverse settings suggests that alcohol use is a meaningful marker of vulnerability in critical illness. Heart failure was the strongest clinical predictor in this cohort, with a more than fourfold increase in the hazards of mortality. This finding aligns with a study from Tanzania [22]. Heart failure frequently coexists with kidney disease [23], which is a particularly challenging combination in Uganda's low-resource settings, due to its disruption of fluid and electrolyte imbalances and increased risk of arrhythmia.

Referral from another facility carried a 2.4-fold higher hazard of death compared to direct presentation. Similar findings from Tanzania showed that referred patients similarly experienced worse outcomes [22]. Delays in transfer allow electrolyte imbalances to progress unchecked, and many referring facilities lack the capacity to stabilise patients adequately before transport. Involvement of a specialist in patient care was associated with a 50% reduction in the hazard of mortality. Patients managed by emergency medicine physicians fared better, likely due to specialised training in recognising critical illness, tailoring interventions, and making timely decisions about escalation of care. The benefit of having an EM physician is well documented in high-income countries [24] and is more useful in low-resource settings where general practitioners have limited critical care experience [25].

Pre-referral electrolyte correction was the most powerful protective factor observed, reducing the hazard mortality by 80%. These interventions, such as administering hypertonic saline for severe hyponatraemia or potassium chloride for symptomatic hypokalaemia, are supported by global evidence among critically ill patients [26,27]. This study extends those findings to the specific context of interfacility transfer in a low-resource setting.

Patients who were transferred to the wards had 90% reduction in the hazard mortality compared to those who remained boarded in the ED. A transfer to the ward was made based on the fact that the patients were stable and possibly waiting for discharge from the hospital. These findings are consistent with the existing literature on the harms of prolonged ED stay [28]. Many deaths in our cohort occurred within the first three days while patients awaited transfer, reflecting ICU bed shortages rather than clinical failures. Prolonged ED boarding leaves patients in a limbo of relative neglect, neither receiving the rapid resuscitation of the ED nor the definitive monitoring of an ICU [29]. Addressing this requires expanding ICU capacity and strengthening the ED's ability to function as a critical care unit with dedicated monitoring and protocols for prolonged stays.

Limitations of the study

This study's strengths include its prospective design, real-time follow-up, and focus on an understudied population, which together provide reliable evidence on electrolyte imbalances in a low-resource setting. However, limitations include its single-centre design, exclusion of trauma patients, lack of serial electrolyte measurements, and potential unmeasured confounding from unassessed factors such as nutritional status and severity scores. Larger multi-centre studies across sub-Saharan Africa are needed to validate and expand these findings.

Conclusion

Electrolyte imbalances are highly prevalent among non-traumatic critically ill patients presenting to a Ugandan ED, with a substantial proportion dying within 14 days of admission. Hyponatraemia, hyperchloraemia, and hypokalaemia were the most common imbalances, and most patients presented with multiple imbalances concurrently. Mortality was highest among those with heart failure, a history of alcohol use, or referral from another facility, risks exacerbated by delayed recognition, limited ICU capacity, and prolonged ED boarding. Conversely, pre-referral electrolyte correction, early specialist involvement, and timely ward admission each conferred significant survival benefits.

These findings underscore the need for routine point-of-care testing, context-specific correction protocols at referring centres, early specialist input, strengthened ICU capacity, and improved inter-facility coordination. Where ICU expansion is not immediately feasible, investing in ED-based critical care capabilities offers a pragmatic alternative. Addressing these gaps provides a clear pathway toward reducing preventable deaths and improving emergency care for critically ill patients across sub-Saharan Africa. Priority actions include equipping referring facilities for pre-transfer electrolyte correction, involving emergency medicine physicians early, expanding ICU capacity, and integrating high-risk features into triage protocols to target the most vulnerable patients.
