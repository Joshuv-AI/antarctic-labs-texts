Methylene blue-guided parenchyma-sparing ex vivo resection and autotransplantation for a type III renal arteriovenous fistula with large renal vein aneurysm: A technical case report
Authors: Ottavia Borghese, Francesco Sposato, Yamume Tshomba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-flow renal arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), type III with vein aneurysms, are rare and often require nephrectomy. A 54-year-old man presented with lumbar pain and hematuria. Computed tomography angiography revealed an 8- × 12-cm right renal vein aneurysm and a type III arteriovenous fistula occupying >30% of the kidney. After ex vivo nephrectomy with cold perfusion, selective methylene blue injection delineated the AVM nidus and parenchyma, guiding resection and excision. The procedure was performed by a multidisciplinary team including a vascular surgeon, transplant surgeon, and urologist. The kidney was reconstructed and reimplanted. Postoperative creatinine levels stabilized at 1.10 mg/dL, and computed tomography angiography confirmed AVM exclusion and preserved perfusion. Methylene blue mapping can enable parenchyma-sparing repair in selected cases.

Renal arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are rare vascular lesions that range from focal, low-flow fistulae to diffuse, high-flow intraparenchymal malformations. Type III AVMs, characterized by extensive nidus formation and brisk arteriovenous shunting, present the greatest therapeutic challenge because of their size, flow dynamics, and propensity to involve multiple segmental vessels.1

Coexisting renal vein aneurysms are uncommon but clinically important; aneurysmal venous dilation increases the risk of catastrophic hemorrhage, may precipitate high-output cardiac failure, and can limit the feasibility and safety of endovascular occlusion by increasing the risk of embolic migration and device instability.1, 2, 3 Contemporary management strategies, therefore, span a spectrum from endovascular embolization, preferable when a discrete feeding artery or compact nidus permits safe occlusion, to open nephron-sparing excision with complex vascular reconstruction. When reconstruction is not feasible, nephrectomy is typically performed.

For anatomically complex, high-flow lesions in which preservation of the renal parenchyma is a priority, ex vivo resection with renal autotransplantation provides an organ-sparing alternative; the kidney is explanted, the nidus is excised under bloodless conditions on the back table, and the organ is reimplanted with vascular reconstruction to restore renal perfusion.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

In this paper, we describe a technical adjunct to this approach (selective methylene blue injection into the AVM nidus), which enhances the intraoperative delineation of abnormal vascular channels, facilitates precise parenchyma-sparing resection, and may reduce warm ischemia time and inadvertent the loss of viable renal tissue.

This report emphasizes the multidisciplinary nature of decision-making and operative execution (vascular surgery, transplant surgery, urology, and interventional radiology) and provides detailed technical steps to improve reproducibility of the technical adjunct.

We followed the CARE case report guidelines.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old man presented with lumbar pain and macroscopic hematuria. The patient’s medical history was notable only for well-controlled hypertension. The baseline serum creatinine level was 0.90 mg/dL.

After multidisciplinary review between a vascular surgeon, urologist, transplant surgeon, and interventional radiologist, ex vivo resection with renal autotransplantation was selected to maximize parenchymal salvage and to minimize the risk of postoperative renal failure.

The transplant surgeon and vascular surgeon jointly led the explantation, back-table resection, and reimplantation; the urologist performed the ureteroneocystostomy and managed urinary reconstruction; and the interventional radiologist participated in preoperative planning.

Written informed consent for surgery and publication of the clinical data and images was obtained from the patient.

Surgical technique

Nephrectomy and ex vivo preparation

A right flank retroperitoneal approach was used to expose the kidney. The renal artery and vein were dissected to their origins and the ureter mobilized.

Short vascular stumps were preserved on the aortic and caval sides to facilitate later reimplantation. The kidney was explanted and immediately flushed on the back table with cold Custodiol solution at 4 °C. The transplant surgeon and vascular surgeon jointly led the explantation, back-table resection, and reimplantation.

Identification of nidus and arterial feeder

The principal arterial feeder and nidus were identified preoperatively on contrast CTA with three-dimensional reconstructions. On the back table, the suspected feeder was confirmed by direct inspection and by a gentle test flush of cold Custodiol (histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate) solution to observe flow patterns before cannulation.

Selective methylene blue mapping

On the back table, the principal arterial feeder to the AVM identified intraoperatively was cannulated with a 5F to 6F catheter. Methylene blue solution was prepared from a 1% stock and was diluted to a working concentration of 0.2%. A total injected volume of 10 mL was used in this case. The concentration and volume were chosen to provide clear parenchymal staining of AVM-perfused tissue while minimizing the systemic dye load and risk of adverse effects. With the venous outflow controlled to limit systemic spill, methylene blue was slowly injected into the arterial feeder/nidus under direct visualization. Venous outflow control on the back table was achieved by proximal occlusion of the renal vein using a soft vascular (bulldog) clamp placed at the renal vein origin and by gentle distal compression with vessel loops; effluent was collected into a basin on the back table to permit direct visual monitoring. The dye preferentially stained AVM-perfused parenchyma, producing a clear demarcation between abnormal and normal tissue. Injection was performed slowly over 20 to 30 seconds with low manual pressure; any brisk venous return would have prompted the immediate cessation of injection and additional venous occlusion. Parenchyma-sparing resection

Dye demarcation guided the precise excision of the stained AVM nidus and aneurysmal sac through sharp dissection and selective ligation of feeding vessels. Fine sutures were used for hemostasis. Care was taken to preserve unstained parenchyma.

Reconstruction and autotransplantation

After reconstruction, the kidney was reimplanted with end-to-end anastomoses for the artery and vein; ureteroneocystostomy was performed with a double-J stent by the urologist. Temporary vascular control during cannulation and back-table work was maintained using bulldog clamps and vessel loops on the preserved arterial and venous stumps.

Ischemia times

The warm ischemia time (explantation to cold flush) was approximately 3 minutes. The cold ischemia time (back-table resection and reconstruction until reperfusion at reimplantation) was approximately 95 minutes. The arterial and venous anastomotic time during reimplantation was approximately 35 minutes. The kidney was maintained at 4 °C throughout back-table work.

Outcome

The immediate postoperative course was uneventful. Serum creatinine levels increased from 0.90 mg/dL preoperatively to 1.10 mg/dL postoperatively and subsequently stabilized within normal limits without dialysis. The patient was discharged in stable condition and scheduled for clinical and imaging follow-up. The 3-month postoperative CTA revealed the exclusion of the AVM and aneurysm with preserved renal perfusion and patent vascular anastomoses (Fig 3). Discussion

This case illustrates that selective methylene blue injection into the AVM nidus during ex vivo renal repair can provide a reliable visual map of the abnormal parenchymal territories, enabling targeted resection and organ preservation. The technique is simple, low cost, and reproducible in centers with renal autotransplantation experience. The key technical elements that we emphasize are preoperative CTA-guided identification of the principal feeder, back-table confirmation with a test flush, preservation of the short vascular stumps for reimplantation, proximal renal vein occlusion using a bulldog clamp and vessel loops to control the venous outflow during injection, slow low-pressure dye injection (0.2%, 10 mL), effluent collection, and post-mapping cold flush to clear the residual dye.

Post-resection arterial or submucosal methylene blue infiltration has been used to map lymphatic drainage and to stain segmental boundaries (ie, hepatic segment demarcation in hilar cholangiocarcinoma and ex vivo arterial infiltration to improve lymph node harvest in colorectal cancer). Studies have reported improved identification rates, higher lymph node yields, or clearer anatomic planes, indicating that methylene blue can make otherwise occult structures visible on the back table.9,10

To the best of our knowledge, there are limited prior reports specifically describing the selective methylene blue mapping of renal AVMs during ex vivo repair. The technical adjunct provided in this paper represents a novel application of an established mapping principle.

The key technical considerations include the careful control of venous outflow during injection to limit systemic dye dissemination, conservative dosing of methylene blue, and awareness of potential adverse effects (serotonin syndrome in patients on serotonergic agents; methemoglobinemia at high doses). We recommend preoperative medication review to exclude serotonergic agents, conservative dosing (as used for the current case), and immediate availability of measures to manage methemoglobinemia, if it occurs.

Alternative strategies (including endovascular embolization, in situ excision with venous bypass, or nephrectomy) remain appropriate in other contexts. Ex vivo repair is best reserved for lesions where parenchymal salvage is feasible and surgical expertise is available.

Limitations

This report describes a single case with short-term follow-up. Larger case series and longer follow-up are required to confirm the reproducibility, safety, and long-term renal outcomes of the technical adjunct. Nevertheless, the described adjunct may expand organ-sparing options for selected patients with complex renal vascular lesions.

Conclusions

Selective methylene blue mapping is a practical adjunct to ex vivo parenchyma-sparing resection of complex renal AVMs and associated venous aneurysms. In selected patients, this technique can facilitate organ preservation and avoid nephrectomy. Further case series and longer follow-up are warranted to define its reproducibility and long-term outcomes.
