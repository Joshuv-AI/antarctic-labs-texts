Spatial distribution and factors associated with HIV testing among adolescent girls and young women in Sierra Leone
Authors: Augustus Osborne, Camilla Bangura, Samuel Maxwell Tom Williams, Alusine H. Koroma, Lovel Fornah, Regina M. Yillah, Bright Opoku Ahinkorah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSierra Leone faces a significant challenge in addressing HIV/AIDS, particularly among adolescent girls and young women. This age group is considered highly vulnerable due to biological factors and social inequalities. Understanding the prevalence of HIV testing in this demographic is crucial for designing effective prevention and treatment strategies. This study investigated the spatial distribution of HIV testing and its associated factors among adolescent girls and young women in Sierra Leone.MethodsData from the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey was used for the study. The sample comprised 6,062 adolescent girls and young women between the ages of 15 and 24. Spatial autocorrelation and Moran’s I statistic were employed to analyze the spatial distribution of HIV testing. An analysis utilising mixed-effect multilevel binary logistic regression was performed to determine the factors associated with HIV testing. The findings were presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and a 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsThe national prevalence of HIV testing among adolescents and young women in Sierra Leone was 42.1% [40.3,43.9]. Kailahun, Kambia, Tonkolil, some parts of the Western rural area, and Bonthe districts were found to be statistically significant hotspot for HIV testing. Whereas, Karene, Falaba, Bo, kenema, and some parts of Pujuhun were statistically cold spot districts. Adolescent girls and young women aged 20–24 [aOR = 1.63, 95% CI = 1.29, 2.07] had higher odds of HIV testing than those aged 15–19. Those with secondary/higher education [aOR = 1.87, 95% CI = 1.40, 2.51] had higher odds of HIV testing than those with no education. The odds of HIV testing was higher among adolescent girls and young women who use the Internet [aOR = 1.75, 95% CI = 1.32, 2.33] than those who did not use internet. Adolescent girls and young women with one [aOR = 16.56, 95% CI = 12.31, 22.29] and two or more parity [aOR = 16.37, 95% CI = 10.86, 24.68] had higher odds of HIV testing than those with no parity. The likelihood of HIV testing was higher among adolescent girls and young women who had sex below 18 [aOR = 4.54, 95% CI = 3.25, 6.34] and those who had sex at 18+ [aOR = 5.70, 95% CI = 3.84, 8.45] compared to those who had never had sex. Adolescent girls and young women who visited health facilities in the past 12 months [aOR = 1.82, 95% CI = 1.46, 2.26] had higher odds of HIV testing than those who did not.ConclusionDespite some positive trends, HIV testing rates among adolescent girls and young women in Sierra Leone remain moderate. Spatial autocorrelation analysis consistently revealed hotspots and cold spots for HIV testing, with Kailahun, Kambia, Tonkolil, some parts of the Western rural area, and Bonthe districts remaining persistent hotspots. Age, education, internet use, sexual history, parity, and healthcare access are significant factors influencing testing behaviour. To improve testing rates, the government and policymakers should prioritize educational campaigns, expand internet access, integrate HIV testing into routine healthcare, and address stigma associated with HIV.

Introduction

HIV testing is a critical process that involves assessing an individual’s HIV status through various diagnostic methods [1]. It serves as a gateway to essential healthcare services, enabling early detection and treatment of HIV, which can significantly improve health outcomes and reduce transmission rates [2]. The benefits of HIV testing extend beyond individual health; they encompass broader public health goals, including the reduction of stigma associated with the virus, increased awareness, and the promotion of safer sexual practices among communities [3]. Early diagnosis through testing allows for timely access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), which can lead to viral suppression, thereby enhancing the quality of life for those living with HIV and decreasing the likelihood of onward transmission [3].

Globally, the statistics surrounding HIV testing among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) are concerning [4]. According to the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), AGYW aged 15–24 years are disproportionately affected by HIV, with more than 60% of new infections occurring in this demographic [4]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the situation is particularly dire, with only 38% of AGYW aware of their HIV status [5]. In Sierra Leone, the prevalence of HIV testing among AGYW remains low, with recent surveys indicating that less than 25% of young women in this age group have ever been tested for HIV [6]. This statistic highlights the urgent need for targeted interventions to improve access to testing services in this vulnerable population.

Several factors influence the likelihood of HIV testing among AGYW. These include socio-economic status, educational attainment, cultural beliefs, and stigma surrounding HIV [7–14]. AGYW from lower socio-economic backgrounds often face barriers such as lack of access to healthcare facilities, financial constraints, and limited knowledge about HIV and testing services. Additionally, cultural norms and stigma can deter individuals from seeking testing, as fear of discrimination or social ostracization may outweigh the perceived benefits of knowing one’s HIV status [7–14]. Understanding these factors is crucial for designing effective interventions that can increase testing rates in this cohort of women.

In Sierra Leone, current policies and programmes aimed at increasing HIV testing among AGYW include the National HIV Strategic Plan and various community outreach initiatives [15]. However, challenges persist, such as inadequate healthcare infrastructure, insufficient funding, and a lack of trained healthcare personnel. Furthermore, the aftermath of the Ebola crisis has strained the health system, leading to decreased access to essential health services, including HIV testing [15]. Addressing these challenges requires a multi-faceted approach that involves strengthening healthcare systems, increasing community awareness, and fostering supportive environments for young women to seek testing.

Despite existing research in Sierra Leone [16–25], there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding the spatial distribution of HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone. This study aims to fill this gap by examining the spatial distribution of HIV testing and its associated factors in this population. The justification for this research lies in its potential to inform policymakers and healthcare providers about the specific barriers faced by AGYW in different regions, thereby enabling the development of targeted interventions. By contributing to the existing literature, this study will enhance understanding of the complexities surrounding HIV testing and ultimately support efforts to improve health outcomes for AGYW in Sierra Leone.

Methods

Study design and sampling methods

The 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Surveys (SLDHS) was used for this study. The SLDHS is a cross-sectional survey which gathers data on demographic, health, and nutritional factors among men and women of reproductive age and children. The 2019 SLDHS was conducted over four months (from May 2019 to August 2019) [26], using a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling design. Detailed information regarding the sampling technique can be found in the final SLDHS report [26]. This study included 6,062 AGYW aged 15-24 . It adhered to the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [27].

Variables

Outcome variable

The study’s outcome variable was HIV testing. In the SLDHS, women were asked the question, “Have you ever been tested for HIV/AIDS?”. The responses were “Yes” or “No”. These responses were binary-coded. In this study, a response of ‘Yes’ was assigned a value of ‘1’ if an AGYW reported ever tested for HIV, whereas a response of ‘No’ was assigned a code of ‘0’ if she had never tested.

Explanatory variables

This analysis comprised nineteen explanatory variables, which were chosen based on their availability in the dataset and insights from prior studies [7, 9–14]. The explanatory variables were respondent’s age, marital status, level of education, working status, the total number of children ever born, health insurance coverage, exposure to television, exposure to radio, exposure to the newspaper, exposure to the Internet, religion, age at first sex, multiple sexual partners, household size, sex of household head, wealth index, residence, and province. Furthermore, the variables were segregated into individual and contextual levels. Aside from the latter five variables, grouped as contextual (household and community level variables), the remaining were individual-level variables. Table 1 shows the coding scheme of the variables.

Spatial analysis

ArcGIS version 4.3.1 was used for spatial autocorrelation, detection, and the interpolation of HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone. The shapefiles for the analysis were downloaded from the Spatial Data Repository [28]. First, a spatial autocorrelation (Global Moran’ I) was carried out to determine if the HIV testing prevalence among AGYW was dispersed, clustered, or randomly distributed across districts in Sierra Leone. Moran’s I values range from − 1 to 1, with positive values indicating clustering, negative values indicating dispersion, and values close to zero indicating a random distribution of HIV prevalence [29]. The z-score and p-values were used to determine the statistical significance of the spatial autocorrelation. Next, we carried out Getis-Ord GI* to see how the spatial autocorrelation of HIV testing prevalence among AGYW varied across different districts in Sierra Leone. Getis-Ord GI* calculates the local spatial autocorrelation, highlighting hotspots and coldspots of HIV testing rates within the region [30, 31]. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in determining the presence of clustering or dispersion. Finally, the Empirical Bayesian Kriging (EBK) technique was used to interpolate HIV testing rates in areas with missing data, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the spatial distribution. EBK is particularly useful in smoothing out variations and predicting values in unsampled locations based on neighboring data points [32].

Other statistical analyses

The other statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).We determined the distribution of the explanatory variables across the outcome variable and used a Pearson chi-square test to show their associations. Next, we performed a multicollinearity test using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The results showed none of the variables under study were of substantial collinearity, with all variables below five. Using four models, a mixed-effect multilevel binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the factors associated with HIV testing. Model I, which did not include any explanatory variables, revealed the changes in HIV testing attributed to the clustering at the primary sampling units (PSU). In Model II, the individual level variables were included, while in Model III, the contextual level variables were included. Model IV included all the explanatory variables. The mixed-effect regression analysis yielded results that included both fixed effects and random effects. The fixed-effect results revealed the correlation between the explanatory factors and HIV testing. The results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The random effect results, however, indicated the variations in HIV testing. All four models used the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values to determine the variation. All the analyses were weighted, and the svyset command in Stata, which contains the sampling weights, one or more stages of clustered sampling, and stratification, was used to deal with the complex nature of the DHS dataset. The DHS dataset provides sampling weights that adjust for the probability of selection at each stage of the sampling process. By applying these weights, we ensured that our estimates reflect the population from which the sample was drawn, accounting for any disproportionate sampling of specific subgroups. This is particularly important in our study to accurately represent the experiences of vulnerable populations. The svyset command allowed us to account for the clustered nature of the sampling design. This means that individuals within the same cluster (e.g., geographic areas or communities) may be more similar to each other than to those in different clusters. By specifying the clustering variable in our analysis, we adjusted the standard errors to account for this intraclass correlation, leading to more reliable statistical inferences. The DHS employs a stratified sampling approach to ensure representation across various demographic and geographic groups. By incorporating the stratification variable into our svyset command, we maintained the integrity of the sample structure and ensured that our results are generalizable to the broader population.

Ethical consideration

Due to the dataset being publicly available, ethical clearance was not required for this work. Prior to using the dataset, we requested and received permission from the Monitoring and Evaluation to Assess and Use Results Demographic and Health Surveys (MEASURE DHS) to utilise the SLDHS. For further information on the ethical considerations and principles associated with the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and its use of data, please see the following website: https://dhsprogram.com/Methodology/Protecting-the-Privacy-of-DHS-Survey-Respondents.cfm.

Results

Background characteristics of AGYW in Sierra Leone

Table 1 shows the background characteristics of AGYW in Sierra Leone. The majority of the study population consisted primarily of those aged 15–19 (56.6%). A significant portion (65.4%) had achieved secondary or higher education, and the majority (68.2%) had never been married or in a union. Despite this educational attainment, economic opportunities seemed limited, with over half (53.4%) not working. Additionally, access to technology and healthcare was low, with 82.4% not using the Internet, 96.7% lacking health insurance and over half (54.1%) had not visited a health facility in the past year. While most (60.6%) had no children, the majority (60.6%) reported having sex before 18. Interestingly, a quarter (26.2%) belonged to the wealthiest wealth category, and over half (52.9%) resided in urban areas.

Bivariate results of the association between the explanatory variables and HIV testing

Table 2 presents the bivariable results of the prevalence and distribution of HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone. The national prevalence of HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone was 42.1% [40.3,43.9]. HIV testing was high among AGYW aged 20–24 (64.8%), those who had no education (47.2%), those who were working (48.5%), AGYW who were married/ cohabiting (66.8%), those who were using Internet (51.9%), and those with one child (77.4%). Again, a higher proportion of AGYW had sex at 18+ (56.2%), visited a health facility in the last 12 months (59.4%), had multiple sexual partners (55.0%), were in the richer wealth index (45.2%), lived in urban areas (42.3%) and in the Western province (45.4%). Reading newspapers or magazines, watching television, being covered by health insurance, sex of household head, wealth index, and place of residence were the explanatory variables not significantly associated with HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone at p < 0.05.

Factors associated with HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone

Table 3 presents results on the factors associated with HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone. AGYW aged 20–24 had higher odds of HIV testing (aOR = 1.63, 95% CI = 1.29, 2.07) compared to those aged 15–19. Those with secondary or higher education had significantly higher odds of HIV testing (aOR = 1.87, 95% CI = 1.40, 2.51) than individuals with no education. The odds of HIV testing was higher among AGYW who used the Internet (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI = 1.32, 2.33) compared to those who did not. AGYW with one (aOR = 16.56, 95% CI = 12.31, 22.29) and two or more children (aOR = 16.37, 95% CI = 10.86, 24.68) showed a higher odd of HIV testing compared to those with no children. The likelihood of HIV testing was higher among AGYW who had sexual intercourse before age 18 (aOR = 4.54, 95% CI = 3.25, 6.34) and those who had sex at 18 or older (aOR = 5.70, 95% CI = 3.84, 8.45) compared to those who had not engaged in sexual activity. Discussion

Our study examined the spatial distribution of HIV testing and the associated factors among AGYW in Sierra Leone. The analysis identified significant hotspots and coldspots in the country, highlighting critical areas for targeted interventions. Specifically, Kailahun, Kambia, Tonkolili, certain parts of the Western rural area, and Bonthe districts emerged as statistically significant hot spot districts, indicating higher rates of HIV testing. In contrast, Karene, Falaba, Bo, Kenema, and some areas of Pujehun were identified as statistically cold spot districts, reflecting lower levels of testing. Factors associated with HIV testing included age, education, internet use, sexual history, parity and healthcare access.

The study found that the prevalence of HIV testing among AGYW in Sierra Leone was 42.1%, which is higher than the 33% reported among young adults in Bo, Sierra Leone [23]. Several contextual factors could explain this finding. For instance, testing facilities are often concentrated in urban areas, which can limit access for rural AGYW who face challenges related to distance and transportation [33]. Additionally, clinic hours may not align with the schedules of AGYW, many of whom are engaged in school or work, potentially hindering their ability to seek testing. User fees associated with testing could also serve as a barrier, particularly for those from low-income backgrounds [33]. Furthermore, fear of stigma and discrimination related to HIV may deter AGYW from accessing testing services [34]. Limited knowledge about HIV transmission, the benefits of testing, and available services can further complicate their willingness to seek care [35]. Additionally, power imbalances and restricted decision-making autonomy for AGYW might impede their ability to access healthcare, including HIV testing [36]. While these factors are suggested based on existing literature, there is a need for more comprehensive research to validate these claims and explore the specific policies and socio-cultural dynamics affecting the spatial distribution of HIV testing in Sierra Leone. This presents a gap that future studies could address, providing a clearer understanding of the barriers AGYW face in accessing HIV testing services.

The findings of our study highlight significant disparities in HIV testing rates among AGYW across various districts in Sierra Leone. Specifically, districts such as Kailahun, Kambia, Tonkolili, certain areas of the Western Rural, and Bonthe have emerged as statistically significant hotspots for HIV testing. This suggests that these districts may have more effective outreach programmes, better access to healthcare facilities, or greater community awareness regarding the importance of HIV testing. The presence of hotspots in these districts could be attributed to several factors. For instance, targeted interventions, such as educational campaigns and mobile testing units, may have been more prevalent in these areas [21]. Additionally, local cultural attitudes towards sexual health and HIV may be more progressive, encouraging higher participation in testing. Understanding the specific strategies employed in these hotspots could provide valuable insights for scaling up testing efforts in other districts. Conversely, the identification of Karene, Falaba, Bo, Kenema, and certain parts of Pujehun as statistically cold spot districts raises concerns about the accessibility and acceptance of HIV testing in these areas. The lower rates of testing among AGYW may indicate a lack of resources, insufficient healthcare infrastructure, or cultural stigmas that discourage individuals from seeking testing [23, 25]. Addressing these barriers is crucial for improving health outcomes in these districts. Moreover, the cold spots may reflect broader socio-economic challenges. Areas with fewer healthcare facilities or limited transportation options may hinder access to HIV testing services. It is essential to conduct further qualitative research to understand the specific barriers faced by AGYW in these provinces. Engaging with community leaders and stakeholders can help identify culturally appropriate strategies to increase awareness and acceptance of HIV testing.

AGYW aged 20–24 in Sierra Leone had higher odds of getting tested for HIV compared to those aged 15–19. Our finding aligns with the previous study in Ghana [14]. By the age of 20–24, AGYW are more likely to be sexually active compared to 15–19 year olds [37]. This heightened awareness of potential risk factors could lead them to seek out HIV testing. This age group might be entering new relationships or starting families, which can motivate them to get tested for their health and the health of potential partners or future children [38]. By 20–24, AGYW might have more independence and control over their time and finances than teenagers. This allows them greater freedom to access testing services without relying on parental permission or support. Older AGYW might have had more opportunities for education and exposure to HIV prevention messages, leading to a better understanding of the importance of testing [39].

We found that AGYW in Sierra Leone with secondary/higher education had higher odds of getting tested for HIV compared to those with no education. Our finding aligns with previous studies in Ghana [14], Zambia [40], Eastern Africa [41], and Tanzania [42]. Secondary and higher education often include sexual health education, potentially increasing knowledge about HIV transmission, prevention methods, and the importance of testing [43]. Educational institutions might provide access to educational materials, workshops, or guest speakers on HIV/AIDS, raising awareness and prompting AGYW to get tested [44]. Secondary schools and higher education institutions are often concentrated in urban areas with better access to healthcare facilities offering HIV testing services.

Our findings showed that AGYW in Sierra Leone who use the Internet had higher odds of getting tested for HIV compared to those who did not. The Internet can provide access to a wealth of information about HIV/AIDS, including transmission, prevention, and testing options. AGYW who use the Internet might be more likely to encounter educational campaigns or resources promoting HIV testing [45]. Online forums, social media groups, or health websites can connect AGYW with others who share concerns about sexual health. These networks can provide support and information about HIV testing.

In Sierra Leone, there were significantly higher odds of HIV testing among AGYW with at least one child compared to those with no children. Our finding aligns with a previous study in Ghana [14]. AGYW who have already given birth are likely to have more frequent contact with the healthcare system for prenatal care, postnatal checkups, or child health visits [46]. This increased interaction with healthcare providers could present opportunities for HIV testing and counselling. National programs or clinic initiatives might integrate routine HIV testing into existing antenatal or postnatal care services [47]. This would ensure easy access to testing for childbearing AGYW.

We found that AGYW who ever had sex, regardless of age at first intercourse, had significantly higher odds of HIV testing compared to those who had not had sex. Our finding aligns with previous studies in Ghana [14], Nigeria [48], and Rwanda [10]. Engaging in sexual activity at any age increases the risk of contracting HIV [49]. This heightened awareness of potential danger could motivate AGYW to get tested to know their status. Sexually active AGYW might be more likely to experience symptoms of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [50, 51]. Seeking treatment for STIs could lead to HIV testing as part of the diagnostic process. AGYW who are sexually active might be more likely to access reproductive health services, including clinics or outreach programmes [52]. These services often promote and offer HIV testing.

AGYW in Sierra Leone who visited health facilities in the past 12 months had higher odds of getting tested for HIV compared to those who did not. Health facilities might offer integrated services where HIV testing is routinely incorporated into general checkups, STI screenings, or other consultations [53]. This gives AGYW convenient access to testing without needing to seek it out specifically. AGYW visiting healthcare facilities for different reasons might be referred for HIV testing based on their symptoms or concerns. This opportunistic testing approach can identify individuals who might not have considered getting tested otherwise. Clinics and health facilities might display educational materials or conduct brief information sessions promoting HIV testing and its importance [54]. This exposure can raise awareness and encourage AGYW to get tested.

Policy and practice implications

The study’s findings highlight critical areas for policy intervention in Sierra Leone to enhance HIV testing rates among AGYW. Public health initiatives should prioritize coldspot areas such as Karene, Falaba, Bo, kenema, and some parts of Pujuhun. Strategies could include mobile testing units, community-based testing programs, and partnerships with local organizations to enhance reach and accessibility. Increasing educational efforts and health literacy programmes, particularly in rural and underserved areas, could improve testing rates. Given that the prevalence of HIV testing is moderate, targeted strategies are essential to address the barriers identified in the research. First, the implementation of school-based testing programs, with options for parental consent, could significantly increase testing rates among AGYW. This approach could leverage the existing school infrastructure to provide accessible services, ensuring that students can receive testing in a familiar environment, thereby reducing stigma and increasing uptake. Second, developing youth-friendly testing clinics with extended hours or mobile testing units can address the accessibility issues faced by AGYW, particularly those in rural areas. By offering services that align with the schedules of AGYW who may be balancing school or work commitments, these clinics can facilitate greater access and convenience, encouraging most of them to seek testing. Integrating comprehensive sexual health education into the national curriculum at all levels is another crucial recommendation. This education would empower AGYW with knowledge about HIV transmission, the benefits of testing, and available resources, ultimately fostering a more informed and proactive approach to their sexual health. Moreover, creating mobile-friendly HIV information and testing resources can help reach AGYW who may lack access to traditional information channels. With a significant portion of the youth population utilizing mobile devices, these resources can provide timely and relevant information, encouraging more young women to pursue testing. Integrating HIV testing into standard procedures for all AGYW visiting healthcare facilities, regardless of the reason for their visit, can normalize the testing process. This policy would ensure that testing becomes a routine part of healthcare interactions, reducing stigma and promoting early diagnosis. Targeted outreach programs focusing on AGYW who are less likely to get tested—such as those without internet access or lower educational attainment—can further bridge the gap in testing rates. By understanding the specific barriers faced by these groups, healthcare providers can tailor their approaches to effectively engage and support these young women. Finally, training healthcare workers to reduce stigma around HIV testing is essential for creating a welcoming environment. This training should include strategies for encouraging responsible parental involvement in adolescent sexual health, which can enhance support for AGYW seeking testing services. By implementing these policy and practice changes, grounded in the study’s findings, Sierra Leone can work towards achieving higher HIV testing rates among AGYW. This, in turn, will facilitate earlier diagnosis, improve access to treatment, and ultimately contribute to a decrease in HIV transmission within the community.

Strengths and limitations

The 2019 SLDHS offers several strengths for this study. The survey uses a multistage sampling design, making its data representative of the entire country. This allows for broader generalisations about AGYW in Sierra Leone. The SLDHS follows a standardised approach across countries, allowing comparisons with other national surveys and regional trends. It collects data on various demographics, sexual behaviour, healthcare access, and HIV testing history. This allows for a comprehensive analysis of factors associated with testing rates. While valuable, the SLDHS also has limitations to consider. While our analysis identifies significant associations between various factors (such as age, education, internet usage, parity, sexual activity, and health facility visits) and HIV testing rates, these relationships should not be interpreted as causal. Again, participants may provide responses that they believe are more socially acceptable or favorable rather than their true behaviors or experiences due to the self-reported nature of the survey. In the context of HIV testing and sexual history, individuals may underreport risky behaviors or overreport testing practices due to the stigma associated with HIV/AIDS and sexual health issues. Again, participants may have difficulty accurately recalling past events, particularly when it comes to sensitive topics such as sexual activity or health-seeking behaviors. This can result in inaccuracies in reporting the timing and frequency of HIV testing or sexual encounters, which may affect the reliability of our findings. For example, individuals may forget instances of testing or misremember the circumstances surrounding their sexual history.

Conclusion

Based on the findings, the study concludes that HIV testing rates AGYW in Sierra Leone are still moderate despite some positive trends. Kailahun, Kambia, Tonkolil, some parts of the Western rural area, and Bonthe districts were found to be statistically significant hotspot districts. Whereas, Karene, Falaba, Bo, kenema, and some parts of Pujuhun are statistically cold spot districts. Factors associated with increased odds of getting tested include age, education, internet use, sexual history, parity and healthcare access. By addressing these factors, Sierra Leone can increase HIV testing rates among AGYW, which is crucial for early diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of further transmission. The government and policymakers in Sierra Leone should develop programmes to encourage and facilitate testing among AGYW. Such programmes would increase awareness about HIV testing and its importance through educational campaigns. The programmes could also expand internet access and utilise online platforms for HIV information and testing resources. Making HIV testing a routine part of healthcare visits for AGYW could also be implemented. The government and other non-governmental organisations should also provide access to comprehensive sexual health education and services that include HIV testing.
