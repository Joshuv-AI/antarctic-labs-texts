Knee dressing selection and infection rates in primary total knee arthroplasty: a single-center retrospective review
Authors: Morgan Pierce, Jewel Stone, Devon Pekas, Chad Carlson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough complication rates following primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are low, prosthetic joint infection can be catastrophic. To mitigate this risk, various postoperative wound dressings are utilized. Anecdotal clinical observations at The Bone and Joint Center suggested increased rates of postoperative skin irritation with a topical skin adhesive dressing compared with a silver hydrofiber dressing. The aim of this study was to compare postoperative skin irritation and infection outcomes between these two dressing types following primary TKA.MethodsA retrospective review was performed of 837 patients who underwent primary TKA between January 1, 2020, and April 30, 2023. Patients received either a silver hydrofiber dressing (Aquacel®, ConvaTec, Bridgewater, NJ, USA) or a topical skin adhesive dressing (LiquiBand®, Advanced Medical Solutions, Plymouth, Devon, UK). A manual chart review was conducted to collect demographic information, perioperative variables, and number of postoperative skin irritations, superficial skin infections, and deep periprosthetic joint infections.ResultsA total of 837 patients were included, 303 of whom received the silver hydrofiber dressing, and 534 received the topical skin adhesive dressing. Thirty-eight patients developed postoperative infections. There was no statistically significant difference between dressing groups in total infection rates (P=0.14), superficial skin infections (P=0.08), nor deep periprosthetic joint infections (P=0.27). Skin irritation occurred less frequently in the silver hydrofiber group (n=1; 0.33%) compared with the topical skin adhesive group (n=26; 4.87%) (P<0.001).ConclusionsFollowing primary TKA, there were no significant differences in total, superficial or deep periprosthetic joint infection rates between a silver hydrofiber dressing and a topical skin adhesive dressing. However, the topical skin adhesive dressing was associated with a significantly higher rate of postoperative skin irritation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA), there were no significant differences in total, superficial, or deep prosthetic joint infection rates between patients treated with a silver hydrofiber dressing (Aquacel®) and those treated with a topical skin adhesive dressing (LiquiBand®).

• The topical skin adhesive dressing was associated with a significantly higher rate of postoperative skin irritation compared with the silver hydrofiber dressing (4.87% vs. 0.33%, P<0.001).

What is known and what is new?

• Postoperative wound management is critical in TKA to minimize the risk of prosthetic joint infection, and a variety of dressings, including hydrofiber and topical skin adhesives, are commonly used. Anecdotal reports suggest that some dressings may be associated with higher rates of skin irritation.

• This study provides evidence from a large retrospective cohort that while infection rates are similar between silver hydrofiber and topical skin adhesive dressings, the latter is associated with a higher incidence of skin irritation, highlighting a safety consideration that has not been systematically quantified before.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should consider the risk of skin irritation when selecting postoperative dressings for TKA, especially for patients with sensitive skin or prior dermatologic issues.

• Future protocols may prioritize dressings that balance infection prevention with skin tolerance to improve overall patient outcomes and comfort.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains one of the most prevalent orthopaedic procedures in the United States, with projections indicating a rise to 1.28 million procedures by 2030 (1). Despite its overall success, postoperative complications remain a significant concern, most notably periprosthetic infection, which is estimated at 1% for primary arthroplasty and up to 10–25% for first-stage or second-stage revisions (2,3). Periprosthetic joint infections have been found to extend hospital stays, increase mortality, necessitate revision surgeries, and create a substantial financial burden to the patient and healthcare system (4-8). For example, surgical site infections (SSIs) following TKA are estimated to cost 8 times more than those without infection (4).

There are many risk factors for periprosthetic joint infection including patient comorbidities, operation duration, non-operative wounds, and sterilization techniques (9). While studies have expanded on many of these variables, the topic of wound dressings following TKA continues to have sparse representation in literature. This area is particularly relevant, as most SSIs occur within 3 months of TKA and are mainly due to staphylococcal species, highlighting the importance of wound closure and postoperative dressings (10).

Following TKA, wound closure can be performed with a multitude of products and techniques (11). Many factors contribute to infection rates, including the material and type of surgical dressing used to close the wound (12-15). Two commonly used dressings include topical skin adhesive dressings and silver-impregnated hydrofiber dressings.

Topical skin adhesive dressings use cyanoacrylate-based adhesives as an alternative to suture or staples. These adhesives provide high tensile strength and demonstrate antimicrobial properties against multiple pathogenic fungi and bacteria species (16). Once polymerized, it provides a barrier against contamination while allowing for innate wound healing to occur, providing an additional protective barrier (17). Silver-impregnated hydrofiber dressings function as cover dressings that transform into a gel upon contact with wound exudate. These dressings protect the wound, reduce maceration, and inhibit bacterial penetration (18-21). Anecdotally, while using these dressings, The Bone and Joint Center has observed allergic-type skin reactions associated with topical skin adhesive dressings, including blistering and dermatitis. These reactions may represent a potential nidus for postoperative infections but, unfortunately, there is currently no head-to-head literature comparing dressings available. The purpose of this study was to investigate whether differences exist in skin irritation and postoperative infection rates following primary TKA between topical skin adhesive and silver-impregnated hydrofiber dressings. We hypothesized that skin irritation and postoperative infection rates would differ between the two dressing types. We present this article in accordance with the TREND reporting checklist (available at https://aoj.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/aoj-2026-1-0005/rc).

Methods

Following approval from the Division of Research & Economic Development, Office of Research Compliance & Ethics (protocol No. IRB0006864) at the University of North Dakota, a retrospective review via electronic medical records was conducted of all patients who underwent primary TKA performed by two orthopaedic surgeons at a single institution between January 1st, 2020 and April 30th, 2023. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Inclusion criteria were: (I) primary TKA (Current Procedural Terminology 27447); (II) use of either silver-impregnated hydrofiber dressing (Aquacel®, ConvaTec, Bridgewater, NJ, USA) (Figure 1) or a topical skin adhesive dressing (LiquiBand®, Advanced Medical Solutions, Plymouth, Devon, UK) (Figure 2) for wound closure; and (III) age ≥18 years. Exclusion criteria included: (I) non-primary TKA; (II) noncompliance with antibiotic usage; (III) patients with allergies to cefazolin; (IV) incomplete medical records or lack of follow-up; and (V) was deemed a vulnerable adult.

A total of 837 total patients met inclusion criteria. One surgeon exclusively utilized the silver-impregnated hydrofiber dressing (n=303), while the other surgeon utilized the topical skin adhesive dressing (n=534) (Figure 3). Wound closure and postoperative protocols were standardized across both surgeons. All cases utilized layered closure with #1 Vicryl for the arthrotomy, 2-0 Monocryl for the subcutaneous layer, and 2-0 Stratafix for skin closure. Postoperatively, patients were made weight-bearing as tolerated, maintained their dressing for 2 weeks, and returned for follow-up at 6 weeks. Patients were permitted to shower with the dressing in place. Skin irritation was defined as the presence of rashes or blistering occurring directly under or adjacent to the dressing. Superficial skin infection was defined in accordance with the Center for Disease Control and Prevention criteria as an infection occurring at or near the surgical site within 30 days of surgery, or within 90 days when an implant is involved (22). Therefore, patients who received antibiotics within 90 days of their index TKA for infection occurring at or near the surgical site with at least one of the following: purulent drainage, positive cultures, or symptoms of localized swelling or erythema were classified as a superficial skin infection. Antibiotic prescriptions issued more than 90 days after surgery or prescribed solely for a stitch abscess were not counted for superficial skin infection. Deep periprosthetic infection was defined in accordance with the Musculoskeletal Infection Society Criteria, including the presence of a sinus tract communicating with the prosthesis, two positive periprosthetic cultures, or a combination of minor criteria such as elevated inflammatory markers, synovial fluid leukocyte count or neutrophil percentage, purulence around the prosthesis, a single positive culture, or positive histologic analysis of periprosthetic tissue and for the purposes of this study, only infections requiring return to the operating room for surgical management were classified as deep periprosthetic joint infection. Total prosthetic joint infection was defined as any postoperative joint infection, including infections requiring reoperation in accordance with our definition of deep periprosthetic joint infection and/or infections treated with antibiotic therapy consistent with our definition of superficial skin infection. Infections initially classified as superficial that subsequently progressed to deep periprosthetic joint infection were counted only once for total postoperative infections.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics were summarized using descriptive statistics. To identify significant differences between groups, Student’s t-tests were performed for continuous variables and Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Multivariable regression models were formulated with age, body mass index (BMI), sex, diabetes, A1C, chewing tobacco, cigars, smoking tobacco, procedure length, and length of stay to determine whether receiving a hydrofiber dressing was an independent predictor for SSI. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P<0.05. Analyses were performed using SAS software V9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 837 patients met inclusion/exclusion criteria, with 303 in the silver hydrofiber dressing group and 534 in the topical skin adhesive dressing group. Among demographic characteristics and comorbidities, there were no significant differences between cohorts including female sex (62.1% vs. 61.8%; P=0.94), chewing tobacco usage (5.9% vs. 5.4%; P=0.76), cigar usage (1.3% vs. 0.9%; P=0.73), smoking tobacco usage (28.4% vs. 31.1%; P=0.41), or length of stay (0.99±0.92 vs. 0.99±0.86 days; P=0.80) (Table 1). The silver hydrofiber dressing group had a higher BMI (34.2±6.1 vs. 31.6±5.7 kg/m2; P<0.001), younger age (67.1±8.3 vs. 70.9±7.8 years; P<0.001), and a greater proportion of patients with diabetes (23.4% vs. 17.0%; P=0.02), and shorter procedure length (48.5±8.0 vs. 59.4±13.2 min; P<0.001) than the topical skin adhesive group (Table 1).

Discussion

In this retrospective review, we analyzed the incidence of postoperative infections and skin irritations in primary TKA patients treated with a silver hydrofiber dressing compared with a topical skin adhesive dressing. We found that while the postoperative infection rate did not differ between the dressing types, the topical skin adhesive dressing was associated with a significantly higher rate of skin irritation than the silver hydrofiber dressing.

Skin irritations differed significantly between groups, with only 0.33% of patients in the silver hydrofiber dressing group experiencing irritation compared to 4.87% in the topical skin adhesive dressing group (P<0.001). This observation is consistent with prior reports of postoperative contact dermatitis associated with topical skin adhesives. Ricciardo et al. described a case series of patients who developed skin irritation following the use of cyanoacrylate-based adhesive dressings such as Prineo (LiquiBand), in some cases requiring removal of the dressing and symptomatic management (23). These results suggest a potential advantage of the silver hydrofiber dressing in terms of skin tolerance and may be particularly relevant for patients prone to skin sensitivities or allergic-type reactions. However, although the absolute number of events in our cohort was relatively low, dressing-related skin reactions may still be clinically relevant as they can lead to patient discomfort and may require treatment with topical corticosteroids or antihistamines, as described in prior studies evaluating postoperative contact dermatitis associated with surgical adhesives (24). Future studies should further characterize the severity and management of these reactions to better understand their clinical impact.

In addition to differences in skin tolerance, cost considerations may influence dressing selection for TKA. At the time of this study, the silver hydrofiber dressing was approximately $40 per unit, whereas the topical skin adhesive dressing was approximately $79 per unit (25). Although no formal cost analysis was performed, this descriptive cost difference may be relevant for institutions when considering postoperative dressing selection.

In summary, while no significant difference in postoperative infection rates was observed between dressing types following primary TKA, the significantly lower incidence of skin irritations with the silver hydrofiber dressing suggests a potential advantage in terms of patient comfort and tolerance. Taken together, lower cost and reduced skin irritation may make the silver hydrofiber dressing a more desirable option, while not demonstrating an observed increase in postoperative infection rates in this cohort. However, given the relatively small number of infections and differences in baseline patient characteristics between groups, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further research is warranted to validate these findings and explore additional factors that may influence dressing selection and clinical outcomes in TKA. Future considerations should investigate if postoperative wound irritations due to the dressings increase postoperative clinic visits. Additionally, accessing the frequency of diphenhydramine or other antihistamine use for dressing-related reactions may provide further insight into patient tolerance and satisfaction.

Strengths

Strengths of our study include its generalizability and the involvement of two surgeons at a single institution, which helped maintain consistency in surgical technique and perioperative protocols. Additionally, the manual chart review enhanced accuracy of identifying postoperative infections and skin irritation. Multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that BMI, age, diabetes, and procedure length were the primary variables differing between dressing groups, allowing for a more controlled comparison of outcomes. However, despite adjustment for measured variables, multivariable analysis cannot fully account for unmeasured surgeon-specific factors such as differences in operative technique, patient selection, or perioperative management that may influence outcomes.

Limitations

Limitations of the study include: (I) its retrospective design; (II) the inclusion of only two surgeons, each exclusively using a single dressing type; and (III) a limited sample size. Because each surgeon consistently used only one dressing type, the study design introduces the potential for confounding by surgeon-specific factors. As a result, differences in outcomes may reflect variations in surgical technique, patient selection, or perioperative protocols rather than the dressing itself. Future studies would benefit from evaluating outcomes when a single surgeon utilizes both dressing types to reduce potential surgeon-related confounding. Alternatively, randomized controlled trials would better help isolate the independent effect of dressing type on postoperative outcomes. Additionally, other wound-related outcomes such as postoperative drainage, delayed wound healing, or non-infectious wound reoperations were not systematically evaluated in this study and may warrant further investigation in future analyses.

Although 38 infections were identified across the cohort, prosthetic joint infection is a relatively rare outcome. The study may therefore be underpowered to detect small but clinically meaningful differences in infection rates between dressing types. Thus, the absence of statistically significant difference should be interpreted with caution, as the possibility of a type II error cannot be excluded. Larger prospective studies would be needed to more definitively determine whether dressing type influences postoperative infection risk following primary TKA.

Other limitations include differences between groups in BMI, age, diabetes prevalence, and procedure length. These baseline differences complicate the interpretation of infection-related outcomes. The silver hydrofiber group had higher BMI and a greater prevalence of diabetes, both established risk factors for postoperative infection, whereas the topical skin adhesive group was older and had longer procedure times, which may also increase postoperative infection risk. Because these risk factors were unevenly distributed between groups, their influence on infection outcomes is complex and cannot be assumed to offset one another. Although multivariable regression was used to adjust for measured confounders, residual confounding may remain and may have influenced the observed infection rates between groups.

Conclusions

Our findings did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in total, superficial, or deep periprosthetic joint infections between the silver hydrofiber dressing and the topical skin adhesive dressing following primary TKA. However, given the retrospective design, baseline differences between groups, surgeon-dependent dressing allocation, and the relatively small number of infection events, these findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered evidence of equivalence between dressing types with respect to infection risk. The topical skin adhesive dressing was associated with a higher incidence of skin irritation compared with the silver hydrofiber dressing. These findings suggest skin tolerance may be an important consideration when selecting wound dressings for patients undergoing primary TKA. Further prospective studies, including randomized designs, are needed to better isolate the independent effect of dressing choice on postoperative outcomes.
