Long-term survival following anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy in advanced urothelial cancer and an assessment of potential prognostic clinical factors: a multicentre observational study
Authors: Chantal F Stockem, Sarah M H Einerhand, Isabel Miras Rodríguez, Youssra Salhi, Esther Pérez, Dimitra R Bakaloudi, Rafee Talukder, Belen Caramelo, Rafael Morales-Barrera, Astrid De Meulenaere, Alessandro Rametta, Andrea Bottelli, Felix Lefort, Patrizia Giannatempo, Christof Vulsteke, Joan Carles, Ignacio Duran, Petros Grivas, Alfonso Gómez de Liaño, Debbie G J Robbrecht, Begoña P Valderrama, Vincent van der Noort, Michiel S van der Heijden
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnti-PD-(L)1 agent are approved as first- and second-line treatment options in advanced urothelial cancer (UC), but information about long-term survival is scarce. There is a need for prognostic factors, as these may help in the decision-making concerning anti-PD-(L)1 in patients with UC. Here, we examined long-term survival following anti-PD-(L)1 in advanced UC and assessed clinical factors for their correlation with survival.MethodsWe collected data from patients with advanced UC treated with anti-PD-(L)1 between 2013 and 2023. Overall- and progression-free survival (OS, PFS) were determined using the Kaplan-Meier method. Independent variables were analysed by uni- and multivariate Cox regression for their association with OS and PFS.ResultsSurvival analyses included 552 patients. Patient characteristics in our cohort were consistent with those of a typical advanced UC population. After median follow-up of 49 months, five-year OS and PFS rates were 16.0% and 6.9% respectively. The absence of visceral and/or bone metastases and elevated C-reactive protein level, gamma-glutamyltransferase level and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio were identified as favourable prognostic factors for OS.ConclusionsA selected subset of patients with advanced UC may experience long-term clinical benefit from anti-PD-(L)1 treatment. We identified prognostic factors that might be used for risk assessment and clinical trial stratification.

Introduction

Survival rates for patients with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic urothelial cancer (UC) are poor, with only between 10 and 15% patients alive at five years [1, 2]. Since the 1970s, the standard frontline treatment for patients with advanced UC has been platinum-based chemotherapy. However, responses to chemotherapy are rarely durable [1, 3]. To date, there are well-known prognostic factors for survival with chemotherapy [4, 5]. In the last decade, agents targeting inhibitory immune checkpoints have shown benefits for the treatment of a range of cancers. By blocking either programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) on T lymphocytes or the PD-1 ligand (PD-L1) on tumour cells, tumour infiltrating T lymphocytes are reinvigorated thereby enhancing an anti-tumour response. The first single-arm studies showing responses with PD-(L)1 blockade in advanced platinum-refractory UC started more than a decade ago [6, 7]. Subsequently, in the phase 3 KEYNOTE 045 trial, second-line pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) was studied [8] and showed overall survival (OS) benefit versus (vs) salvage chemotherapy (paclitaxel, docetaxel or vinflunine; median OS 10.3 vs 7.4 months; hazard ratio (HR) 0.73; p = 0.002) [8]. In the IMvigor 211 study, second-line atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) resulted in a median OS of 8.6 vs 8 months with chemotherapy in the intention-to-treat population (ITT; HR 0.85) [9]. Durable responses occurred more frequently with atezolizumab [9]. Frontline atezolizumab was initially studied in the IMvigor 210 study cohort 1 showing a promising response rate [10], supporting the randomized phase 3 IMvigor 130 trial [11], which demonstrated a median OS of 15.7 with atezolizumab vs 13.1 months for platinum-based chemotherapy in the ITT (HR 1.02) [11]. Pembrolizumab was studied in first-line advanced UC in the phase 2 KEYNOTE 052 trial [6] and the phase 3 KEYNOTE 361 [12]. With a median OS of 15.6 and 16.1 months following pembrolizumab vs 14.3 and 15.2 months following platinum-based chemotherapy in the ITT (HR 0.92) and the PD-L1 positive population (HR 1.01) respectively, no OS benefit was observed with pembrolizumab [12]. In the phase 3 JAVELIN Bladder 100 trial, patients with at least stable disease after 4–6 cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy were randomized to receive switch maintenance avelumab plus best supportive care or best supportive care alone [13]. The trial met its primary endpoint, as avelumab maintenance showed a significant OS benefit compared to best supportive care (HR 0.69; p = 0.001) [13], which was confirmed with longer follow-up [14]. Recently, the combination of frontline pembrolizumab and enfortumab vedotin, an antibody-drug conjugate against nectin-4, and the combination of nivolumab with cisplatin/gemcitabine as first-line therapy obtained FDA approvals based on phase 3 trials demonstrating a survival benefit of 15.4 (HR 0.47; HR < 0.01) [15] and 2.8 months (HR 0.78; p = 0.02) [16] vs platinum-based chemotherapy, respectively [16]. Although several clinical trials evaluated anti-PD-(L)1 efficacy in advanced UC, long-term survival outcomes are scarce, mainly because clinical trials were closed before long-term survival could be evaluated formally. The estimated OS rate at 48 months following pembrolizumab was 19% overall as frontline treatment and 16.6% as second-line therapy [17]. The OS rate at 30 months with second-line atezolizumab was 18% in the ITT [18]. Since we lack knowledge about the long-term survival of patients who are progression-free at one or two years of treatment, it is challenging to accurately advise these patients on their actual prognosis and to optimize the follow-up surveillance schedule. In addition, the knowledge about commonly available clinical variables and their association with survival is limited [19]. A better understanding of putative prognostic factors could not only help appropriately advise patients but could also help on decisions on starting and/or stopping anti-PD-(L)1. Readily and easily available clinical parameters, such as standard-of-care laboratory tests, could potentially serve as prognostic indicators for long-term clinical outcomes. Elevated pre-therapeutic gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) levels for example showed prognostic potential in various malignancies [20–22]. In this study, we aimed to determine long-term survival outcomes in patients with advanced UC treated with anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy in different settings. Furthermore, we explored easily accessible clinical factors and their association with survival. We hypothesized that absence of visceral metastasis would be associated with longer survival.

Methods

Study design

This was a multicentre, observational cohort study, for which information was collected on adults with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic UC treated with ≥ one dose of anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 monotherapy as first- or later line/salvage treatment between 2013 and 2023 at the following institutions: The Netherlands Cancer Institute (Amsterdam, The Netherlands (NL)), Erasmus Medical Centre (Rotterdam, NL), Fred Hutchinson Cancer Centre (Seattle, Washington, United States), Integrated Cancer Centre AZ Maria Middelares (Gent, Belgium), Virgen del Rocío University Hospital (Sevilla, Spain), Vall d’Hebron University Hospital (Barcelona, Spain), Complejo Hospitalario Universitario Insular-Materno Infantil (Las Palmas, Spain), Marqués de Valdecilla University Hospital (Santander, Cantabria, Spain), Hôspital Saint André—CHU de Bordeaux (Bordeaux, France) and Fondazione IRCCS Istituto Nazionale dei Tumori (Milan, Italy). This study was reviewed and approved by the NKI-AVL Institutional Review Board (IRBd22-147). We retrospectively used patient data according to national guidelines and legal requirements. As patients did not enter a clinical study nor underwent study procedures, informed consent was not required. Patients with urothelial carcinoma of any urinary tract origin were eligible for inclusion and both pure UC and histology subtypes/variants were allowed. Included patients were treated with atezolizumab, durvalumab, nivolumab or pembrolizumab. If patients were treated with anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy in multiple lines, only the initial anti-PD-(L)1 treatment line was considered for further analysis. To eliminate the possibility of evaluating long-term chemotherapy effects, patients treated with avelumab switch maintenance therapy were excluded. Patients who received prior ICB in the peri-operative setting were excluded as well.

Objectives

The primary endpoint was to determine long-term OS and PFS. OS was defined as the time between the start of anti-PD-(L)1 until death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the time of analysis were censored at the date of their last follow-up (clinical visit or telephone consultation). Patients who were lost to follow-up were censored at the date they were last known to be alive. PFS was defined as the time from anti-PD-(L)1 initiation to cancer progression or death, whichever came first. Cancer progression was determined based on either radiological progression according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (version 1.1.) or a decline in clinical condition indicative of clinical progression. Patients without progression were censored at the date of their last radiologic evaluation. The secondary endpoint was to identify prognostic factors associated with clinical outcomes. The following variables were considered candidates to affect both PFS and OS: the presence of metastatic disease at initial diagnosis, the site of metastases (lymph nodes only vs visceral and/or bone metastases), cisplatin eligibility, treatment line (frontline vs second-line or higher), C-reactive protein level in mg/L before initiation of anti-PD-(L)1 (as a continuous variable), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level in IU/L before initiation of anti-PD-(L)1 (as a continuous variable), GGT level before initiation of anti-PD-(L)1 in U/L (as a continuous variable), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR; calculated by dividing the absolute neutrophil count by the absolute lymphocyte count; as a continuous variable), steroid use (prednisone ≥ 10 mg daily dose or equivalent) and antibiotic (AB) use around initiation of anti-PD-(L)1. For patients treated with steroids, we collected information on both the timing of steroid usage (within 14 days before anti-PD-(L)1 initiation, at start of anti-PD-(L)1 or within 30 days after anti-PD-(L)1 start) and whether steroid treatment was initiated due to anti-PD-(L)1-related toxicity. The prespecified windows for AB start included AB start within 30 days before anti-PD-(L)1 initiation, at the start of anti-PD-(L)1 treatment or within 30 days after anti-PD-(L)1 initiation.

Statistical methods

To describe patient characteristics, continuous variables were represented as numbers and percentages and categorical variables were depicted as medians and interquartile ranges. As the full date of birth including day and month was not available for all patients, the age at anti-PD-(L)1 initiation was calculated by subtracting the year of anti-PD-(L)1 initiation from the year of birth. The median follow-up was determined using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. A Kaplan Meier model was applied to analyse OS and PFS. Survival times of independent groups were compared using a log-rank test. As the variables we considered relevant for a potential association with survival fulfilled the proportional hazards assumption, a univariate Cox regression model was used to identify variables that were associated with shorter OS and PFS. The variables included in the univariate Cox regression model were the same for both OS and PFS. Following a univariate analysis, a multivariate Cox regression model was constructed. Subsequently, variables with the highest p-value were removed one after the other until all remaining variables had a p-value < 0.05. Next, the continuous variables which appeared prognostic for OS according to the multivariate model were considered for the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. To determine the optimal cut-off value of these continuous variables with respect to sensitivity and specificity, the Youden index was calculated (sensitivity + specificity − 1). IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 was used for the statistical analyses and Graphpad Prism 9 was used for designing the survival curves.

Results

Between 2013 and 2023, 552 patients with advanced UC treated with anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy were identified to be eligible for inclusion in the survival analyses. Characteristics of the population in our cohort are described in Table 1. Gender, age, tumour location, histologic subtype, cisplatin eligibility and the location of metastatic sites were typical for an advanced UC population. Among the 552 patients in our cohort, 228 (41%) received anti-PD-(L)1 as first-line therapy. After a median follow-up of 49 months, 435/552 (79%) patients had died. Median OS was 9 months (95% confidence interval (CI) 7.65–10.35). Five years after anti-PD-(L)1 initiation, a survival rate of 16.0% was observed for all anti-PD-(L)1 treatment lines combined (Fig. 1a). For anti-PD-(L)1 as frontline and second-line treatment, 5-year OS was 21.7% and 12% respectively (Fig. 1c). At the time of analysis, 486/545 (89%) patients had progressive disease following anti-PD-(L)1 therapy. Median PFS was 3 months (95% CI 2.71–3.29) and PFS at 5 years was 6.9% (Fig. 1b). Median OS for frontline, second-, third- and fourth-line were 12, 9, 7 and 2 months respectively and median PFS was 3 months for anti-PD-(L)1 as frontline, second- and third line (Fig. 1c, d). Both OS (p = 0.03) and PFS (p < 0.01) were statistically different when comparing different treatment lines (Fig. 1c, d). For PFS, this significance was largely driven by the difference between first-line and fourth-line anti-PD-(L)1 treatment (HR 4.48; p < 0.001). The significant difference in OS across treatment lines originated from the difference among patients treated with anti-PD-(L)1 as second- (HR 1.29; p = 0.011) and fourth-line treatment (HR 3.41; p = 0.007) compared to those who received frontline anti-PD-(L)1. To evaluate the long-term clinical outcomes in patients who experienced prolonged disease control, we assessed PFS and OS in patients who achieved and maintained disease control within the first and second year after anti-PD-(L)1 initiation. Patients exhibiting stable disease or remission 1 year after starting anti-PD-(L)1 treatment as first, second or later line remained at risk for both disease progression and death for more than 5 years after treatment initiation (Fig. 2a, b). Similarly, this extended risk to have progression and/or mortality was observed in patients who remained free from progressive disease at 2 years after treatment initiation (Fig. 2c, d). Clinical outcome did not differ between patients treated with agents targeting PD-1 vs PD-L1 (OS HR 1.03; p = 0.779; PFS HR 1.03; p = 0.696 Fig. S1). Easily accessible clinical factors such as laboratory values and medication use were studied for their potential association with survival. First, we performed a univariate Cox regression analysis with OS as the clinical outcome. All tested variables, except for cisplatin eligibility, were significantly associated with shorter OS (Table 2). Multivariate HR demonstrated a significant association between shorter OS and the presence of visceral and/or bone metastases (HR 1.79; 95% CI 1.12–2.86; p = 0.015). The continuous laboratory variables C-reactive protein, GGT and NLR were also significantly associated with OS in our multivariate analysis (Table 3). For clinical practicality, a ROC curve analysis was performed to dichotomize these continuous laboratory variables based on sensitivity and specificity. Youden’s Index determined 17 mg/L (sensitivity 35.3%; specificity 82.4%), 47.5 U/L (sensitivity 48.5%; specificity 68.4%) and 3.46 (sensitivity 63.4%; specificity 70%) as most appropriate cut-off value for C-reactive protein, GGT and NLR, respectively (Table S1). Kaplan-Meier curves for OS per prognostic continuous variable identified in our multivariate model confirmed inferior OS rates for patients with C-reactive protein level ≥ 17 mg/L (HR 2.35; 95% CI 1.59–3.48; p < 0.001), GGT level ≥ 47.5 (HR 1.79; 95% CI 1.40–2.28; p < 0.001) U/L and NLR ≥ 3.46 (HR 2.23; 95% CI 1.75–2.83; p < 0.001; Fig. 3).

Based on a univariate analysis for PFS, there was a significant association between shorter PFS and the presence of visceral and/or bone metastases (HR 1.69; p < 0.001), elevated C-reactive protein level (HR 1.00; p < 0.001), elevated LDH level (HR 1.00; p = 0.039), elevated GGT level (HR 1.00; p < 0.001), elevated NLR before treatment start (HR 1.00; p < 0.001) and anti-PD-(L)1 administration in later treatment lines (HR 0.81; p = 0.027; Table 2). Multivariate regression showed a significant correlation between shorter PFS and elevated C-reactive protein level (HR 1.006; 95% CI 1.001–1.01; p = 0.014), elevated GGT level (HR 1.002; 95% CI 1.001–1.003; p = 0.002) and elevated NLR before the start of anti-PD-(L)1 (HR 1.003; 95% CI 1.001–1.005; p = 0.014; Table 3). Our multivariate models for OS and PFS did not show shorter survival in patients who were treated with AB and/or steroids around anti-PD-(L)1 initiation. In addition, cisplatin eligibility, the presence of metastatic disease at initial diagnosis, the treatment line in which anti-PD-(L)1 was given and LDH levels appeared not prognostic for OS and PFS.

Given the association between elevated GGT levels and shortened OS and PFS, we examined the incidence of patients with elevated GGT and liver metastases, as well as the reciprocal relationship. Out of the 156 patients with GGT levels ≥ 47.5 U/L, 102 individuals had liver metastases (65%). Conversely, among the 200 patients with liver metastases, 102 had GGT levels of 47.5 U/L or higher (51%). In patients without liver metastases, OS and PFS were comparable between those with GGT levels ≥ 47.5 U/L and those with GGT levels below 47.5 U/L (Fig. S2). However, patients with liver metastasis had shorter OS and PFS if GGT levels ≥ 47.5 U/L (Fig. S2).

Discussion

Before the introduction of agents targeting the PD-(L)1 axis in 2013, chemotherapy had been the only treatment option for patients with advanced UC. Although the first clinical trials were initiated more than 10 years ago, data on long-term clinical outcomes remains relatively limited [23]. In the current study, we provide relevant data on long-term OS and PFS for patients with advanced UC who were treated with anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy between 2013 and 2023. In a subset of patients, we observed long-term survival following anti-PD-(L)1 treatment. The OS rate at 48 months in our cohort is in line with the survival rates that have been achieved in clinical trials with pembrolizumab in the first and second line [17]. Our results also indicate that a reasonable number of patients who remain progression-free beyond 24 months after anti-PD-(L)1 treatment as first, second or later line is still at risk for progressive disease in the following years. This reflects the importance of long-term clinical follow-up, even in those patients who appear to have a durable remission to anti-PD-(L)1 therapy. It is conceivable a plateau in the PFS and OS curves may form beyond 5 years, however, the small number of patients at risk > 5 years after treatment initiation in our analysis prohibits conclusions. Further additional questions remain unanswered, such as the optimal duration of anti-PD-(L)1 treatment as well as the value of potential re-challenge with these agents. Future studies might provide answers to these questions [24]. The poor prognosis of advanced UC underlines the need to identify prognostic biomarkers [19]. In the current study, we examined easily accessible clinical variables, aiming to elucidate their potential association with long-term OS and PFS. A multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that an increase of the C-reactive protein level prior to the first dose of anti-PD-(L)1 was associated with an increased risk of death. This finding is in line with results of another study which reports on baseline C-reactive protein levels and survival in patients with advanced cancer [25]. However, as that study evaluated OS and PFS in patients with metastatic renal cell cancer who had C-reactive protein levels >10 mg/L within 115 days prior to nivolumab initiation, results are not directly comparable. Nevertheless, elevated levels of C-reactive protein in general are suggested to correlate with poor outcomes in UC [26]. C-reactive protein levels may rise in response to cytokines produced within the tumour micro-environment as result of ongoing tissue damage caused by advanced cancer [27]. Hence, we propose that elevated circulating inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein might be considered a poor prognostic factor for survival, but further validation is warranted. In addition to C-reactive protein, NLR appeared to be another biomarker of inflammation that was independently correlated with poor survival in our cohort. The prognostic potential of NLR has previously been described in various tumour types [28], including UC [29]. In a recent study in advanced UC, NLR was one of the strongest predictors of OS, which led to the development of an easily applicable prognostic tool in which various inflammatory factors were integrated, including NLR [30]. The AUC of NLR as a prognostic factor for OS (0.69) suggests a moderate discriminatory power for NLR based on our ROC curve analysis. We identified 3.46 as the most optimal cut-off value for NLR, which is similar to the NLR cut-off value of 3.65 as a poor prognostic factor in another advanced UC cohort [31]. Based on the uniform results regarding the prognostic potential of NLR together with the fact that NLR can be calculated from an inexpensive routine blood test, NLR could be an effective tool in prognosticating patients. Moreover, we observed that patients with a higher GGT level before the start of anti-PD-(L)1 had shorter OS and PFS. This correlation was previously described in patients with metastatic melanoma [32]. Moreover, elevated GGT levels have been associated with worse outcomes in advanced UC, irrespective of anti-PD-(L)1 use [33]. Closer scrutiny showed that elevated GGT levels were mainly associated with poor prognosis in patients having liver metastases, suggesting that elevated GGT levels could be a potential indication of cancer progression in the liver. Unlike melanoma, where high LDH levels are prognostic for shorter OS [34], we did not observe a negative correlation between baseline LDH levels and long-term survival in our multivariate analysis. The incidence of LDH level ≥ 148 IU/L (upper limit of normal at the Netherlands Cancer Institute) was only ± 25% in our cohort, which suggests that increased LDH levels may be less common in advanced UC compared to melanoma. In addition, we studied LDH as a continuous variable in our regression model, whereas in several melanoma studies, LDH was studied as dichotomous variable with cut-off values ≥ 1.5–2x of the upper normal limit. This discrepancy might have contributed to the absent correlation between LDH and shorter survival in our cohort. As there is no definitive answer to the question whether steroid use during anti-PD-(L)1 therapy is potentially harmful, this topic is an ongoing debate. The results of our multivariate analysis suggest that steroid use around anti-PD-(L)1 initiation did not have a significant impact on OS and PFS, which is consistent with most previous retrospective analyses that have addressed this concern [35–37]. In contrast, patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer treated with steroids at the beginning of anti-PD-(L)1 therapy had worse outcomes [38]. An explanation for the opposing results in this study could be that most patients were prescribed steroids for cancer-related symptoms, such as dyspnoea, fatigue, brain metastases and pain, suggesting that this population has a poor clinical performance. In our cohort, steroid use was indicated relatively evenly for immune-related toxicity and other causes. Therefore, steroid usage before, during or after anti-PD-(L)1 treatment, might not elicit the harmful effects as anticipated, but more data are needed to address this question. It is widely recognized that treatment with AB may induce a microbial disbalance in the gut. The presence of particular bacterial species in the gut has been studied intensively regarding anti-tumour responses following ICB [39, 40]. AB use is therefore suggested to impair ICB efficacy [41]. We evaluated whether AB use would have prognostic potential in patients treated with anti-PD-(L)1 and our data showed no significant correlation between AB use and survival. Comparable findings were obtained in studies in hepatocellular carcinoma [42] and metastatic non-small cell lung cancer [43], but most available studies in literature illustrate contrasting observations [44–48]. Prior findings suggest that the timing of AB use relative to anti-PD-(L)1 initiation could determine its potential impact as prognostic factor [47, 49]. The limited sample size of patients with data on AB usage in our cohort may have contributed to the insignificant association of AB use and OS and PFS. Further limitations of our work include the retrospective nature of this study, the lack of data on cancer-specific survival, response rates and durability of response. We additionally acknowledge the absence of data collection on potential predisposing factors for abnormal laboratory values, which could serve as additional confounding variable. In summary, we observed long-term survival benefits following anti-PD-(L)1 treatment in a subset of patients with advanced UC. Patients who have disease control at one or 2 years after anti-PD-(L)1 initiation are still at risk of progression or death later. The absence of visceral and/or bone metastases, as well as of elevated C-reactive protein level, elevated GGT level and of elevated NLR emerged as favourable prognostic factors. The findings require further validation and may help guide discussions regarding risk assessment and the optimal use of anti-PD-(L)1 in patients with advanced UC.
