Psychosocial trajectories in Germany across a decade of societal crises
Authors: Oliver Huxhold, Lisa J Klasen, Mareike Bünning, Denis Gerstorf, Nadiya Kelle, Elizabeth Muñoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAdults in midlife and old age in Germany have experienced several societal disruptions in recent years. Earlier crises, the 2015 Refugee Crisis and the initial phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, appeared time-limited, and some evidence suggested many older adults adapted well. Since 2021, however, societal conditions have been shaped by overlapping and persistent challenges, including geopolitical instability, rising living costs, and energy insecurity. These developments raised questions about whether psychosocial functioning in later adulthood may be sensitive to broader contextual change.MethodsWe analyzed six Waves of longitudinal data from the German Aging Survey (2014–2024; N = 6,001 at baseline), a representative study of adults aged 40 and older. Changes in depressive symptoms, life satisfaction, and loneliness were examined using latent change score models that allowed modeling of gradual trends alongside period-specific deviations. We also tested whether predictors of change differed across two historical time intervals (2014–2017 vs 2021–2024).ResultsMean-level changes were small or absent between 2014 and 2017, and changes observed during the early pandemic did not persist. All three indicators shifted, however, after 2021: depressive symptoms and loneliness increased, while life satisfaction declined. These patterns appeared broadly distributed in our representative sample rather than concentrated in specific subgroups. Dispositional optimism showed a stronger association with change during 2021–2024.DiscussionThe findings suggest that psychosocial functioning in midlife and old age may vary with changing societal conditions. Continued monitoring is needed to determine whether these shifts reflect temporary fluctuations or a longer-term development.Keywords: Lifespan development, Historical embeddedness, Midlife and old age, Societal crises, Longitudinal population study

Background

One central tenet of lifespan psychological theory holds that aging unfolds through dynamic interactions between individual development and changing environmental conditions (Baltes et al., 1980). This perspective emphasizes that individual functioning is shaped by the broader social and historical context and that development remains open to change throughout life, reflecting a potential for plasticity that is nevertheless constrained (Heckhausen & Buchmann, 2019). A key implication of this perspective is that development is influenced not only by changing personal resources and goals, but also by evolving societal conditions that shape opportunities for agency (Baltes et al., 1980; Drewelies et al., 2019). Research demonstrates that shifts in resource distributions, social networks, family structures, technology, and age norms systematically affect developmental trajectories in midlife and old age (Drewelies et al., 2019). Thus, the rapid societal changes of the past decade form an important backdrop for understanding how individuals adapt in mid- and later life.

In recent decades, many societies around the world have faced growing instability driven by climate change (IPCC, 2023), increasing socio-economic inequality (Polacko, 2021), rapid technological change (Royakkers et al., 2018), and growing political polarization (Levin et al., 2021). These long-term societal pressures appear to place substantial adaptive demands on individuals, particularly on younger people. A growing body of research has documented increasing depressive symptoms (Keyes et al., 2019), declines in life satisfaction (Handa et al., 2023), and rising levels of loneliness (Buecker et al., 2021) among adolescents and young adults in the last two decades. In contrast, older adults appear to have adapted more successfully to these societal stressors. Some studies even suggest that among recently born cohorts of older adults, levels of depressive symptoms and loneliness are lower than among earlier-born cohorts at the same age (Beller et al., 2021; Suanet et al., 2024).

In contrast to long-term societal changes such as growing social inequality, societal crises manifest as rapid disturbances at the societal level that cannot be addressed with the typically available political instruments. At the individual level, societal crises are characterized by a general sense of threat, uncertainty, and urgency (Boin et al., 2018), which may lead to increased perceptions of personal risk and heightened stress levels (Burns et al., 2012). In recent years, Europe and Germany have faced several such crises, including the Global Financial Crisis in 2008, the Eurozone Debt Crisis in 2010, the Refugee Crisis in 2015, and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. Once again, large segments of older adults proved to be astonishingly well-equipped to cope with these crises. Evidence from the COVID-19 pandemic offers a compelling case in point. Although the number of fatalities was especially high among older adults, leading to widespread assumptions about their particular vulnerability, empirical findings revealed that their psychosocial functioning was less negatively affected than that of younger age groups. Across multiple studies, it was the younger adults who experienced the most pronounced declines in psychosocial functioning during the pandemic (Birditt et al., 2021; Daniali et al., 2023; Entringer & Gosling, 2022; van den Boom et al., 2023).

To date, it remains unclear why older adults, despite their physiological disadvantages, often maintain higher psychosocial functioning than younger adults in the face of long-term societal changes and short-term societal crises. One possible explanation is that past experiences with adversity may have strengthened resilience, as studies indicated that prior exposure to natural disasters can reduce anxiety and stress during later events (Kwan & Walsh, 2017). Moreover, compared to younger adults, older adults are more likely to use proactive strategies to avoid stressors and to positively reappraise difficult situations, both of which help alleviate stress (Charles & Piazza, 2024). They also tend to focus more strongly than younger adults on close, emotionally meaningful relationships, which provide social support in times of need (Carstensen et al., 1999). Together, prior experience, adaptive coping strategies, and strong social ties may enable many older adults to adapt successfully to external challenges. However, the scope and intensity of current societal challenges raise questions about whether these potential adaptive capacities will remain sufficient.

The current convergence of multiple crises and societal challenges has increasingly been described as a polycrisis. Lawrence et al. (2024) introduced a framework for conceptualizing this development that has gained prominence across political, economic, and health sciences. In this framework, a polycrisis emerges when fast-moving trigger events, understood as societal crises such as a pandemic, a war, or major disruptions in financial or energy systems, interact with slow-moving societal stresses, including rising socioeconomic inequality and growing political polarization. These interactions may push societies into volatile states of disequilibrium. Lawrence et al. describe three mechanisms that explain how crises become interconnected and self-reinforcing: shared underlying stresses that weaken multiple systems, domino effects in which disruptions in one domain trigger cascading failures in others, and intersystemic feedback loops that further amplify instability. Although originally developed for global analyses, this framework is also relevant for national contexts such as Germany, where overlapping crises in recent years reflect similar patterns of convergence, interaction, and mutual reinforcement.

The Russian war on Ukraine illustrates these processes. While the most devastating consequences are suffered by the Ukrainian population, the conflict has also produced wider systemic disruptions. It triggered domino effects across systems already weakened by societal stresses such as economic inequality and energy dependence. Germany’s industrial sector came under pressure despite policy responses (Eidenmüller, 2023), while the war and ongoing structural shifts contributed to stagflation, as rising prices coincided with stagnant economic growth (Demary & Hüther, 2022; Liadze et al., 2023). These economic difficulties heightened public dissatisfaction and limited fiscal capacities for addressing social and environmental challenges, creating feedback loops that amplify instability. The accumulation of such pressures, further intensified by the war, has fueled a broader cost-of-living crisis across Europe, demonstrating how interconnected disruptions can converge and reinforce one another within a polycrisis context (Broadbent et al., 2023).

As such pressures intensify and interact, individuals are increasingly exposed to overlapping stressors from economic, political, and social domains (McNamara & Bambra, 2025) that may manifest at the individual level as chronic psychological stress (Broadbent et al., 2023; Kałwak et al., 2024). Even though they often met earlier crises with relatively successful adaptation, older adults may be particularly vulnerable when such pressures persist and accumulate. Age-related physiological changes heighten sensitivity to prolonged activation of stress-response systems (Charles & Piazza, 2024). More specifically, when stressors affect multiple life domains and increase regulatory demands, older adults have been shown to exhibit stronger affective and cardiovascular stress responses than younger adults (Wrzus et al., 2013). Moreover, prolonged polycrisis conditions may increasingly undermine the ability to maintain emotionally meaningful social ties, which are particularly important for the well-being of older people (Carstensen et al., 1999). According to the Differential Investment of Resources (DIRe) Model and its extension, the DIRe-Crisis Model, sustaining close relationships requires sufficient capacities, motivation, and social skills (Huxhold, Fiori, et al., 2022; Klasen et al., 2025), all of which may be weakened by persistent stress. Chronic stress may, for example, reduce social motivation and increase avoidance (Birditt et al., 2021; daSilva et al., 2021) and impair socio-emotional abilities such as empathy (Nitschke & Bartz, 2023). Taken together, these considerations highlight the need to understand how the escalating crises of the past decade have shaped psychosocial functioning in midlife and old age.

The current study

Building on these considerations, the present study examines how psychosocial functioning in midlife and old age developed across a decade marked by overlapping societal challenges. Our primary aim is to describe mean-level trends over time. The study period encompasses gradual societal shifts, such as rising socioeconomic inequality, political polarization, and environmental strain, as well as discrete crisis events, including the Refugee Crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. Recent scholarship suggests that the convergence of these developments increasingly resembles an emerging polycrisis in which multiple stressors accumulate and interact. This perspective leads us to expect that the past few years may show changes that deviate from gradual historical and normative age-related trajectories.

Consistent with earlier findings showing no meaningful historical shifts between 2014 and 2017, including the Refugee Crisis, we treat this period as a benchmark for typical age-graded change, following Ehrlich et al. (2024). In contrast, prior longitudinal work indicates emerging period effects in the very early 2020s, including a short-lived decline in psychosocial functioning during the initial COVID-19 shock in 2020 and a return to pre-pandemic levels in 2021 (van Tilburg, 2024; Wettstein et al., 2022). If our theoretical reasoning on chronic societal strain holds, psychosocial functioning should deteriorate again after 2021, when early indicators of the polycrisis, such as rising inflation and broader cumulative strain, became visible in Germany.

We examine three key indicators of psychosocial functioning, namely depressive symptoms, loneliness, and life satisfaction. Depressive symptoms and loneliness capture emotional and social difficulties that often intensify under adverse conditions, whereas life satisfaction reflects a broader evaluative appraisal. Together, these indicators allow us to examine whether different facets of psychosocial functioning respond similarly to acute shocks and sustained societal pressures.

A second aim is to examine heterogeneity in change, with a particular focus on the past few years. Rather than assuming uniform responses, we test whether shifts during the polycrisis vary across subgroups and evaluate these differences relative to the more typical age-related change observed between 2014 and 2017. This approach allows us to assess whether differences between more vulnerable and more resource-rich groups become more pronounced under prolonged crisis conditions. To do so, we consider characteristics commonly linked to differential responses to stress, including gender, education, risk of poverty, and functional health (Diehl et al., 2012), and we test whether changes in psychosocial functioning during the polycrisis period differ between middle-aged and older adults. In addition, we include dispositional optimism as a psychological resource that may buffer against sustained adversity, given its role in supporting approach-oriented coping (Nes & Segerstrom, 2006), reducing depressive symptoms following large-scale disasters (Gero et al., 2021), and lowering exposure to daily stressors in later life (Lee et al., 2022).

Methods

Data

The German Aging Survey (DEAS) is a nationally representative longitudinal study of individuals aged 40 and older in Germany. It employs a multi-cohort sequential design with baseline samples drawn in 1996, 2002, 2008, and 2014 from private households listed in the German citizens’ register (Klaus et al., 2017). Samples were selected using random sampling procedures, and probability weights were applied to ensure representativeness with respect to age, sex, education, and region (East vs West Germany), based on benchmarks from the German Microcensus, a mandatory national survey sampling 1% of the German population.

This study focuses on the 2014 baseline cohort, first assessed in 2014 and followed over 10 years in five additional waves (2017, 2020, 2021, 2023, and 2024). The 2020 and 2024 waves used written questionnaires only, while all others included both questionnaires and interviews. Loneliness and life satisfaction were measured in all waves via questionnaires. Depressive symptoms were assessed during the interviews in 2014, 2017, 2021, and 2023, and through the written questionnaire in 2020 and 2024. The CES-D, used to assess depressive symptoms, is robust to mode effects, ensuring comparability across waves regardless of data collection method (Chan et al., 2004).

The analysis included 6,001 participants (48.93 percent female), with a mean age of 62.2 in 2014. Education levels were high, with 39.41 percent having high, 52.29 percent medium, and 8.28 percent low education. On average, participants took part in three waves (range 1 to 6). The number of individuals assessed at each wave for each of the three outcomes is reported in Supplementary Material Section 1. Missingness analyses indicated that, relative to participants retained through 2024, those lost to follow-up were older and showed lower education, income, functional health, optimism, and psychosocial functioning. No differences emerged for sex. Effect sizes were generally small (d = 0.13–0.28); only education (d = 0.38) and functional health (d = 0.38) exceeded d = 0.30, remaining below the conventional threshold for a medium effect. Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) was used to address selective attrition. Prior simulation studies show that FIML minimizes estimation bias when predictors of missingness are included in the model (Enders, 2008), which was applied in our case.

Indicators of psychosocial functioning

Depressive symptoms were assessed using nine items from the CES-D Scale (Radloff, 1977), which asked participants how often they had experienced various symptoms during the past week. Responses were given on a 4-point scale ranging from “Rarely or not at all, less than 1 day” (1) to “Most or all of the time, 5 to 7 days” (4). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher scores indicated higher levels of depressive symptoms. The internal consistency of the scale was good (Ωw = 0.86). To improve modeling efficiency, three parcels were created, each containing three items, based on a factorial algorithm.

Life satisfaction was measured using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (Pavot & Diener, 1993), which consists of five items. Participants responded on a 5-point scale ranging from “Strongly agree” (1) to “Strongly disagree” (5). All items were reverse-coded so that higher scores reflected greater life satisfaction. The internal consistency of the scale was good (Ωw = 0.87).

Loneliness was assessed using the 6-item version of the widely used De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (De Jong Gierveld & Van Tilburg, 2010), which captures individuals’ subjective perceptions of social integration. Participants responded to each item on a 4-point scale ranging from “Strongly agree” (1) to “Strongly disagree” (4). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher scores indicated greater loneliness. The internal consistency of the scale was good (Ωw = 0.85). As with depressive symptoms, three parcels were created, each consisting of two items, to improve modeling efficiency. Parcel assignment was guided by the factorial structure of the scale.

To enable comparability of changes across different outcomes, all measures were T-standardized using the mean and standard deviation from 2014. To ensure empirical distinctness, cross-sectional latent correlations were estimated at baseline (2014). Correlations were moderate (depressive symptoms–loneliness: r = 0.41; life satisfaction–loneliness: r = 0.56; depressive symptoms–life satisfaction: r = 0.52), indicating related but non-redundant constructs. Descriptive statistics for the 2014 wave are provided in Supplementary Section 1.

Predictors of heterogeneity in changes in psychosocial functioning

Age, gender, and education were treated as stable characteristics and measured at baseline in 2014. Age was measured in years. Gender was coded as a binary variable (0 = male, 1 = female). Educational attainment was coded according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED; OECD, 2015). In the mean-change models, education was represented with two dummy variables—low (ISCED 0–2) and high (ISCED 5–6), using medium (ISCED 3–4) as the reference—to improve the prediction of panel dropout via the auxiliary model. In the heterogeneity analyses, the original three-level ISCED variable was used to avoid convergence issues.

Risk of poverty, functional health, and optimism were time-varying and measured at the beginning of each change interval. Specifically, we used values from 2014 to predict changes in psychosocial well-being between 2014 and 2017, and values from 2021 to predict changes between 2021 and 2024. Risk of poverty was coded as a binary variable indicating whether the individual’s equivalized household income fell below 60 percent of the German national median. Functional health was assessed using the “Physical Functioning” subscale of the Short Form Health Survey (SF-36; Ware & Sherbourne, 1992), with higher scores indicating better physical functioning. Dispositional optimism was measured using five items from Brandtstädter and Wentura (1994), rated on a scale from “Strongly agree” (1) to “Strongly disagree” (4), and recoded such that higher scores indicated greater optimism. Scale scores were computed as the mean of at least three valid responses, and internal consistency was good (Ωw = 0.84).

Measurement invariance

Measurement invariance was tested separately for loneliness, life satisfaction, and depressive symptoms. To establish configural invariance, a measurement model was estimated in which all items or parcels loaded on a latent factor at each time point. For metric invariance, the factor loadings of corresponding items were constrained to be equal across waves. Scalar invariance was tested by additionally constraining the intercepts of corresponding items to be equal over time. To account for method variance, correlations between the same items or parcels across time points were freely estimated in all models.

Given the large sample size, model comparisons relied on changes in the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) rather than chi-square difference tests, which tend to be overly sensitive in large samples. A decrease in CFI of less than 0.01 is generally taken as evidence that the more restrictive model fits the data equally well. For very large samples (N > 1,000), a change of up to 0.02 is considered acceptable (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014).

Life satisfaction and depressive symptoms showed a decrease in CFI of less than 0.01 across all levels of invariance, indicating full scalar invariance. For loneliness, the CFI change from the configural to the metric model was below 0.01, while the change from metric to scalar was 0.013. Although this slightly exceeds the 0.01 threshold, it remains within the acceptable range given the large sample size of 6,001. Thus, scalar measurement invariance can be assumed for all three constructs.

Analytical approach

We conducted two sets of analyses: first, mean-level change in psychosocial functioning over the past 10 years, and second, individual differences in change. Analyses were performed separately for depressive symptoms, loneliness, and life satisfaction.

Mean change was estimated using latent change score (LCS) models with robust maximum likelihood (MLR), and missing data were handled with full information maximum likelihood (FIML). In the mean-change models, attrition-informative variables (age, gender, education, risk of poverty, and functional health) were included as auxiliary variables to support the missing-at-random assumption. In the heterogeneity models, these variables were entered directly as predictors.

Modeling mean trends

To distinguish gradual age-related and historical change from period-specific deviations, we specified combined LCS models including a linear slope across 2014–2024 and discrete change components for 2017–2020, 2020–2021, 2021–2023, and 2023–2024. The linear slope captured gradual age-related and long-term societal change, whereas the period components captured potential deviations during later crisis phases. If the linear slope was not statistically significant, it was removed. The 2014–2017 interval was then added as a period-specific change component and treated as the reference phase for typical age-related change under comparatively stable societal conditions. This approach aligns with latent structural extensions of interrupted time-series modeling (Rioux et al., 2021).

Analyses of heterogeneity in change

In a second step, we examined inter-individual differences in change during two equal intervals (2014–2017 and 2021–2024). Values from 2014 predicted change to 2017, and values from 2021 predicted change to 2024, allowing comparison of change under relatively stable conditions versus a later phase marked by overlapping societal challenges. Although the 2021 assessments may already reflect earlier crisis effects, they capture individuals’ vulnerability at the start of the later interval. In these analyses, baseline levels of the outcome were included to increase precision and distinguish true change effects from pre-existing differences (Hamaker et al., 2015). Baseline levels were not included in the mean-trend models because conditioning on initial status can attenuate estimates of overall change. Predictors were age, gender, education, risk of poverty, functional health, and dispositional optimism, representing structural, health, and psychological resources.

To assess whether predictor effects differed across the two periods, we conducted χ2 difference tests. These tests were performed only for predictors showing a significant association with change in at least one period, because testing equality between two non-significant paths can lead to estimation difficulties in SEM models.

Results

All structural equation models showed an excellent fit to the data, with Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) values below 0.05 and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) values above 0.90. Diagrams of the structural models and a table of fit indices for all estimated models are provided in Supplementary Sections 2 and 3.

Mean trends

As a first step, we estimated models that included a continuous linear slope capturing gradual change across the past 10 years. In none of the three outcomes did this linear slope reach statistical significance (loneliness: slope = −0.048 T-score units per year, p = .41; depressive symptoms: slope = 0.086 T-score units per year, p = .20; life satisfaction: slope = 0.096 T-score units per year, p = .12). Importantly, the estimated period-specific changes were substantively unchanged when the linear slope was included, indicating that the observed shifts cannot be attributed to gradual linear trends over the study period. Consequently, the linear slope was dropped from all subsequent models in favor of estimating discrete change components.

The figure displays the original model estimates together with shaded bands indicating ±1 standard error around the point estimates to illustrate the uncertainty of the trajectories. To ensure consistent reporting across metrics, all effects are presented both as T-score units (mean = 50, SD = 10) and as Cohen’s d. Results revealed that between 2014 and 2017, life satisfaction (changelife: ΔT = 0.17; Cohen’s d ≈ .017, z = 1.376, p = .169), loneliness (changelone: ΔT = −0.16; Cohen’s d ≈ −.016, z = −0.902, p = .367), and depressive symptoms (changedepr: ΔT = 0.17; Cohen’s d ≈ .017, z = 0.809, p = .418) remained stable, with no statistically significant changes observed.

During the period coinciding with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic and the first lockdown in 2020, depressive symptoms and loneliness increased relatively sharply (changedepr: ΔT = 3.15; Cohen’s d ≈ .315, z = 11.837, p < .001; changelone: ΔT = 2.11; Cohen’s d ≈ .211, z = 8.279, p < .001), whereas life satisfaction remained stable (changelife: ΔT = −0.06; Cohen’s d ≈ −.006, z = −0.274, p = .784). By 2021, depressive symptoms (changedepr: ΔT = −3.33; Cohen’s d ≈ −.333, z = −12.204, p < .001) and loneliness (changelone: ΔT = −2.04; Cohen’s d ≈ −.204, z = −8.279, p < .001) had returned to levels comparable to the pre-pandemic period, while life satisfaction showed a small increase (changelife: ΔT = 0.57; Cohen’s d ≈ .057, z = 2.45, p = .014).

In the subsequent period from 2021 to 2023, which overlapped with rising inflation and the beginning of the Russian war against Ukraine, levels of loneliness were stable (changelone: ΔT = −0.20; Cohen’s d ≈ −.020, z = −.805, p = .411), whereas life satisfaction began to decline significantly (changelife: ΔT = 0.81; Cohen’s d ≈ −.081, z = −3.309, p < .001) and depressive symptoms increased significantly (changedepr: ΔT = 0.89; Cohen’s d ≈ .089, z = 2.921, p < .001). This interval thus marks a phase in which psychosocial functioning began to shift again following the pandemic-related adjustment period.

Finally, in 2024, which corresponds to what has been described in public and academic discourse as a phase of intensified polycrisis, life satisfaction declined even more (changelife: ΔT = −0.93; Cohen’s d ≈ −.093, z = −3.781, p < .001), and depressive symptoms increased further (changedepr: ΔT = 2.36; Cohen’s d ≈ .236, z = 8.945, p < .001), reaching levels comparable to those observed during the initial COVID-19 shock in 2020. Loneliness increased significantly (changelone: ΔT = 1.37; Cohen’s d ≈ .137, z = 6.75, p < .001), climbing to nearly two-thirds of the 2020 peak despite the absence of large-scale lockdown measures.

For gender, poverty risk, and age, associations with change were limited and showed no evidence of period differences. Gender was associated with an increase in depressive symptoms in both intervals, but the strength of this association was comparable across periods.

Education and functional health were associated with change in several models, but their effects were largely similar across periods. Higher education was linked to smaller increases in depressive symptoms (2014–2017), smaller increases in loneliness (2021–2024), and more positive change in life satisfaction in both intervals; none of these associations differed significantly across periods. Functional health showed a significant period difference only for depressive symptoms, reflecting a weaker association in 2021–2024.

Optimism showed the clearest period variation. Its associations with changes in depressive symptoms and loneliness were stronger in 2021–2024, as confirmed by significant χ2 tests, with higher optimism linked to smaller increases in both outcomes. Its association with changes in life satisfaction was stable across periods.

Discussion

Alongside growing societal pressures such as globally increasing socio-economic inequality (Polacko, 2021) and political polarization (Levin et al., 2021), Germany has experienced several societal crises during the last 10 years, including the 2015 Refugee Crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic, and a recent conflux of societal disruptions—such as the Russian war in Ukraine, the energy crisis, and stagflation—often described collectively as a polycrisis. From a lifespan psychological perspective, which conceptualizes human development as historically embedded and context-dependent (e.g., Baltes et al., 1980), we assumed that the psychosocial functioning of people in midlife and old age could be adversely affected under such crisis conditions. Consistent with this view, our analyses revealed period-specific shifts in depressive symptoms, loneliness, and life satisfaction that emerged over and above normative aging trends and gradual historical change.

Mean trends across a decade of crises

Specifically, we assumed that people at older ages might be relatively well-positioned to adjust to singular crisis events. This assumption aligns with theoretical accounts suggesting that older adults may draw more strongly on accumulated life experience (Kwan & Walsh, 2017), may rely more on proactive coping (Charles & Piazza, 2024), and may maintain a stronger focus on close, supportive relationships (Carstensen et al., 1999). These factors could facilitate adaptation to crisis conditions, although they were not directly measured in the present study. Our results are consistent with this general assumption. Between 2014 and 2017, we observed no notable changes in psychosocial functioning, suggesting that any effects of the 2015 Refugee Crisis were limited or short-lived. Moreover, although depressive symptoms and loneliness increased in response to the initial COVID-19 shock in 2020, people in midlife and old age returned relatively quickly to pre-pandemic levels despite ongoing restrictions, including social distancing mandates and the winter 2021 lockdown. Life satisfaction did not show a comparable decline in 2020, and some researchers argue that middle-aged and older adults may have adapted their standards for evaluating life quality under temporary crisis conditions (Wettstein et al., 2022). Taken together, these patterns indicate that many individuals in midlife and old age appeared able to maintain psychosocial functioning when confronted with isolated societal crises.

In the introduction, we argued that short-term resilience to isolated crises might not extend to the more complex and persistent societal conditions that characterize the past few years. Following the polycrisis framework (Lawrence et al., 2024), we assumed that the accumulation of interacting stressors across economic, political, and social domains may generate conditions of sustained societal strain. Such a combination of societal pressures has been described as particularly detrimental to mental and physical health (Broadbent et al., 2023), and older adults may be especially sensitive to these conditions. Age-related physiological changes are linked to heightened reactivity to prolonged stress, and multi-domain stressors elicit stronger adverse affective reactions in older than younger adults (Charles & Piazza, 2024; Wrzus et al., 2013). Societal stress may also undermine the maintenance of emotionally meaningful social ties (Klasen et al., 2025). From this perspective, it seemed plausible that psychosocial functioning in midlife and old age would show negative, period-specific shifts during the crisis-laden years.

In line with this assumption, our findings indicate that levels of depressive symptoms in midlife and old age had already begun to rise between 2021 and 2023, and this trend accelerated markedly in the following year. The increase observed between 2023 and 2024 was nearly three times as large as that of the preceding two-year period. Life satisfaction, which appeared largely unaffected during the COVID-19 pandemic, showed a gradual and persistent decline between 2021 and 2024. Loneliness also increased notably between 2023 and 2024 after two years of relative stability. Although loneliness levels remained below those seen during the height of the pandemic, the magnitude of the increase suggests renewed vulnerability in social well-being even in the absence of acute restrictions.

At the same time, the available data do not yet allow firm conclusions about the longer-term trajectory of these developments. Although the recent changes differ from the sharp but temporary downturn observed during the initial COVID-19 shock, it remains uncertain whether psychosocial functioning in midlife and old age will stabilize or recover in the coming years. Future survey waves will therefore be needed to determine whether the observed shifts represent a more persistent change in psychosocial functioning or a temporary deviation from prior levels.

Heterogeneity in periodic shifts?

Our theoretical perspective focused primarily on broad population-level responses rather than specific high-risk subgroups, and the heterogeneity analyses were consistent with this emphasis. Changes in depressive symptoms, loneliness, and life satisfaction during 2021–2024—potentially reflecting cumulative societal pressures during this period—appeared widely distributed across the German population in midlife and older adulthood. Associations with education, gender, poverty risk, and functional health remained comparable across periods, indicating that these characteristics did not relate differently to change under the presumably more strained societal conditions of recent years than they did between 2014 and 2017. Chronological age was likewise not associated with change in any outcome once other predictors were controlled, suggesting that the observed shifts were not confined to particular age groups within midlife and older adulthood. However, the present models capture only linear age-related differences. More complex non-linear age- or cohort-related patterns may still exist. In the German context, some older cohorts experienced war or the economic instability of the post-war period. Such experiences may foster resilience, but they may also reactivate earlier vulnerabilities when individuals are confronted with later societal disruptions. Such potential interactions between cohort-specific experiences and contemporary crises cannot be examined directly with the present design.

Dispositional optimism was the only characteristic whose association with change differed between periods. Specifically, optimism was more strongly related to maintaining lower levels of depressive symptoms and loneliness during 2021–2024 than during 2014–2017, a period more likely characterized by normative aging trends and gradual historical change than by acute societal disruptions. Given that optimism is often linked to more effective coping under stress (e.g., Lee et al., 2022), this pattern may point to a heightened relevance of psychological resources under conditions of prolonged societal strain. However, the present design does not allow conclusions about underlying mechanisms, and future research will be needed to examine the time-varying developmental and contextual processes that may underlie these associations across different historical conditions.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the analyses were not preregistered and rely on a single longitudinal dataset, as no comparable German data covering this historical window were available at the time. Future work will preregister analytic decisions based on the current specifications and examine whether similar patterns emerge once additional longitudinal data covering the post-2023 period becomes available.

Second, the findings reflect developments in Germany and may not generalize to other contexts. Countries differ in the timing, severity, and policy responses to recent crises, which may shape psychosocial trajectories differently. Comparative work across welfare regimes and crisis histories will be essential to identify boundary conditions.

Third, although age was statistically controlled, age, cohort, and historical timing cannot be fully disentangled. The observed shifts may therefore reflect cohort-specific experiences rather than age-related change alone. In addition, the temporal spacing of measurement waves does not allow us to distinguish immediate from potentially lagged effects of societal disruptions.

Finally, although the pattern is consistent with the possibility that prolonged societal strain is increasingly linked to psychosocial outcomes, the proposed stressors and mechanisms linking societal conditions to individual outcomes were not directly assessed, including potentially relevant factors such as perceived stress, perceived economic pressure, or changes in financial security, which may have become increasingly salient during recent years. Future studies incorporating micro-longitudinal or experience-sampling designs may help identify such mechanisms and better explain heterogeneity in change, particularly given that several conventional vulnerability and resilience indicators did not differentiate trajectories in the present study.

Conclusion

The present study adds to growing evidence that psychosocial development in midlife and old age is shaped not only by age-graded processes but also by historical context (Baltes et al., 1980; Drewelies et al., 2019). This perspective highlights the importance of understanding how developmental trajectories unfold within changing social, economic, and political environments. Using ten years of longitudinal data from the German Aging Survey, we examined period-specific shifts in depressive symptoms, life satisfaction, and loneliness and assessed whether recent societal developments—often discussed as a polycrisis—were accompanied by changes in these indicators.

A key feature of the findings is the convergence of patterns across all three outcomes between 2021 and 2024, providing internal replication and increasing confidence that the observed changes reflect period-specific dynamics rather than indicator-specific artifacts. The availability of six measurement waves enabled the modeling of non-linear change using latent change score models, allowing deviations from longer-term trends to be identified within a large, representative sample of middle-aged and older adults in Germany.

While interpretations remain cautious and further measurement waves are required, the post-2021 pattern suggests that psychosocial functioning in later adulthood may shift alongside broader societal dynamics. The present findings therefore support the view that development remains open and context-sensitive across later life, even though the mechanisms linking contextual pressures to individual change remain to be identified.

These developments carry relevance beyond scholarly debate. Changes in loneliness and depressive symptoms are closely tied to social cohesion, institutional trust, and economic participation (Goetzel et al., 2003; Huxhold, Suanet, et al., 2022; Langenkamp, 2023). If such shifts become more widespread, they may weaken collective capacity to manage societal challenges, implying potential feedback loops between individual well-being and societal functioning.

Continued monitoring will therefore be essential to determine whether recent changes stabilize, reverse, or develop into a more persistent deterioration in psychosocial functioning, particularly in light of broader geopolitical and political developments in recent years, including rising international conflicts, increasing political polarization, and growing political tensions across Western societies, including Germany.
