Prevalence and independent predictors of refractive error among adults with diabetes in Addis Ababa: a multicentre hospital-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Kalkidan Teshome Tizazu, Zinachew Mulat Bogale, Aklilu Libasie Assefa, Solomon Zemene Liyih, Matiyas Mamo Bekele

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Refractive error (RE) is a condition in which the optical system of the eye fails to focus parallel rays of light on the retina.1 Transient refractive changes are well-recognised features of diabetes, and affected patients may experience both short-term and permanent refractive alterations. In patients with poor glycaemic control, fluctuations in blood glucose levels are often accompanied by changes in refractive power.2

According to the WHO, RE is the leading cause of visual impairment and the second leading cause of blindness globally, accounting for 43% of all visual impairment.3 A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted between 1990 and 2016 demonstrated substantial regional variation in RE prevalence worldwide.3 Among adults with diabetes, uncorrected refractive error (URE) is an important and potentially preventable cause of visual impairment. A study in the USA reported that 7.2% of adults with diabetes had correctable visual impairment due to RE, while studies in low-resource settings have reported that up to 96.1% of visual impairment may be attributable to URE.4

Fluctuations in blood glucose levels are known to cause changes in the refractive status of the eye, leading to symptoms such as blurred or distorted vision.5 These refractive changes are primarily due to alterations in the shape and thickness of the lens, which affect its focusing ability.6 A meta-analysis indicated that individuals with hyperopic RE are at an increased risk of developing vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy, whereas those with myopia exhibit a lower risk of diabetic retinopathy (DR), highlighting the importance of understanding the refractive status of diabetic patients.7 RE in individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM) can significantly impact visual function, affecting activities such as occupational performance, sports, driving and other daily living tasks, and can therefore reduce the quality of life of people with diabetes.8 A study indicated that nearly two-thirds of adults with diabetes who had visual impairment could correct their vision with an accurate prescription for glasses or contact lenses.9 Therefore, it is crucial for every patient with diabetes to undergo an eye examination at least once a year. Timely examinations, effective glycaemic management and appropriate refraction are vital factors in safeguarding and improving visual health.2

Researchers have found that factors such as age, female sex, education level, cataract, presence of DR, haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level, duration of diabetes, Body Mass Index (BMI), glycaemic control and total cholesterol level are associated with RE among adults with diabetes.8

Despite the growing understanding of ocular complications associated with diabetes, data on the prevalence of RE among this population in Ethiopia remain scarce. While some data are available from studies of eye diseases in general populations that include persons with diabetes in the sampling frame, these studies do not provide specific insights into the actual burden or associated factors of RE within the diabetic population itself. Therefore, the findings of this study will inform diabetic eye care policy by establishing baseline RE prevalence for national diabetes guidelines, identifying high-risk subgroups for targeted screening, quantifying the correctable visual impairment burden to justify spectacle provision programmes and supporting the integration of refraction services into routine DR screening protocols. These evidence-based recommendations will enhance visual health outcomes and quality of life for Ethiopia’s growing diabetic population. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and independent predictors of RE among adults with diabetes in Addis Ababa.

Methods

Study design, area and period

A multicentre, hospital-based, cross-sectional study was conducted in public referral hospitals in Addis Ababa, Central Ethiopia, from 13 May to 17 August 2025. In Addis Ababa, there are four comprehensive specialised hospitals that provide both comprehensive diabetic care and ophthalmic evaluation, namely: St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC), St. Peter’s Specialised Hospital (SPSH), Menelik II Comprehensive Specialised Hospital and Alert Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (ALERT).

Source and study population

All adult diabetic patients attending SPHMMC, SPSH, Menelik II Comprehensive Specialised Hospital and Alert Comprehensive Specialised Hospital (ALERT).

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged 18 years and older diagnosed with DM were included in this study.

Exclusion criteria

Participants with conditions that preclude reliable refraction, specifically corneal opacity, pseudophakia, diabetic macular oedema and proliferative DR, were excluded. These conditions can interfere with the accurate measurement of RE; therefore, their exclusion reduced the risk of measurement error biasing the refractive assessments.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the study design, conduct of the study or plan to disseminate the result of this study to the study participants.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined by using the single population proportion formula. Since there were no previous studies conducted with a similar population, we assumed a proportion of 50% to calculate an adequate sample size. Sampling technique and procedures

A systematic random sampling technique was used to select participants from four hospitals: SPHMMC, Menelik, ALERT and St. Peter’s Hospitals. According to recent data from SPHMMC, at least 1250 adult diabetic patients visit the medical outpatient department (OPD) every month. At Menelik Hospital, there are an estimated 530 adult diabetic patients, while at ALERT and St. Peter’s Hospitals, approximately 625 diabetic patients each visit the medical OPD monthly on average. The sampling interval (k) was determined by dividing the total number of diabetic patients who visited the clinic in the previous month by the required sample size. The sampling interval was calculated as: k=N/n=3030/423 ≈ 7. The sample size was then distributed proportionally among each hospital (figure 1). The first participant was selected using the lottery method, after which every seventh participant was recruited consecutively.

Operational definitions

Refractive error: a spherical equivalent of >+0.50 D or <−0.50 D in either eye on subjective refraction.1

Myopia: a spherical equivalent of <−0.50 D.10

Hyperopia: a spherical equivalent of >+0.50 D.11

Astigmatism: a cylinder power of >0.50 D at any axis.11

Glycaemic control: glycaemic control was categorised based on HbA1c levels as reference category (HbA1c<5.6%), good (HbA1c 5.6%–7.0%), fair (HbA1c 7.1%–8.0%), unsatisfactory (HbA1c 8.1%–10.0%) or poor (HbA1c>10%).12 Poor glycaemic control based on fasting blood glucose (FBG) was defined as FBG>130 mg/dL (16) or FBG<70 mg/dL.13

Diabetic retinopathy: categorised based on the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) criteria.14

Lens opacity: significant cortical and posterior subcapsular opacification was defined as greater than grade 2, and significant nuclear opacification was defined as greater than grade 4, according to the Lens Opacities Classification System III (LOCS III) grading guidelines.15

Body mass index (BMI): classified as lean (male<20; female<19), normal (male 20–25; female 19–24), overweight (male 25–30; female 24–29) or obese (male>30; female>29).12

Total cholesterol: considered high if ≥200 mg/dL.16

Hypertension: defined as a confirmed diagnosis of hypertension with the participant currently on treatment.17

Current cigarette smoker: a participant who smoked at least one cigarette within the last month.18

Current alcohol drinker: a participant who consumed at least one alcoholic drink within the last 12 months.19

Data collection tool and procedure

Data were collected using a semistructured, interviewer-administered questionnaire along with clinical examinations and measurements. The questionnaire was developed by adapting items from relevant literature on similar study topics and included sections on sociodemographic characteristics, ocular factors and clinical variables. During the data collection period, two optometrists (one BSc and one MSc) were responsible for data collection at each hospital and one MSc-level optometrist supervised the data collectors at each site. Data collection was carried out in two phases: the first at the diabetic clinic and the second at the ophthalmic centre.

Phase 1 diabetic clinic

After patients completed their routine physical examination, diagnosis and treatment by the diabetic care physician, the optometrists obtained informed consent from those who voluntarily agreed to participate. The optometrists then assessed the presence of any ocular media opacity using a pen torch. Subsequently, participants were referred to the ophthalmic centres for refraction, anterior segment and fundus examinations. A chart with an attached printed checklist, prepared by the data collectors, accompanied each participant. These checklists were kept with the corresponding charts and served as identifiers to prevent resampling if a participant returned for follow-up during the study period.

Phase 2 ophthalmic clinic

At the ophthalmic clinics, optometrists performed comprehensive ophthalmic examinations beginning with visual acuity (VA) testing. Monocular and binocular unaided VA, as well as VA with refractive correction, were measured using reduced Snellen acuity charts at a distance of 3 m under standard illumination. Following VA assessment, objective refraction was measured using an autorefractor and the results were refined through monocular subjective refraction; the final subjective refraction was then recorded for each eye. Participants eligible for fundus examination underwent pupil dilation with 1% tropicamide in both eyes, followed by a 20 min waiting period. After dilation, the posterior segment was evaluated by an MSc-level optometrist using a slit-lamp biomicroscope in combination with a +90-dioptre Volk lens. Systemic conditions including hypertension, type of diabetes, blood pressure and FBG were recorded on the checklist by reviewing patient charts; these measurements and diagnoses had been performed by physicians at the diabetic clinic. Finally, participants responded to a semistructured questionnaire in Amharic covering sociodemographic and clinical variables. All data collected through the checklist were entered into KoboToolbox and linked to the corresponding patient charts.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured before, during and after data collection. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire underwent forward-and-back translation from English to Amharic and back to English to verify consistency and accuracy. A pretest was then conducted with 22 participants (5% of the expected sample size) at Ras Desta Hospital Diabetic Care Clinic, and necessary modifications were made to the questionnaire and checklist based on the results. Data collectors received adequate training on data collection procedures and tools prior to commencement. During data collection, each data collector was supervised throughout the entire study period. At the end of each day, all collected data were reviewed by the principal investigator to ensure completeness and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

After checking for completeness and consistency, data were entered into KoboToolbox and exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) V.27.0 for analysis. Both descriptive and analytical methods were employed. For descriptive analysis, summary statistics, frequencies and cross-tabulations were computed. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the independent effects of potential predictors on the outcome variable. Variables with a p value<0.20 in the bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify the final predictors of RE among adults with diabetes, after controlling for other independent variables, in order to minimise the risk of omitting important covariates that might be confounded with the main exposure. Adjusted ORs (AOR) with 95% CIs were used to determine significant predictors. A p value of <0.05 in the multivariable model was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 391 participants were enrolled in the study, yielding a response rate of 92.4%. The median age of the participants was 54 years, with an IQR of 46–62 years. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which indicated adequate model fit (p=0.340). Multicollinearity among the independent variables was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance statistics, revealing no significant issues (VIF<2.00).

Of the 391 participants, 235 (60.1%) were female, 21 (5.4%) had no formal education and 67 (17.1%) could read and write only. Among all study participants, 127 (32.5%) had completed secondary school, 27.1% were housewives and 32.2% were engaged in private sector activities (table 1).

Prevalence of RE

Of the 391 participants, 218 (55.7%) (95% CI 50.7 to 60.7) had RE. Among participants with RE, 19.4% had myopia, 27.6% had hyperopia and 8.7% had astigmatism (figure 2).

Factors associated with RE among adults with diabetes

In the bivariate binary logistic regression analysis, older age, female sex, higher HbA1c level, higher FBG level, longer duration of DM, hypertension, higher cholesterol level, DR and cataract were significantly associated with RE among adult diabetic patients.

In the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, older age, female sex, higher HbA1c level, higher FBG level, longer duration of DM, hypertension, DR and cataract remained significantly associated with RE among adult diabetic patients.

Participants older than 62 years had 2.52 times higher odds of RE compared with those younger than 46 years (AOR=2.52, 95% CI 1.19 to 5.33). Female participants had 2.45 times higher odds of developing RE than their male counterparts (AOR=2.45, 95% CI 1.39 to 4.30).

Regarding the duration of DM, the odds of developing RE were 2.45 times higher in the duration of DM≥13 years as compared with the participants who had a duration of DM<5 years (AOR=2.45, 95% CI 1.40 to 5.26).

Participants with FBG levels >179 mg/dL had 3.51 times higher odds of RE compared with those with levels<135 mg/dL (AOR=3.51, 95% CI 1.48 to 8.00). Similarly, participants with HbA1c>10% had 2.74 times higher odds of RE than those with HbA1c of 5.6–7.0% (AOR=2.74, 95% CI 1.01 to 7.30).

Hypertensive participants had 1.81 times higher odds of RE compared with non-hypertensive individuals (AOR=1.81, 95% CI 1.05 to 3.10).

Participants with DR had 3.70 times higher odds of RE compared with those without DR (AOR=3.70, 95% CI 1.94 to 7.00). Similarly, participants with cataract had 2.32 times higher odds of RE compared with those without cataract (AOR=2.32, 95% CI 1.22 to 4.42) (table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of RE was 55.7% (95% CI 50.7% to 60.7%). This finding is comparable with studies conducted in Kenya (58.5%)20 and India (60%),21 possibly because these studies employed a hospital-based, cross-sectional design targeting adult diabetic patients, which may capture similar patterns of RE prevalence.

Our findings are considerably lower than the prevalence reported in China (96%),22 where the study population was older and glycaemic control was directly assessed, potentially increasing the detection of lens-induced refractive changes related to fluctuating glucose levels. Conversely, some studies have documented lower prevalence rates, including those conducted in the USA (7.2%),4 Hong Kong (7.3%)9 and Botswana (29.2%).23 These differences may reflect variations in healthcare access, routine eye screening practices and the availability of refractive correction services. In high-resource settings such as the USA and Hong Kong, regular eye examinations may lead to earlier detection and correction of REs, thereby reducing the proportion of uncorrected cases.

Participants older than 62 years had 2.52 times higher odds of RE compared with those younger than 46 years. This finding is consistent with studies from India21 and Taiwan.16 This association may be explained by age-related lenticular changes, such as nuclear sclerosis, as well as the cumulative effects of prolonged hyperglycaemic exposure, both of which contribute to refractive shifts.24 However, given the cross-sectional design, it is not possible to determine whether age directly influences the development of RE or whether it reflects the accumulation of other risk factors over time.

Female participants had 2.45 times higher odds of developing RE than their male counterparts. This finding is in line with studies conducted in India24 and China.25 This female predominance may be attributed to anatomical and hormonal variations, including shorter axial length and steeper corneal curvature, which are more prevalent in females.26 Additionally, hormonal differences and lifestyle factors may further contribute to sex-related disparities in RE prevalence.27 However, sociocultural and behavioural factors, such as healthcare-seeking patterns, may also play a role and should be considered when interpreting this finding.

Regarding the duration of DM, the odds of developing RE were 2.45 times higher in the duration of DM≥13 years as compared with the participants who had a duration of DM<5 years. This finding is consistent with a study from China.25 The likely explanation involves cumulative metabolic and microvascular changes that progressively affect ocular structures over time.2 5

Participants with FBG levels>179 mg/dL had 3.51 times higher odds of RE compared with those with levels<135 mg/dL. Similarly, participants with elevated HbA1c>10% had 2.74 times higher odds of RE compared with those with HbA1c of 5.6 to 7.0%. These results are consistent with studies from China,22 India21 and Taiwan.16 This may be attributed to poor glycaemic control contributing to refractive instability via sorbitol pathway-mediated lens osmotic changes, while persistent metabolic derangements may promote longer term refractive shifts.24 Nevertheless, the direction of this relationship cannot be firmly established, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors may influence this association.

Hypertensive participants had 1.81 times higher odds of RE compared with non-hypertensive individuals. This finding is supported by evidence that systemic vascular conditions may contribute to ocular changes. While not all studies explicitly assess the role of hypertension, its consistent association with diabetic ocular morbidity supports the present finding.22 23 28 29 This relationship may reflect shared underlying mechanisms or coexisting metabolic abnormalities rather than a direct causal effect.

Participants with DR had 3.70 times higher odds of RE than those without. This finding is in line with previous studies conducted in India.30 The association likely stems from hyperglycaemia-induced changes, including osmotic shifts in the lens causing transient myopia or hyperopia, alongside chronic microvascular damage that alters retinal and vitreous refractive indices over time.7

The odds of RE were 2.32 times higher among participants with cataract than those without. This finding is in line with previous studies conducted in China22 and Ethiopia.1 This association may be due to hyperglycaemia-induced lens opacification and osmotic swelling, which alter the refractive index and curvature of the crystalline lens, leading to induced myopia or hyperopia.31

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study found a relatively high prevalence of RE among patients with diabetes attending the selected referral hospitals in Addis Ababa. By evaluating demographic, clinical and ocular variables simultaneously, independent predictors of RE were identified after multivariable adjustment. The use of verified diabetes cases, standardised measurements (VA, refraction, HbA1c and FBG) and recruitment from multiple hospitals strengthens the reliability of the findings and their relevance to similar hospital-based diabetic populations.

However, the hospital-based, cross-sectional design limits generalisability to the broader diabetic population and may introduce selection bias by over-representing patients engaged in routine care at these specific referral hospitals in Addis Ababa. Causal inference is not possible, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Measurement constraints such as the use of non-cycloplegic refraction and reliance on routine clinical records may also affect the precision of some estimates.

Conclusion

This study found a high prevalence of RE among patients with diabetes attending selected referral hospitals in Central Ethiopia. Older age, female sex, longer diabetes duration, poor glycaemic control (assessed by both FBG and HbA1c), hypertension, DR and cataract were significantly associated with RE. These findings highlight the importance of integrating refractive assessment into routine diabetic eye care, particularly for high-risk subgroups attending hospital settings. Further longitudinal and population-based studies are recommended to better understand the temporal relationships between these factors and RE, and to inform evidence-based service planning. Future studies integrating machine learning models and dynamic glycaemic metrics (eg, continuous glucose monitors-based metrics) may improve risk stratification for diabetic ocular complications and enhance personalised screening strategies.32
