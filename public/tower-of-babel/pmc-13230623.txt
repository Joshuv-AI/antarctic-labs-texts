Assessing future landslide susceptibility and risk escalations under changing LULC and climate conditions in the Western Ghats of Karnataka in India
Authors: Malay Pramanik, Amarnath Hegde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe intensity and frequency of fatal landslides in Western Ghats of India are adversely influenced by human-induced land-use modifications and climate change. Understanding these factors are vital for effective disaster risk reduction strategy for coming days. This study evaluates landslide susceptibility and risk for the year 2050 by incorporating land-use/land-cover (LULC) and future rainfall patterns. The study integrates a Random Forest (RF)-based landslide susceptibility model with an ensemble of Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) rainfall projections, future LULC simulation using a Multilayer Perceptron–Cellular Automata (MLP-CA) framework. It further incorporates an exposure-based Landslide Risk Index (LRI) assessment under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios. The findings show a significant increase in rainfall intensity and urban development by 2050, resulting in an increased risk of landslides. Under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, the very-high susceptibility class is projected to increase from 8.85% in 2024 to 13.25% by 2050, which is approximately 1980 km². In same period and SSP scenario, very-high risk area is expected to increase from 1.18% to 2.73%, which is about 697 km². The most affected areas lie along major transport corridors and densely populated areas of Uttara Kannada, Kodagu and Chikkamagaluru districts in Karnataka state. These results show the combined impact of LULC and climate change on landslides. The study provides crucial information for the development of climate-resilient land-use planning and disaster risk reduction strategies considering probable future scenarios in the Western Ghats region of India.Keywords: Landslide susceptibility, Landslide risk, Shared socioeconomic pathways, Land-use/land-cover change, Western GhatsSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Geography, Geography, Natural hazards

Introduction

Landslides cause substantial economic loss and fatalities every year in the Western Ghats region of India. Vulnerability and exposure to the hazard are often exacerbated by anthropogenic activities like rapid urbanization, deforestation, and subsequent land use/land cover (LULC) changes. Furthermore, climate change is likely to affect landslide occurrence by altering rainfall patterns1,2. Mapping landslide susceptibility and risk is essential for protecting communities now and in the future. The susceptibility map defines areas that have a high likelihood of experiencing landslides. The risk map identifies susceptible areas that have the potential to cause socioeconomic losses. These maps are vital for making informed decisions regarding infrastructure development, land use planning, and disaster risk reduction. A landslide susceptibility map is generated based on past landslide data and causative factors3. Several studies have been done so far, but most of them assume that causative factors are static over time4,5. This assumption limits the practical application of the susceptibility map for long-term landslide risk reduction and mitigation.

Based on temporal variability, the landslide causative factors can be classified as static and dynamic6. Static factors consist of topographic and geological features, including slope gradient, curvature, aspect, elevation, and soil type. Over time, these factors continue to be relatively constant. Whereas the dynamic factors are rainfall patterns and LULC, which experience considerable changes over time. Due to climate change, the frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events are anticipated to increase7,8. Simultaneously, LULC changes caused by infrastructure development and deforestation in hilly regions affect the surface hydrology and soil erosion9,10. The combined effect of these factors will influence the spatial dynamics of landslide susceptibility and risk in the future.

Anthropogenic activities greatly affect the influence of dynamic causative factors in landslide occurance11. Activities such as deforestation, road construction, and urbanization alter the natural slope morphology and weaken the reinforcing effects of vegetation roots. These alterations disrupt hydrological processes by affecting surface runoff and infiltration, which in turn makes slopes more sensitive to rainfall. Future rainfall patterns are commonly obtained from Global Climate Model (GCM) projections developed under the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) and applied to regional-scale assessments. The GCMs are built based on different assumed Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). SSPs represent alternative development trajectories that combine socioeconomic trends with greenhouse gas emission scenarios. Rainfall under higher-emission pathways is projected to intensify and occur more frequently. When these climate-induced rainfall interacts with human-driven LULC modifications, slope instability is substantially amplified. It may lead to higher frequency and wider spatial distribution of landslides. Consequently, human activities not only accelerate LULC transitions but also affect the rainfall threshold for landslide. A handful of studies have attempted to forecast future rainfall and LULC changes according to different SSPs12–16. This offers a chance to assess future landslide susceptibility and risk by considering these dynamic factors.

Suitable modelling techniques should be selected to capture complex aspect of the dynamic landslide susceptibility. Susceptibility mapping approaches can be categorised into heuristic17, statistical, and machine learning-based methods4. Heuristic approaches in susceptibility mapping tend to be subjective and oversimplify the complexity of the causative factors18–20. Statistical methods rely on the assumption of linear relationships and are greatly affected by the quality of the data5,21,22. Machine learning techniques address these challenges by effectively capturing nonlinear relationships, resulting in enhanced accuracy23–25. Techniques such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) and neural networks have found extensive application in the mapping of landslides. However, these models often face difficulties associated with overfitting and sensitivity to parameters. Recent studies indicate that tree-based ensemble methods yield exceptional outcomes in susceptibility mapping26–28. RF and gradient boosting exhibit significant efficacy in capturing nonlinear interactions while reducing the likelihood of overfitting. Consequently, these models are regarded as the most efficient for delineating susceptibility. Very few studies have included dynamic factors in landslide assessments, and most have only evaluated susceptibility without considering the risk involved11,29–31.

Although numerous studies have applied machine learning techniques for landslide susceptibility mapping, most assessments treat key causative factors as static and are limited to present-day conditions4,17,32,33. Relatively few studies considered future climate projections based on CMIP6 and SSPs into landslide susceptibility modelling, and even fewer integrate projected LULC dynamics using cellular automata–based approaches34.Moreover, quantitative assessment of future landslide risk that explicitly combines susceptibility with exposure indicators at a regional scale remains limited. Importantly, to date, no study has systematically projected future landslide susceptibility or risk scenarios for the Western Ghats. Consequently, there is a clear need for landslide susceptibility and risk assessment based on dynamic climatic factors and LULC.

To address the limitations of previous research, this study proposes an integrated framework for evaluating landslide susceptibility and risk. The study aims to generate present-day landslide susceptibility maps using a RF-based model. It further investigates the future landslide susceptibility for the year 2050 by utilising CIMP6 based GCM and dynamic LULC. In addition, the study aims to quantify present and future landslide risk by combining susceptibility with exposure indicators, including population and road density. This integrated framework advances dynamic landslide susceptibility and risk mapping and provides valuable insights for regional disaster risk reduction and mitigation planning.

Study area

The Western Ghats is a mountain range located along the western coastline of India. This region is well known for its ecological diversity and complex terrain. Figure 1 illustrates the study area in India along with the sites of past landslides. The area of interest of this investigation is situated between 12°N and 16°N latitude and 74°E and 76°E longitude, encompassing an estimated area of 45,000 km². It includes seven districts of Karnataka state: Uttara Kannada, Dakshina Kannada, Udupi, Kodagu, Shivamogga, Chikkamagaluru, and Hassan. Elevation in the region ranges from near sea level along the coastal belt to over 1,800 m above mean sea level in the mountainous part. The figure shows few past landslides in lower elevation zones. These events are influenced by local factors such as slope, soil condition, rainfall, and anthropogenic disturbances, rather than elevation alone. Geologically, the area consists of ancient Precambrian crystalline rocks, which are extensively weathered in parts and overlain by colluvial and lateritic soils. The area undergoes significant monsoonal rainfall, mainly occurring between June and September, frequently surpassing 3,000 mm each year in elevated regions. The climatic and geological conditions play a crucial role in the occurrence of landslides. The Western Ghats region represents a diversified combination of urban and rural communities. Shivamogga, Chikkamagaluru, Madikeri, Sirsi, Karwar, and Mangaluru serve as important hubs for administration and commercial activity. Rural communities are dispersed across the region and primarily depend on agriculture, horticulture, and forestry for their livelihoods.

Landslide inventory, elevation, and location of the study area in India.

Historically, this region has experienced a significant number of landslides triggered by rainfall. Analysis of the compiled landslide inventory of more than 1200 landslides from the Geological Survey of India (GSI) indicates spatial variability in historical landslide occurrence across the study area. Across the entire study area, the average landslide density is evaluated based on the data from Bhukosh portal of GSI. Relatively higher landslide densities are observed in Uttara Kannada (0.043 landslides per km²), Shivamogga (0.042 landslides per km²), and Udupi (0.028 landslides per km²), whereas lower densities occur in Dakshina Kannada (0.018 landslides per km²) and Hassan (0.003 landslides per km²). According to the Geological Survey of India (GSI) landslide inventory, landslides in the study area are predominantly rainfall-triggered shallow failures, including shallow soil slides and debris flows. These failures mainly involve soil and weathered rock materials and are typically confined to the near-surface zone.

Data and methods

The overall methodological framework utilises RF based landslide susceptibility modelling and MLP-CA based future LULC simulation. Outputs from the MLP-CA model and CMIP6-based rainfall projections are the inputs to the RF model. The landslide risk is evaluated by integrating susceptibility and exposure to elements at risk such as population and road density. The datasets used in this study were obtained from official data portals of the respective agencies. Details of the data sources and spatial resolution or scale are summarized in Table 1. All spatial datasets were projected to a common coordinate reference system (WGS 84/UTM Zone 43 N) prior to analysis. To ensure spatial consistency among datasets, all layers were resampled to a common spatial resolution. Nearest neighbour resampling was applied to categorical datasets such as LULC and soil maps, while bilinear interpolation was used for continuous datasets including elevation, slope, and rainfall. Spatial data processing and map preparation were carried out using ArcGIS Pro (version 2.9.14). LULC simulation using the MLP-CA model and RF modelling and statistical analyses were conducted in Python (v3.13.8) using the scikit-learn library (v1.7).

Static causative factors

The static causative factors used in this study are elevation, slope, aspect, curvature, soil type, distance to roads, and distance to streams. The spatial distributions of these factors are shown in Fig. 2. Elevation data has been taken from the Cartosat-1 Digital Elevation Model (DEM) at a spatial resolution of 30 m. The same data was used to derive additional topographic attributes of the landscape, such as slope, aspect, curvature, and streams. The soil map used in this study was obtained from the Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD v2.0) of Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), which was revised in 2023. The road network data were obtained as vector layers from the Bhukosh portal of the Geological Survey of India. This vector data was used to produce the distance-to-road raster data. The stream network was derived from the digital elevation model using a flow accumulation approach. A threshold value of 100 cells was applied to the flow accumulation grid to extract the drainage network, following commonly adopted hydrological modelling practices. Distance to streams and distance to roads were computed as Euclidean distance rasters and used directly as continuous predictor variables in the RF model. All these maps were resampled to a uniform 30 m spatial resolution to maintain consistency among all the datasets.

Dynamic causative factors

Rainfall data

Rainfall is a critical dynamic factor influencing the landslide occurrence in the Western Ghats. The datasets were obtained from the Climatic Research Unit Time-Series dataset (CRU TS v4.09). In this study, the period 1995–2024 was selected as it overlaps with the available landslide inventory and other causative factor datasets. Using a recent multi-decadal baseline also reduces the influence of long-term climatic shifts. The source provides monthly rainfall data at a spatial resolution of 0.5° × 0.5°. The mean annual rainfall was computed by averaging monthly data over 30 years (1995–2024). The same was used for the evaluation of the current landslide susceptibility. It is characterised by low emissions and a commitment to robust environmental stewardship. SSP2–4.5 adheres to a path characterised by moderate development and intermediate emissions. SSP3–7.0 adopts a scenario characterised by intense regional rivalry, significant population increases, and a strong dependence on fossil fuel resources. SSP5–8.5 demonstrates swift economic expansion primarily driven by extensive fossil-fuel consumption, resulting in the most significant emission pathway. These scenarios encompass a diverse array of potential socioeconomic and climate outcomes.

A data-driven modelling approach using the MLP-CA algorithm has been employed to produce LULC for the year 2050. This approach effectively reveals the temporal trends of LULC transitions by examining observed change patterns and evaluating the influence of proximity factors like slope, roads, and rivers. The MLP consisted of a single hidden layer and employed a sigmoid activation function in the hidden layer and a softmax activation function in the output layer to estimate class transition probabilities. The model was calibrated using historical LULC transition information and associated driving factors. The MLP component quantifies the transition probability, while the CA component integrates neighbourhood interactions and spatial constraints to effectively simulate realistic dynamics of LULC changes. The proximity factors used in this study are slope, elevation, distance to roads, and distance to rivers. The LULC maps of 2017 and 2024 were used to generate a training dataset and to evaluate transition probabilities. The simulation was conducted in a GIS environment, and the output map representing the projected LULC for 2050 was validated against the logical spatial expansion patterns. The categorical LULC classes has been encoded using integer class labels and directly incorporated into the RF model.

MLP-CA for LULC prediction and mapping

Modelling future LULC transitions necessitates models capable of capturing both the probabilities of transition and the spatial patterns associated with those changes. Traditional methods such as Markov Chain, Cellular Automata (CA), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) have been widely used for evaluating LULC prediction14,41. But these models have certain limitations. For example, Markov Chain does not represent spatial patterns, Cellular Automata relies on fixed neighbourhood rules, and ANNs cannot inherently allocate special changes. To address this limitation few hybrid models have been developed, such as CA–Markov chain and CA-ANN42,43. This study utilises an MLP-CA model to simulate LULC for the year 2050.

The MLP-CA model derives future LULC by learning land-cover transition behaviour from historical maps and spatial driving factors. First, the LULC maps of 2017 and 2024 were overlaid to identify pixel-wise transitions between land-cover classes. Pixels that experienced a change were treated as transition samples, while unchanged pixels represented persistence. For each transition sample, driving factors (neighbourhood constraints) including slope, elevation, distance to roads, and distance to rivers were extracted and used as input features. These transition samples were then used to train a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), which estimates the probability of each land-cover class transitioning to another class under given spatial conditions. The trained MLP thus produces spatially explicit transition probability surfaces for each land-cover class.

Subsequently, the Cellular Automata (CA) component was applied to spatially allocate future land-cover classes by integrating the MLP-derived transition probabilities with neighbourhood interactions within a 5 × 5 moving window. This step accounts for spatial dependence, local competition among land-cover classes, and realistic patch evolution. The future LULC map for the year 2050 was finally generated through iterative allocation based on the combined influence of transition probabilities and neighbourhood constraints. Elements at risk

Quantifying landslide risk requires incorporating spatial exposure indicators that represent the presence and vulnerability of human populations and infrastructure. In this study, two key factors are selected to characterize the elements at risk: population and road density. These indicators collectively evaluate the potential socioeconomic consequences of landslides. Roads are often considered a vital element at risk in the previous studies44, because most shallow landslides occur in the proximity45,46.

The population density layer for the year 2024 has been obtained from the WorldPop database. The spatial distribution of the population density is shown in Fig. 6. The map shows higher concentrations of population around major urban centres such as Mangaluru, Karwar, and Shivamogga. Future population data for 2050 were obtained from the SSP-consistent gridded population projections of Li et al.47. the original dataset of 1 km resolution has been downscaled to 30 m using an area-weighted allocation method to maintain consistency with the preferred geospatial framework. Landslide susceptibility assessment

The future landslide susceptibility has been projected through a machine learning-based approach that incorporates dynamic causative factors across various SSP scenarios. The RF algorithm was utilised for this purpose because of its superior predictive accuracy and established effectiveness in assessing landslide susceptibility. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the enhanced efficacy of RF in comparison to conventional statistical methods and various machine learning approaches48–52. Previous landslide locations for the modeling was obtained from Bhukosh portal of GSI. Non-landslide points were generated using a stratified random sampling approach from areas without recorded landslide events, ensuring no spatial overlap with the landslide inventory. An equal number of landslide and non-landslide points was used to form a balanced dataset, following commonly adopted practices in landslide susceptibility modelling53,54.

The RF algorithm represents an ensemble learning approach that builds numerous decision trees and integrates their results to yield a reliable and precise prediction. The process involves creating several training subsets from the original dataset using the Bootstrap Aggregation method, which employs sampling with replacement so that each subset exhibits some variation. For every subset, a distinct decision tree is constructed by choosing a random selection of features at each node and performing splits based on the feature that exhibits the minimum Gini impurity value. The first node of the decision tree is called root node, and each split creates an internal node. This procedure persists until the predetermined stopping criteria like maximum depth or identical node composition are satisfied and thus, terminal node is formed. When making predictions, all decision trees classify the input data independently, and the majority voting mechanism is used to determine the final class label. Through this ensemble process, RF minimizes overfitting and improves generalization capability55. Figure 8 illustrates the process of the RF model. Categorical variables such as LULC were converted into numerical format using integer encoding, where each category was assigned a unique numeric label. Random Forest is not sensitive to the ordinal nature of these encoded values56. The non-categorical factors were used in their original continuous form without prior classification.

Random Forest Algorithm.

The RF classifier was implemented in Python using the scikit-learn library. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using a grid-based search approach, which is commonly recommended for optimisation of the model55,57. The tested values included number of trees = [100, 150, 200, 250, 300], maximum tree depth = [10, 15, 20], minimum samples for node splitting = [2, 5, 10], and minimum samples per leaf = [1, 2, 4], with the number of features at each split set to the square root of the total features. These ranges were selected based on established Random Forest practices58,59. Each hyperparameter combination was evaluated using bootstrap aggregation with out-of-bag (OOB) estimation enabled. Model performance was assessed using OOB-based AUC values, and the hyperparameter set yielding the highest OOB AUC was selected as the optimal configuration55,60. The optimal configuration includes number of trees = 200, maximum tree depth = 10, minimum samples per leaf = 1, and minimum samples for node splitting = 5.

The susceptibility modelling framework consisted of two primary phases: assessing present-day susceptibility and projecting future susceptibility. The initial phase involved training the RF model with causative factors that reflect the current conditions, resulting in the creation of a susceptibility map. In the next phase, the developed model was employed to forecast landslide susceptibility for the year 2050. It was assumed that static causative factors would remain constant, whereas dynamic factors would be revised according to projections specific to each scenario. The resulting outputs represent landslide susceptibility maps for 2050 under four SSP scenarios.

Landslide risk assessment

A quantitative risk assessment framework has been established by combining the susceptibility index with exposure indicators for both current and future scenarios. The chosen methodology is consistent with established risk assessment frameworks that define risk as a product of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability61,62. In this study, vulnerability is assumed to be spatially embedded within the exposure indicators such as population and road density. Exposure is represented by the population and road density datasets. Population density reflects the distribution of potentially affected human settlements, whereas road density is a proxy for infrastructure exposure. In this study, both exposure indicators were assigned equal weights. Population and road density layers were normalized using min-max scaling to a 0–1 range at 30 m spatial resolution prior to Landslide Risk Index (LRI) computation. The same formulation was utilized for 2050 risk projection using susceptibility outputs and projected population datasets under the four SSP scenarios. Accuracy assessment

The efficacy of the landslide susceptibility model can be assessed using a variety of statistical metrics. The landslide inventory was divided into 70:30 ratio through a stratified random sampling method, a technique that is well-regarded in the literature65,66. 70% of the inventory data was allocated for model training, while the remaining 30% had been allocated aside for testing the model. All accuracy metrics were calculated on the test set to ensure an impartial evaluation of the model. The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC) was employed to measure the model performance in distinguishing between susceptible and non-susceptible locations. In addition, confusion matrix-based classification metrics were also estimated following binarization of the susceptibility probabilities using an optimized threshold. These include accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Results

Future rainfall and LULC predictions

Projected rainfall for the year 2050 reveals significant spatial and temporal variations across the study area. As shown in Fig. 9, all scenarios indicate a pronounced west-to-east gradient in rainfall intensity, which is consistent with the orographic influence of the Western Ghats. The western slopes and coastal districts are expected to receive over 4,500 mm annually. Figure 10 compares the class-wise rainfall distribution between the baseline year (2024) and future projections. The moderate rainfall in 2024 extends over a larger area, while very high rainfall is restricted to the windward slopes due to orographic effects. In comparison, future projections show an increase in higher rainfall classes, indicating a shift toward more intense rainfall conditions. The 4,500–6,000 mm class has been found to expand from less than 1% in 2024 to over 24% by area in 2050 for in all SSP scenarios. Notably, more than 8% of the area has been found to receive annual rainfall of more than 6000 mm, which does not exist in the present scenario. These findings suggest a consistent trend toward intensified rainfall by 2050, regardless of the SSP chosen. This increase in rainfall magnitude and the spatial extent of extreme rainfall zones have direct implications for slope instability, particularly in areas that are already susceptible to landslides.

These zones require prioritized attention in planning and mitigation efforts, as they combine both hazardous terrain conditions and high exposure to the community and infrastructure. Overall, the 2024 risk map offers a baseline for evaluating the spatial interplay of susceptibility and exposure under current conditions, which is essential for establishing reference levels for future scenarios.

The projected escalation in landslide susceptibility and risk is consistent with recent studies from the Western Ghats and other humid tropical mountain regions. Several investigations have shown that slope and rainfall are the dominant controls on landslide occurrence in this region, particularly on slopes steeper than 28° under intense monsoonal rainfall32,67–69. The present study extends these findings by integrating CMIP6-SSP rainfall projections and MLP-CA based LULC simulations, thereby capturing the future dynamics. Recent machine-learning studies have also reported that land-use transitions, especially urban expansion and vegetation loss along hillslopes and transportation corridors, amplify landslide susceptibility when combined with steep terrain and high rainfall70–72. When susceptibility is coupled with population and infrastructure exposure, risk escalation becomes pronounced and the pattern is consistent with recent regional risk-based assessments73.

Conclusions

This study investigates landslide susceptibility and risk in the Western Ghats of Karnataka for the years 2024 and 2050 under various SSP scenarios. The RF susceptibility model was utilized with dynamic causative factors, and the model outputs were employed with exposure elements to evaluate the landslide risk. The following conclusions can be drawn from the findings of this study.

The rainfall projections for 2050 under all SSP scenarios indicate a distinct shift towards higher-intensity rainfall regimes compared to 2024. Under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, approximately one-quarter of the study region is projected to receive annual rainfall in the range of 4500–6000 mm by the year 2050. An additional 8–10% region will enter the > 6000 mm category, which was absent in 2024.
