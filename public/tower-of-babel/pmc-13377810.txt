Future pharmacists in the age of Artificial Intelligence: a mixed-methods study of first-year students in Vietnam
Authors: Tam Ngo Thi, Giang Ha Hai, Long Nguyen Hoang, Duc Hoang Minh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess AI readiness, perceptions, and usage patterns among first-year pharmacy students, and to identify factors associated with AI engagement.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted among 220 first-year pharmacy students. Quantitative data were collected using a structured questionnaire assessing AI readiness (cognition, ability, vision, ethics), perceptions, and usage, followed by multivariable regression analyses. Qualitative insights were obtained through semi-structured interviews with a purposive subsample and analyzed thematically.ResultsStudents showed moderate overall AI readiness, with higher cognition and ability than vision and ethics. AI use was common, primarily for academic tasks, with ChatGPT as the most frequently used tool. Positive perceptions of AI in education predominated, alongside concerns about academic integrity and over-reliance. Higher digital literacy and urban residence were associated with greater AI readiness and more favorable perceptions. Qualitative findings revealed strong interest in structured, pharmacy-specific AI education emphasizing practical application and ethical guidance.ConclusionsFirst-year pharmacy students demonstrate positive attitudes but uneven preparedness for AI. Early, structured integration of AI training that combines technical foundations with ethical and future-oriented perspectives is needed to support responsible AI adoption in pharmacy education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09368-4.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Pharmacy students, AI readiness, Perception, Digital literacy

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing the pharmaceutical industry, offering innovative solutions across the drug discovery, development, and patient care continuum. Globally, AI is applied in drug synthesis planning, formulation development, toxicology prediction, pharmacy automation, and pharmacovigilance, enhancing both efficiency and accuracy [1, 2]. With the rise of machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), natural language processing (NLP), and robotic automation, pharmacy practice is rapidly evolving. These technologies support clinical decisions by predicting therapeutic targets, detecting adverse drug interactions, and optimizing medication dosages [3, 4]. The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated this trend, increasing AI-related pharmaceutical innovations and reducing the time from patent filing to approval [5].

As the pharmaceutical landscape shifts, pharmacy students—future practitioners in AI-augmented environments—must be equipped with both technical literacy and ethical sensitivity. AI readiness involves not only familiarity with digital tools but also the ability to interpret AI-generated outputs critically, ensure responsible data use, and make ethically informed decisions [6, 7]. However, global disparities in digital infrastructure, financial investment, and curriculum design continue to pose significant barriers to AI integration in health education [8, 9]. Pharmacy students’ perceptions of AI influence their willingness to adopt and effectively use these technologies. Studies show that students generally view AI positively, recognizing its capacity to reduce workload, personalize learning, and enhance performance [10, 11]. However, many also express concern over the accuracy and ethical risks of AI, as well as the limited formal training available in their programs. Pilot courses integrating AI platforms, such as Konstanz Information Miner (KNIME) in medicinal chemistry, have proven effective in increasing student engagement and comprehension of AI in pharmaceutical applications [11]. These findings highlight the importance of embedding AI in pharmacy education not only as a technical tool but as a subject of ethical inquiry and practical mastery.

In Vietnam, AI is a national priority under the Digital Transformation Programme, with goals to establish the country as a regional AI hub by 2030 [12]. The government promotes the expansion of AI education through university programs and research centers. In healthcare, AI has begun to be implemented in diagnostic imaging, surgical robotics, and electronic medical record analysis [13]. However, barriers such as underdeveloped infrastructure, limited AI-trained professionals, weak legal frameworks, and fragmented policy coordination still limit its application, especially in pharmacy practice [7]. Currently, there is no standardized national framework for integrating AI competencies into Vietnamese pharmacy curricula, despite growing expectations for pharmacists to operate in data-rich and digitally enhanced environments.

Although studies have shown that Vietnamese medical students are receptive to AI tools like diagnostic support systems and generative AI models [14, 15], research on pharmacy students remains limited. More importantly, no study has focused on first-year students, a critical group whose perceptions are less influenced by curriculum exposure and clinical training. By assessing AI readiness at this early stage, educators can better understand baseline knowledge and digital behavior, which is essential for developing effective, targeted interventions. This study aims to assess AI readiness, perception, and usage among first-year pharmacy students in Vietnam. Specifically, it investigates students’ cognitive, practical, visionary, and ethical preparedness to use AI; their perceptions of both general AI and GenAI tools in education and practice; and their current usage patterns. The study also explores demographic and digital behavior factors associated with AI engagement. Findings are expected to provide foundational insights for pharmacy educators and policymakers working to incorporate AI competencies in Vietnam’s health education systems.

Materials and methods

Study settings and participants

This mixed-method study was conducted in January 2025 at Dai Nam University, a private higher education institution in Hanoi, Vietnam, known for its diverse academic programs, including pharmacy education. In Vietnam, pharmacy education is typically a 5-year undergraduate program delivered primarily through in-person instruction. The target population comprised all first-year students (220 students) enrolled in the Pharmacy program during the second semester of the 2024–2025 academic year. These students were in their second semester of training and had not yet received any formal instruction related to artificial intelligence (AI), health informatics, or digital health in their first-semester curriculum. At the time of the study, there were no formal institutional policies or guidelines governing the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in teaching or learning, and no enterprise or institutionally supported AI tools were officially provided to students or faculty. Students primarily accessed AI tools independently through publicly available platforms.

The study adopted an explanatory sequential design, consisting of two phases: a quantitative survey followed by qualitative interviews. The initial quantitative phase aimed to assess students’ readiness, perceptions, and usage of AI through a structured questionnaire. Based on the quantitative results, a subset of participants was purposively selected for follow-up semi-structured interviews to explore underlying attitudes, contextual factors, and nuanced insights related to their engagement with AI and Generative AI tools.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a first-year student currently enrolled in the university’s pharmacy program, and (2) providing informed consent to participate in both the survey and, if selected, the interview. Students were excluded if they (1) were not present during data collection sessions or (2) submitted incomplete questionnaires lacking responses for key variables related to AI readiness or perception.

A total of 220 eligible students completed the quantitative questionnaire and were included in the analysis (response rate 100%). Among them, 10 students were purposively invited for qualitative interviews, representing a range of demographic characteristics and AI usage levels. The quantitative data collection took place during regular classroom periods with the support of faculty members. Qualitative interviews were conducted in a private room on campus and audio-recorded with consent. Participation in both phases was entirely voluntary, and all respondents were assured of confidentiality and anonymity.

Quantitative data measurement

Quantitative data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire designed to capture pharmacy students’ demographic characteristics, digital engagement, AI readiness, and perceptions regarding the use of artificial intelligence in education. “Urban” and “rural” residence were classified based on official administrative designations in Vietnam. The questionnaire was developed by adapting items from previously validated instruments and tailoring them to the context of Vietnamese pharmacy education.

Specifically, the AI readiness section was adapted from the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale for Medical Students (MAIRS-MS) developed by Karaca et al. (2021) [16], while the perception items were informed by the work of Orok et al. (2024) [17] and further modified for first-year pharmacy students. To ensure contextual and cognitive appropriateness for this population, particularly given their limited clinical exposure, several items from the original scales were reworded to focus on general familiarity, attitudes, and expectations toward AI rather than advanced applications. Prior to full implementation, a pilot test was conducted with 20 first-year pharmacy students to assess the questionnaire’s clarity, cultural relevance, and suitability for this educational level. Following the pilot, 3 items in the perception section were substantially reworded based on student feedback and expert consultation, particularly those requiring advanced clinical knowledge. Content validity was reviewed by three experts in pharmacy education and digital health, who evaluated each item for relevance to the study objectives, clarity of wording, cultural appropriateness, and suitability for first-year pharmacy students. Internal consistency reliability was also evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha: the AI readiness scale yielded a value of 0.87, and the perception scale showed a reliability coefficient of 0.81, indicating good internal consistency.

The finalized version of the questionnaire was administered in January 2025 to all first-year pharmacy students at Dai Nam University, a private higher education institution in Vietnam. Surveys were distributed during regular classroom sessions, and researchers were present to explain the study and address any questions. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all respondents.

AI readiness was measured across four dimensions: cognition, ability, vision, and ethics. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = totally disagree to 5 = totally agree). For each subscale, scores were calculated by summing the numerical responses across all items within that domain. Specifically, the cognition subscale (8 items; score range: 8–40) assessed students’ understanding of AI concepts and functions, while the ability subscale (8 items; range: 8–40) evaluated their confidence in using AI tools. The vision subscale (3 items; range: 3–15) explored students’ expectations regarding future AI applications, and the ethics subscale (3 items; range: 3–15) measured awareness of ethical considerations in AI use. The total AI readiness score was computed by summing the scores of all four subscales, resulting in a possible range from 22 to 110. Higher scores indicate greater levels of AI readiness across cognitive, practical, anticipatory, and ethical domains [16].

Perceptions of AI were assessed through 11 items adapted from Orok et al. (2024) [17], examining students’ attitudes toward both the potential benefits and limitations of AI in learning. All items used the same 5-point Likert scale, and the total perception score was computed by summing all responses. Given the early stage of participants’ training, perception items focused on general educational experiences with AI (e.g., in search engines, productivity tools, or learning platforms), rather than discipline-specific applications.

Qualitative semi-structured interviews

To complement the quantitative component of the study, a qualitative phase was conducted using semi-structured interviews to explore first-year pharmacy students’ perspectives on artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and generative AI (GenAI). The qualitative design aimed to investigate four main themes: students’ awareness of AI/ML and GenAI in general and in pharmacy practice; perceived benefits and challenges associated with the use of these technologies; educational needs and expectations regarding AI/ML and GenAI training in pharmacy curricula; and overall perceptions of the pros and cons of implementing AI-related technologies (S1 File). The interview guide was developed based on a review of relevant literature and expert consultation and was designed to be suitable for participants with limited exposure to professional pharmacy training.

A total of ten first-year pharmacy students were purposively selected from the survey sample to ensure diversity in gender, prior digital experience, and self-reported AI familiarity. The sample included 5 students who reported occasional AI use and 5 who reported frequent use, reflecting variation in digital engagement observed in the survey. In terms of AI familiarity, 4 students were classified as having basic awareness, while 6 reported moderate to high familiarity with commonly used tools such as ChatGPT. Additionally, participants were selected to represent a range of perspectives, including students with predominantly positive perceptions of AI (n = 5) and those expressing concerns or mixed attitudes regarding its use in learning and practice (n = 5). At the time of the study, participants had not received any formal instruction or structured exposure to AI within the pharmacy curriculum, and their understanding of AI was primarily derived from personal use and informal sources.

All interviews were conducted face-to-face in a quiet room on campus in January 2025. Each session lasted between 60 and 90 min and was audio-recorded with prior written consent. The interview guide included open-ended questions and follow-up probes tailored to participants’ academic level. Topics included general awareness and understanding of AI/ML and GenAI, real or perceived applications in pharmacy, perceived opportunities and risks, ethical considerations, and opinions on integrating AI/ML topics into undergraduate training. The language used was adjusted to be accessible to first-year students, focusing on general concepts and encouraging reflection based on personal experience, public media, and early university exposure.

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and anonymized before analysis. Thematic analysis was employed to identify recurring patterns and categories related to each of the four guiding themes. Coding was carried out manually by two researchers to ensure consistency, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Data saturation was considered achieved when no new themes emerged from the last few interviews. This qualitative phase provided additional depth to the quantitative results and helped contextualize students’ responses within their lived experiences and early academic development.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data, AI awareness, usage, and readiness scores. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were used for categorical data. Tobit regression models were employed to examine associations between demographic variables and AI readiness scores across four domains: cognition, ability, vision, and ethics, as these outcomes are bounded composite scores with defined minimum and maximum values. Ordinal logistic regression was used to assess predictors of AI perception and frequency of AI use, given the ordinal nature of these dependent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All quantitative analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.0.

For the qualitative data, a thematic analysis approach was applied to the transcribed interviews to identify key patterns and insights related to students’ perspectives on AI/ML and GenAI. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework: familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report [18]. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts and then met to reconcile differences and refine the coding framework. Themes were generated both deductively—based on the interview guide—and inductively from emergent data. Data saturation was reached after the tenth interview, as no new themes emerged. NVivo software (version 12) was used to support data management and organization.

This integrated analysis allowed for triangulation of findings between the quantitative survey and qualitative interviews, enhancing the validity and depth of the results. The qualitative insights provided contextual understanding of students’ awareness, concerns, and expectations regarding AI/ML and GenAI, complementing the statistical trends observed in the quantitative phase.

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for this research was obtained from an institutional ethics committee. Participation was entirely voluntary. All participants were informed about the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. All data were anonymized, and no personally identifiable information was collected or analyzed.

Qualitative findings

The thematic analysis of the semi-structured interviews with first-year pharmacy students resulted in the identification of four key themes, each reflecting a distinct dimension of student perspectives on the use of AI/ML and GenAI in pharmacy education and practice. These themes included: (1) Awareness of AI/ML and GenAI; (2) Perceived benefits and challenges in pharmacy practice; (3) Overall pros and cons of AI/ML and GenAI; and (4) Educational needs and expectations. Each theme was supported by multiple subthemes and illustrated with direct participant quotations.

Theme 1: awareness of AI/ML and GenAI

Thematic analysis revealed that students’ awareness of AI/ML and GenAI was shaped by general digital exposure rather than formal education or professional context. This theme consists of three interrelated subthemes: (1) General awareness of AI and GenAI tools; (2) Limited understanding of pharmacy-specific applications; and (3) Misconceptions and curiosity about future roles.

Subtheme 1.1: general awareness of AI and GenAI tools in daily life and learning

Most students recognized and frequently used GenAI tools such as ChatGPT, Google Translate, and AI-powered writing assistants. However, many did not initially associate these tools with the term “artificial intelligence” until prompted. Their usage focused on academic support, such as drafting essays, simplifying complex content, and generating study ideas.

“I’ve used ChatGPT for writing assignments and summaries, but I didn’t realize it’s part of AI until now.” (S3).

“I use Grammarly all the time, but I didn’t know it’s actually an AI tool.” (S7).

These tools were viewed as accessible, practical, and user-friendly. Students showed greater familiarity with GenAI than with traditional AI/ML models such as decision support systems or automated dispensing machines.

Subtheme 1.2: limited understanding of pharmacy-specific applications

While students could identify AI/GenAI tools in general life, their knowledge of AI’s role in pharmacy was minimal. Most had not encountered discussions about AI/ML in their coursework or orientation. When asked about AI in pharmacy practice, students either provided vague answers or associated AI with advanced medical procedures or futuristic technologies.

“I think AI is something used in hospitals, like in diagnosis or surgeries, not really in pharmacy.” (S6).

“Maybe AI can check if two drugs interact? I’m not sure, but that would be cool.” (S2).

This limited exposure reflects the current gap between digital literacy and domain-specific application, particularly among first-year students who have not yet taken advanced pharmacy or clinical subjects.

Subtheme 1.3: misconceptions and curiosity about AI’s role in pharmacy

Some participants held misconceptions about what AI can or cannot do, while others expressed genuine curiosity about potential pharmacy applications. A few students believed AI might replace pharmacists entirely, while others were unsure if AI could handle patient interactions or ethical decisions.

“I don’t think AI can talk to patients like pharmacists do, but maybe it can help with calculations.” (S9).

“If AI can manage drug inventory or check for errors, that sounds great, but we don’t know how it works yet.” (S10).

This subtheme reveals a mix of uncertainty and interest among students, suggesting that their current understanding is shaped more by speculation and media exposure than by structured learning.

Overall, students demonstrated basic awareness of AI/ML and GenAI tools through personal and academic use, particularly with widely accessible platforms. However, their understanding of how these technologies apply to pharmacy practice was fragmented and superficial. The results suggest a strong need to introduce foundational AI/ML concepts in early-stage pharmacy education to help students make meaningful connections between digital tools and professional applications.

Theme 2: perceived benefits and challenges in pharmacy practice

Students expressed a range of perceptions regarding the potential of AI/ML and GenAI in pharmacy practice. While most participants recognized the practical benefits of these technologies, they also voiced concerns about limitations, risks, and the future role of pharmacists. The theme is organized into three subthemes: (1) Anticipated benefits in pharmacy operations and patient care (2), Concerns about accuracy, trust, and human replacement, and (3) Mixed feelings on the human–AI relationship in practice.

Subtheme 2.1: anticipated benefits in pharmacy operations and patient care

Many students were optimistic about how AI/ML could support pharmacists in daily tasks. Commonly cited applications included checking drug interactions, managing medication inventory, reducing human error, and supporting clinical decision-making. Participants believed that if used appropriately, AI could improve patient safety, save time, and enhance service quality.

“If AI can help pharmacists avoid mistakes with medications, that would be very useful.” (S1).

“It could remind us of allergies or drug interactions that we might miss.” (S4).

“I think AI can speed up the process, like helping with prescriptions or organizing stock.” (S7).

Even though most students lacked specific knowledge of pharmacy information systems, they were able to imagine plausible use cases for AI based on general exposure to technology and logic.

Subtheme 2.2: concerns about accuracy, trust, and human replacement

Despite the positive expectations, several participants raised concerns regarding the reliability and trustworthiness of AI systems. Accuracy of information, especially in clinical decisions, was a key issue. Some students expressed worry that AI might make mistakes or be too dependent on programming without human judgment. Others feared that heavy reliance on AI could lead to the erosion of pharmacists’ roles and professional autonomy.

“I worry that if AI makes a mistake, who will be responsible?” (S8).

“Sometimes AI gives wrong answers. If that happens in real pharmacy, it could be dangerous.” (S3).

“I don’t want AI to replace pharmacists. We still need people to talk to patients.” (S2).

This subtheme reflects a tension between seeing AI as a helpful tool and fearing its potential to undermine professional expertise and responsibility.

Subtheme 2.3: mixed feelings on the Human–AI relationship in practice

Participants expressed mixed views about how AI and pharmacists might work together in the future. Some believed that AI should remain in a supportive role, assisting pharmacists but not replacing them. Others envisioned more integrated systems, where human professionals and AI collaborate, especially for data-heavy tasks, while human empathy remains central to patient interaction.

“AI can do the calculations and alerts, but pharmacists should still explain things to patients.” (S5).

“It’s okay if AI helps with routine stuff, but patients still need someone to listen and care.” (S10).

“Maybe AI and pharmacists can work together, like a team. Each one does what they’re best at.” (S6).

These perspectives suggest that while students are open to technological innovation, they value the human aspects of pharmacy practice and advocate for balanced, collaborative integration rather than substitution.

In summary, students recognized multiple ways AI/ML and GenAI could enhance efficiency, safety, and accuracy in pharmacy practice. However, they also voiced concerns about system reliability, professional identity, and the limits of machine judgment. The findings reflect a cautiously optimistic view of AI in pharmacy, with students supporting its use as a tool rather than a replacement, and emphasizing the importance of maintaining human connection and ethical responsibility in patient care.

Theme 3: overall pros and cons of AI/ML and GenAI

Students articulated a nuanced perspective on the broader advantages and disadvantages of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and generative AI (GenAI) across educational and professional contexts. While they acknowledged significant benefits in terms of accessibility, productivity, and support for learning, they also raised ethical and practical concerns about over-reliance, misinformation, and the erosion of critical thinking. This theme is presented in three subthemes: (1) Perceived overall advantages (2), Risks and limitations, and (3) The dilemma of convenience versus critical engagement.

Subtheme 3.1: perceived overall advantages

Many students expressed appreciation for the ways AI/ML and GenAI improve academic and personal productivity. GenAI tools, especially ChatGPT, were highlighted as helpful for summarizing difficult topics, generating study ideas, translating text, and enhancing writing skills. In a broader sense, AI was perceived as a time-saving assistant that could help streamline both educational and clinical workflows.

“I like how fast ChatGPT gives me an answer. It saves me time when I study.” (S9).

“Even if I don’t understand a topic, I can ask ChatGPT and get a simpler version.” (S5).

“In the future, AI could help us do paperwork faster and let pharmacists focus more on patients.” (S4).

The convenience and responsiveness of GenAI tools made them especially attractive for self-learning and revision, even if students acknowledged that their understanding of the underlying AI processes was limited.

Subtheme 3.2: risks and limitations

Despite the perceived benefits, students were aware of several limitations associated with AI/ML and GenAI. One common concern was misinformation—students noted that AI-generated answers can sometimes be incorrect or misleading, especially when used uncritically. Another significant worry was plagiarism, as some students admitted to copying content directly without verifying its accuracy or originality. Over-reliance on AI was also seen as a risk to the development of independent thinking and professional judgment.

“Sometimes ChatGPT gives wrong or outdated information, so I have to double-check.” (S6).

“I copied answers from ChatGPT before, and later found out they weren’t even correct.” (S3).

“If we always depend on AI, we might get lazy and stop thinking for ourselves.” (S2).

Students were also skeptical about trusting AI in serious clinical settings, emphasizing the potential consequences of incorrect data when applied to patient care.

Subtheme 3.3: the dilemma of convenience versus critical engagement

A recurring theme in the interviews was the tension between AI’s convenience and the need for critical engagement. Students were often caught between using AI for quick support and recognizing its limitations. While AI was praised for its speed and accessibility, participants acknowledged that relying too heavily on it could hinder their learning and future competence as pharmacists.

“It’s easy to just ask ChatGPT, but sometimes I wonder if I’m actually learning or just getting the answer.” (S8).

“It helps me now, but I don’t want to be the kind of pharmacist who only follows what a computer says.” (S10).

“AI is helpful, but we need to use our own brain too—especially when it comes to people’s health.” (S1).

This subtheme highlights students’ internal dialogue about using AI responsibly—benefiting from it while maintaining human reasoning, ethical awareness, and accountability.

Overall, students recognized AI/ML and GenAI as powerful tools with strong potential to enhance learning and pharmacy practice. However, they remained cautious about accuracy, over-dependence, and ethical risks. Their responses reflected a developing awareness of the importance of balancing the efficiency offered by AI with the need for human judgment and critical thinking. These findings point to the value of integrating digital literacy and ethical reflection into pharmacy training to guide responsible use of AI technologies.

Theme 4: educational needs and expectations

A dominant theme emerging from the interviews was the students’ strong desire for formal training on artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and generative AI (GenAI) within the pharmacy curriculum. Despite frequent informal exposure to these technologies in everyday life and study habits, participants reported a lack of structured instruction and expressed a clear need for guided learning. This theme is structured around three subthemes: (1) Absence of formal instruction in pharmacy training (2), Suggested content and delivery preferences, and (3) Emphasis on ethics and responsible use.

Subtheme 4.1: absence of formal instruction in pharmacy training

All interviewed students confirmed that they had not received any lectures, workshops, or official discussions about AI, ML, or GenAI during their first year of pharmacy education. Their understanding of these technologies came almost exclusively from personal use of platforms like ChatGPT or from general media. Several students voiced concern that their current education was not keeping pace with technological changes in healthcare.

“So far, we’ve had no subjects or even a short talk about AI. Everything I know is from social media or trying out ChatGPT on my own.” (S4).

“We’re learning traditional subjects, but no one has explained how AI might affect our work in the future.” (S7).

Students acknowledged that, while their academic foundation was still being formed, early introduction to AI concepts could help them stay informed and build confidence gradually.

Subtheme 4.2: suggested content and delivery preferences

Participants expressed strong interest in learning not only how AI works, but how it is practically applied in pharmacy practice. Many suggested integrating modules or elective courses focusing on the real-world use of AI tools in drug safety monitoring, patient counseling, data management, and pharmacy automation. Preferred teaching formats included practical workshops, guided tool demonstrations, and clinical case simulations. Several also proposed offering online tutorials or optional AI literacy classes to accommodate different learning styles (Table 5).

Subtheme 4.3: emphasis on ethics and responsible use

Many students emphasized the importance of not only learning to use AI tools, but also understanding how to use them responsibly. They expressed concerns about the accuracy of AI outputs, the potential for plagiarism, and the risks of relying too heavily on automation in both academic and professional settings. Students suggested that AI education should include content on academic integrity, digital professionalism, data privacy, and patient safety.

“If I use ChatGPT for an assignment, I don’t know if that’s cheating or not. We need someone to explain the limits.” (S9).

“AI is helpful, but what if it makes a wrong suggestion with a patient? We need to learn when to trust it and when not to.” (S2).

They also requested more critical reflection activities in the curriculum to build digital responsibility and decision-making skills when using AI technologies.

Finally, the findings suggest the need for a structured, multi-dimensional framework to guide the integration of AI/ML and GenAI into pharmacy education. This framework was developed through triangulation of both quantitative and qualitative results, where identified gaps were informed by survey findings (e.g., moderate readiness scores across cognition, ability, vision, and ethics) and further contextualized by interview themes reflecting limited domain-specific understanding and ethical concerns, while educational needs and preferred delivery methods were primarily derived from qualitative insights (as summarized in Table 5). The framework includes four essential pillars: (1) Foundational knowledge of AI/ML and GenAI concepts; (2) Practical application through tools and pharmacy-specific scenarios; (3) Ethical awareness, including responsible use and academic integrity; and (4) Development of critical thinking skills for evaluating and validating AI-generated outputs. These elements collectively ensure that students are not only digitally literate, but also professionally prepared to navigate AI-integrated pharmacy environments. Discussion

This study explored how first-year pharmacy students engage with artificial intelligence (AI), focusing on their readiness, perceptions, and usage patterns. The findings indicate that while students express strong interest and positive attitudes toward AI, their preparedness is uneven across different domains. Digital familiarity and exposure play a key role in shaping AI readiness, particularly in cognitive and practical dimensions, while vision for future use and ethical engagement show room for improvement. Qualitative findings further revealed that students often rely on GenAI tools for academic support but lack awareness of AI applications in pharmacy practice. They also expressed a strong desire for structured AI education, including practical training and ethical guidance. These patterns mirror global trends in pharmacy education, highlighting both the potential and the challenges of integrating AI into professional training.

Students demonstrated moderate cognitive readiness, with a mean score of 27.81 out of 40, indicating a foundational understanding of AI concepts but also revealing knowledge gaps. This aligns with findings by Busch et al. (2023), where 63% of pharmacy students reported limited general knowledge of AI despite favorable attitudes [19]. Cognitive readiness is essential for meaningful AI integration, as it reflects students’ grasp of key principles, terms, and AI functions [16]. The positive association between tech savviness and cognitive readiness in our findings supports the assertion that digital literacy is a critical enabler of AI competence. To improve this domain, pharmacy curricula should incorporate AI fundamentals early on, ensuring students develop the critical thinking skills needed to assess and engage with evolving technologies [6, 16].

In terms of ability, students scored moderately (mean = 28.53/40), suggesting reasonable confidence in using AI for academic tasks such as completing assignments and retrieving drug information. However, the gap between academic usage and professional application remains notable. As observed by Busch et al. (2023), students who completed AI-focused courses reported higher preparedness for clinical application [19]. The American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) has emphasized the importance of equipping future pharmacists with practical AI skills to determine appropriate usage in medication-related tasks (Schutz et al., 2020) [20]. In our study, tech-savvy students again reported higher ability scores, reinforcing the importance of hands-on exposure. Nevertheless, this “ability” may reflect functional familiarity rather than true competency; for example, retrieving drug information using AI may indicate basic prompt usage but does not necessarily demonstrate critical appraisal of AI-generated outputs. This raises concerns about potential over-confidence and highlights the need to explicitly train students in evaluating accuracy, reliability, and clinical relevance of AI-assisted information. Pharmacy education should therefore provide experiential learning opportunities that simulate real-world scenarios to support the application of AI in clinical contexts.

The vision domain reflected the lowest readiness among students (mean = 10.55/15), indicating uncertainty about AI’s broader role in transforming pharmacy practice. Although students generally support integrating AI into education, fewer were confident in articulating how AI might reshape their future professional roles. This finding echoes Busch et al. (2023), where students expressed a desire for more AI education but lacked clear foresight about its implications [19]. Schutz et al. (2020) argue that AI will increasingly support decision-making and automate routine tasks, suggesting a need for future-oriented thinking in pharmacy training [20]. In our data, students from urban areas and those with high digital literacy showed stronger visionary readiness, implying that environmental and educational exposure contribute to future-oriented AI competence. Addressing this requires interdisciplinary approaches, including case-based discussions and critical dialogues on the long-term integration of AI in healthcare.

Ethical readiness was modest (mean = 10.69/15), highlighting an awareness of ethical concerns but also the need for deeper engagement. As AI tools become more embedded in healthcare decisions, students must understand issues like data privacy, bias in algorithms, and academic integrity. Our findings showed that students with higher ethical readiness also held more favorable perceptions of AI, suggesting that ethical reflection supports responsible adoption. This is consistent with Busch et al. (2023), who found that tech-savvy and senior students were more concerned about ethical and legal dimensions [19]. However, only a small proportion of students in our study reported regularly following healthcare AI updates, pointing to a gap in ongoing ethical awareness. Embedding ethical frameworks and reflective practices into AI-related courses—through debates, case studies, and discussion of real-world dilemmas—will be essential to cultivate ethical competence.

Students’ overall perceptions of AI in pharmacy education were largely positive. A majority agreed that AI enhances learning, improves access to information, and contributes to academic success. These findings are supported by Orok et al. (2024), who found that 85.3% of students believed AI tools like ChatGPT improved academic performance [17]. At the same time, students expressed concern about AI-related distractions, reduced classroom structure, and the potential for academic dishonesty. Similar concerns have been reported in Japan, where students raised questions about AI’s impact on critical thinking and independent learning [21]. These dual perspectives suggest that students are not only receptive to AI’s benefits but are also mindful of its limitations, reinforcing the importance of responsible integration in educational settings.

AI usage among students was high, particularly with tools like ChatGPT, which were used for studying, homework, and research. This reflects international trends where ChatGPT is increasingly embedded in student learning habits [22]. In our study, university curricula and social networks were the main sources of AI exposure. However, relatively few students reported keeping up with AI developments in healthcare, indicating a gap between immediate use and sustained professional engagement. Research by Culp et al. (2024) shows that embedding AI in specific pharmacy courses significantly boosts interest and long-term involvement, suggesting that structured exposure may promote deeper engagement [11].

Our regression results highlight that digital literacy and demographic factors influence AI readiness, perception, and use. Students from urban areas and those identifying as very tech-savvy had higher scores across nearly all domains. AI cognition and ethics, in particular, were significant predictors of positive perceptions toward AI, while cognitive readiness also predicted more frequent use. These findings are aligned with Busch et al. (2023), who emphasize the role of digital competence and ethical reflection in shaping students’ confidence and attitudes toward AI [19]. Notably, similar patterns have been reported among medical students, suggesting that determinants of AI readiness, such as digital literacy and exposure, may be consistent across health disciplines, although differences in clinical training pathways could influence domain-specific application and confidence [6, 14, 16, 19]. This underscores the importance of integrating both conceptual understanding and ethical reasoning in pharmacy curricula to support informed and responsible engagement with AI technologies.

Qualitative findings offered important contextual understanding of students’ engagement with AI/ML and GenAI, particularly their early awareness and exposure. Although students demonstrated high familiarity with GenAI tools such as ChatGPT, mainly for academic purposes like summarizing materials or generating explanations, they showed limited understanding of AI/ML applications in pharmacy-specific contexts. This aligns with findings by Hasan et al. (2024), who reported that while 92.6% of pharmacy students were aware of AI, only a small proportion had a solid grasp of its technical and professional implications [23]. Similarly, Orok et al. (2024) found 88% of Nigerian pharmacy students recognized chat-based AI, but practical knowledge remained shallow [17]. In our study, students often described AI in vague terms, associating it with hospitals or futuristic technologies, rather than with specific tools like drug-interaction checkers or clinical decision support systems used in pharmacy. This gap highlights the need for early and contextualized AI education to bridge familiarity and functional understanding.

Despite their limited technical background, students expressed both enthusiasm and caution when reflecting on the potential integration of AI/ML and GenAI into pharmacy practice. Many participants cited advantages such as improving efficiency, reducing errors, and supporting patient counseling, echoing findings from Hasan et al. (2024), where 96.2% of students in a Middle Eastern study believed AI could enhance pharmacy services [23]. The role of AI in automating routine pharmacy tasks and optimizing medication management has also been acknowledged in other literature [9]. However, our participants also voiced concerns about AI’s reliability, over-dependence, and the potential for displacing pharmacists—a sentiment also documented by previous research [23, 24], which noted widespread anxiety about job security and data privacy. This concern can be further conceptualized as the risk of “deskilling,” where over-reliance on AI may reduce the development or maintenance of core clinical reasoning and decision-making skills [25]. In addition, students worried that GenAI might encourage academic shortcuts, reducing engagement in critical thinking—concerns consistent with a prior study [26], which warned that while GenAI enhances personalized learning, it also risks undermining academic integrity.

A major theme emerging from the interviews was students’ strong demand for structured AI/ML and GenAI education tailored to pharmacy. Almost all participants reported receiving no formal instruction in these areas, and many felt unprepared to use AI responsibly in their future careers. This reflects broader trends in the literature: Busch et al. (2023) found that 72% of students across multiple countries wanted more AI-related content integrated into their curriculum, and those with prior exposure felt significantly more confident in using AI tools professionally [27]. Students in our study emphasized the importance of learning not just how to use tools like ChatGPT, but also how to critically evaluate their outputs, apply them in pharmacy-specific scenarios, and understand the ethical implications of their use. These preferences align with Rammal et al. (2024), who emphasized that without structured training, pharmacy graduates may struggle to effectively leverage AI technologies in clinical environments [9]. Students also suggested active learning formats—such as tool demonstrations and problem-based workshops—over traditional lectures, pointing to an emerging framework for designing AI-integrated pharmacy education that is both practical and ethically grounded. Although this framework was developed within the Vietnamese context, the identified gaps and educational needs are consistent with international literature, suggesting that the proposed model may be transferable to other pharmacy education settings with similar levels of AI integration.

The implications for pharmacy education are far-reaching and underscore the urgency of proactive curriculum reform. Beyond fostering technical proficiency in using AI and ML tools, educators must also address the broader dimensions of AI readiness—cognitive understanding, ethical reasoning, and visionary thinking. As AI technologies become increasingly embedded in healthcare systems, pharmacists must be trained not only to operate tools such as clinical decision support systems or generative AI platforms but also to critically assess outputs, anticipate unintended consequences, and ensure patient-centered care. This requires a paradigm shift in curriculum design, emphasizing interdisciplinary thinking, ethical literacy, and adaptability in a rapidly evolving digital landscape.

Nevertheless, the transformation of pharmacy education faces structural and institutional challenges. A major barrier is the limited AI-related expertise among faculty members, which constrains the ability to develop and deliver relevant content. As highlighted by previous studies [10], institutional readiness, both in terms of digital infrastructure and faculty development, plays a crucial role in determining the success of AI curriculum integration. To overcome these barriers, targeted investments are necessary: training programs for educators, partnerships with AI experts, and access to updated resources and tools. Moreover, the inclusion of AI and data literacy as core educational competencies should be mandated at the national or institutional level. Ethical principles, particularly those related to data privacy, algorithmic bias, and professional accountability, must be interwoven throughout training modules, ensuring that pharmacy graduates not only use AI effectively but also responsibly.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of self-reported data introduces inherent biases, including recall bias and social desirability bias, which may have influenced participants’ responses. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes the ability to establish causality or assess changes over time. Third, the sample was limited to first-year students from a single private university in Vietnam, which restricts the generalizability of findings to other contexts or student populations. It is important to acknowledge that participants were first-year students with limited or no clinical exposure; therefore, their perspectives on “pharmacy practice” largely reflect perceived or imagined roles rather than real-world experience, which may influence the accuracy and depth of their responses. To build on this foundational work, future research should adopt longitudinal designs that track AI readiness and perception over multiple academic years, capturing the impact of evolving curricular and technological exposure. Multi-institutional and cross-cultural studies could help identify context-specific barriers and enablers, while comparative research across health disciplines (e.g., medicine, nursing, public health) may provide insights into interprofessional differences in AI adoption, informing collaborative training models for digital health workforce development.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study affirms that pharmacy students are increasingly engaging with AI tools and hold predominantly positive views regarding their role in education. Readiness to adopt AI is influenced by digital familiarity and exposure, while ethical reflection and foundational understanding of AI predict both perception and frequency of use. As AI continues to redefine healthcare delivery and education, pharmacy programs must adapt by embedding comprehensive AI training, emphasizing ethical considerations, and fostering digital literacy to prepare students.
