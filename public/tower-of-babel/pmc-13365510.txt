Structural analysis of the rural household food security system in Gorgan County, Iran
Authors: Ahmad Abedi Sarvestani, Manizheh Mohammadkhani, Farhad Shirani Bidabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFood security is a complex, dynamic, and multidimensional issue, particularly critical for rural households facing intertwined economic, social, infrastructural, and environmental challenges. This study aims to analyze the structural configuration of the rural household food security system in Gorgan County, northern Iran, using Interpretive Structural Modeling. Thirty-four key factors were identified across five major components–economic, infrastructure & access, socio-demographic, nutrition & health, and external risks- based on literature review and expert input. A structured questionnaire was administered to local public sector experts to quantify the direct and indirect relationships among these factors. Unlike most Iranian rural food security studies that mainly report determinant sets, we combine ISM with MICMAC cross-impact analysis to quantify direct and indirect interdependencies and distinguish upstream drivers from dependent outcomes for scenario-oriented policy design. The findings revealed that the rural household food security system is structurally unstable, primarily influenced by economic factors and external risks such as inflation and climate change. Among factors, inflation has the strongest influence on the system, while household head income is the most critical in terms of dependence. Additionally, components like infrastructure & access and nutrition & health play long-term, outcome-focused roles rather than acting as short-term drivers. The results underscore the importance of prioritizing economic empowerment, enhancing adaptive capacity, and strengthening multi-level policy coordination to improve rural household food security. Introduction

Food security is a fundamental pillar of sustainable development and a key component of social justice and welfare. Beyond its macro-level national and global dimensions, household food security is shaped by interacting economic, social, environmental, and institutional factors1. Rural households are often particularly vulnerable due to their dependence on natural resources, limited access to supportive services, and income fluctuations tied to agricultural activities2. These vulnerabilities are intensified under climate change, economic instability, rural migration, and ineffective support policies, which can undermine the sustainability of rural household food security systems3. Therefore, research should go beyond descriptive accounts and clarify the causal structures and interrelationships among factors that influence the stability of household food security.

Food security is a multidimensional, dynamic, and systemic concept that has evolved substantially since the 1970s4. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization, food security is achieved when “all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life”5. This definition is commonly operationalized through four interrelated dimensions: food availability6, food access7, food utilization8, and stability9. The four dimensions of food security provide a framework for analyzing food security at various levels1. Against this conceptual framing, it is important to examine how these dimensions and drivers have been studied in Iran’s rural context and what gaps remain in explaining their interdependencies.

In Iran, rural household food security is a particularly policy-relevant challenge because households often face concurrent macroeconomic volatility (e.g., inflation, exchange-rate pressures, and food-price instability) alongside resource constraints (e.g., water scarcity and land-use change) and recurring exogenous shocks (e.g., climate extremes). These pressures can cascade through the rural food security system, affecting production conditions, market access, purchasing power, and ultimately dietary outcomes–so identifying how key factors interact is essential for effective intervention design. This motivates the need for a systems-structural analysis that clarifies upstream leverage points for Iranian rural development and food security planning. In this context, a growing body of rural research has examined household food security and highlighted the roles of economic, social, environmental, and institutional determinants operating at both micro and macro scales. The Iranian evidence base is summarized in Table 1, which groups the most frequently reported determinants into key components and highlights the need to examine their interdependencies. This synthesis motivates our study by showing that prior work largely reports determinant sets, while leaving the interdependencies and hierarchical influence structure among these factors underexplored.

Building on this evidence, we move beyond determinant lists by using an ISM–MICMAC structural analysis to map direct and indirect interdependencies among factors, organize them into hierarchical influence levels, and distinguish upstream drivers from dependent outcomes. This also enables a controllability-oriented interpretation of leverage points, highlighting actionable priorities across national/sectoral versus household/local level. This structural focus is particularly relevant because much of the existing empirical work remains limited in capturing feedbacks and multi-step causal pathways across interacting variables. Specifically, our contribution is threefold. First, we translate a theory-driven framework into a context-validated operational variable set for rural Gorgan through expert refinement. Second, we move beyond determinant lists by combining ISM with MICMAC cross-impact analysis to distinguish upstream drivers, linkage (bipolar) factors, and dependent outcomes, and to rank key drivers by total effects and controllability. Third, we use this structural evidence to support scenario-oriented and multi-level policy design by differentiating macro-, sectoral-, and local-level leverage points for improving rural household food security.

Despite valuable insights from prior studies, modelling approaches often struggle to represent feedbacks, indirect effects, and interdependencies and adaptive behaviors across scales23, and much of the existing work relies on static statistical associations without sufficiently addressing causal relationships among interacting variables24. Given continuous change and emerging pressures, future-oriented analysis is essential for anticipating challenges and designing proactive strategies. In this study, cross-impact analysis–combined with scenario development–supports modelling the internal structure of the rural household food security system, identifying key drivers, and exploring plausible futures for more forward-looking policy design. Accordingly, this research develops an analytical framework to evaluate the sustainability of rural household food security by identifying key drivers, examining causal relationships, and developing plausible future scenarios, while explicitly incorporating emerging risks such as climate change.

Gorgan County, a major agricultural hub in northern Iran, features diverse climatic conditions, heterogeneous livelihood systems, and simultaneous challenges such as land-use change, water scarcity, and shifting consumption patterns. These characteristics make its rural areas a suitable case for in-depth investigation of rural food security dynamics25. Foresight-oriented structural approaches that examine interrelationships among variables and enable the projection of plausible futures provide powerful tools for addressing complex issues such as food security26.

Related work

At the household level, socio-economic conditions and household characteristics—such as income, gender of the household head, and household size—are widely associated with food security status27. Prior research also emphasizes the role of age28, employment and education29, and access to adequate, affordable, and culturally acceptable food30. Evidence from Bangladesh shows that household size, income, education, and household-head gender can strongly shape food security outcomes, with female-headed households and those with lower educational attainment more prone to food insecurity31. In rural contexts, farm size, land ownership, soil quality, and access to credit are repeatedly identified as key determinants32,33, and evidence from Ethiopia similarly indicates that these factors can be central in shaping rural household food security28. At broader regional scales, policies and context variables–such as wages, housing costs, food assistance programs, and taxation–also influence household food security34, with evidence from the United States suggesting that wage increases and reduced tax burdens can improve food security35.

Resilience of local food systems to climate impacts is often linked to land tenure and communal resource governance36, and evidence from Africa suggests that strengthening land rights and engaging local communities in resource management can enhance resilience and food security37. Research in India highlights unstable market access and limited income diversification as major drivers of food security instability38. Other studies emphasize women’s empowerment, governance quality, and diversification of economic resources as key drivers of improved household food security, noting heightened vulnerability among women and rural residents39. Related evidence also identifies market access, institutional support, and livelihood resilience as critical components for sustaining rural household food security37. Importantly, relationships among variables in a food security system can be complex, multidirectional, and nonlinear40,41; for instance, stronger social capital may improve access to support resources and income stability, while missing agricultural insurance can weaken the effectiveness of other determinants.

Theoretical framework

From a theoretical perspective, food security is closely linked with concepts such as household resilience42, sustainable livelihoods43, and food justice44. Within the Sustainable Livelihoods Approach, food security is regarded as one of the outcomes of livelihood sustainability45. Households’ ability to cope with external shocks depends on five types of capital. These include natural capital (e.g., land, water resources, and soil quality) and human capital (education, skills, health, and nutrition). They also include financial capital (income, employment, income diversification, crop insurance, and credit access) and physical capital (infrastructure such as roads, markets, and public services). Finally, social capital refers to support networks, social participation, trust, and family cohesion46. A deficiency in any of these capitals can undermine household resilience and reduce food security. Another significant perspective is the Food Systems Approach, which provides a comprehensive analytical framework to examine the stages of production, processing, distribution, marketing, consumption, and waste management, along with their socio-economic and environmental implications47. This approach emphasizes that food security is not solely determined by food production volumes but also by the dynamic interactions among actors, institutions, infrastructure, policies, and the environment throughout the entire food chain. Key variables in this framework include food prices, dietary diversity, calorie intake, food quality and safety, and the scale of food waste. Theoretical contributions in this field identify three central functions of food systems: ensuring food and nutritional security for all, safeguarding environmental sustainability, and supporting fair and sustainable livelihoods for stakeholders. Thus, policies aimed at improving food security must be multi-level, participatory, and adapted to local contexts48. In addition, Amartya Sen’s Entitlement Approach argues that food insecurity does not necessarily arise from food shortages but from inequities in access to resources. This perspective highlights structural inequalities and the role of social justice, identifying barriers such as poverty, lack of land ownership, insecure employment, gender discrimination, and weak support institutions as key factors limiting access to adequate and nutritious food49–52. This theoretical lens provides a foundation for analyzing the causal dimensions of inequality in food security.

Building on the aforementioned approaches and focusing on micro-level rural households, this study presents a theory-driven conceptual framework derived from the literature (Table 2). It defines five broad conceptual clusters—economic, social, infrastructural, nutrition & health, and external risks—and lists illustrative variables at a conceptual level, prior to context-specific operationalization for the case study. The economic cluster includes variables such as income, income diversification, employment, land ownership, crop insurance, and food prices. The social cluster comprises education, social participation, gender of the household head, and social support. The infrastructural cluster encompasses access to markets and public services, as well as food access and support-related variables such as access to food supply centers, distance to food markets, the availability of food production resources (e.g., water and land), food aid, and the efficiency of the food marketing system. The nutrition & health cluster includes variables such as food consumption quality, household dietary diversity, calorie intake level, safe food consumption, and the amount of food waste. Finally, the external risks cluster covers exogenous shocks such as climate change, war and conflict, and pandemics. This integrated framework enables a comprehensive and multidimensional analysis of food security at the micro-level of rural households. By considering economic, social, infrastructural, nutrition & health, and external risk dimensions, it examines the roles of both structural and household-level factors in shaping food security status. Furthermore, by emphasizing dietary quality and health, the framework strengthens attention to the qualitative dimension of food security alongside its quantitative aspect. Analysis based on this framework provides a suitable foundation for targeted policymaking and enhancing the resilience of livelihood systems against climate-related and economic shocks. It also contributes to a better understanding of the complex interactions among policies, institutions, markets, and the living conditions of rural households. For clarity and consistency across the manuscript, these conceptual clusters are operationalized in the subsequent analyses using standardized labels: the “social” cluster is reported as “socio-demographic”, and the “infrastructural” cluster as “infrastructure & access”. Two dimensions emphasized in the reviewed Iranian evidence base- local community participation/social capital and governance/policy performance (Table 1)–are conceptually acknowledged in this framework (Table 2). In the operational ISM/MICMAC model, governance-related mechanisms are represented through policy and institutional proxy variables (e.g., food production support policies, benefiting from economic support, existence of food aid, and efficient food marketing system), while policy-induced uncertainty is reflected through macroeconomic volatility variables (inflation, exchange rate, and food-price fluctuation). We added this clarification to avoid ambiguity between conceptual coverage and operational measurement.

Materials and methods

The area of Gorgan County in Golestan Province is approximately 1,615.2 km². It is located in the northern part of Iran, within the southern region of the Caspian Sea basin (Fig. 1). The County is bordered by the Caspian Sea to the north and the Alborz Mountain range to the south, giving it a diverse landscape that includes plains, hills, and forested areas. According to the 2016 census, Gorgan County has a total population of about 480,541 people. Among this population, around 114,837 individuals live in rural areas. This means that nearly 23.9% of the County’s population resides in villages and rural settlements, while the remaining population lives in urban areas, mainly in the city of Gorgan, which serves as the County’s capital and the provincial center.

Geographic map of the study area. This figure was created by the authors.

The present study employs Interpretive Structural Modeling (ISM), a widely used exploratory method in management research designed to analyze complex systems through an interpretive paradigm. ISM systematically identifies key variables within a multifaceted phenomenon, maps their interrelationships, and explores pathways for development by organizing these variables into multiple hierarchical levels. This approach accommodates interdependencies among criteria, whether individual or grouped, facilitating a comprehensive analysis of the system’s components and their interactions, thereby enhancing understanding of the system’s overall complexity. Complementing ISM, cross-impact analysis offers a robust framework for examining the direct and indirect influences among key system variables53. This technique is extensively applied in foresight and scenario planning studies to evaluate the likelihood of various outcomes within anticipated scenarios54.

This research was conducted in two stages. The first stage aimed to compile and operationalize factors corresponding to the main conceptual components of the rural household food security system (Table 2) in the rural areas of Gorgan County. For this purpose, we combined a targeted literature review with two exploratory key-informant interviews. After extracting candidate factors from prior studies, we interviewed two faculty members specializing in food security who had direct field experience in rural communities. Their feedback was used to clarify definitions, reduce overlap, and ensure contextual relevance. This process resulted in a finalized operational list of 34 variables (Table 3), which was then used to construct the 34 × 34 cross-impact matrix and to run the ISM/MICMAC analyses. A standalone “community participation” indicator was considered during the candidate-factor extraction (Tables 1 and 2) but was not retained as a separate variable after key-informant refinement to keep the cross-impact matrix tractable and to reduce overlap with household resilience and support/institutional access variables.

In the second stage, necessary data were collected from winter 2024 to spring 2025 through questionnaires completed by 25 local public-sector experts, especially those in agriculture, working in the field of agricultural and food production across various areas of Gorgan County (see Appendix A, Table A1 for characteristics of participants in the second stage of the study). The questionnaire consisted of a 34 × 34 cross-impact (pairwise influence) matrix rated on a 0–3 scale; given its length, we do not reproduce the full matrix in the appendix and instead report the complete variable list (Table 3) and scoring scheme in the Methods.

The participants were selected using the snowball sampling method. As ISM/MICMAC relies on structured expert judgment rather than statistical representativeness, the panel of 25 experts provides a reasonable balance between cross-sector expertise diversity and feasibility of completing the cross-impact matrix. Overall, the panel covered multiple relevant local institutions (university and key governmental/agricultural agencies), with a median age of 45 years (range 37–58) and a median work experience of 12.5 years (range 5–30). It should be noted that all methods employed in this study were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations for survey research involving human participants. As the study did not involve any interventional procedures requiring formal institutional approval, the research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Gorgan University of Agricultural Sciences and Natural Resources. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring their voluntary participation in the survey.

Using the finalized list of 34 factors (Table 3), respondents rated the influence of each factor (i) on every other factor (j) in the rural household food security system on a 0–3 scale (0 = no influence, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong). The 34 factors–covering five components (infrastructure & access, economic, socio-demographic, nutrition & health, and external risks)–were then organized into a 34 × 34 direct influence matrix and analyzed using the MICMAC method. Influence (driving power) and dependence were computed as the row-sum and column-sum of the direct influence matrix, respectively. Indirect influence was obtained through iterative matrix multiplications to capture multi-step effects and feedback. Following the MICMAC software recommendations, stability was confirmed after three iterations, with the convergence check reaching 100% for both influence and dependence (Table 4), supporting the reliability of the key-factors matrix. For reporting, we present raw influence/dependence totals; for component-level comparison, we also report mean scores (totals divided by the number of factors in each component). For visualization only, the indirect influence network (Fig. 3) is displayed using a 5% coverage threshold to reduce clutter; this threshold improves readability and does not affect the underlying MICMAC computations. MICMAC analyses were performed in MICMAC software version 6.1.2, using default settings except for the iteration number (3) and the visualization coverage (5%) used for Fig. 3.

Results

Summary of the cross-impact matrix

For the purpose of analysis and achieving equilibrium, the number of iterations recommended by the software was set to three. The matrix fill rate was calculated at 85.72%, indicating that in more than 85% of cases, the examined factors influenced one another. Among the total of 991 relationships, 32 relationships (3.2%) exhibited a strong mutual impact (value 3). 464 relationships (46.8%) showed a medium mutual impact (value 2). 495 relationships (49.9%) had a weak mutual impact (value 1). Finally, 165 relationships (16.6%) demonstrated no impact (value 0). Based on these results, weak-impact relationships accounted for the largest share, while strong-impact relationships represented only a small percentage of the total (Table 5).

Influence and dependence of factors

In the structural analysis matrix, the sum of the values in each row for a given factor is considered its degree of influence, while the sum of the values in each column represents its level of dependence. Among all the factors under study, the inflation rate has the highest level of influence, followed by household capital and the exchange rate. Additionally, the highest level of dependence is associated with the household head income, followed by stress-free food availability and household capital. The relative positions and influence levels of the factors are presented in Figs. 2 and 3, which illustrate their distribution within the direct and indirect influence matrices, respectively. In these visualizations, red lines indicate strong influences between factors, whereas thick dark blue lines represent relatively strong relationships. Gradual lightening and thinning of the blue lines correspond to medium or weak levels of influence, while dashed lines denote very weak influences. Due to the high visual complexity resulting from the full (100%) coverage of factors in the indirect influence matrix, a 5% coverage threshold was applied. This adjustment was introduced to enhance the clarity of the visualization, thereby allowing strong and relatively strong relationships between factors to be interpreted more effectively. Based on the results of the direct impact matrix analysis, the economic component, with a total influence of 692 and a total dependence of 657, holds the highest values among all components. Conversely, the external risks component, with a total influence of 130 and a total dependence of 109, has the lowest values (Table 6).

Food security system stability

In ISM, the key drivers of the rural household food security system, as well as its overall stability, are analyzed. To achieve this, the distribution of factors (variables) is examined within a two-dimensional coordinate plane. Typically, if the dispersion of these factors forms an “L”-shaped pattern, the system is considered stable. In contrast, in an unstable system, the factors tend to cluster around the diagonal axis of the plane (Fig. 5).

System stability and instability based on the distribution pattern of variables (factors).

In the analysis of factors influencing the rural household food security system, the MICMAC method output revealed that the factors were dispersed around the diagonal axis of the two-dimensional plane, indicating the instability of the current rural household food security system. Most variables (13 factors) located in the first quadrant (top right) of the chart are associated with the economic component. Five factors are also associated with the infrastructure & access component. Factors in this area represent bipolar variables, meaning they have both a high influence on other factors and a high dependence on them. The second quadrant (top left), which represents variables with high influence but lower dependence, includes four factors: the agricultural land area, war and conflict, quality food consumption, and pandemics. The third quadrant (bottom left), indicating independent variables, includes seven factors such as family food diversity, household head age, home ownership, caloric intake from food, food waste level, climate change, and mother’s education level. Finally, the fourth quadrant (bottom right), representing dependent variables, contains three factors: non-farm income, distance to food market, and efficient food marketing system (Fig. 6).

Food security drivers

To identify the key drivers in rural household food security, the direct and indirect influences of factors were analyzed. The results of this analysis, presented in Table 8, show the contribution of each factor to the overall direct and indirect influence within the rural household food security system. It is important to note that for the calculations, the MICMAC software raises the matrix to several exponents, producing very large numbers that complicate comparisons. To address this issue, the software output is scaled based on ten thousand calculations55. As is evident, inflation rate, household capital, and exchange rate are the three factors with the highest shares in both direct and indirect influence. Conversely, household sanitary kitchen availability, mother’s education level, and food waste level have the lowest shares in direct and indirect influence. Typically, the identification of key system drivers (food security) is based on the magnitude of their direct and indirect influence, as well as their controllability—that is, the feasibility of policy intervention and management. Nonetheless, the key drivers are categorized into three groups: regardless of controllability (Table 9), controllability at the national or sectoral level (Table 10), and controllability at the household or local level (Table 11).

Discussion

Overall, the structural analysis of the rural household food security system in this study indicates that interventions should prioritize the economic component and the management of external risks, as these two components exert the greatest influence on the rest of the system. In contrast, infrastructure & access, socio-demographic, and nutrition & health mainly play structural, consequential, or reflective roles, with their influence being limited, indirect, or long-term. These differences are not merely a function of the number of variables or numerical values; rather, they reflect the intrinsic nature of each component, differences in intervention feasibility and scale of impact, and the degree to which each component is linked to decision-making levels, institutional structures, and the natural environment. Accordingly, the structural position of each component, the time required for its effects to manifest, and its connection to sectoral and cross-sectoral policies should be explicitly considered in future policymaking to ensure alignment with the true role of each component in sustaining rural household food security.

Based on the obtained results, the economic component shows the highest average impact (driving power) (49.42) and susceptibility (dependence) (46.92), indicating a sensitive and highly critical position in the rural household food security system. Variables such as household head income, household capital, food prices, inflation rate, and exchange rates are shaped by national and regional economic conditions and, in turn, directly influence purchasing power, food consumption patterns, and households’ access to sufficient and healthy food. This bidirectional linkage suggests that the economic component acts as both a driver and an outcome within the system. Therefore, strategies that ignore household economic conditions and the policies affecting them are unlikely to improve rural household food security and may increase vulnerability to structural shocks. This centrality of economic drivers is consistent with Iranian rural evidence emphasizing income, employment, access to productive resources, and food-price pressures as major determinants of food security (Table 1).

The external risks component, with an average impact of 43.33 and a susceptibility of 36.33, is positioned as a highly influential element within the system, exerting considerable effects on other components while remaining relatively unaffected by internal system dynamics. This component includes factors such as climate change, war and conflict, and pandemics- exogenous forces imposed from outside the rural household food security structure. The nature of these threats–often sudden, widespread, and unpredictable–explains their high impact on other factors and relatively low susceptibility. Consequently, strengthening resilience and adaptive capacity against external hazards should be treated as a strategic priority for sustaining rural household food security under crisis conditions. This aligns with Iranian studies highlighting climate stressors (e.g., drought, floods, water scarcity) as exogenous pressures that cascade into rural food-security outcomes (Table 1).

The infrastructure & access component (average impact = 41; susceptibility = 50.75) is positioned as being more influenced by other components than influencing them. Variables such as sufficient food supply centers, distance to food markets, household sanitary kitchen availability, and an efficient food marketing system largely reflect downstream outcomes of broader economic, political, and external conditions. Therefore, this component is better treated as an indicator for monitoring household vulnerability and program performance rather than as a primary entry point for intervention. Improvements typically require public investment and sectoral policies beyond household-level capacity. Consistent with Iranian findings, institutional support, rural infrastructure, and service delivery are repeatedly identified as key enabling conditions, which in our structure mainly shape food security through upstream economic and policy channels (Table 1).

The socio-demographic component, with an average impact of 42.25 and a susceptibility of 39.5, occupies a relatively independent and moderately active position within the system. Its variables–such as the age of the household head, household size, and mother’s education level–primarily represent structural and background household characteristics. These variables are inherently resistant to short-term change and less responsive to immediate policy interventions. Although this component shows slightly higher influence than susceptibility, its effects on the system are gradual, indirect, and largely long-term. This long-term and enabling role is broadly in line with Iranian evidence emphasizing women-related capacities as background conditions that affect food security indirectly over time (Table 1).

Similarly, the nutrition & health component, with an average impact of 40 and a susceptibility of 37.8, displays one of the lowest levels of short-term dynamism among all components. Rather than serving as a driver of change, it mainly reflects the status of other components—such as economic conditions, infrastructure, and social factors. Key variables, including quality food consumption, family food diversity, and safe food consumption, are directly influenced by broader systemic conditions and exert only limited structural influence in return. Thus, while these variables can be important in practice, their structural role in this model is better interpreted as outcome indicators that help assess intervention effectiveness and monitor long-term system performance. Similarly, Iranian studies that stress food awareness and related behavioral dimensions support interpreting nutrition/health variables as important welfare outcomes that respond strongly to upstream constraints (Table 1).

Theoretical interpretation of structural findings

From the lens of Sen’s Entitlement Theory, the prominence of economic and price-related drivers (e.g., income, food prices, inflation, and exchange-rate pressures) is consistent with the idea that food insecurity often emerges through weakened exchange entitlements–that is, households’ reduced ability to command food through income and purchasing power. In this interpretation, the system-level economic drivers identified in our analysis explain why downstream dietary outcomes (e.g., diet quality, caloric intake, and safe consumption) respond strongly to macro- and meso-level economic conditions rather than functioning as direct leverage points on their own.

Interpreted through a Food Systems Approach, the infrastructure & access cluster and market-related variables (e.g., access to supply centers, distance to markets, and the efficiency of the food marketing system) represent critical system interfaces linking production conditions to household access. The MICMAC structure suggests that improving household food security is not only a matter of household choices but also of how well the local food system enables availability, affordability, and physical access–highlighting governance, service delivery, and market coordination as upstream mechanisms that shape multiple downstream outcomes simultaneously.

Finally, within the Sustainable Livelihoods Approach (SLA), the identified drivers and outcomes align with the interaction of livelihood capitals and the vulnerability context. Economic variables reflect financial capital; market access and services reflect physical capital; the availability of production resources (e.g., water/land) reflects natural capital; and education/social support relate to human and social capital. The external risks cluster (e.g., climate change, conflict, and pandemics) operationalizes the vulnerability context that can destabilize livelihood strategies. Hence, the system mapping supports an SLA-consistent interpretation: upstream institutions and shocks shape capitals and strategies, which then manifest as household-level food security outcomes. Together, these three lenses reinforce a common implication of our structural findings: effective policy design should prioritize upstream, system-shaping levers while using outcome variables as monitoring indicators for resilience and program performance.

These component-level patterns are broadly consistent with the Iranian food security literature summarized in Table 1, which repeatedly emphasizes economic constraints alongside infrastructural, socio-demographic, and external risk dimensions in shaping rural household food security. However, the present study extends prior Iranian evidence by moving beyond reporting determinant sets to explicitly mapping direct and indirect interdependencies through ISM and cross-impact structural analysis and by organizing variables into hierarchical influence levels. For example, while many Iranian studies highlight economic variables as important, our modeling specifies their systemic roles by identifying the top-ranked drivers in Table 9 and linking these priorities to the controllability-based interpretations presented next (Tables 10 and 11). This systems-based structure helps avoid treating downstream outcomes as direct leverage points.

The results further show that the dispersion and relative positioning of factors indicate instability in the rural household food security system of Gorgan County. Addressing this instability requires targeted attention to key drivers. The strategic drivers consist of bipolar variables located in the first region of the cross-impact analysis diagram near the diagonal axis. These factors include inflation rate, household capital, exchange rate, household resilience, benefiting from economic support, stable household income, food production support policies, availability of food production resources, and household head income. Because their influence (driving power) and dependence (influenceability) are high, even small changes in them can produce substantial system shifts. However, their controllability differs by governance level. For instance, inflation rate and exchange rate are primarily controllable at the macro level and are less accessible to local policymakers. By contrast, factors such as household capital, economic support, and stable household income combine high influence with greater feasibility of intervention at lower levels.

Accordingly, at the national or sectoral level, key drivers include household capital, household resilience, benefiting from economic support, stable household income, and food production support policies, as well as the availability of food production resources, household head income, and the existence of food aid. At the household and local community levels, factors such as household capital, household resilience, stable household income, household head income, safe food consumption, quality food consumption, household size, family food diversity, home ownership, and house size can be treated as key drivers because they are more amenable to local intervention.

In this study, climate change was classified within the external risks component, with an influence score of 39 and a dependence score of 37. This positioning reflects the relatively independent yet influential role of external risks: they are not strongly affected by other system components but can significantly disrupt other dimensions of rural household food security. Climate change is systemic, gradual, and cumulative; its effects often manifest over the long term and in nonlinear ways. Thus, rather than attempting to eliminate climate impacts at the local level, policy should emphasize resilience and adaptation through income diversification, farmer education, infrastructure strengthening, and improved early-warning and crisis management systems. Moreover, given its persistent indirect influence, climate change should be integrated into future food security planning scenarios56.

Mother’s education level scored 20 in influence and 17 in dependence, placing it among the lowest-ranked variables in the structural model. Nevertheless, numerical position alone should not be interpreted as lack of practical importance. Prior studies and theoretical literature emphasize mothers’ knowledge and health behaviors as pathways to improving dietary quality and household food security decisions. The low systemic influence observed here may reflect weak institutional linkages to upstream policy variables and socio-cultural dynamics that limit women’s participation in formal decision-making. Accordingly, investments in women’s education remain a plausible long-term pathway to improving rural household food security, even if short-term systemic leverage is limited in this structural mapping.

Conclusion

Based on the identification of the economic component and external risks as key drivers of the rural household food security system in Gorgan County, the desirable future scenario should focus on these two axes. To make policy implications more actionable, recommendations are structured by governance level. At the macro (national) level, priority actions should target macro-controllable drivers (inflation, exchange-rate pressures, and staple food price volatility) through strengthened price monitoring/stabilization during shocks and better-targeted social protection for vulnerable rural households. At the sectoral level (agriculture/markets/resources), actions should focus on improving reliability of production resources and inputs (especially water and key inputs), strengthening agricultural risk management (e.g., insurance and extension-linked advisory services), and upgrading market coordination and logistics to reduce access barriers. At the local/community level, interventions should prioritize livelihood stabilization (skills-based microenterprise and local employment support), community-based resilience measures (preparedness and mutual-support mechanisms), and nutrition-sensitive actions to improve diet quality, dietary diversity, and safe food practices. Overall, the desirable scenario is economy-driven, resilient, and preventive–one in which rural households achieve sustainable food security while maintaining the capacity to adapt to external shocks and internal fluctuations. Achieving this scenario requires synergy among governmental, local, and social institutions and the adoption of a cross-sectoral and long-term perspective in rural development policymaking.

A limitation of the present structural model is that it does not include an explicit standalone measure of local community participation or governance quality/policy uncertainty. While governance is partly captured through policy/institutional proxies and macroeconomic volatility variables, future research can extend the cross-impact matrix by adding direct indicators of community participation (e.g., cooperative membership, collective action) and governance effectiveness/policy volatility to test the robustness of the hierarchy and scenarios.

Future research should analyze multiple rural household food security scenarios across regions with different levels of climatic and economic vulnerability. Comparative studies can provide deeper insight into context-specific mechanisms, while evaluations of supportive and developmental policies can strengthen the evidence base for mitigation and resilience building. Qualitative analyses of lived experiences during food and economic crises can also reveal local needs and pathways not captured by structural modeling alone. Finally, studies employing time-series data, data-mining techniques, social network analysis, or system dynamics modeling can help examine long-term trends and forecast potential future instabilities, thereby improving the precision of policy design for food security and rural development.
