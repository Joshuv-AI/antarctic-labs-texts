Knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward postoperative fatigue syndrome among postoperative patients
Authors: Hongshuai Li, Danmei Tong, Hong Chen, Lina Yang, Xinyue Zhang, Lin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study, conducted January 15–June 30, 2025 at Jilin Province FAW General Hospital, assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of postoperative patients regarding postoperative fatigue syndrome (POFS), a multifactorial condition characterized by persistent fatigue, reduced energy, and functional impairment following surgery that cannot be relieved by rest. Among 310 valid responses, knowledge scored a median of 3 out of 20, while attitude and practice medians were 36/45 and 32/45 respectively. Multivariate linear regression identified knowledge (β = 0.348, 95% CI 0.225–0.470, P < 0.001), attitude (β = 0.491, 95% CI 0.350–0.632, P < 0.001), monthly per capita household income of 5,000–20,000 yuan (β = 2.485, 95% CI 1.210–5.107, P = 0.013), and presence of POFS (β = 1.831, 95% CI 0.684–2.979, P = 0.002) as independent factors associated with practice scores. Structural equation modeling suggested potential direct effects of knowledge (β = 0.290, P = 0.009) and attitude (β = 0.361, P = 0.003) on practice. The findings suggest overall poor POFS knowledge, relatively positive attitudes, and suboptimal self-management practices, underscoring the need for targeted patient education to improve recovery outcomes and reduce systemic healthcare burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55940-9.

Introduction

Surgical intervention is a common and important therapeutic approach in modern medicine. Each year, hundreds of millions of patients worldwide undergo various surgical procedures1. The success of surgery depends not only on the precision of intraoperative techniques but also on the quality of postoperative recovery. A good postoperative recovery process is essential for relieving patient discomfort, restoring health, and improving quality of life2. However, the postoperative period is often accompanied by physiological and psychological challenges, such as pain, infection, and complications, that can affect the rehabilitation process and overall well-being3. Thus, comprehensive attention to and optimization of the patient’s postoperative experience and recovery have become critical topics in clinical nursing and rehabilitation medicine4.

Surgical procedures are often followed by a range of postoperative complications that can delay recovery. Among these, postoperative fatigue syndrome (POFS) is a common yet often overlooked condition5. POFS is defined as a multifactorial syndrome characterized by persistent, abnormal fatigue and a lack of energy after surgery, which cannot be alleviated by rest, and is accompanied by physical, cognitive, and emotional impairments6. It is important to note that POFS currently lacks a universally standardized diagnostic instrument; existing studies have used various tools including the Identity-Consequence Fatigue Scale (ICFS), the Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI), and visual analogue scales, reflecting the heterogeneity of its assessment7,8. POFS is not limited to the physical dimension but may also be accompanied by cognitive decline, emotional distress, and other conditions, significantly impacting patients’ daily activities, functional recovery, treatment adherence, and long-term prognosis9–11. Existing studies on POFS mainly focus on identifying core clinical conditions9, addressing its pathophysiological mechanisms12, developing predictive models for high-risk populations6,13, or testing intervention strategies14,15. However, there is little systematic research on patients’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward POFS16. The KAP framework is a well-established theoretical model in health behavior research that posits knowledge as a prerequisite for attitude formation, which in turn influences behavioral practices. This gap in awareness may result in delayed recognition and inadequate management of POFS, hindering recovery and increasing psychological burdens17. Therefore, assessing the current state of postoperative patients’ KAP regarding POFS is essential to provide evidence for targeted education and intervention strategies aimed at improving condition recognition, self-management, and postoperative recovery.

This study aims to conduct a cross-sectional survey to assess postoperative patients’ KAP regarding POFS and generate data to guide the development of effective educational and management strategies that support recovery.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Jilin Province FAW General Hospital from January 15, 2025 to June 30, 2025. Participants were patients who had undergone surgery. During the study period, a total of 358 patients were screened for eligibility. Of these, 48 were excluded: 12 due to age outside the 18–79 years range, 9 due to history of neurosurgery, 15 due to impaired consciousness at 48 h post-operation, and 12 who declined to participate. The remaining 310 patients completed valid questionnaires, yielding a response rate of 86.6% (310/358). Neurosurgical patients were excluded because the neurological sequelae of neurosurgery (e.g., cognitive impairment, headache) may confound the assessment of POFS-related knowledge and self-reported fatigue, making it difficult to distinguish POFS from procedure-specific neurological effects. In this study, POFS was identified based on patient self-report: participants who reported experiencing persistent fatigue that could not be relieved by rest at 48 h post-operation, consistent with the clinical description of POFS7, were classified as POFS-positive. This approach is consistent with prior KAP studies in which self-reported condition experience was used to characterize the study population6,13. Questionnaires were administered at 48–72 h post-operation, after patients had regained stable consciousness and were able to complete the survey independently. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Jilin Province FAW General Hospital (approval number: K2025-001-01), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion Criteria: 1) Patients must be post-operative and maintain clear consciousness 48 h after surgery; 2) Age > 18 and < 79 years. Exclusion Criteria: 1) Unwillingness to participate in the study; 2) History of neurosurgery or presence of cognitive impairment at 48 h post-operation.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire designed for this study was informed by previous literature and relevant domestic and international guidelines7,8. After creating an initial draft, the questionnaire was refined based on feedback from two POFS experts. It was then subjected to a small-scale pilot test involving 49 participants, confirming its reliability with a score of 0.952. After creating an initial draft, the questionnaire was refined based on feedback from two POFS experts (one anesthesiologist and one rehabilitation specialist with > 10 years of clinical experience). Content validity was assessed through expert review, and items with content validity index (CVI) < 0.78 were revised or removed. It was then subjected to a small-scale pilot test involving 49 participants (mean age 47.3 ± 12.1 years; 53.1% female; representative of the target population in terms of age, sex, and surgical type), confirming its overall reliability with a Cronbach’s α of 0.952. Subscale reliability was also satisfactory: knowledge subscale α = 0.921, attitude subscale α = 0.944, and practice subscale α = 0.938. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess construct validity. The CFA model demonstrated acceptable fit: CMIN/DF = 2.813, RMSEA = 0.077, IFI = 0.923, TLI = 0.911, CFI = 0.922, with all factor loadings statistically significant (all P < 0.001), supporting the three-factor structure of the questionnaire.

The final questionnaire, administered in Chinese, was designed with four sections to gather comprehensive data. It began with a section on basic demographic information (including gender, age, educational level, and occupation). This was followed by three specialized sections focusing on knowledge (10 items), attitudes (9 items), and practices (9 items) related to POFS. In the knowledge section, each item was scored on a three-level scale: “Very familiar” (2 points), "Heard of it" (1 point), and “Unclear” (0 points). This yielded a total possible score ranging from 0 to 20. The attitudes and practices sections both used a five-point Likert scale. For both sections, scores ranged from 5 (for “strongly agree”) to 1 (for “strongly disagree”), with a total possible score range of 9 to 45 for each. Scores exceeding 80% of the maximum possible in each dimension were classified as indicative of adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices, respectively16. This threshold was an arbitrary cutoff used for descriptive purposes.

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

All questionnaires were completed by the postoperative patients themselves; no proxy or family-member responses were included. Data collection was facilitated through the distribution of the questionnaire via the online platform Questionnaire Star and through WeChat groups (n = 271, 87.4%). For elderly patients who were not proficient with smartphones, paper questionnaires were administered by a trained research nurse who read the questions aloud and recorded responses as dictated by the patient (n = 39, 12.6%). To minimize mode effects, the paper questionnaire was identical in content and item order to the electronic version, and responses were entered into the database by a dedicated data entry staff member with double-entry verification. To improve data quality and ensure all responses were complete, each IP address was limited to a single submission, and all questions were marked as mandatory. Furthermore, the research team systematically reviewed all responses to verify their completeness, assess internal consistency, and confirm logical validity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Regarding sample size, based on a prior KAP study in a similar population16, we estimated a prevalence of proactive practice of approximately 25%, with a margin of error of 5% and a significance level of 0.05, yielding a minimum required sample of 288; the final sample of 310 exceeded this requirement. Normality of score distributions in each dimension was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data as median with interquartile range (25th–75th percentile). Although individual KAP items used ordinal Likert-type scales, total subscale scores (summed across multiple items) were treated as approximately continuous for the purposes of correlation and regression analyses, consistent with established practice in KAP research16. For two-group comparisons, t-tests were applied to normally distributed data and Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests to non-normal data. For three or more groups, one-way ANOVA with equal variances or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used. Correlations were examined using Pearson coefficients for normal data and Spearman coefficients for non-normal data. Univariate and multivariate linear regression were performed to explore the factors associated with practice scores. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model using a forward stepwise selection procedure (F-statistic criterion). Model diagnostics included assessment of multicollinearity (variance inflation factor < 5 for all predictors), confirming adequate model fit. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using AMOS 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) to explore the relationships between knowledge (K), attitude (A), and practice (P). The hypothesized pathways included (1) a direct effect of knowledge on attitude, (2) a direct effect of attitude on practice, and (3) both direct and indirect effects of knowledge on practice through attitude. The parameter estimation method employed maximum likelihood estimation, and the 95% confidence intervals were derived using Bootstrap ML with 1000 replications. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF; acceptable: 1–5), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; acceptable: < 0.08), incremental fit index (IFI; acceptable: > 0.8), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI; acceptable: > 0.8), and comparative fit index (CFI; acceptable: > 0.8). A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and KAP score

A total of 310 valid questionnaires were analyzed. Among the respondents, 130 (41.94%) were male, with the largest group falling within the 30–45 age range (40.97%). In terms of education, 122 individuals (39.35%) held a bachelor’s degree or higher, and 164 (52.9%) were engaged in intellectual work. A significant majority, 227 participants (73.23%), reported no postoperative complications, while only 88 (28.39%) experienced postoperative fatigue syndrome (POFS), defined as persistent fatigue not relieved by rest at 48–72 h post-operation. Moreover, 290 respondents (93.55%) lived in urban areas, and 227 (73.23%) had received general anesthesia. Minor discrepancies in subgroup totals (e.g., sleep quality: n = 307) reflect three participants who did not provide responses to those specific items; these missing values were handled by pairwise deletion in the corresponding subgroup analyses (Table 1). The KAP scores were 3 (1, 6) (possible range: 0–20), 36 (36, 36) (possible range: 9–45), and 32 (28.75, 35) (possible range: 9–45), respectively. The extremely compressed interquartile range of the attitude score (IQR: 36–36) may point to a ceiling effect in this domain, suggesting that the majority of participants held uniformly positive attitudes toward POFS; this limits the ability to detect meaningful attitudinal differences across subgroups and should be interpreted with caution. The majority of practice scores (79.7%, 247/310) fell below the positive threshold of 36 despite a median score of 32. Analysis of demographic characteristics revealed that participants’ KAP scores varied significantly based on demographic and clinical characteristics. Knowledge scores were particularly influenced by sex, age, education level, household income, medical background, type of surgery, type of anesthesia, postoperative complications, physical activity level, self-care ability, and sleep quality. Attitude scores varied based on postoperative complications. Baseline characteristics and KAP scores.

Distribution of responses to knowledge, attitude, and practice

The analysis of the knowledge dimension revealed that the three questions with the highest proportion of participants selecting the “Not familiar” option were as follows: “The type of anesthesia (e.g., general anesthesia or local anesthesia) has different effects on the occurrence of POFS” (K3), with 79.03% of participants; “Patients experiencing POFS after anesthesia are more likely to have sleep disturbances” (K4), with 75.48%; and “Prolonged anesthesia may increase the likelihood of developing POFS” (K2), with 75.81%. Moreover, more than 70% of the patients were unfamiliar with most knowledge items, indicating a markedly insufficient understanding of POFS (Table S1).

Responses to the attitude dimension showed that 96.13% agreed or strongly agreed that POFS is a very important condition that requires timely attention and treatment (A1). Similarly, 95.48% believed that its management should not rely solely on medication but also include lifestyle adjustments (A2), while 95.16% agreed that comprehensive medical, nutritional, and psychological support is essential for POFS management (A9) (Table S2).

When it comes to related practices, 55.81% sometimes and 23.55% rarely identified POFS and reported them to doctors or nurses (P1), and 44.19% only sometimes discussed medication use with physicians to avoid exacerbating fatigue (P7). In addition, 45.81% sometimes and 9.35% rarely sought multidisciplinary support, including nutritional, psychological, or physical therapy interventions (P9) (Table S3).

Correlations between KAP

Knowledge showed a positive correlation with practice (r = 0.323, P < 0.001), as did attitude with practice (r = 0.270, P < 0.001). Discussion

Postoperative patients demonstrated poor knowledge, positive attitudes, and suboptimal practices toward POFS. These findings underscore the importance of targeted educational interventions aimed at improving patient knowledge and promoting better adherence to recommended practices for the management of POFS.

While recent studies have focused on the clinical features and predictive modeling of POFS, they have not addressed patient-level KAP. For example, prediction models have been developed in both lung cancer6 and gastrointestinal tumor surgery patients13. Liu et al.9 have characterized POFS through core postoperative conditions, and other work has examined condition trajectories after thoracoscopic surgery18. In contrast, our study emphasizes the cognitive and behavioral dimensions of POFS, revealing how limited knowledge and suboptimal practices persist despite favorable attitudes. By adopting a KAP framework, a model grounded in the premise that knowledge informs attitudes, which in turn shape behaviors, we extend the scope of POFS research from condition description and risk prediction to patient self-management, providing a complementary perspective that informs targeted educational interventions.

The knowledge scores were alarmingly low (median 3/20), with over 70% of patients unfamiliar with most items, indicating a substantial gap in POFS-related knowledge. While direct benchmarks from other POFS-specific KAP studies are unavailable given the novelty of this research area, this pattern is consistent with findings in other postoperative condition KAP studies, where patients similarly demonstrate limited awareness of complex postoperative syndromes19,20 Patients unable to recognize or report POFS-related conditions are less likely to adopt timely coping strategies or seek medical help, placing them at higher risk of delayed recovery and complications. This has been observed in esophageal cancer survivors, who frequently reported POFS conditions but still experienced high unmet needs and reduced quality of life after surgery, suggesting that recognition alone is insufficient without adequate knowledge and support19. Higher scores among participants with greater education, higher income, or a medical background reflect the role of health literacy and socioeconomic resources in patient understanding of POFS20,21. Conversely, the lowest scores occurred in older adults, consistent with age-related declines in health literacy and access to educational resources22. Notably, patients with complications such as headache or cognitive dysfunction demonstrated higher knowledge scores, suggesting that adverse experiences may increase receptiveness to counseling and should be leveraged as opportunities for targeted education23. These findings suggest that clinicians should prioritize structured POFS education for socioeconomically disadvantaged and older patients, while policymakers and educators should integrate standardized perioperative education programs to improve condition recognition and self-care, thereby enhancing recovery outcomes.

The uniformly high attitude scores toward POFS in this cohort suggest that patients recognized its clinical significance, consistent with prior findings that postoperative condition burden is widely recognized among surgical patients24. However, the marked discrepancy between attitudes and practices, particularly the low frequency of condition recognition and reporting, suggests that awareness alone does not ensure effective self-care25,26. This gap likely reflects limited self-regulatory capacity, reduced confidence in communicating with providers, or misperceptions about condition severity27. Evidence has shown that structured interventions, such as perioperative transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation, can reduce the incidence of POFS and improve recovery in surgical patients, highlighting the importance of moving from awareness to actionable strategies28. The association between higher practice scores and greater education or a medical background underscores that health literacy is critical for translating awareness into behavior29. Clinicians should therefore pair education with practical training in condition monitoring and communication. Educators and policymakers should incorporate structured self-monitoring tools and routine follow-up counseling into perioperative care to support consistent patient engagement and adherence30.

Both knowledge (β = 0.348, 95% CI 0.225–0.470, P < 0.001) and attitude (β = 0.491, 95% CI 0.350–0.632, P < 0.001) were independently associated with improved POFS-related practices. This finding suggests that cognitive understanding and attitudinal disposition influence practice through separate, parallel pathways rather than a strictly sequential knowledge-attitude-practice chain. Similar patterns have been reported in other KAP studies, in which favorable attitudes did not always correspond to accurate knowledge, reducing the likelihood of consistent self-care behaviors31,32. Monthly per capita household income of 5000–20,000 yuan was also identified as an independent factor associated with practice scores (β = 2.485, 95% CI 1.210–5.107, P = 0.013), likely reflecting greater access to health information, postoperative follow-up, and supportive resources that facilitate adherence33. SEM confirmed that knowledge and attitude each had a direct effect on practice, with no significant indirect pathway from knowledge to practice through attitude. This suggests that education and motivation act independently, and both must be addressed to improve postoperative self-care. For clinicians, this means combining clear, accessible information with practical training in condition monitoring and communication. For policymakers, the priority is to ensure that supportive resources are equitably available to patients at different income levels.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents any causal inferences about the relationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Second, since all measures were self-reported, there is a risk of recall bias and social desirability bias, especially concerning practice-related items. Third, the single-center design and predominantly urban sample (93.55%) limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Fourth, POFS was identified by patient self-report rather than a validated diagnostic instrument, which may introduce misclassification; future studies should employ standardized tools such as the ICFS or MFI8. Finally, the ceiling effect in the attitude domain (IQR 36–36) limits the ability to detect meaningful attitudinal differences and may reflect social desirability bias. Sixth, some subgroup analyses involved small sample sizes (e.g., patients with specific surgery types), which may produce statistically unstable comparisons; these results should be interpreted with caution. Seventh, the mixed-mode data collection (electronic and paper) may have introduced minor mode effects, although steps were taken to standardize the administration process. Finally, the SEM analysis relied on observed associations and model fit indices, and the RMSEA value (0.102) was marginally above the conventional threshold of 0.08, so its findings should be interpreted with caution until validated by longitudinal or interventional studies.

In conclusion, postoperative patients demonstrated poor knowledge, positive attitudes, and suboptimal practices toward POFS. The findings suggest that knowledge and attitude are independently associated with practice through parallel pathways, and both must be addressed to improve postoperative self-care. Interventions should target both knowledge and behavior, bridging the gap between awareness and action to support postoperative recovery outcomes.
