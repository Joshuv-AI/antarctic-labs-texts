Prognostic stratification by the ESTRO/EORTC oligometastatic classification and simplified binary approaches in lung cancer patients treated with stereotactic ablative radiotherapy
Authors: Dan Yao, Xue-ru Zhu, Jin-dong Guo, Xin-yun Song, Wen Feng, Qin Zhang, Hong-xuan Li, Jun Liu, Xu-wei Cai, Wen Yu, Xiao-long Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ESTRO/EORTC classification categorizes oligometastatic disease (OMD) into de-novo, repeat, and induced OMD based on prior disease history and treatment status. However, its prognostic relevance in lung cancer patients treated with stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) remains incompletely characterized in real-world settings. This study evaluated the ESTRO/EORTC classification and two simplified binary stratification approaches derived from its decision framework.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 648 lung cancer patients with 1–5 metastases treated with SABR between 2015 and 2021. Patients were categorized according to the ESTRO/EORTC classification. Two simplified binary stratification approaches derived from the ESTRO/EORTC decision framework were additionally explored: (1) a history-based approach distinguishing patients with prior polymetastatic disease from those without, and (2) a therapy-dependent progression approach distinguishing oligometastatic progression during active systemic therapy from other OMD states. Survival outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression, and prognostic discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index.ResultsWith a median follow-up of 54.5 months, 325 deaths and 552 progression events were observed. Induced OMD was associated with significantly worse outcomes compared with de-novo OMD (OS: 35.7 vs. 68.9 months; PFS: 8.6 vs. 15.5 months; both p < 0.001) and repeat OMD (OS: 35.7 vs. 60.2 months; PFS: 8.6 vs. 14.4 months; both p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between de-novo and repeat OMD. The therapy-dependent progression stratification approach demonstrated distinct survival separation (median OS 34.4 vs. 86.4 months; PFS 8.4 vs. 16.4 months; both p < 0.001), although the difference in discriminatory performance was modest (C-index 0.596 for OS) compared with the ESTRO/EORTC classification (0.565) and the history-based approach (0.563). Differences in discriminatory performance were modest and not statistically significant after multiple-testing correction and bootstrap resampling.ConclusionsThe ESTRO/EORTC classification is associated with survival outcomes in lung cancer patients with OMD treated with SABR, with induced OMD conferring the poorest prognosis. Simplified binary stratification approaches derived from this framework may provide a pragmatic and clinically accessible approach for risk stratification. These approaches should be considered complementary to, rather than replacements for, the full ESTRO/EORTC classification. Prospective validation is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16638-3.

Introduction

Oligometastatic disease (OMD) represents an intermediate state of cancer progression, defined by a limited number of metastases (typically ≤ 5), serving as a bridge between localized and disseminated disease [1]. Evidence from randomized trials and large observational studies indicates that patients with OMD may achieve prolonged survival through multimodal treatment strategies that combine systemic therapy with local metastasis-directed approaches, such as surgery or stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) [2–8]. SABR has emerged as an important local treatment option for selected patients with OMD, especially in cases of oligoprogression and induced oligometastatic states [1, 2, 9]. However, the lack of standardized definitions and patient selection criteria continues to hinder its consistent clinical application.

To address this challenge, the European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (ESTRO) and the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) collaboratively proposed a consensus classification for OMD. This framework divides OMD into three categories: de-novo, repeat, and induced, aiming to capture underlying biological and clinical processes regardless of primary tumor type or lesion count [9]. In addition to its potential prognostic value, the ESTRO/EORTC classification was developed to standardize clinical communication and support trial design. However, its prognostic association, particularly in lung cancer, remains insufficiently characterized [8–10]. Most validations have involved mixed tumor types, and studies focused specifically on lung cancer are limited to small cohorts [7]. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether OMD subtypes are associated with outcomes after SABR, despite its widespread use in treating oligometastatic lung cancer [11].

We therefore evaluated the ESTRO/EORTC classification in a large single-center cohort of lung cancer patients treated with SABR at a high-volume thoracic oncology center. The primary objectives were to describe the distribution of OMD states, assess their prognostic impact on survival, and explore simplified stratification approaches derived from this framework to identify clinically interpretable prognostic categories.

Materials and methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of oligometastatic lung cancer patients treated with SABR at Shanghai Chest Hospital between January 2015 and December 2021. The primary aim was to evaluate the association between the ESTRO/EORTC classification and survival outcomes in a real-world setting. Inclusion criteria were: (1) stage IV or recurrent lung cancer according to the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system; (2) 1–5 metastatic lesions (as defined by the ESTRO-ASTRO consensus [11]); (3) SABR delivered to one or more oligometastatic lesions with an intent of durable local control; (4) age ≥ 18 years; and (5) a minimum fraction dose of 5 Gy. Exclusion criteria included the lack of pathological confirmation of malignancy and SABR delivered in a purely palliative setting without an intent for comprehensive disease control (e.g., local treatment of metastases without systemic or appropriate disease-directed management).

All cases were classified according to the ESTRO/EORTC OMD framework by two independent radiation oncologists (DY and XZ). Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (XF). Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records with approval from the institutional Research Ethics Committee. For distant lymph node metastases, each node was counted separately; regional lymph node involvement was considered a single lesion.

SABR procedure

SABR was defined as the delivery of highly conformal, image-guided radiotherapy in a hypofractionated regimen. Treatment planning was performed with Eclipse v15.1 (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) using CT simulation. Gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated on CT and/or MRI based on the lesion site. For lung or liver lesions, an internal target volume (ITV) was defined on 4DCT to account for respiratory motion. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by adding a 5 mm isotropic margin to the ITV for thoracic lesions, and a 3–5 mm isotropic margin to the GTV for extra-thoracic sites, depending on the location and institutional practice. Organ-at-risk constraints followed institutional guidelines based on RTOG protocols.

Patients were immobilized using vacuum cushions or stereotactic body frames to ensure reproducibility. Image guidance was performed with cone-beam CT before each fraction. Follow-up assessments included physical examinations and imaging (CT or PET/CT) every 3–6 months, in accordance with institutional policy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, median, and range). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as interval from the diagnosis of OMD to disease progression at any site or death from any cause. Given that SABR was not always delivered at the time of OMD diagnosis, this definition may introduce potential lead-time bias. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the diagnosis of OMD to death from any cause. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier methods. Group comparisons were performed with pairwise log-rank tests, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate factors associated with survival outcomes.

The discriminatory power of each classification approach was quantified using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), with formal pairwise comparisons (compareC package), Bonferroni correction, and bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). All analyses were performed in R software, version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the survival and survminer packages (http://www.r-project.org).

The most common sites treated with SABR were lung (n = 349, 53.9%) and brain (n = 222, 34.3%). The complete distribution of lesion sites treated with SABR is listed in Table 1, and biological effective dose (BED) distributions are shown in Figure S2. All patients were successfully categorized according to the ESTRO/EORTC classification (Fig. 1). The most frequent OMD states were induced oligoprogression (n = 174, 26.9%) and metachronous oligorecurrence (n = 143, 22.1%), while induced oligopersistence was the least frequent (n = 18, 2.7%) (Table 1). When categorized into three main groups, 205 patients (31.6%) had de-novo OMD, 232 (35.8%) had repeat OMD, and 211 (32.6%) had induced OMD. Notably, 85 patients (13.1%) underwent SABR treatment again for subsequent oligometastatic events. Driver mutation status is summarized in Table 1, with treatable driver mutations identified in 261 patients (40.3%), while 221 (34.1%) were wild-type and 125 (19.3%) had unknown status.

Survival outcomes

After a median follow-up of 54.5 months, 325 deaths and 552 progression events were recorded. The median PFS was 11.8 months (95% CI: 10.8–13.6 months), and the median OS was 50.6 months (95% CI: 44.8–59.9 months). Patients with induced OMD had significantly shorter median OS (35.7 months, 95% CI: 27.8–43.7 months) compared to those with genuine OMD, including de-novo (68.9 months, 95% CI: 48.9-not reached, p < 0.001) and repeat OMD (60.2 months, 95% CI: 49.4-not reached, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, induced OMD had significantly shorter median PFS (8.6 months, 95% CI: 7.6–10.0 months) compared with de-novo (15.5 months, 95% CI: 12.6–18.1 months, p < 0.001) and repeat OMD (14.4 months, 95% CI: 11.4–16.6 months, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). No significant difference was observed between de-novo and repeat OMD patients for either OS or PFS.

Univariable and multivariable analysis for PFS and OS

The ESTRO/EORTC groups differed significantly in both PFS and OS (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A and B). In the multivariable analysis, de-novo OMD was associated with longer PFS (p = 0.007) and a trend toward longer OS (p = 0.064) compared with induced OMD (Table 2). Repeat OMD was also independently associated with longer PFS (p < 0.001) and longer OS (p < 0.001) compared with induced OMD.

Histology remained prognostic after adjustment. Squamous cell carcinoma was associated with shorter OS (p = 0.033) and PFS (p = 0.027) relative to adenocarcinoma, while small cell carcinoma was associated with markedly shorter OS and PFS (both p < 0.001).

The absence of active systemic treatment prior to SABR was associated with improved outcomes, including longer PFS (HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.48–0.78; p < 0.001) and OS (HR, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.34–0.66; p < 0.001).

Simplified binary stratification approaches

Survival outcomes across the nine ESTRO/EORTC subgroups are shown in Figure S3 and Table S1. Table S2 summarizes survival outcomes derived from the ESTRO/EORTC decision tree, which defines subgroups through sequential binary splits (Q1-Q5) in the decision algorithm, with Kaplan-Meier curves for PFS and OS presented in Figures S4-S7.

Two simplified binary stratification approaches were derived from the ESTRO/EORTC decision framework based on two key questions.

Question 1: Does the patient have a history of polymetastatic disease before the current diagnosis of oligometastatic disease?

Patients were divided into genuine OMD (i.e., de novo and repeat OMD; n = 437) and induced OMD (n = 211). This approach showed significant separation in survival outcomes (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C and D), with median PFS of 14.9 months versus 8.6 months and median OS of 62.3 months versus 35.7 months (Table S4).

Question 2: Are any oligometastatic lesions progressive on current imaging under systemic therapy?

Patients were divided into oligoprogression (groups 3, 6, and 9; n = 308) versus all other states (synchronous/oligorecurrence/oligopersistence; n = 340). This approach also demonstrated distinct separation in survival outcomes (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2E and F), with median PFS of 8.4 months versus 16.4 months and median OS of 34.4 months versus 86.4 months (Table S4).

In univariable analysis, both simplified binary stratification approaches retained independent prognostic significance for both PFS and OS (all p < 0.001) (Tables S7).

Comprehensive assessment of discriminatory power

The discriminatory power of each classification system was quantified using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). As was shown in Table 3, the original ESTRO/EORTC classification demonstrated modest discriminatory performance for OS (C-index = 0.565, 95% CI: 0.502–0.628) and PFS (C-index = 0.568, 95% CI: 0.519–0.616). The therapy-dependent progression approach showed numerically higher C-index values for both OS (0.596, 95% CI: 0.541–0.652) and PFS (0.588, 95% CI: 0.545–0.631), whereas the history-based approach demonstrated comparable performance to the ESTRO/EORTC classification (OS: 0.563; PFS: 0.562). Pairwise comparisons of C-indices are presented in Supplementary Table S11. Formal comparisons demonstrated modest differences; after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing and bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations), no approach demonstrated consistent or robust superiority across statistical approaches.

Discussion

This large-scale validation study demonstrates that the ESTRO/EORTC classification is associated with distinct survival outcomes in lung OMD, with induced OMD exhibiting significantly poorer prognosis (8.6 months PFS and 35.7 months OS) compared to de-novo (15.5 months PFS and 68.9 months OS) and repeat OMD (14.4 months PFS and 60.2 months OS). Beyond this validation, we explored two simplified binary stratification approaches derived from the ESTRO/EORTC framework. These approaches demonstrated modest but clinically interpretable prognostic stratification ability and highlight the clinical heterogeneity within OMD.

Our cohort of 648 lung OMD patients treated with SABR demonstrated a median OS of 50.6 months and PFS of 11.8 months, similar to prior studies, including SABR-COMET trial [6, 8] (41 months OS in oligometastatic cohort with median follow-up 51 months), Gomez study [3] (14.2 months PFS and 41.2 months OS), CURB trial [12] (10.0 months PFS in NSCLC group), among others. Across subgroups, we observed substantial variation in survival outcomes, with an approximate 33-month difference in OS between extreme groups.

Given these findings, an important question is how to better characterize survival heterogeneity among patients with OMD treated with SABR. The ESTRO/EORTC classification provides a structured framework for this purpose, but its complexity has limited clinical adoption. Previously published works [10, 13] have also explored the prognostic relevance of OMD states using this classification system, however, the framework remains complex, limiting its clinical adoption. In our study, patients with genuine OMD - both de-novo and repeat - had significantly longer OS and PFS than those with induced OMD. The OS and PFS of repeat OMD patients were shorter than de-novo OMD patients, although the differences didn’t reach statistical significance. This may reflect residual confounding or limited statistical power within subgroups after multivariable adjustment.

To further differentiate the population, our systematic evaluation of the ESTRO/EORTC decision tree’s five questions - through detailed survival analyses (PFS/OS in Figures S4-7) and survival outcomes (Table S2) - suggested that the first branching criterion (prior history of polymetastatic disease) may represent a clinically relevant discriminator. This history-based dichotomy (genuine versus induced OMD) was associated with a 26.6-month difference in OS, supporting the hypothesis that prior widespread dissemination may capture clinically relevant differences in disease history and tumor behavior.

Multivariable analysis confirmed OMD classification and pre-SABR systemic therapy were independently associated with survival outcomes, although this association may be influenced by confounding by indication, given the strong correlation between systemic therapy exposure and OMD state. Oligoprogression during systemic therapy was also associated with poorer outcomes, consistent with prior observations. Based on this observation, we explored a therapy-dependent progression model, which was associated with a subgroup exhibiting markedly shorter survival. This approach further highlights the clinical heterogeneity within OMD states, although its discriminatory performance remained modest.

The persistently poor outcomes of induced OMD may reflect residual disease characteristics associated with prior metastatic dissemination, aligning with the “late-clone selection” theory from TRACERx [14]. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms. Recent work uncovered two major patterns of progression after initial response to systemic therapy: systemic progression and oligoprogression [15, 16]. Here, NCT03808662, a randomised prospective phase II study, reported the benefit of ablative radiotherapy in the form of SABR for oligoprogressive metastatic cancer, breast cancer and NSCLC, showing longer survival with only modest increases in toxicities [12]. Several ongoing randomised trials (e.g., STOP trial [17], HALT trial, and SUPPRESS-NSCLC [18]) are focusing on evaluating SABR in oligoprogressive disease, a population that shares clinical characteristics with one subgroup identified in our stratification approach. We acknowledge that the ESTRO/EORTC classification serves purposes beyond prognostic discrimination, including standardizing communication across centers, informing trial eligibility and stratification, and capturing clinically relevant disease history (e.g., prior polymetastatic disease and treatment status at progression). Our simplified binary stratification approaches capture only subsets of this information and are not intended to replace the full classification, which remains important for standardizing clinical communication and trial design. Although the therapy-dependent progression model showed clear survival separation, this did not translate into a consistent or clinically decisive improvement in discriminatory performance. Therefore, the modest differences in C-index should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as evidence of superiority for clinical decision-making.

Recent clinical trials have explored the role of SABR in oligometastatic and oligoprogressive disease, including studies such as CURB trial [19], OLIGO trial (OA07.03) [20] and others. These studies highlight the evolving role of local therapy in selected patient populations. Our findings provide additional context by describing survival heterogeneity across OMD states; however, prospective validation is required before these observations can be translated into clinical decision-making.

Advances in imaging and local treatment strategies continue to refine patient selection for metastasis-directed therapy. Emerging approaches, including [18]F-FDG PET/CT-based treatment planning [21], pulmonary metastasectomy [22], and anatomical resection strategies23, highlight the importance of accurate disease characterization and individualized local therapy selection in lung cancer management.

Although SCLC was included in the primary analysis to provide a comprehensive evaluation of lung cancer OMD, we acknowledge its distinct biological characteristics, systemic treatment strategies, and disease trajectory compared with NSCLC. Therefore, sensitivity analyses excluding SCLC were performed. The associations between the ESTRO/EORTC classification, simplified binary stratification approaches, and survival outcomes remained consistent in the NSCLC-only cohort (Supplementary Table S9-10). These findings support the robustness of the observed prognostic patterns while highlighting the need for further validation in histologically homogeneous populations.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias. Second, although SABR was not uniformly delivered to all metastatic lesions, sensitivity analyses restricted to patients receiving complete local treatment yielded consistent results, suggesting that the observed associations were not solely attributable to differences in local treatment extent. Third, although driver mutation status was reported, molecular data were incomplete and were not incorporated into multivariable models, which may confound survival outcomes. Fourth, although sensitivity analyses in NSCLC and SCLC populations supported the robustness of the findings, the inclusion of heterogeneous histological subtypes may still influence generalizability. Finally, the overall discriminatory performance of the evaluated stratification approaches was modest, and external validation is required.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the ESTRO/EORTC classification is associated with survival outcomes in lung cancer patients with oligometastatic disease treated with SABR, with induced OMD conferring the poorest prognosis. Simplified binary stratification approaches derived from this framework may provide a pragmatic approach for risk stratification, although they did not demonstrate superior discrimination over the original classification. Prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.
