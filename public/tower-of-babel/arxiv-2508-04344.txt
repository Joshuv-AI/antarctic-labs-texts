Abstract
. Financial models do not merely analyse markets, but actively shape them. This effect, known as performativity , describes how financial theories and the subsequent actions based on them influence market processes, by creating self-fulfilling prophecies. Although discussed in the literature on economic sociology, this deeply rooted phenomenon lacks mathematical formulation in financial markets. Our paper closes this gap by breaking down the canonical separation of diffusion processes between the description of the market environment and the financial model. We do that by embedding the model in the process itself, creating a closed feedback loop, and demonstrate how prices change towards greater conformity to the prevailing financial model used in the market. We further show, with closed-form solutions and machine learning, how a performative market maker can reverse engineer the current dominant strategies in the market and effectively arbitrage them while maintaining competitive quotes and superior P&L.



1.
Introduction

Financial models and theories are so deeply ingrained in modern financial practice that they decisively shape the markets they are meant to predict. This phenomenon, known as
performativity
, challenges the orthodox scientific view that separates an observed
“world”
from the
“language”
used to describe it. In its
“pure”
, also known as
“Barnesian”
, form,
financial performativity
is defined as

[the] practical use of an aspect of economics that makes the economic processes more like their depiction by economics

(
MacKenzie 2006
)
.

Unlike most scientific domains, where theories seek only to describe external reality, the extensive application of financial models creates powerful feedback loops with the markets on which they act.

Two illustrative examples highlight this widespread effect. When multiple hedge funds adopt similar trading strategies, their collective behaviour drives asset prices in correlated, and thus
performative
, ways. Similarly, in highly concentrated markets, the algorithms of dominant actors impose systematic patterns on price movements, reinforcing the models on which they are based.

Despite its conceptual importance, extensive qualitative treatment, and fundamental impact in empirical studies, financial performativity has not yet been formalised in quantitative terms
(
Unal et al. 2025
)
. In this paper, we address this gap by proposing the first, to the best of our knowledge, formal mathematical framework for performativity in financial markets. We achieve this by embedding model-driven dynamics directly into the stochastic processes that govern the price evolution of financial assets. We apply this framework to the market making problem and derive closed-form solutions showing how a
“performativity-aware”
agent can infer and arbitrage dominant strategies embedded in price formation, while maintaining competitive quotes and outperforming the otherwise optimal market maker of
(
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
)
in terms of P&L. Our results provide the first formal counterpart to the qualitative insights of MacKenzie
(
MacKenzie 2006
)
, highlighting how financial models not only predict but also actively shape market outcomes.

Our Contributions.

To model performativity in a general financial setting, we proceed axiomatically. Specifically, we posit that the evolution of the mid-price
s(t)
of a financial instrument is influenced by

(1)

a time-varying reference process
r(t)
, representing the valuation implied by prevailing financial models or strategies;

(2)

a mean-reverting dynamic that causes
s(t)
to adjust toward
r(t)
; and

(3)

a parameter
\epsilon>0
that governs the speed or intensity of this adjustment, i.e., the degree of performativity present in the system.

Starting from a standard Brownian motion asset price formation model
(
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
)
, this leads to the following stochastic process formulation:

(1)

ds(t)=\underbrace{\left(r(t)-\epsilon s(t)\right)dt}_{\text{performative effect}}\,\,\,+\hskip-5.0pt\underbrace{\sigma\,dW(t).}_{\text{original process}}

Here,
\epsilon
is the
performative sensitivity
that controls how strongly the asset price conforms to the reference
r(t)
based on the model. Unlike standard diffusion processes, which assume a separation between price evolution and the models that describe it, this formulation allows to embed
any
model-driven valuation directly into the price dynamics. This breaks the canonical use of diffusion processes that separate the description of market prices and financial models, explicitly capturing the feedback loop that defines performativity. As
\epsilon
increases, the price is increasingly shaped by the theory that informs market actions, reinforcing the circular feedback loop at the heart of performative dynamics.

Performativity in Market Making.

We instantiate our framework for market making. Let
r(t)
denote the reservation price produced by a dominant market-making strategy;
r(t)
captures the
Market Maker (MM)
’s
biased
valuation for the asset based on objectives such as inventory management, market influence or directional exposure. When performativity emerges, e.g., through strategy crowding or monopolistic influence, the mid-price conforms to this perception and mean reverts to
r(t)
, turning the MM’s strategy into a self-fulfilling prophecy, which misspecifies its own signals.

To illustrate the above, we consider the workhorse inventory-based model of
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
(A&S), which captures core market-making trade-offs (inventory aversion, asymmetric quoting) in a tractable form. By deriving a closed-form solution of the dynamics in (
1
), we show how, under our performativity-adjusted dynamics, the original strategy experiences alpha decay and develops a deterministic drift, violating the martingale property.

We then analytically construct a performativity-aware market maker that distinguishes this performative impact from the noise and makes systematic profits by simultaneously posting competitive quotes and arbitraging the prevailing inventory strategy. Our simulations show that this agent achieves higher expected P&L and lower variance than both the standard inventory strategy and a symmetric-quote benchmark.

To complement the derived MM’s analytical (optimal) policy, we further look beyond the simplifying assumptions made for tractability (i.e., linear approximation). We show that if we equip this closed-form solution with learnable parameters and use a machine-learning meta-heuristic approach, we obtain enhanced results. To our knowledge, this is the first rigorous mathematical and quantitative framework for financial performativity, providing formal evidence on how this phenomenon can be systematically exploited in high-frequency trading.

2.
Preliminaries

A market maker (MM) provides liquidity by continuously quoting ask and bid prices around a reference price, typically the mid-price
s
, at which she is willing to sell or buy an asset. In return, she earns a spread premium from executed limit orders. The ask and bid premia are defined as
\delta^{a}:=p^{a}-s,\,\delta^{b}:=s-p^{b}
, where
p^{a}
and
p^{b}
are the quoted ask and bid prices (typically above and below the mid-price), respectively. The
quoted spread
is given by
\delta:=\delta^{a}+\delta^{b}
, and the MM’s
reservation price
is defined as the midpoint between the quotes,
r:=\frac{p^{a}+p^{b}}{2}=s+\frac{\delta^{a}-\delta^{b}}{2}
. This reservation price captures the MM’s subjective valuation of the asset, influenced by her objectives, such as inventory constraints or directional beliefs.

As is standard
(
Ho and Stoll 1981
;
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
;
Fodra and Labadie 2012
;
Guéant et al. 2012
;
Álvaro Cartea and Jaimungal 2015
;
Spooner and Savani 2020
)
, we assume that executions occur when market orders of sufficient size arrive and match the MM’s quotes. These random arrivals are modeled as independent Poisson processes,
N^{a}
and
N^{b}
, for the ask and bid sides, with intensities

(2)

\lambda^{a}(\delta^{a})=Ae^{-k\delta^{a}},\quad\lambda^{b}(\delta^{b})=Ae^{-k\delta^{b}},

where
A,k\geq 0
capture market order frequency and book depth, respectively. Notice that an execution of a limit order from the MM is a function of its distance from the mid-price with tighter quotes having a higher probability to get filled.

According to the above, the MM’s cash holdings,
X_{t}
, and her inventory,
q_{t}
, at time
t
follow the dynamics
dX_{t}=p^{a}dN^{a}-p^{b}dN^{b}
, and
q_{t}=N^{b}_{t}-N^{a}_{t}
. The MM seeks to maximise the expected utility of her terminal wealth at time
T
using the controls
\delta^{a},\delta^{b}
. Assuming exponential utility with risk aversion parameter
\gamma>0

(
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
)
, the MM’s objective is

(3)

u(s,x,q,t)=\max_{\delta^{a},\delta^{b}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[-\exp\left(-\gamma(X_{T}+q_{T}s(T)\right)\right],

where
s(T)
is the asset’s terminal price. By solving the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB),
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
(A&S) derive the following closed-form expressions for the optimal quoting strategy, i.e., reservation price and quoted spread

(4)

\displaystyle r(t,s)

\displaystyle=s-\gamma q\sigma^{2}(T-t),

(5)

\displaystyle\delta^{a}+\delta^{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)+\gamma\sigma^{2}(T-t),

where
\sigma
is the volatility of the asset. Note that the MM’s quotes are symmetric around her reservation price
r
, but typically skewed with respect to the mid-price
s
as a result of her inventory’s impact on her quotes. When the inventory is positive,
q>0
, then
r<s
, leading to a tighter ask spread
\delta^{a}
and a desire to sell; conversely, when the inventory is negative,
q<0
, then
r>s
, tightening the bid side and indicating the MM’s desire to buy the asset.

3.
Performativity Framework

In market making, it is natural to view the mid-price dynamics as responding to the market makers’ reservation prices, which express their beliefs about the
“fair”
value of the asset and guide their quoting decisions. These quotes reflect not only market conditions, but also the internal logic or biases of the models that generate them. Since ask and bid prices are posted publicly and updated at high frequency, they represent observable outputs that can be incorporated into the underlying price formation process.

To formalize this, recall the general performative feedback model of equation (
1
), where the evolution of the economic variable – here, the mid-price
s(t)
– depends on its own current value and the economic process that it conforms to – here, the reservation price
r(t)=\frac{p^{a}(t)+p^{b}(t)}{2}
of the prevailing financial model. Thus, in the context of market making, equation (
1
) could be rewritten as:
ds(t)=\left(f(p^{a}(t),p^{b}(t))-\epsilon s(t)\right)dt+\sigma\,dW(t)
. Since quotes are typically placed around the mid-price,
s(t)
, we can write the reservation price as
r(t)=s(t)+g(t)
, where
g(t)
is a
strategy-dependent
adjustment term.

Plugging this into the general performativity formulation of equation (
1
), the mid-price dynamics become

\displaystyle ds(t)

\displaystyle=\left[r(t)-\epsilon s(t)\right]dt+\sigma\,dW(t)

\displaystyle=\left[s(t)+g(t)-\epsilon s(t)\right]dt+\sigma\,dW(t)

\displaystyle=\left[g(t)-(\epsilon-1)s(t)\right]dt+\sigma\,dW(t).

Letting
\xi:=\epsilon-1>0
, we can write

(6)

ds(t)=\xi\left(\frac{g(t)}{\xi}-s(t)\right)dt+\sigma\,dW(t),

which expresses that the market price
s(t)
gradually adjusts toward the reservation price
r(t)
, with some decay
\xi>0
and noise term
\sigma dW(t)
. Equation (
6
) takes the form of a stochastic process that mean-reverts toward the time-varying target
g(t)/\xi
. When
s(t)
exceeds this target, the drift becomes negative, pulling the mid-price downward; and vice versa, when it falls below, the drift turns positive, pushing it upward. This transformation decouples
s(t)
from its dependence on the full reservation price
r(t)
, isolating the exact contribution of the strategic term
g(t)
. In particular, it makes explicit how the financial model causes market prices,
s(t)
, to conform to its strategically time-varying mean,
g(t)
, through a dynamic feedback loop.

A performative-aware agent that models these dynamics can solve Equation (
6
) using the standard integrating factor method, which applies to linear stochastic differential equations with time-varying drift. This yields the solution:

s(T)=e^{-\xi(T\!-\!t)}s(t)+\smash{\int_{t}^{T}}\!e^{-\xi(T\!-\!u)}g(u)\,du+\sigma\smash{\int_{t}^{T}}\!e^{-\xi(T\!-\!u)}dW(u).\vskip 3.1298pt

which follows Gaussian distribution with mean

\mu(s(T))=e^{-\xi(T-t)}s(t)+\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\xi(T-u)}g(u)\,du,

and variance,
\text{Var}(s(T))=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\xi}\left(1-e^{-2\xi(T-t)}\right)
. This expression shows that the performative process conforms towards a moving target, driven by the strategy
g(u)
, which heavily influences the mean of the distribution. In particular, the future mid-price
s(T)
results from a decaying memory of the initial price, an aggregated signal of the strategy-dependent adjustment
g(u)
, and a noise term modulated by
\sigma
. The strength of the feedback is captured by the performativity parameter
\xi
which controls how strongly and quickly the price responds to the strategy. As
\xi
increases,
s(T)
approaches
g(T)
, meaning that performative effects dominate market dynamics, or, in other words, that the price reverts fast to the strategy’s feedback.

Finally, we can easily see that the performative process is
not
a martingale, as the expected future value is not equal to the present. This is important in terms of formulation, because we have a strategy-dependent directional bias. This means that if a financial strategy is performative, it creates endogenous risks and market inefficiencies that can be arbitraged by other participants.

Application to A&S

We now assume that the performative strategy corresponds to the A&S model, defined in Equations (
4
) and (
5
). In this setting, the induced price dynamics follows

ds(u)=\left[-\gamma\sigma^{2}q(T-u)-\xi s(u)\right]du+\sigma\,dW(u),\,u\in[t,T],

where the mean reverting force is shaped by the inventory dependent feedback of the A&S reservation price,
g(u)=-\gamma\sigma^{2}q(T-u)
and the MM’s risk aversion
\gamma
. Substituting this form into the general solution for
s(T)
, we have

\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\xi(T-u)}g(u)\,du=-\gamma\sigma^{2}q\int_{t}^{T}(T-u)e^{-\xi(T-u)}du.

Using some standard algebra, the strategy-dependent integral simplifies to a closed-form expression:

\Delta_{\xi}:=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\left[1-e^{-\xi(T-t)}(1+\xi(T-t))\right].

Substituting this into the general solution, the terminal mid-price becomes

(7)

\boxed{s(T)=e^{-\xi(T-t)}s(t)-\gamma\sigma^{2}q\cdot\Delta_{\xi}+\sigma\int_{t}^{T}e^{-\xi(T-u)}dW(u).}

This clearly highlights how the A&S strategy affects the price path: the inventory
q
, risk profile
\gamma
, and time horizon
T-t
directly influence the deterministic drift, while stochastic noise is dampened by the performative parameter
\xi
. The term
\Delta_{\xi}
captures the performative inventory impact, quantifying how strongly the market reacts to quoting under the A&S rule.

4.
Trading Model

We now show how a market maker, aware of performativity effects, can construct a quoting strategy that is optimal with respect to the price distribution induced by the prevailing strategy.

4.1.
Value Function

Following A&S, we assume the agent has an exponential utility over her final P&L. Under this assumption, and given the initial mid-price
s(t)=s

Solving the Optimisation Problem

To solve Equation (

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\boxed{r^{*}_{perf}=e^{-\xi(T-t)}s-\gamma\sigma^{2}\left(q\cdot\Delta_{\xi}+q_{perf}\frac{(1-e^{-2\xi(T-t)})}{{2\xi}}\right).}

Therefore, for the expression of the optimal spreads that we obtained from solving the two maximums we have that

\delta^{a,b}_{*,perf}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)-\frac{1}{4\xi}\gamma\sigma^{2}(e^{-2\xi(T-t)}-1)\pm(r^{*}_{perf}-s),

and since
spread^{*}_{perf}=\delta^{a}_{*,perf}+\delta^{b}_{*,perf}
, this leads to

(12)

\displaystyle\boxed{spread^{*}_{perf}=\frac{2}{\gamma}\log\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)+\frac{1}{2\xi}\gamma\sigma^{2}(1-e^{-2\xi(T-t)}).}

Remark 1 (Linear approximation).

Note that, since we used a linear approximation for the order arrival rates to make them independent of
q
, equation (
12
) technically provides a sub-solution of the HJB equation.
This is because

\frac{A}{k+\gamma}\left(e^{-k\delta_{*,perf}^{a}}+e^{-k\delta_{*,perf}^{b}}\right)\geq\frac{A}{k+\gamma}\left(2-k(\delta_{*,perf}^{a}+\delta_{*,perf}^{b})\right),

which implies that if
\underline{u}(t,s,q_{perf},q,x)
is our solution, then

\underline{u}(t,s,q_{perf},q,x)\leq u(t,s,q_{perf},q,x).

We address this limitation in Section
5
, where we also enhance our analytical expression of
r^{*}_{perf}
with learnable parameters to increase the performance of the performative strategy.

4.3.
Interpreting the Performative MM Policy

The MM’s optimal reservation price as expressed Equation (
11
) illustrates how performative effects influence quoting behaviour.

First, note that
\Delta_{\xi}\geq 0
and
E_{\xi}:=\frac{1}{2\xi}\left(1-e^{-2\xi(T-t)}\right)\geq 0
for all
\xi>0
and
t\leq T
. This implies that the adjustment of the reservation price relative to the mid-price largely depends on the values of
q
and
q_{perf}
. Recall that when the reservation price is below the mid-price, the agent wants to sell (evaluating the asset as overvalued), and conversely, when it is above, the agent wants to buy. For the performative-aware agent, this preference is governed by the term
q\Delta_{\xi}+q_{perf}E_{\xi}
and the current price
s
. Specifically, if this term is positive, the agent’s reservation price satisfies
r^{*}_{perf}<e^{-\xi(T-t)}s\leq s
for all
\xi>0,t\leq T
, indicating that the agent wants to sell regardless of
s
. Figure
1
summarises the performative MM’s quoting strategy across the full range of inventory states.

When the term
q\Delta_{\xi}+q_{perf}E_{\xi}
is negative, the MM’s quoting decision also depends on the scaled mid-price. To identify the inventory regions in which the performative MM changes her behaviour, one can compare Equation (
11
) with the current price
s
. This leads to critical thresholds for
q_{perf}
given by
h(s,t)\pm|q|\frac{\Delta\xi}{E_{\xi}}
, where
h(s,t)=-\frac{s(1-e^{-\xi(T-t)})}{\gamma\sigma^{2}E_{\xi}}
. These thresholds mark the points at which the performative agent’s quoting strategy changes. In contrast to the classical (A&S) agent, the performative MM responds strategically to her anticipated impact on the market, for which she is now aware, leading to more sophisticated quoting behaviour.

To see this, consider, for example, the case when
q\leq 0
and
q_{perf}\geq 0
. Despite already holding a non-negative inventory, the performative MM continues to accumulate inventory until the point
h(s,t)+|q|\frac{\Delta\xi}{E_{\xi}}
is reached. At the same time, the A&S strategy also buys the asset, but because of lack of inventory;
q\leq 0
means she was short selling before. Beyond this point (or the point
|q|\frac{\Delta\xi}{E_{\xi}}
), the perfomative MM’s behaviour becomes even more strategic. She now anticipates the positive deterministic drift in the asset’s price induced by the A&S strategy which signals MMs to buy the asset (when
q\leq 0\Rightarrow g(t)\geq 0
) and holds sufficient inventory to sell and profit from the increasing demand. Thus, with sufficient inventory, the performative MM continues to sell throughout the time that the price rises, up until other MMs shift their quoting behaviour as their inventories increase and eventually turn positive. In other words, the performative MM arbitrages the price until it stops rising due to the desire of the MMs to buy. This highlights how she exploits predictable price dynamics caused by non-performative (here A&S) agents. Similar observations can be made for the symmetric case where
q_{perf}\leq 0
and
q\geq 0
.

Remark 2.

It is worth mentioning that the derived performative MM strategy provides a formal verification of the qualitative insights of
MacKenzie 2006
concerning performative markets. The above modeling and analysis not only captures the self-fulfilling nature of the A&S trading model (through the closed-feedback loop between quotes and asst prices)
but also how these effects can create arbitrage opportunities that aware agents can exploit at least temporarily until the price discrepancies stop to exist. In the following section, we validate these theoretical insights through simulation, confirming the robustness of the performative strategy and its alignment with empirical observations described in
(
MacKenzie 2006
)
.

5.1.
Price Formation

For the simulations we consider a discrete time form of the process in equation (
6
) given by

(13)

s_{n+1}=s_{n}+\left[-\gamma\sigma^{2}q_{n}(T-t_{n})-\xi s_{n}\right]\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\cdot Z_{n},

where
s_{n}=s(t_{n})
,
\Delta t=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
,
Z_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. The first two terms represent the deterministic part of our process, and the third term represents the random noise. As a coherence check for our framework, we illustrate the step-by-step formation of the mid-price, showing how it conforms to the financial model. In Figure
2
, the first panel depicts the A&S impact term of
-\gamma\sigma^{2}q_{n}(T-t_{n})
, which is the main driver of the price. The second panel shows the whole deterministic part,
s_{n}+\left[-\gamma\sigma^{2}q_{n}(T-t_{n})-\xi s_{n}\right]\Delta t
, of the process, which captures the conformity towards the A&S impact. The third panel includes the noise term, producing the complete price path as given by equation (
6
). For illustrative purposes, we amplify the A&S impact by a factor of
10
. In practice, this can be interpreted, for example, as larger amounts of inventory
q
or more crowding of participants using the A&S strategy.

We see that the price trend primarily depends on the model-driven term, with stronger effects causing the price to converges more closely towards its deterministic component. Interestingly, performative fluctuations of the deterministic part may appear as market noise, especially in high-frequency trading, and remain unnoticed. However, in the presence of performativity, not only are they not random, but they are fundamentally determined by the prevailing financial model; in this case, the A&S’s inventory adjustment strategy. As we show next, a performative-aware agent can distinguishe the model impact from actual noise and capitalize on that to systematically make profits.

Simulation Setup

For the simulations, we set the parameters as
T=1
,
\sigma=2
,
k=1.5
,
\Delta t=0.005
,
q_{0}=0
similarly to
(
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
)
. For the MMs’ risk-aversion profile,
\gamma
, we consider three representative cases:
\gamma=0.1
(high-risk),
\gamma=0.5
(medium-risk), and
\gamma=0.8
(low-risk). The risk profile of the original A&S strategy influences her inventory pressure and, thus, the price itself. For the performative parameter
\xi
, we consider 20 points in the range
[0.3,20]
covering weak to strong mean-reversion speeds.

Each simulation unfolds as follows: at each time step
t
(equivalently
n
), the strategy computes quotes
\delta^{a}_{n}
,
\delta^{b}_{n}
and the state variables are updated for
t+\Delta t
(equivalently
n+1
). The quotes are filled probabilistically based on the intensities from equation (
2
). If
\delta^{a}_{n}
is filled, the agent’s inventory,
q_{n+1}
, decreases by one and her wealth in cash changes by
s+\delta^{a}_{n}
. Analogously, if
\delta^{b}_{n}
is filled,
q_{n+1}
increases by one and the agent’s wealth in cash changes by
s-\delta^{b}_{n}
. We assume that the agents can short stock (
q<0
). The P&L is updated as
PnL_{n+1}=x_{n}+q_{n}\cdot s_{n}
and the mid-price dynamics follow equation (
13
). Theoretically the solution of the optimisation problem allows for
\delta^{a}
,
\delta^{b}

\leq 0
; for our strategies, this means that the agent will execute a market order. These simulation dynamics are used for the comparison of four strategies, which we summarize below.

Inventory strategy::

The A&S model computes the reservation price using equation (
4
) and the spread from equation (
5
).

Symmetric Strategy::

Quotes are placed symmetrically around the mid-price without an opinion on the asset’s evaluation (equivalently
r=s
). The strategy’s spread uses the term
\frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{k}\right)
, that is shared by both the A&S model and our performative strategies (see equations (
5
) and (
12
)). This is a natural choice as it isolates the effects of the spread adjustments (second terms) which are unique to each strategy.

Performative Strategy::

Our analytically derived performative strategy which quotes around
r^{*}_{perf}
as given by equation (
11
) with spread given by equation (
12
).

Theta-Enhanced Performative Strategy::

To improve upon our analytical solution and maximise the MM’s utility (recall that our analytical approach yields a sub-solution of the MM’s utility maximization problem, since
\underline{u}(t,s,q_{perf},q,x)\leq u(t,s,q_{perf},q,x)
due to linear approximation), we introduce three learnable parameters, one for each term of the reservation price, and train them on simulation data as a meta-heuristic approach. For training we use Optuna
4
4

4

https://optuna.org
, a machine learning framework for hyperparameter optimization. The resulting reservation price price is, thus, calculated as

(14)

r^{*,\theta}_{perf}=\theta_{0}e^{-\xi(T-t)}s-\gamma\sigma^{2}\left(\theta_{1}q_{market}\cdot\Delta_{\xi}+\theta_{2}q_{perf}E_{\xi})\right)

while the spread remains the same as in the original performative strategy (see equation (
12
)).

5.3.
Results

Our main results are illustrated in Figure
3
. Across all risk-profiles (
\gamma
’s) of the A&S model, the performative strategies exhibit superior PnL profiles compared to the benchmarks. For every
\xi
, both performative strategies achieve higher PnL with lower variance than the A&S strategy. As the performative speed
\xi
increases, the PnL of the performative strategies improves. Both strategies surpass the symmetric strategy’s PnL after a certain break-off point (early on), while maintaining lower variance throughout the entire
\xi
range. Importantly, their variances are consistently narrower than that of the symmetric strategy and close to that of the A&S model.

Note that the fundamental behaviour of the A&S model dictates that as it becomes more risk-averse (
\gamma
increases), its PnL declines and deviates further from the symmetric strategy, since the MM goes to great lengths to avoid accumulating inventory. This reflects the classic trade-off between higher profits with more variance and lower profits with less risk. Our performative strategies overcome this fundamental limitation, validating our theoretical predictions about the behaviour of the performative-aware agent. If the performative agent were just quoting tighter to achieve higher PnL, that would imply a larger variance in her terminal wealth than the symmetrical strategy and greater exposure to accumulating inventory. Instead, she quotes smarter and capitalizes on the deterministic discrepancies caused by the A&S model in the price, directly breaking the inherent limitations of this trade-off.

Interestingly, we notice that the learnable theta-enhanced performative strategy, achieves the break-off point for even smaller values of
\xi
’s and with a steeper PnL increase for every
\gamma
. This means, that with the introduced parameters, the performative strategy becomes more profitable faster, even at low performativity speeds, and outperforms other strategies by a larger margin. This is important because the strategy becomes sensitive enough to spot and capitalise on even small performative effects in the market. Additionally, as
\gamma
increases, the profit margins for the learnable strategy increase since higher risk aversion in the A&S model causes price to skew further. This provides further evidence that performative strategies can distinguish noise from the systematic effects caused by the prevailing financial model in the market.

6.
Conclusions and Future Work

Our work introduced a formal mathematical framework to model the fundamental concept of performativity in financial markets. To capture the circular nature of performativity, we revisited the traditional use of diffusion processes and placed financial models at their core. We showed how equation (
1
) models price dynamics that conform to the prevailing financial model in the market. By instantiating this framework to market making, we derived analytical expressions that validated our approach and formalised the qualitative insights of
(
MacKenzie 2006
)
. Specifically, we showed (a) how price conform towards the prevailing market making model (
Avellaneda and Stoikov 2008
in our case) and (b) how performative effects generate arbitrage opportunities, arising from model misspecification caused by its own use. We constructed performative strategies that outperform traditional benchmarks by overcoming standard return-risk trade-offs and exploiting performative feedback effects.

This work open several directions for future research particularly at the intersection with Machine Learning (ML) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). As AI-driven models increasingly shape financial markets, it is crucial to understand how the price dynamics in equation (
1
) would change if the economic process
r(t)
depends, for instance, on the parameters of a prevailing ML model or on the policy of an RL algorithm. This question, bridging our financial performativity framework with the emerging ML and RL performativity theories, could yield strong theoretical guarantees and practical insights. Finally, extending our framework to other financial applications with stochastic processes in their analysis, such as option pricing, and fitting it to empirical data could provide further quantitative evidence of performativity in real markets.
