Integrating the primary care perspective in the implementation of a diabetes prevention program for patients with recent gestational diabetes: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Katelyn Sushko, Megan Racey, Diana Sherifali, Kelly Smith, Karen Fleming, Sundeep Banwatt, Lorraine Lipscombe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) are at high risk for the development of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Although postpartum diabetes prevention programs (DPPs) reduce the risk of diabetes after pregnancy, the transition from prenatal to postpartum diabetes care is suboptimal. As primary care providers (PCPs) are generally responsible for providing postpartum preventive care, incorporating PCPs’ perspectives in the co-design of a DPP is essential to optimize the integration of prenatal and postpartum diabetes care and DPP implementation.MethodsWe used a qualitative descriptive study design to elicit PCP perspectives on implementing a new multi-centre DPP for patients with recent GDM. We purposefully sampled primary care physicians (PCPs) from Primary Care Networks connected with each DPP centre and conducted a focus group and individual one-on-one interviews. Directed conventional content analysis, based on Valentijn’s integrative functions of primary care, guided the analysis of focus group and interview transcripts.ResultsOur analysis identified three overarching themes that align with Valentijn’s micro, meso, and macro dimensions of integrated care. Micro-level integration focused on improving prenatal to postpartum care transition between providers and leveraging opportunities to integrate maternal and newborn care. Meso-level integration emphasized the need for a team-based model of care that incorporates allied health professionals and optimizes communication between these groups. Integration at the macro level included developing processes to delineate responsibility for postpartum T2D testing while reducing the burden on women through more efficient testing methods.ConclusionsThe PCP perspective provides a valuable addition to the implementation strategy of a new multi-centre DPP for patients with recent GDM. To optimize the integration of prenatal and postpartum diabetes care and uptake of a DPP, PCP recommendations included leveraging newborn care, building on team-based care models, and more efficient postpartum T2D testing processes. Background

Introduction

Women with prior gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) are at high risk for the development of type 2 diabetes (T2D) [1]. For instance, the literature shows that 20–50% of these women will develop T2D within ten years of a GDM diagnosis; 60% will be diagnosed with T2D sometime later in life [2–5]. Due to the consequences associated with T2D, both at a patient and health system level, there is a recent focus on efforts to prevent T2D after pregnancy among women with prior GDM.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) indicate that diabetes prevention programs (DPPs) that provide personalized diet and physical activity support can reduce the risk of T2D after GDM between 24 and 43% [6–12]. However, translating interventions from the research setting to real-world implementation has challenges. Problems with uptake and engagement are particularly salient for programs involving newly postpartum women, which require an effective transition from prenatal to postpartum care at a time when competing demands of new motherhood reduce priority for self-care [13].

In addition to challenges related to the life stage of new motherhood that impact the ability to participate in a DPP, transitions from prenatal to postpartum care following a GDM pregnancy are often hampered by poor communication and integration between providers and organizations across care settings [14]. While prenatal care for GDM patients frequently involves intensive management by a specialized diabetes team, patients are generally discharged from diabetes care after delivery, often without a clear follow-up plan [15]. Even though women are made aware of their elevated risk of T2D and the ongoing need for future support, this care gap can leave them feeling abandoned by their healthcare team [15]. Currently, clinical guidelines recommend that women with GDM undergo postpartum T2D testing between six weeks and six months postpartum and receive counselling on T2D prevention [16]. In most cases, patients seek postpartum care from their primary care provider (PCP). While PCPs consider T2D prevention and testing their responsibility, many report being unaware that their patient had GDM [14], underscoring the need for better integration between prenatal specialty care and postpartum primary care. Evidence also indicates that postpartum T2D testing rates remain low among this population [17–21], even with frequent PCP visits [17], suggesting that women see their PCP for reasons other than T2D prevention and testing.

Even with adequate postpartum T2D follow-up, PCPs often do not have the expertise, resources, and capacity to provide effective T2D preventive counselling [14, 22]. There is growing evidence for the benefits of structured DPPs, which offer T2D prevention education and coaching for lifestyle modification by allied health professionals for high-risk patients, including those with recent GDM [6–12]. Avoiding Diabetes After Pregnancy Together with Moms (ADAPT-M) is a DPP that provides T2D prevention education and health coaching to promote goal-setting and behavioural changes among patients with recent GDM [23]. The pilot RCT demonstrated excellent feasibility and patient-reported positive experiences [23, 24]. A strategy is now underway to integrate ADAPT-M within the local healthcare systems of three Ontario, Canada settings.

While evidence shows that a DPP can be feasibly delivered by certified diabetes educators to GDM patients in the postpartum period [23], program referral, uptake, and implementation require effective integration of prenatal GDM care and postpartum primary care. Integrated care is a concept that seeks to overcome fragmentation by bringing together key aspects of healthcare design and delivery, allowing patients to receive continuity of care across the spectrum, from health promotion and disease prevention to disease management and rehabilitation [25]. Care integration is especially critical for populations living with or at risk of chronic disease [26], such as women with prior GDM. Primary care is at the center of the healthcare system and is frequently the point of coordination across providers and services [27]. Thus, PCPs have critical roles as integrators and navigators of a patient’s healthcare journey. According to Valentijn et al., the integrative functions of primary care occur in three dimensions: (1) micro or clinical integration, (2) meso or professional and organizational integration, and (3) macro or health system integration [28]. Incorporating the PCP perspective into the co-design and implementation of a DPP across these dimensions of integrated care may reduce care fragmentation, enable a smoother prenatal-to-postpartum transition, and contribute to improved DPP implementation. In the current study, we aimed to explore the perspectives of PCPs regarding their role in implementing a new DPP for patients with recent GDM within the context of the dimensions of integrated care.

Methods

Study aim and design

The current study uses a qualitative descriptive design. We employed a focus group and semi-structured interviews to integrate the PCP perspective into the co-design and implementation of our DPP. We received ethics approval from our local ethics review boards (Women’s College Hospital Research Ethics Board, REB # 2024-0010-E; Michael Garron Hospital Research Ethics Board, REB # 923-2404-Mis-420; Trillium Health Partners Research Ethics, REB # 1221). We followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies framework [29] in reporting our study.

Setting, recruitment and participants

We purposefully sampled [30] family physician PCPs from the three Primary Care Networks (PCNs) within the catchment areas of our three DPP implementation centres in Ontario, Canada. Each PCN circulated study information via email and provided the researcher’s contact information to invite them to participate in either a focus group or an interview. The PCPs who were interested in participating contacted us and provided their informed consent. One PCN selected a focus group; the other two selected individual one-on-one interviews. Data collection and analysis were concurrent. We stopped sampling when we found no new themes relevant to the study aims [31]. Each participant received a 50 CAN$ gift card in appreciation of their time.

Data collection

We developed a semi-structured interview guide (Appendix) to incorporate the PCP perspective in the co-design and implementation of our DPP. Participants used secure video conferencing (Zoom) to attend either a virtual focus group or to complete a virtual one-on-one interview. Following data collection, the Zoom recordings were transcribed and imported into NVivo 14 (QRS International Pty Ltd) for qualitative analysis.

Data analysis

We used Hsieh and Shannon’s method of directed content analysis [32]. First, the researcher reviewed each transcript and immersed themselves in the data. Next, each transcript was coded line by line. We collated related codes to develop categories. Then, the categories were re-read and synthesized into themes. Finally, we mapped the resulting themes to Valentijn’s three dimensions of integrated care. We reached thematic saturation following the focus group and seven interviews. However, we conducted three additional one-on-one interviews as confirmation. Using two methods of data collection helped to triangulate and confirm themes. The senior qualitative author also consulted during the analysis process to verify credibility.

Results

Eight PCPs took part in the virtual focus group, while 10 completed one-on-one interviews between June 2024 and February 2025. The focus group participants were from the same PCN. Participants from the other two PCNs were four and six, respectively. The majority of participants were female (56%), completed medical training in Canada (65%), and had been in practice for over 10 years (59%) (Table 1). Data from one participant in PCN 1 on country of medical training and years of practice was missing. Following the completion of content analysis and mapping of results to Valentijn’s conceptual framework, our analysis resulted in three overarching themes coinciding with the micro, meso, and macro dimensions of integrated care.

Integration at the micro level (clinical integration)

PCPs suggested two dimensions of clinical integration. First, they referred to care coordination and integration at an individual level for professionals and institutions, represented by a handover between prenatal specialists and primary care. In the existing process, PCPs provide prenatal care until the woman reaches approximately 24 to 32 weeks’ gestation when care typically transfers to an obstetrician. Patients also undergo GDM testing around this time, between 24 and 28 weeks. If there is a diagnosis of GDM, women are referred to a specialized prenatal diabetes team (diabetes specialist, nurse educator, dietitian) who manages their diabetes care during pregnancy. Once the woman delivers, they are discharged from the diabetes care team and advised to seek ongoing diabetes care from their PCP for postpartum T2D testing and prevention counselling. One PCP said:

"The PCP has the primary role in doing those activities [postpartum diabetes prevention] because ultimately once the baby’s born, mom’s not in the care of gynecology…." [PCP 1]

A focus group (FG) participant confirmed:

"Primary care sees type 2 diabetes prevention in individuals as their wheelhouse." [FG Participant 1]

However, because the PCP stops following the patient once their care transfers to obstetrics, the PCP may be unaware of what transpired during the remainder of the pregnancy. For example, if the woman was diagnosed with GDM after the transfer of care, the PCP may not even be aware that they had GDM. Thus, PCPs heavily relied on specialist notes to inform them of critical tasks that must be completed, such as postpartum T2D testing and prevention counselling. One PCP said:

"When I get that note from the endocrinologist saying ‘this patient will need a diabetes check in six months,’ I send a future message to myself…‘okay, this patient needs a diabetes check.’" [PCP 3]

Another PCP agreed:

"With [the endocrinologist], she was really good at outlining sort of postpartum follow-up and what she recommended and so then often we’d put reminders in the patient’s chart." [PCP 2]

As described by our participants, seamless patient care relies on this critical postpartum handover from specialists to primary care. Unfortunately, in the current healthcare system, care handover can be fragmented and incomplete, with the PCP unaware of the need for key clinical tasks, such as postpartum T2D testing. One PCP explained:

"I think the treatment of GDM is quite well done… But postpartum care I’d say falls short compared to the standard… We would try to catch them for the follow-up but to be honest, I wouldn’t say there’s a good mechanism for flagging us to do diabetes bloodwork for our patients who have been diagnosed with GDM. Sometimes if I see it back in consult notes with a diagnosis of GDM and the specialist says ‘GP [general practitioner] please make sure six weeks postpartum to do bloodwork’ then I’ll set myself a reminder. But if I don’t see it, oftentimes it can be missed." [PCP 8]

Therefore, although PCPs viewed themselves as a critical component of preventative care for their patients, their ability to achieve clinical integration was often hindered by factors outside their control, particularly limited communication from specialists.

For the second dimension of clinical integration, PCPs described integration that combined maternal and offspring care. For PCPs who manage both mothers and their children, frequent well-baby checks in the first postpartum year provide an opportunity to coordinate maternal preventive care. An FG participant noted:

"[I’m] seeing them two to three days after discharge from hospital; sometimes [the] day of discharge." [FG Participant 2]

A PCP expanded:

"In many circumstances, the family doctor is even seeing the patient for the six week postpartum visit too and they’re seeing usually the new patient, the baby… there should be a dual appointment… an opportunity for that kind of care to be carried on through the family doctor." [PCP 1]

Another PCP agreed:

"I see them pretty often because I see their baby so then we just end up doing a visit to ask them how they are as well… I see them within a week after the baby is born and then one month, two months, three months so I usually book it for an appointment with the baby and… I’ll usually do either an A1C [glycated hemoglobin A1C] or the glucose tolerance test [at the three-month visit]." [PCP 5]

Combined maternal and offspring care provided an opportunity to integrate T2D prevention into existing healthcare visits. However, not all PCPs will see their patients as soon or as often as some of our participants. For example, many parents seek newborn care from a pediatrician; maternal care is not within their scope. One PCP noted:

"I think it gets tricky when sometimes the infant goes to a pediatrician and then sometimes I won’t see the mom as frequently. So, if the kid comes to me then typically I am seeing the mom almost every month, you know whenever they come in for the well-baby visits… But yeah, so it varies a lot if I don’t see the infant just because the mom typically doesn’t come in [on their own]." [PCP 4]

From the PCP perspective, newborn care encounters provided an opportunity to integrate maternal preventative care into routine clinical practice.

Overall, when implementing a new DPP for patients with recent GDM, PCPs desired complete handover from prenatal specialists. They would also appreciate a way to ensure that their patients connect with primary care for diabetes follow-up in case newborn care is provided by a pediatrician.

Integration at the meso level (professional and organizational integration)

Regarding integration at the professional and organizational level, participants in our study emphasized the importance of partnerships between healthcare professionals and team-based care. For example, while diet and physical activity counselling are integral to diabetes prevention, there may be limited expertise or time to provide this kind of support in primary care. In contrast, dietitians and nurse educators have mandates, skills and capacity to provide comprehensive lifestyle modification counselling. Ideally, liaising with specialists and allied health professionals and using a team-based model would optimize patient care. One PCP explained:

"Based on the way we work in family med, essentially when you’ve got a newborn, we’re sort of squeezing this baby into our schedule so that we catch the 48-hour visit and the one week and even the one month… So, I think honestly the way we work as family physicians doesn’t allow us to provide that impromptu … diabetes education to a mom who had gestational… the question is… is there a way to build in more support?… A diabetes educator and a dietician and a pharmacist?" [PCP 2]

Another PCP suggested:

"It’s always nice for additional allied health resources to be used by the primary care provider for these kinds of patients, like giving them access to a dietician and stuff like that…it would always be more helpful to give them more support rather than less." [PCP 1]

Another PCP added:

"I think most practitioners would be happy to have this [allied health team members assist with postpartum diabetes counselling and testing]." [PCP 3]

PCPs recognized that their demanding workloads limited the time available to adequately provide preventative care, and viewed allied health professionals as an important resource to help address this gap.

The need for team-based care also extended to integration between the organizations and professionals in primary care and diabetes prevention. Thus, we inquired how PCPs would prefer to receive communication regarding their patient’s enrollment, progress and discharge from a DPP. Unanimously, PCPs preferred to receive brief but informative notes. One PCP said:

"Let that program run, tell me what’s going on, cc me on all the notes, let me know if there’s any recommendations that need to be made on program discharge." [PCP 1]

An FG participant agreed:

"Happy to be notified they are enrolled… brief description of typical program… [another alert when they finish the program stating] patient achieved targets/results, next steps/follow-up." [FG Participant 5]

Another consequence of PCPs’ heavy workload was limited time to monitor their patients’ progress in a diabetes prevention program in detail. To keep PCPs generally informed, brief updates at enrollment and program completion were considered sufficient.

Thus, when implementing a DPP, PCPs desired a means for delegating diabetes-preventative counselling to allied health team members with shared responsibility emphasized. They would also appreciate brief but informative summaries of patient progress within the DPP.

Integration at the macro level (system integration)

System-level integration focused on how practices and policies impact healthcare efficiency and quality. For instance, the lack of clarity regarding responsibility for postpartum T2D testing was seen as contributing to low testing rates and missed T2D diagnoses. PCPs acknowledged the significant demands that accompany new motherhood and highlighted the need for the healthcare system to be proactive and flexible in facilitating testing completion. PCPs suggested that postpartum T2D testing should ideally be the responsibility of the prenatal team (obstetrics or endocrinology). Some of our participants revealed that specialists are already ordering postpartum diabetes bloodwork. One PCP said:

"Typically, it’s [postpartum diabetes testing] one of those things where it should have been handled by the endocrinologist. Like I do consider that part of their job… There are so many other things that they [PCPs] just may not remember and if the patient doesn’t remind them or the endocrinologist doesn’t remind them then it may slip the family doctor’s mind as well." [PCP 7]

Another PCP suggested:

“Ultimately if we wanted to capture the patient to make sure that they’re not lost to follow-up at all, the prenatal team should be giving the requisition and then just informing the primary care provider… you should just like kind of keep them accountable and give them the next step … with a baby coming in and a newborn around and sleep deprivation and all that stuff and then they can maybe hopefully even put it in their calendar, set an alert.” [Participant 1]

Another PCP agreed:

"I think a lot of the OBs will do one more [postpartum] bloodwork… If there is some short-term follow-up by OB or endocrinology, I mean that would be the best for longitudinal care, like continuity of care I mean to be honest. If they were diagnosed with GDM, like if they did follow the patients up to their very first screening bloodwork, I think that would be the best continuity… and then discharge them back to the GP… that would probably be the very best." [PCP 8]

Thus, when implementing a new DPP for patients with recent GDM, PCPs suggested improving care continuity and testing rates with a system-wide standardization of care. In the suggested care standardization, postpartum T2D testing would be the responsibility of prenatal diabetes specialists as part of the transition of care.

Discussion

This study integrated the PCP perspective into a new multi-centre DPP for patients with recent GDM. We mapped our focus group and interview findings to Valentijn’s integrative functions of primary care. The resulting themes focused on integration at the clinical, organizational and system levels. Insights from this study will aid in incorporating the views of primary care, which represent a critical part of integrated care, into our DPP to increase its adoption and implementation (Table 2).

Clinical integration

The two themes that emerged related to clinical integration included the need to improve the transition from prenatal to postpartum care and opportunities to integrate maternal and newborn care. Most important was the critical communication and handover when transitioning patients from the care of diabetes specialists to primary care after delivery. Unfortunately, the literature indicates that the transition of patients from prenatal to primary care after a GDM pregnancy is fraught with poor care coordination and fragmentation [33]. In some cases, care transitions are not consistently achieved, particularly within vulnerable populations. This disparity is associated with the presence of significant language, cultural, and other socioeconomic barriers that impede seamless progression through the healthcare system [34]. During the postpartum period, care transition gaps are evident. There is limited patient engagement in their long-term health planning, coupled with the burden placed on patients to navigate the complexity of transitioning from specialists to general practitioners and insufficient monitoring of postpartum care quality [35]. Best practices to support improved transition of care include providing adequate guidance to patients for postpartum follow-up, employing patient navigators, and monitoring the quality of postpartum care [33, 35]. Primary care could also integrate maternal postpartum follow-up with newborn care to improve the maternal care transition. Dual PCP mother-baby appointments could offer another opportunity to facilitate guideline-based diabetes follow-up for postpartum women who have GDM. However, our participants also noted that some parents take their newborn to a pediatrician for well-baby visits. Therefore, improving the prenatal to postpartum transition process through better handover of care between providers remains a priority to ensure effective diabetes follow-up for high-risk patients.

Organizational integration

Themes related to organizational integration emphasized the need for a team-based model of care that incorporates allied health professionals and effective communication between these groups. The team-based model of care leverages the skill sets of the individuals that make up allied health teams, such as dietitians and pharmacists, resulting in optimized patient care [36]. The PCPs in our study relayed that they may not have the expertise or capacity to provide preventative diabetes education to their postpartum patients. In contrast, allied health team members who have training in diabetes education may be better suited to take on this role. Evidence shows that team-based care increases the breadth and continuity of primary care services [37] and is particularly valuable in disease prevention and management [38–40]. The participants in our study and the literature recommend that within the team-based care model, communication between both groups remains critical to improve care [41].

System integration

Our interviews with PCPs resulted in themes related to system-level integration for facilitating guideline-based postpartum care to decrease loss to follow-up and care delays, particularly for postpartum diabetes testing. Clinical practice guidelines recommend that all women with GDM receive counselling for healthy behaviour change to prevent progression to T2D and undergo postpartum T2D testing within the first 6 months postpartum [16]. The literature indicates that postpartum T2D testing remains low among women with prior GDM [22–26]. The PCPs in this study suggested that a lack of clarity regarding responsibility for postpartum T2D testing may contribute to low testing rates, supported by other studies [1–43]. Our participants recommended reducing the care gap if prenatal diabetes specialists take the responsibility of initiating postpartum T2D testing, as is already occurring in some settings [44]. However, PCPs should be copied on the test requisition to ensure that they are up to date and to promote continuity of care. The PCPs in our study also acknowledged the significant demands that accompany new motherhood, which also contribute to limited testing. The current method for testing, the two-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), requires fasting and can be a time-consuming process for postpartum women. Emerging research shows that utilizing a shorter one-hour OGTT may increase testing and be more effective than the current standard [43]. In the United Kingdom, national guidelines recommend against the OGTT, instead suggesting the fasting plasma glucose [FPG] test from six to 12 weeks postpartum and A1C after 13 weeks [45]. In the early postpartum period, the use of the FPG may reduce logistical barriers associated with the OGTT. Later, after 13 weeks postpartum, A1C can be used as the results are no longer affected by perinatal physiology or treatments and fasting is not required [46]. Developing processes to delineate responsibility for postpartum T2D testing while reducing the burden on women through time-efficient testing may help to facilitate guideline-based postpartum care.

Study strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. We employed a qualitative design, focusing on purposefully selecting key participants and conducting in-depth interviews and a focus group to meet our study aims. We also integrated an established approach to data analysis to help us understand our findings. In addition, PCPs have limited capacity and time. Therefore, our interviews and the focus group were virtual to facilitate our access to this group. The current study also has limitations. These are related to the limits of qualitative research as this methodology does not have high generalizability. Thus, those applying our work to other contexts ought to be mindful. Furthermore, we must acknowledge the potential for self-selection bias among our participants, as PCPs who volunteered may be more motivated regarding diabetes prevention than the general provider population.

Conclusions

The PCP perspective is critical when developing a DPP for postpartum women with prior GDM. The interviews with our participants revealed themes related to care integration at the micro, meso and macro levels. Leveraging the insights gained from PCPs when developing DPPs may help to reduce care fragmentation, facilitate a seamless prenatal-to-postpartum transition, and improve program implementation.
