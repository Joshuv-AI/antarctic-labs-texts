Complementary targeted constructs factor XI/XIa inhibition
Authors: Richard C Becker
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

The north star of drug development has long been described using terms such as patient-specific, disease-specific, and precision medicine, with the goal of delivering the right therapy to the right patient at the right time. The field of oncology has come closest to realizing this vision. Through systematic integration of pathology and mechanistic biology, cancer has been redefined from a site-based diagnosis to a collection of tumors, clonal, and molecularly defined diseases. These insights translated directly into targeted therapies rather than broad syndromic labels.1

Success in the field of oncology rests on several foundational elements, which include deep tissue phenotyping, molecular stratification, and a willingness to redefine disease taxonomy, as mechanistic understanding evolves. Precision in oncology therefore emerged not from static classification, but from an ecosystem that connected biology, diagnostics, and interventional decision-making in a coherent loop.2

The question is whether thrombosis can be characterized at a comparable level of resolution, and whether therapy can be developed and selected with similar goals of care. Conceptually, the answer is yes3; but the pathways are uniquely complex. Thrombotic diseases are not discrete conditions; they are dynamic states arising from interactions among vascular biology, circulating cells, plasma proteins, flow conditions, inflammation, and external stressors. The conditions and disease states vary considerably, and thrombosis is often a phenotype.

Unlike tumors, thrombotic phenotypes can evolve over hours to days in response to infection, surgery, cancer progression, inflammation, autoimmune states, hemodynamic changes, or drug exposure. Platelet reactivity, coagulation factor kinetics, fibrinolytic capacity, endothelial activation, and immune-thrombotic signaling all fluctuate. As a result, therapies are largely “one-size-fits-most,” optimized for population-level risk reduction rather than individual mechanistic drivers.

Advancing toward precision thrombosis management will require a shift from static risk stratification to dynamic biological profiling. This could include multiomic characterization of coagulation and platelet pathways, functional assays that measure thrombin generation or platelet–leukocyte interactions, vascular imaging that reflects endothelial state, and circulating biomarkers of inflammation and immune activation. Ultimately, the oncology model suggests that precision in thrombosis will depend less on discovering a single “silver bullet” and more on building an integrated framework. This framework would align mechanistic biology, adaptive diagnostics, patient context, and therapeutic intent across time. In such a system, drug selection and dosing could evolve with the patient’s biological state, balancing efficacy and bleeding risk in real time.4,5

Contemporary factor XI (FXI)/FXIa–directed drug development includes an expanding landscape of biochemical and molecular approaches, each aligned with different aspects of FXI structure and function. Antisense oligonucleotides reduce hepatic synthesis of FXI, leading to sustained lowering of circulating protein levels. This approach offers predictable pharmacodynamics and infrequent dosing, making it attractive for chronic indications in which adherence is most challenging. Monoclonal antibodies can be engineered to block FXI activation, prevent substrate interactions, or inhibit FXIa catalytic activity. Domain-selective antibodies may preferentially attenuate thrombosis while preserving hemostatic potential, whereas catalytic inhibitors provide more potent and immediate anticoagulation. Small-molecule inhibitors aim to combine the convenience of direct oral anticoagulants with an improved bleeding profile. These agents typically target the active site of FXIa and may be best suited for long-term outpatient indications. Aptamers and peptide inhibitors offer additional modularity, including the potential for rapid reversibility, which may be advantageous in procedural or high-risk settings.

Collectively, these strategies highlight that FXI/FXIa inhibition is not a monolithic concept but a spectrum of interventions that can be aligned with specific clinical needs.6,7 The growing number of FXI/XIa inhibitors that are being developed, necessitates a precision-based approach for indication selection and patient stratification. In the absence of such tailoring, programs risk converging on a “one-size-fits-most” paradigm that dilutes differentiation, clinical value, and overall impact.

Lessons from thrombin and FXa inhibition clearly demonstrate that the site, timing, and reversibility of inhibition profoundly influence efficacy, bleeding risk, and clinical utility. For FXI, inhibition at the level of activation may leave alternative thrombin-generating pathways intact, maintaining hemostasis under stress, whereas complete catalytic inhibition abolishes FXIa-mediated amplification altogether. These mechanistic differences are likely to translate into distinct therapeutic niches rather than a single “best” FXI inhibitor. An example of targeted development is the REGN9933 and REGN7509 program.

Extensive nonclinical characterization of REGN9933 (apple 2–binding) and REGN7508 (catalytic-domain–binding) confirmed high-affinity selective inhibition of intrinsic-pathway thrombin generation without effects on the extrinsic pathway or prothrombin time. In vivo primate studies demonstrated durable activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) prolongation and antithrombotic efficacy without gastrointestinal or surgical bleeding signals. These data directly informed dose selection, safety margins, and biomarker strategy for first-in-human trials.8

First-in-human phase 1 evaluation in healthy volunteers confirmed that selective inhibition of FXI activation via the apple 2 domain produced predictable, dose-dependent intrinsic-pathway modulation without clinically meaningful bleeding. Mechanistically, REGN9933A2 selectively blocks FXI activation by FXIIa through binding to the apple 2 domain, aiming to minimize bleeding risk, whereas REGN7508 binds the catalytic domain to completely inhibit FXI activity for maximal anticoagulation. Together, these studies demonstrate a coherent translational arc from human genetics and basic coagulation biology, through primate thrombosis models, to rational, domain-specific antibody design, and clinical trials. ROXI-ATLAS (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT07175428) is evaluating whether mechanistically differentiated FXI inhibition with REGN9933A2 or REGN7508 can deliver effective anticoagulation with reduced bleeding compared with apixaban in the setting of atrial fibrillation. ROXI-APEX (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT07015905) is evaluating whether REGN7508 can reduce postoperative venous thromboembolism after total knee arthroplasty compared to enoxaparin or apixaban.

FXI/XIa inhibition represents a paradigm shift from empirical anticoagulation toward mechanistically informed precision therapy (Figure 1). By aligning drug design, mechanisms of inhibition, and clinical context, FXI/XIa-directed agents have the potential to expand anticoagulant use into populations historically constrained by bleeding risk. The field now faces a critical inflection point; success will depend on disciplined indication selection, thoughtful trial design, and an appreciation that different mechanisms of FXI/XIa inhibition are tools—not competitors—within a broader therapeutic armamentarium. If executed with rigor, structure and mechanisms of inhibition can truly inform strategy, and FXI/XIa inhibition may finally deliver on the long-standing promise of effective but safer anticoagulant therapy.
