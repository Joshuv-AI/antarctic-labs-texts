iCAT as an open-source platform for axial rotation and high-resolution imaging of organoids and living organisms
Authors: Ondřej Svoboda, Olga Machoňová, Petr Bartůněk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In dynamic light microscopy applications, imaging specimens from multiple angles while maintaining controlled temperature conditions is crucial for comprehensive and accurate analysis. To address these challenges, we present iCAT, an open-source multifunctional accessory designed to enhance light microscopy. It enables specimen rotation along the axial plane, incorporates built-in modules for precise temperature control, features an integrated LED, and includes a camera for real-time specimen monitoring. It can be easily 3D-printed and assembled using readily available electrical components. Combined with any up-right microscope, this versatile device allows researchers to capture detailed images and videos of organoid cultures and live or fixed specimens, such as C. elegans, zebrafish, drosophila, or mouse embryos. The potential applications of iCAT in investigating dynamic cellular processes and complex developmental phenomena are vast, inspiring researchers to explore its possibilities and push the boundaries of biological research.

Introduction

Light microscopy is an invaluable tool in biological research, enabling the visualization and analysis of various biological samples with exceptional detail. However, traditional light microscopy techniques have limitations when imaging specimens from different angles and maintaining precise temperature conditions. These limitations hinder our ability to explore dynamic cellular processes, investigate complex morphological changes, and observe real-time developmental events accurately. Here, we introduce iCAT (Intuitive Controlled Axial Tilt) to overcome these challenges and enhance the capabilities of light microscopy. This novel multifunctional accessory device redefines the capabilities of light microscopy. iCAT enables researchers to rotate specimens along their axial axis, maintain precise temperature control, provide ample illumination via an integrated LED, and capture preview images with its built-in camera for easier specimen navigation. This integrated approach overcomes the limitations of traditional light microscopy, offering researchers a versatile tool to explore the intricacies of biological systems. The iCAT device significantly impacts biological research, enabling new possibilities for studying dynamic cellular processes and complex developmental phenomena. Its applications are diverse, accommodating specimens ranging from human organoids to zebrafish, C. elegans, and mouse embryos. So far, several solutions enabling specimen rotation have been proposed, including light sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM)3–5, vertebrate automated screening technology (VAST) based on automated cellular-resolution platforms6–8, magnetic and acoustofluidic-based rotational devices9,10, and other custom-made devices11–13. These instruments operate on different principles but share the common ability to rotate specimens. However, many of these solutions are not well suited for high-resolution or live imaging applications (Supplementary Table 1). For example, VAST5,7,8 along with other instruments, such as custom built optical projection tomography system (OPT)13, optical projection tomography integrated microscope (OPTiM)12, or sample holder for axial rotation of specimens in 3D microscopy11 do not provide the capability to maintain a stable temperature during acquisition. As a result, live imaging of temperature-sensitive specimens is not feasible with these systems.

On the other hand, LFSM devices rely on lateral illumination of the samples, requiring a thorough setup process to ensure optimal performance and accurate results. While LSFM provides enhanced insights into the deeper layers of a specimen, it may also introduce artifacts, such as stripes or shadows, even when laser alignment is carefully calibrated. Although methods like multidirectional selective plane illumination microscopy (mSPIM) and digital scanned light-sheet microscopy have been developed to mitigate these issues, they add to the complexity of the system14,15. Furthermore, the adaptability of these instruments to standard microscopes is either impossible or highly challenging, with limited versatility. Additionally, many of these systems depend on complex and costly technology, making them inaccessible to a broad segment of the scientific community.

In contrast, the operating principle of iCAT is simple, and it targets these difficulties one by one. It features an accessory for the controlled environment (Supplementary Fig. 1). It is highly modular and can be easily mounted on any upright microscope (Fig. 1a), whether a regular wide-field, stereo, spinning disk, or confocal laser scanning microscope. Therefore, it offers high-resolution imaging with precise detail and high image contrast, typical for scanning techniques that provide high-resolution three-dimensional images with better contrast and detection sensitivity. The entire device can be fully controlled through its graphical user interface (GUI), allowing for remote operation via desktop connections. In addition, iCAT provides control features that enable autonomous operation (Supplementary Fig. 2). As a result, it can be integrated into a “smart home” environment, enabling researchers to manage their imaging setup remotely. This is particularly useful for long-term time-lapse experiments, as it allows continuous monitoring and adjustments without requiring physical presence.

Combined with a motorized stage, it is suitable for multi-position imaging (Supplementary Fig. 1c) while significantly less expensive than any other imaging module. Results and discussion

Design

iCAT is based on a simple 3D-printed design that houses all the necessary components (Supplementary Figs. 1, 3 and Videos 1, 2). An Arduino-based electronic circuit controls the whole device. The entire device is attached to an upright microscope that is responsible for acquiring the images during its use. The specimen of interest is loaded into the FEP tube, and the tube is subsequently inserted into the imaging chamber. To achieve precise axial rotation, iCAT incorporates a controlled motorized mechanism to rotate the sample around its central axis (Supplementary Videos 1 and 3). The desired rotation parameters can be programmed through a Processing-based GUI, and the rotation progress can be monitored in real-time using the built-in camera. The GUI (Supplementary Fig. 4) can also control the image resolution, allowing researchers to control camera speed. Under a low-light environment, the camera can use integrated and adjustable LED light, minimizing the need for further external light sources. iCAT has a built-in thermometer and a Peltier element to maintain a stable environment for studying living species. The thermometer constantly monitors the specimen’s temperature, while the Peltier element regulates the temperature within a specified range across varying ambient conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5). This ensures optimal imaging conditions and facilitates accurate analysis of temperature-sensitive biological processes, including embryonic development. Regarding image quality, we have demonstrated that it is not impaired by mounting the specimen in the FEP tube (Supplementary Figs. 6–8), whose use in LFSM applications is well established1,2. Since the refractive index of FEP for visible light (1.344) is similar to that of water (1.335), it is ideal for use as an immersion fluid filling the imaging chamber.

Adaptability and fabrication

One of the critical advantages of iCAT is its modularity and open-source nature, which promotes accessibility, customization, and collaborative development to contribute to its development and enhancement. iCAT has been tested on ZEISS Axio Zoom.V16, and Axio Examiner microscopes (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10), but it can be easily adapted to any up-right system. With few modifications, it can also be used in combination with inverted microscopes. iCAT can be easily attached to these microscopes, allowing for quick setup and removal. This ensures that the microscope remains functional for everyday use. iCAT can be fabricated using 3D printing technology for less than $50 (Supplementary Fig. 3b and Tables 2, 3), ensuring low-cost production and ease of assembly.

Additionally, all the electronic components and accessories required can be readily obtained for less than $250 (Supplementary Fig. 3c and Tables 2–3), making it feasible for researchers with varying resources and expertise levels to replicate and utilize the device in their laboratories. The 3D model, control circuit, and GUI are well-documented (Supplementary Instructions or https://github.com/osvobo/iCAT) and can be modified to accommodate specific experimental requirements. By sharing the design, software, and documentation, we aim to accelerate scientific progress and provide a versatile tool that can be adapted and customized for specific research needs.

Applications and impact

The multifunctional capabilities of iCAT open new possibilities for biological research, particularly in developmental biology, neurobiology, and cellular imaging. It provides unprecedented flexibility in time-lapse and end-point imaging. It is a powerful tool for experiments requiring precise sample orientation and stable imaging conditions. The device has proven particularly effective in the study of model organisms such as zebrafish, where cell migration, organogenesis, and tissue development necessitate the ability to reposition specimens during time-lapse experiments. We demonstrate iCAT’s usefulness for endpoint and time-lapse imaging of developing organoids. As a showcase, we filled an FEP tube with a single-cell suspension of human or zebrafish hematopoietic progenitors16, allowing them to develop inside. The resulting organoids were then imaged (Fig. 1b, c). In addition, iCAT can be used on a daily basis to document live transgenic (tg) animals (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Video 4) as well as in situ hybridization experiments from multiple angles (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Video 5). To demonstrate iCAT’s ability to perform precise rotation of the specimen, we performed long-term dual-angle imaging. As a result, we observed consistent and repeatable positioning as well as temperature maintenance across multiday experiment imaging sessions (Supplementary Fig. 11).

We further show that iCAT is a powerful and essential tool for fate-mapping progenitor cells during zebrafish gastrulation. At these early and highly fragile stages, determining the embryo’s orientation prior to mounting is not feasible, as high-resolution imaging is first needed to identify reference structures before precise reorientation can be performed. This makes conventional high-resolution microscopy techniques unsuitable for this task. While light-sheet microscopy allows for axial rotation, its resolution is insufficient to resolve the fine cellular details. Other methods fail to maintain the tightly controlled environmental conditions necessary for keeping such fragile embryos viable over long imaging sessions. In contrast, iCAT uniquely combines high-resolution imaging, precise axial reorientation, and stable environmental control. This integration enables continuous, long-term lineage tracing of progenitor cells from the earliest stages of development (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Video 6). Using iCAT, we uncovered progenitor trajectories that would otherwise have remained inaccessible with traditional approaches. In this particular showcase scenario, iCAT was used to elucidate the origin of primitive erythrocytes. For this, we generated double transgenic drl:h2b-dendra, etv2:gfp animals in which the pan-mesodermal draculin (drl) promoter drives the expression of nuclear Dendra, and the endothelial etv2 promoter drives GFP. These embryos were injected with CAAX- and NLS-tagged RFP mRNAs at the 1-cell stage and raised until the 50% epiboly stage, at which point they were imaged. A retrospective analysis of time-lapse recording allowed us to trace clusters of erythroid and endothelial cells back to the 50% stage. According to prevailing hypotheses, erythroid progenitors arise from a bipotent hemangioblast. In contrast, our lineage-tracing experiment demonstrated that the back-traced cells originated from progenitors already committed to erythroid, venous, or arterial lineages at the 50% epiboly stage, thereby arguing against the existence of a common bipotent precursor. This is the first direct evidence indicating that erythroid specification may occur at the onset of gastrulation. Moreover, thanks to its built-in GUI features that ensure full integration with microscope-controlling software, iCAT is also well-suited for tomography imaging (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Video 7). A straightforward setup procedure automatically synchronizes the desired rotation angle with focus and stage movements (Supplementary Video 8), ensuring autonomous tomography acquisition for further in-depth studies of 3D tissue organization in biological specimens.

Conclusion

iCAT represents a significant advancement in light microscopy, addressing the limitations of traditional techniques. By enabling precise specimen rotation, temperature control, illumination, and imaging, researchers can investigate intricate morphological changes and capture real-time developmental events that were previously impossible to observe. Fully integrated into the microscope environment, iCAT is designed to support autonomous image acquisition and is ready for seamless integration with artificial intelligence (AI)-driven analysis. For example, by leveraging scripts available through the ZEISS Open Application Development (OAD) GitHub repository, AI-guided acquisition can be readily implemented and extended to additional dimensions with minimal effort, further expanding the possibilities for advanced microscopy applications. By providing a platform to investigate dynamic processes and complex developmental phenomena, iCAT contributes to our understanding of fundamental biological mechanisms and facilitates discoveries in various fields of study. Moreover, the open-source nature of iCAT, combined with its user-friendly design and straightforward assembly, ensures accessibility, affordability, and adaptability, making it an invaluable tool for researchers in the biological sciences.

Methods

Animals

Zebrafish embryos and adult fish were raised in a circulating aquatic ZebTEC systems (Tecniplast) at 28 °C and maintained according to standard procedures17 and tracked using Zebrabase18. The following transgenic lines were utilized and crossed: Tg(gata1a:DsRed)19, Tg(cd41:EGFP)20, Tg(lmo2:EGFP)21, Tg(fli1:NLS-mCherry)22, Tg(drl:H2B-Dendra2)23, Tg(drl:EGFP)24, flk1:CAAX-mCherry25 and Tg(etv2:EGFP)26. Zebrafish embryos were imaged between 15 somites stage and 3 dpf. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Animal care and experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Institute of Molecular Genetics (13/2016 and 96/2018) in compliance with national and institutional guidelines.

Colony assays

Human peripheral blood was collected from a patient with polycythemia vera. Mononuclear cells were isolated using Biocoll (1.077 g/mL, Merck), counted, and resuspended in MethoCult H4100 (STEMCELL Technologies) semi-solid medium supplemented with 1 IU/mL of recombinant human EPO (rhEPO, EPREX, Janssen-Cilag) at a final concentration of 10 × 103 cells/ml. The cell suspension was loaded into an FEP tube using a borosilicate glass Pasteur pipette and cultivated using iCAT at 37 °C for 8 days. Blood samples were obtained with voluntary, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent in accordance with ethical approval for organoid derivation and data collection. The study was approved by the IMG Committee for Ethics and Handling Recombinant DNA and Human Material.

Whole kidney marrow cells were isolated from six-month-old adult Tg(gata1a:DsRed, cd41:EGFP) zebrafish (one female and one male) and fractionated using Biocoll (1.077 g/mL, Merck). Cells were counted and resuspended in complete methylcellulose16, supplemented with 100 ng/mL of recombinant zebrafish cytokines (Epo, Tpo)16 at a final concentration of 20 × 103 cells/ml. The suspension was loaded into an FEP tube using a borosilicate glass Pasteur pipette and cultivated using iCAT at 32 °C for 2 days.

Microinjections

For CAAX- and NLS-RFP mRNA injection, the following vectors were utilized:

The pCS2-CAAX-RFP vector was constructed using Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs) of StuI-digested pCS2+ backbone with a PCR-amplified TagRFP-CAAX sequence. The TagRFP-CAAX fragment was amplified from pGTag-TagRFP-CAAX-SV40 (Addgene, #117809) using the primers TagRFP-CAAX FWD (CCATCGATTCGAATTCAAGGCCATGGTGTCTAAGGGCGAGGAAC) and TagRFP-CAAX REV (AGTTCTAGAGGCTCGAGAGGTCAGGAGAGCACACACTTGC).

The pCS2-NLS-RFP vector was constructed using Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs) of EcoRI-digested pCS2+ backbone with a PCR-amplified TagRFP fragment and a synthetic NLS sequence (TGCAGGATCCCATCGATTCGATGGCTCCAAAGAAGAAGCGTAAGGTAATGGTGTCTA). The TagRFP fragment was amplified from pGTag-TagRFP-CAAX-SV40 (Addgene, #117809) using the primers TagRFP FWD (CCATCGATTCGAATTCAAGGCCATGGTGTCTAAGGGCGAGGAAC) and TagRFP REV (AGTTCTAGAGGCTCGAGAGGTCAGGAGAGCACACACTTGC).

In vitro transcription of mRNA was performed using the mMessage mMachine SP6 kit (Roche) from NotI-linearized pCS2 vectors. Zebrafish embryos were injected at the one-cell stage with a combination of CAAX-RFP and NLS-RFP mRNA (100 ng/µl each, 5 nl per embryo).

Zebrafish fixation, whole-mount in situ hybridization

Zebrafish embryos were staged at the 15-somite stage and anesthetized using MS-222 (Tricaine) prior to fixation. Embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. etv2 expression was detected by Hybridization Chain Reaction (HCR, Molecular Instruments) using a Standard HCR Probe (v3.0) in combination with a B3 amplifier labeled with 647 fluorophore. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Fluorescence imaging

Fluorescent images were acquired using a ZEISS Axio Examiner microscope with an LSM900 confocal scan head or an Axiocam 705 mono camera, a ZEISS Axio Zoom.V16 with an Axiocam 705 mono camera, an Andor Dragonfly 503 spinning disk microscope with a Zyla-4.2 sCMOS camera, and a ZEISS Lightsheet Z.1 microscope with a PCO.Edge 5.5 sCMOS camera. Maximum intensity projections were generated in ZEN Blue 3.10.103, and opposing angle pairs (e.g., 0° and 180°, 30°, and 210°) were fused using the Fusion tool in Huygens Professional v24.10 (SVI), with microscope parameters automatically loaded. The rotation step size was set to 180°. Views were fused by taking the maximum local pixel values, with the Subtract Background parameter enabled. Animated visualizations of embryos were created using ZEN Blue (ZEISS) or Imaris (Oxford Instruments).

PSF analysis

TetraSpeck™ Fluorescent Microspheres (T7284, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used to evaluate point spread function (PSF) characteristics. Imaging was performed using a ZEISS Axio Examiner microscope equipped with an LSM900 confocal scan head. Microspheres were mounted in low-melting (1:1000) agarose either inside the FEP tube with the iCAT or directly on a microscope plate (without iCAT). Imaging was conducted using a W N-Apochromat 20×/0.5 objective with 1× scan zoom. Excitation was provided by 488 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm laser lines. For each condition (with and without iCAT), three individual Z-stacks at three different positions on a plate or at three different positions and rotational angles within the FEP tube were acquired. PSF measurements were performed using ZEN 3.1.1 software (Zeiss) and its integrated PSF Wizard module. The transverse (Rayleigh) resolution and axial resolution (FWHM) were calculated for three emission channels corresponding to 515 nm, 580 nm, and 680 nm emission maxima. Statistical analysis was performed in Python using Welch’s t-test (two-tailed, unequal variance) to compare transverse and axial resolution values between the FEP and plate conditions for each wavelength. P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) analysis

SNR profiles were obtained from embryos imaged with or without iCAT using a ZEISS Axio Examiner microscope equipped with an LSM900 confocal scan head. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined for signal and noise in both the EGFP and mCherry channels. SNR values were calculated as the ratio of the mean signal intensity to the standard deviation of the noise for each Z-position, using the SNR plugin in ImageJ. The resulting SNR profiles were plotted for both imaging conditions, with solid lines representing measurements acquired with iCAT and dashed lines representing those acquired without iCAT. To enable comparison across datasets, the total Z-range was normalized to the interval [0, 1].

Benchmarking of iCAT’s performance

Benchmarking of iCAT’s rotation precision and long-term temperature recording was performed in a continuous, long-term imaging experiment lasting 44 h, using two distinct angle positions (0° and 60°). During the time-lapse, 1 µm fluorescent beads (Thermo Fisher T8880, dilution 1:1000) mounted in 1% agarose were imaged in both 515 and 580 nm channels at ~71-s intervals. Bead motion was quantified using the TrackMate plugin in ImageJ, and parameters track mean speed (µm/s), max distance traveled (µm), and total distance traveled (µm) were plotted.

3D modeling, animation rendering, and wiring diagram

The 3D model and its animated representations were generated using Fusion 360 (Autodesk). The model is available on GitHub at https://github.com/osvobo/iCAT/tree/main/support/3D. The wiring diagram was created using the Fritzing open-source platform.

3D printing and iCAT assembly

All 3D-printed parts were produced using PLA filaments on a Prusa Mini+ 3D printer (Prusa Research) with a 0.4 mm nozzle and a 0.15 mm layer height. The imaging chamber was printed with transparent material for the first three layers to allow the use of transmitted light from a microscope. The STL and 3MF files are available on GitHub at https://github.com/osvobo/iCAT/tree/main/support/3D. The whole assembly process is described in Supplemental Information and on GitHub at https://github.com/osvobo/iCAT/.

Specimen mounting

A 9 cm segment of clear 0.8 I.D./1.2 O.D. The FEP tube was cut and inserted into the 3D-printed FEP tube adapter, and a 1 cm piece of silicone rubber tube was attached to it. Dechorionated embryos were covered with 0.8% low-melting imaging-grade agarose containing 0.5× Tricaine mesylate in a 3 cm cell culture dish. Alternatively, hematopoietic progenitor cells were resuspended in semi-solid media supplemented with cytokines in a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube. A borosilicate glass Pasteur pipette was inserted into the silicone rubber tube, and the specimen was aspirated to the center of the FEP tube. The FEP tube was gently inserted into the imaging chamber. Grease was applied inside the openings at the top of the chamber using a syringe, sealing the FEP tube in place. The FEP tube adapter screw was tightened to secure it to the axial motor. Any remaining grease on the surface of the FEP tube was removed using soft, lint-free tissue with ethanol. The tube was rotated to ensure thorough cleaning, and the chamber was filled with water. Detailed mounting instructions are demonstrated in Supplementary Video 9 and described in Supplementary Information and on GitHub at https://github.com/osvobo/iCAT/.

Statistics and reproducibility

Boxplot data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and visualized using Python 3.11.7. The Student’s t test is used to compare the means between groups. A p-value of <0.05 was considered to be a significant difference. Violin plots include median, interquartile range and 1.5× interquartile range.
