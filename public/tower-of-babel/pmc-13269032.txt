Use of Heat for the Self-Management of Non-Specific Neck Pain: A Systematic Review
Authors: Denise Wilfling, Ahmad El-Allawy, Cathleen Muche-Borowski, Thomas Kötter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis systematic review aimed to summarise the evidence on the effectiveness of heat therapy for the treatment of non-specific neck pain, one of the most frequently reported musculoskeletal conditions.Materials and MethodsThis review followed established methodological guidelines for systematic reviews. A computerised literature search was conducted in Medline via PubMed in January 2025 using the key terms “neck pain” and “heat”. Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomised controlled trials, and the quality of evidence was graded using the GRADE approach.ResultsThe database search identified 91 studies, of which only two met the inclusion criteria. A total of 67 participants were included. Both studies investigated dry heat therapy using heat wraps or heat cells. While reductions in neck pain and neck-related disability were reported, the evidence base was very limited due to the small number of included studies, small sample sizes, and low methodological quality of both studies.ConclusionThe current evidence regarding heat therapy for non-specific neck pain is highly limited and insufficient to support either a recommendation for or against its use. The available findings are based on only two small studies with considerable methodological limitations, substantially limiting confidence in the conclusions. Nevertheless, given the absence of expected serious adverse effects, heat application may still be considered as a self-management option for patients with non-specific neck pain.Keywords: neck pain, systematic review, heat, thermo

Introduction

Neck pain represents one of the most commonly reported musculoskeletal conditions. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study indicated that neck pain as well as lower back pain are the second leading causes of years living with disability in younger people under 24 years.1 Worldwide, 203 million people are affected by neck pain.2 Neck pain is categorised according to duration into acute neck pain (three weeks or less), subacute neck pain (four to 12 weeks) and chronic neck pain (longer than 12 weeks). Importantly, the prevalence of people suffering from chronic neck pain is rising.3 Furthermore, there is a classification in “non-specific”, “soft tissue”, or “mechanical neck pain”.4 Mechanical neck pain is, for example, caused by disc prolapse, inflammation, fractures, tumours, or whiplash. If no cause for neck pain requiring treatment can be diagnosed, it is defined as non-specific neck pain. One main cause of non-specific neck pain is muscle tension. Potential risk factors for the development are female sex, body mass index (BMI), physical inactivity, duration of computer use, and perceived stress.5 However, evidence does not show a clear association between individual risk factors and the occurrence of neck pain, and suggested causes are multifactorial.5,6 Work-related factors are also essential for the occurrence of neck pain.6 Evidence shows that factors like work time, workload, and body position, as well as factors like low job control, lack of decision-making opportunities, and low ability to influence working conditions, are related to neck pain.5 Nevertheless, in many cases, the cause of neck pain remains unclear. If neck pain becomes chronic, it is associated with disability, subsequently leading to incapacity for work and reduced quality of life.1,7 One major factor related to chronic pain is psychological well-being. Studies showed that anxiety and depression are prevalent in patients with chronic neck pain8,9 and demonstrates a clear association between psychological disorders and neck pain.10 Conversely, factors like stress, depression, anxiety, and mood have been found to be important in the development of neck pain.11 In Europe, the estimated economic burden of chronic neck pain may reach up to 2% of the gross domestic product (GDP) among the working-age population.12 This substantial impact is primarily attributed to healthcare expenditures, insurance costs, productivity losses, and work absenteeism.3

The need for adequate treatment is reflected in the number of visits in general practice. For example, in Germany, neck pain is the third most common reason for consultations with general practitioners.13 However, neck pain is a common condition that requires good coordination and collaboration between physicians and other health professionals, especially physiotherapists. Conservative treatment includes treatment by the general practitioner (eg, analgesics), manual therapy (eg, manipulation, mobilisation), physical therapy, and combinations. Physical therapy is often the treatment of choice for persistent non-specific neck pain. There is a wide range of physical therapy methods, from physical applications to manual therapy and specific exercises.

However, instead of visiting a physician, most patients first try to treat neck pain themselves. Self-management (eg, relaxation, exercises, heat, cryotherapy) is often successful, and no further medical treatment is required. Heat therapy is a widely used therapeutic treatment, especially for lower back as well as neck pain; it is easy to use and with low side effects.14,15 Heat therapy reduces pain and muscle tone. Heat therapy increases tissue temperature, blood flow, metabolism, and connective tissue extensibility. Enhanced blood flow delivers oxygen, nutrients, and proteins to injured tissue, supporting repair. A 1 °C rise in tissue temperature elevates local metabolism by 10–15%, promoting both catabolic and anabolic processes necessary for clearing metabolic by-products and facilitating tissue healing. Methods of heat applications are for example hydrocollator packs, heat wraps, paraffin baths, heat lamps, or warm grain pillows.15 Since heat therapy can be applied easily at home, there is great potential to reduce pain long-term.16

Although heat therapy is widely used in clinical practice and self-management, the current evidence base for its effectiveness in non-specific neck pain remains unclear. Existing reviews have largely focused on general physical therapy interventions or on low back pain, while evidence specific to heat therapy for non-specific neck pain has not been comprehensively synthesized. Consequently, clinicians and patients lack clear evidence regarding the effectiveness of heat therapy on pain, function, and related outcomes in this population. A systematic synthesis of the available evidence is therefore needed to inform clinical practice, support evidence-based self-management recommendations, and identify gaps for future research. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to evaluate and synthesize the available evidence regarding the effectiveness of heat therapy in the management of non-specific neck pain.

Materials and Methods

Aim of this review was to synthesise the current evidence on the use of heat interventions for the self-treatment of non-specific neck pain and develop evidence-based recommendations applying the GRADE approach (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation). This systematic review followed established methods described in the Cochrane Handbook for systematic reviews of interventions17 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis.18 No study protocol was registered.

Literature Search

The search strategy was developed in accordance with systematic review methodology, guided by the PICO (Population–Intervention–Comparator–Outcome) framework, and incorporated both free-text keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms). We did a computerised search in June 2025, using the database Medline via PubMed. Given the clinically focused research question (neck pain and heat therapy), the concentration of evidence in biomedical journals, and MEDLINE’s extensive coverage, PubMed was used as the primary database. PubMed was selected as the primary database because the research question focused on a clinically oriented intervention (heat therapy) for a common musculoskeletal condition, and MEDLINE provides extensive coverage of biomedical, rehabilitation, and physiotherapy-related literature. In addition, PubMed allows comprehensive indexing through MeSH terms and includes the majority of peer-reviewed journals relevant to conservative and physical therapy interventions. No additional eligible studies were identified through the supplementary database screening. To reduce the risk of database bias, we complemented the search with backward and forward citation searches of included records ad a brief screening of additional databases. For this search, the key search concepts “neck pain” and “heat” were used. In addition, the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews identified through the search were examined to identify any additional eligible study. Additionally, we conducted experts in this field to identify grey literature. The results of the searches were imported into Zotero,19 which was used for the entire review process. Characteristics of Included Studies

Selection of Studies

We included randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies assessing any form of heat therapy for the treatment of neck pain. To get included, studies must have included participants with non-specific neck pain, irrespective of pain duration. Studies must have determined results to at least one of the following primary outcomes: pain, quality of life, neck disability, use of medication, anxiety, depression, and ability to work. We excluded studies that included participants younger than 18 years old who were suffering from specific neck pain (eg, disc prolapses, inflammatory neck pain, fractures, tumour, whiplash) and assessed outcomes other than those mentioned above. We included scientific articles published in peer-reviewed journals in English or German. To maximize the inclusion of heat interventions, no restrictions were placed on the year of publication. The screening process was conducted by three reviewers (DW, CMB, AEA). In case of disagreement, a fourth reviewer (TK) was involved.

Data Extraction and Methodological Quality

Three reviewers conducted data extraction (DW, CMB, AEA). Data from each included study were extracted by one reviewer and independently verified by a second reviewer. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a fourth reviewer (TK) consulted to achieve consensus when necessary. Data were extracted into the Cochrane data collection form, including methods, participants, intervention groups, outcomes, and sources. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Two reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies to identify potential sources of bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to achieve consensus.17

Data Synthesis

As studies were too heterogeneous, pooling in meta-analyses was not possible. Accordingly, we described and compared study results (eg, mean differences with standard deviations) at baseline and follow-up. For the primary outcomes, the certainty of the evidence was assessed based on the overall risk of bias, directness of evidence, consistency of findings, and precision of effect estimates. The results were summarised in a “Summary of Findings” table in accordance with the methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook.17 The GRADE method (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) was applied to assess the confidence in effect estimates (quality of evidence)22 as well as the recommendations.23 The quality of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low for each endpoint. This assessment was based on the identified risk of bias in the relevant studies, the inconsistency of study results, the indirectness of the studies, and the imprecision of the effect estimate.24 To determine the overall confidence in the evidence, the ratings of the main outcomes were used.

Results

Description of Included Studies

The systematic literature search identified a total of 91 studies. After title and abstract screening, the full texts of 89 articles were classified as potentially relevant. After reviewing the full texts, only two studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this review. Both studies were published in English. The main reasons for exclusion were including other participants than patients suffering from non-specific neck pain (n = 54), evaluating other interventions than heat therapy (n = 21), assessing wrong outcomes (n = 7), using wrong study designs (n = 3), or availability of full texts in another language than English or German (n = 2). Methodological Quality

Among the two included RCTs,20,21 both studies had an unclear risk of bias regarding most of the items of the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool.17 None of the studies reported information about random sequence generation or allocation concealment. None of the studies provided information about blinding, but due to the nature of intervention, it can be assumed that no blinding took place. Regarding incomplete outcome data and other sources of bias, both studies had a low risk of bias. Moreover, none of the studies published a study protocol. Interventions

In the study by Petrofsky et al (2017)20 participants underwent one-hour sessions of conventional physical therapy consisting of thermotherapy, joint mobility exercises, stretching, isometric training, and postural exercises. These sessions were administered twice weekly for a duration of two weeks in an outpatient clinical setting. In addition, participants were instructed to perform one hour of therapeutic exercise daily at home, which included stretching and isometric activities. For the low-level continuous heat (LLCH) intervention, a dry heat neck wrap (ThermaCare, Pfizer Consumer Healthcare, Richmond, VA) was applied to the posterior neck region, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and worn for six hours.

In the study of Petrofsky et al (2020)21 ThermaCare back wraps (ThermaCare, Pfizer Consumer Healthcare, Richmond, VA) were applied for four hours to two identified trigger points on the shoulder. Trigger points were localized using a fine-needle impedance checker and corresponded to two sites on the upper trapezius muscle near the superior angle of the scapula on the affected side. The heat cells measured 25×40 mm and maintained a temperature of 41 ± 0.5 °C. Details regarding the number of applications and overall treatment duration were not reported.

Measurements and Outcomes

Primary outcomes of interest were neck pain and neck disability. Both studies20,21 assessed neck pain using a VAS (100 mm),25 and one study additionally evaluated neck disability.20 On the VAS, participants indicated their pain intensity along a 100 mm line, with values closer to 100 mm representing greater pain and values near 0 indicating little or no pain. Neck disability was measured using the neck disability index (NDI), a patient-reported questionnaire consisting of 10 items addressing pain, personal care, lifting, reading, headaches, concentrations, work, driving, sleeping, and recreation. Each item is scored from 0 to 5, yielding a maximum score of 50, with higher scores reflecting greater disability.26 Additional reported outcomes included active range of motion (AROM),20 neck muscle strength,20 neck tenderness threshold,21 and pain relief.21 All assessment were performed by a licensed physical therapist. In Petrofsky et al (2017),20 outcomes were measured at the end of the two-week intervention period, whereas in Petrofsky et al (2020),21 outcomes were assessed four hours post-intervention.

Effects of Interventions

Both studies20,21 reported neck pain using VAS. Due to differences in follow-up duration, a meta-analysis could not be conducted. In the trial of Petrosky et al (2017),20 participants in the LLCH group reported a baseline mean pain score of 37.6 ±17.4. After two weeks of heat therapy, pain significantly decreased to 19.0 ± 12.5 (p < 0.05). However, the control group also demonstrated a pain reduction of 8.9 mm. In the study of Petrofsky et al (2020),21 baseline pain was 57.4 ± 19.2 mm, which decreased significantly to 24.1 ± 27.5 mm (p < 0.01) following a 4-hour heat application. In the sham group, pain was also reduced significantly (57.40 ± 19.20 to 47.48 ± 25.45, p = 0.013).

NDI was assessed in one study20 showing a significant improvement favouring the heat intervention group (heat therapy mean ± SD 19.0 ±12.5; control group mean ±SD 29.6 ±10.4). Results of neck muscle strength and AROM also improved significantly after two weeks of heat treatment (p < 0.01).20

In addition, Petrofsky et al (2020)21 evaluated the pressure required to elicit pain at trigger points needed to cause pain (N). The pressure threshold increased from 25.15 ± 10.23 N at baseline to 34.59 ± 10.42 N after heat treatment, indicating a significant reduction in pain sensitivity (p < 0.01). The time to first perception of pain relief was also markedly shorter in the intervention group compared with the sham group (54.1 ± 48.7 vs 137.7 ± 76.8 minutes; p = 0.004). No adverse events were reported.

Discussion

In this systematic literature review investigating heat therapy for non-specific neck pain, only two randomized controlled trials met the inclusion criteria. Both studies reported improvements in pain intensity, and one study additionally found improvements in neck disability, cervical range of motion, and muscle strength following heat application. However, these findings should be interpreted with considerable caution due to the very limited evidence base and the methodological limitations of the included studies.

Importantly, in the study by Petrofsky et al20 (2017), heat therapy was not evaluated as a standalone self-management intervention but rather as an adjunct to an existing physiotherapy program. Consequently, the observed effects cannot be attributed solely to heat application. Furthermore, both included studies were conducted by the same research group, which limits methodological diversity and raises the possibility of systematic bias related to shared study designs, intervention procedures, and outcome assessments. The absence of independent replication substantially restricts the reliability and generalizability of the current findings.

The methodological quality of the included studies was limited overall. Both studies demonstrated an unclear risk of bias across several domains, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, and blinding. In addition, the interventions were insufficiently described, limiting reproducibility and comparability. Sample sizes were small, follow-up periods were short, and no study protocols were available. These limitations reduce confidence in the reported effects and preclude definitive conclusions regarding the clinical effectiveness of heat therapy for non-specific neck pain.

Although the findings of this review are broadly consistent with evidence from studies on other musculoskeletal conditions, such comparisons should be interpreted carefully. A Cochrane review on superficial heat for low back pain reported only small and short-term effects on pain and disability,27 while a meta-analysis on overuse-related muscle pain suggested beneficial effects of heat therapy.28 However, the transferability of findings from low back pain or sports-related muscle pain to non-specific neck pain remains uncertain due to differences in patient populations, symptom mechanisms, and treatment contexts.

Heat therapy is widely used in clinical practice for musculoskeletal pain management. A European survey among health professionals found that heat is frequently recommended for both low back pain and neck pain, mainly because of its perceived safety, relaxation effects, and potential to improve tissue perfusion.16 Nevertheless, widespread clinical use should not be interpreted as evidence of effectiveness. Current evidence remains insufficient to establish clear recommendations regarding indications, dosage, duration, frequency, or optimal modes of application for patients with non-specific neck pain.

The positive findings observed in the included studies may also have been influenced by contextual and non-specific treatment effects. Heat is commonly associated with comfort and symptom relief, and patient expectations may therefore contribute substantially to perceived improvements. Consequently, placebo effects cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the reported outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this review highlights an important gap in the current literature. The identification of only two small randomized controlled trials demonstrates the scarcity of research specifically investigating heat therapy for non-specific neck pain. Therefore, the primary contribution of this review lies less in establishing evidence for effectiveness and more in identifying the need for further high-quality research in this area.

Future studies should include larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, standardized intervention protocols, and clinically relevant outcome measures. Independent replication by different research groups is particularly important to strengthen the reliability and external validity of the evidence base. Until such evidence becomes available, heat therapy may be considered only as a potentially supportive adjunct within a broader self-management strategy, rather than as an evidence-based standalone treatment for non-specific neck pain.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review summarising and describing heat therapy exclusively for the self-management of non-specific neck pain. As a limitation, we conducted a search only in one database (MEDLINE via PubMed) and we did not search on Google Scholar to identify articles that might not be indexed in PubMed. However, it is unlikely that relevant studies were missed, as a brief screening of additional databases did not yield further pertinent studies. Evidence indicates that searching a limited number of databases is often sufficient to comprehensively identify studies on medical interventions.29–31 The assumption that adding numerous databases necessarily improves retrieval is frequently not supported.32 MEDLINE plays a central role in this context: approximately 84–92% of RCTs included in Cochrane Reviews are indexed in PubMed.29,30,33 In addition to its high coverage, MEDLINE’s controlled vocabulary (MeSH)34 and the availability of validated search filters (eg, for RCTs35 and systematic reviews36 facilitate precise and efficient retrieval, supporting our use of MEDLINE as the primary source while acknowledging the residual risk of missed studies. Moreover, experts familiar with the current research landscape were consulted and did not identify any additional relevant publications. Therefore, it can be assumed that the included studies provide a comprehensive representation of the existing research landscape. Therefore, the risk of publication bias is considered to be rather low. Nevertheless, future updates of this review should include additional databases, such as Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, to increase the completeness and robustness of the evidence base. Only a very small number of studies (n = 2) with very small sample sizes and a very short follow-up period were included in this review. This represents a major limitation of this review, which considerably limits the available evidence base and reduces the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn. An additional limitation of this review is that both included studies were conducted by the same research group. This raises the possibility of systematic bias related to shared methodological approaches, intervention delivery, outcome assessment, and reporting practices. Furthermore, the lack of independent replication constrains the diversity of evidence and limits the reliability and generalizability of the findings. As a result, the observed positive effects of heat therapy should be interpreted with caution, as they may not necessarily be reproducible across different clinical settings, populations, or research teams. Independent high-quality randomized controlled trials are therefore essential to confirm the effectiveness of heat therapy for non-specific neck pain and to strengthen confidence in the current findings.

The findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and cannot be considered conclusive. The interventions in both studies were poorly described, which means there is a lack of reproducibility. Nevertheless, the evidence was systematically processed and adhered based on appropriate recommendations for conducting systematic reviews.

The fact that we identified only two randomized-controlled trials addressing heat therapy in patients with non-specific neck pain underscores a significant gap in the literature. High-quality research is urgently needed to determine the optimal modalities, duration, and patient subgroups most likely to benefit from heat as a self-management tool in the context of non-specific neck pain.

As only two studies were included, the current evidence remains insufficient to draw firm conclusions. Further high-quality primary studies from independent research groups are needed to provide more robust and generalizable evidence on this topic.

Conclusion

This systematic review identified only two randomized controlled trials investigating heat therapy for patients with non-specific neck pain. Despite the limited evidence base, both studies reported improvements in pain intensity, and one study additionally demonstrated positive effects on neck disability, cervical range of motion, and muscle strength. These findings suggest that heat therapy may provide short-term symptomatic relief and could serve as a supportive self-management strategy for individuals with non-specific neck pain.

However, the certainty of evidence remains low due to the small number of available studies, limited sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and methodological shortcomings, including unclear risk of bias and insufficient reporting of intervention protocols. Furthermore, one study evaluated heat therapy only as an adjunct to physiotherapy, making it difficult to isolate the specific effects of heat application alone. Consequently, definitive conclusions regarding the clinical effectiveness of heat therapy cannot currently be drawn.

Nevertheless, heat therapy appears to be a safe, inexpensive, and easily accessible intervention with no reported adverse events. As part of a broader self-management approach—including exercise, physical activity, ergonomic modifications, and patient education—heat application may help support symptom management and patient self-efficacy in individuals with non-specific neck pain.

Future research should focus on conducting high-quality randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, standardized intervention protocols, longer follow-up periods, and clinically relevant outcomes. Independent research groups are needed to strengthen the evidence base and to establish clear recommendations regarding the optimal type, dosage, frequency, and duration of heat therapy for non-specific neck pain.
