Irreparable Posterosuperior Rotator Cuff Tears Treated With InSpace Balloon With or Without Arthroscopic Subscapularis Repair: Short‐Term Clinical Outcomes in a Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Yash Sewpaul, Adam Mansour, Alexandra Makenzie, Albert D. Mousad, Casey M. Beleckas, Komal Singh, Samer S. Hasan, Jonathan C. Levy, Michael H. Amini, Robert U. Hartzler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the short‐term clinical outcomes of subacromial balloon implantation and concomitant procedures including subscapularis repair for irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included patients aged ≥40 years who underwent InSpace Balloon implantation between September 2021 and October 2024, with a minimum 12‐month follow‐up. Patients undergoing posterosuperior cuff repair were excluded. The primary outcome was the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score. Secondary outcomes included Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, Simple Shoulder Test, visual analog scale‐pain, range of motion, complications, and reoperations. Subgroup analyses compared patients with versus without subscapularis repair, those aged <65 versus ≥65, and those who underwent implantation as a revision procedure. Minimal clinically important difference and Patient Acceptable Symptom State rates were calculated using cohort‐specific thresholds.ResultsSixty patients met inclusion criteria (mean age: 68.9 ± 8.5 years; mean follow‐up: 22.3 ± 7.9 months). Statistically significant improvements were observed across all patient‐reported outcome measures (American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons: 46.5‐72.2; Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation: 37.1‐67.7; Simple Shoulder Test: 4.6‐7.4; visual analog scale‐pain: 5.2‐2.3; all P < .001). Range of motion improved minimally. Five patients (8%) underwent revision surgery (4 reverse shoulder arthroplasty, 1 bridging graft). Clinical outcomes did not differ significantly based on subscapularis repair. Patients aged ≥65 achieved higher American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores (77.7 vs 61.2, P < .001). There was no difference in outcomes when used as a primary or as a revision operation (P = .74). Minimal clinically important difference was achieved in 72%; 30% met the Patient Acceptable Symptom State threshold.ConclusionsSubacromial balloon implantation provides significant short‐term functional and pain improvements for massive, irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, regardless of subscapularis repair. Higher clinical outcome scores were observed in patients over age 65.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, retrospective therapeutic case series.

The subacromial balloon spacer (SBS) is a biodegradable device for treating the massive, irreparable rotator cuff tear. 1 Although case series and cohort studies from Europe and North America have shown promising results, two Level I randomized controlled trials showed conflicting results; thus, its efficacy remains controversial. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 A randomized controlled trial from the United Kingdom showed statistical inferiority when compared with arthroscopic debridement. 7 These findings were maintained at 2‐year follow‐up. 9 In contrast, the North American randomized trial found noninferiority when compared with partial rotator cuff repair, with over 80% of patients meeting minimal clinically important difference (MCID) and substantial clinical benefit (SCB) for the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score. 8 These divergent results highlight ongoing patient selection and procedural indications for SBS implantation.

Multiple treatment alternatives exist for the massive irreparable rotator cuff tear, including arthroscopic debridement, partial rotator cuff repair, superior capsular reconstruction, tendon transfer, and reverse shoulder arthroplasty. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 The SBS offers an intermediate approach between debridement and complex reconstruction, potentially benefiting patients who are suboptimal candidates for technically demanding procedures and rehabilitation.

The SBS procedure is often performed with concomitant procedures, but few studies examine the effects of these procedures on clinical outcomes. A recent report on a minimalistic approach that eschews adjunctive procedures has shown significant improvements in clinical outcomes after SBS implantation. 15 Biomechanically, an intact subscapularis or reparable upper subscapularis is required for adequate device function, yet no study exists on the effects of concurrent subscapularis repair on outcomes after SBS implantation. 16 The purpose of this study was to evaluate the short‐term clinical outcomes of subacromial balloon implantation and concomitant procedures including subscapularis repair for irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, with primary outcome measured by the ASES score at a minimum 12‐month follow‐up. It was hypothesized that SBS implantation would deliver clinically meaningful improvements in shoulder function and pain, with no effect of subscapularis repair on outcomes.

METHODS

This is a multicenter retrospective review of patients who underwent SBS implantation from 4 experienced shoulder and elbow fellowship‐trained surgeons (S.S.H, J.C.L, M.H.A., R.U.H.) practicing at 4 different institutions. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained for each site prior to study commencement (WCG #2024251). Three of the 4 surgeons were concurrently participating in an industry‐sponsored prospective study of the InSpace device of on‐label use (age ≥65 years, with irreparable massive posterosuperior rotator cuff tears not requiring subscapularis repair, with no, mild, or moderate arthritis). Inclusion criteria for this study included on‐ or off‐label use in patients not enrolled in the previously mentioned study. The patients were adult subjects (≥40 years of age) who had implantation between September 2021 and October 2024 of InSpace (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) with or without concomitant procedures (including subscapularis repair) for irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears as an index or revision shoulder operation with a minimum 1‐year follow‐up. Exclusion criteria were partial or complete repair or reconstruction of the posterosuperior rotator cuff (supraspinatus and infraspinatus), patients refusing enrollment or who could not be contacted, and patients already enrolled in any prospective research study.

During study planning, the 4 operating surgeons discussed that broad agreement existed on their clinical practice indications for InSpace Balloon during the study period. These included failure of nonoperative management greater than 6 months, no or minimal glenohumeral joint degeneration by radiographs, preserved active overhead elevation and external rotation, and primary presenting complaints of pain and/or functional limitation despite preserved active shoulder motion. Moreover, all patients considered for SBS implantation were poor candidates for rotator cuff repair or reconstruction because of one or more of the following: poor quality of the posterosuperior cuff muscle‐tendon units, advanced age, low functional demands, limited tolerance for complex surgical procedures, and/or prolonged immobilization and rehabilitation.

Brief Surgical Technique

Surgery was performed under general anesthesia with a peripheral nerve block in beach chair or lateral decubitus position. Diagnostic arthroscopy was performed, and the articular surfaces were confirmed to be acceptable for joint preservation surgery. The subscapularis and the long head of biceps tendons were assessed and treated as indicated by surgeon preference with debridement, repair, or tenotomy/tenodesis. Posterosuperior rotator cuff irreparability was confirmed intraoperatively by the inability to achieve adequate tendon mobilization to the footprint despite standard releases. Minimal, if any, acromioplasty was performed to preserve the coracoacromial arch, maintain SBS stability, and maximize its ability to effect humeral depression. Minimal subscapularis and bursal debridement were performed to preserve the bursal margins supporting the device and reduce the risk of migration. The SBS was sized and placed according to the manufacturer's recommendations based on intraoperative measurement with a calibrated probe. The SBS was inflated with sterile normal saline until maximum volume was reached. Gentle range of motion (ROM) was performed to confirm SBS stability at full inflation, saline was withdrawn to reach final recommended fill volume, and the SBS was sealed. Portal closure, dressing application, and sling placement were completed in standard fashion.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Patients were immobilized in a sling for a maximum of 10 days. At the first postoperative visit (7‐10 days), the sling was discontinued, and active and active‐assisted ROM exercises, along with isometric strengthening, were commenced. Overly aggressive movement or therapy was avoided during this phase to reduce the risk of SBS migration. Dynamic strengthening exercises were commenced at 8 weeks, with progression to unrestricted activity between 2 and 3 months postoperatively. Patients were counseled about the potential for a temporary increase in discomfort and reduced function around the 3‐month mark, coinciding with SBS deflation, with expectations set for symptom improvement through continued rehabilitation.

Clinical Evaluation

The primary outcome measure was self‐reported ASES score by either an electronic survey instrument for 2 of the 4 centers or final patient follow‐up (minimum of 1 year postoperatively). Secondary outcome measures were also collected in this fashion and included self‐reported or surgeon‐assessed active ROM, Simple Shoulder Test (SST) score, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE), and visual analog scale (VAS)‐pain (0‐10). Self‐reported active ROM was assessed by patient self‐measurement using example photographs, without standard goniometric technique. Patients were instructed to estimate their maximum forward elevation, external rotation at side, and internal rotation by anatomic landmark. Any postoperative complications or reoperations were assessed by a retrospective chart review.

Radiographic Analysis

Preoperatively, patients underwent radiograph and magnetic resonance imaging scans. Images were prospectively assessed by each operating surgeon for Hamada grade on preoperative Grashey view radiograph and Goutallier grading of supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and subscapularis fatty infiltration at the time of clinical care. 17 , 18 Preoperative Hamada and Goutallier did not affect patient inclusion criteria and were not standardized across sites. No routine postoperative imaging was performed or analyzed across the 4 sites.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (v4.1.2; R Core Team 2021). Mean and standard deviations were calculated for all continuous variables. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using Shapiro‐Wilk tests; all variables met assumptions for parametric testing (P > .05). The primary analysis was comparison of pre‐ and final postoperative patient‐reported outcome measure (PROM) data. Paired student's t tests were used to compare means of the patient‐reported outcomes. The proportion of patients achieving MCID for ASES score was calculated using cohort‐specific thresholds derived by the distribution method. MCID was defined as 0.5 × baseline standard deviation (10.6). Because no formal anchor question was included in the postoperative survey, previously published thresholds were used for SCB and Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS) (17.5 and 86.7, respectively). 19 Subgroup analyses for patients who underwent concomitant upper subscapularis repair, patients aged <65 years and ≥65 years, and patients for whom the SBS was a revision procedure were compared using Student's t tests. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. No formal power analysis was performed for the primary outcome.

Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures/Range of Motion

Analysis of PROMs showed significant improvements in ASES, SST, SANE, and VAS (Table 3) across patient groups. Mean ASES scores increased from 47 ± 19 to 72 ± 20 postoperatively (P < .001) (95% confidence interval [CI]: 66‐78). Similar improvements were also observed for SANE (37‐68, P < .001), SST (4.6‐7.4, P < .001), and VAS‐pain (5.2‐2.3, P < .001). For the ASES score, using threshold values of 10.6, 17.5, and 86.7 for MCID, SCB, and PASS respectively, 38 patients (72%, 95% CI: 58%‐82%) met the MCID threshold. Thirty‐four patients (64%, 95% CI: 51%‐76%) met the SCB threshold and 18 patients (30%, 95% CI: 20%‐43%) met the PASS threshold. Self‐reported postoperative ROM showed marginal improvement over preoperative ROM, as summarized in Table 3, despite significant improvements in all patient‐reported outcomes.

Subgroup analyses were also performed on patients who underwent SBS implantation above and below the age of 65 (Table 4). Forty patients above the age of 65 (74 ± 4.8) underwent the procedure and saw significant improvements in ASES scores (49‐78, P < .001). Twenty patients below the age of 65 (59 ± 4.8) also saw significant improvements in ASES scores (42‐61, P = .007). Patients over 65 had clinically and statistically significantly higher postoperative ASES scores (P < .001).

In the 24 (40%) patients for whom the SBS was a revision procedure, there was no significant difference in postoperative ASES scores (70.5 ± 25) when compared with those who underwent SBS implantation as their first procedure (72.9 ± 17.6, P = .74) (Table 4).

No cases of SBS migration or other complications were reported postoperatively. Five patients (8%) underwent revision surgery: 4 patients underwent revision to reverse shoulder arthroplasty, and 1 patient underwent subsequent bridging reconstruction using dermal allograft.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that patients undergoing subacromial balloon implantation for irreparable rotator cuff tears showed clinically and statistically significant improvements in ASES, SANE, SST, and VAS‐pain at a mean follow‐up of 22 months. Most patients met thresholds for MCID and SCB, although fewer met the PASS. Subgroup analysis showed no clinically or statistically significant difference in outcomes with or without upper subscapularis repair, whereas patients ≥65 years achieved both clinically and statistically significantly higher ASES scores than their younger counterparts. No significant difference was observed between patients who underwent SBS implantation as a primary or revision procedure.

Our findings show somewhat lower overall proportions of patients achieving MCID, SCB, and PASS after SBS implantation than the published literature. Fares et al. showed that 90% of patients reached MCID for ASES score after isolated SBS implantation. 15 The US randomized controlled trial reported MCID achievement in 83% and SCB in 82%, with over half meeting PASS thresholds at 2 years. 8 A recent systematic review also found pooled MCID rates of 83% to 87.5%, SCB rates above 80%, and lower, more variable PASS rates between 30% and 56%. 20 One explanation for this might be that the most “ideal” patients (specifically older patients) for SBS during the study period were excluded based on 3 of the 4 surgeons’ participation in an industry‐sponsored, prospective study on the device employing age <65 as a strict exclusion criterion. Additionally, both the US and UK randomized clinical trials showed that control groups (partial repair and debridement, respectively) also showed significant improvements from baseline, suggesting that observed benefits after SBS implantation may be partly attributable to concomitant arthroscopic procedures rather than exclusively to the InSpace device. 7 , 8 Without a comparative control group in this study, the relative contribution of subacromial decompression and debridement to clinical outcomes cannot be isolated. 10

Subgroup analysis revealed that concomitant subscapularis repair did not affect clinical outcomes significantly, with the numbers available. However, this comparison was underpowered to detect small clinically meaningful differences. The utility of other concomitant procedures with SBS implantation has been similarly investigated previously. Maman et al. reported that biceps tenotomy, performed in two‐thirds of patients, had no significant effect on ASES, Constant, or VAS‐pain. 21 These findings parallel our subscapularis repair subgroup, where significant intragroup gains were observed but with no intergroup differences. Fares et al. showed significant improvements in clinical outcomes after isolated SBS implantation, suggesting that clinical improvements are driven by the SBS itself. 15 These studies contrast our findings, suggesting that the balloon's clinical benefit is largely independent of adjunct procedures if biomechanically essential structures (e.g., coracoacromial arch and subscapularis) are functional or repairable. No significant difference in outcome was observed between SBS implantations performed as a primary procedure and performed as a revision, with both groups seeing significant improvements from baseline. This confirms the viability of the SBS as a revision option in patients who have failed primary rotator cuff repair, which can be safe and effective, with a low incidence of early revision.

Patients over 65 years showed significantly greater improvements in ASES scores compared with younger patients. This finding aligns with multiple series reporting favorable outcomes in older cohorts and regulatory guidance, including the US randomized trial. 4 , 8 , 22 , 23 This may reflect both the lower baseline expectations and demands of elderly patients. However, the higher proportion of older patients in this cohort may also reflect the selection bias introduced by concurrent enrollment restrictions in the prospective trial, limiting generalizability of these age‐related findings. As a minimally invasive surgical procedure, SBS implantation thus may be particularly well‐suited to these patients. Our results support that SBS implantation should not be routinely used in younger patients when other more suitable options may be available. Additional high‐quality study comparing debridement or partial repair versus SBS implantation in a younger cohort is warranted.

The current study adds to the growing body of literature on the SBS, showing that it can deliver meaningful clinical improvements in carefully selected patients: particularly in older, low‐demand individuals with preserved active ROM, an intact or reparable subscapularis, and no significant glenohumeral arthritis. In this population, the SBS can be a straightforward, low‐morbidity option with a high likelihood of improved comfort and function. The inclusion criterion of preserved active overhead elevation represents a more liberal parameter than minimum forward elevation thresholds used in some prior trials, therefore affecting comparability of outcomes across studies. 8 Recent meta‐analyses have identified patient age, baseline functional status, and Goutallier grading of the rotator cuff muscles as potential prognostic factors influencing outcomes after SBS implantation. 20 , 24

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. The retrospective design, lack of control group, and high loss to follow‐up (31%) limit this study's findings. Additionally, a substantial number of “ideal” patients were excluded from the study based on the surgeons’ involvement in another Level I study on the device, introducing potential selection bias. Subgroup analyses were underpowered to detect small clinically meaningful differences. Sex‐disaggregated outcome analysis was not performed because of insufficient sample size; further subdivision of already underpowered subgroups would risk type II error and would not yield statistically meaningful comparisons. Additionally, no multivariate analysis was performed. In 2 of the 4 centers, no standardized in‐person postoperative physical examination was performed; outcomes were assessed via electronic survey. Self‐reported ROM measurements lack the reliability and precision of in‐person goniometric assessment. Additionally, no objective strength testing was performed. Postoperative PROMs were collected at variable follow‐up intervals rather than at a standardized fixed time point. Postoperative radiographic outcomes and SBS integrity were not assessed, and no multivariate analysis was performed to correlate preoperative arthritic change and rotator cuff muscle health with postoperative failure. Finally, variability in surgical technique across centers may have increased the heterogeneity of our findings.

CONCLUSIONS

SBS implantation provides significant short‐term functional and pain improvements for massive, irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears, regardless of subscapularis repair. Higher clinical outcome scores were observed in patients over age 65.
