Dimension-Free Bounds for Generalized First-Order Methods via Gaussian Coupling
Galen Reeves

Abstract
We establish non-asymptotic bounds on the finite-sample behavior of generalized first-order iterative algorithms — including gradient-based optimization methods and approximate message passing (AMP) — with Gaussian data matrices and full-memory, non-separable nonlinearities. The central result constructs an explicit coupling between the iterates of a generalized first-order method and a conditionally Gaussian process whose covariance evolves deterministically via a finite-dimensional state evolution recursion. This coupling yields tight, dimension-free bounds under mild Lipschitz and moment-matching conditions. Our analysis departs from classical inductive AMP proofs by employing a direct comparison between the generalized first-order method and the conditionally Gaussian comparison process. This approach provides a unified derivation of AMP theory for Gaussian matrices without relying on separability or asymptotics. A complementary lower bound on the Wasserstein distance demonstrates the sharpness of our upper bounds.

Introduction

Iterative algorithms play a central role in modern high-dimensional statistics and optimization. A broad class of such algorithms — including gradient-based optimization methods and approximate message passing (AMP) — can be represented (after symmetrization) as a generalized first-order method of the form:

\displaystyle x_{t}=Af_{t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t-1})+g_{t}(x_{1},\dots,x_{t-1}).

Constructive Gaussian Coupling:

2.1
Approximate Message Passing

AMP is a powerful framework for designing and analyzing iterative algorithms in high-dimensional inference problems.
It was first proposed by Donoho, Maleki, and Montanari
[
1
]
in the context of sparse linear regression and its asymptotic behavior was proved rigorously by Montanari and Bayati
[
2
]
. Ensuing work has strengthened and generalized these results by relaxing the assumptions on the functions
[

,
5
,
28
,
29

, allowing for a broader class of matrices
[

[

[

While AMP algorithms may vary considerably across applications, many of them can be unified under a common structural framework. After symmetrization, the basic form of an AMP algorithm can be modeled as a special case of the system (
1
) given by

\displaystyle x_{t}=Af_{t}(x_{<t})-\sum_{s<t}b_{st}f_{s}(x_{<s})

(3)

where
(b_{st})_{1\leq s<t}
are scalar debiasing coefficients designed to decorrelate the iterates. The term
\sum_{s<t}b_{st}f_{s}
is referred to as the Onsager correction term
[
1
]
.

Debiasing Coefficients and State Evolution.

State evolution defines a Gaussian process whose mean and covariance are updated recursively in terms of the functions used in the AMP system. While the original formulation of state evolution was tied to row-separable functions
[
2
,
5
]
, this was extended to non-separable pseudo-Lipschitz functions by Berthier et al.
[
28
]
. The state evolution in
[
28
]
defines a zero-mean Gaussian process
(w_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with covariance of the form
\cov(w_{s},w_{t})=\Sigma_{st}\mathrm{I}_{n}
for scalars
(\Sigma_{st})_{s,t\in\mathbb{N}}
. The covariance is initialized with
\Sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\|f_{1}\|^{2}
, where
f_{1}
is a constant function that defined the initialization of the algorithm. The covariance parameters for time
t
are then defined recursively in terms of
\Sigma_{<t}=(\Sigma_{rs})_{1\leq r,s<t}
according to

\displaystyle\Sigma_{st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\langle f_{s}(w_{<s}),f_{t}(w_{<t})\rangle],

(4)

where the expectation is with respect to
w_{<t}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\Sigma_{<t}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})

The link between the AMP recursion in (
3
) and the state evolution process hinges critically on the precise specification of the debiasing coefficients. In practice, the debiasing terms are carefully designed functions of the previous states. For the purposes of theoretical analysis, it is convenient to consider deterministic coefficients that are defined in terms of the evolution process. Assuming the
f_{t}
are weakly differentiable, these coefficients are given by
[
28
]

\displaystyle b_{st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gtr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}}{\mathrm{d}w_{s}}}}\right)}}\right],

(5)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}}{\mathrm{d}w_{s}}
denotes the
n\times n
Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives of
f_{t}
evaluated at
y_{<t}
.

2.2
Generalized First-Order Methods

Generalized first-order methods
[
23
]
extend the classical AMP framework by allowing more general update rules of the form in (
1
). This formulation includes algorithms such as gradient descent, momentum methods, and memory-augmented AMP variants as special cases. Recent work
[
24

25
,
26

27
]
has used this generalization to provide rigorous formulations of dynamical mean-field theory.

Formally, a generalized first-order method of the form given in (
1
) and the original AMP formulation in (
3
) are functionally equivalent in the sense that one can be transformed to the other via a change of variables

]
. However, this mapping is implicit and it can be difficult to see how the corresponding transformation carries over to the state evolution analysis.

State Evolution.

In the context of generalized first-order methods described by (
1
), the objective of state evolution is to establish an explicit mapping from the function sequences
(f,g)
to the parameters
(m,\Sigma)
that characterize the comparison process in (
2
). This mapping was described in
[
23
]
for specific applications, and more recently in
[
34
]
for row-separable functions. However, to our knowledge, there are no general results that apply to non-separable functions without relying on implicit changes of variables that reduce the analysis to the AMP setting.

In the definition below, we propose an explicit recursive construction that maps
(f,g)
directly to the parameters
(m,\Sigma)
. This construction involves scalar coefficients
(b_{st})
, which are closely related to the debiasing coefficients appearing in the AMP framework.

Definition 1

(State Evolution)
.

The mean and covariance are initialized with
m_{1}=g_{1}
and
\Sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\lVert f_{1}\rVert^{2}
. Then, assuming that
f_{t}
and
g_{t}
are square integrable functions of
y_{<t}
, the parameters for time
t
are defined recursively according to

\displaystyle m_{t}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=g_{t}(\cdot)+\sum_{s<t}b_{st}\,f_{s}(\cdot)

(6a)

\displaystyle b_{st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r\leq s}\big[\Sigma^{+}_{\leq t}\big]_{rs}\,\mathbb{E}[\langle y_{r}-m_{r}(y_{<r}),f_{t}(y_{<t})\rangle],

(6b)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\langle f_{s}(y_{<s}),f_{t}(y_{<t})\rangle],

(6c)

where
\Sigma_{\leq t}\coloneqq(\Sigma_{rs})_{1\leq r,s\leq t}
is the leading principal submatrix of
\Sigma
and
(\cdot)^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of a matrix.

In contrast to the standard characterizations of state evolution, this formulation requires only the existence of finite second moments at each iteration and the functions are not required to be differentiable. Assuming differentiability, Stein’s Lemma (a.k.a. Gaussian integration by parts) can be applied to provide an alternative expression for the coefficients in (
6b
) that generalizes gradient-based characterization for AMP given in
[
28
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that the function sequences
(f_{t})
and
(g_{t})
are weakly differentiable and their derivatives are square integrable functions of

. Then, (
6b
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle b_{st}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gtr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}}{\mathrm{d}y_{s}}}}\right)}}\right]+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\gtr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}}{\mathrm{d}y_{\leq t}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{I}_{nt}-\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{\leq t}}{\mathrm{d}y_{\leq t}}}}\right)^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{\leq t}}{\mathrm{d}y_{s}}}}\right)}}\right],

(7)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}}{\mathrm{d}y_{\leq t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times nt}
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{\leq t}}{\mathrm{d}y_{\leq t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nt\times nt}
denote the Jacobian matrices of
f_{t}
and
m_{\leq t}
respectively.

Remark 1
.

The fact that each
m_{s}
depends only on inputs before time
s
implies that
\frac{\mathrm{d}m_{s}}{\mathrm{d}y_{r}}=0_{n\times n}
for all
r\geq s
, and thus the Jacobian matrix of

is a strictly lower triangular block matrix. This ensures that the right-hand side of (

and the coefficients
(b_{rt})_{1\leq r<s}
.

Remark 2
.

The AMP framework can be recovered from this general construction by specifying each

as a function of
(f_{s})_{s<t}
such that

. In such cases, we see that (
7

2.3
Connection with Related Work

There has been growing interest in non-asymptotic guarantees for AMP-type methods, particularly those that aim to characterize finite-sample behavior. Notable results include exponential convergence rates for empirical measures
[
30
,
31
]
, explicit distributional decompositions
[
46
,
32
,
33
]
, refined analysis in the context of the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model
[
47
,
48
]
, and recent progress on entrywise guarantees for generalized first-order methods
[
34
]
. However, most of these results rely on structural assumptions such as separable nonlinearities, which are more restrictive than those used in the asymptotic AMP theory
[
28
]
.

In contrast, this paper develops a direct coupling framework that compares the iterates of a generalized first-order method with a conditionally Gaussian process via a one-shot argument, rather than relying on the inductive techniques common in prior AMP analyses. This coupling yields tight, non-asymptotic bounds on the deviation between the two processes over a fixed number of iterations, under general full-memory and non-separable settings.

Some specific connections are as follows:

•

Rush and Venkataramanan
[
30
]
study AMP in the regression setting under separable nonlinearities, showing exponential convergence of the empirical measure to a limiting process defined via state evolution.
Their analysis allows the number of time steps to scale sub-logarithmically with the dimension according to
T=o(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
).

•

Berthier et al.
[
28
]
and Gerbelot and Berthier
[
29
]
focus on the AMP recursion in (
3
) with non-separable functions and establish convergence with respect to sequences of pseudo-Lipschitz test functions. As shown in
[
18
, Appendix C]
, their results imply and are implied by the existence of a sequence (in
n
) of couplings under which
n^{-1/2}\lVert x_{\leq t}-y_{\leq t}\rVert\to 0
in probability as
n\to\infty
. Under the same regularity conditions, the results in this paper show that the typical deviations of
\lVert x_{\leq t}-y_{\leq t}\rVert
can be bounded independently of the problem dimension.

•

Lu
[
46
]
provides a distributional decomposition of the AMP algorithm for the purposes of enabling rapid simulation of AMP sample paths without generating a full
n\times n
matrix. While our coupling does not follow this route, such decompositions may offer alternative paths to sharp non-asymptotic analysis.

•

Aloui et al.
[
19
]
and Celentano et al.
[
47
]
focus on a symmetric rank-one matrix model (i.e., the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model) and show that AMP can be viewed as an optimization technique to find the critical points of the TAP free energy. By leveraging geometric properties of the local landscape of the objective function near critical points, they provide precise theoretical guarantees in regimes where the number of iterations scales polynomially with the dimension. Our theory differs in scope in the sense that it is designed to be applied under the general setting of the asymptotic theory
[
28
]
.

•

A different approach to the non-asymptotic analysis is proposed in the recent work of Li et al.
[
32
]
and Li and Wei
[
33
]
, who consider a decomposition of the AMP process into an orthogonal basis defined by the sample path. This high level idea of this decomposition is to distinguish between dominant terms, which are approximately Gaussian, and certain residual terms, which are presumed to be negligible. Focusing on special cases of spiked matrix model with separable functions (similar to
[
47
]
), a detailed analysis of the residual terms yields rigorous guarantees for AMP algorithms where the number of iterations scales polynomially with the dimension.

•

Finally, the recent work of Han
[
34
]
focuses directly on generalized first-order methods of the form in (
1
) and establishes non-asymptotic entrywise guarantees under the assumptions of row-separable nonlinearities with bounded first, second, and third derivatives. In particular,
[
34
, Theorem 2.4]
provides an upper bound on the distributional distance between the
i
-th row of
x_{\leq t}
and the corresponding row of the comparison process
y_{\leq t}
for each
i\in[n]
. For a suitably smooth class of test functions, the rate of convergence is
O((\log n)^{c_{1}T^{5}}n^{-1/c_{0}^{T}})
for positive constants
c_{0},c_{1}
. In addition, Han proves a universality result showing that the same results hold when the matrix
A
is drawn from a symmetric ensemble with independent entries and matched first and second moments.
Compared to our results, which apply to general nonlinearities under Gaussian assumptions,
[
34
]
trades off generality for universality, addressing a broader class of matrix ensembles at the cost of stronger smoothness assumptions.

Main Results

We establish theoretical guarantees on the closeness between the distributions of the generalized first-order method in (
1
) and the comparison process in (
2
) by showing that there is coupling, i.e., a joint distribution on the two processes, under which their difference is bounded independently of the dimension with high probability. The significance of these results is that the behavior of generalized first-order methods can be analyzed precisely by studying the much simpler comparison process.

For given number of time steps
T\in[n]
, we represent the sequences generated by (
1
) and (
2
) in terms of the
n\times T
matrices

\displaystyle X=[x_{1},\dots,x_{T}],\qquad Y=[y_{1},\dots,y_{T}].

A coupling of these processes is any specification of a joint distribution on the pair
(X,Y)
whose marginal distributions satisfy (
1
) and (
2
).

We also define the matrix-valued functions:

\displaystyle F=[f_{1},\dots,f_{T}],\qquad G=[g_{1},\dots,g_{T}],\qquad M=[m_{1},\dots,m_{T}].

We use the notation
F(X)
to express the evaluation of function
F
on the matrix
X
.

The following construction defines a joint distribution on the matrix

2
). Moreover, the following identity holds for all
1\leq t\leq r\leq T
:

\displaystyle z_{t}=Aq_{t}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{s\leq r}\langle z_{s},q_{t}\rangle\,q_{s}+\sum_{s\leq r}A^{\prime}_{st}q_{s}.

(10)

.

3.2
Non-Asymptotic Upper Bounds

We now use the coupling in Definition
2
to provide an explicit bound on the coupling error. We will assume throughout this section that
\Sigma
is positive definite with (necessarily unique) Cholesky decomposition
\Sigma=\Omega^{\top}\Omega
. For the next result, we define the
T\times T
random matrices

\displaystyle B_{n}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}(Y-M(Y))^{\top}F(Y),\qquad S_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\Omega^{-\top}F(Y)^{\top}F(Y)\Omega^{-1},

which can be viewed as the sample covariance matrices defined by the rows of
F(Y)\Omega^{-1}
and
(Y-M(Y))\Sigma^{-1}
.

Additionally, we use
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{2,1}
to denote the
\ell_{2}\to\ell_{1}
operator norm, i.e., the sum of the column norms. For an
n\times k
matrix
V=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
, this norm satisfies
\lVert V\rVert_{2,1}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lVert v_{j}\rVert\leq\sqrt{k}\,\lVert V\rVert
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is positive definite with Cholesky decomposition
\Sigma=\Omega^{\top}\Omega
and the functions
f_{t}
and
g_{t}
are Lipschitz with constants
L_{f}
and
L_{g}
for all
t\in[T]
. Then, under the coupling
(X,Y)
from Definition
2
, the event

\displaystyle\lVert X-Y\rVert\leq\big(1+4L_{f}+L_{g}\big)^{T-1}\,\big(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+2\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2r}\big)\cdot\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}

holds with probability at least
1-3e^{-r}
for all
0\leq r\leq n
where

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lVert M(Y)-G(Y)-F(Y)B_{n}\rVert_{2,1}\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}^{-1}

(11a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 52\log_{2}(4T)\sqrt{n}\lVert S_{n}-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}.

(11b)

Theorem
4
shows that coupling error is controlled by the random variables
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
. The term
\Delta_{1}
depends on the mismatch between the parameters
(f,g)
and
(m,\Sigma)
, while
\Delta_{2}
quantifies the deviation between the empirical second moment matrix
\frac{1}{n}F(Y)^{\top}F(Y)
and the target covariance
\Sigma
. For the purposes of analysis, an important property of this bound is that both variables are measurable with respect to the comparison process
Y
. Thus, we can exploit structural properties of
Y
to bound their distributions.

Remark 4
.

In principle, one could attempt to optimize the choice of
(m,\Sigma)
as a function of
(f,g)
to minimize the associated error terms. We note that the term
\Delta_{1}
cannot be completely eliminated because the entries
[B_{n}]_{st}
for
s\geq t
depend on the current and future states, and therefore cannot be used in the definition
m_{t}
, which must depend solely on past information.

Remark 5
.

The term
(1+4L_{f}+L_{g})^{T-1}
arises from an upper bound on the stability of system (
1
) given in Lemma
14
. This part of the analysis can be improved at the cost of some additional complexity in the theorem statement and the assumptions. For example, the bound in Lemma
14
holds under the weaker assumption that there is an affine upper bound on the modulus of continuity of each
f_{t}
and
g_{t}
at the point
y_{\leq t}
.

To provide explicit tail bounds on
(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})
, our strategy is to show that the random matrices
(B_{n},S_{n})
concentrate around their expectations
(B,S)
. Two basic scenarios where such concentration results can be established are as follows:

1.

Independence:
If the functions
f_{t}
and
m_{t}
are row-separable — meaning the
i
-th output depends only on the
i
-th input — then the rows of
Y
and
F(Y)
are independent. Assuming finite second moments, the multivariate central limit theorem implies that
(B_{n},S_{n})
can be approximated by their expectations plus a random fluctuation term of order
1/\sqrt{n}
. Under stronger moment assumptions, sharper convergence rates can be established using matrix concentration inequalities, such as matrix Bernstein’s inequality
[
49
]
.

2.

Lipschitz Continuity:
If each function
f_{t}
and
m_{t}
is Lipschitz continuous, then the matrices
Y
and
F(Y)
can be expressed as Lipschitz continuous functions of i.i.d. standard Gaussian variables. This structure permits the application of Gaussian concentration inequalities to rigorously control the deviations and establish convergence rates.

For our next result, we use the second approach to provide a refined upper bound the coupling error that does not require any separability conditions on the functions. This result is stated with respect to the deterministic
T\times T
matrices

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(Y-M(Y))^{\top}F(Y)],\qquad S

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\Omega^{-\top}\mathbb{E}[F(Y)^{\top}F(Y)]\Omega^{-1}.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is positive definite with Cholesky decomposition
\Sigma=\Omega^{\top}\Omega
and the functions
f_{t}
,
g_{t}
,
m_{t}
are Lipschitz with constants
L_{f},L_{g},L_{m}
for all
t\in[T]
.
Then, under the coupling
(X,Y)
from Definition
2
, the event

\displaystyle\lVert X-Y\rVert

\displaystyle\leq c\cdot(1+4L_{f}+L_{g})^{T-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\big(\psi_{1}+L\,\psi_{2}+L^{3}(\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{r})\big)\,\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}

holds with probability at least
1-5e^{-r}
for all
0\leq r\leq n
where
c
is a universal positive constant,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\coloneqq\log_{2}(2T)+\sqrt{T}(L_{f}+L_{g}+L_{m})(1+L_{m})^{T-1}\kappa(\Sigma)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert M(Y)-G(Y)-F(Y)B\rVert_{2,1}\big]\cdot\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{n}\cdot\lVert S-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

and
\kappa(\Sigma)
is the condition number of
\Sigma
.

We emphasize that Theorem
5
holds generally for any choice of
(f,g,m,\Sigma)
satisfying the Lipschitz continuity assumption and the positive definiteness assumption. If the parameters of the comparison process are matched to the original sequence following the state evolution construction in Definition
1
, then Theorem
5
holds with
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}=0
and Theorem
1
follows immediately as a corollary.

3.2.1
Dependence on Number of Steps.

Under the assumed scaling on the functions,
\lVert x_{t}\rVert
and
\lVert y_{t}\rVert
are typically of order
\sqrt{n}
. For fixed
T
and bounded
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
our result shows that the fluctuations of the coupling error
\lVert X-Y\rVert
are order one, independent of the dimension.

Our findings can also be applied in regimes where
T
increases with
n
. The bound on the coupling error remains meaningful as long as
n^{-1/2}\lVert X-Y\rVert
vanishes asymptotically. Assuming the Lipschitz constants are bounded uniformly with respect to
(T,n)
the upper bound on the typical deviations increases at rate
\exp\{c_{L}\,T\}\kappa(\Sigma)^{3}
where
c_{L}
depends on the Lipschitz constants. A sufficient condition for
n^{-1/2}\lVert X-Y\rVert
to converge to zero in probability is then given by

\displaystyle T+\ln(\kappa(\Sigma))=O(\log n).

(12)

It is important to recognize the role of the condition number in this setting. For many applications of interest, the state evolution converges to a fixed point, and the rate of convergence is exponential in
T
. On the one hand, the rapid convergence means that a fixed number of iterations is often sufficient to get arbitrarily close to the fixed point. On the other hand, closeness to a fixed-point necessarily implies that
\Sigma
is near degenerate. To see this observe that from (
2
), we have
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert y_{t+1}-y_{t}\rVert^{2}]\geq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert w_{t+1}-\mathbb{E}[w_{t+1}\mid w_{\leq t}]\rVert^{2}]
. If this lower bound converges to zero then the smallest eigenvalue of
\Sigma
also converges to zero, causing the condition number to grow without bound.

To interpret our result in the context of the basic AMP recursion, we evaluate with
g_{t}=-\sum_{s<t}b_{st}f_{s}
and
m_{t}\equiv 0
where
b_{st}
are specified according to (
6b
). Assuming each
f_{t}
has Lipschitz constant
L_{f}
, we can apply the general upper bound

\displaystyle\Lip(g_{t})\leq(\sum_{s<t}|b_{st}|^{2})^{1/2}L_{f}\leq\sqrt{T}L_{f}^{2}.

(13)

Evaluating Theorem
5
with these constants, the relevant scaling becomes

\displaystyle T\ln(T)+\ln(\kappa(\Sigma))=O(\log n).

Ignoring the dependence on the condition number, this result is consistent with the scaling condition
T=o(\log n/(\log\log n))
, which was previously established for special cases of AMP
[
30
]
. The extra
\ln(T)
dependence on
T
can be eliminated under stronger assumptions on the functions. For example, if each function has fixed memory that does not increase with
T
(as is often assumed in the AMP literature) then (
13
) implies that
L_{g}=O(L_{f}^{2})
, recovering the condition in (
12
).

3.2.2
Extension to Pseudo-Lipschitz Functions.

Prior work on AMP
[
28
]
has considered functions satisfying a pseudo-Lipschitz condition of the form

\displaystyle\lVert f_{t}(u)-f_{t}(v)\rVert\leq L\|u-v\|\Big(1+\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|u\|\big)^{\alpha-1}+\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|v\|\big)^{\alpha-1}\Big)\qquad\text{for all $u,v\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{t-1}$,}

for positive constant
L
and exponent
\alpha\in[1,\infty)
. The case
\alpha=1
corresponds to the standard Lipschitz condition, while larger values
\alpha>1
allow for a broader class of functions.

Theorem 5 extends naturally to this broader class of pseudo-Lipschitz functions. The key observation is that for any
\rho>0
, the restrictions of a pseudo-Lipschitz function
f
(or
g
) to the set
\mathcal{B}_{n}(\rho)=\{u\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{T}\mid\|u\|\leq\sqrt{n}\rho\}
is Lipschitz continuous, with a Lipschitz constant depending only on
(L,\rho)
. By Kirszbraun’s extension theorem, such functions admit Lipschitz continuous extensions to all of
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{T}
with the same Lipschitz constant.

We can therefore apply the coupling construction from Definition
2
to these Lipschitz extensions, obtaining a coupled pair
(X,Y)
whose error is bounded according to Theorem
5
. Thanks to the structural properties of
Y
and the fact that
\lVert X-Y\rVert
is bounded with high probability, it follows that for sufficiently large
\rho
, the events
\gvec(X)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}(\rho)
and
\gvec(Y)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}(\rho)
both hold with probability at least
1-c_{0}e^{-c_{1}n}
, for some constants
c_{0},c_{1}
. Since the Lipschitz extensions agree with the original pseudo-Lipschitz functions on
\mathcal{B}_{n}(\rho)
, the coupling established for the extensions is valid for the original system as well.

3.3
Lower Bound via Wasserstein Distance

In this section, we establish the tightness of the upper bounds by proving a complementary lower bound on the quadratic Wasserstein distance. This result demonstrates that, in general, the coupling error cannot be improved without imposing additional structural constraints.

The quadratic Wasserstein distance between square integrable probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|u-v\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(u,v)}}\right)^{1/2},

where
\Pi(\nu,\mu)
denotes the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
. Moreover, there exists an optimal coupling that attains this infimum
[
50
, Theorem 4.1]
.

For the random elements such as
X
and
Y
we use
\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\mathcal{L}(Y)
to denote their distributions. The quadratic Wasserstein distance satisfies the sandwich relation

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\mathcal{L}(x_{t}),\mathcal{L}(y_{t}))\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathcal{L}(Y))\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathcal{L}(x_{t}),\mathcal{L}(y_{t}))

(14)

The lower bound follows from relaxing the constraints on the coupling so that it only needs to match the column marginals. The upper bound follows from the triangle inequality.

To obtain an exact expression for the quadratic Wasserstein distance, we consider the simplified setting in which each
f_{t}
is a constant function (i.e., a fixed point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that does not depend on its input) and the functions
g_{t}
and
m_{t}
are linear.

Condition 1
.

For each
t\in T
,
f_{t}
is a constant function (i.e., it does not depend on its input) and
g_{t}
and
m_{t}
are linear functions of the form

\displaystyle g_{t}(x_{<t})=\sum_{s<t}\lambda_{st}\,x_{t},\qquad m_{t}(y_{<t})=\sum_{s<t}\gamma_{st}\,y_{t}

for strictly upper triangular matrices
\Lambda=(\lambda_{st})
and
\Gamma=(\gamma_{st})
.

Under the recursive construction of parameters in Definition
1
, the coefficients
b_{st}
are zero because the
f_{t}
are nonrandom, and the matrices
(\Gamma,\Sigma)
are given by
\Gamma=\Lambda
and
\Sigma=\tfrac{1}{n}F^{\top}F
for all
s,t\in[T]
. Note that Condition
1
implies that
f_{t},g_{t},m_{t}
are Lipschitz continuous with constants

\displaystyle L_{f}=0,\qquad L_{g}=\max_{t\in[T]}\Big(\sum_{s<t}\lambda_{st}^{2}\Big)^{1/2},\qquad L_{m}=\max_{t\in[T]}\Big(\sum_{s<t}\gamma_{st}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Theorem 6
.

Assume that Condition
1
holds. Then, the quadratic Wasserstein distance satisfies

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}(x_{t}),\mathcal{L}(y_{t})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{n-1}{n}(\sqrt{2}-1)^{2}\alpha^{2}_{t}+n\,\Big(\big(1+\tfrac{\sqrt{2}-1}{n}\big)\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}\Big)^{2},

for all
t\in[T]
where

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{[(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}\tfrac{1}{n}F^{\top}F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}]_{tt}},\qquad\beta_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{[(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma)^{-\top}\Sigma(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma)^{-1}]_{tt}}.

From Theorem
6
, we see that the optimal specification of
(\Gamma,\Sigma)
under the quadratic cost is given by
\Gamma=\Lambda
and
\Sigma=\frac{1}{n}(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{n})^{2}F^{\top}F
, which leads to

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}(x_{t}),\mathcal{L}(y_{t})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{n-1}{n}(\sqrt{2}-1)^{2}\alpha^{2}_{t}\quad\text{for all $t\in[T]$.}

For the matched setting
\Gamma=\Lambda
and
\Sigma=\frac{1}{n}F^{\top}F
, the same result holds with the factor
(n-1)/n
replaced by one.

Combining Theorem
6
with (
14
) gives a general lower bound on the second moments of the error for any coupling of
X
and
Y
.

Corollary 7
.

Under Condition
1
, any coupling
(X,Y)
satisfies the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lVert X-Y\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n-1}{n}(\sqrt{2}-1)^{2}\gtr\big((\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}\tfrac{1}{n}F^{\top}F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}\big).

These results show that the quadratic Wasserstein distance is bounded away from zero for all
n
, and thus it is not possible for the coupling error to have vanishing second moments. The following example demonstrates that the optimal coupling error can scale exponentially with
T
even for well-behaved Lipschitz continuous functions.

Example 1

(Autoregressive Process)
.

Suppose that
f_{t}
are constant functions and
g_{t}(x_{<t})=\lambda\,x_{t-1}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
\Lambda
and
(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}
are upper triangular matrices given by

\displaystyle\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda&0&\cdots&0\\
&0&\lambda&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
&&&0&\lambda\\
&&&&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda&\lambda^{2}&\cdots&\lambda^{T-1}\\
&1&\lambda&\cdots&\lambda^{T-2}\\
&&1&\cdots&\lambda^{T-3}\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&1\end{bmatrix}

Assuming
\Sigma=\mathrm{I}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\lambda^{2s}=\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda^{2t}-1}{\lambda^{2}-1},&\lambda\neq 1\\
t&|\lambda|=1\end{cases}

This sequence grows exponentially with
t
for all
|\lambda|>1
.

4
Proofs of Main Results

4.1
The Gaussian Conditioning Approach

The Gaussian conditioning approach was introduced by Bayati and Montanari
[
2
, Lemma 10]
to show that the AMP recursion in (
3
) can be expressed as a conditionally Gaussian sequence — that is the increments are Gaussian with a mean and variance that may depend on the past states. The key idea is to analyze the conditional distribution of the random matrix
A
given the history
x_{<t}
, and then use this to determine the conditional distribution of the next iterate
x_{t}
. A detailed derivation of this argument is presented in Feng et al.
[
40
, Appendix A.2]
. Related decomposition techniques have also appeared previously in the context of discrete-time filtering
[
51
]

In this section, we present a self-contained derivation of the Gaussian conditioning approach in a general setting, which includes the generalized-first order method in (
1
) as a special case. We begin by recalling a basic but important fact: the conditional distributions of jointly Gaussian random variables are Gaussian, even in the presence of a degenerate (i.e., singular) joint covariance matrix.

Lemma 8

(
[
51
, Theorem 13.2]
)
.

Let
(\theta,\xi)
be jointly Gaussian random finite-dimensional vectors with means
m_{x}=\mathbb{E}[\theta]
and
m_{\xi}=\mathbb{E}[\xi]
and covariance matrices
C_{\theta\theta}=\cov(\theta,\theta)
,
C_{\theta\xi}=\cov(\theta,\xi)
, and
C_{\xi\xi}=\cov(\xi,\xi)
. Then the conditional distribution of
\theta
given
\xi
is Gaussian with mean and covariance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\xi]=m_{\theta}+C_{\theta\xi}C_{\xi\xi}^{+}(\xi-m_{\xi}),\qquad\cov(\theta\mid\xi)=C_{\theta\theta}-C_{\theta\xi}C_{\xi\xi}^{+}C_{\xi\theta}

where
(\cdot)^{+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

To proceed, we consider a generalization of (
1
) that allows for general linear operators applied to the matrix. Specifically, we consider the system

\displaystyle\xi_{t}=F_{t}\,\theta+g_{t}

(15)

where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\theta\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{N})
is a standard Gaussian random vector, and
F_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
and
g_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are measurable functions of the states
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{t-1})
. We define
\xi_{\leq t}
,
F_{\leq t}
and
g_{\leq t}
to be the vertical stackings of the first
t
terms, i.e.,

\displaystyle\xi_{\leq t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\xi_{t}\end{bmatrix},\qquad F_{\leq t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}\\
\vdots\\
F_{t}\end{bmatrix},\quad\qquad g_{\leq t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}\\
\vdots\\
g_{t}\end{bmatrix},

Lemma 9

(Gaussian Conditioning)
.

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the conditional distribution of
(\theta,\xi_{t})
given
\xi_{<t}
is Gaussian with condition mean

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\xi_{<t}]

\displaystyle=F_{<t}^{+}(\xi_{<t}-g_{<t})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi_{t}\mid\xi_{<t}]

\displaystyle=F_{t}F_{<t}^{+}(\xi_{<t}-g_{<t})+g_{t}

and conditional covariance

\displaystyle\cov(\theta,\theta\mid\xi_{<t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t}

\displaystyle\cov(\theta,\xi_{t}\mid\xi_{<t})

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t})F_{t}^{\top}

\displaystyle\cov(\xi_{t},\xi_{t}\mid\xi_{<t})

\displaystyle=F_{t}(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t})F_{t}^{\top}.

Proof.

For this proof we will verify that the conditional distribution of
\theta
given
\xi_{<t}
is Gaussian with the stated mean and covariance. The result for the joint conditional distribution of
(\theta,\xi_{t})
given
\xi_{<t}
then follows directly from (
15
) and the fact that
(F_{t},g_{t})
are measurable functions of
\xi_{<t}
.

We proceed by induction on
t
. The base case (
t=1
) follows immediately from noting that
\xi_{1}=F_{1}\theta+g_{1}
where
F_{1}
and
g_{1}
are deterministic.

For the inductive step, assume that for time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the conditional distribution of
\theta
given
\xi_{<t}
is Gaussian with mean
F_{<t}^{+}(\xi_{<t}-g_{<t})
and covariance
(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t})
. This implies that
(\theta,\xi_{<t})
are jointly Gaussian, and thus there exists a decomposition of the form

\displaystyle\theta=F_{<t}^{+}(\xi_{<t}-g_{<t})+(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t})\theta^{\prime}

(16)

where
\theta^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{N})
is independent of
\xi_{<t}
. Introducing the notation

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{t}\coloneqq F_{t}(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t}),\qquad\bar{g}_{t}\coloneqq F_{t}F_{<t}^{+}(\xi_{<t}-g_{<t})+g_{t}

the next term in the sequence can be expressed as

\displaystyle\xi_{t}

\displaystyle=F_{t}\,\theta+g_{t}=\bar{F}_{t}\theta^{\prime}+\bar{g}_{t}.

At this point, the crucial observation is that both
\bar{F}_{t}
and
\bar{g}_{t}
are measurable with respect to
\xi_{<t}
. Thus, we may condition on
\xi_{<t}
and apply Lemma
8
to the pair
(\theta^{\prime},\xi_{t})
to see that

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}\mid\xi_{\leq t}\sim\mathsf{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{F}_{t}^{+}(\xi_{t}-\bar{g}_{t}),\mathrm{I}-\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\bar{F}_{t}}}\right),

where we have used the property
M(M^{\top}M)^{+}=M^{+}
for any matrix
M
. Substituting back into (
16
), it follows that
\theta
given
\xi_{\leq t}
is conditionally Gaussian with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid x_{\leq t}]

\displaystyle=F_{<t}^{+}(\xi_{<t}-g_{<t})+(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t})\bar{F}_{t}^{+}(\xi_{t}-\bar{g}_{t})

(17a)

\displaystyle\cov(\theta\mid\xi_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t})(\mathrm{I}-\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\bar{F}_{t})(\mathrm{I}-F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t}).

(17b)

To complete the proof of the inductive step, it remains to verify that these expressions for the mean and covariance match the ones in the statement of the result. By construction, the row spaces of
F_{<t}
and
\bar{F}_{t}
are orthogonal and together span the row space of
F_{\leq t}
. Hence, the orthogonal projection matrices onto these subspaces satisfy

\displaystyle F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t}+\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\bar{F}_{t}=\bar{F}_{\leq t}^{+}\bar{F}_{\leq t}.

(18)

Using this relation, the conditional variance in (
17b
) simplifies to
\cov(\theta\mid\xi_{\leq t})=\mathrm{I}-F_{\leq t}^{+}F_{\leq t}
, as desired.

The orthogonality of the row spaces of
F_{<t}
and
\bar{F}_{t}
also implies
(\mathrm{I}-F^{+}_{<t}F_{<t})\bar{F}_{t}^{+}=0
and
\bar{F}_{t}^{+}F_{t}F_{<t}^{+}=0
, which can be used to simplify the expression for the mean in (
17a
). In particular, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid\xi_{\leq t}]=\begin{bmatrix}F_{<t}^{+}&\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\end{bmatrix}(\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t}).

Since
\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t}
lies the column space of
F_{\leq t}
, we can write

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}F_{<t}^{+}&\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\end{bmatrix}(\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}F_{<t}^{+}&\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\end{bmatrix}F_{\leq t}F_{\leq t}^{+}(\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=(F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t}+\bar{F}^{+}_{t}F_{t})F_{\leq t}^{+}(\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=(F_{<t}^{+}F_{<t}+\bar{F}_{t}^{+}\bar{F}_{t})F_{\leq t}^{+}(\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t})

\displaystyle=F_{\leq t}^{+}(\xi_{\leq t}-g_{\leq t}),

where the third step follows from the decomposition

, and the final step follows from (

∎

4.2
Proof of Coupling Construction (Theorem

)

In this proof, we first show that construction in Definition

Define the normalized variables

\gvec(A^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}

(19)

4.3
Proof of General Upper Bound (Theorem
4
)

In this proof, we bound the coupling error
\|X-Y\|
under the construction given in Definition
2
. The proof proceeds in four main steps.

Stability Bound.

Let
h_{t}=Af_{t}+g_{t}
denote the full update function for the generalized first-order method. By the assumption that
f_{t}
and
g_{t}
are Lipschitz with constants
L_{f}
and
L_{g}
, we have

\displaystyle\Lip(h_{t})\leq L_{h}\coloneqq\lVert A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}L_{f}+L_{g}.

Applying the discrete-time stability bound in Lemma
14
yields

\displaystyle\lVert X-Y\rVert\leq(1+L_{h})^{T-1}\lVert Y-AF(Y)-G(Y)\rVert_{2,1}.

(20)

Thus, the problem has been reduced to bounding the random variables
\lVert A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\lVert Y-AF(Y)-G(Y)\rVert_{2,1}
.

Decomposition of Coupling Error.

Let
Q=[q_{1},\dots,q_{T}]
and
Z=[z_{1},\dots,z_{T}]
be the
n\times T
matrices generated by
(
8
) and (
9a
), respectively. Recall that the columns of
Q
are obtained by Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to the columns
F
with normalization
\lVert q_{t}\rVert=\sqrt{n}
. Thus, we have the QR decomposition
F(Y)=QR
where
R
is a
T\times T
upper triangular matrix with non-negative diagonal entries defined by
R\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}Q^{\top}F

Using the identity derived from (
10
) with
r=T
, we have

\displaystyle Z=AQ-\tfrac{1}{n}QZ^{\top}Q+QA^{\prime\prime},

where
A^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
is leading principal submatrix of
A^{\prime}
. Multiplying both sides on the right by
R
gives

\displaystyle ZR=AF(Y)-\tfrac{1}{n}QZ^{\top}QR+QA^{\prime\prime}R

(21)

Comparing with
Y=M(Y)+Z\Omega
, we obtain

\displaystyle Y-AF(Y)-G(Y)

\displaystyle=M(Y)-G(Y)-\tfrac{1}{n}QZ^{\top}QR+Z(\Omega-R)+QA^{\prime\prime}R.

We now define the error terms:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\lVert M(Y)-G(Y)-F(Y)B_{n}\rVert_{2,1}\cdot\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}^{-1},\qquad\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}=\lVert R\Omega^{-1}-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

where we recall that
B_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\Omega^{-1}Z^{\top}QR
.

Using the triangle inequality, the bound
\lVert AB\rVert_{2,1}\leq\lVert A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\lVert B\rVert_{2,1}
for conformable matrices
A
and
B
, and the fact that
\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\sqrt{n}
, we can write

\displaystyle\lVert Y-AF(Y)-G(Y)\rVert_{2,1}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert M-G-FB_{n}\rVert_{2,1}+\tfrac{1}{n}\lVert Q(R\Omega^{-1}-\mathrm{I})Z^{\top}QR\rVert_{2,1}

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert Z(\Omega-R)\rVert_{2,1}+\lVert QA^{\prime\prime}R\rVert_{2,1}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1}\,\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}\lVert Z\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\,\lVert R\rVert_{2,1}+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}\lVert Z\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\,\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}+\sqrt{n}\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\lVert R\rVert_{2,1}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\Delta_{1}+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}(2+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2})\lVert Z\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\sqrt{n}(1+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2})\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\Big)\cdot\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}.

(22)

High Probability Bounds.

We employ standard bounds on the operator norms of the Gaussian random matrices. The expectations satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\lVert Z\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}]\leq\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{T}
by
[
53
, Theorem II.13]
and
\mathbb{E}[\lVert A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}]\leq 2
and
\mathbb{E}[\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}]\leq 2\sqrt{T/n}
by
[
53
, Theorem II.11]
.
By Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions (Lemma
10
), it follows that, for all
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sqrt{n}\,\lVert A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq 2\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{2r}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-r}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sqrt{n}\,\lVert A^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq 2\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2r}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-r}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lVert Z\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2r}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-r}.

Combining these bounds with (
20
) and (
22
), we find that the event

\displaystyle\lVert X-Y\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+(2+\sqrt{2r/n})L_{f}+L_{g}}}\right)^{T-1}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\Delta_{1}+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}(2+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2})(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2r})+(1+\widetilde{\Delta}_{2})(2\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2r})\Big)\,\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}

holds with probability at least
1-3e^{-r}
. Restricting this inequality to the setting
r\leq n
and using that
T\leq n
gives the simplified bound

\displaystyle\lVert X-Y\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+(2+\sqrt{2})L_{f}+L_{g}}}\right)^{T-1}\Big(\Delta_{1}+(2+\sqrt{2})(3\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}+\widetilde{\Delta}^{2}_{2})\sqrt{n}+2\sqrt{T}+\sqrt{2r}\Big)\,\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}.

Perturbation Bounds for Cholesky Decomposition.

The term
\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}
measures the difference between the Cholesky factor
R
of the empirical covariance matrix
\frac{1}{n}F(Y)^{\top}F(Y)
and the target matrix
\Omega
. By construction, both of these matrices are upper triangular and have non-negative diagonal entries. Since the set of upper triangular matrices with non-negative diagonal entries is closed under inversion and multiplication the product
R\Omega^{-1}
also has these properties. Thus, we can apply Lemma
12
along with
S_{n}=\Omega^{-\top}R^{\top}R\Omega^{-1}
to see that

\displaystyle 3\widetilde{\Delta}_{2}+\widetilde{\Delta}^{2}_{2}\leq 15\log_{2}(4T)\lVert S_{n}-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}.

Using that
(2+\sqrt{2})15<52
, the stated bound holds with
\Delta_{2}=52\log_{2}(4T)\sqrt{n}\lVert S_{n}-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
.

4.4
Proof of Refined Upper Bound (Theorem

In this proof, we use the Lipschitz continuity assumptions to simplify the general bound on the coupling error in Theorem

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lVert M(Y)-G(Y)-F(Y)B_{n}\rVert_{2,1}\cdot\lVert\Omega\rVert_{2,1}^{-1}

\displaystyle y_{\leq t}=\phi_{\leq t}(w_{\leq t})\qquad\iff\qquad y_{\leq t}-m_{\leq t}(y_{<t})=w_{\leq t}

Under the assumption

(23)

We also recall that the

and

.

Proof of (

(28)

\displaystyle\gvec(Y)

\displaystyle=((\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}\Omega^{\top}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})\gvec(Z)

\displaystyle\cov(\gvec(X))

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}((\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}F^{\top}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})\Pi_{n}(F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})

\displaystyle\cov(\gvec(Y))

\displaystyle=((\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}\Sigma(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})

At this point, we appeal to the closed-form expression for the quadratic Wasserstein distance between multivariate Gaussians
[
54
]
, which is given by

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{N}(\mu_{X},C_{X}),\mathsf{N}(\mu_{Y},C_{Y})}}\right)=\lVert\mu_{X}-\mu_{Y}\rVert^{2}+\gtr\Big(C_{X}+C_{Y}-2(C_{Y}^{1/2}C_{X}C_{Y}^{1/2})^{1/2}\Big)

(29)

where
(\cdot)^{1/2}
denotes the symmetric positive semidefinite square root.

To simplify the analysis, we focus on the individual columns of
X
and
Y

we can write

\displaystyle\cov(x_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}(e^{\top}_{t}(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}F^{\top}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})\Pi_{n}(F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}e_{t}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{n})

\displaystyle=[(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}\tfrac{1}{n}F^{\top}F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}]_{tt}\cdot\mathrm{I}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{n}F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}e_{t}e_{t}^{\top}(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}F^{\top}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}_{t}(\mathrm{I}_{n}+u_{t}u_{t}^{\top})

\displaystyle\cov(y_{t})

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}_{t}\mathrm{I}_{n}

where
u_{t}
is a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\alpha^{2}_{t}=[(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-\top}\tfrac{1}{n}F^{\top}F(\mathrm{I}-\Lambda)^{-1}]_{tt}
, and
\beta^{2}_{t}=[(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma)^{-\top}\Sigma(\mathrm{I}-\Gamma)^{-1}]_{tt}
. Using (
29
) and
(\mathrm{I}+u_{t}u_{t}^{\top})^{1/2}=\mathrm{I}+(\sqrt{2}-1)u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}
, it follows that

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}(x_{t}),\mathcal{L}(y_{t})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gtr\Big(\alpha_{t}^{2}(\mathrm{I}+u_{t}^{\top}u_{t})+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n}-2\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}(\mathrm{I}+(\sqrt{2}-1)u_{t}u_{t}^{\top})\Big)

\displaystyle=(n+1)\alpha_{t}^{2}+n\,\beta_{t}^{2}-2(n+\sqrt{2}-1)\,\alpha_{t}\beta_{t}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{n-1}{n}(\sqrt{2}-1)^{2}\alpha^{2}_{t}+n\,\Big((1+\tfrac{\sqrt{2}-1}{n})\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}\Big)^{2}.

This concludes the proof of Theorem

. ∎

5.1
Gaussian Concentration

We first recall some basic definitions and properties of sub-Gaussian distributions; see e.g.,
[
55
, Section 2.2]
for more details. A real-valued random variable
V
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
if its cumulant generating function satisfies

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(V-\mathbb{E}[V])}]\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2},\quad\text{for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$.}

Among other things, this condition implies that
\var(V)\leq\sigma^{2}
and
\mathbb{P}[V\geq\mathbb{E}[V]+\sigma\sqrt{2r}]\leq e^{-r}
for all
r\geq 0
.

The concentration of measure phenomenon for Gaussian measure provides a tight control on the behavior of functions of Gaussian functionals under various smoothness conditions. In particular, the Tsirelson–Ibragimov–Sudakov Inequality
[
55
, Theorem 5.5]
states that an
L
-Lipschitz function of independent standard Gaussian variables is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
L^{2}
. This leads to the following concentration inequality:

Lemma 10

(Gaussian Concentration Inequality
[
55
, Theorem 5.6]
)
.

Let
z\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{N})
be an
N
-dimensional vector with independent standard Gaussian entries and let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
an
L
-Lipschitz continuous function. Then, for all
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(z)\geq\mathbb{E}[f(z)]+L\sqrt{2r}}}\right]\leq e^{-r}.

The next result provides a concentration bound for a random matrix
H
with the property that any linear combination of the columns is sub-Gaussian. Note that this condition is satisfied automatically if there exits an
L
-Lipschitz function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that
H=f(z)
for
z\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{N})
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a random matrix with the property that
\lVert Hu\rVert
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
L^{2}
for every unit vector
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then, for all
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert H^{\top}H-\mathbb{E}[H^{\top}H]}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq 4\lVert\mathbb{E}[H^{\top}H]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}\,L\sqrt{2r}+2L^{2}(2r+1)\Big]\leq 2\cdot 9^{d}e^{-r}.

Proof.

By a standard
1/4
-net covering argument (see Exercise 4.4.5 (b) and Corollary 4.2.13 in
[
56
]
), the operator norm of a
d\times d
symmetric matrix
A
can be bounded from above according to

\displaystyle\lVert A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|\langle u,Au\rangle|,

where
\mathcal{U}
is a subset of unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with cardinality at most
9^{d}
.

We will apply this inequality to the matrix
A=H^{\top}H-\mathbb{E}[H^{\top}H]
. For each
u\in\mathcal{U}
, we can write

\displaystyle|\langle u,Au\rangle|

\displaystyle=\big|\|Hu\|^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|Hu\|^{2}]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\|Hu\|^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\|Hu\|]^{2}\big|+\var(\lVert Hu\rVert)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\|Hu\|]\big|\|Hu\|-\mathbb{E}[\|Hu\|]\big|+\big|\|Hu\|-\mathbb{E}[\|Hu\|]\big|^{2}+\var(\lVert Hu\rVert)

By Jensen’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}[\lVert Hu\rVert]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\lVert Hu\rVert^{2}]=u^{\top}\mathbb{E}[H^{\top}H]u\leq\lVert\mathbb{E}[H^{\top}H]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
. Furthermore, by the assumptions that
\lVert Hu\rVert
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
L^{2}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\var(\lVert Hu\rVert)\leq L^{2},\qquad\mathbb{P}[\big|\lVert Hu\rVert-\mathbb{E}[\lVert Hu\rVert]\big|\geq L\sqrt{2r}]\leq 2\,e^{-r}

for all
r\geq 0
.
the random variable
\lVert Hu\rVert
. Combining these displays with the union bound gives the stated result.
∎

5.2
Matrix Perturbation Bounds

The Cholesky factorization of a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
S
is the matrix decomposition
S=U^{\top}U
where
U
and an upper triangular matrix with non-negative diagonal entries. If
S
is positive definite then the decomposition is unique and the diagonal entries are strictly positive. The next result is a variation on the perturbation bound given by Edelman and Mascarenhas
[
57
]
.

Lemma 12

(
[
57
]
)
.

Let
U
be a
T\times T
upper triangular matrix (
T\geq 2
) with non-negative diagonal entries. The following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\lVert U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\log_{2}(4T)\,\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\lVert U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 9\,\log_{2}(4T)\,\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

Proof.

The first inequality follows directly from
[
57
, Equation (1.2)]
so all that remains is to prove the second inequality. If
T=1
and then the desired result follows from the basic inequality
|x-1|^{2}\leq|x^{2}-1|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For
T\geq 2
,
we consider two cases. First, if
2\log_{2}(4T)\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
then
\lVert U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
and so
\lVert U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\leq\lVert U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 9\,\log_{2}(4T)\,\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
. Alternatively, if
2\log_{2}(4T)\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}>1
then we can use the bound

\displaystyle\lVert U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\lVert U\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=1+\sqrt{\lVert U^{\top}U\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}

\displaystyle<(1+\sqrt{7/6})\sqrt{2\log_{2}(4T)\lVert U^{\top}U-\mathrm{I}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}.

Squaring both sides and noting that
(1+\sqrt{7/6})^{2}2<9
gives the desired result.
∎

5.3
Stability of Discrete-Time Systems

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Hilbert space and consider the system

\displaystyle v_{t}=h_{t}(v_{<t})+u_{t},

(30)

where
v_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
is the state at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
h_{t}\colon\mathcal{X}^{t-1}\to\mathcal{X}
is measurable function of previous states, and
u_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
is a control term. Let
\phi_{t}\colon\mathcal{X}^{t}\to\mathcal{X}
be mapping from from the control sequence
u_{\leq t}
to the state
v_{t}
such that
v_{t}=\phi_{t}(u_{\leq t})
. Let
\phi_{\leq T}\colon\mathcal{X}^{T}\to\mathcal{X}^{T}
be the mapping from
u_{\leq T}
to
v_{\leq T}
such that
v_{\leq T}=\phi_{\leq T}(u_{\leq T})
.

The following results provide bounds on the stability of (
30
)

Lemma 13
.

Assume that
\operatorname{Lip}(h_{t})\leq L
for all
t\in[T]
. Then,
\phi_{\leq T}
is Lipschitz continuous with

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lip}(\phi_{\leq T})\leq\sqrt{T}(1+L)^{t-1}.

Proof.

Let
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{T}
and set
\delta_{t}=\lVert\phi_{\leq t}(u_{\leq t})-\phi_{\leq t}(u^{\prime}_{\leq t})\rVert
. Since
h_{1}
is a constant function,
\phi_{1}(u_{1})=h_{1}+u_{1}
and
\delta_{1}=\lVert\phi_{1}(u_{1})-\phi_{1}(u^{\prime}_{1})\rVert=\lVert u_{1}-u^{\prime}_{1}\rVert
. For
t=2,\dots,T
we can write

\displaystyle\delta_{t}-\delta_{t-1}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\phi_{t}(u_{\leq t})-\phi_{t}(u^{\prime}_{\leq t})\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert h_{t}(\phi_{<t}(u_{<t}))+u_{t}-h_{t}(\phi_{<t}(u_{<t}))-u^{\prime}_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L\delta_{t-1}+\lVert u_{t}-u^{\prime}_{t}\rVert

Summing the increments yields

\displaystyle\delta_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(1+L)^{t}\lVert u_{T-t}-u^{\prime}_{T-t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(1+L)^{2t}}}\right)^{1/2}\lVert u_{\leq t}-u^{\prime}_{\leq t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T}(1+L)^{T-1}\lVert u_{\leq t}-u^{\prime}_{\leq t}\rVert,

where the second step is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
x=(x_{t})_{t\in[T]}
be generated by (
30
) with control

such that
x_{\leq t}=\phi_{\leq t}(0)

\displaystyle\lVert x-y\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\prod_{t=2}^{T}(1+a_{t})\Big)\sum_{t=1}^{T}(b_{t}+\lVert y_{t}-h_{t}(y_{<t})\rVert)

In particular, if
\Lip(h_{t})\leq L
for all

then

\displaystyle\lVert x-y\rVert

\displaystyle\leq(1+L)^{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert y_{t}-h_{t}(y_{<t})\rVert.

Proof.

The sequence
y
can be generated by (
30

u_{t}=y_{t}-h_{t}(y_{<t})
such that
y_{\leq t}=\phi_{\leq t}(u_{\leq t})

\delta_{1}=\lVert x_{1}-y_{1}\rVert=\lVert u_{1}\rVert

\displaystyle\delta_{t}-\delta_{t-1}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert x_{t}-y_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert h_{t}(x_{<t})-h_{t}(y_{<t})-u_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert h_{t}(x_{<t})-h_{t}(y_{<t})\rVert+\lVert u_{t}\rVert

Summing the increments, up to time

\displaystyle\delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\Big(\prod_{r=s+1}^{t}(1+a_{r})\Big)(b_{s}+\lVert u_{s}\rVert)+b_{t}+\lVert u_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\prod_{r=2}^{t}(1+a_{r})\Big)\sum_{s=1}^{t}(b_{s}+\lVert u_{s}\rVert),

Evaluating at

∎
