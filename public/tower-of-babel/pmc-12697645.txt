Blood Biomarkers for Large Vessel Occlusions: A Systematic Review
Authors: Tanaisha Italia, Ilayda Kayir, Ganesh Chilukuri, Saarang Patel, Shray Alpesh Patel, Avi A. Gajjar, Rashad Jabarkheel, Adam Eberle, Oscar A Marcos‐Contreras, Kyle W. Scott, Scott E Kasner, Ruchira M Jha, Bryan Pukenas, Jan‐Karl Burkhardt, Visish M. Srinivasan, Joshua S. Catapano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDLarge vessel occlusions (LVOs), which account for approximately 25% of ischemic strokes, pose a significant challenge due to their severe impact and need for rapid diagnosis and treatment. Current diagnostic approaches—primarily based on clinical scales and imaging—often lack specificity or delay treatment, severely impacting patient outcomes. This review aims to evaluate the potential of blood biomarkers in improving the accuracy and efficiency of LVO diagnosis.METHODSA systematic review adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines were conducted with specific inclusion/exclusion criteria and multiperson screening. PubMed, Google Scholar, and Embase were searched using targeted queries related to LVO and biomarkers. Studies reporting the diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of blood biomarkers for LVO were included. Data were extracted and synthesized to categorize biomarkers and assess their diagnostic utility.RESULTSSixteen studies were included, categorizing biomarkers into 5 groups: coagulation and hemostasis, acute brain injury, inflammatory, angiogenic growth factors, and metabolic and structural markers.CONCLUSIONSThe review highlights the critical role of biomarker blood testing to enhance LVO diagnosis, especially in acute clinical settings. Coagulation and hemostasis markers such as D‐dimer offer rapid thrombus detection, and acute brain injury and angiogenic biomarkers provide insight into the extent of injury, localization, and vascular response. A combination of biomarkers from multiple categories is needed to provide an accurate clinical picture of LVO in patients. Excluding studies on embolic strokes, including cardioembolic subtypes, may also bias findings by neglecting their distinct biomarker profiles, warranting further investigation to fully assess biomarker utility across stroke etiologies. Despite promising results for multiple biomarkers, including glial fibrillary acidic protein, ubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase‐L1, and D‐dimer, further research is needed to validate these biomarkers in diverse populations and integrate them into clinical practice effectively.

Large vessel occlusion (LVO) involving the major proximal arteries of the carotid and vertebrobasilar circulation accounts for approximately a quarter of strokes, most of which are severe. 1 , 2 Currently, intravenous thrombolytic agents, administered within approximately 4.5 hours of symptom onset and mechanical thrombectomy (MT), are the mainstays of treatment for LVOs. 3 , 4 These treatments have demonstrated significant benefits in reducing disability and improving functional outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS). Although intravenous thrombolysis is available at most regional hospitals, MT can be performed only at comprehensive stroke centers equipped with a neurointerventionalist. 5 The “mothership model” is often used, where regional stroke centers transfer patients to thrombectomy‐capable centers. 5 Given the importance of rapid reperfusion in ischemic stroke, it is essential to use an efficient, rapid, and sensitive triage tool to identify LVOs. 5

Current clinical diagnostic criteria for LVOs have poor sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. 6 Cortical symptoms such as aphasia, apraxia, and neglect have higher sensitivity than motor deficits, but these symptoms are not specific to LVO. 6 Several clinical scales have been developed to identify LVOs and are especially useful in the telehealth setting. 7 A National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≥11 has a sensitivity of ∼85%. 7 The Field Assessment Stroke Triage for Emergency Destination (FAST‐ED), Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Severity Scale, and Prehospital Acute Stroke Severity Scale are all approximately 80% sensitive for LVO. In comparison, the Rapid Arterial Occlusion Evaluation Scale is the most sensitive (89%). 7 However, these clinical scales have extremely poor specificities, between 50% and 70%, resulting in many false transfers. 7

Additionally, computed tomography (CT), CT angiography (CTA), and CT perfusion are vital imaging modalities in the management of acute stroke. Although these imaging techniques are sometimes conducted in specialized mobile stroke units equipped with onboard CT scanners, these units can be costly, geographically limited, and not widely available. 8 As a result, many of these studies are performed at the emergency departments of primary stroke centers. 8 However, this standard practice introduces a critical challenge: the time required for imaging at primary centers often delays patient transfer to comprehensive stroke centers, where advanced interventions like MT can be administered. 9 These delays are especially harmful in cases of LVOs or tandem occlusions, where “time is brain” and every minute significantly affects functional outcomes. 9

An alternative approach—using a reliable biomarker assay—could revolutionize acute stroke triage. A field‐deployable biomarker test capable of rapidly and accurately identifying patients with severe stroke syndromes such as LVOs or distal occlusions would allow first responders to bypass intermediate imaging and transport patients directly to comprehensive stroke centers. 10 This strategy would minimize delays and enhance the likelihood of favorable outcomes. 10 Transitioning from hospital‐based imaging to biomarker‐driven triage in the field would eliminate a key bottleneck, streamline the stroke care process, reduce redundant imaging, and improve timely access to advanced therapies. 10 The development of accurate biomarkers could reduce the economic burdens associated with false diagnosis and reduce delays in time to reperfusion. 10

Hence, we review existing studies on blood biomarkers for LVOs, emphasizing their diagnostic potential and limitations.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. A systematic review was conducted based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines. 11 PubMed, Google Scholar, and Embase were searched with the following query: “(large vessel occlusion[Title/Abstract]) AND ((marker[Title/Abstract]) or (biomarker[Title/Abstract]))” (Figure 1). Articles were screened by 2 independent reviewers, with a third reviewer to resolve conflicts (Figure 1).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses flow diagram of the screening process.

Articles were included if they reported data on the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of biomarkers for diagnosing LVO (Figure 1). Articles that focused on prognostic biomarkers without discussing diagnostic biomarkers were excluded (Figure 1). Similarly, reviews, commentaries, poster presentations, preprints, and articles not written in English were excluded manually by reviewers during the screening process (Figure 1). Both human and animal models are included. Publications on cerebrovascular conditions other than LVO, such as intracranial hemorrhage or transient ischemic attack, as well as other neurological conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease or traumatic brain injury, were excluded (Figure 1). Articles that focused on embolic stroke and imaging biomarkers were also excluded (Figure 1). Search strategy was not restricted by number or age of subjects or year of publication (Figure 1). A reverse bibliometrics search was conducted, and relevant articles were added to the full‐text review. Data were systematically extracted from each study by a medical student and qualitatively appraised by a second student (Figure 1).

To evaluate study quality and internal validity, we used the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 tool. This tool assesses 4 domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each included study was assessed independently by 2 reviewers. Tailored signaling questions were used (Table S2), and results were summarized in Table S1. Sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve were also calculated for key biomarkers, the results of which are listed in Table S3.

RESULTS

Sixteen studies were identified as eligible for data extraction. The biomarkers from these studies can be subdivided into 5 main categories (Figure 2). Coagulation and hemostatic biomarkers include Factor VII activating protease, thrombin‐antithrombin complex, and D‐dimer (Table 1). Acute brain injury markers include glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase‐L1 (UCHL1), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and various types of microRNA (Table 1). Inflammation markers primarily consist of interleukins and tumor necrosis factors (Table 1). Angiogenic growth factors include vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), endostatin, and microRNAs (Table 1). Finally, Metabolic and structural biomarkers mainly consist of mean platelet volume (MPV), cardiac troponin, and insulin‐like growth factor 2 (Table 1).

Coagulation and Hemostatic Biomarkers

D‐dimer levels were investigated in 3 studies: by Gaude et al, Ramos‐Pachon et al, and Durrani et al, all of which observed elevated D‐dimer levels in patients with LVO. 12 , 13 , 14 Tian et al found Factor VII activating protease to be significantly elevated in patients with LVO with poor collateral circulation. 15 Navalkale et al investigated post‐tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) levels of Factor VIII and observed no difference between the median Factor VIII level at baseline and 90 days post stroke. 16 Zhao et al analyzed thrombin‐antithrombin complex, plasmin–α2‐antiplasmin inhibitor complexes, tPA–plasminogen activator inhibitor complexes, and thrombomodulin plasma levels in 423 patients with AIS, dividing the cohort into a massive cerebral infarction group and a nonmassive infarction group. 17 Their analysis revealed that plasmin–α2‐antiplasmin inhibitor complexes, thrombin‐antithrombin complex, and tPA–plasminogen activator inhibitor complexes levels were higher in the massive cerebral infarction group, but thrombomodulin levels showed no significant difference. 17

Acute Brain Injury Markers

GFAP was the most commonly investigated acute brain injury marker in the included studies. GFAP levels were elevated in patients with hemorrhagic stroke, as well as in those with LVO and small vessel occlusion. 18 , 19 , 20 Amalia et al conducted a Mann–Whitney test at a 95% confidence level, revealing significantly higher GFAP levels in patients with LVO compared with patients with small vessel occlusion (0.7 versus 0.4 ng/mL; P = 0.001). 18 In addition to GFAP, Chen et al investigated NfL, tau, and UCHL1 levels before groin puncture, upon completion of thrombectomy, and 24 hours post stroke. 19 NfL, Tau, and UCHL1 levels were higher in patients with LVO than in controls prior to thrombectomy. 19

Inflammatory and Immune Markers

Among studies investigating inflammatory markers, Scott et al observed that interleukin (IL)‐4, IL‐13, IL‐1β, IL‐2, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐16, and IL‐12p70 levels were higher in thrombi from patients with ischemic stroke as compared with cerebral and peripheral plasma. 21 Furthermore, they discovered that IL‐5, IL‐15, IL‐12/IL‐23p40, interferon‐γ, and IL‐17A levels were lower in thrombi than in cerebral and peripheral plasma. 21 Ramos‐Pachon et al reported that IL‐6 levels were higher in patients with LVO compared with patients without LVO. 13

Angiogenic Growth Factors

Scott et al revealed that VEGF‐A, VEGF‐C, and basic fibroblast growth factor levels were higher in thrombi than in cerebral and peripheral plasma from patients with AIS. 21 Additionally, they observed that levels of VEGF‐D, FMS‐related tyrosine kinase 1, placental growth factor, and tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin‐like and EGF‐like domains 2 were higher in blood than in cerebral and peripheral plasma. 21

Metabolic and Structural Biomarkers

In their retrospective study, Dębiec et al identified increased MPV as a predictor of LVO. 22 Wei et al measured plasma chromogranin A levels in 587 patients upon admission and reported that chromogranin A levels were lower in patients with AIS, particularly in anterior circulation LVO. 23 Qin et al presented increased levels of thrombospondin‐1, lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1, insulin‐like growth factor 2, proplatelet basic protein, secreted phosphoprotein 2, cadherin 1, apolipoprotein C‐IV, and apolipoprotein C‐II as indicators of an increased risk of LVO stroke. 24 The levels of insulin‐like growth factor 2, lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1, proplatelet basic protein, and thrombospondin‐1 were significantly elevated in patients with LVO compared with those with AIS due to non‐LVO. 24 Chang et al analyzed cardiac troponin levels in 1234 patients with AIS and determined that patients with LVO were more likely to have positive troponin levels. 25 Rout et al identified 11 metabolites and 41 microRNAs that correlated with infarct volume in patients with LVO. 26

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias was variable across the included studies. Most studies demonstrated low risk in the reference standard domain, as LVO diagnosis was confirmed using CT angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, or digital subtraction angiography. However, the patient selection domain was frequently depicted as high risk due to retrospective designs and unclear inclusion criteria. The flow and timing domain also demonstrated frequent concerns, with variability or missing information on biomarker sampling intervals. The index test domain was generally low risk but often lacked prespecified thresholds (Table S1).

Diagnostic Accuracy Summary

A comparative summary of biomarker diagnostic performance is provided in Tables S3 and S4. Sensitivity and specificity values varied by biomarker and study design. D‐dimer, when combined with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale and atrial fibrillation history, yielded high specificity (98%) but low sensitivity (17%) for LVO. In contrast, multimodal panels such as GFAP+D‐dimer+FAST‐ED achieved a sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 95%. GFAP alone demonstrated sensitivity around 85.5% and specificity near 62%, whereas UCHL1 showed slightly lower performance. MPV had moderate diagnostic performance (AUC 0.66; sensitivity 61%, specificity 73%). Full diagnostic values and clinical interpretation are presented in Supplementary Tables.

Study Heterogeneity

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies in several domains, including biomarker types, sample processing methods, reference standards, and the timing of blood collection. For example, biomarker sampling timepoints ranged from immediately upon emergency department arrival to 7 days post stroke onset. Several studies used retrospective samples, whereas others followed prospective cohorts. Differences in patient populations, stroke etiology, and assay techniques further contributed to variability. These differences limit direct comparisons between studies and emphasize the need for standardization in future biomarker research (Figures 3 and 4).

DISCUSSION

Coagulation and Hemostatic Biomarkers

Coagulation and hemostatic biomarkers are integral to the thrombotic processes underpinning LVO. Factor VIII, a central element of the intrinsic coagulation cascade, plays a critical role in thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation. Elevated Factor VIII levels are associated with increased thrombotic risk and poor outcomes in patients with LVO. 16 Navalkele et al found that Factor VIII decreased transiently after tPA administration, then rebounded within 72 hours, reflecting the dynamic interplay of hemostatic mechanisms following reperfusion therapy. 16

D‐dimer, a fibrin degradation product, is widely used as a marker of thrombogenesis and fibrinolysis. Elevated D‐dimer levels correlate with thrombus burden and stroke severity but lack specificity, as they may also be elevated in malignancy, infection, and peripheral embolism. 12 , 13 Ramos‐Pachón et al reported that combining a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≥10, history of atrial fibrillation, and elevated D‐dimer levels predicted LVO with 98% specificity, albeit with a sensitivity of only 17%. 13 This supports the role of D‐dimer in triage when integrated with clinical parameters.

Additional markers such as thrombin–antithrombin complexes, plasmin–α2‐antiplasmin inhibitor complexes, and tPA–plasminogen activator inhibitor complexes may offer deeper insight into clot dynamics but require further validation. Confounding factors, including inflammatory states and comorbidities such as atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease, may influence biomarker levels and complicate interpretation. 17

Acute Brain Injury Biomarkers

Markers of neuronal and astrocytic injury provide critical information about cerebral damage in LVO. GFAP, an astrocyte‐specific intermediate filament protein, rises rapidly after blood–brain barrier disruption and extensive brain injury. It has demonstrated high sensitivity (85.5%) and moderate specificity (62.07%) in distinguishing LVO from small vessel occlusion. 18 , 19 , 20

UCHL1, a neuron‐specific enzyme involved in protein degradation, has shown similar diagnostic value, with a slightly lower sensitivity (75%). 20 Both GFAP and UCHL1 have also been associated with predicting hemorrhagic transformation. 19

NfL, a cytoskeletal protein released during axonal injury, is another promising marker. In a study by Chen et al, NfL levels increased significantly over time following endovascular thrombectomy, indicating its potential for monitoring neuronal damage. 19 Tau and UCHL1 levels also increased over time, though not always linearly, emphasizing the importance of timing in sample collection.

Despite their utility, these biomarkers may be influenced by other neurological conditions. Standardized, high‐throughput assays are needed to facilitate their widespread clinical use (Table 2).

Inflammatory Biomarkers

Inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor α play key roles in the inflammatory cascade after stroke. IL‐6 has been associated with increased infarct volume and poorer outcomes. 21 In addition, microRNAs regulate inflammatory and immune responses and hold promise as diagnostic biomarkers in LVO. 26

However, the heterogeneity of inflammatory responses and the impact of comorbid conditions complicate their use in acute diagnostics. Scott et al highlighted discrepancies in biomarker levels between peripheral and cerebral plasma and within thrombi, underscoring the challenge of establishing reliable thresholds for stroke diagnosis. 21

Angiogenic and Vascular Growth Factors

VEGF‐A promotes angiogenesis and increases vascular permeability during ischemia, aiding in tissue repair but also contributing to pathological neovascularization. Elevated VEGF‐A has been observed in acute stroke and may have both beneficial and adverse effects. 13 Endostatin, a VEGF antagonist, acts to regulate these angiogenic processes and is linked to poorer outcomes when elevated. 13 These markers can offer prognostic insights but are subject to temporal variability. Their application requires careful timing of blood sample collection and further validation.

Metabolic and Structural Biomarkers

Metabolic biomarkers such as MPV and chromogranin A reflect systemic and neuroendocrine stress responses to stroke. MPV, indicative of platelet activation and aggregation, has been linked with thrombotic risk; an MPV ≥10.2 fL showed 61% sensitivity and 73% specificity for LVO. 22 Elevated chromogranin A levels have been associated with anterior circulation LVO. 23 Despite their potential, these markers are expressed across various tissues, limiting specificity. The LVOCheck‐Opti platform, which integrates heart‐type fatty acid‐binding protein and N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide with clinical scores, is an example of a multimodal approach under development. Although promising, GFAP remains one of the most effective individual biomarkers for LVO. 28

Multimodal Diagnostic Approaches

Combining biomarkers from multiple categories improves diagnostic accuracy. A novel LVOne test, incorporating GFAP and D‐dimer with the FAST‐ED stroke scale, achieved 98% specificity and 82% sensitivity in observational trials. 12 , 28 , 29 A study conducted by Durrani et al similarly incorporated GFAP and D‐dimer with the FAST‐ED scale, demonstrating 81% sensitivity and 94% specificity for patients who presented within 6 hours of symptom onset. 14 Moreover, none of the 13 patients with hemorrhagic stroke were falsely classified as LVO, indicating that this approach may have potential in distinguishing between LVO and cerebral hemorrhage. 14 These multimodal tools capture different pathophysiological dimensions—coagulation, neuronal injury, and vascular remodeling—thereby enhancing triage and treatment decisions.

Clinical Integration

Blood‐based biomarkers such as D‐dimer, GFAP, NfL, and UCHL1 show promise in improving diagnostic accuracy for LVO stroke, particularly when used in combination. These biomarkers can be rapidly assessed in emergency settings and may facilitate prehospital triage, enabling direct transport to thrombectomy‐capable centers. Furthermore, integrating biomarker panels with existing stroke severity scales (eg, FAST‐ED) may improve specificity and reduce false‐positive transfers, especially in resource‐limited environments.

Biomarker‐based diagnostics can complement imaging modalities such as CT angiography and magnetic resonance imaging by offering dynamic physiological data. In prehospital and emergency department settings, point‐of‐care testing for biomarkers like D‐dimer and GFAP may facilitate early identification of LVO and rapid transfer to thrombectomy‐capable centers. 12 , 28 Tracking biomarker trends over time, as demonstrated by Chen et al with NfL, GFAP, tau, and UCHL1, could help assess disease progression and therapeutic efficacy. 19 Recent studies also support the integration of biomarkers into prehospital workflows. Durrani et al demonstrated that combining GFAP and D‐dimer with clinical stroke scales such as FAST‐ED and Rapid Arterial Occlusion Evaluation Scale significantly enhanced triage sensitivity and specificity for LVO, especially for patients who presented in <6 hours from stroke onset. 14

Technological Innovations

Advancements in single‐cell and whole‐genome sequencing have enabled more detailed biomarker discovery and characterization. These techniques help uncover gene expression profiles related to stroke pathophysiology, particularly immune and inflammatory responses. 30 Gene chip technology, such as the 97‐gene panel developed by Tang et al, has demonstrated high specificity in distinguishing ischemic stroke from cardiovascular conditions. 31

Additionally, microfluidic biosensors allow for rapid, low‐volume detection of inflammatory markers, and machine learning algorithms improve biomarker pattern recognition and stroke subtype prediction. However, challenges such as imbalanced training data and generalizability remain. 32 , 33 , 34

Ethical Considerations

Although biomarkers hold substantial promise, their clinical integration must consider equity. Cost‐effective, accessible testing is essential to avoid exacerbating disparities in stroke diagnosis and treatment. 35 Particularly in rural and underserved regions, scalable solutions are critical to ensure timely care for all populations.

If a rapid and accessible biomarker test were integrated into the emergency medical services workflow, it would expedite triage in subsequent locations, including the emergency department. It could also enhance and support the decision‐making process for neurologists, interventional radiologists, and neurosurgeons to determine which surgical or endovascular approaches are likely to be most effective. However, this would require a more advanced understanding of biomarker measurements in relation to the site and onset of the stroke. Introducing a test too early could have the opposite effect and lead to greater confusion. 10

Future Directions

Biomarkers may eventually enable personalized stroke care by predicting outcomes of MT, risk of recurrence, and hemorrhagic transformation. During MT, clot and arterial blood samples offer unique research opportunities to investigate thrombus composition and related biomarkers. 36 In the postacute phase, wearable devices and artificial intelligence‐driven platforms are being explored to tailor rehabilitation based on individual biomarker profiles. 36 Progress in this field will depend on interdisciplinary collaboration among clinicians, researchers, and engineers to develop and validate diagnostic platforms for clinical use. 37

Challenges and Limitations

Although biomarkers such as GFAP, NfL, D‐dimer, osteoprotegerin, and osteopontin have shown promise, specificity remains a limitation. Some markers overlap with non‐LVO stroke or systemic diseases, leading to potential misclassification. 12 , 19

Study limitations include small sample sizes, homogenous populations, inconsistent sampling times, and reliance on nonclinically scalable methods such as Western blotting. 16 , 19 , 22 , 24 A key limitation of this review is the heterogeneity among the included studies. Variations in biomarker subtypes, timing of sample collection, and diverse patient populations highlight the need for further research to establish clinical utility and effectiveness. Furthermore, blood–brain barrier integrity affects biomarker release into peripheral circulation, especially in relation to infarct location and severity. 38 Importantly, this review excluded studies focused solely on embolic strokes, particularly cardioembolic sources. This may introduce selection bias, as cardioembolic strokes—often causing LVO—have distinct biomarker profiles due to factors like atrial fibrillation, endocardial damage, or cardiac thrombi. 14 , 39 , 40 Their exclusion may overlook relevant markers or overstate the specificity of others. As such, additional investigation on embolic subtypes to better evaluate biomarker utility across all stroke etiologies may be warranted. Methodological limitations of the review process—including restricted database searches, language exclusions, and omission of grey literature—may have further narrowed the evidence base. Future studies should prioritize diverse cohorts of variable demographics and stroke etiology, time‐sensitive sampling protocols, and point‐of‐care compatible technologies.

CONCLUSION

LVO remains a devastating condition with significant potential for fatal outcomes and long‐term disability. Beyond the direct clinical effects, LVOs also impose a considerable economic burden, underscoring the urgency of improving diagnostic and treatment strategies. The need for effective acute diagnostic biomarkers for LVOs is greater than ever, yet the current body of literature includes a limited number of studies focused on this critical area. Further exploration is warranted into advanced diagnostic approaches, such as central blood sampling during MT, to address existing limitations. Reliable diagnostic biomarkers have the potential to reduce the catastrophic outcomes associated with LVO by enabling earlier identification and intervention. When integrated with imaging modalities, a combination of blood biomarkers from multiple pathways could streamline the diagnostic process, improving accuracy and efficiency. The present review highlights the critical need for further research and development in this domain. Future studies should focus on validating reliable LVO‐specific biomarkers, with the goal of advancing stroke care and improving patient outcomes.
