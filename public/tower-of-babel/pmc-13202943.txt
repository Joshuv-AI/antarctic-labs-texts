Purulent infectious myositis of deep core muscles, a MRSA infection in India
Authors: Madhulika L Mahashabde, Brugumalla V Nitendra Saketh, Gaurav Ashok Chaudhary, Sai Karthik Guniganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPurulent Infectious Myositis is a common bacterial infection in peripheral limb muscles, but its involvement in deep core muscles is rare. Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most common organisms causing purulent infectious myositis. With an increase in antibiotic resistance in India, many cases are now being reported caused by Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).Case presentationA male farmer in his early 50 s presented with lower back pain, diffuse abdominal pain, significant weight loss, fatigue for one month, and high-grade fever for one week. Clinical examination revealed severe sepsis with tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnoea, and mild diffuse abdominal tenderness. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leucocytosis, elevated inflammatory markers, acute kidney injury, and deranged liver function tests. Imaging studies revealed multiple deep-core muscle abscesses. Ultrasound-guided aspiration yielded pus, and cultures confirmed Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. The patient was diagnosed with purulent infectious myositis complicated by severe sepsis, septic shock, acute kidney injury, and sepsis-associated cholestasis. Treatment comprised drainage of abscesses, prolonged antimicrobial therapy and supportive care. The patient had complete resolution of the abscesses on follow-up.ConclusionThis case emphasises the importance of maintaining a high clinical suspicion for purulent infectious myositis—early diagnosis, combined with aggressive medical and surgical management —to achieve early sepsis management and a good prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13210-z.Keywords: Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus aureus, Purulent infectious myositis, Pyomyositis, Muscle abscesses, Severe sepsis

Background

Purulent infectious myositis is a bacterial infection of skeletal muscles, predominantly affecting limb muscles; deep core muscles are rarely involved and pose a diagnostic challenge [1]. Common bacterial infections in the Indian subcontinent include tuberculosis, typhoid fever, bacterial meningitis, and skin and soft tissue infections [1, 2]. Atypical presentations of common bacterial pathogens continue to pose diagnostic challenges, leading to delayed treatment, increased morbidity, and adverse patient outcomes [3]. Unusual clinical manifestations may arise from several factors, including host immunological status, genetic predisposition, concurrent comorbidities, prior antibiotic exposure, and variations in bacterial virulence factors [4–6].

The rise in antimicrobial resistance, particularly among common bacterial pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, has complicated the clinical course of infections and contributed to the emergence of unusual manifestations [7, 8]. This case report highlights an atypical clinical presentation of a common bacterial infection requiring a high index of clinical suspicion and emphasis on the importance of clinical evaluation, investigations, and awareness of disease patterns in the region. In this case, we want to emphasise the diagnostic difficulties encountered in other resource-limited settings and help clinicians recognise and manage similar presentations.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old male farmer with no comorbidities or significant past medical history presented with lower back pain that had begun approximately one month prior, with symptoms worsening during physical activity. Concurrently, he complained of mild diffuse abdominal pain of similar duration, which was also insidious in onset but non progressive, without any specific aggravating or relieving factors.

Patient reported consistent exposure to dust and soil. Over the course of the month, the patient developed loss of appetite, unintentional weight loss of over 10 kg, fatigue, and progressive generalised weakness interfering with his daily activities. One week before admission, he developed high-grade continuous fever, accompanied by chills, which was temporarily relieved with over-the-counter antipyretics. The patient reported no history of jaundice, loose stools, vomiting, or night sweats during this illness.

On general examination, the patient appeared conscious and oriented. He appeared to have a low body mass index (BMI) of 20 kg/m2, suggesting undernutrition. Vital signs revealed an elevated pulse rate of 120 beats per minute; low blood pressure 80/60 mmHg; body temperature of 102 °F; increased respiratory rate of 28 breaths per minute; and oxygen saturation was 95% on room air. Physical examination revealed mild icterus, with no pallor, cyanosis, clubbing, peripheral oedema, or lymphadenopathy.

Systematic examination of the cardiovascular and respiratory systems did not reveal any abnormalities. Abdominal examination showed a soft abdomen with mild diffuse tenderness and mild hepatomegaly on palpation. The central nervous system examination was unremarkable, with no focal neurological deficits detected.

Tropical fevers, such as dengue, malaria, and chikungunya, were evaluated and found to be negative. Blood cultures and urine cultures were obtained before starting empirical antibiotics.

Outcome and follow-up

Following IV antibiotic therapy (Table 2), Supportive care, and multivitamins, the laboratory investigations—including total leucocyte count, renal and liver function tests, and sepsis markers such as procalcitonin and serum lactate — were normalised. Due to financial constraints, the patient requested early discharge and agreed to continue oral antibiotic therapy at home. The patient was started on oral linezolid 600 mg twice daily for two weeks and was advised to follow up in the outpatient department.

After 2 weeks, the patient presented to the outpatient department with unremarkable laboratory investigations. A contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis (Fig. 2C) was repeated after total antibiotic therapy, showing complete resolution of abscesses in the iliac, psoas, and gluteal regions.

The patient was advised to attend monthly follow-up in the OPD to monitor for abscess recurrence.

At next follow-up

After a month, the patient showed significant clinical improvement and nourishment, and an ultrasound was performed, which showed no recurrence and complete resolution.

Furthermore, after a month, the patient’s BMI improved to 23 kg/m2 (normal range 22–26 kg/m2), with a total weight gain of 6 kg over 3 months.

Discussion

Epidemiological and diagnostic context

Pyomyositis is predominantly a disease of tropical regions, historically termed “tropical pyomyositis,” with an incidence of 1–4 cases per 100,000 population annually in endemic areas. However, its incidence in temperate climates has risen significantly over the past two decades, largely attributed to increasing rates of immunocompromise, HIV, diabetes mellitus, and intravenous drug use. In India, pyomyositis accounts for approximately 1–4% of all surgical admissions in tropical regions, with a well-documented male preponderance (male-to-female ratio 2:1 to 4:1) and a peak incidence in the second to fourth decades of life. Our patient, a male farmer in his early fifties, is slightly older than the reported peak, consistent with the emerging pattern of pyomyositis in immunocompetent individuals with occupational risk factors.

While pyomyositis classically involves the large muscles of the lower limbs — particularly the quadriceps, gluteal, and iliopsoas muscles — involvement of deep core muscles, such as the bilateral psoas, iliacus, and gluteal muscles, without peripheral limb muscle disease, as seen in our patient, is rare and diagnostically challenging. Deep core muscle involvement accounts for fewer than 10% of reported cases of pyomyositis. It is associated with a higher risk of delayed diagnosis due to the absence of the classic triad of localised swelling, pain, and fever that is readily apparent in limb muscle disease. The anatomical proximity of the psoas and iliacus muscles to the retroperitoneum, lumbar spine, and pelvic structures further complicates diagnosis, as symptoms may mimic intra-abdominal pathology, spinal disease, or pelvic sepsis.

Our case illustrates the classic triad of prolonged constitutional symptoms, deep-seated muscle pain, and systemic sepsis in a middle-aged, immunocompetent farmer from India, highlighting the importance of maintaining a high degree of clinical suspicion for this condition, even in atypical presentations involving deep core muscles rather than the more commonly affected limb musculature.

The patient’s occupation as a farmer may have predisposed him to repeated minor musculoskeletal stress and environmental exposure [9]; however, a definite portal of bacterial entry could not be identified.

A comprehensive review by Crum (Supplementary Table 1) demonstrated that even in temperate climates, patients with purulent infectious myositis often have underlying conditions or occupational exposures that increase their susceptibility to this infection [9]. A patient’s agricultural occupation likely contributes to repeated minor muscle injuries, creating portals of entry for hematogenous bacterial seeding.

The clinical presentation in our patient was characterised by an insidious onset with progressive symptoms over several weeks before culminating in severe sepsis and septic shock. This presentation is consistent with the natural history of purulent infectious myositis, which typically progresses through three distinct clinical stages as described by Chauhan and colleagues [3]. Our patient presented in the suppurative stage and progressed beyond the invasive stage, where symptoms are often subtle and nonspecific, making early diagnosis challenging. The subsequent rapid deterioration into systemic disease with severe sepsis, acute kidney injury, and sepsis-associated cholestasis underscores the potentially life-threatening nature of this condition when diagnosis is delayed. Similar cases of delayed presentation resulting in multiorgan dysfunction have been reported in the literature, emphasising the critical importance of early recognition and intervention [10].

The microbiological profile of our patient is MRSA as the causative organism, which is less common than other methicillin-susceptible spp. Strains are increasingly recognised as a significant pathogen in purulent infectious myositis. According to a systematic review by Ngor and colleagues, Staphylococcus aureus accounts for 70–90% of culture-positive cases worldwide, with MRSA comprising 5–30% of these cases, depending on the geographical location and local resistance patterns [11]. The prevalence of MRSA in purulent infectious myositis patients appears to be increasing in parallel with increasing community-acquired MRSA rates worldwide. A study from India by Sharma and colleagues reported MRSA in approximately 15% of their purulent infectious myositis cases, suggesting that our patient’s infection reflects this emerging epidemiological trend in the Indian subcontinent [12]. The presence of MRSA significantly affects treatment strategies and clinical outcomes, necessitating the use of glycopeptide antibiotics or newer agents active against resistant gram-positive organisms.

The pathogenesis of staphylococcal purulent infectious myositis involves complex interactions between bacterial virulence factors and host defence mechanisms. Research by Genestier and colleagues has demonstrated that PVL induces leukocyte destruction and tissue necrosis by forming pores in cell membranes, thereby facilitating bacterial proliferation within muscle tissue [13]. Alpha-toxin, another important staphylococcal virulence factor, has been shown to contribute to muscle tissue damage and inflammatory responses, as demonstrated in animal models by Bubeck Wardenburg and colleagues [14]. Although molecular characterisation of the isolate was not performed, the above studies have demonstrated that MRSA virulence factors contribute to tissue destruction and immune evasion in deep muscle infections.

Accurate and early imaging is critical to the diagnosis and management of pyomyositis, particularly when deep core muscles are involved. In our case, the diagnostic approach used multimodal imaging techniques to localise and characterise deep-seated muscle abscesses accurately. The initial ultrasound examination revealed a heterogeneous collection in the right flank, prompting further investigation with abdominal CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). A comparative study by Soler and colleagues evaluating MRI findings in 43 patients with purulent infectious myositis concluded that MRI is superior to CT in delineating the extent of muscle involvement, identifying early-stage disease, and detecting complications such as osteomyelitis or epidural extension [15]. In our patient, MRI was instrumental in identifying isolated discitis at the L5-S1 level, a complication that would likely have been missed on CT alone, and in accurately mapping the full extent of multi-compartment deep core muscle involvement to guide surgical and radiological drainage.

Laboratory investigations revealed marked elevations in inflammatory markers, including leucocytosis with a neutrophilic predominance, elevated C-reactive protein, and significantly elevated serum ferritin levels, consistent with severe bacterial infection and a systemic inflammatory response. Interestingly, creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels remained within normal limits despite extensive muscle involvement. This finding has previously been documented in purulent infectious myositis and helps differentiate this condition from inflammatory myopathies and rhabdomyolysis [16]. A study by Vigil and colleagues examining purulent infectious myositis in immunocompromised patients noted that normal or minimally elevated CPK levels are characteristic of this condition, as the infection primarily affects the intermuscular fascial planes rather than directly causing myocyte necrosis [16]. This biochemical distinction is clinically helpful when considering differential diagnoses in patients presenting with muscle pain and systemic sepsis.

Management of our patient required a multimodal approach combining source control via surgical drainage, prolonged antimicrobial therapy, and intensive supportive care. Initial empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage with meropenem and teicoplanin was subsequently modified to targeted therapy with linezolid, teicoplanin and vancomycin following culture confirmation of MRSA. Current guidelines for the treatment of MRSA bacteremia and deep-seated infections recommend vancomycin as a first-line agent, with alternatives including daptomycin, linezolid, or ceftaroline for complicated cases or when vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentrations are elevated [21]. A meta-analysis by Kalil and colleagues comparing various antibiotic regimens for MRSA infections revealed that combination therapy with vancomycin and an additional agent may be beneficial in severe cases with bacteremia and deep-seated infections [17]. Our decision to utilise combination therapy with both vancomycin and linezolid was based on the severity of the patient’s presentation with septic shock and multiorgan dysfunction, as well as the extensive nature of the muscle abscesses requiring prolonged therapy and the need for coverage of ICU cross-infection prevention against nosocomial Gram-positive organisms.

The recommended course of treatment is 3–4 weeks of intravenous antibiotics, followed by 2–4 weeks of oral therapy, for a total of 6–8 weeks for complicated cases [18]. Our patient received intensive intravenous treatment for four weeks, followed by oral linezolid for two additional weeks, with documented clinical improvement and radiological resolution of the abscesses on follow-up imaging. A retrospective study by Burdette and colleagues analysing treatment outcomes in patients with purulent infectious myositis revealed that inadequate therapy duration was associated with relapse rates of up to 15%, emphasising the importance of prolonged antibiotic courses to ensure complete eradication of the infection [18].

Surgical intervention plays a crucial role in the management of our patient. A systematic review by Mitsionis and colleagues examining management strategies for patients with purulent infectious myositis concluded that early-stage disease may respond to antibiotic therapy alone. In contrast, suppurative-stage disease with formed abscesses typically requires both drainage and antimicrobial therapy [19].

The prognostic implications of delayed diagnosis and management of purulent infectious myositis are significant, with mortality rates ranging from 1 to 4% in immunocompetent patients but exceeding 30% in those with severe sepsis, multiorgan dysfunction, or significant immunocompromise [20]. However, with aggressive multimodal therapy including intensive care support, appropriate antimicrobial coverage, surgical source control, and prolonged treatment, complete clinical and radiological resolution can be achieved [21]. Our patient’s progression to severe sepsis with acute kidney injury, sepsis-associated cholestasis, and septic shock placed him in a high-risk category for adverse outcomes and underscores the importance of obtaining appropriate microbiological specimens before initiating therapy, adjusting antimicrobial coverage on the basis of culture results and clinical response, and evaluating atypical presentations in a resource-limited setting. The coexistence of MRSA infection involving multiple deep core muscles with associated discitis and septic shock in an immunocompetent patient is rarely reported in the literature.

This case report has certain limitations. Molecular typing of the MRSA isolate, including evaluation for PVL toxin genes, could not be performed due to laboratory constraints. Additionally, serum vancomycin trough monitoring was not available at our centre. However, despite these limitations, the patient demonstrated complete clinical and radiological resolution following appropriate antimicrobial therapy and source control.
