Introduction

Teenage pregnancy is a significant global public health challenge with far-reaching consequences for young mothers, their children, families and society at large.1 Each year, around 21 million girls aged 15–19 in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) become pregnant, with about 12 million of them giving birth.2 3 Globally, the adolescent birth rate has declined from 64.5 births per 1000 girls aged 15–19 in 2000 to 41.3 births per 1000 in 2023.4 However, Latin America and the Caribbean and sub-Saharan Africa still record the highest adolescent birth rates worldwide, with 97.9 and 51.4 births per 1000 girls, respectively, in 2023.4 5 Teenage pregnancy is a major public health concern in LMICs, contributing to high maternal and newborn mortality, with about 70 000 adolescent girls and one million infants dying annually from pregnancy-related causes.6

The consequences of teenage pregnancy extend beyond immediate health outcomes, as early childbearing can derail girls’ otherwise healthy development into adulthood and have negative impacts on their education, livelihoods and health.3 4 Young mothers often experience disrupted educational trajectories, limited employment opportunities and increased likelihood of living in poverty, perpetuating cycles of disadvantage across generations.1 7 8 Furthermore, children born to teenage mothers face elevated risks of developmental delays, behavioural problems and educational underachievement.9 The economic costs of teenage pregnancy have also gained increasing attention, with substantial implications for national productivity and social equity.4 10 11 These cascading effects underscore the importance of addressing teenage pregnancy not only as a health issue but as a multidimensional development challenge central to achieving targets under Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 1 (Zero poverty), SDG 3 (Good health and well-being) and SDG 4 (Quality education).

Given the complexity and multidimensional nature of teenage pregnancy, diverse intervention strategies have been developed and implemented globally, including comprehensive sexuality education, contraceptive access and counselling, community-based programmes, school-based initiatives and policy interventions.12,14 The heterogeneity of these approaches reflects the recognition that effective teenage pregnancy prevention requires tailored strategies that address local contexts, cultural factors and specific population needs. Comprehensive prevention interventions are noted to yield strong economic returns of up to US$5.7015 per dollar spent. However, COVID-19 disruptions increased teenage pregnancy by up to 25% in some LMICs,16 prompting a shift to effective digital and community-based interventions.17 The need to understand which interventions work best under different circumstances has driven researchers to systematically evaluate these diverse approaches. Consequently, there has been a growing body of research on teenage pregnancy interventions.8 18

Recent evidence syntheses show that comprehensive, multicomponent interventions (eg, comprehensive sexuality education, multimedia behaviour-change campaigns and youth-friendly services) are more effective, reducing teenage pregnancy rates by 15–42%, than abstinence-only or single-component approaches.19 20 Other evidence syntheses have highlighted the complexity of teenage pregnancy prevention efforts and their variable effectiveness across different contexts and populations.8 9 12 21 22 However, no overarching synthesis has systematically mapped this body of evidence to provide an overview of the field. The evidence synthesis landscape in teenage pregnancy prevention remains fragmented, with systematic reviews typically examining specific intervention types, target populations or geographical settings in isolation. This fragmentation poses significant challenges for policymakers, programme implementers and researchers who require a comprehensive understanding of the available evidence to make informed decisions and identify critical knowledge gaps.

The primary objective of this scoping review is to map and describe the extent, range and nature of existing systematic review evidence on interventions aimed at addressing teenage pregnancy. Specifically, the review aims to: (1) identify and characterise systematic reviews of interventions aimed at preventing teenage pregnancy; (2) describe the range of intervention types, study populations and geographical contexts covered by existing systematic reviews; (3) examine the methodological characteristics of these systematic reviews; (4) identify key findings and conclusions across existing systematic reviews; and (5) map gaps in the evidence base and provide recommendations for future research.

Methods and analysis

This scoping review will be conducted using the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley23 and enhanced by subsequent recommendations from Levac et al.24 The framework consists of five key stages: (1) identifying the research question; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) study selection; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results. Stage 6 (consultation exercise) was not undertaken due to the evidence-mapping focus, resource limitations and the absence of a primary data collection component, which made consultation less applicable at this stage.23 24 Results will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.25 The review will include a PRISMA flow diagram documenting the study selection process and comprehensive tables summarising study characteristics and findings. Scoping reviews provide a valuable methodology for mapping broad areas of research, particularly when the literature is complex, heterogeneous or has not been comprehensively reviewed.23 24 Unlike systematic reviews that aim to answer specific questions about intervention effectiveness, scoping reviews seek to examine the extent, range and nature of research activity in a topic area while identifying key concepts, gaps in the research and types of evidence available.25 26 The estimated timeline for the review is 6 months, from November 2025 to April 2026. Further details of the review are outlined in our registered protocol.27

For this review, a teenage pregnancy is defined as a pregnancy occurring in females aged 10–19 years, consistent with the WHO’s definition of adolescence as the second decade of life.5 Interventions encompass any deliberate action, programme, policy or strategy designed to prevent teenage pregnancy or support pregnant teenagers and young mothers, reflecting the broad scope of public health efforts intended to improve health outcomes at the population level.28 A systematic review is defined as a literature review that uses explicit, systematic methods to identify, select and critically appraise relevant research and to collect and analyse data from the studies included in the review,29 ensuring transparency and comprehensiveness in evidence synthesis.

Data sources and search strategy

An initial search of protocol registries, including PROSPERO and the Open Science Framework, was conducted on 7 August 2025 and identified no ongoing scoping reviews of reviews on this topic. For the substantive review, a comprehensive literature search will be conducted, covering: (1) key databases including PubMed Central, Embase, CINAHL and ProQuest Central; (2) systematic review repositories including Epistemonikos and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; and (3) Grey literature search of the websites of the WHO, UNICEF, UNESCO, UNFPA (United Nations Population Fund) and World Bank. Additional search methods will include hand-searching Google Scholar and the reference lists of included studies. In line with Godin et al,30 the search in Google Scholar (with the search entry ‘interventions for addressing teenage pregnancy’) will cover the first 10 pages.

The database search strategy will incorporate both controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH) and free-text keywords related to adolescent or teenage pregnancy, interventions or prevention strategies and systematic reviews or meta-analyses. The search will cover review papers published between January 2000 and December 2025 across the selected databases. Only studies published in English will be included, as the review team’s language expertise is limited to English and resources for translation are not available. The search strategies will combine terms related to: (1) systematic reviews or meta-analyses; (2) teenage/adolescent pregnancy; and (3) interventions or prevention programmes. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in online supplemental file 1. The search is planned to end by 15 December 2025. Data will be managed using Covidence and Microsoft Excel. All retrieved records will be imported into Covidence for deduplication, screening and study selection. Extracted data will then be exported to Microsoft Excel for further organisation, cleaning, coding and analysis, ensuring a systematic data management process throughout the review.

Study selection process

Before formal screening, the review team will pilot test the predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria on 10 randomly selected records, following Higgins et al.29 Calibration will continue until inter-rater reliability reaches κ≥0.80 for title/abstract screening and κ≥0.90 for full-text screening.31 The substantive screening process will occur in two stages using the Covidence systematic review software. In the first stage, two reviewers (GD and JG) will independently assess titles and abstracts against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles deemed potentially relevant will move to the second stage, where full texts will be reviewed in detail by two independent reviewers. Any disagreements at either stage will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will document the number of records at each stage and the reasons for exclusion.25

To be included, studies must be systematic reviews or meta-analyses that examine one or more interventions aimed at preventing teenage pregnancy, reducing repeat adolescent pregnancy or addressing the consequences of adolescent pregnancy. Eligible reviews must include populations of females aged 10–19 years or provide disaggregated data on adolescents. Reviews focusing on broader age groups will also be included if adolescent-specific outcomes are reported. No date restrictions will be applied to capture the full historical development of the evidence base. Systematic reviews must report transparent search methods, inclusion and exclusion criteria and syntheses of findings from included studies. Reviews will be excluded if they are non-systematic review types, focus solely on adult populations without adolescent data, are not peer-reviewed, or are published in languages other than English.

Consistent with scoping review methodology, formal quality assessment of individual systematic reviews will not be conducted, as the primary aim is to map the existing evidence rather than evaluate its quality.23 26 32 However, basic methodological characteristics of included reviews (eg, search comprehensiveness, use of quality assessment tools) will be documented to provide transparency about the nature of evidence being mapped.

Data extraction and charting

Following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a standardised data extraction form (see online supplemental file 2) will be pilot-tested on a random sample of five included systematic reviews.25 The form will undergo iterative refinement based on pilot results and team feedback until achieving >95% consistency in data extraction between reviewers.33 Key data fields include study characteristics, methodological characteristics (eg, databases searched, search time frame, use of quality appraisal tools), population variables, interventions, outcomes measured (primary and secondary), main findings and research gaps.

Data extraction will be conducted using Covidence. After the pilot, one reviewer will perform the initial data extraction, and a second reviewer will independently verify the accuracy and completeness of the extracted data. Discrepancies will be flagged in Covidence and resolved through team discussion and consensus, ensuring methodological transparency and rigour.32 This process will be fully documented, and any amendments to the extraction form during the review will be reported in the final publication to maintain auditability.

Data analysis and synthesis

Results will be presented according to the research objectives. Data will be presented through: (1) descriptive numerical summaries (eg, frequencies and percentages) of quantitative data; (2) tables outlining study and intervention characteristics; (3) visual mapping of the evidence base; and (4) narrative synthesis of qualitative data to highlight key themes, trends and evidence gaps. This approach is appropriate for the study’s aim of mapping the scope and nature of available evidence rather than testing hypotheses or estimating effect sizes. This analysis approach mirrors successful scoping reviews in the health field and aligns with recommendations from Pharm et al32 regarding comprehensive data presentation.

Data will be analysed and presented in alignment with the review’s objective to map the scope and nature of existing evidence. Covidence will be used to manage and organise extracted data, allowing for structured synthesis and efficient production of summary outputs. Quantitative data will be analysed using descriptive statistics, such as frequencies and percentages, to summarise intervention types, target populations, geographical distribution and outcome measures. Findings will be presented through tables outlining key characteristics of included reviews and interventions, alongside visual maps (eg, charts, heat maps) to display patterns and identify evidence gaps. Qualitative data will be synthesised narratively to highlight recurring themes, contextual factors and gaps in the literature. This mixed-methods approach ensures a comprehensive and accessible presentation of findings, consistent with PRISMA-ScR and best practices for scoping reviews.25 32

Discussion

This scoping review will contribute to theory by comprehensively mapping systematic review evidence on teenage pregnancy interventions, expected to reveal intervention complexity that supports ecological frameworks over single-component approaches.8 20 Given global patterns of research concentration in high-income countries despite the highest burden in low- and middle-income settings, the review will likely highlight critical research equity gaps and context-dependency issues.4 19 For policy, the resulting evidence map will provide the first comprehensive guide for resource allocation while identifying priority areas for targeted investment.

The anticipated findings will provide strong justification for a subsequent realist review to understand ‘what works, for whom, and under what circumstances’34 The expected intervention complexity and methodological heterogeneity across systematic reviews will provide rich material for developing programme theories about differential mechanisms across contexts. Evidence gaps regarding context-specific factors will align directly with the realist review’s strength in exploring how interventions work differently across varied settings, supporting more effective adaptation strategies.
