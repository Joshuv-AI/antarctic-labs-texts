Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is the most common malignant tumor of the renal pelvis, whereas neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) of the urinary tract account for less than 0.05% of primary urinary tract malignancies [1, 2]. Primary renal well‐differentiated NETs (WDNETs), historically termed renal carcinoid tumors, have been reported in the literature. Romero et al. reviewed 56 renal carcinoid tumors, and Zhang et al. noted more than 160 primary renal WDNETs worldwide by 2023 [3, 4]. Renal pelvic or peripelvic WDNETs, however, remain exceptional [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Renal pelvic masses with apparent cortical invasion are usually interpreted as UTUC; endoscopic assessment and cytology are considered key tools for confirming a urothelial origin. Submucosal or extraluminal tumors may leave the urothelium intact, leading to negative ureterorenoscopy and wash cytology despite a significant mass effect.

Case Presentation

A man in his 70s presented with two episodes of painless gross hematuria on a background of long‐standing lower urinary tract symptoms from benign prostatic hyperplasia. Laboratory tests showed a hemoglobin of 13.3 g/dL and serum creatinine of 0.89 mg/dL. Urinalysis did not demonstrate significant pyuria or proteinuria. Three‐phase computed tomography (CT) urography of the abdomen and pelvis (noncontrast, nephrographic phase, and delayed excretory phases) demonstrated a 31 mm enhancing soft‐tissue mass centered in the right renal pelvis region with apparent cortical invasion (Figure 1A,B). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis confirmed a right renal pelvic mass with diffusion restriction and heterogeneous enhancement without obvious hydroureter (Figure 1C–F). Tc‐99m DTPA renal scintigraphy showed preserved cortical perfusion and secretion bilaterally. The right kidney showed excretion and tracer stasis in the renal pelvis and upper calyx, compatible with nonobstructive hydronephrosis (Figure S1). The split renal function was 53.3% on the right and 46.7% on the left, with a total camera‐estimated glomerular filtration rate of 86.77 mL/min. Flexible ureterorenoscopy showed intact mucosa without visible papillary or sessile lesions. Renal pelvic wash cytology was negative for high‐grade urothelial carcinoma. Despite the negative ureterorenoscopy and cytology, a multidisciplinary uro‐oncology tumor board considered renal pelvic carcinoma with parenchymal invasion highly likely. The patient agreed to the procedure and underwent single‐port robot‐assisted right nephroureterectomy with bladder cuff excision.

Grossly, a well‐demarcated mass measuring 3.2 × 3.0 × 2.5 cm was located in the middle pole of the kidney. The tumor was confined to the kidney, with a sharp interface with the renal parenchyma, and the overlying renal pelvic mucosa was intact (Figure 2). Microscopically, the tumor showed a lobulated, well‐circumscribed proliferation of monotonous round‐to‐oval cells arranged in trabecular and ribbon‐like patterns with focal solid nests (Figure 3A,B). The cells had finely granular chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli. Mitotic figures were rare (< 2 per 10 high‐power fields). There was no necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, or perineural invasion.

The tumor measures 3.2 × 3.0 × 2.5 cm and is sharply circumscribed from the surrounding renal parenchyma, while the overlying renal pelvic mucosa remains grossly intact.

Histopathologic and immunohistochemical confirmation of a well‐differentiated renal neuroendocrine tumor. (A) Low‐power hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) section shows a lobulated, well‐circumscribed tumor beneath the renal pelvic mucosa/urothelium. (B) High‐power H&E demonstrates trabecular and ribbon‐like architecture composed of monotonous round‐to‐oval cells with finely granular chromatin and rare mitotic figures. (C) Immunohistochemistry shows diffuse synaptophysin positivity in tumor cells. (D) Ki‐67 immunostaining highlights focal hotspots up to 4.9%.

Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were diffusely positive for synaptophysin and negative for PAX8, CK7, WT1, and BRAF V600E. The Ki‐67 labeling index was up to 4.9% in focally increased hotspots (Figure 3C,D). Surgical margins were free of tumor. Overall, the findings were compatible with a well‐differentiated renal neuroendocrine tumor (carcinoid), staged as pT1a according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM classification [10]. At 10 months postoperatively, follow‐up MRI of the abdomen and pelvis showed postoperative changes after right nephroureterectomy without definite local recurrence, and standing posteroanterior chest radiography showed no radiographic evidence of pulmonary metastasis.

Discussion

Renal neuroendocrine neoplasms include several biologically distinct entities. Primary renal WDNETs/renal carcinoid tumors should be separated from renal pelvic or peripelvic WDNETs, poorly differentiated renal NECs, urothelial carcinomas with neuroendocrine differentiation, and metastatic NETs involving the kidney or renal pelvis [3, 4]. Among reported renal pelvic or peripelvic NETs, summarized with the present case in Table 1, most presented as solitary pelvic or ureteropelvic‐junction masses with collecting‐system dilatation and neuroendocrine‐marker positivity [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The present case appears distinctive because cross‐sectional imaging suggested a renal pelvic malignancy with apparent cortical invasion suspicious for invasive UTUC, whereas ureterorenoscopy showed intact urothelium and renal pelvic wash cytology was negative. Although these findings were discordant, concern for high‐risk UTUC led to nephroureterectomy, which is the standard treatment for high‐risk UTUC [11, 12].

Histologically, NETs typically show organoid, trabecular, or ribbon‐like arrangements of uniform cells with neuroendocrine‐marker expression [13, 14, 15]. The Ki‐67 labeling index in this case was up to 4.9%, which would fall within the G2 range if commonly used gastroenteropancreatic NET criteria were applied [15, 16]. However, such grading represents an extrapolation, because no kidney‐specific grading or prognostic system has been validated for primary renal WDNETs. Although the tumor was small, well circumscribed, confined to the kidney, and lacked necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, or perineural invasion, the prognostic significance of these features in renal WDNET remains uncertain. Late recurrence or metastasis has been reported even in apparently well‐differentiated renal NETs; therefore, the 10‐month follow‐up in the present case remains insufficient to support any conclusion regarding long‐term prognosis [3, 4, 10, 17, 18].

This case provides several practical learning points. First, when imaging suggests an invasive renal pelvic tumor but ureterorenoscopy and wash cytology are negative, the differential diagnosis should include submucosal NETs, paragangliomas, and other nonurothelial tumors. Second, it highlights the limitations and risks of preoperative biopsy in the renal sinus region, where percutaneous sampling may be technically challenging and associated with bleeding or tumor seeding [19, 20]. Third, surgery is appropriate for both UTUC and localized renal NET. Given the absence of renal pelvic NET‐specific treatment or surveillance guidelines, complete surgical resection remains the mainstay for localized renal WDNET, whereas adjuvant therapies are generally reserved for high‐grade or metastatic disease [4, 17, 18]. Our patient is being monitored with periodic cross‐sectional imaging and laboratory tests, and longer follow‐up is needed to confirm durable disease control.

Conclusion

The differential diagnosis of a renal pelvic mass should include submucosal or nonurothelial tumors, particularly when endoscopic evaluation shows intact urothelium and cytology is negative.
