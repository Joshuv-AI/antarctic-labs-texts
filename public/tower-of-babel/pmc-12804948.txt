Defect dipole alignment mediated by poling for simultaneous optimization of piezoelectricity and carrier concentration in piezocatalysts
Authors: Xiaoli Xu, Ying Wang, Wanwan Cheng, Huan Zhai, Lingbo Xiao, Laishun Qin, Hamideh Khanbareh, Chris Bowen, Yanmin Jia, Zhenhai Wen, Da Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPiezoelectric materials with high piezoelectric coefficients often exhibit low carrier concentrations, which hinders the generation of free charges for piezocatalytic processes. To address this, defect engineering has emerged as a promising approach to adjust the electronic structure and enhance the carrier concentration of piezocatalytic materials. However, the introduction of defects can negatively affect the piezoelectric performance by increasing charge screening and reducing domain wall mobility. In this work, we demonstrate that a high carrier concentration can be achieved without compromising the piezoelectric properties, by applying an external electric field to enhance polarization and rearrange oxygen defect dipoles. This poling process significantly improves both piezoelectric and piezocatalytic properties. Specifically, the effective piezoelectric coefficient of a polarised, oxygen-deficient Bi2WO6-x material increased from d*33 ~ 4.79 pm V‒1 to d*33 ~ 39.89 pm V‒1. Moreover, its carrier concentration remained high, approaching Nd ~ 7.39×1020 cm‒3, when subjected to ultrasonic vibrations. As a result of the high piezoelectric activity and carrier concentration, the piezocatalytic hydrogen production efficiency (per unit power) attained ~3.49 μmol g−1 h−1 W−1. This work enables the design of efficient and energy-saving piezocatalytic materials, providing a significant step forward for hydrogen production via piezocatalytic water splitting. Subject terms: Hydrogen energy, Catalysis

Introduction

Hydrogen, as a renewable energy source, has a series of advantages such as clean and efficient, green and low-carbon, and convenient storage, which is gradually becoming one of the important carriers of global energy transformation and development1–3. As an innovative catalytic technology, the process of piezocatalysis integrates the piezoelectric effect with catalytic reactions, thereby breaking the barrier between mechanical energy and chemical energy. Piezocatalysis therefore provides a route to convert clean and abundant mechanical energy into chemical energy, providing significant development potential4–8. By exploiting ambient sources of vibrational and mechanical energy, the process can not only reduce energy-conversion costs, but could also alleviate our reliance on fossil fuels, embodying the principles of cleanliness, sustainability, and environmental friendliness. To promote highly efficient catalytic reactions, there is a need for both a high carrier concentration (Nd) and efficient methods of carrier separation. While many conventional piezoelectric materials exhibit high piezoelectric coefficients, their low intrinsic number of carriers (typically in the range of Nd = 1017~1019 cm−3)9–12 limits the piezocatalytic activity10,13–16.

Defect engineering is a common approach that is employed to manipulate the electronic structure and properties of advanced materials, thereby effectively enhancing Nd17–20. As an example, Liu et al. investigated the role of oxygen vacancies (OV) for piezocatalysis by studying (Bi1/2Na1/2)TiO3 (BNT) material system. Their work found that an increased oxygen vacancies concentration improved the adsorption of O2 and OH– on the surface of BNT grains, thereby enhancing the charge concentration and facilitating charge transfer from BNT to O2 and OH–. This led to an enhanced piezocatalytic activity through active radical generation20. Dai et al. showed that controlling oxygen vacancies in Sr0.5Ba0.5Nb2O6/Sr2Nb2O7 nanocomposites provided a mechanism to improve the piezocatalytic activity during the application of vibrations by promoting free carrier generation and separation21. However, focusing exclusively on increasing the carrier concentration (Nd) can lead to detrimental outcomes. Firstly, the existence of oxygen vacancies can increase the aging rate of the piezoelectric activity and decrease domain mobility due to domain wall pinning effects, both of which weaken the piezoelectric response20. Secondly, the introduction of oxygen vacancies to increase Nd can enhance the level of screening in a polar material that hinders any polarization reversal behavior, thereby reducing the piezoelectric performance of materials22,23. Therefore, developing new methods to achieve a sufficiently large Nd without compromising the piezoelectric performance will benefit the creating of materials with enhanced piezocatalytic performance.

It has been observed that the electric field and polarization due to the presence of defects, and their directional rearrangement, is an effective approach to improve the piezoelectric properties of materials. This approach has been demonstrated in 2D thin film materials; for example, Park et al. recently induced a large and persistent piezoelectric effect in Gd-doped CeO2 films by rearrangement of oxygen vacancies using an externally applied electric field24. During sintering of Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3-based ceramics, oxygen vacancies compensate for Na vacancies as a result of Bi volatilization. The resulting Na vacancies and oxygen vacancies form defect dipoles with a polarization Pd that align with, or near, the spontaneous polarization (Ps) of the material due to symmetry25. For example, in copper oxide-doped potassium sodium niobate ceramics, Dai et al. used a strategy of polarization-aging-repolarization to align defect dipoles by applying an electric field to pole the material, demonstrating their positive impact on piezoelectric performance26.

In this work, a classical piezoelectric material Bi2WO6 (BWO) was selected as the model material due to its layered perovskite structure, which facilitates both carrier transport, and high piezoelectric performance27–29. An oxygen-deficient Bi2WO6-x (BWO-OV) was prepared by a solvothermal method (Supplementary Fig. S1a–d) and by carefully controlling the intensity and time of an externally applied electric field to achieve polarization, the influence of oxygen vacancies and defect dipole arrangement on piezocatalytic performance of BWO-OV was investigated in detail (Fig. 1a). The effective piezoelectric coefficient of the poled BWO-OV was shown to increase from d*33 ~ 4.79 pm V‒1 to d*33 ~ 39.89 pm V‒1. Moreover, its carrier concentration can remain high, where Nd ~ 7.39 × 1020 cm‒3 when subjected to ultrasonic vibrations. The piezocatalytic hydrogen production efficiency (per unit power) was able to achieve a high value of ~3.49 μmol g−1 h−1 W−1. This work provides new insights into the design and development of efficient and energy-saving piezocatalytic materials, which has applications in important areas, such as piezocatalytic water splitting for hydrogen production.

Results

Material characterization

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) is a powerful technique for characterizing unpaired electrons and is used to investigate the formation and evolution of oxygen vacancies (OV)30. As depicted in Fig. 1b, the pristine BWO sample exhibits a negligible EPR signal. In contrast, the oxygen-deficient BWO-OV sample displays a distinct and symmetric signal at g = 2.003. This signal is widely attributed to unpaired electrons trapped at ionized oxygen vacancies in various oxide semiconductors19, including bismuth- and tungsten-based materials30–32. Notably, after the polarization treatment (samples BWO-P and BWO-OV-P), the EPR signal for BWO-OV-P remains essentially unchanged in both position and intensity. This indicates that the applied electric field, which aligns the ferroelectric domains, does not alter the concentration or charge state of the pre-existing oxygen vacancies, confirming the stability of these defects during the poling process. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, shown in Fig. 1c, provide data to analyze the crystal phase structures of the BWO and BWO-OV samples, before and after polarization. All samples exhibit diffraction peaks at 28.01°, 32.73°, 47.06°, 55.90°, 58.55°, 68.82°, 75.94°, 78.41°, and 87.63°, which correspond to the (131), (200), (202), (133), (262), (004), (333), (460), and (462) planes of BWO (PDF#79-2381), with no impurity peaks detected29. Compared to oxygen-deficient BWO-OV, the intensity of the main diffraction peaks in the oxygen-rich BWO significantly increased, which is attributed to enhanced crystallinity due to high-temperature annealing. It can be seen from the enlarged details of the (131) plane in Fig. 1c that the 2θ of BWO-OV is smaller than that of BWO due to expansion of the lattice spacing as a result of the presence of oxygen vacancies33. After a polarization treatment, the 2θ of the (131) plane for BWO-OV-P slightly increases, which may be due to the rearrangement of oxygen vacancies under the action of the electric field, thereby resulting in a decrease in the lattice distortion of the material.

The high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) images provide microstructural details of the materials, see Fig. 1d–g. The lattice spacing of (131) crystallographic planes of BWO and BWO-OV are 0.319 nm and 0.333 nm, respectively. Internal defects, as a result of the introduction of oxygen vacancies in BWO-OV, lead to lattice distortion of structure and a corresponding increase in the lattice spacing of its (131) planes34. After the polarization treatment, the lattice spacing of the (131) crystal plane of BWO-OV-P slightly decreases, which is in agreement with the XRD results (Fig. 1c). In addition, the specific surface area (SBET, BET: Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) of the poled BWO-OV-P materials increases from 84.68 to 89.95 m² g–1 (a 6.22% increase), while the SBET of BWO-P remains almost unchanged (Fig. 2a–c). The application of a polarization treatment can change the specific surface area of materials, and its effect varies depending on the particle size of the materials, where the smaller the particle size, the greater the effect of a polarization treatment35.

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectra clearly demonstrate the existence of Bi, W, and O elements in all materials (Fig. 2d), where the binding energies were calibrated using the binding energy of C 1s. Both Bi 4f and W 4f spectrums exhibit two peaks, resulting from the levels related to Bi 4f7/2, Bi 4 f5/2 in the Bi3+ chemical state and W 4f7/2, W 4f5/2 in the W6+ chemical state spin–orbit splitting, respectively (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Figs. S2). A more pronounced shift toward larger binding energies is observed in the high-resolution spectra of Bi 4f, W 4f, and O 1s core levels for the poled materials, BWO-P and BWO-OV-P, in comparison with unpoled BWO and BWO-OV (Fig. 2e–h and Supplementary Fig. S2). This shift serves as a clear indication of the changes in the coordination environments of Bi, W, and O atoms residing within the sub-surface layers of the poled BWO-P and BWO-OV-P. The binding energy differences of each element in BWO and BWO-OV before and after poling were calculated (Fig. 2i), where it was observed that after subjecting the materials to a poling treatment, the binding energy differences of all elements between BWO-OV-P and BWO-OV are greater than those between BWO-P and BWO. The oxygen vacancies in BWO-OV are therefore assumed to be rearranged after the polarization treatment, resulting in a reduction in the lattice distortion and lattice spacing, thereby strengthening the interaction between electrons and atomic nuclei, and thereby increasing the binding energy.

Piezoelectric properties

The piezoelectric activity of the BWO and BWO-OV samples before and after polarization were characterized by using piezo-response force microscope (PFM). The amplitude and phase images indicate the presence of clear contrasts for BWO, as shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S3a, with a corresponding average amplitude value of ~300 pm (Fig. 3e) and a maximum phase angle range of 100~120° (Supplementary Fig. S4). After polarization, the BWO-P material exhibits a more pronounced amplitude contrast and phase change (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. S3b), with a higher average amplitude of ~ 350–400 pm (Fig. 3f) and a maximum phase angle distributed over 100~140° (Supplementary Fig. S4). The poling process has therefore effectively enhanced the piezoelectric response of BWO-P by providing a higher degree of dipole alignment36. Compared to BWO, the unpoled oxygen-deficient BWO-OV material exhibits a reduced piezoelectric response compared to BWO (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. S3c), with average amplitude of ~150–200 pm (Fig. 3g) and a maximum phase angle in the range of 60 ~ 100° (Supplementary Fig. S4). This is attributed to the enhanced charge screening effect and reduced domain wall mobility due to the presence of additional oxygen vacancies19. However, after polarization, the BWO-OV-P material demonstrates a significantly enhanced amplitude contrast and a more distinct phase change (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. S3d), with a large average amplitude value of ~500–550 pm (Fig. 3h) and a maximum phase angle distributed between 140 and 160° (Supplementary Fig. S4). A sharp histogram indicates a highly ordered polarization direction, whereas a broad and flat histogram suggests a more disordered state37.

The effective piezoelectric coefficient (d*33) reflects the change in piezoelectric activity quantitatively, as shown in Fig. 3i, there is a change in the amplitude of the BWO-OV and BWO-OV-P materials with an increase of the driving voltage. Based on the linear fitting results, the piezoelectric coefficient of BWO-OV-P is approximately d*33 ~ 39.89 pm V–1, which is more than 8 times that of BWO-OV (d*33 ~ 4.79 pm V–1). The BWO-OV and BWO-OV-P materials exhibit characteristic amplitude-voltage butterfly loops and distinct 180° phase change hysteresis loops under a 10 V reversal field (Supplementary Fig. S5a, b). The maximum amplitudes, indicating enhanced piezoelectric performance due to polarization, are 180.7 pm for unpoled BWO-OV and 383.9 pm for poled BWO-OV-P.

As shown in Fig. 3j, the changes of ferroelectric domains and defect dipoles, Pd, in BWO-OV due to polarization are presented. In accordance with (I), a random distribution of electric domains can be initially observed in the BWO material. Due to the principle of symmetry-consistency of defect dipoles, the presence of defect dipoles and associated Pd can also align along the direction of Ps, or near the Ps direction, in order to minimize the electrostatic energy25. In the oxygen vacancy rich BWO-OV material, both Ps and Pd are randomly oriented when no external polarization field has been applied (Ⅱ). After a polarization treatment, domains will tend to switch and become more aligned with the applied electric field, and both Ps and Pd are aligned parallel to the externally applied electric field, which provides a positive contribution to the piezoelectric response and resulting piezoelectric coefficients.

When the materials are subjected to ultrasonic vibrations, the Nd is increased to varying degrees depending on the carrier concentration and polarization. The Nd for poled oxygen-deficient BWO-OV-P increased from initially Nd ~ 3.93 × 1020 cm3 with no vibration, to Nd ~ 7.39 × 1020 cm3 with vibrations, which is an increase that is more than two times larger than BWO-OV (where ΔNd before and after vibration is 1.54 × 1020 cm3, see Table 1). This large increase in carrier concentration is due to the enhanced piezoelectric response of poled BWO-OV-P, compared to unpled BWO-OV, which increases the number of carriers generated when the material is subject to vibrations.

The EIS results show that the poled BWO-OV-P material has a smaller semicircular Nyquist plot radius than those of BWO, BWO-P, BWO-OV, indicating a lower electron transfer resistance (Fig. 4e)40–42. The piezo-current density results indicate that all BWO samples exhibit sensitive piezoelectric response under vibration, and when the vibration is turned off, the piezo-current density decreases sharply. Compared to BWO, the piezo-current density of BWO-OV increases because of improved carrier separation and transfer due to the introduction of oxygen vacancies19. After polarization, BWO-OV-P provides a stronger driving force for charge separation, thereby exhibiting the largest piezoelectric response (Fig. 4f).

Piezocatalytic performance and mechanism exploration

Experiments to evaluate water splitting for hydrogen production were conducted to investigate the piezocatalytic activity of the materials, see Fig. 5a–f. The hydrogen production of the BWO-based samples exhibited a linear tendency with time during the application of mechanical vibrations (Fig. 5a). By introducing oxygen vacancies, the resulting BWO-OV materials possessed enhanced catalytic activity compared with the initial BWO material. After a polarization treatment, the hydrogen production of poled oxygen-deficient BWO-OV-P was further improved (~87.5 μmol g‒1 h‒1, Fig. 5a). This increase in catalytic activity can be attributed to the enhanced piezoelectric properties and carrier concentration of BWO-OV after polarization, as observed in Figs. 3 and 4. The specific hydrogen evolution rates are summarized in Fig. 5b, which shows that after the polarization treatment, the piezocatalytic efficiencies of both BWO and BWO-OV have been improved to varying degrees, with the efficiency of BWO-OV increasing by 43.0% after polarization; this is more than twice the value of the improvement of BWO (+19.8%) after polarization.

To further optimize the effect of a polarization treatment, we regulated the polarization temperature and polarization voltage. The average hydrogen production rate of BWO-OV-P for a polarization temperature of 100 °C reached ~200.3 μmol g‒1 h‒1, which is nearly twice the rate of hydrogen generation when poled at 25 °C (~93.5 μmol g‒1 h‒1); see Fig. 5c, d. In addition, the average hydrogen production rate for BWO-OV-P also increases with an increase in poling voltage (5, 8, and 10 kV); see Fig. 5e, f. On increasing the polarization temperature and voltage, the defect mobility in the BWO-OV-P material accelerates, and the degree of domain transition is enhanced43,44. This alignment of defective dipoles, in turn, enhanced the piezoelectric performance, thereby accelerating the catalytic reactions. To verify the universality of the polarization-assisted ordered construction of oxygen vacancies and their enhanced piezocatalytic effects, oxygen-rich Bi2WO6 (BWO-2) was synthesized using a hydrothermal method. Oxygen vacancies were introduced through hydrogenation treatment to obtain oxygen-deficient Bi2WO6-x material (BWO-2-OV), and polarization was applied using an externally applied electric field (BWO-2-OV-P). As indicated in Fig. 5g, the piezocatalytic activity of BWO-2-OV was increased from ~155.3 μmol g–1 h–1 to ~240.3 μmol g–1 h–1, and the average H2 production efficiency of BWO-2-OV-P after polarization reached ~349.2 μmol g–1 h–1. Taking into account the influence of power on the piezocatalytic performance, the H2 evolution rate per watt in this work is calculated. A detailed comparison can be seen in Fig. 5h and Supplementary Table S1, which is superior to the vast majority of piezocatalysts reported to date19,21,29,45–54.

A schematic of hydrogen production mechanism can be seen in Fig. 5i. Through the intercept of the horizontal coordinate, the energy of flat band (Ef) of BWO can result in Mott-Schottky curves (Fig. 4c, d). The measured Ef of BWO-OV and BWO-OV-P are −0.91 V vs. SCE. In addition, the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) potential (Ef, RHE) can be measured via the Nernst formula 255,

where the pH value of the Na2SO4 electrolyte is 6.8. The calculated Ef, RHE of BWO-OV and BWO-OV-P are −0.27 V vs. RHE (pH = 6.8). The positive slopes of the Mott-Schottky curves indicate the n-type semiconducting nature of the BWO samples56,57. Usually, the Ef, RHE is ~0.3 V lower than the minimum conduction band (ECB; CB: conduction band) of typical n-type semiconductors58. Hence, the ECB of BWO-OV and BWO-OV-P are −0.57 V. Moreover, the CB level of H+/H2 (pH = 7.0) is ~–0.01 V vs. RHE (pH = 6.8), which is suitable to achieve H+ reduction into H2. Simultaneously, according to the bandgap (Eg), as shown in Supplementary Fig. S6, the valence band potentials (EVB) of BWO-OV and BWO-OV-P can be calculated as 2.28 and 2.51 V. Based on electrostatic interaction, the electric field can regulate the concentration and distribution of charged defects in piezoelectric materials59,60. When piezoelectric effect and lattice vibration-induced polarization fields are activated, the redistribution of electronic potentials induces pronounced band bending61. A directional rearrangement of defect dipoles, Pd, by the electric field and its associated polarization is shown to be an effective way to improve the piezoelectric properties of materials, leading to an enhanced potential (V) difference due to band tilting (ΔV2 > ΔV1) (Fig. 5i)2. Ultimately, the stronger driving force for charge separation in BWO-OV-P (Eq. (3)) generates a higher carrier concentration (Nd) under mechanical vibration, thereby enhancing its hydrogen production capability. Ultrasonic vibration induces the generation and migration of piezoelectric charges to opposite surfaces, creating a polarized electric field that dissociates water molecules into hydrogen ions (H+) and hydroxide ions (OH−) (Eq. (4)). Subsequently, the H+ ions are adsorbed onto the active sites of the catalyst and reduced by the piezo-induced charges (q−) to ultimately form H2 (Eq. (5)).

By acting as a sacrificial agent and consuming piezo-induced q+ to form H+ and hydroxyl alkyl radical intermediate (•CH2OH)57, methanol not only suppresses carrier recombination but also liberates more piezo-induced q−; consequently, more of these unrecombined negative charges react with H+ to produce hydrogen, synergistically enhancing the catalytic efficiency.

Sometimes, the formation of oxygen defect dipoles and the domain structure of the material affect each other, which influences the ferroelectric properties and piezoresponse of defect-containing materials in positive or negative way25,62–65. Apte et al. reported a surprisingly strong piezoelectric response (d33 ≈ 0.56 pm V⁻1) in centrosymmetric 2D MoO2 single crystals, which is comparable to traditional 2D piezoelectrics. They proposed that this apparent piezoelectricity originates from a dielectret state formed by charge trapping at defects and voids during high-temperature synthesis. This effect can be subsequently removed by high-temperature annealing66. In addition, several alternative piezocatalytic approaches and associated opportunities have emerged, encompassing diverse piezoelectric materials (such as flexible piezoelectric materials67, advanced structural designs (including composite materials68–70, and hybrid coupling strategies (such as synchronous multisource energy conversion71. These approaches offer promising application prospects across energy, environmental, and healthcare sectors.

Discussion

This paper demonstrates that the piezocatalytic water splitting efficiency of Bi2WO6 (BWO) is greatly enhanced by combining oxygen vacancy concentration regulation with an externally applied electric field to enhance the polarization. This strategy can provide, and maintain, both a high carrier concentration and a strong piezoelectric polarization field in the BWO material. The application of an electric field to enhance the polarization and produce an ordered arrangement of oxygen defect dipoles is able to contribute and increase piezoelectric activity of BWO. The effective piezoelectric coefficient of a polarised and oxygen-deficient Bi2WO6-x (BWO-OV-P) reaches a high value of d*33 ~ 39.89 pm V‒1, which is more than 8 times higher than the unpoled oxygen-deficient material (BWO-OV). Using Mott-Schottky characterization diagrams, the carrier concentration of BWO-OV-P when subject to vibrations is shown to reach Nd ~ 7.39 × 1020 cm‒3. As a result, the optimal piezocatalytic hydrogen production efficiency improves to ~3.49 μmol g–1 h–1 W–1. This work has significance to provide new approach for the search and design of efficient piezocatalytic materials, and contributes to the development of clean catalytic processes, including hydrogen production.

Methods

Chemical reagents

All materials and solvents were of analytical grade and without any further purification. Sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4 ∙ 2H2O, ≥99.5%), ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥99.7%) and ethylene glycol (C2H6O2, ≥98.0%) were supplied from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Bismuth (III) nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3 ∙ 5H2O, ≥99.0%), was purchased by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Lid. Anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, ≥99.0%) was acquired from Hangzhou Gaojing Fine Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. Deionized water was procured from Meek Chemical Co., Ltd., and Methanol (MeOH, ≥99.5%) was obtained from Aladdin.

Material synthesis

Solvothermal method: The oxygen-deficient Bi2WO6-x (BWO-OV; BWO is short for Bi2WO6; OV is short for oxygen vacancies) materials were fabricated through a one-step ethylene glycol assisted solvothermal method19. In essence, 3.2 mmol of Na2WO4 ∙ 2H2O and 6.4 mmol of Bi(NO3)3 ∙ 5H2O were separately dissolved in an ethylene glycol solution with a volume ratio of 3:7, which is denoted as Solution A and B, respectively. Solution A was slowly dropped into Solution B and stirred on a magnetic mixer for 1 h at room temperature and then transferred to 200 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclaves, which was maintained at 160 °C for 20 h. The obtained products were collected by centrifugation and washed three times using deionized water and ethanol and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the oxygen-deficient BWO-OV sample was placed in muffle furnace at 400 °C for 4 h in an air rich atmosphere to obtain oxygen vacancy-free Bi2WO6 (BWO).

Hydrothermal method: 1.00 mmol of Na2WO4 ∙ 2H2O and 0.823 mmol of C19H42BrN were dissolved in 50 mL deionized water, then 1.99 mmol of Bi(NO3)3 ∙ 5H2O was added to the above solution. The mixed solution was filled with deionized water up to 2/3 of the volume of 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclaves, which was maintained at 160 °C for 24 h. The obtained products are designated as BWO-2. An ark containing the BWO-2 sample was placed in a tube furnace, on purging the inside with Ar gas for about 20 min to expel the air. Then, a 5% H2/Ar mixed gas was introduced and hydrogenated at 500 °C for 100 min (heating rate ~5 °C/min), and finally bismuth tungstate samples containing oxygen vacancies were obtained (denoted as BWO-2-OV).

Polarization process

Powder samples were poled using a corona high voltage polarimeter (SPD20KVCP, China). A known amount of powder (typically 1 g) was taken and evenly place between two copper plates with a diameter of 4 cm as electrodes, the powder samples were dried naturally at room temperature. Then, using a high-voltage direct current polarization device, the powder samples were poled using a poling voltage of 5, 8, 10 kV at 25 °C or 100 °C for 40 min in air, respectively. Therefore, poled BWO and poled BWO-OV are named with BWO-P and BWO-OV-P, respectively (P is short for polarization).

Material characterization

XRD (Shimadzu Smart Lab-SE, Japan) data was collected with Cu Kα (λ = 0.15406 nm) radiation at the scanning rate of 10° min−1. The surface morphology of the as-synthesized samples is investigated using SEM (ZEISS Sigma 300, German). HRTEM (JEOL JEM-2100Plus, Japan) images was used to analyze the growth direction of the nanostructures, primarily to observe the morphology, grain size and growth direction of products. XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) spectra under Al-Kα (hv = 1486.6 eV) irradiation are recorded. UV-vis DRS (Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus, Japan) was performed within the range of 200~800 nm. The bandgap widths were calculated from Tauc plots, where the (ahv)2-(hv) plot which provides a linear absorption edge, and the intercept of the linear absorption edge that extends in the reverse direction on the x-axis is the band gap. The chemical composition, state, vibration and other information of the materials were obtained by the Raman spectrometer (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution, Japan) using a He-Ne laser with a wavelength of 663 nm. EPR (Bruker EMXplus-6/1, German) spectroscopy of the samples is conducted to monitor the signal of oxygen vacancies. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms (Micromeritics ASAP 2460, China) was measured on a gas adsorption analyzer at 77.3 K. The SBET was calculated by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method. Piezo-response amplitude butterfly loops were used to identify the piezoelectric properties. PFM amplitude butterfly loops, topography, amplitude, phase diagrams were carried out by AFM (Dimension Icon, German); the sample powder was pressed into tablets for PFM testing.

Electrochemical measurements

A mass of 10 mg of catalyst was prepared separately and firstly uniformly mixed with 20 μL ethanol, then 20 μL Nafion. The paste was then uniformly coated onto one side of an indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrate, with a coating area of 1 cm2. Mott-Schottky, EIS Nyquist and piezo-current plots of samples were then measured with an electrochemical system (CHI-660E, China) using a three-electrode system. A saturated calomel electrode (SCE) was utilized as a reference electrode, and a 2 × 2 cm2 platinum net served as a counter electrode. ITO glass with the piezocatalysts acted as a working electrode, and the electrolyte solution used was a 0.5 M Na₂SO4. Mott-Schottky plots were measured at a frequency of 1 kHz with/without vibration conditions. Vibration was performed using a KQ-100VDV ultrasonic machine operating at 100 W and 45 kHz. The EIS measurements were performed over a frequency range of 0.1~100 kHz, with an AC amplitude of 5 mV and a DC bias voltage of 1.2 V (vs. SCE) without vibration. During the piezo-current measurement, a double-sided conductive copper adhesive was used between the ITO glass and the samples to strengthen the adhesion. No external voltage was applied.

Evaluation of piezocatalytic performance

The experimental schematic of the piezocatalytic water splitting for hydrogen generation and testing have been shown in Supplementary Fig. S7. The piezocatalytic water splitting performance for hydrogen production was conducted through an ultrasonic system (KQ-100VDV, 100 W, 45 kHz). The specific experimental process was as follows: a mass of 20 mg of a sample were separately added into 13.5 mL deionized water and 1.5 mL MeOH, then placed in the reaction liquid in glass bottles. In order to eliminate the effect of air in the glass bottles on piezocatalytic activity, Ar gas was ventilated for 10 min and then sealed. Subsequently, the sealed bottle was placed in an ultrasonic machine in the dark, and the system temperature was maintained at ~25 °C. Hydrogen production was measured via off-line sampling using a syringe connected to the Gas Chromatograph Analysis Instrument (GC-7820, China). Based on the standard gas data, the peak area for hydrogen was converted into total hydrogen production. The comparative experiment of piezoelectric hydrogen production for each group of samples was repeated at least three times to minimize experimental errors.
