Exploring barriers and facilitators to fall prevention among older adults in Saudi Arabia: a qualitative study
Authors: Neah Albasha, Shahad Alruwaythi, Layan AlDubayyan, Abeer Alasmari, Wassan Almutairi, Mayar Al-Juaid, Reem M AlWhaibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are a leading cause of injury, disability, and hospitalization among older adults in Saudi Arabia. Despite international advances in fall prevention, local barriers, particularly those related to culture, education, and resource availability, remain insufficiently explored.ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the key barriers and facilitators to fall prevention among adults aged 65 and older in Saudi Arabia, and to examine how cultural, educational, and socioeconomic factors influence implementation efforts.MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was employed using semi-structured interviews with 16 healthcare professionals, including nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, and general practitioners, recruited from governmental and private hospitals. Interviews were conducted in Arabic, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. Themes were mapped to the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) to ensure theoretical rigor and contextual depth.ResultsParticipants identified multiple barriers, including patient and caregiver unawareness, cultural stigmas around assistive device use, high workloads, inadequate staff training, and poorly designed environments. Facilitators included comprehensive assessments, tailored education programs, strong family engagement, interdisciplinary teamwork, and availability of assistive technologies. An emergent theme, “person-centered care,” emphasized the need for individualized, flexible strategies beyond the TDF structure.ConclusionEffective fall prevention in Saudi Arabia requires culturally sensitive, system-wide strategies that address both structural constraints and individual-level beliefs. By enhancing interdisciplinary coordination, improving education, and prioritizing patient-centered approaches, healthcare systems can better support safe aging and reduce fall-related risks among older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06884-8.Keywords: Fall prevention, Older adults, Healthcare staff, Healthcare system, Community setting, Patient safety, Socioeconomic, Barriers, Facilitators

Introduction

Background

Falls represent a leading cause of injury, disability, hospitalization, and mortality among adults aged 65 years and older, posing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and communities worldwide. According to recent data from the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), approximately 30% of older adults experience at least one fall annually, resulting in millions of emergency department visits and significant economic costs [9, 10, 35]. In the United States alone, fall-related healthcare costs exceed USD 50 billion per year [6]. Beyond physical injuries such as fractures and head trauma, falls can result in long-term functional decline, loss of independence, and diminished quality of life [31]. Despite the extensive epidemiological evidence available, the majority of existing research has been quantitative, leaving important experiential and contextual dimensions of fall risk and prevention under-investigated. Qualitative inquiries remain scarce, particularly in non-Western and Middle-Eastern settings, where cultural norms and healthcare practices may shape prevention behaviors differently [20, 33].

Falls are rarely caused by a single factor. Instead, they typically result from a combination of intrinsic factors (e.g., chronic conditions, cognitive impairment, balance deficits, polypharmacy) and extrinsic factors (e.g., poor lighting, uneven surfaces, slippery floors, inappropriate footwear) [15]. Environmental hazards, coupled with reduced physiological reserves in older adults, amplify the risk. Consequently, interventions targeting multiple modifiable risk factors have demonstrated greater effectiveness than single-strategy approaches [12, 17, 22]. However, little is known about how healthcare professionals perceive and operationalize these complex risk factors within their own clinical and cultural contexts.

A growing body of literature supports various fall prevention interventions in community-dwelling older adults. These include strength and balance training (e.g., Tai Chi, Otago Exercise Program), medication reviews, environmental modifications, and vision correction. A recent systematic review and network meta-analysis classified fall prevention strategies into single, multi-component, and multifactorial interventions [14]. Single interventions such as structured exercise are highly effective in reducing fall rates. Multi-component interventions that combine exercise, assistive technology, and environmental adaptations also demonstrate strong efficacy. Multifactorial interventions, tailored to individual risk profiles, show moderate success in reducing fall incidence, although their impact on reducing the number of fallers is less consistent [19]. While these findings demonstrate “what works,” there remains limited qualitative understanding of how and why implementation succeeds or fails across settings, underscoring the need for context-specific behavioral frameworks.

Despite strong evidence for fall prevention efficacy, translating these interventions into practice, particularly in resource-limited settings, remains challenging. Common barriers include insufficient training of healthcare providers, lack of interdisciplinary collaboration, high staff workloads, and limited access to assistive devices [25, 32]. Fragmented care systems and poor continuity of care further complicate implementation, especially in rapidly aging populations. These challenges highlight the necessity of exploring behavioral and organizational determinants of implementation, which are often overlooked in quantitative designs.

Fall prevention in the Saudi context

Saudi Arabia faces unique challenges in addressing fall prevention due to its rapidly aging population, increasing prevalence of chronic diseases, and healthcare system transitions. Cultural beliefs, such as viewing aging and falling as inevitable or stigmatizing the use of assistive devices, may reduce adherence to preventive strategies [1, 2]. Additionally, socioeconomic disparities and low health literacy among older adults and caregivers pose significant barriers to the uptake of fall prevention services. Healthcare staff report limited training in geriatric rehabilitation and fall risk management, highlighting a critical implementation gap [3]. However, few Saudi studies have adopted qualitative or theory-driven approaches to examine how cultural expectations, family caregiving roles, and professional practices interact to influence fall-prevention efforts.

Theoretical foundation and role of healthcare professionals

Building on the limited qualitative evidence identified in previous sections, the present study adopts a theory-informed approach to examine how behavioral, organizational, and cultural factors shape fall-prevention practices in Saudi Arabia. Healthcare professionals, including physical therapists, occupational therapists, nurses, and physicians, play a pivotal role in delivering fall prevention programs. However, their ability to implement evidence-based strategies is influenced by organizational, contextual, and behavioral factors. Key staff-related barriers include inadequate geriatric education during professional training, lack of institutional policies supporting fall risk assessments, and time constraints that limit comprehensive patient evaluations [3–5]. Conversely, facilitators such as ongoing professional development, interdisciplinary teamwork, and standardized assessment tools (e.g., Timed Up and Go (TUG), Berg Balance Scale) have been associated with improved fall prevention outcomes [25]. The engagement of family members and caregivers further strengthens adherence, particularly when educational efforts are tailored to the cognitive and physical needs of the older adult.

To address the behavioral and contextual determinants influencing these practices, this study adopts the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [7], a validated synthesis of 33 behavior-change constructs across 14 domains. The TDF provides a systematic lens to explore how knowledge, skills, social influences, and environmental resources interact to shape clinical behavior and decision-making. Applying the TDF in the Saudi context enables identification of the specific barriers and facilitators that determine whether evidence-based fall-prevention strategies are successfully implemented.

While global evidence on fall prevention is abundant, few studies have specifically explored the cultural, educational, and systemic influences on fall prevention efforts within the Saudi healthcare system. This study therefore addresses a clear gap by qualitatively examining healthcare professionals’ perspectives on the behavioral, organizational, and cultural factors shaping fall-prevention practices.

Specifically, the study aims to identify key individual, institutional, and systemic barriers; explore facilitators proposed by healthcare professionals; and examine how cultural norms, education, and socioeconomic status influence implementation [1, 21].

By addressing these objectives, the study contributes to bridging the gap between evidence-based recommendations and real-world practice and supports the development of targeted, context-sensitive interventions tailored to the Saudi healthcare landscape.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design to explore healthcare professionals’ perceptions of barriers and facilitators to fall prevention among older adults in Saudi Arabia. Semi-structured interviews were selected for their flexibility and capacity to generate rich, contextual insights. This design was particularly suitable for understanding behavioral and organizational factors that influence fall prevention. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines to ensure transparency, credibility, and rigor. Data were collected over a four-month period, and ethical approval was obtained from the Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University Institutional Review Board (PNU-IRB #24–0943).

Participants

Eligible participants were licensed healthcare professionals (e.g., physical therapists, occupational therapists, nurses, and general practitioners) aged 21 years and older, with a minimum of three months of direct experience providing care to adults aged 65 years and older. Participants were recruited from both governmental and private hospitals and were required to be fluent in Arabic, as interviews were conducted in that language. Individuals on extended leave or under the age of 21were excluded.

Sampling and recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to ensure the inclusion of diverse healthcare professionals with direct experience in fall prevention. Recruitment was conducted over an eight-week period via targeted outreach to hospitals and direct professional invitations. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new insights or themes emerged from the interviews.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through semi-structured, one-on-one interviews conducted online via Microsoft Teams. The interview guide included eight primary questions and relevant probes, addressing participants’ perceptions of fall risks, barriers to implementation, facilitators, and proposed solutions. Prior to each interview, participants received a clear explanation of the study aims and provided informed consent for participation and audio recording. Interviews lasted approximately 30 min and were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and reviewed for accuracy through comparison with the original audio files. Data collection concluded upon reaching thematic saturation.

Interview guide development and validation

The interview guide was initially developed in English and translated into Arabic [3] (provided as Supplementary File 1). To ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence, a rigorous forward–backward translation process was conducted by two bilingual researchers. This process ensured cultural appropriateness and semantic consistency of the interview items.

Data were analyzed using a hybrid inductive-deductive thematic analysis approach, guided by Hsieh and Shannon [18] and aligned with the TDF [7]. The analysis proceeded in the following steps:

Preparation and familiarization. Interview audios were transcribed verbatim and checked against recordings for accuracy. Two researchers independently read each transcript in full, wrote brief reflexive memos, and noted preliminary concepts in NVivo (v15).

Rigor, trustworthiness, and transferability

Credibility was strengthened through triangulation across researchers during data collection and analysis. Verbatim transcription and iterative review ensured data fidelity. To enhance transferability, a diverse sample was selected across disciplines and institutions. A detailed description of the data collection context, professional roles of participants, and analytic process was provided to support application of findings to similar healthcare settings.

Bias mitigation strategy

Several measures were employed to reduce potential bias. First, purposive sampling helped ensure information-rich cases. Second, the use of a validated, culturally adapted interview guide provided consistency in data collection. Third, interviews were conducted by two trained researchers to minimize interviewer influence. Fourth, transcription and translation were independently performed by three different researchers to enhance linguistic accuracy and reduce subjective distortion. Finally, data coding and theme development involved multiple researchers to ensure inter-rater reliability and to integrate diverse analytical perspectives.

Positionality statement

The research team comprised five Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) students (Shahad Alruwaythi, Layan AlDubayyan, Abeer Alasmari, Wassan Almutairi, and Mayar Al-Juaid) and two faculty supervisors. The student researchers each possessed approximately one year of experience in academic research and had completed certified training in Good Clinical Practice (GCP) accredited by the National Institute of Drug Abuse (NIDA) Clinical Trials Network, ensuring adherence to ethical and methodological standards throughout data collection.

Dr. Neah Albasha, who holds a doctoral degree in geriatric rehabilitation and has published extensively in fall prevention among older adults, contributed expertise in geriatric research design, data interpretation, and qualitative analysis. Prof. Reem Alwhaibi, a consultant physiotherapist with extensive research experience in rehabilitation sciences, provided oversight on methodological rigor, reflexivity, and data triangulation.

The researchers acknowledge that their professional backgrounds in rehabilitation may have influenced their assumptions about the importance of patient safety, interdisciplinary collaboration, and education in fall prevention. To mitigate potential bias, reflexive discussions were conducted throughout the research process, and multiple investigators participated in coding and theme validation to ensure balanced interpretation and analytical transparency.

Results

Participant demographics

A total of thirty five healthcare professionals were assessed for eligibility. Nineteen were excluded(seven incomplete interviews, three technical failures, two voluntary withdrawals, and seven no-show). Sixteen participants completed the interviews and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2). Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the study participants (n = 16). The sample included 10 females and 6 males across four professional groups ((nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, general practitioners; 4 each). Most participants held a bachelor’s degree (n = 12) and worked in governmental hospitals (n = 15). Experience with older adults ranged from ≤ 2 years (n = 4) to ≥ 11 years (n = 3). This distribution provided role and experience variation sufficient for thematic saturation.

Overarching theme 1: socio-cultural-ecological mismatch

Participants described how cultural expectations around independence, unrealistic family practices, and unsuitable physical environments interact to sustain fall risk. Culturally, some older adults resist visible supports because they are perceived as signs of frailty or dependence, which undermines adherence to preventative advice: “The culture in society, especially the value of independence, means older adult patients often do not like anyone to help them.” (RN5). This cultural stance often intersects with family behaviors that inadvertently elevate risk. For example, seemingly caring actions taken for comfort at home conflicted with safety guidance: “They would spread blankets on the floor… I told them they needed to remove the blankets because they could cause slipping… but unfortunately, they were insistent.” (RN2).

These socio-cultural pressures are compounded by environmental hazards within facilities and homes. Clinicians pointed to routine obstacles that remain unaddressed, such as clutter or child-related items in shared spaces: “Older adult patients are sometimes placed in areas with children, and children's belongings, such as toys, can cause tripping hazards.” (PT4). At the community and housing level, participants emphasized that many buildings are not age-friendly by design, limiting modification options: “The way our buildings are constructed today is not necessarily suited for the older adults.” (GP4); “In mountainous regions or traditional-style homes, where the house can't be adjusted to reduce fall risks.” (OT3). Practical access barriers begin even before patients reach care areas, for instance at parking and entry points: “The person can't even open the door to get out of the car or transfer to the wheelchair.” (OT3).

Several participants proposed cross-sector solutions to bridge this mismatch, notably health–engineering collaborations to set public standards for safer design: “The idea is to establish a collaboration… with professionals in the field of engineering so that we can have a certain type of standard available to the public.” (GP4).

Overarching theme 2: systemic fragmentation and resource scarcity

Participants depicted a system strained by equipment gaps, staffing shortages, and fragmented processes, which together dilute fall-prevention efforts. At the facility level, basic safety devices were described as unavailable or poorly maintained: “Honestly, we don’t have equipment, the hospital didn’t really provide it” (RN5); “Sometimes the equipment is available but not properly maintained, malfunctioning, or has other problems” (PT3). Financial and upkeep constraints were recurrent backdrops: “There are things that could be added, but cost and maintenance are always concerns.” (GP1). These resource gaps extend to the continuum of care; a physician noted the absence of long-term care capacity, shifting the burden to families: “This kind of service doesn't exist in Saudi Arabia.” (GP4).

Human resources were likewise stretched, constraining education and reassessment: “Sometimes, due to work pressure, they might not have the time.” (OT3); “You manage 5 or 6 rooms… how do you focus on all of them…?” (RN5). Team communication and handoffs were inconsistent, particularly post-surgery: “Some doctors after completing the surgery would feel like their role was done. They wouldn’t always explain what complications might arise or how rehabilitation is an essential next step.” (OT3); “A third issue is the lack of communication or poor communication within the medical field.” (PT3).

Participants did, however, point to policy-driven levers that help counter fragmentation. Clear protocols were seen to “directly lead to better service.” (GP2); clinicians called for “a dedicated pathway… starting from detection or assessment and ending with treatment.” (GP4). Routine referral systems were described as practical facilitators, “If there are concerns, we refer them” (PT2), and documentation of refusal was used for accountability: “We just note in the system that the patient refused.” (RN4). Leadership and administrative redesign were also cited as enablers, such as protected time for non-clinical work to close loops on risk: “On this administrative day, I review all my patients’ medical files…” (GP2).

Regular Fall Audits were identified as vital for tracking incidents, raising awareness, and guiding policy action: “It's critical that everyone is fully aware of the risk….” (GP2). Color-Coded Safety System —red for mobility and yellow for incidents—under the “Call Don’t Fall” campaign was identified as an effective strategy to enhance staff vigilance and patient compliance: "Every month we connect the days by colors and we do investigation of what happened and how it happened” (RN5).

Overarching theme 3: knowledge–practice gap and engagement shortfalls

Across professions, participants stressed that knowledge does not reliably translate into consistent practice, for staff, patients, or caregivers, especially when engagement is fragmented. Clinicians repeatedly identified education deficits as a root barrier: “The biggest barrier… is patient and caregiver education. Many do not follow safety instructions.” (PT2). For patients with cognitive impairment, clinicians redirected effort toward families: “If the patient is unaware due to cognitive decline, I shift my focus to educating the family…” (PT1). Structured touchpoints (e.g., weekly sessions) were described as workable facilitators: “We hold weekly education sessions for patients and families… even if an entire session is spent ensuring they transition safely.” (OT2).

However, adherence often faltered outside clinical settings, undermining gains: “In the hospital, they might do the exercises, but once he's home, the family doesn't follow through… The most important thing is consistency.” (OT3). Family actions sometimes directly conflicted with safety guidance: “They would spread blankets on the floor… I told them they needed to remove [them] because they could cause slipping… [but] they were insistent.” (RN2). Misjudgment of capability further widened the gap, “Don’t exaggerate, I’m still strong.” (GP1); “Many patients… believe they can walk… when they actually can't.” (OT2).

On the staff side, new graduates or inexperienced staff often lacked geriatric-specific knowledge, requiring default safety routines until judgment matured: “Interns… must use the belt at all times. Over time, they develop the judgment to determine when to use it.” (PT2). Continuous induction programs, hands-on safety training, and collaborative peer learning were identified as key facilitators for bridging staff skill gaps and enhancing practical competence. Use of tools like Timed Up and Go and Morse Fall Risk Scale [24], combined with regular reassessment, was described as facilitating early risk identifications. Participants described practical techniques to bridge knowledge into action, motivational strategies (mirrors, storytelling) to build confidence and adherence: “I use mirrors… so patients can see themselves walking safely which helps boost their confidence.” (PT1); “I share stories of other patients to motivate them.” (PT4). Fall prevention champions, such as health coaches and educators, were also noted as valuable for continuing patient and caregiver education beyond brief consultations: “My consultation time with a patient is limited to 10 to 15 min, but some patients and their caregivers require in-depth education. In such cases, I can refer them to the Health Coach, who continues the education, follows up with them, calls them regularly, and even sends them videos and informational materials.” (GP2).

Finally, several clinicians emphasized a person-centered stance to sustain safe autonomy rather than default restriction: “Our job is to ensure they have the freedom to move safely by eliminating risk factors.” (PT1); “I don't prevent them from moving out of fear of falls because that could lead to other issues.” (RN2). Participants identified individualized, stepwise interventions as key facilitators of fall prevention. Tailoring therapy to patients’ functional and cognitive status, using proper transfers and assistive devices, and combining skill-building with environmental adaptations were reported to enhance confidence and reduce falls.: "These patients need a specific type of exercise, a specific type of nutrition, and specific medical care to make them less likely to experience falls in the future” (GP4).

Discussion

This study explored the barriers and facilitators to fall prevention among older adults in community-based care settings in Saudi Arabia, with particular attention to cultural, educational, and socioeconomic influences. The findings confirm that fall prevention is a complex and multifactorial issue that extends beyond clinical protocols to include individual behaviors, systemic structures, and sociocultural norms. This study provides a unique contribution by contextualizing these barriers within the Saudi healthcare system, where family-centered decision-making, cultural perceptions of aging, and limited geriatric infrastructure intersect to influence the delivery and acceptance of fall prevention practices.

The most frequently cited barriers included limited awareness among patients and caregivers, cultural perceptions normalizing falls as part of aging, physical environmental hazards, and financial constraints limiting access to preventive resources. Unlike findings from East Asian countries such as Japan and South Korea, where strong family support structures enhance fall prevention adherence [28], Saudi participants emphasized that family overprotection sometimes discourages patient mobility or rehabilitation, reflecting a cultural paradox between safety and independence. These findings echo international literature that identifies healthcare system inefficiencies, such as staff shortages and limited training, as persistent obstacles [25, 30]. Furthermore, cultural stigmas surrounding assistive device use and the undervaluation of geriatric care add layers of complexity unique to the Saudi context [1, 8]. This cultural normalization of falls as “part of aging” is consistent with other Middle Eastern studies [11, 16] but contrasts sharply with Western models that frame falls as preventable through behavioral modification and structured programs.

On the other hand, facilitators identified in this study included the use of comprehensive geriatric assessments, ongoing staff and caregiver education, improved access to assistive devices, and strong interdisciplinary collaboration. These facilitators were not only congruent with evidence-based practice guidelines [23] but also reflected actionable solutions proposed by participants themselves. The alignment between proposed solutions and effective strategies underscores the practical knowledge and adaptive problem-solving demonstrated by frontline providers. Notably, these facilitators illustrate the readiness of Saudi healthcare professionals to adapt global fall-prevention strategies to local sociocultural and institutional constraints.

Mapping the findings to the TDF enabled a structured interpretation of the cognitive, environmental, and behavioral determinants of fall prevention implementation. Domains such as Knowledge, Skills, Environmental Context and Resources, and Social Influences were consistently populated with rich, representative data. For example, the emphasis on staff training and patient education aligned with the Knowledge and Skills domains, while barriers related to inadequate equipment and unsafe infrastructure reflected Environmental Context and Resources [7]. This mapping demonstrates that behavioral and contextual determinants within Saudi Arabia are closely intertwined, knowledge gaps often coexist with resource shortages and cultural barriers, highlighting the interdependence between individual and systemic change.

The absence of data from four domains, Optimism, Reinforcement, Intention, and Emotion, offers valuable insight into the professional discourse shaping fall prevention in Saudi settings. These domains may have been underrepresented because healthcare providers in this context tend to frame fall prevention as a procedural and clinical responsibility rather than an emotionally driven or motivational issue. This professional orientation aligns with findings from Turabi et al. [34], who reported that Saudi clinicians often prioritize compliance and protocol over intrinsic motivation in rehabilitation contexts. Additionally, the interview guide’s focus on external barriers (e.g., training, infrastructure) may have de-emphasized introspective or affective components of practice. Future qualitative studies incorporating reflective or narrative methods could elicit these underexplored dimensions.

Beyond structural and cognitive factors, participants also expressed a strong moral and relational dimension to care, prioritizing patient dignity, trust, and autonomy. This shift toward individualized practice signals an ethical evolution in fall prevention philosophy, moving from purely procedural adherence toward humanized care principles. It provides an important conceptual bridge to the emerging PCC discourse, emphasizing empowerment, shared decision-making, and the balance between protection and independence.

Moreover, the recurring theme of PCC emerged independently of the TDF structure, highlighting the importance of individualized approaches that consider the unique physical and psychological status of each older adult. Participants emphasized balancing safety with autonomy and promoting adaptive interventions rather than restricting mobility, a principle that aligns with global calls for humanized, participatory care [26, 27]. This aligns with the international shift toward individualized care frameworks, such as the WHO’s Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) model [35], which emphasize tailoring interventions to personal goals and cultural contexts. The Saudi context adds nuance, where respect for family hierarchy and religious values often influences care preferences and autonomy, underscoring the need to integrate cultural sensitivity within person-centered frameworks.

One ethically charged finding involved divided opinions on the use of physical restraints. While some participants viewed restraints as necessary in specific high-risk scenarios, others strongly opposed their use due to concerns about patient dignity, autonomy, and potential harm. This ethical dilemma reflects broader debates in geriatric ethics surrounding autonomy versus beneficence [13]. The principle of “least restrictive care,” widely endorsed in Western gerontology, advocates for restraint only as a last resort following environmental and behavioral interventions [29]. Our findings reveal that in Saudi community care, ethical decision-making around restraint is often informal and context-dependent, underscoring the need for clear institutional policies and ethics training for healthcare professionals.

Collectively, these findings extend the global understanding of fall prevention by situating behavioral and organizational determinants within Saudi Arabia’s socio-cultural and policy context. They suggest that sustainable progress requires multi-level reform: (1) embedding person-centered and ethical decision-making within geriatric education; (2) developing national guidelines for fall prevention and restraint use; and (3) strengthening health–engineering collaborations to create safer community environments. Such actions would translate research insights into tangible policy and training improvements, ensuring both cultural alignment and long-term impact on patient safety.

Overall, this study uniquely contributes by revealing how sociocultural norms, professional hierarchies, and contextual constraints interact to shape fall prevention in Saudi Arabia, a dimension underrepresented in global literature.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, interviews were conducted through voice-only communication platforms, which may have limited the richness of non-verbal cues and potentially reduced the depth of participant disclosure on sensitive topics. Second, the reliance on self-reported perceptions may introduce bias related to recall or social desirability. Third, while participants represented a range of healthcare roles, all were drawn from community-based settings. As such, the findings may not reflect the experiences of professionals in inpatient or long-term care environments.

Future research directions

To address these limitations, future studies should consider incorporating observational methods to capture real-time behaviors and environmental factors influencing fall prevention. Expanding research to include inpatient care teams, hospital administrators, and policy-makers would provide a multi-level perspective on systemic barriers. Further exploration of the four underrepresented TDF domains, Optimism, Reinforcement, Intention, and Emotion, may enrich the behavioral understanding of fall prevention strategies. Moreover, comparing public and private healthcare settings could uncover disparities in institutional support and resource availability.

Research focusing on policy-level and infrastructure-related determinants, such as national fall prevention guidelines, funding mechanisms, and administrative support systems, is also warranted. Such work can complement our findings and inform the development of national frameworks that standardize and scale best practices in fall prevention.

Conclusion

Fall prevention in older adults requires a holistic, multi-pronged approach that integrates staff development, caregiver engagement, patient empowerment, and organizational reform. This study underscores the critical role of targeted education, particularly for patients with cognitive impairment, and the provision of resources such as assistive devices and tailored exercise programs. Structured induction programs for healthcare professionals, interdisciplinary collaboration, and proactive medication reviews are essential for reducing fall risk and promoting functional independence.

Our findings also highlight the necessity of embedding fall prevention efforts within the cultural and socioeconomic realities of the Saudi context. Interventions must be customized to account for financial constraints, infrastructural barriers, and the influence of societal attitudes toward aging and disability. By prioritizing person-centered, context-sensitive strategies, policymakers and healthcare leaders can enhance the safety and quality of care for Saudi Arabia’s aging population.

Ultimately, implementing effective fall prevention interventions will require not only training and equipment but also a shift in institutional culture, one that values geriatric care, supports cross-sector collaboration, and adopts evidence-based guidelines tailored to local needs. Future national initiatives should aim to standardize protocols, fund staff training, and promote equity in access to fall prevention services across both public and private healthcare systems.
