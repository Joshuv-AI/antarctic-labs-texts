Logic-Driven Semantic Communication for Resilient Multi-Agent Systems
Tamara Alshammari, Student Member, IEEE, Mehdi Bennis, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract
The advent of 6G networks is accelerating autonomy, intelligence, and interconnectedness in large-scale, decentralized multi-agent systems (MAS). While this evolution enables highly adaptive behavior, it also heightens vulnerability to stressors such as environmental changes, faults, misinformation, and adversarial behavior. Existing literature on resilience in decentralized and multi-agent systems largely focuses on isolated aspects, such as fault tolerance or robustness, without offering a principled, unified definition of multi-agent resilience. This gap limits the ability to design systems that can continuously sense, adapt, and recover under dynamic conditions. This article proposes a formal definition of MAS resilience grounded in two complementary dimensions: epistemic resilience, wherein agents recover and sustain accurate knowledge of the environment, and action resilience, wherein agents leverage that knowledge to coordinate and sustain goals under disruptions. We formalize resilience via temporal epistemic logic and quantify it using recoverability time (how quickly desired properties are re-established after a disturbance) and durability time (how long accurate beliefs and goal-directed behavior are sustained after recovery). We design an agent architecture and develop decentralized algorithms to achieve both epistemic and action resilience. We further provide formal verification guarantees, showing that our specifications are sound with respect to the metric bounds and admit finite-horizon verification, enabling design-time certification and lightweight runtime monitoring. Through a case study on distributed multi-agent decision-making under abrupt network stressors, we show that our approach consistently outperforms baseline methods. Collectively, our formal verification analysis and simulation results highlight that the proposed framework enables resilient, knowledge-driven decision-making and sustained operation, laying the groundwork for resilient decentralized multi-agent systems in next-generation communication infrastructures.

III
Epistemic Logic and Semantic Communication

In the previous section, we introduced the agents’ internal models and described how their epistemic states evolve through prediction, observation, and communication. We now formalize these internal representations and communication mechanisms using tools from
temporal epistemic logic
and
Kripke semantics
. Temporal epistemic logic provides a symbolic framework for reasoning about what agents know, do not know, or consider possible
over time
, while Kripke structures supply the semantic foundation that gives these notions a concrete interpretation. Within this framework, each agent
i
’s internal model corresponds to a
temporal-agent slice
of a Kripke structure that encodes its informational perspective on the world at a given moment. Communication among agents is then modeled
not merely as raw data exchange
, but as the
transmission of logical statements
whose truth values are evaluated with respect to these evolving Kripke structures; thus, capturing the
semantic content
of messages rather than their syntactic form.

This section first reviews the necessary background on temporal epistemic logic, introducing the logical language used to express formulas about knowledge and belief as they evolve over time. It then presents Kripke structures as formal models of knowledge and Kripke semantics as the rules that assign meaning to these logical formulas within such structures. Next, we define the agents’ internal epistemic spaces with respect to a Kripke structure, and finally describe how agent communication can be viewed as logic-based
semantic
interaction.

III-A
Background

In multi-agent systems, the dynamic evolution of each agent’s beliefs about the environment is as important as the objective truths of the environment. Classical propositional logic evaluates statements as true or false but does not account for (i) different informational perspectives across agents (the epistemic aspect), or (ii) how those perspectives change over time (the temporal aspect). To address these limitations, we adopt a
temporal epistemic logic
that combines multi-agent epistemic operators with temporal operators. This logic is built on a formal language for expressing formulas about knowledge and belief, and interpreted using Kripke structures and Kripke semantics.

Atoms and Language.

Let
\Omega
be a nonempty set of atomic propositions, and let the discrete time domain be
T=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. We also adopt the convention
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. Although
T
and
\mathbb{N}
denote the same underlying set, we use
T
for absolute time indices (
t\in T
) and
\mathbb{N}
(resp.
\mathbb{N}_{>0}
) for durations/step horizons; to keep timestamps distinct from lengths. With these conventions in place, the temporal–epistemic language
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
is generated by the following grammar, where each expression
\varphi
denotes a well-formed formula of the language
[
27
]
[
44
]
:

\varphi\;::=\;\top\;\big|\;p\;\big|\;\lnot\varphi\;\big|\;\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta)}\,\varphi\;\big|\;\varphi_{1}\mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha]}\varphi_{2}\;\big|\;\mathcal{K}_{i}\varphi,

where the symbol
::=
denotes a
definition by syntactic formation
, commonly used in formal grammars to specify how well-formed formulas are constructed. It reads as “is defined as” or “can be formed as,” and the vertical bars
|
separate alternative constructions. Moreover, the symbol
\top
denotes a formula that is always true,
p\in\Omega
,
\lnot
denotes negation,
\lor
denotes disjunction,
i\in N
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
denote step horizons. Standard connectives such as conjunction
\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}
and implication
\varphi_{1}\!\rightarrow\!\varphi_{2}
are not primitive in the grammar but are defined using negation

Epistemic

Kripke Structure.
To assign meaning to the formulas of the language
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{time}}(\Omega)
and to capture the evolution of agents’ beliefs over time, we employ a

where:

•

•

For each agent

for agent

Kripke Semantics.
Given the Kripke structure
\mathcal{M}_{t}=(W,\{R_{i,t}\}_{i\in N},v)
, the truth of a formula
\varphi
in the temporal–epistemic language
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
is defined via a
satisfaction relation
at evaluation points. An evaluation point is a triple
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)
, where
r:T\to W
is a run; that is, a time-indexed trajectory

\langle r(0),r(1),\ldots\rangle
and
w_{t}=r(t)
is the actual world at time
t

[
20
]
. Intuitively, among all admissible trajectories,
r
is the actual one the system follows (e.g., the trace induced by the external dynamics and disturbances); hence, at each time
t
, it determines the current world
w_{t}
. We write
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\varphi
to mean that
\varphi
is true at time
t
along

in
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. The satisfaction relation is then given inductively by the clauses below; taken together, these clauses constitute the
Kripke semantics
for our temporal–epistemic language.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\top
always holds.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models p
iff
p\in v(w_{t})
.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\lnot\varphi
iff
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\not\models\varphi
.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
iff
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\varphi_{1}
or
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\varphi_{2}
.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta)}\varphi
iff for all

with

we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime}},r,t^{\prime})\models\varphi
.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\varphi_{1}\mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha]}\varphi_{2}
iff there exists

such that

(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime\prime}},r,t^{\prime\prime})\models\varphi_{2}
and for all
t^{\prime}\in[t,t^{\prime\prime})
we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime}},r,t^{\prime})\models\varphi_{1}
.

•

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\mathcal{K}_{i}\varphi
iff for all

with
(w_{t},w^{\prime})\in R_{i,t}
,

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r[t\mapsto w^{\prime}],t)\models\varphi,

where

is the run that agrees with

at all times except that it maps

to
w^{\prime}
.

III-B
Internal Epistemic Spaces

In Section
II
, each agent’s internal state
\iota_{i,t}
was introduced as an abstract representation of what the agent knows or considers possible about the external environment. We now give this notion a formal grounding within the Kripke framework developed above.

For each agent
i\in N
, the internal epistemic space is defined as

I_{i}\;=\;\big\{\,R\subseteq W\times W\;\big|\;R\text{ satisfies chosen frame conditions}\,\big\},

where
R_{i,t}\in I_{i}
denotes the epistemic accessibility relation of agent
i
at time
t
. Moreover, by
frame conditions
we mean structural properties imposed on the accessibility relation to constrain epistemic behavior. Common examples include
reflexivity

(
\forall\,w:\,(w,w)\in R_{i,t}
, meaning that the actual world is always possible) and
transitivity

(
(w,w^{\prime})\in R_{i,t}
and
(w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime})\in R_{i,t}
imply
(w,w^{\prime\prime})\in R_{i,t}
, meaning indistinguishability composes). Intuitively,
(w,w^{\prime})\in R_{i,t}
means that, given its information at time
t
, agent
i
considers world
w^{\prime}
possible when the actual world is
w
. Hence,
R_{i,t}
captures the agent’s uncertainty or indistinguishability among worlds at that time. Thus, an agent’s internal state at time
t
is given by its accessibility relation,

\iota_{i,t}=R_{i,t}\in I_{i},

which encodes all worlds consistent with the agent’s current knowledge. Under this representation,
I_{i}
provides the structural foundation for modeling how agents reason and update their knowledge over time.

Updates of the internal epistemic state.

Recall from Section
II
that agent
i
updates its internal state in response to raised contradictions and new information received through observations and/or messages, via an epistemic action
u^{\mathrm{epi}}_{i,t+1}\in U^{\mathrm{epi}}
. Under the Kripke representation, this becomes an update of the accessibility relation

\iota_{i,t}=R_{i,t}\ \in\ I_{i}
and is given by:

R_{i,t+1}\;=\;\psi_{i}\!\big(R_{i,t},\,u^{\mathrm{epi}}_{i,t+1}\big),

where
\psi_{i}:I_{i}\times U^{\mathrm{epi}}\to I_{i}
is the internal transition map. Typical actions instantiate
\psi_{i}
as follows:

•

Refine
:
remove
accessibility relation to worlds inconsistent with new evidence, yielding a monotone decrease
R_{i,t+1}\subseteq R_{i,t}
.

•

Revise
:
re-wire
accessibility relation among evidence-consistent worlds (may add new accessible worlds or remove existing ones) subject to minimal change.

•

Hold
/
Broadcast
/
Explore
: leave
R_{i,t}
unchanged, inform neighbors, or trigger future evidence acquisition, respectively.

The update
\psi_{i}
is required to (i)
preserve frame conditions
(e.g., reflexivity, transitivity) so that
R_{i,t+1}\in I_{i}
; (ii)
restore consistency
with the current evidence (observation at
t{+}1
and received messages); and (iii) satisfy a
minimal-change
principle (no unnecessary additions/removals of accessibility relations beyond what is needed for consistency). In this way, the abstract update policy of Section
II
is realized concretely as an operation on Kripke accessibility relations.

Remark 1

(Intuitive mapping between formal logic and operational algorithms)
.

To provide an intuitive link between the epistemic concepts introduced in Section
III-A
and the
operational procedures introduced here and used later in Section
V
, we briefly summarize the role of the Kripke
model in practical decision making. Each world represents a candidate
environmental condition, and the accessibility relation encodes which worlds
an agent still considers possible given its observations so far (referred to as accessible worlds). When new
information arrives that does not make any previously inaccessible world
possible, but instead rules out some of the currently accessible ones, the
agent removes exactly those worlds that are no longer compatible with the data
(
refine
). If, however, the agent’s predictions under all currently
accessible worlds fail to explain the newly observed data, this produces a

prediction error
and prompts the agent to collect additional evidence
to discriminate between worlds (
explore
). As such evidence
accumulates and incompatible worlds are eliminated, the agent may reach a point
where its set of possible worlds must be reorganized; that is, when previously
inaccessible worlds become plausible again. In this case, the accessibility
relation is restructured (
revise
), and the agent communicates the
resulting belief change to its neighbors (
broadcast
). When neither
refinement nor revision is warranted, the agent simply keeps its current set
of possible worlds unchanged (
hold
). In this way, the abstract Kripke
semantics provides a conceptual basis for the detection, evidence-gathering,
revision, and communication behaviors implemented by the algorithms in later
sections.

Remark 2

(Collisions as stressors)
.

Although collisions are not modeled explicitly in this paper, the framework can
naturally accommodate them. In this formalism, the behavior of other agents is
treated as part of the environment dynamics and is therefore encoded in each
agent’s epistemic state through the set of accessible worlds. If a collision
occurs (e.g., two agents select incompatible actions) the resulting observation
will contradict the predictions of all worlds in which such a collision should
not happen. This inconsistency is interpreted as a prediction error. The agent then updates its accessibility relation to retain only the worlds consistent with the observed
interaction, and its external policy realigns accordingly. In this way,
potential action collisions are handled as environmental stressors within the
same architecture developed in this section.

III-C
Semantic Communication via Epistemic Logic and Kripke Structures

Conventional communication systems primarily focus on the accurate transmission of symbols over a channel, optimizing metrics such as bit error rate and throughput. These systems treat information as a syntactic entity, largely independent of its meaning or relevance to the receiver. In contrast, semantic communication shifts the emphasis from symbol fidelity to meaning fidelity, prioritizing the successful conveyance of intended meaning and task-relevant information. Achieving semantic communication requires that (i) sender agents transmit compressed representations that preserve meaning, (ii) receiver agents interpret these messages within the appropriate context, and (iii) communication be driven by the task or objective at hand, rather than transmitting all available data indiscriminately. We realize semantic communication in a principled way using the temporal–epistemic language
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{time}}(\Omega)
and a shared Kripke structure with its semantics. Concretely:
(i) senders transmit messages that are logical formulas from
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{time}}(\Omega)
, representing (possibly partial) snapshots of the sender’s epistemic state; that is, what the sender knows or considers possible.
(ii) receivers interpret the semantics (meaning) of these formulas by evaluating them in the shared Kripke structure, which provides the common context for correct interpretation, and
(iii) communication is task-driven, for example, when the task is
achieving resilience
, agents communicate only to indicate that a change in the environment has occurred, allowing others to update their beliefs and policies accordingly.

IV
Resilience Metrics in Multi-Agent Systems

In critical situations, timely information and shared understanding are key to effective response. In our agent architecture, the success of agent
i
’s external policy
\pi_{i}^{\text{ext}}
depends on the accuracy of its epistemic state
\iota_{i,t}
, which the policy is conditioned on. Resilience thus requires agents to
detect
stressors,
recover
their epistemic states and policies after a stressor, and
maintain
accurate epistemic states and optimal policies post-recovery. To capture these aspects, we formalize resilience along two complementary dimensions:

•

Epistemic resilience
: the ability to detect knowledge gaps, recover from discrepancies between the beliefs about the environment (epistemic state) and its actual state, and sustain accurate beliefs after recovery.

•

Action resilience
: the ability to re-align external actions with updated beliefs and maintain optimal external policies post-recovery.

These dimensions reflect not only individual capabilities but also the collective dynamics of multi-agent systems, where resilience is shaped by the formation of mutual knowledge; a shared, evolving understanding that enables coordinated adaptation and recovery. In light of this, we now formally introduce our resilience metric for multi-agent systems, followed by definitions of its two dimensions.

Definition 1

(
System Resilience
)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
be temporal-epistemic formulas representing, respectively, epistemic resilience and action resilience specifications. Then, the system-wide resilience can be represented by the following formula:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{system}}\equiv\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}\land\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}.

(1)

This states that the overall system resilience is achieved if and only if both the epistemic and action resilience properties hold.

IV-A
Epistemic Resilience

Let
\varphi_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
and
\varphi_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
be mutually exclusive formulas (i.e.,
\varphi_{1}\rightarrow\lnot\varphi_{2}
and
\varphi_{2}\rightarrow\lnot\varphi_{1}
) describing some aspect of the environment. Initially,
\varphi_{1}
holds in the actual world, and all agents correctly believe that
\varphi_{1}
is true. That is, at the initial time
t_{0}
, for every agent
i\in N
and every
w^{\prime}\in W
with
(w,w^{\prime})\in\iota_{i,t_{0}}
, we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}},r,t_{0})\models\varphi_{1}
; equivalently,

(\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}},r,t_{0})\models\bigwedge_{i\in N}\mathcal{K}_{i}\varphi_{1}\quad\text{(i.e., }\mathcal{E}_{N}\,\varphi_{1}\text{).}

At some time
t_{v}
, the environment changes (forming a stressor or a disturbance) such that
\varphi_{1}
no longer holds and
\varphi_{2}
becomes true. To achieve epistemic resilience, agents must:

(i)

Recover their epistemic states to the correct belief within a time bound
\alpha_{1}
, meaning that agents come to know that
\varphi_{2}
now holds (
recoverability
). That is, let recovery time be
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
where
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}-t_{v}\leq\alpha_{1}
, for every agent
i\in N
and every
w^{\prime}\in W
with
(w,w^{\prime})\in\iota_{i,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}}
, we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}},r,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}})\models\varphi_{2}
; equivalently,

(\mathcal{M}_{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}},r,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}})\models\bigwedge_{i\in N}\mathcal{K}_{i}\varphi_{2}\quad\text{(i.e., }\mathcal{E}_{N}\,\varphi_{2}\text{).}

(ii)

Maintain the recovered belief for at least a duration
\beta_{1}
(
durability
).

The above conditions can be expressed more compactly in our temporal–epistemic language.
In particular, epistemic resilience requires that all agents know the old fact
\varphi_{1}
until, within
\alpha_{1}
time units, they come to know the new fact
\varphi_{2}
,
and that this knowledge then persists for at least
\beta_{1}
time units.
We formalize this as follows:

Definition 2

(
Epistemic Resilience
)
.

Let
\alpha_{1}
denote the maximum allowable recovery time and
\beta_{1}
the minimum allowable durability duration. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}
denote a formula representing the epistemic resilience of the system. Then, we define epistemic resilience as

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}\equiv\Bigl(\lnot\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}\Bigr)\,\mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha_{1}]}\,\Bigl(\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta_{1})}\,\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}\bigr)\Bigr).

(2)

where:

•

\mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha_{1}]}
is the bounded until operator, ensuring that if the
agents are initially unaware of stressor, they will recover their epistemic states within
\alpha_{1}
time units;

•

\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta_{1})}
is the bounded globally operator, ensuring that
once the agents recover their epistemic states, these epistemic states are maintained for at least
\beta_{1}
time units.

Durability means that the recovered epistemic states remain accurate enough to persist for at least
\beta_{1}
time steps without having agents detecting any contradictions; assuming the environment does not change during this period. This highlights a fundamental
recoverability–durability
trade-off: agents that revise their beliefs quickly may do so based on limited evidence, increasing the risk of incorrect updates and frequent revisions (i.e., lower durability). In contrast, agents that wait for more conclusive evidence may recover more slowly but are more likely to adopt a correct belief that remains stable (i.e., higher durability). To quantify these two factors, and make (
2
) operational, we now extract two scalar quantities that
instantiate its two facets;
epistemic recoverability
and
epistemic durability
.

Definition 3

(
Epistemic Recoverability
)
.

Let
t_{v}
be the violation time at which the environment change (stressor) occurred, and let
\varphi_{2}
be the new environmental condition. Then, the
epistemic recoverability interval

\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
is defined as

\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}=\inf\{\,t\mid t\in T,\;t>t_{v}\text{ and }(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}\}-t_{v},

(3)

This expression defines the epistemic recoverability duration as the smallest interval following the violation time
t_{v}
after which the agents’ epistemic states satisfy the property that every agent in
N
knows
\varphi_{2}
. We have the convention that
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}=+\infty
if the set is empty (i.e., epistemic accuracy is never restored after
t_{v}
). The time at which this recovered epistemic property is first established is then given by:

t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}=t_{v}+\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}.

Definition 4

(
Epistemic Durability
)
.

Let
t_{v}
be the violation time at which the environment change occurred. Then, the
epistemic durability interval

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
is defined as

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}=\inf\{t\mid t\in T,t>t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\text{ \& }(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\not\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}\}-t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}},

(4)

where
\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
denotes that every agent in
N
believes
\varphi_{2}
, and
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\not\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
indicates that this property no longer holds at time
t
in world
w
and that agents have changed their beliefs. In other words,
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
is the smallest time interval after
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
during which the recovered belief persists before being violated. We have the convention that
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}=+\infty
if the set is empty (i.e., epistemic accuracy is never violated after
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
).

While epistemic resilience captures the agents’ ability to recover and maintain accurate beliefs about the environment, resilience does not end at the epistemic level. For agents operating in dynamic environments, knowledge must be translated into appropriate external actions. Even if agents correctly revise their beliefs, a system is not truly resilient unless these beliefs lead to timely and effective adjustments in behavior. We therefore turn to the second dimension:

action resilience
.

IV-B
Action Resilience

Let
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
denote the set of admissible external policies for
agent
i
and let
\pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{ext}}\in\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}

denote the external policy currently implemented by agent
i
at time
t
. Each agent
i
is associated with a specific task-related performance objective
J_{i}
(e.g., expected cumulative reward or cost). Given its finite candidate set of external policies

\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, this objective induces a time-dependent set of
individually
optimal external policies

\Pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opt}}:=\arg\max_{\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}}J_{i}(\pi_{i};t),

which contains the policies that are optimal for agent
i
at time
t
under the current environment condition. At the logical level, we introduce for each agent
i
an atomic proposition

\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
that is interpreted as “agent
i
is
currently acting optimally with respect to
J_{i}
”. That is, the proposition
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}
holds true iff agent
i
’s
current external policy
\pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{ext}}
belongs to
\Pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opt}}
. The group-level predicate is the conjunction

\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}\equiv\bigwedge_{i\in N}\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}},

which holds precisely when every agent executes one of its individually
optimal external policies. No joint optimality or equilibrium concept is
assumed; optimality is evaluated individually for each agent.

Initially, before the stressor occurred at time step
t_{v}
, all agents executed optimal actions with respect to the environment conditions. That is, at times
t^{\prime}<t_{v}
, for every agent
i\in N
and every
w^{\prime}\in W
with
(w,w^{\prime})\in\iota_{i,t^{\prime}}
, we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime}},r,t^{\prime})\models\pi^{\text{opt}}
. Once the stressor occurs at time
t_{v}
and cause a change in agents’ epistemic states at time
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
, agents’ external policies may no longer be optimal. That is, at
t^{\prime\prime}\geq t_{v}
, for every agent
i
and every world
w^{\prime}\in W
with
(w,w^{\prime})\in\iota_{i,t^{\prime\prime}}
, we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime\prime}},r,t^{\prime\prime})\not\models\pi^{\text{opt}}
. To achieve action resilience, agents must:

(i)

Recover their external policies to the optimal ones within a time bound
\alpha_{2}
, meaning that
\pi^{\text{opt}}
now holds (
recoverability
). That is, let recovery time be
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}
where
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}-t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\leq\alpha_{2}
, for every agent
i\in N
and every
w^{\prime}\in W
with
(w,w^{\prime})\in\iota_{i,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}}
, we have
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}},r,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}})\models\pi^{\text{opt}}
.

(ii)

Maintain optimal policies for at least a duration
\beta_{2}
(
durability
).

Similar to epistemic resilience, the above conditions can be expressed more compactly in our temporal–epistemic language.
In particular, action resilience requires that all agents do not execute optimal policies with respect to current environment conditions (
\lnot\pi^{\text{opt}}
holds)
until, within
\alpha_{1}
time units, they recover to execute optimal policies (
\pi^{\text{opt}}
holds), and that they maintain executing optimal policies for at least
\beta_{2}
time units. We formalize this in the following definition.

Definition 5

(
Action Resilience
)
.

Let
\alpha_{2}
denote the maximum allowable recovery time for agents to adjust their external actions based on the recovered collective knowledge, let
\beta_{2}
denote the minimum duration for which the optimal action must be maintained, and let
\pi^{\text{opt}}_{i}
be a proposition indicating that agent
i\in N
is currently acting optimally. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}
denote the action resilience of the system. Then, we define action resilience as

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}\equiv\Bigl(\neg\pi^{\text{opt}}\Bigr)\,\mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha_{2}]}\,\Bigl(\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta_{2})}\,\bigl(\pi^{\text{opt}}\bigr)\Bigr),

(5)

where:

•

\mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha_{2}]}
is the bounded until operator, ensuring that if the agents are initially not acting optimally, they will recover optimal actions within
\alpha_{2}
time units;

•

\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta_{2})}
is the bounded globally operator, ensuring that once the optimal actions are adopted, they are maintained for at least
\beta_{2}
time units.

This formulation reflects a
recoverability–durability
trade-off: agents may choose rapid action adjustments (leading to frequent changes and lower durability) or explore enough before revising their policies (ensuring longer-term stability). As done previously, to quantify these two factors, and make (
5
) operational, we now extract two scalar quantities that
instantiate its two facets;
action recoverability
and
action durability
.

Definition 6

(
Action Recoverability
)
.

Let
t_{v}
be the violation time at which the environment change occurred. Then, the
action recoverability interval

\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}
is given by

\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}=\inf\{\,t\mid t\in T,\;t\geq t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\text{ and }(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}\}-t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}.

(6)

This definition states that
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}
is the smallest time interval after
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
until the system reaches the state where all agents in
N
act optimally. We have the convention
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}=+\infty
if the set is empty (i.e., optimality is never reached after
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
).

Definition 7

(
Action Durability
)
.

Let
t_{v}
be the violation time at which the environment change occurred. Define

t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}=t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}+\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}},

to be the time at which the optimal policy is reached. Then, the
action durability interval

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}
is given by

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}=\inf\{t\mid t\in T,\,t\geq t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}\text{ and }(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\not\models\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}\}-t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}.

(7)

This definition states that
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}
is the minimal time interval after
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}
until the system no longer satisfies the condition that every agent is acting optimally. In other words, it measures how long the recovered, optimal action is maintained before being violated. We have the convention
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}=+\infty
if the set is empty (i.e., optimality is never violated after
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}
).

Next, we present policies and algorithms that achieve epistemic and action resilience in multi-agent systems under the proposed metrics.

V
Achieving Epistemic and Action Resilience: Policies and Algorithms

Having defined the resilience metrics, we now specify mild system assumptions under which
information can flow and stressors are detectable, and then present policies and algorithms
that realize the metrics.

Fig. 5:
Flow diagram of the epistemic policy executed by every agent to achieve epistemic resilience.

Assumption 1

(
Communication Network Connectivity and Reliability
)
.

The communication network is a directed graph
G=(N,E)
that is

strongly connected
: for any
i,j\in N
there exists a directed path

i\leadsto j
. Define the (hop) diameter

l_{\text{comm}}\;:=\;\max_{i,j\in N}l(i,j),

where
l(i,j)
is the length of a shortest directed path from
i
to
j
.
We additionally assume that messages transmitted along edges in
E
are delivered reliably and within one time step.
Thus,
l_{\text{comm}}
serves as an upper bound on the time required to disseminate a contradiction or belief update to the entire network, thereby enabling the formation of mutual knowledge
\mathcal{E}_{N}(\cdot)
.

Assumption 2

(
Timely Local Detection of Contradictions
)
.

Let
\varphi_{1}
denote a proposition that becomes false in the environment at some time
t_{v}
. We assume that there exists at least one agent
i\in N
that can detect this change locally within at most
n_{\text{steps}}
time steps. Formally,

\exists i\in N,\,\exists t_{\text{det}}\in[t_{v},t_{v}+n_{\text{steps}}]\text{ s.t. }({\mathcal{M}}_{t_{\text{det}}},r,t_{\text{det}})\models\mathcal{K}_{i}\lnot\varphi_{1}.

The detection is assumed to be governed by an internal inconsistency condition, such as a threshold-based deviation between predicted and observed observations.

Assumption
2
states that at least one agent can
notice
a stressor from its own predicted observation and the actual observation mismatch. This is a reasonable assumption: if a stressor has no observable effect on any agent’s knowledge (no persistent residual beyond noise) or on task performance, then it is
epistemically silent at that time and there is no “meaningful recovery” to perform.

Assumption 3

(
Minimum Inter-Disturbance Interval
)
.

Let
\{t_{v}^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 1}
be the sequence of disturbance (stressor) times.
There exists
\delta>0
such that for all
k
,

t_{v}^{(k+1)}-t_{v}^{(k)}\;\geq\;\delta.

Equivalently, if a change occurs at time
t_{v}
, any subsequent change occurs at
t_{v}^{\prime}
should satisfy

t_{v}^{\prime}\geq t_{v}+\delta
. Thus, disturbances occur no more frequently than every
\delta
time units.

Assumption
3
prevents adversarially rapid,
back-to-back stressors that would make recovery provably impossible. Feasibility, however, also requires that this inter-disturbance gap be large enough to
accommodate worst-case recovery and durability windows. The following bound captures this
necessary time budget.

Remark 3

(Necessary time-budget for resilience)
.

Under Assumption
3
, a necessary condition for the resilience
specification to be satisfiable is

\delta\;>\;\max\big\{\ \alpha_{1}+\beta_{1},\ \ \alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}\ \big\}.

Indeed, within any inter-disturbance interval one must (i) complete epistemic recovery (at most
\alpha_{1}
) and maintain it for at least
\beta_{1}
, and (ii) after epistemic recovery (at most
\alpha_{1}
), complete action recovery (at most
\alpha_{2}
) and maintain it for at least
\beta_{2}
; hence the interval must exceed both sums. This condition is
necessary but not sufficient
: satisfying it does not by itself
guarantee resilience; appropriate epistemic/action policies and their success
properties are also required.

For the reminder of this section, we work under Assumptions
1
–
3
and we assume that the time-budget bound in Remark
3
holds.

V-A
Achieving Epistemic Resilience

Let
\varphi_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
be a formula representing an environmental condition that holds true prior to the violation time
t_{v}
, but becomes false at
t_{v}
. Let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{2},\cdots,\varphi_{K}\}
be a set of
K-1
mutually exclusive hypotheses, each generated from the language
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\text{time}}(\Omega)
. Each hypothesis characterizes the same environmental aspect, with one hypothesis describing the new condition following a disturbance. Since these hypotheses are mutually exclusive, they cannot be true simultaneously.

To enable agents to detect such environmental changes (that is, to distinguish when the previously valid condition
\varphi_{1}
no longer holds and a new hypothesis in
\Phi
becomes true) we quantify discrepancies between the agents’ internal predictions and the actual environment. As stated in Section
II
, we introduce a measure of prediction inconsistency based on observation residuals. In particular, for any agent
i\in N
, the prediction residual is
\Upsilon\!\big(\,\boldsymbol{y}_{i,t},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i,t}\,\big)
, with
\boldsymbol{y}_{i,t}=s_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i,t}=\varrho_{i}(\iota_{i,t-1},u^{\mathrm{ext}}_{i,t})
, and
\Upsilon
is any distance measure on
Y
(e.g., the
\ell_{2}
-norm). Fix a threshold
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, a window length
L\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, and an exceedance-count threshold
e_{\text{th}}\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
. Define the count of threshold exceedances in the last
L
steps as:

e_{i,t}\;:=\;\sum_{t^{\prime}=t-L+1}^{t}\mathbf{1}\{\,\Upsilon\!\big(\,\boldsymbol{y}_{i,t^{\prime}},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i,t^{\prime}}\,\big)>\varepsilon_{1}\,\}.

(8)

A contradiction is declared by agent
i
at time
t
precisely when

e_{i,t}\ \geq\ e_{\text{th}}.

This exceedance-count mechanism ensures that isolated noisy readings or transient prediction jitters do not trigger contradiction detection; only persistent, statistically meaningful deviations between predicted and sensed observations initiate evidence gathering and epistemic revision.
If no contradiction is detected (i.e.,
e_{i,t}<e_{\text{th}}
), agent
i
selects either
refine
or
hold
, depending on whether new information has arrived via direct observations or messages (if any). Otherwise, agent
i
declares a contradiction at the first time
t
with at least
e_{\text{th}}
exceedances in the last
L
steps ((i.e.,
e_{i,t}\geq e_{\text{th}}
)), namely

t_{\text{det}}^{(i)}\;:=\;\min\Big\{\,t>t_{v}\;\Big|\;e_{i,t}\geq e_{\text{th}}\Big\}.

The earliest network detection time is

t_{\text{det}}\;:=\;\min_{i\in N}\,t_{\text{det}}^{(i)}.

The set of agents that detect the contradiction within the window

[t_{v},\ t_{v}+n_{\text{steps}}]
is

N_{\mathrm{det}}\;:=\;\big\{\,i\in N\ \big|\ t_{\text{det}}^{(i)}\in[t_{v},\ t_{v}+n_{\text{steps}}]\big\}.

By Assumption
2
, this set is nonempty
4
4

4

In practice, choosing
L\leq n_{\text{steps}}
ensures
t_{\text{det}}-t_{v}\leq n_{\text{steps}}
provided there exists at least one agent whose post-change residual exceeds the threshold at least
e_{\text{th}}
times per window.
. After confirming a contradiction, at times
t\geq t_{\text{det}}
, each agent
i\in N_{\mathrm{det}}
picks
u^{\mathrm{epi}}_{i,t}=\textsc{explore}
to explore the environment to collect evidence that discriminates among the hypotheses in
\Phi
. During this exploration phase, each new observation is mapped to a pairwise evidence increment. Specifically, for each pair of hypotheses
\varphi_{k},\varphi_{l}\in\Phi
, each agent
i\in N_{\mathrm{det}}
maintains a
cumulative pairwise evidence
score
\lambda_{i,t}^{k,l}
, which quantifies the evidence in favor of hypothesis
\varphi_{k}
versus hypothesis
\varphi_{l}
, defined as:

\Lambda_{i,t}^{k,l}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t_{\text{det}}+1}^{t}\lambda_{i,t^{\prime}}^{k,l},\qquad k\neq l,

where the per-step score
\lambda_{i,t^{\prime}}^{k,l}
is bounded and has a positive mean gap under the true hypothesis (e.g., log-likelihood ratio, discriminative margin, or consistency vote).
Based on these cumulative scores, agent
i\in N_{\mathrm{det}}
selects its current best hypothesis
\varphi_{\hat{k}_{i}(t)}
via a maximin rule:

\varphi_{\hat{k}_{i}(t)}\;\in\;\argmax_{k:\varphi_{k}\in\Phi}\ r_{i,t}^{k},\quad\text{where}\quad r_{i,t}^{k}\;:=\;\min_{l:\varphi_{l}\in\Phi\setminus\varphi_{k}}\ \Lambda_{i,t}^{k,l}.

Note that any fixed tie–breaking rule may be used. With these maximin scores in place, exploration proceeds until the leading hypothesis is uniformly ahead by a predefined margin
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
. Agent
i
stops collecting evidence at the first time the chosen hypothesis is
sufficiently separated from all competitors:

\tau_{i}\;:=\;\min\Big\{\,t\geq t_{\text{det}}^{(i)}\ \Big|\ r^{\,\hat{k}_{i}(t)}_{i,t}\;\geq\;\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}\Big\}.

The threshold
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
is selected to attain a desired error bound, e.g.

\Pr\!\big\{\hat{k}_{i}(\tau_{i})\neq k^{\star}\big\}\leq\epsilon
,
where
k^{\star}
is the index of the true hypothesis.
Once the separation condition is met, exploration terminates and the agent announces its current best hypothesis. At time
t=\tau_{i}
, agent
i

broadcasts
a signed message

m_{i,\tau_{i}}\;=\;\bigl(\tau_{i},\ \mathcal{K}_{i}\varphi_{\hat{k}_{i}(\tau_{i})},\ r^{\hat{k}_{i}(\tau_{i})}_{i,\tau_{i}}\bigr)

to all other agents with time-to-live
\mathrm{TTL}=l_{\text{comm}}
. If multiple agents in
N_{\mathrm{det}}
broadcast conflicting hypotheses, the network resolves the disagreement by selecting the hypothesis with the highest cumulative evidence among all broadcasts received up to time
t
(with any fixed tie–breaking rule). Once the most plausible hypothesis is selected, the goal shifts from evidence gathering to belief alignment. Each agent
i\in N
sets
u^{\mathrm{epi}}_{i,t}=\textsc{revise}
to revise its epistemic state
\iota_{i,t}
so that all currently accessible worlds satisfy
\varphi_{\hat{k}}
. We then define the epistemic recovery time
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
as the first
t
such that all agents knows
\varphi_{\hat{k}}
:

t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\;:=\;\min\{\,t>t_{v}\mid(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{\hat{k}}\,\}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\;

\displaystyle=\;t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}-t_{v}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(t_{\text{det}}-t_{v})}_{\text{detection delay}}+\underbrace{(\tau_{\mathrm{max}}-t_{\text{det}})}_{\text{evidence aggregation}}+\underbrace{l_{\text{comm}}}_{\text{message propagation}}

\displaystyle\leq\;n_{\text{steps}}\;+\;(\tau_{\mathrm{max}}-t_{\text{det}})\;+\;l_{\text{comm}},

(9)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{i\in N_{\mathrm{det}}}\tau_{i}
is the time stamp of the last report. Note that the dependency of
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
and
\tau_{\mathrm{max}}
on
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
is left implicit for ease of presentation. Figure
5
depicts the proposed epistemic policy executed by each agent and the associated information flow.

Meeting epistemic recoverability specifications.
To meet the epistemic recoverability specification, the evidence threshold
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
should be chosen so that

\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})=t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}-t_{v}\;\leq\;\alpha_{1}.

(10)

Using the broadcast/commit rule, Equations (
9
) and (
10
), and Assumptions
1
-
2
a sufficient condition for recovering within at most
\alpha_{1}
steps is

\tau_{\max}-t_{\text{det}}\;\leq\;\alpha_{1}-n_{\text{steps}}-l_{\text{comm}},

(deterministically or with high probability). This requires that
\alpha_{1}\geq n_{\text{steps}}+l_{\text{comm}}
. If this fails, the pair
(\alpha_{1},\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})
is infeasible under the current sensing/communication regime.

Meeting epistemic durability specifications.
With worst–case disturbance spacing
\delta
(Assumption
3
and Remark
3
), the achieved epistemic durability is lower bounded by

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\ \geq\ \delta-\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}),

provided the post-change hypothesis is correctly identified and remains valid until the next disturbance. Indeed, the threshold
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
trades off the probability of a correct commit,

p(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}):=\Pr\{\hat{k}(t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}})=k^{\star}\}
,
against recovery time
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})
and in turns the durability time
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})
. A practical tuning rule is:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \max_{\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}}\quad&p(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\,\bigl(\delta-\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\bigr)\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&0\leq\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\ \leq\ \min\{\alpha_{1},\ \delta-\beta_{1}\},\end{aligned}

(11)

so that the durability budget is nonnegative and at least meets
\beta_{1}
. Intuitively: gather enough evidence to ensure correctness (so
the belief is likely to persist) but not so much that recovery
consumes the inter-disturbance interval. In practice, pick
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
to meet a target error while satisfying recoverability
(\alpha_{1})
and durability
(\beta_{1})
for nominal parameters (e.g.,
n_{\text{steps}},l_{\text{comm}}
). In turn, this reduces to a one–dimensional line search over a feasible interval
[\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}_{\min},\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}_{\max}]
; if the interval is empty, the specification is
infeasible
.

V-B
Achieving Action Resilience

After epistemic recovery at time
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
, agents must re-align their external
policies to the newly identified condition
\varphi_{\hat{k}}
and sustain
optimal performance. Thus, once beliefs are revised, each agent
i
runs a
best-policy identification loop over its finite candidate set

\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
. At times
t=t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}},t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}+1,\ldots
, agent
i
:

1.

Roll out.
Execute a short rollout of a candidate policy

\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
and record its task cost.

2.

Update.
For each
\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, maintain the
empirical mean cost
\hat{J}_{i,t}(\pi_{i})
and a confidence radius

\zeta_{i,t}(\pi_{i})
calibrated to confidence level
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
; the radius
shrinks as more rollouts are collected. Formally,
\hat{J}_{i,t}(\pi_{i})

is an estimator of the true performance value
J_{i}(\pi_{i};t)
introduced
in Section
IV
, and the confidence interval

[\hat{J}_{i,t}(\pi_{i})-\zeta_{i,t}(\pi_{i}),\,\hat{J}_{i,t}(\pi_{i})+\zeta_{i,t}(\pi_{i})]

is constructed so that, with high probability, it contains

J_{i}(\pi_{i};t)
for all
\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
.

3.

Select/stop.
Explore policies with the smallest lower confidence
bound and stop as soon as one policy is statistically separated from all
others; adopt a policy
\pi_{i}^{*}
if

\hat{J}_{i,t}(\pi_{i}^{*})+\zeta_{i,t}(\pi_{i}^{*})\;<\;\min_{\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}\setminus\{\pi_{i}^{*}\}}\big[\hat{J}_{i,t}(\pi_{i})-\zeta_{i,t}(\pi_{i})\big],

i.e., the worst plausible cost of
\pi_{i}^{*}
is below the best plausible cost of every other policy. Under this condition,
\pi_{i}^{*}
is, with
high probability, an element of the optimal set
\Pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opt}}

associated with
J_{i}(\cdot;t)
.

Having selected such a policy
\pi_{i}^{*}
, we now formalize when the system has
completed
action
recovery. The atomic proposition
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}
is defined
to hold exactly when the agent’s current external policy belongs to the set
\Pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opt}}
:

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\pi^{\mathrm{ext}}_{i,t}\in\Pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opt}}.

The group-level predicate is the conjunction

\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}\equiv\bigwedge_{i\in N}\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}},

which holds precisely when every agent executes one of its individually optimal
external policies. No joint optimality or equilibrium concept is assumed;
optimality is evaluated individually for each agent.

The
action recovery time
is therefore

t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}:=\min\{\,t\geq t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\mid(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}\,\},

and the recovery duration is

\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})=t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}-t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}},

where the dependence on
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
is omitted for readability.

Meeting action recoverability specifications.
To meet the action recoverability specification, the confidence level
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
should be chosen so that
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{act}})\;\leq\;\alpha_{2}
. If the best policy cannot be statistically separated before the deadline
\alpha_{2}
in accordance with
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
and
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
, then the
specification
(\alpha_{2},\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})
is
infeasible
under the current candidate set

\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
and noise/rollout budget. In that case one must adjust
\alpha_{2}
,
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
, and/or
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
, or inject stronger priors.

Meeting action durability specifications.
With worst–case inter–disturbance spacing
\delta
(Assumption
3
and Remark
3
), the
achieved
action durability satisfies

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})\;\geq\;\delta\;-\;\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\;-\;\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}}),

provided that the post–change hypothesis and the optimal policy are correctly identified. Using
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}})\leq\alpha_{1}
gives

\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})\;\geq\;\delta\;-\;\alpha_{1}\;-\;\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}}).

The confidence level
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
trades off between the probability of correct policy identification
p(\eta^{\mathrm{act}})
, against the recovery time
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})
and the durability time
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})
. A practical tuning rule is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \max_{\eta^{\mathrm{act}}}\quad&p(\eta^{\mathrm{act}})\,\Big(\delta-\alpha_{1}-\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{act}})\Big)\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&0\leq\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}(\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}})\leq\min\{\,\alpha_{2},\,\delta-\alpha_{1}-\beta_{2}\}.\end{aligned}

(12)

so the guaranteed durability budget is nonnegative and at least meets
\beta_{2}
.
Intuitively:
explore policies enough to ensure correctness (so the chosen policy is likely to persist)
but not so much that recovery consumes the inter-disturbance interval.
In practice,
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
is chosen to meet a target error, and and satisfy
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
under nominal sensing parameters. This reduces to a one–dimensional line search over a feasible interval

[\eta^{\mathrm{act}}_{\min},\,\eta^{\mathrm{act}}_{\max}]
; if the interval is empty, the specification is
infeasible
.

Computational complexity.

The proposed architecture separates epistemic and action layers, and the
computational overhead can be assessed per agent.
On the epistemic side, each agent maintains a finite hypothesis set
\Phi

and the associated cumulative pairwise evidence scores.
Updating the residual
\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{y}_{i,t},\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i,t})
requires

O\!(\dim Y)
computations, and maintaining the sliding-window
threshold–exceedance counter requires
O\!(1)
computation per step.
During the
explore
phase, updating all pairwise evidence scores and
computing the maximin statistic
r_{i,t}^{k}
require

O\!(|\Phi|^{2})
computations per update step. On the action side, each agent
i
performs best-policy identification over its
finite candidate set
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
.
Updating empirical means and confidence radii, together with evaluating the
stopping condition, requires
O\!(|\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}|)
computations per update step. Overall, the worst-case per-agent per-step computational complexity of the framework is

O\!\big(\dim Y\;+\;|\Phi|^{2}\;+\;|\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}|\big),

that is, linear in the observation dimension and in the number of candidate
external policies, and quadratic in the number of epistemic hypotheses. The framework is compatible with resource-constrained deployments because
its dominant computational terms depend on the cardinalities of the
hypothesis set
\Phi
, the external policy set
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, and the
dimension of the observation space
Y
. These quantities can be made small
by design (e.g., coarse-graining hypotheses, restricting candidate policies,
or reducing observation representations), thereby allowing system designers to
trade model resolution for computational footprint.

VI
Formal Verification of Resilience Properties

We evaluate formulas along an actual run
r
using
(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\cdot
as defined in Section
III
.
A disturbance at time
t_{v}
is a change in the environment such that a pre-change fact
\varphi_{1}

holds for all
t<t_{v}
along
r
, and the post-change fact
\varphi_{2}
holds for all
t\geq t_{v}

along
r
; agents’ relations
R_{i,t}
evolve with
t
.

Theorem 1

(
Soundness of the Resilience Specifications
)
.

If
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{v}},r,t_{v})\models\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}\land\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}
, then along
r
:

1.

The epistemic recovery time
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
defined in (
3
) satisfies
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\leq\alpha_{1}
, and the epistemic durability time defined in (
4
) satisfies
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\geq\beta_{1}
.

2.

The action recovery time
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}
defined in (
6
) satisfies
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}\leq\alpha_{2}
, and the action durability time defined in (
7
) satisfies
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}\geq\beta_{2}
.

Proof.

By the semantics of bounded
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{G}
,

(\mathcal{M}_{t_{v}},r,t_{v})\models\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}
implies there exists
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\in\{t_{v},\ldots,t_{v}+\alpha_{1}\}

such that
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}},r,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}})\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
and

(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime}},r,t^{\prime})\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
for all

t^{\prime}\in\{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}},\ldots,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}+\beta_{1}-1\}
. Hence
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\leq\alpha_{1}
and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\geq\beta_{1}
. Similarly,
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{v}},r,t_{v})\models\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}
yields
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}\in\{t_{v},\ldots,t_{v}+\alpha_{2}\}

such that
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}},r,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}})\models\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}
and

(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime}},r,t^{\prime})\models\pi^{\mathrm{opt}}
for all

t^{\prime}\in\{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}},\ldots,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}+\beta_{2}-1\}
. Thus
\Delta t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{act}}\leq\alpha_{2}
and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{dur}}^{\mathrm{act}}\geq\beta_{2}
.
∎

Corollary 1

(
Bounded-Horizon Completeness (Finite Counterexample)
)
.

If
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{v}},r,t_{v})\not\models\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}\land\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}
, then there exists a finite time

t\in[t_{v},\ t_{v}+B]
witnessing a violation, where

B\;:=\;\max\{\,\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1},\ \alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}\,\}.

Equivalently, to refute
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}\land\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}
it suffices to examine at most
B
steps
after the disturbance.

Proof.

Consider the epistemic part
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}\equiv(\neg\,\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2})\ \mathcal{U}_{[0,\alpha_{1}]}\ (\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta_{1})}\,\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2})
.
If
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{epistemic}}
fails at
t_{v}
, then either:
(i) no
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}\leq t_{v}+\alpha_{1}
exists with
(\mathcal{M}_{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}},r,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}})\models\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
,
so the failure is witnessed within
\alpha_{1}
steps; or
(ii) some such
t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}
exists but
\mathcal{G}_{[0,\beta_{1})}\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
fails, so there is
an
t^{\prime}\in\{t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}},\ldots,t_{\mathrm{rec}}^{\mathrm{epi}}+\beta_{1}-1\}
with
(\mathcal{M}_{t^{\prime}},r,t^{\prime})\models\neg\,\mathcal{E}_{N}\varphi_{2}
,
hence a witness within
\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}
steps.
An identical case split for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{action}}
yields a witness within
\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}
steps.
Taking the maximum of the two bounds gives
B
.
∎

Formal verification entails checking, at design time or at runtime, whether a multi-agent system satisfies the resilience properties under the stated assumptions. In particular, by Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
, verification reduces to bounded reasoning: it suffices to examine at most
B=\max\{\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1},\ \alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}\}
steps after a disturbance.
Since the verification is confined to a finite horizon, the resilience
specifications become compatible with standard bounded model-checking approaches,
such as SAT/SMT-based unrolling, explicit-state exploration over a window of
length
B
, and lightweight runtime monitors that track only the last
B

steps. Thus, the formal results established in this section enable
computationally tractable certification of recovery protocols, knowledge-update rules, and coordination strategies before deployment, as well as efficient runtime monitoring in dynamic environments.

VII
Numerical Results and Analysis

To show the effectiveness of our proposed framework, we study a distributed multi-agent decision-making under abrupt network stressors usecase. In particular, we study a group of networked agents
N
repeatedly selecting among
A=|U^{\mathrm{ext}}|
actions while the environment undergoes changes (stressors). All agents are assumed to share the same set of external actions, i.e.,
U^{\mathrm{ext}}_{1}=\cdots=U^{\mathrm{ext}}_{n}=U^{\mathrm{ext}}
. Each action, denoted
a
for ease of presentation where
a=\boldsymbol{u}^{\mathrm{ext}}\in U^{\mathrm{ext}}
, yields a Gaussian reward with variance
\sigma^{2}
and a mean
\mu_{a}(t)
that might change due to environmental stressors. This models operational regimes where external events suddenly alter performance; e.g., the activation of a strong interferer, mobility-driven shadowing, or the appearance/disappearance of a hotspot.
The group’s goal is to maximize total reward (equivalently, minimize cumulative
regret) over the horizon
T
. In the setting considered here, this
is equivalent to each agent
i
maximizing its own expected reward
J_{i}
, since
all agents face the same action set and reward structure.

This abstraction, commonly referred to in the literature as the multi-agent multi-armed bandit, encapsulates several canonical tasks in networked communication systems where fast, decentralized adaptation is required. In this case, external actions are also referred to as arms.

Classical solutions to the multi-arm bandit problem typically rely on statistical algorithms such as Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)
[
1
]
, Thompson Sampling
[
2
]
, or epsilon-greedy strategies
[
40
]
. These methods balance exploration and exploitation using statistical estimates of action rewards, confidence bounds, or posterior distributions. However, these strategies inherently assume that reward deviations are purely stochastic and unbiased. This assumption fails under adversarial or non-stationary conditions. To handle non-stationarity, one standard strategy is passive adaptation via fading memory. This approach, exemplified by Discounted-UCB, which down-weight stale data to track piecewise-constant drifts
[
16
]
.

In this work, we employ UCB as the underlying algorithm for all baselines and for our approach. In particular, for each action
a
, every agent
i
maintains two running statistics (i)
count
: the number of times agent
i
has pulled action
a
up to current time step, and (ii)
sum
: the cumulative sum of rewards agent
i
has observed from action
a
up to current time step. These two quantities are the sufficient statistics needed to form the empirical mean of action
a
;
\mu_{a}(t)
. Our implementation uses the Gaussian-UCB algorithm found in
[
24
, Chapter 7]
. In the cooperative baseline, agents do not share raw reward histories; they exchange only the per-action counts and sums with their neighbors and average them via a linear consensus step. After mixing, each agent scales the per-agent network averages by the group size
n
to approximate global counts and sums, forming a globalized empirical mean for use inside the UCB decision rule.

Baselines:
We simulate two standard schemes. In the
Independent Discounted-UCB
, each agent runs a discounted-UCB policy using only its own observations, where an exponential forgetting rate enables adaptation to non-stationary rewards. In the
Cooperative Discounted-UCB
, agents are connected through an undirected communication graph and apply a consensus-based discounted-UCB, sharing sufficient statistics with neighbors to accelerate adaptation.

Proposed approach:
We consider two variants of our epistemic (Kripke) framework. In the first variant, as
Light-Coop Kripke
, agents follow independent UCB dynamics and communicate only when announcing a detected change in their knowledge or belief state. In the second variant, referred to as
Cooperative Kripke
, agents operate under cooperative UCB from the outset; they not only exchange belief-change messages but also jointly perform cooperative recovery, pooling both evidence and action statistics to identify and realign to the new correct hypothesis.

Simulation Setup:
We simulate a network of
n=12
agents operating synchronously over a time horizon of
T=2500
steps. The action set consists of
|U^{\mathrm{ext}}|=16
actions (arms), each arm reward’s true mean is randomly picked from interval
[0.1,1.2]
. Agents are connected through an undirected communication graph instantiated as a ring topology. The environment follows a piecewise-stationary structure: each arm’s true mean remains constant within a segment and changes abruptly at a predefined change-point (unknown to agents) representing an external stressor. In the main experiment, the stressor occurs at
t=1400
, causing a reordering of the optimal arms. For cooperative algorithms, agents exchange local statistics with neighbors using Metropolis–Hastings weights and perform one consensus round per decision epoch. All methods are initialized with identical random seeds to ensure that they experience the same stochastic realizations of the environment across trials. Each configuration is repeated over
10
ndependent trials, and reported performance curves show the sample mean together with
95\%
confidence intervals computed over these trials. To improve visualization, the curves are lightly smoothed using a moving average with a window length of
50
time steps. Moreover, to examine resilience under different noise conditions, we conduct simulations for two noise levels:
\sigma\in\{0.5,1.0\}
. This allows us to compare how independent and cooperative exploration balance under varying uncertainty.

The resilience bounds
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
in our experiments are not algorithmic hyperparameters but designer-specified requirements on recovery and durability (see Section
IV
and Section
V
). Since the simulation environment contains only a
single
stressor, these specifications are chosen so that resilience remains feasible within the fixed horizon
T
. In contrast, the detection thresholds
(\varepsilon_{1},L,e_{\text{th}})
govern when contradictory evidence is considered persistent rather than transient. These are selected from pre-change residual statistics (empirically ensuring that the false-alarm probability remains negligible). Finally, the epistemic evidence threshold
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}}
and action confidence threshold
\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
are tuned using the one-dimensional rules in Equations (
11
) and (
12
), respectively.

We model the bandit environment within our epistemic logic as follows. The set of
possible worlds is

W=\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{H}\},

where each world
w_{h}
corresponds to a full vector of arm means

\mu^{(h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
. At any given time, exactly one world is the
true environment condition.

We introduce atomic propositions evaluated world-by-world:

•

Per-arm mean atoms:

p_{a,h}:\;\mu_{a}=\mu^{(h)}_{a}\qquad(a=1,\dots,A;\;h=1,\dots,H),

•

Actionable atoms:

b_{a}:\;a\;\text{is an optimal arm},\quad\text{ i.e., }\quad a\in\arg\max_{a^{\prime}}\mu_{a^{\prime}}.

The
world-identity
formula

\varphi_{h}\;=\;\bigwedge_{a=1}^{A}\,p_{a,h}

characterizes world
w_{h}
. Its actionable consequence is the formula

\psi_{h}\;=\;\bigvee_{a\in\arg\max_{j}\mu^{(h)}_{j}}b_{a},

which states that the selected arm is optimal in world
w_{h}
.
Log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) provide the cumulative pairwise evidence scores used
to discriminate among
\{\varphi_{h}\}_{h=1}^{H}
.

In our simulations, the environment remains in a single world

w_{h}
for a contiguous time interval, and switches deterministically
to another world only at predefined disturbance (stressor) times. We instantiate the abstract optimal-action proposition for agent
i
as

(\mathcal{M}_{t},r,t)\models\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_{i,t}\in\arg\max_{a}\mu_{a}(t),

i.e., agent
i
’s chosen arm is optimal under the true world at time
t
. Here, the external policy
\pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{ext}}
refers to the behavior that
directly determines the arm selected by agent
i
at time
t
, so that

a_{i,t}=\pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{ext}}
.
Thus,
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{opt}}
holds exactly when the agent’s external policy
\pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{ext}}
selects an arm with the highest mean reward under the current environment condition. In this setting, the set
\Pi_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opt}}
consists of all external policies that would be optimal under the current environment at time
t
. Therefore, the expected reward of agent
i
,
J_{i}
, is therefore determined by its external
policy together with the time-varying sequence of worlds

\bigl(w_{h(t)}\bigr)_{t\geq 0}
.

Finally, performance is assessed through standard metrics: fraction of agents selecting the optimal action, total and cumulative rewards, and cumulative regret over time. These shared settings provide a uniform benchmark for contrasting the independent, cooperative, and epistemic (Kripke-based) learning schemes in terms of adaptability, recovery speed, and overall performance under abrupt environmental shifts.

In contrast, Independent-UCB agents respond asynchronously: each agent explores on its own and gradually discovers the new optimum at different times. This de-synchronization produces a smoother aggregate recovery with smaller instantaneous loss but slower overall adaptation.

In contrast, the Kripke-based learners demonstrate significantly faster epistemic and action recovery. The LightCoop-Kripke (independent learning with epistemic communication) quickly detects the regime shift and begins realigning, but requires additional time for all agents to stabilize on the new optimum since action learning remains independent. The cooperative mode benefits from pooled information and thus attains faster epistemic alignment. \eta^{\mathrm{epi}}

Across all regimes, epistemic coordination is the key driver of resilience: our proposed epistemic policy yields shorter recovery windows and higher post-recovery stability than fading-memory baselines. The cooperative-Kripke mode achieves the fastest recovery and lowest regret by pooling both epistemic knwoledge and action statistics. VIII
Conclusion and Future Work

This work presented a novel formal framework for quantifying and designing multi-agent resilience in dynamic systems. We introduced a Kripke-based learning architecture that integrates epistemic resilience (the ability to detect, share, and recover accurate knowledge of world conditions) with action resilience (the ability to realign and sustain optimal behavior after disruptions). Our formulation links these dimensions through measurable recovery and durability times, providing a principled definition of resilience that bridges learning, communication, and control. Through extensive simulations, we demonstrated that the proposed Cooperative-Kripke and LightCoop-Kripke architectures outperform classical baselines such as independent and cooperative discounted-UCB. The results show that epistemic coordination significantly improves both recovery speed and post-change stability, even under limited communication. In particular, Cooperative-Kripke achieves rapid epistemic and action recovery while maintaining high cumulative rewards and minimal regret, whereas LightCoop-Kripke achieves slightly lower performance but with substantially reduced communication load. The experiments also verified the recoverability–durability trade-off, showing that stricter evidence thresholds prolong the evidence collection phase albeit longer stability after recovery.

A promising research avenue lies in formulating the resilience design problem as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP). Such a formulation can encode the logical constraints of epistemic transitions, the temporal budgets on recovery and durability (
\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}
), communication and computation budgets as linear and integer constraints, enabling exact computation of feasible resilience specifications or optimal parameter selections (
\eta^{\mathrm{epi}},\eta^{\mathrm{act}}
) under communication and sensing limits. Solving this MILP provides a systematic method for synthesizing resilient agent configurations and verifying resilience guarantees before deployment. This optimization perspective complements the learning-based approach by offering design-time verification and run-time resilience in a unified framework.

An additional direction for future work is the incorporation of explicit
collision-aware dynamics. In the present formulation, interactions among agents
are treated as part of the environment and therefore enter each agent’s
epistemic state. This allows potential collisions to be interpreted as
unexpected observations that trigger the same epistemic-update mechanisms used
for other stressors. However, extending the framework with explicit
collision models (such as contention-aware reward structures, joint-action
feasibility constraints, or coordination protocols) would enable proactive
avoidance and richer multi-agent interactions in collision-prone settings.
Finally, while our algorithms incorporate safeguards against noisy observations through residual thresholds, exceedance counting, and evidence margins, modeling unreliable communication remains an important future extension.
