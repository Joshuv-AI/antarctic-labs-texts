Asymptotic Momentum of Dirac Particles in One Space Dimension
Kabir Narayanan, Abigail Perryman, A. Shadi Tahvildar-Zadeh

Abstract
We analyze the trajectories of a massive particle in one space dimension whose motion is guided by a spin-half wave function that evolves according to the free Dirac equation, with its initial wave function being a Gaussian wave packet with a nonzero expected value of momentum k k and the positive expected value of energy E = m 2 + k 2 E=\sqrt{m^{2}+k^{2}} . We prove that at large times, the wave function becomes locally a plane wave, which corresponds to trajectories with fixed values for asymptotic momentum k k and asymptotic energy E E or − E -E . The sign of the asymptotic energy is determined by the initial position of the particle. Particles with negative energy will have an asymptotic velocity that is in the opposite direction of their momentum. The proof uses the stationary phase approximation method, for which we establish a rigorous error bound.

Introduction

In his 1923 paper “A Quantum Theory of the Scattering of X-Rays by Light Elements,” Arthur Compton successfully explained the scattering of high-energy “light quanta” in an X-ray beam off electrons in a graphite plate by combining results of the (at the time) new quantum theory with the old classical laws of energy and momentum conservation in particle mechanics. Compton observed that the rays reflected from the graphite plate had a lower frequency than the incident ones, and using the Einstein–deBroglie relations for the quantum of light’s energy and momentum,
E=\hbar\omega
,
\textbf{p}=\hbar\textbf{k}
(with
\omega=c|\textbf{k}|
), he found that the shift in frequency corresponded exactly to the energy of the scattered electron. He gave a formula for the associated scattering angle that he then showed matched his experimental results very well. This work won him a Nobel Prize in 1927, and led to more general acceptance of Einstein’s particle theory of light.

While “photon” was not yet in Compton’s vocabulary, he writes that in contrast to the classical wave theory of light, “we may suppose that any particular quantum of X-rays is not scattered by all the electrons in the radiator, but spends all of its energy upon some particular electron.”
[
2
]
Thus coherent light rays in Compton’s theory behave much like classical billiard balls, and indeed Compton makes use of classical conservation principles of particle mechanics—which are by no means fundamental in interacting quantum systems—to predict the behavior of such particles. By contrast, in orthodox quantum mechanics we cannot speak of light rays, or even electrons, as particles until they are measured, and can only make statistical predictions about the outcomes of measurements (either by taking the expected values of the
observables
(self-adjoint operators) involved, or by postulating collapse rules). Modern renditions of Compton scattering require Quantum Field Theory, in which fields are fundamental, not particles, since the latter can be created and annihilated during the scattering process.

Yet in
[
7
]
Kiessling
et al.
demonstrated that an interacting quantum-mechanical model of a fixed number of electron and photon
particles
in one space dimension is possible via a Lorentz-covariant generalization of Bohmian mechanics known as the “Hypersurface Bohm-Dirac theory”
[
4
]
. Numerical experiments performed in
[
7
]
exhibit photon and electron trajectories which bounce off one another, akin to Compton scattering. Mysteries however remain regarding the relationship between the quantum mechanical notions of energy and momentum associated with operators acting on the Hilbert space of wave functions, and their definite Bohmian counterparts, which are well-defined for a given massive particle’s trajectory.

In this context several questions can be asked: to what extent does the relativistic momentum and energy along a free electron’s trajectory align with the expected values of the corresponding operators? Can we meaningfully ascribe an ‘outgoing’ momentum to a quantum particle despite entanglement effects? Can Compton’s plane wave assumption (used to derive Compton’s formula) be justified using a Bohmian formulation of the scattering process?

In this paper we address the first of these questions by examining the trajectories of a single free electron, and prove that at large times, the electron wave function becomes
locally
a plane wave, at least in the case the initial wave function is a Gaussian wave packet with a non-zero expected value of momentum
k
and with the corresponding positive expected value of energy
E
. The freely evolving wave function then guides the particle to eventually travel with constant velocity corresponding to fixed values of momentum
k_{\mbox{\tiny asym}}=k
and energy
E_{\mbox{\tiny asym}}=\pm E
. The sign of the asymptotic energy is determined by the initial position of the particle, and negative energy particles acquire velocities that are in the opposite direction of their momenta.

Nonrelativistic particles

Free Dirac Particles

According to Dirac

3
]
, a massive spin-half particle such as the electron has a wave function:

\psi_{\text{el}}(t,s)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{-}(t,s)\\
\psi_{+}(t,s)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega t}{2}\int_{s-t}^{s+t}\frac{J_{1}\left(\omega\sqrt{t^{2}-(s-\sigma)^{2}}\right)}{\sqrt{t^{2}-(s-\sigma)^{2}}}\overset{\circ}{\psi_{\pm}}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

where we have set

Using integration by parts, we may rewrite the solution more simply as:

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\overset{\circ}{\psi_{\pm}}(s\pm t)

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega}{2}\int_{s-t}^{s+t}J_{1}\left(\omega\sqrt{t^{2}-(s-\sigma)^{2}}\right)\frac{\sqrt{t\mp(s-\sigma)}}{\sqrt{t\pm(s-\sigma)}}\overset{\circ}{\psi_{\pm}}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

As is apparent from this form of the solution, each component gets transported in one direction with the velocity of light while developing a tail that gets smeared everywhere inside the light cone, thereby propagating also in the other direction. The components of the

(16)

We define the electron

(17)

The

\frac{dQ}{dt}=v^{(\psi_{\text{el}})}(t,Q(t)).

(18)

As we shall see, the fact that an arbitrary solution of (

2.1
Cayley-Klein parameters and the Bloch sphere representation

The spinor
\psi_{\text{el}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
has four real degrees of freedom. It can be decomposed in the following form, known as the
Cayley-Klein parametrization
:

\psi_{\text{el}}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{-}\\
\psi_{+}\end{pmatrix}=Re^{i\Phi/2}\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\frac{\Theta}{2}e^{i\Omega/2}}\\
\sin{\frac{\Theta}{2}e^{-i\Omega/2)}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}.

(19)

Thus
\psi_{\text{el}}
has an alternative description in terms of the four real parameters
(R,\Theta,\Omega,\Phi)
, each a function of
t
and
s
. There is a connection between these and unitary representations of the orthogonal group:
In general, we can represent an element of the
special unitary group

SU(2)
by two complex numbers
x,y
such that
|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}=1
.
SU(2)
maps surjectively (it is a double cover) onto the orthogonal group of rotations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, namely
SO(3)
.
This fact can be used to associate the 2-component spinor
\psi
with a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\vec{n}(\psi)=\frac{1}{R^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2Re(\psi_{-}\psi_{+}^{*})\\
2Im(\psi_{-}\psi_{+}^{*})\\
|\psi_{-}|^{2}-|\psi_{+}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

Plugging in it the expressions for
\psi_{\pm}
from (
19
), we obtain a familiar expression for a unit 3-vector in terms of two of the Cayley-Klein parameters:

\vec{n}(\psi_{\text{el}})=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\Theta\cos\Omega\\
\sin\Theta\sin\Omega\\
\cos\Theta\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

As the wave function
\psi
evolves according to (
10
), the unit vector
\vec{n}
in (
21
) traces out a path on the unit sphere (often called
the Bloch sphere
), providing a convenient way of visualizing the directional changes in
\psi
.

Observe that the third component of the vector in (
21
) is precisely the Bohmian velocity
v^{(\psi)}
in (
17
). In addition to the velocity, one can also define local expressions for the momentum
p^{(\psi)}
, and energy
E^{(\psi)}
of the state
\psi
in terms of the Cayley-Klein parameters
\Theta
and
\Omega
(see
[
9
]

v^{(\psi)}=\cos\Theta,\qquad p^{(\psi)}=m_{\mbox{\tiny el}}\cot{\Theta}\sec\Omega,\qquad E^{(\psi)}=m_{\mbox{\tiny el}}\csc{\Theta}\sec\Omega.

(22)

Note that these are fields defined on the 1-particle configuration space. Thus, similar to the velocity, we can define the momentum and energy of a Dirac particle with trajectory
q(t)
to be
p^{(\psi)}(t,q(t))
and
E^{(\psi)}(t,q(t))
, respectively. At this point, however, it is not clear if these will have any connection to the physical quantities of the same name that could be observed in an experiment. The aim of this paper is to establish such a connection.

2.2
Classical and Quantum notions of Energy and Momentum

In classical mechanics, momentum and energy are of great importance in analyzing particle dynamics. We define non-relativistic momentum and kinetic energy as
mv
and
\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}
, and in relativistic mechanics, we adjust these to:

p=\gamma mv,\qquad E=\gamma m,\qquad\gamma:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}.

(23)

We can see that these coincide with the quantities we called energy and momentum in (
22
) only in the case
\Omega=0
, but not otherwise. This in hindsight should not be surprising since the law of motion for quantum particles is not the classical one. In particular the velocity and momentum of a quantum particle do not have to be aligned, and its energy can be negative.
From now on, we will refer to quantities
p^{(\psi)}
and
E^{(\psi)}
in (
22
) as the
Bohmian momentum
and
Bohmian energy
of the particle.

In orthodox quantum mechanics, we replace observable quantities such as
p
and
E
with operators on the Hilbert space of our wave functions. The momentum operator (in 1-D) is defined as

P:=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial s}.

(24)

Consequently, we may try to associate the momentum of the system at some time with the expected value of this operator, which we define using the inner product on our space:

\langle\psi,P\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi^{\dagger}P\psi\,ds.

(25)

Similarly we may associate the Hamiltonian operator, denoted by
H
, with energy. The Hamiltonian of a system is the operator appearing on the right-hand side of the general Schrodinger Equation that the wave function
\psi
is supposed to satisfy:

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi=H\psi.

(26)

We can take its expected value,
\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle
, to find a candidate for the value for the energy of the system as in (
25
), just as we did for momentum. It is clear however that these must be very different quantities from their Bohmian counterparts. In particular, since the operators
P
and
H
both commute with
H
, both of these expectations are conserved, i.e. independent of time, while the momentum and energy of the particle changes since the guiding velocity
v^{(\psi)}
is time-dependent in general, as we already observed in the non-relativistic case. We can still hope that, just as in the non-relativistic case, these Bohmian counterparts have constant
asymptotic
values that are the same, or at least close to, the expected values of momentum and energy operators.

2.3
Spinor Wave Packets

When solving for trajectories of the electron, we are going to assume that the initial wave function is a
Gaussian wave packet
. This means that the two components of the initial wave function are of the form

\mathring{\psi}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}e^{i\Omega_{0}/2}\\[5.0pt]
\sin\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}e^{-i\Omega_{0}/2}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{2\pi\sigma}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}+ik_{0}s},

(27)

with
\Theta_{0}\in[0,\pi]
,
\Omega_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
,
\sigma>0
, and
k_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
free parameters representing the initial Bloch sphere angles, standard deviation of position, and (as we shall see) mean momentum, respectively.
Unlike plane waves, these Gaussian packets are elements of the Hilbert space.
Of course, they are an idealization, but when plugged into (
15
), they yield a normal distribution with mean
0
and standard deviation
\sigma
, which is physically compelling since we know that normal distributions saturate the Uncertainty Principle (UP), so this type of data can represent a particle that,
to the extent allowed by the UP
, is localized in both position and momentum.

The main drawback of Gaussian wave packet initial data for Dirac’s equation is that, unlike the non-relativistic case of free Schrödinger flow, the free Dirac flow (in the massive case) does
not
preserve the Gaussian profile. As we shall see, a single wave packet, as it evolves under the Dirac flow, will break up into several ones that will continue to interfere with each other so long as their supports overlap. The asymptotic analysis of the wave function and its effect on particle trajectories is thus more delicate than what we saw above in the non-relativistic case.

2.4
Expected Values for Wave Packets

Given a self-adjoint
H
, the general Schrodinger equation in (
26
) yields a
unitary flow

\psi(t,s)=e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi(0,s)

It follows from the above and the self-adjointness of the Dirac Hamiltonian that the expected value of any self-adjoint operator acting on the wave function is conserved for all time

, so long as it commutes with
H
. This means we can construct Gaussian initial data with a particular expected value for the momentum operator, and we know that this expected value is the same at all later times.

From now on, we will be setting
\hbar=1

.

We recall that

, thus the plane wave

is a (pseudo-)eigen function of the operator

and a sign, and that we have

H\Phi^{\pm}_{k}=\pm\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}}\Phi^{\pm}_{k}.

In other words,

, i.e. the spectrum of

is the complement of the interval

\Phi^{\pm}_{k}(s):=e^{iks}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\Theta_{\pm,k}}{2}e^{i\Omega_{\pm}/2}\\[5.0pt]

(28)

where

\Theta_{+,k}:=\tan^{-1}\frac{m}{k},\quad\Theta_{-,k}:=\pi-\Theta_{+,k},\qquad\Omega_{+}:=0,\qquad\Omega_{-}:=\pi.

(29)

Theorem 2.1
.

Given initial data of the form

\mathring{\psi}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}+iks}\begin{pmatrix}k/E\\

with momentum

, the solution to the initial value problem for the Dirac equation (
10
,
12
) as

asymptotically approaches the superposition of two wave packets with energies
E
and
-E
, both with momentum

, that travel in opposite directions. As a consequence, the worldline of a typical particle eventually becomes a straight line, with the particle traveling with a constant velocity to the left or to the right, depending on its initial position.

The proof of the above theorem relies on the Stationary Phase Approximation (SPA) method, applied at a fixed, sufficiently large time
t
and arbitrary position
s
to the components
\psi_{\pm}
of the solution (
2
) of the Dirac equation. Under the assumptions of the theorem these are oscillatory integrals, with the frequency of the oscillation proportional to the parameter
\omega=\frac{mc}{\hbar}
. Thus to apply SPA we need to study the limit
\omega\to\infty
, a regime known as
the classical limit
of the theory (see e.g.
[
8
]
for definition and references.)

3
Classical Limit of the Dirac Equation

The free Dirac equation for a single particle in one space dimension, in natural units where
\hbar=m_{el}=c=1
,
is

-i\gamma^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\psi+\psi=0.

(38)

In Hamiltonian form, this becomes

i\partial_{t}\psi=-i\alpha\partial_{s}\psi+\beta\psi,

(39)

where
\alpha:=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}
and
\beta=\gamma^{0}
.
We assume that initial data has been prescribed for this at
t=0
:

\psi(0,s)=\mathring{\psi}(s).

In the units we have picked, the Compton Wavelength of the electron,
\lambda_{CWE}=1
. To study the classical limit, we imagine that there is a characteristic macroscopic length
L\gg 1
associated with the system (e.g. distance of a detector from the source for the particle.) We introduce
macroscopic variables

t^{\prime}=\frac{t}{L},\qquad s^{\prime}=\frac{s}{L},

and we define the wave function in these variables as

\psi^{L}(t^{\prime},s^{\prime}):=\sqrt{L}\psi(Lt^{\prime},Ls^{\prime}).

This definition assures that for normalized wavefunctions
\psi
, we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi^{L}(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})|^{2}ds^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(Lt^{\prime},s)|^{2}ds=1\qquad\forall t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},

so that normalization is preserved.
The equation satisfied by
\psi^{L}
is

i\partial_{t^{\prime}}\psi^{L}=-i\alpha\partial_{s^{\prime}}\psi^{L}+\omega{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\beta}\psi^{L},

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

denote the smallest positive zero of the Bessel function

. For all

there exists

then there exists a constant

such that for all

and every

Moreover,
\beta(\alpha)\in(0,\frac{j_{0}^{2}}{4(\omega t)^{2}})

Proof.

Let

. Then

\chi=(\alpha-A(\beta))\phi(t+s)-\frac{\omega}{2}\int_{s+t-2\beta t}^{s+t}\frac{J_{1}(\omega\sqrt{t^{2}-(s-\sigma)^{2}})}{\sqrt{t^{2}-(s-\sigma)^{2}}}(t-(s-\sigma))\left(\phi(\sigma)-\phi(s+t)\right)\,d\sigma,

where
\phi(x):=f(x)e^{ip_{0}\omega x}
and

A(\beta):=\frac{\omega}{2}\int_{0}^{2\beta t}\frac{J_{1}(\omega\sqrt{2t\tau-\tau^{2}})}{\sqrt{2t\tau-\tau^{2}}}(2t-\tau)\,d\tau.

Using the assumption on the initial data profile

|\phi(s+t-\tau)-\phi(s+t)|\leq\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{\prime}|\ \tau\leq C(\frac{1}{\sigma^{3/2}}+\frac{\omega|p_{0}|}{\sigma^{1/2}})\tau=:M\tau.

Thus we have
\chi=(\alpha-A)\phi(s+t)+B
where

so that using the simple estimate
|J_{1}(\omega x)/x|\leq\omega/2
for all

we obtain

\displaystyle|B|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2tM(\omega t)^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}z(1-z)dz\leq tM(\omega t)^{2}\beta^{2}\leq\frac{CMt}{(\omega t)^{2}},

provided, as we shall see below,
\beta<{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\sqrt{C}}/(\omega t)^{2}
. Recalling the definition of

|B|<C|p_{0}|/(\sqrt{\sigma}\omega t).

On the other hand, by the Taylor series for

we have that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\omega t)^{2m+2}}{m!(m+1)!}\int_{0}^{\beta}(1-z)^{m+1}z^{m}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\beta(\omega t)^{2})^{m+1}}{(m+1)!(m+1)!}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\omega t)^{2m+2}}{m!(m+1)!}\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{1-(1-z)^{m+1}}{z}z^{m+1}dz,

and by the Taylor series expansion for

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\beta(\omega t)^{2})^{m+1}}{(m+1)!(m+1)!}=1-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\beta(\omega t)^{2})^{m}}{(m!)^{2}}=1-J_{0}(2\omega t\sqrt{\beta})=:g(\beta).

The function

\beta\in(0,\frac{j_{0}^{2}}{4(\omega t)^{2}})

Meanwhile,

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{0}^{\beta}\frac{1-(1-z)^{m+1}}{z}z^{m+1}dz

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\beta}\sup_{0\leq z\leq\beta}\left|\frac{1-(1-z)^{m+1}}{z}\right|z^{m+1}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{m+1}{m+2}\beta^{m+2}.

Thus

which establishes the claim.

3.3
Analysis of Trajectories

We turn our attention to the guiding equation (
18
), taking its right-hand-side to be the velocity field
corresponding to the stationary phase approximation
U
of the wave function
\psi
satisfying (
40
), with initial data of the form (
46
). For the sake of simplicity, we will only do this for
\vartheta=0
, i.e. the case when the initial wave packet coincides with a positive energy eigenfunction. By Corollary
3.2.1
we thus have

U=\begin{pmatrix}U_{-}\\
U_{+}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2E_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}(E_{0}+p_{0})\phi_{-}+(E_{0}-p_{0})\phi_{+}\\
\phi_{-}-\phi_{+}\end{pmatrix},

(60)

where
\phi_{\pm}
are defined in (
59
). Recalling the relations (
22
) with
\Omega_{0}=0
,

v_{0}=\cos\Theta_{0},\qquad p_{0}=\cot\Theta_{0},\qquad E_{0}=\csc\Theta_{0},

(61)

we have

U_{-}=\cos^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}\phi_{-}+\sin^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}\phi_{+},\qquad U_{+}=\sin\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}\cos\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}(\phi_{-}-\phi_{+}).

(62)

The corresponding velocity field is therefore

v^{(U)}(t,s)=\cos\Theta_{0}\frac{\cos^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}|\phi_{-}|^{2}-\sin^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}|\phi_{+}|^{2}}{\cos^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}|\phi_{-}|^{2}+\sin^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}|\phi_{+}|^{2}}+\sin\Theta_{0}\frac{2\mbox{Re}(\phi_{-}^{*}\phi_{+})\sin\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}\cos\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}}{\cos^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}|\phi_{-}|^{2}+\sin^{2}\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}|\phi_{+}|^{2}}.

(63)

From the above it’s already clear that, once the supports of
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{+}
are disjoint, if the particle finds itself in the support of
\phi_{-}
, its velocity will be
v_{0}
, and if it’s in the support of
\phi_{+}
, the velocity will be
-v_{0}
. We now need an ODE argument to turn this into a statement about trajectories.

Consider the guiding equation

\frac{ds}{dt}=v^{(U)}(t,s),

(64)

where the velocity field
v^{(\psi)}
on the right hand side of (
18
) is replaced with
v^{(U)}
in (
63
). Without loss of generality, we are going to assume
p_{0}>0
. We introduce some new variables to simplify this ODE:

x:=\frac{\sqrt{v_{0}}}{\sigma}t,\quad y:=\frac{\sqrt{v_{0}}}{\sigma}s,\quad\eta:=\tan\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2},\quad a:=\frac{2\sigma E_{0}}{\sqrt{v_{0}}}.

(65)

Using (
44
) the ODE then becomes

\frac{dy}{dx}=\cos\Theta_{0}\frac{e^{2xy}-\eta^{2}}{e^{2xy}+\eta^{2}}+\sin\Theta_{0}\frac{2\eta e^{xy}}{e^{2xy}+\eta^{2}}\cos(a\omega x):=F(x,y).

(66)

We can make the above statement about trajectories quantitative by finding
barriers
for solutions of (
66
): We look for curves
y=B_{\pm}(x)
in the
xy
plane with the property that

F(x,y)\leq 0\mbox{ for }y\leq B_{-}(x),\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad F(x,y)\geq 0\mbox{ for }y\geq B_{+}(x).

(67)

If such curves are found, the region in the
xy
plane with
y\leq B_{-}(x)
would be an invariant region, meaning it is mapped into itself under the forward flow, and similarly the region
y\geq B_{+}(x)
. Also in these two regions,
y
becomes a monotone function of
x
, so that in the former case, the trajectory continues towards the left, and in the latter case, towards the right. For fixed
x>0
, it is evident that as
|y|
becomes large, the oscillatory term in the right-hand-side
F(x,y)
in (
66
) dies out and we have

\lim_{y\to\infty}F(x,y)=\cos\Theta_{0}=v_{0},\qquad\lim_{y\to-\infty}F(x,y)=-\cos\Theta_{0}=-v_{0}.

(68)

Hence the velocity, and therefore the momentum, of the particle becomes a constant, as advertised.

To find the
B_{\pm}
curves, we first find
y_{0}(x)
such that
F(x,y_{0}(x))=0
. Let
X:=e^{xy}>0
. Then

F(x,y)=\frac{\cos\Theta_{0}}{X^{2}+\eta^{2}}(X^{2}+2\tan\Theta_{0}\cos(a\omega x)\eta X-\eta^{2})

so that if we define

y_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{x}\ln\frac{\tan\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}}{\tan\Theta_{0}\cos a\omega x+\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}\Theta_{0}\cos^{2}a\omega x}}.

we would then have
F(x,y_{0}(x))=0
and moreover

y\leq y_{0}(x)\implies F(x,y)\leq 0,\qquad y\geq y_{0}(x)\implies F(x,y)\geq 0.

We may thus define

B_{\pm}(x):=\frac{C_{\pm}(\Theta_{0})}{x},\qquad C_{\pm}:=\ln\frac{\tan\frac{\Theta_{0}}{2}}{\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}\Theta_{0}}\mp\tan\Theta_{0}},

(69)

so that

and (
67

Next we observe that the curves

are branches of hyperbolas

\Theta_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4}

C_{+}=0
, so that the hyperbola degenerates to

. Also note that
C_{-}<0
for all
\Theta_{0}
, while
C_{+}
changes sign at
\Theta_{0}=\frac{\pi}{4}

\Theta_{0}<\frac{\pi}{4}

\Theta_{0}>\frac{\pi}{4}

4
Summary and Outlook

In this paper we studied the relationship between the Bohmian momentum (
23
) and the expected value of the momentum operator (
25
) for solutions of the Dirac equation (
10
) with Gaussian wave packet initial data of the form (
27
), for which the latter quantity is
k_{0}
. The initial data depends on parameters
\Theta_{0},\Omega_{0},\sigma
, and
k_{0}
, while another parameter
\omega:=\frac{m_{\text{el}}c}{\hbar}
, appears in the equation. We focused on wave packets that initially coincide with a positive energy eigenfunction, so that
\Theta_{0}
and
\Omega_{0}
are determined via (
29
) in terms of
m_{\text{el}}
and
k_{0}
. We derived the stationary phase approximation for the solution and showed that it corresponds to two wave packets with the same momentum and opposite values of energy that travel in opposite directions, and thus their supports become almost disjoint after enough time has passed. By analyzing the guiding equation (
18
) we showed that as a result, particles will eventually be influenced only by one of these asymptotic wave packets, so that their momentum becomes constant.
Since the momentum approaches a fixed value, the wave function in effect becomes plane-wave-like after enough time has passed, which provides some justification for Compton’s use of the plane wave dispersion relations in his calculations, at least as far as the incoming electron is concerned. Doing a similar analysis for the incoming photon is hampered by the Bohmian momentum being ill-defined for a massless particle, but we believe similar results can be obtained through a limiting argument. Meanwhile, justifying Compton’s use of plane-wave values for the
outgoing
particles requires addressing the entanglement that would result from their interaction. We will leave these issues for future works.
