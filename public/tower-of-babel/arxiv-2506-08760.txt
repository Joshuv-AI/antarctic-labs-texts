Akaike information criterion for segmented regression models
Kazuki Nakajima

Abstract
In segmented regression, when the regression function is continuous at the change-points that are the boundaries of the segments, it is also called joinpoint regression, and the analysis package developed by Kim et al. 2000 has become a standard tool for analyzing trends in longitudinal data in the field of epidemiology. In addition, it is sometimes natural to expect the regression function to be discontinuous at the change-points, and in the field of epidemiology, this model is used in Jiang et al. 2022 , which is considered important due to the analysis of COVID-19 data. On the other hand, model selection is also indispensable in segmented regression, including the estimation of the number of change-points; however, it can be said that only BIC-type information criteria have been developed. In this paper, we derive an information criterion based on the original definition of AIC, aiming to minimize the divergence between the true structure and the estimated structure. Then, using the statistical asymptotic theory specific to the segmented regression, we confirm that the penalty for the change-point parameter is 6 in the discontinuous case. On the other hand, in the continuous case, we show that the penalty for the change-point parameter remains 2 despite the rapid change in the derivative coefficients. Through numerical experiments, we observe that our AIC tends to reduce the divergence compared to BIC. In addition, through analyzing the same real data as in Jiang et al. 2022 , we find that the selection between continuous and discontinuous using our AIC yields new insights and that our AIC and BIC may yield different results. Keywords AIC, Brownian motion, change-point analysis, COVID-19 data, joinpoint regression, model selection, piecewise regression, random walk, statistical asymptotic theory AMS 2000 Subject Classifications Primary: 62J02, Secondary: 62F12.

Introduction

Segmented regression, also known as piecewise regression, can be regarded as a type of change-point analysis and has been used extensively for a long time.
When the regression function is continuous at the change-points that are the boundaries of the segments, it is also called joinpoint regression.
The analysis package developed by
Kim et al. 2000
has become a standard tool to investigate trends in time series data in the field of medical epidemiology.
For example, it is used in annual statistical reports of large-scale projects analyzing central nervous system tumors (the latest is
Ostrom et al. 2023
), and it is also used in analyses of medically important topics such as cancer and cardiovascular risk (
Howlader et al. 2020
and
He et al. 2021
).
Although the slope of the regression function changes in joinpoint regression, it is natural to assume that the value of the regression function itself changes, that is, the regression function is discontinuous at the change-points, depending on the situation.
In fact, in the field of epidemiology, this model is used for analysis in
Jiang et al. 2022
, which is treated as a discussion paper in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B.
The data analyzed in that paper are the number of new COVID-19 infections, and provide estimated regression functions like the red line in the right panel of Figure

On the other hand, it is natural to consider the regression function to be continuous when dealing with the number of new infections, and the purpose of this paper is to estimate such a regression function using a statistically valid method.

\displaystyle y_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}I_{(\tau^{[k-1]},\tau^{[k]}]}(x_{i})(\theta_{1}^{[k]}+\theta_{2}^{[k]}x_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\qquad i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

In addition,

\bm{\tau}=(\tau^{[1]},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau^{[m]})^{\rm T}

\bm{\theta}^{[k]}=(\theta_{1}^{[k]},\theta_{2}^{[k]})^{{\rm T}}

This model has a regression function for each segment

If
\theta_{1}^{[k]}+\theta_{2}^{[k]}\tau^{[k]}=\theta_{1}^{[k+1]}+\theta_{2}^{[k+1]}\tau^{[k]}

\theta_{1}^{[k]}+\theta_{2}^{[k]}\tau^{[k]}\neq\theta_{1}^{[k+1]}+\theta_{2}^{[k+1]}\tau^{[k]}

.

Segmented regression has long been of interest not only from an applied perspective, but also from a theoretical perspective.
This is because the change-point parameter
\tau^{[k]}
requires specific care in statistical asymptotic theory.
For example, let us suppose that the regression function is discontinuous at
\tau^{[k]}
and that
\epsilon_{i}
follows some parametric distribution independently.
In this case, the log-likelihood function is not differentiable at
\tau^{[k]}
and therefore lacks regularity, leading to the development of a specific asymptotic theory.
In fact, since Taylor expansion is not applicable, the maximum likelihood estimator of
\tau^{[k]}
does not have asymptotic normality.
However, when the regression function is continuous at
\tau^{[k]}
, the maximum likelihood estimator eventually satisfies asymptotic normality, as shown in
Feder 1975
.The proof method is specific to segmented regression and for some time there were few extensions of the theory;
Wu 1992
addressed the case where the covariates are multidimensional, and the final form was given by
Liu et al. 1997
.
In addition,
Kim and Kim 2008
deals with cases where there are two variables to consider for segmentation, and what is noteworthy is the sophistication of the proof method, which we will rely on in this paper.
The corresponding author of this paper has developed segmented regression in various directions, not limited to those in
Kim et al. 2000
and
Kim and Kim 2008
, which are summarized in
Kim et al. 2022
.
Regarding the specific asymptotic theory when the regression function is discontinuous at
\tau^{[k]}
, Chapter 3 of
Csörgo and Horváth 1997
deals with this topic; the important papers that first addressed this topic are
Gombay and Horváth 1994
,
Bai 1997
, and
Bai and Perron 1998
.
There are numerous extensions to these methods; notable examples include
Aue and Horváth 2013
, which reviews cases with serial correlation,
Berkes et al. 2009
, which begins the extension to function data, and
Aue et al. 2009
, which handles high-dimensional data with an infinite variance-covariance structure.
Recently, segmented regression has also gained importance in the field of machine learning, with algorithm development being carried out in
Acharya et al. 2016
,
Siahkamari et al. 2020
,
Bemporad 2022
, and others.

In segmented regression, it is natural to assume that the number of change-points is unknown, just as it is usually assumed that the locations of change-points are unknown.
Estimating this number is part of model selection, indicating that model selection is an essential task even in segmented regression.
In fact, the aforementioned
Liu et al. 1997
proposes an information criterion that can be called BIC-type, which is slightly more penalizing than BIC (
Schwarz 1978
), and shows that model selection consistency is achieved regardless of whether the regression function is continuous or discontinuous.
In addition,
Kim et al. 2009
compares test-based methods and several information criteria and concludes that BIC is superior in terms of the probability of selecting the true model.
These BIC-types impose the same penalty on the change-points regardless of whether the regression function is continuous or discontinuous, but this is unnatural considering that the asymptotic properties of the estimators differ between the two.
Therefore,
Kim and Kim 2016
refers to
Zhang and Siegmund 2007
, which derived an information criterion for fundamental change-point models based on the original derivation of BIC, and intuitively proposes different penalty terms for the two, showing that the consistency of model selection is maintained.
In this paper, with the aim of theoretically providing different penalty terms for continuous and discontinuous regression functions, we derive an information criterion based on the original derivation of AIC (Aka73), that is, as an asymptotic bias-corrected estimator of the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
Note that the target of model selection differs from that of BIC, so the goal is not to increase the probability of selecting the true model but to minimize the divergence between the true and estimated structures.
When the regression function is discontinuous, the method of
Ninomiya 2015
, which derived AIC for the basic change-point model, can be used. As a result, a penalty of
6
, which is much larger than the penalty of
2
that appears for each parameter in AIC of regular statistical models, appears for the change-point parameter.
On the other hand, when the regression function is continuous, the penalty for the change-point parameter remains
2
.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, the model to be treated is provided first as a preparation.
There, rather than treating the target of this paper as in (
1
), we consider a natural generalization that is based on generalized linear models or not necessarily piecewise linear.
We then confirm that the maximum likelihood estimator is consistent.
In Section
3
, we first show that the estimators for the continuous segmented regression model are asymptotically normal.
Since the model is not necessarily piecewise linear, it is not possible to show this in the same way as in the method of
Kim and Kim 2008
, and some assumptions are necessary, although the result will be of the same type.
We then derive AIC based on it and see that the resulting penalty for the change-point parameter is
2
.
This AIC is used intuitively (e.g.
Kim et al. 2022
), and our contribution is to give its guarantee in a generalized model.
Since the derivative of the regression function changes abruptly even when the regression function is continuous, one might intuitively think that the penalty for the change-point parameter is
6
, and a naive AIC would define it as such.
In Section
4
, we derive AIC directly for the discontinuous segmented regression model, omitting to mention that the asymptotic properties of the estimators are not different from those of the fundamental change-point model, since it is trivial.
As a result, we see that the penalty for the change-point parameter is
6
.
As in
Ninomiya 2015
, the penalty is set to
2
for the naive AIC.
In Section
5
, we first check through numerical experiments that the derived penalty term accurately approximates the bias that appears when evaluating the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
Then, model selection by the derived AIC, naive AIC, and BIC is performed on artificial data, and the results are compared to confirm that the derived AIC gives successful results in terms of reducing the divergence.
In Section
6
, a real data analysis is performed to obtain Figure
1
.
We also check that the results of these three criteria are reasonably different.
Section
7
gives a summary and discussion.

AIC for continuous segmented regression models

Therefore, using
\tau^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*}={\rm o}(1)
and (
4
), Lemma 19.34 of
van der Vaart 2000
implies that this is uniformly
{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)
.
As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{(\tau^{[k]*},\tau^{[k]}]}(x_{i})(\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})-\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}))(y_{i}-a^{\prime}(\phi(x_{i},\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})))

\displaystyle={\rm o}_{\rm P}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\bigg).

Next, regarding the second term, the continuity of the regression function at the change-points indicates that

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})-\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(|\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})-\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})|+|\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})-\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})|

\displaystyle\ \phantom{\leq}+|\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})-\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})|)^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm O}(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2})

(5)

when
x_{i}\in(\tau^{[k]*},\tau^{[k]}]
.
Therefore, it follows from
\tau^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*}={\rm o}(1)
and (
5
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{(\tau^{[k]*},\tau^{[k]}]}(x_{i})\frac{(\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})-\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}))^{2}}{\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}}a^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\phi}(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]},\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}))={\rm o}_{\rm P}(1).

As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{(\tau^{[k]*},\tau^{[k]}]}(x_{i})(\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]})-\phi(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}))^{2}a^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\phi}(x_{i};\bm{\theta}^{[k]},\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}))={\rm o}_{\rm P}(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}).

Thus, it holds that

\displaystyle G(\bm{\theta}^{[k]},\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*},(\tau^{[k]*},\tau^{[k]}])={\rm o}_{\rm P}(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\bigg).

A similar evaluation can be obtained for

G(\bm{\theta}_{k+1},\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*},(\tau^{[k]*},\tau^{[k]}])
, which completes the proof.
∎

After obtaining the asymptotic expression for the change-point estimator, combining Lemmas
1
and
2
yields the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})
and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}^{*})
converge in distribution to
{\rm N}(\bm{0}_{p(m+1)},\bm{T}^{-1})
and
{\rm N}(\bm{0}_{m},\bm{A}^{{\rm T}}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{A})
, respectively.

Proof.

Since the regression function
\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}I_{(\tau^{[k-1]},\tau^{[k]}]}(x)\phi(x;\bm{\theta}^{[k]})
is continuous at the change-points
\tau^{[1]},\tau^{[2]},\ldots,\tau^{[m]}
, we have
\phi(\hat{\tau}^{[k]};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]})=\phi(\hat{\tau}^{[k]};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k+1]})
for each
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,\allowbreak m\}
.
Since it follows from Theorem
1
that
\hat{\tau}^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*}={\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k+1]})-\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]})

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}+\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k+1]})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]})\bigg)(\hat{\tau}^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*})(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)).

Therefore, it holds that

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k+1]})-\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]})}{\partial\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k+1]})/\partial x-\partial\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]})/\partial x}(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\partial\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})/\partial\bm{\theta})^{{\rm T}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k+1]}-\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})-(\partial\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})/\partial\bm{\theta})^{{\rm T}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})}{\partial\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})/\partial x-\partial\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})/\partial x}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}\times(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)).

The second equality comes from
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}={\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)
in Theorem
1
.
Hence, letting

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{i,j}\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})\bigg)^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})&(i=j=k)\\
\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})\bigg)^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\phi(\tau^{[k]*};\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*})&(i-1=j=k)\\
\bm{0}_{p}&({\rm otherwise})\end{array}\right.

and defining
\{(m+1)p\times m\}
-matrix
\bm{A}
as
(\bm{a}_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq m+1,1\leq j\leq m}
, it can be written that

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}^{*}=\bm{A}^{{\rm T}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)).

(6)

Note that, from Assumption
1
, the term whose inverse is taken in the definition of
\bm{a}_{i,j}
is not equal to
0
.

Now, from Lemmas
1
and Lemma
2
, it holds uniformly for
n
and
\bm{\xi}\in\{\bm{\xi}:\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|={\rm o}(1),\ \|\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}\|={\rm o}(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|)\}
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\bm{\xi};x_{i},y_{i})=H(\bm{\theta})+R(\bm{\theta},\bm{\tau})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*})^{\rm T}(\bm{T}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*})^{\rm T}(\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|\bigg).

(7)

Since it follows from Theorem
1
and (
6
) that
\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|={\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)
and
\|\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}^{*}\|={\rm O}_{\rm P}(\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}-\frac{\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)^{{\rm T}}(\bm{T}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))\bigg(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}-\frac{\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}+\frac{\bm{w}^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}}{2n}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg).

Moreover, since it follows from
\hat{\bm{\xi}}=\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\xi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\bm{\xi};x_{i},y_{i})
that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*}-\frac{\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg\|^{2}={\rm o}_{\rm P}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg),

we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})=\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1),

(8)

which yields the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
.
Also, we obtain from combining (
6
) and (
8
) that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}^{*})=\bm{A}^{{\rm T}}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1),

which yields the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\bm{\tau}}
.
∎

With the above preparation, let us consider

\displaystyle\text{AIC}=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(x_{i},y_{i};\hat{\bm{\xi}})+2{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]

according to the original definition of AIC.
Here, the second term represents the asymptotic bias when the Kullback-Leibler risk (exactly its double minus a constant) is estimated by
-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(x_{i},y_{i};\hat{\bm{\xi}})
.
In this paper, for simplicity, we use convergence in distribution,
\mathop{\rm dlim}_{n\to\infty}
, instead of convergence in mean, and evaluate the asymptotic bias by defining

\displaystyle c^{\rm limit}=\mathop{\rm dlim}_{n\to\infty}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};x_{i},y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})\bigg\}.

Here,
(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})
is a copy of
(x_{i},y_{i})
, that is, a random variable that follows the same distribution independently of
(x_{i},y_{i})
.
From Theorem
2
, the asymptotic bias is evaluated as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumption
1
, for the model in (
2
), where the regression function is continuous at the change-points, it holds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=p(m+1).

Proof.

Letting
\tilde{\bm{w}}
be a copy of
\bm{w}
, it follows from (
7
) and (
8
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};\tilde{x_{i}},\tilde{y_{i}})

\displaystyle=n\bigg\{-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})^{\rm T}(\bm{T}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}^{*})^{\rm T}(\tilde{\bm{w}}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))\bigg\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))^{\rm T}(\bm{T}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))(\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))+(\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))^{\rm T}(\tilde{\bm{w}}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+\bm{w}^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{w}}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1).

Similarly we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};x_{i},y_{i})=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+\bm{w}^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)=\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1),

and these combination implies

\displaystyle 2\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};x_{i},y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\xi}};\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})\bigg)=2(\bm{w}-\tilde{\bm{w}})^{{\rm T}}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1).

Since
{\rm E}[(\bm{w}-\tilde{\bm{w}})^{\rm T}\bm{T}^{-1}\bm{w}]=p(m+1)
, the proof is complete.
∎

From this result, we propose to use

\displaystyle\text{AIC}=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(x_{i},y_{i};\hat{\bm{\xi}})+2p(m+1)

(9)

as AIC for the segmented regression model when the regression function is continuous at the change-points.
This means that the penalties do not change depending on the parameters, that is, the penalty for the change-point parameter is also
2
.

4
AIC for discontinuous segmented regression models

In this section, we consider the case where the regression function
\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}I_{(\tau^{[k-1]},\tau^{[k]}]}(x)\allowbreak\phi(x;\bm{\theta}^{[k]})
is discontinuous at the change-points
\tau^{[1]},\tau^{[2]},\ldots,\tau^{[m]}
.
Let us denote the maximum likelihood estimator of
\bm{\theta}
when the change-point is fixed at
\bm{\tau}
as
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}=(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[1]{\rm T}},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[2]{\rm T}},\ldots,\allowbreak\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[m+1]{\rm T}})^{\rm T}
, i.e.
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}=\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\theta}}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i}))
, and
g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})
as
\hat{g}(\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})
.
When
x_{i}
is determined to be in
(\tau^{[k-1]},\tau^{[k]}]
,
g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})
depends only on
\bm{\theta}^{[k]}
; in this case it is written as
g(\bm{\theta}^{[k]};x_{i},y_{i})
.
Since the regression function is not assumed to be continuous at the change-points,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[k]}
is
\bm{\theta}^{[k]}
that maximizes
\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g(\bm{\theta}^{[k]};x_{i},y_{i})
.
This is the maximum likelihood estimator when a generalized linear regression model is fitted to a set of
(x_{i},y_{i})
with
\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}
, and therefore
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}
at
\bm{\tau}=\bm{\tau}^{*}
, i.e.
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}}^{[k]}
, is consistent.
In this notation, the weak limit considered in the derivation of AIC is

\displaystyle c^{\rm limit}=\mathop{\rm dlim}_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\sup_{\bm{\tau}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{g}(\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})-\sum_{i^{\dagger}=1}^{n}\hat{g}\bigg(\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\tau}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{g}(\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i});\tilde{x}_{i^{\dagger}},\tilde{y}_{i^{\dagger}}\bigg)\bigg).

Here, we take
\sup_{\bm{\tau}}
and
\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\tau}}
in the set of
\bm{\tau}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{g}(\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})
is
{\rm O}_{\rm P}(1)
or positive.

To investigate the asymptotic properties of
\hat{\bm{\tau}}=\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\tau}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{g}(\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})
, we separately consider the log-likelihood for the cases
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
and
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}\neq{\rm O}(n^{-1})
and
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
.
Here,
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
means that
\tau^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
for all
k
, and
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}\neq{\rm O}(n^{-1})
means that
\tau^{[k]}-\tau^{[k]*}\neq{\rm O}(n^{-1})
for some
k
.
When
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
, the difference between
\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g(\bm{\theta}^{[k]};x_{i},y_{i})
and
\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g(\bm{\theta}^{[k]};x_{i},y_{i})
is small,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}
is obviously consistent, as well as
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}}^{[k]}
, i.e.
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}\xrightarrow{\rm p}\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}
.
Then, taking the Taylor expansion of
\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}\allowbreak g^{\prime}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]};x_{i},y_{i})=\bm{0}_{p}
around
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}=\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{0}_{p}=\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})+\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*})(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)),

where where
g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta};x_{i},y_{i})
and
g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta};x_{i},y_{i})
are the first-order derivative vector and second-order derivative matrix of
g(\bm{\theta};x_{i},y_{i})
with respect to
\bm{\theta}
.
Therefore, it can also be written that

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}=\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}-\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)),

and we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}}^{[k]}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}-\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)^{-1}{\rm O}_{\rm P}(1)(1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1))={\rm O}_{\rm P}(n^{-1}).

(10)

In addition, since we know
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}}^{[k]}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}={\rm O}_{\rm P}(n^{-1/2})
as a fundamental property of the maximum likelihood estimator for generalized linear models, it holds
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}={\rm O}_{\rm P}(n^{-1/2})
.
Hence, by taking the Taylor expansion of the maximum log-likelihood with
\bm{\tau}
fixed and applying (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]})^{{\rm T}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}}^{[k]})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}}^{[k]})^{{\rm T}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}}^{[k]})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}},\bm{\tau}^{*};x_{i},y_{i})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1).

Then, defining a two-sided random walk with negative drift as

\displaystyle Q^{[k]}(\tau^{[k]})

\displaystyle\equiv I_{\{\tau^{[k]}<\tau^{[k]*}\}}\sum_{i:\tau^{[k]}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}(g(\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*};x_{i},y_{i})-g(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i}))

\displaystyle\ \phantom{\equiv}+I_{\{\tau^{[k]*}<\tau^{[k]}\}}\sum_{i:\tau^{[k]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]}}(g(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})-g(\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*};x_{i},y_{i})),

it can be seen that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}},\bm{\tau}^{*};x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}Q^{[k]}(\tau^{[k]})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)={\rm O}_{\rm P}(1).

(11)

Using asymptotics for regular statistical models, it can also be seen that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}^{*}},\bm{\tau}^{*};x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)^{{\rm T}}\bigg(-\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g^{\prime\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}}\times\bigg(\sum_{i:\tau^{[k-1]*}<x_{i}\leq\tau^{[k]*}}g^{\prime}(\bm{\theta}^{[k]*};x_{i},y_{i})\bigg)+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\bm{u}^{[k]{\rm T}}\bm{u}^{[k]}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)={\rm O}_{\rm P}(1),

(12)

where
\bm{u}^{[k]}
is a random vector following a multi-dimensional Gaussian distribution
{\rm N}(\bm{0}_{p},\allowbreak\bm{I}_{p\times p})
independently.
From (
11
) and (
12
), when
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}},\bm{\tau};x_{i},\allowbreak y_{i})={\rm O}_{\rm P}(1)
.
On the other hand, when
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}\neq{\rm O}(n^{-1})
, we can show that

\displaystyle{\rm P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})>-M\bigg)\to 0

(13)

for any
M>0
(see Appendix).
Thus, we take
\sup_{\bm{\tau}}
and
\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\tau}}
in the set of
\bm{\tau}
such that
\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{*}={\rm O}(n^{-1})
.
From (
11
) and (
12
), it holds

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\tau}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\tau}},\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sup_{\tau^{[k]}}Q^{[k]}(\tau^{[k]})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\bm{u}^{[k]{\rm T}}\bm{u}^{[k]}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1),

(14)

and furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\bm{\tau}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{g}(\bm{\tau};x_{i},y_{i})=\check{\bm{\tau}}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1),

(15)

where
\check{\tau}^{[k]}=\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\tau^{[k]}}Q^{[k]}(\tau^{[k]})
and
\check{\bm{\tau}}=(\check{\tau}^{[1]},\check{\tau}^{[2]},\ldots,\check{\tau}^{[m]})^{{\rm T}}
.
Then, the asymptotic bias of the Kullback-Leibler risk can be expressed using the two-sided random walk as follows.
Although the proof is similar to that of
Ninomiya 2015
, the definition of the asymptotic bias is given by not using a copy of
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
, which reduces the complexity of the expression (see Appendix).

Theorem 4
.

Under Assumption
1
, for the model in (
2
), where the regression function is discontinuous at the change-points, it holds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\rm E}\bigg[\sup_{\tau}Q^{[k]}(\tau)-\tilde{Q}^{[k]}\bigg(\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\tau}Q^{[k]}(\tau)\bigg)\bigg]+p(m+1),

where
\tilde{Q}^{[k]}(\cdot)
is a copy of
Q^{[k]}(\cdot)
.

From this theorem,
{\rm E}[\sup_{\tau}Q^{[k]}(\tau)-\tilde{Q}^{[k]}(\mathop{\rm argsup}_{\tau}Q^{[k]}(\tau))]
can be regarded as the asymptotic bias due to the change-point parameter
\tau^{[k]}
and
p
the asymptotic bias due to the non-change-point parameter
\bm{\theta}^{[k]}
.

Up to this point, we have treated asymptotics with a fixed amount of change
\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}
; however, the following is often assumed in change-point analysis (see, for example,
Csörgo and Horváth 1997
).

Assumption 2
.

Letting
\alpha_{n}
be some constant sequence satisfying
{\rm O}(1)\neq\alpha_{n}={\rm o}(n)
and
\bm{\Delta}_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}^{[k]}
be some
p
-dimensional constant vector, it holds

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{[k+1]*}-\bm{\theta}^{[k]*}=\alpha_{n}^{-1/2}\bm{\Delta}_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}^{[k]}\qquad(k\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}).

In ordinary statistical analysis, an assumption like Assumption
2
on the true values of different parameters does not change the asymptotic properties of estimators; in change-point analysis, however, it will change the asymptotic properties.
That is, whether or not to set Assumption
2
is essential, and we will assume it in the following.
This is because setting it makes the limiting structure ambiguous as to whether or not there is change, and this is a more realistic asymptotics.
Since the actual data size is finite, the asymptotics adapted to the situation in which it is not completely clear whether or not there is a change in the true structure would clearly be more realistic.

Assumption
2
is not only natural, but also allows us to explicitly express the asymptotic bias of the Kullback-Leibler risk as follows.
Although the proof method is similar to that in
Ninomiya 2015
, we provide it since the definition of asymptotic bias is given in a different form (see Appendix).

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for the model in (
2
), where the regression function is discontinuous at the change-points, it holds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=3m+p(m+1).

From this result, we propose to use

\displaystyle\text{AIC}=-2\log f(\bm{x},\bm{y};\hat{\bm{\xi}})+6m+2p(m+1)

(16)

as AIC for the segmented regression model when the regression function is discontinuous at the change-points.
This means that the penalty for the change-point parameter is
6
, while the penalty for the non-change-point parameter is still
2
.

5
Numerical experiments

We examine the performance of AIC given by (
9
) and (
16
) through numerical experiments using the linear segmented regression model in (
1
), where
\{x_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are independent random variables following the uniform distribution
{\rm U}(0,1)
on the interval
[0,1]
and the standard Gaussian distribution
{\rm N}(0,1)
, respectively.
As one of the comparisons, we consider a criterion that incorrectly captures the non-regularity of the change-point parameter, and denote it as AIC
naive
.
Specifically, we set the penalty term of AIC
naive
to
8m+4
by assigning a value of 6 to the change-point even in continuous cases and to
6m+4
by assigning a value of 2 to the change-point even in discontinuous cases.
In addition, as another comparison, we consider BIC, where the penalty term is set to
(2m+2)\log n
in continuous cases and to
(3m+2)\log n
in discontinuous cases.

Real data analysis

In Sections
4
and
5
, AIC was derived separately for models with change-points that leave the regression function continuous and for models with change-points that make it discontinuous; it can be extended to models with both types of change-points.
That is, when the number of change-points in the continuous case is
m_{1}
and the number of change-points in the discontinuous case is
m_{2}
(
m=m_{1}+m_{2}
), we only have to set the penalty term for AIC to
2p+2m_{1}p+2m_{2}(3+p)=2p(m+1)+6m_{2}
.
By using this for model selection, it is possible to estimate not only the number of change-points, but also whether the regression function is continuous or discontinuous at each change-point.
The information criteria to be compared are AIC
navie
and BIC.
Since AIC
navie
in Tables
2
and
4
, which sets the penalty of the change-point parameter to 6 even in the continuous case, is close to BIC, we leave it at 2 and treat AIC
navie
, which sets the penalty of the change-point parameter to 2 even in the discontinuous case.
That is, the penalty term for AIC
navie
is
2p+2m_{1}p+2m_{2}(1+p)=2p(m+1)+2m_{2}
and that for BIC is
\{p+m_{1}p+m_{2}(1+p)\}\log n=\{p(m+1)+m_{2}\}\log n
.

The real data analyzed are the number of new COVID-19 infections in the United Kingdom for the 240-day period from March 13, 2020 to November 7, 2020.
Supposing a piecewise-linear segmented regression model in (
1
), we have estimated the change-points and the regression function drawn in the left panel of Figure
1
.
In this setting, both AIC
navie
and BIC give the same results as AIC.
In the right panel, the regression function is supposed to be discontinuous at the change-points, as in
Jiang et al. 2022
.
In fact, the result is close to that given by
Jiang et al. 2022
.
In this setting, while BIC gives the same result, AIC
navie
selects more change-points.
Since these are not artificial data, we cannot say whether the left panel or the right panel is closer to the true structure; however, it is more natural that they should be continuous unless there is some reason, such as a change in the way of data collection, and therefore we can infer that some of them are in fact continuous as in the left panel.

Considering the possibility that large fluctuations in the data may make analysis difficult, we reduce the data size to 200 and conduct the estimation in Figure
2
.
In these data, while AIC
navie
gives the same results, BIC selects fewer change-points.
The true structure is still unknown; it is highly possible that there is a discontinuity in the regression function in the later part of the data for some reason.
In order to find out where and how large the discontinuities actually are, we will need to do a more sophisticated modeling adapted to the data.
What we want to insist on in this section is that the results will differ considerably depending on the model selection criteria and that it is therefore important to derive a reasonable criterion.

7
Summary and discussion

Information criteria in segmented regression have so far been developed mainly for BIC-type with model selection consistency from the perspective of increasing the probability of selecting the true model.
In this paper, we have derived an information criterion based on the original definition of AIC from the perspective of making the divergence between the true and estimated structures small.
The reason why this result has not been obtained until now is the existence of asymptotics specific to segmented regression, and we have actually confirmed that when the regression function is discontinuous at the change-points, the penalty for the change-point parameter is 6, as in the result of
Ninomiya 2015
.
When the regression function is continuous at the change-points, the asymptotic normality of the change-point estimators has been derived first by developing
Kim and Kim 2008
.
Its derivation basically follows the flow of
Kim and Kim 2008
; however, because the model is extended to generalized nonlinear ones, it is necessary to change how to use the continuity and to add some assumption.
Then it has been shown that the penalty on the change-point parameter remains 2 despite the abrupt change in the differential coefficients.
In numerical experiments, we have compared the derived AIC with AIC
naive
, which incorrectly captures the peculiarities of the model, and BIC, and confirmed that the derived AIC tends to make the divergence small, as we expected.
In real data analysis, we have treated the data on the number of new COVID-19 infections, which gave the motivation for this study, and judged that some of the jumps in the regression function estimated by
Jiang et al. 2022
would be connected according to our AIC.
We have also confirmed that while the results of the three criteria happened to coincide when the data were analyzed as they were, they returned different results when the time period of the data was slightly changed, that is, the difference in the criteria was not negligible.

One of the important settings in which our method can be extended in the near future will be segmented regression in survival analysis.
While
Liu et al. 2008
and
Ozaki and Ninomiya 2023
apply a Cox model with jumps in the regression function to data from randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials of patients with malignant glioma, changes without jumps can naturally be expected in such data.
Although the model is not included in (
2
) and the partial likelihood is not treated in this paper, it is expected that a risk evaluation similar to that of
Ozaki and Ninomiya 2023
can be performed.
Another near future issue is to extend our method to more recent change-point problems of importance, such as those listed in Section
1
(
Aue and Horváth 2013
,
Berkes et al. 2009
, and
Aue et al. 2009
).
The same derivation should be possible for serially correlated data, and it is expected that for functional and high-dimensional data, the number of change-points as well as the number of principal components, can be estimated by developing
C_{p}
criterion.
A further challenge is the extension of the method for selecting the number of nodes in a spline by
Ruppert 2002
, with a view to contributing to the ongoing topic of selecting the number of hyper-parameters in nonparametric regression (e.g.,
Wood et al. 2016
).
Considering that jumps are allowed in the framework of generalized linear models, it is necessary to develop AIC as in this paper.
Since derivative coefficients vary smoothly in jump-free splines, our method must be extended from this stage, and since regularization estimation is essential, it will be necessary to incorporate methods such as the one in
Ninomiya and Kawano 2016
.
