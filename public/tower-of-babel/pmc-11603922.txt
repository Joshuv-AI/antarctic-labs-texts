Transverse versus longitudinal skin incision in first extensor tendon release for radial styloid tenosynovitis: a randomized controlled trial

Background

De Quervain’s tenosynovitis (DQV) occurs when the extensor pollicis brevis (EPB) and abductor pollicis longus (APL) become trapped within the first extensor compartment [1], causing significant pain at the radial styloid, swelling, and crepitus in certain cases. Notably, women in their 4th to 5th decade are more prone to developing this condition [2]. Many cases of tenosynovitis respond well to conservative treatments, such as rest, splinting, antiinflammatory medications, and steroid injections, achieving complete pain relief in 70% of individuals. Surgical treatment becomes the preferred option when symptoms persist [3–5], as it typically yields favorable outcomes, with 85% of patients experiencing complete resolution of functional impairment and a satisfaction rate of 97.5% [6]. However, despite its effectiveness, surgery carries risks of concurrent or subsequent comorbidities such as iatrogenic nerve damage, hypertrophic scarring, and vessel injury [7–9].

Currently, there is no consensus on the optimal incision technique for treating DQV surgically. The ideal incision should provide adequate exposure while minimizing the risks of nerve damage, recurrence, and scar tissue formation. Various incision types are employed, including transverse, longitudinal, lazy S, and angle-specific approaches, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. A transverse incision offers good exposure and reduces scar formation and postoperative pain, as indicated by visual analog scale assessments, but increases the risk of iatrogenic nerve injury. Conversely, a longitudinal incision provides superior exposure with reduced nerve injury potential, albeit at the cost of potentially increased scar formation and postoperative discomfort due to deviation from natural skin creases [10, 11].

A recent systematic review [7] highlights that prior investigations have not adequately explored the functional outcomes linked to longitudinal skin incisions in comparison to transverse skin incisions among patients diagnosed with DQV. The lack of robust studies and patient cohorts underscores the need for further investigations to definitively substantiate these claims. Therefore, this study primarily aimed to examine and compare the functional outcomes associated with longitudinal and transverse open skin incisions, with secondary objectives focusing on aesthetic outcomes, pain scores, and surgery-related complications.

Methods

Study design

This study was a prospective, randomized, controlled trial comparing transverse and longitudinal skin incisions. It was a single-blinded trial conducted at a tertiary center in Songkla, Thailand. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University (Institutional Review Board [IRB] number EC 64-222-11-1). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was registered on 27/06/ 2023, at clinicaltrials.in.th (TCTR20230627001) and adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Recruitment

Participants were enrolled between September 10, 2021, and June 25, 2023. Eligible participants were 18 years or older, diagnosed with DQV, and had not responded to at least 3 months of conservative treatment. The exclusion criteria included allergy to nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, prior surgery at the same anatomical location, symptoms of traumatic or neoplastic origin, inflammatory joint disease at the affected wrist, bilateral involvement, history of cervical spondylosis, fractures of the upper extremities, systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and rheumatoid arthritis, and inability to complete follow-up forms or lack of self-determination. All diagnoses were confirmed by an orthopedic hand surgeon.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomly allocated to either the longitudinal skin incision (group I) or transverse skin incision (group II) group using block-of-four randomization with computer-generated numbers. The envelopes were opened in the operating room just before surgery. All surgical staff responsible for postoperative care were blinded to the allocation. The investigator opened a sealed opaque envelope containing the allocation code before each radial styloid surgery while preparing for surgical management following the type of skin incision indicated in the envelope.

Technique of surgical management

All surgical procedures were conducted under local anesthesia with tourniquet control, using 1% lidocaine with epinephrine (1:100,000), injected into the radial styloid. A skin incision of approximately 1.5 cm was made over the prominent thickening of the first extensor compartment. The boundaries of the first compartment were clearly identified during the dorsal incision to preserve the integrity of surrounding structures and facilitate optimal access to the APL. A No. 15 scalpel was used to incise the skin and open the first extensor compartment proximally, while Metzenbaum scissors were used to extend the incision distally. Care was taken to avoid excessive retraction during this process, as it could lead to tendon misalignment. Both the APL and EPB tendons were successfully released and carefully repositioned within the first dorsal compartment to ensure they lay flat without bowing or kinking. Before closing the wound, a dynamic assessment of tendon function, including passive and active ranges of motion, was performed to confirm that the tendons glided smoothly and exhibited no signs of subluxation. The skin was sutured using 4 − 0 nylon with three vertical mattress stitches, and subcutaneous sutures were not used. Subsequently, a compressive bandage was applied and maintained for 2 days.

Post-surgical protocol

Wrist splints were not used postoperatively, and patients were encouraged to mobilize their fingers as soon as possible. All patients were prescribed oral naproxen 250 mg twice daily and dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily for the first 5 days. A nurse who was not involved in the study removed the sutures 2 weeks postoperatively. Following suture removal, patients were referred to an independent observer for scar assessment.

Outcome assessment

An independent observer, unaware of the incision type, conducted assessments preoperatively and at 2, 6, and 12 weeks postoperatively. Scar evaluation was performed using the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS) during these intervals in the outpatient department. This validated scale, tailored for linear scars, offers a comprehensive evaluation from both patient’s and clinician’s perspectives [12]. Scores ranged from 1 to 10, with 1 indicating normal skin and 10 indicating the most severe scarring. Patients completed the Thai version of the Patient Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) questionnaire [13] during these visits. Lower PRWE scores indicate improved wrist function, and higher scores reflect worsening symptoms. Wrist pain severity, measured using a verbal numerical rating scale (VNRS) ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain), was assessed preoperatively and 2, 6, and 12 weeks postoperatively.

Study flow

Initially, 80 patients were screened, of whom 10 refused to participate (Fig. 1). At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, symptom duration, preoperative VNRS pain score, and PRWE score. Throughout the follow-up period, no participants withdrew, and baseline data remained consistent among the completers.

Statistical analysis

For both noninferiority and superiority trials with continuous outcomes, a sample size of 35 patients with wrist pain per group was needed to achieve an 80% effect size and a 10% margin (alpha 5%, power 80%). All participants were included based on randomization following the intention-to-treat principle. An interim analysis was planned to halt the trial prematurely if one intervention proved superior. Between-group differences were assessed by calculating improvements in VNRS and Thai PRWE scores from baseline to 2, 6, and 12 weeks post-surgery. A paired t-test was used to analyze changes within the same group over time, determining whether the surgical treatment has led to a significant improvement in pain and functional scores. Group differences were reported using a generalized linear mixed-effects model for repeated measures. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median (interquartile range) for skewed distribution. Outcome measures were analyzed using t-tests and generalized linear mixed models, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R Program Version 3.4.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Results

Of the 70 patients diagnosed with DQV, 35 each were assigned to the longitudinal or transverse skin incision group. All included patients actively participated in the study and underwent open release surgery of the first extensor compartment between September 2020 and June 2023. No patients were lost to follow-up (Fig. 1). The demographic profile consisted of middle-aged female patients, and the dominant (right) side was affected in most patients. The mean symptom duration was approximately 3 months in both groups. Both groups received injections of 10 mg of triamcinolone, delivered in a 1 mL volume approximately 1–2 times before surgery. The results revealed no statistically significant differences between the groups. None of the patients showed signs of skin atrophy before the surgery. Additionally, no statistically significant difference in demographic characteristics was observed between the two groups (Table 1).

Functional score (Thai PRWE score)

Despite randomization, preoperative Thai PRWE scores were significantly higher in the longitudinal incision group than in the transverse incision group. Following surgery, both groups demonstrated marked improvements in Thai PRWE scores; however, no discernible difference in this functional outcome was noted between the two groups at any designated time point (Table 2). Surgical-related complications

Neither group encountered any instance of wound infection. However, one patient in the longitudinal incision group and two patients in the transverse incision group reported temporary paresthesia in the first dorsal web space. All three patients demonstrated a complete resolution of paresthesia by the conclusion of the follow-up period. Despite this occurrence, no statistically significant differences were observed.

Discussion

In this study, we compared postoperative functional outcomes, pain scores, scar satisfaction, and complications, particularly superficial radial nerve injury, between longitudinal and transverse skin incisions in the surgical management of DQV. Our results are similar to those of previous prospective clinical trials on longitudinal [8, 9] and transverse skin incisions [6, 14], which also demonstrated improvements in functional scores and reduced pain scores. The key factor in both approaches is deep dissection targeting the release of the first extensor compartment, which remains consistent regardless of the incision type.

Each of the discussed methods has distinct advantages and disadvantages. Longitudinal skin incisions offer superior visualization of affected tendons, facilitating precise identification and decompression. This approach also mitigates the risk of nerve injury as the incision orientation reduces the likelihood of damaging the superficial radial nerve, which runs longitudinally along the tendon across the wrist. However, this method can result in more noticeable scarring, which may be a cosmetic concern for some patients. In contrast, transverse skin incisions typically result in less noticeable scars due to their alignment with natural skin creases, which is aesthetically preferable for many patients. However, transverse incisions may provide limited exposure to the surgical area, thereby complicating the procedure, especially when extensive exploration or work on the tendons is required. In addition, there is a heightened risk of injury to the superficial radial nerve branches because they run perpendicular to the incision line, increasing the likelihood of accidental damage.

Regarding complications, our study found that one patient in the longitudinal incision group and two in the transverse incision group experienced temporary paresthesia in the first dorsal web space. While our study found no statistically significant differences in complications between the longitudinal and transverse incision groups, it is important to note that the overall rates of complications were indeed higher in the transverse incision group. This suggests that, although the difference was not statistically significant in our analysis, the transverse group experienced a greater incidence of complications, consistent with findings in previous literature [10, 11]. The perpendicular orientation of the superficial radial nerve branches to the incision line increases the risk of inadvertent trauma in transverse incisions. Despite intersecting Langer’s skin tension lines, longitudinal incisions showed no notable differences in scar formation compared to transverse incisions. Hypertrophic scarring involves multiple factors beyond incision orientation, including patient healing responses, skin tension, and postoperative care. Both types of incisions shared similar tissue properties, leading to comparable scar development tendencies. Precise surgical techniques are crucial for minimizing tissue trauma, regardless of incision orientation, requiring delicate tissue manipulation, meticulous suturing, and tension management.

Our findings are consistent with those of recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses [7, 15], which reported that both techniques improve functional scores, reduce pain scores, and show no significant differences in complication rates, particularly regarding hypertrophic scars. The choice between transverse and longitudinal skin incisions depends on several factors, including the surgeon’s preference and experience, patient’s specific anatomy, and extent of the required surgical intervention. Each technique has distinct trade-offs, necessitating a decision tailored to the patient’s needs and clinical context.

This study had a notable limitation, which was the short follow-up period to evaluate long-term complications. The choice of a 3-month evaluation period was based on several considerations. First, our primary objective was to examine the early outcomes of the intervention because this timeframe is critical for assessing initial healing and treatment efficacy. We aimed to capture the immediate functional recovery and early instances of complications, particularly in a population where short-term outcomes are often more relevant to clinical practice. Second, while we recognize that longer follow-up periods can provide additional insights into functionality and scarring, previous literature suggests that significant changes in these parameters may be minimal beyond the initial 3 months of certain interventions. Our data serve as a basis for future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods to validate our conclusions. We hope this study clarifies our rationale and emphasizes our intention to lay the groundwork for subsequent research exploring long-term outcomes.

Conclusions

Both techniques demonstrate similar improvements in functional scores and reductions in pain scores, with no significant differences in complication rates, particularly concerning hypertrophic scars. The decision between transverse and longitudinal skin incisions should consider the surgeon’s expertise, the patient’s anatomical characteristics, and the complexity of the surgical procedure. Each approach presents distinct trade-offs, highlighting the necessity of a personalized, patient-centered strategy to optimize surgical outcomes.
