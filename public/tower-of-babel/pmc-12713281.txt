Chronic gossypiboma mimicking a peri-appendiceal abscess after prior Cesarean sections: a rare surgical “never event”
Authors: Jasser Rchidi, Ghazi Lâamiri, Hazem Beji, Oussama Mghirbi, Mahdi Bouassida, Hassen Touinsi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGossypiboma, a retained surgical sponge, is a rare but serious “never event.” It poses a significant diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific and variable clinical presentation, which often mimics other conditions like intra-abdominal abscesses or tumors.Case presentationA 34-year-old woman with a history of two previous Cesarean sections presented with abdominal pain and vomiting. Physical examination revealed a firm, tender mass in the right iliac fossa. An ultrasound suggested a diagnosis of acute appendicitis with a peri-appendiceal abscess. During diagnostic laparoscopy, a large inflammatory mass adherent to the cecum and terminal ileum was found, leading to conversion to open laparotomy. The mass was excised and found to be a Gossypiboma; a retained surgical sponge encased in a thick fibrous capsule. An appendectomy was performed, and the patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery.ConclusionThis case underscores two critical points in clinical practice. First, a high index of suspicion for gossypiboma is essential in any patient with a prior surgical history who presents with an unexplained mass or pain, as it can mimic common conditions like complicated appendicitis. Second, and more importantly, this represents a profound failure in operative safety protocols. The occurrence of a retained surgical item is universally classified as a preventable “never event,” highlighting a breakdown in systematic safeguards rather than merely an individual error. The ultimate goal must be prevention through a multi-faceted strategy. This includes rigorous adherence to manual sponge counts, the integration of technological adjuncts such as radio-frequency tagged sponges, and the cultivation of a meticulous safety culture within the operating room to protect patients from this avoidable harm.

Introduction

Retained surgical sponges (gossypiboma or textiloma) are defined as a textile material unintentionally left within a body cavity after an operation, eliciting a foreign-body inflammatory reaction that may present as abscess, mass, obstruction, or chronic pain [1]. These events have important clinical and medico-legal consequences, often require reoperation or prolonged treatment, and can profoundly damage the patient-physician relationship and trust in healthcare systems [2].

The term “never event”, describing a serious and preventable patient-safety incident such as retention of a foreign object after surgery, was popularized by the National Quality Forum and is widely adopted in patient-safety literature [3, 4].

Published estimates of the incidence of retained sponges vary widely. Older series reported rates of approximately one per 5,027 inpatient operations [5]. Recent reviews and case-series suggest a range from about one in 1,000 to one in 1,500 intra-abdominal procedures [6].

Gossypibomas are mostly reported after abdominal, pelvic, and obstetric surgeries, including Cesarean deliveries, and analyses show higher risk after emergency operations, unplanned changes in procedure, and when operative teams face distractions or unusual circumstances [7].

To reduce the risk of unintentionally retained foreign objects, multiple perioperative strategies have been promoted; most notably standardized sponge and instrument counts, clear perioperative team communication, and global implementation of the 2009 WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, which has been associated with reductions in operative complications when correctly adopted [8].

Despite these preventive measures, gossypiboma remains under-documented. Underreporting is common due to medico-legal exposure, variable institutional reporting systems, and publication bias toward symptomatic or surgically managed cases [9].

We report a case of chronic gossypiboma mimicking a peri-appendiceal abscess after prior Cesarean sections to illustrate the diagnostic challenge, the lasting impact on the patient, and the continuing need for rigorous adherence to prevention protocols.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with diffuse abdominal pain and repeated vomiting for several days. The patient’s surgical history included two Cesarean sections. The first C-section delivery was performed three years earlier without complications. The second C-section took place eight months prior to her admission and was complicated by significant postpartum haemorrhage, requiring re-operation via a midline laparotomy on postoperative day one. Following this revision surgery, the patient remained hospitalized for approximately six to seven days.

Around 20 days after this hospitalization, she reported mild right iliac fossa pain during routine outpatient follow-up, without fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, or hemodynamic instability. An intra-peritoneal abscess was suspected, and an urgent CT-scan was recommended. However, due to personal circumstances, the patient chose to return home and was subsequently lost to follow-up.

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed tenderness and a palpable firm mass in the right iliac fossa without evidence of herniation along the previous incision. Laboratory workup revealed leukocytosis with elevated C-reactive protein, consistent with a moderate inflammatory syndrome. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated an inflammatory mass in the right iliac fossa suggestive of acute appendicitis with peri-appendiceal abscess.

Due to the unavailability of computed tomography at the hospital and the presumptive diagnosis of complicated appendicitis, diagnostic laparoscopy was indicated. Intraoperative findings revealed a large heterogeneous mass adherent to the cecum and terminal ileum, with omental wrapping and dense adhesions. Due to the mass’s dense adhesions and the limited visualization from prior surgeries, conversion to open surgery was performed through the same midline incision.

The mass was found to consist of a thick fibrous capsule enclosing a retained surgical sponge, consistent with gossypiboma, firmly adherent to the cecum and mesoappendix (Fig. 1). Careful dissection was performed to free the mass without injuring the bowel wall. The mesoappendix was ligated with 2.0 silk sutures, and a standard appendectomy was carried out. The specimen, including the retained gauze, was extracted en bloc (Fig. 2). The patient was permitted oral fluids on postoperative day 2 and resumed normal oral alimentation. She was clinically stable and was discharged on postoperative day 3. She attended outpatient follow-up visits at 2 weeks, 1 month, and 3 months after discharge. At each visit she was asymptomatic, the surgical wound was well healed, and there were no clinical signs of ongoing infection, abscess, or other complications. The patient reported return to her baseline activities.

Discussion

The pathophysiology of gossypiboma is characterized by two distinct tissue responses. The exudative type manifests early after surgery as abscess formation or infection due to inflammatory reaction, while the fibrinous (aseptic) type develops over time, leading to a foreign body granuloma with fibrosis and encapsulation [10]. The chronic form may remain asymptomatic for months or even decades, being discovered incidentally or in front of complications such as obstruction or fistula [10].

The clinical presentation of gossypiboma is notoriously variable, which contributes to diagnostic delays. Patients can present within days or up to 40 years after the index operation [10]. In a large descriptive study of 319 cases, the abdomen and pelvis were the most frequently affected sites, accounting for more than 50% of cases, while pain and palpable mass were the leading presentations [11]. Adhesion, abscess, and fistula formation were reported in 31%, 24%, and 20% of cases respectively [11]. Radiologically, CT scanning remain the main diagnostic tool. On CT, a gossypiboma typically appears as a well-defined mass with a spongiform pattern and trapped gas bubbles, sometimes surrounded by a dense capsule [12]. The unavailability of CT in our case underscores how reliance on clinical suspicion alone can lead to an initial misdiagnosis. MRI can be useful in chronic or complex presentations, especially when a differential diagnosis with neoplasm or abscess is suspected [12].

Definitive treatment consists of surgical removal of the retained material, via laparoscopic approach or laparotomy, depending on its location, adhesions, and associated complications. Laparoscopy is particularly advantageous for diagnosis and removal in selected cases, minimizing morbidity and promoting faster recovery, but open conversion may be required when dense adhesions or nearby viscera are at risk of injury [13]. The surgical principle must be to remove the foreign body in its entirety while meticulously preserving the integrity of surrounding structures. This requires gentle dissection to lift the gauze, followed by a thorough examination of the wound for any occult damage.

Ultimately, the occurrence of a gossypiboma represents a profound failure in the operating room safety system. It is an unforgivable error that should never happen to any patient. Prevention is multi-faceted and non-negotiable.

From a preventive standpoint, gossypiboma is entirely avoidable through strict adherence to standard perioperative safety protocols. The Association of periOperative Registered Nurses (AORN) recommends standardized counting of all sponges, instruments, and sharps at key stages: before incision, at cavity closure, and during final skin closure [14]. If discrepancies occur, the entire surgical team must participate in locating the missing item before proceeding. The American College of Surgeons (ACS) emphasizes that the operating room environment should minimize distractions and interruptions to support accurate counting and focus [15].

Modern adjuncts have also been proposed, including radiofrequency identification (RFID) tags, barcoded sponge systems, and surgical safety software, which have shown a significant reduction in retained item incidents in recent years [16]. Moreover, the World Health Organization’s Surgical Safety Checklist, when rigorously applied, has been shown to reduce both surgical mortality and the risk of preventable errors such as retained items [17].

Prevention depends on a culture of safety shared by all operating room personnel. The National Quality Forum (NQF) and the UK Department of Health classify the presence of a retained surgical item as a never event, underscoring that it represents a failure of system reliability rather than individual negligence [18]. When such an event does occur, it results in significant morbidity, additional healthcare costs, and profound medico-legal implications for both the surgeon and the institution. Rather than focusing on blame, a system-based approach emphasizing continuous education, safety training, and double-verification mechanisms remains the best strategy to ensure patient protection and trust.

A review of the literature identified several recent well-documented cases of intra-abdominal gossypiboma presenting with clinical features mimicking appendicitis or right iliac fossa pathology. These reports were retrieved from PubMed, ResearchGate, and other indexed medical databases. Conclusion

Gossypibomas are rare but serious “never events” with significant clinical and medico-legal consequences. Early recognition is challenging due to nonspecific presentations, which can mimic common pathologies such as peri-appendiceal abscesses. This case highlights the ongoing risk of retained surgical items even after Cesarean sections and reinforces the need for strict adherence to standardized preventive protocols, including careful sponge and instrument counts, effective perioperative team communication, and consistent implementation of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist. Vigilance in both the operative and postoperative period is essential to prevent such avoidable complications.
