First molecular identification and characterization of Taenia lynciscapreoli in the Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx) and Hydatigera taeniaeformis sensu stricto in domestic cats (Felis catus) from Central Asia
Authors: Lyudmila Lider, Rabiga Uakhit, Ainura Smagulova, Aigerim Algozhayeva, Christian Bauer, Vladimir Kiyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigations of taeniid fauna in felids, including the Eurasian lynx, in Central Asia have so far relied exclusively on traditional morphological methods. However, species identification based solely on morphological features can be unreliable. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to identify and characterize taeniid species from the Eurasian lynx and domestic cats in Kazakhstan using molecular techniques. Taeniid cestodes were collected from the intestines of 18 Eurasian lynx from northern and central Kazakhstan. In addition, 856 fecal samples from domestic cats in Astana, Almaty, and Kostanay were examined for cestode proglottids and eggs. Taeniid infection was detected in 7 (39%) of the lynx examined, and 5 (0.6%) of the cats were coproscopically positive for taeniid eggs or proglottids. Genomic DNA was extracted from isolated taeniid specimens obtained from each positive lynx and from taeniid-positive feline fecal samples, and mitochondrial DNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction using species-specific primers and subsequently sequenced. Taenia lynciscapreoli was molecularly identified in the Eurasian lynx, and Hydatigera taeniaeformis sensu stricto (s.s.) in domestic cats. This study represents the first molecular identification and characterization of both taeniid species in Central Asia. Introduction

Taeniid species (Cestoda) are heteroxenous helminths that depend on predator–prey relationships. In wildlife systems, terrestrial carnivores (e.g., canids, felids, and mustelids) act as definitive hosts (DH), harboring adult worms in the small intestine. Infection occurs through the ingestion of larval stages (metacestodes) present in the tissues of herbivorous or omnivorous intermediate hosts (IH), such as even-toed ungulates, lagomorphs, and rodents. The cestode fauna of a given DH species varies depending on the prey species available in its biotope (Jones and Pybus 2001).

Currently, the European lynx (Lynx lynx),including its subspecies, inhabits forest and forest-steppe regions and ranges from Fenno-Scandinavia, Western, Central, and Southern Europe to Central Asian countries (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan), eastern Siberia, and China (Castelló 2020). Its diet consists mainly of wild ungulates, with roe deer, Siberian roe deer, and reindeer as primary prey, whereas domestic ruminants are rarely taken. When ungulates are locally or seasonally scarce or absent, such as in parts of Russia, the lynx relies more heavily on alternative prey, particularly hares and tetraonids (Castelló 2020; Sidorovich 2022; Khorozyan and Heurich 2023a, b).

Consequently, the taeniid fauna of the Eurasian lynx varies depending on prey composition. Reported species include Taenia lynciscapreoli, which was first described in 2016 (Haukisalmi et al. 2016). In this species, L. lynx serves as the primary DH, whereas the wolf acts as an accidental DH. Roe deer, Siberian roe deer, moose, and reindeer function as IH (Haukisalmi et al. 2016; Kołodziej-Sobocińska et al. 2018; Myczka et al. 2020). Other species include Taenia laticollis (Kazlauskas and Matuzevitčius 1981; Valdmann et al. 2004; Deksne et al. 2013; Lavikainen et al. 2013; Kołodziej-Sobocińska et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2021; Konyaev et al. 2022; Safarov et al. 2023; Gevorgyan et al. 2025) and Taenia pisiformis (Kazlauskas and Matuzevitčius 1981; Bagrade et al. 2003; Valdmann et al. 2004; Bykova 2007; Bekshaev et al. 2016; Kornyushin et al. 2022; Safarov et al. 2023), both usinglagomorphs as IH. However, in older studies based on morphological characteristics, T. pisiformis was likely misidentified in some cases and may in fact represent T. lynciscapreoli (Haukisalmi et al. 2016). In addition, Taenia hydatigena (Nekrasov et al. 2008; Valdmann et al. 2004; Safarov et al. 2023; Gevorgyan et al. 2025), Taenia krabbei (Kazlauskas and Matuzevitčius 1981), and Hydatigera taeniaeformis (formerly Taenia taeniaeformis) (Kazlauskas and Matuzevitčius 1981; Valdmann et al. 2004; Safarov et al. 2023), using small ruminants, cervids, and rodents as IH, respectively, have also been reported in the Eurasian lynx.

In contrast to the Eurasian lynx, which has limited contact with human settlements, the taeniid fauna of domestic cats (Felis catus) in urban and rural environments is shaped by their close association with human dwellings and the prey available there, mainly rodents. H. taeniaeformis (sensu lato), which uses rodents as IH, is the dominant, or even the sole, taeniid species infecting domestic cats in Eurasia (Dyachenko et al. 2008; Fang et al. 2015; Aboimova and Karmaliev 2016; Moskvina et al. 2018; Ibragimova 2018; Wierzbowska et al. 2020; Karmaliyev et al. 2023; Furtado Jost et al. 2023; Soroushianfar et al. 2024).

Investigations of taeniid fauna in felids from Central Asia have so far relied exclusively on traditional morphological methods (e.g., Aboimova and Karmaliev 2016; Karmaliyev et al. 2023; Safarov et al. 2023; Lider et al. 2025). However, as noted above, species identification based solely on morphological features can be unreliable (Haukisalmi et al. 2016), and cryptic species cannot be distinguished (Lavikainen et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2021; Dumendiak et al.2025). These diagnostic limitations can be overcome by molecular methods, which have been available for many years. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify T. lynciscapreoli in Eurasian lynx and H. taeniaeformis in domestic cats in Kazakhstan using molecular techniques and to characterize these parasitesfor the first time.

Materials and methods

Animals, locations, and sampling

A total of 18 adult and subadult Eurasian lynx, possibly belonging to the subspecies Lynx lynx wrangeli (“Siberian lynx”) according to Bizhanova et al. (2024), were hunted in four northern and central regions of Kazakhstan (Fig. 1) between November 2022 and February 2025. The animals, of both sexes, were sampled as follows:

These hunting grounds are primarily characterized by a warm-summer humid continental climate (Köppen–Geiger climate classification “Dfb”) or a semi-arid steppe climate (“BSk”) (Beck et al. 2018). The gastrointestinal tracts of lynx were frozen and transported for analysis in refrigerated containers within three days of collection to the Laboratory of Biodiversity and Genetic Resources, National Center for Biotechnology, Astana.

In addition, a total of 856 fecal samples (approximately 5–10 g each) were collected from both client-owned and stray cats in three Kazakh cities – Kostanay (n = 192), Astana (n = 382), and Almaty (n = 282) – between September 2023 and January 2025 (Fig. 1). Sampling was carried out during visits to veterinary clinics or animal shelters that voluntarily participated in the study. Samples were transported to the laboratory in refrigerated containers for analysis (Lider et al. 2025).

Parasitological methods

In the laboratory, lynx intestines were thawed, opened lengthwise, and examined for the presence of helminths using the method described by Skrjabin (1928). Cestode strobilae were fully isolated, washed in physiological saline, counted, and preserved in 70% ethanol for morphological and genetic analysis. The prevalence, mean intensity, and abundance of cestodes were calculated as described by Bush et al. (1997).

Fecal samples from cats were initially examined macroscopically for the presence of cestode proglottids. The samples were then processed using the direct Sheather’s sugar flotation method, with a sugar solution (specific gravity: 1.3) as the flotation medium, to detect worm eggs (Lider et al. 2025). Proglottids and taeniid eggs from positive samples were stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

Scolices and proglottids were stained with hematoxylin–eosin and mounted in glycerin for microscopic examination (Zhaxylykov et al. 2024). Cestodes from lynx were tentatively identified primarily based on the morphology of the rostellar hooks (Lavikainen et al. 2013; Myczka et al. 2020), using a Zeiss Axiolab 5 light microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany) at various magnifications.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from isolated taeniid specimens obtained from each positive lynx and from taeniid-positive fecal samples of cats using the phenol–chloroform method. Pre-homogenized samples were incubated for 2 h at 56 °C in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) with the addition of 5 μL of proteinase K (20 ng/μL). DNA was precipitated with ethanol, purified, dissolved in 1 × TE buffer, and stored at − 20 °C until further analysis (Sharma et al. 2023). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

PCR analysis

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to identify taeniid species using a primer pair targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (COX1: forward 5′-TTTTTTGGGCATCCTGAGGTTTAT-3′ and reverse 5′-TAAAGAAAGAACATAATGAAAATG-3′) (Bowles et al. 1992). PCR amplification was performed using HS-Taq PCR Biomasters (2 ×) (Biolabmix, UK), with 10 pmol of each primer and 100 ng of genomic DNA as thetemplate. The PCR mixture (25 μL total volume) consisted of 5 μL 10 × PCR buffer, 2 μL dNTPs, 1 μL of each primer (10 pmol), 1 μL Taq DNA polymerase (1 U), 14 μL distilled water, and 1 μL DNA. The PCR protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Positive (Taenia hydatigena DNA) and negative (ddH2O) controls were included. Amplification products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis and documented using a GelDoc XR + transilluminator (Bio-Rad, USA).

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

All positive PCR products were subjected to sequencing and genotyping. Amplicons were purified using Exonuclease I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on a SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Applied Biosystems, USA). Resulting nucleotide sequences were visually inspected and manually edited using BioEdit software (version 7.0), and species identification was confirmed by BLAST searches against reference sequences in the NCBI GenBank database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) (last accessed: November 26, 2025).

Nucleotide sequences of the mitochondrial COX1 gene obtained in this study, together with reference sequences of several taeniid species from GenBank, were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm. Reference sequences were retrieved from the GenBank database and selected based on their taxonomic relevance, sequence quality, and availability of homologous gene regions. The dataset included representative sequences of the following species within the family Taeniidae: T. lynciscapreoli (JX860629, KU3324548, MK03479), T. hydatigena (PV422567, OQ317786, OR711633), T. krabbei (PV426924, PV422573), T. crassiceps (PV426940), H. taeniaeformis (PV077679, OQ569229, OQ5692233, PX232713, LC5335291, EU544597), H. kamiyai (PQ463656), H. krepkogorski (MF281972), and H. parva (PQ148392). These sequences were included to ensure accurate phylogenetic placement of the isolates within a representative framework of closely related taxa. As an outgroup, Dipylidium caninum (ON954765) was used. This species belongs to the order Cyclophyllidea but is phylogenetically distinct from members of the family Taeniidae, allowing appropriate rooting of the phylogenetic tree. Phylogenetic relationships among taxa were inferred using the maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates implemented in MEGA 11 software (Tamura et al. 2021).

Results

Parasitological results

Taeniid infection was detected in 7 (39%) of the 18 lynx examined by helminthological necropsy. The number of worms (complete strobilae, with or without a scolex) per animal ranged from 2 to 53, resulting in a mean intensity of 17.6 ± 19.4 worms and a mean abundance of 6.8 ± 14.5 worms (Table 1). Only three scolices, eachwith a more or less complete rostellum, were available for morphological examination. The scolices were relatively small, each bearing four suckers and an armed rostellum containing two concentric rings of large and small hooks. The large hooks had a long, thick, and straight tang with a relatively short, broad, and strongly curved pointed blade (Fig. 2A). The proglottids exhibited lateral genital openings located approximately in the middle of the proglottid and alternated irregularly among proglottids (Fig. 2B). Overall, the morphological characteristics of the tapeworms were similar to T. lynciscapreoli (Haukisalmi et al. 2016; Myczka et al. 2020).

Five (0.6%) of the 856 cats examined were coproscopically positive for taeniid eggs (n = 3) or pregravid/gravid proglottids (n = 2; Fig. 2C). Two of the positive cats were pets and three were strays (Table 1).

Molecular characterization

Mitochondrial COX1 amplicons of the expected size were successfully obtained from all taeniid isolates from lynx (325–377 bp) and cats (344 bp). Nucleotide sequences of representative taeniid isolates obtained inthis study were deposited in GenBank (accession numbers: PX368957, PX368958, PX313672, PZ429579-PZ429582 for T. lynciscapreoli from Eurasian lynx; PX313670, PX313671, PZ429583-PZ429585 for H. taeniaeformis from domestic cats). The mitochondrial COX1 sequences of cestodes from all 7 positive lynxes corresponded to T. lynciscapreoli, while those from 5 positive domestic cats corresponded to H. taeniaeformis, showing sequence similarities of 99–100% to available GenBank entries.

Phylogenetic analysis of the COX1 gene sequences revealed that the representative T. lynciscapreoli isolates (PX368957, PX368958, PX313672, PZ429579-PZ429582) from lynx formed a well-supported clade with reference T. lynciscapreoli sequences from GenBank, confirming their species identity. A closely related but distinct cladewas formed by T. hydatigena sequences, reflecting their phylogenetic relationship. In contrast, T. krabbei and Taenia crassiceps each formed separate, well-defined clades (Fig. 3). The H. taeniaeformis s.s. isolates (PX313670, PX313671, PZ429583-PZ429585) from taeniid egg-positive cats clustered with reference H. taeniaeformis sequences in GenBank, confirming their taxonomic affiliation, and were clearly separated from clades representing Hydatigera krepkogorski, Hydatigera kamiyai, and Hydatigera parva (Fig. 3).

A pairwise fixation index (Fst) based on the mitochondrial cox1 gene was calculated to assess the level of genetic differentiation among the obtained isolates. The Fst values ranged from 0.000000 to 0.211516, indicating low genetic differentiation and suggesting the absence of pronounced population structure among H. taeniaeformis s.s. isolates (Table 2). In addition, pairwise genetic distance analysis revealed very low nucleotide divergence between our isolates and reference sequences of H. taeniaeformis s.s. (0.000–0.0028), which is consistent with intraspecific variation. In contrast, substantially higher genetic distances were observed between our isolates and other members of the genus, including H. krepkogorski (0.116–0.177), as well as H. kamiyai and H. parva, indicating clear interspecific differentiation.

Discussion

This study presents the first molecular identification and characterization of T. lynciscapreoli in the Eurasian lynx and H. taeniaeformis s.s. in domestic cats from Central Asia. To date, infections with T. lynciscapreoli have been diagnosed using molecular methods in lynx as the primary DH and in wolves as an accidental DH, as well as in roe deer, reindeer, and moose as known IH in Finland (Haukisalmi et al. 2016), Poland (Kołodziej-Sobocińska et al. 2018; Myczka et al. 2020), Sweden (Maganira et al. 2019; Kautto et al. 2022), and Russia (Siberia) (Haukisalmi et al. 2016). The present findings in lynx as DH extend the known distribution range of this taeniid species to Central Asia and confirm the existence of its sylvatic life cycle in Kazakhstan.

The local IH of T. lynciscapreoli remain unknown; however, potential IH species in which metacestodes occur in the liver and lungs (Haukisalmi et al. 2016), such as (Siberian) roe deer (Kiyan et al. 2025) and moose (Bauer et al. 2025), are present in the region. Although previous studies detected metacestodes only of T. hydatigena and Taenia multiceps in these ungulate species in northern Kazakhstan (Akhmetov 2011; Kiyan et al. 2025), it is nevertheless expected that they also serve as IH of T. lynciscapreoli in this region. This hypothesis is currently the subject of ongoing investigations.

The present results confirm H. taeniaeformis as the main taeniid species infecting domestic cats across Eurasia (e.g., Dyachenko et al. 2008; Aboimova and Karmaliev 2016; Ibragimova 2018; Wierzbowska et al. 2020; Karmaliev et al. 2023; Soroushianfar et al. 2024). This pattern can be explained by the fact that cats roaming within and around human settlements frequently capture and consume rodents, which serve as the natural IH of this tapeworm species (Miljević et al. 2023; Kirillova and Kirillov 2024).

The potential zoonotic risk of the two identified taeniid species appears to be negligible. There is no evidence of human infection with T. lynciscapreoli (Deplazes et al. 2019), which is consistent with the species’ ecology, as the lynx predominantly inhabits forested areas and has limited contact with human settlements (Sidorovich 2022). For H. taeniaeformis, only a single case of human infection with its metacestode has been reported to date (Stĕrba and Barus 1976).

The main characteristics of H. taeniaeformis identified in domestic cats in this study include genetic homogeneity, clear assignment to sensu stricto, and lack of geographic structuring. These findings are consistent with previous studies from other regions, where H. taeniaeformis s.s. has been shown to exhibit relatively low genetic variability based on mitochondrial markers such as cox1 (Alvarez Rojas et al. 2024). Similar patterns of low intraspecific divergence and clear separation from closely related species have been reported for taeniid cestodes, where intra- and interspecific variation are markedly different (Dai et al. 2012). Similarly, T. lynciscapreoli identified in the Eurasian lynx showed genetic characteristics consistent with previously described isolates. Previous studies have demonstrated that cox1 sequences of T. lynciscapreoli from different regions (e.g., Poland, Finland, and Russia) are often identical or nearly identical, indicating very low genetic variability and lack of strong geographic structuring (Myczka et al. 2020; Kautto et al. 2022). In addition, reported intraspecific divergence within the species is low (approximately 0.3–1.1%), while interspecific differences are substantially higher (Haukisalmi et al. 2016). Overall, no distinct or unique genetic features were observed in the studied populations compared to data from other countries.

One limitation of the present study is the small sample size: only 18 lynx from northern and central Kazakhstan were examined by helminthological necropsy, and cats were sampled from only three Kazakh cities by coproscopy. It remains to be seen whether molecular investigations, using intestinal or scat samples, with larger sample sizes and from other regions where prey composition may differ (e.g., hares), will reveal additional taeniid species, such as T. laticollis, in the Eurasian lynx. Such investigations could include its critically endangered subspecies Lynx lynx isabellina (“Turkestan lynx”) in the Tien Shan and Zhetisu Alatau mountains of southeastern Kazakhstan (Bizhanova et al. 2024). A similar approach would also be appropriate for cats, which should be investigated in other cities and regions, potentially including Asian wild cats (Felis silvestris ornata), to search for additional feline taeniid species, such as Hydatigera kamiyai (Lavikainen et al. 2016) or Hydatigera krepkogorski (Deng et al. 2025).

Conclusions

This study presents the first molecular identification and characterization of T. lynciscapreoli in the Eurasian lynx and H. taeniaeformis s.s. in domestic cats from Central Asia. Further investigations are required to identify the natural intermediate hosts of T. lynciscapreoli in Kazakhstan.
