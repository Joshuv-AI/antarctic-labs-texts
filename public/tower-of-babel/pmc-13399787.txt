Augmented Reality Simulation Can Be Implemented to Successfully Teach Interns Arthroscopy
Authors: Alex M. Meyer, Ryan Serbin, Eric Davis, Claude T. Moorman, Gregg Nicandri, Alison P. Toth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and implement a simulation‐based arthroscopy curriculum for orthopaedic surgery interns to be completed during their required orthopaedic intern skills month and to evaluate its effectiveness, feasibility, and impact on resident confidence.MethodsTwenty‐one first year residents prospectively completed arthroscopic skill evaluation using VirtaMed augmented reality surgical simulator for the Fundamentals of Arthroscopy Surgical Training (FAST), shoulder, and knee models. Participants then completed the self‐confidence score for surgical trainees survey. Then, they completed simulation training modules on each model over the course of their 1‐month intern skills rotation. Each module contained 13‐14 exercises. Following training, skill evaluations were repeated for each module and self‐confidence survey was repeated.ResultsOne hundred percent of eligible participants were enrolled, and 100% completed the curriculum. Participant performance improved for all 3 modules (FAST, knee, shoulder). In the FAST modules, pretest to posttest scores for image centering improved from 64.1% to 95.5% (P < .001), and triangulation and probing improved from 73.5% to 88.4% (P = .019). In the knee modules, performance improved on diagnostic arthroscopy from 49.9% to 88.8% (P = .003) and on loose body removal from 40.0% to 75.3% (P < .001). In the shoulder modules, performance improved on the diagnostic arthroscopy from 89.9% to 98.9% (P = .011) and for loose body removal from 77.8% to 95.2% (P = .002). Self‐confidence scale improved from 15.4 to 21.2 (P < .001). Acceptability of intervention measure, intervention appropriateness measure, and feasibility of intervention was 4.8, 4.6, and 4.7 respectively. Total self‐paced time to completion was 4.9 h for all 3 sections. The shoulder took the most time (1.8 hours), followed by the knee (1.6 hours) and FAST (1.5 hours).ConclusionsThe proposed arthroscopy simulation curriculum showed improvement in speed and skill on the arthroscopy simulator and improved personal confidence for orthopaedic surgery interns. It was able to be completed in less than 5 hours with 100% completion rate.Clinical RelevanceArthroscopy is an essential skill for orthopaedic surgery residents. High‐fidelity simulation is effective at teaching these skills in a safe and controlled environment.

Orthopaedic surgical procedures continue to increase in complexity, yet despite reduced work hours and heightened emphasis on patient safety, 1 residents’ first exposure to a surgical procedure is often in the operating room on real patients. Traditional surgical training paradigms, which rely heavily on cadaveric dissections and supervised clinical experiences, face limitations including ethical concerns, high costs, and restricted availability. 2 Arthroscopy faces additional unique challenges in that it requires bimanual dexterity using a variety of instruments as an extension of the surgeon's hands through triangulation in three dimensions guided by a 2‐dimensional video display. 3 Due to these limitations, the evolution of orthopaedic and other subspecialty surgical training has increasingly incorporated simulation‐based methodologies to enhance the proficiency of procedures including arthroscopy. These surgical simulators have emerged as valuable tools in orthopaedic education, offering risk‐free environments for repetitive practice and objective skill assessment. 4

Simulation in surgical education can be broadly categorized into low‐fidelity and high‐fidelity models. Low‐fidelity simulation refers to inexpensive, simplified trainers such as benchtop models, knot‐tying boards, or box trainers. These are effective for teaching fundamental skills like hand‐eye coordination and triangulation but provide limited realism or objective assessment. 5 In contrast, high‐fidelity simulation incorporates advanced technology, including cadaveric models and virtual or augmented reality (AR) platforms, to closely replicate anatomy, tissue handling, and the operative environment while often providing automated performance metrics. 6 , 7

High‐fidelity simulators differ further in their degree of immersion. Virtual reality (VR) systems immerse the learner in a fully computer‐generated environment, whereas AR systems overlay digital anatomy and pathology onto physical instruments or models, combining haptic realism with virtual feedback. 8 , 9 The VirtaMed ArthroS system (ArthroS Realistic Arthroscopy Training Simulator, VirtaMed, Lithia, FL), for example, incorporates real arthroscopic instruments and joint models with computer‐simulated anatomy, offering both tactile feedback and automated metrics. This hybrid design places it within the category of high‐fidelity, AR‐based arthroscopy simulators. A substantial body of literature supports the efficacy of simulation in improving arthroscopic skills among orthopaedic residents. Yet simulation is still underutilized by residency programs. Rebolledo et al. showed that junior residents trained with a surgical simulator exhibited superior performance in shoulder arthroscopy, evidenced by reduced completion times and fewer technical errors compared to those receiving traditional didactic instruction. 7 Similarly, Yari et al. reported significant improvements in both knee and shoulder arthroscopic skills following VR simulator training with the benefit to all skill levels and the highest benefit for junior residents. 10 Simulation has been shown to improve arthroscopic skills in multiple joints including both the shoulder 11 and knee. 12 The Fundamentals of Arthroscopy Surgical Training (FAST) benchtop low fidelity simulator has been shown to improve arthroscopic performance. 13 Studies have shown that VR simulators can differentiate between skill levels of novice, intermediate, and expert surgeons in shoulder and knee arthroscopy and can improve economy of movement, camera handling, and triangulation skills. 14 Skills acquired on simulation modules has been shown to be transferrable to cadaver models 15 and to the operating room. 16

Despite these advancements, critical questions remain regarding the optimal integration of simulation into residency curricula and its direct impact on clinical performance. The effect of widespread implementation of a formalized curriculum and its effect of residents remains unknown. Although studies affirm skill acquisition in simulated settings, the transferability of these skills to actual surgical procedures warrants further investigation. Moreover, the absence of standardized training protocols and assessment metrics poses challenges in evaluating the true efficacy of simulation‐based education. Since 2013, orthopaedic surgery programs are required by the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery to include 1 month of surgical skill training to first year residents. 17 Hill et al. showed at 3 different institutions the lack of a formal curriculum left their simulators severely underutilized. 18 As outlined above, there is extensive evidence showing that surgical simulation is effective at teaching surgical skills. However, there is a paucity of data for established and effective curriculum for orthopaedic surgery residents. Addressing these gaps is imperative, especially considering the evolving demands on residency programs.

The purposes of this study were to develop and implement a simulation‐based arthroscopy curriculum for orthopaedic surgery interns to be completed during their required orthopaedic intern skills month and to evaluate its effectiveness, feasibility, and impact on resident confidence. We hypothesized that participation in a standardized simulation‐based curriculum will lead to statistically significant improvements in simulator metrics and self‐confidence scores among orthopaedic interns.

METHODS

Subjects

This was a multi‐institutional self‐controlled prospective study in which 21 first‐year residents at 3 academic centers were eligible to participate. Subjects were recruited and consented prior to study commencement. All current orthopaedic interns at the 3 participating institutions were eligible and enrolled. Prior simulation experience was not an exclusion criterion. Orthopaedic surgery interns were chosen for the intervention as they had dedicated time for skills training and likely had the least amount of arthroscopy experience compared to more experienced residents. Participation was voluntary and participants received no compensation for their involvement in the study. The study was evaluated and approved by the institutional review board of all participating institutions. Each study participant was provided a user specific username and password through their residency program that was used to monitor progress. Metrics such as simulation time, date, and score on simulations were recorded for each user over the data collection period. The data collection was performed from July 1, 2024, to May 1, 2025. Each VirtaMed ArthroS surgical simulator (VirtaMed ArthroS; VirtaMed AG, Zurich, Switzerland) used was purchased by each respective institution prior to the above dates. REDCap was utilized for participant data and de‐identified. Simulator scores were provided through VirtaMed.

Timing of the study was determined by the residency program schedule and when the intern was assigned to their skills month. Since July 2013, orthopaedic surgery skills months are required as part of orthopaedic surgery residency curriculum as put forth by the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery and the associated Residency Review Committee. 17 All 3 institutions varied on their timing of their skills months and subsequently delivery of this proposed curriculum. One institution had 2 groups of 4 interns—one in July and one in October of the same academic year. One institution had 4 groups of 2 interns, with two groups in the fall and 2 groups in the spring of the same academic year. The third institution had all 5 interns complete their month at the same time in the fall of the academic year. Prior to beginning, participants received an introduction to the simulator and diagnostic arthroscopy provided by VirtaMed accessed through the simulator. This included proper usage of the simulator, instruments, and software. Then, the participants completed a pretest skills examination using VirtaMed ArthroS surgical simulator for (FAST), knee, and shoulder modules consisting of two‐timed modules, image centering/periscoping and probing on the FAST module and a diagnostic scope and loose body removal for both the knee and shoulder modules (Figure 1A). The FAST module consists of an opaque hollow dome with multiple insertion points for arthroscopic tools that allows for practice of grasping and manipulating of virtual objects in a 3D space while using the arthroscopic camera (Figure 2A). The knee (Figure 2B) and shoulder (Figure 2C) modules apply arthroscopic training and skills learned in the FAST module to clinical applications through a variety of procedures. They consist of a foam and plastic model in which the structures are viewed through the arthroscope and projected on a monitor providing both haptic and tactile feedback of life‐like structures with the virtual projection of pathology such as loose bodies or anatomic pathology. Immediately following pretest examinations, the self‐confidence score for surgical trainees survey was administered. This survey consisted of 6 questions and scored on a 0‐5 Likert scale with 0 representing least confident responses and 5 being the most confidence (Figure 3). The scores were combined for a maximum score of 30.

The FAST module provided a foundation for arthroscopic skills using an opaque dome trainer with multiple portals and a virtual camera display. Exercises emphasized camera navigation, image centering, triangulation, and probing of virtual objects to teach basic bimanual dexterity and instrument coordination.

The knee module introduced clinical applications of arthroscopy in a virtual anatomic knee model with haptic and tactile feedback. Exercises progressed from diagnostic arthroscopy and systematic joint inspection to therapeutic procedures such as loose body removal. These modules emphasized economy of motion, camera handling, and the ability to recognize and address intra‐articular pathology.

The shoulder module similarly utilized a virtual anatomic model of the glenohumeral joint. Early courses focused on diagnostic arthroscopy and systematic visualization of the joint. Later tasks emphasized therapeutic procedures, including loose body removal, requiring triangulation and precise coordination of instruments in the constrained joint space.

Across all modules, progression required achieving a minimum proficiency threshold (75% of available points as defined by the simulator) or completion of 5 attempts before advancing. This ensured repeated deliberate practice until competency benchmarks were met.

The simulator scores procedure after each exercise measures performance using a combination of automated, objective metrics that capture efficiency, accuracy, and technical skill. Each exercise is scored by the simulator's integrated software assessing completion of diagnostic checklists, identification of anatomic landmarks, and successful execution of tasks such as loose body removal. It also includes efficiency measures such as total exercise duration (time to completion) and camera path length (the distance traveled by the arthroscope within the joint), representing precision of instrument handling and economy of motion. The next aspect of evaluation is image centering (ability to maintain relevant anatomy in the field of view) and image orientation (ability to maintain camera orientation stable on the horizontal place). Finally, these metrics are compiled as an overall module score: calculated as a composite of accuracy and efficiency variables and reported as a percentage. 19

These metrics have been shown to possess construct validity, with studies showing that more experienced arthroscopists consistently outperform novices on simulator‐derived measures such as path length, time, and task completion. 7

Then the participants independently completed arthroscopy simulation modules on the VirtaMed FAST device (Figure 1B), knee model (Figure 1C), and shoulder model (Figure 1D) over the course of their 1‐month intern skills rotation. The training modules were self‐paced at the learner's preference. Participants had 24/7 access to the simulator, and participants could use it whenever they saw fit. Each training module consisted of 13‐14 simulation exercises with progressing difficulty with both dominant and non‐dominant hands. For each exercise, a minimal proficiency score had to be obtained, or exercise must have been attempted 5 times prior to progressing to the next exercise. The minimal proficiency score was set by the manufacturer requiring participants to score at least 75% of total possible points.

Outcome Measures

All participants completed the curriculum and all associated evaluations. No participants were lost to follow up. Therefore, all were included in statistical analysis. Once all training modules were completed the same 2 skill evaluations for each the FAST, knee, and shoulder were administered as a posttraining evaluation (Figure 1E). Then, the self‐confidence score for surgical trainees survey was again completed along with feasibility surveys (Figure 4). Statistical analysis was conducted using 2‐tailed T‐test comparing initial module scores with final scores. P value of <.05 was considered significant. The primary outcome was the scores, duration, and camera path length for each evaluation and the confidence trainees reported after doing the procedures.

Feasibility survey including the AIM, Acceptability of Intervention Measure; IAM, Intervention Appropriateness Measure; FIM, Feasibility of Intervention Measure; SCSST, Self‐Confidence Scale for Surgical Trainees.

Power analysis using a significance level of .05 and a power of .8 would require 15 participants per group for a paired t‐test as used in this study to detect a large effect size (Cohen's d = .8).

RESULTS

Simulator Performance

Participant performance improved for both assessments for all 3 modules (FAST, knee, shoulder). In the FAST modules, image centering pretest to posttest scores increased from 64.1% to 95.5%, camera path length decreased from 193.7 to 81.4 cm, and exercise duration decreased from 4.3 to 2.1 minutes, with a P for all 3 variables of < .001. In triangulation and probing, module scores increased from 73.5% to 88.4% (P of .019), camera path length decreased from 65.5 to 38.7 cm (P = .048), and duration decreased from 2.2 to 1.6 minutes (P = .015). In the knee modules, diagnostic arthroscopy scores increased from 49.9% to 88.8% (P = .003), camera path length decreased from 327.5 to 113.1 cm (P = .002), and duration decreased from 7.7 to 2.2 minutes (P < .001). Loose body removal scores increased from 40.0% to 75.3% (P < .001), camera path length decreased from 278.5 to 127.1 cm (P < .001), and exercise duration decreased from 7.0 to 3.3 minutes (P < .001). In the shoulder modules, the diagnostic arthroscopy scores increased from 89.9% to 98.9% (P = .011), camera path length decreased from 136.7 to 63.6 cm (P < .001), and exercise duration decreased from 4.0 to 1.4 minutes (P < .001). Shoulder loose body removal scores increased from 77.8% to 95.2% (P = .002), camera path length decreased from 173.6 to 70.0 cm (P < .001), and exercise duration decreased from 6.5 to 2.4 minutes (P < .001) (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Simulator training significantly improved performance across all modules, with higher task scores and marked reductions in camera path length and exercise duration (all P < .05). Self‐confidence scores increased from 15.4 to 21.2 (P < .001), and feasibility ratings were uniformly high (4.6‐4.8/5). Participants averaged 4.9 hours on the simulator, with shoulder modules requiring the most time. The present study showed significant improvements in first‐year orthopaedic residents’ arthroscopic skills and self‐confidence following a structured VR simulation curriculum implemented at 3 different institutions. Notably, 100% of eligible participants completed the program with an average time of less than 5 hours, with performance enhancements observed across all modules (FAST, knee, and shoulder). Secondarily, the participants found that it was appropriate for their level and beneficial to their education.

These findings align with existing literature, reinforcing the efficacy of AR simulation in orthopaedic surgical training. Aim et al. showed in their systematic review of 10 articles that simulation training was effective for teaching arthroscopy. 20 One study showed improvement on real life operating room scenario. Nine studies showed improvement in simulation skills. All studies used similar high‐fidelity AR simulators as used in this study. 20 Waterman et al. discussed improved procedural times and probe distance which is similar to our findings with improved duration, camera path length, and total scores for all tested procedures. 11

Participants clearly improved their skills, but they also felt more confident in those skills as evidenced by their self‐confidence score for surgical trainees scores. The self‐confidence score for surgical trainees has been used in other specialties such as obstetrics and gynecology, otolaryngology, and neurosurgery. 21 , 22 , 23 Geoffrion et al. found that for obstetrics and gynecology residents, self‐confidence scores were positively correlated with number of cases the participant had completed and was correlated with the performance score in the procedure. 21 Pulijala et al. found that in otolaryngology, the self‐confidence scores were positively correlated with year in training. 22 Our results corroborate these findings as both scores and self‐confidence scores improved. It is important to evaluate how the skills from the simulator transfer to the operating room.

This study as well as multiple other studies have shown that simulation is effective at teaching surgical skills. To maximize these benefits, the authors suggest formal curriculum development. We highlight that this proposed curriculum consisting of 3 modules (FAST, shoulder, and knee) was able to be completed over the course of 1‐month in an average of 4.9 hours. Contrasting to the study by Beaudoin et al., which found their participants improved surgical skills, their participants completed 113 minutes per week of VR training, 12 whereas our participants only completed an average of 73 minutes per week. The participants’ responses to the AIM, IAM, and feasibility portion of the study suggest that the participants enjoyed the modules, and they were appropriate for their level of training. As described in the methods section, each of the 3 institutions have a different schedule on when interns complete their skills month and were still able to complete the modules and associated surveys. One institution has all interns complete their skills month as a group. One institution has 4 groups of 2 interns. Finally, 1 institution has 2 groups of 4 interns. Based on the results of the study, the authors suggest that similar curriculums can be implemented at other institutions especially if simulation resources are already available. One of the major benefits of this type of training is that it required minimal faculty or senior resident oversight and was self‐taught from the modules themselves. As part of this study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of the implementation of this curriculum so that this curriculum or similar curriculums could be implemented at other institutions. These suggestions mirror Hill et al.'s findings in that 86% of their participants agreed that VirtaMed arthroscopic simulator should be a mandatory part of junior resident education. One difference is that they found that only 55% residents agreed that the simulation improved their performance in the operating room. 18 In their study, they accumulated 138.6 hours over 58 months by 4 classes of resident with 6 members in each class. 18 Our study recorded 83.3 hours in 1 month by interns alone. The 4.9 hours per participant is feasible as shown on the feasibility portion of our study but also meets the 3‐5 hour minimum suggested by Rahm et al. for competency to be achieved on simulation modules. 24

Future areas of exploration include participants at different levels of training including more senior residents and fellows to evaluate benefits from a similar training program targeted at their skill level. This could involve specific procedures or other joints such as hip and ankle.

Limitations

There are several limitations within this study. The largest is that the study protocol was entirely performed on the VR simulator, and we did not evaluate the skill improvement of cadaver specimens or in the operating room which limits our transfer validity. We also did not have a control arm of the study comparing to another curriculum. Additionally, our study had a relatively small number of participants with variable timing of the study completion due to residency rotation schedules. Therefore, the authors were unable to control for simulation or operative arthroscopy experience. Although the simulation curriculum was standardized, the rest of the skill rotation could not be standardized. Another limitation is the lack of construct validation of the simulator module score required to “pass” a module which makes it difficult to differentiate a novice surgeon from a more experienced surgeon. These scores are set arbitrarily by the simulator designers at 75% of total possible score or at 5 attempts. The last limitation is cost of the simulator. Rahman et al. performed a cost analysis of VR simulation training, and they found that the VirtaMed simulator used in this study costs $383,400 in 2024 to purchase the simulator and for a 2‐year subscription, 25 whereas Hill reported a cost of $125,000 for a similar simulator purchased in 2015. 18 This is likely the biggest limitation to widespread implementation of the proposed curriculum.

CONCLUSIONS

The proposed arthroscopy simulation curriculum showed improvement in speed and skill on the arthroscopy simulator and improved personal confidence for orthopaedic surgery interns. It was able to be completed in less than 5 h with 100% completion rate.
