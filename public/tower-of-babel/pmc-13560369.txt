Beyond hypoxia and ccRCC: the expanding universe of VHL novel targets and next-generation therapeutics
Authors: Ruiyi Deng, Boyuan Wang, Mingrui Zou, Kaiyuan Mao, Jiaheng Shang, Jianhui Qiu, Jingcheng Zhou, Hua Xu, Xin Ma, Yizhou Wang, Lin Cai, Kan Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) gene encodes the core substrate-recognition subunit of the Cullin2-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, regulating cellular oxygen sensing and proteostasis. While targeting the canonical VHL-hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) axis (e.g. belzutifan) has improved advanced clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) management, intrinsic non-responsiveness and acquired resistance to HIF-2α inhibitors present major clinical limitations. Expanding beyond the HIF-centric paradigm reveals a broader network of non-canonical VHL targets, offering new opportunities for therapeutic development across ccRCC and pan-cancer contexts. This review provides an evidence-graded synthesis of novel VHL targets spanning epigenetic regulation (e.g. ZHX2, SETDB1, METTL3/METTL14), metabolic reprogramming (e.g. NDRG3, TFAM), extracellular matrix architecture (e.g. fibronectin, COL4A2), and immune modulation (e.g. TBK1). Supporting evidence across these targets ranges from direct biochemical ubiquitination to indirect downstream observations, derived predominantly from preclinical cell-line and xenograft models. Disruption of VHL and these non-HIF networks contributes to oncogenesis across diverse malignancies. Translating these mechanistic insights catalyzes two distinct therapeutic modalities. For VHL-deficient malignancies, selectively intercepting unshielded downstream effectors with small-molecule inhibitors provides rational, vulnerability-targeted salvage strategies. Conversely, in VHL-proficient malignancies, the exceptional chemical tractability of VHL has been repurposed by proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs). VHL-recruiting PROTACs hijack operational ligase machinery to degrade targeted oncogenic drivers, including some previously “undruggable” oncogenes. These next-generation degraders offer potential biomarker-driven strategies to overcome current therapeutic bottlenecks in ccRCC and other associated malignancies. However, current evidence derives predominantly from in vitro and animal models. Realizing the clinical potential of VHL-targeted strategies requires addressing key unresolved questions regarding substrate directness, tissue tropism, and resistance trade-offs. Further investigating these core mechanistic and pharmacological bottlenecks will be essential to guide next-generation therapies in VHL-associated malignancies.

Background

VHL and the clinical dilemma in ccRCC

The von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) gene, located on chromosome 3p25, encodes a key tumor suppressor that regulates cellular oxygen sensing and proteostasis [1]. Canonically, VHL functions as the core substrate-recognition subunit of the Cullin2-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase complex (VCB-CR). Under physiological normoxia, VHL selectively binds to prolyl-hydroxylated hypoxia-inducible factor alpha (HIF-α), orchestrating its rapid proteasomal degradation. Conversely, loss or mutation of VHL leads to the stabilization of HIF-α, particularly HIF-2α [1]. The resulting constitutive stabilization of HIF-α initiates a “pseudohypoxic” transcriptional cascade, upregulating downstream effectors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) that promote pathological angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, tumor growth, and metastasis [1, 2].

The canonical VHL-HIF axis is central to the pathogenesis of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), where biallelic VHL inactivation—via somatic mutation, chromosomal 3p deletion, or epigenetic hypermethylation—serves as an early and central event in both hereditary and up to 80% of sporadic ccRCC cases [3, 4]. Importantly, VHL loss alone is insufficient for overt tumorigenesis. Malignant transformation typically requires cooperation with additional genetic alterations, particularly adjacent chromosome 3p tumor suppressors such as PBRM1, BAP1, and SETD2 [5–8]. While the traditional HIF-centric model alone cannot fully capture this multigenic complexity, VHL inactivation remains a foundational event across clones. Therefore, expanding the focus from VHL-HIF to the broader functional network offers a rational strategy to target this heterogeneous disease.

Driven by this complex tumor biology [8, 9], RCC accounts for over 400,000 new global cases annually. As its predominant and most aggressive histological subtype, ccRCC representing approximately 70% of diagnoses [10, 11]. Advanced metastatic ccRCC maintains a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of 8%-11.7%. This poor prognosis is compounded by high recurrence rate, as ~30% of surgical patients experiencing disease recurrence, often progressing to advanced stages characterized by limited therapeutic options [12, 13]. While targeting downstream of classical VHL-HIF pathway via tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has modified management strategies, treatment resistance remains a major limitation to long-term survival [12, 14].

The clinical development of belzutifan, a targeted HIF-2α inhibitor, expanded therapeutic options for VHL disease-associated tumors and pretreated advanced RCC [15]. Clinical responses, however, are heterogeneous rather than uniformly transient. In pretreated advanced ccRCC, a randomized phase III trial (LITESPARK-005) demonstrated an objective response rate (ORR) of 21.9% [16]. Conversely, long-term (50-month) phase II follow-up (LITESPARK-004) in VHL disease-associated RCC demonstrated an ORR of 67%, including 11% complete responses (CRs), and 59% patients remained on treatment [17]. These distinct populations should not be directly compared, but together they indicate that while resistance is not universal, intrinsic non-responsiveness and acquired disease progression remain principal clinical challenges [16–18].

Mechanistically, intrinsic resistance reflects variable tumor HIF dependence. Some VHL-deficient cell lines and xenograft models expressing little HIF-2α or lacking HIF-2-driven transcriptional programs fail to regress despite pharmacodynamic target suppression [19]. Inadequate drug exposure due to pharmacokinetic variability, dose reduction, or treatment-limiting toxicity should be distinguished from tumor-intrinsic resistance [20]. Moreover, intratumoral/interlesional heterogeneity, compensatory HIF-1 or alternative growth programs, and rare pre-existing resistant subclones may also contribute. Acquired resistance, by contrast, emerges after initial response or disease control via verified on-target mutations such as EPAS1/HIF-2α G323E and ARNT/HIF-1β F446L that disrupt drug binding (patient cohorts and preclinical models) or candidate bypass pathways [e.g., sustained CCND1/cyclin D1 signaling (preclinical models)] [21, 22]. Because much mechanistic data derives from early analogue molecules (PT2385/PT2399), direct longitudinal validation in belzutifan-treated patients remains limited. These gaps reinforce the need for predictive biomarkers and complementary HIF-independent strategies [23].

Novel target exploration and paradigm shift

Historically, the understanding of VHL has been predominantly confined to its role in regulating HIF and consequent ccRCC pathogenesis. However, the inescapable therapeutic ceiling underscores a critical limitation: the HIF-centric model incompletely captures the complexity of VHL-related oncogenesis. In fact, the VHL-HIF axis represents only one facet of the complex tumor-suppressive network [24]. Therefore, mapping the expanding universe of non-HIF VHL novel targets has become clinical imperative, urgently required to dismantle entrenched resistance mechanisms and pioneer next-generation therapeutic modalities.

This review provides an evidence-graded synthesis of non-canonical VHL targets, evaluating their biomarker utility and therapeutic applicability. We systematically delineate the expanding network of non-HIF targets across cellular processes. Then we broaden the ccRCC paradigm to evaluate VHL dysregulation across pan-cancer spectrum. Finally, we summary the research progress of small-molecule inhibitors (SMIs) targeting VHL novel targets and assess the dualistic utility of VHL within next-generation proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) therapeutics. By synthesizing these advances of novel biomarkers and therapeutic avenues beyond traditional HIF inhibition, we aim to provide a rational framework to mitigate drug resistance and improve clinical outcomes across diverse VHL-related malignancies.

The non-canonical VHL targets: functional modules & mechanisms

High-throughput interactomics and functional genomics have identified a broad HIF-independent VHL network [25–29]. It is now unequivocally clear that VHL operates far beyond a singular oxygen-sensing switch; rather, it serves as a pleiotropic hub coordinating cellular homeostasis. To address mechanistic heterogeneity and ensure taxonomic precision, we stratify VHL-associated novel targets into four hierarchical classes: (1) E3 ligase substrates; (2) degradation-independent VHL-binding proteins; (3) VHL-stabilized or sterically shielded clients; and (4) VHL-associated downstream effectors or synthetic lethal partners. These interactions are organized into functional modules (Fig. 1). Epigenetic remodeling & chromatin dynamics

ZHX2

Zinc fingers and homeoboxes 2 (ZHX2) was identified as a novel VHL substrate through an in vitro genome-wide cDNA library screening based on the notion that prolyl-hydroxylated proteins could be recognized by VHL E3 ligase [26]. This discovery established a direct mechanism parallel to HIF-α. Under normoxia, prolyl hydroxylases [PHDs, i.e., egg-laying-defective nine (EglN)] hydroxylate ZHX2, prompting VHL-mediated polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Consequently, VHL loss leads to ZHX2 stabilization, as verified in ccRCC and CNS hemangioblastomas [26, 30].

ZHX2 drives oncogenesis via epigenetic reprogramming. Integrated multi-omics reveal that accumulated ZHX2 acts as an essential chromatin-anchoring co-factor. ZHX2 physically interacts with the NF-κB p65 subunit, facilitating its nuclear translocation and co-occupying ~75% of p65 genomic targets—specifically tethering to active promoter regions enriched with H3K4me3 and H3K27ac marks. In ccRCC cell lines and mouse xenograft models, this promotes NF-κB-dependent transcription, conferring resistance to apoptosis [26, 68]. In vitro studies further demonstrate that the Hippo pathway effector Yes1-associated transcriptional regulator (YAP) antagonizes this axis by competing with ZHX2 for p65 binding, thereby repressing NF-κB target genes [69]. Collectively, this sophisticated VHL-ZHX2-NF-κB-YAP crosstalk exemplifies how VHL substrates influence malignant progression and evade programmed cell death via transcriptional modulation.

Beyond chromatin dynamics, cell-based experiments demonstrate that ZHX2 transcriptionally activates mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MEK1)/extracellular regulated protein kinases 1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling [70], upregulating downstream signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) to drive tumor angiogenesis, growth and metastasis in ccRCC. It also induces protective autophagy, mediating sunitinib resistance in vitro [70]. Clinically, elevated ZHX2 expression functions as a prognostic biomarker associated with advanced Fuhrman grade and shorter progression-free survival (PFS) in ccRCC patient cohorts [70]. Preclinically, ZHX2 also serves as a potential predictive biomarker for sunitinib resistance, offering a prospective therapeutic target to overcome treatment failure [70].

SFMBT1

Scm-like with four malignant brain tumor domains 1 (SFMBT1) is a direct, hydroxylation-dependent VHL E3 ligase substrate emerged from the same genome-wide screening effort that identified ZHX2, underscoring the utility of systematic approaches in expanding the VHL substrate repertoire. Under normoxia, EglN1 hydroxylates SFMBT1 at Pro651, creating a recognition interface for VHL-mediated polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [27]. Consequently, VHL inactivation leads to SFMBT1 accumulation, as confirmed biochemically and validated in cell lines and orthotopic ccRCC mouse models. Accumulated SFMBT1 recognizes mono- and di-methylated histone lysines to modulate chromatin dynamics and transcriptional programs. Multi-omics analyses identify sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1) as a key downstream target that is transcriptionally upregulated to drive sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) signaling [27]. SPHK1 converts pro-apoptotic sphingosine into the highly bioactive lipid S1P, which signals through S1P G-protein coupled receptors (S1PRs) to promote cell proliferation and angiogenesis [71].

Clinically, elevated SFMBT1 expression—alone or in combination with high ZHX2 levels—serves as a prognostic biomarker correlated with poorer OS in ccRCC patient cohorts [72]. Preclinically, activation of the SFMBT1–SPHK1–S1P axis operates as a HIF-independent survival bypass mechanism mediating resistance to HIF-2α inhibitors and TKIs, positioning SFMBT1 as a potential predictive biomarker for treatment resistance [27, 71]. Pharmacological inhibition of downstream S1P signaling restores drug sensitivity in resistant ccRCC models, highlighting a rational therapeutic strategy to overcome resistance [71]. This axis may provide a candidate bypass vulnerability in HIF-2α-inhibitor-resistant ccRCC.

SETDB1

Through analysis of the VHL physical interactome, SET domain bifurcated histone lysine methyltransferase 1 (SETDB1) was identified as a direct, hydroxylation-dependent VHL E3 ligase substrate. Under normoxia, EglN-mediated proline hydroxylation targets SETDB1 for VHL-dependent polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Hypoxia or VHL inactivation leads to SETDB1 accumulation, as demonstrated biochemically and in cell line models [25]. Accumulated SETDB1 catalyzes H3K9 trimethylation (H3K9me3) to repress transposable elements (TEs), thereby suppressing the pathological transcription of endogenous double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) and attenuating downstream type I interferon-mediated innate immune sensing [25, 31]. SETDB1 also epigenetically silences key tumor suppressors (e.g., p21) through promoter H3K9 methylation to promote tumorigenesis [32, 73].

Notably, SETDB1 displays context-dependent and double-edged functions in tumor biology. While SETDB1 stabilization promotes immune evasion and cell proliferation in VHL-deficient ccRCC and other solid tumors [31, 32], SETDB1 simultaneously acts as an essential guardian of genome stability under hypoxic stress, where its complete loss induces severe DNA damage alongside immunogenic viral mimicry [25].

Clinically, high SETDB1 expression functions as a prognostic biomarker correlated with advanced disease stage and poor OS across multiple cancers [32, 73]. Preclinically, SETDB1 elevation acts as a predictive biomarker for resistance to ICIs by dampening intrinsic tumor immunogenicity [25, 31]. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of SETDB1 reactivates viral mimicry and restores anti-tumor immunity in mouse xenograft models, positioning SETDB1 as a potential therapeutic target to overcome immunotherapy resistance in VHL-mutant malignancies [25, 31].

VHL-linked epitranscriptomic regulation and RNA fate

Beyond its chromatin-centered roles, VHL also regulates gene expression at the epitranscriptomic level. VHL serves as a non-degradative assembly scaffold for the methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3)/METTL14 N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methyltransferase complex in a HIF-independent manner. Rather than inducing proteasomal degradation, VHL utilizes its E3 ligase domain to facilitate writer complex assembly and transcriptomic m6A deposition [33]. Integrated methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) and RNA-seq identified phosphoinositide-3-kinase regulatory subunit 3 (PIK3R3) as a key downstream effector. VHL-dependent m6A modification enables insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1/2 (IGF2BP1/2)-mediated PIK3R3 mRNA stabilization. Consequently, VHL loss accelerates PIK3R3 transcript decay, releasing PI3K/AKT signaling to promote tumorigenesis in three-dimensional culture and orthotopic mouse models [33]. Thus, reduced PIK3R3 expression may serve as a candidate predictive biomarker for PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitor sensitivity, though further validation remains required. Global m6A abundance may similarly function as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for intact VHL scaffold activity.

Complementing this direct regulatory axis, the m6A demethylase fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) represents a HIF-independent synthetic lethal partner rather than a direct VHL target. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of FTO selectively impairs VHL-deficient ccRCC growth in cell lines and xenografts by restoring m6A and suppressing SLC1A5-mediated glutamine transport [34]. Here, VHL loss functions as a predictive biomarker for FTO inhibitor responsiveness. Additionally, other RNA-modifying enzymes (e.g., NOP2, NAT10, PUS1) influence ccRCC phenotypes but lack evidence of direct VHL regulation, representing broad disease-context markers rather than non-canonical VHL targets [74–76].

Transcriptional rewiring & cell cycle control

RPB1

RPB1 (encoded by POLR2A), the catalytic subunit of RNA polymerase II, is a direct, hydroxylation-dependent VHL substrate. Under normoxia, PHD1 hydroxylates RPB1 at Pro1465 (LGQLAP motif), triggering VHL-mediated polyubiquitination and proteasomal clearance [35, 36]. Rather than following a single oncogenic model, RPB1 regulation reveals a context-dependent biological paradox across distinct tumor settings. In VHL-deficient ccRCC, failure to degrade RPB1 causes its accumulation, altering global transcriptional elongation kinetics [37]. Tissue accumulation of hyperphosphorylated RPB1 serves as a candidate surrogate biomarker for functional VHL loss. Paradoxically, in VHL-proficient malignancies under oxidative stress, an altered PHD1/PHD2 ratio drives Pro1465 hyper-hydroxylation and C-terminal domain Ser5 phosphorylation. Rather than inducing clearance, this modification operates alongside functional VHL to alter elongation speed, selectively upregulating cell-cycle drivers (CDC25A, CDK2, CCNA2) while repressing CDC25B to accelerate tumor growth in cell lines and xenograft models [36]. Thus, while failure of Pro1465-mediated degradation drives RPB1 accumulation in VHL-deficient ccRCC, this hydroxylation mark functions as a stress-adaptive transcriptional driver in VHL-proficient cancers under oxidative stress. This axis represents a potential predictive biomarker to guide cell-cycle or PHD1-targeted therapies based on VHL status and oxidative stress.

pRB

Expanding its regulatory footprint into the core cell cycle machinery, VHL governs the oxygen-sensitive ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal clearance of the retinoblastoma protein (pRB). In VHL-deficient ccRCC, pathogenic VHL ablation triggers pRB hyperstabilization [38]. Concurrently, HIF-driven Cyclin D1 upregulation induces pRB hyperphosphorylation [77], attenuating its canonical E2F-sequestering function while simultaneously unleashing a context-dependent anti-apoptotic function [77, 78], and repressing Ski/Dach domain-containing protein 1 (SKIDA1) to support clonogenic survival in cell lines and xenografts [38].

Because VHL-deficient carcinomas become uniquely addicted to this hyperphosphorylated pRB network for survival, uncoupling this axis exposes vulnerability. Genetic pRB depletion or pharmacological upstream blockade via dual CDK4/6 inhibitors induces striking synthetic lethality across cell lines, xenografts, and cross-species models [39, 40]. Clinically, pRB accumulation and SKIDA1 repression serve as candidate diagnostic biomarkers for functional VHL loss, providing rationales for potential predictive stratification of VHL-deficient tumors for CDK4/6-targeted therapeutics.

B-Myb

B-Myb (Myb-related protein B) is a direct VHL E3 ligase substrate targeted for proteasomal degradation, an interaction inhibited by receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)-mediated (VEGF/PDGF) Tyr15 phosphorylation [41, 79]. B-Myb displays a context-dependent biological paradox. While canonically acting as a pro-proliferative driver in other malignancies [79], B-Myb functions as a tumor suppressor in VHL-deficient ccRCC. Mechanistically, accumulated B-Myb selectively represses HIF-2α-dependent transcriptional programs (regulating CFB, FKBP1B, and SEPT6), where its depletion accelerates ccRCC tumor growth in cell lines and mouse xenograft models [41]. More detailed molecular mechanism remains to be elucidated.

Growth factor signaling axes & signal transduction hubs

EGFR

Beyond indirect HIF-dependent transcriptional regulation, VHL directly functions as an E3 ligase for activated epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), promoting its Casitas B-lineage lymphoma proto-oncogene (c-Cbl)-independent polyubiquitination and proteasomal clearance in cell line models [80]. Loss of VHL impairs this degradation pathway, leading to EGFR accumulation in early endosomes and sustained downstream oncogenic cascades (e.g., Ras/MAPK, PI3K/AKT) [42]. In patient tissue specimens, observational evidence confirms elevated EGFR and TGFα expression in VHL-associated CNS hemangioblastomas, which correlates with tumor cystic expansion [43], supporting EGFR accumulation as a potential predictive marker for hemangioblastoma severity.

EPOR

Erythropoietin receptor (EPOR)-JAK2–STAT5 signaling is responsible for cell proliferation and ultimate differentiation into mature oxygen-carrying red blood cells. Beyond HIF-mediated transcriptional regulation [81], VHL directly acts as an E3 ligase for EPOR, targeting it for proteasomal degradation following PHD3-mediated hydroxylation at Pro419 and Pro426 in cell line models [44]. VHL inactivation aberrantly stabilizes the EPOR, unleashing constitutive downstream JAK2–STAT5 signaling, which is likely involved in the pathogenesis of specific VHL disease phenotypes such as pheochromocytoma and hemangioblastoma [44]. The value of EPOR as a biomarker remains unvalidated, awaiting clinical cohort and trial validation.

Spry2

Sprouty proteins modulate the actions of RTK during development and tumorigenesis [82]. Previous study reported that Spry2 acts as a direct VHL E3 ligase substrate in cell line models. Under normoxia, PHD1-3-mediated hydroxylation at Pro18, Pro144, and Pro160 targets Spry2 for VHL-dependent polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [45]. Hypoxia or pathogenic VHL deficiency leads to Spry2 accumulation, which inhibits fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-elicited ERK1/2 activation while paradoxically protects growth factor receptors from c-Cbl-mediated degradation, thereby contributing toward the disease [45]. The value of Spry2 as a predictive biomarker for RTK signaling dysregulation requires further validation.

AKT

Departing from its classical E3 ligase paradigm, VHL acts as a degradation-independent scaffold regulating the PI3K/protein kinase B (AKT)/mTOR (PAM) pathway in ccRCC. Under normoxia, EglN1-mediated prolyl hydroxylation at Pro125 and Pro347 promotes AKT binding to VHL, which directly recruits the serine/threonine phosphatase protein phosphatase 2A catalytic subunit (PP2AC) to dephosphorylate and inactivate AKT without altering its protein abundance [46]. In cell line and xenograft models, VHL loss or mutation abolishes PP2AC recruitment, causing HIF-independent AKT hyperactivation—a catalyst for aggressive proliferation, metabolic rewiring, and diminished clinical survival. Elevated phospho-AKT (p-AKT) correlates with advanced stage and reduced survival in ccRCC, serving as a proposed prognostic biomarker [83]. Furthermore, p-AKT represents a potential predictive biomarker for sensitivity to PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway inhibitors and a pharmacodynamic marker for monitoring target engagement, although validation in clinical trials remains limited [84].

Metabolic reprogramming & cellular stress sensors

NDRG3

A study utilizing immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry identified N-myc downstream regulated gene 3 (NDRG3) as a direct VHL E3 ligase substrate targeted for proteasomal degradation via PHD2-mediated hydroxylation under normoxia [29]. Under prolonged hypoxia, accumulating intracellular lactate (the ubiquitous byproduct of the Warburg effect) directly binds NDRG3, physically blocking its interaction with VHL and leading to HIF-independent NDRG3 stabilization [47]. Once stabilized, NDRG3 orchestrates a dual-pronged adaptation strategy. In cell line and xenograft models, NDRG3 physically interacts with c-Raf to activate Raf-ERK signaling, thereby driving cell proliferation and tumor angiogenesis [29, 47]. Concurrently, NDRG3 interacts with Syntaxin-5 to decelerate endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-to-Golgi vesicular transport under hypoxic and high-lactate conditions. This stress-induced attenuation of the secretory pathway mitigates ER stress, conserves critical cellular energy reserves, and prevents the lethal accumulation of misfolded proteins in the ER during metabolic starvation [85].

Furthermore, in later stage of hypoxia, upregulated lactate promotes NDRG3 accumulation, which acts as a temporal feedback regulator by suppressing AKT-driven HIF-1α translation and p300 recruitment, thereby inhibiting the metastatic potential in prostate cancer (PCa) [86]. This elegant functional transition shifts the cellular priority from acute hypoxic survival to chronic metabolic and secretory resilience. Conceptually, NDRG3 accumulation serves as a proposed prognostic biomarker for hypoxic metabolic stress adaptation and a hypothetical predictive biomarker for targeting the lactate-NDRG3 axis to overcome therapy-resistant, chronically hypoxic malignancies, though prospective clinical validation remains required.

TFAM

Beyond substrate degradation, VHL regulates mitochondrial biogenesis by physically stabilizing mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), a key activator of mitochondrial DNA replication and transcription. Following EglN3-mediated prolyl hydroxylation (Pro53/66), VHL physically binds TFAM, competitively shielding it from cleavage by the mitochondrial protease LONP1 [48]. In cell line and xenograft models, oncogenic VHL inactivation unleashes unrestrained LONP1 activity, precipitating mitochondrial depletion. This targeted eradication of oxidative phosphorylation capacity metabolically locks ccRCC cells into the Warburg effect, while simultaneously exacerbating ROS accumulation and inflammatory signaling (e.g., p-JNK, LCN2). Clinically, low TFAM expression and mitochondrial depletion correlate with VHL-deficient malignancies including ccRCC and pheochromocytoma, serving as a proposed diagnostic biomarker. Furthermore, high LONP1 activity represents a potential predictive biomarker for sensitivity blockade via bortezomib (a non-selective proteasome inhibitor with LONP1-inhibiting activity), which restores mitochondrial biogenesis and resensitizes VHL-deficient ccRCC to sorafenib in preclinical models [48]. Further clinical validation remains required.

Extracellular matrix & cytoskeletal architecture

Fibronectin

Fibronectin is a high-molecular-weight glycoprotein found in the extracellular matrix (ECM) and bodily fluids, playing key roles in various biological processes such as cell adhesion, migration, tissue repair, and embryonic development [87]. Fibronectin represents a direct non-degradative binding partner and ECM assembly client of VHL. VHL (wild-type but not various missense mutants under stringent conditions) physically interacts with fibronectin in vivo under physiological conditions to facilitate assembly of the insoluble pericellular fibronectin matrix, while also regulating fibronectin transcription independently of HIF [49, 88]. Pathogenic VHL mutations abolish fibronectin binding and matrix assembly, promoting cell proliferation, migration and invasive phenotypes in cell line and xenograft models. Clinically, disrupted fibronectin matrix architecture strongly correlates with VHL-deficient ccRCC tissues and serves as a proposed diagnostic biomarker for VHL inactivation and invasive ECM remodeling [49, 50, 88].

COL4A2

Beyond interacting with fibronectin, VHL regulates ECM architecture through collagen IV. Independently of HIF regulation, VHL acts as a direct non-degradative binding partner and matrix assembly client for collagen IV alpha 2 (COL4A2). Following ER-mediated prolyl hydroxylation of COL4A2, VHL physically binds its unfolded N-terminal chain to facilitate supramolecular collagen IV network assembly [51, 52, 89]. Tumorigenic VHL mutations—including HIF-competent type 2C variants—impair COL4A2 binding and dismantle the ECM, generating a compromised, mechanically permissive TME that actively licenses unbridled vascular infiltration, pathogenic angiogenesis, and aggressive ccRCC invasion in cell line and xenograft models [52, 89]. Clinically, disrupted collagen IV matrix architecture correlates with VHL-deficient ccRCC tissue specimens, making it a proposed diagnostic biomarker for VHL inactivation (specifically type 2C phenotypes) and invasive TME remodeling, pending prospective validation.

AURKA

Previous studies reported that VHL safeguards the primary cilium by restricting Aurora Kinase A (AURKA) via dual convergent mechanisms: directly as an E3 ligase executing non-canonical, PHD-independent ubiquitination and degradation [53], indirectly by suppressing HIF-driven transcriptional activation of AURKA and its co-activator neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally down-regulated 9 (NEDD9)/human enhancer of filamentation 1 (HEF1) [54]. Reciprocally, AURKA phosphorylates VHL [55]. In cell line and xenograft models, VHL loss causes AURKA hyperactivation and HDAC6-mediated tubulin depolymerization, driving primary cilia disassembly, renal cystogenesis, and cell motility. In clinical tissue specimens, elevated AURKA correlates with primary cilia loss and advanced Fuhrman grade [53, 54], serving as potential diagnostic and predictive biomarkers for VHL inactivation and aggressive ccRCC.

aPKC

VHL intrinsically governs epithelial morphogenesis by coupling microtubule stability with atypical protein kinase C (aPKC, including PKCζ/ι) signaling. Directly interacting via its β-domain, VHL functions as a non-degradative E3 ligase client and spatial scaffold for aPKC [56, 90]. VHL selectively ubiquitinates active, phosphorylated aPKC to regulate its membrane anchoring and maintain cell polarity, co-facilitating the endo-lysosomal internalization and the degradation of pro-metastatic α5 integrin. In cell line models, VHL loss uncouples aPKC spatial regulation, resulting in tight junction disassembly, surface α5 integrin accumulation, and increased cell invasion [57, 90, 91]. Consequently, aberrant aPKC localization/activation and α5 integrin stabilization serve as proposed diagnostic and predictive biomarkers for VHL inactivation and invasive ccRCC phenotypes.

Immune modulation & inflammatory signaling

TBK1

TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) functions as a degradation-independent VHL-binding partner rather than a classical ubiquitin substrate. Under normoxia, EglN1-hydroxylated TBK1 (at Pro48) binds to VHL, which recruits protein phosphatase, Mg2 +/Mn2 + dependent 1B (PPM1B). PPM1B dephosphorylates and inactivates TBK1. Conversely, VHL-deficiency or hypoxia impairs PPM1B recruitment, resulting in persistent TBK1 phosphorylated and hyperactive [28].

The downstream effects of TBK1 exhibit distinct context-dependent cell functions. Canonically, stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-activated TBK1 in immune cells drives type I interferon (IFN) expression and anti-tumor immunity. In VHL-deficient ccRCC cells, VHL loss and secondary HIF accumulation contribute to immunosuppressive TME remodeling by stimulating pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., CX3CL1, CCL2), recruiting tumor-associated macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and impairing T-cell function—key drivers of resistance to ICIs. Within this framework, TBK1 is rewired away from IFN signaling. Hyperactivated TBK1 phosphorylates p62 at Ser366, which facilitates the degradation of specific proteins by modulating pro-tumorigenic autophagy signaling, and promotes cell proliferation and xenograft ccRCC growth. Elevated phospho-TBK1 and VHL loss serve as proposed predictive biomarkers for synthetic lethal sensitivity to TBK1 inhibitors [28, 92]. Therefore, aberrant TBK1 activation not only supports pro-survival autophagic flux and metabolic adaptation, but also offers dual therapeutic potential by exploiting synthetic lethality in VHL-deficient ccRCC and potentially modulating myeloid-driven immune evasion to restore ICIs sensitivity.

RIPK1

Receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) is regulated by VHL via a non-canonical, degradation-independent mechanism. Under normoxia, EglN1 hydroxylates RIPK1 on prolyl residues, directly suppressing RIPK1 kinase activity (autophosphorylation at Ser166) and preserving cell survival. Conversely, prolonged hypoxia or VHL loss reduces RIPK1 prolyl hydroxylation and alleviates this inhibition, unleashing RIPK1 kinase-dependent necroptosis and pro-inflammatory signaling [59].

RIPK1 signaling displays functional context-dependency, oscillating between kinase-mediated cell death and scaffold-mediated survival/immunosuppression. Beyond its kinase activity, RIPK1 functions as a molecular scaffold that subverts tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling away from CASP8-dependent apoptosis and toward NF-κB-driven survival. Extrinsically, this scaffold activity drives an immunosuppressive cytokine network that remodels the TME—promoting ARG1+ suppressive myeloid cell accumulation and excluding cytotoxic T and natural killer (NK) cells—thereby mediating resistance to ICIs [93]. Furthermore, another study demonstrated that the O-linked β-N-acetyglucosamine modification (O-GlcNAcylation) of RIPK1 induced sunitnib resistance in RCC by inhibiting RIPK-dependent apoptosis [94]. Supported by biochemical binding assays, RCC cell lines, and mouse tumor models, phospho-RIPK1 (Ser166) and RIPK1 modification status serve as proposed predictive biomarkers for ICI resistance, TKI non-responsiveness, and sensitivity to RIPK1-targeted inhibitors. Further clinical investigations are warranted.

Hierarchical ubiquitin ligase cascades

UBE3B

Expanding its regulatory architecture into inter-ligase crosstalk, VHL regulates hierarchical E3 ubiquitin ligase cascades by directly targeting the subordinate ligase ubiquitin protein ligase E3B (UBE3B) for proteasomal degradation. Operating independently of oxygen tension and EglN1-mediated hydroxylation, the VHL β-domain physically engages UBE3B, driving its K48-linked polyubiquitination specifically at residues K286 and K427 to enforce rapid proteasomal clearance [60]. Reciprocally, UBE3B acts as an oxygen-independent stabilizer of HIF-2α, shielding it from VHL-mediated degradation even when canonical prolyl residues (Pro405/531) are mutated [95].

Recent evidence suggests that UBE3B plays a crucial role in cancer progression and drug resistance [96, 97]. By actively destroying UBE3B, VHL dismantles this oncogenic bypass, thereby imposing a critical secondary failsafe against HIF-2α hyperactivation. Conversely, VHL deficiency leads to UBE3B accumulation, which promotes tumor cell proliferation and pulmonary metastasis, as demonstrated in breast cancer cell lines and mouse xenograft models. Clinically, high protein levels of UBE3B positively correlated with poor OS of breast cancer patients, highlighting elevated UBE3B as a prognostic biomarker for aggressive disease and metastatic risk, as well as a candidate therapeutic target [60, 95].

JADE1

VHL functions as a critical stabilizing chaperone for Jade family plant homeodomain finger 1 (JADE1), a renal-preferential tumor suppressor and E3 ubiquitin ligase [98]. Mechanistically, VHL engages JADE1 via its β-domain and maintains JADE1 protein stability through its α-domain [61]. Notably, truncations or ccRCC-associated VHL missense mutations (e.g., L118P, R167W)—unlike non-ccRCC variants (e.g., Y98H)—selectively abolish JADE1 stabilization, directly linking the loss of JADE1 stability to ccRCC risk [61–63]. Downstream, stabilized JADE1 ubiquitinates both phosphorylated and non-phosphorylated β-catenin to suppress Wnt signaling, while physically binding and repressing endogenous levels of active phospho-AKT1 [61, 64]. Therefore, VHL deficiency leads to JADE1 protein depletion, driving a dual oncogenic surge (concurrent β-catenin and AKT pathway hyperactivation) in ccRCC cell lines and structural models. Database analysis suggested that reduced Jade-1 expression is associated with advanced tumor stage, high pathological grade and more patient deaths [64], making it a poor prognostic factor for ccRCC.

Non-proteolytic topologies & steric shielding

BIM-EL

Similar to JADE1, VHL acts as a critical stabilizing chaperone for the pro-apoptotic BH3-only protein BIM-EL. Following EglN3-mediated prolyl hydroxylation at Pro67 and Pro70, VHL physically binds BIM-EL, sterically blocking adjacent Ser69 residue from ERK-dependent phosphorylation and subsequent proteasomal degradation [65, 66]. Type 2C VHL missense mutations selectively disrupt this interaction while retaining HIF-α degradative capacity, preventing sympathoadrenal lineage cell apoptosis and contributing to familial pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma pathogenesis [99]. In RCC cell lines, VHL loss depletes BIM-EL pools and confers resistance to DNA-damaging chemotherapeutics (e.g., cisplatin), an effect reversed by pharmacological ERK inhibition [100]. Therefore, BIM-EL protein status represents a proposed predictive biomarker for chemotherapeutic non-responsiveness and MEK/ERK inhibitor combination sensitivity in VHL-deficient tumors, as well as a potential diagnostic marker for type 2C VHL-associated pheochromocytoma.

nCLU

VHL functions as a non-proteolytic E3 ubiquitin ligase for nuclear clusterin (nCLU). Mechanistically, VHL engages nCLU via its β-domain to catalyze K63-linked—rather than canonical K48-linked—polyubiquitination, directing nCLU nucleocytoplasmic translocation and cellular morphological changes without inducing proteasomal degradation. Demonstrated primarily in RCC cell lines and human tissue specimens, clusterin expression, secretion, and subcellular localization correlate with VHL functional status independently of HIF-α [67, 101]. Ultimately, this paradigm establishes VHL not merely as a destructive ligase, but as a versatile post-translational spatial coordinator that dynamically fine-tunes intracellular trafficking and signaling networks. However, the physiological function of VHL mediating nCLU ubiquitination and its prospective clinical utility are still unclear and require further investigation.

VHL dysregulation in pan-cancer contexts

Driven by the rapid evolution of multi-omics and high-throughput profiling, contemporary oncology recognizes VHL as a pleiotropic tumor suppressor occurring across several non-renal malignancies. However, VHL functions are highly tissue-dependent, and non-renal tumor phenotypes vary substantially based on cellular lineage. Consequently, canonical VHL mechanisms established in ccRCC cannot be universally extrapolated to other cancer contexts without direct experimental validation. To critically assess non-renal VHL dysregulation, we classify reported alterations into four evidence tiers: (1) genetic/truncal disruptions with demonstrated causality; (2) epigenetic or post-translational suppression of wild-type VHL; (3) context-dependent VHL-associated rewiring; and (4) correlative transcriptomic associations lacking functional validation (Table 2). Thoracic malignancies: lung cancer and mesothelioma

Despite rare somatic mutations (<5%), VHL down-regulation in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) occurs primarily through chromosome 3p25 loss of heterozygosity (LOH) and promoter hypermethylation [102–104]. Epigenetic VHL silencing frequently co-occurs with EGFR mutations, and VHL haploinsufficiency cooperates with FHIT loss to drive lung adenocarcinoma in mice, recapitulating the classic multi-hit 3p-deletion paradigm [102, 103, 105–107]. Notably, VHL exhibits tissue-specific functional divergence: unlike its canonical tumor-suppressive role in ccRCC, VHL exerts strict, HIF-independent control over the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). VHL knockdown in NSCLC cell lines attenuates integrin/FAK signaling, paradoxically reducing cell proliferation and colonization in vitro despite inducing EMT features (E-cadherin loss, elevated α-SMA) [108, 109]. This highlights a highly context-dependent, structural mandate for VHL in the pulmonary microenvironment.

In malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), VHL inactivating mutations occurs in an ultra-rare, distinct clear cell subtype [110–112]. Unlike canonical MPMs driven by BAP1 or NF2 loss, clear cell MPMs feature VHL mutations alongside genomic near-haploidization [130]. In vitro evidence shows that VHL-mutant MPM cell lines depend on HIF-1α for survival, where HIF-1α suppression induces poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP)-mediated apoptosis [110]. Consequently, VHL mutation status represents a potential diagnostic biomarker to distinguish clear cell MPM from classical subtypes and a speculative predictive biomarker for HIF-targeted therapies (e.g., belzutifan) [112]. More prospective clinical evaluation is required.

Breast cancer

In breast cancer, particularly triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), somatic VHL mutations are exceedingly rare. Instead, VHL depletion occurs primarily via post-translational degradation and epigenetic hypermethylation. Mechanistically, cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) phosphorylates VHL at Ser80, recruiting peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase NIMA-interacting 1 (PIN1) to promote WSB1-mediated VHL ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [113]. This targeted eradication confers chemotherapeutic resistance. Pharmacological stabilization of VHL via CDK1 or PIN1 inhibitors (e.g., RO-3306 or ATRA) successfully resensitizes TNBC cell lines to chemotherapy [113]. Furthermore, intact VHL restricts breast cancer progression by targeting the E3 ligase UBE3B for degradation. Conversely, VHL depletion stabilizes UBE3B to upregulate HIF-2α, driving tumor growth and metastasis in preclinical models [60, 95]. Clinically, reduced VHL protein expression correlates with higher tumor grade, lymph node metastasis, and shorter OS, establishing low VHL status as a proposed prognostic biomarker in breast cancer [114, 131].

Thyroid carcinoma

In papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), VHL dysregulation diverges from canonical somatic mutations or promoter hypermethylation. Instead, it occurs predominantly through non-mutational transcriptional and post-transcriptional alterations such as alternative mRNA splicing and microRNA targeting [115, 116]. Clinical observational studies report an inverse correlation between VHL downregulation and aggressive pathological features, including lymph node metastasis and reduced disease-free survival (DFS) [116, 117]. Post-transcriptional silencing by oncogenic microRNAs (e.g., miR-21 and miR-92a) has been implicated in this process [118, 119]. Specifically, in vitro cell-line experiments indicate that miR-21 directly targets VHL to activate PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby promoting and tumor invasion [118]. However, current evidence for VHL in PTC remains largely observational or restricted to cell culture models. Independent in vivo validation and biomarker-driven therapy remain to be further elucidated.

pNets and PPGLs

In neuroendocrine neoplasms, VHL inactivation occurs via distinct genetic and epigenetic mechanisms depending on tumor etiology. While germline VHL mutations represent established genetic drivers of syndromic pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs) and pheochromocytomas/paragangliomas (PPGLs), somatic VHL mutations are rare in sporadic cases. Instead, VHL inactivation occurs in approximately 25% of sporadic pNETs through 3p25 LOH and promoter hypermethylation, leading to pseudohypoxic HIF-1α accumulation and upregulated downstream metabolic effectors [e.g., carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA-9) and the glucose transporter 1 (GLUT-1)] that correlate with shortened DFS [120, 132].

In PPGLs, VHL inactivation (often clustering with SDHx and PHD mutations) contributes to pseudohypoxic metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial suppression [133]. This metabolic subversion is regulated in part by HIF-responsive non-coding RNAs (e.g., miR-210 and lncRNA MALAT1) that orchestrate aggressive metastasis and neuroendocrine dedifferentiation [121, 134]. Clinically, low VHL status and secondary pseudohypoxic markers serve as proposed negative prognostic biomarkers in sporadic pNETs [120]. However, therapeutic strategies targeting the VHL/HIF metabolic axis in neuroendocrine tumors remain largely preclinical, requiring further clinical exploration.

CNS tumor

In central nervous system (CNS) tumors, VHL dysregulation operates through distinct etiology-specific mechanisms. Germline VHL inactivation drives progenitor cell expansion and syndromic CNS hemangioblastomas (the pathognomonic hallmark of VHL disease) via constitutive HIF-1/2α stabilization and cross-talk with JAK2/STAT3 signaling [122, 135]. Conversely, somatic VHL mutations are rare in glioblastoma; instead, wild-type VHL is systematically eradicated via a highly coordinated post-translational and epigenetic web. Transcriptionally, oncogenic microRNAs (e.g., miR-150) strictly silence VHL, establishing deleterious feedback loops that hyperactivates EGFR/AKT and β-catenin signaling [123]. Post-translationally, VHL is aggressively degraded by overexpressed E3 ligases (e.g., FBXO22) and the Wnt-complex protein Daam2 [124, 125, 136]. Furthermore, phosphorylated Profilin-1 (inhibiting VHL binding to HIF-1α) and ID2 (sterically occlude the VHL-Elongin C complex) could shield HIF-1α from the recognition of VHL, therefore stabilizing the HIF and promoting survival during metabolic stress. Preclinical cell-line and xenograft studies indicate that VHL downregulation promotes cell survival, stemness, and invasion [124, 125, 136, 137]. Experimental VHL restoration rapidly reverses this malignant phenotype, induces sudden G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, downregulates anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, and represses critical glioma stem-like cell markers (such as CD133) by re-establishing JAK2/STAT3 inhibition [138, 139]. However, strategies aimed at restoring VHL function or targeting upstream regulators remain experimental, lacking clinical validation in glioblastoma.

Hematological malignancies

In hematological malignancies, VHL dysregulation is predominantly non-mutational, displaying distinct evidence tiers across subtypes. Retrospective cohort studies indicate that VHL promoter hypermethylation correlates with adverse clinical features, such as osteolytic bone lesions in multiple myeloma (MM) [126, 140], and higher International Prognostic Index scores and dismal survival in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [141], making it a potential predictive and prognostic biomarker. In MM, epigenetic ablation of VHL abolishes HIF-1α proteolysis, precipitating local angiogenesis and driving an acidic, hypoxic marrow niche. This microenvironmental rewiring favors malignant plasma cell survival [126]. Regarding DLBCL, in vitro models suggest that oncogenic miR-21 suppresses VHL post-transcriptionally. Pharmacological antagonism of miR-21 (e.g., via curcumin) successfully restabilizes VHL, restricting DLBCL proliferation and inducing apoptosis [127]. Beyond somatic silencing, a single-pedigree report proposed a multi-hit leukemogenesis model wherein germline VHL variants cooperate with acquired somatic mutations (e.g., BCOR, NF1) and genetic modifiers (e.g., ASXL3, CCR7) to drive phenotypic heterogeneity [142]. These findings expand the known phenotypic repertoire of VHL-related disorders, highlight the complex genotype-phenotype interplay, and offer insights for genetic counseling and clinical management of familial hematological disorders.

HNSCC

In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), VHL dysregulation is non-mutational. Although chromosome 3p LOH encompassing the VHL locus is frequent in tongue SCC, VHL protein exhibits dynamic expression. Its cytoplasmic expression spatially expands during premalignant dysplasia and invasive carcinogenesis, tightly co-localizing with the basal proliferative marker CK17 [143, 144]. This aberrant accumulation establishes VHL as a distinct adjunctive histopathological biomarker for early dysplastic transformation, although its expression intensity progressively wanes during tumor dedifferentiation [144].

Preclinically, residual VHL is suppressed post-transcriptionally by oncogenic miR-21, unleashing downstream STAT3 and β-catenin cascades to drive tumor invasion. Pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 (e.g., using HJC0152) indirectly restores VHL levels and dismantles the STAT3/miR-21/VHL/β-catenin axis, restricting tumor growth and invasion in vitro and in orthotopic mouse models [128]. However, this therapeutic approach remains strictly preclinical and warrants further clinical validation.

Therapeutic strategies: from inhibitors to degraders

Translating the established mechanistic of VHL novel targets and pan-cancer landscapes into clinical applications encompasses two primary approaches: targeted SMIs exploiting VHL-deficient vulnerabilities and VHL-recruiting PROTACs.

SMIs targeting novel substrates

The identification of non-canonical VHL novel targets has unveiled new oncogenic pathways and therapeutic vulnerabilities in cancers, particularly ccRCC. TBK1 inhibitors

In VHL-deficient ccRCC, constitutive TBK1 hyperactivation has been proposed preclinically as a synthetic lethal vulnerability, offering rationales for repurposing clinical-stage TBK1 inhibitors. Momelotinib, an FDA-approved dual JAK/TBK1 inhibitor with clinical success in hematological settings (e.g., the phase III MOMENTUM trial for myelofibrosis) [145], conferred limited survival benefits in unselected solid tumor trials [e.g., NSCLC and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)] [146]. It’s potentially attributable to the lack of biomarker-driven patient selection (e.g., VHL status).

Similarly, amlexanox, a dual IKKε/TBK1 inhibitor evaluated in metabolic disorders, suppresses TBK1 activity to disrupt p62-driven oncogenic signaling downstream of VHL loss [147, 157, 158]. Recent preclinical profiling reveals its pleiotropic anticancer efficacy in vitro and in xenograft models across diverse malignancies (e.g., colorectal cancer, glioblastoma and castration-resistant PCa) via the disruption of Hippo/LATS signaling and Rad51-dependent DNA repair [159]. However, its clinical translation is constrained by potential off-target toxicities, narrow therapeutic windows, and an absence of oncological clinical trial validation.

SPHK1 inhibitors

In VHL-deficient malignancies, pathological stabilization of the substrate SFMBT1 upregulates SPHK1 and alters sphingolipid metabolism toward aggressive proliferation [27]. Lacking direct SFMBT1 inhibitors, pharmacological blockade of downstream SPHK signaling offers a rational therapeutic bypass. Preclinically, SPHK1 inhibitors (e.g., PF-543) and SPHK2 inhibitor opaganib (ABC294640) suppress tumor cell proliferation and growth in cell-line and patient-derived xenograft models [160, 161]. Clinically, opaganib has demonstrated safety and systemic pro-survival S1P reduction in phase I/II trials for advanced solid tumors and refractory MM [153, 154]. However, targeting SPHK signaling in VHL-deficient tumors remains hypothetical and preclinical, currently lacking clinical validation in VHL-stratified cohorts and limited by potential metabolic redundancy and dose-limiting toxicities.

AURKA inhibition

In VHL-deficient ccRCC, loss of VHL function preclinically correlates with increased sensitivity to alisertib (MLN8237), a selective, orally bioavailable ATP-competitive AURKA inhibitor [162]. Concomitant targeting of AURKA and downstream nodes, such as the E3 ligase SKP2 (e.g., SZL-P1-41), demonstrates synergistic growth suppression in ccRCC cell-line and xenograft models [163]. Clinically, alisertib has been evaluated in non-VHL-stratified solid tumor trials including paclitaxel-resistant ovarian cancer, endocrine/CDK4/6i-resistant metastatic breast cancer (e.g., TBCRC041) and refractory pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Primarily in combination regimens with chemotherapy or mTOR inhibitors, it demonstrated disease stabilization and partial responses (PRs), though frequently constrained by dose-limiting hematologic toxicities such as neutropenia and leukopenia [150, 152]. Thus, alisertib has exhibited good potential in treating advanced tumors, currently requiring clinical trial verification in VHL-selected patient cohorts.

LONP1-TFAM-related SMIs

In VHL-deficient carcinomas, the mitochondrial matrix protease LONP1 aberrantly degrades the transcription factor TFAM, enforcing the Warburg effect and suppressing oxidative phosphorylation [48]. Although bortezomib (26S proteasome inhibitor) lacks selectivity for LONP1 and exerts broad proteasomal off-target effects, it preclinically stabilizes TFAM, modulates NF-κB signaling, and powerfully resensitizes highly refractory tumors to TKIs in VHL-deficient preclinical models [164]. It played significant roles as therapeutic drugs for various cancers. We mainly focused on the progress in unselected metastatic RCC treatment. Multiple early-phase evaluations of bortezomib—either as monotherapy or combined with sorafenib/bevacizumab—yielded minimal activity [155, 156], confounded by severe proteasome-related toxicities and a lack of biomarker stratification. Selective LONP1 inhibitors remain in early preclinical development; thus, therapeutic exploitation of the VHL-LONP1-TFAM axis in oncology remains an unvalidated hypothesis requiring clinical verification.

Harnessing VHL as a tool: PROTACs

In contrast to therapeutic strategies exploiting VHL loss in VHL-deficient malignancies such as ccRCC, VHL-recruiting PROTACs strictly depend on intact, functional VHL machinery. PROTAC represents a transformative class of heterobifunctional molecules engineered to hijack the ubiquitin-proteasome system for targeted chemical knockdown. These heterobifunctional molecules tether a protein of interest (POI) binder to a high-affinity VHL ligand, facilitating ternary complex (POI-PROTAC-VHL) assembly and subsequent proteasomal degradation of the target [165]. Driven by the favorable structural fidelity and physicochemical properties of VHL-ligands, VHL represents one of the most widely utilized E3 ligases in targeted protein degradation alongside Cereblon [166].

In VHL-proficient contexts, VHL-recruiting PROTACs are characterized by the following advantages (Fig. 4): (1) Substoichiometric event-driven pharmacology and expanded druggable proteome scope: Unlike occupancy-driven SMIs requiring deep active-site pockets and sustained systemic exposure, VHL-recruiting PROTACs operate via event-driven mechanisms. Transient binding to accessible surface pockets triggers target ubiquitination, enabling target depletion at substoichiometric concentrations and theoretically lowering dose-dependent off-target toxicity [167, 168]. Moreover, this catalytic turnover enables the depletion of non-enzymatic scaffolding proteins and historically “undruggable” targets, including p38α, RAS, and selected transcription factors [167, 169, 170]. (2) Enhanced selectivity: Different from non-cooperative ternary assemblies, VHL-recruiting PROTACs can form thermodynamically stable, cooperative ternary complexes (POI-PROTAC-VHL) stabilized by spatial protein-protein interactions. This thermodynamic stability improves degradation selectivity and efficiency, as demonstrated by isoform-specific SGK3 degradation in vitro [166, 171]. (3) Overcoming resistance: Catalytic, event-driven degradation can bypass target point mutations and protein accumulation that impair SMI binding [169]. Preclinically, VHL-recruiting PROTACs have demonstrated efficacy against SMI-resistant variants, such as BCR-ABL mutants [172]. Furthermore, targeted degradation can overcome resistance driven by target overexpression or secondary mutations, as observed with anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) degraders in preclinical NSCLC models refractory to conventional ALK inhibitors (e.g., ceritinib, lorlatinib) [173]. (4) Concomitant pathway modulation: Occupation of the VHL binding pocket by PROTACs can competitively stabilize physiological VHL substrates while degrading the targeted pathogenic protein. DT2216

B-cell lymphoma-extra-large (BCL-XL), a pivotal anti-apoptotic protein, drives oncogenesis by sequestering pro-apoptotic effectors (e.g., BIM, BAX) to evade apoptosis. BCL-XL is frequently overexpressed in various hematologic (e.g., T-cell lymphomas, TCLs) and solid malignancies (e.g., small cell lung cancer, SCLC) [176]. Conventional BCL-XL SMIs (e.g., navitoclax) are constrained by dose-limiting on-target thrombocytopenia due to platelet reliance on BCL-XL for survival [177]. To widen this narrow therapeutic window, DT2216 was engineered as a first-in-class BCL-XL PROTAC that recruits wild-type VHL. Because mature platelets express low levels of VHL, DT2216 selectively induces BCL-XL degradation and triggers BAX/BAK-dependent apoptosis in VHL-proficient tumor cells while sparing platelets [178, 179]. Preclinically, in post-myeloproliferative neoplasm acute myeloid leukemia (post-MPN AML)—a high-risk subset strictly dependent on BCL-XL—DT2216 eradicates CD34+ leukemic stem cells. DT2216 exhibited broad synergistic potential, which resensitizes refractory melanoma to BRAF/MEK targeted therapies [180], synergizes with DHODH inhibitors in PDAC [181], and primes chromophobe RCC for ferroptosis-induced cell death [182]. In ongoing phase I/Ib trials, DT2216 exhibited stable pharmacokinetic profiles, reduced platelet toxicity (transient, reversible thrombocytopenia) and preliminary antitumor activity [stable disease (SD) in 20% patients, median OS: 7.9 months] in refractory malignancies [175]. Though DT2216 has secured FDA Orphan Drug and Fast Track designations for TCL and SCLC, its clinical translation will depend on identifying biomarkers of BCL-XL dependency and combining with MCL-1 or BCL-2 inhibitors to prevent anti-apoptotic signaling redundancy.

KT-333

STAT3 is an oncogenic master regulator driving tumor proliferation, survival, and immunosuppressive microenvironment via PD-L1 upregulation. As a transcription factor lacking deep hydrophobic enzymatic pockets, STAT3 has historically proven difficult to target with conventional SMIs and was considered “undruggable”. To overcome this limitation, KT-333 was developed as a first-in-class VHL-PROTAC that recruits wild-type VHL to induce ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of STAT3, bypassing the need for high-affinity active site occupancy [183, 184]. In an ongoing phase I trial in relapsed/refractory lymphomas and solid tumors (NCT05225584, n = 51), KT-333 demonstrated up to 99% target reduction in peripheral blood and tumor biopsies, abolishing downstream oncogenic signaling (e.g., SOCS3) and inducing IFN-γ transcriptional signature. KT-333 achieved CRs in classic Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and STAT3-mutated NK-cell lymphoma, alongside multiple PRs in cutaneous TCL. It also exhibits manageable safety profiles with main toxicities including stomatitis and fatigue [185]. Validating phase II durability remains crucial next steps.

ASP3082

Activating mutations in the Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) drive approximately 30% of all malignancies. As the most common (29%) mutation in KRAS-mutated cancer, KRAS-G12D has the highest prevalence across diverse carcinomas such as PDAC and colorectal cancer [186]. However, unlike KRAS-G12C variants targeted by covalent inhibitors, KRAS-G12D lacks a reactive cysteine residue, hindering conventional SMIs [186, 187]. ASP3082 was developed as a first-in-class VHL-recruiting PROTAC that achieves durable suppression of downstream effectors (e.g., p-ERK, p-AKT) with exquisite selectivity over wild-type KRAS. Preclinically, despite high lipophilicity and plasma protein binding that necessitate intermittent intravenous administration, ASP3082 effectively induced tumor regression in xenograft models [188].

In an ongoing phase I trial in advanced solid tumors (NCT05382559), ASP3082 demonstrated preliminary clinical activity with reversible transaminase (ALT/AST) elevations as primary toxicities. At the 300 mg dose level, it achieved a 33.3% ORR and a 75.0% disease control rate (DCR), delivering PRs in refractory PDAC and NSCLC [189]. Overcoming high plasma protein binding to optimize free drug exposure and developing strategies to manage intravenous delivery demands represent critical priorities.

PRT3789

Aberrations within the SWI/SNF (BAF) chromatin remodeling complex drive approximately 20% of human malignancies, altering the TME and modulating therapeutic responses to ICIs. The catalytic ATPase activity of this complex is governed by mutually exclusive paralogs: SMARCA2 or SMARCA4. In many cancers (e.g., NSCLC, esophageal) harboring loss-of-function mutations in SMARCA4, cells become dependent on its paralog SMARCA2 for survival, establishing a synthetic lethal vulnerability [190].

PRT3789 was engineered as a first-in-class VHL-recruiting PROTAC that selectively degrades SMARCA2 over SMARCA4 (>1000-fold selectivity). This specificity is governed by a slow ternary complex dissociation rate, which directs polyubiquitination strictly to K1405—a residue located on an extended loop unique to SMARCA2 and absent in SMARCA4 paralog [191]. Preclinically, PRT3789 demonstrated selective SMARCA2 depletion, inducing cell death in SMARCA4-deficient models with a weekly intravenous dosing schedule supported by durable pharmacodynamic suppression [191]. In ongoing phase I trials in advanced SMARCA4-mutated solid tumors, PRT3789 showed preliminary clinical activity. It achieved RECIST-confirmed PRs, tumor shrinkage and prolonged SDs in heavily pretreated NSCLC and esophageal carcinomas with a favorable safety profile [192]. Future clinical implementation hinges on precise patient selection for complete SMARCA4 loss to account for intratumoral heterogeneity and exploring synergistic regimens with epigenetic regulators.

While VHL-PROTACs present distinct pharmacological opportunities, critical biological and translational limitations exist. First, because catalytic degradation mandates an operational VCB-CR complex, these degraders are applicable primarily to VHL-proficient tumors. Conversely, VHL loss, mutation, or genomic inactivation represents a primary intrinsic or acquired resistance mechanism [170, 193]. Second, clinical translation is hindered by unfavorable physicochemical properties (high molecular weight, extensive polar surface area) that impair membrane permeability and oral bioavailability [194], as well as dose-dependent “hook effects” at high systemic exposures that attenuate ternary complex assembly in vivo [170, 171]. Third, because ubiquitination requires cooperative protein-protein interactions rather than simple binding affinity, empirical design is inefficient. Rational optimization increasingly relies on integrated structural biology and computational modeling platforms (e.g., PRosettaC) [195]. Furthermore, prolonged degradation pressure in preclinical models can select for point mutations at the ternary interface or somatic alterations in core ligase machinery [168, 170]. Crucially, current therapeutic claims derive predominantly from in vitro and animal models. Further rigorous validation is required to establish their human safety, therapeutic index, and clinical efficacy. Establishing biomarker-driven patient selection—specifically pre-screening for functional VHL status—will be mandatory to guide clinical development [168, 169].

Discussion and perspective

While intercepting the canonical VHL-HIF axis (e.g., TKIs, ICIs, or HIF-2α inhibitors) has improved advanced ccRCC management, intrinsic non-responsiveness and acquired resistance in a subset of tumors remain major clinical challenges [14, 196, 197]. Accumulating evidence indicates that VHL functions beyond hypoxia regulation by interacting with non-HIF targets across epigenetic modulation (e.g., ZHX2, SETDB1, METTL3/METTL14), metabolic reprogramming (e.g., NDRG3, TFAM), ECM and cytoskeletal architecture regulation (e.g., fibronectin, COL4A2), and immune modulation (e.g., TBK1). The supporting evidence across these novel targets varies from direct biochemical ubiquitination to downstream correlative observations. By systematically mapping the expanding universe of non-HIF substrates, this review redefines VHL as a pleiotropic hub that coordinates proteostasis and cellular homeostasis. Furthermore, non-canonical VHL interactions extend to broad pan-cancer contexts. For example, VHL-mediated polyubiquitination of UBE3B suppresses breast cancer growth and metastasis in preclinical cell-line and xenograft models [60]. Therefore, shedding light on these novel VHL targets is important for redefining VHL-mediated proteostasis and pioneering next-generation therapies across both ccRCC and other malignancies. These targets represent merely a fraction of emerging therapeutic vulnerabilities ripe. Integrating these findings into the broader oncology research context demands a balanced evaluation of both established and emerging pathways to construct more comprehensive molecular frameworks.

Translating these non-HIF mechanisms into clinical interventions presents distinct opportunities and challenges. In VHL-deficient tumors, targeting unshielded non-HIF downstream effectors (e.g., opaganib, alisertib) offers candidate salvage strategies. Conversely, in VHL-proficient malignancies, hijacking operational VHL via PROTACs (e.g., DT2216, KT-333) enables targeted degradation of oncogenic drivers. Nevertheless, clinical realization across both modalities faces substantial hurdles, including pharmacokinetic constraints, potential toxicities, intratumoral heterogeneity, and acquired resistance. Most advances remain at the preclinical or early-phase evaluation stage and must be viewed with cautious optimism, as the heterogeneity of cancer and the complexity of VHL target interactions necessitate rigorous validation. Future progress requires further investigation in robust translational models alongside biomarker-driven patient stratification to verify VHL functional status and target dependency.

Conclusion

This review delineates the evolution of VHL biology from a canonical HIF oxygen sensor to a broader regulator of proteostasis, establishing how non-HIF target networks and dual therapeutic modalities expand the oncology landscape—SMIs targeting non-HIF effectors in VHL-deficient tumors and VHL-recruiting PROTACs in VHL-proficient tumors. Fully realizing this translational potential requires addressing some major unresolved questions: (1) Identifying structural motifs governing non-HIF target engagement and expanding the VHL ligand repertoire. (2) Elucidating how non-HIF targets coordinates tissue-specific tumorigenesis in VHL-deficient versus VHL-proficient malignancies. (3) Deciphering target-bypass pathways in SMIs and the mechanisms of acquires PROTAC resistance. (4) Establishing validated predictive biomarkers—specifically prescreening for functional VHL status—to guide patient stratification. Addressing these core mechanistic and pharmacological bottlenecks will be essential to guide next-generation therapies in VHL-associated malignancies.
