Short-term effectiveness of soft bracing in the treatment of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chunxiao Lu, Xinjing Wu, Jinzhu Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo analyse the short-term effectiveness of soft bracing in the treatment of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) patients in terms of the correction success rate, daily wear time and comfort levels and explore the sources of heterogeneity.MethodsA literature search was performed using PubMed, EMBASE, Web Of Science, and Cochrane Library from the establishment of the literature database to April 2023. According to the evaluation criteria for the effectiveness of bracing from the International Society on Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment (SOSORT) and the evaluation indicators of the success rate in previous studies, the success rate was defined as a Cobb angle ≤ 5° curve progression at maturity and a terminal follow-up angle < 45°. The success rate of soft bracing was compared with that of observation and rigid bracing. The daily wear time and comfort levels of soft bracing were compared with those of rigid bracing. The data were analyzed using RevMan 5.4, Stata 15.0 and IBM SPSS 25.0.ResultsTwelve studies involving 510 patients were included in the meta-analysis. In the single-arm analysis of 12 studies, the success rate of soft braces was 70% [OR = 0.70, 95% CI (0.61, 0.77), P < 0.0001]. In the double-arm analysis, 4 studies reported that the success rate of soft bracing was lower than that of rigid bracing [RR = 0.66, 95% CI (0.53, 0.81), P < 0.0001]. Three studies reported that the success rate of soft bracing was higher than that of observation [RR = 2.02, 95% CI (1.41, 2.89), P = 0.0001]. According to the subgroup analysis, the success rate of the Cobb angle < 30° was 71% [OR = 0.71, 95% CI (0.63, 0.78), P < 0.00001], and the success rate of the prospective studies was 75% [OR = 0.75, 95% CI (0.70, 0.79), P < 0.00001]. The study types were heterogeneous source and the subgroup with a Cobb angle < 30° had a higher success rate and less heterogeneity. The wear time every day of the brace was 20.83 ± 2.08 h for the soft braces and 12.85 ± 2.08 h for the rigid braces, and there was a significant difference between the two groups (t = 10.39, p < 0.001).ConclusionsSoft braces had short-term effectiveness for mild to moderate AIS patients compared with observation groups, and they had a lower success rate and higher daily wear time and comfort levels than rigid bracing. The subgroup of patients with a Cobb angle < 30° had a higher success rate and less heterogeneity in terms of soft bracing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09918-3.

Introduction

Scoliosis is defined as a lateral spinal curvature of 10 degrees or greater, assessed using the Cobb method [1]. The prevalence of AIS is 0.47–5.2%, accounting for approximately 80% of all cases of scoliosis [2] and posing a significant risk to the physical and mental development of adolescents. Braces are recommended as the most important treatment for mild to moderate AIS [3]. Bracing can be classified as rigid bracing or soft bracing on the basis of the materials used to construct the brace. The effectiveness of full-time rigid bracing in the treatment of AIS patients has been documented, but it can decrease muscle endurance and pulmonary function, limit physical activity and lead to skin breakdown [4–7]. In accordance with the 2016 SOSORT guidelines, full-time rigid braces and soft braces are required to be worn all the time (20–23 h per day) to achieve the maximum effect. However, rigid braces worn for a long time are more likely to cause skin breakdown; thus, improving the comfort levels while ensuring the daily wear time is important for reducing adverse reactions [8]. Many studies have shown that soft braces can be worn full-time (20–23 h) and that patients do not report any discomfort [9–11]. Soft bracing has been explored in an attempt to overcome its disadvantages. Soft braces are defined as more comfortable supports made of soft fabric, which is equivalent to the external muscles, providing active soft support and allowing the movement of the spine to adjust the corrective force by changing the tension in the elastic band [12]. However, the main questions are how effective a soft brace is for preventing the progression of scoliosis and whether there are differences in the effectiveness of soft braces compared with rigid braces [13]. Whether soft brace treatment achieves a balance between effectiveness and reduction in brace side effects compared with rigid brace treatment, there is still a lack of systematic reviews that provide evidence for the clinical application of soft bracing. Therefore, the present meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the success rate, daily wear time and comfort levels of soft braces in the treatment of AIS, analyse potential influencing factors and provide references for clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study design and registry

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis. This study was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines and was registered at PROSPERO with the ID CRD42023404462.

Data sources and search strategy

The search strategy included the combinations of subject titles such as “adolescent idiopathic scoliosis”, “idiopathic scoliosis”, “soft brace”, “dynamic orthotic devices”, “effect”, “effectiveness”, “daily wear time”, “comfort”, and “success rate”. The PubMed, Embase, Web Of Science and Cochrane databases were searched for studies reporting the effect of bracing in the treatment of AIS. Publications from the establishment of the literature database to April 2023 were considered.

For example, the PubMed search strategy was as follows: (((((((scoliosis[MeSH Terms]) OR (scolioses[Title/Abstract])) OR (idiopathic scoliosis[Title/Abstract])) OR (adolescent idiopathic scoliosis[Title/Abstract])) OR (spinal deformities[Title/Abstract])) OR (spinal curvature[Title/Abstract])) AND((((((((((((dynamic orthotic devices [MeSH Terms]) OR (soft brace [MeSH Terms])) OR (SpineCor [Title/Abstract])) OR (dynamic brace [Title/Abstract])) OR (soft bracing [Title/Abstract])) OR (flexible devices, orthotic [Title/Abstract])) OR (dynamic orthotic device [Title/Abstract])) OR (flexible orthosis [Title/Abstract])) OR (dynamic orthosis [Title/Abstract])) OR (flexible orthoses [Title/Abstract])) OR (flexible orthose [Title/Abstract])) OR (TriaC brace [Title/Abstract]))) AND ((((((effectiveness[MeSH Terms]) OR (success rate[Title/Abstract])) OR (compliance[Title/Abstract])) OR (efficacy[Title/Abstract])) OR (daily wear time[Title/Abstract])) OR (comfort[Title/Abstract])).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this systematic review were as follows: (1) Population: AIS (curve magnitude < 40 degrees; age < 18 years; Risser ≤ 4). (2) Intervention: Soft bracing. (3) Outcome: Effectiveness of the soft bracing was defined by the success rate, daily wear time and comfort level. The correction success rate was defined as ≤ 5° curve progression at maturity and terminal follow-up < 45°. Terminal follow-up was defined as the time of last follow-up of patients (at least 1 year after discontinuation of brace treatment). Daily wear time could be self-reported or measured objectively, e.g. temperature sensor. (4) Study design: Single-arm studies, including nonrandomized controlled trials (NRCTs); double-arm studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohort studies (PCSs) and retrospective cohort studies (RCSs); At least 1 year of follow-up after the discontinuation of brace treatment.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Duplicate or similar reports. (2) Studies with missing data or unclear outcome indicators. (3) Reviews, conference abstracts, letters, cross-sectional studies. (4) Studies of congenital, neuromuscular and syndromic scoliosis. (5) For literature with the same first author, the same studies were excluded by reading. Title/abstract and full-text screening was done by two independent investigators. When the literature was included in the Meta-analysis, the full text of the relevant article was retrieved to assess its eligibility. References to relevant reports were also reviewed manually to identify other studies.

Quality assessment

Cochrane bias risk assessment tools were used for 2 RCTs. Validated the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies Tool (MINORS) [14] was used for 5 NRCTs, 3 PCS and 2 RCS. MINORS table included 12 items, items 1–8 are applicable to comparative and non-comparative studies and items 9–12 are applicable only to comparative studies. The three authors independently assessed the quality and risk of bias of each included study. For any disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion.

Data synthesis and analysis

For single arm studies, value of p and standard error (SE(P)) were calculated with the following formula: p = ln(odds) = ln[X/(n-X)], SE(p) = SE[ln(odds)] = 1/X + 1/(n - X), where n is the total number of included patients, X is the number of success events, p is the success incidence, and SE(p) is the standard error. OR and 95% CI were the effect measures. Then the actual the converted success rate of soft bracing (p)f and its 95%CI were obtained by the conversion of effect measures, pf = OR/(1 + OR), the lower limit of 95%CI (LL)=LLOR/(1 + LLOR), and the upper limit of 95%CI (UL)=ULOR /(1 + ULOR) were obtained according to the conversion formula. For double arm studies, RR and 95% CI were the effect measures. The odds ratio (OR) was defined as the ratio between the success rate of the soft brace treatment group and the implied baseline success rate = 1, where ‘pf = OR/(1 + OR)’ indicates ‘the converted success rate of soft bracing’. Relative risk (RR) was defined as the ratio of the success rate between soft bracing and observation to that between soft bracing and rigid bracing. If the RR value was > 1, the success rate of the soft brace was higher for AIS patients; otherwise, it was lower [15].

RevMan 5.4 was used for Meta-analysis and publication bias test. Heterogeneity analysis was performed after data processing. If the heterogeneity among the results was low (P ≥ 0.10 and I ² ≤50%), using fixed-effect model. If the heterogeneity among the findings was high (P < 0.10 and I ² >50%), using random effects model. The main sources of heterogeneity were analyzed by subgroup analysis. Stata 15.0 software was conducted using for sensitivity analysis. IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software was used to analyze whether there was a difference in the average daily wear time between soft and rigid bracing .

Results

A total of 540 records were retrieved from the comprehensive search. After full-text screening, a total of 12 studies (including 5 single-arm studies and 7 double-arm studies) were included in this study. Statistical analysis

Single-arm meta-analysis

There was statistical heterogeneity among the 12 studies (P = 0.0002, I² = 69%), and a random effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The results were statistically significant for the success rate of soft bracing for AIS patients [OR = 2.29, 95% CI (1.57, 3.32), P < 0.0001], and the conversion success rate of soft bracing was 70% [OR = 0.70, 95% CI (0.61, 0.77), P < 0.0001] (Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Meta-analysis results of soft bracing success rate. IV: inverse variance; SE: standard error; CI: confidence interval

Meta-analysis results of soft bracing success rate. IV: inverse variance; SE: standard error; CI: confidence interval

Double-arm meta-analysis

Soft bracing and rigid bracing

Among the double-arm studies, 4 (soft bracing group: 171 patients; rigid bracing group: 220 patients) reported a comparison of the success rates of soft bracing and rigid bracing in AIS treatment. There was no significant heterogeneity among the 4 studies (I²= 11%, P = 0.34), so a random effects model was adopted. The combined RR was 0.66 (95% CI (0.53, 0.81), P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4), indicating that the success rate of soft brace group was lower than that in the rigid brace group.

Fig. 4Statistical difference between soft bracing and rigid bracing. M-H: Mantel‒Haenszel, a statistical method commonly used in the fixed-effects model of categorical variables

Statistical difference between soft bracing and rigid bracing. M-H: Mantel‒Haenszel, a statistical method commonly used in the fixed-effects model of categorical variables

Soft bracing and observation

Among the single-arm studies, 3 (soft bracing group: 135 patients; observation group: 184 patients) reported a comparison of the success rates of soft bracing and observation in AIS treatment. There was highly significant heterogeneity among the 3 studies (I²= 61%, P = 0.07), so a random effects model was adopted. The combined RR was 2.02 (95% CI (1.41, 2.89), P = 0.0001) (Fig. 5), indicating that the success rate in the soft brace group was higher than that in the observation group.

Subgroup analysis

Initial Cobb angle

Taking the initial Cobb angle as the grouping factor, two subgroups were obtained: those with a Cobb angle < 30° and those with a Cobb angle > 30°, with I² values of 0% and 79%, respectively. There was no significant heterogeneity within the Cobb angle < 30° group, whereas there was significant heterogeneity within the Cobb angle > 30° group. After the two subgroups were merged, there was medium heterogeneity, suggesting that the initial Cobb angle was a possible source of heterogeneity (Fig. 6). The conversion success rates for Cobb angle < 30° and Cobb angle > 30° were 71% [OR = 0.71, 95% CI (0.63, 0.78), P < 0.00001] and 59% [OR = 0.59, 95% CI (0.39, 0.75), P = 0.39], respectively.

Fig. 6Results of a subgroup analysis of the initial Cobb angle size

Results of a subgroup analysis of the initial Cobb angle size

Study types

Taking study type as the grouping factor, two subgroups were obtained: prospective studies (PS) and retrospective studies (RS), whose I² values were 0% and 0%, respectively. There was no significant heterogeneity within the two groups. After the two subgroups were merged, there was significant heterogeneity, indicating that the study type was the source of heterogeneity (Fig. 7). The conversion success rates of prospective studies and retrospective studies were 75% [OR = 0.75, 95% CI (0.70, 0.79), P < 0.00001] and 46% [OR = 0.46, 95% CI (0.37, 0.56), P = 0.46], respectively.

The success rate of soft bracing in the treatment of AIS was used as an indicator for sensitivity analysis. After the studies were excluded one by one, the results obtained were not significantly affected, indicating that the results of this study was robust (Fig. 8).

Discussion

The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to summarize and analyse the present evidence for the short-term effectiveness of soft bracing in AIS patients. Compared with the observation group, soft bracing was more effective for AIS patients; compared with rigid bracing, soft bracing had higher daily wear times and comfort levels but a lower success rate.

The effectiveness of rigid bracing in the treatment of AIS patients has been documented, but rigid bracing can decrease muscle endurance and pulmonary function, limit physical activity and lead to skin breakdown [4–7], so soft bracing has been explored in an attempt to overcome its disadvantages. Active soft bracing exerts corrective forces in the form of elastic resistance while allowing for spinal motion, so flexible corrective forces are the greatest advantage of soft bracing [43]. Firstly, the elastic band can improve adjustable tension, correct the abnormal and asymmetric muscle tone caused by scoliosis and increase proprioception input. Low-intensity and symmetrical rehabilitation exercises, such as aerobic exercise, breathing exercises, posture and core control training, can be performed while the soft brace is worn by the patient. It can improve postural and neuromuscular control and stabilize spinal deformities [44]. Secondly, soft bracing allows partial movement of the spine, improving respiratory and functional mobility. It can also be accompanied by training therapy [45]. Thirdly, the musculoskeletal balance theory proposes that soft bracing can protect the spine from excessive compression, strengthen the core musculature, and reduce disuse amyotrophy [46, 47]. However, the effectiveness of soft bracing remains controversial.

Therefore, this study conducted a meta-analysis of the effectiveness of soft bracing. According to the evaluation criteria for the effectiveness of bracing from the SOSORT, the success of brace treatment was defined as a Cobb angle progression of less than or equal to 5°. In contrast, if the angle of curve progression was > 5°, the Cobb angle was > 45° after skeletal maturity, and surgery was required, bracing was considered unsuccessful [8, 48]. Although soft bracing has a lower corrective success rate than rigid bracing does, mild AIS patients do not need to be completely restricted in trunk movement and strongly compressed to achieve the desired spinal shape, such as rigid bracing. According to existing studies, soft bracing is recommended for treating mild to moderate AIS patients with a Cobb angle < 30° [8, 23, 24]. However, high quality evidence based medical evidence for the Cobb angle reference range of soft braces in the treatment of AIS is still lacking [49].

The progression and treatment of scoliosis is a long-term process [12], and compliance with bracing is an important factor in realizing its therapeutic potential [35, 50]. The brace wearing time, wearing tightness, patient acceptance of the appearance and comfort of the brace, psychological factors, etc., may affect compliance with bracing [51–53]. Research regarding these factors influencing brace compliance is conflicting. For example, some studies reported that bracing may have a negative effect on psychosocial parameters, such as decreased function/activity, self-image and quality of life, in AIS patients [4, 54, 55]. However, Khoshhal et al. and Schwieger et al. reported that psychosocial parameters were not significantly affected by brace use. Patients can develop self-protective coping skills and adjust to braces over time. The effectiveness of braces reduces the degree of psychological pressure on AIS patients [56, 57]. Current studies have shown that bracing has some effect on patient psychology, but this effect is adjustable and acceptable. Every adolescent experiences similar but unique conditions during treatment, and the associated psychological factors differ across individuals. However, the restrictions of rigid braces on physical function/activity exist objectively, and rigid shells are not comfortable.

This study recorded the daily wear time and comfort levels of the brace for AIS patients in the form of parental reports, which reported that patients can wear the brace full-time (20–23 h) and do not experience any discomfort. However, the full-time rigid brace had a rigid shell, causing skin breakdown. It is difficult for patients to wear rigid braces all the time. The daily wear time of the brace indirectly reflected the comfort superiority of soft bracing over rigid bracing (t = 10.39, p < 0.001).

Some studies have shown that the subjective perception of patients wearing the brace, the daily wear time of braces or the percentage of actual wearing time in the prescription can indirectly reflect the wearing condition of the brace. This research revealed that the daily wear time of the soft brace was 20.83 ± 2.08 h, 92% of the soft bracing studies were defined as full-time, and patients did not report any discomfort [9–11, 17–25]. The daily wear time of rigid bracing was 12.85 ± 0.69 h [26–33], the actual wearing of rigid braces accounted for 18.2–82% of the prescription time [39–42, 58], and the subjective perception of rigid bracing included a negative appearance, psychological problems, skin discomfort, poor breathing, pain, decreased quality of life, and disuse atrophy [31, 35–38, 44]. These influencing factors may decrease the daily wear time. Hasler et al. [59] performed a more complete study assessing compliance with braces in the treatment of AIS with a temperature recorder. However, the study sample size was too small and lacked parallel controls. In addition, we did not assess why the patient did not wear the brace as needed, which may be related to psychological factors, the comfort of the brace or other factors. Wong et al. evaluated the acceptance of soft braces through questionnaires. They showed that the compliance of soft bracing is as limited as that of rigid bracing [22]. The correction force of the soft brace depends on the elastic bands on the device. With increasing wear time of the brace, the tightness and position of the strap may change. Therefore, the tightness of the soft brace may have contributed to the different findings.

The daily wear time and comfort levels of soft braces are valuable research areas, as they can strive to achieve the prescribed wearing time and reduce brace side effects. The soft brace promotes specific corrective movement of the spine through elastic resistance and then strengthens the weak muscle groups on both sides of the spine. Over time, these corrective movements can become habitual or dominant movements, which can help reduce scoliosis. Moreover, long-term sustainable effects can be achieved through exercise of the spinal muscles [58, 60]. However, the current daily wear time and comfort level advantages of soft braces are not yet sufficient to achieve balance with respect to the correction success rate. Bracing treatment focuses more on correcting deformities, which can lead to adverse events associated with rigid braces to some extent. In the future, further exploration of these issues, such as novel brace materials to improve daily wear time and comfort levels while ensuring corrective force and the effectiveness of brace combined exercises for AIS patients, is important. More standardized studies are needed on the objective assessment of the daily wearing time and comfort of soft braces for AIS patients to obtain more reliable daily wear time and comfort level conclusions.

There are several limitations in this study. The evidence level was lower than that of RCTs because on the basis of ethics and patient willingness, relatively few controlled trials exist. The number of studies was relatively small because the longer the follow-up period was, the more difficult the data collection was. On the basis of the SRS criteria, at least 2 years of follow-up from the discontinuation of brace treatment should be considered to assess the effectiveness of bracing. The mean follow-up period of our included studies was approximately 1 year. Therefore, in this study, the short-term effectiveness of soft bracing in the treatment of AIS was investigated. The meta-analysis results included single-arm studies, so the significance of the sensitivity analysis and publication bias analysis was limited.

Conclusions

Soft bracing has short-term effectiveness for mild to moderate AIS patients compared with observation groups, and it has a lower success rate and higher daily wear time and comfort levels than rigid bracing. The subgroup of patients with a Cobb angle < 30° had a higher success rate and less heterogeneity in terms of soft bracing.
