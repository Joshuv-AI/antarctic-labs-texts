Bilateral pulvinar responsive neurostimulation for bilateral multifocal posteriorly dominant drug resistant epilepsy
Authors: Richard Wang, Ariel Sacknovitz, Sima Vazquez, Jose Dominguez, Patty McGoldrick, Steven Wolf, Vishad Sukul, Carrie Muh, Sanjay E. Patra, David E. Burdette
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe four cases of Responsive Neurostimulation (RNS) in the bilateral pulvinar nuclei (PUL) in individuals with drug resistant epilepsy (DRE). This will show that due to widespread PUL connectivity, bilateral PUL RNS may be an option for some individuals with bilateral multifocal epilepsy.MethodsThis study comprises two centers' experience with bilateral PUL RNS for DRE. Patients treated with bilateral PUL RNS at Westchester Medical Center (Valhalla, NY) and Corewell Health (Grand Rapids, MI) between the years 2019 and 2022 were analyzed and described. Presented here are methods for target selection, device programming, and clinical outcomes.ResultsTwo patients with Lennox–Gastaut phenotype (aged 13 and 21 years) with posteriorly dominant discharges were implanted with bilateral PUL electrodes. Additionally, two patients (aged 20 and 31 years) with independent left and right occipital bilateral multifocal seizure onsets were implanted with bilateral RNS devices targeting the ipsilateral PUL and ipsilateral occipital cortex. Subclinical and clinical seizures were captured by RNS electrocorticography (ECoG) in all patients. RNS implantation and treatment was well‐tolerated without adverse effects in all patients. Relative to baseline, two patients had 25% and 50% reduction in disabling seizures, and two patients had 71% and 100% reduction in disabling seizures. Stimulation paradigms utilized high frequency stimulation in both Lennox–Gastaut phenotype patients. Low frequency (individualized to the terminal ictal frequencies) stimulation was effective in the two bioccipital patients.SignificanceRNS with electrode placement targeting bilateral PUL is safe, and no adverse effects have been attributable to the pulvinar electrode placement. PUL responsive neurostimulation is potentially effective for patients with bilateral multifocal, posteriorly dominant DRE. Both high and low frequency responsive stimulation are treatment options. Longer follow‐up will shed light on the ultimate reduction of seizure burden.Plain Language SummaryWe describe four cases where stimulation devices were placed in the Pulvinar area of the thalamus (central sensory area in the brain). This is very unique and different location than where these devices are typically placed. These patients all had great outcomes with marked seizure reduction, demonstrating that this placement is both safe and effective.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy affects nearly 1% of the worldwide population 1 and drug resistant epilepsy (DRE), defined as failure of two appropriate anti‐seizure medication (ASM) schedule trials, affects over 30% of patients with epilepsy 2 with resulting increased morbidity and mortality. Although resective surgical intervention offers a chance at a cure for DRE, half of DRE patients are not candidates for resective therapy. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) and responsive neurostimulation (RNS) are two options for therapy for such patients. DBS therapy is US‐FDA approved for stimulation of the anterior thalamic (ANT) nuclei although targeting of centromedian (CM) thalamic nuclei and the hippocampus have been reported. 1 US‐FDA labeling does not specify targets for leads for the RNS device, manufactured by NeuroPace. RNS targeting is designed to affect the earliest detection of seizures; therefore, thalamic RNS lead placement is within labeling despite absence of randomized controlled data for thalamic RNS.

Bilateral multifocal epilepsy presents a particular challenge for cortical neurostimulation, so thalamic targeting is often considered. Responses of bilateral multifocal epilepsies to ANT DBS is less robust than responses in individuals with temporal lobe epilepsy. 3 , 4 Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) is a bi‐hemispheric multifocal DRE that has been treated by DBS targeting CM with mixed results. 5 In particular, stimulation of the CM can be limited by stimulation‐associated paresthesias.

Published evidence for the feasibility and efficacy of RNS of the pulvinar (PUL) thalamic nuclei is scarce. A case series which showed benefit in seizure reduction for three patients with epilepsy of posterior quadrant onset 6 , 7 built off existing literature showing the possibility of seizure termination by stimulation of the PUL 8 and led to an ongoing clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04692701) to study the effects of stimulation of the median PUL. Current use of RNS in the PUL thalamic nuclei is targeted to patients with failure or non‐applicability of more established epilepsy treatment options, including vagus nerve stimulation and DBS and RNS of seizure foci and ANT nuclei.

Theoretical advantages of RNS of the PUL for selected patients provide impetus for research into establishing its role in refractory epilepsy treatment Targeting of the PUL is believed to be beneficial in these patients due to its extensive connections to the occipital and parieto‐temporal lobes, observed as electrical engagement during seizure propagation. 7 , 9 In addition, as the largest of the thalamic nuclei, the PUL offers an alternative target when neurosurgical access in other deep brain structures is impractical.

In this case series, we describe four patients with bilateral multifocal, posteriorly dominant DRE treated with bilateral pulvinar RNS.

CASE PRESENTATIONS

Case one

History

The patient is a 13‐year‐old boy with past early infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (EIDEE) that progressed to Lennox–Gastaut phenotype, cerebral palsy, respiratory failure requiring tracheostomy, corpus callosotomy, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) device implantation, and ongoing multiple daily seizures that were refractory to ASMs. Since infancy, his seizure types have included generalized tonic–clonic seizures, myoclonic seizures, focal impaired awareness seizures (FIAS), and epileptic spasms. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings include periventricular periatrial white matter fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities, supratentorial and infratentorial volume loss, and callosal volume loss. Electroencephalogram (EEG) findings feature a burst suppression pattern as well as multifocal spike and wave discharges.

Surgical plan and preoperative considerations

Given the patient's multifocal seizure onset and diffuse atrophy on MRI, it was determined that intracranial EEG (icEEG) would not provide significant information to support resective surgery. Neuromodulation with RNS with bilateral thalamic electrodes, either in the CM or PUL nuclei, was considered. Presurgical planning with the ROSA ONE Brain (Zimmer Biomet) neuro‐navigation robotic system indicated that the PUL was the most surgically accessible nucleus in this patient since no clear CM nuclei could be located on magnetization prepared 2 rapid acquisition gradient echoes (MP2RAGE) 10 imaging in this patient.

Operation

Using ROSA stereotactic guidance, depth electrodes were placed in bilateral PUL nuclei. A superior neurosurgical approach was utilized, with access via an entry at the superior parietal lobules and a trajectory lateral to the ventricles and not trans‐ventricular.

Postoperative course

There were no postoperative complications. Stimulation was initiated at 125 Hz. At 3‐month follow‐up, the RNS system captured frequent subclinical seizures throughout the day; therefore, the patient's stimulation parameters were adjusted. At his 5‐month visit, his electrode stimulation patterns were changed from a bipolar stimulation across four contacts to bipolar stimulation across the two deepest contacts on each thalamic electrode, to create new focused stimulation in a limited region of the pulvinar nucleus (Figure 1).

(Top) Patient 1 postoperative electroencephalogram detection of seizure onset from the right pulvinar leads spreading to the left pulvinar. (Bottom) Spectrograph showing bursts at 25 Hz in the bilateral pulvinar leads with the right spreading to the left pulvinar.

Overall, in 11 months of stimulation, he had a decreased total clinical seizure activity from a baseline of 12–25 to 5–12 seizures/day per family report. At 16‐month follow‐up, overall seizure activity had decreased by 25% (Tables 1 and 2).

Case two

History

This patient is a 21‐year‐old male with LGS, cerebral palsy, shunted hydrocephalus, and developmental delay with daily seizures refractory to multiple ASMs. He had multiple resective epilepsy surgeries as well as corpus callosotomy in the past. Genetic analysis revealed the patient to be a PEX12 carrier for autosomal recessive Zellweger disorder, which is not considered to be pathogenic. EEG findings feature multifocal and generalized spike and slow wave discharges with occipital predominance and bursts of generalized paroxysmal fast activity (GPFA), diagnostic of LGS.

Surgical history

In 2015, the patient had an RNS placed with two cortical strips at the right sub‐temporal and left frontal regions as well as bilateral depth electrodes that were intended to be in his ANT. Initially, cortical strips were connected to the stimulator without benefit. Therefore, 2 years later, the left frontal strip was disconnected and the left ANT electrode was connected in its place along with the right temporal strip. This still did not provide sufficient benefit, so 2 years later, the right temporal strip was also disconnected, and stimulation targeted bilateral ANT electrodes. Seizures were detected from the right thalamic lead, but the lead did not appear to be capturing the seizure onsets, and multiple stimulation paradigms failed to provide significant benefit. Imaging demonstrated that the electrodes may not have been optimally placed in the ANT nuclei. Therefore, in 2022, the thalamic depth electrodes were removed and replaced with electrodes at the bilateral PUL, using ROSA stereotactic guidance. The PUL was chosen due to a lack of good localization of his CM or ANT nucleus on neuroimaging despite using an MP2RAGE (Figure 2). 10 Similar to patient one, a superior surgical approach was utilized via the frontal lobes, with a trajectory lateral to the ventricles and not trans‐ventricular.

(Left) Patient 2 anterior–posterior radiograph showing depth electrode placement in bilateral pulvinar thalamic nuclei in the red circles. (Right) Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging with leads at overlaid targets showing placement in bilateral pulvinar thalamic nuclei.

Postoperative course

There were no postoperative complications. Stimulation was initiated at 125 Hz. RNS captured the seizure onset at the bilateral PUL. There was clear electrographic evidence of seizure onset in his PUL electrodes which was not apparent on his prior ANT electrodes, which were placed at another institution prior to the availability of the ROSA stereotactic system (Figure 3).

At 10‐month follow‐up, patient has been noted to be much more alert, with an overall 50% reduction in total clinical seizure frequency from a baseline of 8–10 seizures/day (Tables 1 and 2).

Case three

History

This patient is a 24‐year‐old, right‐handed woman who developed seizures at the age of 7 years. Her epilepsy manifests as focal impaired awareness and rare focal to bilateral tonic–clonic (in epilepsy monitoring unit [EMU]) seizures. Seizures are quantified in Table 1. Multiple trials of ASMs proved ineffective. A presurgical evaluation in 2014, at the age of 15 years, included unremarkable brain MRI and a scalp EEG showing bioccipital seizure onsets with a right emphasis. IcEEG monitoring was performed in early 2016 with electrodes targeting bilateral temporo‐occipital, parietal, and occipital structures. Over 50 FIAS were recorded with regional onsets simultaneously involving multiple electrodes in left greater than right occipital regions.

Surgical history

In March 2016 the patient underwent implantation of an RNS device and leads overlying the left occipital convexity and left basal occipital regions. FIAS frequency decreased but more FIAS‐associated falls occurred, so at the patient's request, the RNS and leads were explanted in July 2018. After FIAS gradually returned to their pre‐RNS frequency and falls persisted, bilateral RNS was considered. In May 2021, using robotic stereotactic navigation for placement of all depth electrodes with bone fiducial head computed tomography (CT) and MRI Stealth protocol with gadolinium for registration, the patient underwent implantation of right and left RNS device with active depth leads targeting ipsilateral PUL (lateral to medial approach) and occipital lobes (Figure 4A,B).

Postoperative course

Stimulation was initiated with an initial burst of 100 ms, 200 Hz occipital stimulation followed by a second burst of 5 s pulvinar stimulation at 125 Hz. Mild improvement was evident by 3 months, at which time lead‐to‐lead low frequency 5 Hz (approximate terminal frequency of her icEEG‐recorded FIASs) stimulation was initiated. She experienced an approximately 75% reduction in all disabling seizures (characterized as FIAS and Focal to Bilateral Tonic–Clonic Seizures [FBTC]) for 11 months until she had a fall, fracturing the right RNS lead with a subsequent increase of seizures. This necessitated replacement in December 2022 with a return of 75% seizure reduction postoperatively.

Case four

History

This patient is a 35‐year‐old, right‐handed man who developed seizures at the age of 11 years. His epilepsy manifests as focal aware, FIAS, and FTBC seizures. Multiple trials of ASMs proved ineffective. The patient had a VNS implanted which did not reduce his seizures. A presurgical evaluation in 2004, at the age of 16 years, included an MRI showing heterotopic periventricular (PVNH) gray matter bilaterally, and scalp EEG showing focal ictal and interictal activity arising from right, left, and bilateral parieto‐occipital foci. Repeat presurgical evaluation in 2015 confirmed the previous findings, and icEEG monitoring was performed in early 2016 with electrodes targeting bilateral temporo‐occipital, parietal, and occipital structures as well as the posterior heterotopias. Over 70 focal aware and focal impaired awareness seizures were recorded with regional onsets independently involving right and left lateral and medial parieto‐occipital regions – greater than 75% arising from the right with rapid propagation to the left (Figure 4A).

Surgical history

In April, 2016 the patient underwent laser ablation of his right‐sided PVNH with Visualase laser ablation system (Medtronic, Inc) with two different trajectories. Fiberoptic ablation probes were placed with robotic guidance (ROSA, Medtech). Left‐sided PVNH were ablated utilizing a similar methodology in June 2016. The RNS device and leads were placed in September 2016. Active leads consisted of a right basal occipital and right lateral occipital cortical strip leads. An inactive cortical strip was placed in the right occipital inferolateral region.

Although the patient improved, he continued to have disabling seizures. It was decided to proceed with a second RNS System placement. The existing RNS System did not allow for our typical surgical planning MRI, so a CT angiogram was fused with his MRI with gadolinium done in 2016 to ensure accurate fusion and subsequent registration. Additional leads were placed, which included bilateral pulvinar depth leads targeting the mid‐inferior pulvinar bilaterally and a left occipital depth lead. The depth electrodes were placed with robotic (ROSA) guidance after bone fiducial registration. The left neurostimulator was connected to the left pulvinar depth lead and the left mesial occipital strip lead. The right neurostimulator was connected to the right pulvinar depth lead and right basal occipital strip lead.

Postoperative course

Stimulation was initiated with an initial burst of 100 ms, 200 Hz occipital stimulation followed by a second burst of 2 s pulvinar stimulation at 125 Hz. As charge density was increased, disabling seizures (FIAS and FBTC) decreased in frequency although occasional disabling seizures recurred with a curious phenomenon that FIAS would be acutely exacerbated for 24 h following each increased in charge density. At his 14‐month post bilateral RNS visit, a prolonged right occipital FIAS occurred immediately following another increase in charge density. This prolonged FIAS resolved within seconds of reprogramming to lead‐to‐lead low frequency 5 Hz (approximate terminal frequency of his icEEG‐recorded FIASs) stimulation. He has been free of disabling seizures since that time.

DISCUSSION

This case series provides evidence that the pulvinar thalamic nucleus is a safe, effective, and viable target for RNS lead placement in Lennox–Gastaut phenotype and bilateral posterior quadrant focal epilepsy, likely via the pulvinar's broad cortical interconnectivity. Drug resistant epilepsy continues to challenge treating clinicians in cases of multifocal or generalized onset seizures, in cases with distorted brain architecture, and in pediatric cases where evidence for RNS is scarce. Establishing the viability of the PUL nuclei as sites for RNS lead implantation will provide another treatment option for properly selected patients, as well as patients with few other remaining options for therapy.

Anatomical reasoning

The pulvinar thalamic nuclei have established anatomical advantages and theoretical connective advantages for use as a target for RNS. The pulvinar is the largest of the primate thalamic nuclei, and it has reciprocal connectivity with broad cortical regions. 11 The medial PUL has broad reciprocal connections (amygdala as well as frontal, parietal, temporal, insular, and cingulate cortices), and the inferior PUL has reciprocal connections with the ipsilateral middle temporal lobe, medial superior temporal lobe, and fundus of the superior temporal lobe. 12 These extensive reciprocal connections between the pulvinar and ipsilateral cortical regions provide neuroanatomical support for it to be a site of effective neuromodulation. 13 Furthermore, the emergence of stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) has facilitated simultaneous neocortical and thalamic recording, and sEEG approaches have demonstrated medial PUL reflection of temporal lobe seizures, 14 , 15 , 16 providing further evidence for the thalamus as a central hub for the propagation of cortical seizures and a site for preemptive termination with neurostimulation.

Seizure detection and electrode implantation

Clinical evidence for the pulvinar thalamic nuclei as a site of RNS has evolved from studies of open‐loop deep brain stimulation in epilepsy. The landmark Stimulation of the Anterior Nucleus of the Thalamus for Epilepsy (SANTE) study demonstrated benefits of open‐loop deep brain stimulation on the anterior thalamic nuclei in follow‐ups at 2 years, 3 5 years, 17 and 10 years. 18 Subsequent studies utilizing closed‐loop responsive neurostimulation in deep brain targets have shown the benefit of the detection and early disruption of seizure in pediatric and adult patients via RNS with electrode implantation in the CM or ANT nuclei, 19 , 20 with more limited evidence in the pulvinar nuclei. 6 The possible usage of the pulvinar thalamic nuclei as a stimulation and detection center is attractive due to its large size 6 and thus surgical accessibility, and closed‐loop responsive neurostimulation has shown certain quality‐of‐life benefits over open‐loop neurostimulation due to its ability to tune electrical pulses to early patterns of seizure onset. 6 , 7

This case series also provides new clinical evidence that detection of seizure onset with responsive neurostimulation is possible via electrode implantation in the pulvinar thalamic nuclei, which is vital for triggering the RNS system to send signals to terminate seizures, as well as to provide clinicians with data for fine‐tuning of the system to individual patients. The two patients with Lennox–Gastaut phenotype and LGS did not have cortical leads placed, but seizures were identified by the pulvinar leads. In contrast, the two patients with bioccipital, multifocal onsets exhibited near‐simultaneous onsets in cortical and pulvinar leads. In dysmorphic/distorted brain anatomy, thalamic targeting is difficult, rendering direct targeting of ANT and CM inexact. The second patient previously had electrodes which had targeted the ANT, but the contacts did not appear to have been optimally placed within the epileptogenic network due to lack of consistent ictal accompaniment. The relative size and location of the PUL makes it a more accurate target for electrode placement, seizure detection, and seizure termination.

Stimulation settings

With the relative novelty of responsive neurostimulation in DRE treatment compared to other treatment options for epilepsy, optimal stimulation paradigms have not yet been defined. High frequency stimulation (HFS) is the more widely used initial stimulation protocol for invasive techniques, 21 stemming from the initial programming protocols in the SANTE trial (145 Hz), 3 and worries that low frequency stimulation (LFS) may worsen seizures. 22 Recent research has shown that HFS produces more activation in certain networks than LFS, and that both HFS and LFS differ in patterns of deactivation and clustering. 21 , 23 In our case series, all four patients were started on initial HFS, before two patients were adjusted to an LFS protocol, based on literature showing benefit through LFS for patients that do not improve on HFS. 22 The two patients adjusted to an LFS protocol experienced a further reduction in seizure activity, with one patient seizure‐free at last follow‐up (48 months post‐op). Further research is necessary to demonstrate the benefits and indications of different RNS stimulation frequencies for DRE patients.

Complications and adverse effects

These four patients from two separate centers had no adverse perioperative complications, thereby supporting the ability to safely target the PUL with neuronavigational systems. In addition, there are no demonstrable stimulation side effects of stimulation of PUL as seen in ANT and CM stimulation, in line with previous reports demonstrating the safety of targeting the pulvinar. 16 RNS has historically had low rates of perioperative complications in line with other properly implanted intracranial devices, 24 , 25 usually related to implant‐site infections.

Limitations

There are inherent limitations in this study. The seizure frequency and severity are both caregiver‐reported metrics in seizure diaries. The RNS data is then reviewed by the clinicians after clinic visits and compared to caregiver‐reported data. While it is the industry standard, seizure reports are dependent on caregiver consistency. In addition, this is a case series of four patients and as such our findings cannot currently be used to make recommendations on clinical care. Lastly, although each of the cases included follow‐up outside of the post‐implantation window, further follow‐up would further illustrate the effectiveness of our treatment paradigm, as DBS and RNS have been found to continually improve seizure reduction over time. 17

CONCLUSION

The pulvinar thalamic nucleus is a viable target for responsive neurostimulation lead placement in Lennox–Gastaut phenotype and bilateral posterior quadrant focal epilepsy. An advantage over other targets is its size, location, and connectivity, allowing for neurosurgical access in abnormal brain architecture and utilization of multifocal or generalized seizures. In our four patient case series, targeting the pulvinar thalamic nucleus proved effective in the reduction of seizures, safe in its lack of perioperative and long‐term complications, and tolerable in its lack of cognitive side effects.
