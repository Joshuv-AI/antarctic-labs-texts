Infective Endocarditis Mimicking Reactive Arthritis
Authors: Hudson Lin, Claudia See, Neal Shah, Soonmee Cha, Kirk Jones, Kreshnik Zejnullahu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfective endocarditis (IE) in young adults can present with nonspecific symptoms mimicking alternative diagnoses.Case SummaryA 29-year-old man presented to the emergency department with erythematous, pruritic papules on his shins and ankles with bilateral knee pain and swelling. After the initial work-up for septic or inflammatory arthritis, he was empirically treated for reactive arthritis with systemic steroids. A month later, he re-presented with headache, dark urine, and unintentional 40-lb weight loss. Imaging revealed multiple ischemic strokes in the bilateral cerebelli and right thalamus. He received a diagnosis of Streptococcus mitis endocarditis with severe aortic regurgitation and septic brain emboli, underwent mechanical aortic valve replacement, and was treated for 4 weeks with antibiotics.DiscussionNonspecific symptoms of IE can delay diagnosis and risk complications, including stroke and valvular destruction. Reactive arthritis should remain a diagnosis of exclusion.Take-Home MessageMaintain suspicion for IE in patients with unexplained systemic inflammation and arthralgias.

History of Presentation

A 29-year-old previously healthy man initially developed a pruritic, erythematous papular rash on his shins and ankles. Three days later, he experienced bilateral knee pain and swelling. Two months prior, he had a fever, nausea, vomiting, and nonbloody diarrhea that self-resolved after a trip to urban Mexico. He presented to a local emergency department (ED), where physical examination revealed bilateral knee swelling without warmth or erythema and a regular rate and rhythm with no murmurs. Laboratory work-up identified elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP]: 82 mg/L [reference: 0-9 mg/L], erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR]: 50 mm/h [reference: 0-10 mm/h]) and normal knee radiographs. Urinalysis demonstrated 2+ protein, 7 WBC/hpf, and 4 RBC/hpf, with moderate squamous epithelial cells and few bacteria. Autoimmune serologies including antinuclear antibody, rheumatoid factor, and cyclic citrullinated peptide were negative. A right knee joint aspiration attempt was unsuccessful due to a lack of significant effusion. His primary care provider obtained a negative infectious work-up for syphilis, HIV, gonorrhea, chlamydia, and Lyme disease and prescribed a short course of prednisone 40 mg daily for 5 days. One week later, the patient presented to urgent care with persistent knee pain, painful swelling of the left second toe, and distal paresthesias in his hands and feet. He was referred to rheumatology, which assessed that his symptoms of oligoarthritis and dactylitis, preceded by a febrile illness, were most consistent with reactive arthritis, and sulfasalazine and a prolonged steroid taper were initiated. No cardiac examination was documented at this visit.

Over the following weeks, the patient developed diffuse headaches, intermittent diarrhea, and lower extremity weakness requiring a cane for ambulation. In addition, he endorsed a 40-lb unintentional weight loss since he first developed symptoms. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed multiple subacute posterior circulation infarcts involving both cerebellar hemispheres and the right thalamus (Figure 1). Because of worsening symptoms and magnetic resonance imaging results, the patient re-presented to the emergency department. On examination, he was tachycardic (122 beats/min) with no documented murmurs or neurologic deficits. Laboratory evaluation showed elevated high-sensitivity troponin 787 ng/L (reference: 0-53 ng/L), ESR 72 mm/h, CRP 152 mg/L, aspartate aminotransferase 41 U/L (reference: 0-37 U/L), alanine transaminase 71 U/L (reference: 0-60 U/L), and alkaline phosphatase 186 U/L (reference: 26-137 U/L). Past Medical History

He had no chronic medical conditions. He reported occasional cocaine, psilocybin, and marijuana use but denied intravenous drug use. He consumed 4-7 alcoholic drinks per week and smoked less than 1 pack of cigarettes per day. Three months prior, he obtained a right calf tattoo. He worked as a tenant law paralegal, frequently visiting poorly maintained housing environments.

Investigations

On arrival, he was febrile (38.9 °C) and tachycardic (130 beats/min), with widened pulse pressure (127/46 mm Hg). Grade V/VI holosystolic and grade III/IV diastolic murmurs were noted at the right upper sternal border. On neurologic examination, the patient had word-finding difficulty and a slowed gait. On skin examination, a splinter hemorrhage was noted on his left index finger. Oral and maxillofacial surgery found no dental caries on physical examination, and the patient reported no recent dental symptoms or dental work.

Initial laboratory testing showed WBC count 10.9 U/L (reference: 3.40-10.0 U/L), CRP 142.7 mg/L, ESR 43 mm/h, lactate dehydrogenase 322 U/L (reference: 125-243 U/L), troponin I 1.27 μg/L (reference: 0-0.04 μg/L), international normalized ratio 1.3 (reference: 0.9-1.2), alkaline phosphatase 176 U/L (reference: 40-130 U/L), and procalcitonin 0.93 μg/L (reference: <0.26 μg/L). These findings, combined with fever and septic emboli, fulfilled modified Duke criteria for definite IE.1

Management

The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit and treated with intravenous vancomycin and ceftriaxone. Despite multiple ischemic strokes, neurology determined that there was no contraindication to early valve replacement due to the absence of mycotic aneurysms and low hemorrhagic conversion risk.

On hospital day 3, he underwent mechanical aortic valve replacement (25-mm Abbott St Jude valve). A large 3-cm vegetation was found on the noncoronary cusp. Excised valve pathology demonstrated destructive IE (Figure 3). He was anticoagulated initially with heparin and transitioned to lifelong warfarin. Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uncomplicated except for 1 episode of supraventricular tachycardia treated medically and migraines that resolved with rimegepant. At 4-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated full neurologic recovery, resolution of joint symptoms, and normal function of the mechanical aortic valve, with engagement in cardiac rehabilitation and psychotherapy. He returned to work without physical limitations.

Discussion

This case illustrates IE presenting with prominent musculoskeletal and neurologic manifestations in a young adult with no predisposing cardiac abnormalities or injection drug use. The diagnosis was initially obscured due to early musculoskeletal symptoms and the absence of documented murmurs or other classic endocarditis stigmata—Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, and Roth spots—which occur in fewer than 5% of cases.2

S mitis, a viridans group streptococcus, is part of normal oral flora and the most common streptococcal cause of IE.3 While the patient was found to have good dentition on physical examination and no history of dental procedures, whereas the bedside oral examination may not fully exclude occult dental pathology, especially in the absence of imaging. Thus, any subclinical disease or mucosal inflammation could have served as a pathway for bacteremia. Additionally, transient bacteremia from viridans group streptococci can commonly occur with routine daily activities, with the intensity proportional to the magnitude of tissue trauma, density of oral microflora, and degree of inflammation.4

Immunologic vascular phenomena, such as migratory oligoarthritis and dactylitis, are reported in up to one-third of IE cases and may closely mimic reactive arthritis.5 Leukocytoclastic vasculitis has also been described in association with endocarditis and may explain the erythematous papular rash early in the disease course.6 Other misdiagnoses are common in the literature, including cases attributed to COVID-19 infection7 or antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies–associated glomerulonephritis and vasculitis.8,9 In this case, corticosteroid exposure—before IE was recognized—may have facilitated progression of valvular destruction and septic embolization.

Reactive arthritis is a diagnosis of exclusion and typically presents 1-4 weeks after gastrointestinal or genitourinary infection.10 Although oligoarthritis of the knees and dactylitis raised suspicion for reactive arthritis, other classic findings such as enthesitis, conjunctivitis, and urethritis were absent. Additionally, the preceding gastrointestinal illness occurred more than 2 months before the onset of symptoms, exceeding the typical postinfectious window. The papular rash at the onset of symptoms would have also been atypical for reactive arthritis.

Ultimately, the patient's preceding infection was remote, systemic inflammatory burden was profound, and new cardiac and neurologic findings strongly refuted the initial diagnosis. The modified Duke criteria provide a structured approach to IE diagnosis by incorporating clinical, microbiologic, and echocardiographic evidence.1 Early blood culture collection and echocardiography are critical in patients presenting with systemic inflammation, new murmurs, weight loss, or embolic phenomena.

Conclusions

IE may masquerade as inflammatory or reactive arthritis, particularly in young adults without traditional risk factors. Early recognition requires clinical vigilance, prompt microbiologic evaluation, and echocardiography. Immunosuppressive therapy should be deferred until IE is excluded.
