35 | Antepartum Depressive Symptoms and Perceived Health Status Across the First Year Postpartum Among Low‐Income Individuals

Colleen Judge‐Golden1; Elizabeth L. Gray1; Brittney R. Williams2; Sydney L. Raucher2; Charlotte M. Niznik1; Joesph M. Feinglass2; William A. Grobman3; Lynn M. Yee2

1Northwestern University, Chicago, IL; 2Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, Chicago, IL; 3Brown University, Providence, RI

Objective: Perinatal depression is a major source of maternal‐child morbidity, with potential implications for engagement in health care. We evaluated associations of moderate to severe antepartum depressive symptoms with perceived health status during the antepartum and postpartum (PP) periods, assessed by patient‐reported outcome (PRO) measures, among low‐income individuals.

Study Design: Secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial of a PP patient navigation intervention among English‐ or Spanish‐speaking, low‐income birthing people enrolled at >30 weeks (w) gestation. The primary exposure was antepartum Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) score >9, indicating moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Outcomes were standardized PRO measures assessed at enrollment, 4–12 w PP, and 11–13 months PP. Bivariable analyses evaluated differences in PROs by PHQ‐9 score category at each timepoint. Multivariable logistic or linear regression models estimated the association of antepartum PHQ‐9 >9 with PROs, with a priori adjustment for potential confounding by age, parity, and intervention group assignment.

Results: Of 405 randomized individuals, 364 (90%) enrolled while pregnant, at a median gestational age of 35.3 w. Antepartum PHQ‐9 was > 9 for 47 participants (13%). Antepartum PHQ‐9 >9 was associated with several concurrent PROs, including lower perceived physical and mental health, decreased patient activation and self‐efficacy, and increased perceived stress (Table 1). These associations persisted across PROs at both PP timepoints and in multivariable analyses (Table 2). Antepartum PHQ‐9 >9 was also strongly associated with PHQ‐9 >9 at 4–12 w PP (aOR 5.5, 95% CI 2.8, 10.8) and 11–13 months PP (aOR 5.4, 95% CI 2.7, 10.7). There were no differences in prenatal or PP care satisfaction by PHQ‐9 score.

Conclusion: Antepartum PHQ‐9 score >9 was associated with persistently worse perceived health status and self‐efficacy during pregnancy and across the first year PP. Research is needed to identify interventions that can improve PROs among pregnant individuals with depression.

36 | The PrePOP Randomised Trial – Oral Multi‐Lactobacillus Probiotic Versus Placebo to Increase Vaginal Lactobacillus Crispatus

Gillian A. Corbett1; Aaron Walsh2; Conor Feehily3; Gonçalo Correia4; Benedetta Soldati5; Anthony Rafferty6; David MacIntyre4; Paul D. Cotter7; Siobhan Corcoran8; Fionnuala M. M. McAuliffe9

1UCD Perinatal Research Centre, UCD School of Medicine, University College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland; 2Teagasc Food Research Centre, Cork, Ireland; 3University of Glasgow, Glasgow, United Kingdom; 4Imperial College London, London, United Kingdom; 5National Maternity Hospital, National Maternity Hospital, Ireland; 6UCD Perinatal Research Centre, Dublin, Ireland; 7Teagasc Food Research Centre, Dublin, Ireland; 8National Maternity Hospital, Dublin, Ireland; 9University College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland

Objective: Vaginal dominance by Lactobacillus crispatus has been associated with reduced risk of spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB). Oral probiotic supplementation has been proposed as a strategy to modulate the vaginal microbiome, although evidence from adequately powered randomised trials is limited. We aimed to evaluate whether an oral multi‐strain lactobacilli probiotic increased vaginal detection of L. crispatus in pregnant women at high risk of sPTB.

Study Design: This double‐blind, placebo‐controlled randomised trial was conducted at the specialist Preterm Birth Service at the National Maternity Hospital, Dublin, Ireland. Pregnant women in early pregnancy were assigned (1:1) to receive an oral probiotic or matched placebo. The oral probiotic contained four Lactobacilli: L. crispatus, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Limosilactobacillus gasseri, and Limosilactobacillus mulieris. The primary outcome was vaginal detection of L. crispatus after at least 12 weeks of oral treatment. Analyses were conducted using generalised linear mixed models.

Results: Of 175 women randomised, 154 were evaluable for the primary outcome. In the intetntion to treat analysis, the model‐estimated probability of L. crispatus detection increased from 37% (95% CI 18–49%) at baseline to 49% (95% CI 37–56%) after 12 weeks of treatment in the probiotic group, whereas it decreased from 47% (95% CI 33–53%) to 40% (95% CI 20–50%) in the placebo group, with a significant time × treatment interaction (p = 0·018). The odds of detection Lactobacillus crispatus increased from baseline to 12 weeks of treatment in the probiotic group (OR = 2·99, 95% CI 1·10–8·14, p = 0·032), but not in the placebo group (OR = 0·52, 95% CI 0·19–1·40, p = 0·190). Strain‐level analysis indicated that increased detection was attributable to expansion of indigenous L. crispatus strains, with confirmed engraftment of the probiotic strain in the vagina of one participant.

Conclusion: In pregnant women at high risk of sPTB, oral multi‐strain lactobacilli supplementation was associated with a modest increase in vaginal detection of L. crispatus after 12 weeks.

37 | Disparities in Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine Meeting Abstract Acceptance by Author Home Country Income Status

Frank B. Williams1; Ukachi N. Emeruwa2; Sarah Boudova3; Sarah Araji4; Abdulfetah Abdulkadir Abdosh5; Anju Ranjit6; Felagot Taddese Taddese5; Lauren A. Plante7; Jessica S. Chung8; Martha A. Tesfalul9

1Ochsner Health, Ochsner Health, LA; 2University of California San Diego, San Diego, CA; 3University of Illinois Chicago, Chicago, IL; 4Baylor College of Medicine, Houston, TX; 5St. Paul's Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC), Addis Ababa, Ethiopia; 6University of California, San Francisco, San Francisco, CA; 7Penn State College of Medicine, Hershey, PA; 8Medical College of Wisconsin, Milwaukee, WI; 9UCSF, San Francisco, CA

Objective: Authors from low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMIC) may have limited access to training in research design & reporting. We hypothesize that LMIC based authors have lower acceptance rates to Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine (SMFM) Pregnancy Meetings.

Study Design: This was a retrospective cohort study of scientific abstracts submitted to SMFM meetings from 2020 through 2026. Abstract topic, late breaking status, acceptance & presentation type were extracted from the electronic abstract management system. LMIC status was determined if the submitting author affirmed “an author of this submission is a practicing obstetrician from a low/middle income country.” Exposure of interest was author home country income status. Primary outcome was abstract acceptance. Regression modeling controlling for conference year, late‐breaking status, and topic generated odds of acceptance by income status produced adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results: Over 7 conferences, 14,092 abstracts were submitted, including 1242 (8.2%) from LMIC authors. The proportion of LMIC author abstracts decreased over time from 12.0% in 2020–21 to 7.7% in 2025–26. Similar to non‐LMIC authors (N = 1743), Clinical Obstetrics was the most common LMIC‐author topic (N = 170). Compared to non‐LMIC authors, LMIC author abstracts accounted for a higher proportion of Doppler (15.4%) & Antepartum Fetal Assessment (11.5%), as well as Public/Global Health (14.6%), contributing fewer submissions in Health Policy (4.8%), Health Equity (5.0%) & Basic Science (6.2%). Overall abstract acceptance was 60.6%, with near‐constant number accepted each year despite submission fluctuations (Figure). LMIC‐authors had lower overall acceptances (55.0%) compared to non‐LMIC authors (61.1%, p < 0.01, Figure). Controlling for year, late‐breaking status & topic, LMIC authorship was associated with 26% lower acceptance (aOR 0.74, 95% CI 0.65, 0.83).

Conclusion: Fewer abstract acceptances from LMIC authors may represent an opportunity for cross‐country & cross‐institutional mentorship to enhance research quality & reporting.

38 | Association of Neonatal Intensive Care Unit Admission With Patient‐Reported Outcomes Among Low‐Income Birthing People

Sydney L. Raucher1; Jecca R. Steinberg2; Ying Cheung1; Brittney R. Williams1; Joesph M. Feinglass1; Laura Diaz1; Ka'Derricka Davis1; Michelle A. Kominiarek1; Willliam Grobman3; Lynn M. Yee1

1Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, Chicago, IL; 2UCSF School of Medicine, San Francisco, CA; 3The Warren Alpert Medical School of Brown University, Providence, RI

Objective: Although giving birth to a neonate with a complex medical course is associated with postpartum mood disorders, less is known about other aspects of postpartum well‐being. We aimed to evaluate the association of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission with patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) including perceived health and well‐being among low‐income birthing people in the first year postpartum.

Study Design: This is a secondary analysis of data from a randomized trial of a postpartum patient navigation program in which participants (≥16 years old, English‐ or Spanish‐speaking, publicly funded prenatal care) were randomized to usual care or lay navigator support for one year postpartum. PROs were administered by research staff at 4–12 weeks and 11–13 months postpartum and were scored using standard methods. PROs were compared by NICU admission status. Multivariable regression models were used to evaluate associations between NICU admission and PROs, adjusting for maternal age.

Results: Of 405 enrolled participants (1/2020–6/2023), 401 had neonatal data available, of whom 64 (16%) had an infant admitted to the NICU. These participants were younger than those without NICU admission (p < 0.001) but were otherwise demographically similar. NICU admission was associated with lower patient‐reported self‐efficacy at 4–12 weeks postpartum (p = 0.007; Table). After adjusting for maternal age, NICU admission remained significantly associated with lower self‐efficacy scores at 4–12 weeks postpartum (aβ −5.2, 95% CI −10.0 to −0.4, p = 0.03). This difference was not observed at 11–13 months postpartum. Other PRO domains (global health, patient activation, informational support, perceived stress, postpartum knowledge, care satisfaction, breastfeeding self‐efficacy, postpartum preparedness, and depressive symptoms) did not differ by NICU admission status.

Conclusion: Among low‐income birthing people, although NICU admission was associated with lower postpartum self‐efficacy at 4–12 weeks postpartum, it was not associated with multiple other aspects of health and well‐being in the early or late postpartum periods.

39 | Food Insecurity and Social Vulnerability Increases the Risk for Peripartum Cardiomyopathy With Racial Differences

Kayla Sompel1; Elise Shalowitz2; Elizabeth Kudron2; Shannon Son3; David Kao2

1University of Colorado School of Medicine, Aurora, CO; 2Colorado Center for Personalized Medicine, University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus, Aurora, CO; 3University of Colorado Department of Maternal Fetal Medicine, Aurora, CO

Objective: Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is a leading cause of global maternal mortality with higher morbidity and mortality associated with African Ancestry. While socioeconomic factors contribute to racial disparities in PPCM, the role of food insecurity is unknown.

Study Design: Hospital discharge data were obtained from 11 U.S states (2003–2023). Demographics, comorbidities, and pregnancy data were obtained using ICD‐9 and −10 codes. PPCM was defined as diagnosis 30 days antepartum to 150 days postpartum. Social vulnerability index (SVI) determined based on postal code using data from the Centers for Disease Control. Standard US Department of Agriculture Food Access Research Atlas definitions for ‘food deserts’ were utilized Enrollment rate in federal Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) also included. Multivariable logistic regression assessed associations with PPCM.

Results: Among 20,052,810 deliveries, 18,015 patients with PPCM were identified. Rates of Medicaid enrollment, poverty, total SVI and indices of each individual SVI pillar were higher in PPCM patients, indicating a greater degree of vulnerability compared to patients withoutPPCM (p < 0.001 for all). Patients with PPCM were more likely to live in food deserts (21% vs. 16%, p < 0.001), even when controlled for vehicle access, this disparity persisted [OR 1.37]. Figure 2 shows multivariable analysis with race stratification demonstrating stronger associations between Native American's with PPCM and greater distance from home to hospital [OR 2.5], and residing in a food desert [OR 2.4]. Higher overall SVI was strongly associated with white [OR 3.3] and Asian [OR 4.0] patients with PPCM. SNAP enrollment was minimally associated with an increased risk of PPCM for Hispanic patients only [OR 1.1], suggesting government food assistance may negate the risk of PPCM related to financial food insecurity.

Conclusion: Food insecurity was associated with increased likelihood of PPCM, particularly for native patients.

40 | Social Determinants of Health and Stillbirth Risk: A Two‐Cohort Analysis

Claire M. Dunn1; Hallie Holland2; Selin Everett3; Jesse Valliere4; Kathryn J. Gray1

1University of Washington, Seattle, WA; 2University of Washington School of Medicine, Seattle, WA; 3Stanford SOM, Stanford / Stanford, CA; 4Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, MA

Objective: Stillbirth affects 1 in 160 US pregnancies with marked racial/ethnic disparities. Social determinants of health (SDOH) plausibly influence risk, yet comprehensive data are lacking. We aimed to assess SDOH and stillbirth risk, post‐stillbirth workup disparities, and individual‐level SDOH factors associated with pregnancy loss.

Study Design: Two‐cohort retrospective study. The UW cohort included all deliveries at University of Washington hospitals (2011–2024); zip code‐derived Area Deprivation Index (ADI) scores and insurance data were abstracted from the EHR. Workup completion (autopsy, placental pathology, genetic testing) was assessed for stillbirths. The All of Us cohort provided individual‐level SDOH survey data; 137 items were analyzed via logistic regression.

Results: UW cohort (n = 25,932; 178 stillbirths): Neighborhood deprivation showed a dose‐response with stillbirth risk, rising 3.8‐fold from least‐ to most‐deprived areas (ADI 7–8: 17.11 vs. 4.46/1,000, p = 0.005). Publicly insured patients faced both a 1.52‐fold higher stillbirth rate and markedly lower rates of complete workup after loss (24.2% vs. 49.1%, p = 0.02). American Indian/Alaska Native patients had the highest stillbirth rate (25.1/1,000). All of Us cohorts (n = 8,182 pregnancies; 1551 losses): 86/137 (63%) SDOH items were significantly associated with pregnancy loss across domains of discrimination, food insecurity, chronic stress, and social isolation. Discrimination based on sexual orientation showed the strongest association (OR = 10.24, 95% CI 4.69–22.34); food insecurity conferred over 3‐fold increased odds (OR = 3.19, 1.96–5.20). Adequate prenatal care was strongly protective (10–12 visits OR = 0.27, 0.18–0.41).

Conclusion: SDOH are broadly associated with stillbirth risk. Area deprivation confers a 3.8‐fold increase within a single health system. Publicly insured patients face a double burden: higher stillbirth rates and lower rates of complete workup. SDOH effect sizes rival established biomedical risk factors, supporting SDOH‐targeted interventions as a critical tool for reducing disparities in care following pregnancy loss.
