Dynamic evolution and impact mechanism of human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries: Evidence from the Yangtze River Delta region of China
Authors: Kui Zhao, Yanbo Wu, Zuxin Kuang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractChina's “demographic dividend” has gradually disappeared since 2013, but the problem of human capital mismatch prevails in various industries and sectors, and improving human capital mismatch and optimizing the allocation of human capital has become an important measure for high-quality economic development. This study uses panel data at the provincial level in the Yangtze River Delta region from 2012 to 2021 and employs spatial econometric models to quantitatively analyze the index of human capital mismatch, the impact mechanism, and spatial interaction effects in strategic emerging industries. The results show that the spatial agglomeration and dependence of human capital mismatch decreased in the Yangtze River Delta region, and the index of human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries is generally insufficient. The shortage of talent supply within industries is particularly severe in high-end equipment manufacturing and biotechnology industries. Second, fiscal subsidies have exacerbated human capital mismatches in both local and neighboring areas, with significant spatial spillover effects. Urbanization has exacerbated local human capital mismatches but has improved the degree of human capital mismatch in neighboring areas. The index of industrial structural adjustment is generally conducive to alleviating human capital mismatches. Distorted markets and financial levels have accelerated human capital mismatches by distorting the efficiency of human capital allocation. Third, regional heterogeneity significantly influences the spatial interaction effects of human capital mismatches. This study contributes to improving the situation of human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries in the Yangtze River Delta and provides theoretical and policy recommendations for local governments.Keywords: Human capital mismatch, Strategic emerging industries, Human capital, Spatial agglomeration effect, Yangtze river delta, China

Introduction

As a developing country with a large population, China has abundant labor resources [1]. Since the reform and opening up [2], urbanization and higher education development have provided a solid foundation for human capital accumulation, and China's demographic advantage has gradually transformed into a human capital advantage. According to the “China Human Capital Report 2022,” as of 2020, China's total human capital is 310.8 trillion yuan, a 10.9-fold increase from 1985, with an average annual growth rate of 7.5 %. Moreover, the proportion of people with a college education or above in the labor force increased from 1.0 % in 1985 to 21.8 % in 2020, and the average years of education increased from 6.1 years to 10.7 years. The “demographic dividend” created by human capital accumulation and population quality improvement has provided favorable conditions for China's sustained economic growth [3]. However, with the ageing of China's population and the decline in the birth rate [4], the average age of the labor force is increasing rapidly. Meanwhile, the overall growth rate of the labor force in China shows a phased declining trend [5], indicating that the simple population dividend's driving force on economic growth is gradually weakening and causing labor costs to increase [6], becoming an important factor restricting China's long-term economic growth. For various reasons such as urban-rural labor, market segmentation, and the slow process of factor marketisation, China's huge human capital scale has not formed a comparative advantage in human capital, resulting in serious mismatches [7]. This makes the factor function and externality of human capital unable to maximize utilization, which hinders the release of human capital dividends and becomes an important reason for social and economic problems such as reduced economic output and income inequality (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Since China's economy entered the “new normal” and high-quality development became an important feature of the new economic normalcy, the development of various industries requires sufficient human capital as support. Strategic emerging industries are the engine of China's high-quality economic development [8], including energy conservation and environmental protection, information, biology, high-end equipment manufacturing, new energy, new materials, new energy vehicles, and other technological innovation industries, which play a significant leading and driving role in the overall and long-term development of the economy and society [9]. As the leader of regional economic integration development, the Yangtze River Delta region has good momentum of growth in strategic emerging industries. According to data from the China Commercial Industry Research Institute, in 2020, the total output value of strategic emerging industries in Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Anhui accounted for 40 %, 37.8 %, 33.41 %, and 40.3 % of the total output value of industries above a certain scale, respectively, far exceeding the national average of 25 % during the same period.

The 20th National Congress of the Communist Party of China proposed the development of strategic emerging industry integration clusters to improve total factor productivity and promote qualitative and quantitative growth of the economy. However, total factor productivity, which represents the efficiency and quality of economic growth, has not been maintained at a high level. The contribution of human capital to economic growth in China is much lower than that of developed economies, partly due to the misallocation of human capital between industries [10]. Misallocation of human capital can manifest in two forms: under allocation and overallocation. Under-allocation can have a restraining effect, hindering the driving force of strategic emerging industries in leading high-quality economic development. Overallocation can lead to crowding effects, resulting in the waste of talent resources and a reduction in the efficiency and innovation power of human capital. The key measure to sustain economic growth is to improve the efficiency of human capital allocation. How can we improve the quality through the cultivation and accumulation of human capital to offset the structural contradiction in labor supply? This study mainly explores the misallocation of human capital in strategic emerging industries from a regional perspective. On the one hand, it is conducive to analyzing the practical challenges faced by the development of strategic emerging industries from the perspective of human capital, providing an objective basis for improving the efficiency of human capital allocation in industries, deepening the supply-side reform of human capital in industries and enhancing the effectiveness of human capital in industries. On the other hand, it is conducive to guiding the rational spatial layout of strategic emerging industries in regions from an industrial perspective, unleashing the potential of human capital in strategic emerging industries, consolidating the material foundation for high-quality economic development, and providing policy insights for effectively enhancing the quality and reasonable growth of economic development in the Yangtze River Delta region under the new situation of supply-side structural reform.

Since Schultz proposed the concept of human capital in 1972, it has become an important perspective for explaining the sources of economic growth and economic disparities between countries, regions, and industries in growth theory [11]. Based on the literature, early research mainly focused on the problem of mismatched labour factors and considered labour mismatch as an important cause of the decline in total factor productivity (TFP) and output losses [12]. Temple [13] investigated the impact of cost differences in the dual labor market causing urban-rural income disparities on total productivity and found that labour market rigidity could cause labor mismatch. Subsequently, most scholars began to explore the issue of human capital mismatch affecting technological innovation [14], industrial upgrading [15], sustainable development [16], and other areas. Vollrath [17] used the Mincer [18] regression method to estimate the effect of the human capital mismatch between different sectors on total factor productivity in 14 developing countries. The results showed that eliminating human capital mismatches caused by distorted human capital prices between different sectors would increase output growth in most developing countries by nearly 5 %. Some scholars have tested the relationship between economic growth, human capital, and production structure and found that human capital and a country's dynamic process of production specialization are key elements in promoting economic growth [19,20]. The relationship between human capital and structural changes in high-knowledge-intensive industries significantly affects economic growth [21]. Studies at the industry and sector levels also confirm the existence of human capital mismatch issues [22]. China's education model and structural contradictions in social demand are prominent, and higher education cannot effectively meet the dynamic demand for human capital for economic and social development [23], resulting in an unreasonable distribution of human capital intensity in various industries in China, with the intensity of human capital in public and state-owned monopoly sectors being overwhelmingly dominant [24].

Compared with developed countries' empirical data, the degree of human capital mismatch in China's industries of the same type is more serious [25], and the crowding effect caused by the excessive allocation of human capital and the inhibitory effect caused by the insufficient allocation of human capital coexist. For innovative sectors, the lack of high-quality and abundant technology human capital inflows can lead to a decrease in factor rewards and efficiency [26]. Therefore, solving the problem of human capital mismatch between high-tech sectors is the challenge facing China's current high-quality economic development [27].

Generally, scholars have discussed the causes and impacts of the misallocation of human capital from a theoretical perspective, exploring the issue of human capital allocation from the perspective of the overall economy or specific sectors. Currently, there is limited research on the misallocation of human capital in high-tech industries. As China is the largest developing country globally, the transformation of its industrial structure and the pursuit of high-quality economic development have attracted significant attention. Studying the misallocation of human capital in China's strategic emerging industries has both theoretical and practical significance for other developing countries seeking to improve the quality and upgrade their high-tech industries.

Compared to existing research, this study may contribute in the following ways. First, it constructs a regional model of the misallocation of human capital in strategic emerging industries, analyzing the dynamic evolution and impact mechanisms of human capital misallocation using time-series data. This expands the research on the misallocation of labor and capital resources. Second, while existing research explores the misallocation of human capital in different industries in China from various angles, this study focuses on the Yangtze River Delta region using provincial panel data from 2012 to 2021. It specifically examines the factors of physical capital and human capital using the Cobb-Douglas production function and the perpetual inventory method. The study employs the least squares dummy variable (LSDV) method to calculate elasticity coefficients, optimizes the measurement method for human capital misallocation, applies the spatial Durbin model (SDM) for spatial effect decomposition, and conducts robustness tests using generalized least squares (GLS) and system generalized method of moments (SYS-GMM). This provides a comprehensive analysis of the temporal and spatial evolution of human capital misallocation in the Yangtze River Delta region's strategic emerging industries. Third, the study uses a geographic weight matrix to explore spatial interaction effects and examines the existence of spatial interaction effects of fiscal subsidies, urbanization, industrial structure adjustment index, and market financial level on the misallocation of human capital in strategic emerging industries. The research findings provide a theoretical basis and policy insights for improving the misallocation of human capital in the Yangtze River Delta's strategic emerging industries.

Literature review

Definition and theoretical exploration of human capital mismatch

According to endogenous economic growth theory [28], human capital misallocation manifests as a disparity between the marginal returns of school education and the marginal returns generated from accumulated work experience, hindering the realization of individual-level human capital value [5]. The resource allocation theory suggests that human capital misallocation stems from the compatibility between two production factors: physical capital and labor. Human capital misallocation refers to the mismatch between human capital as a production factor and other production factors, leading to a decrease in productivity and hindering the maximization of output efficiency [6].

Theoretical research and empirical evidence have shown that human capital misallocation is prevalent in regional, industrial, and sectoral contexts in China. First, the gradient nature of regional economic development in China results in differences in the remuneration of human capital factors, leading to an uneven growth of human capital in the eastern, central, and western regions. Human capital tends to be excessively concentrated in economically developed areas, and the misallocation of human capital in these three regions is characterized by “some improvement,” “fluctuations,” and “continuous deterioration”, respectively [29]. Second, the dualistic economic structure between urban and rural areas, the household registration system, and government administrative intervention profoundly influence the flow of human capital among industries and sectors. Human capital intensity, represented by the public sector and state-owned monopolies, holds an absolute advantage, while productive sectors and other sectors have lower human capital intensity. Third, there is a structural contradiction between the supply of talent from school education and the demand for talent in society. The overall proportion of human capital stock in China is relatively low, with a shortage of high-level talent. As the economy shifts from input-driven to innovation-driven growth, it is necessary to promote the structural upgrading of human capital at different levels [30].

By summarizing the research content of the endogenous economic growth theory and resource allocation theory on human capital misallocation, this study suggests that an appropriate theoretical model should be employed to depict the two factors of physical capital and human capital, and then measure the degree of human capital misallocation, providing a theoretical basis for further exploring the impact mechanism of human capital misallocation.

Mechanisms of the impact of human capital mismatch

Human capital mismatch is closely related to the level of economic development, and the stage characteristics of economic development will affect the efficiency of the market allocation of human capital factors [31]. During the process of economic development from the primary stage to the advanced stage, there is a reverse “U"-shaped relationship between economic agglomeration and human capital mismatch. The inflexion point of economic agglomeration is restricted by the degree of distortion of human capital prices, and a tolerant attitude towards human capital mismatch helps to alleviate the structural contradiction between human capital supply and demand. Because economic transformation and upgrading can dynamically adapt to the supply and demand of human capital and reduce the dependence on human capital mismatch in time and space, it is conducive to improving the adverse effects of human capital mismatch on economic growth [32].

According to the Petty-Clark Theorem, as a country's level of economic development improves, labor resources first flow from the primary industry to the secondary industry and then gradually to the tertiary industry. Driven by comparative advantages in different industries, the flow of human capital from the agricultural sector to the non-agricultural sector is an inevitable law [33]. Therefore, industrial restructuring has become an important mechanism that affects human capital mismatch. Some scholars believe that the flow of labor between different industries is closely related to industrial restructuring [34]. In China, a developing country, the difficulty of industrial restructuring is an important reason for human capital mismatch. The human capital mismatch in the industrial structure causes a distortion in the ratio of production technology to human capital input, resulting in a decreasing marginal output of human capital factors, which reduces industrial labor productivity [4]. When the original labor-intensive industries lose cost advantages and technology-intensive industries are not yet mature [[35], [36], [37]], human capital mismatch will lead to the industrial structure falling into the dilemma of a “comparative advantage vacuum.” In theoretical research on the hindrance of industrial structure upgrading by human capital mismatch, human capital mismatch mainly hinders industrial structure upgrading by suppressing technological innovation, promoting deviations in industrial structure, and widening the income gaps between sectors [27].

The impact mechanism of urbanization on human capital mismatch is reflected in the change in human capital accumulation with an increase in urbanization level, which shows an “S"-shaped feature [38]. In the early stages of urbanization, human capital accumulation accelerates. When the urbanization rate reaches a certain level, the inflection point effect of human capital accumulation appears. Owing to the limitations of the urban scale, the speed of human capital accumulation will slow down or even stagnate. During this period, it is necessary to reasonably guide the spatial flow of human capital. When the urbanization rate crosses the established threshold, it can promote the benign growth of human capital accumulation [39]. Looking back at China's urbanization process, we should objectively recognize the impact of urbanization on human capital mismatches.

On the one hand, urbanization pursuing population growth has increased the overall level of human capital accumulation. However, the dual urban-rural system structure and urbanization under government administrative intervention have exacerbated the degree of human capital mismatch in different industries and departments, such as income gap, counter-urbanization, and distortion of the human capital market, which have caused a certain degree of erosion effect on the quality of human capital accumulation [40]. To improve the impact of urbanization on human capital mismatch, attention must be paid to people-oriented new-type urbanization construction, coordinating the population, space, and industry, and promoting the quantity and quality of human capital accumulation with new-type urbanization. The accumulation of human capital will improve the efficiency of urban construction, realize the coordinated development of urban and rural areas, and promote the sustainable development.

Model design

Given the different applicability of the research subjects, there are five main methods for measuring human capital mismatch. First, a relative indicator is used for measurement, such as calculating the departmental human capital mismatch by the proportion of research personnel to people with corresponding education levels or calculating the industry human capital mismatch by dividing the proportion of people with corresponding education levels in the industry by the proportion of value-added in the industry. Second, we equate human capital with labor resources, using the output elasticity of capital and labor in the production sector to represent the degree of labor resource mismatch between the agricultural and non-agricultural sectors based on their respective wage levels and their output share of total output. Third, based on the employment structure ratio of each department and the human capital allocation adjustment factor, we first measure the concentration of human capital and then use the concentration ratio of human capital to characterize the index of human capital mismatch. Fourth, based on dynamic cross-sectional survey data from the corresponding year, we use the total amount of human capital and the rate of return on education (human capital cost) to construct data at the “individual-industry” two-level hierarchy to determine the degree of distortion between industry human capital cost and marginal output. Fifth, referring to the Aoki model, assuming a market competition status and industry product homogeneity, measuring the relative distortion coefficient of human capital prices based on the marginal revenue and cost of industry factors, where the price distortion coefficient is positively correlated with human capital cost, and the higher the cost of obtaining human capital for each industry, the relatively insufficient the allocation of human capital and vice versa.

The above measurement methods each have their own advantages and disadvantages, such as the one-sidedness of using a single indicator to measure the mismatch of human capital, the accuracy of estimating education years and lifelong education returns, and the differences in the quantity and quality of human capital across different industries. This study takes strategic emerging industries as the sample entity to explore the content of mismatched human capital from both temporal and spatial dimensions. Building upon existing research methods, the study adopts the Cobb-Douglas production function and assumes that the factor market is an imperfectly competitive market, allowing for free movement of different factor markets and relaxing the requirement for consistency in cross-sectional data.

The index of industrial structure adjustment has a positive effect on the mismatch of human capital in the local area. While it may worsen the mismatch in the short term due to the upgrading of industrial structure, the overall effect is negative, indicating that industrial structure upgrading helps improve the mismatch of human capital. Market financial development has a positive impact on the mismatch of human capital both in the local area and neighboring regions. As the financial market becomes more developed, the distortion of financial capital prices relative to human capital prices increases, leading to higher substitutability of human capital. This, in turn, inhibits the efficiency of human capital allocation and exacerbates the mismatch of human capital.

Further analysis

The analysis of the impact mechanism of human capital mismatch shows that fiscal subsidies, urbanization, industrial structure adjustment, and market financial development have varying degrees of influence and spatial spillover effects on the mismatch of human capital in the local area and neighboring regions. Due to differences in economic foundations and resource endowments, the explanatory power of individual variables for human capital mismatch is limited. Are there spatial interaction effects among these variables in influencing the mechanism of human capital mismatch? The regional heterogeneity of human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries is not reflected.

Therefore, this study conducted a more in-depth exploration using a geographic weight matrix. The results in Table 7 show that the interaction effects of fiscal subsidies, urbanization, and the index of industrial structure adjustment are more significant in influencing human capital mismatch. The more developed the economy is, the higher the urbanization rate, and the larger the index of industrial structure upgrade. Fiscal subsidies can attract human capital to flow into strategic emerging industries, but they may also cause a differentiation of human capital “stock” and “increment” in the industry. The interaction effect between fiscal subsidies and market financial development is beneficial for improving human capital mismatch. A more stable market financial environment with reasonable loan interest rates and capital factor prices can effectively stimulate the supply of human capital and improve the efficiency of human capital allocation.

Comparing the determination coefficients of Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Anhui, the regional heterogeneity of spatial interaction effects on human capital mismatch is significant. For example, accelerating industrial structure adjustment and leveraging the combined effects of market finance and fiscal subsidies have a higher policy effect on human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries in Anhui Province than in other provinces and cities. Considering the actual situation of the province, adopting differentiated measures to solve the problem of human capital mismatch is particularly important.

Conclusion and policy recommendations

Conclusion

Based on panel data from the Yangtze River Delta region, this study empirically analyses the index, influencing mechanisms, and spatial interaction effects of human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries using the Cobb-Douglas production function and spatial econometric models. The research findings are as follows.

Theoretical significance

By examining the spatiotemporal dynamics of human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries, this study highlights the importance of addressing this issue to unleash the potential of human capital and enhance its allocation efficiency. It expands the academic perspective on human capital mismatch and provides theoretical support for regional-level studies on human capital in strategic emerging industries.

Practical significance

a.In the context of reduced spatial agglomeration and dependence on human capital mismatch in the Yangtze River Delta region, the overall trend of the human capital mismatch index in strategic emerging industries indicates an under-allocation of human capital. The results of the influencing mechanisms suggest that tailored optimization of fiscal subsidies, accelerated industrial structure adjustment, people-oriented urbanization, enhanced talent supply within industries, and improved financial market environment are crucial measures to address human capital mismatch.

The results of the influencing mechanisms suggest that tailored optimization of fiscal subsidies, accelerated industrial structure adjustment, people-oriented urbanization, enhanced talent supply within industries, and improved financial market environment are crucial measures to address human capital mismatch.

b.Given the representative nature of the sample data in terms of temporal span and regional geographic features, the research findings on human capital mismatch in strategic emerging industries in the Yangtze River Delta region not only provide important policy implications for local industrial upgrading and optimization but also offer a practical reference for other developing countries in achieving high-quality economic development and industrial transformation and upgrading.

Policy recommendations

Based on the conclusions, the following policy implications are proposed. First, in response to the problem of surplus human capital but insufficient allocation in strategic emerging industries in the Yangtze River Delta region, it is important to strengthen the cultivation of talent resources in strategic emerging industries. This can be achieved by aligning education with industry talent demand, increasing the structural supply of talent through education, and improving the quantity and quality of human capital. Capitalizing on the advantages of the regional integration strategy, the government should incentivize the human capital market through fiscal subsidies, promote market-oriented reforms, and improve mechanisms for talent introduction, cultivation, utilization, and cooperation within the industrial talent chain. This will stimulate the vitality of the human capital market, promote the upgrading of the human capital structure, and provide policy support for enhancing the quality of human capital. Second, to address the influencing mechanisms and spatial interaction effects of human capital mismatch, targeted strategies should be implemented to prevent and mitigate distortions in the human capital market and improve allocation efficiency. First, deepening financial market reforms and establishing a robust price mechanism for capital factors will eliminate administrative distortions in human capital and create a favorable financial market environment for human capital supply. Second, accelerating local industrial structure upgrades, driven by the optimization of industrial structure, will lead to the dynamic matching of human capital and industrial structure. Finally, it is crucial to guide the spatial mobility of high-quality talent resources, promote the integration of urban and industrial development, and gradually alleviate the spatial spillover effects of human capital mismatch. Finally, addressing regional heterogeneity in human capital mismatch requires different approaches for economically developed provinces and cities with high urbanization levels and rapid industrial structure upgrades. It is necessary to exercise moderate control over fiscal subsidies to avoid crowding-out effects on local human capital mismatch and siphoning effects on neighboring areas. For economically relatively backward provinces, leveraging the leverage effect of fiscal subsidies is crucial. Increasing investment in local talent for strategic emerging industries, promoting the upgrade of industrial structure in coordination with financial market development, and addressing the insufficient supply of human capital will improve the allocation efficiency of human capital in strategic emerging industries and unleash the potential of industrial human capital.

Limitations

This study mainly explores the current situation of the development of strategic emerging industries in China's Yangtze River Delta region and the existing problems from the perspective of human capital mismatch. Due to geographical and time constraints, empirical data cannot comprehensively portray the trend of human capital mismatch in China's strategic emerging industries, and in subsequent studies, we can consider conducting comparative analysis at the level of different regions and then analyze the strategic emerging industries in each region of China from the perspective of China's human resource mismatch expand the space for academic research and policy revelation.
