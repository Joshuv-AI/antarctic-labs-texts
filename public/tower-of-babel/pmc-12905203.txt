Nonparametric quantile regression captures regional variability and scaling deviations in Atlantic surfclam length–weight relationships
Authors: Gorka Bidegain, Marta Sestelo, Patricia L. Luque, Eric N. Powell, Arantza Irirarte, Ibon Uriarte, Daphne Munroe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The universality of the allometric model for describing the length–weight relationship in marine species has been questioned, particularly for some invertebrates such as sea urchins, clams, and barnacles. In such cases, nonparametric regression models may offer improved flexibility and capture specific patterns-such as inflection points in growth curves-not identified by standard parametric models. These features can support the identification of biologically meaningful thresholds relevant to fisheries, including size-dependent yield. Nonparametric quantile regression further enhances inference by characterizing variability across the entire distribution of body condition. Here, we assess the comparative performance of parametric and nonparametric regression models for the Atlantic surfclam, Spisula solidissima, using data collected from three regions along the U.S. Atlantic coast (Virginia, Delaware/Maryland, and New Jersey). First, we compare two mean regression approaches —a classic allometric model and a kernel-based nonparametric alternative— using a bootstrap-based procedure. Second, we apply quantile regression to both parametric and nonparametric frameworks to investigate size-dependent variation in growth patterns. Model selection for mean regressions was based on a hypothesis test contrasting the allometric model versus a general nonparametric alternative, while the quantile regressions were evaluated using a goodness-of-fit test derived from the cumulative sum of the gradient vector. Our results indicate that the allometric model provides a better fit in the mean regression context, while the nonparametric model proves more effective for quantile regression, particularly in detecting condition-dependent deviations and regional variability. Other long-lived marine bivalves, such as Arctica islandica and Mercenaria mercenaria, which show environmentally driven variation in growth and condition, may similarly benefit from modeling approaches that distinguish central from marginal populations.

Introduction

The Atlantic surfclam Spisula solidissima (Dillwyn, 1817) is a bivalve that commonly inhabits sandy sediments from the surf zone to 50 m1,2 in the western North Atlantic continental shelf from the southern Gulf of St. Lawrence (Canada) to Cape Hatteras (North Carolina, USA)3–5. The S. solidissima fishery is one of the most valuable of the U.S commercial shellfisheries. Since 1982, the NOAA National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) has conducted resource assessment surveys every two-three years5,6. The management of S. solidissima is based on data from these surveys, landings, and modeling studies of population trends to establish the status of the stock relative to biological reference points establishing an overfished state and defining overfishing limits6,7.

Declines in growth rates and maximum size, likely a function of rising temperature8–11have been accompanied in some regions (e.g., Delmarva Peninsula) by increased mortality and lower condition index12,13. Superimposed on these trends is a well-documented range shift with the distribution of the stock moving to the north and offshore into deeper waters to evade warmer bottom water temperatures5,6,14,15. The latest stock assessment found that S. solidissima is not overfished and overfishing is unlikely to occur in the following years7; however, rapid warming of the northwestern Atlantic Ocean has produced regional challenges to the fishery in terms of declining stock abundance, declining LPUE (landings per unit of effort), and increased restrictions to fishing grounds due to the increasing overlap of the Atlantic surfclam with another biomass dominant, the ocean quahog Arctica islandica15–17. Despite the importance of the S. solidissima fishery and its management efforts, further research into its biology and ecology is still needed, for example into those aspects which can supply information on the physiological condition and/or the growth patterns potentially changing in the future as warming of the Northwestern Atlantic continues18. The physiology of an animal is related to its evolutionary fitness19,20; physiological adaptability is an indicator of reproductive success and of the ability to cope with ecological and environmental factors21,22. For example, surfclam maximum size as estimated by the von Bertalanffy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\infty }$$\end{document} parameter is strongly influenced by summer temperatures which influence the balance between assimilated energy import and respiratory energy demand, ultimately limiting maximum size8–10. Food supply, consequently, is an important modulator of maximum growth rate and maximum size8,23. In addition, snails, sea stars, crabs, fish, and shrimp predate on surfclams accounting in some areas for 50-100 % of Atlantic surfclam mortality24–27, and potentially also affecting condition index and growth rate.

A proxy of condition index can be the length-weight relationship of a population or subgroup of S. solidissima. Clams with a heavier weight for a given length will be in better physical condition than those with a lighter weight. A number of population dynamics models have taken advantage of this fact8,10,28–30. An allometric equation classically has been applied in defining the relationship between length and weight31–33. The length-weight relationship has been explored beyond the classic allometric approach for a few marine species, including two clam species (R. decussatus, R. philippinarum)34, and gooseneck barnacles (P.pollicipes)35,36. For these three species, the authors employed mean-based nonparametric models, which successfully identified fluctuations in the terminal segments of the regression curves, that is, for large individuals. Furthermore, these models exhibited superior goodness of fit with the data in comparison to the conventional allometric model.37 further analyzed the length-weight relationship in sea urchins (S. droebachiensis) using nonparametric quantile regression detecting variability in sea urchin growth patterns throughout the length distribution and between depths. Quantile regression is a statistical method that can be used to detect and analyze various behaviors, including those related to biological phenomena. It allows for the estimation of conditional quantile functions, which express quantiles of the conditional distribution of a response variable as functions of observed covariates38. Although ordinary least-squares regression is widely used to characterize mean length–weight relationships, it may not fully capture variability between individuals of the same length. In surfclams, such variability can result from differences in physiological conditions, age structure, or environmental drivers such as temperature and food availability8–11. Nonparametric quantile regression can address this limitation by identifying subpopulations in different condition states that are of ecological and fisheries relevance, by detecting local deviations or inflection points in growth patterns that may not be apparent in mean-based models, and by assessing regional heterogeneity that may manifest differently across quantiles. Thus, nonparametric quantile regression in surfclams may provide a complementary approach extending applications such as34 in estuarine clams to offer a more comprehensive understanding of distributional heterogeneity and regional differences in surfclam condition, thereby enhancing the ecological interpretability of the results.

In this study, we examine the growth patterns of the Atlantic surfclam (S. solidissima) across its length distribution using data from the US NMFS-NEFSC surfclam surveys conducted in 2005 and 2008 off the coasts of Virginia, Delaware/Maryland, and New Jersey. We analyze the length-weight relationship by comparing the classic allometric model with a nonparametric alternative, employing bootstrap-based procedures to assess their relative goodness of fit as mean regression models. The estimations for both models, parametric and nonparametric, rely on ordinary least squares and kernel smoothers, respectively. Additionally, we also use a boosting-based quantile regression approach to compare the parametric and nonparametric growth curves, offering a comprehensive evaluation of the growth patterns across the length distribution.

Methods

Data collection

Surfclams were collected from the US surfclam stock during stock assessment surveys conducted in 2005 and 20086,8,39. The fishery operates within the US Exclusive Economic Zone at depths of approximately 20-60 meters, extending from the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay to Georges Bank. This study focuses on three regions, labeled strata 9, 13, and 21 in the assessments conducted prior to 2017 when the stratum map was revised40. These strata are located off Virginia, Delaware and Maryland, and New Jersey, respectively, showing a latitudinal gradient from south to north (Fig. 1)39. Strata 9, 13, and 21 account for the bulk of the area occupied by the Atlantic surfclam south of Hudson Canyon and, until the reopening of Georges Bank in the 2010s41, contributed the majority of the fishable biomass6. Consequently, the number of stations occupied in this region per survey is extensive, and accordingly these strata provide the data-rich dataset on lengths and weights necessary for the present analysis. The region has also contributed a considerable fraction of the research on Atlantic surfclam demographics, summarized by14, including studies focused on the relationships of length and weight8,10,11,13,42. For the purposes of this analysis, data from two comprehensive surveys of these strata are used, the 2005 and 2008 surveys. These surveys took place after the well-documented regime shift of the early 2000s that resulted in a substantial Atlantic surfclam mortality event circa 2000 and a well-documented change in the regional demographics of the population5,12,42–44.Fig. 1Locations of study regions, labeled strata 9, 13, and 21. Adapted from39.

Locations of study regions, labeled strata 9, 13, and 21. Adapted from39.

Shell length and weight data were collected in 2005 and 2008 from 926 individuals-268 from stratum 9, 324 from stratum 13, and 334 from stratum 21. Shell length was measured to the nearest millimeter, and wet meat weight, after removing excess water with a paper towel, was recorded to the nearest gram. The individual shell length and weight measurements were used to model the length-weight relationship.

Length-weight relationship

The length-weight relationship was explored for each stratum (9, 13, 21) using mean regression and quantile regression models. In both cases, the performance of two models were compared: the potential regression model (the classic allometric model)45 and a nonparametric regression model estimated using local polynomial kernel smoothers46,47.

Results

The mean estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals for weight (WT) using both the allometric and nonparametric models, were evaluated for the full dataset as well as for each individual stratum (9, 13, 21) (Table 1). These data are also visualized in Figs. 2 and 3, which respectively show the regression curves and first derivatives for the allometric and nonparametric models. As expected from the structure of the allometric model, both the estimated WT values and their first derivatives show a continuous increase with LT (Fig. 2). Overall, the width of the confidence intervals increases with length, particularly under the nonparametric model. The surfclam size where the maximum growth rate is obtained, that is, the size at the maximum weight gain per unit of size (length) estimated using the first derivative of the local polynomials, is not observed (Fig. 3, right). However, the nonparametric curves offer greater flexibility, capturing local fluctuations in growth. The first derivative in the nonparametric curves shows plateaus and inflection points around 100-150 mm for all data together and strata 9 and 21. The southern stratum 9 exhibits relatively higher WT estimates and steeper derivatives at larger sizes (130–150 mm) in both models. However, no individuals larger than 150 mm are observed in this stratum.

The scaling exponent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\beta }$$\end{document} is highest in stratum 9 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\beta }= 2.95$$\end{document}), indicating the steepest growth curve in this southern region, i.e., a weight gain that is more rapid, particularly at larger sizes. Stratum 13 shows the lowest exponent (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\beta }= 2.72$$\end{document}), implying a slower increase in weight with length and potentially reflecting environmental or physiological constraints. The allometric function for stratum 21 presents an intermediate value (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\beta }\approx 2.80$$\end{document}).

The goodness-of-fit test for the allometric model applied to the mean regression using the full dataset indicates that the null hypothesis is not rejected (p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.297$$\end{document}), which suggests that the allometric model is an appropriate choice for modeling these data. At the stratum level, the test results are mixed. In stratum 9, the null hypothesis is rejected (p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.015$$\end{document}), implying that the allometric model may not provide an adequate fit in this region. In contrast, the null hypothesis is not rejected for stratum 13 (p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.59$$\end{document}) and stratum 21 (p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= 0.41$$\end{document}), supporting the adequacy of the allometric model in these areas.

Discussion

A bootstrap-based procedure was applied to compare the performance of the classic allometric model and a nonparametric alternative as mean regression approaches for modeling the length-weight relationship of S. solidissima along the U.S. Atlantic coast. In addition, we used the same procedure to compare the corresponding quantile regression models. This nonparametric quantile regression analysis in surfclams extends the work of34 on estuarine clams by moving beyond mean-based nonparametric regression to characterize the entire weight distribution.

Overall, the classic allometric model proved to be a more suitable mean regression approach for modeling the length–weight relationship of S. solidissima, outperforming the nonparametric alternative, except in the southern region of Virginia (stratum 9) (Table 5).13 reviewed various allometric models describing the length–weight relationship for S. solidissima, reporting scaling exponents (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) ranging from 2.62 to 3.12– range consistent with most bivalve species33,57. This aligns with the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta = 2.81$$\end{document} (95% CI: 2.76–2.86) obtained in the present study for the Atlantic coast. Regionally, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} coefficient is relatively higher in the southern region of Virginia (2.95) compared to estimates for the more northern regions of Delaware/Maryland (2.72) and New Jersey (2.80). The length-weight relationship for surfclams in stratum 9 differs from the other two strata not only in the scaling coefficient but also in the poorer performance of allometric model overall. Stratum 9 is near the southern edge of the range where warming bottom water temperatures have reduced maximum size11 and where a southern form of the species (S. S. similis) appears to make only a small contribution to the population structure58. The allometry of the southern form in this region remains undescribed, however. These findings may be of relevance for other long-lived marine bivalves that experience strong latitudinal or depth-related gradients in environmental conditions59–61. Species such as Arctica islandica or Mercenaria mercenaria, which also exhibit variation in growth and condition across environmental gradients, may benefit from similar modeling approaches that distinguish central from marginal populations.

The allometric relationship provides valuable results for fisheries management and stock assessment as it aids in estimating a range of important stock descriptors including the size structure of shellfish populations, the assessment of growth rates, and estimates of fecundity. The advantages of the nonparametric model in comparison with the allometric model observed in previous studies in shellfish34–37 are not visible in the present case. The allometric model assumes a fixed relationship between weight and length, typically following a power-law function33. The nonparametric models have a more flexible fit for the data and are capable of identifying specific patterns towards the end of the regression curve, patterns that remain undetected by the allometric model35,62. This attribute implies that the allometric model may lead to the loss of valuable biological information63.

In this context, the overall weak performance of the nonparametric model applied here may result in several ways. First, the sampling dates of the study (June) may align with the onset of gametogenesis in S. solidissima and this potentially introduces bias to the curve (skewing of the curve), given that weight relative to size is higher during this developmental phase than during the resting period. Along the New Jersey coastline, the documented spawning window extends from late June to early August64. Notably, spawning initiation may manifest as early as late May or early June65. Further to the south, in the state of Virginia, the onset of spawning may occur as early as May, concluding by July66. The southern subspecies, Spisula solidissima similis, is distinguished by earlier reproductive development with spawning occurring during the spring to early summer period67,68. Second, the reduction in the expected number of large individuals due to warming temperatures limiting maximum size limits information at the higher end of the curve, although the potential influence of fishing in reducing the number of the largest individuals cannot be excluded9. Future studies should consider sampling across different seasons with gear characterized by improved selectivity for smaller and larger sizes, in order to evaluate the impact of gametogenesis on weight at length and explore the potential effects of scarcity of large sizes in the modeled dataset. These sources of uncertainty are not exclusive to S. solidissima and suggest that future applications of nonparametric and quantile regression models in shellfish stock assessments should carefully consider reproductive timing, thermal gradients, and gear selectivity, especially in regions with strong seasonal or spatial variability69,70.

Plausibly for the same reasons, the size of S. solidissima for which the maximum growth rate is obtained (i.e. maximum weight gain per unit of length estimated using the first derivative) is not resolved in this analysis (Fig. 3, right panel).35,37 and34,36 calculated this maximum growth rate at the length for which the first derivative of the regression curve reached its maximum with the primary goal of achieving the maximum long-term yield from the fishery. This criterion ensures that beyond this point, the weight gain from one size to the next diminishes35. The apparent absence of a maximum in the first derivative for S. solidissima may indicate a differential in growth dynamics for one of the largest nonsymbiont-containing bivalves relative to other bivalves, but the relationship may also be obscured by the broad environmental conditions in stratum 9 which covers a substantial portion of the continental shelf in a region where the cross-shelf gradient in environmental conditions likely introduces considerable variability into the allometry of the species on a local scale. The wider range in first derivatives of the allometric quantile regression and the presence of an inflection point in the first derivative of the nonparametric relationship may point additionally to the environmental variability of stratum 9.

The mean-based regression models have limitations when applied to marine organisms compared to quantile regression analysis. The advantages of quantile regression models, including robustness to outliers, comprehensive analysis of the entire distribution, flexibility in modeling heterogeneity, sensitivity to distributional changes, incorporation of prior covariates, and improved predictive performance71,72, make them well-suited for investigating the length-weight relationship in shellfish species. These advantages contribute to a more accurate understanding of the factors influencing the growth and development of shellfish and a more complete picture of the lower body condition, providing valuable information for evaluating effects of management actions on individual populations73,74. Allometric and nonparametric quantile regressions have been applied to explore the length-weight relationship of marine organisms including sea urchins37 and fish75 offering a comprehensive understanding compared to conventional weight equations derived from linear or quadratic regression methods. Although the allometric model here adequately described the mean length–weight relationship, quantile regression was necessary to uncover distributional heterogeneity. For instance, lower quantiles identified individuals with reduced condition that may be linked to environmental stressors or reproductive stage, while upper quantiles highlighted maximum growth potential. Such insights are critical for fisheries management, which requires understanding not only the central trend but also the tails of the distribution that determine resilience and yield variability.

Thus, the methodological framework developed here is potentially transferable to other marine species where management decisions depend on accurate modeling of biomass or yield based on length or size distributions70. Application to other taxa could help identify analogous thresholds of condition and performance across different ecosystems.

Overall, in this study, the quantile regressions show the nonparametric approach to be a better choice when compared to the allometric model except for the upper quantiles (Table 5). The quantile regression models show that S. solidissima exhibits different growth patterns for the quantiles studied. Individuals found in the lower quantile exhibit a reduced weight for a given length, likely signifying inferior physical condition compared to those with a higher weight. Thus, condition indices may act as indicators for evaluating the length-weight relationship within a population or subgroup21,22,28.

The more rigid behavior of the allometric model with limitations in capturing variability across different quantiles, inability to accommodate diverse growth patterns, and its susceptibility to boundary effects and missing data make it a less suitable approach for modeling the length-weight relationship of S. solidissima.37 applied the same quantile regression technique to both allometric and nonparametric models, finding that the allometric quantile regression model performed better than the nonparametric model for the sea urchin S. Droebachiensis. The model revealed variations in sea urchin growth patterns across length distributions and depths, which the authors suggested to be related with disparities in food availability, competition, and wave exposure between different depths.

Concerning the identification of maximum growth estimated through the first derivative, this is most evident at the upper quantile (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{0.90}$$\end{document}), where a peak occurs around a length of 175 mm (Fig. 5A, right). Notably, the non-parametric estimators display boundary effects, likely due to data gaps at both the lower and upper size ranges, possibly reflecting limitations of the sampling gear at smaller sizes and the effects of warming temperatures constraining maximum size42. More clearly than in other quantiles, individuals in the lower quantile appear to reach a plateau between 100–150 mm or maximum weight gain per unit of size at lengths between 140–150 mm across (Fig. 4 ). The inferred inferior physical condition of individuals in this quantile may explain the slower growth and this maximum beyond which the weight gain per unit of size decreases. Once more, upcoming sampling should integrate extended periods throughout various season including smaller and larger sizes to assess how gametogenesis influences maximum body growth and to investigate the consequences of the limited representation of sizes in the variation of the weight gain per unit of size.

Rapidly rising bottom water temperatures in the northwestern Atlantic are impacting the demographics of many species76,77. Among these is the Atlantic surfclam, a species noteworthily showing changes in growth and maximum size as anticipated by the temperature-size rule which states that maximum size should vary inversely with temperature within a species’ range along a latitudinal gradient or cross-shelf gradient in temperature (Powell et al. submitted). Recent studies by9 and11 conform to this expectation. Studies pertinent to the impact of rising temperatures on species’ demographics have focused on the changing parameterizations of growth curves, the von Bertalanffy growth model being a prime example. For S. solidissima, the influence of changing growth dynamics directly impinges on the stock assessment and management of the species, including the continuing robustness of survey design78 and limitations imposed on the appropriateness of size limits42,79. What has not been investigated is the interaction of shifts in the growth model relative to the allometric relationship of length and weight even though, as in many bivalve species, landings and the economics of the fishery depend upon measures of volume and yield, whilst management is determined using biomass-based reference points. Indeed, the study by13 remains the only comprehensive evaluation of latitudinal and cross-shelf variation in condition index, a primary determinant of yield. Results of quantile regression presented in this study agree with an expectation that variations in growth at age should feed forward into variations of weight at length. The present study provides a strong recommendation for further investigation of these relationships, including alternative strategies in which the mean structure is allowed to vary across quantiles.
