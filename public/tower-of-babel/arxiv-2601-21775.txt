Differentiable Knapsack and Top- k k Operators via Dynamic Programming
Germain Vivier-Ardisson, Michaël E. Sander, Axel Parmentier, Mathieu Blondel

Abstract
Knapsack and Top- k k operators are useful for selecting discrete subsets of variables. However, their integration into neural networks is challenging as they are piecewise constant, yielding gradients that are zero almost everywhere. In this paper, we propose a unified framework casting these operators as dynamic programs, and derive differentiable relaxations by smoothing the underlying recursions. On the algorithmic side, we develop efficient parallel algorithms supporting both deterministic and stochastic forward passes, and vector-Jacobian products for the backward pass. On the theoretical side, we prove that Shannon entropy is the unique regularization choice yielding permutation-equivariant operators, and characterize regularizers inducing sparse selections. Finally, on the experimental side, we demonstrate our framework on a decision-focused learning benchmark, a constrained dynamic assortment RL problem, and an extension of discrete VAEs.

Introduction

Many learning tasks rely on selecting a discrete subset of variables, or items, whether to learn sparse latent representations or to optimize resource allocation. The standard Top-
k
operator selects subsets under a fixed cardinality constraint, and the
Knapsack
problem
[
20
]
generalizes this structure by supporting non-uniform item weights, enforcing a total capacity limit on their sum.

While the structure of these constraints can provide necessary inductive bias, the resulting operators are piecewise constant, with either zero or undefined Jacobian. As a result, integrating them into neural networks is challenging as they break the differentiable computation graph, preventing the backpropagation of meaningful gradients.

To bridge this gap, current research often relies on continuous relaxations or regularization for gradient estimation. Solver-agnostic,
black-box
approaches, such as perturbation with stochastic noise
[
4
,
27
]
or piecewise affine interpolation
[
40
]
, are flexible but computationally expensive, often requiring multiple solver calls. Conversely, specialized differentiable layers for ranking and Top-
k
operations, often based on optimal transport or regularized linear programming
[
11
,
8
]
, lack the generality to handle the non-uniform weights inherent to Knapsack constraints. Furthermore, many existing methods restrict the choice of regularization (e.g., to Shannon entropy), limiting control over key properties like sparsity.

In this work, we address these limitations by revisiting the structural similarities between Knapsack and Top-
k
problems. We cast both as instances of dynamic programming (DP), and build upon the framework of
23
to propose a unified differentiable formulation. Specifically, we make the following contributions:

•

In
Section

4
, we derive differentiable relaxations of the Knapsack and Top-
k
problems by regularizing the
\max
operators within the underlying Bellman DP recursions.

•

In
Section

4.2
, we show that Shannon entropy is the
unique
separable regularization function yielding
permutation-equivariant
relaxed operators, and characterize regularizers inducing
sparse
item selections (
Sections

4.2
and
4.2
).

•

In
Section

5.1
, we derive efficient vector-Jacobian product computations, allowing the integration of the proposed operators into differentiable programming pipelines.

•

In
Section

5.2
, we prove the existence of a distribution underlying the proposed operators (
Section

5.2
), and provide an ancestral sampling algorithm for stochastic forward passes.

•

We provide a principled supervised learning approach using our operators as output layers in
Section

5.3
, by showing how to compute gradients of associated Fenchel-Young losses
[
6
]
.

•

In
Section

6
, we compare our DP-based losses against decision-focused learning baselines, evaluate our Knapsack operators on a constrained dynamic assortment RL problem, and benchmark our proposed differentiable Top-
k
operators on a Fenchel-Young extension of discrete VAEs.

Background and related work

Knapsack operators.

The 0/1 Knapsack problem for
n
items with values given by
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, weights
{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
, and capacity
C\in{\mathbb{N}}
, corresponds to the problem of finding a subset of items with maximal value with a total weight restricted by the maximal capacity
C

[
20
,
15
]
. It can be written as the following integer linear program:

\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}y_{i}\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}y_{i}\leq C\,.

(1)

We denote by
{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}\triangleq\left\{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\mid\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle\leq C\right\}
the set of feasible item selections. The optimal value of Problem (
1
) is denoted by
{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
, and
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
is the corresponding maximizer, or
Knapsack operator
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})\triangleq\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle,\quad{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle.

Danskin’s theorem gives
\nabla{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
when the maximizer is unique
[
12
]
. However, since
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
is piecewise constant, its Jacobian
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
is either zero or undefined, preventing the backpropagation of gradients.

Top-
k
operators.

The variational form of the Top-
k
selection problem is a notable slight variation of Problem (
1
), where item weights are given by
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
, and the capacity is
C=k
for some
k\in[n]
. Moreover, the inequality constraint becomes an equality, so that the problem writes:

\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}y_{i}\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}=k\,.

(2)

With a slight abuse of notation (overloading Knapsack notations for
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
and
C=k
), we respectively denote by
{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}\,
,
{\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}\,
, and
{\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}
the feasible set, value and operator in this case. Note that
{\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}})=(\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\in\text{Top-$k$}({\bm{\theta}})\}})_{i=1}^{n}
is also known as the
Top-
k
mask
of
{\bm{\theta}}
.

Smoothed maximum operators.

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a strictly convex regularization function. The smoothed maximum operator
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined as:

\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\triangleq\max_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{d}}\left\{\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-\Omega({\bm{q}})\right\}=\Omega^{*}({\bm{\theta}}).

Since
\Omega
is strictly convex,
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}
is differentiable, with:

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\argmax_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{d}}\left\{\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-\Omega({\bm{q}})\right\}=\nabla\Omega^{*}({\bm{\theta}}).

Typical choices for the regularization function
\Omega
include Shannon’s negative entropy, which yields the
log-sum-exp
operator and
softmax
gradients, and quadratic (or Gini’s negative entropy) regularization, which leads to the
sparsemax
operator
[
21
]
. This choice controls the smoothness and sparsity of the resulting operator: we discuss its consequences in our setting in
Section

4.2
.

Differentiating through combinatorial solvers.

Integrating discrete combinatorial solvers into differentiable pipelines faces the challenge of uninformative gradients, as their output is piecewise constant. A line of research differentiates the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions of linear or mixed integer programs
[
19
,
13
]
. One can also treat the solver as a
black-box
oracle.
40
propose a method to compute gradients via piecewise affine interpolation of the solver’s output.
Alternatively, perturbation-based methods
[
4
,
27
]
smooth the operator by adding stochastic noise to input parameters and estimate gradients via Monte-Carlo.
10
apply these perturbation techniques specifically to Top-
k
selection. While these approaches are flexible, they can be computationally expensive due to the need for multiple solver calls.

Differentiable Top-
k
and sorting.

Existing approaches typically relax Problem (
2
) using regularized optimal transport
[
11
,
41
]
, regularized linear programming
[
2
,
8
,
30
,
34
]
, or
smoothed sorting networks
[
28
,
29
]
.
These methods, however, are deterministic and cannot be used as a stochastic layer. Closest to our work,
1
derive a differentiable Top-
k
operator based on factorizing the entropy of distributions on
k
-subsets. By adopting a broader DP-based perspective, we strictly generalize that framework: we recover similar algorithms with Shannon entropy-based regularization, and obtain new sparse operators with Gini or Tsallis regularization. Crucially, unlike all aforementioned approaches, our framework naturally handles Knapsack constraints and non-uniform weights. Furthermore, it supports both deterministic and stochastic layers.

DP for structured prediction.

In NLP, dynamic programming with the standard
(\max,+)
semiring has been used for
k
-subset selection
[
22
,
26
]
.
Dynamic programs can be smoothed out using second-order expectation semirings
[
18
]

or by replacing
\max
operators in the Bellman recursion with smoothed
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}
operators
[
23
]
.

3
Knapsack and Top-
k
as dynamic programs

Knapsack case.

Problem (
1
) famously exhibits optimal substructure, allowing for a DP solution
[
20
,
15
]
. Indeed, let
V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]
be the optimal value of the subproblem considering only the first
i\leq n
items with a capacity
c\leq C
:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\triangleq\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{i}}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}\theta_{l}y_{l}\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}w_{l}y_{l}\leq c\,.

To compute this, we can compare two possible decisions for item
i
. We can skip it and set
y_{i}=0
, yielding value
V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c]
. Alternatively, we can pick it (only if
w_{i}\leq c
) and set
y_{i}=1
, yielding value
\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c-w_{i}]
. This logic leads to the following recursion:

\displaystyle\!\!V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]=\begin{cases}V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c]\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{if $w_{i}>c$,}\\[5.0pt]
\max\bigl(V^{\bm{w}}[i\!-\!1,c],\theta_{i}\!+\!V^{\bm{w}}[i\!-\!1,\!c\!-\!w_{i}]\bigr)\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\\
\text{else.}\end{cases}

(3)

The optimal value of Problem (
1
) is therefore given by
V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]={\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
. This recursion runs in
\mathcal{O}(nC)
time and space, and is initialized with
V^{\bm{w}}[0,\colon]=V^{\bm{w}}[\colon,0]=0
. An illustration of this on an example is given in
Figure

6
.

Top-
k
case.

For Problem (
2
), let
V^{\bm{1}}[i,j]
be the optimal value when selecting
exactly

j
items from the first
i
:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{1}}[i,j]\triangleq\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{i}}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}\theta_{l}y_{l}\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}y_{l}=j\,.

The equality constraint implies that any subproblem
(i,j)
with
j>i
is infeasible. Consequently, if item
i
is required to reach the target count (i.e.,
i=j
), we are forced to pick it, as skipping would lead to an infeasible state. We enforce this behavior by initializing the boundary
V^{\bm{1}}[0,1\colon]
with
-\infty
values, which naturally propagate to infeasible states. The
\max
operator then automatically discards invalid transitions, and the recursion simply writes:

V^{\bm{1}}[i,j]=\max\left(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{1}}[i\!-\!1,j\!-\!1],\;V^{\bm{1}}[i\!-\!1,j]\right).

The optimal value of Problem (
2
) is therefore given by
V^{\bm{1}}[n,k]={\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}})
. This recursion runs in
\mathcal{O}(nk)
time and space, and we illustrate it on an example in
Figure

7
.

Unified framework.

Since in the Top-
k
setting we always have
w_{i}=1\leq c
,
Equation

3
effectively serves as a unified recursion for Problems (
1
) and (
2
). The Top-
k
case is then simply a specific instance of the Knapsack with
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
and
C=k
, the only distinction being that the row
[0,1\colon]
of the DP table is initialized with
-\infty
rather than
0
to enforce the strict equality constraint.

Backtracking.

These recursions yield the optimal values of Problems (
1
) and (
2
). To recover the maximizing mask
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
, one can
backtrack
from state
(n,C)
, i.e., iterate from
n
down to
1
, and set
y_{i}
to
0
or
1
based on whether
V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]
was derived from a ”pick” or ”skip” transition.

As
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
corresponds to a subgradient of
{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
, this backtracking procedure is formally equivalent to a special case of backpropagation
[
7
]
. We provide a full derivation with such a differentiation viewpoint in
Section

B.1
.

Algorithmic smoothing

We now algorithmically smooth the dynamic programs defined in

{proposition}

[Characterization of equivariance]

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a convex,
separable
regularization function such that
\Omega({\bm{q}})=0
if
{\bm{q}}\in\{{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2}\}
, and let
S_{n}
be the group of permutations. We have:

\displaystyle\forall(\sigma,{\bm{w}})\in S_{n}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{n},\;\sigma\circ\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}={\bm{y}}^{C}_{\sigma({\bm{w}}),\Omega}\circ\sigma

\displaystyle\textbf{if and only if}\quad\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}\quad\text{for some}\;\gamma\geq 0.

In the Top-
k
case, since
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
, only item values are really permuted, giving a more compact formulation:

\displaystyle\forall\sigma\in S_{n},\;\sigma\circ\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}=\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}\circ\sigma\iff\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}.

The proof, given in
Section

A.2
, uses the equivalence between the permutation-invariance of a function and the permutation-equivariance of its gradient, and the fact that only
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
yields an
associative
operator
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}
.

We now characterize the regularizers that enable the relaxed operators
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}
to map to vertices
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
of the moment polytope
{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
, i.e., to produce sparse item selections.

{proposition}

[Characterization of sparsity]

Let
\Omega({\bm{q}})\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{2}\omega(q_{i})
be a separable, strictly convex regularization function, with
\omega
differentiable on
(0,1)
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The derivative of
\omega
is bounded on
(0,1)
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\omega^{\prime}(t)>-\infty\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to 1^{-}}\omega^{\prime}(t)<+\infty.

(2)

\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}
can produce sparse item selections, i.e.:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}\subseteq\mathsf{range}(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}).

Furthermore, if (1) and (2) hold, define the threshold
\tau_{\Omega}\triangleq\omega^{\prime}(1^{-})-\omega^{\prime}(0^{+})
, and the local advantage of skipping item
i\!\in\![n]
at capacity
c\in\{w_{i},\dots,C\}
as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{i,c}\triangleq V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]-(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]).

We have:

\displaystyle\forall c\in\{w_{i},\dots,C\},\;\Delta_{i,c}\geq\tau_{\Omega}\implies(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))_{i}=0\,.

The proof is given in
Section

A.3
. For Gini regularization

The proposed differentiable operators can be used as hidden or output layers in any end-to-end differentiable pipeline. We denote by

{\bm{\theta}}=f_{W_{1}}({\bm{x}})

W_{1}

f_{W_{2}}

In

5.1
Deterministic forward pass and VJPs

In the hidden layer case, the most direct approach is to use the relaxed operator

{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
during training, directly feeding its output to the downstream function
f_{W_{2}}

To learn the upstream parameters
W_{1}

Since

\nabla^{2}\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
, which is symmetric. Thus, for any cotangent vector

is the transpose of the JVP
(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})){\bm{z}}
.

We compute it using a
reverse-over-forward
approach, by backpropagating through the computation of the directional derivative
\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{z}}\rangle
, which we derive in
Section

B.3
. The special structure of our DAG, which has repeated edges and constant ones, leads to important changes compared to the general case of

(see
Section

D.3
). The pseudo-code is given in
Algorithm

, with derivations in

5.2
Stochastic forward pass and surrogate gradients

The method described in
Section

5.1
is susceptible to a train-test mismatch. Although the network is trained on continuous vectors
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
, inference often requires hard item selections
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
. This is particularly true for semantically discrete tasks where passing a soft, averaged vector
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
to
f_{W_{2}}
would be nonsensical. We propose to bridge this gap via sampling.

{proposition}

[Underlying distribution]

Let
\bm{Q}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}
be the output of
Algorithm

1
. Let
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
be the distribution on binary vectors defined autoregressively by:

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(Y_{i}=1|Y_{i+1},\dots,Y_{n})\triangleq Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}\left[i,\,C_{i}\right]

for all
i\in\{n,\dots,1\}
, where
C_{i}\triangleq C-\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}Y_{j}w_{j}
is the remaining capacity for items
\{1,\dots,i\}
.

Then, the support of
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
is a subset of
{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}\,
, and its expectation matches the relaxed operator:

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}}[Y]\in{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}).

Moreover, in the scaled Shannon entropy-regularized case
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
, we recover the Gibbs distribution:

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}({\bm{y}})=\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}.

Section

5.2
, proved in
Section

A.4
, shows the existence of a distribution
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
underlying the proposed operators, and yields a tractable
{\mathcal{O}}(n)
ancestral sampling algorithm to sample from it. We give the pseudo-code in
Algorithm

3
.

Moreover, the structure of
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
enables to efficiently compute the probability of any item selection
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
as:

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}({\bm{y}})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c_{i}]+(1-y_{i})(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c_{i}])\right),

where
c_{i}\triangleq C-\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}y_{j}w_{j}
is the remaining capacity for items
\{1,\dots,i\}
.
This is very useful as it enables assessing the probability of a given Top-
k
or Knapsack assignment.

To enforce hard item selections during training, we therefore propose to replace the relaxed output
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
with a discrete sample
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
from
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
in the forward pass.
To bypass the non-differentiability of the sampling operation, we adopt the following surrogate gradient approach:

(1)

During the forward pass, we treat the layer as a stochastic node by sampling
{\bm{y}}\sim\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
using
Algorithm

3
. We can then compute any loss
{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},f_{W_{2}}({\bm{y}}))
.

(2)

During the backward pass, we treat the layer as the deterministic node
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}}[Y]
, with VJP computed by
Algorithm

4
. Thus, we use:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},f_{W_{2}}({\bm{y}}))\!\triangleq\!\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\right)\!\cdot\!\nabla_{\bm{y}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},f_{W_{2}}({\bm{y}})),

and then backpropagate
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{x}},f_{W_{2}}({\bm{y}}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as a cotangent to upstream learnable parameters
W_{1}
.

A similar ”stochastic forward, deterministic backward” approach is also proposed in
1
, although restricted to the Top-
k
setting and to the use of Shannon entropy-based regularization only (thus preventing sparsity of expected item selections and distribution supports).

5.3
Output layers and Fenchel-Young losses

We now provide a principled framework for supervised learning with the proposed operators as output layers.

Moment polytope regularization.

For any
\Omega:\triangle^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
used to smooth the DP recursion (
3
) into
Equation

4
, define the regularization function
{\Omega^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
as the Fenchel conjugate of
\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}
with domain
{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
. The corresponding
Fenchel-Young loss

[
6
]
is given by:

\displaystyle L_{{\Omega^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\!:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\!\times\!{\mathsf{conv}}({\Omega^{C}_{\bm{w}}})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})+{\Omega^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{y}})\!-\!\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle.

This loss has several desirable properties
[
6
, Proposition 2]
:

(1)

It is convex in
{\bm{\theta}}
for all
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
,

(2)

It is differentiable with gradient given by
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{{\Omega^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{y}})=\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})-{\bm{y}}
,

(3)

It is non-negative for all
({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
, and equals
0
if and only if
{\bm{\theta}}
is such that
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{y}}
.

Thus, computing gradients reduces to computing a forward pass of the relaxed layer
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
using
Algorithm

2
, and backpropagating the difference between its output and the ground-truth item selection
{\bm{y}}
.

6.2
Dynamic assortment with Knapsack constraints

We evaluate our differentiable Knapsack operators on a dynamic assortment problem
[
37

9

. The agent manages a store of
n=20

Method.

We employ the structured RL (SRL) framework of
14
, where the actor maps states to item logits
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}
. While the original method relies on the perturbation framework of
4

. Furthermore, we substitute perturbed optimization with ancestral sampling using

3
to generate targets
\hat{{\bm{y}}}
.

Baselines.

We compare our method against standard perturbation-based SRL and PPO
[
35
]
. We also include a

Fenchel-Young discrete VAE

We now evaluate our relaxed Top-
k
operators within a discrete variational auto-encoder (DVAE,
32

E_{W_{1}}
maps an input
{\bm{x}}
to latent style parameters

and selection logits
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

The discrete latent selection is obtained via either

5.1
and

{\bm{e}}_{i}

{\bm{z}}_{i}

{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{x}}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\cdot D_{W_{2}}([{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{z}}_{i}]).

To train this model, we minimize the sum of a standard MSE reconstruction loss on

{\bm{z}}

{\bm{y}}

Conclusion

We proposed a unified DP framework for differentiable Knapsack and Top-
k
operators, and proved that the regularization choice is structural for key properties such as permutation equivariance and sparsity. Our operators offer an efficient solution for enforcing combinatorial constraints in differentiable pipelines, whether as deterministic or stochastic hidden layers, or as output layers for supervised learning.

Impact statement

This paper introduces differentiable Knapsack and Top-
k
operators via dynamic programming.
We do not foresee any specific ethical or societal implications arising directly from this work.

\displaystyle=\left\{\sigma({\bm{y}}^{\prime})\mid{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n},\;\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle\leq C\right\}

(since
\sigma^{-1}(\{0,1\}^{n})=\{0,1\}^{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sigma({\bm{y}}^{\prime})\mid{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}\right\},

where we denote
\sigma^{-1}({\bm{y}})\coloneqq{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
for clarity.

Thus, we have in fact:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{C}_{\sigma({\bm{w}}),\Omega}\left(\sigma({\bm{\theta}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\sigma({\bm{w}})}}\frac{\exp\left(\langle\sigma({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma\right)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\sigma({\bm{w}})}}\exp\left(\langle\sigma({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma\right)}\cdot{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\frac{\exp\left(\langle\sigma({\bm{\theta}}),\sigma({\bm{y}}^{\prime})\rangle/\gamma\right)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\exp\left(\langle\sigma({\bm{\theta}}),\sigma({\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime})\rangle/\gamma\right)}\cdot\sigma({\bm{y}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\frac{\exp\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma\right)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\exp\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\rangle/\gamma\right)}\cdot\sigma({\bm{y}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\frac{\exp\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma\right)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}}\exp\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\rangle/\gamma\right)}\cdot{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\right).

We now show that Shannon’s negative entropy is the only choice of a convex, separable regularizer yielding permutation-equivariant relaxed operators.

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a convex regularization function. We assume it is separable, i.e., such that
\Omega({\bm{q}})=\omega(q_{1})+\omega(q_{2})
for some lower semi-continuous convex function
\omega:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, we assume that
\Omega({\bm{q}})=0
for
{\bm{q}}\in\{{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2}\}
(i.e.,
\omega(0)+\omega(1)=0
), and that
\Omega
yields the permutation-equivariance property for the relaxed layers, i.e., that we have:

\displaystyle\forall(\sigma,{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{w}})\in S_{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{n},\quad{\bm{y}}^{C}_{\sigma({\bm{w}}),\Omega}(\sigma({\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle=\sigma\left({\bm{y}}^{C}_{{\bm{w}},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\right),

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}(\sigma({\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle=\sigma\left({\bm{y}}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\right).

We must show that we then necessarily have
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
for some
\gamma\geq 0

must necessarily yield an

\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}

More precisely, we will assume that
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}
is not associative for the sake of contradiction, and show that the permutation-equivariance property cannot be verified.

To do so,

and
A.2
will prove useful.

{lemma}

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be convex, with
\Omega({\bm{q}})=\omega(q_{1})+\omega(q_{2})
, where
\omega:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is lower semi-continuous, and such that
\Omega({\bm{q}})=0
for
{\bm{q}}\in\{{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2}\}
. We have, for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{c\to+\infty}\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(x+c,0)-c=x\,.

{lemma}

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be any differentiable function. Then, we have:

\displaystyle f\circ\sigma=f\quad\text{for all $\sigma\in S_{n}$}\quad\iff\quad\nabla f\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ\nabla f\quad\text{for all $\sigma\in S_{n}$\,.}

That is, a differentiable function is permutation-invariant if and only if its gradient is permutation-equivariant.

First, we prove the equivalence between the boundedness of

onto the vertices
{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}

Assume
\omega^{\prime}
is bounded at the endpoints. We show that
{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}\subseteq\mathsf{range}(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega})
.

As shown in
6
, since
\omega^{\prime}
is bounded, the local gradient mapping
\nabla\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}
has a finite saturation threshold. Specifically, let
\tau_{\Omega}\triangleq\lim_{t\to 1^{-}}\{\omega^{\prime}(t)\}-\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\{\omega^{\prime}(t)\}<\infty
. For any inputs
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
, if the gap
|a-b|\geq\tau_{\Omega}
, the regularized maximizer saturates to a hard decision:

\displaystyle a-b\geq\tau_{\Omega}\implies(\nabla\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b))_{1}=1,\quad b-a\geq\tau_{\Omega}\implies(\nabla\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b))_{1}=0.

(5)

We now establish a bound on the difference between the values of the smoothed and hard DP tables.

{lemma}

Let
M_{\Omega}\triangleq\sup_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{2}}|\Omega({\bm{q}})|<\infty
. For any
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
i\in\{0,\dots,n\},\;c\in\{0,\dots,C\}
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lvert V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]({\bm{\theta}})-V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]({\bm{\theta}})\right\rvert\leq iM_{\Omega}.

We prove
Section

A.3
by induction in
Section

A.7
.

Now let
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
be an arbitrary feasible item selection. As
{\bm{y}}
is a vertex of the polytope
{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
, there exists a score vector
{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}=\argmax_{{\bm{\mu}}\in{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})}\langle{\bm{\mu}},{\bm{s}}\rangle={\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{s}}).

We define the set of active states traversed by the optimal path corresponding to
{\bm{y}}
in the DP table. Let
c_{n}\triangleq C
. For
i
from
n
down to
1
, we define the sequence of capacities
c_{i-1}\triangleq c_{i}-y_{i}w_{i}
. The relevant decision for item
i
occurs at state
(i,c_{i})
.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, consider the hard local advantage of skipping item
i
at the active state (we make the dependency of the DP tables on the input scores explicit for clarity):

\displaystyle\Delta^{\text{hard}}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})\triangleq V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c_{i}]({\bm{s}})-(s_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c_{i}-w_{i}]({\bm{s}})).

Since
{\bm{y}}
is the unique global maximizer, the hard decision at every step of the optimal path must be strict.
If
y_{i}=0
(skip is optimal), then
V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c_{i}]({\bm{s}})>s_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c_{i}-w_{i}]({\bm{s}})
, so
\Delta^{\text{hard}}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})>0
.
If
y_{i}=1
(pick is optimal), then
\Delta^{\text{hard}}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})<0
.

Define
\Delta^{\star}
as the minimum absolute value of these hard local skipping advantages along the path:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\star}\triangleq\min_{i}|\Delta^{\text{hard}}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})|>0.

Now, let
\lambda>0
and consider the scaled score
\lambda{\bm{s}}
.
First, notice that since
{\mathcal{N}}_{\bm{y}}\triangleq\left\{{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\argmax_{{\bm{\mu}}\in{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})}\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\mu}}\rangle={\bm{y}}\right\}
is a cone (it is the normal cone to the polytope
{\mathsf{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}})
at extreme point
{\bm{y}}
) and we have
{\bm{s}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\bm{y}}
by definition, we also have
\lambda{\bm{s}}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\bm{y}}
. Therefore, we have
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}(\lambda{\bm{s}})={\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{s}})={\bm{y}}
.

Moreover, the hard DP value tables coincide up to scaling, so that
\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot V^{\bm{w}}[\colon,\colon](\lambda{\bm{s}})=V^{\bm{w}}[\colon,\colon]({\bm{s}})
. One can simply see this from the corresponding sub-problems:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{i}}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}\lambda s_{l}y_{l}\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}w_{l}y_{l}\leq c

\displaystyle=\lambda\cdot\left(\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{i}}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}s_{l}y_{l}\quad\text{s.t.}\;\sum_{l=1}^{i}w_{l}y_{l}\leq c\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]({\bm{s}}).

Define now the smoothed advantage:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\Omega}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})

\displaystyle=V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c_{i}]({\bm{s}})-(s_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c_{i}-w_{i}]({\bm{s}})).

We have:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})-V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]({\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})-\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\bm{w}}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lvert V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})-V^{\bm{w}}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{iM_{\Omega}}{\lambda}

by
Section

A.3
.

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c](\lambda{\bm{s}})=V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]({\bm{s}}).

Using the triangle inequality and
Section

A.3
again, we can further bound the deviation from the hard advantage:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta^{\Omega}_{i,c_{i}}(\lambda{\bm{s}})-\Delta^{\text{hard}}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c_{i}](\lambda{\bm{s}})-V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c_{i}]({\bm{s}})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c_{i}-w_{i}](\lambda{\bm{s}})-V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c_{i}-w_{i}]({\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}(i-1)M_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{\lambda}(i-1)M_{\Omega}

Specifically, we need:

\displaystyle|\Delta^{\Omega}_{i,c_{i}}(\lambda^{\star}{\bm{s}})|

\displaystyle\geq\tau_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\iff\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda^{\star}}\Delta^{\Omega}_{i,c_{i}}(\lambda^{\star}{\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau_{\Omega}}{\lambda^{\star}}.

Using the reverse triangle inequality on

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta^{\Omega}_{i,c_{i}}(\lambda{\bm{s}})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\Delta^{\star}-\frac{2nM_{\Omega}}{\lambda}\geq\frac{\tau_{\Omega}}{\lambda}.

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{\lambda}\Delta^{\Omega}_{i,c_{i}}(\lambda{\bm{s}})\right\rvert>\Delta^{\star}-\frac{2nM_{\Omega}}{\lambda}\geq\Delta^{\star}-\frac{2nM_{\Omega}}{\lambda^{\star}}=\frac{\tau_{\Omega}}{\lambda^{\star}}\geq\frac{\tau_{\Omega}}{\lambda},

Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\frac{2nM_{\Omega}}{\lambda}\leq\frac{2nM_{\Omega}}{\lambda^{\star}}=\frac{2nM_{\Omega}}{2nM_{\Omega}+\tau_{\Omega}}\Delta^{\star}<\Delta^{\star}\leq|\Delta^{\text{hard}}_{i,c_{i}}({\bm{s}})|\;.

Plugging this into
Section

For a specific item

The global output component is obtained via the aggregation formula
(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))_{i}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

By definition of
\bm{Q}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\left(\begin{cases}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]&\text{if $w_{i}>c$,}\\[5.0pt]
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}\bigl(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]\;V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]\bigr)&\text{else,}\end{cases}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $w_{i}>c$,}\\[5.0pt]
\left(\nabla\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}\left(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}],\;V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]\right)\right)_{1}&\text{else,}\end{cases}

The local advantage of skipping is defined as
\Delta_{i,c}\triangleq b-a
. Thus, applying the saturation property established in
Equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{i,c}\geq\tau_{\Omega}\implies b-a\geq\tau_{\Omega}\implies Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=0.

Since the marginal probabilities

and local gates

Consequently,
(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))_{i}=0

Support of the distribution.

We wish to show that for any
{\bm{y}}
sampled from
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}

{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
(i.e.,
\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle\leq C

The sampling procedure in
Algorithm

3
samples
y_{i}
sequentially from
i=n
down to
1
. Note that the remaining capacity
C_{i}
available for items
\{1,\dots,i\}
is initialized at
C_{n}=C
and updated as
C_{i-1}=C_{i}-y_{i}w_{i}

Consider the step for item
i
with current remaining capacity
C_{i}

If

, the forward DP recursion in
Equation

4

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,C_{i}]=V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,C_{i}].

Consequently, the derivative with respect to
\theta_{i}
is zero:

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,C_{i}]=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,C_{i}]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=0.

By definition of the distribution
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
, the probability of selecting item

\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i}=1\mid C_{i})=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,C_{i}]

This ensures that the capacity constraint is never violated at any step. Thus,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}w_{i}\leq C
, and the support of
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
is a subset of
{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}
.

Expectation as the relaxed operator.

We now show that
{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}}[Y]=\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
. Since
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
, and recalling from
Section

B.2
that the gradient is computed as:

\displaystyle(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))_{i}=(\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))_{i}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c],

we must show that the marginal probability of selecting item

under
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
, denoted

, is equal to
\sum_{c=1}^{C}E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
.

Let
C_{i}
be the random variable representing the remaining capacity for items
\{1\dots i\}
during the ancestral sampling process. The probability of selecting item

is:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i}=1)=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i}=1\mid C_{i}=c)\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i}=c).

By definition of the sampling distribution,
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i}=1\mid C_{i}=c)=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
. Thus, it suffices to show that
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i}=c)=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
holds for all

.

We proceed by induction on
i
, moving backwards from
n
to
1
.

•

Base case (
i=n
):
The sampling always starts with capacity
C
. Thus
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{n}=C)=1
and
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{n}=c)=0
for
c\neq C
.
Matching this, the backward pass in
Algorithm

2
initializes
E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]=1
and
E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,c]=0
otherwise. The base case holds.

•

Inductive step (
i<n
):
Assume
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i+1}=c)=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]
for all
c
.
We express the probability of the capacity
C_{i}
at step
i
. The capacity
c
at step
i
can be reached from step
i+1
in two ways:

(1)

We had capacity
c
at
i+1
and chose
y_{i+1}=0
(skip).

(2)

We had capacity
c+w_{i+1}
at
i+1
and chose
y_{i+1}=1
(pick).

Formally:

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i}=c)=\;

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i+1}=c)\times\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i+1}=0\mid C_{i+1}=c)

\displaystyle+\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i+1}=c+w_{i+1})\times\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i+1}=1\mid C_{i+1}=c+w_{i+1}).

Plugging the definition of the sampling probabilities as
\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{k}=1|C_{k}=\cdot)\triangleq Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[k,\cdot]

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(C_{i}=c)=\;

\displaystyle E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c](1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}].

This is exactly the backward recursion derived for

in

Thus,

represents the marginal probability that the remaining capacity is

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}}[Y_{i}]=\mu_{i}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=(\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))_{i}\,,

which finally gives:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}}[Y]=\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}).

Recovery of the Gibbs distribution.

Let
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
. The smoothed maximum becomes the

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)=\gamma\log(\exp(a/\gamma)+\exp(b/\gamma)).

The forward recursion becomes:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=\gamma\log\left(\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]/\gamma)+\exp((\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}])/\gamma)\right).

The local probability

becomes the standard softmax probability:

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp((\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}])/\gamma)}{\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]/\gamma)},

\displaystyle 1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]/\gamma)}{\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]/\gamma)}.

Now consider the probability of a full sampled vector

be the sequence of remaining capacities realized during sampling (with

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=n}^{1}\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}(y_{i}\mid c_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=n}^{1}\frac{\exp\left((y_{i}\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c_{i-1}])/\gamma\right)}{\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c_{i}]/\gamma)}.

Note that the term

\displaystyle\pi^{\bm{w},C}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[0,c_{0}]/\gamma)}{\exp(V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]/\gamma)}\prod_{i=n}^{1}\exp(y_{i}\theta_{i}/\gamma)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\theta_{i}}{\gamma}\right)=\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma).

Proof.

Let
x,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(x+c,0)-c

\displaystyle=\max_{p\in[0,1]}\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}x+c\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}p\\
1-p\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle-\Omega\left(\begin{pmatrix}p\\
1-p\end{pmatrix}\right)-c

\displaystyle=\max_{p\in[0,1]}(x+c)p-\omega(p)-\omega(1-p)-c

\displaystyle=\max_{p\in[0,1]}xp+c(p-1)-\omega(p)-\omega(1-p).

Define:

\displaystyle h_{c}(p)\triangleq xp+c(p-1)-\omega(p)-\omega(1-p).

Since
\omega
is lower semi-continuous,
p\mapsto\omega(p)+\omega(1-p)
also is lower semi-continuous. Thus,
h_{c}
is upper semi-continuous, as the difference between a continuous function and a lower semi-continuous one. The quantity
\max_{p\in[0,1]}h_{c}(p)
is thus well-defined by Weierstrass’ extreme value theorem, and
h_{c}
attains its maximum on
[0,1]
. We are computing
\ell\triangleq\lim_{c\to+\infty}\max_{p\in[0,1]}h_{c}(p)
.

Lower bound.
By definition of the maximum, we have:

\displaystyle\max_{p\in[0,1]}h_{c}(p)

\displaystyle\geq h_{c}(1)

\displaystyle=x-\omega(1)-\omega(0)

\displaystyle=x-\Omega({\bm{e}}_{1})

\displaystyle=x.

This gives us a lower bound
\ell\geq x
for the limit.

Upper bound.
We now show that
\limsup_{c\to+\infty}\max_{p\in[0,1]}h_{c}(p)\leq x
. For any
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let
p^{\star}_{c}\in\argmax_{{p\in[0,1]}}h_{c}(p)
, which is well-defined by upper semi-continuity of
h_{c}
. First, we show that
p^{\star}_{c}\xrightarrow[c\to+\infty]{}1
. From the lower bound, we know
h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})\geq h_{c}(1)=x
. Substituting the definition of
h_{c}
, we have:

\displaystyle xp^{\star}_{c}+c(p^{\star}_{c}-1)-\omega(p^{\star}_{c})-\omega(1-p^{\star}_{c})\geq x

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle c(p^{\star}_{c}-1)\geq x(1-p^{\star}_{c})+\omega(p^{\star}_{c})+\omega(1-p^{\star}_{c}).

Since the function
\alpha(p)\triangleq x(1-p)+\omega(p)+\omega(1-p)
is lower semi-continuous on the compact set
[0,1]
, it is bounded below and attains it minimum. Thus, we have
c(p^{\star}_{c}-1)\geq K
, where
K\triangleq\min_{p\in[0,1]}\alpha(p)>-\infty
.

Since
p^{\star}_{c}\in[0,1]
, we have
p^{\star}_{c}-1\leq 0
, giving:

\displaystyle\frac{K}{c}\leq p^{\star}_{c}-1\leq 0,\quad\text{(for $c>0$)}

which implies
p^{\star}_{c}-1\xrightarrow[c\to+\infty]{}0
, i.e.,
\lim_{c\to+\infty}p^{\star}_{c}=1
.

We now find the limit of
h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})
. Define
\beta(p)\triangleq xp-\omega(p)-\omega(1-p)
. Since
\omega
is lower semi-continuous,
\beta
is upper semi-continuous. We can write
h_{c}(p)=\beta(p)+c(p-1)
. Since
p^{\star}_{c}\in[0,1]
, we have
c(p^{\star}_{c}-1)\leq 0
for
c\geq 0
, giving the inequality:

\displaystyle h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})=\beta(p^{\star}_{c})+c(p^{\star}_{c}-1)\leq\beta(p^{\star}_{c}).

Now we can take the
\limsup
:

\displaystyle\limsup_{c\to+\infty}h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})\leq\limsup_{c\to+\infty}\beta(p^{\star}_{c}).

By upper semi-continuity of
\beta
, since
\lim_{c\to+\infty}p^{\star}_{c}=1
, we have:

\displaystyle\limsup_{c\to+\infty}\beta(p^{\star}_{c})\leq\beta(1)=x-\omega(1)-\omega(0)=x-\Omega({\bm{e}}_{1})=x.

Since we have
\liminf_{c\to+\infty}h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})\geq x
from the lower bound and
\limsup_{c\to+\infty}h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})\leq x
, we conclude that the limit exists and is equal to
x
:

\displaystyle\ell=\lim_{c\to+\infty}\max_{p\in[0,1]}h_{c}(p)=\lim_{c\to+\infty}h_{c}(p^{\star}_{c})=x.

∎

\displaystyle f(\sigma({\bm{x}}))=f({\bm{x}}),\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Thus,
f\circ\sigma=f
for all
\sigma\in S_{n}
.
∎

A.7
Proof of
Section

A.3

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
i
.
For the base case
i=0
, we have
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[0,c]=V^{\bm{w}}[0,c]=0
, so the bound holds trivially.
Assume the bound holds for
i-1\in\{0,\dots,C-1\}
, and consider item
i
and capacity
c
.

If
w_{i}>c
,
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]
and
V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]=V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c]
. By applying the induction hypothesis to
i-1
, the error remains bounded by
(i-1)M_{\Omega}\leq iM_{\Omega}
.

If
w_{i}\leq c
, let
a=\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c-w_{i}]
and
b=V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c]
. We have:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}],V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c])

\displaystyle=\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a+\epsilon_{a},b+\epsilon_{b}),

where
|\epsilon_{a}|,|\epsilon_{b}|\leq(i-1)M_{\Omega}
by applying the induction hypothesis at
i-1
to the two terms.
Using the property
|\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}({\bm{x}})-\max({\bm{x}})|\leq M_{\Omega}
and the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle|V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]|

\displaystyle=|\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a+\epsilon_{a},b+\epsilon_{b})-\max(a,b)|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a+\epsilon_{a},b+\epsilon_{b})-\max(a+\epsilon_{a},b+\epsilon_{b})|+|\max(a+\epsilon_{a},b+\epsilon_{b})-\max(a,b)|

\displaystyle\leq M_{\Omega}+\max(|\epsilon_{a}|,|\epsilon_{b}|)

\displaystyle\leq M_{\Omega}+(i-1)M_{\Omega}=iM_{\Omega}.

∎

Appendix B
Algorithmic derivations

B.1
Backtracking as a backward pass

We now turn to the derivation of the backward pass, which enables to compute
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})
and
{\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla{\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}})
. In this unregularized case, we recover the idea of backtracking. We will write everything in the Knapsack case, as everything holds for the Top-
k
case by letting
C=k
and
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
(the slight change due to the difference in padding values of the DP table does not impact differentiation).

Let
E^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\coloneqq\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}
be the main object of interest in this derivation, and define:

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\coloneqq\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $w_{i}>c$,}\\
\mathbbm{1}\left(\left\{\max\left(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c-w_{i}]\;,\,V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c]\right)=\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}[i-1,c-w_{i}]\right\}\right)&\text{else,}\\
\end{cases}

so that the matrix
\bm{Q}^{\bm{w}}
stores a track of the maximizers in
Equation

3
during the forward pass (we have
Q^{\bm{w}}[i,c]=1\iff
item
i
is needed to reach value
V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]
). As
V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]
only directly influences
V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]
and
V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]
, we have:

\displaystyle E^{\bm{w}}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}+\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=E^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}+E^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}.

Then, from the recursion in
Equation

3
, we have:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]&\text{if $w_{i+1}$>c,}\\
\max\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}[i,c-w_{i+1}]\;,\;V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\right)&\text{else,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}V^{\bm{w}}[i,c+w_{i+1}]&\text{if $0>c$,}\\
\max\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\;,\;V^{\bm{w}}[i,c+w_{i+1}]\right)&\text{else,}\end{cases}

which further gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $w_{i+1}>c$,}\\
\mathbbm{1}\left(\left\{\max\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}[i,c-w_{i+1}]\;,\;V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\right)=V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\right\}\right)&\text{else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=1-Q^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $w_{i+1}>c$,}\\
\mathbbm{1}\left(\left\{\max\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\;,\;V^{\bm{w}}[i,c+w_{i+1}]\right)=\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\right\}\right)&\text{else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=Q^{\bm{w}}[i+1,j+1]\,.

Thus, we finally have the following backward recursion:

E^{\bm{w}}[i,c]=E^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c])+E^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times Q^{\bm{w}}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}].

The recursion is initialized with
E^{\bm{w}}[n,C]=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}=1
, and
\forall c<C,\;E^{\bm{w}}[n,c]=0
.

The hard maximizing mask
{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}
is then recovered by noting that since
\theta_{i}
only directly influences the row
V^{\bm{w}}[i,1\colon]
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1}^{C}E^{\bm{w}}[i,c]\times Q^{\bm{w}}[i,c],

giving the following compact expression for the maximizing mask:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})=(\bm{E}^{\bm{w}}[1\colon,1\colon]\circ\bm{Q}^{\bm{w}}[1\colon,1\colon])\cdot\bm{1},

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product and
\bm{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
is the all-ones vector.

B.2
Derivation of the layer

We now turn to the derivation of the backward pass to compute the relaxed layers. We will write everything in the Knapsack case, with
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
. All results also hold for the Top-
k
case, by setting
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
and
C=k
(the only slight change due to the difference in the padding values for
\bm{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}
does not impact differentiation).

We first recall here the smoothed recursion given in
Equation

4
:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=\begin{cases}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]&\text{if $w_{i}>c$,}\\
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}\left(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}],\;V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c],\right)&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Define the following objects:

\displaystyle E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]},

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}.

The matrix
\bm{Q}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}
is computed and stored during the forward pass (see
Algorithm

1
). As we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(x_{1},x_{2})\in\triangle^{2},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad\frac{\partial\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial x_{2}}=1,

One can easily check that we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]}

\displaystyle=1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c].

(8)

These identities will prove useful in the following. We can now derive the computation of
E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
. As
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
only directly influences
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]
and
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]
, we have (similarly to the unregularized case, see
Section

B.1
):

\displaystyle E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,j]}.

Moreover, using
Equations

8
and
7
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\,.

Thus, we have the following backward recursion:

E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}].

(9)

The recursion is initialized with
E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}=1
and
\forall c<C,\;E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,c]=0
. The gradient
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}
is then recovered by noting that, since
\theta_{i}
only directly influences the row
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,1\colon]
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c].

Finally, we get the following compact expression:

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=(\bm{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[1\colon,1\colon]\circ\bm{Q}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[1\colon,1\colon])\cdot\bm{1},

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product and
\bm{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
is the all-ones vector.

B.3
Derivation of the directional derivative

We now turn to the computation of the directional derivative
\langle\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}),\,{\bm{z}}\rangle=\langle\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}),\,{\bm{z}}\rangle
of the soft value in direction
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(i.e., the inner product between the layer and a cotangent vector
{\bm{z}}
). We will write everything in the Knapsack case, with
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
. All results also hold for the Top-
k
case, by setting
{\bm{w}}=\bm{1}
and
C=k
(the only slight change due to the difference in the padding values for
\bm{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}
does not impact differentiation).

Let
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a direction. We define here:

\displaystyle\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\triangleq\left\langle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}},{\bm{z}}\right\rangle.

Since
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
only directly depends on
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]
,
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]
and
\theta_{i}
, we have:

\displaystyle\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}},{\bm{z}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}+\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}+\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}\times\frac{\partial\theta_{i}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\,,\,{\bm{z}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]}\times\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]+\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]}\times\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]+\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}\times z_{i}.

Then, using
Equations

8
and
7
gives the following forward recursion:

\displaystyle\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]+z_{i}\right)\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]+\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]),

(10)

which is initialized with
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[0,\colon]=0
, and ends at
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]=\left\langle\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}},{\bm{z}}\right\rangle=\langle\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{z}}\rangle
.

B.4
Derivation of the vector-Jacobian product

Here, we derive the computation of the vector-Jacobian product
{\bm{z}}^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))
for a given cotangent vector
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. As stated in
Section

5.1
, since we have
\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}=\nabla\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}
, the Jacobian
\nabla\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
is equal to the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
, so it is in fact symmetric for all
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Thus, we can view the VJP
{\bm{z}}^{\top}(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))
as the corresponding JVP
(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})){\bm{z}}
, for any tangent/cotangent vector
{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Further, we use the fact that
(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})){\bm{z}}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\langle\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{z}}\rangle
, and focus on the differentiation of the directional derivative
\langle\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{z}}\rangle=\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]
. Thus, our goal is to compute
\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}
.

As
\theta_{i}
only directly influences the rows
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,1\colon]
and
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,1\colon]
, we have:

\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}\times{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}\times{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}.

(11)

We now turn to the derivation of every element in
Equation

11
needed to compute
\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\theta_{i}}
.

To begin with, the second term is already computed as we simply have
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
.

Now, we compute the third term
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}
. As
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
only directly influences
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]
and
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]
, we have the recursion:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}=\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}\times\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}+\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}\times\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}.

However,
Equation

10
gives:

\displaystyle\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]+z_{i+1}\right)\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]+\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]),

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]+z_{i+1}\right)\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]+\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c+w_{i+1}]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]).

(13)

Thus, as
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
only directly influences
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]
and
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]
, we get, by differentiating
Equations

12
and
13
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}].

Thus, we have in fact the following recursion:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}=\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}],

and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}
is defined by the exact same recursion as

, which is given by
Equation

9
. As they are both initialized by
\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]=1
and
\forall c<C,\;\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,c]}=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,c]=0
, the two sequences are in fact equal:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c].

Now, we derive the red term
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}
. As

does not influence
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]
,
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]
,
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]
, or

, the differentiation of the forward recursion in

gives:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]+z_{i}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]+z_{i}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}.

Finally, for the orange term, let us define:

\displaystyle\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\triangleq{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}.

As
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
only directly influences
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]
,
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]
,
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]
and
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]
, we have:

\displaystyle\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}+\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}\times\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}\times\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}+\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}\times\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}.

Thus, we need to compute the two terms
\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}
and
\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}
in order to get a recursive formulation of
\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}
.

As
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
does not influence
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}],\,\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
or
z_{i+1}
, differentiating
Equation

12
gives on the one hand:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}.

On the other hand, as
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
does not influence
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
or
\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c+w_{i+1}]
, differentiating
Equation

13
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c+w_{i+1}]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}.

Thus,
\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]={\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}
is given by the following backward recursion:

\displaystyle\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c+w_{i+1}]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}.

Thus, to summarize our results, we have (for any choice of
\Omega
):

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c+w_{i+1}]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}\,,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\,,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}

\displaystyle=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\,,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]+z_{i}\right)\times\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}\,.

Entropic setting.

In the entropy-regularized case, where
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}\left(\exp\left[\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]-V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\right)/\gamma\right]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}\exp\left[\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]-V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\right)/\gamma\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma}(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\;,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}\left(\exp\left[\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\right)/\gamma\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}{\gamma}\right)\exp\left[\left(\theta_{i+1}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\right)/\gamma\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}])Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\left(\exp\left[\left(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]-V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\right)/\gamma\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}{\gamma}\right)\exp\left[\left(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]-V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\right)/\gamma\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\right)Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\;.

So in this setting, our four terms write:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])+\dot{E}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]\times\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c-w_{i+1}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]+z_{i+1}\right)\times(-\frac{1}{\gamma})(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c])Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c]

\displaystyle+E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\times\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c+w_{i+1}]+z_{i+1}\right)\times\frac{1}{\gamma}(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}])Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i+1,c+w_{i+1}]\,,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}\frac{\partial V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\,,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[n,C]}{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}}

\displaystyle=E^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\,,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{\partial\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}]-\dot{V}^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]+z_{i}\right)\times\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(1-Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\right)Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\,.

D.3
Mapping with the Differentiable Dynamic Programming framework

In this section, we formally cast our proposed smoothed recursions as instances of the differentiable dynamic programming (DDP) framework introduced by
23
.

DDP framework.

The DDP framework considers optimization problems framed as finding a highest-scoring path on a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
. The edge scores are parameterized by
{\bm{\Theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|\times|{\mathcal{V}}|}
, where
\Theta_{u,v}
is the score of edge
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{E}}
. The value of a node
v
, denoted
V_{v}({\bm{\Theta}})
, is defined recursively by the Bellman equation:

\displaystyle V_{v}({\bm{\Theta}})\triangleq\max_{u\in{\mathcal{P}}_{v}}\left(\Theta_{u,v}+V_{u}({\bm{\Theta}})\right),

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{v}
denotes the set of parents of node
v
. The smoothed recursion is obtained by replacing the
\max
operator with
\max_{\Omega}
.

Knapsack DAG construction.

To map the Knapsack problem defined by weights
{\bm{w}}
and capacity
C
to this framework, we construct a DAG where nodes represent the states of the dynamic program.

•

Nodes (
{\mathcal{V}}
).
The set of nodes is the grid of DP states
{\mathcal{V}}=\{(i,c)\mid 0\leq i\leq n,\,0\leq c\leq C\}
. The source node is
(0,0)
and the sink node is
(n,C)
.

•

Edges (
{\mathcal{E}}
) and Scores (
{\bm{\Theta}}
).
The transitions from row
i-1
to row
i
correspond to the decision of selecting item
i
or not. For each node
(i,c)
with
i,c\geq 1
:

(1)

Skip item
i
:
An edge exists from
(i-1,c)
to
(i,c)
representing the decision
y_{i}=0
. The score is
\Theta_{(i-1,c),(i,c)}=0
.

(2)

Pick item
i
:
If
c\geq w_{i}
, an edge exists from
(i-1,c-w_{i})
to
(i,c)
representing the decision
y_{i}=1
. The score is
\Theta_{(i-1,c-w_{i}),(i,c)}=\theta_{i}
.

This local construction is illustrated in
Figure

9
.

•

Boundary conditions.
As explained in
Section

3
, boundary conditions of the form
V_{(i,0)}({\bm{\Theta}})=0
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and
V_{(0,c)}({\bm{\Theta}})=0
for all
c\in\{0,\dots,C\}
are imposed. Indeed, the first row and first column of the DP table are set to
0
, which initializes the recursion.

In order to formally cast this as an instance of DDP (where only the source node is assigned a fixed value of
0
), we add artificial edges between the source node
(0,0)
and every boundary node
(i,0)
and
(0,c)
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
c\in\{1,\dots,C\}
, with edge scores
{\bm{\Theta}}_{(0,0),(i,0)}={\bm{\Theta}}_{(0,0),(0,c)}=0
.

\displaystyle=\max\left(\underbrace{0}_{{\bm{\Theta}}_{(0,0),(i,0)}}+V_{(0,0)}({\bm{\Theta}})\right)=V_{(0,0)}({\bm{\Theta}})=0,

\displaystyle V_{(0,c)}({\bm{\Theta}})

\displaystyle=\max\left(\underbrace{0}_{{\bm{\Theta}}_{(0,0),(0,c)}}+V_{(0,0)}({\bm{\Theta}})\right)=V_{(0,0)}({\bm{\Theta}})=0.

Problem formulation.

The agent manages a store containing
n=20
items over a finite horizon of
T=80
time steps. Each item
i
is assigned a static weight
w_{i}
drawn uniformly from
[5]
, a static price
s_{i}>0
, and possesses a dynamic feature vector
{\bm{x}}^{(t,i)}
. At each time step
t
, the agent selects a subset of items (an assortment)
{\bm{y}}^{(t)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to display. This selection is subject to two operational constraints:

(1)

Knapsack constraint:
the total weight of displayed items must not exceed the store’s capacity, i.e.:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,y^{(t)}_{i}\leq C\triangleq 40.

(2)

Inventory constraint:
an item
i
may only be displayed if it remains in stock (
I^{(t)}_{i}\geq 1
). All items are initialized with a uniform inventory of
I^{(0)}_{i}\triangleq I^{(0)}=5
.

In our precise setting, there are exactly
|{\mathcal{Y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}|=989034
feasible assortments when every item is in stock.

Customer choice model.

Customer behavior is simulated using a multinomial logit model. Given an assortment
{\bm{y}}^{(t)}
, the customer purchases item
i\in\{j\in[n]\mid y^{(t)}_{j}=1\}
or selects the ”no-purchase” option (
i=0
) according to the following probabilities:

P(i|{\bm{y}}^{(t)})=\frac{\exp(u^{(t)}_{i})}{1+\sum_{j\in S_{t}}\exp(u^{(t)}_{j})},

(14)

where
u^{(t)}_{i}=\langle{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{x}}^{(t,i)}\rangle
represents the utility of item
i
derived from a hidden linear customer model
{\bm{\phi}}
.

Endogenous uncertainty.

The environment features strong endogeneity in both feature evolution and state transitions. First, the inventory evolves deterministically:
I^{(t+1)}_{i}=I^{(t)}_{i}-1
if item
i
is purchased, creating a coupling between immediate rewards and future availability. Second, item features evolve based on customer decisions. Specifically, if item
i
is purchased, its ”satisfaction” feature (part of
{\bm{x}}^{(t,i)}
) increases by
1\%
. Furthermore, a ”hype” feature evolves based on a rolling window of the last 5 purchases: the most recently purchased item receives a hype boost of
+2\times 10^{-2}
, while items purchased
2
to
5
steps ago suffer a decay of
-5\times 10^{-3}
. Finally, a time feature increments linearly by
9/T
at each step.

State space.

The state
s_{t}
provided to the agent is a concatenation of static and dynamic information resulting in a feature dimension of
12\times n
. Specifically, the 12-dimensional vector
{\bm{x}}^{(t,i)}
for item
i
is constructed as follows: indices
x^{(t,i)}_{0},x^{(t,i)}_{1}
contain static latent attributes;
x^{(t,i)}_{2}
is the dynamic hype;
x^{(t,i)}_{3}
is the dynamic satisfaction;
x^{(t,i)}_{4}
is the static price
\theta_{i}
; and
x^{(t,i)}_{5}
is the current time step. These base features are augmented by their dynamics:
x^{(t,i)}_{6},x^{(t,i)}_{7}
contain the one-step difference in hype and satisfaction, while
x^{(t,i)}_{8},x^{(t,i)}_{9}
contain their cumulative change since
t=0
. Finally,
x^{(t,i)}_{10}
contains the normalized inventory
I^{(t)}_{i}/I^{(0)}
and
x^{(t,i)}_{11}
contains the normalized weight
w_{i}/C
. The reward
r_{t}
is defined as the price
s_{i}
of the purchased item
i
, or
0
if no purchase occurs. The objective is to maximize cumulative revenue
\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
.

Greedy policy.

As a simple baseline, we include a greedy actor, which always displays available items that maximize the total price:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{(t)}_{\text{greedy}}\triangleq

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}y_{i}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}y_{i}\leq C,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\forall i\in[n]\mid y_{i}=1,\;I^{(t)}_{i}\geq 1.

This is effectively implemented using a regular Knapsack operator, by using an artificial weights vector
\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}
such that
\hat{w}^{(t)}_{i}=w_{i}
if
I_{i}^{(t)}\geq 1
, and
\hat{w}^{(t)}_{i}=C+1
if
I_{i}^{(t)}=0
. We then have
{\bm{y}}^{(t)}_{\text{greedy}}={\bm{y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}({\bm{s}})
.

Expert policy.

Finding the optimal policy is computationally intractable. We define a myopic expert policy baseline as follows at each time step
t
, the expert computes the assortment
{\bm{y}}^{(t)}
that maximizes the expected immediate revenue, subject to current constraints:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{(t)}_{\text{expert}}\triangleq

\displaystyle\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sum_{i\in[n]\mid y_{i}=1}s_{i}\cdot P(i|{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}y_{i}\leq C,

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\forall i\in[n]\mid y_{i}=1,\;I^{(t)}_{i}\geq 1.

To compute
{\bm{y}}^{(t)}_{\text{expert}}
, we enumerate every feasible item selection (there are
989034
of them in our configuration), and compute the corresponding purchase probabilities. Naturally, this baseline requires access to hidden information (the customer model
{\bm{\phi}}
defining purchase probabilities
P(i|{\bm{y}})
), and is computationally costly.

SRL actors.

The actors in the SRL-based methods are paramterized by a feed-forward neural network
f_{W_{1}}
mapping states
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{12\times n}
to item logits
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{}^{n}}
, which are then mapped to feasible assortments by the hard Knapsack operator
{\bm{y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}})
, where
\hat{w}^{(t)}_{i}=w_{i}
if
I_{i}^{(t)}\geq 1
, and
\hat{w}^{(t)}_{i}=C+1
if
I_{i}^{(t)}=0
(effectively enforcing the inventory constraint).

Exploration and gradient computation.

In standard SRL
[
14
]
,
K
exploration targets are generated by perturbing the predicted logits
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}
with a random variable
Z
(which follows, e.g., a centered Gaussian distribution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
) to get samples:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\triangleq{\bm{y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}+\varepsilon Z^{(k)})\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}\;,

where
\varepsilon>0
is a hyperparameter and the
Z^{(k)}
are i.i.d. samples. Then, the target
\hat{{\bm{y}}}
is defined by aggregating these samples via a softmax operation using Q-values predicted by a critic network
\psi_{W_{2}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\psi_{W_{2}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})}{\tau}\right)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\exp\left(\frac{\psi_{W_{2}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{(k^{\prime})})}{\tau}\right)}\cdot{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathsf{conv}}\left({\mathcal{Y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}\right).

Finally, a perturbation-based Fenchel-Young loss
[
4
]
is used to compute gradients with respect to
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}
, which are then backpropagated to upstream weights
W_{1}
. This gradient is estimated via Monte-Carlo as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\varepsilon}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}\,;\hat{{\bm{y}}})\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\bm{y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}+\varepsilon Z^{(m)})-\hat{{\bm{y}}},

where the
Z^{(m)}
are again i.i.d. samples.

What we propose for our DP-based SRL agents instead, is to use
Algorithm

3
to produce
K
exploration actions:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\sim\pi^{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)},C}_{\hat{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}\;,

and to use our DP-based Fenchel-Young losses to compute exact gradients

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}}(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}\,;\hat{{\bm{y}}})={\bm{y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)},\Omega}-\hat{{\bm{y}}},

using
Algorithm

2
. Importantly, we only need to call
Algorithm

1
once to produce intermediate outputs for
Algorithms

2
and
3
, yielding computational gains.

PPO Baseline.

We adapt proximal policy optimization (PPO)
[
35
]
to the combinatorial setting following the COaML-pipeline framework detailed in
14
. In this formulation, the PPO agent does not directly output discrete actions. Instead, the actor network
f_{W_{1}}
predicts a latent score vector
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which parameterizes a multivariate Gaussian policy
\pi_{\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}}({\bm{\eta}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{\eta}}\mid\hat{{\bm{\theta}}},\sigma^{2}{\bm{I}})
. During the rollout, a continuous score vector
{\bm{\eta}}
is sampled from this distribution and passed to the hard Knapsack solver to produce the discrete item selection
{\bm{y}}^{(t)}={\bm{y}}^{C}_{\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{(t)}}({\bm{\eta}})
. From the perspective of the PPO algorithm, the actor’s action is the sampled continuous vector
{\bm{\eta}}
, the combinatorial solver is treated as a deterministic transition function within the environment dynamics, and the optimization objective is to adjust the mean
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}
of the score distribution to maximize returns. The policy ratio and clipping are computed on the continuous densities of the perturbed scores
{\bm{\eta}}
. We anneal the exploration standard deviation
\sigma
linearly during training.

Hyperparameters.

We train all agents for
10^{4}
episodes, performing
10
update iterations with a batch size of
32
at the end of each episode for SRL, and
100
iterations for PPO to compensate for its on-policy data efficiency. We maintain a replay buffer with a capacity of
10^{4}
transitions and use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
8\times 10^{-5}
for all networks. For the SRL agents, we set the perturbation noise scale
\varepsilon
and the regularization strength
\gamma
to the same value, annealing it linearly from
5.0
to
1.0
over the course of training. We use
K=32
exploration samples for target action creation, and the softmax aggregation temperature
\tau
is annealed linearly from
1.0
to
0.5
. For PPO, we use a clipping ratio of
\epsilon=0.2
, and the exploration standard deviation
\sigma
is annealed linearly from
2.0
to
1.0
. All experiments were executed on the CPU of an Apple M1 Max processor with 64 GB of RAM.

Derivation of the FY-DVAE Objective.

Standard DVAEs maximize the ELBO:

\displaystyle\log p_{W_{2}}({\bm{x}})\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim q_{W_{1}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}})}[\log p_{W_{2}}({\bm{x}}\mid Y)]-D_{\text{KL}}(q_{W_{1}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}})\mid\mid p(\cdot)).

In the standard setting DVAE setting of, e.g.,
1
, where the approximate posterior is parameterized as a Gibbs distribution
q_{W_{1}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})=\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{y}})\propto\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)
with
{\bm{\theta}}=E_{W_{1}}({\bm{x}})
, and the prior
p
is the uniform distribution on
k
-subsets
p\propto 0
, the KL divergence regularization term writes:

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\bm{\theta}}\mid\mid p)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\pi_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}})\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}})}{p({\bm{y}})}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\pi_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}})\log\left(\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}|}\right)+\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\pi_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}})\log\left(\pi_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}})\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(|{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}|\right)+\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle)}\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle-\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle)\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(|{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}|\right)-\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle)+\left\langle\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle)}{\bm{y}},\;{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=A(\bm{0})-A({\bm{\theta}})+\langle\nabla A({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\theta}}\rangle,

where
A({\bm{\theta}})=\log\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)
is the cumulant function of
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
. We recognize this expression as a Fenchel-Young loss generated by the convex conjugate of the cumulant,
A^{*}
. Indeed, we have:

\displaystyle L_{A^{*}}(\bm{0}\,;\nabla A({\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle\triangleq A^{**}(\bm{0})+A^{*}(\nabla A({\bm{\theta}}))-\langle\bm{0},\nabla A({\bm{\theta}})\rangle\quad\text{\cite[citep]{[\@@bibref{AuthorsPhrase1Year}{blondel_learning_2020}{\@@citephrase{, }}{}]}}

\displaystyle=A(\bm{0})+(\langle{\bm{\theta}},\nabla A({\bm{\theta}})\rangle-A({\bm{\theta}}))\quad\text{($A^{**}=A$ and Fenchel's equality)}

\displaystyle=A(\bm{0})-A({\bm{\theta}})+\langle{\bm{\theta}},\nabla A({\bm{\theta}})\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\bm{\theta}}\mid\mid p).

Here, we used the fact that
A
is convex and lower semi-continuous to apply the Fenchel-Moreau biconjugation theorem, and Fenchel’s equality
A^{*}(\nabla A({\bm{\theta}}))+A({\bm{\theta}})=\langle{\bm{\theta}},\nabla A({\bm{\theta}})\rangle
.

We generalize this to our smoothed DP setting by using a generalized approximate posterior
q_{W_{1}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})=\pi^{\bm{1},k}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}({\bm{y}})
, and by replacing
A^{*}
with the generalized moment polytope regularization function
{\Omega^{k}_{\bm{1}}}
, defined in
Section

5.3
as the conjugate of the smoothed value
\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}
. The resulting regularization term is the Fenchel-Young loss between the uniform prior parameters
\bm{0}
and the relaxed item selection
\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
:

\displaystyle L_{{\Omega^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}(\bm{0}\,;\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))

\displaystyle\triangleq({\Omega^{k}_{\bm{1}}})^{*}(\bm{0})+{\Omega^{k}_{\bm{1}}}(\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))-\langle\bm{0},\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\rangle

\displaystyle=(\langle{\bm{\theta}},\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\rangle-\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))+\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}(\bm{0})-\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})+\langle{\bm{\theta}},\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\rangle.

By construction, as
\Omega=-H^{s}
yields
\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}=A
and
\pi^{\bm{1},k}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}=\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
(see
Sections

G.1
and
5.2
), this formulation recovers the standard KL divergence objective used in
1
as a special case, while enabling the use of regularizers that incentivize sparse latent representations (e.g., Gini or
1.5
-Tsallis). This framework also extends the Fenchel-Young variational inference framework of
36
, which introduced Fenchel-Young regularization for learning continuous VAEs, to the discrete setting of latent distributions on
k
-subsets. Moreover, the gradient of this Fenchel-Young regularization term is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{{\Omega^{k}_{\bm{1}}}}(\bm{0}\,;\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))=(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}))\cdot{\bm{\theta}},

that is, it is simply the JVP of the relaxed layer
\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}
in the direction of its input
{\bm{\theta}}
, efficiently computed via
Algorithm

4
.

Dataset Generation.

We construct each data point in our Stacked MNIST dataset by sampling
k=3
distinct digit classes
\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}\subset\{0,\dots,9\}
without replacement. For each class
c_{j}
, we sample a random image
{\bm{x}}^{(j)}
from the standard MNIST dataset. The input
{\bm{x}}
is the pixel-wise average:
{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\bm{x}}^{(j)}
. The ground truth is the
k
-hot vector
{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{10}
indicating the present digits. We generate
120,000
training examples and
20,000
test examples.

Architecture.

The model architecture captures both the presence and the style of the digits using a decomposed latent space with dimensions
n=10
for the discrete part and
d_{z}=32
for the continuous part.
The encoder
E_{W_{1}}
is a convolutional neural network consisting of a stack of strided convolution layers (kernel size
3\times 3
, stride
2
, ReLU activation), which progressively downsample the spatial dimensions while increasing channel depth. The resulting flattened feature map is fed into two separate dense projection heads: one outputting the selection logits
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{10}
for the discrete mask
{\bm{y}}
, and another outputting the parameters
[{\bm{\mu}},\log{\bm{\sigma}}^{2}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{10\times 2d_{z}}
for the
n=10
independent Gaussian style posteriors
{\bm{z}}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{\mu}}_{i},\text{diag}({\bm{\sigma}}_{i}^{2}))
.

The mapping from selection logits
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}
to mixing coefficients
{\bm{y}}
in the discrete bottleneck depends on the considered method:

•

for the hard Top-
k
baseline, the mapping is simply
{\bm{y}}={\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathcal{Y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}
,

•

for the Gumbel Top-
k
baseline, we sample i.i.d. Gumbel noise

G.1
Properties of the relaxed values and operators

The relaxed values and operators have the following properties:

(1)

The smoothed output values
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
and
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
are convex in
{\bm{\theta}}
.

(2)

Define
\Omega_{d}:\triangle^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as
\Omega_{d}({\bm{q}})\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega(q_{i})
for any integer
d
. Then, the difference between the smoothed and the unregularized values are bounded above and below, for all
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:

\displaystyle(N_{C}-1)L_{N_{C}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{max}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}})-\mathsf{max}^{C}_{{\bm{w}},\Omega_{2}}({\bm{\theta}})\leq(N_{C}-1)U_{N_{C}},

\displaystyle(N_{k}-1)L_{N_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}})-\mathsf{max}^{k}_{\bm{1},\Omega_{2}}({\bm{\theta}})\leq(N_{k}-1)U_{N_{k}},

where
L_{d}
and
U_{d}
are lower and upper bounds of
\Omega_{d}
on
\triangle^{d}
, and with
N_{C}\triangleq(C+1)(n+1)
,
N_{k}\triangleq(k+1)(n+1)
.

(3)

The proposed layers converge to the original, hard operators for vanishing regularization:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{{\bm{w}},\,\gamma\Omega}^{C}\xrightarrow[\gamma\to 0^{+}]{}{\bm{y}^{C}_{\bm{w}}}({\bm{\theta}}),\quad{\bm{y}}_{\bm{1},\,\gamma\Omega}^{k}\xrightarrow[\gamma\to 0^{+}]{}{\bm{y}^{k}_{\bm{1}}}({\bm{\theta}}).

Proof.

These properties stem from a direct application of results in
23
to our special DAG and edge weights, after mapping our framework to the DDP framework as described in
Section

D.3
. The constants
N_{C}
and
N_{k}
emerge from the size of the corresponding DAG.
∎

Appendix H
Explicit derivations

In this section, we provide explicit derivations for the quantities
Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
and their partial derivatives used in the algorithms.
Recall the definition of the smoothed value function
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]
from
Equation

4
:

\displaystyle V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]\triangleq\begin{cases}V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]&\text{if $w_{i}>c$,}\\[5.0pt]
\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}\bigl(\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}],\;V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c]\bigr)&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We focus on the non-trivial case where
w_{i}\leq c
. We define the local inputs
a
and
b
as:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\triangleq\theta_{i}+V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c-w_{i}],

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\triangleq V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i-1,c].

The smoothed maximum is defined as the optimal value of the regularized objective:

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)\triangleq\max_{q\in[0,1]}J(q\,;a,b),\quad\text{where }J(q\,;a,b)\triangleq qa+(1-q)b-\Omega({\bm{q}}).

Here,
{\bm{q}}=(q,1-q)^{\top}
and
\Omega({\bm{q}})=\omega(q)+\omega(1-q)
is a separable strictly convex regularization function. We denote the unique maximizer by
q^{*}(a,b)
.
The partial derivatives required for the algorithms are given by Danskin’s theorem and the chain rule:

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]

\displaystyle=q^{*}(a,b),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}(a,b).

In the following, we derive
q^{*}
and
\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}
for specific choices of
\Omega
. The resulting explicit expressions are summarized in
Table

1
, together with the corresponding expression of
V^{\bm{w}}_{\Omega}[i,c]=\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)=J(q^{*}(a,b)\,;a,b)
.

H.1
General derivative computation

The optimization problem
\max_{q\in[0,1]}J(q\,;a,b)
is equivalent to the minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{q\in{\mathbb{R}}}\quad

\displaystyle\Phi(q)\triangleq\omega(q)+\omega(1-q)-qa-(1-q)b

s.t.

\displaystyle g_{1}(q)\triangleq-q\leq 0,

\displaystyle g_{2}(q)\triangleq q-1\leq 0.

Let
\mu,\lambda\geq 0
be the KKT multipliers associated with
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
respectively. The KKT stationarity condition necessitates:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(q^{*})-\omega^{\prime}(1-q^{*})-(a-b)-\mu+\lambda=0.

(15)

We now distinguish two regimes for the sensitivity
\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}
.

Case 1: interior solution.

Assume the optimal solution lies in the strict interior
q^{*}\in(0,1)
. The box constraints are inactive and we have
\mu=\lambda=0
. The stationarity condition
Equation

15
simplifies to:

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(q^{*})-w^{\prime}(1-q^{*})=a-b.

Differentiating with respect to
a
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=\frac{1}{w^{\prime\prime}(q^{*})+w^{\prime\prime}(1-q^{*})}.

(16)

Case 2: boundary solution.

We now consider the case where the solution saturates at a boundary. This occurs if and only if the regularizer has bounded gradients at the endpoints.
Assume
\lim_{q\to 1^{-}}\omega^{\prime}(q)=L<\infty
and
\lim_{q\to 0^{+}}\omega^{\prime}(q)=l>-\infty
.
Let
\tau_{\Omega}\triangleq L-l
.
We prove that if
a-b\geq\tau_{\Omega}
, then
q^{*}=1
and
\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=0
.

Let
q^{*}=1
. The active constraint is
g_{2}(q)\leq 0
, so
\mu=0
and
\lambda\geq 0
. The stationarity condition
Equation

15
becomes:

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(a-b)-(\omega^{\prime}(1)-\omega^{\prime}(0))

\displaystyle=(a-b)-\tau_{\Omega}.

Dual feasibility requires
\lambda\geq 0
, which holds if and only if
a-b\geq\tau_{\Omega}
.
Since the problem is strictly convex, the KKT conditions are sufficient for optimality. Thus, for all
(a,b)
such that
a-b>\tau_{\Omega}
, the unique minimizer is
q^{*}=1
.
In this open region,
q^{*}
is constant with respect to
a
, implying:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=0.

A symmetric argument shows that if
a-b\leq-\tau_{\Omega}
, then
q^{*}=0
and the derivative is zero.

H.2
Shannon entropy

We consider
\Omega({\bm{q}})=-\gamma H^{s}({\bm{q}})\triangleq\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{2}q_{i}\log q_{i}
, which implies
\omega(q)=\gamma q\log q
. The gradients of
\omega
become unbounded at the boundaries (
\lim_{q\to 0}\omega^{\prime}(q)=-\infty
), forcing the solution to be strictly interior for any
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Maximizer.

The stationarity condition
Equation

15
yields:

\displaystyle\gamma\log q^{*}-\gamma\log(1-q^{*})=a-b\iff\gamma\log\left(\frac{q^{*}}{1-q^{*}}\right)=a-b.

Solving for
q^{*}
yields the logistic function
q^{*}=\sigma(\frac{a-b}{\gamma})\triangleq(1+\exp((b-a)/\gamma))^{-1}
.

Partial derivative.

The derivative is computed via
Equation

16
derived in Case 1:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime\prime}(q^{*})+\omega^{\prime\prime}(1-q^{*})}=\left(\frac{\gamma}{q^{*}}+\frac{\gamma}{1-q^{*}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}{\gamma}.

Smoothed maximum.

Substituting the optimal probability
q^{*}
back into the objective function
J
yields the standard
log-sum-exp
as expected:

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)=\gamma\log\left(\exp(a/\gamma)+\exp(b/\gamma)\right).

H.3
Gini entropy

We consider
\Omega({\bm{q}})=-\gamma H^{g}({\bm{q}})\triangleq\frac{\gamma}{2}(\|{\bm{q}}\|_{2}^{2}-1)
, which implies
\omega(q)=\frac{\gamma}{2}q^{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}
.

Maximizer.

In this case, we have:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(q)-\omega^{\prime}(1-q)=\gamma q-\gamma(1-q)=2\gamma q-\gamma,

so that
Equation

15
becomes:

\displaystyle 2\gamma q^{*}-\gamma-(a-b)-\mu+\lambda=0\implies q^{*}=\frac{a-b+\gamma+\mu-\lambda}{2\gamma}.

We analyze the solution based on the active constraints using the complementary slackness conditions
\mu q^{*}=0
and
\lambda(q^{*}-1)=0
.

(1)

Inactive constraints (
\mu=0,\lambda=0
):

we have
q^{*}=\frac{a-b+\gamma}{2\gamma}
.
Primal feasibility
0<q^{*}<1
requires
0<\frac{a-b+\gamma}{2\gamma}<1
, which simplifies to
-\gamma<a-b<\gamma
.
In this region,
\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=\frac{1}{2\gamma}
.

(2)

Active lower bound (
q^{*}=0
):

we have
\lambda=0
and
\mu\geq 0
.
The stationarity condition yields
0=\frac{a-b+\gamma+\mu}{2\gamma}
, which implies
\mu=-(a-b+\gamma)
.
Dual feasibility
\mu\geq 0
requires
a-b+\gamma\leq 0\iff a-b\leq-\gamma
.
In this region,
q^{*}
is constant, so
\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=0
.

(3)

Active upper bound (
q^{*}=1
):

we have
\mu=0
and
\lambda\geq 0
.
The stationarity condition yields
1=\frac{a-b+\gamma-\lambda}{2\gamma}
, which implies
2\gamma=a-b+\gamma-\lambda
, or
\lambda=a-b-\gamma
.
Dual feasibility
\lambda\geq 0
requires
a-b-\gamma\geq 0\iff a-b\geq\gamma
.
In this region,
q^{*}
is constant, so
\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=0
.

Combining these three cases, we recover the projection onto
[0,1]
:

\displaystyle q^{*}=\clip_{[0,1]}\left(\frac{a-b+\gamma}{2\gamma}\right).

Partial derivative.

The partial derivative is constant and only non-zero on the non-clipped region:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|a-b|<\gamma\}}.

Smoothed maximum.

The regularization term simplifies to
\Omega({\bm{q}})=\gamma(q^{2}-q)
for any
{\bm{q}}=(q,(1-q))^{\top}
. Substituting the optimal
q^{*}
into the objective function
J
yields:

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)=q^{*}a+(1-q^{*})b-\gamma((q^{*})^{2}-q^{*}).

When saturation occurs (i.e.,
|a-b|\geq\gamma
), we have
q^{*}\in\{0,1\}
. In this case, the regularization term vanishes and we recover the hard maximum
\max(a,b)
. In the interior region (i.e.,
|a-b|<\gamma
), substituting
q^{*}=\frac{a-b+\gamma}{2\gamma}
yields the following quadratic form:

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)=\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{4\gamma}+\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{4}.

H.4
1.5-Tsallis Entropy

We consider
\Omega({\bm{q}})=-\gamma H_{1.5}^{T}({\bm{q}})\triangleq\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{q_{i}^{\frac{3}{2}}-q_{i}}{\frac{3}{2}(\frac{3}{2}-1)}
, which implies
\omega(q)=\frac{4\gamma}{3}q^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{2\gamma}{3}
and
\omega^{\prime}(q)=2\gamma\sqrt{q}
.

Maximizer.

The stationarity condition for an interior solution (
q^{*}\in(0,1)
) requires:

\displaystyle 2\gamma\sqrt{q^{*}}-2\gamma\sqrt{1-q^{*}}=a-b.

Let
C\triangleq\frac{a-b}{2\gamma}
. We seek to solve
\sqrt{q^{*}}-\sqrt{1-q^{*}}=C
.
The function
f(q)=\sqrt{q}-\sqrt{1-q}
is strictly increasing on
[0,1]
with range
[-1,1]
.

•

If
|C|>1
(i.e.,
|a-b|>2\gamma
), no interior solution exists. The monotonicity of the objective forces the solution to the boundary:
q^{*}=1
if
C>1
, and
q^{*}=0
if
C<-1
.

•

If
|C|\leq 1
, we square the stationarity equation:
q^{*}-2\sqrt{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}+(1-q^{*})=C^{2}
, which rearranges to
2\sqrt{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}=1-C^{2}
. Squaring again yields the quadratic equation
4(q^{*})^{2}-4q^{*}+(1-C^{2})^{2}=0
.
The roots are
q_{\pm}=\frac{1\pm|C|\sqrt{2-C^{2}}}{2}
.
To satisfy the sign of the original equation
\sqrt{q^{*}}-\sqrt{1-q^{*}}=C
, we must select the root such that
\mathrm{sgn}(\sqrt{q^{*}}-\sqrt{1-q^{*}})=\mathrm{sgn}(q^{*}-\frac{1}{2})=\mathrm{sgn}(C)
. Since
q_{+}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
q_{-}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we must then choose
q_{+}
when
C\geq 0
and
q_{-}
when
C\leq 0
. Moreover, since we have
C\geq 0\implies|C|=C
and
C\leq 0\implies-|C|=C
, this yields the unique solution
q^{*}=\frac{1+C\sqrt{2-C^{2}}}{2}
.

Combining the boundary and interior cases and denoting,
\bar{C}\triangleq\clip_{[-1,1]}(C)
, the closed-form solution is
q^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\bar{C}\sqrt{2-\bar{C}^{2}}\right)
.

Partial derivative.

Using
\omega^{\prime\prime}(q)=\gamma q^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, the derivative in the interior (where
|\bar{C}|<1
) is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{*}}{\partial a}=\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{q^{*}}}+\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{1-q^{*}}}\right)^{-1}.

If
|\bar{C}|=1
, the derivative is 0.

Smoothed maximum.

The value is obtained by substituting the optimal
q^{*}
into the objective. Using the definition
\Omega({\bm{q}})=\frac{4\gamma}{3}(q^{\frac{3}{2}}+(1-q)^{\frac{3}{2}}-1)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{\Omega}(a,b)

\displaystyle=q^{*}a+(1-q^{*})b-\Omega({\bm{q}}^{*})

\displaystyle=q^{*}a+(1-q^{*})b+\frac{4\gamma}{3}\left(1-(q^{*})^{\frac{3}{2}}-(1-q^{*})^{\frac{3}{2}}\right).

Unlike the Gini case, this expression does not admit a simple polynomial form in terms of
a
and
b
, and is computed using the closed-form solution for
q^{*}
derived above.
