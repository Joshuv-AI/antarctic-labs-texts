Development and psychometric validation of a culturally adapted instrument to assess nurses’ competence in delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life
Authors: Bahman Aghaei, Mohammad Reza Heidari, Reza Norouzadeh, Mohammad Abbasinia, Saeid Hosseinoghli, Yaser Saeid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study developed and validated a culturally grounded instrument to assess Iranian nurses’ competence in delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life.MethodsUsing a sequential exploratory mixed-methods design, qualitative interviews with 18 nurses and a systematic review of 37 studies identified three core domains: Preparation, Conveying the Bitter Truth, and Consolation. Psychometric analyses were subsequently conducted within this mixed-methods framework to validate the developed instrument. These informed a 26-item scale, psychometrically tested with 284 nurses.ResultsThe final instrument demonstrated strong content validity (S-CVI/Ave = 0.915), a clear three-factor structure explaining 66.82% of total variance (KMO = 0.934, P < 0.001), and high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.945; ICC = 0.95). Items captured culturally specific strategies such as gradual truth-telling, strategic timing, non-distressing language, and empathic consolation.ConclusionThis validated scale provides a contextually relevant tool for evaluating and improving nurses’ communication in end-of-life care, with implications for education, clinical practice, and cross-cultural research in palliative and death-related contexts. Future studies should apply confirmatory factor analysis and evaluate predictive validity across diverse settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02167-0.

Introduction

Delivering bad news, particularly in end-of-life care, is one of the most emotionally and ethically demanding responsibilities in nursing. The task becomes even more complex when the recipients are patients’ family members, who often serve as surrogate decision-makers and primary sources of emotional support during the final stages of a loved one’s life [1, 2]. How distressing information is conveyed can profoundly influence families’ psychological well-being, coping ability, and satisfaction with care [3]. Despite its critical importance, bad news delivery is inconsistently addressed in clinical practice, especially in cultural contexts where full disclosure of terminal illness is not universally accepted [4]. In Iran, cultural norms and familial dynamics shape communication practices, and families may prefer to shield the patient from awareness of a terminal diagnosis, placing healthcare providers in ethical tension between patient autonomy and family preferences [4, 5]. Nurses, who maintain the most continuous contact with patients and families, play a pivotal but often informal role in mediating difficult conversations [1, 6]. Many nurses report inadequate preparation and lack of standardized protocols for these emotionally charged interactions [7, 8].

In healthcare, bad news refers to information that significantly alters a patient’s or family’s expectations regarding diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment trajectory, often eliciting strong emotional reactions such as anxiety, sadness, or anger [9, 10]. In Iran, the delivery of bad news is often physician-centered, and nurses play an important role in clarifying information, supporting families emotionally, and continuing communication after disclosure [1, 11]. Recent commentaries have questioned the framing of “breaking bad news,” suggesting alternatives like “serious illness communication” or “difficult news” to emphasize patient-centered approaches [12]. In this study, the term is used operationally to denote information that significantly changes expectations about illness or treatment trajectory. Existing frameworks such as SPIKES, PEWTER(Prepare, Evaluate, Warning, Tell, Emotional Response, Reassure/Refer), ABCDE(Advance preparation, Build a therapeutic environment/relationship, Communicate well, Deal with patient/family reactions, Encourage and validate emotions), and BREAK(Background, Rapport, Explore, Announce, Kindling) offer guidance but were developed primarily for physician–patient communication and are often oncology-focused, limiting their direct applicability to Iranian nursing practice [13–17].

Iranian healthcare emphasizes family-centered communication, collective decision-making, and spiritual coping, requiring nurses to integrate emotional support, phased disclosure, and culturally sensitive strategies [5, 18]. Sadat-Hosseini’s “TAT” model (Teamwork, Assessment, Truthfully Communicate) provides an Iranian-Islamic framework incorporating team coordination, spiritual considerations, and gradual disclosure [5]. However, most existing communication frameworks have been developed primarily in physician–patient contexts and remain largely theoretical, with limited empirical testing in nursing practice (e.g., oncology‑focused models such as SPIKES and ABCDE) [13–16, 19]. Moreover, while several instruments assess clinicians’ knowledge or attitudes toward difficult communication, comparatively few tools specifically measure nurses’ actual communication skills with families of patients nearing end of life [20]. This study addresses this gap by developing and psychometrically validating a culturally grounded tool and examining relationships between nurses’ demographic and professional characteristics and their competence in delivering bad news. By centering the family and grounding the instrument in indigenous nursing experiences and Islamic ethical principles which emphasize compassion, collective decision-making, and relational care, this work provides both a practical assessment tool and a culturally relevant framework for serious illness communication in end of life care this work provides both a practical assessment tool and a culturally relevant framework for end-of-life communication [5, 21, 22].

Methods

For the purpose of this study, “bad news” was operationally defined as information that significantly changes a patient’s or family’s perception of the future in a negative or life-altering way. This study used a sequential exploratory mixed-methods design, comprising an initial qualitative phase to conceptualize the construct and generate scale items, followed by a quantitative phase to assess the psychometric properties of the newly developed instrument. This approach was chosen to ensure that the final scale was both deeply rooted in the lived experiences of Iranian nurses and rigorously validated against established statistical standards. To enhance transparency and reproducibility, the qualitative phase was designed and reported following COREQ guidelines, the integration of qualitative and quantitative findings adhered to the GRAMMS framework, and the development and psychometric evaluation of the instrument followed the methodological recommendations of Streiner and Kottner (2014) [23–25].

Phase 1: Qualitative development

Conceptual framework and item generation

The qualitative phase aimed to explore and define the core dimensions of nurses’ competence in delivering bad news to families of patients at the end of life. Data were collected through two complementary approaches: (1) a systematic review of the literature and (2) semi-structured, in-depth interviews with experienced nurses. A structured systematic search was conducted to clarify the concept of delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life. Multiple Iranian and international databases were searched using predefined Persian and English keywords. The search process included removal of duplicates, screening of titles and abstracts, and full-text review based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The review aimed to inform item generation rather than to conduct a standalone systematic review. For the literature review, we searched electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, Magiran, SID, and IranMedex using a combination of keywords such as “bad news,” “end of life,” “family,” “breaking bad news,” and “nurses,” along with their Persian equivalents. Included studies were peer-reviewed empirical or theoretical articles published in English or Persian that addressed the communication of bad news to families in end-of-life contexts. Following screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts, 37 studies were selected for thematic analysis. Concurrently, we interviewed 18 nurses purposively selected for their direct experience communicating with families of dying patients and for having at least two years of clinical experience in intensive care units (ICUs), emergency departments, oncology wards, or palliative care settings. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private setting, audio-recorded with participant, and transcribed verbatim. The interview guide began with open-ended questions such as: “How do you typically inform families about a patient’s critical or terminal condition?” and “What strategies do you use when delivering news of a patient’s death?” Follow-up probes were then used to clarify, expand on, and deepen participants’ responses. Data analysis followed conventional content analysis [26]. Transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve immersion. Meaning units were identified, condensed into initial codes, and subsequently organized into subcategories and categories through an iterative process of constant comparison and abstraction. The research team held regular meetings to discuss coding decisions, resolve discrepancies, and ensure analytical rigor. Semi-structured interviews were conducted by the corresponding author, Dr. Reza Norouzadeh, with 15 Iranian nurses experienced in delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life. Each interview lasted approximately 40–60 min and was audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis was used to identify key codes and themes. To enhance trustworthiness, preliminary codes and themes were reviewed by a subset of participating nurses (member checking, n = 5) to confirm their accuracy and resonance with actual experiences. Additionally, two co-authors with expertise in qualitative research independently reviewed the coding and theme development (peer debriefing) to ensure credibility and minimize researcher bias. Findings from this qualitative phase, alongside the literature review, were systematically integrated to form the preliminary domains of the instrument: Preparation, Conveying the Bitter Reality, and Consolation. Overlapping themes were merged, culturally specific practices identified in interviews were added, and discrepancies were discussed by the research team until consensus was reached [23, 25] (Fig. 1). Trustworthiness was further enhanced through prolonged engagement and maximum variation sampling encompassing diversity in gender, age, education, clinical experience, and work setting.

Phase 2: Psychometric validation

Based on the qualitative findings, an initial 30-item draft instrument was developed using a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, 5 = always), with higher scores indicating greater perceived competence in delivering bad news. Face validity was assessed qualitatively through interviews with 10 nurses and quantitatively using the Item Impact Score (frequency [%] × importance), retaining items with scores ≥ 1.5.

Content validity was evaluated using contemporary adaptations of Lawshe’s method, which provides a robust framework for calculating the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) for quantitative content assessment in social and health sciences contexts [27]. A panel of 11 experts, including nursing faculty, clinical specialists, and methodologists, reviewed the items. Items with a CVR greater than 0.59 and a CVI ≥ 0.78 were retained, and the overall scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was calculated to evaluate the instrument’s aggregate content validity. A predetermined importance threshold of 0.78 was set for item retention during content validity assessment [28]. Items with content validity index (CVI) below this threshold were revised or removed. In addition to qualitative item generation, the importance of each item was evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not important to 5 = very important). Participants were asked to rate the perceived importance of each item in assessing nurses’ competence in delivering bad news. Items with a mean importance score below the predetermined threshold were reviewed and revised or removed. This step was conducted prior to exploratory factor analysis to ensure the practical relevance of the retained items. Construct validity was examined via exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with principal component analysis and Varimax rotation. The number of factors to retain was determined using multiple complementary criteria to enhance methodological rigor. These included eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser’s criterion), visual inspection of the scree plot, and parallel analysis. In the parallel analysis, observed eigenvalues were compared with those generated from randomly simulated datasets of the same size; only factors with observed eigenvalues exceeding the corresponding randomly generated eigenvalues were retained [29]. Conceptual interpretability and theoretical coherence of the factor structure were also considered in the final decision. Following item reduction, exploratory factor analysis was repeated on the final 26-item solution using the same criteria to confirm the stability of the factor structure.

Sampling adequacy and factorability were assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity, as recommended in contemporary multivariate analysis guidelines [30]. Factors with eigenvalues > 1 and items with factor loadings ≥ 0.40 were retained [29]. Reliability was assessed using multiple approaches. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, which is considered a more robust reliability coefficient in contemporary psychometric research [31]. Test–retest reliability was examined in a subsample of 20 nurses who completed the instrument twice, one week apart. The subsample size was determined in accordance with methodological recommendations suggesting that 15–30 participants are adequate for estimating stability coefficients in instrument development studies, and participants were selected based on availability and willingness to complete the questionnaire twice. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated, with ICC ≥ 0.70 indicating acceptable stability [32]. Interpretability was assessed by examining missing responses, score distributions (skewness and kurtosis), and floor/ceiling effects (defined as > 15% of respondents scoring the lowest or highest possible scores). Differences in mean competence scores across educational levels were examined using one-way ANOVA. Given the unequal group sizes and the potential violation of the homogeneity of variance assumption, the Games–Howell post hoc test was applied, as it does not assume equal variances and is appropriate for unequal sample sizes. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethics

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahed University (IR.SHAHED.REC.1399.111). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses and were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Results

A systematic literature review was conducted to clarify the concept of “delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life.” Comprehensive searches were performed across both Iranian (SID, MagIran, IranMedex, IranDoc) and international databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar), using Persian and English keywords such as “family,” “bad news,” “breaking bad news,” “delivering bad news,” “communicating bad news,” “disclosure of bad news,” and “end of life.” In the literature search, 1,344 relevant articles were initially retrieved using the keyword “communicating bad news.” Searches with related terms found 873 articles for “breaking bad news,” 432 for “delivering bad news,” 210 for “disclosing bad news,” and 146 for an alternative phrasing of the same concept. Subsequently, adding the keyword “end of life” narrowed the results to 187 published articles. Further refinement by including the term “family” reduced the pool to 96 relevant publications. After applying the inclusion criteria and excluding studies that focused on delivering bad news to families of neonatal or pediatric patients, 37 articles remained that showed the strongest conceptual alignment with the focus of the present study (Table 1).

Preparation

This category included two subcategories: situational analysis and preliminary groundwork. Nurses emphasized the necessity of assessing both the patient’s clinical status (e.g., acuity, prognosis) and the family’s psychological, emotional, and cultural readiness prior to disclosure. As one ICU nurse stated: “The reality is that delivering bad news is extremely difficult. I always pay close attention to the patient’s condition; whether they are stable or in active dying and tailor my approach to the family accordingly.” (P.12) Preparation also involved environmental and temporal considerations, such as ensuring a private, quiet setting, avoiding late-night disclosures, and gradually preparing families by allowing bedside presence or using indirect language initially (e.g., “Your loved one’s condition is worsening”). Another nurse highlighted the importance of timing: “I usually don’t announce death after midnight. I wait until the first opportunity in the morning. However, it’s also problematic to delay too long; for example, the family might arrive in the morning to see their loved one and find an empty bed!” (P.18).

Regarding preparation, the participant said: “I asked them to call the calmer brother and signaled my colleague to prepare the necessary items. As we walked toward the exit, I asked, ‘Do you know what to do now?’ Without mentioning the death directly, I simply said, ‘first, get the identification documents and personal belongings ready.’” (P.8).

Conveying the bitter truth

This category included transparent information-sharing and facilitating acceptance. In the end-of-life care literature “Conveying the bitter truth” refers to a process whereby the patient or family gradually comes to understand through supportive communication that the patient’s condition is serious, irreversible, and likely fatal. This process requires patience, empathy, balanced transparency, and the gradual repetition of information neither offering unrealistic hope nor delivering the news of death suddenly or bluntly. In this study, nurses stressed the importance of delivering information clearly, honestly, and compassionately, while avoiding false hope. They adapted their communication to the family’s health literacy and cultural norms, often paraphrasing physicians’ technical explanations into lay terms. Nurses frequently described translating complex medical terminology into language that families could understand: “The oncologist said the cancer had spread and that further treatments might not help. I told the family in simple words: ‘The cancer has moved to other parts of his body, and the medications we tried won’t work anymore. His body is very weak, so stronger treatments won’t help.’ I tried to be honest but gentle, so they could understand without being scared.” (P8). In situations where families struggled to grasp complex medical information, nurses emphasized the importance of simplifying explanations and checking for understanding: “The doctor used a lot of medical terms, and the family looked confused. I tried to explain it in simple words: ‘His kidneys are not working well, and the medicine helps slow the process. Don’t worry about the medical names, just focus on what it means for him.’ I make sure to repeat things and check they really understand.” (P2). A key strategy was reinforcing that the care team had exhausted all reasonable interventions: “I assure them: ‘We’ve done everything possible. No one has neglected your loved one.’ This helps them accept the inevitability without blaming the staff.” (P.7).

Nurses attempted to help families understand and come to terms with the inevitable deterioration of the patient’s condition. However, in some cases, families remained unable to accept the acute reality. One participant quoted: “A patient with acute liver hemorrhage had emergency surgery but suffered cardiac arrest 20 minutes post-op due to severe trauma and critically low hemoglobin. Despite clear explanations from both nurses that the event was unpredictable and not due to error, the family refused to accept it and blamed us.” (P.5). Participants acknowledged the tension between truth-telling and cultural sensitivities in the Iranian context, where families may resist direct disclosure of terminal diagnoses. To navigate this, nurses employed a gradual, non-alarming approach: “I never give false hope—it’s simply wrong. But I also avoid being blunt. I try to speak in a way that prepares them emotionally, without shocking them.” (P.17).

Consolation

This category comprised emotional soothing and empathic engagement. Nurses employed multiple techniques to mitigate distress, including empathetic listening, validating emotions, using non-alarming language (e.g., avoiding “he’s dying” in favor of “his body is struggling”), and highlighting the “good death” (e.g., “He is no longer in pain”). Empathy was not merely verbal but contextual: “When the patient had passed, I told the family: ‘He suffered greatly, but now he’s free from that pain and torment.’” (P.4).

Nurses emphasized the comforting, empathetic dimension of breaking bad news, often using personal disclosure and emotional reassurance to calm and support families. As one nurse shared: “I’d gently comfort them… I’d even share my own experience like losing my 3-year-old daughter to show I truly understand their pain. This helps them feel seen, settle emotionally, and regain some strength. We tell them, ‘we’re not stone-hearted…we feel deeply too.’ By saying things like, ‘My own mother-in-law had a stroke just like this,’ we build connection, show empathy, and help ease their distress.”(P.13).

While empathetic engagement was the predominant strategy, a notable subset of experienced nurses highlighted the reality of potential family aggression, particularly in high-acuity settings like Emergency and ICU where sudden deaths occur. This challenge, while not ubiquitous, was described as a critical safety concern requiring specific management strategies. Nurses described mitigating risks by maintaining physical distance, involving security personnel, and deferring communication to senior staff when hostility was anticipated. This aggression often stemmed from families’ inability to accept the suddenness of death or perceived negligence. One participant described a scenario where “the family refused to accept it and blamed us despite clear explanations of medical unpredictability” (P.5). However, nurses emphasized that effective consolation sometimes requires a deliberate delay in disclosure, particularly when there is a risk of aggression. One emergency nurse recounted an experience that reshaped their approach: “I once informed a father of his child’s death; he rushed toward the door in anger and kicked it until it was damaged. Since then, I have realized that in emergency situations, if the family is visibly agitated, it is safer to postpone delivering the news until the initial shock subsides.” (P.1). Nurses also described managing potential family aggression by maintaining physical distance, involving security if needed, and deferring communication to senior staff when hostility was anticipated: “If the family becomes aggressive, I step back, stay calm, and sometimes ask security to intervene. My priority is safety for them and for us.” (P.16).

Quantitative findings: psychometric validation

Face and content validity

Face validity assessment confirmed clarity and relevance of items. All items achieved an impact score above 1.5 based on importance ratings. Content validity evaluation resulted in the removal of three items (CVR < 0.59) and merging of one item, yielding a 26-item scale (Appendix 1). The scale-level CVI/Ave was 0.915, indicating excellent content validity.

Participant characteristics

The final instrument was administered to 284 nurses (mean age 35.46 ± 7.93 years; 58.5% female) from ICU, CCU, oncology, and emergency settings (Table 4). The mean total competence score was 92.4 ± 12.7 (range 58–125), indicating moderate to high self-reported competence.

Construct validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using principal component analysis with Varimax rotation confirmed the three-factor structure. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.934, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 2983.649, P < 0.001). Based on the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) results reported in the study, the 26-item instrument was structured into three conceptually coherent and factors that align closely with the qualitative findings. Specifically, items 20 through 26 loaded onto the first factor, which was conceptually labeled “Consolation.” This domain encompasses a set of nurse behaviors aimed at alleviating emotional distress and fostering psychological comfort, including managing emotional and psychological distress, demonstrating empathy, offering reassurance, actively listening to family members, establishing verbal communication, and accurately perceiving the thoughts and feelings of families during end-of-life care. Items 1 through 7 constituted the second factor, labeled “Preparation.” These items reflect preparatory competencies such as assessing the patient’s clinical condition, evaluating the family’s physical and emotional readiness, laying the groundwork for disclosure, pre-planning communication scenarios, timing the delivery appropriately, arranging a calm and private environment, and gradually preparing the family to receive distressing news. This factor directly corresponds to the “Preparation” theme identified in the qualitative phase. The third factor, termed “Conveying the Bitter Truth,” included items 10, 11, 14, 15, 16, 17, and 18. These items capture nurses’ skills in transparently communicating the patient’s critical or terminal condition while providing reassurance that all possible medical interventions have been exhausted. This domain emphasizes truthful yet culturally sensitive information-sharing and aligns with the qualitative subtheme of conveying harsh realities without inducing false hope. Additionally, certain items initially cross-loaded on two minor factors (Factors 4 and 5), but were conceptually reallocated based on thematic coherence. For instance, item 19 (“I speak to the family about the patient’s honorable and peaceful death when delivering bad news”) was integrated into the Consolation factor due to its comforting and meaning-making function. Similarly, item 9 (“From the moment a critically ill patient is admitted, I prepare myself to deliver bad news”) and item 8 (“I bring family members to the bedside before delivering bad news”) were assigned to the Preparation factor, as they reflect anticipatory and environmental readiness strategies. Items 12 (“I arrange counseling for the family when delivering bad news”) and 13 (“I consult experienced colleagues before delivering bad news”) were incorporated into the Conveying the Bitter Truth factor, as they support effective, collaborative, and informed communication. This final three-factor demonstrates strong construct validity, with all items exhibiting factor loadings ≥ 0.45, and collectively explaining 66.82% of the total variance (Table 5). The scree plot of initial eigenvalues showed a clear elbow after the fifth factor, with subsequent eigenvalues leveling off below 1, supporting the retention of five factors in the exploratory factor analysis (Fig. 2).

Reliability

Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.945, and for the three subscales ranged from 0.892 to 0.921. McDonald’s omega was 0.944, confirming internal consistency. Test-retest reliability (n = 20, 1-week interval) showed the high reliability in “Preparation” (ICC = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, p < 0.001), “Conveying the Bitter Truth” (ICC = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98, p < 0.001), and “Consolation” (ICC = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.90–0.95, p < 0.001). The overall scale also exhibited strong reliability (ICC = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97, p < 0.001).

Interpretability and group differences

Interpretability analyses showed minimal missing data (< 1%), a normal score distribution (skewness=−0.498, kurtosis= −1.113), and low ceiling (2.1%) and floor (0.4%) effects, confirming the scale’s ability to detect variability across respondents. Finding showed nurses who were married, held a PhD, worked in surgery or internal medicine, had > 15 years of experience, held leadership positions, or had received palliative care training reported significant higher self-reported competence in delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life. In assessing the sensitivity (responsiveness) of the questionnaire, there was a significant difference among nurse groups with different educational levels. Post hoc comparisons using the Games–Howell test indicated that nurses with a doctoral degree had significantly higher mean competence scores compared to other educational groups (Table 6). After item development, scoring rules were established. Given the use of a Likert-type scale ranging from “Always” (5) to “Never” (1), total scores were computed by summing item responses. To facilitate interpretation and cross-factor comparison, raw scores for each factor were linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale using the formula: Transformed Score = [(Observed Score – Minimum Possible Score)/(Maximum Possible Score – Minimum Possible Score)] × 100. Factor 1 (preparation) included 9 items with a raw score range of 9–45; factor 2 (conveying the bitter reality) also included 9 items with a raw score range of 9–45; and factor 3 (consolation) comprised 8 items with a raw score range of 8–40. For example, a nurse scoring 25 on factor 3 would have a transformed score of approximately 53%. Higher transformed scores indicate greater competency in delivering bad news to families of patients at the end of life.

Discussion

This study was designed to develop and validate a culturally grounded instrument for assessing nurses’ competencies in delivering bad news to families of patients at the end of life. The integrative literature review informed the initial item pool by identifying cross-cultural communication principles such as gradual disclosure, family-centered timing, and empathic consolation which were then refined through qualitative interviews to reflect Iranian socio-cultural norms. By synthesizing findings from 37 international and local studies, the review ensured that scale items were grounded in both global best practices (e.g., SPIKES, ABCDE) and context-specific strategies (e.g., indirect language, bedside preparation), directly contributing to the content validity and cultural relevance of the final 26-item instrument. The findings reveal a tripartite framework of nursing skills: preparation, conveying the bitter reality, and consolation that collectively shape effective communication in emotionally charged end-of-life contexts. This discussion synthesizes these findings in light of existing theoretical models and empirical literature, while highlighting the instrument’s novelty, psychometric robustness, and implications for clinical practice and cross-cultural adaptation.

The qualitative phase identified three core domains that constitute nurses’ communicative competence when disclosing bad news to families. The first domain, preparation, encompasses situational analysis and contextual framing strategies such as selecting an appropriate time and private setting, assessing family readiness, and gradually preparing relatives through bedside presence or prognostic cues. These practices align closely with established protocols like SPIKES and ABCDE, which emphasize environmental control, timing, and psychological readiness as foundational to effective disclosure [13, 46]. Iranian nurses in this study often deferred disclosure until the morning shift even after a patient’s nighttime death demonstrating a culturally informed approach to timing that emphasizes emotional containment rather than immediate communication. This contrasts with Western models that often advocate for prompt disclosure but resonates with regional values emphasizing emotional protection and family cohesion.

Interestingly, while team-based preparation is central to the Iranian-Islamic “Tat” model a culturally adapted framework for delivering bad news which emphasizes Teamwork, Assessment, and Truthful Communication [5], participants in this study seldom reported using formal interdisciplinary coordination when delivering bad news. Instead, they typically turned to informal advice from more experienced colleagues, indicating that institutional support systems for bad-news communication are still limited in Iranian clinical settings. Some nurses also described involving a knowledgeable family member to help prepare others for difficult news. However, this approach was not endorsed by the expert panel and is not well supported by existing evidence. Future research should carefully examine the effectiveness and ethical consequences of assigning disclosure responsibilities to family members.

The second domain, conveying bitter truth, highlights nurses’ commitment to honesty in delivering prognostic information clearly and without false hope while adapting their approach to cultural norms. Participants consistently described sharing difficult news gradually and non-confrontationally, reflecting a pragmatic balance between ethical transparency and the perceived need to protect families from overwhelming distress, a tension commonly observed in non-Western settings [19]. Unlike the U.S.-centric principle of “truth-telling,” which prioritizes patient autonomy, Iranian nurses appear to adopt a relational autonomy model, wherein disclosure is calibrated to the family’s emotional capacity and cultural expectations. Crucially, this does not equate to concealment; rather, it represents a contextualized form of veracity that avoids abruptness and medical jargon which is consistent with recommendations in the SUNBURN protocol (a trauma-specific framework emphasizing structured support and clear communication) [64].

The third domain, consolation, extends beyond emotional support to include empathetic presence, active listening, and validation of grief responses. Nurses described creating space for silence, acknowledging distress without rushing to “fix” it, and reassuring families that all possible care had been provided. This aligns with the SHARE model (which focuses on Support, Honesty, Assurance, Respect, and Empathy) emphasis on trust-building post-disclosure to reduce depressive symptoms [65]. The integration of emotional attunement as a discrete skill mirroring the “understanding emotions” step in SUNBURN highlights a holistic view of communication that treats empathy not as an add-on, but as a core clinical competency. The subsequent quantitative phase demonstrate a 26-item instrument with strong psychometric properties: a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.945 and a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value of 0.934 indicate exceptional internal consistency and sampling adequacy. While direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in cultural context and target populations, these values are robust and compare favorably with other instruments in the field, such as González-Cabrera et al.‘s 25-item questionnaire (α = 0.816; KMO = 0.683) [20]. This suggests strong reliability and validity within this specific cultural setting, supporting the instrument’s utility for evaluating nursing competence in end-of-life communication.

Critically, this instrument is the first to focus exclusively on communication with families of patients nearing end-of-life. While existing instruments (e.g., the Communication of Bad News Scale) assess general disclosure skills, they rarely account for the unique emotional, ethical, and logistical challenges of terminal prognoses in non-acute settings. By basing its items on culturally specific behaviors like waiting for the right moment to share difficult news or using time spent at the patient’s bedside to gently prepare families this tool better reflects real communication practices in Middle Eastern and other collectivist healthcare settings.

Theoretical and practical implications

The three-part framework identified in this study both supports and expands existing models. It draws on key elements from SPIKES (setting the scene, assessing understanding), ABCDE (showing empathy, planning next steps), and SUNBURN (supporting emotional processing), but adds culturally specific adaptations especially the careful pacing of truth-telling and the priority given to the family’s emotional well-being over individual patient autonomy. This challenges the idea that Western communication models should apply universally and highlights the need for culturally responsive approaches in global palliative care. From a clinical perspective, this validated instrument can be used to assess training needs, measure communication quality, and support nursing curriculum development. Since formal training in delivering bad news is rare among Iranian nurses, the tool can help design targeted interventions that combine global best practices with local cultural values. Its high reliability also makes it well-suited for longitudinal studies examining how communication training affects family outcomes—such as anxiety, trust in clinicians, and adjustment to bereavement.

Limitations

This study relied on self-reported data, which may be affected by social desirability bias, and the sample was drawn only from urban hospitals in Iran, limiting generalizability. Additionally, the test–retest reliability was assessed on a relatively small subsample of 20 nurses, which may limit the generalizability of stability estimates. The practice of involving family members in delivering bad news remains debated.

Conclusion

This study introduces a psychometrically robust and culturally appropriate instrument for assessing nurses’ competence in delivering bad news to families of patients nearing end-of-life. Developed through in-depth qualitative insights and validated with rigorous quantitative methods, the tool reflects a three-dimensional model truthfulness, preparation, and compassion rooted in the collectivist values of Iranian healthcare. As global health systems seek to humanize end-of-life care, such context-sensitive tools are vital for improving clinical practice and fostering culturally informed communication in palliative settings.

Future studies should validate this instrument in diverse settings, apply confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the three-factor structure, and examine how nurses’ communication skills affect family outcomes. Experimental studies on family involvement in delivering bad news are also warranted.
