Synchronous Tumor of Colorectal Adenocarcinoma and Epstein‐Barr Virus‐Positive Diffuse Large B‐Cell Lymphoma: A Case Report
Authors: Fen Liu, Linlin Fan, Hong Yu, Zhiyong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe third reported case of synchronous colorectal adenocarcinoma and EBV‐positive DLBCL in an 87‐year‐old patient confirms the rarity of this synchronous tumor. Despite segmental resection, the patient died from postoperative complications, highlighting the need for multidisciplinary research into pathogenesis and management.Keywords: colorectal adenocarcinoma, diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma, Epstein‐Barr virus, synchronous tumor

Introduction

Colorectal adenocarcinoma is the predominant histological subtype of colorectal cancer, accounting for approximately 90% of all cases [1]. In contrast, the synchronous occurrence of colorectal adenocarcinoma and diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is rare, with an estimated incidence of only 2 per million individuals [2]. Although Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV) is a well‐established oncogenic driver in several malignancies, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma and EBV‐positive gastric cancer, its pathogenic role in colorectal carcinogenesis remains poorly understood. To the best of our knowledge, only two cases of colonic adenocarcinoma coexisting with EBV‐positive DLBCL have been reported in the English‐language literature to date [3, 4]. Herein, we report a third case involving an 87‐year‐old man who presented with a synchronous collision tumor comprising both malignancies. We discuss the diagnostic challenges, potential pathogenic mechanisms, and clinical implications of this rare entity, aiming to enhance clinician awareness and inform future multidisciplinary management.

Case History

An 87‐year‐old man presented with abdominal pain and bowel obstruction. He had no family history of tumors. Laboratory investigations revealed an elevated carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) level of 92.8 U/mL. Serological examination showed no evidence of immunosuppression. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated thickening of the transverse colon with concentric stenosis (Figure 1A), along with multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the abdominal cavity, retroperitoneum, and right iliac fossa (Figure 1B). An 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose whole‐body positron emission tomography (PET) scan, performed at an outside institution, demonstrated intense FDG avidity in the transverse colon mass and in the regional lymphadenopathy, with no evidence of hypermetabolic foci elsewhere to suggest distant metastases or extra‐abdominal lymphadenopathy. At presentation, the patient had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 2. Subsequently, a transverse colectomy was performed to resect the tumor and regional lymph nodes.

Methods

Gross pathological examination revealed a mass measuring 6.0 × 5.0 × 2.5 cm, accompanied by pericolic lymphadenopathy with lymph nodes ranging from 2.0 to 5.0 cm in diameter. The cut surface appeared pale and firm. Microscopically, the colorectal adenocarcinoma component exhibited prominent glandular differentiation (Figure 2A), whereas the resected lymph nodes showed diffuse infiltration by medium‐to‐large atypical cells with irregular nuclear contours, coarse chromatin, and visible nucleoli in a subset of cells (Figure 2D). Immunohistochemically, the carcinoma cells were positive for panCK (Figure 2B), CK20, and CDX2, and negative for LCA, Syn, and CgA. In contrast, the lymphoid proliferation expressed CD20, BCL2, and LCA (Figure 2E), with a Ki‐67 proliferation index of approximately 60% and MYC expression in about 20% of cells. The lymphoma cells were negative for CK, CD10, MUM1, cyclin D1, CD21, CD5, BCL6, and CD30. EBV‐encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization (ISH) revealed scattered positive cells in both the lymphoma component (Figure 2F) and the interstitial lymphocytes of the adenocarcinoma (Figure 2C), accounting for less than 10% of cells. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrated no genetic abnormalities involving MYC, BCL2, BCL6, or the 11q23.3/11q24.3 locus. Following radical surgical resection, postoperative pathological examination confirmed the colorectal carcinoma as stage pT3N1aM0 according to the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging criteria, and DLBCL as Lugano stage IA.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient did not receive further radiotherapy or chemotherapy due to his poor postoperative performance status. His clinical condition progressively deteriorated, and he succumbed to multiorgan dysfunction approximately 2 weeks after surgery.

Discussion

The prevalence of multiple primary cancers is relatively low, ranging from 0.46% to 4.1% in China and 1.6% to 10.7% in other countries [5]. The synchronous occurrence of colorectal adenocarcinoma and DLBCL is rare, with only ten documented cases to date [6]. Among these, our case represents the third report of EBV‐positive DLBCL in this setting. A thorough pre‐operative evaluation and a multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach are essential to tailor individualized treatment plans.

The clinicopathological characteristics of the 11 documented cases, including the present one, are summarized in Table 1 [3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Among these, there were 8 male and 3 female patients, with a median age of 78 years (range: 62–88 years). Tumor distribution across the colorectum was as follows: cecum (n = 3), ascending colon (n = 3), transverse colon (n = 1), descending colon (n = 1), rectosigmoid junction (n = 1), distal rectum (n = 1), and synchronous involvement of the ascending and sigmoid colon (n = 1). Regarding EBV status, three cases (including the present one) were explicitly confirmed as EBV‐positive DLBCL coexisting with colon adenocarcinoma [3, 4], one case was EBV‐negative [13], and the EBV status of the remaining cases was not reported. Furthermore, one case was complicated by concurrent myelodysplastic syndrome [14]. Therapeutic strategies varied widely, from surgery alone to multimodal chemo‐radiotherapy, and clinical outcomes ranged from 2 weeks to 4 years, underscoring the prognostic uncertainty and the absence of standardized management guidelines for this rare entity.

Distinguishing synchronous primary tumors from metastatic disease or lymphoma mimicking carcinoma is clinically challenging due to overlapping radiological and endoscopic features [15]. In our case, conventional imaging could only suggest a malignant mass with regional lymphadenopathy. Definitive differentiation of the dual histological components relied entirely on postoperative histopathology and comprehensive immunohistochemical profiling. This underscores the critical necessity of expert pathological review and an MDT approach to accurately stage the disease and guide subsequent management.

The EBV is a well‐established oncogenic virus associated with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer, and various lymphomas, but its role in colorectal cancer remains controversial [16]. In the present case, EBER‐ISH identified EBV primarily within the lymphoma cells and the interstitial lymphocytes of the adenocarcinoma, while the carcinoma cells themselves were negative. This topographic distribution suggests that EBV likely contributes to lymphomagenesis in the DLBCL component. In contrast, its presence in the adenocarcinoma microenvironment likely reflects a non‐transforming, reactive lymphocytic infiltration rather than a direct oncogenic role in the epithelial component. These findings underscore the pathogenic independence of the two synchronous malignancies, characterized by EBV‐driven lymphoproliferation in one component and conventional adenocarcinogenesis in the other.

Currently, there is no established treatment protocol for colorectal adenocarcinoma coexisting with EBV‐positive DLBCL. As summarized in Table 1, management strategies across the 11 reported cases are highly heterogeneous. Some patients were managed with surgery alone, while others received postoperative chemotherapy, including R‐CHOP or R‐CVP, or chemoradiotherapy [3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In our patient, despite successful tumor resection, poor postoperative performance status precluded the safe administration of any adjuvant chemotherapy or immunochemotherapy, leading to rapid clinical decline. This case illustrates the fundamental therapeutic dilemma in managing such synchronous dual malignancies. The need for systemic therapy must be carefully weighed against the patient's physiological reserve, particularly in the elderly population. For future similar cases, a staged approach may be considered, prioritizing surgical decompression followed by dose‐attenuated or sequential systemic therapy based on recovery. However, the prognosis remains guarded. Given the extreme rarity of this entity, systematic data collection through collaborative registries is needed to pool real‐world clinical experiences and inform future practice.

In conclusion, we present a unique case of synchronous primary colon adenocarcinoma and EBV‐positive DLBCL, illustrating the diagnostic challenges posed by rare co‐occurring malignancies in the gastrointestinal tract. Given the extreme rarity of this entity, isolated case reports cannot establish definitive diagnostic or therapeutic pathways. Systematic data accumulation and further translational research are therefore imperative to elucidate its epidemiological, clinicoradiological, molecular, and clinical characteristics.
