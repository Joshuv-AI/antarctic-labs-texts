Assessing fidelity of interventions to foster shared decision making in clinical encounters: Development of an implementation-focused fidelity checklist for use with electronic conversation aids
Authors: Jennifer L Ridgeway, Christina M LaVecchia, Michael R Gionfriddo, Megan E Branda, Mitchell W Clayton, Margaret M Paul, Sandra A Hartasanchez, Marleen Kunneman, Iris G Hargraves, Victor Montori, Kelly J Louks, Victor M Montori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesConversation aids (CAs) support shared decision making (SDM) by presenting options and enabling the collaborative co-creation of care plans in clinical encounters. Fidelity assessment in implementation studies may require distinction between implementation of SDM and of the CA, as suboptimal CA implementation can disrupt SDM. We aimed to develop and pilot test an observer-based instrument for assessing implementation fidelity in a study of SDM using an electronic CA.MethodsCandidate items were guided by definitions of intervention components and constructs from (a) the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's SHARE Approach, and (b) a conceptual framework of implementation fidelity. The instrument and coding manual were developed using video-recorded encounters. We assessed response distributions and inter-observer agreement.ResultsThe final instrument included 10 items assessing clinician behaviors and 12 potential moderators (3 tool integration, 4 quality of delivery, and 5 participant responsiveness). Observer agreement was moderate to very good on all items. It was lowest for the item assessing whether the tool displayed risk with auto-populated patient data like age and cholesterol.ConclusionAn adapted conceptual framework for implementation fidelity guided checklist development in key domains related to CA implementation and how clinicians use CAs to foster SDM.InnovationThis study demonstrates the feasibility of observing implementation fidelity of SDM interventions and introduces an approach that distinguishes fidelity of intervention implementation from the clinical practice of SDM. This distinction is critical for interpreting effectiveness outcomes in hybrid effectiveness–implementation studies and targeting implementation strategies to bolster routine adoption of SDM.Keywords: Shared decision making, Implementation science, Fidelity, Observation, Decision aid

Introduction

Implementation science focuses on the translation of evidence-based interventions into routine clinical practice. The success of implementation efforts is evaluated using a range of implementation outcomes, including acceptability (stakeholders' perceptions that an intervention is agreeable or satisfactory), appropriateness (the perceived fit or relevance of the intervention for a given setting), adoption (uptake of the intervention), penetration (the extent to which the intervention is integrated within the setting), and feasibility (the extent to which the intervention can be successfully carried out in that setting) [1]. Fidelity, defined as the extent to which the core elements of the intervention are delivered as intended by its developers, is one of the most commonly reported implementation outcomes [2]. Fidelity is necessary to determine whether the intervention caused the effect or whether failures in effectiveness were related to the intervention or deviations in how it was implemented or used.

Fidelity assessment, while specific to the intervention, is generally considered multidimensional and should be guided by conceptual framing of how the intervention works [3]. A 2023 review proposed a four-component framework of fidelity (adherence, dosage, quality, and participant responsiveness) [4], while the five components outlined in the widely-used framework by Dane and Schnieder [5] have been cited in the definition of fidelity as an implementation outcome (adherence, exposure to the intervention, quality of delivery, participant responsiveness or involvement, and program differentiation) [2]. A conceptual framework of implementation fidelity, introduced by Carroll and colleagues uses similar constructs to portray fidelity as including the content, frequency, and amount of the intervention delivered, while further identifying four potentially related factors that may moderate the degree to which the intervention is implemented with fidelity [6]. These include intervention complexity (where simple interventions may be easier to implement with fidelity than complex ones), facilitation strategies (where support strategies like education and feedback are thought to improve fidelity), quality of delivery (where delivery in a way that is appropriate for achieving the outcome is likely to moderate the effect), and participant responsiveness (where engagement by deliverers and recipients may moderate the effect) [6].

Fidelity assessment of complex behavioral interventions may be especially challenging given multiple intersecting parts, with challenges in assessing delivery at the point of intervention (including competence) and challenges related to implementation [7], [8], [9]. Shared decision making (SDM) is a method of patient-centered care that takes place within a conversation where patients and clinicians are working together to understand the patient's situation and together form a sensible response [10], [11]. Some SDM encounters use conversation aids (CAs) to support SDM with visual depictions of risks and treatment options [11], [12]. CAs have been shown in randomized efficacy trials to promote SDM, enhance patient knowledge of treatment options, and increase satisfaction [13], [14].

Over the last decade, a growing number of studies have evaluated strategies to foster routine implementation of SDM, assessing a range of implementation outcomes, including clinician adoption, acceptability, feasibility, and fidelity [15], [16], [17], [18]. These studies require investigators to determine the extent to which these implementation strategies and interventions are deployed and used as intended; fidelity deficits could explain why the target (the practice of SDM) was not fully fostered, and why the benefits expected from SDM were not fully realized.

It is customary to assess SDM in clinical encounters using patient and clinician questionnaires after the visit or using observer instruments to evaluate real-time or audio-video recorded clinical encounters. Observer instruments have been used in numerous SDM trials involving CAs [14], [19], [20]. For example, OPTION-5 quantifies the effort the clinician makes in: 1) drawing attention to or re-affirming, a problem; 2) reassuring the patient or re-affirming that the clinician will support the patient in becoming informed; 3) giving information about or re-affirming/checking understanding of the options; 4) supporting the patient in examining, voicing, and exploring his/her personal preferences; and 5) integrating the patient's preferences [21]. Effort is scored from 0 = No effort to 4 = Exemplary effort. However, these and other available assessments are not designed to examine the extent to which interventions to enable, support, and promote SDM, such as CAs, were implemented as intended.

We planned an assessment of fidelity as part of a hybrid implementation-effectiveness study of SDM for cardiovascular (CV) risk reduction [22] and determined that assessing implementation fidelity of the CA was fundamental given prior observations that low-fidelity uses of the CA could fail to support or even disrupt SDM conversations (e.g., when clinicians offer inaccurate information about risks and benefits of treatment options) [23]. This manuscript describes our process of developing and pilot testing an implementation fidelity assessment instrument using constructs from an existing implementation fidelity framework. It also presents challenges and opportunities for future development for trials that include implementation strategies and outcomes.

Methods and rationale

Our objective was to develop an instrument for use evaluating fidelity as an implementation outcome in an SDM trial using an encounter CA embedded in the electronic health record (EHR). While EHR data can identify whether a CA was opened in a clinical encounter, assessing if potential conversation-modifying elements were available and used requires examination of patient-clinician interactions, typically captured in video or audio recordings. Review of existing SDM observation instruments did not identify an observer measure that specifically included items to assess implementation of CAs, although prior work reviewing video-recorded encounters from SDM trials used a checklist containing other fidelity items, including whether the clinician described the portrayed risk [24].

The conceptual framework for implementation fidelity [6] has been cited in multiple studies [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]; but to our knowledge, it has not previously been applied in studies of interventions to foster SDM or in developing fidelity instruments for SDM implementation studies. For this study, we focused on the constructs from the framework that are most relevant to observable aspects of implementation of the CA to foster SDM, as our intent was to assess fidelity through observation of video-recordings of routine clinical encounters. Using the language of the Carroll et al. model, we considered the SDM-enabling CA to be the “intervention” and “implementation fidelity” to be clinician adherence to SDM behaviors in conversations. We used the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality's (AHRQ) SHARE Approach, which is a model of essential elements for SDM, to operationalize adherence to clinician behaviors: Seek your patient's participation, Help your patient explore and compare treatment options, Assess your patient's values and preferences, Reach a decision with your patient, and Evaluate your patient's decision [10], [32]. SHARE, which focuses on clinician behaviors that contribute to SDM, emerged as an appropriate approach because of its conceptual similarity to the adherence construct in the model and because of the potential to use SHARE constructs to develop observable items for fidelity assessment. The SHARE approach has been evaluated in terms of clinician understanding and confidence engaging in SDM [33], has been found to improve some healthcare outcomes (e.g., incidence of unplanned dialysis) [34], and has been leveraged in the design of electronic tools to support SDM conversations [35]. Specifically, clinical decision support tools can remind clinicians: a decision needs to be made (Seek), provide patients and clinicians with information about treatment options (Help), offer ways for patients to share information about their preferences with clinicians, e.g., through the patient portal before an appointment (Assess), deliver information to help make a decision, including information about the impact of treatment changes (Reach), and document treatment decisions in the EHR for future evaluation and return to SDM (Evaluate) [35].

We hypothesized that the way a CA is implemented in the electronic workflow, the quality of delivery (i.e., the quality with which clinicians use the CA) and participant responsiveness are moderators in the model, as they have the potential to strengthen or weaken the effect (Fig. 1) and, subsequently, the expected outcomes of high-quality SDM, such as risk-concordant care plans and improved decision conflict, treatment burden, and quality of life. Although CAs support both clinician and patient responsiveness (i.e., engagement), assessment of patient engagement separately may help elucidate whether conversation-modifying interventions such as CAs enable and support the conversational contributions of patients, clinicians, or both.

Intervention and implementation

Clinical practice guidelines recommend SDM for CV risk reduction [36]. The CA used in the parent trial, CV Prevention Choice, was designed to translate this recommendation into practice, fostering SDM during encounters between clinicians and at-risk patients. As previously described, CV Prevention Choice was developed with clinicians in routine care [37], as an extension of the Statin Choice CA, a tool shown effective in fostering SDM in several randomized trials [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]. The tool drew information, including personal and clinical characteristics, from the patient record (e.g., age, blood pressure, cholesterol) to auto-populate the CA (Fig. 2, Panel A) and to estimate the patient's 10-year risk of CV events, displayed using state-of-the-art risk communication approaches (e.g., words, numbers, and a pictograph of 100 people; Fig. 2, Panel B). Preventive interventions, including lifestyle options (diet, exercise, or smoking cessation) or medication options, could be selected to review their effect on this risk (Fig. 2, Panel C) and consider other relevant issues about them, including costs and side effects (Fig. 2, Panel D). After selecting one or a combination of these preventive options, the patient and clinician can view a side-by-side comparison of the patient's risk as-is and the revised risk if these preventive options were adopted (Fig. 2, Panel E). The individualized information and the decision made could be documented in the tool and copied into the After Visit Summary, a patient summary of the visit (Fig. 2, Panel F) [45].

In the parent trial, participating sites received standard procedures for embedding CV Prevention Choice in the Epic EHR, including the ability to customize some features as needed, e.g., medication integration [45]. The parent trial also used formative evaluation and implementation mapping to select and deploy tailored implementation strategies that addressed local needs [22]. These included educational outreach, reminders, and the use of clinical champions to influence adoption.

Procedures

This report summarizes our work to develop an observer checklist, which is one aspect of comprehensive fidelity assessment (i.e., observations, self-report surveys, intervention logs). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. The study team consisted of social scientists and clinicians with expertise in SDM and implementation science and who were experienced in using observer instruments in clinical trials and observational studies. Procedures were similar to proposed practical steps for developing fidelity and engagement measures, wherein the team begins with review of existing measures, development of a framework that outlined intervention components and content (e.g., review of intervention manual or program theory) and guidance on how to use it (e.g., how to decide which score to give), as well as development and pilot testing of checklists and coding guidelines (e.g., independent coding by two researchers with discussion to amend coding guidelines) [3]. For this project, feedback was elicited from content experts, rather than from users, and pilot testing was limited to coding coverage and concordance.

Item development

We report fidelity tool development in two phases: 1) Item Development, which included steps to identify components, content, and coding guidance and 2) Item Assessment (Fig. 3). In Phase 1, members of the study team reviewed 10 videos of clinical encounters from a repository of clinical encounter videos from our research team's prior trials evaluating encounter CAs. They sought to identify instances of clinicians using the CA in the encounter, using SHARE constructs and components from our adapted conceptual model of implementation fidelity to categorize these instances. These notes were later reviewed to identify examples of observable behaviors. Additional candidate items were identified by reviewing the parent trial protocol and extracting observable features from the intervention and implementation descriptions to characterize what constituted “implementation as intended.” For the participant responsiveness domain, the team considered the manner in which patients are intended to participate in SDM when the CA is used in the encounter. This phase produced an initial set of candidate items.

Study procedures.

We created two types of response options. The first was a Likert set of categorical responses for items where the team anticipated that they would be able to observe variability of delivery or implementation, e.g., that there may be degrees to which something was implemented as intended. We added a − 1 option to indicate use of the CA that undermined rather than fostered the practice of SDM and a “not applicable/not assessable” option for when the action of interest could not be assessed, e.g., was not captured by the camera's position or the recording was interrupted before the behavior, if it happened, could be observed. The second type of response option was for the presence or absence of the observable item (coded as Yes, No, Unclear).

The initial set of checklist items was pilot tested and refined using a sample of 15 video recordings from a previous SDM trial (Dataset 1) [46]. Characteristics of individuals in the recorded encounters are shown in Table 1. Two trained coders, who also had expertise in rhetoric, pharmacy, and implementation science, working independently, reviewed these videos in rounds of five. The observers met after each round to discuss their coding, align their usage of the checklist, and identify difficulties in item wording. When both coders agreed that item meaning was unclear or that there was overlap with other items, the items were edited or abandoned. The team documented the details for all changes in the checklist, including the problem being addressed and the rationale for the change.

Item assessment

To assess items (Phase 2), the two coders from Phase 1 then independently applied the revised checklist to a new sample of 29 video recordings collected for the parent trial (Dataset 2) [22]. Coders continued to discuss their coding and rationale for each item, as well as suggest changes for the checklist, using discussion notes in the coding manual to populate examples and clarify ways to differentiate scoring (e.g., why a coder might score a 3 instead of a 4). For the first 15 video recordings (Phase 1), observers were allowed to adjust their scoring after discussion; subsequently (Phase 2), independent coding was retained, and discussion was only used to inform development of the coding manual. Coding was entered into the Remote Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database software [47], [48] for data management.

Data analysis

Analysis included descriptive statistics of response frequencies for each item (to understand the range of responses observers used), coding concordance between observers (i.e., the number of times the observers' coding was in agreement as a share of all coding on an item), and Gwet's chance-corrected agreement coefficient (AC1), which is the conditional probability that two randomly selected raters will agree [49], [50] and where higher numbers indicate better agreement (0.8–1 = very good; 0.6–0.8 = good; 0.4–0.6 = moderate; 0.2–0.4 = fair; less than 0.2 = poor). Cohen's Kappa statistic is reported on the amount of agreement beyond chance about categorical assessments. These analyses were limited to the Phase 2 video set, as checklist items changed during Phase 1. Analyses were conducted using SAS software v9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Item development

Table 2 describes the assessment domains included with examples of content for each component. For this study, tool integration examples were specific to implementation of a CA in the electronic workflow during a clinical encounter: the CA launched within the clinical encounter, and it auto-populated data from the EHR to estimate personalized CV risk without the need for manual data entry or correction. For quality of delivery, we focused on aspects most relevant to CA implementation to distinguish it from the practice of SDM per se, e.g., correctly interpreting risk and risk reduction in the 100-person pictograph. Similarly, participant responsiveness included examples where patients engaged with the CA, for example, asking about visual information. Using this as a guide, we drafted 22 items in 3 domains: 10 about SDM-fostering clinician behaviors, 7 about tool integration and quality of delivery, and 5 about participant responsiveness.

Scenario from video-recorded encounter with example coding.

In this scenario, a 60-year-old Black male patient is in clinic for routine primary care. The visit starts with a review of health issues. About 10 min into the visit, the clinician mentions CV risk and the fact that the patient is on a statin, has “good” blood pressure, is “on the cusp” of obesity, and has a family history of vascular issues. The patient notes that he wishes his mother had visited the doctor more and taken care of her health, and the clinician responds by saying that they have a tool to stratify risk. Coders indicated that the tool launched as intended in an EHR encounter but that it did not autopopulate completely with the patient's information.

The patient follows the explanation and occasionally points at the screen as the clinician navigates the tool and describes current risk and the impact of adding or removing treatment options such as exercise and a Mediterranean diet. Although there were issues auto-populating the CA for the discussion and it is not clear that the clinician thought the patient's risk was worthy of discussion during the visit, the clinician navigated the CA mostly as intended, and the patient was engaged and interacted with the information. This may have fostered the clinician practice of SDM. Having the visual prompt of the various treatment options may have nudged discussion of options such as diet and exercise that otherwise would not have happened given the perceived benefit of the statin.

Challenges and recommendations

For some items, observers used only a few response options. Items only coded with the extremes of the scale (e.g., whether the tool launched as intended and whether it auto-populated as intended) may indicate that they would be better coded as Yes/No/Unclear. Coding discussions on the latter item, however, suggest that simply dichotomizing the response options may lose important variability. As a potential moderator, it may be better to capture instances where the tool auto-populated completely as intended (all information correct and complete), where it auto-populated as intended for only a few variables, or where it did not auto-populate at all as intended.

Our work did confirm the observability of most participant responsiveness items, and our assumption that these items could be coded as Yes/No was supported by the coding process (e.g., coding notes). These findings support the “modular” design of our approach, which may facilitate the inclusion of moderator domains in studies that use other SDM observer instruments. In those situations, study teams may pilot test items from implementation and participant responsiveness domains to determine whether these domains are distinct from the types of clinician actions (i.e., those describing the practice of SDM per se) that may be already included in other extant observer measures of SDM.

We also identified several challenges in applying the instrument that warrant further consideration and development. The coders in this study were trained and experienced in applying other coding frameworks, such as OPTION−12, to video-recorded encounters from similar studies. Still, they noted difficulties distinguishing between strong scores (i.e., 3 and 4) or between weak scores (i.e., 1 and 2), especially on clinician SDM-fostering behavior items. Our solution was to provide more detailed descriptions for each score, along with concrete examples in our coding manual (see Supplementary File 1). Further validation is needed to explore collapsing coding to a smaller set of options. Key challenges in observing implementation may hinder application of this tool in future video-recorded studies: for example, video recordings that fail to clearly capture whether the CA auto-populated from the EHR, difficulties the clinician had navigating the CA, or whether the CA's documentation features were used. There were times when the camera placement was unable to capture adequate detail, which meant coders had to rely on clinician or patient narrations made while using the CA. There were also times when the camera was only set to record specific segments of the encounter rather than its entirety. These challenges are not unique to this study and underscore the need for additional clinic and staff training during research studies (such as video-recording) while maintaining, as much as possible, normal clinical workflows and usual ways of interacting, as required for pragmatic implementation trials, such as the parent trial.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

Studies to foster routine implementation of SDM deploy implementation strategies and assess their impact on a range of implementation outcomes, including clinician adoption, acceptability, feasibility, and fidelity [15], [16], [17], [18]. In the case of fidelity, investigators need to determine the extent to which implementation strategies and interventions are deployed and used as intended. Implementation studies of SDM face a challenge that stems, in part, from whether SDM is viewed as the intervention [51] or clinical practice to be implemented, or as a strategy for increasing adoption of another evidence-based practice [52]. In our hybrid implementation-effectiveness study of a CA to foster SDM, fidelity was conceptualized as fidelity of the SDM-promoting interventions and fidelity of implementation of SDM itself. Assessing whether clinicians using the tool to foster SDM were able to use it as intended, separately from whether they were able to collaborate with patients using SDM to form plans of care, is critical to explaining effectiveness outcomes. We endeavored to create a fidelity assessment approach that treated SDM as an evidence-based practice, the implementation of which is a process that required consideration of who and what needs to change to support SDM outcomes.

We incorporated SHARE domains as observable indicators of adherence to the intended SDM-fostering practice, while assessing tool integration, quality of delivery, and participant responsiveness as potential moderators of whether the CA could support the practice of SDM as intended. A CA may launch correctly and display accurate information but still fail to foster SDM. Conversely, observable SDM behaviors may occur even when technical integration of the CA is imperfect. Assessing both dimensions, therefore, helps distinguish whether lower-than-expected effectiveness reflects limitations in the tool, limitations in how the tool was implemented, or incomplete enactment of SDM itself as the intended practice. Separate fidelity assessments can also support more precise implementation strategy mapping by aligning improvement efforts with their likely mechanisms of change. Implementation strategies should be mapped to outcomes via clear mechanisms of change [53]; in our work, items based on SHARE constructs could be mapped to strategies that target clinician behaviors, while implementation items may map to strategies that adapt and tailor the CA to context or provide technical assistance.

Although the items in this observer instrument are specific to implementation of CV Prevention Choice, many items capture generalizable features of how electronic CAs are integrated and employed during encounters. This approach is consistent with fidelity procedures that begin with specification of intervention components and how they are intended to function in practice. Future studies will use the instrument to assess encounters from other studies that use a CA to foster SDM (including in other disease contexts). Consideration of intervention components should also orient the researcher's selection of different instruments to capture SDM as clinician practice or SDM as the study outcome, including situations where novel measures of the occurrence of SDM are needed [54]. In this study, SHARE was selected for its focus on clinician behaviors, but competence may also involve relationship building [8]. Future work should examine whether participant responsiveness ought to be conceptualized differently when SDM is the target or whether implementation strategies, such as consumer engagement, could improve the implementation process.

This work focused on a tool to assess observable aspects of implementation fidelity; but additional tools, including program logs and participant surveys, may be needed to capture aspects of implementation that cannot be easily observed. Multiple methods and data sources are commonly used in process evaluations assessing fidelity. Likewise, the Carroll et al. conceptual framework also draws attention to other moderating factors, including the use of implementation strategies outside of each encounter [6]. While not necessarily observable in encounters, implementation strategies (e.g., academic detailing, audit and feedback, point of care reminders) are key features of implementation trials that target implementation outcomes, including fidelity. An expanded version of our implementation fidelity approach may need to include items assessing whether these strategies were deployed as intended, for example, whether clinicians using the CA participated in available training on its use. This may be a critical consideration for hybrid effectiveness-implementation trials, such as the parent trial for this study, that evaluate SDM to improve risk-treatment concordance and assess implementation processes (e.g., embedding the CA within the electronic workflow, deploying academic detailing and peer support) designed to enable and support it. Next steps for this work include further explication of the model to include data on implementation strategies, as well as surveys on clinicians' attitudes toward SDM and the CA.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of program theory and a conceptual framework to ground the investigation. Observers who completed the coding were also trained and experienced in the use of observer rating instruments, which bolsters confidence in the trustworthiness of the coding, the quality of discussions about coding choices, and the content of the coding manual.

Limitations include the small number of video-recorded encounters; although the limited number of adaptations suggests information sufficiency for item development, additional data may be needed to further develop the coding manual. Future validation studies with larger samples are also needed to examine whether SHARE-based adherence items, tool integration, quality of delivery, and participant responsiveness function as empirically distinct domains. Likewise, development was in varied healthcare settings, but there was limited heterogeneity of patient characteristics, and the instrument may not be sensitive to cultural factors. Adaptations may be needed to ensure relevance in different contexts, i.e., in different healthcare systems or populations, while maintaining core components related to mechanisms of change. Similarly, patients and clinicians were not involved in instrument development. Although the use of video-recorded routine clinical encounters and involvement of clinicians on the study team may have lessened the effect, future work, including work to ensure the tool or future adaptations are sensitive to unique patient contexts, should engage patients and clinicians in content validation.

Innovation

This study is innovative in its conceptual framing. Here, we demonstrate the feasibility of observing implementation fidelity of interventions to foster SDM (in this case, use of the CA as intended) and introduce an approach that distinguishes fidelity of CA implementation from, and links it to, the clinical practice of SDM that the CA is intended to support. This modified conceptual model of implementation fidelity guided our thinking about moderators and adherence when assessing how well clinicians practiced SDM. This framing extends implementation fidelity assessment for communication interventions by clarifying that “high fidelity” is not simply technical performance of a tool. In EHR-enabled and other tool-supported communication interventions, high fidelity also requires evidence that the tool was used in a way that supports the intended communication process, in this case SDM itself.

This distinction can help investigators interpret results in hybrid implementation-effectiveness studies and refine implementation strategies. For example, low fidelity related to tool integration may call for EHR redesign or technical assistance, whereas low fidelity in SHARE behaviors may call for clinician training or coaching. While this approach was developed for a hybrid implementation-effectiveness study, its usefulness may also extend to healthcare improvement efforts, as improvement teams are tasked with identifying root causes of care gaps (including variations in quality), deploying targeted interventions, and measuring success using process and patient-centered outcomes.

The SHARE approach was selected to identify clinician behaviors, in part, because it is amenable to assessment of observable behaviors and maps to strategies that could support clinician practice of SDM, including clinician training. There is a growing resource base of SHARE-informed interventions to promote SDM, including clinical decision support resources. Our work may support future EHR-enabled implementation studies evaluating SHARE and the implementation conditions under which EHR tools are most effective.

Conclusion

Our effort to develop an observer instrument, using the conceptual framework for implementation fidelity as a guide, suggests that observers are able to identify observable SDM moderators, including aspects of how patients and clinicians interact with tools such as CAs meant to foster SDM. The “modular” design of our approach may facilitate adaptation of these domains for use alongside other SDM observer instruments when investigators aim to evaluate implementation fidelity of an intervention like a CA separately.
