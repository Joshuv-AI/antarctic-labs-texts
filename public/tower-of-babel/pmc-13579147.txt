T cell-derived CCL5 drives macrophage immunometabolic remodeling in chronic prostatitis via the CCR5-ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α axis
Authors: Xianhong Liu, Boyang Li, Xianchao Dou, Wenyue Ju, Weihao Liu, Fei Zhang, Chen Jin, Yifeng Lu, Ronghao Wang, Xianguo Chen, Chaozhao Liang, Meng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) affects millions of men, yet the immune circuits that sustain sterile prostatic inflammation and pain remain poorly defined. Here we identify a T cell-macrophage metabolic signaling axis that drives chronic prostatitis. Integrated analysis of mouse experimental autoimmune prostatitis, chronically inflamed human prostate tissue, single-cell transcriptomes and spatial profiles revealed a population of glycolytic, HIF-1α-activated inflammatory macrophages enriched in diseased prostate niches. T cell-derived CCL5 was markedly induced and spatially positioned adjacent to CCR5+ macrophages, where it licensed M1-like polarization, glycolytic remodeling and inflammatory transcription. Using T cell-specific Ccl5 knockout mice, we established CCL5 as a genetic driver of macrophage inflammation, prostate tissue injury and pelvic pain hypersensitivity, and these pathogenic features were further suppressed by CCL5 neutralization or pharmacological CCR5 blockade. Mechanistically, rmCCL5 stimulation promoted CCR5-sensitive ERK1/2 activation, PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation, enhanced the PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, and increased HIF-1α occupancy at the Il1b and Nos2 promoters. Targeting ERK1/2, PKM2 remodeling or HIF-1α collapsed this program and protected against prostatitis pathology. Together, these findings show how T cell-derived chemokine signals are translated into macrophage metabolic reprogramming and persistent inflammatory activation, nominating the CCL5-CCR5-ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α pathway as a genetically validated and pharmacologically tractable therapeutic axis in CP/CPPS.Keywords: Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome, Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, T cell-derived CCL5, CCR5, Macrophage immunometabolism, PKM2/HIF-1α

Introduction

Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) is the most common form of prostatitis and one of the most prevalent urological conditions in men younger than 50 years [1,2]. It is characterized by persistent or recurrent pelvic pain, accompanied by variable lower urinary tract and sexual symptoms, and by a substantial impairment of quality of life [2,3]. Population- and clinic-based studies estimate a worldwide prevalence of roughly 8%, placing CP/CPPS among the most frequent chronic pelvic pain disorders [4,5]. Despite this clinical burden, treatment remains largely empirical and is dominated by symptom-directed anti-inflammatory or alpha-blocker therapy [6,7].

Emerging evidence from human studies and experimental models indicates that adaptive immunity, particularly autoreactive T cells, initiates prostatic inflammation [[8], [9], [10]]. The chronically inflamed prostate, however, is dominated by activated macrophages despite only modest and often transient T cell accumulation [11]. How adaptive immune cues are relayed to macrophages, and how a limited T cell response is converted into durable, macrophage-driven inflammation, remains poorly understood. A key clue comes from immunometabolism: upon activation, inflammatory macrophages shift toward aerobic glycolysis, which not only meets their bioenergetic demands but also reprograms inflammatory gene expression [12,13]. HIF-1α is a central regulator of this immunometabolic state, coupling glycolytic flux to the transcription of inflammatory genes, including Il1b [[14], [15], [16]]. PKM2, in turn, has emerged as a moonlighting metabolic enzyme that can be diverted from its canonical glycolytic role toward nuclear gene-regulatory functions [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Whether this metabolic-to-transcriptional circuitry is engaged downstream of a defined chemokine signal in chronic tissue inflammation, rather than in the settings of acute inflammation and cancer in which it has mostly been characterized, has remained unclear [[21], [22], [23]]. We previously reported that CCL5 is prominently elevated in chronic nonbacterial prostatitis and that the CCL5/CCR5/SHP2 axis sustains M1-like macrophage polarization through STAT1 and NF-κB signaling [24]. Because CCR5 can activate ERK1/2 and CCL5 can influence immune-cell glucose metabolism [25], we hypothesized that CCL5/CCR5 signaling might also drive a distinct macrophage immunometabolic program in CP/CPPS.

Here we define a spatially organized T cell-macrophage immunometabolic circuit that converts a limited adaptive immune signal into durable, macrophage-driven prostatic inflammation. By integrating mouse experimental autoimmune prostatitis (EAP) with human chronically inflamed prostate tissue, single-cell transcriptomics and spatial profiling, we show that T cell-derived CCL5 acts on CCR5+ macrophages to license M1-like polarization, glycolytic reprogramming and inflammatory transcription. Genetic deletion of T cell-derived Ccl5, together with antibody neutralization and pharmacological CCR5 blockade, establishes this chemokine as a causal driver of macrophage inflammation, prostate tissue injury and pelvic pain. Mechanistically, CCL5-CCR5 signaling engages ERK1/2 to phosphorylate PKM2 at Ser37, promoting nuclear PKM2 accumulation and enhanced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction and HIF-1α-associated inflammatory gene regulation. Collectively, these findings delineate a CCL5-CCR5-ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α axis that links chemokine sensing to macrophage metabolic-transcriptional remodeling and represents a pharmacologically tractable vulnerability in CP/CPPS.

Materials and methods

Human samples and ethics

Serum samples were collected from 37 outpatients with chronic prostatitis-like symptoms (CP-LS) and 11 age-matched healthy volunteer controls at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University for CCL5 measurement. The diagnosis of CP-LS and the participant inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined as described in our previous study [24]. Prostate tissue specimens were obtained from surgically resected tissues of patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and histologically confirmed prostate inflammation, and were used for immunofluorescence analysis. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animal models, prostatitis induction and in vivo interventions

Male wild-type mice on the C57BL/6 or NOD background were used as appropriate for the indicated prostatitis models. T cell-specific Ccl5 conditional knockout mice were generated using the Cre-loxP system by crossing Ccl5fl/fl mice, in which key exonic regions of Ccl5 were flanked by loxP sites, with Lck-Cre transgenic mice. Lck-Cre+;Ccl5fl/fl mice served as T cell-specific Ccl5 knockout mice, whereas Cre-negative Ccl5fl/fl littermates served as controls. Genomic DNA PCR followed by agarose gel electrophoresis was used for genotype confirmation. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water. Details of the wild-type strains and T cell-specific Ccl5 knockout mice used in this study, as well as genetic constructs, are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 7

EAP was induced in male mice by prostate antigen immunization with adjuvant, according to established protocols. Age-matched controls received adjuvant alone [11,24,26]. EAP mice were used for pharmacological intervention experiments. Where indicated, EAP mice received recombinant mouse CCL5 (rmCCL5), the CCR5 antagonist Maraviroc, the ERK1/2 inhibitor SCH772984, the PKM2 tetramer stabilizer TEPP-46 or the HIF-1α inhibitor Acriflavine. The corresponding doses, routes and treatment schedules are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

For genetic validation of T cell-derived CCL5, prostatitis was induced by local intraprostatic administration of carrageenan. Mice were assigned to four groups according to genotype and carrageenan treatment: (1) Ccl5fl/fl mice without carrageenan, (2) Ccl5fl/fl mice with carrageenan, (3) Lck-Cre+;Ccl5fl/fl mice without carrageenan and (4) Lck-Cre+;Ccl5fl/fl mice with carrageenan. This design allowed the effects of T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion to be assessed under basal conditions and during carrageenan-induced prostatitis. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized and prostate tissues were harvested for histology, flow cytometry, cytokine measurement, immunofluorescence staining and molecular analyses.

Behavioral testing, histology and inflammation scoring

Prostatitis-associated tactile allodynia was assessed by von Frey filament testing. Calibrated von Frey filaments were applied to the lower abdominal/pelvic region, and the response frequency to mechanical stimulation was recorded as an index of referred pelvic pain hypersensitivity. Behavioral assessments were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation.

Prostate tissues were fixed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Inflammatory cell infiltration, glandular architecture and stromal changes were evaluated by blinded observers. Histological inflammation was graded with an established semi-quantitative scoring system: 0, no inflammatory cell infiltration; 1, mild infiltration with scattered mononuclear cells; 2, moderate infiltration with focal inflammatory aggregates and perivascular inflammatory cuffs; and 3, severe inflammation with extensive infiltration involving the prostate parenchyma. Total inflammation scores were calculated as described previously.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomic analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), including GSE164695, GSE172357, GSE179615, GSE183676 and GSE226237 [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. Spatial transcriptomic datasets, including 10x Visium data (GSE178934), were also retrieved from GEO. These datasets were used to characterize immune cell composition, macrophage heterogeneity and spatial organization within the prostate microenvironment.

Spatial deconvolution of the transcriptomic data was performed with cell2location [32], a probabilistic modeling framework that infers the spatial distribution of cell types within tissue sections. Cell-cell communication was analyzed with COMMOT [33] to infer ligand-receptor signaling interactions and the directionality of CCL5-CCR5 communication within the spatial transcriptomic data.

Macrophage polarization states were quantified with AddModuleScore [34]. In human datasets, the metabolic pathway activity of inflammatory macrophages was inferred with COMPASS to assess pathway-level alterations relative to other stromal and immune populations [35]. For prostatitis-related single-cell datasets, macrophage subpopulations were re-analyzed from previously published datasets generated by our group-including re-clustering, differential expression and functional enrichment analysis-to further delineate the transcriptional and functional states of inflammatory macrophages [11]. The sequencing datasets used and analyzed in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Cell culture, CD3+ T-cell isolation and T cell-macrophage co-culture

RAW264.7 macrophages and iBMDMs were maintained in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Macrophages were stimulated with rmCCL5. Where indicated, cells were pretreated with Maraviroc, TEPP-46, Acriflavine or SCH772984 before rmCCL5 stimulation, and the inhibitors were maintained during stimulation. Cell-line and primary-cell information is provided in Supplementary Table 4, and working concentrations of the pharmacological reagents are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Mouse CD3+ T cells were isolated from spleens using a commercial isolation kit. Spleens were mechanically dissociated through a 70-μm strainer, followed by ACK lysis to remove red blood cells. Splenocytes were resuspended in PBS-based sorting buffer containing 2 mM EDTA and 2% FBS, incubated sequentially with the biotin-antibody cocktail and streptavidin magnetic beads at 4°C, and subjected to magnetic separation. Enriched CD3+ T cells were washed, resuspended in complete medium, and used for macrophage co-culture.

To model paracrine communication between T cells and macrophages, a 0.4-μm Transwell co-culture system was used. Isolated mouse CD3+ T cells were stimulated using plate-bound anti-CD3ε antibody (coated overnight at 4°C) together with soluble anti-CD28 antibody, and then co-cultured with RAW264.7 macrophages or iBMDMs. An anti-CCL5 neutralizing antibody was added where indicated. After co-culture, macrophages were collected to analyze polarization, inflammatory gene expression, PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation, PKM2 subcellular localization and related molecular mechanisms. Working concentrations and reagent information are provided in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6

Flow cytometry, MitoSOX analysis and immunofluorescence

Fresh mouse prostate tissues were collected in pre-cooled PBS, minced into small pieces and digested in DMEM containing Collagenase D (1 mg/mL), Collagenase Type I (0.5 mg/mL) and DNase I (100 μg/mL) at 37°C for 40 min, with gentle mixing every 10 min. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of DMEM containing 10% FBS. The resulting cell suspensions were passed through a 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300-400 × g for 5 min. Red blood cells were lysed where necessary, and the remaining cells were washed with PBS before flow-cytometric staining. For splenic macrophage analysis, spleens were mechanically dissociated through a 70-μm cell strainer, followed by red blood cell lysis and washing with PBS. Single-cell suspensions from mouse prostate tissue, spleens, or cultured macrophages were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies before flow-cytometric analysis. Debris and doublets were excluded based on FSC/SSC characteristics and FSC-A versus FSC-H. In spleen and prostate-tissue samples, macrophages were gated as CD11b+F4/80+ cells, followed by analysis of CD86+ M1-like and CD206+ M2-like populations. In cultured RAW264.7 macrophages and iBMDMs, F4/80+CD86+ cells were quantified as the M1-like population. For MitoSOX analysis, macrophages were incubated with MitoSOX Red (5 μM) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark, washed and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. MitoSOX fluorescence was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on fixed tissue sections or cultured cells using standard procedures. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI staining. Images were acquired by confocal microscopy and analyzed under identical acquisition settings. Antibody and reagent information is provided in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6, respectively.

Western blotting, subcellular fractionation and DSS cross-linking

Whole-cell, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using standard fractionation procedures and quantified by BCA assay. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, followed by ECL detection and imaging on a Tanon system. Antibody details are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

To evaluate PKM2 oligomeric status, DSS cross-linking was performed. Cells were washed with PBS to remove amine-containing components and treated with 0.5 mM DSS at room temperature for 30 min. Cross-linking was terminated by addition of 20 mM Tris-HCl for 15 min, after which cells were lysed and subjected to SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis to resolve PKM2 monomers, dimers and tetramers.

ELISA, RT-qPCR, lactate measurement and metabolic flux analysis

Cytokine ELISA. Cytokine levels, including CCL5, IL-1β and TNF-α, were measured in serum, tissue homogenates or culture supernatants using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. After incubation of standards and samples on antibody-coated plates at 37°C, biotinylated detection antibodies and HRP-conjugated reagents were sequentially applied, followed by TMB development. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and concentrations were calculated using standard curves. Kit information is provided in Supplementary Table 6.

RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA following standard procedures. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a real-time PCR system under standard cycling conditions. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to Actb. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Lactate assay. Intracellular lactate levels were measured using a colorimetric assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were lysed after PBS washing, and lysates were incubated with working reagent at 37°C in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and lactate levels were normalized to total protein. Kit information is provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Seahorse metabolic flux analysis. ECAR and OCR were measured using a Seahorse XF analyzer. RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded at 2 × 104 cells per well and treated with rmCCL5 for 24 h. Where indicated, cells were pretreated with Maraviroc, SCH772984, TEPP-46 or Acriflavine for 1 h. Glycolysis stress tests were performed by sequential injection of glucose, oligomycin and 2-deoxyglucose, whereas mitochondrial stress tests used oligomycin, FCCP and rotenone/antimycin A. Data were analyzed using Wave software according to manufacturer guidelines. Kit information is provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Proteomic/phosphoproteomic analysis and protein-protein docking

Global proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling of rmCCL5-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages was performed by mass spectrometry to identify regulated glycolysis-related proteins and phosphorylation sites; this analysis nominated PKM2 and PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation as candidate nodes for further mechanistic study. The potential interaction interface between PKM2 and HIF-1α was examined by in silico protein-protein docking using available structural models, which was used to explore a potential interaction interface consistent with the experimentally observed PKM2-HIF-1α association.

Plasmids, PKM2 mutants and HIF-1α reporter assay

Mouse wild-type and mutant PKM2 constructs were generated in a pcDNA3.1-3×Flag vector. The non-phosphorylatable PKM2-S37A and phosphomimetic PKM2-S37D mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis and verified by DNA sequencing. For PKM2 reconstitution, endogenous Pkm expression in RAW264.7 macrophages was knocked down with siRNA targeting the mouse Pkm 3′-UTR. Because the exogenous PKM2-WT, PKM2-S37A and PKM2-S37D plasmids contained only the coding sequence and lacked the endogenous Pkm 3′-UTR, they were resistant to this siRNA. Pkm-knockdown macrophages were then transfected with empty vector, PKM2-WT, PKM2-S37A or PKM2-S37D, followed by rmCCL5 stimulation, and were used to analyze PKM2 subcellular localization, macrophage polarization, HIF-1α reporter activity and the PKM2-HIF-1α interaction. Plasmids and genetic constructs are listed in Supplementary Table 8.

HIF-1α transcriptional activity was assessed using an HRE-driven dual-luciferase reporter assay. Cells were co-transfected with HRE-firefly and pRL-TK Renilla plasmids and stimulated with rmCCL5 for 12 h. Where indicated, cells were pretreated with Maraviroc, SCH772984, TEPP-46 or Acriflavine. PKM2 rescue experiments were performed in Pkm-knockdown cells using WT or mutant constructs. Luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla. Plasmids and constructs are listed in Supplementary Table 8.

Co-immunoprecipitation and ChIP-qPCR

Co-immunoprecipitation was used to examine the interaction between PKM2 and HIF-1α. For endogenous interaction assays, RAW264.7 macrophages or iBMDMs were treated with rmCCL5 with or without the indicated inhibitors and lysed in immunoprecipitation buffer. Equal amounts of lysate were incubated with anti-PKM2 antibody, anti-HIF-1α antibody or control IgG at 4°C, followed by Protein A/G beads to capture immune complexes. Immunoprecipitates and input samples were analyzed by Western blotting. In reciprocal assays, PKM2 or HIF-1α was immunoprecipitated and the corresponding partner was detected by Western blotting.

To examine whether PKM2 Ser37 status modulates PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, RAW264.7 macrophages were first engineered to express HA-tagged HIF-1α, followed by reconstitution with PKM2-WT or phosphorylation mutants (S37A and S37D). After rmCCL5 stimulation and/or inhibitor treatment as indicated, cells were subjected to co-immunoprecipitation. Flag-PKM2 was immunoprecipitated with an anti-Flag antibody, and co-precipitated HIF-1α-HA was detected with an anti-HA antibody. Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation was performed by capturing HIF-1α-HA with an anti-HA antibody and immunoblotting for Flag-PKM2 with an anti-Flag antibody. Normal IgG served as a negative control.

ChIP-qPCR was performed to assess HIF-1α occupancy at inflammatory gene promoters. Chromatin from cross-linked and sonicated macrophages was immunoprecipitated with anti-HIF-1α antibody or IgG control, and enriched DNA was quantified by qPCR. Where indicated, cells were pretreated with Maraviroc, TEPP-46 or SCH772984. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Major instruments and software

Details of the major instruments and software used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 9.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise indicated. For human studies, n represents individual participants or tissue specimens, as specified in the corresponding figure legends. For animal experiments, n represents individual animals, whereas for cell-based experiments, n represents independent biological experiments. Technical replicates were averaged before statistical analysis and were not treated as independent observations. Statistical tests were selected according to the experimental design and data characteristics. The principal statistical methods included an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test for comparisons between two independent groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple-comparisons test for comparisons among three or more groups. Pearson's correlation analysis was used to assess the association between serum CCL5 concentrations and NIH-CPSI scores. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Glycolysis/HIF-1α signaling is activated in inflammatory macrophages during chronic prostatitis

The overall study design is summarized in Fig. 1A. To characterize prostatitis-associated pathology, we first established an experimental prostatitis model. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed marked inflammatory cell infiltration, disruption of glandular architecture, stromal expansion and loss of normal glandular structures in prostate tissue from model mice relative to controls (Fig. 1B). Histological scoring confirmed a significant increase in prostate inflammation in model mice (Fig. 1C), and von Frey testing showed a higher response frequency to mechanical stimulation, indicating the development of prostatitis-associated pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 1D).

To characterize macrophage states during prostatitis, we applied our previously established macrophage annotation framework [11], which identified inflammatory (Inflam-Macro), repair-associated (Repair-Macro), tissue-resident (TRM-Macro), anti-inflammatory (Anti-Inflam-Macro), complement-associated (Comp-Macro), and proliferating (Prolif-Macro) subsets (Fig. 1E). Consistent with previous observations [11], inflammatory macrophage features were enriched in prostatitis samples. Polarization scoring showed that Inflam-Macro cells had a higher M1 score, whereas TRM-Macro cells were relatively enriched for M2-related features (Fig. 1F), consistent with a shift of inflammation-associated macrophages toward an M1-like state in the inflamed prostate. To further assess macrophage polarization in vivo, we examined macrophage phenotypes in the spleen by flow cytometry. The proportion of CD86+ M1-like cells among CD11b+F4/80+ splenic macrophages was significantly increased in EAP mice (Fig. 1G and H, and Supplementary Fig. 1A), whereas the proportion of CD206+ M2-like cells was decreased (Fig. 1I and J), indicating a systemic shift toward a proinflammatory macrophage phenotype. Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence staining of prostate tissue showed increased CD68+ macrophage accumulation and enhanced iNOS expression in EAP mice (CD68+/DAPI+: 4.24% vs 15.13%; CD68+iNOS+/CD68+: 16.54% vs 30.64%, CTR vs EAP; Fig. 1K). ELISA further revealed elevated TNF-α and IL-1β levels in prostate tissue from EAP mice, supporting a pronounced local M1-like inflammatory response (Fig. 1L).

We next asked whether inflammation-associated macrophages also undergo metabolic reprogramming. KEGG enrichment analysis of the Inflam-Macro subset showed significant enrichment of the HIF-1 and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways (Fig. 1M). Consistent with this pathway-level enrichment in the mouse prostatitis dataset, Hif1a was prominently expressed in inflammatory macrophage populations from EAP prostate tissue (Fig. 1N). To assess the relevance of these findings to human disease, we analyzed chronically inflamed human prostate samples. Human prostate macrophages resolved into distinct subsets and showed enrichment of M1-like polarization features in inflammation-associated macrophage populations (Fig. 1O and P), suggesting that macrophage inflammatory remodeling is conserved between mouse prostatitis and human inflamed prostate tissue. Metabolic module analysis further showed increased glycolysis/gluconeogenesis activity in inflammation-associated macrophages (Fig. 1Q, and Supplementary Fig. 1B), supporting glycolytic reprogramming within the inflamed prostate niche. Consistent with the single-cell pathway enrichment, immunofluorescence staining showed enhanced iNOS expression in M1-like macrophages, accompanied by increased GLUT1 and HIF-1α signals (Fig. 1R). Collectively, chronic prostatitis was characterized by prostate inflammation, pain hypersensitivity and M1-like macrophage polarization, with glycolysis/HIF-1α activation emerging as a key immunometabolic feature of inflammatory macrophage remodeling.

CCL5-CCR5 signaling drives macrophage inflammatory activation and glycolytic reprogramming

To identify upstream signals associated with macrophage inflammatory and metabolic activation in CP/CPPS, we revisited our previous 48-plex cytokine screen, which highlighted RANTES/CCL5 as one of the most prominently elevated mediators in patients with CP-LS [24]. In the present cohort, serum CCL5 was likewise increased and positively correlated with NIH-CPSI scores (Fig. 2A and B), linking CCL5 to clinical disease activity and prompting investigation of its role in macrophage polarization, glycolytic reprogramming and HIF-1α activation.

To determine whether CCL5 directly regulates macrophage inflammatory activation, we stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages with rmCCL5. Flow cytometry showed that rmCCL5 markedly increased the proportion of F4/80+CD86+ M1-like macrophages (Fig. 2C and D, and Supplementary Fig. 2A), and Western blotting confirmed increased expression of iNOS and CD86 after rmCCL5 treatment (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 2B). RT-qPCR further showed upregulation of Il1b and Tnf mRNA in rmCCL5-treated macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 2C), indicating that rmCCL5 is sufficient to induce M1-like inflammatory activation. We next asked whether rmCCL5 also promotes the metabolic phenotype seen in inflammatory macrophages during prostatitis. Seahorse ECAR analysis showed that rmCCL5 significantly increased glycolysis and glycolytic capacity (Fig. 2F and G), and lactate production rose accordingly (Fig. 2H). OCR analysis further showed that rmCCL5 reduced maximal respiration and ATP-linked oxygen consumption (Supplementary Fig. 2D), indicating impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and a shift toward a glycolysis-biased state. Consistent with these changes, HRE-driven dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that rmCCL5 significantly enhanced HIF-1α-dependent transcriptional activity (Fig. 2I). Together, these findings show that rmCCL5 promotes macrophage inflammatory activation and glycolysis-biased metabolic remodeling, accompanied by increased HIF-1α-dependent transcriptional activity.

We then tested whether exogenous rmCCL5 could aggravate prostatitis-associated pathology in vivo. Compared with EAP model mice, rmCCL5-treated EAP mice showed enhanced pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 2J) and more pronounced histological inflammatory changes in prostate tissue (Fig. 2K), although the increase in semiquantitative histological inflammation scores did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2L). Flow cytometry further showed an increased proportion of M1-like macrophages in the model + rmCCL5 group (Fig. 2M and N). These results indicate that rmCCL5 can exacerbate prostatitis progression and promote macrophage M1-like polarization in vivo.

To define the cellular source of CCL5 and its likely target population within the inflammatory microenvironment, we integrated single-cell and spatial analyses. Single-cell analysis showed that CCL5 was predominantly expressed in T lymphocyte-associated populations (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Multiplex immunofluorescence further demonstrated CCR5 expression in CD68+ macrophages and spatial proximity between CCL5+ T cells and CCR5+ macrophages in both model mouse prostate tissue and human chronically inflamed prostate samples (Fig. 2O). Consistently, spatial transcriptomic deconvolution combined with COMMOT analysis predicted a CCL5-CCR5 communication signal directed from T cells to macrophages within inflammatory regions (Fig. 2P, Supplementary Fig. 2F). These data provide spatial evidence that T cell-derived CCL5 may act in a paracrine manner on CCR5+ macrophages in the inflamed prostate microenvironment.

To assess the contribution of CCR5 signaling to the inflammatory and metabolic effects of rmCCL5, we treated macrophages with the CCR5 antagonist Maraviroc. Maraviroc significantly suppressed rmCCL5-induced expansion of F4/80+CD86+ M1-like macrophages and reduced iNOS and CD86 expression (Fig. 2Q–S, Supplementary Fig. 2G). Metabolic analysis showed that Maraviroc attenuated the rmCCL5-induced increases in glycolysis and glycolytic capacity (Fig. 2T, Supplementary Fig. 2H). Consistent with these functional changes, immunoblot analysis showed that rmCCL5 increased HK2, GLUT1, and LDHA protein abundance, whereas these increases were attenuated by Maraviroc (Supplementary Fig. 2I). Maraviroc partially restored the rmCCL5-induced reductions in maximal respiration and ATP-linked oxygen consumption (Supplementary Fig. 2J–K), and decreased lactate accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 2L). In parallel, flow-cytometric MitoSOX analysis showed that rmCCL5 significantly increased MitoSOX fluorescence intensity, whereas Maraviroc attenuated this increase (Fig. 2U; Supplementary Fig. 2M–N). Together with the changes in inflammatory markers, these findings indicate that rmCCL5-induced M1-like activation and immunometabolic reprogramming of macrophages depend, at least in part, on CCR5 signaling. Finally, we asked whether CCR5 blockade could mitigate rmCCL5-driven aggravation of prostatitis in vivo. Maraviroc significantly reduced rmCCL5-induced mechanical hypersensitivity (Fig. 2V) and partially improved prostate histopathology (Fig. 2W), although the reduction in histological inflammation scores did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2X). Flow cytometry further showed that Maraviroc attenuated rmCCL5-induced M1-like macrophage polarization (Fig. 2Y), whereas HRE-driven reporter analysis showed reduced HIF-1α-dependent transcriptional activity following Maraviroc treatment (Fig. 2Z). Collectively, these findings link elevated CCL5 in CP-LS to macrophage inflammatory and glycolytic remodeling and support an important contribution of CCR5-associated signaling.

To genetically validate the role of T cell-derived CCL5 in prostate inflammation in vivo, we generated T cell-specific Ccl5 conditional knockout mice using the Cre-loxP system, with loxP sites flanking key exonic regions of Ccl5 (Fig. 3A). Lck-Cre mice were crossed with Ccl5fl/fl mice to generate T cell-specific Ccl5 knockout mice and littermate controls (Fig. 3B). Genotyping PCR with gel electrophoresis confirmed the floxed allele and the Lck-Cre transgene, indicating successful generation of the T cell-specific Ccl5 conditional knockout model (Fig. 3C).

To evaluate the contribution of T cell-derived CCL5 to local prostate inflammation, we induced prostatitis with carrageenan and compared Ccl5fl/fl control mice with Lck-Cre+;Ccl5fl/fl mice under basal and inflammation-induced conditions. Von Frey testing showed that carrageenan increased the response frequency to mechanical stimulation, and that T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion partially alleviated this pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 3D). H&E staining and histological scoring further showed that carrageenan-induced prostate inflammation was attenuated in Lck-Cre+;Ccl5fl/fl mice (Fig. 3E and F). Flow cytometric analysis of CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages showed that carrageenan increased the proportion of CD86+ M1-like macrophages and decreased that of CD206+ M2-like macrophages, whereas T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion reduced the M1-like expansion and partially restored the M2-like population (Fig. 3G, and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Consistently, ELISA showed that T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion reduced the carrageenan-induced increases in TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 3H). Immunofluorescence staining further showed that T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion reduced the accumulation of CD68+iNOS + M1-like inflammatory macrophages in inflamed prostate regions after carrageenan challenge (Fig. 3I). Collectively, T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion alleviated carrageenan-induced prostate inflammation and pain hypersensitivity while suppressing macrophage M1-like polarization and inflammatory cytokine production in vivo. These genetic data reinforce T cell-derived CCL5 as a key upstream signal that promotes macrophage inflammatory activation in the prostatitis microenvironment.

CCL5/CCR5 signaling induces PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear PKM2 accumulation

Proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling of rmCCL5-stimulated macrophages identified PKM2 as a prominent glycolysis-related node altered by CCL5 signaling, with changes in both protein abundance and phosphorylation (Fig. 4A). Although the overall phosphorylation of PKM2 tended to decrease, phosphorylation at Ser37 was markedly increased (Fig. 4B and C, and Supplementary Fig. 3B), indicating site-specific phosphorylation remodeling rather than a uniform rise in total PKM2 phosphorylation. PKM2 and its Ser37 phosphorylation were therefore selected for further mechanistic study. Western blotting validated the omics findings: rmCCL5 increased PKM2 protein, and the CCR5 antagonist Maraviroc attenuated this upregulation (Fig. 4D and E); rmCCL5 also markedly increased p-PKM2 Ser37 levels, an effect partially suppressed by Maraviroc (Fig. 4F and G, Supplementary Fig. 4A). These results indicate that CCL5-mediated regulation of PKM2 expression and Ser37 phosphorylation depends, at least in part, on CCR5 signaling.

Because the noncanonical functions of PKM2 are closely tied to its subcellular localization, we next examined whether rmCCL5 affects PKM2 nuclear distribution. Immunofluorescence staining and line-scan analysis showed increased nuclear enrichment of PKM2 after rmCCL5 stimulation, which was reduced by Maraviroc (Fig. 4H). Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation further showed that rmCCL5 promoted the nuclear accumulation of PKM2, particularly its Ser37-phosphorylated form, whereas CCR5 blockade with Maraviroc markedly attenuated this effect, as reflected by reduced nuclear p-PKM2/PKM2 and p-PKM2/H3 ratios (Fig. 4I and J; Supplementary Fig. 4B). Consistently, chemical cross-linking assays demonstrated that rmCCL5 shifted PKM2 toward an altered oligomeric state, and this change was reversed by Maraviroc (Fig. 4K and L). Thus, CCL5/CCR5 signaling enhances PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation, accompanied by PKM2 nuclear accumulation and a change in its functional state. To test whether the functional state of PKM2 contributes to rmCCL5-induced macrophage inflammatory and metabolic phenotypes, we treated cells with the PKM2 tetramer stabilizer TEPP-46. TEPP-46 markedly reduced rmCCL5-induced expansion of F4/80+CD86+ M1-like macrophages and decreased expression of iNOS, CD86 and inflammatory mediators (Fig. 4M–O), and RT-qPCR and ELISA confirmed reduced rmCCL5-induced IL-1β and TNF-α (Fig. 4P, Supplementary Fig. 4C). Metabolically, TEPP-46 attenuated rmCCL5-induced increases in iNOS and GLUT1 signals (Fig. 4Q). TEPP-46 also lowered ECAR, glycolysis, and glycolytic capacity, while partially restoring maximal respiration and ATP production (Fig. 4R). Consistent with these functional metabolic changes, immunoblot analysis showed that TEPP-46 attenuated the rmCCL5-induced increases in HK2, GLUT1, and LDHA protein abundance (Supplementary Fig. 4D). TEPP-46 further decreased lactate production and HIF-1α reporter activity (Fig. 4S and T). In parallel, flow-cytometric MitoSOX analysis showed that TEPP-46 significantly attenuated the rmCCL5-induced increase in MitoSOX fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Collectively, CCL5/CCR5 signaling increases PKM2 expression and Ser37 phosphorylation with accompanying nuclear accumulation, and the inhibitory effects of TEPP-46 on M1-like activation, glycolysis and HIF-1α activity identify PKM2 functional remodeling as a key molecular link between CCL5/CCR5 signaling and macrophage inflammatory metabolic reprogramming.

Relative to macrophages cultured alone, co-culture with CD3+ T cells markedly increased the proportion of F4/80+CD86+ M1-like RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 5B and C) and upregulated iNOS and CD86 protein (Fig. 5D), indicating that T cells can promote macrophage M1-like inflammatory activation through paracrine signaling.

To determine whether this effect was mediated by T cell-derived CCL5, we added an anti-CCL5 neutralizing antibody to the co-culture. CD3+ T cell co-culture increased p-PKM2 Ser37 levels in RAW264.7 macrophages, and anti-CCL5 treatment blunted this response (Fig. 5E). Consistently, confocal imaging showed enhanced nuclear accumulation of PKM2 in macrophages co-cultured with CD3+ T cells, which was reduced by anti-CCL5 (Fig. 5F). In parallel, anti-CCL5 significantly attenuated the co-culture-induced increase in F4/80+CD86+ M1-like macrophages (Fig. 5G and H) and reduced iNOS and CD86 protein (Fig. 5I). These findings indicate that T cell-induced macrophage M1-like activation, PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and PKM2 nuclear accumulation depend, at least in part, on CCL5.

To validate these findings in a more physiologically relevant macrophage model, we performed parallel experiments in iBMDMs. As in RAW264.7 cells, CD3+ T cell co-culture increased p-PKM2 Ser37 levels and promoted M1-like activation in iBMDMs, and anti-CCL5 treatment partially reversed these effects (Fig. 5J–M), supporting a pro-inflammatory role of T cell-derived CCL5 in a primary-like macrophage context. Collectively, T cell-derived CCL5 promotes macrophage M1-like activation, PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation through paracrine signaling, and CCL5 neutralization attenuates these effects, identifying T cell-derived CCL5 as an upstream driver of PKM2-centered macrophage remodeling in the inflamed prostate.

CCL5 drives PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to promote macrophage inflammation

To determine whether PKM2 functional remodeling contributes to rmCCL5-driven prostatitis pathology in vivo, experimental prostatitis model mice were treated with rmCCL5 followed by intervention with TEPP-46. rmCCL5 aggravated pelvic mechanical hypersensitivity, whereas TEPP-46 markedly reduced the response frequency to mechanical stimulation (Fig. 6A). Histological analysis showed that rmCCL5 exacerbated inflammatory cell infiltration, glandular disruption and stromal inflammatory expansion in prostate tissues, while TEPP-46 attenuated these pathological changes (Fig. 6B). Consistently, histological inflammation scores showed a downward trend following TEPP-46 treatment, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6C). Flow cytometric analysis further showed that rmCCL5 increased CD86+ M1-like macrophages and decreased CD206+ M2-like macrophages, whereas TEPP-46 suppressed M1-like macrophage expansion and partially restored the M2-like population (Fig. 6D). In parallel, ELISA analysis showed that TEPP-46 reduced the rmCCL5-induced increase in IL-1β and TNF-α levels (Fig. 6E). These findings identify PKM2 functional remodeling as a driver of rmCCL5-aggravated prostatitis pathology, macrophage inflammatory polarization and cytokine production in vivo.

To define the role of PKM2 Ser37 in macrophage responses to rmCCL5 stimulation, we employed a mutant rescue strategy. Endogenous PKM2 expression in RAW264.7 macrophages was first silenced using siRNA targeting the mouse Pkm 3′-UTR (Fig. 6F), followed by reconstitution with siRNA-resistant Flag-tagged wild-type PKM2 (PKM2-WT), the phosphomimetic mutant PKM2-S37D, or the non-phosphorylatable mutant PKM2-S37A (Fig. 6G). Following rmCCL5 stimulation, nuclear Flag-PKM2 was readily detected in PKM2-WT-reconstituted cells and was further enhanced in PKM2-S37D cells, whereas PKM2-S37A showed impaired nuclear accumulation (Fig. 6H). These data suggest that Ser37 phosphorylation promotes rmCCL5-induced nuclear translocation of PKM2.

We next asked whether this Ser37-dependent nuclear remodeling was functionally linked to macrophage inflammatory activation. Flow cytometry showed that PKM2-WT and PKM2-S37D supported rmCCL5-induced expansion of CD86+ M1-like macrophages, whereas PKM2-S37A blunted this response (Fig. 6I and J). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the Ser37-dependent nuclear localization pattern: PKM2-WT and PKM2-S37D displayed prominent nuclear enrichment after rmCCL5 stimulation, whereas PKM2-S37A showed weaker nuclear localization and remained predominantly extranuclear (Fig. 6K). Consistently, HIF-1α luciferase reporter assays showed that PKM2-WT and PKM2-S37D potentiated rmCCL5-induced HIF-1α transcriptional activity, while PKM2-S37A markedly weakened this effect (Fig. 6L). Together, these results identify PKM2 Ser37 as a phosphorylation-dependent switch that converts CCL5 signaling into nuclear PKM2 activity, HIF-1α-dependent transcription and macrophage inflammatory remodeling in chronic prostatitis.

PKM2 Ser37 remodeling enhances PKM2-HIF-1α interaction and inflammatory gene regulation

Given that rmCCL5 promoted PKM2 nuclear accumulation and HIF-1α transcriptional activity, we next asked whether nuclear PKM2 is associated with HIF-1α-dependent inflammatory gene regulation. Protein docking predicted a potential interaction interface between PKM2 and HIF-1α (Fig. 7A). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation confirmed that rmCCL5 enhanced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, whereas Maraviroc attenuated complex formation, indicating that this interaction is promoted by CCL5-CCR5 signaling (Fig. 7B–E). We then examined whether PKM2 Ser37 status modulates PKM2-HIF-1α interaction under rmCCL5 stimulation. In rescue experiments, PKM2 interacted with HIF-1α, with the phosphomimetic Ser37D mutant showing stronger binding than PKM2-WT, whereas the non-phosphorylatable Ser37A mutant exhibited reduced HIF-1α interaction (Fig. 7F and G). These findings indicate that PKM2 Ser37 status modulates PKM2-HIF-1α association in rmCCL5-stimulated macrophages. Functionally, inhibition of HIF-1α with Acriflavine suppressed rmCCL5-induced F4/80+CD86+ M1-like macrophage expansion and reduced iNOS and CD86 expression in vitro (Fig. 7H and I). Consistently, Acriflavine reduced rmCCL5-induced Il1b and Tnf expression (Supplementary Fig. 5A), further supporting the involvement of HIF-1α activity in rmCCL5-driven macrophage inflammatory activation.

We next examined whether Acriflavine also affected the metabolic remodeling induced by rmCCL5. Acriflavine partially restored maximal respiration and ATP production (Supplementary Fig. 5B–C) and reduced lactate accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Consistent with these functional metabolic changes, immunoblot analysis showed that Acriflavine attenuated the rmCCL5-induced increases in HK2, GLUT1, and LDHA protein abundance (Supplementary Fig. 5E). In parallel, flow-cytometric MitoSOX analysis showed that Acriflavine significantly reduced the rmCCL5-induced increase in MitoSOX fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 5F). Together with the preceding inflammatory findings, these results show that Acriflavine attenuates rmCCL5-induced macrophage inflammatory activation and glycolysis-biased metabolic remodeling.

We next examined whether the inhibitory effects of Acriflavine observed in vitro were accompanied by protection against rmCCL5-aggravated prostatitis in vivo. Acriflavine reduced pelvic mechanical hypersensitivity, attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration and glandular disruption, and lowered histological inflammation scores (Fig. 7J–L). It also decreased IL-1β and TNF-α levels and reduced CD86+ M1-like macrophage polarization while partially restoring CD206+ M2-like macrophages (Fig. 7M–O). To further determine whether the CCL5-CCR5-PKM2 signaling axis regulates HIF-1α recruitment to inflammatory gene promoters, ChIP-qPCR analysis was performed. rmCCL5 treatment significantly increased HIF-1α enrichment at the Il1b and Nos2 promoter regions (Fig. 7P). In contrast, both Maraviroc and TEPP-46 attenuated rmCCL5-induced HIF-1α occupancy at these promoters (Fig. 7Q and R). Collectively, these findings show that PKM2 Ser37 remodeling is associated with enhanced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction and increased HIF-1α occupancy at inflammatory gene promoters, supporting the involvement of this signaling module in macrophage inflammatory gene regulation and prostatitis pathology.

ERK links CCL5-CCR5 signaling to the PKM2-HIF-1α inflammatory transcriptional program

We next sought to delineate how CCR5 signaling is transmitted to the PKM2-centered transcriptional program. Given that ERK1/2 is a canonical downstream MAPK effector of CCR5 activation, we first examined ERK1/2 phosphorylation following rmCCL5 stimulation. Western blot analysis showed that rmCCL5 markedly increased ERK1/2 phosphorylation in RAW264.7 macrophages, whereas Maraviroc attenuated this response, indicating that CCL5 activates ERK1/2 through CCR5 (Fig. 8A and B, Supplementary Fig. 6A).

We then used the ERK inhibitor SCH772984 to determine whether ERK signaling couples CCL5-CCR5 activation to PKM2-HIF-1α remodeling. SCH772984 markedly reduced rmCCL5-induced nuclear accumulation of Ser37-phosphorylated PKM2 and concomitantly suppressed HIF-1α reporter activity, indicating that ERK activation promotes PKM2 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to sustain HRE-driven HIF-1α-dependent transcription (Fig. 8C and D; Supplementary Fig. 6B). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation further showed that ERK inhibition weakened rmCCL5-induced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, whether HIF-1α or PKM2 was used for immunoprecipitation (Fig. 8E and F). These data place ERK upstream of CCL5-induced PKM2 nuclear accumulation, PKM2-HIF-1α complex formation and HIF-1α transcriptional activation. Functionally, SCH772984 reduced rmCCL5-induced iNOS and CD86 expression and decreased the proportion of F4/80+CD86+ M1-like macrophages (Fig. 8G and H; Supplementary Fig. 6C). Metabolic flux analysis showed that ERK inhibition attenuated rmCCL5-induced increases in ECAR, basal glycolysis and glycolytic capacity, while partially restoring maximal respiration and ATP-linked oxygen consumption (Fig. 8I–L). Consistent with these functional metabolic changes, immunoblot analysis further demonstrated that SCH772984 attenuated the rmCCL5-induced increases in HK2, GLUT1, and LDHA protein abundance (Supplementary Fig. 6D). SCH772984 also reduced rmCCL5-induced Il1b and Tnf expression as well as lactate production (Supplementary Fig. 6E–F). In parallel, flow-cytometric MitoSOX analysis showed that SCH772984 significantly reduced the rmCCL5-induced increase in MitoSOX fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 6G). Collectively, these results identify ERK signaling as an important intermediate node linking CCL5-CCR5 activation to PKM2-HIF-1α-dependent macrophage inflammatory and glycolytic remodeling. In vivo, SCH772984 alleviated rmCCL5-aggravated prostatitis pathology in EAP mice. Compared with rmCCL5-treated EAP mice, SCH772984-treated mice showed reduced pelvic mechanical hypersensitivity and improved prostate histopathology, although the reduction in semiquantitative histological inflammation scores did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 8M and N). ERK inhibition also decreased CD86+ M1-like macrophage polarization, partially restored CD206+ M2-like macrophages and reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels (Fig. 8O and P). ChIP-qPCR further showed that SCH772984 attenuated rmCCL5-induced HIF-1α enrichment at the Il1b and Nos2 promoters (Fig. 8Q), supporting a role for ERK in linking CCL5-CCR5 signaling to HIF-1α-dependent inflammatory transcription. We next investigated whether this transcriptional program could reinforce CCL5 signaling itself. Single-cell analysis showed that Ccl5 expression was enriched in inflammatory macrophage subsets, particularly in the Inflam-Macro population (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Consistently, rmCCL5 stimulation upregulated Ccl5 mRNA expression in macrophages, whereas blockade of CCR5, inhibition of ERK signaling, disruption of PKM2 nuclear remodeling, or suppression of HIF-1α activity attenuated this induction (Supplementary Fig. 7B). ChIP-qPCR further demonstrated increased HIF-1α enrichment at the Ccl5 promoter following rmCCL5 stimulation, which was reduced by Maraviroc, SCH772984 and TEPP-46 (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Collectively, these findings suggest that inflammatory macrophages, upon receiving T cell-derived CCL5 signals, may establish a HIF-1α-dependent positive feedback loop that amplifies local CCL5 signaling and sustains inflammatory activation.

Collectively, this study defines a T cell-macrophage immunometabolic circuit in chronic prostatitis. In this circuit, T cell-derived CCL5 activates macrophage CCR5-ERK1/2 signaling, leading to PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation, nuclear PKM2 accumulation, and enhanced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction and HIF-1α-associated inflammatory transcription. This signaling axis converts chemokine sensing into glycolytic M1-like macrophage remodeling, inflammatory gene transcription, prostate tissue inflammation, and pain hypersensitivity, thereby establishing a mechanistically coherent and therapeutically targetable pathway in macrophage-driven chronic prostatic inflammation (Fig. 9).

Working model of the T cell-derived CCL5-CCR5-associated ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α program in chronic prostatitis. T cell-derived CCL5 engages CCR5 on macrophages and induces coordinated metabolic and transcriptional remodeling of PKM2. CCL5 increases overall PKM2 abundance in parallel with enhanced glycolytic activity, while CCR5-ERK signaling specifically promotes PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation. CCL5 stimulation is also accompanied by an increased PKM2 dimer-to-tetramer ratio, a state associated with non-canonical nuclear co-regulatory activity. Nuclear phosphorylated PKM2 interacts with HIF-1α, while increased HIF-1α occupancy at the Il1b, Nos2 and Ccl5 promoters contributes to inflammatory gene regulation. CCL5-induced metabolic remodeling is also accompanied by increased MitoSOX fluorescence. Together, these metabolic and nuclear functions of PKM2 promote M1 macrophage inflammatory activation, sustain prostatic inflammation, and exacerbate pain hypersensitivity. T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion and pharmacological targeting of CCR5, ERK, PKM2 remodeling or HIF-1α attenuate prostatitis-associated pathology in vivo, whereas CCL5 neutralization disrupts T cell-induced macrophage activation in vitro.

Discussion

A central question in CP/CPPS research is how a relatively limited adaptive immune response is translated and amplified into persistent, macrophage-dominated tissue inflammation and pain [11,24,36,37]. This study identifies a spatially organized T cell-macrophage immunometabolic circuit that provides a new mechanistic framework for explaining this process. By integrating experimental prostatitis models, inflamed human prostate tissues, single-cell and spatial omics analyses, T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion, and interventions targeting distinct signaling nodes, we found that T cell-derived CCL5 serves as a key upstream signal driving macrophage inflammatory activation and metabolic reprogramming. Through activation of a CCR5-associated ERK-PKM2 signaling program, CCL5 promotes PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation, nuclear accumulation, and enhanced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, thereby facilitating glycolytic reprogramming and inflammatory gene expression and ultimately promoting M1-like macrophage activation, prostate inflammation, and pain hypersensitivity. These findings establish a continuous mechanistic cascade linking intercellular immune communication and metabolic enzyme repurposing to inflammatory transcriptional output, providing a new explanation for how adaptive immune signals are converted into persistent macrophage-driven inflammation.

This study further extends our previous finding that CCL5 is markedly elevated in chronic nonbacterial prostatitis and promotes M1-like macrophage polarization through the CCR5-SHP2-STAT1/NF-κB pathway [24]. Whereas our previous work primarily demonstrated that CCL5 activates canonical proinflammatory signaling in macrophages, the present study further shows that CCL5 reinforces the proinflammatory macrophage state by remodeling glucose metabolism and metabolism-dependent transcriptional programs. CCL5 may therefore regulate the inflammatory macrophage phenotype through two complementary mechanisms: activation of canonical inflammatory pathways, including STAT1 and NF-κB, and induction of ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α-mediated metabolic-transcriptional reprogramming. Spatial omics analysis revealed close spatial proximity between CCL5-positive T cells and inflammatory macrophages within the lesions. T cell-macrophage coculture, CCL5 neutralization, and T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion further supported a paracrine role for T cell-derived CCL5. Although spatial proximity and computational inference alone are insufficient to establish a direct ligand-receptor causal relationship, the combined genetic, antibody-neutralization, and coculture evidence supports T cells as an important cellular source of CCL5 in the inflammatory prostate microenvironment. At the tissue level, rmCCL5 treatment was also accompanied by an increased proportion of CD86+ M1-like macrophages and a decreased proportion of CD206+ M2-like macrophages, whereas interventions targeting PKM2, HIF-1α, or ERK partially reversed this shift to varying degrees. These findings suggest that CCL5-driven immunometabolic reprogramming not only enhances proinflammatory macrophage activation but may also shift the relative phenotypic composition of CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages toward an M1-like state within the prostate.

The principal mechanistic novelty of this study lies in positioning the PKM2-HIF-1α metabolic-transcriptional module within a spatially organized T cell-macrophage communication network in chronic prostatitis. PKM2 is not only a key metabolic enzyme in glycolysis but can also exert noncanonical nuclear regulatory functions through changes in its conformation and subcellular localization. Previous studies have linked PKM2 to Warburg-like metabolism in macrophages, HIF-1α/STAT3 activation, and IL-1β expression [17,19,[38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. However, how this program is initiated and persistently maintained by intercellular signals within chronically inflamed tissues remains incompletely understood [43]. Our proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses identified PKM2 and its Ser37 residue as key nodes altered following CCL5 stimulation. Further experiments showed that CCL5 promoted PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation. Pharmacological stabilization of the PKM2 tetramer with TEPP-46 [44] markedly attenuated the inflammatory and metabolic phenotypes induced by CCL5, supporting the notion that alteration of the functional state of PKM2 represents an important link between upstream chemokine signaling and macrophage immunometabolic reprogramming.

PKM2 mutant rescue experiments further established the functional importance of Ser37 phosphorylation. The phosphomimetic S37D mutant enhanced PKM2 nuclear accumulation, PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, and HIF-1α transcriptional activity, whereas the nonphosphorylatable S37A mutant attenuated these effects. These findings support a role for Ser37 phosphorylation in promoting the transition of PKM2 from a cytoplasmic metabolic enzyme to a nuclear coregulator, rather than representing a merely accompanying change. Moreover, the enhanced PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, together with the corresponding increase in HIF-1α enrichment at the Il1b and Nos2 promoter regions, supports the involvement of nuclear PKM2 in HIF-1α-dependent inflammatory gene regulation. These observations provide a mechanistic basis for the ability of CCL5 to sustain the proinflammatory macrophage state through functional repurposing of a metabolic enzyme.

ERK1/2 served as an important signaling intermediary in this process. ERK inhibition not only reduced PKM2 Ser37 phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation but also attenuated PKM2-HIF-1α interaction, HIF-1α transcriptional activity, glycolytic remodeling, and inflammatory gene expression. These results support ERK1/2 as an important signaling relay connecting upstream CCL5 stimulation to PKM2 nuclear remodeling and HIF-1α-associated transcriptional activity. Previous studies support this signaling relationship by showing that, in different macrophage models, CCL5-CCR5 signaling activates ERK1/2 through the Gαi-MEK-ERK pathway and promotes inflammation-related responses, suggesting that CCR5 may serve as an upstream receptor linking CCL5 sensing to ERK activation [45,46]. Consistent with these findings, pharmacological blockade with the selective CCR5 antagonist maraviroc attenuated CCL5-induced ERK1/2 activation and downstream PKM2 remodeling, further supporting the involvement of CCR5-associated signaling in this process.

It should be noted, however, that CCL5 can also signal through CCR1 and CCR3 [47,48]. Previous studies have suggested that CCR1 may participate in CCL5-induced MAPK activation and M1-like polarization in certain macrophage models [47]. Thus, our findings support an important role for CCR5-associated signaling in the CCL5-induced ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α program, although contributions from other receptors, including CCR1 and CCR3, cannot be excluded. Although ERK-mediated nuclear translocation of PKM2 has previously been studied primarily in the context of tumor-associated transcriptional programs [49,50], our findings extend this mechanism to chronic non-neoplastic tissue inflammation and suggest that ERK-PKM2 signaling may constitute an important interface between chemokine sensing and metabolic-transcriptional coupling in macrophages. Beyond glycolytic remodeling, CCL5 treatment also increased the MitoSOX fluorescence signal, and this effect was attenuated by pharmacological interventions targeting CCR5, ERK, PKM2, or HIF-1α, providing additional evidence of an increased mitochondrial superoxide-associated signal accompanying CCL5-induced macrophage metabolic remodeling. In parallel, CCL5 stimulation increased HIF-1α enrichment at the Ccl5 promoter and elevated endogenous Ccl5 mRNA expression in macrophages, suggesting a potential HIF-1α-associated feed-forward amplification mechanism involving macrophage-derived CCL5. Such a mechanism may help convert an initial paracrine signal from T cells into a more persistent local inflammatory output.

The immunometabolic program identified in this study provides several potential therapeutic entry points at different levels of the signaling cascade. At the upstream level, T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion alleviated prostate inflammation and pain, whereas CCL5 neutralization attenuated macrophage inflammatory activation and PKM2 remodeling induced by T cell coculture, suggesting that restricting T cell-derived CCL5 may disrupt the initiation of this inflammatory circuit. At the receptor level, maraviroc, a CCR5 antagonist clinically approved for the treatment of CCR5-tropic HIV-1 infection [51], attenuated CCL5-induced macrophage inflammatory and metabolic activation in vitro and alleviated prostate inflammation, histopathological injury, and pain hypersensitivity in vivo. At the signaling relay level, ERK inhibition disrupted signal transmission from CCR5-associated signaling to the PKM2-HIF-1α program. At the metabolic-transcriptional level, pharmacological stabilization of the PKM2 tetramer with TEPP-46 [44] attenuated rmCCL5-induced inflammatory and metabolic responses, whereas inhibition of HIF-1α with acriflavine [52,53] reduced macrophage activation and prostate tissue inflammation. The convergence of genetic deletion, antibody neutralization, mutant rescue, and mechanistically distinct pharmacological interventions provides complementary mechanistic evidence supporting the involvement of the CCL5-CCR5-ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α axis in chronic prostatitis. Collectively, these findings suggest that targeting this pathway at the chemokine, receptor, signaling relay, metabolic-transcriptional, or transcription factor level may provide a mechanism-based strategy complementary to current symptom-directed therapies for CP/CPPS.

This study has several limitations. First, although we integrated genetic, pharmacological, single-cell, and spatial omics evidence, the findings obtained from human tissues remain largely associative and do not directly establish a causal role for this immunometabolic program in human CP/CPPS. Future studies should examine the relationships among CCL5 levels, macrophage metabolic states, symptom severity, and treatment responses in larger cohorts with well-defined clinical phenotypes and longitudinal follow-up data. Second, T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion was evaluated primarily in the carrageenan-induced prostatitis model, whereas other experiments were conducted using the experimental autoimmune prostatitis model. Although the consistency of the findings across models strengthens the robustness of our conclusions, differences in the mechanisms of inflammatory initiation, patterns of immune-cell infiltration, and disease progression between these models may affect mechanistic interpretation. Third, although the role of PKM2 was further validated through Ser37 mutant rescue experiments, the functions of ERK and HIF-1α were assessed mainly using pharmacological interventions, which may have off-target effects. Future studies incorporating macrophage-specific gene deletion or conditional mutant models are therefore required to further define the cell-intrinsic roles of these signaling nodes. Fourth, the involvement of CCR5 was evaluated mainly through pharmacological blockade with maraviroc, without genetic validation of Ccr5 or parallel comparisons among different CCL5 receptors. Therefore, although the current findings support an important role for CCR5, the involvement of other receptors, including CCR1 and CCR3, cannot be excluded. Fifth, flow cytometric analyses in this study relied primarily on CD86 and CD206 to characterize macrophage phenotypes and reported the relative proportions of each subset within CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages. Because tissue-normalized absolute cell counts were not performed, the absolute abundance of M1-like and M2-like macrophages in prostate tissues remains undetermined. Sixth, although the current evidence supports the joint involvement of PKM2 and HIF-1α in inflammatory gene regulation, the precise molecular mechanism through which these factors cooperate to regulate inflammatory transcription remains to be elucidated.

In conclusion, this study identifies a macrophage immunometabolic circuit driven by T cell-derived CCL5. Through a CCR5-associated ERK-PKM2/HIF-1α signaling program, CCL5 promotes PKM2 Ser37-dependent nuclear remodeling, HIF-1α-dependent inflammatory gene regulation, and glycolytic reprogramming, thereby driving M1-like macrophage activation, prostate inflammation, and pain hypersensitivity. T cell-specific Ccl5 deletion, CCL5 neutralization in vitro, and multilevel interventions targeting ERK, PKM2, and HIF-1α provide complementary evidence supporting the involvement of this immunometabolic program in the pathogenesis of chronic prostatitis. These findings provide a new mechanistic framework for understanding how T cell-derived signals are converted into persistent macrophage-driven inflammation and suggest that the CCL5-driven macrophage immunometabolic program may represent a potential therapeutic axis worthy of further investigation in CP/CPPS.
