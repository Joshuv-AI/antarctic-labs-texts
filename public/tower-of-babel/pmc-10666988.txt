The impact of protrusion size on pain, range of motion, functional capacity, and multifidus muscle cross-sectional area in lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Rabia Tugba Kilic, Sedef Yildirimalp, Cetin Sayaca
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objective:To investigate the effect of protrusion amount on pain, lumbar mobility, physical dysfunction, and the cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle in patients with lumbar disc herniation.Methods:54 male patients aged 20 to 50 years were included in this study in 2 groups. The control group (n = 18) consisted of patients with disc herniation without nerve root compression, and the study group (n = 36) consisted of patients with lumbar disc herniation and nerve root compression in 3 subgroups according to the amount of protrusion. The multifidus muscle cross-sectional area and the amount of protrusion were measured using Magnetic Resonance Imaging. Pain intensity was measured using the Visual Pain Scale, physical dysfunction was measured using the Oswestry Disability Index, flexion-extension was measured with TiltMeter, and trunk rotation was measured with smartphone applications called iPhone Compass.Results:There were no differences in pain levels (P > .05), mean trunk rotation movements of the affected and unaffected sides (P = .001, P = .001, P = .983, and P = .954, respectively), Oswestry Disability Index results (P = .967), or cross-sectional area sizes of the multifidus muscle (P = .866, P = .552, P = .787, respectively). There was a difference between the groups in terms of the mean trunk flexion and extension movement values (P = .001). The regression analysis indicated that there was no significant correlation between the binary variables, and the models exhibited a low explanatory rate for the dependent variable.Conclusion:A relationship has been identified between lumbar disc herniation and low back pain, lumbar movement limitation, and physical functionality. Nevertheless, subsequent to the regression analysis, it became evident that the binary variables did not exhibit a noteworthy relationship, leading to a decrease in the explanatory capacity of the models for the dependent variable. In forthcoming studies, it is advisable to contemplate the augmentation of the subject pool or the incorporation of multiple independent variables into the regression analysis as potential strategies to enhance the model capability in elucidating variations in the dependent variable.

1. Introduction

Pain is one of the most important findings in intervertebral disc herniation.[1] As the amount of degeneration in the disc increases, the pressure on the nerve increases, which increases the pain intensity.[2] Although the primary complaint of patients with lumbar disc herniation is low back pain, a decrease in the range of motion of the waist is also associated with increased pain. During patient evaluation, the range of motion in the waist was observed to decrease below normal values.[3]

Currently, significant advancements have been witnessed in the field of medical practice.[4] The progress in medicine is influenced by a multitude of factors.[5] However, despite the emergence of novel techniques, lumbar disc herniation continues to affect a substantial number of individuals, presenting a significant public health concern.[6]

The stability of the lumbar spine is reliant on the paraspinal muscles, including the psoas muscles, erector spinae, and multifidus.[7] In response to prolonged and intense mechanical strain, muscles adapt by increasing their activity and density.[8] Recently, it has been reported that patients with L4-5 lumbar disc herniation exhibit changes in the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the psoas muscle. Moreover, age-related atrophy of paraspinal muscles is known to occur, which may contribute to disc herniation, sagittal imbalance, and postural instability in affected individuals.[6]

Long-term pain and inactivity seen in patients with intervertebral disc hernias cause atrophy of the lumbar muscles. Lumbar muscles, which weaken and fatigue rapidly due to atrophy, cannot provide sufficient support to the vertebral column and cause an increase in load on surrounding structures.[9] In patients with chronic low back pain, a decrease in the multifidus muscle length, which is the most important supporter of the vertebral column, and an increase in the amount of fat were observed.[10] The multifidus muscle is the main stabilizer muscle of the lumbar spine and has been reported to play an important passive biomechanical role in the body. The development of atrophy in the multifidus muscle for any reason disrupts waist biomechanics and causes a vicious cycle, which causes the person to suffer from back problems.[10] Although there is no clear consensus on how the multifidus muscle plays a role or is affected in disc herniation, studies have shown that the load on the multifidus muscle increases in patients with disc herniation.[11]

Muscle CSA is widely considered to be indicative of muscle volume.[12] In the case of paraspinal muscles, their CSA has been found to have a certain degree of correlation with their capacity to generate force, as force is directly proportional to the CSA of the muscle.[6]

There are studies in the literature on pain, physical function, and the cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle in individuals with lumbar disc herniation.[13,14] Although there is a study demonstrating a correlation between lumbar disc herniation and paraspinal muscle degeneration, indicating that severe fat infiltration is associated with nerve root compression, and the severity of fat infiltration increases in the presence of nerve root compression,[15] we have not come across any research investigating the impact of protrusion size in lumbar disc herniation on pain, lumbar mobility, physical function, and the cross-sectional area of the lumbar multifidus muscle. This study was designed with the aim of exploring the relationship between the symptoms and the radiologically detected disc herniation and how it may manifest in the clinical presentation. The investigation seeks to understand how the presence of disc herniation observed during radiological evaluation may be associated with symptoms and subsequently impact the clinical condition of the patients. Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the effect of protrusion amount on pain, lumbar motility, physical function, and cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle in patients with lumbar disc herniation.

2. Method

2.1. Design, setting, and participants

This was a prospective cross-sectional study. Ethics committee approval was obtained before the initiation of the study, which was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients who agreed to participate in the study were informed in detail about the purpose of the study and the evaluation methods to be applied before the study, and they all provided signed informed voluntary consent forms. The trial protocol has been registered in the Clinical Trials Registry (NCT05276986).

Male patients who were admitted to the Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation Clinic between October 1, 2018 and May 31, 2019 with complaints of low back pain, diagnosed with lumbar disc herniation, and whose magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed were included in this study. Only male subjects were included in the study to prevent the effect of sex on muscular cross-sectional area.

Male patients age range 20 to 50 years, with low back pain for at least 3 months, who were diagnosed with lumbar disc hernia, and who had MRI results were included in the study. Patients were excluded from the study if they presented with spine deformities, degenerative changes, malformations, pelvic disorders, limb length discrepancies, additional coxarthrosis, rheumatological diseases, infections, a past history of lumbar surgery and/or low back treatment, vertebral fractures, spondylolisthesis, epidural injections, radiofrequency ablations, obesity, or a history of malignancy.

Furthermore, individuals who engage in regular sports activities at least 3 days a week were also excluded from the study.

2.2. Cross-sectional area assessment of the lumbar multifidus muscle

The cross-sectional area measurements were conducted on patients with lumbar disc herniation at the L4-5 and L5-S1 intervertebral levels, as these levels are commonly affected by the condition.[16] The measurements were performed by the same radiologist to ensure consistency and accuracy in the evaluation process. The patients were placed in the supine position to ensure that the vertebrae were equidistant from the examination coils (RF data-receiving units under the table), and the knees were raised to reduce lordosis.

Multifidus muscle cross-sectional area measurements were performed by drawing the outer borders of the muscle using the DICOM Browser program on sections parallel to the disc space passing through the hernia level. To determine the edge, fat tissues surrounding the muscle tissue were not included in the measurement area, but the areas inside the fat tissues were included. Since the intervertebral spaces are not parallel to the axial plane at every level and lumbar lordosis is different in everybody, by measuring the section angle and the angles of the inferior part of lumbar lordosis with the axial plane, the angle between the vertical axis of the lumbar multifidus muscle and the cross-section plane was calculated. The calculated angle A was used for angular correction of the calculated cross-sectional area (CSA = cross-sectional area × Cos A) (Fig. 1). With these procedures, we aimed to determine the true cross-sectional area of the muscle and standardize the findings.[10] Lumbar multifidus muscle cross-sectional area measurement results were calculated for the affected and unaffected sides and as the sum of both sides.

(A) Determination of the demarcation lines. (B) The measurement angle of the multifidus muscle.

2.3. Measurement of the protrusion amount

Precise evaluation of the protrusion amount was performed by the same radiologist using routine MRI. The assessment was performed by measuring the distance of the protruding disc material from the posterior edge of the vertebral corpus, forming the bone spinal canal to the posterior edge of the vertebral corpus[17] (Fig. 2).

Measurement of the amount of protrusion.

2.4. Pain intensity assessment

During the experiment, the visual analogue scale was employed to measure the maximum pain experienced by the participants at rest, during nighttime, and during activity. The scale consists of a horizontal straight line. The line has a value of 0 at the beginning and 10 at the end, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 indicates unbearable pain.[18] The patient was asked to mark the pain he felt on this line, and the point he marked was measured in cm and recorded.

2.5. Lumbar normal joint movement assessment

Before the evaluation, to warm up the lumbar region locally, the patient performed rhythmic rotation movements of the trunk from the hips to the right and left in the supine position, with the knees bent, feet, and soles in contact with the bed, and the legs adjacent.

A smartphone application (TiltMeter -advanced level and inclinometer) was used to measure flexion-extension, and its validity and reliability were established by Pouahmadi et al The T12-L1 level of the patient standing upright was marked on the skin. While the smartphone was in contact with the T12-L1 level, the patient was asked to lean forward without bending the knees, aiming to touch the floor with their fingertips, and the flexion range of motion was recorded. In the same position, the patient was asked to take the trunk as far back as possible, and the extension range of motion was measured and recorded.[19]

The iPhone Compass smartphone application was used to measure body rotation. The T1-2 level was marked while the patient was sitting in an upright position with his feet touching the ground. During measurement, the patient was asked to hold a ball placed between both knees by squeezing his legs to prevent pelvic compensation. To prevent the upper limb, especially the scapulothoracic region, from contributing to movement, the patient was asked to hold a cane placed at the acromioclavicular level with his hands crossed over the shoulders. For the measurement, the smartphone was kept in full contact with the marked T1-T2 level, and the patient was asked to rotate the body to the right and left as much as possible, with the head facing forward, without disturbing his position. The right and left rotation ranges of motion were recorded in degrees.[20]

2.6. Physical function assessment

Objective measurement of physical dysfunction was performed using the Turkish version[21] of the oswestry disability index.[22] In this index, there are 10 items assessing the changes in activities of daily living caused by pain: pain intensity, personal care, lifting, sitting, walking, standing, sleeping, social life, travel, and occupational/homework. Each question has 6 options, ranging from 0 to 5. Participants were asked to mark the most appropriate options for each question. The total oswestry disability index score varied from 0 to 50. As the total score increases, the level of disability also increases.

2.7. Statistical analysis

In the power analysis conducted to determine the number of individuals participating in the study, the alpha significance level was set at 0.05, and the desired power value was set to 0.95. The effect width was found to be 1.00 from the formula d = x1̅̅̅ − x2̅̅̅ss. This value was calculated based on the mean and standard deviation values in the publication titled “The effectiveness of different physiotherapy programs in low back pain on pain, endurance, and quality of life.”[23] The ratio of the study:control groups that will participate in the study was taken as 2 1 in order to obtain more reliable results. The total number of participants included in the study was 54 (18 in the control group and 36 in the study group). These calculations were performed using G * Power 3.1.9.2 software.

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences Version 25.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences Inc. Chicago, IL) was used for statistical analysis of the data obtained from the study. The normality of the variables was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk Normality test. Parametric tests were used in the significance tests for variables that were normally distributed. The statistical significance level was set at P < .05. In the analysis of the visual analogue scale, normal range of joint motion, the Oswestry Disability Index, and cross-sectional area of the lumbar multifidus muscle, since the data showed normal distribution and there were more than 2 groups (control, min-protrusion, mid-protrusion, max-protrusion), One-Way ANOVA was used in the difference test. The Bonferroni test was used to test from which group the difference occurred and how much difference there was in cases where there was a difference between the groups. The relationship between the dependent and independent variables was analyzed using the Pearson Correlation Test, which is a parametric test. Correlation analysis was performed using the following relationship values: 0, No relationship, 0.01 to 0.29: Weak relationship, 0.30 to 0.59: Moderate relationship, 0.60 to 0.99: Strong relationship; and 1, Full relationship.[24] Regression analysis was performed to explain the relationship between variables.

3. Results

A total of 56 patients with lumbar disc herniation were invited to participate in the study. One patient was excluded from the study because he did not want to participate in the evaluations owing to excessive pain, and 1 patient had a lumbar region operation. Eighteen patients with disc herniation but without nerve root compression were included in the control group (Fig. 3). Thirty-six patients with lumbar disc herniation with nerve root compression were divided into 3 groups according to the amount of protrusion: min-protrusion group with protrusion <4 mm (n = 12), mid-protrusion group with protrusion between 4 and 6 mm (n = 12), and max-protrusion group with protrusion >6 mm (n = 12)

4. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effect of the protrusion amount on pain intensity, normal range of motion of the lumbar region, physical function level, and lumbar multifidus muscle cross-sectional area in male patients with lumbar disc herniation. It was found that the amount of protrusion restricted trunk flexion and extension movements.

The most important finding of the present study was that there was no relationship between the amount of protrusion and cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle. A literature review showed that the multifidus muscle may undergo atrophy owing to nerve compression. The reason for this atrophy may be prolonged compression of the nerve that innervates the muscle. In a study in which patients with and without nerve root compression were compared with healthy controls, the bilateral multifidus cross-sectional area was measured by MRI at the L3-4, L4-5, and L5-S1 levels; in patients with nerve root compression, it was reported that atrophy in the lumbar multifidus was significant at the L4-5 and L5-S1 levels on the affected side.[25] Functional disorders of the lumbar muscles may be related to changes in the structure of the lumbar muscles, and lumbar muscle degeneration in low back pain is characterized by a macroscopic decrease in the cross-sectional area of the muscle and an increase in fat infiltration.[26] In patients with unilateral nerve root compression at level L5, measurements of the lumbar multifidus muscle at the L4-5 and L5-S1 levels revealed more atrophy in the cross-sectional area than in the side without nerve root compression.[27] In a similar study, in patients with unilateral posterolateral disc herniation causing nerve root compression at the L4-5 or L5-S1 level, a significant relationship was found between the intensity of nerve root compression and the amount of fat infiltration in the lumbar multifidus muscle on the affected side, and the multifidus muscle on the side with lumbar disc herniation was shown to have a higher amount of fat infiltration than the side without.[14] Moreover, in another study conducted in 31 patients with posterolateral disc herniation and nerve root compression in the L4-L5 intervertebral space, in the lumbar multifidus cross-sectional area measurements performed at all lumbar levels of the patients on MRI, it was reported that the cross-sectional areas of the right and left multifidus muscles are symmetrical, and more fat infiltration was observed at the spinal cord levels adjacent to the disc herniation.[28] In studies conducted to detect atrophy in the lumbar multifidus in patients with low back pain, we believe that measurement of fat infiltration in the muscle is more important than measurement of the cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle.

A study aligned with our perspective emphasized the importance of incorporating measurements of muscle atrophy related to fat infiltration or functional cross-sectional area (CSA) to obtain a more precise evaluation of muscle mass.[29]

Indeed, another study conducted by the same authors demonstrated that fat infiltration exhibited a significant correlation with root compression, and the severity of fat filtration further escalated when root compression was present.[15] In our study, in which the multifidus cross-sectional area was calculated in patients with nerve compression, we found that the amount of protrusion had no effect on the cross-sectional area of the lumbar multifidus muscle. However, this was thought to be due to the measurement of the total cross-sectional area without isolating the fat infiltrates in the muscle, while calculating the cross-sectional area of the lumbar multifidus muscle.

The lumbar region multifidus muscle cross-sectional area of all patients was the largest in the group with a protrusion amount of 4 to 6 mm. Studies have reported that decreased lumbar flexion range of motion may be the cause or result of fat infiltration in the muscle.[30] In the patient group with a protrusion amount of 4 to 6 mm, normal flexion and range of motion limitation were the highest, suggesting that fat infiltration may be excessive in this group. Although the amount of fat infiltration was not measured, the higher cross-sectional area of the multifidus muscle of the patients in this group may be a result.

Among all patients participating in the study, the group with the smallest cross-sectional area of the lumbar multifidus muscle was the group with the amount of protrusion <4 mm. In previous studies, the primary reasons for the development of atrophy in the lumbar multifidus were microgravity, inactivity, bed rest, and incorrect posture habits. The position in which this muscle shows activation is the standing upright position, where full extension of the trunk is required. When standing upright, the mechanoreceptors located lateral to the intervertebral discs are stimulated and the lumbar multifidus muscle contracts.[31] The lumbar multifidus muscle cross-sectional area was the smallest in this group, which can be explained by the fact that they were faced with microgravity, inactivity, or erroneous posture, and the extension normal range of motion was lowest in this group.

In the literature, it is seen that patients diagnosed with lumbar disc herniation most frequently present to healthcare institutions with complaints of back pain.[32] In addition to low back pain, which is the most important symptom in patients diagnosed with lumbar disc herniation, symptoms such as pain radiating to one or both legs, strength defects, and hypesthesia are also observed. While stretching the posterior annulus fibrosus and posterior longitudinal ligament containing pain fibers is shown to cause localized blunt pain in the waist, the pain spreading to the leg is caused by the compression of the disc material on the nerve roots.[33] According to another opinion, pain related to lumbar disc herniation is caused by inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, which are released from the annulus fibrosus as a result of disc injury into the environment in the nucleus pulposus, affecting the free nerve endings.[34] In the case of mechanical compression/nerve root compression, the dorsal root ganglia may cause pain in the form of sciatica, which spreads to the leg on the same side by creating pressure on the small nerves around it. As the amount of degeneration in the disc increases, the intensity of pain increases in accordance with the compression of nerve.[2] In one study, 134 patients with chronic lower back pain were divided into 2 groups, with or without nerve root compression, and the relationship between pain intensity and the amount of root compression on MRI was investigated. There was no difference in pain intensity between the groups with and without nerve root compression and the group without it.[35] Similarly, in our study, there was no difference in pain severity between patients grouped according to protrusion amount. The data we obtained from the study showed that an increase in the amount of protrusion had no effect on pain severity felt by the patients at night, at rest, and during activity. The findings of low back pain did not differ between patients with and without nerve root compression, and the amount of protrusion of the patients with nerve root compression did not affect pain severity. In the literature, it is maintained that, in addition to low back pain, inflammation should occur as a result of compression of the nerve root for leg pain to occur.[36] In conclusion, in lumbar disc hernia, which is known to cause pain, it can be thought that the severity of inflammation rather than the amount of protrusion has a greater effect on pain severity.

Evaluation of the normal range of motion of the lumbar region in patients diagnosed with lumbar disc herniation is an important parameter in the functional examination of the vertebral column. Pain that occurs during lumbar movements and the limitations observed in movements suggest a pathology related to the lumbar region.[37] Many studies have reported a decrease in lumbar range and speed of motion in patients with low back pain.[38,39] Although it was stated that lumbar joint movements may be restricted due to low back pain, there is no evidence that these limitations in movements directly affect the physical functionality of patients.[40] Functional limitations in the lumbar region can also be observed without the limitation of normal joint motion.[41] It was found that all patients with lumbar disc herniation included in our study had limited degrees of trunk flexion and extension, and the right and left trunk rotation levels were within normal limits. Based on the data obtained, we believe that, in patients with lumbar disc herniation, nerve root compression restricts trunk flexion and extension movements, and the amount of protrusion negatively affects the normal range of motion of the trunk, which is a reversible effect.

Studies evaluating the effect of low back pain on functional insufficiency have reported that pain negatively affects daily life and causes physical dysfunction.[42] In terms of physical function impairment levels, it was determined that the level of daily living activities of all patients participating in the study was “insufficient” according to the survey scoring. The fact that there was no significant difference between the groups in terms of physical dysfunction suggests that the main factor affecting function is not the amount of disc herniation (protrusion).

The most important limitation of this study was that when calculating the lumbar multifidus muscle cross-sectional area, existing fat infiltrates could not be isolated using the technique used to measure the area within the boundary lines of the muscle. It has been revealed that it is composed of continuum of many contributors (end plates, fatty infiltration in the paraspinal muscles and interrelated with other segments. Besides the multifidus, the erector spinae and psoas muscles should be analyzed.[43,44] It will be useful to consider this factor when evaluating the results.

Another limitation is that BMI may not be a reliable anthropometric parameter in the clinical setting. In recent studies, a new index has been developed, the subcutaneous fat index, which has been shown to be closely related to spine degeneration and even fatty infiltration in the paraspinal muscles, whereas BMI is not.[45,46]

Another limitation of our study is that we employed the method utilized by Furness et al[20] for measuring lumbar mobility in healthy subjects within the methods section, rather than adapting it specifically for patients with low back pain.

The outcome of the regression analysis indicated a lack of substantial association between the binary variables, leading to a diminished explanatory capacity of the models for the dependent variable. This could be attributed to 2 potential factors. Firstly, if the sample size is limited, augmenting the number of subjects in subsequent studies could yield more robust outcomes. Secondly, the omission of other variables that might influence the dependent variable could have occurred within the model. Enhancing the model explanatory power for the dependent variable could be achieved by incorporating multiple independent variables. These considerations represent potential limitations of the study.

5. Conclusion

Based on the data gathered in this study, a correlation between lumbar disc herniation and low back pain, lumbar movement restriction, and physical functionality has been identified. However, subsequent to the regression analysis, it became evident that the binary variables did not exhibit a significant relationship, leading to a reduced explanatory capability of the models for the dependent variable. This phenomenon could be attributed to 2 potential factors. Firstly, when dealing with a limited number of subjects, augmenting the study subject pool in future research endeavors might yield more robust results. Secondly, it plausible that certain variables influencing the dependent variable were excluded from the model. The incorporation of multiple independent variables could potentially enhance the model ability to elucidate variations in the dependent variable. It is imperative for future studies to consider these aspects.
