EvoEmo: Towards Evolved Emotional Policies for LLM Agents in Multi-Turn Negotiation
Yunbo Long, Liming Xu Lukas Beckenbauer Yuhan Liu Alexandra Brintrup

Abstract
Recent research on Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning in Large Language Models (LLMs) has demonstrated that agents can engage in complex , multi-turn negotiations, opening new avenues for agentic AI. However, existing LLM agents largely overlook the functional role of emotions in such negotiations, instead generating passive, preference-driven emotional responses that make them vulnerable to manipulation and strategic exploitation by adversarial counterparts. To address this gap, we present EvoEmo, an evolutionary reinforcement learning framework that optimizes dynamic emotional expression in negotiations. EvoEmo models emotional state transitions as a Markov Decision Process and employs population-based genetic optimization to evolve high-reward emotion policies across diverse negotiation scenarios. We further propose an evaluation framework with two baselines—vanilla strategies and fixed-emotion strategies—for benchmarking emotion-aware negotiation. Extensive experiments and ablation studies show that EvoEmo consistently outperforms both baselines, achieving higher success rates, higher efficiency, and increased buyer savings. This findings highlight the importance of adaptive emotional expression in enabling more effective LLM agents for multi-turn negotiation.

Introduction

“Emotions aren’t the obstacles to a successful negotiation while they are the means.”

— Chris Voss,
Never Split the Difference

Extensive behavioral research has shown that human decision-making systematically deviates from classical economic assumptions of pure rationality, being dynamically shaped by psychological biases and emotional states rather than solely by stable personality traits
(
Hilbert 2012
;
Baumeister et al. 2012
;
Riaz et al. 2012
)
.
While modern Large Language Models (LLMs) have made progress in replicating personality-driven behavioral patterns through Chain of Thought (CoT)
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
, the role of emotion in shaping decisions remains relatively
understudied

(
Liu and Long 2025
)
, especially compared to
static
human trait-based approaches
(
Huang and Hadfi 2024
)
.
Yet, emotional dynamics are crucial in fine-grained negotiation scenarios such as price bargaining
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
, where emotions directly influence on tactical choices (e.g., frustration prompting premature concessions, or excitement triggering overly aggressive bids), with immediate consequences for negotiation outcomes.
In contrast, personality traits capture broad behavioral tendencies (e.g., a neurotic individual’s overall risk aversion) but cannot account for adaptive, moment-to-moment adjustments that define real-world negotiations.
While modern alignment techniques like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Gao et al. 2025
)
and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Kasbouya and Sael 2025
)
have enabled LLMs to recognize and respond to human emotional cues (e.g., expressing sympathy in response to distress), these capabilities remain fundamentally
reactive
.
Current approaches excel at inferring emotions through CoT reasoning
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, yet they fail to leverage these insights for
strategic
purposes—namely, actively expressing emotions to influence negotiation trajectories and assert dominance across multi-turn interactions.
This limitation is particularly problematic in negotiation domains shaped by affective dynamics, most notably in price bargaining, where current LLM agents exhibit three fundamental deficiencies relative to human negotiators:

•

Tactical Inflexibility
.
Human negotiators dynamically modulate emotional signals (e.g., expressing strategic anger to anchor prices or feigned patience to elicit concessions), whereas LLM agents typically default to static response patterns, rendering their behavior both predictable and susceptible to exploitation
(
Kroczek et al. 2025
)
.

•

Adversarial Naivety.

Their proficiency in emotion recognition paradoxically becomes a vulnerability: while able to detect signals such as frustration or empathy, LLM agents remain incapable of distinguishing genuine affect from manipulative tactics (e.g., feigned urgency in price negotiations)
(
Huang and Hadfi 2024
)
.

•

Strategic Myopia.

Unlike humans who proactively shape emotional trajectories across interactions (e.g., building rapport before demanding concessions)
(
Bosse et al. 2013
)
, LLMs lack meta-reasoning about emotional cause-and-effect, remaining reactive rather than generative in managing affective dynamics.

These deficiencies explain why LLMs, despite advanced reasoning capabilities, may systematically underperform humans in emotion-sensitive negotiations, particularly in price bargaining, where strategic emotion regulation is paramount.

To address these limitations, we propose
EvoEmo
, an evolutionary reinforcement learning framework for optimizing emotion policies for multi-turn, emotion-sensitive negotiations.
Our approach employs population-level evolutionary learning to discover optimal emotion transition rules, iteratively refining policies based on rewards achieved during negotiations.
Evolutionary operations—including crossover and mutation—enable efficient exploration of the policy space and propagation of high-reward emotional strategies.
By combining the exploration advantages of population-based optimization with the sequential decision-making framework of reinforcement learning, EvoEmo provides an effective approach for evolving complex emotional policies that that are challenging to optimize with gradient-based methods alone.

Additionally, we introduce an cross-model negotiation framework comprising two baselines—vanilla and fixed-emotion strategies.
The vanilla baseline is emotion-free, with no explicit prompting regarding emotions, while the fixed-emotion baseline maintains a consistent emotional profile throughout the negotiation.
This framework enables systematic, emotion-aware benchmarking of negotiation capabilities across multiple state-of-the-art LLMs.
Through extensive experiments, we examine how different LLMs vary in their ability to strategically modulate emotional expression to influence pricing outcomes while simultaneously defending against adversarial emotional manipulation in multi-turn bargaining scenarios.
Our results show that evolutionarily optimized emotion policies substantially enhance LLM agents’ adaptive bargaining capabilities, achieving much better performance in terms of negotiation success rate, buyers’ saving rates, and efficiency (in total rounds of negotiation).
This framework establishes a new benchmark for emotion-aware negotiation research and highlights the critical importance of adaptive emotional intelligence in next-generation interactive AI systems.

2
Related Work

While personality traits shape general negotiation tendencies (e.g., agreeableness correlating with cooperative strategies)
(
Huang and Hadfi 2024
)
, emotions dominate real-time bargaining dynamics through three mechanisms:
(1)
Temporal Alignment
– negotiations unfold through rapid conversational turns (seconds to minutes), aligning in timescale more closely with emotional fluctuations (millisecond facial expressions, mood shifts over minutes), at times overriding the long-term influence cycles of personality and conditioning
(
Griessmair et al. 2015
)
;
(2)
Strategic Flexibility
– to maximize chance of success in negotiations humans dynamically adopt context-specific emotional states (e.g., tactical anger to signal firmness) to simulate tactical scarcity and temporarily override personality defaults (cite); and
(3)
Interactive Amplification
– emotional contagion is tactically leveraged to create affective feedback loops, where emotional stance shapes the response of the opponent throughoutthe exchange (e.g. feigned irritation is sparking opponent anxiety, affecting behavior over turns)
(
Olekalns and Druckman 2014
)
.
This fine-grained affective interplay drives dynamic shifts in bargaining power during human negotiations.
Current LLM-based negotiation systems fall short in replicating these dynamics:
Frameworks like AgreeMate
(
Chatterjee et al. 2024
)
and ACE
(
Shea et al. 2024
)
demonstrate proficiency in inferring emotions through CoT reasoning, but they lack mechanisms for strategic emotional adaptation—the ability to dynamically escalate or soften emotions to control negotiation dominance and maximize positive negotiation outcome on side of the represented party.
For example, AgreeMate’s modular architecture optimizes fixed buyer/seller roles but cannot evolve emotional+ strategies amid negotiation.
Similarly, ACE’s coaching system detects errors but cannot simulate the real-time emotional volatility that characterize human bargaining.
As a result, current LLM agents are largely reactive, rather than proactive, in their negotiation behavior.

Recent advances in reinforcement learning have significantly improved negotiation capabilities for LLM-based agents.
Methods such as Q-learning
(
Watkins and Dayan 1992
)
and PPO
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
have been adapted to optimize negotiation policies via reward maximization.
More recent work such as GENTEEL-NEGOTIATOR
(
Priya et al. 2025
)
further advances polite negotiation dialogue systems through mixture-of-expert reinforcement learning and specialized rewards for negotiation strategy, including politeness, and dialogue coherence.
Similarly, multi-agent evolutionary algorithms such as EvoAgent
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
offer a promising pathway to enhance negotiation strategies through population-based optimization, where selection, crossover, and mutation drive emotion policy evolution.
However, these approaches face fundamental limitations for
emotion-aware
negotiation scenarios: Generally grounded in reinforcement learning, they struggle to optimize agent policies within the sparse-reward, high-volatility nature of negotiation settings. Consequently, they remain ill-suited for the more fine-grained adaptation strategies that are required to dynamically react to opponent input and proactively maximize negotiation success outcomes as turns progress.
Further, evolutionary algorithms, though effective for global optimization, typcially evaluate candidate policies based on terminal outcomes. Subsequently, they tend to provide weak credit assignment to the nuanced interplay between consecutive emotional states that characterize human-like multi-turn negotiation behavior.
To address these limitations, our approach combines evolutionary exploration with reinforcement learning principles, enabling efficient emotion policy optimization while preserving the temporal dependencies essential for effective emotion-aware, multi-turn negotiation strategies.

3
EvoEmo Framework

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the workflow of the EvoEmo framework.

We present
EvoEmo
, a framework for evolutionary optimization of emotional negotiation policies, as illustrated in
Figure 1
. Our approach consists of two main stages:
(1)
Negotiation Setup
: We configure both buyer and seller agents using state-of-the-art LLMs. Each product is tokenized with its name, description, and role-specific parameters. Sellers receive the product’s target price as the initial selling price and a cost price that establishes their minimum acceptable threshold. Both sellers and buyers are configured with complementary market information. Detailed prompt structures are provided in
Section 8.3
in the Appendix.
(2)
EvoEmo Optimization
: We propose an evolved reinforcement learning framework for multi-turn negotiation simulation, in which buyers’ emotion policies
\pi_{\omega}^{i}
are updated through an evolutionary algorithm with multi-agent dialogue simulation. Each generation begins by initializing a population of
m
emotional sequences with randomized transition patterns. These policies are evaluated through multi-turn negotiations, with fitness scores assigned via a reward function
R(S)
. The population then evolves through selection, crossover, and mutation operations, producing improved emotional policies
\hat{\pi}_{\omega}^{i}
for the next generation. These new policies are again evaluated through negotiation simulations to identify optimal policies
\pi_{\omega}^{i}*
, and the buyers’ emotion policy is updated accordingly. The optimization continues iteratively until the reward function converges.

3.1
Emotion-Aware MDPs

We formalize the negotiation process as a MDP, denoted
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R})
.
The state space
\mathcal{S}
captures the negotiation context through tuples
(t,e_{t},p_{t})
, where
t\in\mathbb{N}
is the current number of turn of negotiation,
e_{t}
represents the agent’s emotion state drawn from a 7-dimensional emotion space
\mathcal{E}
comprising
anger
,
disgust
,
fear
,
happiness
,
sadness
,
surprise
, and
neutral
, and
p_{t}
encodes the price context, including both the current offer and historical price points.
The action space
\mathcal{A}
encompasses all possible negotiation responses generated by the LLM.
Transition dynamics
\mathcal{P}
are determined by the policy
\pi_{\omega}
, which governs emotional state transitions through
e_{t+1}\sim\pi_{\omega}(e|s_{t})
, where
\pi_{\omega}(e|s_{t})=\text{softmax}(\text{LLM}(s_{t},e_{t},\tau_{t}))
represents the emotion distribution generated by the LLM conditioned on the current state and temperature
\tau_{t}
.

3.2
Policy Representation

Each negotiation policy
\pi_{\omega}=(E,T,\mathbf{P})
encodes three core components that govern the agent’s emotional behavior:
(1) an emotion sequence
E=(e_{1},...,e_{n})\in\mathcal{E}^{n}
defining the agent’s planned emotional trajectory across negotiation turns, where
\mathcal{E}
represents the 7-dimensional emotion space;
(2) temperature parameters
T=(\tau_{0},\delta)
that control response stochasticity through an exponential decay schedule
\tau(t)=\max(0.1,\tau_{0}\cdot(1-\delta)^{t})
, with
\tau_{0}
determining initial exploration and
\delta
governing the decay rate; and
(3) a transition matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times 7}
, where each entry
P_{ij}=\mathbb{P}(e_{t+1}=j|e_{t}=i)
specifies the probability of transition between emotion states, constrained by
\sum_{j=1}^{7}P_{ij}=1
to maintain valid probability distributions across all emotional state transitions.

3.3
Reward Function

The evolutionary optimization evaluates policies through a reward function, which can be interpreted as a
fitness
score (a term commonly used in evolutionary computing) measuring negotiation effectiveness:

R(S)=\mathbf{1}_{\text{success}}\cdot\alpha\cdot\frac{b(S)}{e(S)}

(1)

where
\mathbf{1}_{\text{success}}
is an indicator function equal to 1 if negotiation succeeds and 0 otherwise;
b(S)\in[0,1]
represents the normalized buyer savings (as a percentage of the initial price);
e(S)
denotes negotiation efficiency, measured by the number of negotiation rounds; and
\alpha
is a weighting coefficient that balances the contribution of buyer savings relative to efficiency.

3.4
Evolved Reinforcement Learning

Our framework formulates the search for an optimal emotional policy
{\pi_{\omega}^{i}}
as an evolutionary reinforcement learning problem, optimizing the policy’s emotional transition parameters through a generational cycle of evaluation and population-based refinement. Each iteration begins by deploying the current population of
m
candidate policies
{\pi_{\omega}^{i}}
within the multi-turn negotiation simulation, which is governed by the LLM
\mathcal{M}
and a set of interaction prompts. The execution of each policy
{\pi_{\omega}^{i}}
produces a full emotional state and dialogue sequence, whose performance is quantified by the reward function
R(S^{i})
. Following this evaluation phase, policies are probabilistically selected for reproduction proportionally to their scaled reward, with selection probability

P(\pi_{\omega}^{i})=\frac{\exp(R(S^{i})/\lambda)}{\sum_{j}\exp(R(S^{j})/\lambda)}

(2)

where
\lambda
moderates selection pressure. To ensure elitism and monotonic improvement, the top
\rho
policies are preserved unchanged. Subsequently, genetic operators are applied: uniform crossover recombines parameters from selected parent policies with probability
p_{c}
to generate offspring, while mutation randomly perturbs emotional parameters with probability
p_{m}
to sustain exploration.This process produces a new population of policies
{\hat{\pi}{\omega}^{i}}
, and then an optimal emotional negotiation policy
\pi{\omega}^{*}
is selected which is then deployed in the next round of negotiation simulation to evaluate its performance and compute new rewards.

This algorithm terminates under one of two condition:
(1) the maximum fitness improvement remains below
\epsilon=0.01
for
five
consecutive generations, indicating convergence achieved; or
(2) the evolution process reaches
G
generations, exhausting the maximum computational budget.
This termination strategy balances thorough exploration of the policy space with computational efficiency, enabling the discovery of effective emotion transition patterns that preserve natural negotiation behavior.
The complete algorithmic implementation is presented in
Section 8.2
in the Appendix.

4
Experimental Setting

Datasets and LLMs.

Likewise to
Huang and Hadfi 2024
, we selected a subset of negotiation cases from the CraigslistBargain dataset
(
He et al. 2018
)
—a widely-used benchmark for negotiation studies—for evaluation.
This subset comprises 20 distinct multi-turn negotiation scenarios spanning diverse product categories, including electronics, furniture, vehicles, and housing.
Each scenario specifies:
(1) product details (name, category, and description),
(2) agent-specific target prices for the seller, and
(3) emotion annotations that support realistic bargaining dynamics.
The selected dataset spans a wide price range ($50–$5,000) and includes varied item conditions (brand new or used), thereby enabling comprehensive evaluation of negotiation policies under heterogeneous market conditions.
Representative state-of-the-art LLMs from leading AI providers—GPT-5-mini (OpenAI), Gemini-2.5-Pro (Google), and DeepSeek-V3.1.1 (DeepSeek)—were selected to power both buyer and seller agents in our experiments.
These models were chosen for their demonstrated strengths in conversational capabilities, strategic reasoning performance, and accessibility via APIS, providing comprehensive coverage across diverse model architectures.

Baselines.

For evaluation, we focus on buyer agents with different emotion policies while keeping seller agents fixed in a
vanilla
setting, where agents receive no explicit emotional prompts.
We define two baselines for comparison.
The first baseline comprises only
vanilla
agents, where neither buyer nor seller receives emotional guidance.
This setup ensures that both agents act solely according to their intrinsic emotional tendencies and strategic reasoning abilities, providing a reference point that reflects default negotiation behavior.
The second baseline pairs a vanilla seller and a
fixed-emotion
buyer, where the buyer maintains a constant emotional profile (e.g., happy or neutral) throughout the negotiation.
By comparing these baselines with a setup in which the buyer’s emotion is optimized via our proposed approach—
EvoEmo
, we can quantify the impact of emotions on negotiation outcomes and assess the effectiveness of EvoEmo in enhancing LLM-agent-based, emotion-driven negotiations.

Experimental Setup.

Simulated negotiations are conducted to evaluate the impact of different buyer emotion configurations—vanilla, fixed-emotion, or EvoEmo-optimized—on negotiation outcomes.
In all simulations, as mentioned earlier, sellers receive no emotional prompts, establishing a controlled environment to examine how the buyer’s emotional profile influences negotiation dynamics when interacting with a vanilla seller powered by LLMs trained on real-world data.
Moreover, all three LLMs described previously are employed to power both buyer and seller agents, resulting in
nine
distinct buyer-seller pairings for the negotiation experiments.

All negotiations begin with the seller’s initial offer, as depicted in
Figure 1
.
Performance is evaluated using three key metrics:
(1)
Negotiation Success Rate
(%), the percentage of dialogues that result in an agreement;
(2)
Buyer’s Savings
(%), the percentage of the reduction between the seller’s initial price and the final agreed-upon price; and
(3)
Negotiation Efficiency
, the total number of dialogue turns between buyer and seller.
Experiments were conducted across all nine LLM pairings, and results compared the two baselines with EvoEmo, reported as the mean and standard deviation over 20 distinct negotiation scenarios.
The negotiation framework was implemented using LangGraph, with a maximum of 30 dialogue turns per negotiation session.
Additionally, a third-party agent based on
GPT-4.1
was employed to serve as a mediator, monitoring the negotiation in real time.
This agent analyzes the dialogue history to classify each negotiation into one of three states:
(1) accepted (agreement reached),
(2) breakdown (negotiation failed), or
(3) ongoing (active negotiation).
This mechanism ensures consistent and impartial evaluation of negotiation outcomes across all simulations.

Configuration and Ablation Studies

EvoEmo incorporates two key classes of configurable elements:
(1) evolutionary parameters, which define the core mechanics of the framework, and
(2) hyperparameters, which are tuned to achieve optimal performance.
The primary evolutionary parameters include the emotion transition matrix
R
and the temperature decay
\delta
, both optimized during evolution.
Four hyperparameters are systematically tuned:
elitism rate (
\rho\in{0.1,0.25,0.5}
),
mutation rate (
p_{m}\in{0.1,0.25,0.5}
),
crossover rate (
p_{c}\in{0.5,0.75,1.0}
), and
population size (
m\in{10,20,50}
).
The best-performing configurations from these search spaces are adopted to report experimental results.
Across all experiments, the number of evolutionary iterations is set to 5, and the temperature controlling emotion transition dynamics is fixed at 0.9.

To assess the contribution of key design choices, we conduct ablation studies with the seller agent fixed to
GPT-5-mini
and buyers varied across the three LLM architectures.
These studies evaluate:
(1) the effectiveness of the ratio-based reward function (
b(S)/e(S)
) compared to weighted-sum alternatives (
\alpha_{1}b(S)+\alpha_{2}e(S)
);
(2) robustness to variations in the emotion transition temperature; and
(3) stability across evolutionary iterations.

Buyers Savings

As shown in
Figure 2
, the proposed framework—EvoEmo—consistently achieves the
highest
buyer savings across all buyer-seller pairings, demonstrating its clear superiority over both baselines (vanilla and fixed-emotion settings).
The results reveal two intriguing insights:

First
, with respect to emotional strategies, buyers adopting fixed
negative
emotions (e.g., anger and sad) frequently outperform the vanilla baseline.
This effect is particularly evident for multiple seller models facing the buyers who continuously express disgust or sadness, suggesting that LLM seller agents are more likely to concede when confronted with persistently negative emotional signals. This finding underscores that sustained negative affects are non-trivial factors that significantly influence negotiation dynamics and outcomes.
Conversely, buyers with fixed positive emotions, such as happy and surprise, obtain lower savings relative to the vanilla, indicating that seller agents defend prices more effectively when interpreting the buyer’s mood as positive, perhaps perceiving less urgency to offer concessions.

Second
, performance differs markedly across LLMs.
On the seller side, Gemini-2.5-pro demonstrates the strongest price defense against both vanilla and fixed-emotion buyers, yet remains vulnerable to the adaptive emotion optimized by EvoEmo.
On the buyer side, the results are model-dependent: the Gemini-based buyer achieves the highest savings against the GPT-5-mini seller, while the GPT-5-mini buyer performs most effectively against the DeepSeek-V3.1 seller.
Notably, no buyer model demonstrated a significant advantage when negotiating against the robust Gemini-2.5-pro seller, highlighting its strength as a challenging negotiation opponent.

Success vs. Efficiency

Buyers equipped with the EvoEmo-optimized emotion profile consistently achieve near 100% success rates and do so more efficiently than those using vanilla or fixed-emotion settings, requiring way fewer rounds to reach an agreement.
These results clearly demonstrate EvoEmo’s superiority over both baselines.

Interestingly, negotiation efficiency shows no strong correlation with any specific fixed emotional strategy, including the vanilla baseline.
However, a clear pattern emerges in success rates: buyers employing fixed
positive
emotions, as well as the vanilla setting without predefined emotions, consistently achieve higher success rates than those using fixed
negative
emotions.
This suggests that although negative emotions may occasionally lead to more favorable agreed prices, they inherently carry a higher risk of negotiation breakdown.

6
Limitations

While this study shows that emotion is a
non trivial
factor in a negotiation—and a buyer agent with adaptive emotion profile, optimized via EvoEmo, can achieve much better performance in price negotiation scenarios—it also has several limitations that need further research:

•

Limited Emotional Spectrum and Baseline Comparisons.

This study considers only seven basic emotional states, which may not capture the full complexity of human emotional expression in real negotiations.
Additionally, the baseline comparisons are limited to fixed-emotion and emotion-neutral strategies, omitting potentially informative comparisons such as random emotional sequences.

•

Scenario Dependence and Generalization Challenges.

The evaluation is conducted on 20 daily negotiation scenarios focusing on conventional commercial contexts, raising questions about potential selection bias and limited generalizability.
EvoEmo’s effectiveness remains unverified in more diverse negotiation domains, particularly high-stakes, emotionally charged scenarios such as debt collection, legal dispute resolution, or crisis negotiations where emotional dynamics may differ substantially from standard commercial settings.

•

Interpretability of Emotional Strategies.

The black-box nature of LLM responses and evolutionary optimization makes it difficult to explain why specific emotional sequences succeed in particular negotiation contexts.

•

Simulation-to-Reality Gap.

Validation through LLM simulations may not capture human expertise, and computational intensity limits real-time adaptation in practical deployments.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper examines the role of emotion in LLM-based negotiation and introduces EvoEmo, an evolutionary reinforcement learning framework for optimizing emotional sequences.
Our results show that emotion is a non-trivial factor in successful negotiation, with EvoEmo’s optimized emotional policies consistently improving performance by achieving higher success rates, greater efficiency, and enhanced buyer savings compared to both vanilla and fixed-emotion baselines.
The ability to dynamically adapt emotional states proves critical for effective multi-turn bargaining, enabling agents to strategically leverage emotional intelligence in negotiation.
Beyond the limitations outlined in
Section 6
, future work will examine the ethical implications and behavioral consistency of evolved policies, with particular attention to the emergence of deceptive or compromising behaviors.

EvoEmo Algorithm

The EvoEmo algorithm, showned in Algorithm
1
, is an evolutionary optimization framework designed to learn optimal emotion transition policies for LLM-based negotiation agents. The algorithm maintains a population of emotion policies that evolve over generations through selection, crossover, and mutation operations. Each policy represents a sequence of emotional states that guide the buyer agent’s behavior during negotiation. The process begins with random initialization of emotion sequences and iteratively improves them by evaluating their performance in simulated negotiations between buyer, seller, and mediator agents. Policies are selected based on their negotiation success rewards, which consider deal acceptance, utility terms, and time efficiency. The best-performing policy emerges after multiple generations of evolutionary optimization, providing an emotionally intelligent strategy for effective multi-agent negotiations.

8.3
Prompts

Prompts for both buyers and sellers

This section details the prompting strategies for both buyers and sellers in the negotiation environment. Our prompts are designed to achieve two primary objectives: (1) to ensure genuine transactional intent where buyers demonstrate authentic purchase motivation and sellers exhibit legitimate willingness to sell products; and (2) to establish a cooperative trading environment where both parties show flexibility to reach agreements without excessive rigidity.

As shown in
Figure 4
,
Figure 5
, and
Figure 6
, our prompt engineering incorporates psychologically-grounded negotiation principles that encourage value-creating behaviors rather than purely distributive bargaining tactics. The seller prompt (
Figure 4
) emphasizes product knowledge and reasonable flexibility, while the buyer prompt (
Figure 5
) focuses on authentic interest and strategic concession patterns. The negotiation check prompt (
Figure 6
) ensures proper dialogue flow and agreement validation.

This comprehensive prompting design specifically prevents the negotiation from degenerating into infinite midpoint bargaining, where participants mechanically alternate offers by computing arithmetic averages of current bids. Furthermore, our approach discourages participants from becoming overly fixated on marginal price differences that could otherwise impede successful deal-making, instead fostering a collaborative environment conducive to reaching mutually beneficial agreements.
