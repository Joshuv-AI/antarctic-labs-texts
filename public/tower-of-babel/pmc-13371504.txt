Incomplete vaccination among children aged 12–35 months in West Arsi Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Habtemu Jarso, Daniel Yohannes, Tsige Gebru, Lalisa Kebebe, Taye Mengistu, Demeke Tolera, Buli Teshite, Biftu Geda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaccine-preventable diseases remain significant contributors to illness and death among children. Globally, immunization stands out as one of the most effective and economical public health interventions. In the West Arsi zone, the results of the Rapid Convenient Survey (RCS) were inconsistent with the DHIS-2 report regarding the coverage of full vaccination.ObjectiveTo determine the proportion of incompletely vaccinated children aged 12–35 months and identify associated factors.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the West Arsi Zone from June 23 to August 17, 2023. A total of 1,316 children were selected using a multi-stage stratified random sampling technique. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with mothers or caretakers and by reviewing immunization records, utilizing a structured questionnaire programmed in Open Data Kit (ODK). The data were cleaned and analyzed using Stata version 17. A binary logistic regression model was developed to identify associated factors.ResultsThe prevalence of incomplete vaccination was 43.6% (95% CI: 40.9, 46.3) by recall and/or card review, and 36.8% (95% CI: 33.7–39.9) by card review only. OPV0 and MCV2 had the highest incompleteness. The main reasons reported for incompleteness were the unavailability of the vaccine/vaccinator and not knowing the need for the next dose. Incomplete vaccination was associated with accessibility of the kebele, availability of a refrigerator at the health post, residence of the respondent, age of the respondent, respondent’s education level, child sex, child age, respondent’s awareness of HEWs, respondent’s knowledge about immunization, means of transport to the health facility, wealth index, and participation in the Productive Safety Net Program.ConclusionsThe study revealed a high proportion of incomplete vaccination among children, with coverage varying across different vaccines. Major gaps were observed in OPV0 and MCV2. A wide range of socio-demographic, health system, and accessibility related factors were significantly associated with incomplete vaccination. Enhancing caretaker awareness, improving health facility readiness, and addressing social and cultural barriers could improve vaccine uptake.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07020-0.Keywords: Incomplete vaccination, fully vaccinated, 12–35 months, Ethiopia

Introduction

Vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) remain a major health burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly among children [1]. Immunization is a highly effective public health intervention, preventing millions of deaths annually [1–5].

Despite the introduction of the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) globally and in Ethiopia, challenges persist. By the end of 2021, 81% of children had received 1 dose of measles vaccine by their second birthday, and 71% of children received 2 doses of measles vaccine according to national immunization schedules [1].

Ethiopia launched its EPI in 1980 [3, 6] and introduced the second dose of measles vaccine (MCV2) in 2019 [3, 7, 8]. The government has shown strong commitment to increase EPI access and utilization by training health extension workers (HEW) and setting up 15,000 health posts [3]. A strategic plan was developed in April 2015 with the primary objective to achieve at least 90% national coverage and 80% in every district with all vaccines by 2020 [6].

However, achieving high coverage of full vaccination has continued to be challenging in Ethiopia. In 2019, 44% of Ethiopian children aged 12–23 months received all basic vaccines. Almost three in five children (59%) received first dose of measles vaccine (MCV1) and just 9% of children aged 24–35 months received MCV2. A 2022 UNICEF’s report showed that an estimated 1.13 million children in Ethiopia were not fully protected by measles vaccines [9].

A study in eastern Ethiopia indicated that only 39% of children aged 12–24 months were fully vaccinated from 2017 to 2021. Another study in Wolaita Zone reported that 66.1% of children aged 12–23 months were fully vaccinated from January to March 2021 [10]. A study conducted across selected districts of the Oromia region showed that there was approximately a 46% dropout rate between the first and second dose of the measles vaccine in 2021 [11].

Today, Ethiopia has the third highest number of cases associated with measles outbreaks [9]. The number of confirmed cases of measles was almost five-fold increased between 2021 and 2022 [12, 13]. This sharp increase is likely attributed to low population immunity, compounded by concurrent epidemics, conflict, forced displacement and other humanitarian crises that disrupted routine vaccination services. Only 36% of confirmed cases have received one dose or more of the measles-containing vaccine (MCV) [12, 13]. Low population immunity (MCV1 and MCV2 coverage) in 2021 was estimated at 54% and 46% respectively, according to WHO UNICEF Estimates of National Immunization Coverage (WUENIC) [12, 13].

Studies have indicated that incomplete vaccination was associated with place of delivery [14, 15], maternal age, place of residence, employment status, wealth index, antenatal care follow, and birth order [14], as well as knowledge of mothers/caretakers, educational status of father, time to reach health facilities, not being informed about the type of vaccine received, lack of parental discussion on vaccination, and lack of participation in pregnant mother conferences [15].

In the West Arsi Zone, results of Rapid Convenient Surveys (RCS) were inconsistent with DHIS-2 reports, indicating a large number of incompletely immunized children. The current study aimed to determine the proportion of children incompletely vaccinated and to identify factors associated with incomplete vaccination among children aged 12–35 months.

Conceptual framework

We adopted Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Service Use (ABMHSU), which was first developed in the 1960s and has gone through four phases since then. The model indicated that predisposing, enabling, need factors combine to influence health behavior. A detail of the Andersen’s model adopted was published elsewhere [16].

Methods and materials

Study setting, design and period

A community based cross-sectional study was conducted from June to August 2023 in West Arsi Zone, Oromia region. The population of the zone was officially estimated at 2,929,894 in mid-2022. There were 5 public hospitals, 85 health centers and 343 health posts which routinely provide vaccination to under five children in the district.

Study population

The study population was children aged 12–35 months and their mothers/caretakers who lived in West Arsi Zone for at least 6 months. Infants were excluded to minimize underestimation of vaccination coverage because some vaccines like Measles vaccine may not be due yet at 9 or 10 months (i.e. to included children with delayed doses). Most caregivers are likely to have completed vaccination visits by age 12–23 months. This is the period used by most vaccination studies in Ethiopia [17–21]. We extended this period to 35 months to include children with delayed second dose of Measles vaccine, consistent with previous studies [22–25]. Children whose mothers/caretakers were unable to respond due to illness or difficulty of speaking/hearing were also excluded.

Sampling

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula. We assumed a dropout prevalence of 45.4% in Oromia region [26]. A 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error were considered. To estimate dropout separately for children aged 12–23 months and 24–35 months (i.e., two strata), we applied a design effect of 1.5 and accounted for a maximum non-response rate of 10%. Children were selected using a stratified multistage random sampling technique. Kebeles (the lowest administrative unit) were first classified as accessible or hard-to-reach. From each statum, 30% of kebeles were randomly selected, yielding 70 accessible kebeles and 24 hard-to-reach kebeles. Within these, children were chosen using systematic random sampling, resulting in 941 children from accessible kebeles and 375 from hard-to-reach kebeles. Details of the sampling procedure have been published in a related study conducted by the same author in the same study area and period [16].

Data collection methods, tools and data collectors

Data were collected by face-to-face interview with the mothers or immediate caretakers and review of child immunization card. Sixteen Level IV (diploma level) and Bachelor of Science (BSc) nursing graduates collected the data after attending two-days training and pre-testing of data collection procedure. A child vaccination card, booklet, or other record was found at home only for 41% and 26% of children aged 12–23 months and 24–35 months respectively during data collection for the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) 2019 [27]. A semi-structured questionnaire was used for interview. A questionnaire was prepared by reviewing EDHS data collection tool and previous similar studies. Open Data Kit (ODK) template of questionnaire was prepared, downloaded to mobile or tablet devices and used for data collection. Geographic location of each household was captured by Global Positioning System (GPS).

Measurement

Vaccination status was assessed by review of vaccination card and/or recall (history). Mother/immediate caretaker was interviewed for the child’s immunization history if the card could not be accessed. Respondent’s knowledge of immunization was assessed using five questions, including four structured items and one-open ended question. These items examined whether vaccination can prevent infectious diseases, which specific diseases are prevented, the appropriate age to begin vaccination, the appropriate age to complete vaccination, and whether dropping out of the vaccination schedule is perceived as acceptable. Three knowledge questions had one correct answer and were scored out of 1 point. One question had nine correct answers and was scored out of 9 points, while another question had two correct answers and was scored out of 2 points. The total score across all knowledge questions was 14 points. Given that the knowledge scores were normally distributed, the mean score was used to classify participants’ knowledge as good or poor, consistent with several prior studies in Ethiopia [28–31]. Attitude towards vaccination was assessed using four items measured on a five-point Likert scale (raging from strongly disagree to strongly agree). The items included opinion about childhood immunization, whether the children should receive full course of vaccination, whether the number of vaccines given at once is too many, and whether the vaccination is painful. The total score ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude toward vaccination. Attitude was classified as positive, neutral, or negative based on the modified Bloom’s cut-off points for attitude classification, consistent with previous studies [32–37]. Details of knowledge questions and attitude items are provided in the supplementary material (Additional File 1).

Operational definitions

Incomplete vaccination (synonyms: default, dropout, not fully vaccinated, partially vaccinated, missed doses): a child is said to be incompletely vaccinated if he/she has missed any single vaccine included in the national EPI program, BCG to Measles dose-1 for a child aged 12–23 months and BCG to Measles dose-2 for child aged 24–35 months, except OPV0 [38]. OPV0 was excluded from the operational definition of incomplete vaccination because this vaccine is administered within the first two weeks after birth and is received by only a small proportion of children (37.8%).

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were cleaned and analyzed using Stata 17. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the data. Proportions of vaccination statuses were computed. To identify the factors associated with incomplete immunization status of a child, a standard binary logistic regression was developed, as incorporating a multilevel structure (clustering at the primary health care unit) did not significantly improve model fit. A total of 33 variables were selected and included in multivariable logistic regression, regardless of their p-value in bivariate analysis, because there was an adequate number of events (574 partially vaccinated and 742 fully vaccinated children). The variables were chosen based on their public health relevance and practical importance to ensure adequate control for confounding.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was checked in linear regression using variance inflation factor (VIF) before multiple regression and none was found (maximum VIF = 2.26). Statistical significance of a variable in multiple regression model was declared at p-value < 0.05. Model fitness was evaluated by Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p.value = 0.597) and percentage of correct classification (64.0%).

Qualitative data collection, analysis and presentation

Qualitative data on reasons for dropout were collected from mothers/caretakers at the same time as quantitative data. Mothers/caretakers were asked to list the reasons for their child’s dropout from the vaccination schedule, and their responses were recorded by trained data collectors.

In addition, data on reasons for dropout were obtained from approximately 50 Health Extension Workers (HEWs) who attended Integrated Refresher Training (ITR) sessions from four woredas (Shashemene Zuria, Shalla, Siraro, and Wondo). During the training, HEWs were asked to independently list as many reasons for dropout as possible on a white paper using either local languages or English.

Responses from mothers/caretakers and HEWs were separately entered into Dedoose software, coded and analyzed using thematic approach. Results were presented separately in text and graphs to triangulate the quantitative findings.

Data quality management

Data collection was supervised by two BSc graduate nurses who had experience of research data collection and research team who checked completeness and consistency of the collected data each day. Two-days training was provided for data collectors and supervisors on sampling approach, data collection methods and tools, and ethics considerations during data collection.

Open Data Kit (ODK) was used to improve the quality of data. Name of data collector, starting and end time of interview, and Global Positioning System (GPS) were included into the template to make sure the household is truly visited and the respondent is interviewed. The ODK template was prepared in English and Afan Oromo. The questionnaire was translated into Afan Oromo by language expert. The questionnaire translated to Afan Oromo was back translated to English by different language expert to ensure consistency of translation. Afan Oromo version of the ODK template was used to interview the respondent. For some respondents Amharic or another language was used for interview. In such cases, Afan Oromo version of ODK template was translated to the language of interview on spot either by data collector or translator. The language of interview and whether translator was used was specified in such cases. Data collectors were warned not to have any contact with HEWs. The questionnaire was pre-tested and modifications were made based on the findings of pre-test.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

A detail of the profiles of assessed kebeles (health posts) was published elsewhere [16]. A total of 1316 respondents were interviewed (941 respondents were from accessible kebeles and 375 were from hard-to-reach kebeles). Three-quarters (75.5%) of the respondents were from rural, most were female (84.1%), the majority (54.7%) were in the 25–34 years age group with a mean age of 29.8 (SD = 7.2), most were Oromo (94.4%) and most were Muslim (81.5%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of incomplete vaccination and reported reasons

Nearly half (43.6%, 95% CI: 40.9–46.3) of children aged 12–35 months were incompletely vaccinated (39.8% for those aged 12–23 months and 47.8% for those aged 24–35 months). A vaccination card was reportedly provided for 93.1% (94.0% for those aged 12–23 months and 92.1% for those aged 24–35 months). However, only 75% had the vaccination card available at the time of data collection (78.0% for those aged 12–23 months and 71.7% for those aged 24–35 months). More than one-third (36.8%, 95% CI: 33.7–39.9) of children aged 12–35 months were incompletely vaccinated (32.0% for children aged 12–23 months and 42.5% for children aged 24–35 months) by card review only (Table 4).

Reasons reported by HEWs for dropout

Health Extension Workers (HEWs) who attended Integrated Refresher Training (IRT) on June 14, 2023 were asked to report reasons for vaccination dropout. A total of 135 responses were obtained from more than 50 HEW from Shashemene Zuria, Shalla, Siraro & Wondo districts. The leading reason reported was population movement: migration to towns in search of work due famine, movement to lakes in search of water, and displacement from the area due to security problems. This issue was most likely prominent in Shalla district, where more than one-fourth of reporting HEWs were based.

The second most common reason was workload and inadequate human resources. HEW mentioned that there was excessive workload, that not every zone of each kebele had a HEW and that one or two HEWs could not adequately cover kebeles with populations exceeding 10,000. The third major reason involved other health-worker related problems, including: failure to correctly communicate the benefits of immunization, failure to vaccinate daily (health posts not opened on some days), limited use of outreach sites, HEW’s lack of attention to vaccination, postponing BCG and measles vaccines to specific days, inadequate follow-up of HEWs by health centers, HEW’s absence from the venue by appointment date, lack of follow-up in various ways for children who start and drop out, not appropriately getting the message, not accurately telling side effects, and lack of focus on vaccination. The fourth leading reasons reported were lack of transport and distance. The fifth leading reason reported was awareness or attitude related problems. These include uneven community perception, mother refusing to bring the child by saying, “the child cries or gets fever when vaccinated or the child has evil eye”. The mothers also fail to return by saying, “the child got severe disease following previous dose of vaccination”. Some mothers or caretakers also consider immunization as useless because it is free. Lack of logistics especially vaccines and refrigerator are other reasons reported by HEWs. Other maternal or caretaker factors such as mother getting ill after delivery, forgetting appointment, not availing at outreach, mothers/caretakers taking children with them to the market and starting at one site and moving to other sites are also reported by HEWs.

Discussion

We conducted a community-based cross-sectional study from 23 June to 17 August 2023 in West Arsi Zone, Oromia region, to determine the extent of incomplete vaccination and to identify factors associated with vaccination status among children aged 12–35 months. We found nearly half (43.6%) of the children surveyed were incompletely vaccinated. The main reasons reported by respondents for missed doses were vaccine/vaccinator unavailability and unawareness of the need for the next dose. Factors independently associated with incomplete (partial) vaccination were accessibility of the kebele, availability of refrigerator at health post, residence place of the respondent, age of respondent, age of index child, sex of index child, respondent’s education, means of transport to HF, respondent knowing HEW, respondent’s knowledge about immunization, participation in the productive safety net program (PSNP), and wealth index.

Vaccination coverage exceeded 80% for most vaccines and doses except for OPV0, MCV1 and MCV2 whose coverages were sub-optimal (< 80%). This finding was in agreement with the report of previous study [11]. The proportion of children aged 12–23 months who were fully vaccinated (60.2%) was higher than the EDHS 2019 report (44%) [27]. The finding was also higher than the 45% reported by a large scale population-based longitudinal study conducted in eastern Ethiopia, which included 14,198 children aged 12–24 months in 2020 [14]. This difference is expected as differences in time and interventions to improve immunization coverage are expected to bring a change in immunization coverage. Despite improvements in childhood immunization coverage in the zone, there are still a large number of children who are incompletely vaccinated. The result of the current study was lower than the 66.1% reported in a study of 635 children aged 12–23 months in Wolaita zone, southwest Ethiopia from January to March 2021 [10].

The prevalence of incomplete vaccination in the current study was higher than the prevalence pooled from previous Ethiopian studies [39]. MCV1 coverage among children aged 12–35 months was 78.3% while MCV2 coverage among children aged 15–35 and 24–35 months was 62.8% and 62.2%, resulting in a dropout rate of 22.1% and 21.1% respectively. The coverage of MCV2 observed in our study was higher than the report of previous study [11]. The dropout rate observed in our study was more than twice of the WHO’s maximum tolerable dropout level of 10% [40, 41] and Ethiopia’s 5% ambitious target [41], although not as high as the 46% dropout rate reported in a study conducted by the Oromia Regional Health Bureau (ORHB), Ministry of Health (MOH) and CDC across selected districts of the Oromia region in 2021 [9, 11]. The dropout rate from BCG to Measles2 was 36.6% and 37.2% among children aged 15–35 and 24–35 months, respectively. This may explain why Ethiopia now has the third highest number of cases associated with measles outbreaks [9]. The dramatic increase in measles cases may be explained by a low population immunity combined with other factors. Although the low population immunity in the current study is better than the 54% and 46% low population immunity (MCV1 and MCV2 coverage, respectively) estimated for 2021 by the WHO-UNICEF Estimates of National Immunization Coverage (WUENIC), 95% population vaccination coverage is required to achieve herd immunity and stop measles circulation [13].

Regarding factors associated with dropout from vaccination, the odds of dropout was 40% higher for a child from hard-to-reach kebele than for to a child from an accessible kebele. This could be due to lack of transport for vaccinators to avail vaccines and themselves, and for parents/caretakers to take children to health facilities. The odds of dropout for a child from a kebele without a refrigerator at the health post was more than two times higher compared to a child from a kebele with a refrigerator at the health post. This could be due to a lack of vaccines or vaccinators at the health post without a refrigerator. Unavailability of vaccine/vaccinator at health facility was also the leading reason reported by respondents for missed doses.

The odds of dropout for a child whose respondent was aged 15–24 and 25–34 years were 67% and 64% higher, respectively, while it was more than twice as high for a child whose respondent was older than 45 years compared to a child whose respondent was aged 35–44. This may be because younger mothers/caretakers often have less experience with health services, while older mothers/caretakers may face competing responsibilities or have lower health-seeking behavior, making them more likely to miss vaccination schedules. Moreover, the odds of dropout for a child aged 24–35 months was 44% higher compared to a child aged 12–23 months. This may because caretakers perceive continued vaccination as less beneficial as children grow older. The odds of dropout for a female child was 40% higher than for a male child which may be attributed to cultural influences.

The odds of dropout for a child whose respondent had no formal education was 36% higher than for a child whose respondent had some formal education. Systematic reviews of previous studies also reported that maternal education was associated with incomplete vaccination [39, 42]. The odds of dropout for a child whose respondent used an animal to travel to the health facility was 69% higher than for a child whose respondent walked to the health facility. This might be because such a respondent may live in a hard-to-reach kebele or a far rural area.

The odds of dropout for a child whose respondent did not know HEW was 84% higher than for a child whose respondent knew HEW. This could be due to the fact that a respondent who did not know HEW was less likely to receive counseling on immunization and therefore, higher likely to fail to bring a child for next appointments of vaccination. Similarly, the odds of dropout for a child whose respondent had poor knowledge about immunization was 37% higher than for a child whose respondent had good knowledge. In a systematic review [39] and other case-control studies [42–45], the maternal knowledge about immunization or schedule was associated with incomplete vaccination. Similar results were reported in another previous study [46] where the odds of dropout for a child whose respondent did not get counseling from health workers was seven times higher than for a child who respondent got counseling. Unaware of the need for return for the next doses was also the second leading reason reported by respondents for missed doses. Previous study also reported that unaware of the need to return for second-dose was also the major reason reported by mothers for missing second-dose measles vaccine [7]. Although postnatal care (PNC) was not assessed and maternal vaccination during pregnancy (TT vaccination) was not significantly associated with dropout in the current study, not attending PNC and not receiving TT vaccination were associated with increased odds of dropout in previous studies [39, 43, 45, 46]. This could be due to increased awareness about immunization, decreased fear of side effect and increased confidence in immunization program as a result of counseling and experiences at TT vaccination and PNC sessions.

The odds of dropout for a child whose respondent did not participate in a productive safety net program was 64% higher than for a child whose respondent did participate. Similarly, the odds of dropout were 2.6 and 2 times higher, respectively, for a child whose respondent’s wealth index was in the poorest and middle lower categories while it was 72% higher for a child whose respondent’s wealth index was in the middle category compared to the richest category.

Although the strength of this study is that it is community-based and includes both hard-to-reach and accessible kebeles of the zone in a proportionally representative way, the study is not without its limitations. Although we planned to survey 104 kebeles (27 hard-to-reach and 77 accessible) from 13 woredas and 3 town administrations, we were able to survey 94 kebeles (22 hard-to-reach and 72 accessible) from 12 woredas and 3 town administrations. This could affect the true picture of children’s vaccination status. The use of recall (history) may also have induced misclassification bias (overestimation of vaccination status) because of either recall or social desirability biases.

Conclusions

Although the use of recall (history) may have overestimated vaccination status, the level of incomplete vaccination was unacceptable. These results confirmed the findings of rapid convenient surveys (RCS) conducted by the zonal health department. Regarding the factors associated with vaccination status of the children, reasons reported by respondents as part the current study, reasons reported by HEWs on training conducted two weeks before the current study and factors identified by logistic regression were more or less the same. Unawareness of the need for the next dose and absence of vaccine/vaccinator were the main reasons reported for missed doses. Factors identified as associated with vaccination status were mainly linked to availability and accessibility of vaccine and vaccinator, exposure to information about immunization, and wealth status of households.

The availability of vaccinators (health workers) and an adequate supply of vaccines at health facilities nearby the residential areas, community mobilization to create sufficient awareness of the expanded immunization program, and the provision of sufficient details and written instructions to caretakers on when and where to return, could improve the immunization status of children in the zone.

Contributions of the study

The study was initiated by the Zonal Health Office of West Arsi Zone after a rapid convenience survey indicated that DHIS-2 reports were overestimating full vaccination coverage. To confirm the results of the rapid survey with a scientifically sound approach and to challenge the accuracy of the DHIS-2 reports, data were collected from 94 kebeles (70 accessible and 24 hard-to-reach), involving a total of 1,316 mothers/caretakers. In addition, qualitative data on reasons for dropout were collected from approximately 50 Health Extension Workers (HEWs) attending Integrated Refresher Training (IRT) sessions from four woredas.

While it is well recognized that vaccination rates are low in many developing nations, our study provides context-specific findings for the West Arsi Zone. We demonstrated that DHIS-2 substantially overestimated full vaccination coverage compared to both our survey and the rapid convenience survey (RCS). We also showed that the determinants identified through regression analysis were consistent with reasons reported. Unawareness of the need for subsequent doses and absence of vaccine/vaccinator were the leading reasons for missed doses. While these issues are known broadly, our study highlights their persistence in hard-to-reach kebeles and among vulnerable households, confirming that accessibility and communication gaps remain critical barriers.

Regarding programmatic implications for stakeholders, our findings emphasize the need for:

◦ Ensuring adequate supply of vaccines and vaccinators at nearby health facilities.

◦ Strengthening community mobilization and awareness creation through HEWs and WDA/HDA networks.
