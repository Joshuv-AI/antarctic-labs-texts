Ensemble machine learning for predicting costs and cost influencers in breast cancer surgery
Authors: Junlin He, Qing Lu, Xin Qin, Aodan Yang, Mengyang Pang, Yang Yang, Xin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVarious treatments and therapies for breast cancer surgery patients significantly influence breast cancer treatment costs, necessitating a comprehensive analysis of cost determinants to optimize cost management strategies.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 19,094 eligible individuals from SHA2011 inpatient sample (2016–2020) in China. Ensemble models (gradient boosting trees) were trained using 10-fold cross-validation. Permutation importance and partial dependence analyses identified key drivers of total hospitalization costs (inflation-adjusted). The analysis encompassed variables covering patient characteristics, hospital attributes, treatments, and comorbidities.ResultsThe most influential predictor of total costs was LOS, which increased predicted charges by approximately 145 USD per day (training model of RMSLE 0.474, 95% CI 0.466–0.483). Contrary to expectations, patients with a higher drug ratio (> 30%) exhibited lower total costs. Hospital location, number of beds, and radiotherapy also emerged as significant cost factors. In models excluding LOS, the strongest predictors were drug ratio, number of beds, general hospital admission, tumor surgery admission, and radiotherapy.ConclusionThis study elucidated the intricate interplay of factors that affect healthcare expenses in breast cancer surgery patients. These findings informed targeted cost management strategies and resource allocation, offering valuable insights for enhancing the quality and efficiency of breast cancer care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13814-2.

Background

Breast cancer has become one of the most prevalent cancers among women in China and many Western countries [1]. It is projected that between 2020 and 2034, both the incidence and mortality rates of breast cancer will increase in China, necessitating effective prevention and management strategies to control this disease [2]. Annually, more than a million women worldwide are diagnosed with breast cancer, and conventional treatment for breast cancer is surgery preceded, followed, or both, by adjuvant therapy [3]. Therefore, predicting the total costs for breast cancer patients holds vital significance, especially for resource allocation and healthcare policy development in countries such as China and other middle-income nations.

Machine learning (ML) is gaining traction among clinical practitioners for making reliable clinical predictions driven by data. Moreover, ML held the potential to be an effective tool for reducing healthcare costs [4]. While the paramount concern for patients was the availability of scientifically sound treatment methods, cancer treatment costs can be exorbitant. Identifying the most influential factors affecting surgical costs through prediction models can lead to valuable recommendations for reforming China’s healthcare insurance policies and significantly reducing out-of-pocket costs for patients [5]. The financial toxicity of breast cancer patients, which refers to the potentially negative economic impact of cancer treatment on patients, was particularly high, especially in middle-to-low-income countries [6]. In all age groups, healthcare costs for women with breast cancer exceeded those for noncancer patients and women with early-stage breast cancer, necessitating specific healthcare subsidy policies to improve the health outcomes of vulnerable breast cancer patients [7]. Establishing a predictive model for total breast cancer surgical costs and identifying the strongest predictive factors for surgical costs was of paramount importance.

Presently, several scientific investigations have leveraged machine learning (ML) models to prognosticate hospital expenditures across diverse medical conditions, encompassing colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and pituitary tumors [8–10]. Additionally, ML algorithms have been employed to anticipate comprehensive costs following outpatient hip arthroscopy, considering the presence of patient comorbidities [11]. These ML models had been instrumental in predicting hospital costs and formulating precise cost containment strategies. Existing frameworks for breast cancer costs often focus on broader treatment episodes or fail to provide direct, validated cost-prediction tools at the surgical hospitalization level. In parallel with the evolving landscape of big data analysis methodologies, there was a burgeoning body of research dedicated to the utilization of machine learning for the accurate estimation of treatment expenditures. Notably, artificial neural networks (ANNs) have been effectively established to forecast hospitalization durations, appraise hospitalization costs, and devise patient-specific payment models (PSPMs) based on the intricate nature of patients’ medical profiles [12]. Furthermore, ML models have been meticulously engineered to anticipate rates of resource utilization and discern heightened risks of hospital readmissions [13]. However, the creation of ML models in the realm of breast cancer surgery has been comparatively less extensive. The cost gap persists due to breast cancer surgery’s complexity, driven by diverse procedures, neoadjuvant therapies, comorbidities, and hospital-level variations—factors that traditional models struggle to capture. Our study addresses this gap by introducing an ensemble machine-learning model specifically designed to predict total hospitalization costs for breast cancer surgery in China. By leveraging gradient boosting trees, our approach offers an improvement over existing methods by robustly handling these intricate, non-linear interactions and identifying the most influential cost drivers with greater precision [14].

Our study aimed to utilize ML to predict total costs for breast cancer patients who underwent surgery using health administrative datasets from 2016 to 2020 in northern, central, and southern China. Permutation importance and partial dependence analyses were used to identify the most influential factors affecting breast cancer surgical costs. We aimed to showcase the versatility of this model, which could find wider applications within the realm of breast cancer surgical procedures.

Methods

Database

We conducted a comprehensive analysis utilizing a large, deidentified health administrative database encompassing data from three provinces in northern, central, and southern China over the period spanning 2016 to 2020. Our dataset was meticulously constructed in accordance with the System of Health Account 2011 guidelines, and the methodologies for data sampling and standardization have been previously described [15]. Our final dataset comprised an extensive collection of 7,904,664 inpatient hospital admissions sourced from approximately 278 healthcare institutions. These institutions were systematically sampled, representing approximately 23% of the stratified sample of hospitals across the three regions in China. This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of China Medical University. This retrospective database study was granted a waiver of informed consent by the IRB, as it involved analysis of existing de-identified records.

Patient selection

Our study included admissions extracted from the database spanning the years 2016 to 2020, and a meticulous screening process was employed to determine eligibility for inclusion. Initially, admissions were identified based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes pertaining to malignant neoplasms of the breast (C50.0, C50.1, C50.2, C50.3, C50.4, C50.5, C50.6, C50.8, C50.9). Subsequently, admissions were further filtered using ICD-10 procedure codes associated with mastectomy (19301, 19302, 19303, 19304, 19305, 19306, 19307). These procedure codes encompassed various types of mastectomy procedures, ranging from partial and simple mastectomies to complete and radical procedures, with or without the removal of axillary lymph nodes. Furthermore, we enforced a minimum age criterion, limiting our cohort to female patients aged 18 or older.

Variable selection and outcomes

The primary outcome under investigation was total hospital expenditure, quantified in US dollars, which employed the central parity rate (6.8974) of the Renminbi (RMB) to the US dollars from 2020 [16]. To select potential predictors for this outcome, we identified 53 variables based on their established relevance in healthcare cost literature and clinical significance (for a complete list, see Supplementary Table 1). The rationale for our selection was to capture a multi-dimensional view of cost drivers. The study incorporated key domains such as patient characteristics (e.g., age, insurance type), which are known to influence health service utilization and payment structures [17]; clinical and treatment details, with comorbidities systematically identified using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Software [18] to account for case complexity; and hospital characteristics like level and region, which reflect systemic variations in service capacity and pricing. Crucially, as a direct measure of resource consumption, we included Length of Stay (LOS), widely recognized as a dominant predictor of total hospital costs [19, 20] and thus a central variable in our analysis. To account for inflation, all costs were adjusted using the consumer price index, which reached 625.13 in 2016 and 686.5 in 2020 [21]. The final analytical dataset of 19,094 patients contained complete records for all selected variables.

Guided ML ensemble construction and validation

The dataset (n = 19,094) was randomly partitioned into a training set (80% of the data) and a holdout test set (20%). This constitutes an internal validation strategy, where the holdout set was reserved exclusively for testing the final model’s performance on unseen data from the same source population. The model was trained on the pre-processed (centered and scaled) training data using a 10-fold repeated cross-validation to tune hyperparameters and ensure robustness.

A predictive model was developed using the Stochastic Gradient Boosting (GBM) algorithm. The performance of the final trained GBM model was evaluated on both the training data and the unseen holdout test set using the Root-Mean-Square Logarithmic Error (RMSLE). RMSLE was chosen as the primary evaluation metric because it is less sensitive to large absolute errors and is well-suited for right-skewed outcome variables like healthcare costs. The 95% confidence interval for the RMSLE was calculated using a bootstrapping procedure with 1,000 resamples (boot package) to provide a measure of uncertainty around the performance estimate.

To understand the inner workings of the “black box” GBM model, the relative importance of each predictor was quantified using permutation-based variable importance. To visualize the marginal effect of individual variables on the predicted total costs, Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) were generated. PDPs illustrate how the model’s prediction changes, on average, as a specific feature varies across its range, while holding all other features constant. In our plots, the Y-axis directly represents the predicted total cost in USD, thereby showing the isolated monetary impact of each predictor on the model’s output.

LOS is well-established as a primary driver of total hospital expenditure. To account for its dominant effect, we developed two distinct models: one including LOS and another excluding it. This dual-model approach enabled us to efficiently identify and prioritize other relevant cost predictors whose effects might otherwise be masked [22]. Furthermore, to investigate specific associations of interest, sensitivity analyses using propensity score matching (PSM) were employed to explore the influence of drug ratios and tumor surgery admissions on costs.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 19,094 patients, total hospitalization costs were analyzed across baseline characteristics. Mean total costs were $2,561 ± 2,040, reflecting significant variability. Costs increased with longer stays (> 25 days: $5,488 vs. 1–3 days: $1,047–1,293). Older age (> 60 years) was a significant cost determinant. Breast surgery admissions cost more than non-breast admission; tumor surgeries were cheaper than non-tumor. Emergent admissions had significantly higher costs. General hospitals, insurance type, drug ratio (> 30%), and OOP ratio (> 61%) influenced costs. Hospital tier, location, bed capacity, and volume affected expenses. Secondary malignancies, metastasis, infections, and treatments like radiotherapy/endocrine therapy increased costs. These patterns highlight the complex interplay of clinical, demographic, and systemic factors shaping breast cancer surgery costs (Supplementary Table 2).

Model evaluation

To evaluate the effectiveness of our chosen ensemble method, we compared its predictive performance against simpler baseline models. Our ensemble method demonstrated substantially higher predictive accuracy than the baseline models, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. For the model with LOS, 42 of the initial 53 variables were confirmed to be important, 9 were rejected, and 3 tentative variables were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). The gradient boosted tree regressor ensemble, including LOS, showed robust predictive performance for total costs (RMSLE 0.474, 95% CI 0.466–0.483), even on unseen data (holdout RMSLE 0.492), outperforming the LASSO regression (holdout RMSLE = 0.651) and the standard linear regression model (holdout RMSLE = 0.652).

In comparison, for the model without LOS, 39 of the initial 53 variables were confirmed to be important, 12 were rejected, and 3 tentative variables were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, this ensemble model exhibited lower predictive fidelity (RMSLE 0.624, 95% CI 0.613–0.634; holdout 0.623) for the outcome of interest, though it still performed better than the LASSO regression (holdout RMSLE = 0.701) and the standard linear regression model (holdout RMSLE = 0.702).

Length of stay (LOS) emerged as the dominant factor, with costs escalating from $1,108 (SE: [± 33]) for a 1-day stay to $4,965 (SE: [± 109]) for 30 days. Hospital region significantly influenced costs, with central China ($2,706, SE: [± 41]) and northern China ($3,213, SE: [± 48]) exceeding southern regions ($2,714, SE: [± 42]). Meanwhile, patients with drug ratios > 30% exhibited paradoxically lower total costs ($2,175, SE: [± 43] vs. $2,495, SE: [± 37]), suggesting potential cost-shifting effects (Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

With respect to the LOS model, we conducted PSM to investigate the perplexing observation that patients with a drug ratio exceeding 30% exhibited lower total costs and achieved a good balance (Supplementary Fig. 3). After matching, for the drug ratio > 30% group, PSM (n = 1,111/group) showed significantly lower mean total costs ($1,626 vs. $2,606, P < 0.001) and treatment costs ($113 vs. ¥288, P < 0.001). Costs in diagnostic, check, test, and other categories were also reduced (P < 0.001), with bed fees nearly similar (P = 0.053) (Table 1).

Discussion

Efficient healthcare resource allocation is critical for patient access to essential resources. Total hospitalization costs reflect real resource utilization, and identifying modifiable cost drivers can inform cost management strategies. This study used machine learning to develop ensemble models predicting total actual expenses for breast cancer surgery patients—the first such direct estimation in this population. While these costs are vital for policymakers and patients, the link between actual expenses and out-of-pocket (OOP) costs remains complex. The findings from our model can be compared with several related studies. Models developed for colorectal and gastric cancer by Lee et al. [23] and Pratyusha et al. [24] also demonstrated the utility of ML algorithms like neural networks and decision trees in predicting hospital charges. Similarly, Muhlestein et al. [25] used ensemble models to identify cost drivers for pituitary tumor surgery.

Age group, admission type (tumor surgery vs. emergent), hospital type, insurance type, drug ratio, OOP ratio, hospital level, location, annual volume, bed capacity, secondary malignant bone tumors, postoperative wound infection, port removal/placement, pleural effusion, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, endocrine therapy, and supportive care were significantly associated with total costs. These findings align with prior studies [26, 27].

LOS was the strongest predictor of total costs, with charges increasing by ~ 145 USD per day. Hospital region (higher costs in southern China) and bed capacity (larger hospitals linked to higher costs) also significantly influenced charges, reflecting regional disparities and infrastructure impacts. Radiotherapy before surgery correlated with significantly higher costs [28]. These results emphasize optimizing LOS and tailoring cost strategies to regional and hospital-specific contexts [29].

An important finding is the nuanced role of hospital characteristics in driving costs. The higher expenses observed in tertiary and high-volume hospitals are likely attributable to greater resource intensity and overhead. In contrast, the lower costs found in specialized cancer hospitals suggest that procedural focus and standardized clinical pathways can generate significant efficiencies. This indicates that while institutional scale is a major cost driver, specialization is a key factor in cost containment for specific procedures like breast cancer surgery, likely through optimized workflows and enhanced team expertise.

Drug ratio

Contrary to China’s policy to limit drug ratios for cost control, patients with a higher drug ratio (>30%) had lower total expenses. Sensitivity analysis (PSM) confirmed this paradox: reduced drug costs were offset by higher fees in non-drug categories (bed, diagnosis, tests, nursing), suggesting hospitals may shift revenue streams to unrestricted fees. This paradox aligns with China’s 2017 zero-markup drug policy, which eliminated hospital drug profits but incentivized revenue shifting to non-drug services (e.g., diagnostics, bed fees) [30–32]. Zixuan et al. observed a decline in drug fees but a dramatic rise in aggregate inpatient, inpatient diagnosis, inpatient laboratory [33]. Thus, hospitals may offset lost drug revenue by increasing fees in unrestricted categories, explaining lower total costs despite high drug ratios. This contrasts sharply with most other health systems. In the United States, for instance, under DRG-based prospective payment systems, high-cost drugs (especially in oncology) are often reimbursed separately or through “pass-through” payments, making them a direct and potent driver of higher total expenditures [34]. Similarly, in European systems like Germany or France, pharmaceuticals remain a significant and positive contributor to overall hospital expenses [35]. Our finding underscores how administrative policies can create counter-intuitive economic incentives not typically observed elsewhere.

Admission type

Breast surgery admissions were associated with higher average costs than non-breast surgery admissions were. Similarly, tumor surgery admissions were associated with lower costs than nontumor surgery admissions were. Several factors are likely to contribute to this trend. First, surgical teams specializing in oncology tend to possess a high level of expertise, resulting in streamlined and efficient procedures. This expertise not only reduces surgical time but also minimizes anesthesia duration and post-anesthesia recovery time. Consequently, the overall cost burden on patients is reduced. Emergent admissions were linked to significantly greater expenses than nonemergent admissions. Emergency admissions, which typically involve patients with severe complications or inflammatory breast cancer, are associated with higher costs. Weekend admissions did not exhibit a substantial difference in costs. These findings underscore the importance of specialized care and surgical expertise in healthcare cost management [1, 36, 37].

Hospital characteristics

Our finding that larger, higher-tier hospitals incur greater costs aligns with a well-established global trend where major academic and tertiary centers exhibit higher cost structures, often due to a more severe case mix, academic responsibilities, and greater case complexity, as documented in the US and Canada [38, 39]. Our study confirmed this principle in the Chinese context, where costs were significantly higher at general, provincial-level, and tertiary hospitals, as well as those with greater bed capacity and patient volume. Thus, despite differences in terminology (e.g., ‘hospital tier’), the underlying economic principle—that institutional scale, scope, and mission are key cost drivers—is consistent across international health systems.

Comorbidities

Our study identified varied cost impacts of comorbidities in breast cancer surgery. Hypoproteinemia showed no significant cost effect, likely due to mild symptoms or low prevalence. Emphysema and pneumonia, though rare, also had minimal cost influence, possibly reflecting efficient respiratory management [40]. Conversely, lung lesions, joint pain, and chemotherapy-induced bone marrow suppression increased costs, requiring specialized care and prolonged stays. Osteoporosis correlated with lower costs, potentially due to effective preventive strategies [41]. Metastasis and secondary malignant bone tumors exhibited mixed cost effects, emphasizing the need for personalized care. Gallstones, gallbladder polyps, renal cysts, inguinal hernias, and postoperative wound infections raised costs through additional interventions. Other comorbidities—hepatitis B, anemia, chronic superficial gastritis, 17α-hydroxylase deficiency syndrome, lacunar infarcts, mammary duct ectasia, arrhythmia, heart disease, and hepatic steatosis—also increased expenses via prolonged hospitalization and specialized care [42].

Treatment

The presence or absence of various treatments and therapies among breast cancer surgery patients significantly influenced total costs. Radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, supportive care, port removal/placement and immunotherapy were associated with higher costs [43], while traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) treatment was linked to lower costs, but this might be due to the small sample size of patients who received TCM [44]. There was no significant difference in the cost of chemotherapy [45]. This is consistent with international findings. In health systems like Japan’s, where fee-for-service components still exist alongside bundled payments, each advanced treatment modality adds a discrete layer of cost. Even within bundled payment systems like DRGs, complex cases requiring multi-modal treatments (like surgery plus radiotherapy) are often assigned to higher-weighted, more expensive DRG categories or may trigger outlier payments [46]. This demonstrates a global pattern: the use of advanced and multi-modal therapeutic technologies is a primary driver of increased healthcare expenditure, irrespective of the payment system.

Strengths and limitations

Our study possesses several notable strengths. First, we harnessed a large, multicenter database that adhered to rigorous scientific sampling methods and accounting guidelines, bolstering the credibility of our research. Moreover, our findings extend beyond the immediate scope of breast cancer surgery, demonstrating potential applicability to a wider healthcare landscape. We also adopted state-of-the-art machine learning techniques to directly forecast total expenses following breast cancer surgery, introducing a novel approach to cost prediction. To ensure a comprehensive analysis, we incorporated a diverse array of variables, encompassing crucial factors such as LOS, hospital region, bed capacity, radiotherapy, and drug ratio. This holistic approach allowed for a nuanced exploration of the various drivers influencing healthcare costs. Our study’s robustness is further underpinned by the application of rigorous statistical methodologies, including permutation importance, partial dependence analyses, and PSM. These methods not only facilitated the assessment of variable significance but also enhanced the overall reliability and validity of our findings.

We also acknowledge several limitations. Primarily, the observational nature of our study means we cannot establish causality between variables and costs; associations should not be interpreted as causation. While we performed internal validation, we did not perform external validation on an independent dataset from different provinces or healthcare systems. Therefore, while the identified cost drivers are internally valid, the model’s generalizability to other populations or settings requires further confirmation in future studies. It also does not account for the impact of the most recent advancements in treatment or other unmeasured patient-specific factors that could influence cost dynamics. Finally, the analysis was confined to economic outcomes, primarily total hospital expenditure. The administrative database used for this study lacked effectiveness outcomes. Furthermore, it did not contain patient-reported outcomes, including quality-of-life (QoL) measures. The absence of this effectiveness data also rendered a formal cost-effectiveness analysis infeasible. Future research could aim to address these limitations by using more current data and exploring the influence of evolving treatments, effectiveness and patient preferences.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the developed model has the potential to be integrated into real-world clinical decision-support systems, empowering clinicians and hospital administrators to forecast costs and optimize resource allocation for individual patients. These insights can inform the redesign of clinical pathways to focus on LOS optimization rather than rigid drug ratio restrictions. Ultimately, by providing a nuanced understanding of cost drivers, our work can guide policymakers and payers toward developing more sophisticated and equitable reimbursement models, fostering a more efficient and sustainable healthcare ecosystem for cancer care.
