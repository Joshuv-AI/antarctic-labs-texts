Genetically predicted childhood traits and parental health and risk of pediatric psychiatric disorders: A 2-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jielan Zhou, Hao Liu, Rong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The etiology of pediatric psychiatric disorders is complex, involving intergenerational influences and a child’s own developmental health. We aimed to investigate the potential effects of genetically predicted childhood traits (childhood obesity, absence epilepsy, intelligence) and parental health traits (longevity, Alzheimer disease, severe depression) on the risk of several childhood and adolescent psychiatric disorders. We employed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) design using summary statistics from large-scale genome-wide association studies. Data for parental health exposures were primarily from the UK Biobank. Data for childhood trait exposures were from various consortia. Data for outcomes – conduct disorder, mixed conduct and emotional disorders, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and broader behavioral/emotional and social disorders – were sourced from FinnGen and the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, among others. We used the inverse-variance weighted method for the primary analysis, with MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode as additional analyses. To test the robustness of the results, we conducted sensitivity analyses using MR-Egger regression, Cochran Q test for heterogeneity, the MR-pleiotropy residual sum and outlier test, and a leave-one-out analysis. Genetic liability for childhood obesity was associated with an increased risk of ASD (odds ratio = 1.06, P = .016) and ADHD (odds ratio = 1.09, P = .026), even though these associations did not withstand multiple testing correction. No other robust, statistically significant causal associations were identified. Sensitivity analyses showed limited evidence of bias from horizontal pleiotropy for the main findings. Our findings provide MR evidence supporting potential links from genetic liability for childhood obesity to increased risks of ASD and ADHD. These results highlight the importance of considering a child’s early-life health trajectory in the etiology of pediatric psychiatric disorders.

1. Introduction

Childhood psychiatric disorders represent a diverse group of conditions affecting a substantial proportion of children and adolescents globally, with European prevalence estimates for any mental disorder approaching 15%.[1] Conditions such as conduct disorder (CD), characterized by persistent patterns of aggression and rule violation,[2] attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), a common neurodevelopmental condition often persisting into adulthood,[3] and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), defined by deficits in social communication and restricted/repetitive behaviors,[4] contribute significantly to long-term individual and societal burden. Understanding their origins is paramount for effective prevention and treatment.[5]

The etiology of these disorders is recognized as highly complex, arising from intricate interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures.[6] Twin studies consistently demonstrate substantial heritability for ADHD and related externalizing behaviors, as well as for ASD.[7] While genetic studies, including twin studies and GWAS, consistently point to a significant heritable component,[8,9] environmental influences operating at different developmental stages are also recognized as critical contributors.

A child’s own health status and specific characteristics during critical developmental windows are increasingly hypothesized to play a causal role in shaping their psychiatric risk. For example, conditions like childhood obesity,[10] childhood-onset epilepsy,[11] and variations in childhood intelligence[12] are significant pediatric health indicators that have been observationally linked to an increased likelihood of developing psychiatric disorders. However, disentangling whether these associations represent true causal effects, shared underlying genetic vulnerabilities, or confounding by common environmental factors remains a significant challenge in observational research.[13]

Beyond the child’s immediate characteristics, factors within the family environment and the health of parents also represent crucial intergenerational influences. Observational studies have linked various parental psychiatric conditions (depression) with an increased risk of ASD and behavioral problems in offspring.[14] Some studies even suggest stronger maternal than paternal associations for certain disorders like ASD.[15] Furthermore, observational links between parental neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer disease (AD) and offspring neurodevelopmental conditions like ADHD have been reported, potentially indicating shared familial or genetic risk factors. However, interpreting these observational findings is challenging due to potential confounding by shared socioeconomic status, family environment, and overlapping genetic liabilities.[16]

Mendelian randomization (MR) provides a valuable tool to dissect potential causal relationships by using genetic variants as unconfounded proxies for exposures.[17] The random allocation of alleles at conception mimics aspects of a randomized controlled trial, mitigating conventional confounding and reverse causation. Recent MR studies have successfully explored causal links between comorbid conditions like epilepsy and psychiatric disorders.[18] This makes MR particularly well-suited for exploring the causal impact of potentially modifiable factors, whether they are traits characterizing the child’s own development (trait-to-trait) or inherited predispositions related to parental health (intergenerational).[19]

In this study, we employed a 2-sample MR framework to investigate the potential effects of genetically predicted childhood traits (childhood obesity, childhood absence epilepsy, and childhood intelligence) and parental health traits (longevity proxied by age at death, susceptibility to Alzheimer disease, and liability to severe depression) on the risk of a comprehensive set of childhood and adolescent psychiatric disorders (CD, mixed conduct and emotional disorders [MCED], ADHD, ASD, behavioral and emotional disorders, and social disorders). We hypothesized that genetic predispositions underlying certain childhood health traits or parental conditions might exert potential influences on offspring psychiatric risk.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

We implemented a 2-sample MR framework using data from publicly available GWAS to assess the potential effects of parental health traits on childhood psychiatric disorders (Fig. 1). The study adheres to the core principles of MR: the genetic variants (instruments) are robustly associated with the parental health traits; the instruments are independent of confounders of the exposure–outcome association; and the instruments influence the childhood psychiatric disorders only through the parental health traits (no horizontal pleiotropy).[20]

2.2. GWAS summary statistics were obtained for childhood psychiatric disorders

GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the following publicly available datasets, with participants primarily of European ancestry. For CD, data were sourced from FinnGen release R5 (https://r5.finngen.fi/, ID: finn-b-F5_CONDUCT), comprising 407 cases and 215,763 controls. MCED were assessed using FinnGen release R12 (ID: KRA_PSY_CODUCTEMOT_EXMORE), which included 933 cases and 362,304 controls. For ADHD, summary statistics were derived from a Psychiatric Genomics Consortium/iPSYCH meta-analysis (ID: ebi-a-GCST005362), encompassing approximately 32,102 individuals across contributing studies (case/control numbers varied).[21] ASD data were obtained from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium Autism group (ID: GCST010513), consisting of 18,381 cases and 27,969 controls.[22] Additionally, data for broader categories of behavioral and emotional disorders with onset in childhood/adolescence (ID: finn-b-F5_BEHEMOCHILD) and social disorders starting in childhood/adolescence (ID: finn-b-KRA_PSY_CHILDSOC) were also sourced from FinnGen. All contributing studies obtained relevant ethical approvals.

2.3. Data sources for parental health traits and childhood traits (exposures)

Genetic association summary statistics were obtained for 2 categories of exposures. For parental health traits, data were primarily obtained from the UK Biobank (UKB) cohort (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). We incorporated age at death as a proxy for parental health primarily because it reflects the cumulative influence of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors on long-term survival,[23] thereby offering a robust indicator of overall parental well-being and its potential impact on offspring development. Paternal lifespan was assessed using age at death data (UKB ID: ukb-b-11303, N = 3,41,118), while maternal lifespan was evaluated through a separate dataset (UKB ID: ukb-b-12687, N = 2,73,111). For neurodegenerative disease, paternal Alzheimer disease status was analyzed from dataset ukb-b-323 (N = 3,99,793), with corresponding maternal data from ukb-b-14699 (N = 4,23,738). Psychiatric risk was measured through severe depression diagnoses, using paternal data from ukb-b-5942 (N = 3,99,499) and maternal data from ukb-b-10807 (N = 4,23,217). For childhood traits, GWAS summary statistics were obtained from large consortia, including for childhood obesity (EGG Consortium, ID: ieu-a-1096), childhood absence epilepsy (ILAE Consortium, ID: ieu-b-13), and childhood intelligence (SSGAC, ID: ieu-a-16). Ethical approval for this study was not required because we used only publicly available summary-level GWAS data that had already received ethical approval.

2.4. Instrumental variable (IV) selection

We selected IVs using a structured approach. First, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the exposure at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8 for age at death, AD) were identified. For severe depression, childhood obesity, childhood absence epilepsy, and childhood intelligence, where fewer than 3 independent SNPs were identified, we adopted a relaxed threshold P < 5 × 10−6. We retained SNPs with a minor allele frequency > 0.01.[24] Subsequently, to ensure independence, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning using the 1000 Genomes Project European panel reference, applying a stringent LD threshold (r2 < 0.001) within a 10,000 kb window. If a selected primary IV was unavailable in the corresponding outcome GWAS, we searched for proxy SNPs in high LD (r2 > 0.8) using the same reference panel and utilized them if found.[25,26] Finally, the strength of the resulting IV set for each exposure was evaluated using the F-statistic, calculated with standard formulae, ensuring F > 10 to minimize weak instrument bias.[27]

2.5. Two-sample MR analysis

The primary analysis employed the random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, which synthesizes the causal estimates from each IV, weighted by their inverse variance, to provide an overall causal estimate.[28] Odds ratios (ORs) were computed using the generate_odds_ratios (mr_res) function. This method yields the most precise estimate, assuming either no pleiotropy or balanced pleiotropy. ORs and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to represent the change in the risk of the outcome per unit increase in the log-odds of the exposure. For binary exposures, a unit increase in the genetically predicted exposure represents an increase in the underlying genetic liability to the trait, rather than a direct or quantitative increase in symptoms or clinical severity. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, we utilized several alternative MR methods with different underlying assumptions: MR-Egger regression, which can provide estimates adjusted for directional pleiotropy.[29] The weighted median method, robust when at least 50% of the instruments are valid, and the weighted mode method, which bases the estimate on the most common causal effect size among instruments.[30]

2.6. Sensitivity and pleiotropy assessment

We conducted several analyses to assess potential violations of MR assumptions. Heterogeneity among individual IV causal estimates was quantified using Cochran Q statistic within the IVW and MR-Egger frameworks; significant heterogeneity (P < .05) could indicate pleiotropy or other model violations.[31] The presence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (where IVs have effects on the outcome not mediated by the exposure) was formally tested using the MR-Egger regression intercept test (P < .05 suggesting significant directional pleiotropy).[32] We employed the MR-PRESSO (pleiotropy residual sum and outlier) method to detect and potentially correct for horizontal pleiotropy by identifying outlier IVs.[33] Furthermore, leave-one-out analyses were performed, systematically removing 1 IV at a time and recalculating the IVW estimate, to assess whether any single IV unduly influenced the overall result.

2.7. Statistical software and multiple testing

All MR analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6 or similar) in R (version 4.0.5; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Visualization of results, including scatter plots (showing individual SNP effects vs exposure effects), forest plots (displaying causal estimates from each IV and pooled estimates), funnel plots (assessing heterogeneity/small-study bias), and leave-one-out plots, were generated using functions within the same package. Statistical significance for the primary IVW analyses was determined using a threshold of P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Potential effects of genetically predicted childhood traits on psychiatric disorders

For childhood traits, IV selection yielded sets ranging from 4 (for childhood intelligence) to 16 (for childhood absence epilepsy) SNPs (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). All sets exhibited mean F-statistics substantially > 10 (range of means: 21.21–31.24), indicating they are strong instruments suitable for MR analysis. During the analysis, some IVs required special handling; for instance, when analyzing childhood absence epilepsy against ADHD, 2 SNPs were unavailable in the outcome data, and 1 was replaced with a proxy SNP (rs9961251 was proxied by rs9946018). Similarly, 1 palindromic or weak IV was removed from the childhood obesity analysis (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2).

The primary IVW analysis revealed that genetic liability for childhood obesity (Fig. 2A, B, D, E and Table 1) was associated with a nominally significant increased risk of ASD (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.12, P = .016, false discovery rate (FDR) = 0.677) and ADHD (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.18, P = .026, FDR = 0.234). In contrast, genetically predicted childhood absence epilepsy and childhood intelligence did not show significant associations with the investigated psychiatric disorders in the primary analysis, although a suggestive association was noted between epilepsy and MCED (P = .057, FDR = 0.342, Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3).

3.2. Potential effects of genetically predicted parental health on offspring psychiatric disorders

For parental health traits, IV selection yielded sets ranging from 4 (for paternal Alzheimer disease) to 43 (for maternal age at death) SNPs (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4). All instrument sets were robust, with mean F-statistics well above 10 (range of means: 23.05–459.60). Throughout the analyses, a number of SNPs were not found in the summary data for the outcome disorders and could not be matched with a proxy SNP, leading to their exclusion. For example, when analyzing the effect of paternal age at death on ADHD and ASD, 2 SNPs and 1 SNP were excluded, respectively. Similar exclusions of unavailable SNPs occurred across most parental health-offspring disorder pairings (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

The primary IVW analysis identified an inconclusive association between genetic liability for paternal severe depression and an increased risk of conduct disorder (OR = 2.93 × 10−5, 95% CI: 1.06 × 10−8–8.07 × 10−2, P = .01, FDR = 0.36; Table 1). Nonetheless, this counter-intuitive association should be interpreted with caution due to an exceptionally small effect estimate. Scatter plots visually represented the positive association driven by the IVs, while forest plots showed the individual SNP effects and the overall IVW estimate (Fig. 2C). No other associations between the investigated parental health traits and the 4 childhood psychiatric disorders reached statistical significance (all IVW P > .05; Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 6).

3.3. Sensitivity analyses

MR-Egger intercepts provided little evidence of directional pleiotropy across most analyses (P > .05). Cochran Q indicated low heterogeneity for most associations (P > .05; Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 7). Funnel plots for most analyses appeared symmetrical, suggesting limited bias from directional pleiotropy (Fig. 3A–C). Sensitivity analyses did not indicate significant pleiotropy for the obesity-ASD and obesity-ADHD associations. An initial suggestive association between epilepsy and ASD was no longer significant after outlier removal by MR-PRESSO. Leave-one-out plots confirmed that the paternal depression and CD association was not driven by any single influential IV (Fig. 3D–F). MR-PRESSO identified outliers for paternal age at death predicting ADHD (2 SNPs) and ASD (1 SNP), but outlier removal did not substantively alter the null findings (Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content 8). No outliers were detected by MR-PRESSO for the paternal depression and CD analysis.

4. Discussion

This 2-sample MR study investigated potential links between childhood traits and parental health on the risk of a range of pediatric psychiatric disorders. Our findings suggest that genetic predispositions related to a child’s own health status (specifically childhood obesity) may contribute to the risk of specific pediatric psychiatric disorders (Fig. 4).

A simplified directed acyclic graph illustrates the association between childhood obesity and ADHD and ASD. ADHD = attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, ASD = autism spectrum disorder.

Genetic liability for childhood obesity was potentially associated with an increased risk of both ASD and ADHD. These results provide MR evidence suggesting that factors related to genetic predisposition to obesity may contribute to the etiology of these neurodevelopmental conditions. Observational studies have consistently reported high rates of overweight and obesity among children with neurodevelopmental disorders,[34] and this link has been specifically noted for both ASD and ADHD across the lifespan.[35] Our findings align well with previous reports, where we suggest that genetic liability to obesity in early life is associated with modest increases in liability to ASD and ADHD traits. However, whether this reflects pathways initiated by adiposity itself, by metabolic or inflammatory perturbations, or by horizontal pleiotropy cannot be disentangled. Therefore, we cannot conclude that childhood obesity, as a modifiable state, directly causes either disorder. Nevertheless, distinct eating behaviors have been identified in children with ADHD and autistic traits, which may underlie the increased obesity risk and point to complex etiological pathways.[36] Longitudinal designs that separate genetic susceptibility from actual weight gain and that test whether BMI reduction alters neurodevelopmental trajectories are needed before policy recommendations can be considered.

In contrast, the tentative link between paternal severe depression and offspring CD risk warrants careful consideration, particularly given the paucity of existing research specifically addressing paternal effects. To date, studies have predominantly focused on maternal psychopathology, often underlining maternal depression as a critical determinant of child outcomes. However, our findings highlight that maternal severe depression does not exhibit a robust association with CD, suggesting that the singular focus on maternal influences may overlook important paternal contributions. While observational studies frequently report associations between parental psychopathology and child behavioral outcomes such as ASD,[37] establishing causality remains a major challenge.

Notably, in our study, the potential link between genetically predicted paternal severe depression and offspring CD risk presents a more complex picture, requiring careful consideration. Our primary IVW analysis for paternal severe depression and CD yielded a statistically significant nominal association (P = .01) but with an exceptionally small effect estimate (OR = 2.93 × 10−5, 95% CI: 1.06 × 10−8–8.07 × 10−2). This result indicates a substantial reduction in risk, which is highly unusual from a biological standpoint and contradicts typical clinical observations and expectations about parental depression’s impact on offspring, which usually points towards increased risk. Furthermore, the weighted median estimator, which offers robustness to some invalid instruments, produced a result in the opposite direction, suggesting an increased risk (OR = 4.44, 95% CI: 0.89–22.13; P = .05), reaching the edge of nominal statistical significance. Furthermore, this counter-intuitive association conflicts with a consistent body of literature reporting higher rates of externalizing problems in children of depressed fathers.[38,39] The contradiction, coupled with the unstable IVW effect, suggests that the IVW estimate is driven by weak-instrument bias rather than a true causal reduction in risk. Consequently, the findings regarding paternal severe depression and CD risk from this study are inconclusive and warrant further investigation with potentially refined MR approaches or alternative designs that can better account for potential complexities.

To date, studies have predominantly focused on maternal psychopathology, often underlining maternal depression as a critical determinant of child outcomes. Interestingly, our study did not observe a robust association between genetically predicted maternal severe depression and CD, ASD, or ADHD, contrasting with some observational findings. This might suggest that the component of genetic risk for maternal depression captured by common variants does not strongly causally drive the risk for these specific offspring disorders, or that environmental mediation, specific critical periods, or nonadditive genetic effects are more crucial, thereby warranting a reevaluation of conclusions drawn solely from observational designs. While observational studies and biological plausibility might suggest mechanisms linking parental depression to offspring externalizing behaviors (e.g., shared genetic vulnerability between depression and externalizing traits,[40,41] impaired parenting or disrupted family dynamics associated with parental depressive symptoms,[42] or even epigenetic factors[43]), our unstable MR results for paternal depression prevent us from drawing conclusions about whether these mechanisms underlie a causal effect in this context. Nevertheless, divergent postnatal environmental exposures, such as heightened support and surveillance for depressed mothers versus unmitigated paternal interaction patterns, could obscure maternal genetic effects while accentuating paternal associations.[44,45] Further research is required to clarify the true nature of the association and explore potential mechanisms if a causal link (likely towards increased risk, as suggested by Weighted Median and observation) is subsequently established.

The null findings for parental age at death and Alzheimer disease suggest that common genetic variants influencing parental longevity or late-onset neurodegeneration may not be strong genetic determinants of these specific early-onset psychiatric disorders in offspring. This might contrast with observational studies,[16] potentially highlighting the role of confounding (e.g., shared environment, socioeconomic factors) in those reported associations. The lack of a robust association between maternal severe depression and the outcomes is noteworthy, given the extensive literature on maternal depression’s impact.[46] Our MR results suggest that common genetic liability to maternal depression does not robustly cause these offspring disorders, implicating environmental mediation, critical periods, or nonadditive genetic effects.

Strengths of this study include the application of an MR design to enhance causal inference, the use of large-scale GWAS summary statistics for improved statistical power, and the investigation of a broad spectrum of both child-specific and parental exposures alongside multiple pediatric psychiatric outcomes. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of findings against assumptions like horizontal pleiotropy, providing reassuring evidence for the observed significant associations.

However, limitations must be acknowledged. First, MR relies on assumptions (valid instruments, no horizontal pleiotropy, linearity) that may not be fully met, despite reassuring sensitivity analyses. For the association between paternal severe depression and offspring conduct disorder, the extremely low OR highlights potential issues with model assumptions or effect scaling, possibly due to the binary, retrospectively ascertained exposure in UKB (low prevalence, under-reporting, recall bias, and high “do-not-know” responses) that flattens the SNP-exposure gradient and destabilizes the Wald ratio. Second, GWAS primarily capture common variant effects and may miss contributions from rare variants; diagnostic accuracy varies across consortia; and analyses were limited to European ancestry populations. Sample overlap between exposure/outcome GWAS was likely minimal but cannot be entirely excluded. Third, statistical power might still be insufficient to detect modest true effects, potentially explaining some null findings. Fourth, MR estimates average population effects and cannot elucidate specific mechanisms (genetic vs environmental mediation). Fifth, some exposures, such as paternal severe depression and childhood absence epilepsy, are binary and low-prevalence traits derived from self-reported or clinically ascertained data. For these traits, genetically predicted exposure reflects underlying genetic liability rather than a continuous or directly modifiable exposure. As a result, MR estimates may exhibit wide confidence intervals and should be interpreted cautiously. Sixth, after FDR correction for multiple testing, none of the MR estimates remained significant, including the initially suggestive associations between obesity and ASD/ADHD. Consequently, our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive evidence of causal relationships. Seventh, relaxing the genome-wide significance threshold to P < 5 × 10−6 for several exposure instruments increases their vulnerability to weak-instrument bias, thereby tempering the reliability of the corresponding MR estimates. Finally, the exposures are proxies: age at death is an imperfect measure of health, and GWAS diagnoses rely on reported data, potentially introducing misclassification.

In conclusion, this MR study provides evidence supporting potential links between genetic liability to childhood obesity and an increased risk of ASD and ADHD. We found limited MR evidence for the effects of parental longevity, parental Alzheimer disease, or maternal severe depression on these outcomes via common genetic variants. These findings emphasize the complex interplay of factors contributing to pediatric psychiatric risk, highlighting the importance of considering both a child’s own health trajectory and paternal mental health as potential causal influences. Future research should aim to replicate these findings and delineate the specific biological and environmental pathways involved.
