Styloid–C1 internal jugular vein compression associated with spontaneous cervical epidural hematoma: A case report
Authors: Masaki Yoshimura, Maki Narui, Kazuki Kobayashi, Saya Koh, Satoru Iwasaki, Kentarou Naitou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Spontaneous cervical epidural hematoma (SCEH) is a rare condition, and its etiology remains unclear in the majority of cases. Styloidogenic jugular venous compression syndrome (SJVCS) represents impaired internal jugular vein (IJV) outflow between the styloid process and the C1 vertebra and has been associated with several venous hypertension–related disorders, which likely include SCEH.Case Description: A patient presented with an acute SCEH immediately after straining. Imaging revealed a narrow styloid–C1 corridor with the IJV coursing directly posterior to the styloid process (i.e., a retrostyloid configuration). Cervical computed tomography angiogram demonstrated morphologic features suggesting jugular venous compression at the J3 level. Notably, neither an arterial lesion nor an arteriovenous malformation was identified.Conclusion: The anatomical configuration, suspected dynamic venous obstruction, and temporal association with straining supported a venous pathophysiological mechanism resulting in an SCEH attributed to a narrow styloid–C1 corridor with the IJV.

INTRODUCTION

Styloidogenic jugular venous compression syndrome (SJVCS) has recently been described as a cause of extracranial venous outflow obstruction in which the internal jugular vein (IJV) is compressed between the styloid process and the C1 vertebra.[1,6-10] This condition has been associated with intracranial venous hypertension, papilledema, and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.[8,10] However, a potential relationship between SJVCS and spinal epidural hematoma has not been previously documented. Here, however, we report a patient who likely developed a spontaneous cervical epidural hematoma (SCEH) due to dynamic styloid–C1 jugular venous compression.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A patient presented with a sudden onset of posterior neck pain but without a focal neurological deficit immediately after straining. There was no history of trauma, anticoagulant use, or bleeding disorder. The cervical magnetic resonance (MR) demonstrated an epidural C2–C4 hematoma compressing <50% of the spinal canal and cord [Figure 1]. The computed tomography angiogram (CTA) revealed a narrow corridor between the styloid process and the C1 vertebra at the J3 level; the minimal distance between the styloid process and the C1 lateral mass was approximately 2 mm on axial CTA [Figure 2]. The IJVs coursed directly posterior to the styloid processes, producing a bilateral retrostyloid configuration in the absence of arterial dissection, an aneurysm, or arteriovenous malformation. Dynamic MR venography showed positional changes in visualization of the jugular and its collateral venous filling between cervical flexion and extension [Figure 3]. Here, the patient was successfully managed conservatively, as the hematoma gradually spontaneously resolved with preservation of neurological function.

DISCUSSION

SCEHs are widely considered to be of venous origin.[2,3,9] Because the epidural venous plexus is valveless and directly communicates with the cranial venous system, a sudden increase in venous pressure may be transmitted directly to the cervical epidural space. Here, the acute onset of hemorrhage after straining suggested a transient rise in venous pressure. The presence of a narrow styloid– C1 corridor and a retrostyloid IJV configuration provided a plausible anatomical substrate for transient jugular outflow obstruction during such physiological stress. Impaired jugular venous drainage may theoretically elevate venous pressure within the intracranial and spinal venous systems. Thus, transient jugular obstruction during valsalva maneuvers could transmit increased venous pressure to the cervical epidural venous network. Recent evidence suggests that venous compression in SJVCS likely depends not only on length but also on spatial orientation and relationships with surrounding structures.[4] Mantovani et al. demonstrated considerable variability in the morphometric relationship between the styloid process and adjacent anatomy, suggesting that clinically significant venous compression may occur even without marked styloid elongation.[4] A summary of previously reported cases and relevant literature is shown in Table 1.

CONCLUSION

Dynamic compression of the IJV, between the styloid process and the C1 vertebra, may represent a previously underrecognized mechanism contributing to the development of SCEHs.
