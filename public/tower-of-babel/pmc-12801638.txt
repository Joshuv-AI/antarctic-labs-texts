Association between angiopoietin-1 and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: a cross‑sectional study
Authors: Nan Wang, Jie Zhang, Jingjing Zhou, Xiaohan Shen, Yu Li, Yini Ke, Youming Li, Zhenya Lu, Chaohui Yu, Yi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimAngiogenesis is considered a vital mechanism of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), while angiopoietin-1 (ANGPT1) is a crucial regulatory factor of angiogenesis. However, whether circulating ANGPT1 level is related to MASLD remains unclear.MethodsTo conduct this cross-sectional study, adults who underwent their annual health examinations at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University during January, 2024 to March, 2024 were enrolled. Anthropometric and biochemical parameters were measured. Mann–Whitney U test, chi-squared (χ2) test, Spearman’s analysis, step-wise univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to analyze the role of ANGPT1 in MASLD.ResultsA total of 169 MASLD patients and 204 non-MASLD controls were enrolled. MASLD patients had significantly higher serum level of ANGPT1 than non-MASLD controls (44.37(36.86–50.43) versus 35.17(26.10-43.89), ng/ml, P < 0.001). Serum ANGPT1 levels were positively related to the prevalence of MASLD (from 5.71% in the first quartile, to 37.32%, 53.71%, 95.24% in the second, third, and fourth quartiles, respectively, Ptrend < 0.001). Both univariate and multivariate stepwise logistic regression show positive correlation between ANGPT1 and MASLD (odds ratio (OR) 1.088, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.063–1.113, P < 0.001; adjusted OR 1.084, 95% CI 1.038–1.133, P < 0.001, respectively). These positive correlations remain significant in different subgroups of age, gender, w/o obese or hyperglycemia (adjusted OR 1.031–1.172, all P < 0.01).ConclusionsThis study revealed a positive association between serum ANGPT1 and MASLD, suggesting its potential as both a diagnostic and a therapeutic target.Keywords: Angiopoietin-1, MASLD, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has already become the most common chronic liver disease and has caused substantial economic burden with a global approximate prevalence of 30% [1–3]. Currently, there are few highly specific non-invasive diagnostic tools for MASLD and liver biopsy still remains as the gold standard for diagnosis [2]. Regarding therapy, resmetirom, though approved by U.S. food and drug administration (FDA), would only benefit part of a specific subset (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis), leaving a large therapeutic gap for other MASLD patients [4, 5].

The pathophysiological mechanism of MASLD is complicated, involving energy metabolic disorder, gut-adipose tissue-liver axis imbalance, insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and angiogenesis [6–8]. Low-grade systemic inflammation plays a crucial role in mediating hepatic and most extrahepatic complications of MASLD [9, 10]. Atherogenic dyslipidemia is considered a key pathophysiological component and one of the “origins” of MASLD, which underscoring the significance of the cardio-hepatic axis in disease progression [9]. Pathological angiogenesis, which is not only a component of chronic inflammation but also intimately linked to intrahepatic vascular health, is a well-documented feature of MASLD in both rodent models and patients [11, 12]. It has been considered as a vital mechanism of MASH, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [12]. In normal quiescent states, the proliferation of endothelial cells is mainly regulated by survival signals such as angiopoietin-1 (ANGPT1, also known as Ang-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [11, 13]. In pathological conditions, stress factors like hypoxia and inflammation could induce the secretion of proangiogenic signals such as VEGF, angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT2, also known as Ang-2) and fibroblast growth factor, which greatly boost the activation and proliferation of endothelial cells and contribute to the construction of neo vessels [11, 12].

First identified as a secreted ligand for Tie2 in 1996 [14], ANGPT1 is a member of angiopoietin/tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin-like and EGF homology domains receptor (ANG/Tie) family and plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular stability and quiescence [15]. In contrast, ANGPT2 is a competitive antagonist of ANGPT1 at the Tie2 receptor [16]. Previous studies have established ANGPT2 as a contributing factor to MASLD progression, where its elevation promotes angiogenesis, fibrosis, and tumor formation [17, 18]. Elevated Angpt2 gene expression and decreased Angpt1 gene expression had been previously detected in a mouse model of MASH [17], but no study had ever examined the association between serum ANGPT1 levels and MASLD.

According to the above, we assume that serum ANGPT1 level may be related to human MASLD prevalence. And to figure out the correlation between circulating ANGPT1 level and MASLD, we conduct this cross-sectional study in Chinese adults. We depicted and compared participants’ metabolic profiles and explored the correlation between ANGPT1 and other metabolic indicators in MASLD patients and healthy controls. Moreover, we performed subgroup analyses (age, gender, w/o obese or hyperglycemia) to further investigate the correlation between ANGPT1 and MASLD in different subpopulations. Given the fact that current recommended noninvasive tests (NITs) had insufficient diagnostic accuracy (< 90%) [19] and that many patients with MASLD still have unmet therapeutic needs, investigating the potential of ANGPT1 as a diagnostic and therapeutic target for MASLD is of considerable significance.

Methods

Study population

Adults who underwent their annual health examinations at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University during January, 2024 to March, 2024 were enrolled. Participants who met any one of the following criteria were excluded: (i) consuming alcohol 210 g/week or more for males and 140 g/week or more for females; (ii) having other chronic liver diseases such as viral hepatitis; (iii) suffering from malignancy [18, 20–22], hereditary angioedema [23], glaucoma [24], acute infection; (iv) having a previous history of cardio-cerebral vascular event [25]; (v) pregnancy. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University approved the protocol and the study was performed in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki (6th revision, 2008). The ethical review approval number is IIT20231272A. Since this was a retrospective study that only involved re-analyzing existing samples and clinical data, informed consent was waived.

Clinical examinations

Age, sex, height, and body weight data were collected. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight (in kilograms) divided by the square of height (in meters). Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured with an automated sphygmomanometer in the sitting position via standard protocols after the subjects at quietly for 5 min. Pulse pressure (PP) was calculated as SBP minus DBP. Waist circumference (WC) was measured with a non-stretchable measuring tape positioned midway between the lowest rib and the superior border of the iliac crest as the patient exhaled normally [26]. Overnight fasting blood samples were collected from each subject, and complete blood count was performed using Sysmex XE-2100 autoanalyzer (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) while serologic tests were performed via a Hitachi 7600 autoanalyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Total white blood cell count (WBC), differential counts (neutrophils (N), lymphocytes (L)), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT) were recorded and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as N divided by L while platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) was calculated as PLT divided by L. Serum levels of albumin (Alb), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), total bilirubin (TB), direct bilirubin (DBIL); indirect bilirubin (IBIL), creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA), glomerular filtration rate (GFR), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C), homocysteine, ferritin, glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), calcium and ANGPT1 were recorded. The serum ANGPT1 level was measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Q15389, RayBiotech, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The assay was conducted in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. According to the manufacturer, the minimum detection limit for the assay was 30 pg/ml. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) are < 10% and < 12%, respectively. All serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis. To ensure the integrity of the analyte, all samples were subjected to only a single freeze-thaw cycle.

Diagnosis of MASLD, obesity and hyperlipidemia

Diagnosis of MASLD was based on abdominal ultrasound using a 3.5-MHz transducer (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany), according to the guidelines on the management of MASLD given by European Association for the Study of the Liver [2]. MASLD was diagnosed with the presence of ultrasonography appearances coupled with at least one of the following criteria: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dl or a history of hypoglycaemic medication use, overweight or obesity (waist girth ≥ 90 cm for men and waist girth >80 cm for women or BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or specific drug treatment, HDL-C ≤ 40 mg/dl for men, and ≤ 50 mg/dl for women or lipid-lowering treatment, plasma triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or lipid-lowering treatment [27].

BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 was defined as obesity [28] while WC ≥ 90 cm in males and ≥ 80 cm in females was defined as central obesity [29]. Participants with triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L were diagnosed as hyperlipidemia [30]. The imaging data were analyzed independently by two sonologists who were blinded to the clinical and group allocation of the subjects.

Statistical analysis

Ssample size calculation was conducted with PASS (Power Analysis and Sample Size software, version 2025; NCSS, LLC.). Tests for two means (simulations) was used to estimate the sample size. The means observed in our pilot data was 43.02 ng/ml for MASLD group and 39.64 ng/ml for control group, and the standard deviation was 10. Anticipating a potential 15% rate of data unavailability and with a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 168 participants per group were needed.

Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for testing the normality, and all anthropometric and biochemical parameters were described as median and interquartile range (IQR) due to their nonnormal distribution. Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons of continuous variables, while chi-squared (χ2) test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. Spearman’s analysis was used to find out the correlations between serum level of ANGPT1 and prevalence of MASLD as well as other biomarkers of MASLD. Step-wise univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis (backward: conditional; cutoff for entry 0.05, for removal 0.10) were performed to evaluate the risk factors for MASLD. Gender, age, SBP, PP, WC, BMI, WBC, NLR, PLR, AST, GGT, Cr, UA, TBA, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C and ANGPT1 were entered into the original equation when the multivariate logistic regression was performed for the first time. In subgroup analyses, variables for different models were adjusted as follows: SBP, BMI, ALT, AST, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C and ANGPT1 were entered into the original equation for age groups; SBP, BMI, ALT, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C and ANGPT1 were entered into the original equation for gender groups; age, gender, SBP, PP, WC, WBC, NLR, PLR, ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, TBA, Cr, UA, TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C and ANGPT1 were entered into the original equation for BMI groups; SBP, WC, BMI, ALT, AST, HDL-C, LDL-C and ANGPT1 were entered into the original equation for TG groups. All statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software by Dotmatics). P < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered of statistical significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the participants

169 MASLD patients and 204 non-MASLD controls were finally enrolled, and the clinical characteristics of the participants were compared (Table 1). It demonstrated that MASLD patients were older (40 vs. 34, P = 0.001) and male predominant (80.5% vs. 45.6%, P < 0.001), and were characterized with higher SBP, DBP, BMI, WC (all P < 0.001). Significant differences had been detected in blood tests: Complete blood count showed that MASLD patients had higher counts of white blood cell (6.46*10^9 vs. 5.56*10^9, P < 0.001), neutrophil (3.61*10^9 vs. 3.04*10^9, P < 0.001), lymphocyte (2.23*10^9 vs. 1.98*10^9, P < 0.001), and platelet (249*10^9 vs. 241*10^9, P = 0.028); liver function tests found that MASLD patients had higher serum levels of ALT (25 U/L vs. 23 U/L, P < 0.001), AST (21 U/L vs. 17 U/L, P < 0.001), ALP (77 U/L vs. 65 U/L, P < 0.001), GGT (30 U/L vs. 15 U/L, P < 0.001); renal function tests showed MASLD patients had higher serum Cr (80 µmol/L vs. 70 µmol/L, P < 0.001) and UA (377 µmol/L vs. 295 µmol/L, P < 0.001), as well as lower GFR (103.8 ml/min vs. 107.6 ml/min, P = 0.001); plasma metabolic results revealed that MASLD patents had higher FPG (4.59 mmol/L vs. 4.40 mmol/L, P < 0.001), glycated HbA1c, TG (5.7% vs. 5.5%, P = 0.001), TC (4.8 mmol/L vs. 4.46 mmol/L, P < 0.001), LDL-C (2.83 mmol/L vs. 2.50 mmol/L, P < 0.001), VLDL-C (0.83 mmol/L vs. 0.51 mmol/L, P < 0.001) and homocysteine (11.8 µmol/L vs. 10.5 µmol/L, P = 0.014), and lower levels of serum HDL-C (1.04 mmol/L vs. 1.37 mmol/L, P < 0.001); meanwhile, MASLD patients also had higher serum ferritin levels (223.1 µg/L vs. 90.9 µg/L, P = 0.002) than healthy controls. These results suggested that MASLD patients had less favorable metabolic profiles compared with non-MASLD controls.

Association between ANGPT1 level and MASLD

In patients with MASLD, serum level of ANGPT1 was significantly higher than non-MASLD controls (44.37(36.86–50.43) versus 35.17(26.10–43.89.10.89), ng/ml, P < 0.001; Table 1; Fig. 1). To explore the value of ANGPT1 in distinguishing MASLD patients, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was drawn and the area under the ROC curve (AU-ROC) was calculated to measure the sensitivity and specificity. The AU-ROC value was 0.729 for ANGPT1, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.679–0.770 (p < 0.001), indicating fair diagnostic accuracy. At the optimal cut-off of 35.82 ng/ml, the Youden Index was 0.332 (Fig. 1).

To explore the association between ANGPT1 level and MASLD prevalence as well as other metabolic disordered features, all participants were classified into four quartiles based on their serum ANGPT1 levels, (-< 23.24, 23.24–38.90, 38.90–54.57, and > 54.57 ng/ml, respectively). The prevalence of MASLD in the first quartile was 5.71%, and increased to 37.32%, 53.71%, 95.24% in the second, third, and fourth quartiles, respectively (chi-squared P < 0.001 and ‌Bonferroni‌ correction suggested significant differences between every two adjacent quartiles; Fig. 2). The prevalence of obesity, central obesity and hypertriglyceridemia also had similar trend as MASLD which increased along with ANGPT1 elevation (Fig. 2).

These results suggested that serum ANGPT1 is elevated in MASLD patients and participants with higher serum ANGPT1 levels are more susceptible to MASLD and other metabolic disturbance.

Correlations between ANGPT1 and diverse metabolic indicators

In order to investigate the correlation between ANGPT1 and other metabolic indicators, Spearman’s correlation test was performed. For anthropometric parameters, the correlation coefficient was 0.192 (95%CI 0.089–0.290, P < 0.001) for SBP, 0.299 (95%CI 0.200–0.391.200.391, P < 0.001) for WC, and 0.265 (95%CI 0.165–0.360, P < 0.001) for BMI. Representative laboratory test parameters were also included: for complete blood count, the result was 0.262 (95%CI 0.162–0.357, P < 0.001) for WBC; for liver and renal function indicators, the correlation coefficient of AST, Cr and UA was 0.126 (95%CI 0.006–0.243, P < 0.05), 0.142 (95%CI 0.038–0.244, P < 0.01) and 0.140 (95%CI 0.036–0.241, P < 0.01), respectively; and as for metabolic indicators, the result was 0.292 (95%CI 0.193–0.385, P < 0.001) for TG, and 0.131 (95%CI 0.027–0.233, P < 0.05) for LDL-C. (Fig. 3) These results indicated ANGPT1 as a potential MASLD biomarker, the rise of which may reflect adverse metabolic states.

Association between ANGPT1 and risk of MASLD

To investigate association between serum ANGPT1 and risk of MASLD, univariate and multivariate stepwise logistic regression was conducted. In univariate model, significant positive correlation between ANGPT1 and the risk of MASLD (odds ratio (OR) 1.088, 95% CI 1.063–1.113, P < 0.001; Table 2) was found, as well as several other metabolic related indicators such as BMI (OR 1.747, 95%CI 1.557–1.960, P < 0.001), AST (OR 1.167, 95%CI 1.105–1.233, P < 0.001), Cr (OR 1.033, 95%CI 1.018–1.048, P < 0.001), UA (OR 1.012, 95%CI 1.009–1.015, P < 0.001),TG (OR 3.634, 95%CI 1.263–2.152, P < 0.001), HDL-C (OR 0.011, 95%CI 0.004–0.031, P < 0.001), and LDL-C (OR 1.816, 95%CI 1.329–2.483, P < 0.001). And multivariate model results suggested that the positive correlation between ANGPT1 and MASLD was independent from SBP, WC, BMI, WBC, AST, TG, and LDL-C (OR 1.084, 95% CI 1.038–1.133, P < 0.001; Table 3).

Subgroup analyses of the association between ANGPT1 and MASLD

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, it was found that serum ANGPT1 level was positively associated with MASLD. First, MASLD patients had significantly higher ANGPT1 levels. Second, elevated serum ANGPT1 levels were related with higher MASLD prevalence as well as several other metabolic disorders (obesity, central obesity, and hypertriglyceridemia). Third, strong correlation between serum ANGPT1 levels and several metabolism-related indicators had been detected, which indirectly supported the relationship between ANGPT1 levels and MASLD. Fourth, elevated serum ANGPT1 was independently associated with an increased risk of MASLD, and this positive association existed in all subgroups, including male and female, obese and non-obese, young participants and elder counterparts, and people with hypertriglyceridemia or not.

MASLD is considered as the manifestation of metabolic syndrome in the liver [9, 31], making it reasonable that MASLD participants had poorer metabolic profiles compared with non-MASLD controls. As presented in this study, MASLD patients had higher blood pressure, BMI and WC. Patients with MASLD also demonstrated higher levels of liver enzymes, Cr, and UA, as well as elevated blood lipids and blood glucose compared to healthy controls, which indicated higher risk of liver injury, impaired renal excretory function as well as metabolic disorder. Notably, slightly higher white blood cell counts (including both neutrophil and lymphocyte counts) and serum ferritin levels were detected in MASLD patients, indicating that they might be in certain inflammatory conditions [32].

Prior to our investigation, no study had examined the association between serum ANGPT1 levels and MASLD. And in this study, we found a positive correlation between serum ANGPT1 levels and MASLD, with consistent findings across all predefined subgroups, further demonstrating the robustness of the conclusion.

For the past decades, diverse serum biomarkers and combined panels for non-invasive diagnosis of MASLD have been developed [33]. Classic fibrosis markers such as N-terminal propeptide of Type III procollagen (PIIINP) can partially reflect the severity of liver fibrosis, but may lack specificity and may be influenced by fibrosis in other organs [34, 35]. Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF-21), a hormone mainly produced by the liver, is regarded as a promising diagnostic and therapeutic target, while its lack of specificity remains a limitation [36–38]. Distinct from these fibrosis markers, ANGPT1, as a vascular stabilizing factor, may bring new possibility to the diagnosis of MASLD.

Angiogenesis has been reported as a crucial section not only in the pathogenesis of MASLD [11], but also in the progress of tumor, cardiovascular diseases, kidney diseases, other metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and its complications, etc [39–41]. A recent study found significantly prolonged P wave peak time and dispersion in MASLD patients [10], suggesting that chronic inflammatory and vascular remodeling pathways activated in the liver may exert systemic effects [42]. American Heart Association (AHA) had previously proposed the term cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome to describe the adverse interplay among these conditions [43, 44]. In accordance with this, this study found that MASLD participants had significantly higher FPG levels, as well as higher serum Cr and UA levels than healthy controls. As a ubiquitously acting and non-specific vascular factor, ANGPT1 may serve as a critical mediator linking the multi-organ pathology in CKM syndrome.

Angiogenesis can be defined as a hypoxia-stimulated, and growth factor-dependent process [12]: primitive lipid accumulation in the liver induces hypoxia, thereby activating hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs); [45] HIFs upregulate the expression of proangiogenic factors such as VEGF family and ANGPT family [46, 47], and these factors eventually boost pathological angiogenesis. Meanwhile, angiogenesis induced by hypoxia further exacerbates cellular hypoxia [11, 48], forming a vicious cycle that ultimately leading to hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis.

Normally, ANGPT1 is a protective factor whose major function includes promoting healthy angiogenesis and vascular perfusion, stabilizing endothelial cells (ECs) and counteracting VEGF-induced EC permeability, as well as inhibiting inflammatory responses [13, 15, 49, 50]. Meanwhile, ANGPT2, which could competitively combine to Tie2 and had contradictory physiological function to ANGPT1, had been reported as a promoter of MASLD [17].

Previous study had found elevated Angpt2 gene expression and decreased Angpt1 gene expression in a mouse model of MASH [17], which was not entirely consistent with our results. The non-homologous nature of the research subjects (mouse and human) may be one of the reasons for this discrepancy, while the divergent trends in transcription and protein levels might arise from disparities in multi-level regulation. Given the observed elevations in white blood cell counts (including both neutrophils and lymphocytes) and serum ferritin levels in MASLD patients, the elevated serum ANGPT1 may represent a compensatory mechanism, which aims at suppress the overall inflammatory state by countering the pro-inflammatory and destabilizing effects of ANGPT2 [13]. However, it was also reported that in murine liver fibrosis models induced by CCl4, hepatic stellate cells were the main source of hepatic ANGPT1, which subsequently promoted liver angiogenesis and fibrosis [51]. Hence, for MASLD patients progressing to advanced fibrosis stages, the elevation of serum ANGPT1 might reflect a failure of compensatory mechanisms overwhelmed by sustained injury during advanced disease progression.

Exact role of elevated serum ANGPT1 in MASLD is unclear, but according to literature, potential mechanistic model related with HIF/VEGF/protein kinase B (AKT)/ANGPT1 axis has been proposed: lipid accumulation induces HIFs, thereby upregulating VEGF signals and activating phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signal pathway [47]; AKT1 then enhances ANGPT1 expression by activating Notch3 [52]. As a vascular stablizing and anti-inflammatory factor, ANGPT1 could inhibit both forkhead transcription factor FKHR (FOXO1) and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signals by upregulating PI3K/AKT pathway, which subsequently downregulates the expression of ANGPT2 and inflammatory factors, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), et al. [15, 53, 54]. Further studies would testify these hypotheses in MASLD animal models combined with human serum data.

However, there were also several limitations of this study: First, the relatively small sample size and retrospective design constrained the availability of propensity score matching analysis and validity of causal relationship between ANGPT1 and MASLD. In this concern, population-base prospective cohort study is in need, especially concerning HOMA-IR, medication data, comorbidities, and possible assay preanalytic variability. Second, although abdominal ultrasonography is not the gold standard for diagnosing MASLD, its non-invasiveness, convenience, cost-effectiveness, and widespread clinical availability make it a rational and feasible tool for diagnosing fatty liver in this study. Third, in this study, we only focused on role of ANGPT1 in MASLD, while concomitant changes in other angiopoietin isoforms, such as ANGPT2 and ANGPTL3, need further experiments. Fourth, current study centered on hepatosteatosis patients, future study would discuss the role of ANGPT1 in advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis groups along with dynamics.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study conducted in Chinese adults revealed the positive association between serum ANGPT1 and MASLD, which suggested its potential as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target of this disease.
