Surgically treated intracranial arteriovenous fistulas with hemorrhage, resulting in complete obliteration: illustrative cases
Authors: Ako Matsuhashi, Kei Yanai, Satoshi Koizumi, Gakushi Yoshikawa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Intracranial arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are defined as anastomoses between cerebral or meningeal arteries and dural venous sinuses or cortical veins. This fistula used to be acknowledged as a rare disease, and the incidence of intracranial dural AVFs was previously reported to be 0.15–0.29 cases per 100,000 person-years. However, with the improvement in imaging modalities, the incidence of intracranial AVF has increased. The crude incidence of all cranial and spinal arteriovenous shunts is reported to be 2.04 cases per 100,000 person-years. The incidence of dural AVF is 1.04 cases per 100,000 person-years, exceeding that of cerebral arteriovenous malformation, which is 0.805.1,2 In general, its management consists of endovascular treatment, stereotactic radiosurgery, and direct surgery.3,4 With the recent rise of liquid embolization material, endovascular embolization is preferred in a majority of cases.5,6 Especially for dural AVFs, transarterial embolization (TAE) with liquid embolization material has recently become the primary treatment. Transvenous coil embolization is still preferred in AVFs with supply from arterial branches supplying cranial nerves, predominant internal carotid artery or vertebral artery (VA) feeders, and potential extracranial-intracranial collateral anastomosis.7 However, incompletely treated AVFs or cases in which anatomical features prevent endovascular treatment require direct surgery. Here, we present 3 cases of intracranial AVFs that presented with hemorrhage and were treated with direct surgery, achieving complete obliteration and favorable outcomes.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 75-year-old woman presented with sudden pain in her right face and general weakness. Brain computed tomography (CT) showed intracerebral hemorrhage on the right cerebellar peduncle (Fig. 1A). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) showed a pial AVF of the right cerebellum. Feeders were branches of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA), and a draining vein crossed ventral to the brainstem and drained into the left superior petrosal sinus (Fig. 1B and C).

An arrowhead indicates the tip of the microcatheter in the feeder, and an arrow shows the drainer. Angiogram from the microcatheter that is cannulated in the feeder (E). The drainer crosses ventral to the brainstem, and this angiogram shows no flow into the normal cerebral blood flow. Transarterial embolization using N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) was performed (Fig. 1D). Digital subtraction angiography from the microcatheter that was cannulated in a branch of the PICA showed the drainer crossing ventral to the brainstem and showed no flow into the normal cerebral blood flow (Fig. 1E). We injected 33% NBCA into the branch of the PICA, and blood flow into the drainer decreased significantly (Fig. 1F). A few pial arteries ventral to the midbrain seemed to be feeding the AVF as well, but we figured it was not safe to inject liquid embolization material into such arteries considering the risk of liquid migration to the normal arteries. Transarterial embolization was completed, confirming flow reduction of the AVF. However, DSA on postoperative day (POD) 7 showed a recurrence of the pial AVF.

The patient then underwent lateral suboccipital craniotomy via a retrosigmoid approach. After the cerebellomedullary fissure was opened and the cerebellum was retracted, the root entry zone of the trigeminal nerve and the pial arteries, which were feeding the pial AVF, was observed. A more caudal view showed cranial nerves VII and VIII and a branch of the PICA, which was occluded with NBCA. The drainer crossing ventral to the midbrain was observed further into the view, and a clip was applied at the exit of the draining vein from the fistula (Fig. 1G). Postoperative DSA showed complete obliteration of the pial AVF (Fig. 1H). Unstable gait due to the initial hemorrhage on the cerebellar peduncle persisted, but no new neurological deficit was detected postoperatively. The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility with a modified Rankin scale score of 3.

Case 2

An 86-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with sudden headache. Brain CT showed diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and intracerebral hemorrhage in the temporal lobe (Fig. 2A). Digital subtraction angiography showed a dural AVF at the cavernous sinus (CS) (Fig. 2B–D). The diagnosis was SAH (World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies [WFNS] grade I) due to the CS dural AVF (Borden III, Cognard IV). The main feeders were the CS branch of the right middle meningeal artery (MMA), accessory meningeal artery (AMA), artery of the foramen of rotundum (AFR), right inferolateral trunk of the internal carotid artery, and the left AMA. The fistula was at the right anterior part of the CS, and all of the shunting blood flow drained into the superficial middle cerebral vein (SMCV) and then into the superficial sagittal sinus. The SMCV was the only drainer. There were multiple varices on the SMCV.

Case 2. A cavernous sinus (CS) dural AVF presented with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Brain CT (A) shows diffuse SAH and intracerebral hemorrhage in the temporal lobe. Arterial phase (B) and late arterial phase (C) of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) angiograms and arterial phase of the right external carotid artery (ECA) angiogram (D), anteroposterior view, show the dural AVF at the CS. The right middle meningeal artery (MMA), accessory meningeal artery (AMA), and artery of the foramen of rotundum (AFR) are the main feeders, and all of the shunting blood flow drains into the superficial middle cerebral vein (SMCV) and then into the superficial sagittal sinus (SSS). Right ECA angiogram, lateral view, before (E) and after (F) TAE. An arrow indicates the AFR and an arrowhead indicates the AMA, both of which were embolized. Operative view (G) during microsurgical clipping of the drainer. The clip is applied on the drainer just distal to the CS. Postoperative ECA angiogram (H), lateral view, shows complete obliteration of the CS dural AVF.

The patient underwent endovascular treatment. Transvenous embolization (TVE) was attempted, but the microcatheter was unable to be cannulated into the fistulous point. Therefore, instead of TVE, we conducted TAE, embolizing the feeders that were considered safe to embolize, the right AMA and the AFR. The CS branch of the MMA was not embolized because of the anastomosis with the ophthalmic artery. NBCA and Embosphere (300–500 µm, Merit Medical Systems) were injected, and coils were placed in the feeders to reduce the shunting blood flow (Fig. 2E and F). However, DSA conducted on POD 7 showed a residual dural AVF.

On the 19th day after the onset of SAH, confirming remission of the cerebral spasm due to SAH on magnetic resonance imaging, the patient underwent right frontotemporal craniotomy, and the SMCV in which the shunting flow drained from the CS was clipped (Fig. 2G). All of the varices on the SMCV shrunk immediately after the clipping. Intraoperative indocyanine green video-angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the draining vein. Postoperative DSA showed complete obliteration of the dural AVF (Fig. 2H). The patient underwent rehabilitation and recovered with no neurological deficits, with a modified Rankin scale score of 2.

Case 3

A 54-year-old man was transferred to our hospital with the diagnosis of SAH (WFNS grade II). Computed tomography showed a diffuse SAH ventral to the medulla, and DSA revealed an AVF at the craniocervical junction (CCJ) (Fig. 3A and B). There were multiple feeders from the radiculomeningeal arteries from the right VA that drained into the intradural veins. The fistulous point was on the dura mater at the C1 level. Shunting blood flow drained into the contralateral internal jugular bulb, then into the internal jugular vein, with some flow refluxing into the sigmoid sinus. There were multiple varices on the drainer. This was diagnosed as CCJ dural AVF (Borden II, Cognard IIa+b).8,9

Arterial phase of the right VA angiogram (B), anteroposterior view, shows the AVF at the CCJ. There are multiple feeders from the radiculomeningeal arteries from the right VA that drained into the intradural veins. Shunting blood flow drains into the contralateral internal jugular bulb, then into the internal jugular vein, with some flow refluxing into the sigmoid sinus. Operative view (C) during microsurgical clipping of the drainer. The clip is applied on the drainer just distal to the fistula on the dura mater. Postoperative VA angiogram (D) shows complete obliteration of the CCJ dural AVF.

On the day of presentation, the patient underwent lateral suboccipital craniotomy. Occipital bone was drilled out to open the right side of the foramen magnum, and laminectomy of the right half of C1 was conducted to expose the V3 portion of the VA. The VA was exposed extradurally and was exposed distally to the point where the VA penetrated the dura mater. There was a radiculomeningeal artery from the VA feeding the AVF. After dissecting the dura mater, the cisterna magna was opened; and after suctioning the SAH, a draining vein and varices were observed ventral to the medulla. The spinal root of cranial nerve XI and the VA were observed. After observing the draining vein more proximally to observe the fistulous point on the dura mater, a clip was applied intradurally at the exit of the draining vein from the fistula on the dura mater (Fig. 3C).

Postoperative DSA showed complete obliteration of the AVF, and the patient recovered with no neurological deficit (Fig. 3D). The patient was discharged with a modified Rankin scale score of 0.

Discussion

The natural history of dural AVFs with cortical venous reflux that present with aggressive symptoms such as intracranial hemorrhage is poor. In such aggressive dural AVFs, the annual rate of intracranial hemorrhage and nonhemorrhagic neurological deficits is known to be 7.4%–19.0%, and the annual mortality rate is 3.8%.10 With regard to pial AVFs, the association of the conservative management of pial AVFs with high mortality due to acute or subsequent fatal bleeding has been reported as well.11 Therefore, urgent treatment is recommended for AVFs that present with hemorrhage. Unlike with low-grade dural AVFs, close observation or radiosurgery that takes 1–3 years to achieve complete obliteration is not an option.12

With the development of endovascular treatment devices including liquid embolization materials, the majority of AVFs are successfully treated with endovascular embolization.5 Transvenous embolization to occlude the proximal draining vein to eliminate the risk of intracranial hemorrhage is also considered in sinus-type AVFs. However, there are cases in which the anatomy prevents endovascular access or in which complete obliteration cannot be achieved.13

With regard to pial AVFs that present with hemorrhage, past reports have described cases in which direct surgery was effective in achieving complete obliteration.11 A literature review of 83 cases of pial AVF revealed a complete obliteration rate of pial AVF by surgical treatment and endovascular treatment as 96.8% and 86.5%, respectively. Hybrid endovascular and surgical treatment had an obliteration rate of 85.7%.14 Other reports showed 16 cases of pial AVFs, 6 of which presented with intracranial hemorrhage. All 6 cases were managed with endovascular treatment. Five achieved complete obliteration, and one had incomplete obliteration, although the fistula was spontaneously thrombosed at the angiographic follow-up.15 The ideal endovascular treatment is when an adequate amount of embolic agent is injected into the feeder without interrupting the normal cerebral blood flow, which is not always possible due to pial feeders. Optimal treatment for pial AVF differs depending on the location of the fistula and number of feeders and drainers.

With regard to the treatment of CS dural AVFs, endovascular embolization is widely accepted as an effective and safe option. Transvenous embolization is the standard treatment considering the risk of embolizing feeders with branches that supply cranial nerves in TAE.16,17 However, an aggressive case of CS dural AVF was treated with both endovascular treatment and surgery, as in our case.18 The patient was first treated with decompressive craniectomy, followed by TAE, with the intent of conducting TVE later. Unfortunately, the patient experienced a second hemorrhage; therefore, emergent TVE was attempted. However, the microcatheter was unable to reach the fistulous point; therefore, surgical disconnection of the venous drainage from the fistulous point was conducted, and complete obliteration was achieved. Cavernous sinus dural AVFs with aggressive behavior result from a restricted drainage route due to secondary thrombosis neighboring the CS.19 Therefore, it is common to encounter cases with an obstructed route to the CS or compartmentation within the CS, complicating the transvenous approach to the fistula.18 In such cases, direct surgery is an effective alternative procedure. Other treatment options for CS dural AVF include direct microsurgical cannulation of the microcatheter into the SMCV, followed by TVE.20

A systematic review of cases of CCJ dural AVF showed that the obliteration rates after microsurgery and endovascular treatment were 98% and 56%, respectively. Among those with residual lesions, patients underwent either additional microsurgery or stereotactic radiosurgery.21 Ischemic infarction is the most common complication of endovascular treatment, accounting for 11% of major complications. It is mostly associated with endovascular procedures of the spinal arteries.22 In a series of 12 cases of CCJ dural AVF that presented with SAH, 1 patient who had undergone stereotactic radiosurgery soon presented with a second SAH, resulting in death, 1 patient who had undergone endovascular treatment required direct surgery to achieve complete obliteration, and 8 patients that had undergone direct surgery had no exacerbation of neurological symptoms after the operation and achieved complete obliteration.23 With the development of new embolic agents, endovascular treatment is expected to become more effective; however, overall consensus can be achieved that direct surgery is effective in treating CCJ dural AVF, especially in cases presenting with SAH, in which immediate obliteration is needed.

Observations

As we have shown in 3 cases, surgical obliteration is important, especially in cases presenting with hemorrhage and in which immediate and complete obliteration is necessary. When AVFs involve the dural sinus, as in our case 2, TVE is usually the first choice of treatment.24 Arteriovenous fistulas with cortical venous drainage alone without dural sinus involvement, as in our cases 1 and 3, are best treated with interruption of the draining vein close to the fistula. Transarterial embolization can be a curative treatment; however, it is not safe to use embolic agents in branches supplying cranial nerves or in predominant internal carotid artery or VA feeders, as in case 3. Moreover, there are always cases in which endovascular treatment is technically challenging, as in case 2, or has resulted in incomplete occlusion, as in case 1. In such cases, surgical treatment is indicated.

Lessons

Even though all 3 cases presented with hemorrhage, all recovered favorably. Despite the recent rise in endovascular treatment, it is important to recognize situations in which such treatment is not suitable. Direct surgery is effective in such cases to offer the best possible outcome.
