Optimal control strategy for electric vehicle powered by PV arrays and battery using sliding mode control and linear quadratic regulator
Authors: Bibi Tabassam Gul, Atif Rehman, Hammad Iqbal Sherazi, Abdullah Alburidy, Abdulrahman Alsafrani, Omar Alrumayh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study proposes an optimal control strategy for an electric vehicle (EV) powered by PV arrays and a battery employing a current-fed converter topology regulated via sliding mode control and linear quadratic regulator (LQR). To enhance the efficiency of the photovoltaic system, an ANFIS-based MPPT approach is implemented. Current-fed converters are particularly attractive for EV applications due to their inherent advantages in continuous input current, magnetic isolation, and high reliability under regenerative braking conditions. An LQR controller is then systematically designed to minimize a quadratic cost function, balancing tracking performance and control effort. The proposed scheme ensures robust voltage regulation and smooth tracking of the reference currents. Simulation results of SMC demonstrate better performance in terms of dynamic response and current ripple minimization compared to a linear quadratic regulator. The proposed system has been validated through Delfino F28379D in a hardware-in-the-loop setup.

Introduction

The transition from fossil fuel-based energy systems to renewable energy sources (RESs) has gained momentum due to growing concerns over environmental pollution and resource depletion. Internal combustion engine (ICE)-based vehicles remain a major source of greenhouse gas emissions. Electric vehicles (EVs), by eliminating tailpipe emissions, present a viable solution for sustainable transportation1–3. Although hybrid electric vehicles currently dominate the market, a broad-scale movement toward fully electric mobility is increasingly expected.

The global transition toward sustainable energy has further accelerated the adoption of EVs as a cleaner alternative to ICE vehicles4. To enhance their environmental and operational benefits, researchers are integrating renewable energy sources such as photovoltaic (PV) systems and battery energy storage systems (BESS)5. These integrations reduce reliance on fossil fuels and enable decentralized, self-sufficient energy management in modern transportation systems. To harness the full potential of the PV subsystem, a robust MPPT technique is required to ensure consistent operation near the maximum power point under varying conditions.

Although conventional MPPT techniques such as perturb and observe (P&O) and incremental conductance (IC) are widely adopted for their simplicity, they often face challenges under rapidly changing irradiance or partial shading conditions–leading to tracking delays and power losses6. In contrast, the Adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system offers a data-driven and intelligent control mechanism that learns from dynamic input patterns and adjusts the control action accordingly7. By integrating neural learning with fuzzy inference, ANFIS-based MPPT provides faster convergence, reduced steady-state oscillations, and improved tracking accuracy under nonlinear and time-varying conditions. Therefore, ANFIS is adopted in this work to ensure optimal power extraction from the PV system, which directly supports the proposed energy management and control framework for intelligent EV charging.

This research focuses on the implementation of optimal control strategies for EVs powered by a hybrid PV–BESS system. The objective is to achieve efficient and reliable real-time energy management by coordinating the power flow between the PV array, battery storage, and electric vehicle8. The control strategy aims to maintain system stability and enhance overall system performance under variable load conditions9. Ultimately, this approach contributes to the broader goal of advancing clean and intelligent mobility solutions.

Among various control strategies, sliding mode control (SMC) has gained attention for its robustness against disturbances and model uncertainties, making it suitable for nonlinear and time-varying systems. SMC provides fast convergence and strong disturbance rejection, which are critical in dynamic EV scenarios10–12. In contrast, the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) is a classical optimal control technique known for smooth control with minimal energy usage13. However, LQR is primarily effective in linearized systems and lacks robustness under strong nonlinearities.

In this work, SMC and LQR controllers are implemented and evaluated independently. The goal is to provide a fair comparison between the two methods in the context of managing power flow in a PV–battery hybrid energy storage system (HESS) for EVs. The evaluation focuses on key performance indicators such as dynamic response, current ripple, voltage regulation, and control effort. Simulation and hardware results confirm the superior performance of SMC under nonlinear and real-time conditions, highlighting its suitability for EV applications.

System configuration

Figure 1 illustrates the configuration of the proposed PV-battery integrated energy system, where both the photovoltaic (PV) array and the sodium-ion battery are connected to a common DC bus via current-fed converters.

Reference generation through ANFIS

In this work, an ANFIS-based MPPT strategy is developed using solar irradiance and PV cell temperature as input variables, which significantly influence the maximum power point (MPP) of photovoltaic systems17. The system is trained using data to accurately map irradiance and temperature to the optimal MPP voltage. Leveraging the strengths of fuzzy logic and neural learning, the ANFIS model estimates the reference voltage for maximum power extraction under dynamic environmental conditions18.

Figure 2 illustrates the training dynamics of the ANFIS model, which converged smoothly over 250 epochs to a final error of 0.3146, indicating efficient learning behavior. Figure 3 presents the internal structure of the ANFIS model, including five layers responsible for fuzzification, rule generation, normalization, defuzzification, and output aggregation. This layered structure enables the model to interpret nonlinear relationships in a compact and interpretable format.

Photovoltaic array (PV)

The PV array serves as the primary energy generation unit in the proposed system, converting solar irradiance into electrical energy via the photovoltaic effect19. The array consists of multiple PV modules connected in series and parallel to achieve the desired voltage and current levels for DC applications. Incident photons excite electrons within the semiconductor material of the PV cells, generating electron-hole pairs. The movement of these charge carriers across the p–n junction produces direct current (DC) electricity. The efficiency of the PV array is influenced by environmental factors such as irradiance, temperature, and shading. In this study, the PV array is connected to a DC–DC converter, controlled by an MPPT algorithm, to ensure maximum power extraction under varying conditions.

Sodium-ion battery

A sodium-ion (Na-ion) battery is employed as the main energy storage component due to its cost-effectiveness, environmental sustainability, and comparable performance to lithium-ion batteries. Sodium is abundantly available and evenly distributed worldwide, making it a promising alternative for large-scale energy storage applications20. The Na-ion battery used in this system offers stable charge–discharge characteristics and strong thermal stability, making it well-suited for integration with PV systems.

The Na-ion battery operates similarly to lithium-ion batteries, with sodium ions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Na}^+$$\end{document}) moving reversibly between the cathode and anode through an electrolyte during charge and discharge cycles. During charging, sodium ions are extracted from the cathode and intercalated into the anode, typically composed of hard carbon. During discharge, the ions return to the cathode, releasing the stored energy21.

Linear quadratic regulator (LQR)

LQR is a feedback control technique that computes the optimal control input by minimizing a cost function involving state and control variables23. It ensures system stability while penalizing large state deviations and excessive control use. In the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) framework, the cost function is defined as:

25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} J = \int _{0}^{\infty } \left( x^{T} Q_1 x + u^{T} R u \right) dt \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This cost function represents the performance index that the LQR controller aims to minimize over an infinite time horizon. The first term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^{T} Q_1 x$$\end{document} penalizes deviations of the state vector x from the desired trajectory, with the matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_1$$\end{document} being positive semi-definite and used to weight the importance of each state variable. The second term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u^{T} R u$$\end{document} penalizes the control effort, where R is a positive definite matrix that assigns weights to the magnitude of the control input u. By appropriately selecting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_1$$\end{document} and R, a balance can be achieved between state regulation and energy-efficient control24. The optimal control law derived from minimizing this cost function ensures that the system behaves stably and efficiently, avoiding excessive control action while achieving desired performance.

26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} K = R_l^{-1} B_{in}^{T} P \end{aligned}$$\end{document}For the LQR controller, the weighting matrices were set as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q = 1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = 0.01$$\end{document} based on system performance tuning. These values were selected to prioritize tight state regulation while avoiding excessive control effort. The resulting optimal gain matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = 10$$\end{document} was computed using the Riccati equation. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_l^{-1}$$\end{document} minimizes excessive control effort, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_{in}^{T}$$\end{document} reflects how control inputs influence the system, and P is the solution of the Riccati equation that balances state error and control cost. This gain matrix K ensures that the control law \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u =- Kx$$\end{document} stabilizes the system while optimizing its performance.

The Riccati equation is a key component in linear quadratic regulator design. It is an algebraic matrix equation given by:

27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} PA + A^{T}P - PB_{in}R_l^{-1}B_{in}^{T}P + Q_l = 0 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, P is a symmetric positive-definite matrix that must be solved to compute the optimal feedback gain K. The equation ensures that the cost function–balancing state errors and control effort–is minimized over an infinite horizon. In this equation, A and B are system matrices, Q penalizes state deviations, and R penalizes control effort. Solving this Riccati equation yields a matrix P that encapsulates the future cost associated with each state, enabling the computation of a stabilizing and optimal gain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = R_l^{-1} B_{in}^{T} P$$\end{document}. The Riccati equation thus acts as the mathematical backbone of the LQR controller.

As the current-fed converter under consideration has three states, the corresponding state weighting matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_l$$\end{document} is a 3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 3 positive semi-definite matrix. The diagonal elements of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_l$$\end{document} penalize deviations in the key state variables: PV-side inductor current \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_1)$$\end{document}, battery-side inductor current \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_2)$$\end{document}, and the DC bus voltage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_3)$$\end{document}. Comparative analysis

A comparative analysis between the SMC and LQR controllers reveals that the SMC achieves a significantly faster rise time of 0.4991 s compared to 0.4996 s for LQR. However, it also results in a lower overshoot of 3.7771% than LQR’s 5.1444%, indicating better transient performance. Additionally, the SMC offers a slightly shorter settling time, 4.0006 s in contrast to LQR’s 4.0007 s, thereby confirming its superior damping characteristics and quicker stabilization. Furthermore, the undershoot observed in the SMC response is only 0.0007, markedly lower than the 0.0111 exhibited by LQR, further demonstrating the robustness and precision of SMC in handling dynamic deviations.

Hardware in loop results

To assess the real-time performance of the proposed controller, a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) experiment is conducted. The controller and system model, representing a hybrid electric vehicle, are implemented on a TMS320F28379D dual-core microcontroller25. The hardware communicates with a host PC running MATLAB/Simulink via a USB interface26. Within this setup, the microcontroller computes duty cycles in real time based on the control logic, which are then applied to the switching devices of the power converter.

To reduce computational complexity and ensure real-time execution, the system model is discretized. This enables focus on control performance while maintaining essential system behavior. The sampling interval for discretized control execution is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50 \mu s$$\end{document}. An overall control-to-actuation delay of approximately 1.5 s is observed, which includes signal acquisition, computation, and PWM signal output. The system remained stable under real-time constraints, including discretization, sampling delay and as validated by smooth current tracking and no oscillatory behavior in the output.

The experimental arrangement is depicted in Fig. 8, showing the link between the controller board and the PC. The activation of blue LEDs on the microcontroller confirms that the control algorithm and system model are properly loaded and executed on the hardware platform27.

Results from the HIL test are depicted in Fig. 9, incorporating the effects of discretization and sampling delay. The outcomes affirm the controller’s ability to execute accurately in real time, demonstrating stable current tracking and validating the designed control framework under practical conditions.

Conclusion

This paper presented an optimal control framework for a PV–battery-powered electric vehicle system utilizing a current-fed converter topology. By integrating LQR and SMC controllers, along with an ANFIS-based MPPT scheme, the proposed method achieved efficient energy management, precise current regulation, and stable DC bus voltage control. Simulation results validated that while both controllers show comparable performance, the SMC offers marginal improvements in rise time, overshoot, and settling behavior, particularly under dynamic conditions, which may be advantageous in practical scenarios. The real-time implementation on the Delfino F28379D microcontroller further confirmed the practicality and effectiveness of the control scheme under hardware constraints.

Future work will focus on a comprehensive comparison of the proposed strategy with other established energy management systems, including rule-based, fuzzy logic, and neural-network-driven approaches, to further benchmark control efficiency and adaptability. Additionally, the integration of cost-based optimization and analysis of economic feasibility will be explored to assess the practical deployment potential of the system. Scalability to larger EV fleets and its impact on long-term battery health and degradation under different usage profiles will also be investigated. Furthermore, future work may explore advanced variants of sliding mode control to mitigate chattering effects and enhance control smoothness under high-frequency switching conditions. Real-time validation will be carried out under varying load and grid-interactive scenarios to evaluate system resilience, communication requirements, and grid support capabilities. Future work will also consider internal parameter variations–such as inductance changes, battery resistance, and sensor inaccuracies–to evaluate the robustness of the proposed control strategy under realistic conditions.
