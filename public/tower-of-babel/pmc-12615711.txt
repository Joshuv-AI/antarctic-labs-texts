An innovative foundation for enhancing the mechanism of transferring vertical loads on sandy soil
Authors: Naif Alsanabani, Wagdi Hamid, Ahmed Alnuaim, Khalid Al-Gahtani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an innovative foundation design to enhance the mechanism of vertical load transfer to the soil, thereby improving structural stability and sustainability. Traditional foundations, such as isolated footings and mat foundations, often face performance and cost challenges when subjected to high loading conditions, as their capacity to transfer load efficiently to the soil is limited. The proposed “teeth foundation” consists of a shallow foundation with multiple projections, optimizing load distribution and minimizing subsidence. This foundation is particularly suitable for structures on sandy soils, where conventional shallow foundations can struggle to cost-effectively manage settlement and bearing capacity under heavy loads without requiring huge dimensions. Using the PLAXIS 3D finite element program, the behavior of teeth foundations for different numbers of projections, projection depths, and foundation depths under various conditions is analyzed and compared to conventional isolated foundations. The study highlights the potential of teeth foundations to replace both isolated and mat foundations, offering significant cost savings and reduced environmental impact. The main finding revealed that the average improvement ratio, defined as the ratio of the bearing capacity of the teeth foundation to that of the corresponding isolated footing, was 1.48. This research contributes to the field by addressing the transition from isolated footings to more efficient foundation systems, providing valuable insights for decision-makers and stakeholders in construction project sustainability and performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23688-3.Keywords: Bearing capacity, Teeth foundation, Depth, PLAXIS, SettlementSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

The functional performance of structures and their sustainability are significantly influenced by the design and implementation of their foundations. Foundations serve as the critical interface between structures and the ground, distributing loads to prevent excessive soil stresses and enhancing stability against natural forces such as earthquakes and floods1. The role of foundations extends beyond mere structural support; they are pivotal in the sustainability process of construction projects. It is evident in the exploration of sustainable materials in foundation construction, which can affect the structural behavior under loads, demonstrating the potential for reduced environmental impact through material selection. This conceptual extension underscores the multifaceted role of foundations in ensuring long-term sustainability and stability, both structurally and organizationally. Innovative foundation designs, such as those incorporating tensioning members for pretensioned structures or advanced anchoring systems, further illustrate the evolving nature of foundation technology in enhancing structural performance and sustainability2–4. These advancements reduce CO2 emissions by optimizing material transportation and construction processes, aligning with global sustainability goals2,5. The importance of considering different framing methods in reducing the embodied carbon in foundations also highlights the role of structural engineers in promoting sustainability through design choices6. Additionally, addressing challenges posed by difficult foundation soils and inadequate designs through consolidation solutions emphasizes the necessity of adaptable and resilient foundation strategies for sustainable structural performance. In summary, foundations play a crucial role in structures’ functional performance and sustainability, from supporting physical loads to embodying principles of environmental stewardship and organizational stability7.

The isolated footing, also known as a separate foundation, is considered the most cost-effective choice for various foundations due to its easy installation and reduced reinforcement requirements. However, challenges arise when the superstructure load applied to the footings increases. In such cases, the combined foundation option is employed when there is an overlap in the loading spaces of adjacent separate foundations. This option significantly escalates the construction cost and complexity. Conversely, mat foundations are utilized when the total area covered by isolated foundations exceeds 50% of the building area, substantially increasing material and construction costs.

In this study, an innovative foundation called a teeth foundation will be developed, which outperforms single foundations in terms of load transfer, stress reduction, and minimization of potential subsidence caused by applied forces. A teeth foundation consists of a single foundation with several protrusions whose depth does not exceed the width of the foundation. The behavior of these foundations under various vertical loads and different soil conditions will be analyzed using the PLAXIS program. A comparison will be made between the behavior of the teeth foundation and the corresponding isolated foundations. The load-settlement curve of the foundation modeled using PLAXIS 3D will be compared with the real load-settlement curve of the corresponding foundation in order to validate the program-generated model.

The outcomes of this study will benefit decision-makers and stakeholders. The results will help address the issue of transitioning from isolated footings to a common foundation by adopting this improved foundation type due to its superior performance. Similarly, isolated foundations may be replaced with teeth foundations if the total area covered by isolated footings exceeds half of the building area, eliminating the need for mat foundations, resulting in significant cost savings for materials and construction expenses.

Literature review

This section is partitioned into two sections. The initial section focuses on the study’s topic by defining the teeth foundation and discussing the considerations in selecting an appropriate foundation type. The second section describes the finite element program used in the study.

Define teeth foundation

A teeth foundation can be defined as a shallow foundation with several projections at the base level; its height ranges from zero to the shallow foundation width (B), as shown in Fig. 1. To ensure a distinct difference between the projection (teeth) and the concrete pile, it is essential for the height of the projection to be smaller than the minimum depth or height of the pile. This distinction ensures that the projection stands out and is easily distinguishable from the main body of the pile. By maintaining a noticeable height difference, the projection serves its intended purpose effectively and provides a clear visual contrast to the concrete pile.

Teeth foundation geometry.

The geometric constraint that the projection height must not exceed the foundation width (B) or a practical limit of 4 m is a fundamental design rule, not an arbitrary choice. This constraint is imposed for two primary reasons: geotechnical behavior and constructability. Theoretically, it ensures the projections remain squat, forcing the primary load-transfer mechanism to be the confinement of the soil mass between the teeth, creating a composite, rigid block that behaves as an enhanced shallow foundation. If were significantly greater than B, the behavior would shift towards that of individual piles, engaging deep foundation mechanics (e.g., significant skin friction along slender elements and end-bearing) and defeating the purpose of a simple, monolithic shallow system. Practically, the 4-m limit represents a standard threshold for open-trench excavation without requiring specialized shoring or deep foundation equipment, thereby preserving the system’s construction simplicity and cost-effectiveness. This constraint is what definitively differentiates the teeth foundation from a piled raft and establishes its unique operational domain.

The innovative foundation differs from a deep foundation in the construction process and height of projection. Regarding construction processing, the innovative foundation (teeth foundation) has only three processes: excavation, reinforcing, and pouring concrete, as shown in Fig. 2. On the other hand, the deep foundation generally consists of the holing of piles, pile reinforcing, concrete pouring of the pile, reinforcing of the pile cap, and concrete pouring for the pile cap, as indicated in Fig. 3. It is important to recognize that the concrete of the head piles should be broken before the pile cap reinforcing stage to connect the pile reinforcement with the pile cap reinforcement. Based on the construction process of the two foundation types, the teeth foundation is easy to install and close to the construction of the single footing (excavation, reinforcement, and pouring).

Teeth foundation processing.

Deep foundation processing.

It is important to differentiate the “teeth foundation” concept from a short-piled raft foundation, as the two differ fundamentally in their geotechnical behavior, design intent, and construction process. While a short-piled raft uses discrete, slender piles to reduce settlement by transferring load to deeper strata, a teeth foundation utilizes squat, non-slender projections with a low height-to-width ratio. The main mechanism of the teeth is not individual load transfer but rather the confinement of the soil mass between the projections. This mechanism generates a composite soil-concrete block that behaves as a single, deeper, and more rigid shallow foundation, thereby enhancing the overall bearing capacity by extending the shear failure plane deeper into the ground. Furthermore, dissimilar the often separate construction processes for piles and rafts, the teeth foundation is designed to be a monolithically cast unit, aligning its construction methodology with that of a conventional shallow footing (as shown in Fig. 2), not a deep foundation system. Therefore, the term “teeth foundation” is used to emphasize its role as a geometric enhancement of a shallow foundation’s interaction with the soil, rather than as a hybrid deep foundation system.

This design aims to enhance the interaction between the foundation and the subsoil, to optimize structural stability and reduce settlement, as will be demonstrated in the Results and Discussion section.

The geometric constraints on projection height are fundamental to the “teeth foundation” concept and characterize it methodologically from a piled foundation, where the pile’s length is an output of the analysis. However, the teeth foundation is devised as an enhanced shallow foundation, where the projections are not designed as individual piles. The constraint that the projection height (Hpr) remains smaller than the foundation width (B) is a deliberate design choice to ensure the projections function primarily to confine the soil and generate a composite action, rather than transferring load to a deep bearing stratum.

This technique is not methodologically backward; rather, it represents a different design philosophy. By intentionally keeping the projections short and squat, their behavior is controlled by shallow foundation mechanics—improving the soil mass immediately below the footing—rather than the deep foundation mechanics of skin friction and end-bearing along a slender element. This defining constraint ensures the system’s novelty and its construction efficiency as a monolithic shallow footing. The minimum pile lengths8 are therefore not used here to calculate a required projection length, but to establish a clear upper-bound threshold that differentiates this innovative shallow foundation concept from conventional deep foundation elements.

The concept of optimizing pile length has been further explored in various contexts. For instance, Suro et al.9 introduced the idea of Short Piled Raft foundations, which combine shorter piles with a raft to reduce settlement, with specific pile spacing identified through optimization studies to maintain stability. Štrukelj and Macuh10 analyzed load transfer mechanisms to demonstrate how reducing pile lengths for cooling tower foundations can result in significant cost savings without compromising safety or performance. Similarly, Waal et al.11 developed a method for sinking piles to depths where soil resistance parameters are met, ensuring stability while minimizing unnecessary pile penetration. These approaches emphasize the importance of understanding soil-structure interaction to achieve efficient and practical designs.

Advanced methodologies also support the reduction of pile lengths through data-driven techniques. Zhang et al.12 employed a Bayesian approach to incorporate pile load test results into the design, demonstrating that shorter piles can be justified with higher resistance factors. Nakanishi and Takewaki13 developed an optimal design method for piled raft foundations that minimizes total pile length under settlement constraints, highlighting the critical role of raft-pile-soil interaction in determining performance. Additionally, Khouravifar and Zafir14 utilized p-y curves to prevent excessive pile lengths due to brittle failure responses, underscoring the importance of accurate modeling in foundation design. These studies collectively illustrate the technical, environmental, and material considerations required to determine the minimum length of piles.

The simplified construction process of teeth foundations also highlights their advantages over traditional deep foundations. Unlike deep foundations, which involve pile installation, pile cap reinforcement, and additional concrete pouring, teeth foundations require only excavation, reinforcement, and concrete pouring. Takeuchi Construction Co15. emphasized the benefits of simplified construction methods, while Frisk and Lokkas et al. discussed how streamlined processes can reduce material use and improve sustainability2,3. These findings align with teeth foundation designs, which aim to minimize material costs without compromising performance.

General principles of soil-structure interaction, such as those described by Budhu1, are foundational to this study. However, the specific geometric constraints defining the teeth foundation are derived from its core design philosophy and practical considerations. The rule that the projection height (Hpr) should not exceed the smaller of the foundation width (B) or a practical limit of 4 m is a crucial design choice. This constraint ensures two key outcomes:

For geotechnical behavior, it guarantees that the projections remain squat and act integrally with the foundation slab to confine the soil, promoting the behavior of an enhanced shallow foundation rather than a piled raft system. Considerations in the selection of foundation

There are multifaceted considerations in foundation selection. The cost of constructing foundations varies significantly across different types, influenced by material quantities, building characteristics, and the foundation’s design and implementation efficiency. Hok Soeng et al.16 conclude that shallow foundations on properly compacted backfill, specifically weak sand, offer a more cost-effective alternative to deep foundations for low-rise buildings. Conversely, crawl space foundations with stem walls were considered the priciest option. This cost disparity arises from differences in material quantities and overall expenses, which fluctuate according to building dimensions, aspect ratios, and elevation requirements17. Alsanabani et al.18 highlighted the importance of selecting the appropriate foundation type through a value engineering approach, considering criteria such as safety, buildability, and environmental impact, which can significantly affect project costs. Mets et al.19 discussed innovative solutions like wedged piles and screw piles in Estonia, which have led to substantial cost reductions in foundation construction by decreasing concrete content and utilizing design cooperation between piles and grillage. These examples highlight a broader trend in foundation engineering toward geometric and systemic optimization to achieve performance targets with greater material efficiency. Nawaz et al.20 introduced an optimization process for designing shallow foundations that balances construction costs with technical requirements. Their findings demonstrate that applying Vesic’s theory can lead to substantial cost savings compared to traditional design methods. These studies emphasize the multifaceted considerations in foundation selection, including cost, safety, environmental impact, and the potential for innovation and efficiency improvements in construction practices.

FEM software used

The PLAXIS 3D program offers several advantages in geotechnical engineering applications. It enables the analysis of road embankment stability with different reinforcing materials like geocells21. Additionally, it supports the design and analysis of bridge foundations under diverse loading conditions, adhering to different engineering codes22. It facilitates rapid, accurate three-dimensional geological modeling23. Additionally, PLAXIS 3D allows for the analysis of settlements in pile foundations under varying conditions, such as different numbers of piles and water table considerations24. The program’s capabilities include finite element analysis, stress/strain evaluations, and pore water pressure assessments, providing a comprehensive tool for geotechnical engineers to assess and optimize the performance and safety of various structures and foundations.

Determine the main parametric study

Based on the information presented in the introduction section, the main parameters that describe the geometry of the foundation presented in Fig. 1 are the width (B) and length (L) of the foundation, the depth of projection (Hpr), the number of projections (Npr), and the cross-sectional area of the projections. Additionally, essential parameters that control the behavior of the foundation include subsoil conditions and embedment depth (Df). In this study, a square foundation with a width of 2.00 m was considered to eliminate the effect of aspect ratio. The cross-section of each projection was also square, with a width of 0.20 m. Both the horizontal dimensions of the foundation and projections were kept constant. However, parameters such as Hpr, Npr, Dp, and sand conditions were varied to capture the behavior of the innovative foundation design.

Develop FEM model

PLAXIS 3D software (Version 22.02.00.1078). Available at: https://www.bentley.com/software/plaxis/, is a powerful finite element software program that analyzes geotechnical systems. Its role in investigating geotechnical systems includes several vital functions, such as modeling complex geometries, soil-structure interaction, and stress and deformation analysis. Therefore, the PLAXIS 3D was utilized in this paper. PLAXIS-3D provides boundary conditions beyond standard fixities, allowing for a more accurate representation of far-field soil behavior. Although real-world scenarios involve an infinite domain, this must be approximated as a finite domain in the geometry model. In order to simulate far-field behavior by absorbing stress increments, appropriate boundary conditions are essential. During static loading, the boundary conditions were set as follows: the nodes on the vertical symmetry plane perpendicular to the X-direction were fixed in the X-direction, the nodes on the vertical symmetry plane perpendicular to the Y-direction were fixed in the Y-direction, and the nodes on the bottom plane were fixed in all directions. To minimize the impact of boundaries on the FEM outcomes, the boundary distance of the model in static analysis should be at least five times the structure’s dimensions25. For dynamic analysis, an even greater value is recommended. Bhatia26 suggested that the dimension of the soil region should range from six to eight times the dimension of the structure. In some FEM programs, such as Abaqus, infinite elements are employed at the edge of the domain to represent an infinite horizontal domain. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify the optimal static-analysis boundary domain. This analysis examined the domain size and boundary fixity for sizes 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15 times the diameter of the foundation. To ascertain the impact of domain size on settlement, the vertical displacement of the foundation under a static load (comprising its weight and an additional 10 kN) was analyzed for various domain extents. In this analysis, the foundation was represented using a plate element, assuming the concrete’s linear elastic behavior characteristic. A visual representation of the system’s boundary conditions can be found in Fig. 5.

Analysis of the correlation between foundation settlement and the extent of the boundary domain revealed a significant reduction in boundary effects at larger domain sizes. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the rate of settlement increase diminishes noticeably beyond a boundary domain five times the foundation’s diameter. A settlement difference of approximately 6.5% still exists between the 5D and 15D domains, indicating that boundary effects are not eliminated. However, this reducing influence suggests that further increases in domain size produce progressively smaller gains in accuracy at a significant computational cost. Acknowledging this, and to guarantee the model was sufficiently large to minimize these residual effects to an acceptable level, the model’s dimensions were extended to 7.5 times the foundation dimensions for all subsequent analyses. This choice represents a well-established compromise between capturing far-field soil behavior with high accuracy and maintaining computational efficiency.

Influence of the boundary domain on the foundation settlement.

Consequently, the model’s dimensions were extended to 7.5 times the dimensions of the foundation model. Within the PLAXIS-3D software, element sizes can be set to five distinct levels of refinement. To ensure a high degree of accuracy in capturing stress concentrations and deformation patterns around the foundation, a Fine mesh setting was consistently applied across the entire model domain. It resulted in a typical model consisting of approximately 35,209 10-node tetrahedral elements, with an average element size of approximately 0.75 m in the region immediately surrounding the foundation and its projections. This level of discretization was deemed sufficient to ensure that the numerical results were independent of the mesh density.

Implement the validation process of the developed model

Before analyzing the innovative teeth foundation, it was essential to validate the accuracy of the numerical modeling procedures employed in PLAXIS 3D. To achieve this, an independent validation was performed using a well-documented case study from the literature: the foundation mat of the Savings Bank Building in Adelaide, Australia, as detailed by27 and also analyzed by28.

A new finite element model was built in PLAXIS 3D, carefully replicating the geometric and material properties of the case study. The modeling included the 33.5 m by 39.5 m raft foundation, the stratified soil profile, and all specified material parameters such as elastic moduli and Poisson’s ratios, as described in the original study. The model was then subjected to the reported distributed pressure of 134 kN/m2. The settlement profile predicted by this independent PLAXIS 3D model was then compared against the results published by28.

The results of this validation are visualized in Fig. 9, which plots the settlement predicted by the current study’s PLAXIS 3D FEM against the solution presented by Chow28. The close convergence between the two curves demonstrates that the modeling techniques, meshing strategy, and boundary conditions adopted in this study are capable of accurately predicting foundation settlement. This successful validation provides confidence in the reliability of the numerical model for its subsequent application to the parametric analysis of the teeth foundation.

Consequently, the behavior of the foundation will be studied concerning several parameters, including the length of the overhang, the depth of the foundation, the number of overhangs, and the nature of the foundation soil.

To accurately capture the soil-structure interaction, which is critical to the behavior of the teeth foundation, interface elements were defined along all foundation-soil boundaries. These zero-thickness elements allow for the simulation of realistic slip and friction between the concrete and the surrounding sand. The strength of these interfaces was controlled by the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion and linked to the adjacent soil’s properties through an interface strength reduction factor (R_inter). For this study, a value of R_inter = 0.8 was adopted. It is a typical and conservative value used for interfaces between cast-in-place concrete and sandy soils, representing the fact that the interface is likely to be weaker and smoother than the surrounding soil.

Results and discussion

The behavior of the teeth foundation is influenced by several interrelated factors, including the number of projections (Npr), the depth of projections (Hpr), the foundation embedment depth (Dp), and the properties of the surrounding soil. The following aspects were analyzed in greater detail to provide a deeper understanding of the teeth foundation’s performance:

Effect of number and depth of projections

The load settlement curves of the foundation rested in medium sand for different Dp were shown in Fig. 9 (a), Fig. 9 (b), and Fig. 8 (c) for Np of 1, 4, and 5, respectively. The bearing capacity of the foundation was determined based on load-settlement curves. Determining the bearing capacity of a foundation from a load-settlement curve involves several methods, each of which aims to interpret the data obtained from load tests to estimate the maximum load the foundation can support without experiencing excessive settlement or failure. Common methods include the double tangent, log–log, secant modulus, hyperbolic, 20% settlement criteria, elastic settlement, Brinch Hansen 80% criterion, and Hansen’s method. In this paper, the 20% settlement criteria method was used to find the Qu, where the load that induces settlement of 20% of the width of the foundation is considered the Qu.

Figure 11 (a) shows that the curves differ slightly for Np of 1. Hence, the Npr of 1 has a minor influence on load settlement curve performance and the bearing capacity of the teeth foundation (Qu_TF). On the other hand, the projection depth significantly influences the performance of the load-settlement curve for Np of 4 and 5, as shown in Figs. 11(b) and (c), respectively.

Load-settlement curve of the innovative foundation for (a) Npr = 1, (b) Npr = 4, and (c) Npr = 5.

An interesting nuance is observed when comparing the initial stiffness for the shallowest projections (Dp/B = 0.25). The configuration with four projections (Fig. 11b) exhibits slightly higher stiffness than the configuration with five projections (Fig. 11c). This result can be attributed to the mechanism of soil confinement. For very short projections, the central fifth “tooth” is not deep enough to effectively confine the soil or contribute significant friction. Instead, it may produce a point of stress concentration and disrupt the formation of a single, efficient pressure bulb directly beneath the raft. In contrast, the four corner projections are more effective at anchoring the extents of the developing failure surface. This effect diminishes as projections become deeper, at which point the enhanced frictional area and confinement provided by the fifth projection contribute to a superior overall performance.

The depth of the projections plays a critical role in enhancing bearing capacity due to its direct influence on skin friction and surcharge stress. As the projection depth increases, the contact area between the foundation and the surrounding soil grows, allowing for greater mobilization of skin friction. This observation is consistent with findings by Pereira Garcia (2023), who noted that deeper foundations provide greater resistance through skin friction, contributing to overall stability and load-bearing capacity.

It is important to address the apparent contradiction between the observed benefit of increasing projection depth and the definitional limitations imposed on the teeth foundation’s height (i.e., Hpr < B). The findings, supported by Pereira Garcia (2023), confirm the general principle that deeper embedment and increased skin friction enhance bearing capacity. However, the “teeth foundation” is not proposed as an infinitely deep system but rather as an optimized intermediate foundation type.

The height limitation is an intentional design constraint that defines the system’s operational and economic scope. It ensures the foundation (1) behaves as a monolithic, enhanced shallow footing governed by soil confinement, rather than transitioning to a piled raft system with different mechanics, and (2) remains economically and logistically simple to construct using shallow excavation techniques. Therefore, the goal is not to maximize depth in absolute terms, but to achieve the most significant performance gain possible within the constraints that keep the foundation simple, cost-effective, and distinct from a deep foundation system. The results of this study explore the performance improvements within this specific, optimized domain.

Additionally, the surcharge stress exerted on the soil beneath the foundation increases with projection depth. This stress improves the soil’s ability to resist vertical loads, particularly in dense and medium sands. Budhu1 emphasized that deeper embedment leads to higher confining pressures, which enhance the soil’s load-bearing capacity and reduce settlement. This concept supports the results of the study, where the enhancement of bearing capacity increased significantly with Dₚ/B values ranging from 0.25 to 1.00.

The number of projections also substantially impacts the foundation’s performance. Increasing Nₚ enhances the total contact area with the soil, leading to better load distribution and reduced stress concentration. This finding aligns with the principles described by Suro et al. (2016), who demonstrated that increasing pile or projection spacing in short-piled raft foundations improves settlement performance by optimizing load transfer to the subsoil.

The observed performance improvements, where bearing capacity increases with both projection depth (Dp/B) and number (Npr), validate the efficacy of the chosen geometric constraints. The results demonstrate that significant gains (e.g., up to 1.62) are achievable even while strictly adhering to the Npr ≤ B rule. This empirically supports the design philosophy that substantial enhancement can be realized by optimizing the shallow foundation’s interaction with the near-surface soil, without resorting to deep foundation elements. The constraint is thus proven to be not a limitation but an optimization parameter that successfully defines a new category of foundation. It effectively captures the point of diminishing returns for this specific mechanism, where further increases in depth would transition the system into a different behavioral regime (i.e., a piled raft) with associated increases in complexity and cost, which is beyond the intended scope of this innovative design.

Moreover, as observed in the study, the influence of Np becomes more pronounced at higher projection depths. It is due to the cumulative effect of increased contact area and enhanced skin friction around each projection. Štrukelj and B. Macuh10 Highlighted similar behavior in cooling tower foundations, where increasing the number of load transfer elements (e.g., piles or projections) improved load-bearing capacity without compromising safety or performance.

It is also important to consider the effect of element spacing on group efficiency. While closer spacing in traditional pile groups can reduce efficiency, the “teeth foundation” leverages this proximity as its primary mechanism. The system is designed not as a group of individual piles but as a monolithic unit where the projections’ key function is to confine the soil. The resulting stress overlap, therefore, is not a detriment but the means of creating a composite soil-concrete block with enhanced stiffness and strength. The consistent increase in capacity noted with more projections indicates that, for the configurations tested, the significant benefits of this composite action and enhanced soil confinement far outweigh any potential adverse efficiency effects seen in conventional pile systems. It highlights a fundamental behavioral difference: the teeth improve the soil mass, while traditional piles are designed to bypass it.

While the results demonstrate that increasing the projection depth (Dₚ) positively impacts the bearing capacity of the teeth foundation, this enhancement comes with potential trade-offs in terms of construction difficulty and costs. These factors must be carefully considered when determining the optimal projection depth for a given project. For example, deeper projections require more extensive excavation and precision during construction. Unlike shallow projections, where excavation is straightforward, deeper projections increase the risk of disturbances to the surrounding soil and require additional measures, such as temporary supports or bracing, to maintain stability during excavation. The need for precision in aligning and installing deeper projections adds complexity to the construction process. Misalignment of projections can lead to uneven load distribution, potentially compromising the foundation’s performance.

Conventional deep foundations are often necessary to avoid weak, highly compressible upper soil layers and transfer loads to a deeper, competent bearing stratum. The teeth foundation, in its current form, is not intended as a substitute for deep foundations in such soil profiles. It is fundamentally a ground improvement technique for shallow foundations, designed to enhance the bearing capacity and stiffness of relatively competent near-surface soils, such as the medium-to-dense sands investigated in this study. In a scenario with soft upper soil layers, the teeth foundation would not be able to overcome the challenges of widespread soil failure or consolidation settlement. Its effectiveness relies on the presence of a reasonably capable soil mass immediately below the footing that can be improved through confinement. Therefore, its primary application is in situations where a conventional shallow footing is marginally inadequate, and the teeth provide a cost-effective performance boost to avoid the much larger expense of transitioning to a mat foundation or a full deep foundation system.

Effect of sand condition

Conclusion

This study introduced and analyzed an innovative foundation system, termed the "teeth foundation," designed to enhance the vertical load transfer mechanism to the soil. Using the PLAXIS 3D finite element program, the study investigated the behavior of teeth foundations under varying conditions, including different numbers of projections, projection depths, foundation depths, and sand densities. The results demonstrated significant performance improvements compared to traditional isolated footings and mat foundations, offering a more sustainable and cost-effective alternative. The key findings revealed an average improvement ratio of 1.48 in the teeth foundation’s bearing capacity compared to isolated footings, highlighting a substantial enhancement in vertical load transfer. Additionally, the bearing capacity improvement ratio (RBearing) increased with the number of projections (Npr) and the depth of projections (Dp/B), reaching a value of 1.62 for five projections and a Dₚ/B ratio of 1.00. Embedding the foundation deeper also provided notable benefits, with an 83.25% improvement in bearing capacity observed for a foundation depth of Dp/B = 0.6 compared to a surface foundation. Furthermore, the density of the sand was shown to play a critical role, with the bearing capacity in dense sand being over five times higher than in loose sand, and the teeth foundation consistently demonstrating improvements across all soil conditions.

The advantages of the study are manifold. Teeth foundations significantly enhance load transfer by increasing the contact area and leveraging skin friction, making them suitable for various soil conditions. They also offer considerable cost efficiency by eliminating the need for mat foundations in many cases, reducing material and construction costs while providing comparable or superior performance. Additionally, the simplified construction process of teeth foundations minimizes environmental impact by cutting CO₂ emissions associated with material production and transportation. The adaptability of teeth foundations to various soil conditions, foundation geometries, and structural requirements further underscores their versatility as a solution for diverse engineering challenges.

However, the study also has limitations. The analysis relied on numerical modeling using the PLAXIS 3D program, which assumes idealized soil and foundation behavior, potentially deviating from real-world conditions where soil heterogeneity and construction imperfections exist. Although the model was validated against an existing foundation design, further experimental and field-scale validation is needed to confirm the results across diverse real-life scenarios.

While this study successfully demonstrates the potential of the teeth foundation through rigorous numerical modeling, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The validation was primarily numerical, confirming the modeling setup against an existing case study. The novelty of the teeth foundation concept itself warrants further validation through physical experiments, such as laboratory-scale load tests or centrifuge modeling, to conclusively verify its performance under real-world conditions.

It is important to acknowledge that the scope of this study is primarily confined to the analysis of the teeth foundation under vertical static loading, which was the principal design objective. Consequently, its performance under complex loading scenarios, such as the lateral and cyclic forces typical of seismic or significant wind events, represents a key limitation and a necessary direction for future research.

Hypothetically, the projections (teeth) are expected to offer substantial resistance to lateral loads. They would act as shear keys, mobilizing passive earth pressure on their leading faces and significant side friction along their vertical surfaces. This mechanism would likely result in a superior lateral capacity compared to a conventional shallow footing of the same overall footprint, which relies mainly on base friction and passive pressure on a single face.

Under cyclic or seismic loading, the behavior is more complex. While the confinement of soil between the projections might enhance its resistance to cyclic degradation or liquefaction, repeated load reversals could also lead to the formation of gaps (“gapping”) between the soil and the projections and a progressive degradation of soil stiffness. Therefore, comprehensive numerical modeling with advanced soil constitutive models, complemented by experimental validation through shake table tests, is essential to fully characterize the foundation’s dynamic performance and expand its applicability to seismically active regions.

Additionally, a limitation exists in the comparative methodology. The analysis quantified the performance gain by adding projections to a standard isolated footing, rather than comparing against a conventional footing with an equivalent, normalized material volume (e.g., a thicker or wider slab). The rationale for this approach is that the teeth foundation’s primary advantage is geotechnical—it engages a deeper soil mass. It mobilizes skin friction in a way that simply increasing material volume in a flat footing cannot replicate as effectively. This merit positions the design as a cost-effective intermediate solution, enhancing performance without the need to transition to far more material-intensive systems like mat foundations. Nevertheless, future optimization studies should include such normalized analyses to rigorously quantify the design’s geometric and material efficiency.

In conclusion, this research marks a significant step toward transitioning from isolated and mat foundations to more efficient and sustainable foundation systems. The teeth foundation offers a cost-effective alternative with enhanced performance, making it an attractive solution for modern construction projects. Nevertheless, further experimental validation and exploration of its behavior under varying load conditions are recommended to address identified limitations and ensure practical applicability.
