Beyond Per-Thread Lock Sets: Multi-Thread Critical Sections and Dynamic Deadlock Prediction
Martin Sulzmann

Abstract
. Lock sets are commonly used for dynamic analysis of deadlocks. The standard per-thread lock set construction only considers locks acquired in the same thread, but is unaware of locks acquired in another thread. This leads to false positives and false negatives. The underlying issue is that the commonly used notion of a critical section on which the lock set construction relies ignores events from other threads. We give a trace-based characterization of critical sections that drops this restriction. Critical sections are no longer restricted to a single thread and can cover multiple threads. Such forms of critical sections exist, are natural, and correct the standard formulation. We show how to soundly approximate the trace-based characterization via partial order relations. Thus, we obtain an improved lock set construction that can still be efficiently computed and allows us to remove false positives reported by the DIRK deadlock predictor and remove false negatives by extending the SPDOffline deadlock predictor. We integrate various lock set constructions with increased precision in an extension of SPDOffline. Our extensions remain sound (no false positives) but are more complete (fewer false negatives) w.r.t. SPDOffline. For an extensive standard benchmark suite we can also show that the performance is not affected.

Introduction

Bugs like deadlocks in concurrent software are notoriously hard to find
due to the non-deterministic nature of concurrent programs.
Our focus here is on resource deadlocks
in programs that make use of shared variables and locks
but without any message-passing primitives.
We follow the common assumption that
each release operation occurs in the same thread
as the prior acquire operation.
A resource deadlock arises if
there is a set of threads
where each thread in the set tries to acquire a lock that is held by some other thread in the set.

A well-studied line of work analyzes alternative thread interleavings
to predict potential resource deadlocks from observed executions
(
Havelund 2000
;
Bensalem and Havelund 2005
;
Joshi et al. 2009
;
Cai and Chan 2014
;
Samak and Ramanathan 2014a
;
Zhou et al. 2017
;
Kalhauge and Palsberg 2018
;
Cai et al. 2021
;
Tunç et al. 2023
)
.
The common method of these works is the search for
deadlock patterns
.
A deadlock pattern represents a cyclic chain
among lock acquisitions in the observed execution
where the locks held by the acquires in the chain are disjoint.
The search for such patterns relies on the
lock set
method
(
Dinning and Schonberg 1991
)
;
the set of locks that are held (acquired) but not yet released at a certain point in the execution.
To the best of our knowledge,
all prior works assume that locks only protect operations in the acquiring thread.
This can lead to false deadlock warnings and even missed deadlocks
because locks can protect operations across thread boundaries.

Summary.

Lock sets are used to construct deadlock patterns.
The standard per-thread lock set construction only considers locks acquired in the same thread. This restriction can lead to
false positives and false negatives
when using deadlock patterns to predict deadlocks in observed executions.
We address these limitations by introducing
corrected constructions of lock sets and deadlock patterns.

Our contributions and outline.

Specifically, our contributions are:

•

We give a trace-based characterization of critical sections
and lock sets where we drop the restriction
that locks can only protect events in the same thread (
Section

4
).

•

We employ the corrected lock set definition to cover a larger class
of deadlock patterns and predictable deadlocks.
Important properties such as stuckness of predictable deadlocks
are retained (
Section

5
).

•

We describe a family of lock set functions defined in terms of partial orders
that soundly approximate the trace-based characterization of a lock set.
The standard per-thread lock set construction is a member of this family represented by the thread-order relation.
Under thread-order, two events are ordered based on their textual position but only if they are in the same thread.
More expressive partial orders include last write dependencies and other forms of must-happen dependencies (
Section

6
).

•

We show how to compute the various lock set functions and
the resulting deadlock patterns
efficiently (
Section

7.1
).

•

We integrate the various lock sets and resulting deadlock patterns
into the state of the art deadlock-prediction algorithm SPDOffline
(
Tunç et al. 2023
)
.
Our extensions of SPDOffline retain soundness and
covers a larger class of predictable deadlocks
(
Section

7.2
).

•

We validate our extensions of SPDOffline in practice.
The measure the performance we consider a large set of standard benchmark traces.
To measure the precision we consider traces derived from C programs (
Section

8
).

Our implementation including benchmark traces and
simple C programs showing that critical sections covering multiple threads
exist is available at
https://zenodo.org/records/17310954
.

Section

2
gives an overview of our work.

Section

3
introduces basic definitions and notations.

Section

9
summarizes related work.

Section

10
concludes the paper.
Proofs for all results stated are given in the appendix.

This section reviews the standard per-thread lock set construction known from the literature
and deadlock patterns which rely on lock sets.
We show that per-thread lock set construction has some inherent limitations
and explain our approach how to correct these limitations.

2.1.
Lock Sets for Deadlock Prediction

We review the use of lock sets for deadlock prediction with the example in
Figure

4
.
The diagram on the left represents a program run
represented by a trace of events
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDL}}}
.
We often represent traces in tabular notation
where each row contains exactly one event, in the column of the executing thread.
The textual order (from top to bottom) reflects the observed temporal order of events.

Events correspond to shared-memory/lock/thread operations executed by some thread.
Operation
\mathtt{fork}(t)
creates a new thread
t
and
operation
\mathtt{join}(t)
synchronizes with the termination of thread
t
.
We write
l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}
to denote locks and
x,y,z
to denote shared variables.
Operations
\mathtt{acq}(l)/\mathtt{rel}(l)
acquire/release lock
l
.
Operations
\mathtt{rd}(x)/\mathtt{wr}(x)
are shared memory read and write operations on
x
.
The same operation may appear multiple times in a trace, thus
we use indices as in
e_{i}
to uniquely identify events in the trace.
Trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDL}}}
can be more compactly represented
as the list
[e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6},e_{7},e_{8},e_{9}]
.

In trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDL}}}
(left)
all events in thread
t_{1}
take place before the
events in thread
t_{2}
, without apparent problem.
Based on trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDL}}}
we reason about alternative schedules that
could have taken place and manifest a bug.
For example, we can swap the order among acquires on lock
l_{2}
.
This then leads to the
reordered trace prefix

\text{\hyperref@@ii[trace:LocksetsForDLReordered]{$T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDLReordered}}}$}}=[e_{1},e_{2},e_{6}]
(right).
The highlighted acquire events
e_{3}
and
e_{7}
are not part of trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDLReordered}}}
. Including either event would break the lock semantics by acquiring a lock already acquired but not yet released by the other thread.
Hence, we refer to
e_{3}
and
e_{7}
as blocked events.

To predict such deadlocks, the standard approach is to
derive from the trace a
deadlock pattern
.
A deadlock pattern is a set
\{q_{1},...,q_{n}\}
of events
where event
q_{i}
represents the request to acquire
lock
l_{i}
in some thread
t_{i}

and the following two conditions are satisfied:

(DP-Cycle):

The lock acquired by
q_{i}
is held by
q_{(i\mathbin{\%}n)+1}
.

(DP-Guard):

The locks held by each pair
q_{i}
,
q_{j}
are disjoint.

Condition
DP-Cycle
states that there
is a cyclic chain of lock acquisitions and
condition
DP-Guard
states
that there is no common guard lock that prevents
the lock acquisitions to take place in some execution.
Crucially, both conditions rely on the notion
of
locks held
(aka
lock set
)

The standard lock set definition in the literature is as follows.
Lock
l
is in the lock set of event
e
, written

l\in{\mathit{L}H}_{std}(e)
if

(CS-Enclosed):

e
is enclosed by acquire event
\mathtt{acq}(l)

and release event
\mathtt{rel}(l)
, and

(CS-Match):

there are no other acquires and releases on
l
in between, and

(CS-Per-Thread):

e
is in the same thread
as
\mathtt{acq}(l)
and
\mathtt{rel}(l)
.

Thanks to condition
CS-Per-Thread
,
we can easily check conditions
CS-Enclosed

and
CS-Match

by considering the textual order of acquire-release
pairs
\mathtt{acq}(l)
and
\mathtt{rel}(l)

that are in the same thread as
e
.
This leads to the standard
per-thread
lock set construction.

For trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDL}}}
we record
the (per-thread) lock sets in some extra column.
We omit entries if they represent the empty set.
For example, for event
e_{3}
that acquires
lock
l_{2}
we find
the lock set
{\mathit{L}H}_{std}(e_{3})=\{l_{1}\}
because

e_{3}
is surrounded by events
e_{2}
and
e_{5}
.

Based on the lock set information, we can derive
for each event
e
that acquires lock
l
a

(
Bensalem and Havelund 2005
;
Joshi et al. 2009
)

of the form
{\langle t,l,{\mathit{L}H}_{std}(e)\rangle}

where
t
refers to the thread that acquires lock
l
and

{\mathit{L}H}_{std}(e)
is the
set of locks held by this thread while acquiring lock
l
.
For trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:LocksetsForDL}}}
,
we obtain two lock dependencies:

{\langle t_{1},l_{2},\{l_{1}\}\rangle}
and
{\langle t_{2},l_{1},\{l_{2}\}\rangle}

The acquired lock

\{l_{2}\}

of the second dependency and and the acquired lock

of the second dependency

\{l_{1}\}

Hence, condition

The underlying lock sets are disjoint as

Hence, condition

This yields the deadlock pattern

2.2.
Per-Thread Lock Set yields False Positives and False Negatives

It is well-known that lock set-based methods are prone to false positives.
Numerous approaches attempt to reduce the number of false positives. For instance,

Kalhauge and Palsberg 2018
were the first to
employ an SMT solver to exhaustively explore reorderings to eliminate infeasible false positives.
However, condition
CS-Per-Thread

of the standard lock set construction is
an inherent source of false positives as well as false negatives
as motivated by the examples in
Figure

1

and
Figure

2
.
We investigate the issue in more detail
and consider two specific executions represented
as traces in
Figure

5
.
As before we record the lock sets in some extra column.
We only show the lock sets for some events.
For space reasons, resulting lock dependencies are
put underneath traces.

Trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:FPExample}}}
results from the
program in
Figure

1
.
We use labels
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
and
t_{3}

to refer to threads
Main
,
A
and
B
.
The standard method finds
dependencies
{\langle t_{2},l_{2},\{l_{3}\}\rangle}
and

{\langle t_{3},l_{3},\{l_{1},l_{2}\}\rangle}
(ignore the grey
l_{1}
in the figure for now, it does not belong to the standard lock set).
Based on these dependencies we can argue that

\{e_{4},e_{11}\}
satisfies
DP-Cyle
and
DP-Guard
.
Hence,
\{e_{4},e_{11}\}
represents a deadlock pattern and we issue a deadlock warning.

This warning is a false positive because events
e_{10}-e_{13}
in thread
t_{2}
and
events
e_{3}-e_{6}
in thread
t_{3}
are guarded by the common lock
l_{1}
.
However, lock
l_{1}
is not part of the dependency
{\langle t_{2},l_{3},\{l_{1}\}\rangle}

because the standard lock set construction does not consider locks
acquired in a different thread.
See condition
CS-Per-Thread
.

It turns out that in every valid reordering, the acquire event
e_{8}
will take place
before events
e_{10}-e_{13}
and the matching release event
e_{15}
will take place afterwards.
This example illustrates the need for a corrected definition of lock sets
where locks can protect requests across thread boundaries.
Then, the lock dependency constructed in
t_{2}

includes lock
\{l_{1}\}
. Thus,
the false positive is eliminated
because the lock sets are no longer disjoint.
Hence,
DP-Guard
is violated.

The construction of deadlock patterns based on standard lock sets
also leads to false negatives.
Consider trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:FNExample}}}
resulting from
the program in
Figure

2
.
The program contains a deadlock, but
standard lock sets only yield the dependency

{\langle t_{3},l_{2},\{l_{1}\}\rangle}
.
Hence, there is no deadlock pattern and no deadlock warning is issued.
The corrected lock set construction
adds lock
l_{2}
to the lock set of event
e_{4}
.
This then results in the dependency

{\langle t_{2},l_{1},\{l_{2}\}\rangle}
which gives rise to a cyclic dependency
with
{\langle t_{3},l_{2},\{l_{1}\}\rangle}
where
the underlying lock sets are disjoint.
Thus,
\{e_{4},e_{9}\}
satisfies
DP-Cyle
and
DP-Guard

and we correctly issue that there is a deadlock.

2.3.
Beyond Per-Thread Lock Sets

We correct the standard lock set definition
by dropping condition
CS-Per-Thread
.
Without condition
CS-Per-Thread
,
the check
e
is enclosed by
\mathtt{acq}(l)
and
\mathtt{rel}(l)

in condition
CS-Enclosed
can no longer be carried
out by considering the textual order among events.
To ensure that event
e
is protected by lock
l
,
we demand that under
all

alternative scheduling of events, and for each schedule,
event
e
is enclosed by acquire event
\mathtt{acq}(l)

and release event
\mathtt{rel}(l)
with no other acquires and releases
on
l
in between.
This then allows to discover locks that protect events across thread boundaries.
Thus, the false positive and false negative in the above examples can be removed.

As there can be exponentially many alternative schedulings,
the above corrected lock set definition will not lead to a practical algorithm.
To obtain an efficient algorithm, we approximate the set of alternative
schedulings via partial orders derived from the trace.
This is something well-explored in the data race prediction setting
(
Lamport 1978
;
Smaragdakis et al. 2012
;
Roemer et al. 2018
;
Kini et al. 2017
;
Genç et al. 2019
;
Sulzmann and Stadtmüller 2020
)
.
Given some partial order
<
among events in the trace,
lock
l
is in
e
’s lock set if

\mathtt{acq}(l)<e<\mathtt{rel}(l)
.

In the data race setting, partial orders usually

overapproximate
.
If two events
e
and
f
are ordered
this does not necessarily mean that
f
cannot appear before
e

in some alternative schedule.
But if
e
and
f
are unordered then we can guarantee
that there must be an alternative schedule
where
e
and
f
appear right next to each other.
This then satisfies the condition for a data race
and ensures that only true positive data races are predicted.

In our setting, we require partial orders that
underapproximate
.
If
e<f
then we can guarantee that
e
must appear before
f

in any alternative schedule of events.
But if
e
and
f
are unordered it could be the case that

e
always appears before
f
.
Underapproximation guarantees that
if
\mathtt{acq}(l)<e<\mathtt{rel}(l)
,
then
e
is is enclosed by
\mathtt{acq}(l)
and
\mathtt{rel}(l)

under all alternative schedules.

The most simple underapproximating partial order is
the
thread-order
relation.
We define
e<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}f
if
e
appears before
f
in
trace
T
and
e
and
f
are in the same thread.
Effectively, this then yields the standard per-thread
lock set construction.
But there is ample space for further underapproximating partial orders.
For example, the
fork-join-order
relation
<_{\mbox{\tiny fj}}

that extends
<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}
by including fork-join dependencies.

We will consider a number of different partial orders
suitable for our purposes (
Section

6
)
and show how to effectively compute lock sets and deadlock patterns
based on these partial orders (
Section

7.1
).
We integrate the various lock set constructions into the state of the art
deadlock predictor SPDOffline
(
Tunç et al. 2023
)

(
Section

7.2
).
The resulting extensions of SPDOffline remain sound (no false positives),
become more complete (fewer false negatives) and performance is not affected
(
Section

8
).

3.
Preliminaries

We introduce some standard notations, following
related work in predictive analyses of concurrent programs
(
Sen et al. 2005
;
Smaragdakis et al. 2012
;
Tunç et al. 2023
)
.

3.1.
Events and Traces

We consider dynamic analyses which observe the behavior
of a concurrent program represented as a trace.
A trace is a list of events reflecting a single execution of a concurrent program under the sequential consistency memory model
(
Adve and Gharachorloo 1996
)
.
Each event is connected to some concurrency primitive
such as acquire/release of locks (mutexes) and writing/reading of shared variables.
We use
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots
and
x,y,z
for lock and shared variables, respectively.

Definition 3.1 (Events and Traces).

\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}c@{}lll@{}c@{}llr@{}}\alpha,\beta,\delta&{}::=&1\mid 2\mid\ldots&\text{(unique event ids)}&e&{}::=&(\alpha,t,op)&\text{(events)}\\
t,s,u&{}::=&\tau_{1}\mid\tau_{2}\mid\ldots&\text{(thread ids)}&T&{}::=&[]\mid e:T&\text{(traces)}\\
op&{}::=&\mathtt{rd}(x)\mid\mathtt{wr}(x)\mid\mathtt{req}(\ell)\mid\mathtt{acq}(\ell)\mid\mathtt{rel}(\ell)\hfil\hfil\hfil\hfil\hfil&\text{(operations)}\end{array}

We write
[o_{1},\dots,o_{n}]

for a list of objects as a shorthand of
o_{1}:\dots:o_{n}:[]
and use the
operator “
{}\mathbin{\cdot}{}
” for list concatenation.

An event
e
is represented by a triple
(\alpha,t,op)

where

\alpha
is a unique event identifier,

op
is an operation,
and

t
is the id of the thread in which the operation took place.
The main thread has thread id
\tau_{1}
.
The unique event identifier unambiguously identifies events under trace reordering.
We often omit identifier and/or thread when denoting events, and omit parentheses if only the operation remains, writing
e=(t,op)
or
e=op
instead of
e=(\alpha,t,op)
.

The operations
\mathtt{rd}(x)
and
\mathtt{wr}(x)
denote reading of and writing to a shared variable
x
, respectively.
Operations
\mathtt{acq}(\ell)
and
\mathtt{rel}(\ell)
denote acquiring and releasing a lock
\ell
, respectively.
We also include lock requests
\mathtt{req}(\ell)
to denote the (possibly unfulfillable) attempt at acquiring a lock
\ell
, following, e.g.,
(
Kalhauge and Palsberg 2018
)
.
For brevity, we omit operations
\mathtt{fork}(t)
and
\mathtt{join}(t)
.

Our tabular notation for traces has one column per thread. The events
for a thread are lined up in the thread’s column and the trace
position corresponds to the row number.
See trace
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:FNExample}}}
in
Figure

6(a)
.
We model fork and join events via shared variable operations.
For example, events
[e_{2},e_{3}]
emulate
\mathtt{fork}(t_{2})

and events
[e_{7},e_{8}]
emulate
\mathtt{join}(t_{2})
.
For brevity, we do not protect the read/write operations by some locks.
Acquires
e_{5}
and
e_{12}
are preceded by some lock request events.
For brevity, we omit lock request events for acquires
e_{1}
and
e_{10}
.

Notation
\thd(e)
extracts the thread id from an event.
We define
\@lock(e)=\ell

if
e=(t,\mathtt{acq}(\ell)
,
e=(t,\mathtt{rel}(\ell))
or
e=(t,\mathtt{req}(\ell))
.
We write
e\in T
to indicate that
T=[e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}]
and
for some
k\in\{1,...,n\}
we find that
e=e_{k}
, defining also
\@pos_{T}(e)=k
.
The set of events in a trace is then
\evts(T)=\{e\mid e\in T\}
, and the set of thread ids in a trace is
\thds(T)=\{\thd(e)\mid e\in T\}
.
For trace
T
and events
e,f\in\evts(T)
, we define trace order:
e\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f
if
\@pos_{T}(e)<\@pos_{T}(f)
.

3.2.
Well Formedness

Traces must be well formed, following the standard sequential consistency conditions for concurrent objects
(
Adve and Gharachorloo 1996
;
Said et al. 2011
;
Huang et al. 2014
)
.

Definition 3.2 (Well Formedness).

Trace
T
is
well formed
if all the following conditions are satisfied:

(WF-Acq):

For every
a=(t,\mathtt{acq}(\ell)),a^{\prime}=(t^{\prime},\mathtt{acq}(\ell))\in T
where
a\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a^{\prime}
, there exists
r=(t,\mathtt{rel}(\ell))\in T
such that
a\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a^{\prime}
.

(WF-Rel):

For every
r=(t,\mathtt{rel}(\ell))\in T
, there exists
a=(t,\mathtt{acq}(\ell))\in T
such that
a\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r
and there is no
r^{\prime}=(t^{\prime},\mathtt{rel}(\ell))\in T
with
a\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r^{\prime}\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}r
.
We define
\@rel_{T}(a)=r
and
\@acq_{T}(r)=a
.

(WF-Req):

For every
a=(t,\mathtt{acq}(\ell))\in T
, there exists
q=(t,\mathtt{req}(\ell))\in T
such that
q\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a

and there is no event
e\in T
such that
q\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a
and
\thd(e)=t
.
For every
q=(t,\mathtt{req}(\ell))\in T
,
if
e\in T
where
q\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
and
\thd(e)=t

and there is no other
f\in T
such that

q\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
and
\thd(f)=t
,
then
e=(t,\mathtt{acq}(\ell))
.

WF-Acq
states that a previously acquired lock can only be acquired after it has been released.
Similarly,
WF-Rel
states that a lock can only be released after is has been acquired but not yet released.
Note that these conditions require matching acquires and releases to occur in the same thread.

WF-Req
states that each acquire must be immediately preceded by a lock request.
Requests do not have to be fulfilled by a corresponding acquire event.
However, if there is no acquire that fulfills a request, no further events
can come after the request in that thread.
Request events are not strictly necessary but simplify up-coming
definitions that reason about ‘blocked’ operations.
For brevity, we often omit requests from example traces.
For example, see
T_{\text{\ref*{trace:FNExampleLW}}}
in
Figure

6(a)
.
For such cases we assume that requests precede each acquire implicitly.

For brevity, we often drop the
well-formed
assumption
imposed on traces. All traces we consider here
are well-formed.

3.3.
Trace Reorderings

Trace-based dynamic analyses aim to expose bugs based
on some program run represented as a trace
T
.
Bugs such as deadlocks rarely show up directly.
Hence, we wish to
predict
a deadlock
by considering some alternative scheduling of the events in
T
that
lead to a deadlock.
Naively, we could consider all permutations of the original trace
T
.
However, not all such reorderings are feasible in the sense of being reproducible by executing the program with a different schedule.
In addition to well formedness, a reordering must guarantee that (a) program order and (b) last writes are maintained.
A reordering maintains program order if, in any thread, the order of events is unchanged.
For guarantee (b), note that every read observes some write on the same variable: the last preceding such write.
Last writes are then maintained if the write observed by any read is unchanged.
Guarantee (b) is particularly important to ensure that the control flow of the traced program is unaffected by the reordering, e.g., when the read is used in a conditional statement.

Reorderings do not have to run to completion, so we consider reordered
prefixes
of traces.

Definition 3.3 (Correctly Reordered Prefix).

The
projection
of
T
onto thread
t
, denoted
\proj(T,t)
, restricts
T
to events
e
with
\thd(e)=t
.
That is,

e\in\proj(T,t)
if and only if
e\in T
and
\thd(e)=t
,
and

e\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{\proj(T,t)}}f
implies
e\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f
.

Take
e=\mathtt{rd}(x),f=\mathtt{wr}(x)\in T
.
We say that
f
is the
last write
of
e
in
T
, denoted
f=\@lw_{T}(e)
, if
f
appears before
e
with no other write on
x
in between.
That is,

f\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
,
and
there is no
g=\mathtt{wr}(x)\in T
such that
f\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}g\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}e
.

Trace
T^{\prime}
is a
correctly reordered prefix
of
T
if the following
conditions are satisfied:

(CRP-WF):

T^{\prime}
is well formed and
\evts(T^{\prime})\subseteq\evts(T)
.

(CRP-PO):

For every
t\in\thds(T^{\prime})
,
\proj(T^{\prime},t)
prefixes
\proj(T,t)
.

(CRP-LW):

For every
e=\mathtt{rd}(x)\in T^{\prime}
where
f=\@lw_{T}(e)
we have that
f=\@lw_{T^{\prime}}(e).

We write
\@crp(T)
to denote the set of correctly reordered prefixes of
T
.

Condition
CRP-LW
follows the formulation in
(
Mathur et al. 2018
)
and uses function
\@lw
to guarantee that last writes are maintained under reordering.
There are other formulations
(
Tunç et al. 2023
)
that
make use of a "reads-form" function which is just a different name for
function
\@lw
.

4.1.
Critical Section

We say event
f

must be preceded

by event
e

under all trace reorderings
,
written
e<_{\mbox{\tiny crp}}^{T}f
, if
\forall T^{\prime}\in\@crp(T)
where
f\in T^{\prime}

we have that
e\in T^{\prime}
and
e\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T^{\prime}}}f
.

Definition 4.1 (Critical Section).

Suppose there are events

a=(t,\mathtt{acq}(l)),r=(t,\mathtt{rel}(l))\in T
for
some lock
l
.
We say that
e\in T
is in the

critical section enclosed by acquire
a

and release
r
under all schedules
,
written

e\in{\mathit{C}S}_{T}(a,r)

if the following two conditions are met:

(CS-Enclosed):

a<_{\mbox{\tiny crp}}^{T}e
and
e<_{\mbox{\tiny crp}}^{T}r
.

(CS-Match):

There is no release event
r^{\prime}=(t,\mathtt{rel}(l))\in T
and

T^{\prime}\in\@crp(T)

such that
a\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T^{\prime}}}r^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T^{\prime}}}e
.

Condition
CS-Enclosed
states that event
e

must be preceded by acquire
a
and release
r
must be preceded by event
e
.
This means that event
e
is enclosed
by the critical section represented by acquire
a
and release
r

and captures the intuitive understanding that event
e
is protected by lock
l
.
Condition
CS-Match
guarantees that
r
is the matching release for the acquire event
a
.

4.2.
Lock Set

Based on the above characterization of critical section
we can define the notion of a lock set.
Lock
l
is held by some event
e
if the event is part of a critical section for lock
l
.

Definition 4.2 (Locks Held).

The
lock set for an event
e\in T
under all schedules
is defined by

{\mathit{L}H}_{T}(e)=\{(l,t)\mid\exists a,r\in T\ \mbox{such that}\ a=(t,\mathtt{acq}(l))\ \mbox{and}\ e\in{\mathit{C}S}_{T}(a,r)\}
.

Our definitions correct earlier definitions
as we no longer impose the requirement
that
e
belongs to the same thread as
a
and
r
.
This also means that events from distinct threads can held the same lock.
Therefore, lock sets contain pairs
(l,t)

where
t
refers to the thread that acquired and released lock
l
.

5.
Lock Set-Based Deadlock Prediction

We revisit a standard method for dynamic deadlock prediction.
For a specific program run represented by some trace
T

we calculate all lock (set) dependencies in
T
and
check for cycles among them.
A
lock dependency
represents the situation
that a request event holds a certain set of locks.
A
cyclic chain of lock dependencies

where the underlying lock sets are disjoint
is referred to as a
deadlock pattern
.

Definitions of the above notions occur in many forms in the literature.
Our formulation below largely follows
(
Tunç et al. 2023
)
.
We generalize these definitions and make them parametric
in terms of the lock set function
L_{T}

that maps the set
\evts(T)
of events in
T

to sets of pairs consisting of lock variables and thread ids.
In this section, the only specific instance of
L_{T}
we consider
is
{\mathit{L}H}_{T}
from
Definition

4.2
.
In later sections, we introduce sound approximations
of
{\mathit{L}H}_{T}
that can be efficiently computed.

Let

Definition 5.3 (Deadlock Pattern).

Let

For every

5.2.
Predictable Deadlocks are Stuck Deadlock Patterns

Earlier formulations of predictable deadlocks

rely on a notion of

An event

In essence, enabled events are ‘blocked’ acquires that arise in some

In our formulation of deadlock patterns we use request

Definition 5.4 (Final Event).

Given trace
T

\thd(e)=\thd(f)

Request events are final and their corresponding acquires are enabled.
Here is our reformulation of predictable deadlocks
(
Tunç et al. 2023

using final instead of enabled events.

6.
Partial Order-Based Critical Section and Lock Set

We give a characterization of the notions of a critical section
and lock set in terms of partial order relations
under which the set of predictable deadlocks
as described by
Definition

5.5
can be soundly approximated.
We review various partial orders that can be found in the literature
and discuss if they are suitable for our purposes.

6.1.
Must Happen Before Approximation

Definition 6.1 (Partial Order).

Given some trace
T

is a binary relation among events
\evts(T)
which is
irreflexive, antisymmetric and transitive.

We often say

for short and
use symbol

to refer to some partial order

The next definitions
rephrase

4.1
and

.
To make a clear distinction, we will
prefix labels with ‘

Definition 6.2 (

-Critical Section).

Let

be some partial order.
Suppose there are events

and

(

There is no release event

and

The

6.3.
Underapproximating Partial Order Relations

Fortunately, there are a number of (underapproximating) partial orders
that satisfy the
MHB-criteria
.
In the following, we consider three examples.
All three examples are well-known and
can be found in one form or another in the literature.
Their use for the purpose of lock set-based dynamic deadlock prediction
is novel, however.

The most simple partial order is the one that
only orders events in the same thread based on their textual position.

Definition 6.10 (Thread-Order (
TO
)).

Given trace
T
, we define the
thread-order (
TO
)

as the smallest strict partial order
<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}
on
\evts(T)

such that
e<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}f
if

e\mathrel{<_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}f
and
\thd(e)=\thd(f)
.

We strengthen thread-order by including last-write dependencies.

Definition 6.11 (Last-Write-Order (
LW
)).

Given trace
T
, we define the
last-write-order (
LW
)

as the smallest strict partial order
<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}
on
\evts(T)

such that

(a)
:

<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}\subseteq<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}
,

(b)
:

e<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}f
if
e=\@lw_{T}(f)
.

Recall that for read
f
, function
\@lw_{T}(f)
yields
the write on the same shared variable that immediately precedes

f
in trace
T
.

The final partial order imposes an ordering among critical sections.
WCP
(
Kini et al. 2017
)
and SDP
(
Genç et al. 2019
)
order critical sections
but are not suitable because they overapproximate and
violate the
MHB-criteria
.
We follow WDP
(
Genç et al. 2019
)
and PWR
(
Sulzmann and Stadtmüller 2020
)

and only order critical sections if absolutely necessary.
We only consider ‘conflicts’ if they involve last write dependencies.

Definition 6.12 (Release-Order (
RO
)).

Given trace
T
, we define the
release-order (
RO
)

as the smallest strict partial order
<_{\mbox{\tiny ro}}^{T}
on
\evts(T)

such that

(a)
:

<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}\subseteq<_{\mbox{\tiny ro}}^{T}
,

(b)
:

r<_{\mbox{\tiny ro}}^{T}f
if

e\in{\mathit{C}S}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}}(a,r)
and

f\in{\mathit{C}S}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}}(a^{\prime},r^{\prime})
,

a=(\_,\mathtt{acq}(l))
,
a^{\prime}=(\_,\mathtt{acq}(l))

and
e<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}f
.

Examples that show the above partial orders in action are coming up shortly.
We first state some important properties.

{\mathit{L}H}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}}
corresponds to the existing (standard) lock set construction.
All three partial orders
<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}

<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}
and
<_{\mbox{\tiny ro}}

and yield an (increasingly)

7.
Implementation

We integrate the partial order-based lock set constructions
into the state of the art deadlock predictor SPDOffline
(
Tunç et al. 2023
)
.
SPDOffline operates
offline
by taking as input a trace to compute
a set of
sync-preserving
deadlocks.
Sync-preserving deadlocks are a subclass of the set of predictable deadlocks
where in the witness trace the relative order of acquires on the same lock
is the same as in the input trace.
All of our results also apply to sync-preserving deadlocks
and all of the deadlocking examples in the main text of this paper
are sync-preserving.

SPDOffline has two phases:

(Phase 1):

Computation of deadlock patterns.

(Phase 2):

Checking if a particular instance of a deadlock pattern
represented as a set
\{q_{1},...,q_{n}\}
of requests has
sync-preserving witness where
q_{1},...,q_{n}
are final.

Both phases are implemented by
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
.

Algorithm

1
computes deadlock patterns
where we make use of the partial order-based lock set constructions.

Algorithm

2
follows closely the original description
(
Tunç et al. 2023
, Algorithm 2)
.
We first consider the computation of deadlock patterns (
Section

7.1
)
before discussing the integration into SPDOffline (
Section

7.2
).

For efficient implementation of partial orders we make use of vector clocks
(

;

)
.
We repeat some standard definitions for vector clocks.

Definition 7.1 (Vector Clocks).

A
vector clock

V
is a list of time stamps of the form

[i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}]
.
We assume vector clocks are of a fixed size
n
.

Time stamps
are natural numbers, and a time stamp at position
j
corresponds to the thread with identifier
t_{j}
.

We define

[i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}]\sqcup[j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}]=[{\mathit{m}ax}(i_{1},j_{1}),\ldots,{\mathit{m}ax}(i_{n},j_{n})]

to
synchronize
two vector clocks, taking pointwise-maximal time stamps.
We write
{\mathit{i}nc}(V,j)
to denote incrementing the vector clock
V
at position
j
by one,
and
V[j]
to retrieve the time stamp at position
j
.

We write
V<V^{\prime}
if
\forall k\colon V[k]\leq V^{\prime}[k]\wedge\exists k\colon V[k]<V^{\prime}[k]
,
and
V\mathrel{||}V^{\prime}
if
V\not<V^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}\not<V
.

Algorithm

1
computes deadlock patterns based on the
<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}
partial order.
We refer to such deadlock patterns as
LW
deadlock patterns
.
In case of deadlock patterns based on the
<_{\mbox{\tiny ro}}^{T}
partial order
the differences only concern the computation of vector clocks.
Something that has been studied extensively
(
Smaragdakis et al. 2012
;
Kini et al. 2017
;
Genç et al. 2019
;
Sulzmann and Stadtmüller 2020
)
.
Hence, we focus on the construction
LW
deadlock patterns.

Function
computeLWDeadlockPattern
takes as input
a trace and yields a set of
LW
deadlock patterns.
We maintain several state variables when processing events.
For every thread
t
, we have a vector clock
\textit{Th}(t)
.
Vector clocks
L_{W}(x)
represent the last write event on variable
x
.
Initially, all time stamps are set to zero (
4
,
5
and
6
;
\bar{0}
denotes a sequence of zeros), and
\textit{Th}(t)
is set to 1 at position
t
(
4
).
We use these vector clocks to compute the
<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}
partial order.
See the processing of read (
38
) and write (
35
) events.

For brevity, we omit explicit request events.
We assume that the vector clock before processing acquire events
implicitly represents requests.

When processing acquire and release events, we maintain two kind of lock sets.
We maintain the standard lock set
{\mathit{L}H}_{std}
(corresponds to
{\mathit{L}H}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}}
)
and the ‘global’ lock set
{\mathit{L}H}_{all}
.
The set
{\mathit{L}H}_{all}
overapproximates the set
{\mathit{L}H}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}}
.
When processing an acquire, we check for all locks
l^{\prime}

that are not in
{\mathit{L}H}_{std}
but could be potentially
in
{\mathit{L}H}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}}
.
Lock
l^{\prime}
is a potential candidate, if the operation that has acquired lock
l^{\prime}

is in a
LW

l
.
To perform this check, we record the vector clock of each
acquire operation (
23
).
We then use this information to check
if
l^{\prime}
is a potential candidate in
{\mathit{L}H}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}}
(
15
).
We collect all potential candidates in
gs
.

If
gs
is empty and the standard lock set is non-empty,
we can immediately record a (standard) lock dependency (
18
).
For fast access, we use a map to record lock dependencies.
There may be several acquires that share the same keys.
We store all acquires in a vector using their vector clock as a representative.

If
gs
is non-empty we add the
potentially guarded

lock dependency

(t,l,\textit{Th}(t),{\mathit{L}H}_{std}(t),gs)

to the list

.
By

we mean that when processing
the release of any of the potential candidates in

we need to verify if the release is in a
LW

with the current acquire.

When processing a release operation on lock
l
,
we consult

to check if
l
appears as a potential guard in any

For convenience, we make use of set notation when processing list elements.
We use pattern matching notation to refer to element
p

(t^{\prime},l^{\prime},V,ls,gs)
(
26

In our actual implementation, we do not naively iterate over the entire list
but selectively check if a release could be a potential guard.

If the current release is a potential guard, we need to check
that this release is in a
<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}
ordering relation with the prior acquire
that is potentially protected by lock
l
.
This check can be again carried out via vector clocks.
For each lock dependency
(t^{\prime},l^{\prime},ls)
, we also record the vector clock
V

of the acquire at the time we computed dependency
(t^{\prime},l^{\prime},ls)

and the potential guards
gs
.
Hence, we only need to check if
V<\textit{Th}(t)
.
If yes, the potential guard could be verified as
an element in
{\mathit{L}H}_{<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}}
(
28

Finally, we compute deadlock patterns satisfying
conditions
DP-Cycle
and
DP-Guard
(
10

We assume thread-indexed lock sets.
Each lock has an attribute to identify the thread from where
the lock results from. For brevity, we omit details.

For efficiency reasons, our representation of deadlock patterns
differs from
Definition

5.3

In
Definition

5.3
, we represent
a deadlock pattern as a set
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
of requests.
In

7.1
, each request

is represented

We use the vector clock
\textit{Th}(t)

before processing of the acquire
(t,acq(l))
to represent the request.
All (request) vector clocks that are connected to the
same lock dependency
(l,t,ls)
are stored in a map

,

Each value in the map is a list of (request) vector clocks.

(F_{1},...,F_{n})

where each

This is referred to as an

(
Tunç et al. 2023
)

and simplifies checking for true positives
among instance
(V_{1},...,V_{n})
where

is an element in

.

7.2.
SPDOffline Integration

Algorithm

2
describes the second phase of SPDOffline.
In this version we use
LW
deadlock patterns.
But it is easy to use standard or
RO
deadlock patterns instead
by adjusting the vector clock computations in the first phase.
We then verify if each abstract deadlock pattern entails
a sync-preserving deadlock (
8
).
For convenience we assume that vector clocks in the list
F_{i}
are in thread
t_{i}
.
Each instance of an abstract deadlock pattern
is verified by procedure
compSPClosure

(
Tunç et al. 2023
, Algorithm 1)
.
Via the
next

Importantly, the soundness guarantee of SPDOffline also holds for our extended version thanks to
Lemma

5.6
.
Time complexity of SPDOffline is also not affected based on the following reasoning.
Let
N
be the number of events,
K
be the number of threads and
C
be the number of critical sections (acquire-release pairs).
Then, computation of (additional) lock dependencies takes time
O(N\cdot K\cdot C)
because in each step (
1,...,N
)
in
Section

7.1
we might have
O(C)
lock dependency candidates to consider and each vector clock operation takes time
O(K)
.
Generally,
K
and
C
are treated like constants. Thus, computation of lock dependencies takes time
O(N)
.
Under this assumption we can also argue that computation of vector clocks to compute partial orders
<_{\mbox{\tiny to}}^{T}
,
<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}
and
<_{\mbox{\tiny ro}}^{T}
takes time
O(n)
.
See
(
Kini et al. 2017
)
where the WCP algorithm is shown to have linear (in
N
) time complexity.
Computation of
RO
vector clocks is similar to WCP vector clocks as for both cases we need to track (standard) critical sections
and check if they contain some conflicts.

Related Work

Havelund 2000
and

Harrow 2000

were the first to identify deadlocking situations via a circularity in the lock order dependency among threads.

Bensalem and Havelund 2005

make explicit use of lock sets to formalize lock-order graphs that capture the lock order dependency for the threads.

Joshi et al. 2009

introduce lock dependencies on a per-thread basis.
The advantage compared to lock-order graphs is that locks
resulting from the same thread can be more easily detected
compared to the lock-order graph representation.
A deadlock warning is issued if we find
a cyclic chain of lock dependencies
satisfying conditions
DP-Cycle
and
DP-Guard
.
There are numerous works
(
Cai et al. 2020
;
Samak and Ramanathan 2014b
;
Zhou et al. 2017
;
Cai and Chan 2014
;
Kalhauge and Palsberg 2018
;
Cai et al. 2021
;
Tunç et al. 2023
)
that are built upon this idea.
All of the above works rely on the standard lock lock set construction.

The technical report
(
Mathur et al. 2023
)

of the conference paper
(
Tunç et al. 2023
)

gives a Java program that corresponds
to
Figure

1
to show that
the DIRK deadlock predictor
(
Kalhauge and Palsberg 2018
)
yields false positives
but does not define multi-thread critical sections,
does not analyze false negatives, and
does not propose corrected definitions of critical sections and lock sets.

10.
Conclusion

Pretty much all prior dynamic deadlock detection methods assume per-thread lock sets
and critical sections restricted to a single thread. This assumption is simply false.
Critical sections that cover multiple threads exist,
can be observed in simple programs and invalidate the standard foundation.
We give corrected definitions based on a trace-based characterization of critical sections and
lock sets to deal with programs where locks protect events across thread boundaries.

We introduce some underapproximations of the general construction
that can be implemented efficiently via the use of partial order methods.
We study their theoretical properties such as the impact
on the precision of lock set-based deadlock precision.
We can guarantee that predictable deadlock patterns
employing the partial order-based constructions are still true positives (
Lemma

5.6
).

Our contribution is orthogonal to any particular deadlock predictor.
Computation of more general deadlock patterns as described
by
Algorithm

1
can be integrated into
iGoodLock
(
Joshi et al. 2009
)
,
UNDEAD
(
Zhou et al. 2017
)
,
SeqCheck
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
and others.
We choose the SPDOffline deadlock predictor
because
Algorithm

2
removes false positives,
and SPDOffline is the standard sound predictor operating under this model.
Hence, we integrate the partial order-based constructions into
the SPDOffline deadlock predictor.
The resulting extensions of SPDOffline remain sound (no false positives)
and we are able to cover a larger class of deadlocks (fewer false negatives).
Our extensive set of performance benchmarks highlight two key findings:
Generalized lock sets are practically feasible with no performance penalty, and
additional lock dependencies do arise, even though they do not yield additional
deadlocks on this benchmark set.
For traces derived from C programs we can show that the precision (fewer false negatives) is improved.

There are several avenues for future work.
We follow the standard fixed-reads-from assumption
under the sequential consistency model used in all recent
predictive analyses; e.g. consider
(
Smaragdakis et al. 2012
;
Kini et al. 2017
;
Cai et al. 2021
;
Tunç et al. 2023
)
.
Tracking (written/read) values and covering more relaxed memory models
is an interesting topic for future work
but requires substantial changes to the theory and implementation.
Other directions include the development of
further partial orders that are suitable for our purposes
and to investigate the limitations
of per-thread lock sets in the context of data race prediction methods.

\@spCrp(T)=\{T^{\prime}\in\@crp(T)\mid\text{$T^{\prime}$ is sync preserving}\}
.

In words, a correctly reordered prefix is sync preserving if critical sections protected by the same lock appear in source-trace order.
Based on sync-preserving reorderings we can refine
the notion of a predictable deadlock.

We consider a specific class of predictable deadlocks
where the witness is sync-preserving.

Appendix C
Sync-Preserving Deadlock Prediction

Definition C.1 (Sync-preserving Closure
(
Tunç et al. 2023
, Definition 3)
).

Let
T
be a trace and
S\subseteq\evts(T)
.
The
sync-preserving closure
of
S
in
T
, denoted
\@spClosure_{T}(S)
, is the smallest set
S^{\prime}
such that

(SPC-Closure)

S\subseteq S^{\prime\prime}
,

(SPC-LW)
for every
e,f\in T
such that
e<_{\mbox{\tiny lw}}^{T}f
,
f\in S^{\prime}
implies
e\in S^{\prime}
,
and

(SPC-SP)
for every distinct acquires
a,a^{\prime}\in T
with
\@lock(a)=\@lock(a^{\prime})
,
a\mathrel{\leq_{\text{\tiny Tr}}^{T}}a^{\prime}
implies
\@rel_{T}(a)\in S^{\prime}
.

We generally assume that
S
equals the set
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
of requests that characterize
a deadlock pattern.

SPC-Closure
makes sure that at the least the requests are included.

SPC-LW
ensures that thread order and last-write dependencies are satisfied.

SPC-SP
makes sure that sync-preservation holds
by ensuring that earlier critical sections are closed before later ones are opened.

The following result shows that the sync-preserving closure determines exactly the events necessary to form a sync-preserving reordering.

Lemma C.2 (
(
Tunç et al. 2023
, Lemma 4.1)
).

Let
T
be a trace and let
S\subseteq\evts(T)
.
There exists
T^{\prime}\in\@spCrp(T)
such that
\evts(T^{\prime})=\@spClosure_{T}(S)
.

This then leads to an elegant method to verify if a deadlock pattern
implies a sync-preserving deadlock.
Build the sync-preserving closure of requests
and check that none of the matching acquires are included.

Lemma C.3.

Let
{\mathit{d}p}_{T}L{\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}}
be a deadlock pattern.

\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
is a sync-preserving predictable deadlock iff

\@spClosure_{T}(\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\})\cap\{\@acq_{T}(q)\mid q\in\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}\}=\emptyset
.

The above result generalizes the earlier
(
Tunç et al. 2023
, Lemma 4.2)
.
Lemma 4.2 only covers the case where
L
equals the standard lock set function
{\mathit{L}H}_{to}
.
The proof of Lemma 4.2. can be naturally extended to
deadlock patterns and sync-preserving predictable deadlock
induced by
{\mathit{L}H}_{T}
and its approximations.

In combination with results
Lemma

5.6
and
Lemma

6.14

this shows that we can cover a larger class of sync-preserving deadlocks
and no false positives are reported.
