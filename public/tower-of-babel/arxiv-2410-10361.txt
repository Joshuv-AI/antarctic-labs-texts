Constrained Consensus-Based Optimization and Numerical Heuristics for the Few Particle Regime
Jonas Beddrich, Enis Chenchene, Massimo Fornasier, Hui Huang, Barbara Wohlmuth

Abstract
Consensus-based optimization (CBO) is a versatile multi-particle optimization method for performing nonconvex and nonsmooth global optimizations in high dimensions. Proofs of global convergence in probability have been achieved for a broad class of objective functions in unconstrained optimizations. In this work we adapt the algorithm for solving constrained optimizations on compact and unbounded domains with boundary by leveraging emerging reflective boundary conditions. In particular, we close a relevant gap in the literature by providing a global convergence proof for the many-particle regime comprehensive of convergence rates. On the one hand, for the sake of minimizing running cost, it is desirable to keep the number of particles small. On the other hand, reducing the number of particles implies a diminished capability of exploration of the algorithm. Hence numerical heuristics are needed to ensure convergence of CBO in the few-particle regime. In this work, we also significantly improve the convergence and complexity of CBO by utilizing an adaptive region control mechanism and by choosing geometry-specific random noise. In particular, by combining a hierarchical noise structure with a multigrid finite element method, we are able to compute global minimizers for a constrained p-Allen–Cahn problem with obstacles, a very challenging variational problem.

Introduction

In this paper we are concerned with theoretical guarantees for global optimization, namely
ensuring computability of global minimizers

x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x)

(1.1)

of a possibly nonconvex and nonsmooth objective function
\mathcal{E}:\overline{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Here
\mathcal{D}
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
its closure. This problem can be rightfully considered the ultimate challenge of mathematical optimization, because there are no clear directional or geometrical principles to guide the search for global minima
Banholzer et al. (2024)
;
Vinod et al. (2022)
. Local optimization techniques like gradient descent or (quasi-)Newton’s methods struggle with nonconvex problems because they often get trapped in possibly numerous local minima.
Even solving a simple polynomial equation can highlight these limitations, requiring multiple independent runs and possibly randomizing the initial conditions to be able to find all solutions.

Very-many-runs first or second order methods and metaheuristics, such as Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarm Optimization, Ant Colony Optimization, and other evolution strategies
Blum and Roli (2003)
;
Gendreau and Potvin (2013)
, have achieved extraordinary empirical successes in global optimization and have been extensively benchmarked, as evidenced by platforms like COCO
Hansen et al. (2021)
.
For Simulated Annealing the convergence in probability can be guaranteed by the large time analysis of the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation with vanishing diffusion by leveraging
\log
-Sobolev inequalities as forms of Polyak–Łojasiewicz conditions, see
Chizat (2022)
and the older results
Chiang et al. (1987)
;
Geman and Hwang (1986)
;
Holley and Stroock (1988)
. Unfortunately, the theoretically guaranteed rate of convergence in time
\mathcal{O}(\log\log(t)/\log(t))
of Simulated Annealing is rather poor, see
(
Chizat, 2022
, Theorem 4.1)
.
Instead, proving the global convergence of the widely used multi-agent methods Genetic Algorithms
Reeves (2010)
and Particle Swarm Optimization
Kennedy and Eberhart (1995)
with quantitative rates has remained an elusive challenge for decades, until recent breakthroughs did connect these to multi-agent dynamics
Borghi and Pareschi (2023)
;
Huang et al. (2023)
;
Grassi and Pareschi (2021)
;
Carrillo et al. (2018)
. More specifically, the most recent groundbreaking paradigm of Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO)

CBO aims at fusing the cooling strategy of Simulated Annealing
Kirkpatrick et al. (1983)
;
Kirkpatrick (1984)
;
Chiang et al. (1987)
;

Kennedy and Eberhart (1995)

Carrillo et al. (2010)
or the Hegselmann–Krause opinion formation model
Hegselmann and Krause (2002)

In the meanwhile, the original CBO model
Pinnau et al. (2017)
has been adapted to address a multitude of different problems, namely solving for multiple minimizers, saddle point problems, equilibria of games, multiobjective optimizations and more. It has been also employed with success in a large spectrum of real-life applications. The related literature is quite vast by now. Rather than attempting to include a necessarily incomplete account of this very fast growing field, we refer to the review paper
Totzeck (2021)
, and to
Fornasier and Sun (2025)
for a more recent and relatively comprehensive report. Below we limit ourselves to recall the relevant results, which are more focused on the specific subject of the present work.

In the following let
\mathcal{D}\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an open convex set with the boundary
\partial\mathcal{D}
and, by
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
we denote again the closure of
\mathcal{D}

The scope of the present paper is to close one of a relevant gaps left in the literature, that is providing a suitable formulation for CBO and related theoretical guarantees to solve global optimization problems of the type (

) constrained on convex domains with boundary.

It should be mentioned that the results included in this paper are not the only recent contribution aiming at formulating a CBO method for constrained optimization.

On the one hand, for the sake of minimizing running cost, it is certainly desirable to keep the number of particles quite small instead. On the other hand, reducing the number of particles implies a diminished capability of the algorithm of exploration the optimization domain and forming a consensus on the location of the global minimizer. Hence numerical heuristics are needed to ensure that CBO in the few-particle regime mimics and behaves as the many-particle one.

As the second relevant contribution of this paper, in this work we also significantly improve the convergence and complexity of CBO in the few-particle regime by utilizing an adaptive region control mechanism and by choosing geometry-specific random noise.
In particular, we show that these new heuristics are extremely powerful, allowing to solve the global optimization of the Rastrigin function in dimension

A complete theoretical study of these numerical results is left for future research.

3
Main technical challenges

This paper provides the first rigorous proof of
global
convergence for CBO in convex domains with boundaries by developing a new mean-field formulation and analytic framework that extends Wasserstein contractivity and the quantitative Laplace principle to constrained settings.

The main insights of our novel analysis is that the presence of a boundary does not destroy the exponential-in-time contractivity of CBO towards global minimizers and that the algorithm (
1.8
) is precisely the right one to discretize the dynamics.

In particular, we aim to address a significant gap in the literature: although the numerical scheme (
1.8
) has been known since its initial formulation in
Bae et al. (2022)
, no proof of global convergence has been provided to date. This gap arises for two main reasons. First, a proper analytical approach was developed only very recently in
Fornasier et al. (2024)
for unconstrained optimization. Second, the approach in
Fornasier et al. (2024)
relies on contractivity estimates involving the Wasserstein distance, as in (
1.6
). While this argument works well for unconstrained optimization, it has remained elusive how to adapt it to domains with boundaries, and how the presence of boundaries would affect estimates involving Wasserstein-like distances. To emphasize such difficulty, let us reiterate that in all existing CBO literature on constrained optimization, authors have consistently sought to avoid dealing directly with boundaries
Bae et al. (2022)
;
Borghi et al. (2023b)
;
Carrillo et al. (2023)
;
Carrillo et al. (2024a)
;
Fornasier et al. (2020)
;
Fornasier et al. (2021)
;
Fornasier et al. (2022a)
. (The only exception is the recent work
Hinds et al. (2025)
already discussed above at the end of the previous section.)

One of the main contributions of this paper is the identification of a proper formulation of the mean-field approximation (
2.4
), which enables inequalities such as (
5.8
) that are crucial in estimates involving Wasserstein-like distances, such as (
5.4
). Without finding the right formulation and carefully developing the entire argument, it is by no means obvious that adapting the system to reflective boundary conditions would permit contractivity of the functional (
5.4
), making this a central and nontrivial achievement of our work. Indeed, for minimizers precisely at the boundary, one could have been concerned that reflecting boundary conditions could contribute to counteract the convergence, an effect that the theoretical results of the present paper eventually excludes, at least for convex domains (for nonconvex domains, this issue remains open).

Another central technical tool used in the paper
Fornasier et al. (2024)
is the so-called
quantitative Laplace principle
, which was again formulated to work for problems defined on the entire Euclidean space. Proposition
5.1
, Proposition
5.2
, Proposition
5.5
, and Corollary
5.3
finally allow for the adaptation to bounded domains. Without such a proper formulation, it would have not been possible to deal with the analysis for the global minimizers laying precisely at the boundary of the domain, a situation very common in optimization.

As a further contribution, the proofs of convergence as in Theorem
5.4
and Theorem
5.7
are simpler compared to the formulation in
Fornasier et al. (2024)
, offering a more straightforward approach to further developments.

Although these theoretical results are already significant advances for the reliable use of CBO for constrained optimization, we felt interesting to explore how flexible and effective CBO can be for constrained optimization, by pushing a bit further the boundaries of numerical experiments beyond classical benchmarks
Momin Jamil (2013)
as reported so many times in other previous studies
Borghi et al. (2023b)
;
Borghi et al. (2023a)
;
Pinnau et al. (2017)
;
Totzeck and Wolfram (2020)
;
Hinds et al. (2025)
.
Therefore, we introduce novel heuristics tailored to few-particle regimes, including adaptive region control and geometry-dependent noise. The numerical experiments we report demonstrate that, even with a small number of particles, with such heuristics CBO can successfully solve problems that previous studies could not address. We also explore new challenging applications, such as the reliable computation of ground states of nonconvex energies, and show for the first time in the CBO literature that the
p
-Allen–Cahn variational problem with obstacles can be solved efficiently and robustly, highlighting the adaptability of CBO to various geometries and problems.

5

In this section, we present our main result about the global convergence in mean-field law for cost functions satisfying the following conditions.

Assumption 3
.

Throughout this section we are interested in the objective function
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\mathcal{D}})
, for which

1.

there exists a unique
x^{*}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\mathcal{E}(x^{*})=\min\limits_{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x)=:\underline{\mathcal{E}}
.

2.

there exists some
\mathcal{E}_{\infty},R_{0},\eta,\nu>0
such that

\eta|x-x^{*}|\leq|\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}|^{\nu}\mbox{ for all }x\in B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}\,,

(5.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}<\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}\mbox{ for all }x\in(B_{R_{0}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}\,.

(5.2)

3.

For any
q>0
, there exists some
r\in(0,R_{0}]
such that

|\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}|\leq q,\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}\,.

(5.3)

In this section we define

\mathcal{V}(t):=\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*}|^{2}]\,,

(5.4)

then one has the following lemma

Lemma 5.1
.

The functional
\mathcal{V}(t)
defined in (
5.4
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{V}(t)}{dt}\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|+\sigma^{2}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}

(5.5)

Proof.

It is easy to compute that

\displaystyle d|\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*}|^{2}=2(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})dX_{t}+\sigma^{2}|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}dt\,.

(5.6)

which implies

\displaystyle d\mathcal{V}(t)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))]dt-2\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot n(\overline{X}_{t})\mathbf{I}_{\partial\mathcal{D}}(\overline{X}_{t})d|\overline{L}|_{t}]

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}]dt

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))]dt+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}]dt

\displaystyle=-2\lambda\mathcal{V}(t)-2\lambda\mathbb{E}[(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot(x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))]+\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|+\sigma^{2}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}\,,

(5.7)

where in the second inequality we have used the fact that

-2(\overline{X}_{t}-x^{*})\cdot n(\overline{X}_{t})\leq 0

(5.8)

for all
\overline{X}_{t}\in\partial\mathcal{D}
and
x^{*}\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}
, since
\mathcal{D}
is convex and
n(\overline{X}_{t})
is the outward normal vector at
\overline{X}_{t}
. This completes the proof.
∎

In the following, we shall use the notation for a
\ell^{\infty}
-ball with a radius
r
and the center
x^{*}
, i.e.
B_{r}(x^{*}):=\{x:~|x-x^{*}|_{\infty}\leq r\}

\mathcal{E}
around the minimizer

\mathcal{E}_{s}:=\sup_{x\in B_{s}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}|\,.

(5.9)

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{E}
satisfies Assumption
3
. For any
t>0
and
s\in(0,R_{0}]
let
\mathcal{E}_{s}
be defined as in (
5.9
),
0<q\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\infty}}{2}
, and
r:=\max\{s\in(0,R_{0}]:~\mathcal{E}_{s}\leq q\}

then we have

\displaystyle|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|\leq\frac{(2q)^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q\right)}{\rho_{t}\left(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\}\right)}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\rho_{t}(dx)\,.

(5.10)

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{r}\geq r>0
, and using Jensen’s inequality one can deduce

|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|\leq\int_{B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\frac{\omega_{\alpha}(x)\rho_{t}(dx)}{\|\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t})}}+\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\frac{\omega_{\alpha}(x)\rho_{t}(dx)}{\|\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t})}}\,.

(5.11)

The first term is bounded by
\widetilde{r}
since
|x-x^{*}|\leq\widetilde{r}
for all
x\in B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*})
. Moreover, it follows from Markov’s inequality that

\displaystyle\|\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t})}\geq

\displaystyle\exp(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}}))\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x))\geq\exp(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}}))\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}}))\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:\mathcal{E}(x)\leq(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}})\})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}}))\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})\,.

(5.12)

Then for the second term we have

\displaystyle\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\frac{\omega_{\alpha}(x)\rho_{t}(dx)}{\|\omega_{\alpha}(\cdot)\|_{L_{1}(\rho_{t})}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}}))\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})}\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\omega_{\alpha}(x)\rho_{t}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-\alpha\min_{x\in(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x))}{\exp(-\alpha(\mathcal{E}_{r}+\underline{\mathcal{E}}))\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})}\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\rho_{t}(dx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha(\min_{x\in(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x)-\mathcal{E}_{r}-\underline{\mathcal{E}})\right)}{\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})}\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\rho_{t}(dx)\,.

(5.13)

Thus for any
\widetilde{r}\geq r>0
we obtain

\displaystyle|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|

(5.14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha(\min_{x\in(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x)-\mathcal{E}_{r}-\underline{\mathcal{E}})\right)}{\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})}\int_{(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|\rho_{t}(dx)\,.

Next we choose
\widetilde{r}=\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}=\frac{(q+\mathcal{E}_{r})^{\nu}}{\eta}\geq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{r}^{\nu}}{\eta}=\frac{\left(\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}|\right)^{\nu}}{\eta}\geq\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-x^{*}|=r

(5.15)

by using Assumption
3
. Additionally one notice that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x)-\underline{\mathcal{E}}\geq\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}_{\infty},\quad\mbox{ if }x\in(B_{R_{0}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}\,,\\
(\widetilde{r}\eta)^{\frac{1}{\nu}},\quad\mbox{ if }x\in B_{R_{0}}(x^{*})\cap(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}\,.\end{cases}

(5.16)

Thus, since
(\widetilde{r}\eta)^{\frac{1}{\nu}}=q+\mathcal{E}_{r}\leq 2q\leq\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{x\in(B_{\widetilde{r}}(x^{*}))^{c}\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{E}(x)-\mathcal{E}_{r}-\underline{\mathcal{E}}\geq(\widetilde{r}\eta)^{\frac{1}{\nu}}-\mathcal{E}_{r}=q+\mathcal{E}_{r}-\mathcal{E}_{r}=q\,.

(5.17)

Inserting this and the definition of
\widetilde{r}
into (
5.14
), we conclude the result.
∎

To eventually apply the above proposition, one needs to ensure that
\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})=\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{t}\in B_{r}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}})
is
bounded away from
0
for a finite time horizon
T
. To do so, we employ a rather technical argument inspired from
(
Fornasier et al., 2024
, Proposition 23)
, and introduce the mollifier
\phi_{r}^{x_{0}}:~{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, with
r>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
defined by

\displaystyle\phi_{r}^{x_{0}}(x):=\begin{cases}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\exp\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{r^{2}-\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}}\right),&\text{ if }x\in B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\mathcal{D}\\
0,&\text{ else }.\end{cases}

(5.18)

So we have
\mbox{Im}(\phi_{r})=[0,1],\mbox{supp}(\phi_{r})=B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\mathcal{D},\phi_{r}\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{x_{k}}\phi_{r}^{x_{0}}(x)&=-2r^{2}\frac{\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}}{\left(r^{2}-\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}\phi_{r}(x),\\
\partial_{x_{k}^{2}}^{2}\phi_{r}^{x_{0}}(x)&=2r^{2}\left(\frac{2\left(2\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}-r^{2}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}-\left(r^{2}-\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(r^{2}-\left(x-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{4}}\right)\phi_{r}(x)\,.\end{aligned}

(5.19)

The proof will be split into two cases:

5.1
Global convergence proof when

is bounded

In this subsection we assume that

is bounded. Namely, there exists some diameter

such that

First, we get the following result, whose proof is postponed to the Appendix.

.

) up to any time

This result directly implies

.

Let
(\overline{X}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}

. Especially when

and

.

Proof.

Case 1
: if

, then one may let

in Proposition
5.2
and immediately gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{t}\in B_{r}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}})\geq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}^{x^{*}}(\overline{X}_{0})]\exp(-\vartheta t)\,.

∎

Now we are ready to prove the global convergence result, which provides a rate for the variance function

within a prescribed time-range.

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume that
\mathcal{E}
satisfies Assumption
3
, and
\lambda,\sigma
satisfy
2\lambda>\sigma^{2}
. Furthermore assume the initial data satisfies
\mathcal{V}(0)>2\varepsilon
for any prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon>0
, and
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\widetilde{r}}^{\widetilde{x}^{*}}(\overline{X}_{0})]>0
with
\widetilde{x}^{*},\widetilde{r}
to be determined later. Let
\tau\in(0,1)
and choose
\alpha
to be sufficiently large satisfying

\alpha\geq\frac{\vartheta T_{\varepsilon}-\log(\frac{1}{2}c_{5}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\widetilde{r}}^{\widetilde{x}^{*}}(\overline{X}_{0})])}{c_{4}}\,,

(5.24)

where
\vartheta
comes from Corollary
5.3
,

T_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{(1-\tau)(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{V}(0)}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad c_{5}:=\min\left\{\frac{\tau(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})}{4(\lambda+\sigma^{2})},\sqrt{\frac{\tau(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right\}\,,

and

c_{4}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\left(\eta\frac{c_{5}\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu}},\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\right\},\quad r:=\left\{\max_{s\in(0,R_{0}]}:~\mathcal{E}_{s}\leq c_{4}\right\}\,,

and
\widetilde{x}^{*}\in D
and
0<\widetilde{r}<r
are choosen such that
B_{\widetilde{r}}(\widetilde{x}^{*})\subset B_{r}(x^{*})
.
Then there exists some
0<T_{\ast}\leq T_{\varepsilon}
such that
\mathcal{V}(t)
satisfies

\mathcal{V}(t)\leq\mathcal{V}(0)\exp(-(1-\tau)(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})t)\quad\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,T_{\ast})\,.

(5.25)

and it reaches the prescribed accuracy at time
T_{\ast}
, namely
\mathcal{V}(T_{\ast})=\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We define

T_{\alpha}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:~\mathcal{V}(t)=\varepsilon\right\}\,,

(5.26)

and for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}]
let

C_{\alpha}(t):=c_{5}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(t)}=\min\left\{\frac{\tau(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})}{4(\lambda+\sigma^{2})},\sqrt{\frac{\tau(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right\}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(t)}\,.

(5.27)

Here the time
T_{\alpha}
represents the first time when the variance
\mathcal{V}(t)
reaches the prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon
, and it depends on
\alpha
because
\mathcal{V}
does.
It is obvious that
T_{\alpha}>0
is because of the assumption on the initial data that
\mathcal{V}(0)>2\varepsilon
.
According to the definition of
T_{\alpha}
one has
\mathcal{V}(t)>\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
and
\mathcal{V}(T_{\alpha})=\varepsilon
. Next we will prove that
T_{\alpha}\leq T_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{V}(t)
decreases exponentially on
[0,T_{\alpha})
.

Case
T_{\alpha}\leq T_{\varepsilon}
: It follows from Proposition
5.1
that for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})

\displaystyle|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|\leq\frac{(2q_{t}^{\alpha})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{t}^{\alpha}\right)}{\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})}\int|x-x^{*}|\rho_{t}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2q_{t}^{\alpha})^{\nu}}{\eta}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha q_{t}^{\alpha}\right)}{\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(t)}\,,

(5.28)

where we have constructed

q_{t}^{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\left(\eta\frac{C_{\alpha}({t})}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu}},\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\right\}\,.

(5.29)

Then it holds

c_{4}=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\left(\eta\frac{c_{5}\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\nu}},\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\right\}<q_{t}^{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_{\infty}

(5.30)

because
C_{\alpha}(t)>c_{5}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, here we let

r=\left\{\max_{s\in[0,R_{0}]}:~\mathcal{E}_{s}\leq c_{4}\right\}\,.

(5.31)

By construction, these choices satisfy
r\leq R_{0}
and
\frac{(2q_{t}^{\alpha})^{\nu}}{\eta}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}({t})}{2}
.

Moreover,
\rho_{t}(\{x\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:x\in B_{r}(x^{*})\})=\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{t}\in B_{r}(x^{*})\cap\overline{\mathcal{D}})\geq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\widetilde{r}}^{\widetilde{x}^{*}}(\overline{X}_{0})]\exp(-\vartheta t)
holds according to (
5.21
), where
\vartheta
depends only on
d,r,\lambda,\sigma
and
|\mathcal{D}|
.
This concludes that for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})

\displaystyle|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}({t})}{2}+\frac{\exp\left(-\alpha c_{4}\right)\exp\left(\vartheta T_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\widetilde{r}}^{\widetilde{x}^{*}}(\overline{X}_{0})]}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(t)}\leq C_{\alpha}(t)\,,

(5.32)

where we choose
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
with

\alpha_{0}=\frac{\vartheta T_{\varepsilon}-\log(\frac{1}{2}c_{5}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\widetilde{r}}^{\widetilde{x}^{*}}(\overline{X}_{0})])}{c_{4}}\,.

(5.33)

Let us recall the upper bound for the time derivative of
\mathcal{V}(t)
given in Lemma
5.1
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{V}(t)}{dt}\leq-(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)+2(\lambda+\sigma^{2})\mathcal{V}(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|+\sigma^{2}|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|^{2}\,.

(5.34)

Then using the definition of
c_{5}
and the estimate from (
5.32
) one can deduce that

(5.35)

which by Gronwall’s inequality leads to

\mathcal{V}(t)\leq\mathcal{V}(0)\exp(-(1-\tau)(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})t)\mbox{ for }t\in[0,T_{\alpha})\,.

(5.36)

Case
T_{\varepsilon}<T_{\alpha}
:
By the definition of
T_{\alpha}
we know
\mathcal{V}(t)>\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon}]
.
Then following the same argument as in the first case, one can conclude

\mathcal{V}(t)\leq\mathcal{V}(0)\exp(-(1-\tau)(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})t)\mbox{ for }t\in[0,T_{\varepsilon}]\,.

(5.37)

Using the this estimate the fact that
T_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{(1-\tau)(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})}\log(\frac{\mathcal{V}(0)}{\varepsilon})
implies
\mathcal{V}(T_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
, which is a contradiction. Thus this case can never happen.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the global convergence result as in Theorem

Theorem 5.7

and
\widetilde{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}
and

are chosen such that

Then there exists some

T_{\alpha}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:~\mathcal{V}(t)=\varepsilon\quad\mbox{or}\quad|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|=2c_{5}\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(0)}\right\}\,,

According to the definition of

where we have constructed

because

By construction, these choices satisfy

5.3
Quantitative convergence result for the
numerical scheme

We will use the Euler–Maruyama scheme from
Słomiński (2001)
to solve the particle system (

) numerically.
For this purpose we define the orthogonal projection operator

\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}(x):=\arg\min_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}}|x-z|\,,\quad\text{for}\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,.

(5.58)

Note that for a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the projection
\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}(x)
exists and is uniquely determined due to the convexity of
\overline{\mathcal{D}}
. Given a time horizon
T>0
and a time discretization
t_{0}=0<\Delta t<\dots<K\Delta t=T
of
[0,T]
. Then the Euler–Maruyama scheme amounts to:

\displaystyle X_{(k+1)\Delta t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\Big(X_{k\Delta t}^{i}-\Delta t\lambda(X_{k\Delta t}^{i}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N}))+\sigma D(X_{k\Delta t}^{i}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N}))N^{i}(0,\Delta t)\Big)

\displaystyle X_{0}^{i}

\displaystyle\sim\rho_{0},\quad i=1,\dots,N\,,

(5.59)

where
\{N^{i}(0,\Delta t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent Gaussian random vectors with zero mean and covariance matrix
\Delta t\textbf{Id}_{d}
. Now collecting results from Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
5.4
we can establish a quantitative convergence result for the numerical scheme (
5.59
). We do so by controlling the following discrete counterpart of (
5.4
):

\mathcal{V}_{N}(t):=W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t}^{N},\delta_{x^{*}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{t}^{i}-x^{*}\right|^{2}\right]\,,\quad t=k\Delta t,k\in[K]\,.

(5.60)

We are now ready to prove the main result of this paper (cf. Theorem
1.1
):

Theorem 5.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
and Theorem
5.4
(or Theorem
5.7
), let
\{(X_{k\Delta t}^{i})_{k=1,\dots,K}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be the iterations generated by Euler–Maruyama scheme (
5.59
) with
K\Delta t=T_{*}
, where
T_{*}
comes from Theorem
5.4
(or Theorem
5.7
) such that
\mathcal{V}(T_{*})=\varepsilon
for any prescribed accuracy
\varepsilon>0
. Then the final iterations fulfill the following quantitative error estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{N}\left(T_{*}\right)\leq 3C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t\log(1/\Delta t)+3C_{\mathrm{MFA}}\frac{1}{N}+3\varepsilon\,,

(5.61)

where
C_{\mathrm{MFA}}
comes from Theorem
4.3
, and
C_{\mathrm{NA}}
depends on
\lambda
,
\sigma
,
\alpha
,
d
,
T_{*}
,
N
and
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\{(X_{t}^{i})_{t\in[0,T_{*}]}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{(\overline{X}_{t}^{i})_{t\in[0,T_{*}]}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are are solutions to the CBO particle system (
2.1
) and
N
independent copies of solutions to the mean-field dynamics (
2.4
) up to time
T_{*}=K\Delta t
respectively. Then
we split the error

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{N}\left(T_{*}\right)\leq

\displaystyle 3\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(X_{K\Delta t}^{i}-X_{T_{*}}^{i})\right|^{2}\right]+3\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(X_{T_{*}}^{i}-\overline{X}_{T_{*}}^{i})\right|^{2}\right]

(5.62)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 3\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{X}_{T_{*}}^{i}-x^{*}\right|^{2}\right]\,,

which divide the overall error into an approximation error of the Euler scheme, the mean-field limit estimate error and the optimization error in mean-field law. The first term on the right hand side of (

(

Finally the third term follows from Theorem

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{X}_{T_{*}}^{i}-x^{*}\right|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon\,.

6
Numerical Experiments and Applications

In this section, we present numerical experiments, which are performed in Python, partially based on CBXpy
Bailo et al. (2024)
, and are available for reproducibility at
https://github.com/echnen/CBO-with-boundaries
. Let us introduce now the rationale of these experiments.

The success of the CBO algorithm lies in its unique blend of exploration and exploitation. It achieves exploration through the diffusion of a large number of particles, while exploitation is facilitated by harnessing collective information to form a consensus point that approximates the global minimizer. The synergy of these effects—exploration through particle diffusion and exploitation via the consensus point—becomes increasingly powerful as the number of particles grows. Theorem
5.8
guarantees that, as the number of particles
N
increases, the algorithm converges in expectation at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
. This indicates that the accuracy improves significantly with more particles. However, the constants in the error bound (
5.62
), especially
C_{\mathrm{MFA}}
, can increase exponentially with certain problem parameters, such as dimensionality. Consequently, it is challenging to directly apply these convergence results when using the CBO algorithm (
5.59
) with a limited number of particles
N
. In the few-particle regime, the effectiveness of the CBO algorithm exploration and exploitation mechanisms diminishes. To ensure successful convergence even with fewer particles, we must augment these mechanisms with specialized heuristics. These enhancements should enable the algorithm to keep dynamics similar to those observed with a large number of particles, thereby preserving its effectiveness despite the reduced particle count.

In this section, we introduce two key strategies to enhance the exploration and exploitation capabilities of the CBO algorithm in a few-particle regime.

1.

Enhanced Exploitation:
To improve exploitation, we leverage the insight that the consensus point typically remains within an exponentially shrinking region around the global minimizer. Indeed, combining (
5.32
) or (
5.52
), for which

|x^{*}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|\lesssim\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(t)}

with the exponential decay in (
5.25
) or (
5.41
)

\mathcal{V}(t)\leq\mathcal{V}(0)\exp(-(1-\tau)(2\lambda-\sigma^{2})t)\quad\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,T_{\ast})\,.

we conclude that there is an exponentially shrinking
trust-region

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t})|\lesssim\exp(-(1-\tau)(\lambda-\sigma^{2}/2)t)\}
in which we can assume that particles are moving with high-probability. Based on this observation, even with fewer particles, we will---optimistically---constrain them to stay within progressively shrinking balls centered around the consensus-point, following a carefully planned shrinking schedule. Admittedly, such optimism would be misplaced if few particles would not explore enough, but this is counteracted by the Heuristic 2. which enforces at the same time an enhanced exploration
2
2

2

Another reasoning supporting Heuristic 1. is that trust-region methods
Conn et al. (2000)
, which are connected to CBO as explained in the Appendix, are known to converge (at least to local minimizers). While in trust-region methods the search is based on a quadratic interpolation and a guess-and-accept/reject mechanism for the trust radius, in CBO the search is based on stochastic exploration and the definition of consensus-point, which determine the trust-region. We refer to the Appendix for more insights.
.

2.

Enhanced Exploration:
We propose a heuristic that boosts exploration by increasing the volatility in the noise. Specifically, we allow the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
to exceed
2\lambda
, contrary to the conditions set by Theorem
5.4
. This adjustment aims to compensate for the reduced number of particles by providing greater variability in their movements.

These enhancements are designed to ensure that the dynamics of the algorithm remain effective and similar to those observed with a larger number of particles, even when operating in a few-particle regime.

To our knowledge, these two enhancement mechanisms—boosting exploration through increased noise volatility and enhancing exploitation by constraining particles to shrinking regions around the global minimizer—have not been explored in the existing literature. However, they enable us to obtain numerical results that were previously unobserved in previous numerical experiments, see, e.g.,
Borghi et al. (2023b)
;
Borghi et al. (2023a)
;
Pinnau et al. (2017)
;
Totzeck and Wolfram (2020)
.

For example, it has been well-documented that the standard CBO algorithm struggles to minimize the Rastrigin function in higher dimensions (e.g.,
d\geq 20
), regardless of how reasonably large is the number of particles used. Remarkably, our enhanced CBO implementation surpasses this dimensionality barrier, demonstrating consistent convergence with a moderate number of particles. This breakthrough suggests that our approach can significantly improve the algorithm performance in high-dimensional optimization problems. We illustrate these findings in Section
6.1
and Section
6.2
.

Moreover, as noted in
Carrillo et al. (2021)
;
Kalise et al. (2023)
;
Fornasier et al. (2022b)
, incorporating anisotropic noise or jump process noise can significantly enhance exploration in the few-particle regime, especially for high-dimensional optimization problems. This observation implies that adaptively shaping the noise based on the specific application is a crucial heuristic for optimizing the performance of the CBO algorithm. Tailoring the noise to suit the problem at hand can provide additional benefits, particularly when dealing with complex, high-dimensional landscapes.

Below, we will demonstrate how these adaptive noise strategies, combined with our previously discussed enhancements, contribute to improved convergence and robustness of the CBO algorithm even with a reduced number of particles. In particular, in Section
6.3
we explore the efficient computation of global minimizers of 1D
p
-Allen–Cahn energies with boundary conditions and additional obstacle constraints. The problem is solved by combining a hierarchical approximation and noise shaping within a multigrid finite element method. Given the existence of continua of (local) minimizers of the
p
-Allen–Cahn energy, the variational problems is extremely challenging. Yet, our hierarchical CBO solver does converge robustly to the global minimizer by using very few particles, despite the high-dimensionality of the optimization problem and, very importantly, without starting from initial data in the vicinity to the sought solution. To our knowledge, this is the first example in the literature of use of CBO for solving problems in scientific computing. These very promising results suggest that CBO could be employed to solve other challenging nonlinear equations, certainly a very interesting direction for future research.

Partial differential equations and optimization problems are inherently intertwined, as many PDE systems emerge as gradient flows of free energy functionals. Noticeably, the Allen–Cahn and Cahn–Hilliard equations can be derived as the
L^{2}
and
H^{-1}
gradient flows of the Ginzburg–Landau (GL) free energy functional, respectively, see, e.g.,
Feng and Prohl (2003)
;
Lee et al. (2014)
. By employing CBO with hierarchically structured noise, we efficiently compute the global minimizer of a variational
p
-Allen–Cahn-type problem within a finite element framework.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{GL}(v)
be the
p
-GL free energy functional over the integral
[0,1]

\mathcal{E}_{GL}(v)=\int_{[0,1]}\frac{1}{p}\left|\nabla v(s)\right|^{p}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}F(v(s))\mathrm{d}s,

(6.5)

where
F(v)=(v-w_{1})^{2}(v-w_{2})^{2}
is a so-called double well potential. The minimization problem reads as

v^{*}=\arg\min_{v\in V}\mathcal{E}_{GL}(v)\,,

(6.6)

where
V=\{v\in W^{1,p}[0,1]\,|\,v(0)=v_{0},v(1)=v_{1}\}
. We discretize the continuous minimization problem using the finite element method. The interval
[0,1]
is discretized into
M
one-dimensional elements
T_{j}=[(j-1)h,jh]
,
j=1,\ldots,M
of equal length
h=M^{-1}
, and we define the finite element space
V_{M}

\displaystyle V_{M}

\displaystyle=\big\{v\in V\quad

\displaystyle v|_{T_{j}}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(T_{j}),\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\big\}.

(6.7)

Expanding
v\in V_{M}
in terms of first-order Lagrange basis functions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{M}
, such that
v=\sum_{j=0}^{M}\phi_{j}x_{j}
, whereby
x_{0}=v_{0}
,
x_{M}=v_{1}
, we obtain the discrete formulation

x^{*}=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(\frac{h}{p}\left|\frac{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}{h}\right|^{p}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\widetilde{F}_{j}\right).

(6.8)

The integral over the double-well potential
\tilde{F}_{j}=\int_{(j-1)h}^{jh}F(v(t))\mathrm{d}t
can be calculated numerically or even analytically.
The optimization problem is high-dimensional with
d=M-1
and can be associated with
V_{M,0}=\text{span }\{\phi_{j},j=1,\ldots,M-1\}
. Due to the double-well potential, numerous local minima do exist, which, depending on
\epsilon
, retain arbitrarily steep basins of attraction. Since the GL free energy function depends on the gradient of
v
, Gaussian noise is badly suited for the optimization, as it does not reflect the smoothness of the solution. Therefore, naively applying CBO for fine discretizations requires a large number of particles, small time steps, and large times, and thus, becomes unfeasible due to the computational cost.

6.3.1
Hierarchical structured noise

We overcome these limitations using a hierarchical approach similar to a multi-grid method. We set
M=2^{m}
and solve subsequently the optimization problem over a nested sequence of finite element spaces
V_{2}\subset V_{4}\subset V_{8}\subset\ldots\subset V_{2^{m}}
using the final particle distribution of the previous run as the initial particle distribution of the following. Thereby, we exploit that CBO solves the coarse problem efficiently and can find the solution for fine resolutions, provided that the initial particle distribution is sufficiently close to the solution. Similar hierarchical approaches are well studied in the context of solving obstacle problems; see
Hüeber and Wohlmuth (2005)
;
Gräser and Kornhuber (2009)
;
Wohlmuth (2011)
.

Alternatively, the hierarchical approach can be implemented using the highest resolution and solely changing the noise of the algorithm. During the
i
th run, at each time step and for every particle, we replace the standard anisotropic noise vector
\beta=\sigma D(X_{k\Delta t}^{j}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N}))N^{j}(0,\Delta t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
defined in (
1.8
) with
{\beta}^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, such that

{\beta}_{l}^{i}=\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{l}^{m}(s)\sum_{k=1}^{2^{i}-1}\phi_{k}^{i}(s)\beta_{k2^{(m-i)}}\mathrm{d}s,\quad l=1,\ldots,2^{m}-1,

(6.9)

where
\{\phi_{l}^{m}\}_{l=1}^{2^{m}-1}
and
\{\phi_{k}^{i}\}_{k=1}^{2^{i}-1}
are first-order Lagrangian bases of
V_{2^{m},0}
and
V_{2^{i},0}
, respectively. Accordingly, the initial particle distribution of the first CBO run is drawn with respect to the coarsest resolution. We note that by this construction, we automatically decrease the correlation length of our noise with each refinement step. The resulting particle distributions are visualized in Figure
10
.

For the numerical experiment, we choose
v_{0}=0.5
,

Appendix

Consensus-Based Optimization within the global optimization landscape

Here, we refer to Figure
1
, which illustrates how Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) fits within the broader landscape of global optimization. We begin with the original 2017 paper
Pinnau et al. (2017)
, where the authors introduced CBO as a hybrid approach, combining elements of Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Simulated Annealing (SA). While the connections in
Pinnau et al. (2017)
were initially based on arguments by analogy, subsequent works have rigorously established them. In particular,
Grassi and Pareschi (2021)
;
Cipriani et al. (2022)
showed that CBO can be derived as the zero-inertia limit of a suitably formulated version of PSO. This insight enabled the first rigorous analysis of global convergence for PSO, as proposed in
Huang et al. (2023)
.

The fact that CBO is a form of SA was made rigorous in the paper
Fornasier et al. (2024)
, where one of us proved that as the number of particles
N\to+\infty
and for
\alpha\to+\infty
, each particle tends to the solution of the following annealed Langevin dynamics:

dX(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{F}(X(t))+\sigma|X(t)-x^{*}|dB_{t},

where
\mathcal{F}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}
.

Further surprising connections of CBO with other relevant global optimization methods have been derived in
Riedl et al. (2023)
through the Euler-Maruyama approximation of the CBO dynamics (
1.2
):

\displaystyle X^{i}_{(k+1)\Delta t}

\displaystyle=X^{i}_{k\Delta t}-\Delta t\lambda(X^{i}_{k\Delta t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N}))+\sqrt{\Delta t}\sigma|X^{i}_{k\Delta t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N})|N^{i}_{k}(0,1),

(6.10)

By scaling
\lambda=\frac{1}{\Delta t}
one obtains

\displaystyle X^{i}_{(k+1)\Delta t}

\displaystyle=X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N}))+\sqrt{\Delta t}\sigma|X^{i}_{k\Delta t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{k\Delta t}^{N})|N^{i}_{k}(0,1),

(6.11)

and, consequently, the so-called
Consensus Hopping scheme (CH)
as introduced in
Riedl et al. (2023)

\displaystyle X_{k+1}^{CH}=X_{\alpha}(\mathcal{N}(X_{k}^{CH},\sigma_{k}^{2})),

(6.12)

which is the consensus point of a Gaussian centered in the previous consensus point with a suitable variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
. Let us first notice that the CH scheme is an instance of
(1,\lambda)
Evolution Strategies (ES), with
\mu=1
parent and
\lambda=N
offspring
4
4

4

We recall that a
(\mu,\lambda)
-Evolution Strategy
Auger and Hansen (2005)
maintains a population of
\mu
candidate solutions, called parents. In each iteration, the algorithm generates
\lambda
new candidate solutions, called offspring, by applying a “mutation” to the parents. The mutation operator typically adds random perturbations drawn from a normal distribution to the parent solutions. (Compare (
6.12
).)
. Proofs of global convergence for ES were obtained long ago, for instance in
Rudolph (1997)
;
Rudolph (1998)
, using arguments based on Markov chains. However, most classical proofs of global convergence for ES do not provide explicit convergence rates. Based on the connection between CBO and ES obtained through (
6.10
)–(
6.12
), it is now possible to also establish exponential convergence rates, as discovered in the forthcoming paper
Fornasier et al. (2025)
. Connections between CBO and ES have been further investigated in
Roith et al. (2025)
.

By the Laplace principle, the explicit expression for the consensus point in (
6.12
) can be approximated, for a suitable scaling of
\alpha\to\infty
with respect to
\sigma_{k}\to 0
, by

\displaystyle X_{k+1}^{CH}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}(\mathcal{N}(X_{k}^{CH},\sigma_{k}^{2})

(6.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\fint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,e^{-\alpha\left(\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}\alpha}|x-X_{k}^{CH}|^{2}\right)}dx

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{2\tau}|x-X_{k}^{CH}|^{2},

which can be readily recognized as a
Minimizing Movement Scheme (MMS)
or a
Proximal Point Method
. In other words, the iteration of CH follows approximately a Gradient Descent (GD), a surprising connection that was recently made rigorous in
Riedl et al. (2023)
, where it was shown that CBO can be interpreted as a suitable stochastic relaxation of GD.

Furthermore, the MMS is just one instance of a broader class of proximal point schemes of the form

\displaystyle X_{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x)+\frac{1}{p\tau^{p-1}}|x-X_{k}|^{p},

(6.14)

for any
1<p<\infty
. As
p\to+\infty
, such schemes converge to

\displaystyle X_{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in B(X_{k},R_{\tau})}\mathcal{E}(x),

(6.15)

which restricts the search for the next approximation to an optimal point within a
Trust Region (TR)
. Notice that for
\tau\to 0
suitable piecewise interpolations of both iterations in (
6.14
) and (
6.15
) converge to a gradient flow trajectory with different time reparametrizations, a well-known insight since the 1993 groundbreaking work of De Giorgi
De Giorgi (1993)
. Hence, the conceptual path from (
6.12
) to (
6.15
) illustrates how CBO is also connected to both GD and TR methods
Conn et al. (2000)
.
We conclude mentioning the work
Borghi and Pareschi (2025)
where
Genetic Algorithms (GA)
are also put in connection to CBO through kinetic models
Borghi et al. (2024)
.

In conclusion, CBO occupies a very central position (Figure
1
) within a composite landscape of related methods for global optimization. In light of these connections, the powerful mathematical techniques very recently developed for analyzing the global convergence of CBO (starting with
Carrillo et al. (2018)
;
Carrillo et al. (2021)
;
Fornasier et al. (2024)
;
Ha et al. (2021)
;
Ko et al. (2022)
;
Huang et al. (2025)
), as further developed in this paper, have also enabled further breakthroughs, such as describing the global convergence of PSO
Huang et al. (2023)
and deriving convergence rates for ES
Fornasier et al. (2025)
. We believe that, given their broad applicability, the significance of these mathematical techniques cannot be overstated, and they have the potential to inspire further advances in global optimization; this paper wants to represent a meaningful contribution in that direction.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)_{k}^{2}\left(2\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}-r^{2}\right)\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}

(6.36)

by using
(1-c)^{2}\leq(2c-1)c
. Hence (
6.28
) holds and we have
T_{1,k}(\overline{X}_{t})+T_{2,k}(\overline{X}_{t})\geq 0
.

Subset

K_{1,k}\cap K_{2,k}\cap B_{r}(x_{0}):
Notice that when
(\overline{X}_{t})_{k}=(X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}
, we have
T_{1,k}=T_{2,k}=0
, so in this case there is nothing to prove. If
(\overline{X}_{t})_{k}\neq(X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}
, or
\sigma^{2}(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}(\rho_{t}))_{k}^{2}>0

(\sigma>0)
, we exploit

\overline{X}_{t}\in K_{2,k}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)_{k}\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}}{\left(r^{2}-\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{-\left|\left(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)_{k}\right|\left|\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}\right|}{\left(r^{2}-\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}

(6.37)

\displaystyle>\frac{2\lambda\left(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)_{k}\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}}{\widetilde{c}r^{2}\sigma^{2}\left|\left(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)_{k}\right|\left|\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}\right|}\geq-\frac{2\lambda}{\widetilde{c}r^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

(6.38)

Using this,
T_{1,k}
can be bounded from below

\displaystyle T_{1,k}(\overline{X}_{t})

\displaystyle=2r^{2}\lambda\left(\overline{X}_{t}-X_{\alpha}\left(\rho_{t}\right)\right)_{k}\frac{\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}}{\left(r^{2}-\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}}\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})

(6.39)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{\widetilde{c}\sigma^{2}}\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})=:-\vartheta_{3}\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})\,.

(6.40)

Moreover since
\overline{X}_{t}\in K_{1,k}
and
2(2c-1)c\geq(1-c)^{2}
implied by the assumption, one has

2\left(2\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}-r^{2}\right)\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\geq\left(r^{2}-\left(\overline{X}_{t}-x_{0}\right)_{k}^{2}\right)^{2}\,,

(6.41)

which yields that
T_{2,k}(\overline{X}_{t})\geq 0
.

Concluding the proof:
Collecting estimates from above, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})]}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[T_{1,k}(\overline{X}_{t})+T_{2,k}(\overline{X}_{t})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[(T_{1,k}(\overline{X}_{t})+T_{2,k}(\overline{X}_{t}))\textbf{I}_{K_{1,k}\cap K_{2,k}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[(T_{1,k}(\overline{X}_{t})+T_{2,k}(\overline{X}_{t}))\textbf{I}_{K_{1,k}\cap K_{2,k}^{c}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[(T_{1,k}(\overline{X}_{t})+T_{2,k}(\overline{X}_{t}))\textbf{I}_{K_{1,k}^{c}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}]\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-d(\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}+\vartheta_{3})\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})]=:-\vartheta\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})]\,.

(6.42)

An application of Gronwall’s inequality concludes that

\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{t}\in B_{r}(x_{0}))\geq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{t})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{r}(\overline{X}_{0})]\exp(-\vartheta t)\,.

(6.43)

∎
