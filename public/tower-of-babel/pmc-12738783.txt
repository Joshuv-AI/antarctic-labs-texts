Regeneration-repair-strengthening mechanism in the oxidation film damage region of copper-nickel alloys under magnetic field effects
Authors: Jiamu Wang, Ke Gong, Xiuqi Hu, Mingsi Yang, Yuanming Wang, Feixiong Mao, Liping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopper-nickel alloy pipes in marine environments are prone to scouring by solid particles, which disrupt the bilayer film and induce scratches. These scratched regions are more vulnerable to perforation, potentially leading to premature pipe failure. Thus, rapidly repairing the film and restoring its corrosion resistance is a major challenge. This study simulates the “film damage-regeneration” process of copper-nickel alloys in marine environments, researching the regeneration of the film in scratched regions of a 70/30 Cu-Ni alloy in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution, under both nonmagnetic and magnetic field conditions. The results demonstrate that the application of a magnetic field accelerates the initial regeneration of the film and the recovery of its corrosion resistance, and through synergistic interactions with the scratched microregions, it alters the local pH conditions, suppresses the re-deposition of Cu2O, and significantly enhances the enrichment of NiO on the surface of the regenerated film. Ultimately, a single-layer film structure with enhanced corrosion resistance is formed.Subject terms: Corrosion, Materials science

Introduction

Copper–nickel alloys are key materials for pipelines in marine environments due to their excellent thermal conductivity and resistance to seawater corrosion1–5. They are widely used in condenser and heat exchanger pipelines in coastal power plants and ships. Among these, the 70/30 Cu–Ni alloy demonstrates superior performance under extreme conditions3,4,6,7, making it particularly favored. The corrosion resistance of copper–nickel alloys in seawater is attributed to the formation of a bilayer protective film on their surface, particularly the inner film, which consists of Cu2O containing Ni element and NiO1,3,8. The addition of Ni reduces the concentration of cation vacancies in Cu2O, thereby enhancing its corrosion resistance. The presence of NiO further improves the electrical resistance, providing effective protection to the alloy substrate. In addition, several existing studies have proposed further strategies to enhance the corrosion resistance of copper–nickel alloys, including alloy modification and pre-formation of oxide films9–13. Alloy modification involves the introduction of trace elements to promote the rapid formation of a more stable and compact oxide layer during the early stages of service. Pre-formation techniques, such as pretreatment with sodium dichromate or ferrous sulfate, help to form a more protective oxide film before the material is placed into service. When the film remains intact, copper–nickel alloys exhibit exceptional corrosion resistance.

However, Copper-nickel alloy pipelines, widely employed in practical applications, are frequently subjected to the erosive action of solid particles present in seawater. This process disrupts the protective film on the pipeline surface, resulting in the formation of scratches. Such scratched regions are significantly more susceptible to localized perforation, ultimately accelerating premature pipeline failure3,14–18. This damages the protective film on the surface, exposing the alloy substrate to the marine environment and significantly accelerating localized corrosion. At the same time, the regenerated protective film requires a long growth period to regain its corrosion resistance, during which it remains highly susceptible to further damage. This results in the gradual thinning of the alloy substrate in the scratched area, ultimately leading to perforation. Moreover, corrosion in a pipeline is “contagious”; once corrosion occurs at a certain point, it spreads rapidly, and if not repaired in time, it may cause more serious damage. Therefore, quickly and effectively restoring the integrity and corrosion protection of the protective film is a key challenge in extending the service life of pipelines. However, due to the complex structure of the pipelines and the prohibitively high financial burden of disassembly and handling, repairing the damaged protective film becomes exceedingly difficult. Moreover, there have been no reports of in-situ pipeline repairs. To address this issue, two potential solutions are proposed: one is to accelerate the regeneration of the protective film to enable self-repair, the other is to enhance the corrosion resistance of the regenerated film to balance the corrosion tendency between the damaged and undamaged areas. Consequently, the application of an external magnetic field as a noncontact conditioning method demonstrates the potential to achieve these goals simultaneously, providing an innovative solution for film repair.

The effect of magnetic fields on metal electrochemical reactions has been extensively investigated in previous studies19–27. These studies primarily focus on the Lorentz force acting on moving charged particles and the magnetic field gradient force acting on paramagnetic particles23,28. Both forces can enhance material exchange, thereby influencing metal electrochemical reaction processes. However, the specific effects of the magnetic field are significantly influenced by experimental conditions—such as the magnetism and concentration of ions in the electrolyte, the type of metal, and the strength and orientation of the magnetic field and may even produce two completely opposite outcomes: “decelerating reaction” or “enhancing reaction”. Existing research mainly focuses on the impact of magnetic fields on the corrosion of iron alloys, stainless steels, and aluminum alloys, while the effects on copper-nickel alloys have not yet been explored. Notably, according to our preliminary experimental results (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and 3), a perpendicular magnetic field can significantly accelerate the formation of a protective film on 70/30 Cu-Ni alloy, endowing it with superior corrosion resistance. Furthermore, when the pipeline’s film is damaged and scratched, the uniformity of the localized induced magnetic field is disrupted, creating a gradient magnetic field accompanied by a gradient in magnetic energy29. The 70/30 Cu–Ni alloy contains approximately 30% Ni, and Ni2+ exhibits significant paramagnetism, which may further influence the regeneration process of the film in the scratched area in response to the gradient magnetic field, thereby enhancing the local repair effect. In summary, influencing the mass transfer of substances by applying a magnetic field offers a viable approach for achieving in-situ repair of protective film on copper–nickel alloy pipelines.

Herein, we systematically investigated the regrowth behavior of 70/30 Cu-Ni alloy film regenerated in scratched areas under both nonmagnetic and 0.1 T magnetic field conditions in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution, simulating the “film damage-regeneration” process in marine environments. Under the application of a magnetic field, the regrowth rate of the film in the scratched area was significantly accelerated, and its corrosion resistance was notably enhanced. Further TEM, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Raman spectroscopy analyses indicated significant changes in the structure and composition of the regenerated film under the magnetic field, forming a unique single-layer film structure with the enrichment of NiO on the surface. The formation of this surface NiO-enriched single-layer film structure is closely related to the coupling effect between the magnetic field and the microenvironment of the scratch area. Additionally, we elucidated the regeneration mechanism of the 70/30 Cu–Ni alloy film under the combined influence of the magnetic field and the scratched microenvironment. This study provides a feasible approach for in situ repair of copper–nickel alloy pipeline film, with significant implications for the sustainable use of marine infrastructure in the future.

Results

Figure 1a shows the SEM image of the film regenerated in the scratched area under nonmagnetic conditions. Some particulate products are observed on the surface of the regenerated film. At 1 day, the particulate products are small and present only in specific regions of the film. As the regeneration time increases, the particulate products gradually cover the entire surface, and their size increases, stabilizing at day 7 (Supplementary Fig. 4), with little subsequent change. Figure 1b shows the SEM image of the regenerated film in the scratched area under a 0.1 T magnetic field. Compared to the regenerated film produced under nonmagnetic conditions, the film surface remains relatively smooth, free of particulate deposits, and exhibits no noticeable changes even as the regeneration time is extended. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5) reveals an enrichment of Cl elements in the areas with particulate products, while the film regenerated under the magnetic field shows an enrichment of Ni elements. Therefore, it can be inferred that the particulate products on the film under nonmagnetic conditions may primarily consist of the outer film formed by basic copper chloride in the duplex protective film of copper–nickel alloy, while the film regenerated in the presence of the magnetic field consists mainly of the inner film, and the result was further confirmed in the later tests. This difference may be attributed to the effect of the magnetic field.

Figure 1c shows the SVET test image of the regenerated film in the scratched area under nonmagnetic conditions. In Fig. 1c, a clear reaction signal is observed at the scratch, with a sharp and wide current peak. At the beginning of film regeneration, the peak current density difference between the scratched region and the surrounding area was 51 μA cm−2. As regeneration progressed, this difference gradually decreased to 32 μA cm−2. After 9 days, the peak current density difference further declined to 10 μA cm−2. Combined with the SEM results, as the regeneration time increases, the film gradually grows and stabilizes, and the trend of the electrochemical reaction weakens. However, compared to the surrounding area, the current density at the scratch remains higher, indicating that it still exhibits a higher tendency for electrochemical reaction30,31. Figure 1d presents the SVET test image of the regenerated film under a 0.1 T magnetic field. At the initial stage of regeneration, a reaction signal is observed at the scratch, but the difference in current peak density (19 μA cm−2) is much lower than that of the sample under nonmagnetic conditions. As regeneration progresses, the current peak density difference gradually decreases from 19 μA cm−2, with the reaction signal becoming negligible after 5 days. After 9 days of regeneration, the current density in the scratched region decreased to below that of the surrounding areas, with a difference of −5 μA cm−2. This indicates that the regenerated film shows better corrosion resistance than the adjacent film, with a marked reduction in the tendency for electrochemical reactions. The application of a magnetic field is shown to not only accelerate film growth in the scratched area but also significantly enhance its corrosion resistance.

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the regeneration process of thin films in the scratch-damaged regions of copper-nickel alloys under both no magnetic field and 0.1 T magnetic field conditions, and conducted comprehensive characterization and analysis of their structural composition and corrosion resistance. The main findings of the study include: 1. Under the coupled effect of the magnetic field and the scratch microenvironment, a single-layer regenerated film with unique structural characteristics was formed. 2. The applied magnetic field significantly accelerates the regeneration rate of the oxide film in the damaged region and the recovery of corrosion resistance, achieving rapid repair. To gain deeper insight into the specific influence of the magnetic field on the regeneration mechanism of the film in the scratched region, this paper provides a systematic and detailed discussion of the regeneration processes under both nonmagnetic and applied magnetic field conditions in the following sections.

Film regeneration mechanisms under nonmagnetic conditions

During the initial reaction stage, when the protective film is damaged, exposing the metal surface, copper dissolves as Cu+, accompanied by the dissolution of Fe and Ni, as shown in Fig. 5a. These ions then combine with Cl− in the solution to form the copper(I) chloride anion CuCl2−. This process represents the primary dissolution mechanism of copper-nickel alloys in seawater and sodium chloride solutions, as described by the following Eqs. (1)–(3)1,6,36.

As the reaction progresses, oxygen is gradually consumed, and replenishment in the scratched area becomes difficult, leading to a decrease in oxygen concentration, which in turn slows the Eq. (4). This explains why film regrowth is slower at the beginning of the reaction. However, as the reaction continues, an oxygen concentration gradient forms between the inside and outside of the scratch, creating an oxygen concentration cell that accelerates the anodic Eq. (1) occurring in the scratched microregion. This acceleration increases the dissolution of copper, resulting in the accumulation of metal cations such as Cu+. Cu ions and electrons must migrate through the Cu2O film, and the film growth process shifts from the dissolution and formation of Cu2O described in Eqs. The inner Cu2O film formed by inward growth with a slow rate is more compact and hence more protective than the outer Cu2O film formed by reprecipitation from dissolved species1,6,38. However, in this study, during the early stages of film regeneration, experimental results revealed the presence of the inner film even in regions where the outer film was absent. This indicates that, in the early stages of the reaction, the formation of Cu2O occurs via Eq. (5) and (9) simultaneously, rather than sequentially. At the same time, nickel oxide also forms within the inner film, and its formation mechanism has been studied6, so we shall not elaborate further here.

Notably, other studies have shown that after copper-nickel alloys are immersed in seawater, the inner film of the film is primarily composed of Cu2O and nickel oxides, while the outer film mainly consists of Cu2(OH)3Cl and Cu2O, and Cu2(OH)3Cl is predominantly found at the interface between the outer film and the solution1,6,38. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 15, TEM analysis of the film after 5 days of immersion for the un-scratched samples is consistent with the findings of previous studies. However, in this study, TEM results for the regenerated film in the scratched region reveal that a significant amount of Cu2(OH)3Cl nanoparticles are still present at the interface between the inner and outer layers, as well as in the middle of the outer layer. Moreover, the distribution of Cu2(OH)3Cl increases significantly throughout the outer layer as the regeneration time progresses (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 12). This may be due to the localized pH drop in the scratched area as the reaction progresses, gradually intensifying the acidic environment. As a result, Cu2O in the outer film continuously transforms into Cu2(OH)3Cl. Due to the inferior corrosion resistance of Cu2(OH)3Cl, the transformed film no longer provides adequate protection, thereby promoting the further conversion of Cu2O to Cu2(OH)3Cl, as shown in Fig. 5c. As the proportion of Cu2(OH)3Cl in the outer layer increases, the corrosion resistance progressively declines, while Cu2O at the interface of the inner and outer films gradually transforms into Cu2(OH)3Cl. As the thickness of the inner film decreases, Cu+ ions and electrons are more easily transported, accelerating the oxidation process toward the substrate and resulting in inward progression of corrosion. This phenomenon explains why, in the later stages of film regrowth, the thickness of the outer film increases significantly, while the inner film undergoes relatively little change, the overall mechanism of the reaction is shown in Fig. 5e. This process may also elucidate why copper–nickel alloy pipelines often experience corrosion-induced perforations before reaching their expected service life. When the interior of these pipelines is compromised by sandstone or localized pitting, conditions similar to those at a scratch site can develop, triggering the corrosion process described above and ultimately leading to perforation failure.

Film regeneration mechanisms under the synergistic influence of magnetic fields and scratch microenvironments

The effect of the magnetic field on the regeneration of the film is mainly influenced by the Lorentz force and the magnetic field gradient force. These forces affect the mass transfer kinetics and thus the regeneration of the film. The Lorentz force, denoted as FL, arises from the interaction between the magnetic field and the current, and can be mathematically expressed as Eqs. (10) and (11):

When charged particles move in the magnetic field, the Lorentz force deflects their trajectory, initiating Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects. This effect is equivalent to the stirring effect of the solution, its influence can reduce the thickness of the diffusion layer, thereby accelerating the mass transfer process of the solute. The reduction in thickness increases the supply of active substances, leading to an increase in the mass transfer limiting current. Additionally, MHD convection accelerates the renewal of the liquid, resulting in a decreased ion concentration gradient at the electrode surface40, which makes interfacial charge transfer more active and consequently speeds up the dissolution rate of Cu, Ni, and Fe atoms in the metal matrix, as represented by Eqs. (1), (12), and (13):

Furthermore, the MHD effect promotes the mass transfer of reactants such as oxygen, facilitating the entry of oxygen into the scratch micro-region and thereby accelerating the cathodic reaction. Consequently, the magnetic field accelerates the kinetics of the electrochemical reaction through the aforementioned processes, thus accelerating the cathodic reaction, as shown in Fig. 5e.

The Lorentz force accelerates the initial formation of the Cu2O film during the early stages of regeneration. However, as the film regenerates, the current density gradually decreases, reducing the effect of the Lorentz force during the later stages of the reaction.

The presence of scratches disturbs the uniformity of the magnetic field in the region, creating a magnetic field gradient, which increases the magnetic flux density in the scratched region24,26,41. Oxygen molecules are paramagnetic due to the presence of two unpaired electrons in their molecular orbitals. Similarly, Ni2+ ions exhibit paramagnetism as a result of their [Ar] 3d⁸ electron configuration, where two unpaired electrons in the 3d orbital generate a magnetic moment through electron spin. Fe2+ ions, with an electron configuration of [Ar] 3d⁶, contain four unpaired electrons, also making them strongly paramagnetic. In contrast, diamagnetic species such as Cu+ and CuCl₂− possess completely filled electron orbitals with all electrons paired. When subjected to an external magnetic field, these paired electrons produce an induced magnetic moment that opposes the applied field, resulting in a repulsive magnetic effect. Consequently, diamagnetic species tend to migrate toward regions of lower magnetic flux density. The resulting magnetic field gradient exerts a directional force: paramagnetic species such as Ni2+, Fe2+, and O2 are driven toward regions of higher magnetic flux density (i.e., the scratched area), while diamagnetic species such as Cu+ and CuCl₂− are repelled toward lower flux density zones (i.e., away from the surface), as illustrated in Fig. 5f. Moreover, to further validate the aforementioned process, we conducted COMSOL simulations to model the magnetic flux density distribution and the migration behavior of key species in the scratched region. The results, as shown in the supplementary material, are consistent with the descriptions provided above (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17 and Supplementary Movie 1).

Under the combined influence of the magnetic field gradient and Lorentz forces, the ions exchange and oxygen influx into the scratched region are accelerated, thereby enhancing the initial regeneration rate of the film. Furthermore, this mechanism prevents the formation of an oxygen concentration cell, which would otherwise lead to a decrease in pH. As the regeneration time increases, paramagnetic cations such as Ni2+ and Fe2+ accumulate significantly in the scratched region under the influence of the magnetic field gradient force. Local charge compensation occurs, and negative ions such as OH− and Cl− are drawn into the scratched region, while positive ions like Cu+ and H+ are repelled, as shown in Fig. 5g.

On one hand, this effect hinders the formation of Cu2O by the redeposition of Cu+ through Eqs. (1)–(3) and (5), leading to film regeneration under the influence of the magnetic field primarily driven by Eq. (9). Consequently, the regenerated film exhibits a single-layer film structure composed solely of the inner film; on the other hand, it induces an increase in OH- concentration and a decrease in H+ concentration within the scratched region, thereby raising the pH in that area. According to the thermodynamic equilibrium diagram37, under alkaline conditions, the re-oxidation of Cu2O is primarily driven by Eq. (7), where Cu2O is more likely to transform into CuO rather than Cu2(OH)3Cl. Cu2(OH)3Cl is unstable in environments with pH > 7 and dissolves. This explains why the regenerated film under a 0.1 T magnetic field exhibits a monolayer structure, which is essentially the inner layer of the original bilayer film—lacking Cu2(OH)3Cl but containing traces of CuO. Consequently, the film regenerated under these conditions is more compact, exhibiting enhanced corrosion resistance and reduced surface roughness.

Furthermore, existing studies on NiO in copper–nickel alloys indicate that NiO is primarily distributed within the inner layer of the film, with a notable enrichment at the substrate/film interface. NiO is generally regarded as playing a passivating role, contributing significantly to the high electrical resistance of the film. In this study, however, under magnetic field conditions, the regenerated film in the scratched region not only forms a single-layer film structure composed solely of the inner layer but also exhibits an enrichment of nickel oxide at the film interface/solution boundary.

Since the content of iron in the substrate is limited, and its products have little effect on improving the corrosion resistance of thin films, we only discuss its impact on the environment and the reaction, and its reaction products are not shown in the mechanism diagram.

In previous studies on the effect of magnetic fields on metal oxide films, the magnetic field typically only affects the density and roughness of the oxide film, and no reports have shown that a magnetic field can directly alter the structure of the oxide film. Therefore, we believe that the structural changes observed in this study are caused by the combined effect of the scratching environment and magnetic field. To further verify this conclusion, we also conducted immersion experiments on the un-scratched 70/30 Cu–Ni alloy under a magnetic field (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19), and found that its structure remained bilayer. This further confirms that the results obtained from this study were due to the combined effect of the scratching environment and the magnetic field.

Moreover, although the accelerating effect of the magnetic field on film regeneration has been thoroughly discussed in this study, it is important to note that in certain electrochemical systems, magnetic fields may also induce inhibitory effects. For example, the Lorentz force can accelerate electrochemical reactions by inducing MHD convection. In this study, such an effect facilitated the initial stage of film regeneration. In systems with confined geometries, such as occluded corrosion pits (OEPs), corrosion processes mainly rely on the localized accumulation of H⁺ and Cl− ions to sustain autocatalytic reactions44. However, MHD-induced convection may dilute the concentration of these autocatalytic species at the pit bottom, reduce local acidity, and thereby suppress the autocatalytic corrosion process, ultimately slowing down the reaction rate. Whether the effect of magnetic gradient force leads to an acceleration or deceleration of the corrosion process depends on the magnetic properties of the metal and its reactants. Reactants with different magnetic susceptibilities—paramagnetic or diamagnetic—tend to migrate in opposite directions under a magnetic gradient. For example, in studies on the inhibition of pitting corrosion in ferromagnetic metals under a magnetic field, the magnetic permeability of the ferromagnetic material is much higher than that of the surrounding medium, causing magnetic flux lines to locally concentrate near the alloy surface and form a significant magnetic gradient. Typically, the magnetic induction is stronger near the edges, driving reactants out of the pit, which reduces their concentration inside the pit, slows down the reaction rate, and thus achieves corrosion inhibition45. In the present study, the 70/30 copper–nickel alloy is not a ferromagnetic metal. The observed magnetic gradient force arises from geometric discontinuities that lead to the accumulation of magnetic flux density in the scratched region, forming a zone of high magnetic flux. Under the influence of this gradient magnetic force, oxygen is promoted to migrate toward the scratch area, accelerating the regeneration of the oxide film. At the same time, the re-deposition of copper oxides is suppressed, promoting direct oxidation and resulting in a denser, single-layer film structure. Additionally, the enrichment of Ni2+ in the regenerated region facilitates the doping of Ni into Cu2O and the formation of NiO, thereby improving the corrosion resistance of the film. Moreover, the increase in pH caused by the charge-compensation effect effectively inhibits the transformation of Cu2O into Cu2(OH)3Cl, a less corrosion-resistant compound, further enhancing the protective performance of the regenerated film.

In addition, to evaluate the long-term stability of the regenerated film, we conducted extended immersion tests, which demonstrated that the film formed under an applied magnetic field maintained favorable corrosion resistance even after prolonged exposure (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). Furthermore, to validate the broader applicability of the magnetic field’s effect on film regeneration in damaged regions, we performed supplementary experiments under varying temperatures, magnetic field strengths and the sharpness of the scratches. Although some differences were observed under different conditions, the role of the magnetic field remained consistent with the mechanisms discussed above (Supplementary Figs. 21–26).

In summary, the application of a perpendicular magnetic field accelerates the regrowth of the oxide film in the scratched region through the synergistic effects of the Lorentz force and the gradient magnetic force. Furthermore, the gradient magnetic field’s influence on paramagnetic species facilitates the formation of a unique single-layer film structure in the scratched area, with a pronounced enrichment of Ni element and NiO on its surface, significantly enhancing the corrosion resistance of the regenerated film. Based on these findings, we propose that the application of a magnetic field is an effective method for the in-situ repair of copper-nickel alloy pipelines, offering significant implications for the long-term durability and sustainability of marine engineering infrastructure.

Methods

Copper-nickel alloy sample preparation

The commercial 70/30 Cu–Ni alloy was used in this test, and the chemical composition of the alloy is shown in Supplementary Table 2. The size of the specimen was 10 mm × 10 mm × 3 mm block, and the electrochemical specimen were soldered with copper wires, and then the samples were sealed with epoxy resin, leaving the working surface of 10 mm × 10 mm. Samples were first ground using a series of sandpapers (grit sizes: 400#, 600#, 800#, 1200#, 1500#, 2000#), then mirror polishing treatment was performed using 0.25 μm monocrystalline diamond suspension, after rinsing with deionised water, the samples were degreased in acetone in ultrasonic bath, then removed and dried in a desiccator.

Copper-nickel alloy oxide film and the preparation of scratches

The polished 70/30 Cu–Ni alloy was immersed in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution at 25 °C for 14 days. Once the oxide film on the alloy surface had fully grown and stabilized, a scratch was made on the oxide film using a scratch tester, exposing the metallic substrate and ensure the consistency of the scratch depth. The final step of the experiment was to reimmerse the sample in the 3.5 wt% NaCl solution to simulate the regeneration process of the oxide film.

Magnetic field devices

In this study, the magnetic field was applied using a setup assembled and fixed with commercially available neodymium magnets, as schematically illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. 1. The sample orientation was precisely adjusted to be perpendicular to the magnetic field direction, and the magnetic field strength at the sample surface was maintained at 0.1 T using a Gauss meter.

Material characterizations

The sample morphology was examined using a Zeiss Sigma 300 field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. Elemental composition was determined by EDS at 15 kV. XPS measurements were performed with an AXIS SUPRAX XPS system, the signals were collected after sputtering for 0 and 60 s. All the binding energies in the XPS analysis were calibrated by C 1s signal at 284.8 eV and fitted with Avantage (version 6.9) software. Raman spectroscopy was conducted using a Renishaw in Via Reflex Raman spectrometer, and the samples were excited with the 532 nm spectral line with a laser power of 1 mW and spectra recorded in the range of from 100 to 1200 cm−1. FIB sample preparation was carried out using a Thermo Scientific Helios-G4-CX dual-beam SEM, the target scratch area was located under SEM mode, and a platinum protective layer was deposited to prevent structural damage during ion beam milling; then, a high-current ion beam was used for coarse cutting to prepare a cantilever structure, which was transferred onto a TEM grid using a micromanipulator; subsequently, a low-current ion beam was employed for fine thinning until achieving a suitable thickness for transmission electron microscopy observation; finally, a low-energy ion beam was applied for surface cleaning to remove milling damage and ensure sample quality. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted on a Talos F200X microscope at 200 kV, while high-resolution imaging was performed on a Thermo Fisher Spectra 300 double-aberration-corrected TEM. TEM data were analyzed using DigitalMicrograph (version 3.7) software.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements, including potentiodynamic polarization tests and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), were conducted using a Gamry electrochemical workstation with a conventional three-electrode system at room temperature. The 70/30 copper-nickel alloy samples served as the working electrode, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) was used as the reference electrode, and a platinum foil electrode (1 cm2) acted as the counter electrode. Prior to each EIS measurement, open circuit potential (OCP) was monitored for 30 min to ensure system stability. EIS measurements were then carried out over a frequency range of 100 kHz–10 mHz, using a 10 mV sinusoidal perturbation (vs.OCP). Potentiodynamic polarization tests were conducted on parallel samples in 3.5 wt% NaCl solution, with a scanning range from −0.8 V to 0.8 V (vs. SCE) at a scan rate of 0.1667 mV/s. All electrochemical data were analyzed using Gamry Echem Analyst software (version 6.11).
